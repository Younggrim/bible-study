*Setting:* The temple vision of chapters 40–48 continues (573 BC). God's glory has returned to the temple (ch 43), and chapter 44 set the rules for who may come near: the faithful priests of Zadok's line serve at the altar, the other Levites serve in the courts, and the prince may sit in the closed east gate. Chapter 45 moves outward from the temple to the land around it, and from the priests to the prince. It sets aside a holy district at the center of the land, gives the prince his own estate so he will not seize anyone else's, demands honest weights and measures, sets the offerings the people give and the prince provides, and begins a new calendar of feasts. The exiles listening had lost their land entirely. The chapter promises it back, but on God's terms: God at the center, and justice from the top down.

#### The Holy District (vv1–6)
- **"When you allot the land as an inheritance, you shall set apart for the LORD a portion" (v1).** "Allot" is literally "cause to fall," from casting lots, as Joshua divided the land (Josh 14:2). "Portion" is *terumah*, a "contribution" or "lifted-up offering," the word used for the priests' share of sacrifices. The land itself gives God a tithe-like offering before anyone else gets a share. The full division of the land comes in chapters 47–48. This holy center is described first because everything else is arranged around it.
- **"25,000 cubits long and 20,000 cubits broad" (v1).** The traditional Hebrew text reads 10,000 for the width, and the Greek translation reads 20,000. The ESV follows the Greek, because vv3–5 describe two strips of 10,000 each, and 48:8–20 adds a city strip to make a perfect square. At the long cubit of about 20.5 inches, 25,000 cubits is roughly 8 miles (13 km), so the whole district with the city was a square about 8⅓ miles on each side. Some, including the KJV and JFB, take the unit as reeds, which would make it six times larger. The ESV and most modern versions supply "cubits."
- **"A square plot of 500 by 500 cubits shall be for the sanctuary" (v2).** This is the walled temple area of 42:15–20. **"Fifty cubits for an open space around it"** is a buffer, about 85 feet, so nothing common pressed against the sanctuary. The word for "open space" is the one used for the pastureland around the Levites' towns (Num 35:2–5).
- **"The sanctuary, the Most Holy Place" (v3).** The temple stands in the priests' strip as the Most Holy Place once stood inside the tabernacle. The Tyndale notes point out the pattern: the same graded holiness that ran from the Most Holy Place through the inner and outer courts now spreads across the land. The closer to the center, the holier.
- **"For the priests, who minister in the sanctuary and approach the LORD… a place for their houses" (v4).** The priests had no tribal inheritance (44:28). They do not get fields to farm. They get a place to live near the house they serve.
- **"Another section… for the Levites… as their possession for cities to live in" (v5).** Under Joshua the Levites were scattered through forty-eight towns across all the tribes (Num 35:1–8; Josh 21). That served to put teachers of the law among all the people. Here they are gathered next to the sanctuary. The traditional Hebrew text reads "twenty chambers" here. The ESV follows the Greek translation, "cities to live in," which needs only a small change in the Hebrew letters.
- **"The property of the city… 5,000 cubits broad… It shall belong to the whole house of Israel" (v6).** The city, never named Jerusalem in these chapters, gets the narrower southern strip (48:15–19). Jerusalem had been "the city of David," captured by David and in a sense his own (2 Sam 5:6–9). This city belongs to no single tribe or ruler. It belongs to everyone. Its name is revealed only at the very end of the book (48:35).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the holy portion "the first-fruits that sanctify the lump," borrowing Paul's picture (Rom 11:16): set aside first for God, it makes the whole land holy. JFB reads the measures as reeds, noting that cubits are named in v2 but not in v1, and so pictures a much larger area. Guzik, following Vawter and Hoppe, takes the units as cubits and works out the area of each strip. He also notes, with Wiersbe, the change from Levites scattered among the tribes to Levites gathered near the sanctuary.

#### The Portion for the Prince (vv7–25)
- **"To the prince shall belong the land on both sides of the holy district" (v7).** The prince's estate runs east to the Jordan and west to the Mediterranean on either side of the central square. The Tyndale notes observe what this does: the prince is honored with a large holding right beside the sanctuary, but the temple, not the palace, is at the center. Compare 43:8, where the old palace shared a wall with God's house.
- **"My princes shall no more oppress my people, but they shall let the house of Israel have the land according to their tribes" (v8).** This is why the prince is given land. A ruler with his own estate has no excuse to grab anyone else's. Israel's kings had done exactly that. Samuel warned the people that a king "will take the best of your fields" (1 Sam 8:14), and Ahab and Jezebel had Naboth killed for his vineyard (1 Kings 21). The law even forbade the prince from taking the people's inheritance "to thrust them out of their property" (46:18).
- **"Enough, O princes of Israel! Put away violence and oppression" (v9).** The vision suddenly speaks to the present. "Enough" is the same cry as in 44:6. The leaders of Ezekiel's own day are told to change now, not wait for the future. "Execute justice and righteousness" is the job description of a king in Israel (Jer 22:3, 15–16; Ps 72:1–4). King Jehoiakim had built his palace with unpaid labor (Jer 22:13–17).
- **"Cease your evictions of my people" (v9).** "Evictions" is a rare word meaning "drivings-out." The prophets condemned the powerful who "join house to house, who add field to field" (Isa 5:8) and who "covet fields and seize them" (Mic 2:2). Losing family land in Israel meant losing a family's place in the covenant inheritance.
- **"You shall have just balances, a just ephah, and a just bath" (v10).** Honest measures were a law of Moses (Lev 19:35–36; Deut 25:13–16), and cheating was a constant prophetic complaint (Amos 8:5; Mic 6:10–11). Here the command comes right after the warning to princes. The Tyndale notes suggest why: the prince collected the people's offerings (v16), and a ruler who rigged the scales could skim the difference.
- **"The ephah and the bath shall be of the same measure… one tenth of a homer" (v11).** The ephah was the dry measure (perhaps about 5 to 6 gallons, though estimates vary widely), and the bath was the liquid measure of the same size. The homer was ten times that. "Homer" (*chomer*) is spelled with the same consonants as the word for donkey, and it is often explained as a donkey's load, though that link is not certain.
- **"The shekel shall be twenty gerahs" (v12).** This matches the law of Moses (Exod 30:13). A gerah was a small weight, perhaps a grain or bean. **"Twenty shekels plus twenty-five shekels plus fifteen shekels shall be your mina."** That adds up to sixty shekels, the Babylonian standard, though the Greek translation reads fifty. Why the odd way of stating it is not clear. JFB suggests three weights or coins of different sizes. The point is plain enough: one fixed standard, not whatever the seller happened to use.
- **"This is the offering that you shall make" (v13).** The people give a set share: one sixtieth of their wheat and barley (a sixth of an ephah from each homer), one hundredth of their oil (v14), and one sheep in two hundred (v15). These were modest amounts, much less than a tithe, because they were for the temple offerings only.
- **"From the watering places of Israel" (v15).** The Hebrew is unusual, and the Greek reads "from all the families of Israel." The ESV keeps the Hebrew: sheep from Israel's well-watered pastures. The offerings were "to make atonement for them." "Atonement" (*kipper*) means to cover or wipe clean, so that the people were put right with God.
- **"All the people of the land shall be obliged to give this offering to the prince" (v16).** The people give to the prince, and the prince is responsible for the temple's festival offerings. David and Solomon gave lavishly for the temple (1 Chr 29:1–5; 2 Chr 8:12–13), and Hezekiah and Josiah provided animals from their own flocks for the great festivals (2 Chr 30:24; 35:7).
- **"It shall be the prince's duty to furnish the burnt offerings, grain offerings, and drink offerings" (v17).** The prince's highest honor is to serve as the people's sponsor before God, providing for worship on feasts, new moons and Sabbaths. He does not offer the sacrifices himself. The priests do that (46:2). King Uzziah was struck with leprosy for burning incense (2 Chr 26:16–21), and the line between ruler and priest is kept clear here.
- **"In the first month, on the first day of the month… purify the sanctuary" (v18).** The new year began in spring, in the month later called Nisan (Exod 12:2). The cleansing rite here is not in the law of Moses. The nearest parallel is the Day of Atonement, when the sanctuary itself was cleansed (Lev 16:16–19), but that fell in the seventh month, and Ezekiel's calendar does not mention it at all.
- **"Put it on the doorposts of the temple, the four corners of the ledge of the altar, and the posts of the gate" (v19).** The blood marks the house, the altar and the gate, the three key places of approach. Blood on doorposts at the start of the first month inevitably recalls the first Passover in Egypt (Exod 12:7, 22–23).
- **"On the seventh day of the month for anyone who has sinned through error or ignorance" (v20).** The law made a sharp distinction between sins done "with a high hand," in open defiance, and unintentional sins (Num 15:22–31; Lev 4:2). Even sins committed without knowing could stain God's house. The Greek translation reads "in the seventh month, on the first day," giving a cleansing at the start of each half of the year, and some scholars prefer it. The ESV follows the Hebrew.
- **"In the first month, on the fourteenth day of the month, you shall celebrate the Feast of the Passover" (v21).** Passover, followed by the seven days of Unleavened Bread, remains as in Exodus 12 and Leviticus 23:5–8. It is the one feast named in the chapter.
- **"The prince shall provide for himself and all the people of the land a young bull for a sin offering" (v22).** The prince offers for "himself" as well as the people. This is a main reason many readers think the prince is not the Messiah, who had no sin to atone for (Heb 7:26–27).
- **"Seven young bulls and seven rams… on each of the seven days" (v23).** This is much more than Numbers 28:19–24 required (two bulls, one ram and seven lambs each day). JFB and the Tyndale notes both remark on the change. The grain offerings (v24) are also larger: an ephah for each bull and ram, and a hin of oil (about a gallon) with each.
- **"In the seventh month, on the fifteenth day… for the seven days of the feast" (v25).** This is the Feast of Booths, or Tabernacles (Lev 23:33–36), though the text does not name it. It gets the same offerings as Passover, six months later.
- **What is missing.** Moses gave Israel three pilgrim feasts: Passover/Unleavened Bread, Weeks (Pentecost), and Booths (Deut 16:16). Ezekiel names only two, and he leaves out the Day of Atonement and the Feast of Trumpets. Interpreters explain the gaps differently. Some think the vision is a selection, not a full calendar. Some think the changes mean a new order in which sin is dealt with by regular cleansing rather than one great annual day. Those who expect a future millennial temple often say the Day of Atonement is absent because its meaning has been fulfilled in Christ. The text itself does not say.
- **Commentators:** Henry joins the prince's estate to its purpose: "My princes shall no more oppress my people," applying Isaiah's promise that God will make "the officers peace and the exactors righteousness" (Isa 60:17). He also stresses that "It shall be the prince's part to provide all the offerings." JFB observes that "the standard weights were lost when the Chaldeans destroyed the temple," which helps explain why a fixed standard needed restating, notes the Greek reading of fifty shekels, and says the new-year cleansing was "not mentioned under the Levitical law." He links it to the later rededication under Judas Maccabeus. JFB has no note on vv9–11 or on v25. Guzik takes v9 as a present call to Ezekiel's own leaders as well as a future promise, recalls Feinberg's comparison with Ahab and Naboth, and with Clarke admits that the annual cleansing has no precedent in Moses. He follows Alexander that there will be no special Day of Atonement in that future age.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter pictures a land with God's house at its center, a ruler who has enough and so takes nothing, honest weights in every market, and a year that opens with blood on the doorposts of God's house. Every part of it points beyond itself. The prince must offer a sin offering "for himself," but Jesus is the King who "has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins" (Heb 7:27). He is "Christ, our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7), and his blood cleanses not a building but his people, even "the unintentional sins of the people" (Heb 9:7, 14). Under his rule, the justice the princes of Israel were told to practice, "Put away violence and oppression, and execute justice and righteousness," finally becomes the shape of a kingdom.
