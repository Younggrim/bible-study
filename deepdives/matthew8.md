*Setting:* Galilee, early in Jesus' ministry, around AD 28. The Sermon on the Mount has just ended with the crowds amazed at Jesus' authority in teaching (7:28–29). Chapters 8 and 9 now show that same authority in action. Matthew gathers ten miracles here, mostly in three groups of three, with short scenes about discipleship in between. Many of the stories come in a different order in Mark and Luke. Matthew is arranging by theme, not strict sequence, which ancient biographers often did. In this chapter Jesus touches the untouchable, welcomes a Gentile, heals with a word, calls for costly discipleship, commands the sea, and drives out demons. Each scene raises the question the disciples ask at the storm: "What sort of man is this?"

#### Jesus Cleanses a Leper (vv1–4)
- **"When he came down from the mountain, great crowds followed him" (v1).** The words echo Moses coming down from Sinai (Exod 34:29). The teacher of chapters 5–7 now acts.
- **"A leper" (v2).** Biblical "leprosy" covered a range of skin diseases, not only what is now called Hansen's disease. The law declared the sufferer unclean: he had to tear his clothes, cover his lip, cry "Unclean, unclean," and live outside the camp (Lev 13:45–46). It meant losing family, worship and touch. Lepers were treated almost as the living dead. When Naaman's letter asked the king of Israel to cure leprosy, the king tore his robes: "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" (2 Kings 5:7). Curing it was God's work.
- **"Knelt before him" (v2).** The verb (*proskyneō*) means to bow down or prostrate oneself. Matthew uses it often, including for the magi (2:11) and the disciples in the boat (14:33). It can mean homage to a king or worship of God, and Matthew's readers would hear the second meaning growing louder through the Gospel.
- **"Lord, if you will, you can make me clean" (v2).** He has no doubt of Jesus' power, only of his willingness. Few would have wanted to come near a leper.
- **"Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him" (v3).** Touching a leper made a person unclean (Lev 5:3). Jesus could have healed with a word, as he does in the next story. He chose to touch. Instead of the leper's uncleanness passing to Jesus, Jesus' cleanness passed to the leper. This man may not have felt a human touch in years.
- **"I will; be clean" (v3).** His answer repeats the man's words: "if you will… I will." The healing is immediate.
- **"Say nothing to anyone, but go, show yourself to the priest" (v4).** Leviticus 14 set out a detailed ceremony for a healed leper, with birds, offerings and a priest's inspection. Jesus sends the man to keep the law (5:17), which would also be "a proof to them": a priest in Jerusalem would have to declare clean a man healed by Jesus. The command to silence fits Jesus' pattern of not stirring up crowds who wanted a wonder-worker or a political Messiah. Mark says the man spread the news anyway, so Jesus could no longer enter towns openly (Mark 1:45).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the leper's request brief, "expressive of a confidence that knew no doubt," and of Jesus' reply says "How majestic those two words!" Henry and Guzik both treat leprosy as a picture of sin: it corrupts, isolates and makes a person dead while living. Guzik adds that the touch met a need for compassion more than a need for power. He also cites Barclay that the word for kneeling is used elsewhere only for worship of God, but that goes too far: Matthew uses the same verb for a servant bowing before his master (18:26). The meaning depends on the context.

#### The Faith of a Centurion (vv5–13)
- **Capernaum (v5).** This fishing town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee was Jesus' base (4:13). It sat on a main trade road and had a customs post (9:9) and a small garrison.
- **"A centurion" (v5).** He commanded about a hundred soldiers. In Galilee he probably served Herod Antipas rather than Rome directly, but he was a Gentile and part of the occupying order. Luke adds that he loved the Jewish nation and had built their synagogue, and that he sent Jewish elders and then friends to ask for him (Luke 7:3–6). Matthew tells it more briefly, as if he came in person, the way one might say "the general took the city" when his soldiers did. The request is his either way.
- **"My servant is lying paralyzed at home, suffering terribly" (v6).** The word (*pais*) can mean "boy," "child" or "servant." Luke uses *doulos*, "slave," and adds that the servant was dear to him (Luke 7:2). A Roman officer pleading for a slave was unusual.
- **"I will come and heal him" (v7).** A devout Jew entering a Gentile home risked becoming ceremonially unclean (Acts 10:28). Some scholars read Jesus' words as a question: "Shall I come and heal him?" That would test the man as Jesus later tests the Canaanite woman (15:21–28). Either way, Jesus is willing to go.
- **"Lord, I am not worthy to have you come under my roof" (v8).** A man with real power and status calls himself unworthy before a Jewish teacher. He also spares Jesus the problem of entering his house.
- **"For I too am a man under authority" (v9).** His logic is military. He obeys those above him and is obeyed by those below him, because he carries the authority of Rome behind him. He sees that Jesus carries God's authority, so sickness must obey him the way soldiers obey an order. Distance does not matter. "Only say the word."
- **"He marveled" (v10).** The Gospels record Jesus marveling at only two things: this man's faith (also Luke 7:9) and the unbelief of Nazareth (Mark 6:6). "With no one in Israel have I found such faith." The great example of faith in this Gospel so far is a Gentile soldier.
- **"Many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (v11).** The prophets pictured a great feast God would spread for all peoples on his mountain (Isa 25:6–8) and nations streaming to Zion (Isa 2:2–3; 49:12; 59:19). The Gentiles will sit at the patriarchs' table, as God promised Abraham (Gen 12:3). Matthew's Gospel began with Gentile magi coming to worship and ends with "make disciples of all nations" (28:19).
- **"The sons of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness" (v12).** "Sons of the kingdom" means those who assumed their birth guaranteed a seat. Jesus reverses it. "Outer darkness" is a phrase only Matthew uses (also 22:13; 25:30), the dark outside a lit banquet hall. "Weeping and gnashing of teeth," grief and bitter anger, appears six times in Matthew. Jesus speaks of judgment soberly and often.
- **"Let it be done for you as you have believed" (v13).** The servant is healed "at that very moment," at a distance, by a word.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Jesus' praise "an honour to a Gentile" and a rebuke to Israel's unbelief. Guzik notes how considerate the centurion was, both to his servant and to Jesus, and draws from the banquet a picture of heaven as a place of rest, fellowship and many nations. JFB does not comment on this section in Matthew, sending readers to Luke 7:1–10, and treats Luke's order as the original setting.

#### Jesus Heals Many (vv14–17)
- **"Peter's house… his mother-in-law" (v14).** Peter was married, and Paul later mentions that Peter's wife traveled with him (1 Cor 9:5). Archaeologists at Capernaum have uncovered a house that was turned into a place of Christian worship within a century or so and later had an octagonal church built over it. Many think it was Peter's house, though that cannot be proved.
- **"He touched her hand, and the fever left her, and she rose and began to serve him" (v15).** No one asks this time. Jesus sees and acts. Her healing is complete enough that she gets up and serves at once, with no days of recovery. "Serve" (*diakoneō*) is the word behind "deacon," and she responds to grace by serving.
- **"That evening" (v16).** Mark explains why the crowd came at sunset: it was the Sabbath, and people waited until it ended to carry the sick (Mark 1:21, 32). Jesus "cast out the spirits with a word and healed all."
- **"He took our illnesses and bore our diseases" (v17).** Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4, from the passage about the suffering servant. Matthew's wording follows the Hebrew, which speaks of sickness and pain, rather than the Greek Old Testament of his day, which turned the verse into "he bears our sins." The two belong together. Sickness is part of the curse that came with sin, and the servant carries both. Peter quotes the next verse about sin: "by his wounds you have been healed" (1 Pet 2:24; Isa 53:5).
- **What this means for healing.** Christians differ on how much physical healing the cross guarantees now. Some traditions teach that healing is promised to faith in this life. Most read Matthew to say that Jesus' healings were signs that the servant had come, and that the full undoing of sickness comes with the resurrection body (Rom 8:23; Rev 21:4), though God still heals in answer to prayer (James 5:14–15).
- **Commentators:** Henry, and Guzik after him, use Peter's marriage to argue against requiring priests to be celibate. Guzik reads Isaiah 53:4 as covering both spiritual and physical healing, with full physical healing waiting for the resurrection, and notes that the variety of people and methods in these healings shows that God chooses how he heals. JFB passes over this section, sending readers to Mark.

#### The Cost of Following Jesus (vv18–22)
- **"He gave orders to go over to the other side" (v18).** The crowd is growing, and Jesus moves away from it. The two short scenes that follow interrupt the miracles to ask what following him really involves.
- **"Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go" (v19).** A scribe, an expert in the Law, volunteers. Students usually chose their rabbi, and he is offering himself. He calls Jesus "Teacher," a respectful title, but in Matthew it is usually what outsiders call Jesus.
- **"Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (v20).** This is the first time "Son of Man" appears in Matthew, and it was Jesus' favorite way of speaking of himself. It comes from Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" receives everlasting dominion from God. In Hebrew idiom it can also simply mean "a human being" (Ps 8:4). Jesus fills it with both: the glorious one of Daniel, who has less of a home than a fox. Jesus does not turn the scribe away. He tells him the truth about the cost.
- **"Lord, let me first go and bury my father" (v21).** Burying a parent was one of the most sacred duties in Judaism, important enough that some rabbis exempted mourners from other religious duties. Many scholars think the father may not yet have died: the man may be saying "let me wait until my father dies and I have my inheritance," which could take years. Even if the father had just died, Jesus' answer is meant to shock.
- **"Follow me, and leave the dead to bury their own dead" (v22).** The likely meaning is: let the spiritually dead bury the physically dead. Jesus is not against honoring parents (15:4–6). He is saying that his call outranks every other claim, even the most sacred family duty. Only God could make that kind of claim (10:37). Luke adds a third would-be follower and the line about putting a hand to the plow and looking back (Luke 9:57–62).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the first man the "rash" disciple and the second the "procrastinating" one, and takes the father to be old but not yet dead. Henry thinks the scribe hoped for worldly advantage, and says that devotion to God must come before even devotion to parents. Guzik notes that Jesus never made discipleship sound easier than it is, in contrast to much modern evangelism, and that the title Son of Man combines the glory of Daniel 7 with the humility of Psalm 8.

#### Jesus Calms a Storm (vv23–27)
- **"His disciples followed him" (v23).** Right after the scenes about following Jesus, the disciples follow him into a boat, and into a storm.
- **"A great storm on the sea" (v24).** The Sea of Galilee lies about 700 feet below sea level, surrounded by hills cut by valleys. Cool air from the heights can funnel down and whip the lake into dangerous waves with little warning. Matthew's word for storm is *seismos*, normally "earthquake" or "shaking." He uses it again for the earthquakes at the cross and the empty tomb (27:54; 28:2). It was a violent upheaval.
- **"But he was asleep" (v24).** This storm, told in three Gospels, is the only time Jesus is shown asleep. He was truly human and truly tired. It recalls Jonah asleep below deck in a storm (Jonah 1:5), but the contrast is sharp: Jonah slept running from God, and Jesus sleeps trusting his Father (Ps 4:8).
- **"Save us, Lord; we are perishing" (v25).** Their cry is a prayer, and it is the right one, even if fear is driving it.
- **"Why are you afraid, O you of little faith?" (v26).** "Little faith" (*oligopistos*) is a word Matthew uses for disciples who have some faith but let fear swamp it (6:30; 14:31; 16:8). Jesus speaks to their fear before he speaks to the storm.
- **"He rose and rebuked the winds and the sea, and there was a great calm" (v26).** "Rebuked" is the word used for driving out demons. The Old Testament says that God alone rules the raging sea: "you still them" (Ps 89:9), and "he made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed" (Ps 107:29). A "great storm" gives way to a "great calm."
- **"What sort of man is this, that even winds and sea obey him?" (v27).** This is the question the whole chapter raises. The answer comes in the next scene, from an unexpected source.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks the storm was allowed "for their sakes," to train their faith, and traces their panic to weak faith rather than no faith. Guzik sees Jesus' sleep as true humanity and the calming of the sea as true deity, and passes on Adam Clarke's suggestion that Satan may have stirred the storm, which is only a guess. JFB defers to its notes on Mark.

#### Jesus Heals Two Men with Demons (vv28–34)
- **"The country of the Gadarenes" (v28).** This is on the east side of the lake, mostly Gentile territory in the Decapolis, a league of Greek-style cities. Manuscripts and Gospels differ on the name: Matthew has Gadarenes, Mark and Luke Gerasenes, and some manuscripts have Gergesenes. Gadara was a town about six miles from the lake, and its territory may have reached the shore. Gerasa was a larger city farther away, which may have given its name to the wider region. Many place the event near Kursi on the eastern shore, where the hills drop steeply to the water. The variety reflects naming of a region, not a contradiction about what happened.
- **"Two demon-possessed men" (v28).** Mark and Luke tell of one man, named Legion (Mark 5:1–20; Luke 8:26–39). Matthew often mentions two where others mention one (two blind men in 20:30). Probably the other Gospels focus on the more prominent man who spoke and later told his story. Two witnesses also met the law's standard for testimony (Deut 19:15).
- **"Coming out of the tombs, so fierce that no one could pass that way" (v28).** Tombs were unclean places. These men lived among the dead, and they were violent. Mark adds that chains could not hold the man and that he cut himself with stones.
- **"What have you to do with us, O Son of God?" (v29).** Here is the answer to the disciples' question. The demons know exactly who he is. Satan called him "Son of God" in the wilderness (4:3, 6), and now the demons do. Knowing who Jesus is does not make them his (James 2:19).
- **"Have you come here to torment us before the time?" (v29).** The demons know there is an appointed day of judgment (Rev 20:10; Jude 6). What they did not expect was the Judge arriving early. In Jesus, the end-time kingdom has broken into the present (12:28).
- **The pigs (vv30–32).** A large herd of pigs shows that this was Gentile land, since Jews did not keep them. The demons beg to be sent into the pigs, and Jesus says one word: "Go." The herd plunges down the steep bank and drowns. Christians have long asked why Jesus allowed the loss. The text does not say. Possibilities include visible proof that the men were truly freed, a picture of what the demons meant to do to the men, and a sign of the destructive nature of evil spirits. The sea, which in the Bible often stands for chaos, swallows them, just after Jesus has silenced it.
- **"All the city came out… they begged him to leave their region" (v34).** The men are healed, and the town's reaction is fear and loss. They count the cost of the pigs and ask Jesus to go. Mark adds that the freed man begged to come with Jesus but was sent home to tell what God had done (Mark 5:18–20).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the demons confess Christ yet remain his enemies, so knowledge alone does not mark a saint, and of the townspeople he writes, "There are a great many who prefer their swine before their Saviour." He also suggests the pigs gave visible proof of spirits to Sadducees, who denied them, which is his own guess. Guzik thinks Matthew names two men where Mark and Luke name one probably because one was the more striking case. JFB comments on this event under Mark.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every scene in this chapter points to who Jesus is. He touches the unclean and makes them clean. He commands sickness at a distance and the sea at close range, as only God does. He bears our illnesses, as the servant of Isaiah 53, on the way to bearing our sins. He has nowhere to lay his head, yet demons tremble at his word. And he gives the answer to every sickness, storm and evil power that the gospel reveals: the Son of God has come. Some, like a Gentile soldier, believe and are welcomed to the feast. Others, faced with the same Lord, ask him to leave.
