*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Chapter 26 closed the long law section (chapters 12–26) with Israel and the LORD declaring themselves bound to each other. Chapter 27 is a pause in Moses' speeches. Here Moses gives instructions for a ceremony to be held once Israel is inside the land: large plastered stones with the law written on them, an altar on Mount Ebal, and the twelve tribes divided between two mountains to hear the covenant read and answer "Amen." Moses himself would not be there. Joshua carried it out after the victory at Ai (Josh 8:30–35). Chapter 28 then sets out the full blessings and curses of the covenant. Moses first mentioned this ceremony back in 11:26–30.

#### The Altar on Mount Ebal (vv1–8)
- **"Moses and the elders of Israel commanded the people" (v1).** This is the first time in Deuteronomy that the elders speak with Moses. In v9 the priests join him. Moses is about to die, and the leaders who will carry on are shown standing with him. The law does not depend on one man.
- **"On the day you cross over the Jordan… you shall set up large stones and plaster them with plaster" (v2).** "On the day" is a Hebrew way of saying "at the time." The ceremony actually took place some days later, after Jericho and Ai fell (Josh 8). Shechem, between Ebal and Gerizim, was about 20–30 miles from the crossing point, in the heart of the land.
- **The plaster and the writing (vv2–3, 8).** This was a known ancient practice: coat stones with lime plaster and write on the smooth white surface while it is fresh, or paint letters on it. Egyptian monuments used similar methods, and an inscription written in ink on plaster has been found at Deir Alla in the Jordan Valley (the Balaam text, about 800 BC). The stones were a public notice board. Israel's law was not a secret priestly code. It was written out **"very plainly"** (v8) for anyone to read.
- **"All the words of this law" (v3).** How much was written is debated. The Tyndale notes think the space suggests the Ten Commandments. JFB thinks it was more likely the blessings and curses. Others suggest the core laws of chapters 12–26. Large stones covered in plaster could hold a good deal of text. Joshua 8:32 says Joshua wrote "a copy of the law of Moses."
- **"On Mount Ebal" (v4).** Mount Ebal (about 3,080 feet) and Mount Gerizim (about 2,850 feet) face each other across a narrow valley at Shechem, where Abraham built his first altar in the land (Gen 12:6–7) and Jacob bought land (Gen 33:18–20). Ebal is to the north and fairly bare. Gerizim to the south is greener. The Samaritan Pentateuch reads "Gerizim" here instead of "Ebal." The Samaritans later built their temple on Gerizim, and most scholars see the change as a later alteration to support that. Jesus' conversation with the Samaritan woman ("our fathers worshiped on this mountain," John 4:20) took place in the shadow of these hills.
- **"An altar of stones. You shall wield no iron tool on them" (v5).** This repeats the altar law from Sinai (Exod 20:25). Canaanite altars were usually built of cut blocks. Israel's altar was to be of rough field stones, untouched by human craftsmanship. Nothing human was to be added to the place where God meets sinners, and nothing was to be carved that could become an image. An archaeological site on Mount Ebal, excavated by Adam Zertal in the 1980s, has been identified by some as this altar, though others think it was a watchtower or farm building. The question is still debated.
- **"Burnt offerings… and peace offerings… and you shall rejoice" (vv6–7).** Burnt offerings were wholly burned, and spoke of atonement and full dedication. Peace offerings (sometimes called fellowship offerings) were shared, and the worshipers ate part of them in God's presence (Lev 3; 7:11–15). So the order is law written, sacrifice made, and then a joyful meal.
- **The altar on the mountain of curse.** The altar is put on Ebal, the mountain of the curses (v13), not Gerizim, the mountain of blessing. Many readers have seen meaning in that: sacrifice and atonement are provided exactly where the law's curse falls.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Deuteronomy 27 opens Moses' third sermon, on the covenant, after a first on history and a second on law. He follows Morgan: the law demands obedience, and the altar shows the only way back to God after failure. He also says the uncut stones teach that human skill must not take attention from what God provides. JFB thinks Moses "learned the art from the Egyptians," and says the stones were left "in their natural state, as if a chisel would communicate pollution to them." He sees in the stones and altar "the law which condemned, and the typical expiation," and calls these "the two great principles of revealed religion." Henry: "The word and prayer must go together."

#### Curses from Mount Ebal (vv9–26)
- **"Keep silence and hear, O Israel: this day you have become the people of the LORD your God" (v9).** Israel had been God's people since Sinai. The Tyndale notes explain that each renewal of the covenant made them his people in a fresh way. **"Keep silence"** is a rare word, a call to hush before something solemn.
- **The tribes divided (vv12–13).** Six tribes stand on Gerizim **"to bless"**: Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Joseph and Benjamin, all sons of Leah and Rachel, Jacob's two wives. Six stand on Ebal **"for the curse"**: Reuben and Zebulun (Leah's sons) with Gad, Asher, Dan and Naphtali, the sons of the servant women. Reuben lost his firstborn place by his sin with his father's concubine (Gen 35:22; 49:3–4), which may be why he is placed here. In Joshua's ceremony the ark and the priests stood in the valley between the two groups (Josh 8:33). The valley acts like a natural amphitheater, and visitors have tested that a voice carries well there.
- **"The Levites shall declare… in a loud voice" (v14).** Levi is listed among the tribes on Gerizim (v12), but the Levites who carried the ark also stood in the middle to read. Probably the Levitical priests read and the rest of the tribe stood with Gerizim.
- **Twelve curses, and no blessings.** The chapter records only curses. Joshua 8:34 says Joshua read "the blessing and the curse." Some think the blessings were the same statements turned positive ("Blessed is the one who does not…"). Others think Moses records the curses alone because chapter 28 will spell out both in full. Whatever the reason, the effect is sobering.
- **"Cursed be" (vv15–26).** The Hebrew *'arur* is a declaration, "cursed is." It means being placed under God's judgment and cut off from his blessing. JFB points out that it is a statement, not a wish. "Cursed be" in the ESV is a traditional rendering.
- **"And all the people shall answer and say, 'Amen'" (v15).** *Amen* comes from a root meaning firm, sure, reliable. To say "Amen" is to say "Yes, so it is, so let it be." The people agree with God's verdict and put themselves under it. The Tyndale notes say that by doing so they became witnesses against their own future disobedience.
- **"Who… sets it up in secret" (v15).** Idolatry comes first, against the first two commandments. The key word is **"in secret."** Many of these sins are the kind done where no judge could see them: a hidden idol, moving a boundary stone at night, misleading a blind man, a murder in the dark (v24). Courts could not reach them, so they were placed under God's own curse. The curse on the hidden idol is matched by one on the hidden killing.
- **"Anyone who dishonors his father or his mother" (v16).** "Dishonors" is *qalah*, "treat lightly, despise," the opposite of "honor," which comes from a word for weight (5:16). The Tyndale notes say duty to parents ranked just below duty to God.
- **"Moves his neighbor's landmark" (v17).** Boundary stones marked each family's share of the land God had given (19:14). Shifting one was quiet theft, and it robbed not just a neighbor but his descendants (Prov 22:28; 23:10–11).
- **"Misleads a blind man on the road" (v18).** A cruel joke or a cheat against someone who cannot fight back (Lev 19:14). Jesus used the picture for spiritual leaders: "if the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit" (Matt 15:14).
- **"Perverts the justice due to the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow" (v19).** The same three vulnerable groups as in 24:17 and 26:12. They had no one to speak for them in court, so God speaks for them.
- **Four sexual sins (vv20–23).** Sleeping with a father's wife (22:30), with an animal, with a half-sister or sister, or with a mother-in-law. These are drawn from Leviticus 18 and 20. They are sins that happen within the household, behind closed doors, and they wreck families.
- **"Strikes down his neighbor in secret" (v24).** Murder where there is no witness, so that no court could convict (compare 21:1–9).
- **"Takes a bribe to shed innocent blood" (v25).** A judge or witness paid to condemn an innocent man to death. It is murder dressed up as justice (Exod 23:7–8). Judas took money to betray "innocent blood" (Matt 27:4), and the priests bought a field with it.
- **"Cursed be anyone who does not confirm the words of this law by doing them" (v26).** The twelfth curse gathers up everything. "Confirm" is *yaqim*, from *qum*, "make stand, establish, uphold." It is not enough to agree with the law. It must be kept. The Greek Old Testament adds "all" ("in all the words of this law"), and the Samaritan text has it too. Paul quotes the verse in that form: "Cursed be everyone who does not abide by all things written in the Book of the Law, and do them" (Gal 3:10). His point is that no one keeps the whole law, so everyone who relies on law-keeping stands under this curse.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the curses as a list of sins God sees even when people do not. He argues from v26 that no one can fully do "all the words of this law," so all are under the curse. He follows Clarke on the missing "all" in the standard Hebrew text, but Clarke's claim, from Jerome, that Jewish scribes removed it on purpose is not supported by evidence. Henry says the curse falls on "not only those who do the evil which the law forbids, but those also who omit the good which the law requires," and that "the last reaches all." JFB notes that Rachel's and Leah's sons had "the most pleasant and honorable office" of blessing, and that the tribes answered "in full simultaneous shouts of 'Amen.'" JFB has no notes on vv14–26.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul quotes the last verse of this chapter and then gives the answer to it: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us, for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'" (Gal 3:10–13, quoting Deut 21:23). All Israel said "Amen" to the curses on Ebal, and every honest reader has to say "Amen" too, because the list reaches the secret sins no court ever saw. But on the same mountain as the curse stood an altar of uncut stones, with sacrifices and a meal of peace. The cross stands where the curse falls. Jesus took the curse so that "the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles" and we might receive the Spirit by faith (Gal 3:14). And at Shechem, beneath these same two mountains, he told a Samaritan woman that the hour was coming when worship would no longer depend on Gerizim or Jerusalem, but would be "in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24).
