*Setting:* Babylonia, probably 591–589 BC, shortly after the dated message of 20:1 and before the siege of Jerusalem began in January 588 BC (24:1–2). Chapter 20 ended with a parable of a forest fire in "the south," and the people brushed it off: "Is he not a maker of parables?" (20:49). Chapter 21 takes the picture away and says it plainly, then says it again in poetry, mime and a map. In Hebrew Bibles the chapter begins at 20:45, which makes the parable and its explanation one unit. The word that holds the chapter together is "sword" (*ḥerev*), which appears more than a dozen times. Behind it all is the political situation: Zedekiah and the king of Ammon had both been plotting against Babylon with Egypt's encouragement (Jer 27:3), and Nebuchadnezzar would soon march west to punish them. Which city would he strike first?

#### The Lord Has Drawn His Sword (vv1–32)
- **"Set your face toward Jerusalem and preach against the sanctuaries" (v2).** This explains the forest parable of 20:46–47 phrase by phrase. "The south" is Jerusalem, "the forest" is the land of Israel, and "preach" uses the same verb, literally "let your word drip." **"The sanctuaries"** is plural, perhaps the temple's holy places, or perhaps the many shrines that had multiplied in the land.
- **"Behold, I am against you and will draw my sword from its sheath" (v3).** The enemy Judah has to fear is not Babylon but the LORD. Babylon is only the hand that will hold the sword (v11). **"Both righteous and wicked."** This corresponds to the green tree and the dry tree of 20:47. It seems to clash with chapter 18, where each person is judged for his own sin. But chapter 18 speaks of each person's standing before God, life or death under his judgment. This chapter describes what war does to a city. When an army breaks in, it does not ask who is godly. Jeremiah, Daniel and Ezekiel himself were righteous men who suffered in the national disaster, yet God kept them through it.
- **"It shall not be sheathed again" (v5).** The sword stays out. No last-minute reprieve will come this time, as it did for Hezekiah in 701 BC (2 Kings 19:35–36).
- **"Groan; with breaking heart and bitter grief, groan before their eyes" (v6).** Literally "with breaking of the loins," a picture of doubled-over pain, like a woman in labor clutching her sides (Isa 21:3). This is another sign-act, but it is not acting. Ezekiel's groaning is real grief, done in public so that people will ask why.
- **"Every heart will melt… all knees will be weak as water" (v7).** The Hebrew says knees will "go with water," which may mean trembling, or may be a blunt picture of terror's effect on the body. Either way, it describes total collapse, the same words as 7:17. **"Behold, it is coming, and it will be fulfilled."**
- **"A sword, a sword is sharpened and also polished" (v9).** This is the song of the sword, a short war poem with the beat of a chant. The repeated "sword, sword" may echo a battle cry. Sharpened for killing, polished to flash in the sun so that it terrifies before it strikes. **"Polished to flash like lightning" (v10).** The word is literally "to have lightning," the same word used of God's flashing sword in Deuteronomy 32:41.
- **"Or shall we rejoice? You have despised the rod, my son, with everything of wood" (v10).** This half-verse is one of the most obscure lines in Ezekiel, and every translation is a guess. The likeliest sense is this. "The rod" or "scepter" (*shevet*) of "my son" recalls Genesis 49:9–10, where Judah is a lion's whelp (literally "my son") and the scepter will not depart from him. The people may have been saying, "Should we rejoice? The scepter of Judah is safe, God promised it." The sword's reply is that it despises that scepter as if it were any common stick of wood. Other translations take it differently, and no one can be sure. Verse 13 picks up the same words: **"what could it do if you despise the rod?"** Here the rod may be God's discipline, which Judah had already rejected.
- **"Cry out and wail… strike therefore upon your thigh" (v12).** Slapping the thigh was a gesture of grief and shame (Jer 31:19). **"It is against all the princes of Israel."** Rank will protect no one.
- **"Clap your hands and let the sword come down twice, yes, three times" (v14).** Clapping here is not applause but a gesture of anger or triumph over an enemy (25:6; Num 24:10). The doubled and tripled sword means repeated, unrelenting slaughter. Some commentators see three blows in the three Babylonian attacks on Judah (605, 597 and 586 BC). JFB sees Zedekiah's capture, the city's fall, and the later killing after Gedaliah's murder. The simpler sense is that the sword strikes again and again.
- **"Cut sharply to the right; set yourself to the left" (v16).** The sword is addressed directly, like a soldier given orders. It swings in every direction. **"I also will clap my hands, and I will satisfy my fury" (v17).** God joins the gesture he gave Ezekiel.
- **"Mark two ways for the sword of the king of Babylon to come… make a signpost" (v19).** Now a map. Ezekiel likely scratched it on the ground or on a clay brick, as he had drawn Jerusalem in 4:1. A road comes out of Babylon and splits at a fork. One branch leads to **"Rabbah of the Ammonites"**, the capital of Ammon on the site of modern Amman in Jordan. The other leads to **"Judah, into Jerusalem the fortified" (v20).** Both had rebelled. The fork was probably somewhere in Syria, perhaps near Riblah, where Nebuchadnezzar had his field headquarters (2 Kings 25:6).
- **"He shakes the arrows; he consults the teraphim; he looks at the liver" (v21).** Three methods of divination are named, all well documented. Belomancy: arrows marked with the names of the possible targets were shaken in a quiver, and the one that came out first gave the answer. Teraphim were household idols, used for oracles (Gen 31:19; Judg 17:5; Zech 10:2). Hepatoscopy, reading the liver of a sacrificed sheep, was Babylon's most respected method. Archaeologists have found clay models of sheep livers from Mesopotamia, marked into zones with omens written on them, used to train diviners. A Babylonian king would not launch a campaign without such an omen.
- **"Into his right hand comes the divination for Jerusalem" (v22).** The arrow marked "Jerusalem" comes up. The verse then rushes through the siege: battering rams, war cries, ramps and siege towers. The irony is deep. A pagan king using forbidden methods (Deut 18:10) gets the right answer, because the LORD who controls the lot (Prov 16:33) has already chosen.
- **"But to them it will seem like a false divination" (v23).** The people of Jerusalem will laugh it off. What do pagan omens mean to them? **"They have sworn solemn oaths."** This may mean the oaths of loyalty Zedekiah's government swore to Babylon (17:13–19), now broken. **"He brings their guilt to remembrance."** Nebuchadnezzar remembers their broken oath, and so does God.
- **"Because you have made your guilt to be remembered… you shall be taken in hand" (v24).** "Remember" and "remembrance" appear four times in two verses. Judah had forgotten God (16:22), but its sins had not been forgotten. Its transgressions are **"uncovered"**, laid bare for all to see.
- **"And you, O profane wicked one, prince of Israel, whose day has come" (v25).** Now Zedekiah is addressed directly, though not by name. "Profane" (*ḥalal*) can also mean "pierced" or "slain," and some translations read it that way. He was the LORD's anointed, but he had profaned his office by idolatry and broken oaths. **"The time of your final punishment"** means literally "the time of the iniquity of the end."
- **"Remove the turban and take off the crown" (v26).** The word for turban (*mitsnefet*) is used elsewhere only for the high priest's headdress (Exod 28:4, 37–39). Some think Ezekiel means that both priesthood and kingship will be stripped away, which happened in 586 BC, when the temple burned and the king was blinded. Others take the turban here simply as part of the king's royal regalia. **"Exalt that which is low, and bring low that which is exalted"** is the same reversal as 17:24.
- **"A ruin, ruin, ruin I will make it" (v27).** The threefold word (*'awwah*) is the Hebrew way of saying something to the utmost, like the "holy, holy, holy" of Isaiah 6:3. The kingdom of David will be overturned completely.
- **"Until he comes, the one to whom judgment belongs, and I will give it to him" (v27).** This line deliberately echoes Genesis 49:10, "until tribute comes to him" (or, in an old rendering, "until Shiloh comes"). The Hebrew sounds alike. Jacob promised that the scepter would stay with Judah until its rightful owner came. Ezekiel says the crown is coming off, and it will stay off until that one comes. Interpreters read "the one to whom judgment belongs" in two ways. Many, including Henry, JFB and Guzik, see the Messiah, the true heir of David who has the right to rule. The Tyndale notes suggest that in context it may first mean Nebuchadnezzar, to whom God hands over the right to judge Jerusalem (23:24), with the scepter to be restored later. The word *mishpat* can mean either "judgment" or "rightful claim." Both readings agree that after Zedekiah, no king would sit on David's throne until God himself gave it.
- **"Concerning the Ammonites and concerning their reproach" (v28).** When Nebuchadnezzar turned toward Jerusalem, Ammon must have felt relieved and even gloated. Ammon's "reproach" was its mocking of Judah's fall (25:3, 6). Later the king of Ammon was behind the murder of Gedaliah, the governor Babylon left in Judah (Jer 40:14; 41:1–2). The sword that passed Ammon by was only delayed. **"While they see for you false visions" (v29).** Ammon had its own diviners promising safety, and they were as wrong as Jerusalem's prophets.
- **"Return it to its sheath. In the place where you were created… I will judge you" (v30).** This is disputed. Some read it as words to Ammon: put away your sword, it will not save you, for you will be judged in your own land. Others, including the Tyndale notes, take it as words to Babylon's sword, now that its work is done. God's tool will be judged in its own homeland, as Jeremiah also foretold (Jer 25:12; 50–51). Either way, no nation God uses to judge others is exempt from judgment.
- **"I will deliver you into the hands of brutish men, skillful to destroy" (v31).** Josephus says Nebuchadnezzar campaigned against Ammon and Moab in 582 BC. Later, desert peoples from the east overran the region (25:4).
- **"You shall be no more remembered" (v32).** This is the sharpest contrast of the chapter. Israel would be judged but restored, and its sins would be "remembered" so that it could repent. Ammon would simply fade from history. As a nation, it did disappear, absorbed into Arab peoples in later centuries.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the sword glitters to the terror of those against whom it is drawn," and of Ezekiel's groaning, "The example of Christ teaches us to lament over those whose ruin we declare." On v27 he writes that "the Lord would overturn the government of Judah, till the coming of Him whose right it is." JFB says the cutting off of righteous and wicked is "not contradictory of Eze 18," because Ezekiel is describing "the mere outward aspect of the indiscriminate universality of the national calamity." It describes the arrow divination as practiced among the Arabs and pictured in sculptures from Nineveh, and it calls Zedekiah the one who "desecrated by idolatry and perjury his office as the Lord's anointed." JFB reads v27 as Messianic and says the threefold "overturn" shows "the awful certainty of the event." Guzik agrees that the national disaster does not cancel chapter 18's teaching about personal judgment, and he follows Wright, Feinberg and others in reading v27 through Genesis 49:10 as fulfilled in Jesus. The Tyndale notes take the more cautious view described above.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel watched the crown come off David's line and saw no king to take it up. "Until he comes, the one to whom judgment belongs, and I will give it to him." Six centuries later an angel told a young woman in Nazareth, "the Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David" (Luke 1:32). Jesus is the one whose right it is. The Father "has given all judgment to the Son" (John 5:22), and the risen Christ says, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (Matt 28:18). Pagan kings shook arrows at a crossroads, but even their omens ran on God's plan. The same God who directed Nebuchadnezzar's march directed the events that brought his Son to Jerusalem to die. And the prophet who groaned with a breaking heart over a city's coming ruin points to the Savior who wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41–44) before he took the sword of God's judgment on himself (Zech 13:7).
