*Setting:* Judah, about 735–715 BC. Judah has had two long, mostly faithful reigns under Uzziah and Jotham (ch 15). In the north, Israel is in its death throes: kings murdered one after another, its northern and eastern lands already torn away by Assyria (15:29). The great power is now Assyria under Tiglath-pileser III, who is swallowing the small kingdoms of the west one by one. Pekah of Israel and Rezin of Damascus have formed an alliance to resist him, and they want Judah in it. Into this crisis comes Ahaz, one of the worst kings in Judah's history. This is the same moment when Isaiah meets Ahaz with the promise of a sign: "the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel" (Isa 7:14). Ahaz refuses to trust God, buys Assyria's help instead, and comes home from Damascus with a new altar for the temple. The chapter has a single ESV heading.

#### Ahaz Reigns in Judah (vv1–20)
- **"In the seventeenth year of Pekah… Ahaz… began to reign… and he reigned sixteen years" (vv1–2).** The dates are complicated by overlapping reigns, as usual in this period. The Tyndale notes suggest Ahaz was co-regent with his father before ruling alone, and the sixteen years probably run from about 731 to 715 BC. His full name was Jehoahaz, "the LORD has grasped." Tiglath-pileser's records list "Jehoahaz of Judah" among the kings who paid him tribute. The Bible shortens it to Ahaz, as if dropping God's name from it.
- **"He did not do what was right in the eyes of the LORD his God, as his father David had done" (v2).** Most kings of Judah are measured against David. The previous few kings fell short ("nevertheless, the high places were not taken away"), but Ahaz gets no praise at all.
- **"He walked in the way of the kings of Israel" (v3).** Judah's king copies the north, which was already under judgment for its calves and Baal worship. Chronicles says he made metal images for the Baals (2 Chr 28:2).
- **"He even burned his son as an offering" (v3).** The Hebrew is literally "he made his son pass through the fire," the phrase used for child sacrifice to the god Molech (Lev 18:21; Deut 18:10). Chronicles says "sons," plural, and places it in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom, just south of Jerusalem (2 Chr 28:3). That valley, *Ge-Hinnom*, became so cursed by these sacrifices that its name, Gehenna, became the New Testament word for hell (Jer 7:31–32; Matt 5:22). The law calls these **"the despicable practices of the nations whom the LORD drove out."** The Canaanites lost the land for exactly these sins (Deut 12:31), and Judah's king was now doing them.
- **"On the high places and on the hills and under every green tree" (v4).** This is the language Deuteronomy uses for Canaanite worship sites (Deut 12:2). It became a stock phrase in the prophets for spiritual adultery (Jer 2:20; Hos 4:13). Ahaz was not just tolerating the high places, as the better kings did. He was worshipping at them himself.
- **"Rezin king of Syria and Pekah… came up to wage war on Jerusalem, and they besieged Ahaz but could not conquer him" (v5).** This is the Syro-Ephraimite war, about 734 BC ("Ephraim" being the northern kingdom). Their aim was to remove Ahaz and put "the son of Tabeel" on the throne, a puppet who would join their anti-Assyrian alliance (Isa 7:6). That would have ended David's dynasty. Chronicles records terrible losses in Judah and a great many captives taken north, who were sent home after the prophet Oded rebuked Israel (2 Chr 28:5–15).
- **Isaiah meets Ahaz (Isa 7).** While Ahaz was inspecting Jerusalem's water supply, perhaps preparing for siege, Isaiah came to him and said: "Be careful, be quiet, do not fear," calling the two kings "two smoldering stumps of firebrands" (Isa 7:3–4). God offered Ahaz any sign he wanted, "deep as Sheol or high as heaven." Ahaz refused with pious-sounding words, "I will not put the LORD to the test," because he had already decided on Assyria. So God gave a sign anyway: Immanuel, "God with us" (Isa 7:10–14). Kings says nothing about this meeting, but it lies behind every line of vv5–9. "If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all" (Isa 7:9).
- **"Rezin the king of Syria recovered Elath for Syria… and the Edomites came to Elath" (v6).** Elath, on the Gulf of Aqaba at the southern tip of the land, was Judah's port to the Red Sea, rebuilt by Uzziah (14:22). In Hebrew "Aram" (Syria) and "Edom" look very similar; the letters *r* and *d* are nearly identical. Many scholars, and JFB, think the original said Edom throughout. Either way, Judah lost its southern port. Chronicles adds that the Edomites and Philistines also raided Judah (2 Chr 28:17–18).
- **"I am your servant and your son" (v7).** This is the standard language of a vassal to his overlord. Ahaz voluntarily makes himself Assyria's subject. The irony is heavy. The king of David's line was meant to be God's son: "You are my Son" (Ps 2:7; 2 Sam 7:14). Ahaz gives that title to Tiglath-pileser instead. **"Come up and rescue me"**: the word "rescue" (*yasha'*, "save") is the word Isaiah's own name is built on, "the LORD saves." Ahaz looks for salvation to the wrong king.
- **"Ahaz also took the silver and gold that was found in the house of the LORD… and sent a present" (v8).** "Present" is literally "a bribe" (*shochad*), a word the law condemns (Exod 23:8). Kings of Judah had stripped the temple before to pay off enemies (1 Kgs 15:18; 2 Kgs 12:18), but Ahaz does it to buy an alliance God had told him he did not need.
- **"The king of Assyria marched up against Damascus and took it, carrying its people captive to Kir, and he killed Rezin" (v9).** This happened in 732 BC. It looks like success, and in the short run it was. Isaiah had said the threat would vanish anyway (Isa 7:16). Amos had foretold that the people of Syria "shall go into exile to Kir" (Amos 1:5). Kir's location is unknown, but Amos says it was where the Arameans had come from in the first place (Amos 9:7), so the deportation sent them back where they started. Chronicles adds that Tiglath-pileser "afflicted him instead of strengthening him" (2 Chr 28:20–21). Judah was now a vassal, paying tribute for the rest of its life as a kingdom.
- **"When King Ahaz went to Damascus to meet Tiglath-pileser" (v10).** Vassal kings were summoned to pay homage to the conqueror. At Damascus Ahaz **"saw the altar"** and sent the priest Uriah a detailed drawing and model. Scholars debate whether it was an Assyrian or an Aramean altar. Chronicles says Ahaz sacrificed to the gods of Damascus, reasoning, "Because the gods of the kings of Syria helped them, I will sacrifice to them" (2 Chr 28:23), which is strange logic when Syria had just been crushed. Henry: "Infidels have often been remarkable for believing ridiculous falsehoods."
- **"Uriah the priest built the altar… before King Ahaz arrived" (v11).** The priest who should have resisted rushed to obey. He is very likely the same "Uriah the priest" whom Isaiah called as a reliable witness (Isa 8:2). A man trusted for his integrity went along with the king. Compare the priest Azariah, who stood up to Uzziah when he tried to burn incense (2 Chr 26:17–18).
- **"The king drew near to the altar and went up on it and burned his burnt offering" (vv12–13).** Ahaz offers the standard offerings: burnt, grain, drink and peace offerings (Lev 1–3). He may only have presided, as kings did at dedications (1 Kgs 8:62–64), but the wording echoes Jeroboam, who "went up to the altar" at Bethel (1 Kgs 12:32–33). The offerings are God's; the altar is a copy of a pagan one.
- **"The bronze altar that was before the LORD he removed" (v14).** Solomon's bronze altar stood in the courtyard in front of the temple (1 Kgs 8:64; 2 Chr 4:1). Its pattern went back to the altar God showed Moses (Exod 27:1–8). Ahaz moves it aside to the north and puts his new altar in the central place. JFB: "the sin in this affair consisted in meddling with" the altar whose pattern God had given. The first altar was made by revelation; the new one by taste.
- **"On the great altar burn the morning burnt offering and the evening grain offering" (v15).** All the regular worship, the daily sacrifices (Num 28:3–8), the king's offerings and the people's, moves to the new altar. **"But the bronze altar shall be for me to inquire by."** "Inquire" (*baqar*) probably means examining the entrails of sacrificed animals for omens, a common practice in Assyria and Babylon. God's altar has been turned into a fortune-teller's table. Henry: "God's altar had hitherto been kept in its place, and in use; but Ahaz put another in the room of it."
- **"Uriah the priest did all this, as King Ahaz commanded" (v16).** The sentence is short and damning. The priest's obedience to the king is total.
- **"He cut off the frames of the stands… and he took down the sea from off the bronze oxen" (v17).** These were Solomon's great bronze works (1 Kgs 7:23–39): ten wheeled stands with basins for washing, and "the sea," a huge bronze basin resting on twelve bronze oxen. Ahaz stripped the bronze panels and oxen, most likely to pay his Assyrian tribute, and set the sea on a stone base. Jeremiah later mentions the bronze oxen among the things Babylon carried away (Jer 52:20), so some may have been replaced or not all removed.
- **"The covered way for the Sabbath… and the outer entrance for the king he caused to go around the house of the LORD, because of the king of Assyria" (v18).** The details are obscure. The "covered way" may have been a canopy or a royal box used on the Sabbath, and the king's entrance a private way from palace to temple. Removing or rerouting them may have been a sign of submission: the vassal king gave up symbols of royal standing, or closed off a link between palace and temple that Assyria might see as a fortification. Henry reads it as "contempt upon the sabbath." Chronicles says Ahaz finally shut the temple doors altogether and set up altars "in every corner of Jerusalem" (2 Chr 28:24–25).
- **"Ahaz slept with his fathers… and Hezekiah his son reigned in his place" (v20).** Chronicles adds that Ahaz was not buried in the royal tombs (2 Chr 28:27). The surprise is his son. Hezekiah, the son of the worst king so far, became one of the best, and his first act was to reopen and cleanse the temple (2 Chr 29:3). Isaiah 14:28 dates an oracle "in the year that King Ahaz died."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Few and evil were the days of Ahaz," and "those whose hearts condemn them, will go any where in a day of distress, rather than to God." Of the Assyrian rescue he says, "The sin was its own punishment." Guzik ranks Ahaz as probably Judah's worst king and draws on Isaiah 7 for the war and the Immanuel sign. He applies the Damascus altar to churches that borrow the world's methods, notes Ahaz's love of novelty (even his sundial, 2 Kgs 20:11), and stresses that every advantage, from a godly father to Isaiah and Micah preaching, meant nothing without a real relationship with God. He also identifies Uriah with Isaiah's "faithful witness." JFB calls Ahaz's reign a time of "voluptuousness and religious degeneracy of all classes," prefers the "Edomites" reading in v6, and says the alterations of v18 were made "from fear of the king of Assyria." JFB does not comment on vv19–20.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ahaz had God's own offer of a sign and turned it down for the help of a tyrant. He called the king of Assyria "my father" and moved God's altar aside for one he liked better. Yet in the very crisis Ahaz mishandled, God gave the promise of Immanuel, "God with us" (Isa 7:14), and Matthew sees it fulfilled when Jesus is born of the virgin Mary (Matt 1:22–23). Ahaz looked for a saviour in the wrong king. The true Son of David is God's Son, not Assyria's, and he did not strip the temple but called himself its true dwelling (John 2:19–21). He is the one altar and sacrifice God himself provided, not one copied from the nations (Heb 13:10–12).
