*Setting:* Isaac has been born at last, when Abraham was 100 (21:5), and Sarah's laughter has turned to joy (21:6). Ishmael and Hagar have been sent away (21:14), so Isaac is now the only son left in Abraham's household and the one through whom God said his offspring would be named (21:12). Abraham has made peace with Abimelech, dug a well at Beersheba in the dry south of Canaan, planted a tamarisk tree there and called on "the Everlasting God" (21:33). He has lived there "many days" (21:34), and Isaac has grown from a baby to a boy able to carry a load of firewood up a mountain. The dates for the patriarchs are approximate, but this is probably sometime around 2000 BC. After decades of waiting, everything God promised now rests on one young life. Then God asks for that life. Jewish tradition calls this chapter the *Akedah*, "the binding," and it is one of the most searching stories in the whole Bible.

#### The Sacrifice of Isaac (vv1–24)
- **The ESV keeps the whole chapter under one heading.** The story of the mountain runs through v19. A short family report about Abraham's brother Nahor follows in vv20–24 with no new heading, and it is covered at the end of this section.
- **"After these things God tested Abraham" (v1).** The narrator tells the reader at the start what Abraham does not know: this is a test. The verb is *nasah*, which the lexicon defines as "test, try, prove." It is the word for God proving Israel in the wilderness "to know what was in your heart" (Deut 8:2), and for testing a sword before battle (1 Sam 17:39). The King James "God did tempt Abraham" uses "tempt" in its older sense of "put to the proof." God does not entice anyone to sin (Jas 1:13). He tests in order to bring out and strengthen faith that is already there.
- **"Here I am" (v1).** In Hebrew this is one word, *hinneni*, literally "behold me." The lexicon notes that it is the answer of someone ready to listen and obey. It comes three times in this chapter at its three turning points: to God (v1), to Isaac (v7), and to the angel (v11). Abraham is equally present to God and to his son.
- **"Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love" (v2).** The command narrows like a closing hand: your son, your only one, the one you love, Isaac. "Only" is *yachid*, "only one," used for an only child, as of Jephthah's daughter (Judg 11:34). Ishmael is still alive, but he has been sent away, and Isaac is the only son of the promise. This is the first time the Bible uses the word "love" (*ahab*), and it is a father's love for a son. The old Greek translation renders "your only son" as "your beloved son," the phrase the Father speaks over Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:11).
- **"Go" (v2).** The Hebrew is the same unusual phrase, *lekh-lekha*, that God used when he first called Abram: "Go from your country" (12:1). These are the only two places in Genesis where it occurs. The first call asked Abraham to give up his past, his father's house. This one asks him to give up his future.
- **"The land of Moriah" (v2).** The only other mention of Moriah is 2 Chronicles 3:1, where Solomon builds the temple "on Mount Moriah" in Jerusalem. Most Jewish and Christian readers have taken this to be the same place, so the ridge where Abraham offered Isaac would later be where Israel's sacrifices were offered. The lexicon notes that the old Greek translation reads "the high land" here instead of a place name, and the Samaritans placed the event on their own mountain, Gerizim. The Bible's own link is the Chronicles verse.
- **"Offer him there as a burnt offering" (v2).** A burnt offering (*olah*, "what goes up") was killed and then wholly burned on the altar, with nothing kept back for the worshiper. God is asking for everything.
- **Why would God ask this?** The question is a real one, and readers have felt its weight for centuries. Child sacrifice was practiced by some of Israel's neighbors (2 Kgs 3:27), and the law later condemns it as something God hates (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31; Jer 7:31). The reader knows from v1 that it is a test, and the ending shows God never meant Isaac to die. The Tyndale notes say the real offering God wanted was Abraham's will, surrendered to him. The story does not make child sacrifice acceptable. It shows the God of Abraham stopping the knife and providing the offering himself.
- **"Abraham rose early in the morning" (v3).** There is no recorded argument, and no word about what Abraham felt. Compare chapter 18, where he pleaded at length for Sodom. Here, where his own son is at stake, he says nothing and gets up early. He saddles the donkey, takes two servants, and splits the wood himself. The silence of the narrator is part of the power of the story.
- **"On the third day" (v4).** From Beersheba to the Jerusalem hills is roughly 50 miles, two to three days on foot. That gave Abraham three days to turn back, and he didn't. Isaac was, in his father's mind, as good as dead for three days.
- **"I and the boy will go over there and worship and come again to you" (v5).** "Worship" is *shachah*, to bow down. "Come again" is plural in Hebrew: "*we* will come back." Was Abraham hiding the truth from the servants, or did he believe both of them would return? Hebrews gives the inspired answer: "He considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead, from which, figuratively speaking, he did receive him back" (Heb 11:19). God had promised that offspring would come through Isaac (21:12), and Isaac had no children yet. So if God took Isaac, God would have to give him back.
- **"The boy" (v5).** The same Hebrew word (*na'ar*) is used for the "young men" Abraham brought along in v3, so it doesn't tell us Isaac's age. He is old enough to carry the wood up a mountain. Jewish and Christian writers have guessed widely. The historian Josephus said 25. Some rabbis said 37, since they linked Sarah's death at 127 (23:1) to news of this event. JFB says "upwards of twenty." The text doesn't say, but he was clearly not a small child, and an old man of over a hundred could not have bound him if he had resisted.
- **"He laid it on Isaac his son… the fire and the knife" (v6).** The son carries the wood, and the father carries what will kill. "So they went both of them together" comes here and again in v8, framing the only conversation between them in the Bible.
- **"Where is the lamb for a burnt offering?" (v7).** These are the only words Isaac speaks in the chapter. "Lamb" is *seh*, one of the flock, a sheep or a goat. It is the word for the Passover lamb (Exod 12:3) and for the one led "to the slaughter" in Isaiah 53:7.
- **"God will provide for himself the lamb" (v8).** "Provide" is the ordinary Hebrew verb "to see" (*ra'ah*). Literally, "God will see to the lamb for himself." To "see to" something is to provide it. This answer is honest without saying everything, and it is truer than Abraham may have known. God did see to it in v13, and centuries later John the Baptist pointed and said, "Behold, the Lamb of God" (John 1:29).
- **"Abraham built the altar… and bound Isaac his son" (v9).** The verbs slow the scene to a crawl: built, laid in order, bound, laid him on the altar. "Bound" is *aqad*, which the lexicon notes occurs only here in the whole Old Testament. It gives the chapter its Jewish name, the *Akedah*. Isaac does not resist. Christians have long seen in his silence the one who "opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7).
- **"Took the knife to slaughter his son" (v10).** "Slaughter" is *shachat*, the technical word for killing a sacrificial animal (Lev 1:5; Exod 12:6). The narrator does not soften what Abraham was about to do.
- **"Abraham, Abraham!" (v11).** The doubled name marks urgency and closeness, as when God calls "Moses, Moses" (Exod 3:4) and "Samuel! Samuel!" (1 Sam 3:10). The angel of the LORD speaks as God himself ("from me," v12), as he did to Hagar (16:10–13). As with chapter 16, some Christians see a preincarnate appearance of the Son here, while others see an angel speaking with God's full authority.
- **"Now I know that you fear God" (v12).** God already knew Abraham's heart, so what does "now I know" mean? Some read it as language put in human terms, like "I will go down to see" (18:21). Others stress that God's knowledge here is the knowledge of a test actually passed, faith shown in action and not only foreseen. Either way the point is that Abraham's faith is now proven. To "fear God" in Genesis means to reverence and obey him (20:11; 42:18).
- **"You have not withheld your son, your only son" (v12).** "Withheld" is *chasak*, "keep back, keep for oneself." The old Greek translation uses the verb "spare," and Paul uses it in Romans 8:32: God "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all." Abraham was spared from doing what God himself would one day do.
- **"A ram, caught in a thicket by his horns" (v13).** Abraham "lifted up his eyes," the same phrase as v4. On the third day he saw the place of sacrifice, and now he sees the provision. He offers the ram "instead of his son." That phrase states the idea of substitution plainly, one life given in place of another, the idea at the heart of Israel's sacrifices (Lev 1:4) and of the cross.
- **"The LORD will provide" (v14).** In Hebrew this is *Yahweh yireh*, from the same verb "see" as v8 (older English Bibles write "Jehovah-jireh"). The ESV footnotes give "will see" and, for the saying, "he will be seen." Both are true: the LORD sees the need and provides, and on his mountain he lets himself be seen. "The mount of the LORD" later became a name for Zion, the temple hill (Isa 2:3; Ps 24:3). The Tyndale notes say that later Israelites read this verse as speaking to their own worship in Jerusalem.
- **"By myself I have sworn" (v16).** This is the only place in Genesis where God is shown swearing an oath, and later promises look back to it (26:3; 50:24). Hebrews explains: "since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself" (Heb 6:13). The promise was already certain. The oath was given so Abraham and his children could be certain of it too (Heb 6:17–18).
- **"I will surely bless you, and I will surely multiply your offspring" (v17).** The Hebrew doubles each verb for emphasis, something like "blessing I will bless you." The images of stars (15:5) and sand gather up the earlier promises. "Possess the gate of his enemies" means to take their cities, since the gate was where a city was defended and governed. Rebekah's family will speak the same blessing over her when she leaves to marry Isaac (24:60).
- **"In your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed" (v18).** This repeats the promise of 12:3. "Offspring" is the Hebrew word "seed," which can mean many descendants or one. Here the ESV footnote at v17 ("Or *their*") shows how the singular and plural sense shift. Paul builds on this ambiguity: "It does not say, 'And to offsprings,' referring to many, but referring to one… who is Christ" (Gal 3:16).
- **"Because you have obeyed my voice" (v18).** Does this mean Abraham earned the blessing? The promise was already given freely in chapters 12 and 15, long before this test. James says Abraham was "justified by works when he offered up his son Isaac," and explains that "faith was completed by his works" (Jas 2:21–22). Paul, using chapter 15, says Abraham was counted righteous by faith before he did anything (Rom 4:1–5). Most Protestant readers reconcile them this way: Paul speaks of how a person is put right with God, and James speaks of how that faith shows itself to be real. Catholic and Orthodox readers tend to stress more strongly that faith and obedience are one living response. Both agree that Genesis 22 shows a faith that obeys.
- **"So Abraham returned to his young men" (v19).** Isaac is not mentioned. The text says simply that "they arose and went together," so most readers assume he came down with his father. Some Jewish traditions imaginatively wondered where Isaac went, and some Christian readers note that the next time Isaac appears in the story is at his wedding (24:62–67). That is reading between the lines, not the text. Abraham goes home to Beersheba.
- **"Milcah also has borne children to your brother Nahor" (vv20–23).** News arrives from the family back in Mesopotamia. Nahor stayed behind when Abraham left (11:31; 24:10), and his wife Milcah was also his niece (11:29). The word "also" sets her children beside Sarah's son: both branches of the family are growing. The list has a clear purpose, and the narrator flags it with a pointed aside: "Bethuel fathered Rebekah" (v23). Abraham has just been promised offspring through Isaac, and here, in the next breath, is the name of Isaac's future wife. Rebekah is the only daughter in the list.
- **Twelve sons (vv20–24).** Nahor has eight sons by Milcah and four by his concubine Reumah, twelve in all, like Ishmael (25:13–16) and later Jacob. A concubine was a secondary wife with fewer rights. Some names reappear elsewhere. Uz is the name of Job's land (Job 1:1), and Elihu, the young man who speaks near the end of Job, is "the Buzite" (Job 32:2), so Job's world may be connected to this family, though the link is not certain. Chesed may be the ancestor of the Chaldeans (Hebrew *Kasdim*). The list is short and not padded. It ties the story of the mountain to the story of the wedding.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the opening command well: "every word here is a sword in his bones: the trial is steeled with trying phrases." JFB likewise says, "Every circumstance mentioned was calculated to give a deeper stab to the parental bosom." On "tested," JFB and Henry agree with the reading above: God did "not incite to sin (Jas 1:13), but try, prove," in JFB's words. Henry observes that "Strong faith is often exercised with strong trials." On Abraham's early start, Henry writes, "Those that do the will of God heartily will do it speedily." Guzik stresses that Abraham trusted the Promiser and not merely the promise, and he reads v5 with Hebrews 11:19 as real faith in resurrection. Guzik says "worship" in v5 is the Bible's first use of the word for worship toward God. The lexicon shows the same verb, *shachah*, already used when Abraham bowed to his visitors (18:2) and Lot to the angels (19:1). So v5 may be the first time it is plainly worship of God, but the word itself appears earlier. On Isaac carrying the wood, Henry says it was "that he might typify Christ, who carried his own cross." JFB thinks Abraham could not have gone through with it "had not Isaac, then upwards of twenty years of age displayed equal faith in submitting." On v12 JFB says, "The sacrifice was virtually offered," and Guzik adds that God often accepts a willing heart as the deed itself. On the ram, Henry is direct: "Christ was sacrificed in our stead, as this ram instead of Isaac." JFB links the ram to Jesus' words that Abraham "rejoiced that he would see my day" (John 8:56). Guzik lists the parallels between Isaac and Jesus: a beloved son, a willing offering, wood carried up the hill, the same mountain, and a third-day deliverance. On v14 Henry writes, "Where God sees and provides, he should be seen and praised." On the oath in v16 he says, "By myself have I sworn; for he could swear by no greater," and on v18, "Christ is the great blessing of the world." All three agree on the main lines. Their differences are in how far they press the details as pictures of Christ, with Guzik going furthest. On vv20–24, Henry says the list is there "To make way for the following story of the marriage of Isaac to Rebekah," and Guzik adds only a note on what a concubine was. JFB has no comment on vv5–8, 10 or 20–24, and it treats vv13–19 together in one note.

#### Pointing to Christ
This is the place where the New Testament itself teaches readers to look. A father offers his only, beloved son. The son carries the wood up the hill and does not resist. Isaac's question, "Where is the lamb?", hangs over the rest of the Old Testament until John the Baptist answers it: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). On Moriah, God stopped the knife and provided a ram in Isaac's place. Near that same ridge, if Chronicles points where most readers think it does, God did not spare his own Son "but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32). No ram was caught in the thicket for him. He was the substitute. Abraham received Isaac back from death "figuratively speaking" (Heb 11:19). God received his Son back from death on the third day in fact. The story is not about Abraham's heroism. It shows a God who asks for everything and then provides, at his own cost, the offering he requires.
