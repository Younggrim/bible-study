*Setting:* The golden calf has been destroyed, about three thousand of the worst offenders have died, and Moses has pleaded for the people, offering even to be blotted out of God's book (32:30–35). God has promised that the journey to Canaan will go on, but with an angel, not with himself. Chapter 33 is about the one question that now matters most: will God himself go with his people? It is one of the most intimate chapters in the Old Testament. Moses talks with God "as a man speaks to his friend" (v11), asks to see his glory, and is hidden in a rock while the LORD passes by. The new tablets and the renewed covenant follow in chapter 34.

#### The Command to Leave Sinai (vv1–6)
- **"Depart; go up from here, you and the people whom you have brought up out of the land of Egypt" (v1).** God still speaks of Israel as the people *Moses* brought up, as he did in 32:7. The relationship is strained. Yet the promise to the patriarchs still stands: **"the land of which I swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob."** God will keep his oath even to a faithless people.
- **"I will send an angel before you, and I will drive out the Canaanites" (v2).** The six nations listed here are the peoples of Canaan named often in the Pentateuch (3:8, 17; 23:23). The people would get the land, the victory and the milk and honey.
- **"But I will not go up among you, lest I consume you on the way" (v3).** This is the shock. The whole point of the tabernacle plans (chs 25–31) was "that I may dwell in their midst" (25:8; 29:45–46). Now God says he will not go among them, and the reason is mercy as much as anger. A holy God living in the middle of a stiff-necked people would mean their destruction. Who is "an angel" here? Earlier God promised "my angel," in whom was "my name" (23:20–23), an angel so closely linked with God himself that many Christians see him as the pre-incarnate Son. The Tyndale notes suggest this may be a lesser angel, a messenger who could lead them without God's own presence living among them. Others think it is the same angel, and the loss is that God would go before them but not dwell in their midst.
- **"When the people heard this disastrous word, they mourned" (v4).** Here is the first sign of real repentance. They could have taken the deal: land, victory and prosperity without God's presence. They mourned instead. They understood that the gifts without the Giver were not worth having.
- **"Take off your ornaments, that I may know what to do with you" (v5).** Jewelry was worn at feasts. Earrings had just gone into the calf (32:2–3), and the jewelry they still wore may have been tied to that festival or to the religion of Egypt. Jacob's household gave up their earrings along with their foreign gods (Gen 35:2–4). Removing ornaments was a sign of mourning and humility. **"That I may know what to do with you"** does not mean God lacked information. It means the verdict was still open, and their response would matter.
- **"From Mount Horeb onward" (v6).** Horeb is another name for Sinai. They stopped wearing their finery from then on, a lasting sign that they had learned something.
- **Commentators:** Henry says losing God's presence was worse to them than any hardship, and that God withheld his presence while still inviting them to repent. Guzik sees God's refusal to go up as a test: would the people be content with God's blessings without God himself? JFB treats the ornaments as an Eastern sign of mourning, and notes that here God is "represented as determined to do what He afterwards did not," speaking in a way people could understand.

#### The Tent of Meeting (vv7–11)
- **"Moses used to take the tent and pitch it outside the camp, far off from the camp" (v7).** This is not the tabernacle, which had not yet been built (chs 35–40). It was a simpler tent, perhaps Moses' own, used as a meeting place with God for the time being. The verbs describe what Moses "used to" do, a regular habit, probably from this point until the tabernacle was finished. When the tabernacle was built, it stood in the middle of the camp, with the tribes arranged around it (Num 2). This tent stood "far off." The distance spoke for itself: God was no longer at home among them.
- **"Everyone who sought the LORD would go out to the tent of meeting" (v7).** The door was not shut. Anyone who wanted to seek God could still go out to him, but they had to leave the camp to do it. Hebrews 13:13 uses similar language of Jesus: "Let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured."
- **"All the people would rise up, and each would stand at his tent door, and watch Moses" (v8).** The whole nation watched their mediator go. Their hope hung on whether God would meet him.
- **"The pillar of cloud would descend and stand at the entrance of the tent" (v9).** The cloud that had led them out of Egypt (13:21–22) now came down to the tent door. The people's response is simple and right: **"all the people would rise up and worship, each at his tent door"** (v10). They worshiped from a distance, but they worshiped.
- **"The LORD used to speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (v11).** "Face to face" is an idiom for direct, personal conversation. It does not mean Moses saw God's full form, since v20 says no one can see God's face and live. Numbers 12:8 explains it: with other prophets God spoke in visions and dreams, but with Moses "mouth to mouth, clearly, and not in riddles." Abraham was called God's friend (Isa 41:8; Jas 2:23). Jesus says to his disciples, "I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you" (John 15:15).
- **"His assistant Joshua the son of Nun, a young man, would not depart from the tent" (v11).** "Young man" (*na'ar*) here means an attendant or aide rather than a boy. Joshua was already the army commander (17:9–13). He had gone partway up Sinai with Moses (24:13), and now he stays at the tent when Moses goes back to the camp. The future leader is learning to love the place where God speaks.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the move outside the camp as a warning of what their sin had cost them, yet sees hope in the open door for anyone who sought the LORD. He notes that the lawgiver had become their intercessor. He also suggests Joshua stayed so the tent would not stand empty, ready to hear if God spoke. JFB says it was probably Moses' own tent, its removal "the first step in the threatened abandonment," and reads the descending cloud as a sign that Moses' prayers were prevailing. Guzik takes "face to face" as open, intimate fellowship rather than a sight of God's form, citing Numbers 12:8.

#### Moses' Intercession (vv12–23)
- **"You have not let me know whom you will send with me" (v12).** Moses is not satisfied with an angel. He presses God on the point.
- **"I know you by name, and you have also found favor in my sight" (v12).** "Favor" is *chen*, "grace." Noah "found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (Gen 6:8). Moses builds his whole prayer on God's own words to him. Five times in this passage the word "favor" appears (vv12, 13 twice, 16, 17).
- **"Please show me now your ways, that I may know you" (v13).** Moses asks for something more than help. He wants to understand God's ways, his character and how he works, so that he may know God himself. Psalm 103:7 remembers it: "He made known his ways to Moses, his acts to the people of Israel." The people saw what God did. Moses learned why.
- **"Consider too that this nation is your people" (v13).** Again Moses hands the people back to God, as in 32:11. They are not Moses' people. They are God's.
- **"My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest" (v14).** "Presence" is *panim*, literally "face." God's face, his own personal presence, will go. Isaiah later remembers this: "the angel of his presence saved them" (Isa 63:9). "Rest" means settled peace and security in the land (Deut 12:9–10; Josh 21:44). Hebrews 4 picks up the word and says the rest Joshua gave was not the final rest God had in mind (Heb 4:8–9).
- **"If your presence will not go with me, do not bring us up from here" (v15).** Moses would rather stay in the desert with God than go to the promised land without him. **"Is it not in your going with us, so that we are distinct… from every other people?" (v16).** What made Israel different from the nations was not their land, their law or their army. It was God's presence with them.
- **"This very thing that you have spoken I will do" (v17).** God agrees. Moses' prayer has been answered in full.
- **"Please show me your glory" (v18).** "Glory" is *kavod*, from a root meaning "heavy," the weight and splendor of God's presence. Moses has seen God's glory as fire on the mountain (24:17) and in the cloud. He has spoken with God face to face. Yet he wants more. It is not greed. It is the longing of someone who knows God and wants to know him fully. David prayed the same way: "One thing have I asked of the LORD… to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD" (Ps 27:4).
- **"I will make all my goodness pass before you and will proclaim before you my name 'The LORD'" (v19).** God answers the request for glory by showing goodness. His glory is not first of all blinding light. It is his character. And he will proclaim his name, *YHWH*, which in Hebrew thought means his very nature. Chapter 34 shows what this means: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (34:6).
- **"I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will show mercy on whom I will show mercy" (v19).** This echoes the sound of the divine name itself, "I AM WHO I AM" (3:14). God's mercy is free. No one can earn it or demand it. Moses had just watched God spare a people who deserved nothing. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 9:15 to show that salvation "depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy" (Rom 9:16).
- **"You cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live" (v20).** God's full glory is too much for a sinful, mortal person. Other people were amazed to have seen God and lived (Gen 32:30; Judg 13:22), but they saw him in a form suited to them. John says, "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (John 1:18; see also 1 Tim 6:16).
- **"There is a place by me where you shall stand on the rock" (v21).** God himself provides the place of safety. **"I will put you in a cleft of the rock, and I will cover you with my hand until I have passed by" (v22).** The same hand that shields Moses is what keeps him from seeing too much. It is a picture of both protection and mercy. Centuries later Elijah came to "the mount of God," stood at the mouth of a cave, and heard God pass by in a low whisper (1 Kgs 19:8–13). Many think it was the same place.
- **"You shall see my back, but my face shall not be seen" (v23).** "Back" (*'achor*) means the hinder side, the back part. It is used elsewhere of the back of the tabernacle (26:12) and of the hindquarters of the bronze oxen in the temple (1 Kgs 7:25). These are human terms for God, who is spirit. Moses would see what was left behind as God's glory passed, like the afterglow of the sun after it has set. It was a real sight of God, but a partial one.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "God's goodness is his glory," and sees Christ in the cleft of the rock: "It is in the clefts of this rock that we are secured from the wrath of God, which otherwise would consume us" (compare 1 Cor 10:4). On seeing God's back he says, "We cannot be said to look at God, but rather to look after him," and adds that a fuller sight is promised later. JFB says simply that "the face, hand, back parts, are to be understood figuratively." Guzik notes that "presence" is literally "face" and that "glory" comes from a root meaning weight, both of which the lexicons support. He resolves the tension between v11 and v20 the way most interpreters do: v11 describes close fellowship, v20 the full sight of God's essence. He suggests the "back" means the after-effects of God's passing glory, and says the word is rarely used of a body. The lexicon does show it used of the hindquarters of animals and figures, so it is not quite as rare as he suggests, though the point stands. He links the chapter to John 1:14 and 2 Corinthians 3:18.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses wanted three things: God's presence with his people, to know God's ways, and to see his glory. All three are given fully in Jesus. He is "Immanuel," God with us (Matt 1:23), the presence that goes with his people "to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20). He is "the way" (John 14:6). And in him the glory Moses glimpsed from behind is seen face to face: "We have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14), "the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6). Moses needed a rock to hide him from God's glory. Believers are hidden in Christ, the rock struck for them, and so they can look on that glory and live. One day "they will see his face" (Rev 22:4).
