*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapter 15 ended with the firstborn animals that were eaten "before the LORD your God year by year." Chapter 16 sets out the yearly calendar those meals belonged to: three great festivals, Passover with Unleavened Bread in spring, Weeks at the wheat harvest, and Booths at the autumn ingathering. The festival laws already appeared in Exodus 23 and 34 and in Leviticus 23. Deuteronomy's emphasis is new: all three are to be kept "at the place that the LORD will choose" (said six times in this chapter), and the whole household, rich and poor, is to rejoice together. The chapter then turns to the judges who will keep justice in every town, and ends by forbidding Canaanite objects of worship beside God's altar.

#### Passover (vv1–8)
- **"Observe the month of Abib" (v1).** *Abib* means "fresh ears" of grain, the season when barley was ripening, roughly March–April. After the exile the month took its Babylonian name Nisan (Esth 3:7). God had made it the first month of Israel's year (Exod 12:2), so the nation's calendar began with its rescue. **"Brought you out of Egypt by night."** Pharaoh gave the order at night (Exod 12:31), and Israel marched out the next day (Num 33:3).
- **"The Passover sacrifice… from the flock or the herd" (v2).** The Passover animal itself was a lamb or young goat from the flock (Exod 12:5). "Or the herd" most likely takes in the other sacrifices of the festival week, since "Passover" could name the whole feast. When Josiah and Hezekiah kept the Passover, cattle were given alongside the lambs (2 Chr 30:24; 35:7–9).
- **"At the place that the LORD will choose, to make his name dwell there" (v2).** This is the big change. In Egypt the lamb was killed at each family's door. In the land it must be killed at the one sanctuary. God's "name" dwelling there means he himself is present and known there (12:5, 11). Keeping the feast in one place guarded it from being blended with local shrines and their gods. It also gathered the whole nation in one place as one people. When Josiah centralized the Passover after years of neglect, Scripture says no such Passover had been kept since the days of the judges (2 Kgs 23:21–23). By Jesus' day Jerusalem's population swelled at Passover, and that is why he and his disciples were there for his last Passover (Luke 22:7–15).
- **"The bread of affliction—for you came out of the land of Egypt in haste" (v3).** Unleavened bread recalled a people who left before their dough could rise (Exod 12:34, 39). It was the bread of slaves on the run, and eating it each year said, "This is where we came from." The Hebrew *lechem 'oni* still opens the Passover Haggadah, the order of the Jewish Passover meal: "This is the bread of affliction." Elsewhere leaven often stands for corruption that spreads (Matt 16:6; 1 Cor 5:6–8), and the Tyndale notes are careful that the first reason for unleavened bread was simple haste.
- **"That all the days of your life you may remember" (v3).** Remembering is a main theme of Deuteronomy (5:15; 15:15; 16:12). The festival was a lesson Israel acted out every year, so that each new generation would know the story as its own.
- **"No leaven shall be seen with you in all your territory" (v4).** Even at home, while the men were at the sanctuary, the whole land kept the feast. **"Nor shall any of the flesh… remain all night until morning."** As in Egypt (Exod 12:10), the Passover meat was eaten that night or burned.
- **"In the evening at sunset, at the time you came out of Egypt" (v6).** The timing matched the first Passover "at twilight" (Exod 12:6). Each year Israel relived the very hour of its rescue.
- **"You shall cook it and eat it" (v7).** Exodus 12:9 says "roasted, not boiled," and the verb here (*bashal*) usually means "boil." But it is a general word for cooking. Second Chronicles 35:13 uses the same verb for roasting the Passover "with fire," so the two passages need not clash. **"In the morning you shall turn and go to your tents."** Either back to the pilgrims' camps around the sanctuary, or home after the festival. The Hebrew allows both.
- **"On the seventh day there shall be a solemn assembly" (v8).** *'Atsereth* is a sacred gathering for worship. The same word is used for the closing day of Booths (Lev 23:36). The week began and ended with the people stopping work to stand before God.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the three explanations for "the herd" (other animals were allowed, the cattle belonged to the wider feast, or they were extra offerings) and sees the feast fulfilled in Christ "our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7). JFB takes "sacrifice the passover" in v2 to mean "the whole feast," including the extra offerings, and reads v7 as releasing the people at the end of the week, not the first morning. Matthew Henry turns the feast into a call to memory: "Never should a believer forget his low estate of guilt and misery, his deliverance, and the price it cost the Redeemer."

#### The Feast of Weeks (vv9–12)
- **"You shall count seven weeks" (v9).** The count began "from the time the sickle is first put to the standing grain," that is, when the barley harvest began at Passover time. Leviticus 23:15–16 counts from "the day after the Sabbath" and comes to fifty days, which is why the Greek-speaking Jews called it *Pentēkostē*, "fiftieth." Jewish groups later argued about which Sabbath was meant. The Hebrew name, *Shavu'ot*, simply means "weeks." Seven sevens of harvest days ended in a feast at the wheat harvest, around late May or early June.
- **"With the tribute of a freewill offering from your hand" (v10).** "Tribute" translates *missath*, a word found only here in the Old Testament, probably meaning "a sufficient amount" or "a measure." No fixed amount is set. **"As the LORD your God blesses you."** The size of the harvest set the size of the gift. Paul's rule for giving is the same: "as he may prosper" (1 Cor 16:2).
- **"You shall rejoice before the LORD your God" (v11).** This is the first of three commands to rejoice in the chapter (vv11, 14, 15). Deuteronomy's festivals are not grim duty. They are a family feast in God's presence.
- **"You and your son and your daughter, your male servant and your female servant, the Levite… the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow" (v11).** Eight kinds of people are named, and the last four had no land of their own and no harvest to celebrate. The poor were not to watch from the edges. They were to eat at the table. Slaves were to rest and feast alongside their masters.
- **"You shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt" (v12).** This was why. Israel knew from history what it felt like to have nothing, so they must not shut out those who still had nothing.
- **Later tradition.** Exodus 19:1 places Israel's arrival at Sinai in the third month, about seven weeks after leaving Egypt. From this, later Jewish tradition kept Weeks as the anniversary of the giving of the law. The text of Deuteronomy does not make that link, but the tradition was widespread by the time of the New Testament.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that no sin offering is named here, only a freewill gift and joy, and he sees the feast fulfilled at Pentecost in Acts 2, when the Spirit was poured out and the first great harvest of believers came in (Acts 2:41). JFB records the link to Sinai: the feast commemorated "the giving of the law." Henry: "Holy joy is the heart and soul of thankful praises," and "our duty must be our delight, as well as our enjoyment."

#### The Feast of Booths (vv13–17)
- **"The Feast of Booths seven days" (v13).** *Sukkoth*, "booths" or "shelters," rough huts of branches. Leviticus 23:42–43 says Israel lived in them for the week to remember the wilderness years in tents. It came in the seventh month (late September or October), **"when you have gathered in the produce from your threshing floor and your winepress"**: grain threshed, grapes and olives pressed, the year's work done. Exodus 23:16 calls it the Feast of Ingathering.
- **"You shall rejoice in your feast" (v14).** The same list of the household and the needy is repeated. Moses does not assume they will remember to include the widow. He says it again.
- **"So that you will be altogether joyful" (v15).** The Hebrew is short and strong: *'akh sameach*, "only joyful" or "truly joyful." Booths was the happiest feast of the year, so well known that later Jewish writers called it simply "the Feast" (compare 1 Kgs 8:2). Every seventh year the law was read aloud to the whole nation at this festival (31:10–13), and after the exile Ezra's reading at Booths brought "very great rejoicing" (Neh 8:13–18).
- **"Three times a year all your males shall appear before the LORD" (v16).** This repeats Exodus 23:14–17 and 34:23. The men were required to go. Women and children came as well, as the household lists in vv11 and 14 show, and Luke 2:41 shows Mary going with Joseph. Exodus 34:24 adds a promise: while the men are away, "no one shall covet your land." Going up was an act of trust that God would guard what they left behind.
- **"They shall not appear before the LORD empty-handed. Every man shall give as he is able" (vv16–17).** No one came to God's house with nothing in his hands. But the measure was "as he is able," literally "according to the gift of his hand." God asked for a response to what he had given, not an amount beyond reach.
- **Why only three feasts?** Leviticus 23 also lists the Feast of Trumpets and the Day of Atonement, which fell in the same autumn month as Booths. Deuteronomy names only the three pilgrimage feasts, because its concern here is the journey to the one sanctuary.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Jesus himself went up to these feasts (Luke 2:41; John 7:2, 10), and he connects Booths with the future rest of God's people, when the nations will come up to keep it (Zech 14:16). JFB says the requirement fell on men only, women being spared the journeys for practical reasons, and records the old Jewish custom that weddings were not held during the great feasts, so that one joy would not crowd out another. Henry on v17: God's people "must rejoice in their receivings from God, and in their returns of service and sacrifice to him."

#### Justice (vv18–20)
- **"You shall appoint judges and officers in all your towns" (v18).** *Shophetim* were judges. The *shoterim* were their officials. The same word was used of Israelite foremen in Egypt (Exod 5:14), and here they were probably clerks and officers who carried out the court's decisions. Jewish readers still call the section from here to 21:9 *Shoftim*, "Judges." Moses had set up a chain of judges in the wilderness (1:13–17; Exod 18). Now justice must be local, in every town. Cases were heard "in the gate" (21:19; 22:15; Ruth 4:1–2), the open space inside the city entrance where the elders sat.
- **"With righteous judgment" (v18).** The Tyndale notes explain this as a fixed standard of fairness that no public pressure or private interest can move.
- **"You shall not show partiality" (v19).** Literally "you shall not recognize faces," that is, let who someone is decide the case. The same command forbids favoring the poor just because they are poor (Lev 19:15; Exod 23:3). James applies it to the church that seats the rich man in the best place (Jas 2:1–9).
- **"A bribe blinds the eyes of the wise and subverts the cause of the righteous" (v19).** Almost word for word from Exodus 23:8. A bribe does not only buy a corrupt judge. It blinds a good one. Samuel's sons "took bribes and perverted justice," and the people's answer was to demand a king (1 Sam 8:3–5).
- **"Justice, and only justice, you shall follow" (v20).** The Hebrew repeats the word: *tsedeq tsedeq tirdoph*, "justice, justice you shall pursue." The verb means to chase, to run after. Justice is not something Israel waits to happen. It is something they hunt down. **"That you may live and inherit the land."** Justice was not only good government. Israel's life in the land depended on it, and the prophets later named injustice as one cause of the exile (Isa 1:21–23; Amos 5:11–15).
- **Commentators:** All three stress justice close to home. Henry counts local courts a great mercy, since justice was near, quick and cheap, and says, "All personal regards must be laid aside, so that right is done to all, and wrong to none." Guzik draws out the Hebrew idiom of "recognizing faces." JFB describes the officers as heralds or bailiffs who carried out the sentences, and notes that the gate was the place of public business.

#### Forbidden Forms of Worship (vv21–22)
- **"You shall not plant any tree as an Asherah beside the altar of the LORD" (v21).** Asherah was a Canaanite goddess, the consort of the chief god El in texts from Ugarit, and her symbol was a sacred tree or a carved wooden pole. Inscriptions found at Kuntillet 'Ajrud in the Sinai, from around 800 BC, actually bless people "by YHWH… and his Asherah." That is exactly the mix this verse forbids: the LORD's altar with a goddess beside it. Later King Manasseh set up an Asherah in the temple itself (2 Kgs 21:7), and Josiah burned it (2 Kgs 23:6).
- **"You shall not set up a pillar, which the LORD your God hates" (v22).** A *matstsebah* was a standing stone. Jacob and Moses had set up pillars as memorials to the LORD without blame (Gen 28:18; Exod 24:4). But in Canaan standing stones belonged to Baal worship (7:5; 12:3), and in Israel's worship they would be read the same way. What was once harmless had become a sign of another religion, so God forbade it.
- **These verses go with what follows.** The ESV heading runs on into 17:1, which forbids blemished sacrifices. All three verses deal with corrupting the worship at God's altar, and they sit between laws about judges because a judge's first duty was to protect the true worship of God (17:2–7).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the point of v21 as keeping Israel from copying pagan customs: "Care is taken to prevent following the idolatrous customs of the heathen." He argues that true worship needs no groves or staging to make it solemn. JFB notes that "grove" can mean a tree, a grove or a wooden image, and that a pillar could serve as a pedestal for an idol. Guzik makes the point that the danger was not open idolatry but adding Canaanite objects to the worship of the LORD.

#### Pointing to Christ
Each feast in this chapter finds its fullness in Jesus. He was crucified at Passover, the true Lamb, so that "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7). At the Feast of Weeks, fifty days later, he poured out his Spirit and the first harvest of the church was gathered (Acts 2). At Booths he stood up in the temple and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37), and John says the Word "dwelt," literally "pitched his tent," among us (John 1:14). The just judge Israel needed is the one of whom Isaiah wrote, "He shall not judge by what his eyes see… but with righteousness he shall judge the poor" (Isa 11:3–4). And the joy of the feasts, with the widow and the slave at the same table, is a picture of his kingdom, where many will come and sit at table with Abraham (Matt 8:11).
