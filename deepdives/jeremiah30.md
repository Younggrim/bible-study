*Setting:* For twenty-nine chapters Jeremiah has mostly torn down. Now, with Babylon closing in on Jerusalem, God tells him to write down words of rebuilding. Chapters 30–33 are often called the "Book of Consolation," the most hopeful stretch of the book. Chapters 32 and 33 are dated to the final siege, about 588–587 BC, and many scholars think chapters 30 and 31 come from the same dark months, though some of the poems may be older. The letter of chapter 29 promised the exiles a return after seventy years (29:10–14) and closed with "I will restore your fortunes" (29:14). Chapter 30 picks up that phrase and runs with it. The promises reach both halves of the old nation: Judah, being carried off to Babylon, and Israel, the northern tribes scattered by Assyria back in 722 BC. They begin with the return from Babylon, but they keep reaching further, to a coming King and a renewed covenant.

#### Restoration for Israel and Judah (vv1–24)
- **"Write in a book all the words that I have spoken to you" (v2).** Jeremiah's earlier sermons were spoken in the temple courts and the city gates. These words are to be written because they are for people who are not yet in the room: exiles far away, and generations who would live to see them come true. In chapter 36 a whole scroll of his judgments will be burned. This book of hope is written to outlast the fire.
- **"Days are coming… when I will restore the fortunes of my people, Israel and Judah" (v3).** The phrase from 29:14 again, *shub shebut*, a full turning-around of fortune. It frames this whole section (30:3, 18; 31:23; 32:44; 33:7, 11, 26). Notice "Israel and Judah." The northern kingdom had been gone for more than a century, yet God has not forgotten it. The promise is for one reunited people. **"Take possession of it."** The language of Joshua's conquest. The return will be a second entry into the land.
- **"We have heard a cry of panic, of terror, and no peace" (v5).** The first poem opens not with joy but with screaming. "No peace" is *ein shalom*, the reverse of the false prophets' "peace, peace" (6:14; 8:11). The healing that follows only makes sense against this wound.
- **"Can a man bear a child? Why then do I see every man with his hands on his stomach like a woman in labor?" (v6).** A deliberately shocking picture. Warriors double over, clutching themselves like women in childbirth. Jeremiah has used labor pains for terror before (4:31; 6:24; 22:23). **"Every face turned pale."** The Hebrew word, *yeraqon*, is used elsewhere of crops withered by blight (Deut 28:22; Amos 4:9). Faces have gone the sickly green-yellow of blasted grain.
- **"That day is so great there is none like it; it is a time of distress for Jacob" (v7).** "That day" is the prophets' language for the Day of the LORD, when God steps in to judge (Joel 2:11; Zeph 1:14–15). "Distress" is *tsarah*, a narrow, hemmed-in place, the same word Jacob himself used of the day he fled from Esau (Gen 35:3). The patriarch's name stands for the whole nation. **"Yet he shall be saved out of it."** Not saved *from* it, but brought through it and *out* of it. The trouble is real, and it will not be the end.
- **"The time of Jacob's trouble" and how readers take it.** The older translation of v7, "the time of Jacob's trouble," has become a well-known phrase. Many interpreters read it of Babylon's conquest and the exile, with the rescue at the return under Cyrus. Others, especially dispensational readers, see it as also pointing to a final great tribulation for Israel before Christ's return, linking it with Daniel 12:1 ("a time of trouble, such as never has been") and Jesus' words in Matthew 24:21. Both readings agree that the first fulfillment was in Jeremiah's own century, and both take seriously that the language of vv8–9 reaches beyond it.
- **"I will break his yoke from off your neck, and I will burst your bonds" (v8).** A pointed reversal. In chapters 27–28 Jeremiah wore a wooden yoke to say Babylon's rule could not be shaken off, and Hananiah broke it and lied that God would break Babylon's yoke in two years (28:2, 10–11). Now the true prophet says the yoke *will* be broken, in God's time and by God's hand. The change of pronoun, from "his yoke" to "your neck" and back, is typical of Hebrew poetry turning from talking about Israel to talking to Israel.
- **"Foreigners shall no more make a servant of him. But they shall serve the LORD their God" (vv8–9).** The same verb, *abad*, "to serve," is used both ways. Israel will stop serving foreign masters and start serving its true Master. Freedom in the Bible is never freedom from all service. It is release from the wrong lord to the right one.
- **"And David their king, whom I will raise up for them" (v9).** David had been dead four hundred years. The phrase means a king from David's line, and the line of kings was about to end. Zedekiah would be the last king of Judah. So this is a promise of a coming Son of David, the one 23:5 called "a righteous Branch." Ezekiel, prophesying at the same time, uses the same language: "my servant David shall be king over them" (Ezek 34:23–24; 37:24), and Hosea had said Israel would "seek the LORD their God, and David their king" (Hos 3:5). No Davidic king reigned after the exile. Christians have always read the promise as fulfilled in Jesus, "the Son of David" (Matt 1:1; Luke 1:32–33; Acts 2:30). **"Raise up"** is the verb used for God raising up deliverers in Judges (Judg 2:16) and a prophet like Moses (Deut 18:15).
- **"Then fear not, O Jacob my servant… nor be dismayed, O Israel" (v10).** Verses 10–11 appear again, almost word for word, in 46:27–28. They sound very like Isaiah 41:8–14 and 43:1–5. "Jacob my servant" is a title of tenderness, not of shame. **"I will save you from far away."** Distance is no obstacle to God. **"Jacob shall return and have quiet and ease, and none shall make him afraid."** The picture of settled, unthreatened life the prophets used for the Messiah's reign (Mic 4:4; Zeph 3:13).
- **"I will make a full end of all the nations among whom I scattered you, but of you I will not make a full end" (v11).** The world empires that scattered Israel, Assyria and Babylon, have long since vanished into history. Israel's survival as a people is a remarkable thread through the centuries. The same promise, "I will not make a full end," appeared in the middle of the judgments (4:27; 5:10, 18).
- **"I will discipline you in just measure, and I will by no means leave you unpunished" (v11).** "In just measure" is literally "with judgment" (*lammishpat*), that is, with fairness and proportion, not with uncontrolled fury. Mercy here does not mean God shrugs at sin. It means the discipline is measured and has a purpose, the way a father corrects a son (Heb 12:5–11). Jeremiah had prayed exactly this: "Correct me, O LORD, but in justice; not in your anger" (10:24).
- **"Your hurt is incurable, and your wound is grievous" (v12).** The second poem (vv12–17) begins in a doctor's language. Jeremiah has asked before, "Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?" (8:22), and has spoken of his own "wound incurable" (15:18). **"There is none to uphold your cause, no medicine for your wound" (v13).** Two pictures at once: a law court with no advocate to plead the case, and a sickroom with no bandage or remedy.
- **"All your lovers have forgotten you" (v14).** The "lovers" are the allies Judah ran to instead of God, especially Egypt, and before that Assyria (2:18, 36; 4:30; 22:20–22). Egypt's army did march out during the siege, and then went home (37:5–7). **"I have dealt you the blow of an enemy."** The hardest line in the poem. The blows that fell on Jerusalem came through Babylon, but behind Babylon stood God. **"Because your guilt is great, because your sins are flagrant."** "Flagrant" means numerous, piled up. The line is repeated in v15 for emphasis.
- **"Why do you cry out over your hurt?" (v15).** Not a cold dismissal of pain but a call to look at its cause. The people were crying over their wounds and not over their sins. The wound is God's doing (**"I have done these things to you"**), and therefore the cure will be God's doing too.
- **"Therefore all who devour you shall be devoured" (v16).** A startling "therefore." Logic would expect "your guilt is great, *therefore* you are finished." Instead, the very hopelessness of Judah's case becomes the reason for God to act. The verse runs in four lines of exact payback: devourers devoured, captors taken captive, plunderers plundered, predators made prey. Babylon would fall to Persia in 539 BC (25:12–14; 50–51).
- **"For I will restore health to you, and your wounds I will heal" (v17).** The wound no physician could treat (vv12–13) God himself will heal. "Health" is literally new flesh growing over a wound. **"Because they have called you an outcast: 'It is Zion, for whom no one cares!'"** "Outcast" (*niddachah*) is used of a woman driven out or a stray animal driven off. The nations' sneer, "no one cares," is precisely what moves God to care. The despised condition of Zion becomes the occasion of his compassion.
- **"I will restore the fortunes of the tents of Jacob and have compassion on his dwellings" (v18).** The third poem (vv18–22) turns from healing to rebuilding. "Tents" recalls Israel's early life as a people of shepherds and families. **"The city shall be rebuilt on its mound."** "Mound" is *tel*, the hill of ruins left when a city is destroyed and rebuilt again and again. Near Eastern cities rose layer upon layer on their own rubble. Jerusalem would not be relocated. It would rise again on the same ground. **"The palace shall stand where it used to be."** "Palace" may be the royal fortress or possibly the temple. The returning exiles did rebuild both the temple (Ezra 3–6) and the walls (Neh 2–6).
- **"Out of them shall come songs of thanksgiving, and the voices of those who celebrate" (v19).** The sound of the city returns. Jeremiah has repeatedly announced the silencing of "the voice of mirth and the voice of gladness" (7:34; 16:9; 25:10). Here it comes back, and 33:11 will promise it again. **"I will multiply them… I will make them honored."** "Honored" is *kabad*, to be weighty, given glory. A people reduced to a remnant will be made great again.
- **"Their children shall be as they were of old, and their congregation shall be established before me" (v20).** "As of old" looks back to the golden years under David and Solomon. "Congregation" (*edah*) is the word for Israel gathered as a worshiping assembly in the wilderness. The nation will again stand before God as his people.
- **"Their prince shall be one of themselves; their ruler shall come out from their midst" (v21).** For decades Judah's kings had been puppets set up by Egypt and Babylon (2 Kgs 23:34; 24:17). The coming ruler will be one of their own, as Moses said the king must be "from among your brothers" (Deut 17:15). "Prince" here is *addir*, "majestic one," not the usual word for king. The word may be chosen on purpose for a ruler who is more than a political figure.
- **"I will make him draw near, and he shall approach me, for who would dare of himself to approach me?" (v21).** This is the most striking line in the chapter. "Draw near" and "approach" are priestly words. In Israel the king did not enter the holy place, and when King Uzziah tried to burn incense he was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21). This ruler will approach God, and God himself will bring him near. "Who would dare" is literally "who is he who has pledged his heart" or "staked his life" to approach. The verb is *arab*, used of going surety for someone, exactly what Judah did when he offered his own life as a pledge for Benjamin: "I will be a pledge of his safety" (Gen 43:9; 44:32). To come near the holy God uninvited was to risk one's life. This ruler will do it, at God's invitation, as both king and priest. That points beyond any king of Judah to the one Psalm 110:4 calls "a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" and Zechariah 6:13 says will "sit and rule on his throne" and be "a priest on his throne."
- **"And you shall be my people, and I will be your God" (v22).** The covenant formula, the heart of every covenant God made with Israel (Exod 6:7; Lev 26:12). Jeremiah has quoted it before as a broken promise (7:23; 11:4). Here it is restored, and 31:33 will make it the center of the new covenant.
- **"Behold the storm of the LORD! Wrath has gone forth, a whirling tempest" (v23).** Verses 23–24 repeat almost exactly 23:19–20, where they warned the false prophets. Here they come right after the promises. Restoration does not cancel judgment. The storm that falls on the wicked, including the nations that devoured Israel, is part of how the good future arrives.
- **"Until he has executed and accomplished the intentions of his mind" (v24).** "Intentions" is from the same root as "plans" in 29:11. God's purposes in judgment and in mercy are one plan, and nothing will turn it back. **"In the latter days you will understand this."** "Latter days" (*acharit hayyamim*) can mean simply "afterward," but in the prophets it often looks toward the time of God's final work (Isa 2:2; Hos 3:5, where it follows "David their king"). Some things about God's dealings only become clear in hindsight.
- **Commentators:** All three read the coming ruler of vv9 and 21 as Christ. Henry says the governor is one "made like unto his brethren," and on the "pledged his heart" line he says Christ "did engage his heart" to approach God for us, binding himself freely. JFB renders the ruler as "their Glorious One," says "of themselves" means he will be a Jew, and sees in "draw near" the Messiah's priestly office. Guzik also sees a king-priest in the line of Melchizedek here, citing Psalm 110 and Zechariah 6. On v9, Guzik goes further than most: he notes the usual reading of "David" as the Messiah but argues that it may describe the resurrected David himself ruling under Christ in the Millennium, a view JFB and Henry do not take. Guzik and JFB both see a near and a far fulfillment in the "time of distress for Jacob," the near one at Babylon and a fuller one still to come. On v15 Henry has a sharp line: "Incurable griefs are owing to incurable lusts." JFB notes the word for "pale" properly means "the color of herbs blasted and fading," which the lexicon supports. Guzik, citing Trapp, observes that a people crying "I am ruined" needs instead to say "I have sinned."

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter moves from a wound no doctor can heal to a King no one expected. Into a nation about to lose its throne, God promises "David their king, whom I will raise up." And he promises more than a king: a ruler from among the people who will come near to God, pledging his own life to do it, as Judah once pledged himself for his brother. Jesus is that one. He is the Son of David who reigns, and the great high priest who "entered once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12). Because he drew near, his people can "draw near with confidence to the throne of grace" (Heb 4:16), and the old covenant words come true for them: "You shall be my people, and I will be your God."
