*Setting:* Jerusalem, in the early 30s AD, perhaps a year or two after Pentecost. The church now numbers many thousands. It meets daily in the temple's Solomon's Portico and in homes, shares its goods so that no one is in need (4:34), and has just been threatened by the Sanhedrin and told not to speak in Jesus' name (4:18–21). Chapter 4 ended with Barnabas selling a field and giving the money. Chapter 5 opens with a couple who copy his gift but not his heart, then turns to a second, harsher arrest. The danger to the church comes first from inside, then from outside.

#### Ananias and Sapphira (vv1–11)
- **"But a man named Ananias" (v1).** "But" ties this story straight to Barnabas (4:36–37). Luke often sets a good example beside a bad one. The name Ananias, a form of Hananiah, means "the LORD has been gracious," and Sapphira probably means "beautiful." They did a thing that looked just like Barnabas's gift.
- **"With his wife's knowledge he kept back for himself some of the proceeds" (v2).** *Nosphizomai* means to set apart for oneself, to embezzle or pilfer. Paul uses it of slaves stealing from masters (Titus 2:10). The Greek Old Testament uses the same verb of Achan, who kept back some of the plunder God had claimed at Jericho (Josh 7:1). Israel's first sin after entering the land and the church's first sin from within are told with the same word, and both end in death.
- **"Why has Satan filled your heart?" (v3).** The church was full of the Spirit (4:31). Here a heart is filled by Satan instead. Luke used the same idea of Judas (Luke 22:3). Ananias is still responsible: "you have contrived this deed in your heart" (v4).
- **"To lie to the Holy Spirit… You have not lied to man but to God" (vv3–4).** Peter says first "the Holy Spirit," then "God," of the same act. It is one of the clearest places in the New Testament where the Spirit is treated both as a person, someone who can be lied to, and as God.
- **"Did it not remain your own?… was it not at your disposal?" (v4).** The sin was not keeping some money. Giving was voluntary. They were free to keep the land, or sell it and keep all the money, or give part and say so. The sin was pretending to give everything to win the honor Barnabas had earned. It was a lie about generosity.
- **"He fell down and breathed his last" (v5).** Peter pronounces no sentence. He exposes the lie, and God acts. Luke uses the rare verb "breathed his last" (*ekpsychō*) only here and of Herod struck down by God (12:23).
- **"The young men… wrapped him up and carried him out and buried him" (v6).** In Jerusalem's heat, burial usually took place the same day. The young men may simply have been the younger and fitter members, not officers.
- **"After an interval of about three hours his wife came in" (v7).** She does not know. Peter gives her a chance to tell the truth.
- **"Tell me whether you sold the land for so much" (v8).** Perhaps he points to the money at his feet. She repeats the lie.
- **"To test the Spirit of the Lord" (v9).** To "test" God is to push him to see how far one can go, as Israel did in the wilderness (Exod 17:2; Deut 6:16; compare 1 Cor 10:9). They had agreed to see whether God would notice.
- **"Buried her beside her husband" (v10).** They agreed in the lie and they lie together in the grave.
- **"Great fear came upon the whole church" (v11).** This is the first time Luke uses the word "church" (*ekklēsia*) in Acts. It meant an assembly of citizens in Greek cities and was the usual word in the Greek Old Testament for the assembly of Israel. The church is God's gathered people, and fear here means awe at a holy God among them (compare 2:43).
- **Why so severe?** Similar sudden judgments mark other beginnings: Nadab and Abihu at the start of the tabernacle worship (Lev 10:1–3), Achan at the start of the conquest (Josh 7), Uzzah when the ark came to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6:6–7). At each new start God shows that he is holy and his presence is not to be treated lightly. Paul warns that some in Corinth had died for taking the Lord's Supper carelessly (1 Cor 11:30). The marvel is less that God judged these two and more how often he is patient.
- **Were they believers?** Luke does not say. Many think they were genuine but deeply sinful believers, disciplined by death as in 1 Corinthians 11:32, "so that we may not be condemned along with the world." Others think their hypocrisy showed they never truly believed. The text leaves it with God.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both link the story to Achan, and Guzik's note that *nosphizomai* is the Septuagint's word in Joshua 7 checks out in the lexicon. JFB says the lie to the Spirit implies "the distinct personality and the proper divinity of the Holy Ghost," and that "the coolness with which they planned the deception aggravated the guilt." Guzik locates the deeper sin in pride, wanting a reputation for total devotion, more than in greed, and says their final state cannot be known. Matthew Henry sees both sins: "covetous of the wealth of the world, and distrustful of God," and adds that the punishment "was in reality mercy to vast numbers," since it protected the young church.

#### Many Signs and Wonders Done (vv12–16)
- **"Many signs and wonders were regularly done… by the hands of the apostles" (v12).** The church had prayed for exactly this (4:30). God answered. The ministry of the apostles looks like Jesus' own (Luke 4:40–41).
- **"All together in Solomon's Portico" (v12).** The covered colonnade on the east side of the temple's outer court (3:11) was their regular meeting place, in public, under the council's nose.
- **"None of the rest dared join them, but the people held them in high esteem" (v13).** After Ananias and Sapphira, no one joined lightly or out of curiosity. "The rest" may mean outsiders who admired them from a distance. Respect from outside and caution about joining went together.
- **"More than ever believers were added to the Lord" (v14).** Not "added to the church," but "to the Lord." **"Both men and women."** Luke often notes women among the believers (8:12; 17:4, 12).
- **"That as Peter came by at least his shadow might fall on some of them" (v15).** Luke reports what people hoped for. He does not say the shadow healed anyone, though v16 says "they were all healed." Elsewhere God meets people through a garment's fringe (Luke 8:44) and through Paul's handkerchiefs (19:12). The power is God's, not in the shadow. In the ancient world a shadow was often thought to carry something of the person.
- **"From the towns around Jerusalem" (v16).** The gospel begins to move outward into Judea, as Jesus said it would (1:8). People come from the surrounding towns before the church goes to them, which it will do only when persecution scatters it (8:1, 4).
- **Commentators:** Guzik and the Tyndale notes both stress that the shadow verse reports people's faith rather than a method, and Guzik compares it to the woman touching Jesus' garment. Guzik also remarks that so far people come to the apostles rather than the apostles going out, and that God will use persecution to send them. JFB sees here "the predicted greatness of Peter" at its height. Matthew Henry: "Christ will work by all his faithful servants; and every one who applies to him shall be healed."

#### The Apostles Arrested and Freed (vv17–42)
- **"The high priest… the party of the Sadducees, and filled with jealousy" (v17).** The ruling priests are jealous of the crowds and the influence the apostles are gaining. Mark says Pilate knew it was out of envy that the chief priests had handed Jesus over (Mark 15:10).
- **"The public prison" (v18).** This time all the apostles are arrested, not just Peter and John (4:3).
- **"An angel of the Lord opened the prison doors" (v19).** A fine irony: the Sadducees did not believe in angels (23:8). God will free Peter by an angel again (12:7–10).
- **"Go and stand in the temple and speak to the people all the words of this Life" (v20).** Not "Escape and hide," but "Go back to the very place you were arrested." "This Life" is the new life that comes through the risen Jesus, "the Author of life" (3:15).
- **"The council, all the senate of the people of Israel" (v21).** Luke stresses a full gathering of the Sanhedrin and the elders. They send for prisoners who are not there.
- **"The prison securely locked and the guards standing at the doors" (v23).** No break-in, no escape. The guards did not notice anything. Luke enjoys the scene: the whole council assembled, and the defendants are back in the temple teaching.
- **"Not by force, for they were afraid of being stoned by the people" (v26).** The apostles come quietly. They do not use the crowd's support to resist.
- **"You intend to bring this man's blood upon us" (v28).** The high priest will not say Jesus' name: "this name," "this man." He fears being blamed for an innocent man's death. Yet before Pilate the crowd had shouted, "His blood be on us and on our children!" (Matt 27:25).
- **"We must obey God rather than men" (v29).** The principle from 4:19, now stated flatly. Socrates told his Athenian judges almost the same thing four centuries earlier, so the line would have sounded noble to Greek readers too. It does not license disobeying authorities in general, but it does draw the line where human law forbids what God commands.
- **"Whom you killed by hanging him on a tree" (v30).** "Tree" (*xylon*) means wood, here the cross. Peter is echoing Deuteronomy 21:22–23: anyone hanged on a tree is under God's curse. The leaders meant to brand Jesus as cursed. Paul explains that this is exactly how Christ "redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13; compare 1 Pet 2:24).
- **"Leader and Savior, to give repentance to Israel and forgiveness of sins" (v31).** "Leader" is *archēgos*, the same word as "Author" in 3:15. Even repentance is God's gift. Peter offers the very council that killed Jesus his forgiveness.
- **"And so is the Holy Spirit, whom God has given to those who obey him" (v32).** Two witnesses: the apostles who saw, and the Spirit whose works the city can see. The law required two witnesses (Deut 19:15).
- **"They were enraged and wanted to kill them" (v33).** "Enraged" is literally "sawn through," cut to the quick. Compare the crowd at Pentecost, "cut to the heart" (2:37), which led to repentance. Same message, opposite response.
- **"A Pharisee in the council named Gamaliel" (v34).** Gamaliel the Elder, grandson (by tradition) of the great teacher Hillel, was the most honored rabbi of his day. The Mishnah says that when he died "the glory of the Law ceased." Saul of Tarsus studied under him (22:3). Pharisees, unlike Sadducees, believed in the resurrection and were popular with the people, so the Sadducees had to listen to him.
- **Theudas and Judas (vv36–37).** Gamaliel cites two failed movements. **Judas the Galilean** led a revolt against Roman taxation at the census of AD 6 (Josephus mentions him too), and his movement fed the later Zealots. **Theudas** is the hard one. Josephus describes a Theudas who led a crowd to the Jordan, promising to part it, and was beheaded around AD 45, about ten years *after* Gamaliel spoke, and long after Judas. Scholars answer this in different ways. Many note that Theudas was a common name and that Josephus says there were countless uprisings after Herod's death in 4 BC, so Gamaliel may mean an earlier Theudas whom Josephus does not name. Others think either Luke or Josephus got the dates confused. The point of the argument is not affected: movements of man come to nothing.
- **"If it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them" (v39).** Gamaliel's advice is shrewd and, in this case, God used it to spare the apostles. But it is not a complete test of truth. Some false movements have lasted for centuries, and some true work looks like failure for a while. **"You might even be found opposing God!"** That is exactly what Saul, his own student, will be told on the Damascus road (26:14).
- **"They beat them" (v40).** Probably the synagogue flogging of "forty lashes less one" (Deut 25:3; 2 Cor 11:24), a severe and humiliating punishment. The verb *derō* originally meant to skin or flay, but by this time it had come to mean to beat or thrash, as it does throughout the New Testament.
- **"Rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name" (v41).** A paradox in the wording: "counted worthy to be dishonored." Jesus told them, "Blessed are you when people… revile you… on account of the Son of Man. Rejoice in that day" (Luke 6:22–23). Peter later writes the same (1 Pet 4:13–16). "The name" alone now means Jesus.
- **"Every day, in the temple and from house to house" (v42).** Public and private, the large gathering and the home. The council said, "speak no more in this name." Luke ends: they "did not cease… preaching that the Christ is Jesus."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "There is no prison so dark, so strong, but God can visit his people in it, and, if he pleases, fetch them out," and, on v41, "Reproach for Christ is true preferment." JFB calls "the words of this life" a "beautiful expression for that Life in the Risen One," and holds that Gamaliel's Theudas is "not the same" as Josephus's. Guzik sees Gamaliel as a fence-sitter whose wait-and-see advice is wise in the short run but a poor rule, since success is not the measure of truth. Guzik's suggestion that the verb for beating here means skinning goes beyond the evidence. The lexicon gives "flay" as the word's original sense but "beat, thrash" for every New Testament use.

#### Pointing to Christ
The God who is present in his church is holy, and he will not be lied to. The same God opens locked doors for his witnesses and sends them back to the temple with "all the words of this Life." At the center is Jesus, hung on a tree like one cursed, now raised and exalted as Leader and Savior, who gives repentance and forgiveness even to the council that killed him. To be dishonored for his name becomes an honor, because he first bore the shame for his people.
