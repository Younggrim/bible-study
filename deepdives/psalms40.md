*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David." Psalm 39 ended in the dark: "Look away from me… before I depart and am no more." Psalm 40 opens in the light: "I waited patiently for the LORD; he inclined to me and heard my cry." The psalm has two halves that seem to run backwards. It begins with thanksgiving for a rescue already given (vv1–10) and ends with a fresh cry for help in new trouble (vv11–17). That is how real faith often goes: yesterday's deliverance gives courage for today's prayer. Verses 13–17 appear again, almost word for word, as Psalm 70, so they seem to have been used as a prayer on their own. No occasion is named. The psalm is best known because Hebrews 10:5–10 quotes vv6–8 as the words of Christ himself as he came into the world. Readers differ on how to hold the two together. JFB reads the whole psalm as Christ speaking in his human nature. Most Christian interpreters read it as David's own prayer that, in vv6–8 especially, describes an obedience only Jesus fully lived.

#### He Drew Me Up from the Pit (vv1–5)
- **"I waited patiently for the LORD" (v1).** The Hebrew doubles the verb: literally "waiting, I waited." It stresses how long and how hard he waited. Psalm 37:7 told the righteous to "wait patiently." Here is a man who did, and lived to tell it.
- **"He inclined to me and heard my cry" (v1).** "Inclined" means to bend or stoop down, as one leans toward a sick person to catch a weak voice. JFB: "as if to catch the faintest sigh."
- **"He drew me up from the pit of destruction" (v2).** The Hebrew is *bor sha'on*, literally "pit of roaring" or "pit of tumult." The lexicon notes that *sha'on* is the roar of rushing water (65:7), so the picture may be a pit with water crashing in. A "pit" (*bor*) was often a cistern, a bottle-shaped hole cut in rock to store rainwater. Joseph was thrown into one (Gen 37:24). Jeremiah was lowered into one, "and there was no water in the cistern, but only mud, and Jeremiah sank in the mud" (Jer 38:6). JFB points to that story. The "pit" is also a common picture for the grave and Sheol (28:1; 30:3).
- **"Out of the miry bog" (v2).** Literally "mud of mire." The deeper someone struggles in mud like that, the deeper he sinks. He cannot get out by effort. Someone has to pull him out from above.
- **"And set my feet upon a rock, making my steps secure" (v2).** From sinking mud to solid rock. The rock is a frequent picture of God himself (18:2; 62:2). Jesus' parable of the wise builder uses the same contrast (Matt 7:24–27).
- **"He put a new song in my mouth" (v3).** Even the song is God's gift. A "new song" in the Psalms is a fresh song for a fresh act of God (33:3; 96:1; 98:1). In Revelation the redeemed sing "a new song" to the Lamb (Rev 5:9).
- **"Many will see and fear, and put their trust in the LORD" (v3).** One person's rescue becomes others' testimony. "See and fear" is a wordplay in Hebrew (*yir'u… yira'u*, "they will see… they will fear"). The fear here is reverent awe that leads to trust.
- **"Blessed is the man who makes the LORD his trust" (v4).** A beatitude like Psalm 1:1, 32:1 and 34:8. The opposite is to "turn to the proud," people who trust themselves, and "those who go astray after a lie," which may mean false gods. Idols are often called "lies" in the prophets (Amos 2:4; Jer 16:19).
- **"Your wondrous deeds and your thoughts toward us" (v5).** God does not only act for his people. He thinks about them, plans for them. "Thoughts" are his purposes. Psalm 139:17–18 says the same: "How precious to me are your thoughts, O God! How vast is the sum of them!" Jeremiah 29:11: "I know the plans I have for you."
- **"None can compare with you!… they are more than can be told" (v5).** The ESV reads "none can compare"; the phrase can also mean "none can set them in order before you," that is, no one can list them all. Either way, God's goodness outruns any attempt to count it.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the pit and clay as including "doubts and fears about the eternal state," which have troubled many of God's children, and says "Christ is the Rock on which a poor soul can alone stand fast." He adds that where God gives steady hope "he expects there should be a steady, regular walk." Guzik passes on Poole's reading of the doubled verb as persistent, diligent waiting. JFB links the pit to Jeremiah's cistern and says the plural "us" in v5 "accords with the union of Christ and His people."

#### I Delight to Do Your Will (vv6–10)
- **"In sacrifice and offering you have not delighted" (v6).** This does not mean God rejected the sacrifices he himself commanded in the Law. It means sacrifices were never the goal. They were meant to express a heart given to God. Samuel told Saul, "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22). David says in 51:16–17 that God wants "a broken and contrite heart." The prophets repeat it (Hos 6:6; Mic 6:6–8; Isa 1:11–17). Four kinds of offering are named: sacrifice (animal offerings in general, especially peace offerings), offering (grain offerings), burnt offering and sin offering. Together they cover the whole sacrificial system.
- **"But you have given me an open ear" (v6).** The Hebrew is literally "ears you have dug for me." The verb *karah* means to dig, used for digging a well or a pit. The lexicon explains it as hollowing out the ear: God has given him the ability to hear and obey. Isaiah's Servant says, "The Lord GOD has opened my ear, and I was not rebellious" (Isa 50:5). Some connect it with the slave who chose to stay with his master for life and had his ear pierced with an awl (Exod 21:6), but that passage uses a different verb, "bore," and mentions one ear, not two. The basic idea is clear: God wants listening, obedient ears more than animals on an altar.
- **"A body you have prepared for me" (Heb 10:5).** The Septuagint, the Greek translation used by the early church, reads this line differently: "a body you have prepared for me." No one knows exactly how this arose. Some think the translators gave a free paraphrase, reasoning that the ear stands for the whole body offered in obedience. Others think a copying error in the Greek turned "ears" into "body." Hebrews quotes the Greek and builds on it: Christ came in a real human body to offer that body as the one true sacrifice, "and by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all" (Heb 10:10). The two readings point the same way. The Hebrew says God wants an obedient servant; the Greek says how God provided one.
- **"Then I said, 'Behold, I have come'" (v7).** This is the language of a servant reporting for duty, "Here I am" (1 Sam 3:4; Isa 6:8). Hebrews hears it on the lips of the Son entering the world.
- **"In the scroll of the book it is written of me" (v7).** "The scroll of the book" is most likely the Law of Moses. For David as king, the Tyndale notes point to Deuteronomy 17:18–20, where the king was to write out a copy of the law and read it all his life. For Jesus, the whole Scripture was written about him: "these are the Scriptures that bear witness about me" (John 5:39; Luke 24:27, 44). JFB mentions that the phrase could also mean "written *on* me," that is, prescribed for me.
- **"I delight to do your will, O my God; your law is within my heart" (v8).** Here is the heart God wanted all along. Not obedience as a burden, but delight. The new covenant promise is that God would write his law on his people's hearts (Jer 31:33). Jesus said, "My food is to do the will of him who sent me" (John 4:34), and in Gethsemane, "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). What David could only partly say, Jesus fully lived.
- **"I have told the glad news of deliverance in the great congregation" (v9).** "Told the glad news" is the Hebrew verb *basar*, to bring good news. The Septuagint translates it with *euangelizō*, the New Testament word for preaching the gospel. "Deliverance" here is literally "righteousness": he has announced God's righteousness, his faithful, saving action. "The great congregation" is the gathered worshippers at the festivals.
- **"I have not hidden your deliverance within my heart" (v10).** God's law is *in* his heart (v8), but God's deliverance must not be *kept* in his heart. Five qualities of God are named in vv9–10: righteousness, faithfulness, salvation, steadfast love and faithfulness again. He has kept back none of them.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv6–8 as foretelling "the setting apart of our Lord Jesus to the work and office of Mediator," the "Substance" to which the sacrifices pointed. He takes the "volume of the book" as the eternal "covenant of redemption" recorded "in the close rolls of the Divine decrees." That is a theological reading beyond what the text requires; JFB, more plainly, says the book is the Pentateuch. JFB also says Hebrews "does not lay any stress" on the Septuagint's "body" clause, since the argument rests on Christ doing God's will. Guzik follows the ear-boring reading of v6 and notes that two ears are mentioned, so he reads it as total surrender. The lexicon's verb is "dig," not the "bore" of Exodus 21, so that link is suggestive at most. Guzik also passes on Horne's reading, "made me obedient," as in Isaiah 50:5.

#### Be Pleased, O LORD, to Deliver Me (vv11–17)
- **"As for you, O LORD, you will not restrain your mercy from me" (v11).** A deliberate echo. David said "I have not restrained my lips" (v9). Now he trusts God not to restrain his mercy. JFB reads it as a confident statement, "will not withhold." "Mercy" is God's tender compassion, from the word for a mother's womb.
- **"For evils have encompassed me beyond number" (v12).** God's wonders were "more than can be told" (v5). Now his troubles are "beyond number." The two counts stand side by side.
- **"My iniquities have overtaken me, and I cannot see" (v12).** This trouble comes partly from his own sin, as in Psalms 38 and 39. The iniquities are pictured as pursuers who have caught up with him. "I cannot see" may mean he cannot look up for shame, or that his eyes have grown dim with exhaustion (38:10).
- **"They are more than the hairs of my head; my heart fails me" (v12).** The same comparison is used of David's enemies in 69:4. "My heart fails me" is literally "my heart has forsaken me." His courage is gone.
- **Can Christ say v12?** If the whole psalm is Christ's voice, as JFB holds, then "my iniquities" is a problem, since Jesus had no sin. JFB answers that "iniquities" here means the penalties of sin, not sins themselves, and connects the verse with Gethsemane. Others say Christ took our sins as his own when he bore them (2 Cor 5:21; Isa 53:6). Most modern interpreters read vv11–17 as David's prayer, and see vv6–8 as the part the New Testament applies directly to Christ.
- **"Be pleased, O LORD, to deliver me! O LORD, make haste to help me!" (v13).** This is where Psalm 70 begins. "Be pleased" is *ratsah*, the verb the Law uses for God accepting an offering with favor (Lev 1:4; 22:27). God did not delight in sacrifices for their own sake (v6). David asks him instead to be pleased to save.
- **"Let those be put to shame… who seek to snatch away my life" (v14).** David asks that his enemies' plans fail and that they be turned back in disgrace. JFB calls this "not necessarily imprecatory" (not simply a curse) but a confident expectation of what God's justice will do, and says it fits with Christ praying for his murderers. Paul applies a similar line from Psalm 69 to those who reject Christ (Rom 11:9–10).
- **"Who say to me, 'Aha, Aha!'" (v15).** The jeering cry of people enjoying someone's fall (35:21, 25). Mockers said much the same at the cross (Mark 15:29).
- **"But may all who seek you rejoice and be glad in you" (v16).** He prays for others as well as himself. "Those who love your salvation" are people who delight in what God does to rescue. JFB: they delight in it being given "to others as well as themselves." Their constant cry is "Great is the LORD!"
- **"As for me, I am poor and needy, but the Lord takes thought for me" (v17).** "Poor and needy" is the standard phrase for the helpless who depend on God alone (35:10; 86:1). "Takes thought" is the verb behind "your thoughts toward us" in v5. The God whose thoughts toward his people are beyond counting is thinking about this one poor man.
- **"You are my help and my deliverer; do not delay, O my God!" (v17).** The psalm that began with patient waiting (v1) ends by asking God not to wait. Both are faith. Patience and urgency belong together in prayer.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The best saints see themselves undone, unless continually preserved by the grace of God," and "No griefs nor poverty can render those miserable who fear the Lord." He says the sight of our sins "in their own colours" would drive us to despair but makes a Saviour welcome. Guzik draws out the pattern of v13, "Be pleased, O LORD," as a way to pray, asking God to delight in answering. JFB ties "thinketh upon me" (v17) to "provides for me" and to Christ's prayers "with loud cries and tears" (Heb 5:7).

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews hears Psalm 40 as the Son's words on entering the world: "Behold, I have come to do your will, O God" (Heb 10:7). Every animal on every altar pointed toward something God actually wanted: a human being who would gladly do his will with his whole heart. No one ever did, until Jesus. His ears were always open, his delight was always the Father's will, and his body was the offering that ended all the others, "once for all" (Heb 10:10). He went down into the pit of death and was drawn up again in resurrection, and now he leads his people in a new song (Heb 2:12). The pit, the mire and the cry for help in this psalm belong to every believer. The rock and the new song are given because Christ came and did what was written of him.
