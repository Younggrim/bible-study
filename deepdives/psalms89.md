*Setting:* "A Maskil of Ethan the Ezrahite." Ethan was one of the wise men of Solomon's day, named beside Heman, the author of Psalm 88 (1 Kgs 4:31). He may be the same as Ethan the Levite singer whom David appointed with Heman and Asaph (1 Chr 15:17–19), though that is not certain. Psalm 89 closes Book 3 of the Psalter (Pss 73–89), and it is the great psalm of God's covenant with David. In 2 Samuel 7 God promised David, through the prophet Nathan, that his offspring would sit on his throne forever. The first two-thirds of this psalm celebrate that promise in soaring language (vv1–37). Then comes a jolt: "But now you have cast off and rejected" (v38). A king of David's line has been humiliated, his walls broken and his crown in the dust, and the psalmist asks how that squares with God's oath. The occasion is not named. JFB suggests Absalom's revolt, Henry mentions both the split of the kingdom after Solomon and the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon in 586 BC, when King Jehoiachin was taken into exile and King Zedekiah was blinded and led away in chains (2 Kgs 24–25). Many scholars think the exile fits best, but that would put it centuries after Solomon's Ethan, and some suggest a later singer of the same guild or a later editor. What is clear is the tension: a sworn promise on one side, a ruined throne on the other. The psalm holds both and does not let go of either. "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) appears seven times and "faithfulness" (*'emunah*) seven times.

#### I Will Sing of the Steadfast Love of the LORD (vv1–4)
- **"I will sing of the steadfast love of the LORD forever" (v1).** The psalm opens with a vow of praise, knowing full well where it will go in v38. That is deliberate. Ethan sets God's character down first, before he looks at the wreckage. *Chesed* is God's loyal covenant love, love that has bound itself by promise. "Faithfulness" is its partner: God is reliable, he keeps his word. JFB ties the "mercies" to Isaiah 55:3, "my steadfast, sure love for David," which Paul quotes of the resurrection of Jesus (Acts 13:34).
- **"Steadfast love will be built up forever; in the heavens you will establish your faithfulness" (v2).** "Built up" echoes the wordplay of 2 Samuel 7, where David wanted to build God a house, and God said instead that he would build David a house, a dynasty (2 Sam 7:5, 11, 27). Guzik makes this connection. God's love is like a building that keeps rising, and his faithfulness is as fixed as the sky.
- **"I have made a covenant with my chosen one; I have sworn to David my servant" (v3).** Now God's own words are quoted, a summary of 2 Samuel 7:12–16. A covenant is a binding commitment. An oath is God putting his own name behind it. The covenant with David is unconditional in its final outcome: the line will last. The rest of the psalm unfolds these two verses.
- **"I will establish your offspring forever, and build your throne for all generations" (v4).** "Offspring" is literally "seed." The promise reaches beyond Solomon to a son who would reign forever (Isa 9:6–7; Luke 1:32–33).

#### Who Is Like the LORD? (vv5–18)
- **"Let the heavens praise your wonders, O LORD… in the assembly of the holy ones" (v5).** Before he speaks of David, Ethan lifts his eyes to God's heavenly court. "The holy ones" and "the heavenly beings" (v6, literally "sons of God," as the Tyndale notes say) are angels. Even they cannot compare with the LORD. He is "greatly to be feared in the council of the holy ones" (v7). The point is that a promise is only as good as the one who made it, and this one has no equal.
- **"O LORD God of hosts, who is mighty as you are… with your faithfulness all around you?" (v8).** "Hosts" are armies, angelic and starry. Faithfulness surrounds God like a robe or an escort. It is not something he does now and then. It is part of who he is.
- **"You rule the raging of the sea; when its waves rise, you still them" (v9).** In the ancient world the sea was the image of chaos and danger. Canaanite stories told of the storm god Baal defeating Yam, the sea god. The Bible borrows the picture to say that the LORD alone rules the sea, with no battle needed (Gen 1:2, 9; Job 38:8–11).
- **"You crushed Rahab like a carcass" (v10).** *Rahab* (meaning "storm" or "arrogance") is the sea monster of chaos (Job 26:12) and a poetic nickname for Egypt (Ps 87:4; Isa 30:7). It is not the woman of Jericho, whose name is a different Hebrew word. The verse looks back to the Exodus, when God shattered Egypt at the sea "with a mighty arm" (Exod 15:6; Deut 4:34). Isaiah joins the two pictures: "Was it not you who cut Rahab in pieces… who dried up the sea?" (Isa 51:9–10).
- **"The north and the south, you have created them; Tabor and Hermon joyously praise your name" (v12).** Every direction belongs to God. Tabor is the rounded hill rising out of the Jezreel Valley in Galilee. Hermon is the snowy mountain on Israel's far northern border, over 9,000 feet high. A small hill and a great mountain, side by side, both praising their maker.
- **"Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne; steadfast love and faithfulness go before you" (v14).** This is one of the great summaries of God's rule (97:2). His throne rests on right dealing, and love and faithfulness go ahead of him like heralds before a king. JFB pictures them as his "ministers."
- **"Blessed are the people who know the festal shout" (v15).** The "festal shout" (*teru'ah*) is the joyful trumpet blast and cheering of Israel's feasts (Lev 23:24; Num 10:10). JFB reads it as knowing the spiritual blessings the feasts stood for. To "walk in the light of your face" is to live under God's favor, the blessing of Numbers 6:25.
- **"Our shield belongs to the LORD, our king to the Holy One of Israel" (v18).** "Shield" is a title for the king, the people's protector (47:9; 84:9). The king is God's, and so is the people's safety. That verse is the bridge into the covenant with David.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the sea and Rahab imagery as borrowed from pagan myth and turned against it: Yahweh is not one god fighting others, he simply rules. He also suggests Rahab may stand for every proud enemy of God. Henry divides the first half into praise for God's mercy and truth, awe at his glory, and the happiness of those who live in his favor. JFB sees in vv8–14 God's control of the sea and of Egypt, "the first great foe of Israel."

#### I Have Found David, My Servant (vv19–37)
- **"Of old you spoke in a vision to your godly one" (v19).** The "godly one" (*chasid*) is probably Nathan, who received the covenant "in a vision" (2 Sam 7:17), as JFB and Spurgeon (cited by Guzik) suggest. Some manuscripts read the plural, "your godly ones," meaning Israel.
- **"I have found David, my servant; with my holy oil I have anointed him" (v20).** God went looking and "found" him, the shepherd boy no one thought to call in (1 Sam 16:1, 11–13). Paul quotes this line in his sermon at Pisidian Antioch on the way to announcing Jesus as David's son (Acts 13:22–23). "Anointed" is the verb *mashach*, from which comes *mashiach*, "anointed one," Messiah, and through Greek, "Christ."
- **"My faithfulness and my steadfast love shall be with him" (v24).** The two words of v1 are now attached to the king. God promises protection from enemies (vv22–23), a reach from "the sea" to "the rivers" (v25), the Mediterranean to the Euphrates, the borders promised to Abraham (Gen 15:18).
- **"He shall cry to me, 'You are my Father, my God, and the Rock of my salvation'" (v26).** God promised David's son, "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Sam 7:14). The king was God's adopted son, ruling on his behalf (Ps 2:7).
- **"I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" (v27).** "Firstborn" (*bekor*) in the Bible is often a matter of rank, not birth order. Israel is God's "firstborn son" (Exod 4:22), and Ephraim is called firstborn though younger (Jer 31:9). It means the heir, the one with first place. "Highest" is *'elyon*, the word used of God himself as "Most High." JFB: "firstborn" means chief, "most beloved, or distinguished," and he links it to Colossians 1:15. No earthly king of David's line ever ruled over "the kings of the earth." The words wait for one greater. Revelation calls Jesus "the faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings on earth" (Rev 1:5), almost certainly echoing vv27 and 37.
- **"If his children forsake my law… then I will punish their transgression with the rod" (vv30–32).** This is a father's discipline, straight from 2 Samuel 7:14: "When he commits iniquity, I will discipline him with the rod of men." The covenant does not ignore sin. Individual kings could be punished, even removed.
- **"But I will not remove from him my steadfast love or be false to my faithfulness" (v33).** The family line itself would not be cast off. Discipline, yes; abandonment, no. That is the heart of the promise, and it is exactly what the second half of the psalm will test.
- **"Once for all I have sworn by my holiness; I will not lie to David" (v35).** God has no one higher to swear by, so he swears by himself (Heb 6:13–18). "Holiness" means his utter difference from everything created, and his purity. The Hebrew oath form is literally "if I lie to David," with the curse left unspoken, as JFB notes. God binds himself as strongly as language can.
- **"His throne as long as the sun before me. Like the moon it shall be established forever, a faithful witness in the skies" (vv36–37).** The sun and moon have kept their courses since creation. God says his promise to David is as sure as the sky (Jer 33:20–21, 25–26 makes the same argument). Look up at night and see a witness to God's word.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the promises partly fulfilled in Solomon and fully only in Jesus, especially the firstborn of v27 and the endless throne. JFB treats v27 as pointing to David's "typical relation to Christ," and on v20 explains "found" as God seeking out and selecting David. Henry reads the covenant with David "as a type of Christ" and urges "a lively faith in his covenant with the Redeemer." The Tyndale notes add that God's faithfulness to David's line matches his faithfulness in holding creation in order.

#### You Have Cast Off and Rejected (vv38–45)
- **"But now you have cast off and rejected; you are full of wrath against your anointed" (v38).** The turn is sudden and shocking. "Your anointed" is the reigning king of David's line. Everything God promised in vv19–37 seems to have been undone, point by point. God promised to strengthen his hand (v21); now God has "exalted the right hand of his foes" (v42). God promised his enemies would not humble him (v22); now "all his enemies rejoice" (v42). God promised an exalted horn (v24); now his crown is "defiled in the dust" (v39).
- **"You have renounced the covenant with your servant" (v39).** "Renounced" is a rare verb (used elsewhere only in Lam 2:7), meaning to spurn or abhor. Ethan is not calling God a liar. He is saying what it feels like. Guzik reads it this way, as the language of perception and pain, not an accusation of bad faith. The bluntness is honest lament, the kind God invites.
- **"You have breached all his walls… All who pass by plunder him" (vv40–41).** The king is pictured as a vineyard with its walls broken down, open to every passerby (80:12). JFB notes the images: broken hedges, a useless sword (v43), a youth aged before his time (v45).
- **"You have cut short the days of his youth; you have covered him with shame" (v45).** If the setting is the exile, this fits Jehoiachin, who was eighteen when he became king and reigned only three months before Babylon took him away (2 Kgs 24:8–15). But the text does not name him, and Christians have long heard more in it.

#### How Long, O LORD? (vv46–51)
- **"How long, O LORD? Will you hide yourself forever?" (v46).** "How long" is the classic cry of lament (13:1; 79:5). It assumes the wrath will end. The question is when.
- **"Remember how short my time is!… What man can live and never see death?" (vv47–48).** Ethan pleads his mortality. If God waits too long, he will die without seeing the promise kept. Psalm 90, which opens Book 4, takes up exactly this theme of human frailty. Guzik notes that only Jesus has ever answered the question of v48, by rising from the power of the grave.
- **"Lord, where is your steadfast love of old, which by your faithfulness you swore to David?" (v49).** The two great words of v1 come back one last time, now as a question. That is the psalm's honesty in a single line. He does not stop believing in God's love and faithfulness. He holds God to them.
- **"How I bear in my heart the insults of all the many nations… they mock the footsteps of your anointed" (vv50–51).** "Bear in my heart" is literally "in my bosom," carrying the shame close (JFB compares 69:9). The nations mock "the footsteps" of the king, every step he takes. Their mockery is really aimed at the God who made the promise.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this second half a lament for "the present calamitous state of the prince and royal family," followed by reasoning with God and a plea for relief. JFB: these pleas are pressed because the enemies have reproached God himself. The Tyndale notes observe that the psalmist wonders whether he will even live to see God's anger end.

#### Blessed Be the LORD Forever (v52)
- **"Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and Amen" (v52).** Each of the five books of Psalms ends with a doxology (41:13; 72:19; 89:52; 106:48; and Ps 150 for the last book). This one closes Book 3, as the Tyndale notes and JFB say, and was probably added by those who arranged the Psalter. Yet its place here preaches. After the hardest questions in the book, the last word is praise. The editors did not resolve the tension. They put the doxology next to it and trusted God.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads it as "returning confidence" (compare 34:1–3). Guzik agrees it likely marks the end of Book 3 while also serving as the psalm's own close.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 89 is the question the Old Testament could not answer by itself: how can God swear that David's throne will last forever and then let it fall into the dust? The answer is Jesus. The angel told Mary, "the Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David, and he will reign… forever, and of his kingdom there will be no end" (Luke 1:32–33). He is the anointed one who was truly "cast off," whose days were cut short in his youth, who was mocked by all who passed by (Matt 27:39) and covered with shame, crowned with thorns instead of gold. Then God raised him from the power of Sheol (v48), and made him "the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings on earth" (Rev 1:5). The "sure mercies of David" (Isa 55:3) are, Paul says, the resurrection itself (Acts 13:34). God did not lie to David. "For all the promises of God find their Yes in him. That is why it is through him that we utter our Amen to God for his glory" (2 Cor 1:20).
