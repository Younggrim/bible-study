*Setting:* Jerusalem, late 700s BC, with the Assyrian army a constant threat. Micah's second round of messages (chs 3–5) began by condemning Jerusalem's leaders and announcing that Zion would be ploughed like a field (3:12). Then came a vision of the LORD's mountain raised above all others, with nations streaming to it and swords beaten into ploughshares (4:1–5). Chapter 4 ended with Zion in labour, carried to Babylon, rescued there, and finally threshing the nations who gloated over her. Chapter 5 brings the round to its climax: a King born in little Bethlehem, a shepherd whose origins are ancient, who will be his people's peace. Seven centuries later, when the wise men asked Herod where the Messiah would be born, the priests and scribes answered from this chapter (Matt 2:4–6). In the Hebrew Bible, v1 is the last verse of chapter 4, so the verse numbers there run one lower (5:2 in English is 5:1 in Hebrew).

#### The Ruler to Be Born in Bethlehem (vv1–6)
- **"Now muster your troops, O daughter of troops; siege is laid against us" (v1).** The fourth "now" in a row (4:9, 10, 11; 5:1). Jerusalem is under siege. The verb (*gadad*) can mean "gather in troops," but its common meaning is "gash yourself," the pagan mourning rite (1 Kgs 18:28; Deut 14:1). BDB lists Micah 5:1 under "gather in troops," but the double sense may be intended: rally your soldiers, or slash yourselves in grief.
- **"With a rod they strike the judge of Israel on the cheek" (v1).** A slap on the face was the deepest insult in the ancient world (1 Kgs 22:24; Job 16:10). "Rod" (*shebet*) is also the word for a ruler's sceptre, and "judge" (*shophet*) sounds similar. The enemy's sceptre strikes Israel's ruler. Micah calls him "judge" rather than "king," perhaps because the kings of his day had failed to be kings at all. In 586 BC King Zedekiah was captured, made to watch his sons executed, and blinded (2 Kgs 25:7). Christians have long noticed the echo in Jesus, Israel's true ruler, struck on the head with a reed and slapped in the face before his crucifixion (Matt 26:67; 27:30; John 18:22).
- **"But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah" (v2).** "But" turns from the humiliated ruler to the coming one. Bethlehem means "house of bread," Ephrathah means "fruitful," and the double name marks it out from another Bethlehem in Zebulun (Josh 19:15). Ephrathah was the old clan name of the district (Gen 35:19; Ruth 1:2; 4:11). Rachel was buried on its road. Ruth gleaned there. David was born there (1 Sam 17:12) and anointed there (1 Sam 16). Micah sends us back to the beginning of David's line, as though God were starting it over.
- **"Who are too little to be among the clans of Judah" (v2).** "Clans" is *'alphei*, the "thousands" of Judah, the military and tribal units it was divided into. Bethlehem was a village of a few hundred people, five miles south of Jerusalem, so small it could hardly field its own unit. In Matthew the scribes quote the verse as "you… are by no means least among the rulers of Judah" (Matt 2:6). Matthew is not contradicting Micah. He is drawing out the result: the town that was least is made great by the one born there. JFB reconciles them the same way.
- **"From you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel" (v2).** "For me" means God is speaking: this ruler comes on God's behalf, as God's king. "Ruler" is *moshel*, not the usual word for king (*melek*). It may be deliberate. Kings had failed. This one will truly rule. Everyone in Jesus' day understood this verse to be about the Messiah: "Has not the Scripture said that the Christ comes from the offspring of David, and comes from Bethlehem, the village where David was?" (John 7:42).
- **"Whose coming forth is from of old, from ancient days" (v2).** "Coming forth" (*motsa'ot*) is a rare plural noun from the same verb as "come forth" earlier in the verse. BDB gives "his origin." The play is deliberate: he will come forth from Bethlehem, but his coming forth is far older. "From of old" (*qedem*) and "from ancient days" (literally "from days of *'olam*") can mean the far past or eternity. Readers differ on how far back Micah means. Some read it as the ancient origin of David's royal line, using the same phrase as Amos 9:11 ("as in the days of old"). Many Christian interpreters, including JFB, read it as eternity, "the eternal generation of the Son." The Tyndale notes take a middle line that points the same way: a ruler whose existence reaches back into the distant past suggests a figure who is more than human. Isaiah, his contemporary, calls the coming child "Mighty God, Everlasting Father" (Isa 9:6). The New Testament makes the claim plainly: "In the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1), and "before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58).
- **"Therefore he shall give them up until the time when she who is in labor has given birth" (v3).** "He" is God. Because the true ruler is coming, the present suffering has a time limit. God hands his people over to their enemies, but only "until." **"She who is in labor"** picks up Zion's labour pains in 4:9–10. It may mean Zion herself bringing forth her deliverance. Many readers, including JFB, see an echo of Isaiah's sign to King Ahaz a few years earlier: "the virgin shall conceive and bear a son" (Isa 7:14). Both prophets, in the same city at the same time, speak of a woman whose child brings rescue.
- **"Then the rest of his brothers shall return to the people of Israel" (v3).** The scattered will come home and the divided kingdom will be one again (Ezek 37:15–28). "His brothers" makes the ruler one of them, a kinsman.
- **"And he shall stand and shepherd his flock in the strength of the LORD" (v4).** "Stand" means he will be firm and steady, unlike the struck judge of v1. "Shepherd" was the oldest picture of a king in the ancient Near East, and the word God used when he chose David: "You shall be shepherd of my people Israel" (2 Sam 5:2). Ezekiel promises "one shepherd, my servant David" (Ezek 34:23). This shepherd does not lean on chariots or Assyria, as Ahaz did. He rules **"in the majesty of the name of the LORD his God."**
- **"They shall dwell secure, for now he shall be great to the ends of the earth" (v4).** His rule is not limited to Judah. The nations who streamed to Zion in 4:1–2 are now under this King. The angel told Mary the same: "He will be great… and of his kingdom there will be no end" (Luke 1:32–33).
- **"And he shall be their peace" (v5).** Literally "And this one shall be peace." He does not only bring peace or make peace. He is it. Paul writes almost the same words: "For he himself is our peace" (Eph 2:14). Henry says, "None but he whose goings forth were from of old, from everlasting, was fit to be ruler in Israel."
- **"When the Assyrian comes into our land and treads in our palaces" (v5).** Assyria was the threat in Micah's day. In 701 BC Sennacherib swept through Judah, and his own annals boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." Assyria here stands for every enemy of God's people, as Babylon does in Revelation.
- **"Then we will raise against him seven shepherds and eight princes of men" (v5).** "Seven… eight" is a Hebrew idiom of the form "x, and x plus one," meaning enough and more than enough (Amos 1:3; Prov 30:18; Eccl 11:2). Seven was the number of completeness, so "eight" means even more than full. Under the great Shepherd, God will raise up as many under-shepherds as are needed. JFB thinks of the apostles. Henry: "When God has work to do he will not want fitting instruments to do it with."
- **"They shall shepherd the land of Assyria with the sword, and the land of Nimrod at its entrances" (v6).** "Shepherd… with the sword" is a grim pun: they will "shepherd" Assyria the way a sword shepherds. Nimrod was the mighty hunter who founded the first cities of Mesopotamia, including Babel and Nineveh (Gen 10:8–11). "The land of Nimrod" covers both Assyria and Babylon. The invader will be invaded.
- **"He shall deliver us from the Assyrian" (v6).** "He" is the Ruler from Bethlehem. Hezekiah was not this king. The Tyndale notes point out that the expected deliverer did not appear in 701 BC, nor in Zerubbabel after the exile. Only one later son of David fits.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Beth-lehem signifies the house of bread, the fittest place for him to be born in who is the bread of life," and "Those that threaten ruin to the church of God hasten ruin to themselves." JFB sees the slapped judge of v1 as foreshadowing Christ's own treatment (Isa 50:6), reads "goings forth… from everlasting" as the strongest possible statement of infinite duration, and takes "seven shepherds" as "a full and sufficient number." Guzik also connects "house of bread" with Jesus as the bread of life (John 6:35), and with Spurgeon he plays on Ephrathah's meaning, "fruitful." He argues from v2, with Adam Clarke, that one whose origins are from eternity must be God, and he sees appearances of the Angel of the LORD in the Old Testament as Christ before his birth.

#### A Remnant Shall Be Delivered (vv7–15)
- **"The remnant of Jacob shall be in the midst of many peoples like dew from the LORD" (v7).** The scattered remnant lives among the nations, and its presence is a blessing. In a dry land with long rainless summers, dew was life for crops (Gen 27:28; Deut 33:13). It comes silently, overnight, from God. Hosea uses the same picture: "I will be like the dew to Israel" (Hos 14:5).
- **"Which delay not for a man nor wait for the children of man" (v7).** Dew and rain do not wait for permission or human effort. The blessing comes on God's schedule, by God's power. JFB: this is "entirely God's work." Henry says the remnant will be like "crystal drops, as the water of life."
- **"Like a lion among the beasts of the forest… and there is none to deliver" (v8).** The same remnant, among the same "many peoples," is also a lion. Two pictures stand side by side: dew that refreshes and a lion that tears. The people of God bring life to some and judgment to others. Paul says the same of the gospel: "to one a fragrance from death to death, to the other a fragrance from life to life" (2 Cor 2:16). JFB says the lion image refers to their power to strike terror into opponents, "not in respect to its cruelty." Henry reads it as strength "in the strength of God, to resist and overcome their spiritual enemies."
- **"Your hand shall be lifted up over your adversaries, and all your enemies shall be cut off" (v9).** "Cut off" (*karath*) starts a drumbeat. It comes back four more times in vv10–13.
- **"In that day… I will cut off your horses from among you and will destroy your chariots" (v10).** Now the surprise: God turns from cutting off Israel's enemies to cutting off Israel's own false supports. Horses and chariots were the tanks of the ancient world. The law forbade Israel's king to "acquire many horses" (Deut 17:16), and the prophets kept warning against trusting them (Isa 31:1; Ps 20:7). Lachish was probably the city that taught Judah this trust (1:13). God will take away what his people leaned on, so that they lean on him.
- **"And I will cut off the cities of your land and throw down all your strongholds" (v11).** Fortified cities were Judah's other security. In the peace of God's kingdom, walls are no longer needed (Zech 2:4–5).
- **"I will cut off sorceries from your hand, and you shall have no more tellers of fortunes" (v12).** The occult practices the law strictly forbade (Deut 18:10–12) were common under Ahaz and would flourish again under Manasseh (2 Chr 33:6). They were another way of trying to control the future without God.
- **"I will cut off your carved images and your pillars… and you shall bow down no more to the work of your hands" (v13).** "Pillars" (*matsevot*) were standing stones set up at shrines, often linked with male fertility gods. Isaiah mocked people who bow to what their own fingers have made (Isa 2:8). Hezekiah's reform smashed many of these (2 Kgs 18:4), but Micah looks to a cleansing that goes deeper than any one king's reform.
- **"I will root out your Asherah images from among you and destroy your cities" (v14).** Asherah was a Canaanite mother goddess, worshipped through wooden poles or trees set up beside altars. The Tyndale notes explain that the stone pillars and wooden poles together likely carried sexual symbolism. God will pull them up by the roots.
- **"In anger and wrath I will execute vengeance on the nations that did not obey" (v15).** The chapter ends with the nations again. Those who would not listen (*shama'*, "hear," Micah's opening word) face God's justice. God's people are purified, and his enemies judged. The way to escape the wrath is the way the nations took in 4:2: come and learn his ways.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God will take away the horses "lest they should be tempted to trust in them." JFB writes that God's people must be stripped of all "creature trusts" to rest on the LORD alone. He links "Israel's foes are Jehovah's foes" to v9, and sees in the Maccabean victories (160s BC) a partial foretaste of v8. Guzik points out that God removes not only evil things like idols and sorcery but also good things like horses and cities, when they have taken his place. He reads vv7–15 as finally fulfilled in the millennial kingdom.

#### Pointing to Christ
Few Old Testament verses point to Jesus as directly as Micah 5:2. When the wise men came looking for a newborn king, the scribes opened Micah and sent them to Bethlehem (Matt 2:1–6). There, in the house of bread, was born the one who calls himself the bread of life. His coming forth was from of old, for he is the eternal Son (John 1:1–2, 14). Like the judge in v1, he was struck on the cheek. Like the ruler of v4, he is the Good Shepherd who stands and feeds his flock (John 10:11). And he is what v5 says: not merely the bringer of peace but peace itself, "for he himself is our peace" (Eph 2:14). Through him the remnant of God's people becomes dew among the nations, carrying life to the ends of the earth.
