*Setting:* Jesus has spent some months in Judea after the Passover of chapter 2, and his disciples have been baptizing more people than John the Baptist (3:22–26). Chapter 3 showed a respected Jewish teacher coming to Jesus at night. Chapter 4 sets beside him a Samaritan woman with a broken past who meets Jesus at noon. John seems to arrange the two on purpose: a man and a woman, an insider and an outsider, a rabbi and a stranger. The scene is Jacob's well in the hill country of Samaria, between Judea in the south and Galilee in the north, among a people the Jews regarded as half-breeds and heretics. The chapter ends back in Cana, with Jesus' second sign.

#### Jesus and the Woman of Samaria (vv1–45)
- **"When Jesus learned that the Pharisees had heard" (v1).** Jesus' growing following has drawn the attention of the same Pharisees who questioned John (1:24). Rather than provoke an early clash, Jesus leaves. His "hour" has not yet come (2:4). The other Gospels add that John the Baptist was arrested around this time (Mark 1:14).
- **"Although Jesus himself did not baptize" (v2).** This corrects any misunderstanding of 3:22. Jesus' disciples did the baptizing. Perhaps this kept people from boasting that Jesus himself had baptized them (compare 1 Cor 1:13–17).
- **"He had to pass through Samaria" (v4).** Samaria lay directly between Judea and Galilee, and the route through it took about three days. Many strict Jews avoided it by crossing the Jordan and going up the east side. "Had to" (*dei*) is the word John uses for divine necessity (3:14, 30). Geography made the route natural, but the word suggests Jesus also had an appointment to keep.
- **Who were the Samaritans?** When Assyria conquered the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC, it deported many Israelites and settled foreign peoples in the land. They intermarried with the Israelites who remained and at first mixed the worship of the LORD with their own gods (2 Kings 17:24–41). After the exile, the returning Jews refused Samaritan help to rebuild the temple (Ezra 4:1–5), and the Samaritans built their own temple on Mount Gerizim. A Jewish ruler, John Hyrcanus, destroyed it around 128 BC. By Jesus' day the Samaritans accepted only the five books of Moses as Scripture and looked for a coming prophet like Moses. Each side despised the other.
- **"Sychar, near the field that Jacob had given to his son Joseph" (v5).** Sychar was probably a village near ancient Shechem, by modern Nablus. Jacob bought land at Shechem (Gen 33:18–19), gave a portion to Joseph (Gen 48:22), and Joseph's bones were buried there (Josh 24:32). The ground was full of Israel's history.
- **"Jacob's well was there" (v6).** This is one of the most securely identified sites in the Holy Land. The well still exists, under a Greek Orthodox church, and it is more than 100 feet deep. The Old Testament never mentions Jacob digging it, but local tradition linked it with him.
- **"Jesus, wearied as he was from his journey" (v6).** The Word who made all things (1:3) is tired and thirsty. John, who stresses Jesus' deity more than any Gospel, is just as clear about his real humanity (11:35; 19:28).
- **"About the sixth hour" (v6).** Counting from sunrise, this is about noon, the heat of the day. Women usually drew water in the cooler morning or evening, and in company. A woman coming alone at noon may have been avoiding the others. Some scholars think John counts hours from midnight (making it 6 a.m.), but most take it as noon.
- **"Give me a drink" (v7).** Jesus starts by asking for help. In doing so he crosses three lines at once: a Jewish man speaking to a woman in public, a Jew addressing a Samaritan, and a rabbi addressing a woman with her reputation.
- **"Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" (v9).** The Greek verb (*synchraomai*) can mean "associate with" or, more narrowly, "use vessels in common with." Some Jewish teachers regarded Samaritan women as permanently unclean, so drinking from her jar would make Jesus ritually unclean. Either way, the woman is astonished.
- **"If you knew the gift of God" (v10).** The roles reverse. The thirsty stranger is the one who has water to give. "Living water" in everyday speech meant running water, from a spring or stream, as opposed to still water in a cistern. In the Old Testament God himself is "the fountain of living waters" (Jer 2:13), and the prophets promised living waters flowing from Jerusalem in the last days (Zech 14:8; Ezek 47:1–12). Later John explains that Jesus meant the Spirit (7:37–39).
- **"Are you greater than our father Jacob?" (v12).** It's another misunderstanding, full of irony. The answer is yes. John's readers know it, and the woman will soon discover it. She calls Jacob "our father," claiming Samaritan descent from the patriarchs, which Jews disputed.
- **"A spring of water welling up to eternal life" (v14).** "Welling up" (*hallomai*) is literally "leaping" or "springing." It is used of the lame man who "leaping up" walked (Acts 3:8). Jesus' gift does not have to be hauled up from a deep well. It becomes a spring inside the person. Isaiah promised "you shall draw water with joy from the wells of salvation" (Isa 12:3).
- **"Go, call your husband" (v16).** This is the turn of the conversation. The woman wants the water for convenience (v15). Jesus gently brings her to the truth about herself, because the living water cannot be received while her life stays hidden.
- **"You have had five husbands, and the one you now have is not your husband" (v18).** Jesus knows "what was in man" (2:25), as he knew Nathanael (1:48). The text doesn't say why she had five husbands. Some may have died, and in that culture only men could normally initiate divorce, so she may have been sinned against as well as sinning. But her present arrangement is clearly outside marriage. Notice that Jesus credits her honesty twice ("You are right… What you have said is true"). He exposes her without crushing her.
- **"Sir, I perceive that you are a prophet" (v19).** For a Samaritan, "a prophet" may point toward the one prophet like Moses they expected (Deut 18:15–18), whom they called the *Taheb*, "the restorer."
- **"Our fathers worshiped on this mountain" (v20).** Mount Gerizim rose right above the well, about 2,900 feet high. It was the mountain of blessing in Deuteronomy 11:29 and 27:12. The Samaritan version of the Pentateuch reads "Gerizim" in Deuteronomy 27:4 where the Jewish text reads "Ebal," which supported their claim that God commanded an altar there. Changing the subject to this old argument may be a way of dodging Jesus' probing. It may also be a sincere question: if she wants to make things right with God, where is she supposed to go?
- **"Neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem" (v21).** Jesus answers both her dodge and her question. The coming "hour," his death and resurrection, will make the argument about holy places obsolete (2:19–21). Within forty years the Jerusalem temple would be gone as well.
- **"Salvation is from the Jews" (v22).** Jesus does not pretend the two sides are equal. The Samaritans had cut themselves off from the prophets and Psalms, so they worshiped "what you do not know." God's saving purpose came through Israel, and the Messiah is a Jew (Rom 9:4–5). But salvation is "from" the Jews, not limited to them, as the end of this scene shows.
- **"In spirit and truth" (vv23–24).** One preposition covers both words, so they belong together. "Spirit" means worship that comes from the heart and is enabled by the Holy Spirit, not tied to a building or a mountain. "Truth" means worship that fits who God really is, as revealed in Jesus, who is himself "the truth" (14:6). "God is spirit" (v24) does not mean God is a vague force. It means he is not physical or limited to one place, so no single place can contain him (1 Kings 8:27).
- **"The Father is seeking such people" (v23).** God is not just waiting for worshipers to find him. He is looking for them. At this very moment he is seeking one, a woman at a well.
- **"I who speak to you am he" (v26).** Literally, "I am, the one speaking to you." In context it means "I am the Messiah," but John's readers will hear the same phrase "I am" (*egō eimi*) again with greater weight (8:58; 18:5–6), echoing God's name in Exodus 3:14. This is Jesus' clearest early statement that he is the Messiah. He makes it not to a council of rabbis but to a Samaritan woman. Among Samaritans the title carried less political danger than among the Jews (6:15).
- **"They marveled that he was talking with a woman" (v27).** The disciples are shocked, but they have learned not to question him. Some rabbinic sayings discouraged men from talking with women in public, even their wives.
- **"The woman left her water jar" (v28).** She came for water and leaves without it. Like the fishermen who left their nets, she has found something greater. John may also mean that she plans to come back.
- **"Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did" (v29).** The woman who avoided the townspeople now goes looking for them. "Come and see" echoes Philip's invitation to Nathanael (1:46). Her question, "Can this be the Christ?", expects a cautious "perhaps." She doesn't argue. She invites them to see for themselves.
- **"I have food to eat that you do not know about" (v32).** Another misunderstanding: the disciples think someone brought him lunch. Jesus' deepest nourishment is doing his Father's will, which he has just been doing. He will say at the cross, "It is finished" (19:30), using the same root as "accomplish his work" here (v34).
- **"There are yet four months, then comes the harvest" (v35).** This may be a proverb about the gap between sowing and reaping, or it may mean it was literally four months before harvest, around December or January. Either way, Jesus points to a harvest that is ripe right now. "Lift up your eyes" may be literal: the Samaritans were coming across the fields toward them, perhaps in light-colored clothing.
- **"One sows and another reaps" (v37).** Normally this saying was a complaint about unfairness. Here it is joyful. "Others have labored" (v38) may mean the prophets, John the Baptist, Jesus himself and the woman. The disciples will reap what they did not plant. Philip later sees a great harvest in Samaria (Acts 8:4–25).
- **"Many Samaritans… believed… because of the woman's testimony" (v39).** An outcast becomes the first evangelist in John's Gospel to a whole town.
- **"He stayed there two days" (v40).** A Jewish rabbi accepts Samaritan hospitality. "Stay" (*menō*) is John's word for abiding (1:38–39; 15:4).
- **"This is indeed the Savior of the world" (v42).** The townspeople move from secondhand faith to their own. "Savior of the world" appears only here and in 1 John 4:14 in the New Testament. Roman emperors were hailed by this title in inscriptions. The Samaritans, outsiders to Israel, see that Jesus is not only Israel's Messiah but the world's.
- **"A prophet has no honor in his own hometown" (v44).** This verse is puzzling, because v45 says the Galileans welcomed him. Three main views: (1) his "hometown" means Nazareth, and he avoided it; (2) it means Judea, the land of the prophets, where he was born and where he had just met resistance, so he went to Galilee; (3) the Galileans' welcome was itself shallow honor, based on signs, which is not real honor. The third fits John's theme well (2:23–25), and the sharp word in v48 supports it.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the route through Samaria was "for a geographical reason, no doubt… but certainly not without a higher design." Henry sees in Jesus' weariness his real humanity: "Toil came in with sin, and therefore Christ, having made himself a curse for us, submitted to it." All three treat the woman's move to Gerizim as a dodge, though Guzik allows it may have been honest confusion. JFB says she "shifts the subject from a personal to a public question." On v14, JFB says the believer becomes "not a cistern… but a fountain." On the reproof, Henry notes, "Reproofs are ordinarily most profitable when they are least provoking." On v26, JFB says Jesus "scarce ever said anything like this to His own people, the Jews." Henry suggests the woman left her jar partly so that Jesus could drink, and on v38 says the prophets sowed and the apostles reaped: "The reapers share in the joy of harvest." JFB counts the earlier laborers as "the Old Testament laborers, the Baptist, and by implication Himself." On v44 JFB takes "his own country" as Nazareth, while Guzik reads the Galileans' welcome as a shallow familiarity that fell short of real honor.

#### Jesus Heals an Official's Son (vv46–54)
- **"Cana in Galilee, where he had made the water wine" (v46).** John deliberately links this sign to the first one. The two Cana signs frame a section that runs from 2:1 to 4:54.
- **"An official whose son was ill" (v46).** "Official" is *basilikos*, "royal," meaning someone in the service of the king, here Herod Antipas, who ruled Galilee and was popularly called "king." He may have been a Jew or a Gentile. His home was Capernaum, about sixteen miles from Cana, and a full day's walk downhill. This is probably a different event from the healing of the centurion's servant (Matt 8:5–13), though both happen from a distance. There the man is a Gentile soldier with a servant, here a royal official with a son, and the details of the conversation differ.
- **"Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe" (v48).** "You" is plural. Jesus is speaking to the Galilean crowd as much as to the official. He is not refusing to help. He is pointing out the kind of faith that only responds to the spectacular.
- **"Sir, come down before my child dies" (v49).** The father doesn't argue. He just repeats his desperate request. He thinks Jesus must be physically present to heal.
- **"Go; your son will live" (v50).** Jesus doesn't go. He sends the man home on the strength of a word. "The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and went on his way." That is a different kind of faith from v48: no sign yet, only Jesus' promise.
- **"Yesterday at the seventh hour the fever left him" (v52).** About 1 p.m. The man didn't rush home that evening, which suggests a settled trust. He met his servants the next day. The timing matches exactly.
- **"And he himself believed, and all his household" (v53).** His faith grows in stages: first a request, then trust in Jesus' word, then full faith confirmed. "Household" includes family and servants. Whole households coming to faith becomes a pattern in Acts (Acts 16:15, 31–34).
- **"The second sign" (v54).** John is numbering the signs in Galilee. After this he stops counting, but the signs continue, each one showing more of who Jesus is (20:30–31).
- **Commentators:** JFB describes the official as a "courtier, king's servant, or one connected with a royal household," and says Jesus' blunt reply in v48 was meant to deepen a faith that was already real. JFB stresses that the fever left "the very hour" Jesus spoke. Guzik notes that the man's rank meant nothing in the face of his son's illness, and that Jesus tested him by giving him nothing to hold but a word. He adds that signs can confirm God's messengers but cannot change hearts by themselves.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus asks a Samaritan woman for a drink and ends up giving her the living water that only God can give. He knows everything she has ever done and still offers her the gift. He tells her that worship will no longer be bound to Gerizim or Jerusalem because the true temple is standing in front of her. Through her, a town of outsiders comes to know him as "the Savior of the world." Then, back in Galilee, he heals a dying boy with a word spoken sixteen miles away. Jesus' word carries his power, and the faith that rests on that word, before any sign, is the faith John wants his readers to have.
