*Setting:* Jerusalem, a few years after David's sin with Bathsheba, perhaps around 985 BC. Nathan had told David, "the sword shall never depart from your house" and "I will raise up evil against you out of your own house" (12:10–11). This chapter is where that begins. David's sons were born to different wives (3:2–5). Amnon, the eldest, was the son of Ahinoam of Jezreel and the crown prince. Absalom, the third son, and his full sister Tamar were children of Maacah, daughter of Talmai, king of Geshur, a small Aramean kingdom east of the Sea of Galilee. In a royal household with many wives, each mother's children formed their own circle, and full brothers felt bound to protect their sisters. This is one of the darkest chapters in the Bible. It tells of a rape and a murder inside the king's family, and it tells them plainly. The narrator does not soften what happened to Tamar, and neither should we.

#### Amnon and Tamar (vv1–22)
- **"Absalom, David's son, had a beautiful sister… And… Amnon, David's son, loved her" (v1).** The verse opens and closes with "David's son." The chapter is about what happens among the children of David. "Loved" here is the ordinary word for love, but the story soon shows it was something else. Tamar's name means "palm tree," a picture of grace and fruitfulness. She shares it with another wronged woman in Judah's family line (Gen 38).
- **"Amnon was so tormented that he made himself ill" (v2).** Royal daughters were kept apart and guarded. "It seemed impossible to Amnon to do anything to her." He did not think of marriage, which the law forbade between half-brother and half-sister (Lev 18:9, 11; 20:17). He thought only of getting her.
- **"Jonadab… was a very crafty man" (v3).** Literally "very wise." He is David's nephew, son of David's brother Shimeah (Shammah in 1 Sam 16:9). The Tyndale notes compare his "wisdom" to the serpent's craftiness in Eden (Gen 3:1). In a royal court a "friend" was often an adviser. This one advises evil.
- **"I love Tamar, my brother Absalom's sister" (v4).** Amnon does not say "my sister." He calls her Absalom's sister, as if to put distance between himself and what he wants.
- **"Lie down on your bed and pretend to be ill" (v5).** Jonadab lays out the plan, and David becomes the unwitting messenger. The "cakes" Amnon asks for (v6) are *lebibot*, a word built on *lēb*, "heart." They may have been heart-shaped cakes or a dish thought good for the sick. There may be a grim play on words: he wants her to make "heart-cakes" while his heart is set on harm.
- **"Then David sent home to Tamar" (v7).** David "sent" in chapter 11 to take Bathsheba. Now his sending delivers his daughter into danger. He does not see what is happening.
- **"She took dough and kneaded it and made cakes in his sight" (v8).** The writer slows down over her innocent, careful work. Then "he refused to eat" (v9), sends everyone out and calls her into the inner room.
- **"Come, lie with me, my sister" (v11).** Now he says "my sister." His words echo Potiphar's wife grabbing Joseph: "Lie with me" (Gen 39:12). Joseph could run. Tamar cannot.
- **"No, my brother, do not violate me, for such a thing is not done in Israel" (v12).** Tamar is the wisest voice in the chapter. "Violate" is the verb *ʿānâ*, to humble or force, the same verb used of Shechem and Dinah (Gen 34:2). "Outrageous thing" is *nebālâ*, the word for a deep moral outrage, especially sexual crimes (Gen 34:7; Deut 22:21; Judg 20:6). She appeals to God's law for Israel, to her own future ("where could I carry my shame?") and to his ("you would be as one of the outrageous fools," v13). The word for "fool," *nābāl*, is Nabal's name (1 Sam 25:25).
- **"Please speak to the king, for he will not withhold me from you" (v13).** Was marriage really possible? The law forbade it, though Abraham had married his half-sister long before the law was given (Gen 20:12). Most commentators think Tamar was saying anything she could to get away. Some think rules at the royal court were looser than the law. Either way, her words show she was looking for any way out.
- **"Being stronger than she, he violated her" (v14).** The writer states it plainly. She resisted, and he overpowered her. The blame lies entirely with Amnon.
- **"Then Amnon hated her with very great hatred" (v15).** The writer piles up the words: "the hatred with which he hated her was greater than the love with which he had loved her." What he called love was desire for himself. When he had taken what he wanted, she became a reminder of his guilt. **"Get up! Go!"** is two curt words in Hebrew.
- **"This wrong in sending me away is greater than the other" (v16).** Under the law, a man who violated an unmarried woman had to marry her and could never divorce her (Exod 22:16; Deut 22:28–29). That law was meant to protect a woman who would otherwise be left with no future in that culture. A half-sister could not lawfully marry him, but throwing her out made her disgrace public and left her with nothing at all.
- **"Put this woman out of my presence" (v17).** In Hebrew he says only "put *this* out," without even the word "woman." She has become a thing to him. **"Bolt the door after her"** tells the household she was the one at fault.
- **"A long robe with sleeves, for thus were the virgin daughters of the king dressed" (v18).** This is the same rare Hebrew phrase used for Joseph's robe (Gen 37:3), the only other place it appears. It marked royal status and, for Tamar, her unmarried state. Like Joseph's robe, it is torn.
- **"Tamar put ashes on her head and tore the long robe… and went away, crying aloud" (v19).** These were signs of mourning (Esth 4:1; Job 2:12). She does not hide what was done to her. She mourns it openly as a death.
- **"Hold your peace, my sister. He is your brother; do not take this to heart" (v20).** Absalom guesses at once what happened. His words sound like comfort but they silence her. Inside he is already planning revenge (v22). **"So Tamar lived, a desolate woman, in her brother Absalom's house."** "Desolate" (*shōmēmâ*) is used for a land laid waste. It is the last we hear of her. Absalom later names his own daughter Tamar (14:27).
- **"When King David heard of all these things, he was very angry" (v21).** And he does nothing. The Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek Old Testament add a line that the ESV gives in a footnote: "But he would not punish his son Amnon, because he loved him, since he was his firstborn." Whether or not the line is original, it fits the story. Perhaps David felt he had no moral standing after his own sin. Perhaps he could not bring himself to punish the heir. Either way, the king who was supposed to bring justice (8:15) failed his own daughter.
- **"Absalom spoke to Amnon neither good nor bad, for Absalom hated Amnon" (v22).** "Neither good nor bad" is a Hebrew way of saying "nothing at all." The silence is the most dangerous thing in the room.
- **Commentators:** All three see Amnon's "love" as lust. Matthew Henry: "Amnon hated Tamar because she would not consent to his wickedness… and so threw all the blame upon him." He also says plainly: "Had he hated the sin, and loathed himself for it, we might have hoped he was penitent." Guzik stresses that Tamar was right to mourn openly rather than accept the voice of shame, and that all the blame rests on Amnon. He also points out that a father's indulgence of his own desires, in David's many marriages and in Bathsheba, set a pattern his son followed. JFB explains that in polygamous households a sister was under the special care of her full brother, which is why Absalom takes it on himself. JFB thinks Amnon may not have seen the relationship as improper, which reads too much into his silence. The Tyndale notes are clearer: Amnon was not seeking marriage at all. Henry is the one commentator who quotes the extra line in v21, that David would not grieve Amnon "because he loved him."

#### Absalom Murders Amnon (vv23–33)
- **"After two full years Absalom had sheepshearers at Baal-hazor" (v23).** Sheep-shearing was a harvest festival with feasting and drinking (1 Sam 25:2–8, 36, where Nabal gets drunk at his). Baal-hazor is usually placed at Jebel el-Asur, the highest hill in the central hill country, about 15 miles north of Jerusalem. Absalom has had two years to plan.
- **"Please let the king and his servants go with your servant" (v24).** Absalom invites David first, expecting him to decline. When he does, Absalom asks for Amnon. David hesitates: "Why should he go with you?" (v26). He senses something but gives way under pressure (v27). Once again David sends a child to harm.
- **"Mark when Amnon's heart is merry with wine… then kill him" (v28).** David made Uriah drunk before arranging his death (11:13). Now Amnon is made merry with wine and killed. **"Do not fear; have I not commanded you? Be courageous and be valiant."** Absalom twists the language of holy war, the words spoken to Joshua (Josh 1:9), into orders for murder.
- **"Each mounted his mule and fled" (v29).** Mules were the mount of royalty in David's day (18:9; 1 Kings 1:33). The princes run for their lives, not knowing how far Absalom's plan goes.
- **"Absalom has struck down all the king's sons, and not one of them is left" (v30).** The first report is wildly wrong, and David believes it. He tears his garments and lies on the ground (v31), as Tamar tore her robe in v19.
- **"Amnon alone is dead. For by the command of Absalom this has been determined from the day he violated his sister Tamar" (v32).** Jonadab, who gave the advice that began all this, now calmly explains what Absalom has done. He seems to have known all along. He is the kind of man who survives at court by always knowing the most.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Absalom offered to repair a breach of the seventh commandment by breaking the sixth, "as if they were not all alike sacred." He and JFB both suspect ambition mixed with revenge: with Amnon dead, Absalom was next in line, if Chileab (3:3) had died. Guzik passes on Trapp's comparison: David committed adultery, made Uriah drunk and had him killed; Amnon committed incest, was made drunk and was killed. Henry notes David was "unwittingly made accessory to both" crimes, sending Tamar to Amnon and Amnon to Absalom. JFB places Baal-hazor about eight miles northeast of Jerusalem, closer than the Tyndale notes' 15 miles north. The exact site is uncertain.

#### Absalom Flees to Geshur (vv34–39)
- **"But Absalom fled" (v34).** The phrase is repeated three times in vv34–38. Premeditated murder had no refuge under the law (Num 35:20–21), not even the cities of refuge. Absalom goes to his grandfather Talmai, king of Geshur (v37), outside David's reach.
- **"The king's sons came and lifted up their voice and wept" (v36).** The watchman sees the princes coming, and Jonadab is proved right. The palace that had celebrated victories now weeps "very bitterly."
- **"David mourned for his son day after day" (v37).** Which son? The text does not say. It is most naturally Amnon, but the next verses suggest the grief slowly turns toward the son who is absent.
- **"Was there three years" (v38).** Two years waiting for revenge, three years in exile. Absalom is gone five years from the day Tamar was wronged.
- **"The spirit of the king longed to go out to Absalom, because he was comforted about Amnon" (v39).** The Hebrew is hard. The ESV, following the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek, reads "the spirit of the king longed." Others translate "the king ceased to go out against Absalom," meaning his anger cooled and he gave up on punishment. Both readings agree that time softened David. Chapter 14 shows that he still did not act.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees David repeating with Absalom the same indulgence he showed Amnon, wanting reconciliation without facing the wrong. JFB notes that the law left Absalom no sanctuary and that his grandfather "would, doubtless, approve of his conduct." Henry, like the ESV, reads v39 as David's affection returning once his grief for Amnon wore off.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter has no hero. A son takes, a father fails to act, a brother silences his sister and then kills, and a princess is left desolate. It shows what David's own sin set loose, and what power without righteousness does to the weak. Tamar's cry, "such a thing is not done in Israel," is the cry of every victim for a king who will judge rightly. The Bible promises that King: one who "shall not judge by what his eyes see… but with righteousness he shall judge the poor" (Isa 11:3–4), who "will not break" the bruised reed (Isa 42:3), and who himself was stripped, shamed and put outside the city (Heb 13:12). Jesus does not ask the wounded to "hold your peace." He sees, he names the wrong, and he will one day put every wrong right.
