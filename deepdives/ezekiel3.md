*Setting:* The summer of 593 BC, by the Chebar canal in Babylonia. Ezekiel, a priest taken captive with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC, has seen the glory of the LORD on its throne above the living creatures and the wheels (ch 1). In chapter 2 God named him "son of man," sent him to "a rebellious house," and held out a scroll written front and back with "lamentation and mourning and woe" (2:10). Chapter 3 finishes the call. Ezekiel eats the scroll, is given a forehead harder than flint, is carried back to the exiles, and sits stunned for a week. Then God gives him two more pieces of his job description: he is a watchman who must warn, and he is a prisoner whose tongue God himself will bind and loose. Jerusalem is still standing, and most of the exiles expect to go home soon. That expectation is what Ezekiel has to break.

#### Ezekiel's Call, continued (vv1–15)
- **"Eat whatever you find here. Eat this scroll" (v1).** The chapter break falls in the middle of the scene. God had already said "open your mouth and eat what I give you" (2:8), and now he names the food. The order matters: first eat, then "go, speak to the house of Israel." A prophet does not pass on a message he has not first taken in himself.
- **"Feed your belly… and fill your stomach with it" (v3).** This is more than a taste. "Belly" and "stomach" are the inner parts, the seat of feeling in Hebrew thought. The word has to become part of him. Jeremiah, his older contemporary in Jerusalem, said the same: "Your words were found, and I ate them, and your words became to me a joy" (Jer 15:16). Whether Ezekiel ate a physical scroll or this happened within the vision, the point is the same: total, inward reception.
- **"It was in my mouth as sweet as honey" (v3).** A scroll full of woe tastes sweet. The message is grim, but the word itself, received from God's own hand, is good (Ps 19:10; 119:103). John eats a little scroll in Revelation 10:9–10 that is sweet in his mouth but bitter in his stomach. The bitterness comes for Ezekiel too, a few verses later (v14). The Tyndale notes draw a contrast with Eden: Adam's eating in disobedience turned bitter, while Ezekiel's eating in obedience is sweet.
- **"Not sent to a people of foreign speech and a hard language" (vv5–6).** Literally "deep of lip and heavy of tongue," a vivid way of saying "unintelligible." Ezekiel lives in Babylon surrounded by people speaking Akkadian and Aramaic, so the picture is close at hand. **"Surely, if I sent you to such, they would listen to you."** That is the sting. The problem with Israel is not language but will. Jonah's preaching moved pagan Nineveh to repent (Jonah 3:5), and Jesus said Tyre and Sidon would have repented at the miracles Galilee shrugged off (Matt 11:21).
- **"They are not willing to listen to me" (v7).** Israel's rejection of the prophet is really a rejection of God. Jesus said the same to his disciples: "The one who rejects you rejects me" (Luke 10:16). **"A hard forehead and a stubborn heart"** repeats 2:4. The forehead is where shame shows. A hard forehead cannot blush (Jer 3:3).
- **"I have made your face as hard as their faces" (v8).** There is a play on Ezekiel's name here. *Yeḥezqe'l* means "God strengthens" or "God hardens," and the verb in "hard" is from the same root (*ḥazaq*). God answers their hardness with a man whose very name says God will make him harder. This is not stubbornness for its own sake but courage that will not bend under glares and mockery.
- **"Like emery harder than flint" (v9).** The Hebrew *shamir* is a very hard stone, probably emery or corundum (KJV "adamant"). Zechariah 7:12 uses it of hearts made "diamond-hard" against the law, and Jeremiah 17:1 of a point used for engraving. Flint was the hardest common stone the Israelites knew. Ezekiel's forehead is to be harder still. **"Fear them not, nor be dismayed at their looks"** repeats the charge of 2:6.
- **"Receive in your heart, and hear with your ears" (v10).** The order is surprising: heart before ears. Ezekiel is to be inwardly ready to take in every word before he even hears it. Verse 10 restates the eating of the scroll in plain terms.
- **"Go to the exiles, to your people" (v11).** The audience narrows from "the house of Israel" to the community of exiles in Babylon. "Your people" ties him to them. He is not a foreigner preaching at them but one of the captives. **"Whether they hear or refuse to hear"** is the third time this refrain has appeared (2:5, 7). His job is faithfulness, not results.
- **"Blessed be the glory of the LORD from its place!" (v12).** The vision of chapter 1 now departs, and Ezekiel hears behind him the roar of wings and wheels, "the voice of a great earthquake." The words as they stand are an odd cry of praise, and the ESV footnote gives an alternative: by changing one similar-looking Hebrew letter, *barukh* ("blessed") becomes *berum* ("when it rose"), giving "as the glory of the LORD arose from its place." Many scholars prefer that reading because it fits the scene, since the glory is leaving. The Hebrew text as handed down reads "blessed," and the old translations follow it. Either way the meaning is clear: the glory lifts and goes.
- **"The Spirit lifted me up and took me away" (v14).** This is the first of several times the Spirit carries Ezekiel (8:3; 11:1, 24; 37:1; 43:5). The text does not tell us whether this was bodily or in vision, and it does not need to.
- **"I went in bitterness in the heat of my spirit" (v14).** "Bitterness" is *mar*, the same word Naomi used of herself ("call me Mara," Ruth 1:20). "Heat" is the word for burning anger. The honey of v3 has turned bitter in his stomach. He has absorbed God's own anger at sin, and he also faces years of thankless work among people he loves. **"The hand of the LORD being strong upon me"** is again the root *ḥazaq*: God holds him to the task even while his feelings churn. The best prophets were never cold.
- **"The exiles at Tel-abib" (v15).** The name is probably Babylonian *til abubi*, "mound of the flood," meaning an old ruin mound left by floodwaters, which captives were often settled on to rebuild. In Hebrew the sound suggests "hill of spring grain" (*'abib* is the month of new barley ears). Modern Tel Aviv took its name from this verse. The site in Babylonia has not been found, though the Chebar canal ran near Nippur.
- **"I sat there overwhelmed among them seven days" (v15).** "Overwhelmed" (*mashmim*) means stunned, desolate, appalled. It is the same root used later of Jerusalem's coming desolation (6:4, 6). Ezekiel takes on beforehand the shock his people will feel. Seven days was the length of mourning for the dead (Gen 50:10) and the time Job's friends sat silent beside him (Job 2:13). It was also the length of a priest's ordination (Lev 8:33). Ezekiel was born to be a priest. His real ordination was this silent week among the exiles.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Henry both read the eating as the preacher's need to feed on Scripture himself before feeding others, and Guzik warns that handling the Bible for teaching is not the same as taking it in. Henry on v1: ministers must "eat that thou findest," taking the whole message, pleasant or not. Guzik notes that foreigners in Israel's history often honored prophets more than Israel did (Naaman, 2 Kings 5), and that the hardened forehead is a picture of courage. On v14 Guzik weighs whether the bitterness was anger, grief or reluctance and leans toward anger. JFB calls it "sadness on account of the impending calamities." JFB prefers to read v15 as "I beheld them sitting there," but the Hebrew more naturally reads that Ezekiel himself sat, as the ESV has it.

#### A Watchman for Israel (vv16–27)
- **"At the end of seven days, the word of the LORD came to me" (v16).** The silence breaks with a new part of his commission. This watchman charge is repeated almost word for word in 33:1–9, when news of Jerusalem's fall arrives. The two passages bracket the book's long section of judgment.
- **"I have made you a watchman for the house of Israel" (v17).** A watchman (*tsopheh*) stood on the city wall or a hilltop and blew the trumpet when he saw an army coming (2 Sam 18:24–27; 2 Kings 9:17). Earlier prophets used the image (Isa 21:6; Jer 6:17; Hos 9:8; Hab 2:1). The twist here is that the coming enemy is God himself. Ezekiel's warnings come "from me," from the same LORD who sends the judgment. God warns because he does not want to destroy (18:23, 32).
- **"If I say to the wicked, 'You shall surely die'" (v18).** This is the language of covenant curse and of the death sentence in the law (Gen 2:17; Lev 20). Here it most likely refers to dying under the coming judgment, by sword, famine and plague, rather than a full teaching on eternal destiny. The New Testament carries the principle further, but Ezekiel's first hearers would have heard the threat of the siege.
- **"His blood I will require at your hand" (v18).** The idiom comes from the law of murder (Gen 9:5). A watchman who kept silent would be treated as though he had killed the people he failed to warn. The wicked man still dies "for his iniquity," his own guilt, but the silent prophet shares the guilt of his death.
- **"But you will have delivered your soul" (v19).** If he warns and they refuse, he is clear. Paul echoes this exactly at Ephesus: "I am innocent of the blood of all, for I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God" (Acts 20:26–27). God measures the messenger by faithfulness, not by how many listen.
- **"If a righteous person turns from his righteousness" (v20).** Ezekiel must warn insiders as well as outsiders. Past goodness is no shelter for present rebellion: "his righteous deeds that he has done shall not be remembered." Chapter 18 develops this at length.
- **"And I lay a stumbling block before him" (v20).** The word *mikshol* is something that trips the foot. Ezekiel often uses it of idols, "the stumbling block of their iniquity" (7:19; 14:3). God does not tempt anyone to sin (James 1:13). The sense is that when a person turns from God, God brings him into the test or the judgment that exposes what is already in his heart, and he falls. Interpreters differ on whether the stumbling block here is the occasion of sin or the punishment that follows it.
- **Can a righteous person be lost?** Christians read v20 differently. Many in the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions see a real warning that a believer can fall away. Many in the Reformed tradition see a person whose righteousness was outward and not from a changed heart, and they note that Ezekiel's concern is the covenant community's conduct, not a theology of conversion. Both agree on the plain point: no one can lean on yesterday's faithfulness while walking away today.
- **"He shall surely live, because he took warning" (v21).** The section ends on hope. Warning is meant to save. The watchman's best day is when the alarm is heeded and nothing bad happens.
- **"Go out into the valley" (v22).** The word (*biq'ah*) means a broad plain, open flat land. The same word describes the valley of dry bones (37:1). Away from people, God meets him again.
- **"The glory of the LORD stood there… and I fell on my face" (v23).** This is Ezekiel's second sight of the glory, and it floors him as the first one did (1:28). Familiarity does not make God ordinary. **"The Spirit entered into me and set me on my feet" (v24)** repeats 2:2 word for word. God again supplies the strength to stand.
- **"Go, shut yourself within your house" (v24).** A strange first assignment for a man just commissioned to speak. From now on, people will have to come to him (8:1; 14:1; 20:1). Several of Ezekiel's sign-acts in the following chapters take place at or near his house.
- **"Cords will be placed upon you" (v25).** The Hebrew does not say who puts them there. Some think his fellow exiles would literally restrain him in hostility. Others take the cords as God's own restraint, paired with God binding his tongue in the next verse. In 4:8 it is God who says, "I will place cords upon you." Most interpreters read this as a picture of his limited movement, not a description of ropes on his wrists.
- **"I will make your tongue cling to the roof of your mouth, so that you shall be mute" (v26).** Ezekiel will not be completely silent, because he speaks oracles for the next seven years. The point is **"unable to reprove them."** The word *mokiaḥ* means one who reproves or argues a case, an arbiter or go-between (Job 9:33 uses it of the mediator Job longed for). Ezekiel is barred from ordinary conversation and from pleading for the people. He speaks only when God gives him words. This restriction lasts until the night before news of Jerusalem's fall reaches him: "my mouth was opened, and I was no longer mute" (33:22; see 24:27).
- **"But when I speak with you, I will open your mouth" (v27).** His silence is itself a message. A people who will not listen lose the gift of a prophet who talks with them. What is left is the bare word of God.
- **"He who will hear, let him hear; and he who will refuse to hear, let him refuse" (v27).** The call closes with the refrain once more, now turned toward the hearers. The responsibility moves from the prophet to the people. Jesus' "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (Matt 11:15; 13:9) sounds the same note, and it echoes again to the churches in Revelation 2–3.
- **Commentators:** Henry makes this a charge to ministers, who must "give them warning from me" and who "shall have their reward, though they be not successful." Guzik stresses that a watchman's first work is proclaiming God's message, not hunting for other people's faults, and he warns that a watchman who sounds false alarms will not be believed when the real enemy comes. He reads "die" as physical death under covenant curse. JFB says the repetition shows "the warning is to be inculcated continually," and reads the "righteous" man of v20 as righteous in outward conduct, not truly regenerate. On the stumbling block JFB holds that "God gives men over to judicial blindness," and mentions that Calvin took it as the ruin itself. JFB also claims Ezekiel alone among the prophets is called a watchman, but Hosea 9:8 and Habakkuk 2:1 use the same image. JFB calls the cords "not literally, but spiritually," the restraint the people's rebellion put on his preaching. JFB adds that God silencing his prophets is "God's sorest judgment." Henry finds comfort in the promise of v27: God's prophets may be silenced for a while, but he will open their mouths again in his time. Guzik lays out the main readings: opponents restraining him, a sign of Israel's helplessness, God's own restriction, or a picture of God's silence toward a deaf people. He treats the whole of 2:1–3:27 as one long commissioning. The Tyndale notes take the muteness as a bar on intercession: the time for pleading had passed until judgment fell.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel ate the word until it filled him, felt in his own spirit both its sweetness and God's burning grief over sin, and sat stunned among the people he was sent to warn. Jesus is the Word himself, not a man who swallowed a scroll (John 1:14), and he wept over the city that would not hear (Luke 19:41–44). He was the faithful watchman who warned of judgment to come. Then he did what no watchman could: he stood under that judgment himself. Like Ezekiel he was bound with cords and "opened not his mouth" before his accusers (Isa 53:7; John 18:12). But his silence was not a sign that the time for mercy had passed. It was the mercy, since he went quietly to die for the rebellious house. And because he did, the refrain "he who will hear, let him hear" is now an open invitation.
