*Setting:* Galilee, probably spring of AD 29, about a year before the crucifixion. John's Gospel places the feeding of the five thousand near Passover (John 6:4), and Mark mentions "green grass" (Mark 6:39), which fits spring. Chapter 13 ended with Jesus rejected in his hometown of Nazareth. Chapter 14 opens with a darker rejection: the ruler of Galilee has executed John the Baptist. From here to chapter 17, Jesus often withdraws from crowds and hostile rulers and spends more time with his disciples. Yet these withdrawals become the setting for some of his greatest works: feeding thousands in the wilderness and walking on the sea. The chapter ends with the disciples worshiping him as the Son of God.

#### The Death of John the Baptist (vv1–12)
- **"Herod the tetrarch" (v1).** This is Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, the king who tried to kill the infant Jesus (ch 2). When his father died in 4 BC, Rome split the kingdom, and Antipas got Galilee and Perea. "Tetrarch" originally meant ruler of a fourth part, and came to mean a minor ruler below a king. He ruled until AD 39. Jesus called him "that fox" (Luke 13:32).
- **"This is John the Baptist. He has been raised from the dead" (v2).** John did no miracles (John 10:41), so Herod's idea makes little sense except as guilt. A conscience that has killed a prophet sees him everywhere. Others had their own guesses about who Jesus was (16:14).
- **"For the sake of Herodias, his brother Philip's wife" (v3).** The story behind this is tangled. Herodias was Herod the Great's granddaughter, so she was also Antipas's niece. She had married another of Herod the Great's sons, a half-brother of Antipas, whom the Gospels call Philip (not the Philip who ruled the northeast). On a visit to Rome, Antipas fell for her. He divorced his wife, a daughter of King Aretas IV of Nabatea, and Herodias left her husband to marry him.
- **"It is not lawful for you to have her" (v4).** The law forbade marrying a brother's wife while the brother was alive (Lev 18:16; 20:21). "Had been saying" suggests John said it more than once, and publicly. He told a ruler what he did not want to hear, and he lost his freedom for it.
- **Josephus's account.** The Jewish historian Josephus says that Antipas imprisoned John at Machaerus, a hilltop fortress east of the Dead Sea, and executed him because his popularity might lead to a revolt. The two accounts fit together. Matthew gives the personal reason, Josephus the political one. Josephus adds that when Aretas later defeated Antipas in battle (AD 36), many Jews saw it as God's punishment for killing John. In AD 39 the emperor Caligula exiled Antipas and Herodias to Gaul.
- **"He feared the people" (v5).** Herod wanted John dead but feared the crowds. Mark adds a more complicated picture: Herod feared John, knowing he was righteous, and liked to listen to him though he was "greatly perplexed" (Mark 6:20). It was Herodias who wanted him dead.
- **"The daughter of Herodias danced before the company" (v6).** Josephus names her Salome. She was probably in her teens, the Greek word suggesting a young woman of marriageable age. Dancing before a banquet of drinking men was not something a royal daughter would normally do, which shows how low this court had sunk.
- **"He promised with an oath to give her whatever she might ask" (v7).** Mark adds "up to half of my kingdom" (Mark 6:23), the words of King Ahasuerus to Esther (Esth 5:3). The contrast is sharp: Esther used her moment to save her people, and this girl uses hers to kill a prophet.
- **"Prompted by her mother… the head of John the Baptist here on a platter" (v8).** "Here" means now, before Herod could sober up or change his mind. A platter was a serving dish from the banquet table.
- **"The king was sorry, but because of his oaths and his guests" (v9).** Matthew calls him "king," which was what people called him though Rome never gave him the title. His sorrow did not change his course. He feared looking weak in front of his guests more than he feared shedding innocent blood. An oath to do evil should be broken, not kept. Jesus had already warned against rash oaths (5:33–37).
- **"His disciples came and took the body and buried it, and they went and told Jesus" (v12).** John's followers do for him what Joseph of Arimathea later does for Jesus (27:57–60). Then they bring the news to Jesus. The forerunner's death points ahead to the death of the one he prepared the way for (17:12).
- **Commentators:** Henry has a series of sharp lines: "A guilty conscience suggests every thing that is frightful"; Herod was one of those men who "fear being hanged for that which they do not fear being damned for"; and "Many a man sins with regret, that never has any true regret for his sin." JFB says the murdered prophet "haunted his guilty breast like a specter," and refers to Mark for the rest of the story. Guzik notes that Herod feared people more than God, and that Herodias's quick request left no time for him to think again.

#### Jesus Feeds the Five Thousand (vv13–21)
- **"He withdrew from there in a boat to a desolate place by himself" (v13).** Matthew connects the withdrawal to John's death. Jesus may have been grieving, and Herod's interest was also a danger. Mark and Luke add that the Twelve had just come back from their mission and needed rest (Mark 6:30–31), and Luke places the event near Bethsaida (Luke 9:10), on the northeast shore of the lake.
- **"He had compassion on them and healed their sick" (v14).** His plans for rest were interrupted, and he responded with compassion, not irritation (9:36).
- **"Send the crowds away to go into the villages and buy food" (v15).** The disciples' idea was sensible. It was late and they were in a remote place.
- **"They need not go away; you give them something to eat" (v16).** "You" is emphatic in Greek. Jesus puts the problem in their hands, knowing they cannot solve it. John says Jesus asked Philip where to buy bread, "to test him, for he himself knew what he would do" (John 6:5–6).
- **"We have only five loaves here and two fish" (v17).** John adds that they were a boy's barley loaves, the cheap bread of the poor, and small fish, probably dried or pickled (John 6:9). "Only" is the disciples' word. Jesus' answer is "Bring them here to me" (v18).
- **"He ordered the crowds to sit down on the grass" (v19).** Mark says they sat in groups of hundreds and fifties (Mark 6:40), which recalls how Moses organized Israel in the wilderness (Exod 18:21). Israel was fed by God in the wilderness, and now a new Israel is fed in a wilderness place.
- **"He looked up to heaven and said a blessing. Then he broke the loaves and gave them" (v19).** The Jewish head of a household blessed God before a meal: "Blessed are you, O Lord our God, King of the world, who brings forth bread from the earth." Notice the four verbs: took, blessed, broke, gave. Matthew uses the same four at the Last Supper (26:26). Early Christians could not miss the link.
- **"And the disciples gave them to the crowds" (v19).** Jesus could have fed them directly. He chose to work through his disciples. The food multiplied as it was given out.
- **"They all ate and were satisfied" (v20).** Not a token bite, but a full meal. Psalm 23's shepherd who prepares a table and makes his sheep lie down in green pastures is not far away.
- **"Twelve baskets full of the broken pieces left over" (v20).** These were wicker baskets of the kind Jews carried for food when traveling. The number twelve may hint at the twelve tribes and the twelve apostles. The leftovers recall Elisha, who fed a hundred men with twenty loaves "and they ate and had some left" (2 Kings 4:42–44). Jesus does it on a far greater scale.
- **"About five thousand men, besides women and children" (v21).** Only Matthew adds "besides women and children." Counting men was the custom. With women and children, the crowd may have been well over ten thousand.
- **All four Gospels.** Apart from the resurrection, this is the only miracle recorded in all four Gospels. John follows it with Jesus' teaching "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35) and notes that the crowd wanted to make him king by force (John 6:15).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the food "was multiplied, not in the heap at first, but in the distribution of it," and says "Those whom Christ feeds, he fills." JFB notes that this is one of the very few places where all four Gospels run parallel, and refers readers to Mark. Guzik sees the miracle as a messianic sign echoing the manna, and draws the lessons of using what is at hand, trusting God's unlimited supply and wasting nothing.

#### Jesus Walks on the Water (vv22–33)
- **"Immediately he made the disciples get into the boat" (v22).** "Made" is strong, almost "compelled." John explains why: the crowd was about to seize him and make him king (John 6:15). Jesus got the disciples out of a heady, dangerous situation before they got swept up in it.
- **"He went up on the mountain by himself to pray" (v23).** At a crucial moment, with the crowds wanting a political king, Jesus spends the night with his Father. Matthew shows him praying alone here and in Gethsemane (26:36–44).
- **"Beaten by the waves, for the wind was against them" (v24).** Unlike the storm in 8:24, this is not said to be life-threatening. It is exhausting. They had been rowing for hours and getting nowhere. John says they had gone only three or four miles (John 6:19).
- **"In the fourth watch of the night he came to them, walking on the sea" (v25).** The Romans divided the night into four watches, and the fourth ran from about 3 to 6 a.m. They had been rowing most of the night. The Old Testament says God alone "trampled the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8) and that his path was "through the sea… yet your footprints were unseen" (Ps 77:19). Jesus does what only God does.
- **"It is a ghost!" (v26).** The word is *phantasma*, an apparition. Sailors were superstitious, and a figure on the water in the dark before dawn was terrifying.
- **"Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid" (v27).** "It is I" is literally "I am" (*egō eimi*). It can simply mean "It's me." But in this setting, with Jesus walking on the sea like God, it also echoes God's name revealed to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exod 3:14), and God's words in Isaiah: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you… I am he" (Isa 43:2, 10). Mark has the same words. John makes the theme explicit in his Gospel.
- **"Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the water" (v28).** Only Matthew tells this part. Peter's "if" is not doubt so much as a request for a word to act on. He will not step out without Jesus' command.
- **"So Peter got out of the boat and walked on the water" (v29).** For a few steps, a fisherman did what only God does, because he was trusting Jesus' word.
- **"But when he saw the wind, he was afraid, and beginning to sink" (v30).** Nothing had changed about the wind. What changed was where Peter was looking. His cry, "Lord, save me," is one of the shortest prayers in the Bible and one of the best. It is also what "Jesus" means: "the LORD saves" (1:21).
- **"Jesus immediately reached out his hand and took hold of him" (v31).** Peter did not have to save himself or swim back to the boat. Jesus caught him.
- **"O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" (v31).** "Little faith" is real faith, but weak (6:30; 8:26). "Doubt" (*distazō*) comes from a root meaning "two" and means to waver or hesitate. It appears only here and at the resurrection, where some disciples "worshiped him, but some doubted" (28:17). Peter's faith was split between Jesus and the wind.
- **"Those in the boat worshiped him, saying, 'Truly you are the Son of God'" (v33).** In chapter 8 the disciples asked, "What sort of man is this?" (8:27). Now they answer. This is the first time in Matthew that the disciples together call Jesus "the Son of God," preparing for Peter's confession in 16:16. Mark, by contrast, says the disciples were astounded "for they did not understand about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (Mark 6:52). Both are true: they worshiped and still had a long way to go.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws several lessons: "Those are not Christ's followers that do not care for being alone"; Peter's request shows that "True love will break through fire and water"; and "The strongest faith and the greatest courage have a mixture of fear." Guzik pictures Peter walking while he looked at Jesus and sinking when he looked at the wind. Following R.T. France, he describes doubt as being divided rather than focused, which matches the lexicon's "hesitate." He calls the worship in the boat the first open recognition of Jesus as Son of God. JFB refers readers to John and Mark for this section.

#### Jesus Heals the Sick in Gennesaret (vv34–36)
- **"They came to land at Gennesaret" (v34).** This was a small, very fertile plain on the northwest shore of the lake, between Capernaum and Magdala. Josephus praised its fruit and called it the ambition of nature. They had set out for the other side and landed back in Herod's territory, perhaps blown off course by the wind.
- **"They sent around to all that region and brought to him all who were sick" (v35).** People who knew him brought others. Faith spreads through those who have met Jesus telling their neighbors.
- **"That they might only touch the fringe of his garment" (v36).** The "fringe" was the tassels Jewish men wore on their cloaks (Num 15:38–39). Word of the woman healed by touching them (9:20–22) had evidently spread. "As many as touched it were made well." "Made well" is the word for being brought safely through, related to "save."
- **Commentators:** Henry's lesson: "We must not eat these spiritual morsels alone," since those who find Christ should bring others to him, as the people of Gennesaret did. Guzik notes the contrast with strict groups who avoided crowds for fear of uncleanness, and compares the fringe to Paul's handkerchiefs and Peter's shadow in Acts (Acts 5:15; 19:12), points of contact for faith.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two kings and two banquets stand side by side in this chapter. Herod's feast ends with a prophet's head on a platter, given out of pride and fear. Jesus' feast in the wilderness ends with thousands fed and twelve baskets left over, given out of compassion. Herod is haunted by a dead man he thinks has risen. Jesus will die and truly rise. The one who fed Israel in the wilderness, who walks on the sea and says "I am," reaches out his hand to a sinking disciple. The bread he broke on the hillside pointed to his body broken at the cross, given so that "whoever feeds on me… will live because of me" (John 6:57).
