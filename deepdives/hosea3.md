*Setting:* Hosea is preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel in the mid-700s BC. Chapter 1 told of his marriage to Gomer and their three children, whose names announced judgment. Chapter 2 turned to God's own voice: a lawsuit against unfaithful Israel that ended, surprisingly, with God courting his wife again and betrothing her forever. Chapter 3 returns to Hosea's home and is told in his own words ("the LORD said to me"). Gomer has left and is living with another man, perhaps fallen into debt or slavery. God tells Hosea to go and love her again. In five short verses, the prophet acts out the whole message of the book: love that pursues, pays a price, waits, and restores. It is one of the shortest chapters in the prophets, and one of the most moving.

#### Hosea Redeems His Wife (vv1–5)
- **"Go again, love a woman" (v1).** "Again" ties this to the first command in 1:2. The command is not "marry" but "love," and it is in the imperative: love is something Hosea is told to do, an act of will before it is a feeling. JFB thinks the woman is called just "a woman," not "your wife," because she is now living apart from him. Most interpreters agree she is Gomer; it would make little sense as a picture of God restoring Israel if Hosea were told to love some other woman. A few scholars have thought this was a second woman, or that chapter 3 retells chapter 1 from Hosea's side, but the word "again" and the flow of chapters 1–3 make Gomer the natural reading.
- **"Who is loved by another man and is an adulteress" (v1).** Some translations read "loved by her husband" (the Hebrew *rea* can mean "friend," "companion" or "lover"), but the ESV's "another man" fits the sentence and the story. Gomer is not merely gone. She belongs, for now, to someone else.
- **"Even as the LORD loves the children of Israel, though they turn to other gods" (v1).** This is the reason for the command, and the heart of the book. Hosea's love for Gomer is meant to mirror God's. The word "love" (*ahav*) appears four times in this one verse. God's love for Israel did not begin because she was faithful, and it did not end when she was unfaithful (Deut 7:7–8; Hos 11:1–4, 8–9).
- **"And love cakes of raisins" (v1).** Cakes of pressed raisins appear elsewhere as festive food (2 Sam 6:19; Song 2:5). Here they are likely food used in pagan worship, offered to the gods or eaten at their harvest festivals. Jeremiah describes the people of Judah baking cakes for "the queen of heaven" (Jer 7:18; 44:19), and the Tyndale notes link raisin cakes with the worship of the goddess Asherah. There is irony in the phrase: Israel "loves" a sweet snack at a pagan feast while the LORD loves Israel. JFB calls them "pressed cakes of dried grapes" offered to idols.
- **"So I bought her" (v2).** The text does not explain why Hosea had to pay. The most common reading is that Gomer had fallen into slavery, perhaps through debt, or that her lover or master demanded payment to release her. Some think Hosea paid her debts or a fee to the man she was living with. Whatever the details, Hosea had to buy back his own wife. The verb used here (*karah*) is rare and can mean "acquire" or "bargain for."
- **"Fifteen shekels of silver and a homer and a lethech of barley" (v2).** A homer was a donkey's load of grain, a large measure, and a lethech was probably half a homer. The mixed payment, part silver and part grain, may suggest Hosea did not have enough silver and made up the rest in barley. Barley was the cheaper grain, worth about half as much as wheat (2 Kings 7:1, 16), and it was the food of the poor and of animals. Many scholars estimate the whole price was around thirty shekels, the price of a slave in the law (Exod 21:32) and, much later, the price paid for Jesus' betrayal (Matt 26:15; Zech 11:12–13). That estimate is uncertain, but the point is clear: Gomer was bought at a low price, the value of a slave, and Hosea paid it anyway.
- **"You must dwell as mine for many days" (v3).** Hosea brings her home, but not straight back to the full intimacy of marriage. There is a waiting period. She must not "play the whore, or belong to another man." This is not punishment for its own sake. It is a time of separation from her old life, a time to break old habits and rebuild trust. JFB says she is "separated for a time, not divorced."
- **"So will I also be to you" (v3).** The Hebrew is brief, literally "and I also to you." Most take it to mean Hosea will also wait, staying faithful to her and not taking another wife, nor yet resuming marital relations. He will share her waiting. Guzik stresses the mutual commitment: Hosea binds himself as much as he binds her.
- **"For the children of Israel shall dwell many days without king or prince" (v4).** Now Hosea explains the sign. As Gomer waits, Israel too will wait many days, stripped of the things she had leaned on. Guzik groups them in three pairs. *King or prince*: political power. Israel's kingdom would end in 722 BC, and it never had its own king again. *Sacrifice or pillar*: worship. Sacrifice could be offered to the LORD, but the sacred stone pillars (*matsevah*) were associated with Canaanite shrines and were forbidden (Deut 16:22). *Ephod or household gods*: ways of seeking guidance. The ephod was a priestly garment, sometimes used to ask God for decisions (1 Sam 23:9–12), though it could also become an idol (Judg 8:27). Household gods (*teraphim*) were small images, like those Rachel stole from her father (Gen 31:19), used in fortune-telling (Ezek 21:21). The list mixes legitimate and illegitimate things on purpose. Israel would lose not only her idols but also the true worship she had corrupted. Everything would go quiet.
- **How long are the "many days"?** The ten northern tribes went into Assyrian exile and never returned as a nation. Judah went into exile in Babylon and came back, but without a king of its own on David's throne. After the Romans destroyed the temple in AD 70, sacrifice ceased entirely, and it has not resumed. JFB, quoting the medieval Jewish scholar Kimchi, applied v4 to the Jewish people's long state "without king and without sacrifice" in his own day, "the last nineteen centuries," while also noting that they had largely been without idols in that time.
- **"Afterward the children of Israel shall return and seek the LORD their God" (v5).** The waiting has a purpose and an end. "Return" (*shuv*) is the Old Testament's main word for repentance, literally to turn back. They will seek God himself, not his gifts, as they once sought Baal for his.
- **"And David their king" (v5).** David had been dead for over two centuries. This means a king from David's line, the one God promised would rule forever (2 Sam 7:12–16). Israel's rebellion in 1 Kings 12 was a rejection of both God's worship in Jerusalem and David's house ("What portion do we have in David?", 1 Kings 12:16). True return means coming back to both. Jeremiah (30:9) and Ezekiel (34:23–24; 37:24–25) speak the same way of "David" as the coming shepherd-king. Jewish interpreters, as JFB notes from Kimchi, read this as the Messiah. Christians see it fulfilled in Jesus, "the son of David" (Matt 1:1). Some, including Guzik, also expect a literal reign in a future millennial kingdom.
- **"They shall come in fear to the LORD and to his goodness" (v5).** The phrase is lovely: they will come trembling, but drawn by his goodness. The verb can mean "come trembling" or "come quickly, eagerly," as a bird flutters to its nest (11:11). JFB says this fear is reverence awakened by God's goodness, "not fear of torment." The people who once credited Baal with every good gift (2:5, 8) will come to the real source of goodness.
- **"In the latter days" (v5).** This phrase in the prophets often points to the time of God's final saving work (Isa 2:2; Mic 4:1). The New Testament says those days began with Christ (Acts 2:17; Heb 1:2).
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws from v1 that love is an act of will that can be obeyed, not just a feeling that comes and goes, and he notes that the law permitted divorce for adultery but never required it. On v2 he mentions Boice's view that Gomer was bought out of slavery, and calls it possible but not certain, preferring the view that Hosea paid to bring her out of prostitution. JFB reads the price as a slave's, paid partly in barley, the coarse food of slaves, a picture of how cheap Israel had made herself. Henry sums up the chapter as showing "the bad character which the people of Israel now had," "the low condition which they should be reduced to by their captivity," and "the blessed reformation that should at length be wrought upon them in the latter days." Guzik places the fullest fulfilment of v5 in a millennial reign, while JFB sees it in Messiah, "the antitype of David."

#### Pointing to Christ
Hosea went to the marketplace and bought back a wife who had left him, paying a slave's price in silver and cheap barley. That is the clearest Old Testament picture of redemption: to "redeem" is to buy back. The New Testament says Christ did this for his unfaithful people: "you were bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:20), "not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). Hosea did not wait for Gomer to come back on her own; he went to find her. "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Rom 5:8). And as Hosea brought her home to a time of waiting that pointed to a full restoration, so the church now lives "in the latter days," already bought and already his, waiting for the wedding day when "David their king," the Son of David, returns for his bride.
