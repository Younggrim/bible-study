*Setting:* Psalm 98 carries the shortest title in the Psalter, one Hebrew word, *mizmor*, "A Psalm," with no author, occasion or tune. The author and date are unknown. It is a close twin of Psalm 96: both begin "Oh sing to the LORD a new song," both end with the same lines about the LORD coming to judge the earth, and both widen out from Israel to the nations to creation itself. But where Psalm 96 calls the nations away from idols, Psalm 98 looks back on a victory God has already won and calls the world to celebrate it. Much of its language matches Isaiah 40–55, the prophecies of Israel's return from exile: v3 echoes Isaiah 52:10 almost word for word. So many scholars connect the psalm with the joy of God's people brought home from Babylon, though it could be sung over any great rescue. The psalm has three stanzas of three verses each: what God has done (vv1–3), how his people should praise (vv4–6), and how creation joins in (vv7–9). Isaac Watts based "Joy to the World" (1719) on this psalm. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow its three stanzas.

#### He Has Done Marvelous Things (vv1–3)
- **"Oh sing to the LORD a new song" (v1).** The same opening as Psalm 96:1. A "new song" answers a new act of God. Miriam did not borrow an Egyptian song at the Red Sea, and Deborah did not borrow Miriam's. Each fresh rescue deserves fresh praise.
- **"For he has done marvelous things!" (v1).** "Marvelous things" (*nifla'ot*) are wonders beyond human power, the word used for God's deeds in Egypt (Exod 3:20; Ps 78:4, 11). This time the reason for the song comes immediately. Praise in the Psalms always has content.
- **"His right hand and his holy arm have worked salvation for him" (v1).** The right hand and arm are pictures of strength in action. God's "holy arm" appears again in Isaiah 52:10: "The LORD has bared his holy arm before the eyes of all the nations." To bare the arm is to roll back the sleeve for hard work or battle. "Worked salvation" (*hoshi'ah*) means he has won the victory. The phrase "for him" stresses that God did it by himself, with no help. Isaiah says the same: "his own arm brought him salvation" (Isa 59:16; 63:5).
- **"The LORD has made known his salvation" (v2).** The victory is not a secret. **"He has revealed his righteousness in the sight of the nations."** In Isaiah 40–55 "salvation" and "righteousness" often appear together as two sides of one act (Isa 46:13; 51:5–6, 8). God's righteousness here is not only his moral perfection but his faithfulness in action, putting things right as he promised.
- **"He has remembered his steadfast love and faithfulness to the house of Israel" (v3).** "Remembered" in Scripture does not mean God had forgotten. It means he acted on his promise (Gen 8:1; Exod 2:24). *Hesed* (steadfast love) and *emunah* (faithfulness) are his covenant loyalty and reliability. The rescue happened because God kept his word to Israel, not because Israel deserved it.
- **"All the ends of the earth have seen the salvation of our God" (v3).** This line is nearly identical to Isaiah 52:10. What God did for Israel was done publicly, before the whole world. His promise to Abraham always had the nations in view (Gen 12:3).
- **The Magnificat.** Mary's song in Luke 1 draws on these verses: "he who is mighty has done great things for me… He has shown strength with his arm… He has helped his servant Israel, in remembrance of his mercy" (Luke 1:49, 51, 54). Simeon, holding the baby Jesus, said, "my eyes have seen your salvation that you have prepared in the presence of all peoples" (Luke 2:30–31). Both heard the fulfilment of Psalm 98 in the birth of Christ.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "a song of praise for redeeming love is a new song," and "if the grace of God put a new heart into our breasts, it will therewith put a new song into our mouths." He applies the "right hand and holy arm" to Christ's victory over Satan and death, won when "there was none to help." He reads salvation and righteousness together: salvation is "the redemption itself, and righteousness the way in which it was wrought." JFB notes that "gotten… victory" is literally "made salvation," and that "holy arm" means "arm of holiness," God's power joined with his moral perfections. He ties v3 to the promise to Abraham (Gen 12:3; 18:18). Guzik observes that God's people are to praise him not only for saving but for making the salvation known, since no one would have discovered it alone. He adds that the New Testament shows this fulfilled beyond what the psalmist could have foreseen.

#### Make a Joyful Noise to the LORD (vv4–6)
- **"Make a joyful noise to the LORD, all the earth" (v4).** The verb (*heri'a*) is the shout raised to greet a king or celebrate a victory. Zechariah 9:9 uses it: "Shout aloud, O daughter of Jerusalem! Behold, your king is coming to you," fulfilled when Jesus rode into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday (Matt 21:5).
- **"Break forth into joyous song and sing praises!" (v4).** "Break forth" (*pitschu*) means to burst open, as a bud bursts or a shout erupts. Isaiah uses it for creation and the redeemed bursting into song (Isa 44:23; 49:13; 52:9: "Break forth together into singing, you waste places of Jerusalem"). Joy cannot be held in.
- **"Sing praises to the LORD with the lyre, with the lyre and the sound of melody!" (v5).** The lyre (*kinnor*) was the small stringed instrument David played (1 Sam 16:23). "Melody" (*zimrah*) means music, a song accompanied by playing. The repeated word builds the sound.
- **"With trumpets and the sound of the horn" (v6).** Two different instruments. The "trumpets" (*chatsotsrot*) were long, straight, silver trumpets, made for the priests to sound at feasts and in battle (Num 10:1–10; 2 Chr 5:12–13). The "horn" (*shofar*) was a ram's horn, blown at Sinai (Exod 19:16), at the fall of Jericho (Josh 6:4–5) and when a new king was crowned (1 Kgs 1:34, 39; 2 Kgs 9:13).
- **"Make a joyful noise before the King, the LORD!" (v6).** Here is the point of all the music. It is a coronation. The shouts, strings, trumpets and horn are what Israel did when a king took the throne. The King here is the LORD himself.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the new King should be welcomed "as new kings are, with acclamations of joy and loud shouts, till the earth ring again, as when Solomon was proclaimed" (1 Kgs 1:40). Since it is "a victorious King" being celebrated, he says it is right to use "not only… the soft and gentle melody of the harp" but "the martial sound of the trumpet and cornet." JFB reads "make a loud noise" as "burst forth" (Isa 14:7; 44:23), and "before… King" as "hail Him as your sovereign." Guzik notes that temple worship was famous for its noise (Ezra 3:13, where the sound was "heard far away"). He takes the instruments either as accompanying the singing or as themselves praising God.

#### Let the Sea Roar (vv7–9)
- **"Let the sea roar, and all that fills it" (v7).** This repeats Psalm 96:11. The sea that once roared against God (93:3–4) now roars for him. **"The world and those who dwell in it!"** echoes Psalm 24:1.
- **"Let the rivers clap their hands" (v8).** Clapping greeted a king at his coronation (2 Kgs 11:12). Here rivers do it, perhaps pictured in the splash and rush of moving water. Isaiah gives the same gesture to trees: "all the trees of the field shall clap their hands" (Isa 55:12). The "rivers" (*neharot*) are the same word as the "floods" that lifted their voice against God in 93:3.
- **"Let the hills sing for joy together" (v8).** Mountains, which melted like wax in 97:5, now sing. All creation responds to its Maker.
- **Why creation rejoices.** This is poetry, but it is not empty. Scripture teaches that creation was caught up in human sin (Gen 3:17–18) and will share in human redemption. Paul says creation "waits with eager longing" and will be "set free from its bondage to corruption" (Rom 8:19–22).
- **"Before the LORD, for he comes to judge the earth" (v9).** The psalm's closing lines repeat the end of Psalm 96 with small changes. Psalm 96:13 has "he comes, for he comes"; here it is said once. Psalm 96 has "in his faithfulness"; here it is **"with equity"** (*mesharim*), the word from 96:10.
- **"He will judge the world with righteousness" (v9).** The victory of vv1–3 is past. The judgment of v9 is still coming. The psalm holds both. God has already acted for his people, and that is the pledge that he will one day put the whole world right.
- **Commentators:** JFB says that "inanimate nature is also summoned to honor Him who triumphs and rules in righteousness and equity." Henry calls the setting up of Christ's kingdom "a matter of joy and praise" in which "all the children of men" may have benefit, and says even inanimate creatures are summoned to welcome him. Guzik sees the same widening circles as in Psalm 96, from Israel to the nations to creation, and links the end of the psalm to the full restoration of all things.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 98 celebrates a victory God won by his own right hand and holy arm, with no one to help. The New Testament sees that victory in Jesus. Mary and Simeon heard this psalm at his birth (Luke 1:46–55; 2:30–32). Isaiah, whose words the psalm echoes, goes on immediately to describe the servant who would be "marred, beyond human semblance," who would bear the sins of many (Isa 52:13–53:12). That is the arm of the LORD laid bare (Isa 53:1). Jesus won the decisive victory over sin and death at the cross and empty tomb, "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame" (Col 2:15), and the good news is now told to "the ends of the earth" (Acts 1:8). The King's coming in v9 is still ahead, which is why Watts' carol, though sung at Christmas, looks forward as much as back: "He rules the world with truth and grace, and makes the nations prove the glories of His righteousness."
