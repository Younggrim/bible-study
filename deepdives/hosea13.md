*Setting:* These are probably among Hosea's last messages to the northern kingdom, in the final years before Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 BC. Israel's last king, Hoshea, had been caught playing Assyria and Egypt against each other (12:1), and the Assyrian army was about to close in for a three-year siege (2 Kgs 17:5–6). Chapter 12 traced Israel's deceit back to Jacob. Chapter 13 is Hosea's darkest chapter, headed in the ESV "The LORD's Relentless Judgment on Israel." It moves through Ephraim's rise and fall, God's faithfulness since Egypt, the forgetting that came with prosperity, God turning on Israel like a wild animal, the failure of their kings, and a birth that never comes. Then, in v14, come words Paul would later sing over the empty tomb: "O Death, where is your sting?"

#### The LORD's Relentless Judgment on Israel (vv1–16)
- **"When Ephraim spoke, there was trembling; he was exalted in Israel" (v1).** Ephraim was the leading tribe of the north. Joshua came from it (Num 13:8), as did Jeroboam I, the first northern king (1 Kgs 11:26), and other tribes feared and deferred to it (Judg 8:1–3; 12:1). "Trembling" (*retet*) appears only here in the Old Testament. In the old days Ephraim had weight.
- **"But he incurred guilt through Baal and died" (v1).** The turning point was Baal worship, which King Ahab made official about 870 BC (1 Kgs 16:31–33). Hosea says the nation "died" then. It was still walking around, but it was already a dead man, cut off from the only source of life. JFB calls Baal worship "a more heinous offense than even the calves."
- **"And now they sin more and more, and make for themselves metal images" (v2).** "Metal images" is the word used for the golden calf at Sinai (Exod 32:4, 8). "Skillfully made" is literally "according to their understanding": worship invented by human cleverness, not received from God. **"All of them the work of craftsmen"**: Hosea's repeated jab (8:6). A thing a craftsman made cannot be a god.
- **"Those who offer human sacrifice kiss calves!" (v2).** The Hebrew is literally "sacrificers of man." It may mean "those who sacrifice, being men, kiss calves," a scornful picture of human beings bowing to cattle. Or it may mean what the ESV says: people who offer human sacrifice, which Israel did in its last years (2 Kgs 17:17), now kiss idols. Kissing an idol was an act of worship (1 Kgs 19:18: "every mouth that has not kissed him"). Either way the values are upside down: people die and statues are kissed.
- **"Like the morning mist… like the dew… like the chaff… like smoke from a window" (v3).** Four pictures of things that vanish. The first two were used of Israel's fleeting love in 6:4: "Your love is like a morning cloud, like the dew that goes early away." Chaff blew away when grain was tossed in the wind on an exposed threshing floor (Ps 1:4). Houses had no chimneys; smoke drifted out of a lattice window and was gone. Their idols could not give them permanence.
- **"But I am the LORD your God from the land of Egypt" (v4).** The same words as 12:9, echoing the opening of the Ten Commandments (Exod 20:2–3). **"You know no God but me, and besides me there is no savior."** This is the heart of the chapter. Israel had tried Baal, calves, kings, Assyria and Egypt. None of them had ever saved them. Isaiah later says it the same way: "besides me there is no savior" (Isa 43:11). The Hebrew for savior, *moshia*, comes from the same root as the names Hosea, Joshua and Jesus.
- **"It was I who knew you in the wilderness, in the land of drought" (v5).** "Knew" means more than awareness. It is covenant care, choosing and providing for them (Amos 3:2). The Greek translation reads "shepherded you," from a very similar Hebrew word, and that fits the next verse.
- **"When they had grazed, they became full… and their heart was lifted up; therefore they forgot me" (v6).** Four steps: fed, full, proud, forgetful. This is exactly what Moses warned would happen in the land: "lest, when you have eaten and are full… then your heart be lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God" (Deut 8:12–14; compare 32:15, "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked"). Hard times had kept them close. Good times sent them away.
- **"So I am to them like a lion; like a leopard I will lurk beside the way" (v7).** The God who shepherded them now becomes the beast that hunts them. Lions, leopards and bears all lived in Israel in Bible times (1 Sam 17:34; 2 Kgs 2:24; Song 4:8). The leopard lies in wait in the thicket by the path and springs. Some manuscripts read "by the road to Assyria," but most follow the Hebrew text.
- **"Like a bear robbed of her cubs" (v8).** This was proverbial for blind fury (2 Sam 17:8; Prov 17:12). **"I will tear open their breast"**: literally "the enclosure of their heart," the ribcage or the membrane around the heart. The language is meant to shock. God is not a tame pet, and the Assyrians were famously savage. But this is a God of love whose love has been spurned, and the fury is the other side of that love (compare 5:14).
- **"He destroys you, O Israel, for you are against me, against your helper" (v9).** The Hebrew is very compressed, literally "It destroys you, Israel, that against me, against your help." The point is that Israel's ruin is self-inflicted. JFB: "thy destruction is of thyself." God is called Israel's "help" (*ezer*), the same word used of God in the Psalms (Ps 33:20; 121:1–2) and of Eve as Adam's helper (Gen 2:18). They have set themselves against the only help they had.
- **"Where now is your king, to save you in all your cities?" (v10).** The answer was obvious: in an Assyrian prison (2 Kgs 17:4). "Save" picks up "savior" from v4. **"Those of whom you said, 'Give me a king and princes'"** recalls Israel's demand to Samuel, "Give us a king to judge us," which God called a rejection of himself as king (1 Sam 8:5–7).
- **"I gave you a king in my anger, and I took him away in my wrath" (v11).** This can be read of Saul, given in answer to a sinful demand and then removed. It can also describe the whole history of the northern kingdom, born in rebellion against David's line (1 Kgs 12) and running through a string of kings who ended in assassination (2 Kgs 15). Sometimes the worst judgment is getting what one demanded.
- **"The iniquity of Ephraim is bound up; his sin is kept in store" (v12).** The picture is of documents tied up and sealed for safekeeping, or of treasure stored for later (Deut 32:34; Job 14:17). God's delay is not forgetfulness. The record is being kept for the day of reckoning.
- **"The pangs of childbirth come for him, but he is an unwise son" (v13).** Labour pains often stand for sudden, unavoidable crisis (Isa 13:8; Jer 13:21). Hosea twists the picture: Ephraim is both the mother in agony and the baby who will not be born. **"At the right time he does not present himself at the opening of the womb."** A baby that does not come out in time dies and endangers its mother. The moment of crisis was Ephraim's chance to be born anew through repentance, and Ephraim stayed stuck.
- **"I shall ransom them from the power of Sheol; I shall redeem them from Death" (v14).** Sheol is the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead, the grave. "Ransom" (*padah*) and "redeem" (*ga'al*) are the two great words for rescue in the Old Testament: paying a price to free a slave, and the kinsman stepping in to buy back what was lost (Exod 13:13; Ruth 4). Death and Sheol appear like two powers holding Israel captive.
- **A question or a promise?** Translators and commentators divide here. The Hebrew has no question marks, so the first two lines can be read as promises (ESV, KJV) or as questions expecting "no": "Shall I ransom them…? Shall I redeem them…?" (NIV, NRSV). The next lines, "O Death, where are your plagues? O Sheol, where is your sting?", can then be a taunt of death's defeat, or a summons calling death to come and do its work. The last line, **"Compassion is hidden from my eyes,"** fits a threat: God will not change his mind about judgment. The Tyndale notes take the whole verse as a threat. JFB, Henry and Guzik read it as a promise that shines through the judgment. Both readings take the context seriously, and the immediate context (vv15–16) is certainly judgment.
- **"O Death, where is your sting?" (v14).** However Hosea's first hearers took it, the Holy Spirit had more in mind. Paul quotes this line, close to the Greek translation, at the climax of his chapter on the resurrection: "O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?… thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 15:55–57). The God who has power to summon death has power to destroy it. In Christ, the threat becomes a taunt.
- **"Though he may flourish among his brothers" (v15).** "Flourish" (*yaphri*) is a pun on Ephraim, "fruitful" (Gen 41:52; Hos 9:16). **"The east wind, the wind of the LORD, shall come, rising from the wilderness."** The scorching desert wind (12:1) is a picture of Assyria sweeping in from the east. It will dry up Ephraim's springs and **"strip his treasury of every precious thing."** Assyrian records boast of the plunder carried from Samaria.
- **"Samaria shall bear her guilt, because she has rebelled against her God" (v16).** In English Bibles the chapter ends here. In Hebrew Bibles this is 14:1. The final picture is the horror of ancient siege warfare: **"their little ones shall be dashed in pieces, and their pregnant women ripped open."** These were documented atrocities of the time (2 Kgs 8:12; 15:16; Amos 1:13). Hosea does not soften what rebellion against "her God" was about to cost. Samaria fell in 722 BC, and Sargon II boasted of deporting more than 27,000 of its people.
- **Commentators:** Henry ties Ephraim's greatness to its early reverence: "While Ephraim kept up a holy fear of God… so long he was very considerable." He also observes, "Abused goodness calls for greater severity." Guzik, drawing on Hubbard, sums up v2 as values turned upside down: craftsmen's work treated as gods, children offered and animals kissed. Guzik also notes that prosperity breeds forgetfulness. JFB notes that bereaved, "none is more savage than a she bear," and reads v12 as sin "kept as it were safely sealed up." On v14 Henry says God "will destroy death and the grave," and both Guzik and JFB tie the verse to Christ's victory in 1 Corinthians 15. JFB reads "compassion is hidden" as God refusing to change his purpose of fulfilling his promise, while the Tyndale notes take it as God refusing to relent from judgment. The word "repentance" in older versions (KJV) means God changing his mind, not Israel repenting.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Besides me there is no savior" (v4). Israel looked everywhere for salvation except to the LORD, and every substitute failed. The chapter ends in the grave and in the ruins of Samaria. Yet the God who says this is also the one who says, "I shall ransom them from the power of Sheol." Jesus, whose name means "the LORD saves," is that ransom. He entered death itself, paid the price and walked out of the tomb, so that Paul can take Hosea's words and turn them into a song of triumph: "O death, where is your sting?" The sting of death is sin, and Christ has drawn it.
