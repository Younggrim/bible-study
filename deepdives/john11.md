*Setting:* It is early in AD 30 (on the most common dating), a few weeks before the Passover at which Jesus will die. After the Feast of Dedication in winter, when the crowd tried to stone him for saying "I and the Father are one" (10:30–31), Jesus withdrew across the Jordan to the place where John had baptized (10:40). The raising of Lazarus is the last and greatest of the signs John records before the cross, and the climax of the first half of the book. It is the most dramatic display of Jesus' power over death, and it is also the event that seals his own death. The Sanhedrin decides to kill him because of it. John tells the story with great tenderness, and it shows Jesus' divine authority and his human grief more clearly than almost any other passage.

#### The Death of Lazarus (vv1–16)
- **"Lazarus of Bethany, the village of Mary and her sister Martha" (v1).** Lazarus is the Greek form of the Hebrew *Eleazar*, "God has helped." It was a common name, and this is not the Lazarus of Jesus' parable in Luke 16, though some have noticed the striking link with that story's ending: "neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead" (Luke 16:31). Bethany was a village on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, about two miles from Jerusalem (v18). It is today's al-Eizariya, the Arabic name still preserving Lazarus's. Luke tells of Jesus visiting Martha and Mary, with Martha busy serving and Mary sitting at his feet (Luke 10:38–42).
- **"Lord, he whom you love is ill" (v3).** The sisters don't ask Jesus to come or tell him what to do. They simply state the need and appeal to his love. "Love" here is *phileō*, the love of friendship.
- **"This illness does not lead to death. It is for the glory of God" (v4).** Lazarus will die, so Jesus means death will not be the final outcome. As with the man born blind (9:3), the suffering becomes the place where God's work is displayed. "So that the Son of God may be glorified through it": in John, "glorified" points toward the cross. This sign will set in motion the events that lead to Jesus' death and resurrection (vv46–53; 12:23).
- **"Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So… he stayed two days longer" (vv5–6).** The "so" is startling. Because he loved them, he waited. Love here uses *agapaō*. From Bethany beyond the Jordan it was about a day's journey. If Lazarus was buried the day he died, as was usual, and had been in the tomb four days when Jesus arrived (v17), he had probably already died by the time the messenger reached Jesus. The delay didn't cause his death. It let the miracle be beyond all doubt and let the sisters' faith be stretched further than it would have been by a healing.
- **"Are there not twelve hours in the day?" (v9).** The disciples have warned that the Jews were just trying to stone him (v8). Daylight was divided into twelve hours, whatever the season. The day is the time the Father has given for Jesus' work, and it can't be cut short. Walking "in the day" by the Father's will, Jesus is safe. The one who walks "in the night" stumbles, "because the light is not in him" (v10). Jesus is the light (8:12; 9:5), so those who follow him won't stumble.
- **"Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I go to awaken him" (v11).** "Sleep" was a common way to speak of death, in both the Old Testament (Dan 12:2) and the New (Acts 7:60; 1 Thess 4:13–14). For believers it says something deeper: death is real, but temporary. The disciples take Jesus literally: "if he has fallen asleep, he will recover" (v12).
- **"Lazarus has died, and for your sake I am glad that I was not there" (vv14–15).** Jesus is not glad that Lazarus died, but glad that the disciples will see something that will deepen their faith. They are about to need it.
- **"Let us also go, that we may die with him" (v16).** Thomas, best known for doubting after the resurrection (20:24–29), here shows grim courage. "Thomas" is Aramaic for "twin," and "Didymus" (the name in the Greek text) is the Greek word for the same. He expects Jesus to be killed in Judea and is ready to go and die with him. His faith is pessimistic, but loyal. He would rather die with Jesus than live without him.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws a lesson from the sisters' message, which pleads Jesus' love rather than their own, and says of the delay, "he loved them and therefore he lingered," adding that "God hath gracious intentions even in seeming delays." JFB says the delay let things reach their worst, for "the ways of divine are not as the ways of human love." Guzik notes the pattern in John 2 and 7, where Jesus seems at first to turn down those close to him and then acts on the Father's timing. On v11, Henry writes, "A good Christian, when he dies, does but sleep." JFB says Thomas "could not and cared not to survive his Master's sacrifice," and Guzik calls his words loyal despair. Guzik also passes on a church tradition that Thomas looked like Jesus, which is legend, not text.

#### I Am the Resurrection and the Life (vv17–27)
- **"Lazarus had already been in the tomb four days" (v17).** Jews buried the dead quickly, usually the same day, because of the climate and because touching a corpse made a person unclean. A later rabbinic tradition held that the soul hovered near the body for three days hoping to return, and departed when it saw the face begin to decay. Whether that belief was widespread in Jesus' day is uncertain, but four days left no room for anyone to say Lazarus had only been unconscious.
- **"Many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary to console them" (v19).** Mourning customs were elaborate. The first seven days were the most intense, and friends and neighbors came to sit with the family. The crowd from Jerusalem means the family was well known, and it means many witnesses will see what happens.
- **"Martha… went and met him, but Mary remained seated in the house" (v20).** The two sisters act just as Luke describes them: Martha active, Mary still (Luke 10:38–42).
- **"Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died" (v21).** It's honest grief, with perhaps a hint of reproach. Both sisters say exactly the same thing (v32), probably words they had said to each other many times over those four days.
- **"But even now I know that whatever you ask from God, God will give you" (v22).** Martha has real faith in Jesus' prayers. It's not clear she expects him to raise Lazarus. Her objection at the tomb (v39) suggests not. But she trusts him.
- **"Your brother will rise again… I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day" (vv23–24).** Jesus' words can be taken as ordinary comfort, the kind any mourner would offer. Martha takes them that way. She believes in the final resurrection, as the Pharisees did (Dan 12:2; Acts 23:8), and the Sadducees did not. But it feels far away.
- **"I am the resurrection and the life" (v25).** This is the fifth "I am" saying with an image. Jesus moves resurrection from the distant future into his own person. He does not say he will bring resurrection, or that he has the power to raise people. He says he *is* the resurrection. Life in the full sense is found in him (1:4; 5:26). The "last day" Martha believes in is present in the one standing in front of her.
- **"Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die" (vv25–26).** There are two promises. First, believers who die physically will live, raised to life. Second, believers who are alive will "never die," meaning they will never experience death as final separation from God. "Never" is the emphatic double negative. Physical death is real, but for those who belong to Jesus it is not the end.
- **"Do you believe this?" (v26).** Jesus presses the question personally. It is the question the whole Gospel puts to its readers (20:31).
- **"Yes, Lord; I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world" (v27).** Martha's confession is one of the greatest in the New Testament, matching Peter's (Matt 16:16) and echoing John's stated purpose almost word for word (20:31: "that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God"). "I believe" is perfect tense: "I have come to believe and do believe." Martha, often remembered for being busy in the kitchen, makes a confession of faith as full as any apostle's.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reports the tradition about the soul hovering for three days as background, citing older scholars. JFB doesn't mention it and simply shows how the four days fit the journey. On Martha, JFB calls her confidence real though sometimes "overclouded." On v25 JFB says "the whole power to restore, impart, and maintain life, resides in" Jesus, and that for the believer death is "swallowed up in life." Guzik stresses that Jesus says he *is* the resurrection, a claim only God could make, and notes that the question "Do you believe this?" calls for trust, not debate. JFB says Martha grasped that resurrection belonged to the Messiah's office, even if she did not understand everything.

#### Jesus Weeps (vv28–37)
- **"The Teacher is here and is calling for you" (v28).** Martha tells Mary "in private," perhaps so she could meet Jesus without the crowd, or because Martha knew the danger to Jesus so close to Jerusalem. "The Teacher" is a respectful title.
- **"She fell at his feet" (v32).** In each of her three appearances in the Gospels, Mary is at Jesus' feet: learning (Luke 10:39), grieving (here) and worshiping (12:3). She says the same words as Martha, but adds no "even now" and no confession. Her grief is too heavy.
- **"He was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled" (v33).** "Deeply moved" is *embrimaomai*, a strong word. In classical Greek it described a horse snorting. In the New Testament it usually means a stern warning (Mark 1:43) or indignation (Mark 14:5). The lexicon gives "to be moved with anger" for this verse. So what is Jesus angry about? Probably death itself, the enemy that has torn this family apart, and perhaps the sin that brought death into the world. Some also see anger at the crowd's hopeless wailing or at their unbelief. "Greatly troubled" (*tarassō*) is literally "troubled himself," the same word for Jesus' distress in 12:27 and 13:21. This is not calm sympathy. It is the reaction of a warrior facing the enemy he is about to defeat.
- **"Jesus wept" (v35).** This is the shortest verse in most English Bibles, and one of the most profound. The verb (*dakryō*, to shed tears) is different from the loud wailing (*klaiō*) of Mary and the mourners (v33). Jesus cries quietly. He knows that in a few minutes Lazarus will walk out of the tomb, and still he weeps. His tears are real human grief for a friend and real compassion for the sisters. The God of Scripture is not distant or unmoved (Isa 63:9). The Greek philosophers taught that the gods could not feel. Here God in the flesh stands at a grave and cries.
- **"See how he loved him!" (v36).** Some read his tears rightly. Others wonder why he didn't prevent the death, since he had opened the eyes of the blind man (v37). That's a fair question, and not necessarily a sneer. It's the question the sisters had already asked.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads *embrimaomai* as anger directed at death, the enemy Jesus was about to confront, citing Calvin's picture of a wrestler preparing for a contest. The lexicon supports that sense. JFB takes it as Jesus "powerfully checked his emotion," holding back his tears, which is a less likely meaning. Henry calls it real inward sympathy, "a deep and hearty sigh," and also allows some displeasure at the mourners' excessive grief. On v35, JFB and Guzik both note the difference between Jesus' quiet tears and the loud wailing around him, and JFB says the "wages of sin" lay under his sorrow. Henry calls Jesus "a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief," and says tears of compassion make Christians more like Christ. Guzik takes the bystanders' question in v37 as genuine sorrow, not mockery.

#### Jesus Raises Lazarus (vv38–44)
- **"It was a cave, and a stone lay against it" (v38).** Wealthier families used tombs cut into the rock, with benches or niches for bodies. A large stone, sometimes a round, rolling disk, closed the entrance. Lazarus's tomb is described much like the tomb of Jesus (20:1), one of several ways this story previews Jesus' own burial and resurrection.
- **"Take away the stone" (v39).** Jesus could have moved it himself. He asks others to do what they can do, and he does what only he can do.
- **"Lord, by this time there will be an odor" (v39).** Martha's practical objection shows she had not expected a resurrection. It also stresses that Lazarus was truly dead and decaying.
- **"Father, I thank you that you have heard me" (v41).** Jesus thanks God before anything has happened, as though the answer were already given. He had evidently prayed earlier. "I said this on account of the people standing around, that they may believe that you sent me" (v42). He doesn't need to pray aloud for his own sake. He prays aloud so that no one will think this is magic or his own independent power. Everything he does comes from the Father (5:19).
- **"He cried out with a loud voice, 'Lazarus, come out'" (v43).** "Cried out" (*kraugazō*) is a shout. It is the word the crowds will use to shout "Crucify him!" (19:6, 15). Jesus had said, "an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out" (5:28–29). Here is a preview.
- **"The man who had died came out, his hands and feet bound with linen strips, and his face wrapped with a cloth" (v44).** Bodies were wrapped in strips of linen with spices, and a separate cloth (*soudarion*) covered the face. Lazarus shuffles out still bound. Compare Jesus' tomb, where the linen cloths were left lying and the face-cloth folded by itself (20:5–7). Lazarus came back to ordinary life and would die again. He needed others to unbind him. Jesus will rise to a new kind of life, never to die again (Rom 6:9), and he will leave his grave clothes behind.
- **"Unbind him, and let him go" (v44).** Jesus brings the dead to life. His people are given the work of helping set the living free. Many have seen in this a picture of the church's role with new believers.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the stone was removed so all could see the body was truly dead. On v41 he urges readers to "meet the first appearances of the return of prayer with early thanksgivings," as Jesus did. JFB says Jesus thanked God for an answer to prayer already given, and that the people's part, moving the stone and unbinding him, prepared for and completed what only Jesus could do. Guzik draws the same lesson: God does what only he can do and gives people what they can do. Guzik stresses that Lazarus was restored to mortal life, not raised to resurrection life like Jesus. Henry sees in the loud call both the gospel call that wakes dead souls and a preview of the final resurrection.

#### The Plot to Kill Jesus (vv45–57)
- **"Many of the Jews… believed in him, but some of them went to the Pharisees" (vv45–46).** Here is the same division as always, but at its sharpest. The greatest sign produces both faith and a report to the authorities.
- **"The chief priests and the Pharisees gathered the council" (v47).** This is the Sanhedrin, the supreme Jewish council of seventy-one members, led by the high priest. "What are we to do? For this man performs many signs." They don't deny the miracles. Their problem is what to do about them.
- **"The Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation" (v48).** "Our place" probably means the temple (Acts 6:13–14), and perhaps their own positions of power. A popular messianic movement could look like a revolt, and Rome crushed revolts brutally. The irony is bitter: about forty years later, in AD 70, the Romans did come and destroy both the temple and the nation, after a revolt led by men claiming to be deliverers.
- **"Caiaphas, who was high priest that year" (v49).** Joseph Caiaphas was high priest from AD 18 to 36, an unusually long term that shows how well he worked with the Roman governors. "That year" doesn't mean the office rotated annually. It means he was high priest in that fateful year. His father-in-law Annas had been high priest before him and still held great influence (18:13). In 1990 an ornate bone box (ossuary) inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas" was found in Jerusalem. Many scholars think it belonged to him or his family.
- **"It is better for you that one man should die for the people" (v50).** Caiaphas means it as political calculation: sacrifice one troublemaker to save the nation from Rome. John sees a deeper meaning Caiaphas never intended: "being high priest that year he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation" (v51). In the Old Testament the high priest sometimes received God's guidance for the people (Exod 28:30; Num 27:21). God speaks through Caiaphas despite his intentions, as he once spoke through Balaam (Num 22–24). "For" (*hyper*) is the language of substitution. Jesus will indeed die in the place of others.
- **"To gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad" (v52).** This is John's comment, not Caiaphas's. Jesus' death reaches beyond Israel to all whom God will gather from the nations, the "other sheep" of 10:16. The prophets promised God would gather his scattered people (Isa 43:5–6; Ezek 37:21–22).
- **"From that day on they made plans to put him to death" (v53).** Earlier attempts to kill Jesus were spontaneous, a crowd picking up stones. Now it is official policy. Ironically, Jesus' gift of life to Lazarus leads to his own death. And by his death, Jesus gives life to the world.
- **"A town called Ephraim" (v54).** Most identify it with modern et-Taiyibeh, about twelve to fifteen miles north of Jerusalem, on the edge of the wilderness. Jesus withdraws, not from fear, but because his hour is set for Passover, a few weeks away.
- **"Many went up… before the Passover to purify themselves" (v55).** Anyone who had become ritually unclean, for example by contact with a corpse, needed a week-long purification before eating the Passover (Num 9:6–12; 19:11–12). So pilgrims arrived early. This is the third Passover in John (2:13; 6:4), and the last.
- **"What do you think? That he will not come to the feast at all?" (v56).** The crowds in the temple are talking about him. The authorities have issued what amounts to a warrant: anyone who knows where he is must report it (v57). The stage is set for chapter 12 and the final week.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the council admitted Jesus' signs were real but feared losing their position, and that "our place" probably means the temple. He calls Caiaphas's reasoning logical but not moral. JFB says Caiaphas intended only political expediency but was "so guided" that his words became "a divine prediction," and points out that v52 is the Gospel writer's comment, not part of Caiaphas's speech. On v53, JFB says the council "expressed what the party was secretly wishing." Guzik ties v52 to the "other sheep" of 10:16. JFB places Ephraim between Jerusalem and Jericho, while the Tyndale notes and most modern scholars put it north of Jerusalem. Guzik says Jesus withdrew there not out of fear but because his hour had not yet come.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus stands at the grave of a friend and weeps, and then, with a shout, calls him out of death. He is fully human, moved with grief and anger at what death does to the people he loves, and fully divine, the resurrection and the life in person. The raising of Lazarus is a preview of the day when "all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out" (5:28). It is also the event that sends Jesus to the cross. Caiaphas says one man should die for the people, and without knowing it he states the gospel: Jesus will die in place of the people to gather God's scattered children into one. Lazarus came out still bound in grave clothes and would die again. A few weeks later Jesus would leave his grave clothes folded in an empty tomb and never die again. Because of that, the question he asked Martha stands for every reader: "Do you believe this?"
