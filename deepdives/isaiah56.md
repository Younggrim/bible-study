*Setting:* Chapter 56 opens the last major part of Isaiah, chapters 56–66. Chapters 40–55 spoke comfort to exiles in Babylon. These last chapters seem to look at life back in the land: a community with a temple and altar, Sabbaths, leaders and watchmen, and the same old sins. Many scholars think these chapters come from a later prophet or circle of Isaiah's followers after the return from exile (the "Third Isaiah" view). Others, along with long Jewish and Christian tradition, hold that Isaiah himself wrote them, looking ahead. Either way, the message fits the moment the book has reached. The free invitation of chapter 55 is now worked out: who may come into God's house, and how those inside must live. The chapter begins with the most generous welcome in the Old Testament to outsiders, then turns sharply to Israel's own leaders, who are asleep on the job.

#### Salvation for Foreigners (vv1–8)
- **"Keep justice, and do righteousness, for soon my salvation will come" (v1).** "Righteousness" appears twice in this verse with two senses. People are to *do* righteousness, right living. God's *righteousness* is about to be revealed, which here means his saving faithfulness in action, the parallel to "my salvation" (46:13; 51:5). God does not say "live rightly so that I will save you." He says "live rightly *because* I am about to save you." Grace comes first, and the response is shaped by it. John the Baptist called for the same thing before the Messiah came (Luke 3:8–14).
- **"Blessed is the man who does this… who keeps the Sabbath, not profaning it" (v2).** The Sabbath was the sign of the covenant between God and Israel (Exod 31:13–17). In exile, without a temple, Sabbath-keeping became one of the clearest marks of belonging to the LORD. "Profaning" means treating something holy as ordinary. **"Keeps his hand from doing any evil."** Worship and daily conduct go together. JFB says the two halves cover the whole of duty: worship of God and a holy life.
- **"Let not the foreigner who has joined himself to the LORD say, 'The LORD will surely separate me from his people'" (v3).** A "foreigner" is a non-Israelite. Some had attached themselves to Israel's God, as Ruth had (Ruth 1:16). But the Law limited them. Ammonites and Moabites were barred from the assembly (Deut 23:3–6), and others had to wait generations (Deut 23:7–8). After the exile, Nehemiah and Ezra read those laws and separated foreigners from the community (Neh 13:1–3; Ezra 9–10), mostly over marriage to idol-worshippers. A foreigner who truly loved the LORD might well fear being pushed out. God says: do not say it.
- **"Let not the eunuch say, 'Behold, I am a dry tree'" (v3).** A eunuch was a man who had been castrated, often a royal servant in a foreign court. The Law barred such a man from the assembly of the LORD (Deut 23:1), and a priest with damaged reproductive organs could not serve at the altar (Lev 21:20). Without children, he had no one to carry on his name, the deepest loss in Israel's culture. A "dry tree" bears no fruit and will be cut down.
- **Why eunuchs here?** Isaiah had told King Hezekiah that some of his own descendants would be "eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon" (39:7). Daniel and his friends were placed under "the chief of the eunuchs" (Dan 1:3–7), and many think they were made eunuchs themselves. So this promise may have touched sons of David's own line who had been mutilated in exile. They would not be thrown out.
- **"To the eunuchs who keep my Sabbaths, who choose the things that please me and hold fast my covenant" (v4).** Three marks of genuine faith: keeping God's day, choosing what pleases him, holding fast to his covenant. "Hold fast" means grip tightly, as someone holds on in a storm. These are not outsiders by heart, only by body.
- **"A monument and a name better than sons and daughters" (v5).** "Monument and a name" is *yad vashem* in Hebrew. *Yad* means "hand," and by extension a memorial marker. Childless Absalom set up a pillar in the King's Valley, saying, "I have no son to keep my name in remembrance," and it was called "Absalom's monument" (*yad*, 2 Sam 18:18). The man without sons gets a better memorial, "in my house and within my walls." Israel's national Holocaust memorial in Jerusalem takes its name, Yad Vashem, from this verse, honoring the many who were murdered with no one left to carry their names.
- **"An everlasting name that shall not be cut off" (v5).** There is a painful wordplay. The law of Deuteronomy 23:1 describes the eunuch with the verb "cut off" (*karat*). God promises him a name that will never be "cut off," the same word. He who was cut will never be cut off. The previous chapter ended with the same promise for God's own name (55:13).
- **"The foreigners who join themselves to the LORD, to minister to him, to love the name of the LORD, and to be his servants" (v6).** Look at the words used of these Gentiles. "Minister" (*sharat*) is a word normally used of priests and Levites serving in the temple (Deut 10:8). "His servants" is the plural that began at 54:17, the people of the Servant. Foreigners are not tolerated on the edge. They are given the language of insiders.
- **"These I will bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer" (v7).** Mount Zion, where the temple stood. Isaiah had seen the nations streaming there to learn God's ways (2:2–4). **"Their burnt offerings and their sacrifices will be accepted on my altar."** Israel's own offerings had been rejected when they came from unjust hands (1:11–15). Gentile offerings from loving hearts will be welcome.
- **"My house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (v7).** This is the heart of the chapter. Solomon had prayed at the temple's dedication that God would hear the foreigner who came to pray there (1 Kings 8:41–43). In Jesus' day, the only part of the temple a Gentile could enter was the outer court. Stone notices, one of which was found in 1871, warned foreigners not to go further on pain of death. That outer court was where the money-changers and animal sellers had set up shop. When Jesus drove them out, he quoted this verse: "Is it not written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations'? But you have made it a den of robbers" (Mark 11:17). Mark alone keeps "for all the nations." The marketplace had filled the one space where the nations could pray.
- **"Who gathers the outcasts of Israel… 'I will gather yet others to him besides those already gathered'" (v8).** The "outcasts" are scattered Israelites coming home (11:12). The "others" are Gentiles. Jesus said, "I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also… So there will be one flock, one shepherd" (John 10:16). Paul says Christ has broken down "the dividing wall of hostility" between Jew and Gentile and made one new people (Eph 2:11–19).
- **The Ethiopian eunuch.** In Acts 8, Philip meets an Ethiopian eunuch returning from worship in Jerusalem, where he could not have entered the inner courts. He was reading Isaiah 53, a few columns before this promise. He asked, "What prevents me from being baptized?" and nothing did (Acts 8:36–38). Luke seems to tell the story as Isaiah 56 coming true: a foreigner and a eunuch brought fully into God's people.
- **Does this cancel the Law?** Isaiah does not explain how this fits with Deuteronomy 23. Some read it as a prophecy of a coming age when God would remove the barriers, fulfilled in Christ. Others read Deuteronomy as dealing with pagan practices, so a eunuch or foreigner who truly turned to the LORD was never meant to be excluded. Either way, Isaiah shows the Law's restrictions were not God's final word about who belongs to him. Faith, love and covenant loyalty are what count.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv1–2 "a solemn charge… to make conscience of our duty" if we hope to benefit from the gospel promises. On v3 he says, "Unbelief often suggests things to the discouragement of good people," which is exactly what God answers here. Guzik says God's word outweighs the eunuch's feeling of being shut out, and applies it to finding worth in God's recognition rather than others' acceptance. He mentions the Yad Vashem memorial as a picture of v5. JFB says the Gentile believers "shall no longer be confined as proselytes were, to the outer court," and reads the sacrifices of v7 spiritually, with Christ's cross as the true altar. JFB also notes that the Hebrew for "place" (the ESV's "monument") literally means "hand," which the lexicon confirms.

#### Israel's Irresponsible Leaders (vv9–12)
- **"All you beasts of the field, come to devour" (v9).** The mood changes completely. God calls wild animals to come and eat. The animals are enemy nations who will prey on an unprotected people (Jer 12:9; Ezek 34:5). The flock is defenseless because its guards have failed. Many scholars see this as the beginning of a section running into chapter 57 about Israel's sins.
- **"His watchmen are blind; they are all without knowledge" (v10).** Watchmen stood on the walls to warn of danger (52:8; Ezek 3:17; 33:1–9). Prophets and leaders were God's watchmen. A blind watchman is worse than useless. He gives a false sense of safety.
- **"They are all silent dogs; they cannot bark" (v10).** Shepherds kept dogs to warn them of wolves and thieves (Job 30:1). A dog that will not bark is the picture of leaders who will not warn. They see sin and say nothing. Henry: "they were dumb dogs, that would not give warning of it." **"Dreaming, lying down, loving to slumber."** "Dreaming" may also mean "panting" or "raving." They are not just asleep. They love it.
- **"The dogs have a mighty appetite; they never have enough" (v11).** The same leaders who are lazy are greedy. Literally they are "strong of soul," with an appetite that is never satisfied. **"But they are shepherds who have no understanding."** Ezekiel 34 is the longest version of this charge: shepherds who feed themselves instead of the sheep. God promises to be the shepherd himself (Isa 40:11; Ezek 34:11–16).
- **"They have all turned to their own way, each to his own gain" (v11).** This echoes 53:6, "we have turned, every one, to his own way." The leaders do exactly what the sheep did. Each looks out for himself.
- **"Come… let me get wine; let us fill ourselves with strong drink" (v12).** Drinking leaders were an old charge in Isaiah (5:11–12, 22; 28:7). **"And tomorrow will be like this day, great beyond measure."** Life is good and will only get better. That confidence is the opposite of watchfulness. Jesus told of a servant who said, "My master is delayed," and began to beat the other servants and drink with drunkards, only for the master to come on a day he did not expect (Matt 24:48–51). James warns against boasting about tomorrow (Jas 4:13–14).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv9–12 "a high charge… against the watchmen of Israel, that were careless and unfaithful," and the start of a sermon of rebuke that runs into the next chapters. He calls them "greedy dogs that can never have enough." JFB reads the "beasts" as the nations God used to punish Israel, from the Babylonians to the Romans, and notes that the drinking was "habitual and intentional." Guzik contrasts these leaders with the faithful David of 55:3–5 and sees in v12 the careless confidence of a world, and perhaps a church, unready for Christ's return.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter holds two pictures side by side: an open house for the outsider, and shepherds who feed themselves. Jesus fulfils both. He cleared the temple's outer court because it was meant to be "a house of prayer for all the nations." He is the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep, the opposite of the greedy dogs of v11 (John 10:11). And he gathers "other sheep" besides Israel into one flock. In him the foreigner is no longer separated and the eunuch is no longer a dry tree. Both receive a name in God's house that will not be cut off.
