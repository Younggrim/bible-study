*Setting:* This is the second half of the first appendix story (chapters 17–18), set early in the judges period, probably within a few generations of Joshua. Chapter 17 ended with Micah of Ephraim pleased with himself: a private shrine with silver images, an ephod, household gods, and a hired Levite as priest, so that "the LORD will prosper me" (17:13). Now the tribe of Dan arrives. Dan had been given land on the coastal plain west of Judah, between the hills and the sea (Josh 19:40–46), but the Amorites "pressed the people of Dan back into the hill country" (1:34), and later the Philistines held that plain. Samson came from the edge of it, from Zorah and Eshtaol (13:2, 25). Instead of taking the land God gave them, the Danites look for easier land somewhere else, and on the way they take Micah's religion with them. Joshua 19:47 records the same migration in a single sentence.

#### Danites Take the Levite and the Idol (vv1–31)
- **"In those days there was no king in Israel" (v1).** The refrain from 17:6 returns, in its short form. It frames everything that follows: no one checks a tribe that robs a fellow Israelite and wipes out a peaceful town.
- **"No inheritance… had fallen to them" (v1).** This is not literally true. Dan's lot had fallen (Josh 19:40). What they lacked was possession of it. The phrase tells the story from Dan's point of view: they speak as though God had left them out, when they had failed to take what he gave.
- **Five men from Zorah and Eshtaol (v2).** These are Samson's home towns, where the Spirit of the LORD first stirred him (13:25). The echo is pointed. Samson at least troubled the Philistines. His tribe now turns away from the Philistine problem altogether and goes north.
- **"They recognized the voice of the young Levite" (v3).** Perhaps they knew him, or perhaps they recognized his southern accent, from Judah (17:7). Their three questions, "Who brought you here? What are you doing in this place? What is your business here?", are the right questions to put to a Levite serving at a private shrine. Nobody waits for a real answer.
- **"He has hired me" (v4).** The Levite describes his ministry in one word: a wage. The Hebrew verb is the ordinary word for hiring a laborer.
- **"Inquire of God, please" (vv5–6).** The Danites want a word from God, probably through the ephod (17:5). The priest answers smoothly: "Go in peace. The journey on which you go is under the eye of the LORD." The words are pious and vague. "Under the eye of the LORD" can mean "he approves" or simply "he sees it," and the Hebrew is as open as the English. Like a fortune-teller's answer, it pleases the client without committing to anything.
- **Laish (v7).** Laish (called Leshem in Josh 19:47) lay at the far north, at the foot of Mount Hermon, beside one of the springs of the Jordan. Its people lived "after the manner of the Sidonians," that is, like the Phoenicians of the coast: traders, not warriors. They were "quiet and unsuspecting," wealthy, and isolated, "far from the Sidonians" and with "no dealings with anyone." Excavations at Tel Dan have uncovered a large earlier city with massive earth ramparts and a mud-brick gateway with an arch, one of the oldest arched gates known. By the Danites' day the place may have been a smaller, unwalled town.
- **"Behold, it is very good… God has given it into your hands" (vv9–10).** The report echoes Joshua and Caleb almost word for word: "The land… is an exceedingly good land" (Num 14:7), "the LORD has given all the land into our hands" (Josh 2:24). But the setting turns it upside down. The spies of Moses's day were sent into a land God had promised and a people under his judgment. These spies have found a soft target outside the land Dan was given, and they claim God's backing for it on the word of a hired priest.
- **600 armed men (v11).** The number is small for a whole tribe (Num 26:43 counted 64,400 Danite men), which suggests only part of Dan moved. Compare Gideon's 300 against a vast army (7:7). These 600 are going against a town that will not fight.
- **Mahaneh-dan, "west of Kiriath-jearim" (v12).** The name means "camp of Dan." Kiriath-jearim was a town on the border of Judah and Benjamin, where the ark later rested for twenty years (1 Sam 7:1–2). The name Mahaneh-dan also appears in 13:25, where it is placed between Zorah and Eshtaol. Either the name moved with the tribe, or there were two such camps.
- **"Consider what you will do" (v14).** The scouts' remark is a hint, not a question. They want the shrine, and the 600 at the gate make sure they will get it.
- **The theft (vv15–18).** The scene is staged carefully. The five greet the Levite and ask "about his welfare," literally "about his *shalom*," while the 600 armed men stand at the gate. The list of stolen objects is repeated in full, carved image, ephod, household gods, metal image (vv14, 17, 18, 20), in slightly different orders. The repetition slows the story down so the reader can watch Micah's religion being carried out of his house piece by piece.
- **"Put your hand on your mouth… be to us a father and a priest" (v19).** They borrow Micah's own words from 17:10. "Is it better for you to be priest to the house of one man, or… to a tribe and clan in Israel?" It is a promotion, and the Levite's answer comes before he speaks: **"the priest's heart was glad"** (v20). He picks up the gods himself. He had sold himself once to Micah for ten pieces of silver. He sells himself again for a bigger congregation.
- **"Putting the little ones and the livestock and the goods in front of them" (v21).** A tribal migration, families and all. The fighting men go at the rear because they expect pursuit.
- **"You take my gods that I made… and what have I left?" (v24).** This is the sharpest line in the chapter. Micah's gods are "my gods that I made," and they cannot stop a man from carrying them off. Isaiah later mocks the same thing: gods that must be carried, that cannot save themselves (Isa 46:1–2, 7). Micah began by stealing from his mother and ends being robbed by stronger thieves.
- **"Lest angry fellows fall upon you" (v25).** Literally "men bitter of soul," the same phrase used of Hannah's anguish (1 Sam 1:10) and of David's discontented men (1 Sam 22:2). Here it is a threat: these are dangerous men. Micah does the arithmetic and goes home (v26). Might decides everything.
- **"A people quiet and unsuspecting" (v27).** The phrase from v7 is repeated, and the effect is deliberate. The Danites strike them "with the edge of the sword" and burn the city, the language of holy war (Josh 6:21, 24). But no command of God stands behind this attack. Laish was not one of the Canaanite cities Israel was told to destroy, and Dan had a God-given land of its own still in Philistine hands.
- **"There was no deliverer" (v28).** All through Judges the LORD raised up deliverers for Israel. Here the word is used of the victims: Laish had no one to save it. The valley "that belongs to Beth-rehob" is the upper Jordan valley, near an Aramean territory to the northeast (Num 13:21; 2 Sam 10:6).
- **"They named the city Dan" (v29).** It became the northern limit of Israel, so that "from Dan to Beersheba" meant the whole nation (20:1; 1 Sam 3:20). Jacob had said of this tribe, "Dan shall be a serpent in the way, a viper by the path" (Gen 49:17). The Tyndale notes read this episode as that word fulfilled.
- **"Jonathan the son of Gershom, son of Moses" (v30).** Only here is the Levite named, and the name is a shock. Gershom was Moses's son (Exod 2:22), so "son" here means descendant, perhaps grandson. Moses's own family is running an idol shrine. In the Hebrew text a small letter *n* is written above the line in the name, turning "Moses" (*Mosheh*) into "Manasseh" (*Menasheh*). Jewish tradition explains this as a scribal change made out of respect for Moses, to spare his name the shame. Many old English versions followed it (KJV "Manasseh"). The ESV and most modern translations follow the reading without the raised letter, "Moses," which some Greek and Latin manuscripts also have.
- **"Until the day of the captivity of the land" (v30).** This most naturally means the Assyrian exile of the northern tribes in 722 BC (2 Kings 15:29; 17:6). Dan was also where Jeroboam later set up one of his golden calves (1 Kings 12:28–30), so the site stayed a center of false worship for centuries. Some scholars suggest a slightly different reading, "the captivity of the ark," which would point to the Philistines' capture of the ark (1 Sam 4) and match v31.
- **"As long as the house of God was at Shiloh" (v31).** The real house of God stood at Shiloh, a day's walk from Micah's house. Dan set up a rival. The contrast between Micah's "house of gods" (17:5) and the "house of God" closes the story.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the Danites' plunder of Micah's gods a small beginning that grew large: "how great a matter does a little fire kindle!" On Laish he writes, "Many are brought to destruction by their security." He discusses the raised letter in v30 and the rabbis' explanation, but he is cautious about it, following Bishop Patrick, who thought the priest might have come from another Levite family. JFB sees "low morality and strong religious feeling" side by side in the Danites, and blames Dan's original loss of land on "indolence, or a lack of energy." He also traces the saying "from Dan to Beer-sheba" to this renaming. Guzik notes the irony of a tribe willing to fight a long way off for land it did not need while neglecting the land God gave it. He presents the priest as someone driven by gain and status rather than loyalty, and points out that Micah's gods could not protect themselves.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everyone in this chapter treats God as a means to an end. The Danites want a blessing on their plans, the Levite wants a bigger post, and Micah wants his gods back. The idols that could be made, carried and stolen were worthless from the start. Jesus is the opposite of the hired priest: "The hired hand… sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees… I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11–13). And where Dan took what was not theirs by force, Christ gives what could never be taken: an inheritance "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven" (1 Pet 1:4).
