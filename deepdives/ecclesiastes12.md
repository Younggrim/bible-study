*Setting:* This is the last chapter of Ecclesiastes and the end of the Preacher's long search for what lasts "under the sun." Chapter 11 told the young to rejoice in their youth but to remember that "God will bring you into judgment," because "youth and the dawn of life are vanity" (11:9–10). Chapter 12 finishes that thought with one of the most beautiful poems in the Bible, a picture of growing old and dying, told as a great house falling into ruin (vv1–7). Then the book's opening words return, "Vanity of vanities" (v8). The last six verses (vv9–14) step back and speak *about* the Preacher in the third person, describing his work as a teacher and drawing the book's final conclusion: "Fear God and keep his commandments." Many scholars think an editor or student added this epilogue. Others think the author, writing in his role as "the Preacher," closes his own book this way. The Tyndale notes take the first view. Either way, the epilogue is part of the inspired book and gives the key for reading the whole. For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### Remember Your Creator in Your Youth (vv1–8)
- **"Remember also your Creator in the days of your youth" (v1).** "Also" ties this to 11:9–10: enjoy your youth, and also remember God in it. To "remember" in Hebrew is more than recalling a fact. It means to act on it, to keep someone in mind and live accordingly. When God "remembered" Noah, he acted to save him (Gen 8:1). This is the first time the book calls God "Creator." Since Genesis 1–2 the Bible has said that the one who made people has a claim on them. "Creator" is plural in form in the Hebrew. Most scholars take it as a "plural of majesty," like *Elohim*, the usual word for God. JFB reads it as a hint of the Trinity (Gen 1:26). The plural alone cannot prove that, though Christians have long seen the Creator's work as that of Father, Son and Spirit (John 1:3; Col 1:16).
- **"Before the evil days come and the years draw near of which you will say, 'I have no pleasure in them'" (v1).** The "evil days" are not wicked days but hard ones, the years of old age, when strength, health and pleasure fade. The word "before" opens the poem and will come three times (vv1, 2, 6). Remember God before it is too late to serve him with full strength.
- **"Before the sun and the light and the moon and the stars are darkened" (v2).** The poem begins with a darkening world. Light is fading, and "the clouds return after the rain." In Israel's dry season, clouds after rain meant clearing weather. In old age, troubles keep coming back, one storm after another, with no sunshine between. Some see the dimming of the mind or the senses. Others see it as death itself, or even the end of the world, since the prophets use the same images for the Day of the LORD (Isa 13:10; Joel 2:10).
- **"The keepers of the house tremble, and the strong men are bent" (v3).** From here the poem is usually read as a picture of the aging body as a great house. The old Jewish and Christian reading, followed by Guzik and JFB, matches each image to a body part. The "keepers of the house" (guards) are the arms and hands, now shaking. The "strong men" are the legs, now bowed. The Tyndale notes say the NLT spells this out. But the poem may also describe an actual household in decline, its servants weak and its doors closed, as a picture of death's approach. Many modern scholars think it works on both levels.
- **"The grinders cease because they are few, and those who look through the windows are dimmed" (v3).** Grinding grain was women's daily work in every house. Here the "grinders" are the teeth, fewer now, and chewing slows. "Those who look through the windows" are the eyes, growing dim. Isaac's eyes were too dim to see (Gen 27:1), and so were Eli's (1 Sam 3:2).
- **"The doors on the street are shut... the sound of the grinding is low" (v4).** The house is shut up, quieter, cut off from the street. This may be hearing going, so the sounds of the town fade. Or the lips closing over toothless gums. The "sound of the grinding" was the background noise of every village. When it falls silent, life is slowing.
- **"One rises up at the sound of a bird, and all the daughters of song are brought low" (v4).** The old wake early, at the first chirp of a bird. But "the daughters of song," the voice and singing, or the ear's ability to enjoy music, are faint. Barzillai, at eighty, told David he could no longer "hear the voice of singing men and singing women" (2 Sam 19:35).
- **"They are afraid also of what is high, and terrors are in the way" (v5).** Heights and steps become dangerous, and every walk outdoors carries the fear of a fall.
- **"The almond tree blossoms" (v5).** The almond tree blooms in late winter with pale pink blossoms that turn white, a picture of white hair. Its Hebrew name (*shaqed*) means "the wakeful one," because it is the first tree to bloom (Jer 1:11–12).
- **"The grasshopper drags itself along" (v5).** The grasshopper, normally quick and light, now drags itself. Some take this as the old person's stiff, slow walk. JFB pictures a shriveled, stooped old man.
- **"And desire fails" (v5).** The word here (*'abiyyonah*) appears only once in the Bible, and it is the caperberry. In the ancient Near East it was eaten to stir appetite and sexual desire. The Tyndale notes say so, and Guzik passes on Kidner's note to the same effect. When even the caperberry no longer works, appetite and desire are gone. That is why the ESV translates the sense rather than the word.
- **"Because man is going to his eternal home, and the mourners go about the streets" (v5).** "Eternal home" (*beit 'olamo*) is literally "his house of forever." Elsewhere in the ancient world the phrase was used of the grave. Guzik, with Deane, sees in it a hint of eternity, a glimpse beyond the grave. The mourners in the street may be the hired professional mourners waiting for the death (Jer 9:17; Amos 5:16), or simply the people already grieving.
- **"Before the silver cord is snapped, or the golden bowl is broken" (v6).** Four pictures of death, two of costly things and two of common ones. A golden lamp bowl hung on a silver chain: when the chain snaps, the bowl falls and the light goes out. A clay pitcher breaks at the spring, and the wheel used to lower it into the well breaks, so no more water can be drawn. Life is precious like gold and silver, and fragile like clay. JFB, following older interpreters, matched these to the spinal cord, skull, veins and heart. Most readers today take them as pictures rather than anatomy. Paul calls us "jars of clay" (2 Cor 4:7).
- **"The dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it" (v7).** This reverses creation. "The LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Gen 2:7). At death the dust goes back to the ground, as God said after the fall (Gen 3:19), and the spirit (*ruach*) goes back to God. Back in 3:21 the Preacher asked, "Who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward?" Now he says it does. The Tyndale notes say this suggests a belief in life after death. It does not yet show the full Christian hope of resurrection, but it points in that direction.
- **"Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher; all is vanity" (v8).** The poem, and the Preacher's own words, end where they began (1:2). *Hebel*, breath, vapor. Under the sun, everything passes, even life itself. The book's frame is closed. What comes next tells readers how to live inside that frame.
- **Commentators:** Henry says we should remember God "early, while the body is strong, and the spirits active," and adds that "if sin had not entered into the world, these infirmities would not have been known" (Rom 5:12). Guzik passes on Clarke's practical point: we belong to God, and he deserves our first and best years, not our leftovers. He insists the poem ends not in darkness but in an "eternal home." JFB, following Smith's anatomy, reads the silver cord as the spinal marrow and the golden bowl as the skull, "golden" because of "the vital preciousness of its contents," and takes v7 as plain teaching that the spirit survives the body.

#### Fear God and Keep His Commandments (vv9–14)
- **"Besides being wise, the Preacher also taught the people knowledge" (v9).** The voice changes. Someone speaks *about* the Preacher. He did not keep his wisdom to himself. He taught it. "Weighing and studying and arranging many proverbs with great care" describes careful craft: testing sayings, studying them and putting them in order. This fits Solomon, who "spoke 3,000 proverbs" (1 Kgs 4:32), and the men of Hezekiah who later "copied" more of them (Prov 25:1). JFB notes "weighed" is the literal sense.
- **"The Preacher sought to find words of delight, and uprightly he wrote words of truth" (v10).** Two aims: beauty and truth. He wanted words that were pleasing and well put, and also words that were honest and right. The epilogue vouches for him. However dark parts of his search have been, he wrote truthfully about what he saw.
- **"The words of the wise are like goads" (v11).** A goad was a long stick with a pointed metal tip, used to drive an ox forward when plowing (1 Sam 13:21). Jesus told Paul, "It is hard for you to kick against the goads" (Acts 26:14). Wise words prod and sting, and they push people in the right direction. This book does exactly that.
- **"Like nails firmly fixed are the collected sayings" (v11).** Where goads move, nails hold. Wise sayings fix truth in place in the memory. The "collected sayings" may mean the gathered proverbs, or "masters of collections," the teachers who gathered them. Guzik, via Kidner, sums it up: goads spur the will, and nails stick in the memory.
- **"They are given by one Shepherd" (v11).** The Shepherd is almost certainly God, the "Shepherd of Israel" (Ps 80:1; Ps 23:1; Isa 40:11). The words of the wise, though written by people, come from one divine source. This is the Old Testament's way of saying what 2 Timothy 3:16 says: "All Scripture is breathed out by God." The Tyndale notes also mention a second reading, that the wise teacher is like a shepherd guiding a flock. Henry: "The Shepherd of Israel is the Giver of inspired wisdom." JFB names him as "the Spirit of Jesus Christ," and Christians rightly hear the Good Shepherd in it (John 10:11).
- **"My son, beware of anything beyond these" (v12).** "My son" is the voice of a teacher to a student, as in Proverbs 1–7. "Beyond these" means beyond the words of the wise given by the one Shepherd. Be careful with what goes past them.
- **"Of making many books there is no end, and much study is a weariness of the flesh" (v12).** This is not a rejection of learning. The Preacher himself studied hard. It is a warning that endless reading and research, apart from the fear of God, wears a person out and never arrives. Guzik passes on Kidner's warning that research can become an addiction. JFB says much study "wearies the body, without solidly profiting the soul." Paul warned of those "always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (2 Tim 3:7).
- **"The end of the matter; all has been heard" (v13).** Here is the conclusion of the whole book.
- **"Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man" (v13).** To "fear God" is not terror but reverent awe that shapes all of life. It is "the beginning of wisdom" (Prov 9:10). The Preacher has spoken of it before (3:14; 5:7; 7:18; 8:12–13), but only here does he add "keep his commandments," and this is the book's only mention of God's commandments. "The whole duty of man" is literally "this is the whole of man" (*zeh kol ha'adam*). The word "duty" is supplied. Guzik, with Eaton, reads it as "this is what every person is for," and JFB as "the whole man," the full ideal of humanity. It is not one duty among many. It is what being human means.
- **"For God will bring every deed into judgment, with every secret thing, whether good or evil" (v14).** This is why life is not finally *hebel*. If God judges every deed, then every deed matters. Nothing is meaningless. The Preacher has said it before (3:17; 11:9), and here it is his last word. The New Testament says the same: "We must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ" (2 Cor 5:10), and "God judges the secrets of men by Christ Jesus" (Rom 2:16). Guzik passes on Kidner's thought that a God who cares this much about each deed means no act is pointless. In Jewish synagogues, when this book is read aloud, v13 is repeated after v14 so that the reading does not end on judgment.
- **Commentators:** Henry asks, "Why does God record in his word, that ALL IS VANITY, but to keep us from deceiving ourselves to our ruin?" and paraphrases v13: "this is all that concerns man." Guzik passes on Eaton's note that this is the only place the book speaks of God's commandments, and Kidner's line that the fear of God puts everything else in its place. JFB reads v14 as the test that shows what is vanity and what is solid: future judgment.

#### Pointing to Christ
The book ends with three things that point beyond itself. First, death: dust returning to the earth and spirit to God, with only a hint of what lies beyond. Christ shone light into that darkness. He "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10), and he promises that the dust will rise. Second, the "one Shepherd" who gives the words of the wise. Jesus says, "I am the good shepherd" (John 10:11), and in him "are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3). Third, judgment. The Father "has given all judgment to the Son" (John 5:22). For those who trust him, that judgment has already fallen on him at the cross. The Preacher's last word is the fear of God and his commandments. The gospel does not cancel that call. It makes it possible, because the one who kept God's commandments perfectly gives his righteousness to those who trust him. Under the sun, all is vanity. In the Son, "your labor is not in vain" (1 Cor 15:58).
