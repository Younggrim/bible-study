*Setting:* "A Maskil of Asaph." A *maskil* is a psalm meant to teach or make wise, and this is the longest one, the second-longest psalm after 119. It is history set to song: Israel's story from the exodus to King David, retold so that children would learn it and not repeat their parents' failures. It ends with David on the throne and Zion chosen, and it says nothing about the split of the kingdom after Solomon (930 BC), so many scholars date it to the time of David or Solomon, when Asaph's guild led worship. Others place it later, in Hezekiah's reign, as a warning to Judah after the northern kingdom (often called "Ephraim") fell in 722 BC. The psalm is not in strict time order. It tells the wilderness story (vv12–39), then steps back to the plagues of Egypt (vv40–55), then moves on to the days of the judges and the loss of Shiloh (vv56–64), and finally to God's choice of Judah and David (vv65–72). Again and again the same pattern repeats: God acts in grace, the people forget and rebel, God judges, and yet he does not destroy them. Psalm 77 ended with God leading his flock by Moses and Aaron. Psalm 78 ends with God giving that flock a shepherd king.

#### Tell the Coming Generation (vv1–8)
- **"Give ear, O my people, to my teaching" (v1).** "Teaching" is *torah*, instruction. The singer speaks like a teacher or a prophet calling the nation to listen (49:1–4; Deut 32:1). Henry notes that the history is "put into a psalm or song that it might be the better remembered."
- **"I will open my mouth in a parable; I will utter dark sayings from of old" (v2).** "Parable" is *mashal*, a saying that compares one thing to another to teach a lesson. "Dark sayings" (*chidot*) are riddles or puzzles. Israel's history is not obscure, but its meaning has to be grasped: why did the people keep failing, and why did God keep his promises anyway? JFB: "its right apprehension required wisdom and attention." Matthew quotes this verse to explain why Jesus taught in parables: "I will utter what has been hidden since the foundation of the world" (Matt 13:34–35). Jesus did what Asaph did, using stories to show how God's kingdom works, and in him the hidden meaning of Israel's history comes into the open.
- **"We will not hide them from their children" (v4).** The point of the psalm is handing on the faith. What must be passed on is "the glorious deeds of the LORD, and his might, and the wonders that he has done," not just rules but God's acts.
- **"He established a testimony in Jacob… which he commanded our fathers to teach to their children" (v5).** This is Moses' charge: "You shall teach them diligently to your children" (Deut 6:6–7, 20–25). Four generations appear in vv5–6: the fathers, their children, the next generation, and "the children yet unborn."
- **Three purposes (v7).** "That they should set their hope in God and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments." Hope, memory and obedience belong together. Forgetting what God has done is the root of the disobedience the rest of the psalm describes.
- **"Not be like their fathers, a stubborn and rebellious generation" (v8).** The phrase comes from the law about a son who will not listen (Deut 21:18). "Whose heart was not steadfast" means not fixed or prepared. The word comes back in v37. The psalm is honest: the ancestors are not heroes but warnings.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Kidner, stresses that Scripture leaves no room for parents to stay neutral about passing on faith. He quotes Spurgeon's point that no minister can replace a parent's prayers and teaching. Henry agrees that the aim is to keep the next generation from being "a stubborn and rebellious generation" like their fathers.

#### Ephraim Forgot His Works (vv9–16)
- **"The Ephraimites, armed with the bow, turned back on the day of battle" (v9).** Ephraim, the son of Joseph, became the leading northern tribe and the site of Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood. No battle in the record fits exactly. Some think of Ephraim's failure to drive out the Canaanites (Judg 1:29), others of the defeat at Aphek when the ark was lost (1 Sam 4). Many take it as a picture: a well-armed tribe that lost its nerve because it lost its faith. The next verses give the cause. JFB calls Ephraim "a strong, well-armed tribe, and, from an early period, emulous and haughty" (Judg 8:1; 12:1).
- **"They forgot his works" (v11).** Forgetting is the theme of the whole psalm (vv7, 11, 42). Henry: "Weapons of war stand men in little stead without a martial spirit, and that is gone if God be gone."
- **"In the fields of Zoan" (v12).** Zoan, called Tanis by the Greeks, was an important city in the northeastern Nile delta, near the land of Goshen where Israel lived (Num 13:22). It stands for the whole region where the plagues fell.
- **"Made the waters stand like a heap" (v13).** The words come straight from the Song of the Sea: "the floods stood up in a heap" (Exod 15:8). **"A cloud… a fiery light" (v14)** is the pillar that guided them (Exod 13:21–22).
- **"He split rocks in the wilderness" (vv15–16).** Water came from the rock at Rephidim at the start of the journey (Exod 17:6) and at Kadesh near the end (Num 20:11). "As from the deep" means as abundantly as from the great waters under the earth. Paul later says "the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4).
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads v9 spiritually and applies it to the armor of God (Eph 6:10–18): God provides what his people need for the fight, but they can still turn back by not using it. He notes that Boice found no historical battle that fits. The Tyndale notes recall that Ephraim received a special blessing from Jacob (Gen 48:15–20), which makes its failure sadder.

#### Can God Spread a Table in the Wilderness? (vv17–31)
- **"Yet they sinned still more against him" (v17).** The water from the rock should have built trust. Instead they wanted more. **"They tested God in their heart" (v18)** means they put him on trial, demanding he prove himself on their terms. Jesus refused to do the same: "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test" (Matt 4:7; Deut 6:16).
- **"Can God spread a table in the wilderness?" (v19).** The question sounds reasonable, but it is mockery. It sets limits on God. Psalm 23 answers it: "You prepare a table before me" (23:5). Henry: "Those that set bounds to God's power speak against him."
- **"Because they did not believe in God and did not trust his saving power" (v22).** This is what roused God's anger: not hunger, but unbelief. The story behind it is Numbers 11, when the people wailed for meat and "fire of the LORD burned among them" at Taberah (Num 11:1–3).
- **"He… opened the doors of heaven, and he rained down on them manna" (vv23–24).** "Doors" or "windows" of heaven usually bring rain (Gen 7:11; Mal 3:10). Here they bring bread. **"The grain of heaven."** In John 6:31 the crowd quotes this verse to Jesus to demand a sign, and he answers that he himself is "the true bread from heaven" (John 6:32–35).
- **"Man ate of the bread of the angels" (v25).** The Hebrew is literally "bread of the mighty ones" (*'abbirim*). The Greek Old Testament and most translations take the "mighty ones" as angels, so the phrase means heavenly food. JFB has "bread of the mighty," and the Wisdom of Solomon, an intertestamental Jewish book, also calls manna "angels' food." Henry: "such as angels, if they had occasion for food, would eat and be thankful for."
- **Quail (vv26–29).** God sent an east wind and a south wind, and quail fell around the camp "like dust… like the sand of the seas" (Num 11:31–32). Migrating quail still cross Sinai in spring and autumn, and come down exhausted. God used the natural order, but the timing and the scale were his.
- **"While the food was still in their mouths, the anger of God rose against them" (vv30–31).** Numbers says, "While the meat was yet between their teeth… the LORD struck down the people with a very great plague." The place was named Kibroth-hattaavah, "graves of craving" (Num 11:33–34). Psalm 106:15 draws the lesson: "he gave them what they asked, but sent a wasting disease among them." "The strongest of them" means the sturdiest men, the ones who seemed safest.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees two sins in v19: discontent with what God had given, and doubt about what God could give. He tells how the evangelist J. Edwin Orr took v19 as a promise during a faith journey in the 1930s. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says unbelief provokes God more than many sins that look worse.

#### Flattery and Compassion (vv32–39)
- **"In spite of all this, they still sinned" (v32).** Neither kindness (manna) nor judgment (the plague) changed them. **"So he made their days vanish like a breath" (v33).** "Breath" is *hevel*, the word Ecclesiastes uses for "vanity." The whole generation died in the wilderness without seeing the land (Num 14:22–35), and Psalm 90, the prayer of Moses, mourns exactly this.
- **"When he killed them, they sought him… They remembered that God was their rock" (vv34–35).** They said the right words and believed the right doctrine. "Redeemer" is *go'el*, the family member who buys a relative back (77:15).
- **"But they flattered him with their mouths; they lied to him with their tongues" (v36).** Their repentance lasted as long as the danger. Isaiah says the same of his generation, and Jesus quotes it: "this people draw near with their mouth… while their hearts are far from me" (Isa 29:13; Matt 15:8). **"Their heart was not steadfast" (v37)** echoes v8.
- **"Yet he, being compassionate, atoned for their iniquity and did not destroy them" (v38).** This is the center of the psalm. "Compassionate" (*rachum*) is God's own name from Exodus 34:6. "Atoned" is *kipper*, the word used for the sacrifices of the Day of Atonement. It means God covered their sin and turned his wrath away. "He restrained his anger often" — the record shows this again and again, often through Moses' prayers (Exod 32:11–14; Num 14:13–20). Henry: "the true reason is, He is full of compassion."
- **"He remembered that they were but flesh, a wind that passes and comes not again" (v39).** The people forgot God (v11), but God remembered them, and remembered how weak they were. Psalm 103:14 says the same: "he remembers that we are dust."
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Trapp, compares their repentance to iron that softens in the fire and hardens again once it cools. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, suggests that Moses' intercession lies behind God's restraint. JFB sums up the section: God "in pity, withdrew His hand for a time, yet their general conduct was rebellious." The Tyndale notes observe that they had the right doctrine but did not let God change their hearts.

#### The Plagues in Egypt (vv40–55)
- **"How often they rebelled… and grieved him in the desert!" (v40).** "Grieved" means caused him pain. God is not unmoved by his people's sin (Isa 63:10; Eph 4:30, "do not grieve the Holy Spirit").
- **"They tested God again and again and provoked the Holy One of Israel" (v41).** The KJV has "limited the Holy One of Israel," a famous phrase. The Hebrew verb is rare, and most modern lexicons and translations give "pained" or "provoked." So the verse is mainly about hurting God, not restricting him, though unbelief does shut people out of what God offers (Matt 13:58). JFB links the testing to the retreat at Kadesh (Deut 1:19–33). "The Holy One of Israel" is Isaiah's favorite name for God.
- **"They did not remember his power or the day when he redeemed them" (v42).** So the psalm goes back and remembers for them. Seven of the ten plagues are retold (vv44–51), in a different order from Exodus, and with poetic freedom, as Psalm 105:26–36 also does.
- **The plagues (vv44–48).** Rivers turned to blood (Exod 7:20). Swarms of flies (Exod 8:24). Frogs (Exod 8:6). Locusts (Exod 10:13–15). Hail that struck vines and sycamore figs, a key crop in Egypt (Exod 9:25). "Frost" may be hailstones or freezing rain. Cattle given over to hail and "thunderbolts," lightning (Exod 9:23). Many readers see each plague as a judgment on Egypt's gods: the Nile, the frog-headed goddess Heqet, the sky and the land (Exod 12:12, "on all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgments").
- **"A company of destroying angels" (v49).** Literally "a sending of messengers of evils," that is, angels who bring disaster. They are not fallen angels but God's agents of judgment, like "the destroyer" of the Passover night (Exod 12:23). JFB: "many were perhaps employed, and other evils inflicted."
- **"He struck down every firstborn in Egypt… in the tents of Ham" (v51).** Ham, son of Noah, was the ancestor of Egypt (Gen 10:6), so "Ham" is a poetic name for Egypt (105:23). "The firstfruits of their strength" is from Jacob's blessing of Reuben (Gen 49:3).
- **"He led out his people like sheep… the sea overwhelmed their enemies" (vv52–53).** God is the shepherd (77:20). **"To his holy land, to the mountain which his right hand had won" (v54).** The mountain may mean the hill country of Canaan, or Zion itself (Exod 15:17). **"He drove out nations before them"** and gave each tribe its land (v55; Josh 13–21).
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes the KJV's "limited" as a real warning that unbelief hinders God's work, though he notes Kidner's view that "provoked" is the better translation, which matches the lexicons. Henry keeps "limited the Holy One of Israel." Guzik also points to the resurrection as the greatest display of the power Israel forgot (Eph 1:19–20).

#### Shiloh Forsaken (vv56–66)
- **"Yet they tested and rebelled against the Most High God" (v56).** Settled in the land, the next generations did no better. This is the time of the judges, when "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 17:6; 2:11–13).
- **"They twisted like a deceitful bow" (v57).** A bow that is warped or slack looks fine until you shoot it, and then the arrow goes wild. Hosea uses the same picture of Ephraim (Hos 7:16). Henry: "like a deceitful bow, which seemed likely to send the arrow to the mark." JFB: "which turns back, and so fails to project the arrow."
- **"With their high places… with their idols" (v58).** High places were hilltop shrines, often to Canaanite gods. "Jealousy" in God is not petty envy. It is a husband's right love for his wife, refusing to share her (Exod 20:5; Deut 32:16, 21).
- **"He forsook his dwelling at Shiloh, the tent where he dwelt among mankind" (v60).** Shiloh, in the hill country of Ephraim, held the tabernacle from Joshua's day (Josh 18:1). In about 1050 BC Israel took the ark into battle against the Philistines at Aphek as if it were a lucky charm. The ark was captured, Eli's sons the priests died, and Eli fell dead at the news (1 Sam 4). Jeremiah later warned Jerusalem: "Go now to my place that was in Shiloh… and see what I did to it" (Jer 7:12–14). Excavations at Shiloh show the site was destroyed around this time. Henry: "he justly forsook it, and then all its glory departed."
- **"Delivered his power to captivity, his glory to the hand of the foe" (v61).** "His power" and "his glory" mean the ark (132:8). When the news came, Phinehas's wife named her son Ichabod, "the glory has departed from Israel" (1 Sam 4:21–22).
- **"Their young women had no marriage song… their widows made no lamentation" (vv63–64).** So many young men died that there were no weddings, and the slaughter was so great that widows could not hold proper mourning. "Their priests fell by the sword" refers to Hophni and Phinehas (1 Sam 4:11).
- **"Then the Lord awoke as from sleep, like a strong man shouting because of wine" (v65).** A bold image. God had seemed asleep while his people suffered (44:23). Now he rises like a warrior roused, shouting with energy. It is not about drunkenness but about a strong man stirred to action. Isaiah uses a similar picture: "The LORD goes out like a mighty man… he cries out, he shouts aloud" (Isa 42:13).
- **"He put his adversaries to rout; he put them to everlasting shame" (v66).** The KJV reads "smote his enemies in the hinder parts," which many link to the tumors God sent on the Philistines while they held the ark (1 Sam 5:6–12). The Hebrew can mean "drove them back." Either way, the ark came home without Israel lifting a sword (1 Sam 6), and David later crushed the Philistines (2 Sam 5:17–25).
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Kidner, notes the irony that the people God used to judge Canaan's idolatry became idolaters themselves. He stresses that the glory lost at Shiloh was not the ark as an object but God's presence with an obedient people. Henry keeps the KJV's "then the Lord awaked as one out of sleep." JFB on v65 points to Isaiah 42:13.

#### He Chose David His Servant (vv67–72)
- **"He rejected the tent of Joseph; he did not choose the tribe of Ephraim, but he chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion, which he loves" (vv67–68).** God moved the center of worship from Shiloh, in Ephraim's territory, to Jerusalem in Judah. This is not a rejection of Ephraim's people from salvation (the Tyndale notes stress this) but of Ephraim's leadership. Jacob had already promised that "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10). Henry: "the rejection of Shiloh is the election of Zion." For northern listeners after the kingdom split, this was a pointed reminder of where God had placed his name.
- **"He built his sanctuary like the high heavens, like the earth, which he has founded forever" (v69).** The temple on Zion was meant to be as lasting as heaven and earth. JFB: "Exalted as" the heights and "abiding as—the earth."
- **"He chose David his servant and took him from the sheepfolds" (v70).** David was the youngest son, out with the sheep when Samuel came (1 Sam 16:11). God said, "I took you from the pasture, from following the sheep, that you should be prince over my people Israel" (2 Sam 7:8). Henry: "He was taken from the sheep-folds, as Moses was" (Exod 3:1).
- **"From following the nursing ewes he brought him to shepherd Jacob his people" (v71).** A shepherd who had walked slowly behind ewes with lambs learned patience and care. That was his training for kingship. In the ancient Near East kings were often called shepherds of their people.
- **"With upright heart he shepherded them and guided them with his skillful hand" (v72).** "Upright heart" means integrity, a whole heart. "Skillful hand" means understanding, wise skill. David was far from sinless, but the psalm ends the long story of unsteady hearts (vv8, 37) with a shepherd whose heart was whole toward God (1 Kgs 9:4). Henry: "according to the integrity of his heart."
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes, citing Boice, that God always makes new beginnings: after Shiloh, Zion; after failure, a new shepherd. He adds that Jesus, David's greater Son, is the shepherd this ending points toward. Kidner, cited by Guzik, says the hope of the whole story is God's persistent goodness, not Israel's improvement.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 78 is a long record of forgetting, but it ends with a choice of grace: a mountain God loves and a shepherd king with an upright heart. Matthew tells us its opening words were fulfilled in Jesus' parables (Matt 13:35), and its end is fulfilled in him too. He is the true bread from heaven that the wilderness generation ate and still died (John 6:31–35, 49–51). He is the Rock that followed them (1 Cor 10:4). God's compassion that "atoned for their iniquity" (v38) found its full expression in the cross, where atonement was made once for all. And he is the Son of David, the Good Shepherd, whose heart was wholly upright and who lays down his life for the sheep (Ezek 34:23; John 10:11). The psalm warns each generation not to be like their fathers, and it gives the reason to hope that they need not be: God has chosen a shepherd who will not fail.
