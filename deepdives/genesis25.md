*Setting:* This chapter is a hinge in Genesis. Sarah has died at 127 and been buried in the cave of Machpelah, the one piece of Canaan Abraham owned (ch 23). Abraham's servant has brought Rebekah from Abraham's relatives in Mesopotamia, and Isaac, living in the Negeb, has married her and "was comforted after his mother's death" (24:67). Abraham is about 140. Chapter 25 closes his story, gives a short account of Ishmael's family, and then opens a new section: "These are the generations of Isaac" (v19). In a few pages it covers about seventy-five years, from Abraham's last marriage to Jacob and Esau as grown men. Genesis has done this before: the line that is not chosen is listed briefly and set aside, and then the story follows the chosen line in detail (compare Cain in 4:17–24).

#### Abraham's Death and His Descendants (vv1–18)
- **"Abraham took another wife, whose name was Keturah" (v1).** The text doesn't say when. The Tyndale notes say probably, though not necessarily, after Sarah's death. JFB translates "had taken," placing it earlier. Verse 6 counts her among Abraham's "concubines," and 1 Chronicles 1:32 calls her "Abraham's concubine": a real wife, but of secondary rank, whose sons had no claim on the inheritance. If the marriage did come after Sarah died, Abraham fathered six sons past the age of 137, long after his body was "as good as dead" (Rom 4:19).
- **The sons of Keturah (vv2–4).** Most of the sixteen names are peoples of the Arabian desert. *Midian* matters most later. Midianite traders bought Joseph (37:28), Moses found refuge and a wife among the Midianites (Exod 2:15–21), and Midian oppressed Israel in Gideon's day (Judg 6). *Sheba* and *Dedan* were trading peoples (Ezek 27:20–22). The same names appear under Cush in 10:7, so these were probably regions where several family lines mixed. The Tyndale notes see these peoples as a partial fulfillment of the promise that Abraham would be "the father of a multitude of nations" (17:4).
- **"Abraham gave all he had to Isaac… to the sons of his concubines Abraham gave gifts" (vv5–6).** Abraham settles his estate while he is alive. The other sons are provided for and sent "eastward," as Ishmael had been sent away (21:14). The line of promise is kept clear. In Genesis, going east often means moving away from the place of blessing (3:24; 4:16; 13:11).
- **"175 years" (v7).** Abraham came to Canaan at 75 (12:4), so he lived a hundred years as a sojourner in the land God promised him and owned only a grave there. By the chapter's own numbers he lived to see Jacob and Esau turn fifteen (21:5; 25:26). His death is told here, before their birth, so that his story is finished before Isaac's begins.
- **"In a good old age, an old man and full of years" (v8).** God had promised exactly this: "you shall be buried in a good old age" (15:15). "Full" means satisfied, as after a meal. His life was complete.
- **"Gathered to his people" (v8).** The verb is *asaph*, "gather." This is not the same as burial. Abraham is gathered to his people in v8 and buried in v9, and his ancestors' graves were far away in Ur and Haran. The phrase is used of Ishmael (v17), Isaac (35:29), Jacob (49:33), Aaron and Moses (Num 20:24; Deut 32:50). Many readers see in it an early hint that the dead still exist and are joined to those who went before. Jesus spoke of the patriarchs as alive to God: "He is not God of the dead, but of the living" (Matt 22:32).
- **"Isaac and Ishmael his sons buried him" (v9).** The two half-brothers, separated since Ishmael and Hagar were sent away, stand together at their father's grave. The text says nothing more about their relationship. Isaac, the heir, is named first. They lay Abraham beside Sarah in the cave he bought (ch 23). Isaac, Rebekah, Leah and Jacob would be buried there too (49:29–32; 50:13).
- **"After the death of Abraham, God blessed Isaac his son" (v11).** The blessing passes to the next generation. Isaac lives at Beer-lahai-roi, "the well of the Living One who sees me," where God met Hagar (16:13–14).
- **"These are the generations of Ishmael" (v12).** "Generations" is *toledot*, the word that marks the main sections of Genesis (2:4; 6:9; 11:27). Ishmael gets his own section. He does not carry the covenant, but he is not forgotten.
- **"Twelve princes according to their tribes" (vv13–16).** God had said of Ishmael, "He shall father twelve princes, and I will make him into a great nation" (17:20). Here is that promise kept, before Jacob's twelve sons are born. Several names reappear later. *Nebaioth* and *Kedar* were herding peoples (Isa 60:7), and "the tents of Kedar" became a byword (Ps 120:5; Song 1:5). *Tema* was an oasis town in northwest Arabia (Isa 21:14), *Dumah* gets a short oracle (Isa 21:11), and *Jetur* is probably the ancestor of the Itureans of Luke 3:1.
- **"137 years… gathered to his people" (v17).** The ESV puts this verse in parentheses. Ishmael's death is told in the same words as Abraham's.
- **"From Havilah to Shur… He settled over against all his kinsmen" (v18).** Shur was the desert between southern Canaan and Egypt, where Hagar had fled (16:7). Havilah was probably in north-central Arabia. The ESV footnote says the Hebrew verb is "fell," which could mean his lot fell there, he settled there, or he died there. "Over against all his kinsmen" repeats what the angel told Hagar before Ishmael was born (16:12). The phrase can mean "alongside" or "in defiance of," and the Tyndale notes take it as hostility. Either way, the word spoken to Hagar came true.
- **What the lists are for.** These verses are easy to skim, and that is partly the point. They show God keeping every word, even the promises to sons who would not carry the covenant line. They also tell Israel who its neighbors were: the desert peoples to the south and east were relatives, descended from Abraham. Then the story moves on.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks Keturah was "probably the chief of his maid-servants." On the gifts, he calls it "justice to provide for them" and "prudence to settle them in places distant from Isaac." On v8 he writes simply, "Death gathers us to our people." JFB, on the brothers at the grave: "Death often puts an end to strife, reconciles those who have been alienated." JFB also notices what is missing: "We might have wished some memorials of his deathbed experience; but the Spirit of God has withheld them." Guzik notes that only Moses is mentioned more often than Abraham in the New Testament, and stresses that God's work did not end with so great a man but passed on to Isaac. On Ishmael, Henry observes that "many that are strangers to the covenants of promise are yet blessed with outward prosperity." JFB covers Ishmael's line in one section note and has no comment on vv2–4, 8, 11 or 13–17.

#### The Birth of Esau and Jacob (vv19–28)
- **"These are the generations of Isaac" (v19).** The longest *toledot* section in Genesis runs from here to Isaac's death (35:29). Isaac is mostly in the background. The real subject is Jacob.
- **"Bethuel the Aramean of Paddan-aram" (v20).** Paddan-aram is the region around Haran in northern Mesopotamia, where Abraham's relatives stayed. Israel would later confess, "A wandering Aramean was my father" (Deut 26:5).
- **"Isaac prayed to the LORD for his wife, because she was barren" (v21).** Like Sarah, Rebekah can't have children, and the promise again depends on what only God can do. Isaac married at 40 and became a father at 60 (v26), so this was twenty years of waiting. "Prayed" is *athar*, a word for earnest pleading, always addressed to God. The same verb in passive form gives the answer: "the LORD granted his prayer," literally "let himself be entreated." Abraham once tried to solve Sarah's barrenness through Hagar. Isaac prays. Barren women whose sons come from God form a pattern through Scripture: Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel (30:22), Hannah (1 Sam 1), and Elizabeth (Luke 1:7).
- **"The children struggled together within her" (v22).** The verb is *ratsats*, which the lexicon defines as "crush" or "oppress," much stronger than "struggle." Hosea looks back on it: "In the womb he took his brother by the heel" (Hos 12:3).
- **"Why is this happening to me?" (v22).** The Hebrew is clipped, roughly "If so, why this, I?" The ESV footnote offers "why do I live?" In her distress Rebekah "went to inquire of the LORD." The verb *darash*, "seek, consult," is the usual word for seeking God's guidance, often through a prophet (1 Sam 9:9). The text doesn't say where she went. The Tyndale notes suggest a prophet, perhaps Abraham himself, still alive and called a prophet in 20:7. Others think of prayer at an altar. The text says only that "the LORD said to her."
- **"Two nations are in your womb" (v23).** The answer is a four-line poem. The twins are the ancestors of Israel and Edom, whose rivalry runs through the Old Testament: Edom refused Israel passage (Num 20:14–21), David subdued Edom (2 Sam 8:14), Edom later broke free (2 Kgs 8:20–22), and Obadiah is a whole book against Edom. The Herods of the Gospels were Idumeans, of Edomite descent.
- **"The older shall serve the younger" (v23).** This overturns the normal order, where the firstborn led the family. Genesis keeps choosing the younger: Abel, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, and Ephraim over Manasseh (48:19).
- **How Paul uses this verse.** In Romans 9:10–13 Paul quotes v23 with Malachi 1:2–3 ("Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated"). His point is that God chose when the twins "were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad… not because of works but because of him who calls." Christians read this in different ways. Reformed interpreters generally see God's choice of individuals for salvation, with Jacob and Esau as examples. Many Arminian and Wesleyan readers stress that the oracle names "two nations" and that Malachi speaks of Israel and Edom, and they see God choosing a people for a role in his plan rather than settling one man's eternal destiny. Both agree on what Paul denies: the choice was not earned.
- **"Red, all his body like a hairy cloak" (v25).** Two wordplays introduce Esau. "Red" is *admoni*, which sounds like Edom (v30). "Hairy" is *se'ar*, which sounds like Seir, the mountain country of Edom (36:8). The newborn already looks like the nation he will father. The same word *admoni* describes David as "ruddy" (1 Sam 16:12), so red is not an insult in itself.
- **"His hand holding Esau's heel, so his name was called Jacob" (v26).** "Heel" is *aqeb*, and Jacob is *Ya'aqob*. The ESV footnote gives both senses: "He takes by the heel," or "He cheats." Esau will later make the bitter pun himself: "Is he not rightly named Jacob? For he has cheated me these two times" (27:36). Similar names are known elsewhere in the ancient Near East, and many scholars think the name was first short for a prayer like "May God protect." The Tyndale notes take this view, saying the name took on its darker meaning from Jacob's grasping character.
- **"A skillful hunter, a man of the field… a quiet man, dwelling in tents" (v27).** Esau is an outdoorsman. Jacob stays among the tents and flocks, the life of Abraham and Isaac. "Quiet" is *tam*, older versions "plain." The same word calls Job "blameless" (Job 1:1), but the lexicon puts this verse under a softer sense, "sound" or settled. It describes Jacob's temperament and way of life, not his moral character, as the next scene makes plain.
- **"Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, but Rebekah loved Jacob" (v28).** Literally, "because game was in his mouth." Isaac's love comes with a reason, and the reason is his appetite. No reason is given for Rebekah's, though she may have had the oracle in mind. Each parent has a favorite, and the split will tear the family apart in chapter 27. Jacob will repeat it with Joseph (37:3).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that even the promised son received children only through long waiting and prayer. Henry: "It is a great relief to the mind to spread our case before the Lord, and ask counsel at his mouth." Henry also applies the struggle in the womb to the believer's inner conflict with sin: "A holy war is better than the peace of the devil's palace." That is an application, not the text's own meaning. Guzik takes the Romans 9 reading, recalling Spurgeon's reply that his difficulty was not why God hated Esau but why he loved Jacob. Guzik reads "hated" as not chosen for the covenant role, and notes that Esau prospered (33:4–9). Guzik says *tam* means "wholeness" rather than weakness. The lexicon supports "complete, sound," but not a moral wholeness in this verse. Henry does read it morally, as "an honest man that always meant well." On v28, JFB warns that "the distinction made between the children led, as such conduct always does, to unhappy consequences." JFB has no comment on vv20 or 22–26.

#### Esau Sells His Birthright (vv29–34)
- **"Jacob was cooking stew" (v29).** "Cooking" is from *zud*, "boil, seethe." The lexicon shows the same verb elsewhere means "act presumptuously" (Exod 21:14; Deut 18:20). The Tyndale notes hear a wordplay: Jacob is boiling over in grasping at the blessing. Whether the author meant the double sense is a judgment call. The irony is plain either way. The hunter comes home empty, and the man at home has the food.
- **"Let me eat some of that red stew" (v30).** "Let me eat" is *laat*, found only here in the Bible. The lexicon gives "swallow greedily," and later Hebrew used it for stuffing cattle. Esau doesn't even name the dish: literally "the red, this red stuff." The nickname Edom, "red," stuck to him and his descendants.
- **"Sell me your birthright now" (v31).** The birthright, *bekorah*, was the firstborn's right. The law later gives the firstborn a "double portion" (Deut 21:17), and with it went leadership of the family. 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 shows a birthright could be lost or transferred. In this family it also carried God's promises to Abraham. Scholars often cite a tablet from Nuzi in Mesopotamia in which a man sells inheritance rights to his brother for three sheep, though how close the parallel is remains debated. Jacob doesn't feed his brother as a brother. He names a price.
- **"I am about to die" (v32).** Esau probably exaggerates. He eats, drinks and walks away, with no sign of real danger. The words may also mean "I'll die someday anyway." Either way he weighs a future blessing against a present hunger and chooses the hunger.
- **"Swear to me now" (v33).** Jacob makes it binding with an oath. He grabs at what God had already promised him (v23), by his own scheming and at his brother's weak moment. The narrator doesn't praise him. It will take years and a night of wrestling at the Jabbok before Jacob learns to receive the blessing rather than seize it (32:24–30).
- **"He ate and drank and rose and went his way" (v34).** Four quick verbs, with no word, no second thought, no regret. Then the verdict: "Thus Esau despised his birthright." The verb is *bazah*, "regard with contempt," the same word for those who "despised the word of the LORD" (Num 15:31). The narrator puts the weight of blame on Esau. Jacob was grasping, but Esau treated a gift from God as worth less than a bowl of stew. Hebrews draws the lesson: let no one be "unholy like Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal" (Heb 12:16). "Unholy" (KJV "profane") means treating sacred things as common.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the birthright "a spiritual privilege," "Esau's by providence but Jacob's by promise." He is hard on Esau ("Never was there such a foolish bargain as this which Esau now made") and gentle with Jacob, even praising him: "Those are wise indeed that are wise for another world." The Tyndale notes are much harder on Jacob, calling the bargain ruthless and presumptuous. Guzik stands between them: Jacob schemed for what God had already promised, but Esau bears the greater blame. He passes on Luther's point, by way of Leupold, that the deal was hollow on both sides, since Jacob bought what was already his and Esau sold what was not his to sell. On "I am about to die," JFB reads a hunter's daily risk to his life, while Guzik takes it as "I'll die someday anyway." JFB quotes Bishop Hall: "There was never any meat, except the forbidden fruit, so dear bought, as this broth of Jacob." Guzik turns to the believer's birthright in Christ (Eph 1:3–14) and warns against trading it for passing pleasures. JFB has no comment on vv33–34.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter shows God's choosing grace before it shows anyone deserving it. The promise passes to Isaac rather than to Keturah's sons or Ishmael, and then, before the twins are born, to Jacob, a grasping younger brother who looks nothing like a hero. Paul saw the gospel in that: God's purpose stands "not because of works but because of him who calls" (Rom 9:11). The child of promise comes again through barrenness and prayer, as the promised Son would at last come through a virgin's womb. And Esau's bargain shows by contrast what the gospel gives. Esau traded a lasting inheritance for one meal. In Christ, believers receive "an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading" (1 Pet 1:4) and are counted among "the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven" (Heb 12:23). They do not get there by grabbing like Jacob, but because the true Firstborn (Col 1:18) gave himself so that the unworthy could share his birthright.
