*Setting:* In 586 BC the Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Jerusalem, burned Solomon's temple and carried most of the survivors of Judah into exile in Babylonia (2 Kings 25; 2 Chr 36:17–21). Earlier deportations had already taken Daniel (605 BC) and King Jehoiachin with Ezekiel and thousands of others (597 BC). For about fifty years the exiles lived along the canals of southern Iraq, where they "wept, when we remembered Zion" (Ps 137:1). Then the world changed. Cyrus the Great, king of Persia (559–530 BC), conquered the Medes, then Lydia in Asia Minor, and in October 539 BC his army took Babylon almost without a fight, the night Daniel 5 describes. Cyrus now ruled the largest empire the world had yet seen, stretching from the Aegean Sea to the borders of India. Ezra 1 opens in his first year as king of Babylon, 538 BC. The book of Ezra and the book of Nehemiah were one book in the Hebrew Bible, and together they tell the story of the return from exile and the rebuilding of the temple, the people and the walls. Ezra himself does not appear until chapter 7, about eighty years after these events. Chapters 1–6 cover the first return and the rebuilding of the temple (538–516 BC). The first three verses of Ezra repeat almost word for word the last two verses of 2 Chronicles (36:22–23), so the story picks up exactly where Chronicles left off, and the two books are closely linked. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading, "The Proclamation of Cyrus." The subheadings below divide it at its natural breaks.

#### The Proclamation of Cyrus (vv1–4)
- **"In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia" (v1).** Cyrus had been king of Persia since 559 BC, so this means his first year as ruler of Babylon, counted from his conquest in 539. By the Babylonian reckoning that was 538 BC. JFB, following the older chronology of its day, dates it 536 BC and notes that the decree took effect the following year.
- **"That the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah might be fulfilled" (v1).** Jeremiah had prophesied that the exile would last seventy years: "When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you, and I will fulfill to you my promise and bring you back to this place" (Jer 29:10; also 25:11–12). Counting from the first deportation in 605 BC to the return in 538 gives about sixty-seven years. Counting from the temple's destruction in 586 to its rebuilding in 516 gives exactly seventy. Many take "seventy years" as a round figure for a full lifetime of judgment. Daniel, still alive in Babylon, read Jeremiah's prophecy in this very period and prayed for its fulfillment (Dan 9:1–2). JFB calls the phrase about Jeremiah "a parenthetic statement of the historian," the narrator's comment rather than part of Cyrus's decree.
- **"The LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus" (v1).** "Stirred up" (*he'ir*) means roused, awakened. The same verb returns in v5 for the exiles themselves: God stirred the king to send them and stirred the people to go. The prophets had used this very word for God raising up a conqueror from the east: "Who stirred up one from the east whom victory meets at every step?" (Isa 41:2, 25; also Jer 51:11). The Tyndale notes stress that God remains in control even of rulers who do not know him. Proverbs says the same: "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will" (Prov 21:1).
- **"He made a proclamation throughout all his kingdom and also put it in writing" (v1).** "Proclamation" is literally "he caused a voice to pass," that is, heralds announced it aloud in towns across the empire. It was also written down. A second, more formal version of the decree, in Aramaic, the official language of the Persian Empire, turns up later in the royal archives at Ecbatana (Ezra 6:2–5). It gives the temple's dimensions and orders the costs paid from the royal treasury.
- **"The LORD, the God of heaven, has given me all the kingdoms of the earth" (v2).** It is startling to hear a pagan king name Israel's God. Cyrus was not a convert. The Tyndale notes describe him as a Zoroastrian, though historians debate how fully Persian kings followed Zoroaster at this early date. What is clear is his policy. A clay cylinder found at Babylon in 1879, the Cyrus Cylinder, now in the British Museum, records his own account of the conquest. There he credits Babylon's god Marduk with choosing him, and says he returned the images of gods to their shrines and gathered displaced peoples back to their homes. Cyrus spoke to each people in the language of its own religion. In Babylon he honored Marduk. To the Jews he honored "the LORD, the God of heaven." "God of heaven" is a title used often in the Persian period (Ezra 5:11–12; Neh 1:4; Dan 2:18), a way of naming the true God in terms a foreign court could understand.
- **"He has charged me to build him a house at Jerusalem" (v2).** About 150 years earlier, Isaiah had named Cyrus as the one who would say of Jerusalem, "She shall be built," and of the temple, "Your foundation shall be laid" (Isa 44:28). God even called him "his anointed" (*meshicho*, Isa 45:1), the only non-Israelite given that title. "For the sake of my servant Jacob… I call you by your name, I name you, though you do not know me" (Isa 45:4). Cyrus may have known those prophecies. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the first century AD, says he read them and was moved to fulfill them. The Tyndale notes suggest Daniel, who served at the Persian court (Dan 6:28), could have shown them to him. That is possible, but the text does not say so.
- **"Whoever is among you of all his people, may his God be with him, and let him go up" (v3).** This is the last line of 2 Chronicles (36:23), and the last words of the Hebrew Bible in its traditional order are "let him go up." To "go up" (*ya'al*) to Jerusalem was the regular phrase for pilgrimage, since Jerusalem stands high in the hills. The invitation is open: "whoever." No one is forced. The Tyndale notes explain the policy behind it. The Assyrians and Babylonians had controlled conquered peoples by deporting and scattering them. The Persians sent them home, expecting gratitude, loyalty and taxes in return.
- **"He is the God who is in Jerusalem" (v3).** To Cyrus, the LORD was a god tied to a particular place. The reader of Ezra knows more: this God had just given Cyrus "all the kingdoms of the earth." Cyrus spoke more truly than he understood.
- **"Let each survivor… be assisted by the men of his place with silver and gold" (v4).** "Survivor" (*nish'ar*) is the language of the remnant, those left after judgment (Isa 10:20–22). "The men of his place" are the neighbors where each exile lived. The Tyndale notes think this mainly means fellow Jews who chose to stay in Babylon, helping those who went. Others think non-Jewish neighbors gave too, as the next verses may suggest. "Freewill offerings" (*nedavah*) were voluntary gifts for God's house, the same word used for the gifts that built the tabernacle (Exod 35:29).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The hearts of kings are in the hand of the Lord," so even a foreign king's generosity is God's doing. JFB thinks Cyrus knew of the prophecies naming him (Isa 44:28), probably through Daniel, and says the claim to rule "all the kingdoms of the earth" was close to the literal truth for Persia's power at that time. Guzik passes on Yamauchi's point that Persian kings took prophecy and oracles seriously, which makes it plausible that Cyrus listened to Isaiah. He cites Kidner on the Persian policy of ruling many peoples while respecting their customs, and Selman's observation that the phrase "build him a house" echoes God's promise to David that his son would build God's house (1 Chr 17:11–12).

#### The Exiles Prepare to Return (vv5–6)
- **"Then rose up the heads of the fathers' houses of Judah and Benjamin, and the priests and the Levites" (v5).** The "fathers' houses" were extended family clans, the basic unit of Israelite society. Judah and Benjamin were the two tribes of the southern kingdom, the ones taken to Babylon. The northern tribes had been scattered by Assyria nearly two centuries earlier (2 Kings 17:6), though some of their people had joined Judah over the years (1 Chr 9:3). Poole, as Guzik reports, points out that the leaders named were the main ones, not necessarily the only ones.
- **"Everyone whose spirit God had stirred to go up" (v5).** The same verb as v1. God moved the king, and God moved the people. Not everyone went. Only those whose spirits God stirred. The Tyndale notes point out that many Jews stayed in Babylon, where they had homes, businesses and families. Babylonian business records from later in the Persian period, such as the archives of the Murashu family firm at Nippur, include many Jewish names, showing that a large community remained. Going meant a journey of some 900 miles, about four months on foot (Ezra 7:9), to a ruined city surrounded by hostile neighbors. Those who went were a minority who cared more for God's house than for their own comfort. Guzik lists the hardships ahead: a long and dangerous trip, a city in ruins, little money, enemies around them and a land still under foreign rule.
- **"To rebuild the house of the LORD that is in Jerusalem" (v5).** The purpose was first of all worship. They went not mainly to reclaim property or rebuild a nation but to rebuild the temple. Adeney, as Guzik reports, says they went back as a church more than as a state.
- **"All who were about them aided them with vessels of silver, with gold, with goods, with beasts, and with costly wares" (v6).** This echoes the exodus. When Israel left Egypt, the Egyptians gave them "silver and gold jewelry, and clothing" (Exod 12:35–36), as God had promised (Exod 3:21–22). Isaiah had described the return from Babylon as a new exodus (Isa 43:16–19; 48:20–21; 52:11–12). Once again God's people leave a foreign land with its wealth in their hands. JFB describes the neighbors' "hearty good will and great liberality."
- **"Besides all that was freely offered" (v6).** The voluntary gifts for the temple were counted separately from the help given for the journey. Those who stayed could still share in the work by giving.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Whatever good we do, is owing to the grace of God." He notes that the same proclamation reached everyone, but only those whose spirits God stirred answered it. JFB calls the leaders "the paternal and ecclesiastical chiefs" of the captivity. Guzik calls the returnees a minority stirred by God's Spirit, and notes Yamauchi's observation that the Babylonians, unlike the Assyrians in the north, did not resettle foreign peoples in Judah, which left the land less mixed with pagan worship.

#### The Temple Vessels Restored (vv7–11)
- **"Cyrus the king also brought out the vessels of the house of the LORD that Nebuchadnezzar had carried away" (v7).** Nebuchadnezzar took temple treasures in several stages (2 Kings 24:13; 25:13–17; 2 Chr 36:7, 18; Dan 1:2). He "placed them in the house of his gods," a public claim that Babylon's gods had defeated Israel's God. Belshazzar later drank wine from them at his feast while praising "the gods of gold and silver," and that was the night Babylon fell (Dan 5:2–4, 30). Jeremiah had promised: "They shall be carried to Babylon and remain there until the day when I visit them, declares the LORD. Then I will bring them back and restore them to this place" (Jer 27:22). The return of the vessels was proof that God had not been defeated after all.
- **"Cut in pieces"?** 2 Kings 24:13 says Nebuchadnezzar "cut in pieces all the vessels of gold" from the temple. That seems to clash with their return here. JFB suggests the word may mean "cut off" or stripped from the temple rather than smashed. Poole, as Guzik reports, took the same view. It is also likely that some vessels were destroyed or melted down and others were kept, since the deportations happened at different times.
- **"In the charge of Mithredath the treasurer" (v8).** Mithredath is a Persian name, "given by Mithra," the name of a Persian god, the same name as the later Greek "Mithridates." "Treasurer" (*gizbar*) is a Persian loanword. These small details fit the Persian setting well. He "counted them out," a formal handover, item by item.
- **"Sheshbazzar the prince of Judah" (v8).** "Prince" (*nasi'*) means a leader or chief, a title used for tribal heads. Later Sheshbazzar is called the governor Cyrus appointed, and is said to have laid the temple's foundations (Ezra 5:14–16). Yet Ezra 3:8–10 credits Zerubbabel with laying the foundations, and Haggai and Zechariah know only Zerubbabel as governor (Hag 1:1; Zech 4:9). Who was Sheshbazzar? There are three main views. Some, including JFB, identify him with Zerubbabel under his Babylonian court name, as Daniel was also called Belteshazzar (Dan 1:7). Others think he was Shenazzar, a son of King Jehoiachin (1 Chr 3:18), making him Zerubbabel's uncle. A third view, held by the Tyndale notes, sees two different leaders who worked together, with Zerubbabel taking over later. Kidner, as Guzik reports, suggests that Sheshbazzar may have been the official, Persian-appointed leader while Zerubbabel was the popular leader of the people. The text does not settle it.
- **"This was the number of them" (vv9–10).** The inventory lists gold and silver basins, "censers," bowls and "other vessels." Some of the Hebrew words are rare and their exact meaning is uncertain. "Basins" (*'agartelim*) appears only here. "Censers" (*machalafim*) also appears only here. The KJV guessed "knives," and the word may come from a root meaning to cut or to pass through. The list is the kind of record any royal treasury kept, and it shows how carefully the holy things were handled.
- **"All the vessels of gold and of silver were 5,400" (v11).** The items listed in vv9–10 add up to only 2,499 (30 + 1,000 + 29 + 30 + 410 + 1,000). The apocryphal book of 1 Esdras, retelling this event, gives a total of 5,469, and Josephus gives other figures. The Tyndale notes suggest the list names only the main types and not every item. JFB thinks "the larger vases only are mentioned" in the list while the total includes the rest. Others think a copying error crept into one of the numbers, since Hebrew numbers were easy to miscopy. Calmly put: the list and the total do not match, and the reason is uncertain. The point is not affected: thousands of holy vessels went home.
- **What was missing.** Guzik notes that the greatest treasures are not listed: the ark of the covenant, the golden lampstand, the table, the altars. The ark is never mentioned again in the Old Testament after the exile. Jeremiah had said a day would come when people would no longer say "the ark of the covenant of the LORD," because "Jerusalem shall be called the throne of the LORD" (Jer 3:16–17). The second temple would be built without it.
- **"When the exiles were brought up from Babylonia to Jerusalem" (v11).** "Brought up" is passive. The exiles did not bring themselves. God brought them, through Cyrus. The journey itself is not described. Ezra 2 lists who went.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the larger parallel: "The bringing back the Jews from captivity, represents the redemption of sinners by Jesus Christ." JFB treats Sheshbazzar as Zerubbabel's court name and suggests the total of v11 includes smaller vessels not listed. Guzik passes on Kidner's remark that the transfer of these vessels from Babylon to Jerusalem marked a turning point in history, and Maclaren's point that the careful list reflects the joy with which the vessels were received.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezra 1 shows God moving the most powerful man in the world to send a handful of exiles home to rebuild his house. The Cyrus whom Isaiah called "anointed" was a shadow of a greater Anointed One. Cyrus freed captives by decree. Jesus freed them by his death, "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18), redeeming people from a deeper exile than Babylon. Cyrus ordered the temple rebuilt. Jesus said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," speaking "about the temple of his body" (John 2:19–21). In him God came to dwell with his people in a way the second temple never could, which is why it was fitting that the ark never came back. And the invitation of v3 still stands, wider than before: whoever belongs to his people may go up. Those whose spirits God stirs answer it, like the minority who left Babylon, and they journey toward "the Jerusalem above" (Gal 4:26), a city whose builder and maker is God (Heb 11:10).
