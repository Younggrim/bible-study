*Setting:* Judah in the last years before the first exile, probably late in Jehoiakim's reign and the brief reign of his son Jehoiachin (598–597 BC). After the poems and prayers of chapters 11–12, Jeremiah is given the first of his acted-out signs. Prophets sometimes preached with actions as well as words: Isaiah walked barefoot (Isa 20), Ezekiel lay on his side for months (Ezek 4), and Jeremiah will later smash a clay jar (ch 19) and wear an ox yoke (ch 27). Here he buys a linen loincloth, wears it, buries it, and digs it up ruined. Two short sayings follow, about wine jars and about pride, and the chapter ends with a direct word to the young king and his mother, whose crowns are about to fall, and the famous question, "Can the Ethiopian change his skin?"

#### The Ruined Loincloth (vv1–11)
- **"Go and buy a linen loincloth" (v1).** The word (*ʾezor*) means a garment worn around the waist, close to the body. Some take it as an undergarment, a short wrap or kilt (so the ESV and the Tyndale notes), and others as a sash or waistband worn over the tunic, like a belt. Either way, it was the item of clothing that clung closest. Linen was the fabric of priests' garments (Lev 16:4; Ezek 44:17–18), and Jeremiah was from a priestly family (1:1). A fine linen garment on a prophet who probably wore rough cloth would have caught people's eye.
- **"Do not dip it in water" (v1).** He is to wear it unwashed. The point may be that it grows dirty with wear, a picture of Judah's growing stain, or simply that it should stay new and stiff, never laundered, so that what ruins it later is not use but burial. The text does not explain, and interpreters differ.
- **"Go to the Euphrates and hide it there in a cleft of the rock" (v4).** The Hebrew is *Perat*, normally the Euphrates, about 350 miles north and east of Jerusalem by road. A round trip there took weeks, and Jeremiah apparently made it twice. That has led to three main views.
- **Three readings of the journey.** (1) He literally went to the Euphrates, twice, perhaps with a trading caravan. Morgan, Henry and the Tyndale notes accept this. (2) The whole sign happened in a vision, as JFB and many older interpreters (Calvin among them) held. (3) He went to a place much closer with a similar name: the town of Parah (Josh 18:23), near the spring at Wadi Farah, about four miles northeast of Anathoth, where there are rock clefts and running water. On this view the name was chosen because it sounded like the Euphrates. The text says plainly that he went, so a real journey fits best, but the point is the same in every reading: the garment was taken toward Babylon, and that is where it was ruined.
- **"The loincloth was spoiled; it was good for nothing" (v7).** After "many days," perhaps months and through a rainy season, the linen had rotted in the damp rock. The verb "spoiled" (*shachat*) means ruined or corrupted.
- **"Even so will I spoil the pride of Judah and the great pride of Jerusalem" (v9).** Same verb. Judah's pride, the very thing she was proudest of, would rot away by the river of Babylon. Leviticus 26:19 had warned, "I will break the pride of your power."
- **"Who refuse to hear… who stubbornly follow their own heart… have gone after other gods" (v10).** Three steps downward: closing their ears to God, following their own hearts, and serving idols. Ignoring God's word leaves a heart that follows itself, and a heart that follows itself always finds an idol.
- **"As the loincloth clings to the waist of a man, so I made the whole house of Israel… cling to me" (v11).** "Cling" (*dabaq*) is the word used for a man holding fast to his wife (Gen 2:24) and for Israel holding fast to the LORD (Deut 10:20; 30:20). God bound his people to himself as closely as clothing to skin.
- **"That they might be for me a people, a name, a praise, and a glory" (v11).** Israel was meant to be God's finery, the thing he wore with pride before the nations (Deut 26:19; Exod 19:5–6). **"But they would not listen."** Five words that undo the whole purpose. The same four terms come back in 33:9, where restored Jerusalem will be "a name of joy, a praise and a glory" to God before all nations.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks the garment was a fine sash worn over the clothes, "such as officers wear," not an undergarment, and he defends a literal journey, since the Euphrates was the border of the promised land (Josh 1:4) and travel to Babylon was common. JFB holds that the journey took place in the prophet's "inward vision," arguing that a trip of more than two hundred miles twice is unlikely, and notes Henderson's suggestion that *Perat* might be a nearby place. Guzik, citing Morgan, believes Jeremiah really traveled there and back twice, and quotes Spurgeon on the value of acting out truth so people notice. All three agree the meaning is what matters: what God bound close to himself had become useless.

#### The Jars Filled with Wine (vv12–14)
- **"Every jar shall be filled with wine" (v12).** The jar (*nevel*) was a large clay storage jar for wine. The saying sounds like a proverb, the kind of cheerful line people repeat at a feast: "Every jar will be full!" It may have meant "good times are coming" or "things will turn out fine."
- **"Do we not indeed know that every jar will be filled with wine?" (v12).** The people answer like those who have heard a platitude: of course they will. They hear the words as a promise of plenty.
- **"I will fill with drunkenness all the inhabitants of this land" (v13).** Jeremiah turns the saying around. The people themselves are the jars, and what will fill them is not festive wine but the stupor of God's judgment. In Scripture the "cup of wrath" makes people stagger and fall (25:15–29; Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17). Every level of society is named: kings on David's throne, priests, prophets and ordinary citizens.
- **"I will dash them one against another, fathers and sons together" (v14).** Drunken men stumble into each other. Clay jars knocked together shatter. Both pictures fit. The nation will turn on itself and break apart. Psalm 2:9 uses the image of dashing pottery for God's judgment on rebels. **"I will not pity or spare or have compassion."** Three words for mercy, all withheld. The time for warnings is running out.
- **Commentators:** JFB thinks the jars were wineskins, since the Hebrew *nevel* can mean a skin bottle. Guzik, citing Harrison, takes it as the largest clay storage jar, which fits the picture of jars dashed together better. JFB also points out that the reply "of course" was not even literally true, since such vessels held water and milk too; the people "affected not to understand" the figure. Guzik passes on Kidner's sense that the proverb meant roughly that everything would turn out fine in the end.

#### Exile Threatened (vv15–27)
- **"Hear and give ear; be not proud" (v15).** The loincloth was about pride (v9), and this poem begins there. Humility is the doorway to hearing God.
- **"Give glory to the LORD your God before he brings darkness" (v16).** To "give glory to God" meant to confess the truth before him. Joshua told Achan, "give glory to the LORD… and tell me now what you have done" (Josh 7:19), and the Pharisees used the same phrase to the blind man in John 9:24.
- **"Before your feet stumble on the twilight mountains" (v16).** A traveler caught in the hill country at dusk could not see the path and might fall. **"While you look for light he turns it into gloom."** The word for "gloom" is *tsalmavet*, the "shadow of death" of Psalm 23:4. The window for repentance is closing like the light at sunset (John 12:35).
- **"My soul will weep in secret for your pride" (v17).** Jeremiah will grieve alone, where no one sees. He does not preach judgment with satisfaction. **"Because the LORD's flock has been taken captive."** He speaks as though the exile has already happened.
- **"Say to the king and the queen mother" (v18).** This most likely points to Jehoiachin, who became king at eighteen and ruled only three months in 597 BC, and his mother Nehushta (2 Kgs 24:8). In Judah the queen mother (*gebirah*) held real power and is often named alongside the king (1 Kgs 15:13). With so young a king, her influence was great. Both were taken to Babylon (2 Kgs 24:12, 15; Jer 22:26; 29:2). **"Take a lowly seat, for your beautiful crown has come down from your head."** The thrones will be traded for the dust.
- **"The cities of the Negeb are shut up… all Judah is taken into exile" (v19).** The Negeb is the dry south of Judah. Even the towns farthest from the northern invader are closed, either besieged or deserted with no one left to open the gates. "Wholly taken into exile" is poetic exaggeration of the size of the disaster, though the first exile in 597 BC took the king, the court, the craftsmen and ten thousand leading citizens (2 Kgs 24:14).
- **"Lift up your eyes and see those who come from the north. Where is the flock that was given you?" (v20).** Jerusalem, pictured as a shepherd, is asked where her flock went. The people entrusted to her care are gone.
- **"Those whom you yourself have taught to be friends to you" (v21).** The Hebrew is difficult, but the sense seems to be that Judah had courted Babylon as an ally (2 Kgs 20:12–19, when Hezekiah showed Babylon's envoys his treasures), and now the friends she cultivated will rule over her. **"Pangs… like those of a woman in labor"** is the usual biblical picture of sudden, unavoidable anguish (4:31; 6:24).
- **"Why have these things come upon me?" (v22).** The people genuinely ask why. The answer is blunt: "the greatness of your iniquity." **"Your skirts are lifted up and you suffer violence."** Jerusalem is pictured as a woman publicly shamed and assaulted, the treatment of captive women in ancient war (Isa 47:2–3; Nah 3:5). The picture is meant to shock.
- **"Can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard his spots?" (v23).** "Ethiopian" is *Kushi*, a person from Cush, the land south of Egypt along the upper Nile (modern Sudan). Cushites were known in Judah. One would later rescue Jeremiah from the cistern (38:7–13). The verse makes no judgment about skin color. It simply says that a Cushite cannot change his skin any more than a leopard can remove its spots. **"Then also you can do good who are accustomed to do evil."** "Accustomed" means trained or taught. Sin practiced long enough becomes a second nature. Judah cannot reform herself. This is one of the clearest statements in the Old Testament that human beings need more than good advice. They need a new heart, which God will promise in 24:7 and 31:33.
- **"I will scatter you like chaff driven by the wind from the desert" (v24).** Chaff is the light husk blown away when grain is winnowed (Ps 1:4). The desert wind is the hot east wind that blows hard in spring and autumn (4:11).
- **"This is your lot, the portion I have measured out to you" (v25).** "Portion" is literally "the portion of your measures," like a ration doled out. Judah had chosen her share when she "forgot me and trusted in lies."
- **"I myself will lift up your skirts over your face" (v26).** God himself will expose what Judah tried to hide. Nahum 3:5 says the same to Nineveh.
- **"Your adulteries and neighings, your lewd whorings, on the hills in the field" (v27).** "Neighings" recalls 5:8, where Judah's men were like "well-fed, lusty stallions, each neighing for his neighbor's wife." The worship on the hills was the Canaanite fertility cult, which involved ritual sexual acts. Spiritual and physical unfaithfulness went together.
- **"How long will it be before you are made clean?" (v27).** The chapter ends with a question, not a door slammed shut. The Hebrew is literally "you will not be clean, after how long yet?" It can sound like despair, but it is also a longing. God expects that one day his people will be clean. Ezekiel 36:25–26 promises exactly that: "I will sprinkle clean water on you… and a new heart I will give you."
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies the king and queen mother as Jehoiachin and Nehushta, "who, as the king was not more than eighteen years old, held the chief power," and reads v16's darkness as a traveler "overtaken by nightfall" on the mountains. Henry draws a lesson from v17 about how godly people should mourn others' sins, and JFB notes that weeping "in secret" shows Jeremiah humbled himself "not self-righteously condemning them." Guzik, citing Clarke, reads the call to give glory to God as a call to confess sin so that the disaster might be turned away. On v23 Henry says sin had become "a second nature to them" and that "custom in sin is a very great hindrance to conversion," but adds that "there is an almighty grace that is able to change the Ethiopian's skin."

#### Pointing to Christ
God bound his people to himself like a garment, and they rotted. Jesus is the faithful Israel who "clung" to the Father perfectly, the one in whom God was finally given "a name, a praise, and a glory" (John 17:4). Jeremiah's question, "Can the Ethiopian change his skin?", has a clear answer: not by himself. That is why Jesus said, "You must be born again" (John 3:7), and why Paul says that "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Cor 5:17). The cup of wrath that would leave Judah staggering is the cup Jesus asked to have removed and then drank himself (Matt 26:39). And the last line, "How long will it be before you are made clean?", is answered by his word to the leper: "I will; be clean" (Mark 1:41), and by the promise that "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7).
