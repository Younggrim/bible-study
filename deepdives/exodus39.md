*Setting:* Israel is camped at Mount Sinai, in the first year after leaving Egypt. Bezalel, Oholiab and the skilled workers have finished the tent, its furniture and the courtyard (36:8–38:31). Now they make the clothing for the priests, following the design God gave Moses in chapter 28, and then the whole project is carried to Moses for inspection. Much of the chapter repeats earlier instructions almost word for word. What is new is the refrain running through it, "as the LORD had commanded Moses," and the scene at the end, which deliberately echoes the end of creation week.

#### Making the Priestly Garments (vv1–43)
- **"Finely woven garments, for ministering in the Holy Place" (v1).** These were working clothes, not costumes. The priests wore them to serve, and God had said Aaron's garments were "for glory and for beauty" (28:2). The same blue, purple and scarlet yarns used for the inner curtains and the veil are used here, so the priest was dressed to match the house he served in.
- **"As the LORD had commanded Moses" (vv1, 5, 7, 21, 26, 29, 31).** The phrase closes each piece of clothing, seven times in the garment section, and it comes seven more times in chapter 40. The repetition is the point. Nothing was improvised. After the golden calf, when the people made worship up for themselves (32:1–6), every detail is now done God's way.
- **"They hammered out gold leaf, and he cut it into threads" (v3).** This detail is not in chapter 28. Gold was beaten into thin sheets, cut into fine strips and worked into the woven fabric of the ephod, so it shimmered with gold through the colors.
- **The ephod and its shoulder stones (vv2–7).** The ephod was a sleeveless outer vest or apron, held by shoulder straps and a woven band at the waist (28:6–14). On each shoulder sat an onyx stone engraved with six tribal names, **"stones of remembrance for the sons of Israel"** (v7). Whenever Aaron went before God, he carried the whole people on his shoulders.
- **The breastpiece (vv8–21).** It was a pouch of the same fabric, folded double, **"a span"** square (v9), about 9 inches (23 cm), the width of a spread hand. Twelve gems sat in four rows, each **engraved with the name of a tribe** (v14). In 28:29–30 it is called the "breastpiece of judgment," because it held the Urim and Thummim, used to seek God's decisions. So Aaron carried Israel on his shoulders and over his heart.
- **What were the stones?** The ESV's sardius, topaz, carbuncle, emerald and the rest are best guesses. Several of the Hebrew names appear only in lists like this, and ancient gem names often meant something different from the modern words (ancient "sapphire" was probably lapis lazuli). Revelation 21:19–20 draws on this list for the foundations of the New Jerusalem, a city built on the names of God's people.
- **"So that the breastpiece should not come loose from the ephod" (v21).** Gold chains, gold rings and a blue cord locked the breastpiece to the ephod. The names over the priest's heart could not slip away while he served.
- **"The robe of the ephod woven all of blue" (v22).** The robe was worn under the ephod. The blue (*tekhelet*) was an expensive dye. Israelites were later told to wear a blue cord in their tassels to remember God's commands (Num 15:38–39), so the color marked something set apart for God.
- **"A binding around the opening, so that it might not tear" (v23).** The neck was reinforced. The high priest was forbidden to tear his garments (Lev 21:10). It is striking that at Jesus' trial the high priest Caiaphas "tore his robes" (Matt 26:65), the very thing the law told him not to do, while Jesus' own tunic was "seamless, woven in one piece" and was not torn (John 19:23–24). Some see a deliberate contrast. John does not draw it, so it is best held loosely.
- **"A bell and a pomegranate, a bell and a pomegranate" (v26).** Pomegranates, a fruit of the promised land (Num 13:23; Deut 8:8), alternated with gold bells around the hem. The bells were heard when Aaron went in and came out, "so that he does not die" (28:35). His presence before God was audible to the people waiting outside.
- **Coats, turban, caps, undergarments and sash (vv27–29).** Ordinary priests wore white linen. The **linen undergarments** were for modesty (28:42–43; compare 20:26), since nothing about worship at God's altar was to expose the body as pagan rites often did. The **sash** was the one colored piece, embroidered in the tabernacle's colors.
- **"The plate of the holy crown… 'Holy to the LORD'" (v30).** The Hebrew word for "crown" is *nezer*, which BDB defines first as "consecration" and then "crown." It is the same root as the Nazirite vow (Num 6). The gold plate was a sign of being set apart. Chapter 28 adds that Aaron wore it so that he "shall bear any guilt" from the people's offerings, so that they might "be accepted before the LORD" (28:38). The Tyndale notes suggest "holy crown" may be a formal title, since chapter 28 does not use it.
- **"Thus all the work of the tabernacle… was finished" (v32).** "Finished" is *kalah*, the verb of Genesis 2:1–2: "the heavens and the earth were finished… God finished his work." The work began in 36:2, and the Hebrew dating suggests it took only months (compare 19:1 and 40:17).
- **The great inventory (vv33–41).** Every piece is brought to Moses: frames, bars, coverings, the ark, the table, the lampstand, the altars, the oil and incense, the courtyard hangings, the pegs, the garments. Listing it all again is a way of saying that nothing was left out.
- **"Moses saw all the work, and behold, they had done it" (v43).** The wording echoes Genesis 1:31: "God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good." Creation ends with seeing, finishing and blessing (Gen 1:31–2:3), and so does the tabernacle. Many scholars see the tabernacle as a small model of a restored creation, the place where God would again live among his people as in Eden.
- **"Then Moses blessed them" (v43).** The Tyndale notes draw the contrast with chapter 32. When the people tried to reach God their own way, Moses came down to judgment (32:19–28). Now, when they have done it God's way, he blesses them. The workers were unpaid volunteers. The blessing was their reward.
- **Commentators:** Guzik says almost nothing on the garments, only pointing back to chapter 28, and saves his comment for the end: Moses alone had seen the pattern on the mountain (25:9), so he was the right person to check every detail, and the people's obedience deserves to be called remarkable. Henry notes that the garments are called clothes "to do service in," so honor in God's house is for working, not for show, and he warns against bringing similar pomp into Christian worship now that the reality in Christ has come. On vv32–43 he marvels that the work was "done suddenly" because many willing hands worked together, and he observes that the craftsmen submitted their work to Moses rather than judging it themselves. JFB explains how the gold was beaten and cut into thread, compares the engraved plate to Egyptian signet rings, and says Moses accepted the work and asked God to bless the workers.

#### Pointing to Christ
Aaron wore Israel's names on his shoulders and over his heart, carried a plate saying "Holy to the LORD," and bore the guilt of the people's holy things so they could be accepted. Hebrews says Christ is the high priest these garments pictured: "holy, innocent, unstained" (Heb 7:26), who "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through him (Heb 7:25). Aaron's holiness was a gold plate tied on with a cord. Christ's is his own. And the closing scene, with the work finished, seen and blessed, looks ahead to another "It is finished" (John 19:30), the work that makes a people fit for God to dwell among.
