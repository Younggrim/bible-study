*Setting:* Still the plains of Moab, in the last months before Israel crosses the Jordan. Chapter 33 ended with God's command to drive out the Canaanites and divide the land by lot. Now God draws the map. For the first time since the promise to Abraham (Gen 12:7; 15:18–21), the land of Canaan is marked out border by border, and God names the men who will divide it. It reads like a property deed, and in a sense that is what it is: God's title to the land, handed to his people before they have set foot in it.

#### Boundaries of the Land (vv1–15)
- **"The land of Canaan as defined by its borders" (v2).** God gives the land a definite shape. These borders match fairly closely what Egyptian records from roughly 1500–1200 BC called Canaan, the Egyptian province in western Syria-Palestine. So God is not inventing a territory. He is handing over the very region known as Canaan in that era.
- **The southern border (vv3–5).** It runs from the southern tip of the **Salt Sea** (the Dead Sea) in an arc west to the Mediterranean. **The ascent of Akrabbim** means "Scorpion Pass," a steep climb southwest of the Dead Sea still known by that name. The line dips south of **Kadesh-barnea**, the oasis where Israel spent so many years, so the place of their great failure falls just inside the promised land. **The Brook of Egypt** is not the Nile but a seasonal stream bed, usually identified with Wadi el-Arish in northern Sinai, that marked the edge of Egypt's reach.
- **"The Great Sea and its coast" (v6).** The Mediterranean was the natural western border. In practice Israel rarely controlled much of the coast. The Philistines held the southern plain and the Phoenicians the north for most of Israel's history.
- **The northern border (vv7–9).** It runs from the Mediterranean to **Mount Hor**, a different mountain from the one where Aaron died (33:38). This one was somewhere in the Lebanon range, though no one knows exactly which peak. From there it reaches **Lebo-hamath**, either "the entrance of Hamath," the gap leading north toward the city of Hamath, or a town named Lebo, probably modern Lebweh in Lebanon's Beqaa Valley. The spies had once walked as far as this point (13:21). **Zedad**, **Ziphron** and **Hazar-enan** lie somewhere in what is now Lebanon and southern Syria.
- **The eastern border (vv10–12).** It comes down from the far north to **the Sea of Chinnereth**, the Sea of Galilee. The name is probably from *kinnor*, "harp," for the lake's shape. "The shoulder… on the east" is the steep ridge rising from the lake's eastern shore. Then the line follows the Jordan down to the Dead Sea. The Jordan rift is a natural wall, and it makes a clear boundary.
- **The land is bigger than Israel ever held.** Israel never controlled all of this at once except, roughly, in the days of David and Solomon, when the kingdom reached "from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of Egypt" (1 Kgs 8:65). The borders describe the land God gave in full, and the history that follows shows how much of it Israel failed to take (Judg 1). Ezekiel's vision of a restored Israel repeats these same borders almost point by point (Ezek 47:15–20), which shows the promise was never forgotten.
- **"To the nine tribes and to the half-tribe" (vv13–15).** Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh have already received land east of the Jordan (ch 32). Notice that the land God defines here as Canaan ends at the Jordan. The eastern territory was conquered and given, but it lay outside Canaan proper. That small fact explains later tensions, when the western tribes wondered whether the eastern tribes truly belonged (Josh 22:19, 24–25).
- **"Toward the sunrise" (v15).** A poetic way of saying east, the direction the tribes face from Canaan back across the river.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws a lesson on contentment: "God sets bounds to our lot; let us then set bounds to our desires, and bring our mind to our condition." He says the land's small size was "abundantly compensated by the extraordinary fruitfulness of the soil," and that "Those that have their portion in heaven have reason to be content with a small pittance of this earth." JFB says the western border is the least uncertain: "it is universally allowed to be the Mediterranean." It reads the northern Mount Hor as a peak in the Lebanon range and the entrance of Hamath as the valley of Baalbek, which is one of several guesses. Guzik says the borders never matched Israel's actual territory at any point, slightly further than the Tyndale notes, which say David and Solomon came close. He passes on Matthew Poole's three reasons for the borders: to restrain Israel from grabbing land like other nations, to encourage them to conquer, and to guide the division. He applies it to accepting God-given limits as wise rather than as punishment.

#### List of Tribal Chiefs (vv16–29)
- **"Eleazar the priest and Joshua the son of Nun" (v17).** The priest and the military leader divide the land together, as Moses ordered in 32:28. Joshua 14:1 shows them doing exactly this. God appoints the dividers before a single town has been taken. Naming them in advance is itself an act of faith that the conquest will happen.
- **"One chief from every tribe" (v18).** Each tribe has its own representative, so no one can complain later that the division was rigged. Land disputes were (and are) one of the bitterest causes of conflict in any community.
- **"Of the tribe of Judah, Caleb the son of Jephunneh" (v19).** Of all the chiefs named, only Caleb appears earlier in Numbers. Forty years before, he and Joshua were the two spies who said "we are well able to overcome it" (13:30). Now he is nearly eighty, still standing, and chosen to divide the land he once urged Israel to take. When the time comes he asks for the hill country of Hebron, where the giants lived, and takes it (Josh 14:6–15).
- **New names for a new generation (vv20–28).** Every other chief is new. The leaders of chapters 1, 7 and 13 have died. Many of these names carry the name of God, *El*: **Shemuel** ("name of God"), **Elidad** ("God has loved"), **Hanniel** ("grace of God"), **Elizaphan** ("my God has protected"), **Paltiel** ("God is deliverance"), **Pedahel** ("God has redeemed"). The new generation's names are a small witness to faith.
- **The order of the tribes.** The list runs Judah, Simeon, Benjamin, Dan, Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Issachar, Asher, Naphtali. That is roughly south to north, in the order of the territories each tribe would actually receive in Joshua 15–19. It is as though the list was laid over the map.
- **"Whom the LORD commanded to divide the inheritance" (v29).** The chapter ends where it began, with God's command. The land is his to give, and the division is under his direction.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes "The nominees were ten princes for the nine and a half tribes," each answerable to Joshua and Eleazar, and that naming them before crossing the Jordan both encouraged faith and headed off later disputes. Guzik notes the task was potentially explosive, which is why the most trusted leaders were given it, and he sees Eleazar the priest as a picture of Christ, the high priest who secures his people's inheritance. JFB does not comment on the individual names.

#### Pointing to Christ
God drew the borders of the land before Israel owned a single field in it. The inheritance was secure because God had defined it, not because Israel had earned it. The New Testament speaks of the believer's inheritance the same way, but larger. Abraham's heirs are promised not just Canaan but "the world" (Rom 4:13), and Jesus says the meek "shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5). That inheritance is "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you" (1 Pet 1:4). Joshua divided the land under a priest. Christ is both the leader who brings his people in and the high priest who guarantees their share (Heb 4:8–10, 14). And as Caleb, who had wholly followed the LORD, lived to receive his portion, so those who belong to Christ will receive theirs.
