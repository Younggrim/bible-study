*Setting:* Lamentations is five poems of grief written after the Babylonians burned Jerusalem and its temple in 586 BC (Jer 52; 2 Kgs 25). The book names no author. Jewish and Christian tradition, going back to the Greek translation (the Septuagint) and the Talmud, has credited it to Jeremiah, who watched the city fall and had already written laments (2 Chr 35:25). Many modern scholars think it may come from another eyewitness, or a circle of them. Either way, the poems were written close to the events, by someone who saw the siege, the famine and the fires. The Hebrew title is *'Ekah*, "How!", the cry that opens chapters 1, 2 and 4. Chapters 1–4 are acrostics: each stanza begins with the next letter of the 22-letter Hebrew alphabet, as if grief must be spelled out from A to Z. In chapter 1 each stanza has three lines, and the city of Jerusalem is pictured as a woman. In the first half (vv1–11) a narrator describes her. In the second half (vv12–22) she speaks for herself. Jews still read Lamentations each year on Tisha b'Av, the fast that remembers the temple's destruction.

#### How Lonely Sits the City (vv1–22)
- **"How lonely sits the city that was full of people!" (v1).** The word "How" (*'ekah*) is a funeral cry, the opening of a dirge. Isaiah uses it of Jerusalem's moral fall ("How the faithful city has become a whore," Isa 1:21), and Moses used it when he sighed, "How can I bear by myself the weight… of you" (Deut 1:12). Sitting on the ground was the posture of mourning (Job 2:13; Ezek 26:16).
- **"How like a widow has she become… a princess among the provinces has become a slave" (v1).** Three reversals in one verse: full to empty, great to widowed, princess to forced laborer. A widow in the ancient world was the picture of vulnerability, without a protector or provider. The poem says "like" a widow. Her husband, the LORD, has not died, but she has been left without his protection.
- **"She weeps bitterly in the night… among all her lovers she has none to comfort her" (v2).** The "lovers" are the nations and gods Judah trusted instead of the LORD: allies like Egypt (Jer 37:5–10) and the idols prophets often pictured as lovers (Jer 2:20–25; 3:1–2; Ezek 16). **"None to comfort"** becomes the chapter's refrain. It sounds five times (vv2, 9, 16, 17, 21). Isaiah 40 answers it: "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God" (Isa 40:1).
- **"She dwells now among the nations, but finds no resting place" (v3).** Moses had warned of this exact curse: "among these nations you shall find no respite, and there shall be no resting place for the sole of your foot" (Deut 28:65). Much of Lamentations reads like the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28 and Leviticus 26 coming true. **"Her pursuers have all overtaken her in the midst of her distress"**: literally "between the narrow places," like travelers trapped in a pass.
- **"The roads to Zion mourn, for none come to the festival" (v4).** Three times a year the roads had been crowded with pilgrims singing the Psalms of Ascents (Ps 120–134). Now even the roads grieve. **"Her priests groan; her virgins have been afflicted"**: no temple for the priests, no weddings for the young women.
- **"Her foes have become the head" (v5).** Another covenant curse: "He shall be the head, and you shall be the tail" (Deut 28:44). **"Because the LORD has afflicted her for the multitude of her transgressions."** Here, in verse 5, the poet names the cause. This is not bad luck, or the gods of Babylon beating the God of Israel. The LORD himself has done it, and for a reason.
- **"Her princes have become like deer that find no pasture" (v6).** Exhausted animals with no strength to run. Zedekiah and his officers fled by night and were caught (Jer 52:7–8).
- **"Jerusalem remembers… all the precious things that were hers from days of old" (v7).** Memory makes grief sharper. **"They mocked at her downfall."** The Hebrew word for "downfall" (*mishbat*) is rare and sounds like "sabbath." Older translations rendered it "mocked at her sabbaths," which the King James follows. JFB connects it with the seventy years of exile that gave the land its neglected sabbath rests (2 Chr 36:21). Most modern translations read it as "ceasing" or "ruin."
- **"Jerusalem sinned grievously; therefore she became filthy" (v8).** The Hebrew for "filthy" here is a rare form. The major Hebrew lexicon (BDB) takes it as the same word as v17's *niddah*, the ritual impurity of a woman during her period, which kept her separate from worship (Lev 15:19–24). Some translations take it from a verb meaning "to shake the head" and render "an object of scorn" (BSB). Both fit: she is unclean, and people shake their heads at her. **"They have seen her nakedness"**: the shame of a woman stripped in public, the same image the prophets used for punishment of covenant unfaithfulness (Jer 13:22, 26; Ezek 16:37).
- **"Her uncleanness was in her skirts; she took no thought of her future" (v9).** She never considered where her sin was leading. Moses had prayed that Israel would "discern their latter end" (Deut 32:29). **"O LORD, behold my affliction, for the enemy has triumphed!"** For the first time, the woman herself breaks into the narrator's speech, with a cry to God.
- **"She has seen the nations enter her sanctuary, those whom you forbade to enter your congregation" (v10).** The law kept Ammonites and Moabites out of the assembly (Deut 23:3), and only priests could enter the holy place. Now Babylonian soldiers walk into the Holy of Holies and carry off its treasures (Jer 52:17–23). Ammon and Moab joined the gloating (Ezek 25:3, 8).
- **"They trade their treasures for food" (v11).** The famine of the siege (Jer 52:6) forced people to sell family heirlooms for a little bread. **"Look, O LORD, and see, for I am despised."** Her second short prayer.
- **"Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by?" (v12).** Here the city takes over the poem, speaking in the first person. She stops travelers on the road and asks them to look. **"If there is any sorrow like my sorrow… which the LORD inflicted on the day of his fierce anger."** "The day of the LORD" was the day Israel expected God to judge its enemies (Amos 5:18–20). Zion discovers that she has become its target.
- **"From on high he sent fire… he spread a net for my feet" (v13).** The images pile up: fire in the bones, a hunter's net, a person **"stunned, faint all the day long."** Jerusalem was literally burned (Jer 52:13).
- **"My transgressions were bound into a yoke" (v14).** The Hebrew verb is rare, but the picture is plain: her own sins are woven together into a yoke, the wooden bar that fastened oxen to a plow, and put on her neck. Jeremiah had worn a yoke through the streets to show Judah must serve Babylon (Jer 27:2; 28:10–14). Sin, which once seemed like freedom, has become slavery.
- **"He summoned an assembly against me" (v15).** The word for "assembly" (*mo'ed*) is the same word used for the appointed festivals of v4. Instead of a feast, God has appointed a gathering of armies. **"The Lord has trodden as in a winepress the virgin daughter of Judah."** Grapes were crushed underfoot in a stone vat, and the red juice was a familiar picture of blood. Isaiah uses it of God's judgment on Edom (Isa 63:1–6), and Revelation of the final judgment (Rev 14:19–20; 19:15).
- **"For these things I weep; my eyes flow with tears; for a comforter is far from me" (v16).** The Hebrew literally doubles the word: "my eye, my eye runs down with water." The worst pain is not the loss itself but the absence of anyone to comfort her.
- **"Zion stretches out her hands, but there is none to comfort her" (v17).** The narrator steps in for one stanza. Outstretched hands are a gesture of prayer and of begging. **"Jerusalem has become a filthy thing among them"**: the *niddah*, the ritually unclean woman people stay away from.
- **"The LORD is in the right, for I have rebelled against his word" (v18).** This is the theological center of the chapter. "In the right" is *tsaddiq*, "righteous." Zion does not accuse God of injustice. She agrees with his verdict. Confession like this is the beginning of hope, because it puts her back in honest relationship with the God who disciplines (compare Dan 9:7, 14; Neh 9:33).
- **"I called to my lovers, but they deceived me" (v19).** The allies of v2 again. Egypt's army had come and gone (Jer 37:5–11). **"My priests and elders perished in the city, while they sought food."** Even the leaders starved.
- **"Look, O LORD, for I am in distress; my stomach churns; my heart is wrung within me" (v20).** Hebrew places feelings in the body: the inner organs churn, the heart turns over. **"Because I have been very rebellious."** Again she owns her guilt. **"In the street the sword bereaves; in the house it is like death."** Moses' words: "Outdoors the sword shall bereave, and indoors terror" (Deut 32:25). No place is safe.
- **"They are glad that you have done it. You have brought the day you announced; now let them be as I am" (v21).** The enemies rejoice. Zion asks God to bring on them the same day of judgment he has brought on her. **"Let all their evildoing come before you, and deal with them as you have dealt with me" (v22).** This is a prayer for justice, not personal revenge. She asks God to be consistent: if he judges sin in his own people, he will surely judge it in the nations who gloated. The prophets promise exactly that (Jer 25:29; 50–51; Obad 15). **"My groans are many, and my heart is faint."** The chapter ends without an answer, still in pain.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws attention to the acrostic as grief expressed from A to Z, and notes that the writer, whom he takes to be Jeremiah, shows no hint of "I told you so," though he had warned the city for forty years. He insists the chapter blames neither fate nor history but the LORD's just judgment, and treats the prayer for the enemies' punishment (vv21–22) as zeal for God's glory, not private revenge. JFB agrees on that last point, saying such prayers are lawful when they concern "God's glory and His people's welfare." JFB quotes the eighteenth-century scholar Lowth on the book: "Every letter is written with a tear, every word the sound of a broken heart." On v12 JFB sees a foreshadowing of Christ, whom the words more fully suit, and on v18 calls Zion's confession "a sure sign of repentance." Matthew Henry: "Jerusalem became a captive and a slave, by reason of the greatness of her sins," and on v12, "Her outward sufferings were great, but her inward sufferings were harder to bear, through the sense of guilt." The Tyndale notes identify the "lovers" as the fertility gods Baal and Asherah and the "friends" as allies like Egypt.

#### Pointing to Christ
Christians have long heard Jesus in verse 12: "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look and see if there is any sorrow like my sorrow." Jerusalem suffered for her own transgressions. Jesus, outside the same city's walls, suffered for others, while those who passed by wagged their heads (Matt 27:39), and the yoke of sin that Zion wore (v14) he took on himself (Isa 53:6). The chapter's refrain is "none to comfort." The gospel answers it: God sends "another Comforter," the Holy Spirit (John 14:16, KJV), and Christ is "the consolation of Israel" whom Simeon waited for (Luke 2:25). Zion's honest confession, "The LORD is in the right, for I have rebelled" (v18), is still the way back to God. The one who confesses his sins finds the Lord "faithful and just to forgive" (1 John 1:9).
