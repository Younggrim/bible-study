*Setting:* Isaiah 10 ended with God felling the proud forest of Assyria, cutting down "the great in height" with an axe (10:33–34). Chapter 11 begins in that same picture of a forest of stumps. But this time new life grows from one of them. The house of David itself has been cut down to almost nothing: Ahaz has failed, and Isaiah foresees a day when David's royal tree will be felled (6:13; 39:6–7). Babylon ended the reigning line of David's kings in 586 BC. Out of that stump God will raise a King unlike any before him. Chapter 11 fills in the portrait of the child of 9:6–7: his character, his justice, the peace of his kingdom, and the great homecoming of God's scattered people. It is probably from late in Isaiah's ministry, after Assyria had devastated the north, when Israelites were already scattered among the nations.

#### The Righteous Reign of the Branch (vv1–16)
- **"There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse" (v1).** "Stump" (*geza'*) is what is left after a tree is cut down, close to the ground. Assyria was felled like a forest and would never rise again (10:33–34). David's house would also be felled, but its stump would sprout. Isaiah ended his call with the same hope: "the holy seed is its stump" (6:13).
- **Why "Jesse" and not "David"?** Jesse was David's father, a farmer in the small town of Bethlehem (1 Sam 16:1). Naming Jesse takes the royal family back to its humble beginnings before it had any crown. The new King will come not from palace grandeur but from obscurity, as David himself did, the youngest son out in the fields. Micah, Isaiah's contemporary, says the ruler will come from Bethlehem (Mic 5:2).
- **"And a branch from his roots shall bear fruit" (v1).** "Branch" here is *netser*, a green sprout. Many scholars think Matthew is thinking of this word when he says Jesus lived in Nazareth so that the prophets' words would be fulfilled: "He shall be called a Nazarene" (Matt 2:23). The town's name sounds like *netser*, and Nazareth was a tiny village, a fitting place for a shoot from a stump. Matthew says "prophets," plural, so he may be summing up a theme rather than quoting one verse. Other prophets use the related word *tsemach* for the same promised King (Jer 23:5; Zech 3:8).
- **"The Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him" (v2).** The Spirit came upon Israel's judges and kings for particular tasks, sometimes for a while and then departed (Judg 14:6; 1 Sam 16:13–14). This King will have the Spirit *resting* on him permanently. At Jesus' baptism the Spirit descended "and remained on him" (John 1:32–33), and Jesus read Isaiah's related words in Nazareth's synagogue: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me" (Luke 4:18; Isa 61:1).
- **"The Spirit of wisdom and understanding, the Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge and the fear of the LORD" (v2).** Three pairs. Wisdom and understanding are insight into how things really are, the gift Solomon prayed for (1 Kgs 3:9). Counsel and might are the ability to make right plans and the power to carry them out, echoing "Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God" (9:6). Knowledge and the fear of the LORD are a close relationship with God and a reverent obedience to him. Ahaz had none of these.
- **The "seven spirits."** Counting "the Spirit of the LORD" with the six qualities gives seven. The Greek Septuagint rendered "the fear of the LORD" in v2 as "godliness" and then added "the fear of God" in v3, giving seven distinct gifts. From this, later Christian tradition, especially in the Catholic church, spoke of "the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit." Many interpreters connect this with "the seven spirits who are before his throne" in Revelation (Rev 1:4; 5:6), meaning the Spirit in his fullness.
- **"His delight shall be in the fear of the LORD" (v3).** "Delight" is literally "his smelling," from a verb used for enjoying the scent of an offering (Gen 8:21; Amos 5:21). Reverence for God is like a sweet fragrance to him. Some take it as a keen "nose" for what is true and godly, so that he can sniff out the real thing from the fake.
- **"He shall not judge by what his eyes see, or decide disputes by what his ears hear" (v3).** Human judges can be fooled by appearances, rumors and clever arguments. Samuel was nearly fooled by Eliab's good looks before David was chosen: "the LORD looks on the heart" (1 Sam 16:7). This King sees the heart. John says Jesus "knew all people… he himself knew what was in man" (John 2:24–25).
- **"With righteousness he shall judge the poor, and decide with equity for the meek of the earth" (v4).** The opposite of Jerusalem's judges, who robbed the poor and took bribes (1:23; 5:23; 10:1–2). This King's courtroom is on the side of the weak. "Meek" means the lowly and afflicted. Jesus said, "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5).
- **"He shall strike the earth with the rod of his mouth, and with the breath of his lips he shall kill the wicked" (v4).** The King does not need Assyria's rod (10:5). His word is his rod. He speaks, and it is done. Paul draws on this verse for Christ's return: "the Lord Jesus will kill [the lawless one] with the breath of his mouth" (2 Thess 2:8). Revelation pictures a sharp sword coming from his mouth (Rev 19:15).
- **"Righteousness shall be the belt of his waist, and faithfulness the belt of his loins" (v5).** A belt held a long robe tight so a man could work or run. It was the first thing put on for action. This King is dressed in righteousness and faithfulness. They hold everything else together. Paul uses the same picture for the believer's armor: "the belt of truth" and "the breastplate of righteousness" (Eph 6:14).
- **"The wolf shall dwell with the lamb" (v6).** The scene changes from the King's justice to the peace of his kingdom. Natural enemies live side by side: wolf and lamb, leopard and young goat, lion and calf. "Dwell" is the word for a guest staying under someone's roof. "A little child shall lead them." The animals that killed the flock are herded by a toddler.
- **"The lion shall eat straw like the ox" (v7).** Predators become plant-eaters, as in the beginning, when every creature was given green plants for food (Gen 1:30). The curse that turned creation violent is undone. Paul says creation itself "waits with eager longing" to be "set free from its bondage to corruption" (Rom 8:19–21).
- **"The nursing child shall play over the hole of the cobra" (v8).** The deadliest danger in the field, a snake's hole, becomes a baby's playground. The serpent, the ancient enemy from Genesis 3:15, no longer harms. In Isaiah's later version of this scene, "dust shall be the serpent's food" (65:25).
- **Literal or figurative?** Christians read vv6–9 in different ways. Some take it literally, as a real transformation of the animal world in the age to come, whether in a millennial reign of Christ or in the new creation. Others read the animals mainly as pictures of people: violent and gentle nations, oppressors and victims, at peace under the Messiah's rule, which begins in the church where enemies become brothers. Many hold both together: the gospel's peace between people now is a foretaste of a renewed creation later. The text itself paints a world where all creation is put right.
- **"They shall not hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain" (v9).** "My holy mountain" is Zion, the mountain lifted up in 2:2, now pictured as the center of a world at peace.
- **"For the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD as the waters cover the sea" (v9).** The reason for the peace: everyone will know God. Not just information about him, but the personal knowledge Israel lacked (1:3; 5:13). The sea is not partly covered by water. Water *is* the sea, filling every depth. Habakkuk repeats this line (Hab 2:14), and it recalls the seraphim's cry: "the whole earth is full of his glory" (6:3).
- **"In that day the root of Jesse, who shall stand as a signal for the peoples" (v10).** Now he is called the *root*, not just the shoot. He is the descendant of Jesse and also the source of Jesse's line. Jesus calls himself "the root and the descendant of David" (Rev 22:16). The "signal," a banner on a hilltop, once summoned Assyria against Israel (5:26). Now it is the King himself, lifted high, drawing the nations to him. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 15:12 to show that God always meant the Gentiles to hope in Christ.
- **"Of him shall the nations inquire, and his resting place shall be glorious" (v10).** "Inquire" means seek him out for guidance, the opposite of seeking mediums (8:19). "Resting place" is the word used for the ark's resting place in the temple (Ps 132:8, 14). Where he dwells will be "glory," *kavod*, the glory that once filled the tabernacle.
- **"The Lord will extend his hand yet a second time to recover the remnant" (v11).** The first time was the exodus from Egypt. The second will be a new exodus, gathering God's scattered people from every direction. The list sweeps the known world: Assyria and Egypt (north and south), Pathros (Upper Egypt), Cush (the upper Nile, roughly modern Sudan), Elam (east of Babylon, in modern Iran), Shinar (Babylonia), Hamath (Syria) and "the coastlands of the sea," the islands and shores of the Mediterranean to the west. Jews really did end up scattered across all these places. In Acts 2, on the day of Pentecost, Jews "from every nation under heaven" heard the gospel in Jerusalem, many from these very regions (Acts 2:5–11).
- **"He will raise a signal for the nations and will assemble the banished of Israel… from the four corners of the earth" (v12).** "Four corners" is literally "four wings," the four directions. The same banner that draws the nations (v10) also gathers Israel. Jews and Gentiles come to the same King.
- **"Ephraim shall not be jealous of Judah, and Judah shall not harass Ephraim" (v13).** The northern and southern kingdoms had been rivals since the days of the judges and enemies since the kingdom split (1 Kgs 12). In chapter 7 Ephraim had marched against Judah. Under the shoot of Jesse the ancient feud ends. Ezekiel pictures the two sticks of Judah and Joseph joined into one in God's hand under one king, "David my servant" (Ezek 37:15–24).
- **"They shall swoop down on the shoulder of the Philistines… Edom and Moab… the Ammonites shall obey them" (v14).** The united people overcome their old neighbor-enemies. The "shoulder" of the Philistines is the slope of their coastal hills, and the reunited people swoop down on it like birds of prey. In the prophets these nations often stand for all the enemies of God's people. Amos 9:11–12 speaks of David's fallen booth restored and possessing "the remnant of Edom and all the nations who are called by my name," and James quotes that passage to explain Gentiles coming into the church (Acts 15:16–17). That suggests one way Christians have read the promise: the nations are won, not wiped out.
- **"The LORD will utterly destroy the tongue of the Sea of Egypt" (v15).** "Tongue" is a bay or gulf, probably the arm of the Red Sea crossed in the first exodus. God will dry it up again. "The River" is the Euphrates. God will split it with "his scorching breath" into seven shallow streams, so that people can cross "in sandals," without even getting their feet wet. The two great rivers that stood between Israel and its exiles, the waters of Egypt and of Assyria, will no longer block the way. Revelation pictures the Euphrates dried up too (Rev 16:12).
- **"There will be a highway from Assyria for the remnant that remains of his people, as there was for Israel when they came up from the land of Egypt" (v16).** The chapter ends with a road home. Isaiah returns to this highway again and again: "a highway shall be there, and it shall be called the Way of Holiness… the ransomed of the LORD shall return" (35:8–10; 40:3; 62:10). It was partly fulfilled when exiles returned from Babylon in 538 BC. John the Baptist's call, "Prepare the way of the Lord" (Matt 3:3; Isa 40:3), announced its fuller fulfilment.
- **Commentators:** All three read the shoot as Christ. JFB explains the "stem" as "literally, 'the stump' of a tree cut close by the roots," the house of David brought low, and notes that the branch is also "the root" (Rev 5:5; 22:16). It rules out Hezekiah, since he "was already come." Matthew Henry reads the chapter as moving from God's nearer deliverances to "a prophecy concerning Messiah the Prince," and outlines "his rise out of the house of David," "the justice and equity of his government," "the peaceableness of his kingdom" and "the accession of the Gentiles to it." He sees Hezekiah's good reign as a partial picture. Guzik stresses the humble origins in naming Jesse, says David's royal line was dormant for some six hundred years until Jesus came, and links the seven-fold Spirit to the seven lamps of the tabernacle lampstand and Revelation. On v3 Guzik and JFB both note the Hebrew idea of "scent," JFB rendering it "quick-scented in the fear of Jehovah," which the Hebrew verb supports. On vv6–9 JFB allows the animals "may be figures for men" but thinks "a literal change in the relations of animals" more likely. Guzik places it in Christ's future thousand-year reign. JFB argues that because the first restoration was literal, the second will be too, and locates Pathros, Cush, Elam and Shinar.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah gives us the clearest early portrait of the Messiah's character. He comes from a cut-down family in an unimportant town. The Spirit rests on him without measure. He sees hearts, defends the poor, and conquers by his word. Jesus grew up in Nazareth, the "branch town." The Spirit came on him and stayed. He judged the woman caught in adultery not by what the crowd saw but by what he knew, and he blessed the meek. One day "the breath of his mouth" will end all evil. Around him gather the nations and the scattered of Israel, enemies made one, as Paul says, "he himself is our peace, who has made us both one" (Eph 2:14). The peaceable kingdom where no one hurts or destroys is where his story is headed: a creation full of the knowledge of the LORD, and the Root of Jesse lifted up as a banner that draws the whole world home.
