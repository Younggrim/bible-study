*Setting:* Babylonia, in the months after Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC. The exiles had lost everything: city, temple, king and land. Their neighbors, Edom above all, were already moving into the emptied hill country of Judah and mocking it (ch 35). Ezekiel's earlier messages had pronounced doom on the same "mountains of Israel" because of the idols worshiped on them (ch 6). Now he speaks to those mountains again with the opposite message. The chapter has two halves. The first promises that the land will be reclaimed, replanted and repopulated. The second goes deeper: the people who defiled the land will be cleansed, given a new heart and filled with God's own Spirit. These verses, with Jeremiah 31:31–34, are the great Old Testament promises of the new covenant, and Jesus seems to have had them in mind when he told Nicodemus he must be "born of water and the Spirit" (John 3:5).

#### Prophecy to the Mountains of Israel (vv1–15)
- **"O mountains of Israel, hear the word of the LORD" (v1).** Ezekiel addresses the land itself, as he addressed Mount Seir in the chapter before. The two passages are deliberate mirrors: Edom's mountains will be desolate (35:3), and Israel's mountains will be restored. The hill country was the heart of Israel, where most of its towns and farms lay.
- **"The enemy said of you, 'Aha!' and, 'The ancient heights have become our possession'" (v2).** "Aha!" is a jeer of triumph (25:3; 26:2). "Ancient heights" (*bamot olam*) could mean "everlasting hills," a phrase from Jacob's blessing and Moses' blessing (Gen 49:26; Deut 33:15). The land that God promised forever had now, the enemies said, become theirs.
- **"Crushed you from all sides… the talk and evil gossip of the people" (v3).** The land was plundered and also slandered. The nations treated its ruin as proof that its God was weak.
- **"The mountains and the hills, the ravines and the valleys, the desolate wastes and the deserted cities" (v4).** The same list used in 6:3 to announce judgment now introduces comfort. Every part of the land is addressed.
- **"I have spoken in my hot jealousy against… all Edom" (v5).** God's "jealousy" (*qin'ah*) is not petty envy. It is the passionate commitment of a husband to his marriage and of an owner to what is his. "My land" is the key phrase: the nations took it "with wholehearted joy and utter contempt," but it was never theirs to take (Lev 25:23).
- **"I swear that the nations that are all around you shall themselves suffer reproach" (v7).** Literally "I lift up my hand," the gesture of taking an oath. The mockers will be mocked.
- **"But you, O mountains of Israel, shall shoot forth your branches and yield your fruit to my people Israel, for they will soon come home" (v8).** The land is told to get ready, like a house prepared for guests. **"Soon"** is literally "they are near to come." About fifty years later, in 538 BC, the first exiles did return (Ezra 1–2).
- **"For behold, I am for you" (v9).** God said to Seir and to the false shepherds, "I am against you" (35:3; 34:10). To the mountains of Israel he says the reverse. **"You shall be tilled and sown"** reverses the desolation.
- **"I will multiply on you man and beast, and they shall multiply and be fruitful" (v11).** This is the language of creation, "be fruitful and multiply" (Gen 1:28; 9:1). The return will be a kind of new creation. **"And will do more good to you than ever before."** The restoration will not just bring back the old days. It will be better.
- **"You shall no longer bereave them of children" (vv12–14).** The nations said the land "devours people." The same accusation was made by the faithless spies in Numbers 13:32: "a land that devours its inhabitants." In Ezekiel's day it seemed true: war, famine and plague had emptied it, exactly as the covenant curses warned (Lev 26:38; Ezek 5:17). The land was not the real cause. Israel's sin was. Now the land's bad name will be taken away.
- **"No longer cause your nation to stumble" (v15).** In v14 the written Hebrew reads "cause to stumble" and the traditional margin reads "bereave," which the ESV follows there. In v15 "stumble" stands. Either way, the land will no longer be a place of loss and disgrace.
- **How is this fulfilled?** The first fulfilment was the return from Babylon, when the land was resettled and the temple rebuilt. Christians differ on what comes after that. Many see the promises reaching their full meaning in Christ and the renewed creation, where the whole earth becomes God's people's inheritance (Matt 5:5; Rom 4:13). Others, especially dispensational interpreters, also expect a future, literal restoration of the land of Israel to the Jewish people, and some see the modern return and replanting of the land as a beginning of it. All agree the land is God's and the promises are sure.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the mountains are spoken to "as if they could hear," and that God answers the neighbors' plunder and mockery with jealousy for his land. JFB sees the mountains of Israel as standing "in contrast to 'Mount Seir,'" explains v3 as Edom panting after Israel's inheritance like "a beast after its prey," and links v13 to the spies' report in Numbers 13:32. Guzik, following Block, notes the wordplay that turns "I am against you" into "I am for you." He applies vv8–12 partly to the reforestation and farming of the modern state of Israel, which he calls only a beginning, and cites Mark Twain's 1867 description of a barren land as a contrast.

#### The Lord's Concern for His Holy Name (vv16–21)
- **"They defiled it by their ways and their deeds" (v17).** Now God explains why the land was emptied. The problem was not the land or the Babylonians. It was Israel.
- **"Like the uncleanness of a woman in her menstrual impurity" (v17).** The Hebrew *niddah* is the term from Leviticus 15:19–24 for ritual impurity during a woman's monthly period. In the law this was not sin. It was a natural state that kept a person away from the sanctuary for a time. Ezekiel uses it as a vivid picture of something that made Israel unfit to come near God's holy presence. The point is defilement, not moral shame for women.
- **"For the blood that they had shed in the land, for the idols" (v18).** Two sins sum up all the rest: murder and idolatry. The law said bloodshed pollutes the land (Num 35:33–34), and the land would "vomit out" a people who defiled it (Lev 18:24–28).
- **"I scattered them among the nations… in accordance with their ways and their deeds I judged them" (v19).** The exile was fair. It was what the covenant had warned would happen (Deut 29:22–28).
- **"They profaned my holy name… 'These are the people of the LORD, and yet they had to go out of his land'" (v20).** This is the surprise of the passage. Once in exile, the Israelites dishonored God's name not mainly by new sins but by being there at all. The nations reasoned that Israel's God must be weak, since he could not keep his people in his own land. "Profane" (*chalal*) means to treat something holy as common. God's reputation among the nations was tied to the condition of his people.
- **"But I had concern for my holy name" (v21).** The verb means to have pity on or to spare. God's concern for his own honor is the hinge that turns the chapter from judgment to hope.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Israel "gave a handle of reproach to the heathen against God," so that the nations judged Israel's God by Israel's exile, and reads the image of v17 as ritual uncleanness (Lev 15:19). Henry stresses that Israel's sins defiled the land and their exile profaned God's name. Guzik, citing Alexander and Taylor, says the scattering led the nations to think Israel's God was powerless, a mere tribal god, and God intended to show that he is Lord of the whole earth. Guzik applies this to the church, whose conduct also affects how the world sees God's name.

#### I Will Put My Spirit Within You (vv22–38)
- **"It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of my holy name" (v22).** This sounds harsh but is the ground of hope. If God's mercy depended on Israel deserving it, there would be none. Because it depends on his own name, it is certain. Psalm 106:8 says the same of the Red Sea: "he saved them for his name's sake." Grace, by definition, does not look for a reason in the one receiving it.
- **"I will vindicate the holiness of my great name… when through you I vindicate my holiness before their eyes" (v23).** "Vindicate the holiness" translates the verb *qadash*, "to show as holy." Israel's exile made God look weak. Israel's restoration and transformation will show him to be holy and powerful. Jesus taught his disciples to pray for this first: "hallowed be your name" (Matt 6:9).
- **A note on the manuscripts.** One very early Greek manuscript of Ezekiel (Papyrus 967, from around AD 200) lacks the end of verse 23 through verse 38. All the Hebrew manuscripts and the other ancient translations include them. Most scholars think the papyrus simply dropped the passage, while a few think the verses were added later. The ESV, like all major translations, includes them.
- **"I will take you from the nations… and bring you into your own land" (v24).** First the outward return. Then comes the inward change, which is the heart of the chapter.
- **"I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean" (v25).** The law used sprinkled water for purification, most famously the "water for impurity" mixed with the ashes of a red heifer and sprinkled on someone defiled by contact with the dead (Num 19:17–19; compare Ps 51:7). Here God himself does the sprinkling. **"From all your idols I will cleanse you."** The defilement of v17 is washed away. Hebrews draws on this picture: "our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water" (Heb 10:22).
- **"I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you" (v26).** In Hebrew thought the heart (*lev*) is not mainly the feelings. It is the center of the person: thinking, choosing and desiring. The "spirit" here is the inner disposition that drives a person. In 18:31 God commanded Israel to "make yourselves a new heart and a new spirit." Here he promises to give what he commanded. Augustine later prayed, "Give what you command, and command what you will."
- **"I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh" (v26).** A heart of stone is cold, hard and unresponsive to God, like the tablets of stone the law was written on, which could command but not change anyone. A heart of flesh is alive and responsive. Ezekiel gave a shorter form of this promise in 11:19. Paul draws the contrast: the new covenant is written "not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts" (2 Cor 3:3).
- **"I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes" (v27).** This goes further than anything before. In the Old Testament the Spirit came on particular people for particular tasks: judges, kings, prophets (Judg 3:10; 1 Sam 16:13). Here he is promised to dwell within all God's people, and the result is obedience. Jeremiah describes the same change as the law written on the heart (Jer 31:33). Ezekiel says the Spirit will do the writing. At Pentecost Peter announced that this had begun (Acts 2:16–18, 38), and Paul says that the Spirit lives in every believer (Rom 8:9).
- **"Born of water and the Spirit."** When Nicodemus could not understand Jesus' words about being "born again," Jesus said, "Are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things?" (John 3:10). Many interpreters think Jesus expected him to know this passage, where water and Spirit together bring the new heart.
- **"You shall be my people, and I will be your God" (v28).** This is the covenant formula, found from Exodus to Revelation (Exod 6:7; Rev 21:3).
- **"I will summon the grain and make it abundant" (vv29–30).** God speaks to the grain as a master calls a servant. The famines of the curse will end.
- **"Then you will remember your evil ways… and you will loathe yourselves" (v31).** It is grace, not judgment, that brings real repentance. When people see how good God has been to them, they see their sin clearly for the first time. This is not self-hatred that pushes people away from God. It is the shame of being forgiven (16:61–63; Rom 2:4).
- **"It is not for your sake that I will act… Be ashamed and confounded for your ways" (v32).** The chapter repeats v22 so the lesson sticks: grace leaves no room for boasting (Eph 2:8–9).
- **"This land that was desolate has become like the garden of Eden" (v35).** Eden is the picture of the world as God meant it. The restored land is a return to paradise. Isaiah says the same of Zion: "he will make her wilderness like Eden" (Isa 51:3). **"I am the LORD; I have spoken, and I will do it" (v36).** God stakes his name on the promise.
- **"This also I will let the house of Israel ask me to do for them" (v37).** Earlier God refused to be consulted by idolatrous elders (14:3; 20:3, 31). Now he invites prayer. God's promises do not make prayer pointless. They are what prayer asks for.
- **"Like the flock for sacrifices, like the flock at Jerusalem during her appointed feasts" (v38).** At Passover and the other great feasts, Jerusalem was packed with pilgrims and with countless sheep brought for sacrifice. The empty towns of Judah will be crowded like that, with "flocks of people," a callback to the sheep of chapter 34.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God's reasons for mercy come entirely from within himself, and Israel's unworthiness only makes his goodness shine more clearly. He describes the stone heart as "insensible and inflexible" and the new heart as "a soft and tender heart," and says God "causes them to walk in his statutes by an internal principle." JFB ties the sprinkling to the red heifer's water and calls the heart of flesh "impressible and docile, fit for receiving the good seed." He sees the reforms after the exile as only "an earnest of the full renewal." Guzik, following Wright, links v25 to Jesus' words to Nicodemus, and notes Block's observation that where Jeremiah speaks of God's law written within, Ezekiel speaks of God's Spirit within. He insists that grace does not make believers casual about sin (v31), and from v37, quoting Spurgeon and Poole, that God's promises are fulfilled through prayer, not in place of it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel saw that a return to the land would not be enough. The people needed new hearts, and only God could give them. That gift came through Jesus, who poured out the promised Spirit after his death and resurrection (John 7:39; Acts 2:33). Paul calls it the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit, poured out on us "richly through Jesus Christ our Savior" (Titus 3:5–6). And God still does it "for the sake of my holy name": not because anyone deserves it, but because he will show the world who he is by what he makes of the people who bear his name.
