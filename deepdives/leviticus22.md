*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Chapter 21 set standards for the priests' own bodies, families and mourning. Chapter 22 continues with the holy things they handled: who could eat the sacred food, and what kind of animals could be offered. The first half (vv1–16) is for the priests. The second half (vv17–33) is for "all the people of Israel" and the foreigners living among them, since anyone might bring an offering. Six times in chapters 21–22 God says, "I am the LORD who sanctifies," three of them in this chapter (vv9, 16, 32).

#### Holiness and the Priests, continued (vv1–16)
- **"So that they abstain from the holy things" (v2).** "Abstain" is literally "separate themselves." The holy things were the parts of the people's offerings that God gave to the priests as their food (6:16–18; 7:31–36). A priest who was unclean had to keep his hands off them, or he would **"profane my holy name."** Handling God's gifts carelessly was a way of treating God himself carelessly.
- **"Cut off from my presence" (v3).** The penalty is stated in an unusual way. It is not only "from his people" but "from my presence." A priest who presumed to eat holy food while unclean would lose the very nearness to God that his calling gave him.
- **What made a priest unclean (vv4–6).** The list gathers up the causes from chapters 11–15: skin disease, a bodily discharge, touching a corpse, a seminal emission, touching a **"swarming thing"** (*sherets*, small creatures that teem on land or in water, 11:29–31), or touching an unclean person.
- **"When the sun goes down he shall be clean" (vv6–7).** For lesser uncleanness the remedy was simple: wash, wait until evening, and then eat. Israel's day began at sundown, so cleanness came with the new day. **"Because they are his food."** The rules were strict, but God was not trying to starve his priests.
- **"He shall not eat what dies of itself or is torn by beasts" (v8).** Such meat had not been properly drained of blood (17:13–15). Ezekiel, himself a priest, later protests that he had never eaten it (Ezek 4:14; 44:31).
- **"Lest they bear sin for it and die" (v9).** Nadab and Abihu's deaths (10:1–2) gave this warning teeth. The priests in Malachi's day, centuries later, still despised God's table (Mal 1:6–7).
- **Who may eat (vv10–13).** The holy food belonged to the priest's household. A **"lay person"** (literally "a stranger," anyone not of a priestly family), a visiting guest or a hired worker could not eat it. But a slave bought by the priest, or born in his house, could, because he was part of the household and had no other food. A priest's daughter who married outside the priesthood ate at her husband's table instead. If she came home widowed or divorced and childless, **"as in her youth,"** she ate at her father's table again. Belonging to the household, not merely being in the house, decided who ate.
- **"If anyone eats of a holy thing unintentionally" (v14).** An ordinary Israelite who ate priestly food by mistake had to repay it **"and add the fifth of its value,"** the same 20 percent added in the guilt offering for misusing holy things (5:14–16). Even an accident against God's property was not shrugged off.
- **"Cause them to bear iniquity and guilt" (vv15–16).** The sense is debated. It probably means the priests must not let the people's offerings be treated as common food, which would bring guilt on the people as well. A careless priest made the whole nation's worship unclean.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "A careless or irreverent use of things consecrated to God tends to dishonor the name," and says priests were held to a higher standard, not exempted. On vv4–6 it adds that the washing marked restored cleanness, but "the water was ineffectual" to cleanse by itself. Guzik takes encouragement from the evening cleansing: uncleanness was not permanent, and each day could begin clean, so believers should confess their sin and move on rather than keep punishing themselves for yesterday. He also notes that slaves in a priest's house were treated as family members.

#### Acceptable Offerings (vv17–33)
- **"Any one of the house of Israel or of the sojourners" (v18).** Foreigners living in Israel could bring offerings to the LORD. The rules for acceptable animals applied to everyone equally.
- **"If it is to be accepted for you it shall be a male without blemish" (v19).** The key word in this section is "accepted" (vv19, 20, 21, 23, 25, 27, 29). The point of an offering was to be accepted by God on the worshiper's behalf (1:3–4). An animal with a **"blemish"** (*mum*, the same word used of priests in 21:17–23) would not be accepted.
- **The list of defects (v22).** Blind, disabled, mutilated, with a discharge, an itch or scabs. This closely matches the priests' list in 21:18–20. The offerer and the priest were both to bring God what was whole.
- **Freewill and vow offerings (vv21, 23).** A peace offering could be given to give thanks, to keep a vow, or simply as a freewill gift (7:11–16). An animal with **"a part too long or too short"** could be given as a freewill offering, but not to pay a vow. A vow was a debt already owed to God, and it had to be paid in full.
- **Castrated animals (vv24–25).** No animal with damaged testicles could be offered, and Israel was not to buy such animals from foreigners to offer either. **"You shall not do it within your land"** is often taken (by Jewish tradition and some modern interpreters) as forbidding the castration of animals in Israel at all, though the sentence may simply refer to offering them.
- **Malachi's complaint.** Many centuries later, after the exile, the prophet Malachi accused Judah's priests of exactly what this chapter forbids: "When you offer blind animals in sacrifice, is that not evil?… Present that to your governor; will he accept you?" (Mal 1:8). God deserves at least what people would give a human ruler.
- **"Seven days with its mother" (v27).** A newborn animal could be offered from the eighth day on, like the firstborn in Exodus 22:30. The week let it grow strong and showed whether it was healthy. The eighth day is also the day of a baby boy's circumcision (12:3), the day a full week has passed.
- **"You shall not kill an ox or a sheep and her young in one day" (v28).** The reason is not stated. Most see a lesson in tenderness, much like the laws against boiling a young goat in its mother's milk (Exod 23:19) and taking a mother bird with her young (Deut 22:6–7). God's law cares about the way his creatures are treated, even in sacrifice.
- **"A sacrifice of thanksgiving… eaten on the same day" (vv29–30).** A thanksgiving offering had to be eaten the day it was given, stricter than the two days allowed for other peace offerings (19:6; 7:15–16). A thanksgiving feast was meant to be shared generously and at once, not stored.
- **"I am the LORD who sanctifies you, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (vv31–33).** The closing gives the reason for all of it. God's name is holy; he means to be **"sanctified among the people of Israel,"** that is, honored as holy by how they live and worship; and he is the God who rescued them. Jesus taught his disciples to pray the same thing: "hallowed be your name" (Matt 6:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry on vows: "What was, before the vow, in our own power, as in the case of a free-will offering, afterwards is not." On v28 he thinks killing mother and young together "looked ill-natured towards the species." JFB grounds the unblemished rule in "a sense of natural propriety," since no one would give a king a damaged gift, and says animals "were not considered perfect nor good for food till the eighth day." Guzik links these laws to Malachi 1, notes that by Jesus' day the temple's inspection of animals had become a money-making business (Matt 21:12–13), and finds four motives for obedience in vv31–33: who God is, his holy name, his sanctifying work and his rescue from Egypt.

#### Pointing to Christ
"It shall be perfect to be accepted." No worshiper could bring himself to God that way, so he brought a perfect animal in his place. Every one of those animals pointed ahead to "a lamb without blemish or spot," Jesus Christ (1 Pet 1:19), who "offered himself without blemish to God" (Heb 9:14). Because his offering was accepted, believers are now "accepted in the Beloved" (Eph 1:6, KJV). And the chapter's question about who may eat the holy food has a new answer. The priestly food was for those who belonged to the household, by birth or by purchase. In Christ, people are both: born again into God's family and "bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:20). They are welcome at his table.
