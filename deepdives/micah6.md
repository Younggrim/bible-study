*Setting:* Judah, late 700s BC, probably in the years of Ahaz or early Hezekiah. Micah's third and final round of messages begins here, once more with "Hear" (6:1; compare 1:2; 3:1). Like the first two, it moves from judgment to hope, ending in one of the most beautiful passages in the prophets (7:18–20). Chapter 6 is set as a courtroom. God brings a lawsuit against his people, with the mountains as the jury. He reminds them of everything he has done for them, the people answer with a desperate question about what sacrifice would satisfy him, and the prophet gives the most famous answer in the Old Testament: "do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God." The chapter then turns to the marketplaces of Jerusalem, where rigged scales and lying tongues show how far the people are from that answer.

#### The Indictment of the LORD (vv1–5)
- **"Arise, plead your case before the mountains, and let the hills hear your voice" (v1).** The Hebrew word for "indictment" or "case" (*rib*) is a legal term for a lawsuit or formal dispute. Prophets often use this "covenant lawsuit" form (Isa 1:2; Hos 4:1; Jer 2:9). When God made his covenant with Israel, Moses called heaven and earth as witnesses (Deut 4:26; 30:19; 32:1), and Joshua set up a stone as witness (Josh 24:27). Ancient treaties called the gods of both nations as witnesses. Israel had one God, so creation itself testifies. The mountains were there when the covenant was made, and they have watched every year since.
- **"You enduring foundations of the earth" (v2).** The mountains are old and unchanging, the perfect witnesses. **"The LORD has an indictment against his people, and he will contend with Israel."** "Contend" is the verb form of *rib*. Henry: "Sin begets a controversy between God and man."
- **"O my people, what have I done to you? How have I wearied you? Answer me!" (v3).** This is the surprise. The plaintiff does not open with accusations but with a wounded question. God invites his people to testify against *him*. If they have a complaint, let them bring it. The words are tender, "O my people," repeated in v5. Jeremiah heard the same question: "What wrong did your fathers find in me that they went far from me?" (Jer 2:5). In the old Latin Good Friday service these words became the "Reproaches," sung as if from the cross: "My people, what have I done to you?"
- **"For I brought you up from the land of Egypt and redeemed you from the house of slavery" (v4).** God answers his own question by listing what he *has* done. "Redeemed" (*padah*) means to buy free, to pay a ransom. "House of slavery" is the phrase from the opening of the Ten Commandments (Exod 20:2). Every commandment rested on this act of rescue.
- **"And I sent before you Moses, Aaron, and Miriam" (v4).** God gave them leaders: Moses the lawgiver, Aaron the priest, and Miriam the prophetess who led the women in song after the Red Sea (Exod 15:20–21). It is notable that Miriam is named alongside her brothers as one of the leaders God sent. These were gifts. Compare the rulers and prophets of Micah's own day (ch 3).
- **"Remember what Balak king of Moab devised, and what Balaam the son of Beor answered him" (v5).** As Israel camped on the plains of Moab, King Balak hired the famous seer Balaam to curse them. Three times Balaam opened his mouth to curse and blessed instead (Num 22–24). God would not let his people be cursed. "Devised" is the same verb as in 2:1, 3: Balak plotted, as Judah's land grabbers plotted.
- **"And what happened from Shittim to Gilgal" (v5).** Shittim was Israel's last camp east of the Jordan. It was also where Israel sinned with the women of Moab and worshipped Baal of Peor, on Balaam's advice, and 24,000 died in the plague (Num 25:1–9; 31:16). Gilgal was Israel's first camp in the promised land, after God parted the Jordan, where the people were circumcised and kept the Passover (Josh 3–5). Between the two lay Israel's worst failure and God's greatest faithfulness. He brought them across anyway.
- **"That you may know the righteous acts of the LORD" (v5).** "Righteous acts" (*tsidqot*) here means his saving acts, the deeds that prove he has kept faith with them. The same phrase is used in Deborah's song (Judg 5:11). Israel's problem was not that God had failed them but that they had forgotten (Deut 8:11–18).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God reasons with us, to teach us to reason with ourselves," and "Hear now what he saith, for, first or last, he will be heard." JFB marvels that the supreme Lord would condescend to justify his dealings before human beings. He notes that Balaam died in Midian and never crossed to Gilgal. The phrase "from Shittim" points to Balaam's counsel, which led to Israel's sin there. Guzik's point is that Balaam could never make God curse Israel. The only way Israel came under a curse was by bringing it on itself through sin.

#### What Does the LORD Require? (vv6–8)
- **"With what shall I come before the LORD, and bow myself before God on high?" (v6).** The speaker is a worshipper, speaking for the people. The question sounds like the entry questions asked at the temple gate: "Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?" (Ps 15:1; 24:3). But here it comes from someone who has just been found guilty and wants to know the price of settling the case.
- **"Shall I come before him with burnt offerings, with calves a year old?" (v6).** The offers rise in value with each line. A year-old calf was a prime animal (Lev 9:3). A burnt offering was burned whole, nothing kept back.
- **"Will the LORD be pleased with thousands of rams, with ten thousands of rivers of oil?" (v7).** Now the numbers swell to royal size. Solomon offered thousands of animals at the temple's dedication (1 Kgs 8:63). Olive oil was used in grain offerings, but "rivers" of it is fantasy. The worshipper is bidding higher and higher, as though God were a merchant holding out for a better price.
- **"Shall I give my firstborn for my transgression, the fruit of my body for the sin of my soul?" (v7).** The final offer is the most terrible: a child. This was no mere figure of speech in Micah's day. King Ahaz had burned his own sons (2 Chr 28:3), and the king of Moab sacrificed his firstborn on the wall when he was losing a battle (2 Kgs 3:27). God's law condemned it absolutely (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31). Henry: "God hates robbery for a burnt-offering." The worshipper's whole idea of God is wrong. He thinks God wants to be paid, and that the more it costs, the better. Guzik, following Waltke, hears resentment in the question too: what more can God possibly want?
- **"He has told you, O man, what is good" (v8).** There is no secret. The answer has been given already, in the law and the prophets (Deut 10:12–13). "O man" is *'adam*, humanity in general. This is God's will for every human being, not only for priests or Israelites.
- **"And what does the LORD require of you" (v8).** "Require" is *darash*, "seek." The Chronicler uses the same word for people seeking God (2 Chr 26:5). Here God is the one seeking, and what he seeks is not a thing but a life.
- **"But to do justice" (v8).** *Mishpat* is the word Micah has hammered all through the book (3:1, 8, 9). It means treating others rightly, protecting the weak, giving each person their due. The land grabbers of chapter 2 and the bribed judges of chapter 3 had reversed it. Justice is something you *do*.
- **"And to love kindness" (v8).** "Kindness" is *chesed*, one of the richest words in the Old Testament. It means loyal love, faithful kindness, the steadfast love God shows to his covenant people (Exod 34:6; Ps 136). It is not enough to do kind things grudgingly. The Hebrew says to *love* *chesed*, to delight in it. Guzik makes the same point: don't merely show mercy; love doing it.
- **"And to walk humbly with your God" (v8).** "Humbly" translates a rare word (*hatsnea'*), found only here and in Proverbs 11:2 ("with the humble is wisdom"). BDB gives "be modest, humble," with a possible root sense of "retiring," so some translate "walk carefully" or "attentively." Either way, it is the opposite of the proud men "walking haughtily" in 2:3. "Walk with" is the language of a relationship. Enoch and Noah "walked with God" (Gen 5:24; 6:9). This is not a list of rules but a way of life lived in God's company.
- **Three prophets in one verse.** Micah's three phrases sum up the message of the eighth-century prophets. Amos cried for justice (Amos 5:24). Hosea pleaded for *chesed* (Hos 6:6). Isaiah called for humble faith before the Holy One (Isa 57:15; 66:2). The Talmud tells how the 613 commandments of the law were summed up by David in eleven (Ps 15), by Isaiah in six (Isa 33:15), by Micah in three, by Isaiah again in two (Isa 56:1), and finally by Habakkuk in one: "the righteous shall live by his faith" (Hab 2:4).
- **Is Micah rejecting sacrifice?** No. God himself commanded the sacrifices. The point is that offerings without obedience are worthless, the same teaching found in 1 Samuel 15:22, Psalm 51:16–19 and Hosea 6:6. Jesus quotes the same priority to the Pharisees, who tithed their spices and "neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness" (Matt 23:23).
- **Not a way to earn God's favour.** Verse 8 is not an easier price tag. The worshipper of vv6–7 was trying to buy God off, and v8 is not a cheaper bid. It describes the life of people who already belong to God, the redeemed of v4, responding to grace. And it is a standard no one keeps perfectly, which is why the book ends not with v8 but with God "pardoning iniquity" (7:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We must not appear before the Lord empty," but the sacrifices the people offer cannot take away sin. "One stream of the blood of Christ is worth ten thousand rivers of oil." JFB sees "what is good" as pointing beyond the law's sacrifices, which were shadows, to the good things of the Messiah. He calls justice and mercy the moral ends that God prefers to sacrifices, which are only means, and links the verse to Jesus' words in Matthew 23:23. Guzik quotes Spurgeon that true humility cannot be put on but grows from knowing who God is. Spurgeon lists many areas in which to walk humbly, from Bible reading to dealings with sinners.

#### Destruction of the Wicked (vv9–16)
- **"The voice of the LORD cries to the city" (v9).** The city is Jerusalem. God's voice calls out in its streets like Lady Wisdom in Proverbs (Prov 1:20–21).
- **"And it is sound wisdom to fear your name" (v9).** The Hebrew here is difficult. "Sound wisdom" (*tushiyyah*) is a wisdom word (Prov 2:7). The standard Hebrew text reads "will *see* your name," while the Greek and other versions read "*fear* your name." The ESV follows the latter. Either way, it is wise to take God seriously before it is too late (Prov 1:7).
- **"Hear of the rod and of him who appointed it!" (v9).** The "rod" is the punishment that is coming, and God is the one who appoints it. Isaiah calls Assyria "the rod of my anger" (Isa 10:5). Henry: "Every rod has a voice, and it is the voice of God that is to be heard in the rod of God." The Hebrew word (*matteh*) can also mean "tribe," so some translate "Hear, O tribe," but most follow the ESV.
- **"The treasures of wickedness in the house of the wicked, and the scant measure that is accursed" (v10).** "Scant measure" is literally "a lean ephah." The ephah was a standard dry measure for grain, roughly half a bushel. A merchant who shaved down his measuring basket gave less grain for the same price. The law commanded "a full and fair measure" (Deut 25:15), and Amos condemned those who "make the ephah small and the shekel great" (Amos 8:5).
- **"Shall I acquit the man with wicked scales and with a bag of deceitful weights?" (v11).** Before coins, payment was weighed out in silver on a balance, using stone weights carried in a bag. A crooked merchant kept two sets, a heavy one for buying and a light one for selling (Deut 25:13–16). "A false balance is an abomination to the LORD" (Prov 11:1). Archaeologists have found many Judean stone weights from this period marked with their value, and their actual weights vary. God's question answers itself: he cannot declare the guilty innocent (Exod 23:7). JFB says God cannot treat the impure as pure.
- **"Your rich men are full of violence; your inhabitants speak lies" (v12).** "Violence" (*chamas*) is the word that described the world before the flood (Gen 6:11). It is not only the rich. "Your inhabitants," the whole city, have made lying normal.
- **"Therefore I strike you with a grievous blow, making you desolate because of your sins" (v13).** Some versions read "I have begun to strike you," meaning the judgment has already started.
- **"You shall eat, but not be satisfied… you shall sow, but not reap" (vv14–15).** These are "futility curses," taken from the warnings of the covenant: "You shall carry much seed into the field and shall gather in little… You shall have olive trees throughout all your territory, but you shall not anoint yourself with the oil" (Deut 28:38–40; compare Lev 26:26). The people had gained wealth by cheating. Now their wealth will not satisfy, will not last, and will end up in enemy hands. Amos (5:11) and Haggai (1:6) use the same pattern. **"You shall tread olives, but not anoint yourselves with oil; you shall tread grapes, but not drink wine."** The whole year's work, and nothing to show for it.
- **"For you have kept the statutes of Omri, and all the works of the house of Ahab" (v16).** The bitter irony is that Judah would not keep God's statutes but faithfully kept Omri's. Omri founded the northern capital Samaria (1 Kgs 16:24) and a dynasty so strong that Assyrian records still called Israel "the house of Omri" long after his line was gone. His son Ahab brought in Baal worship and murdered Naboth for his vineyard (1 Kgs 16:30–33; 21). That is exactly the land grab Micah condemned in 2:1–2. The connection came through marriage. Ahab's daughter Athaliah married into Judah's royal family and brought Baal worship with her (2 Kgs 8:18, 26–27). The "statutes of Omri" may also mean his policies of power, wealth and foreign alliances.
- **"That I may make you a desolation, and your inhabitants a hissing" (v16).** "Hissing" means whistling in shock and scorn at a ruined city (Jer 19:8). Ahab's house was wiped out by Jehu (2 Kgs 9–10). Judah, having copied it, will share its fate. **"So you shall bear the scorn of my people."** The Greek translation reads "of the peoples," the scorn of the nations.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The same wickedness continued from one generation to another," and "No laws nor customs are of force against the divine command." JFB says Judah copied Israel's impiety in spite of their rivalry, and that bearing God's name without the reality only increases the shame. Guzik quotes C. S. Lewis's famous line that pain is God's megaphone to rouse a deaf world, and Boice on how, when a society breaks down morally, the wicked rise and the righteous are pushed aside. With Adam Clarke he identifies Omri's statutes as gross idolatry.

#### Pointing to Christ
"What have I done to you?" God asked, and the answer was nothing but good. Centuries later the Church set those words on the lips of Christ on the cross, because there God's people did their worst to him and he answered with love. The worshipper of v7 asked whether a firstborn son would pay for sin, and God's answer was no, never yours. Yet God himself gave his own firstborn, his only Son, "for the sin of the world" (John 1:29; Rom 8:32). And the life of v8, justice, steadfast love and humble walking with God, is the life Jesus lived perfectly. He "humbled himself" (Phil 2:8). He fulfilled what we could not, and his Spirit now makes that life grow in those who are his (Rom 8:3–4).
