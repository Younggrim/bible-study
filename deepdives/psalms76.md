*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A Psalm of Asaph. A Song." Psalm 76 celebrates a sudden, overwhelming victory in which God himself broke an enemy army outside Jerusalem, and the soldiers "sank into sleep" without lifting a hand. The Greek Old Testament adds to the title "a song concerning the Assyrian," and most interpreters, Henry and JFB included, connect it with the night in 701 BC when "the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians," and King Sennacherib went home to Nineveh (2 Kgs 19:35–36; Isa 37:36–37). If so, "Asaph" means one of the guild of singers descended from David's musician. Henry also suggests Jehoshaphat's victory, when the sons of Asaph led the singing (2 Chr 20:14–28). The Tyndale notes allow either a recent victory or a remembrance of earlier rescues. The psalm is a close companion to Psalms 46 and 48, which also tell of kings coming against Zion and being turned back. After Psalm 75 promised that God would judge at his set time, Psalm 76 shows him doing it. The psalm has four stanzas of three verses each, marked by two *Selahs*.

#### In Judah God Is Known (vv1–3)
- **"In Judah God is known; his name is great in Israel" (v1).** Not known by theory but by what he has done. Other nations had gods of stone. Judah had a God who acted (147:19–20). Judah and Israel together may reflect Hezekiah's time, when people from the northern tribes came to worship in Jerusalem after the north fell (2 Chr 30:1–11).
- **"His abode has been established in Salem, his dwelling place in Zion" (v2).** Salem is the old name of Jerusalem, the city of Melchizedek, "king of Salem" and priest of God Most High (Gen 14:18; Heb 7:1–2). It is related to *shalom*, peace. "Abode" (*sok*) means a thicket or lair, and "dwelling place" (*me'onah*) can mean a den. Both words are used of a lion's lair (10:9; Jer 25:38; Amos 3:4). Kidner and Boice, cited by Guzik, draw out the picture: God lives in Zion like a lion in its den, and whoever attacks his city will meet the lion. Isaiah used this very image for God's defense of Jerusalem against Assyria: "As a lion… growls over his prey… so the LORD of hosts will come down to fight on Mount Zion" (Isa 31:4). Guzik adds that the Puritan town of Salem in Massachusetts took its name from this verse.
- **"There he broke the flashing arrows, the shield, the sword, and the weapons of war" (v3).** "Flashing arrows" is literally "the flames of the bow" (*rishpei-qashet*), fiery arrows used to set cities and siege works alight, or arrows flashing like lightning. JFB: "literally, 'thunderbolts'" (78:48). God snaps the whole arsenal. Psalm 46:9 has the same scene: "he breaks the bow and shatters the spear; he burns the chariots with fire." **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** Henry: "In Judah God was known as he was not known in other nations, which made the favour the greater, inasmuch as it was distinguishing." He notes that the victory is said to happen "there," in Zion, because it came "in answer to the prayers which God's people there made to him" (2 Kgs 19:14–19; 2 Chr 20:5). "Public successes are owing as much to what is done in the church as to what is done in the camp." He adds that God's dwelling is a tabernacle, "a movable dwelling," so his presence must never be taken for granted.

#### Glorious Are You (vv4–6)
- **"Glorious are you, more majestic than the mountains full of prey" (v4).** "Glorious" (*na'or*) means shining, radiant. The "mountains full of prey" may picture the enemy armies as predators on the hills, gorged with plunder, or the mountains where God, the lion, has made his kill. JFB takes them as "great victorious nations, as Assyria." The Greek Old Testament reads "everlasting mountains," which reflects a slightly different Hebrew. Either way, God outshines them all. The Tyndale notes point out a wordplay: "glorious" (*na'or*) here and "feared" (*nora'*) in v7 use nearly the same letters.
- **"The stouthearted were stripped of their spoil; they sank into sleep" (v5).** "Stouthearted" (literally "mighty of heart") means the bravest warriors. The plunderers are themselves plundered. "Sank into sleep" is literally "they slept their sleep," the sleep of death (13:3; Jer 51:39). JFB: "died." The story of Sennacherib's army fits closely: "when people arose early in the morning, behold, these were all dead bodies" (2 Kgs 19:35).
- **"All the men of war were unable to use their hands" (v5).** Literally "did not find their hands." They reached for their weapons and could not.
- **"At your rebuke, O God of Jacob, both rider and horse lay stunned" (v6).** God does not need weapons. A word of rebuke is enough. The Hebrew line reads "both chariot and horse." "Lay stunned" (*nirdam*) means fell into a deep sleep, the word used for Jonah asleep in the ship's hold (Jonah 1:5). The verse echoes the Song of the Sea: "the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea" (Exod 15:1, 21). The Assyrian chariot corps was the terror of the ancient world (Isa 36:8–9), and it lay silent.
- **Commentators:** Henry contrasts God with "the great and mighty ones of the earth… mountains of prey, oppressive to all about them. It is their glory to destroy; it is thine to deliver." On v5 he says the enemy "have slept, not the sleep of the righteous, who sleep in Jesus." JFB reads "chariot and horse" as "those fighting on them." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, sees the stouthearted stripped in death.

#### But You Are to Be Feared (vv7–9)
- **"But you, you are to be feared! Who can stand before you when once your anger is roused?" (v7).** "You" is doubled for emphasis, as Clarke, cited by Guzik, notes. If an army of 185,000 cannot stand, who can? Nahum asked the same thing about Assyria's capital: "Who can stand before his indignation?" (Nah 1:6). Revelation asks it at the end: "the great day of their wrath has come, and who can stand?" (Rev 6:17).
- **"From the heavens you uttered judgment; the earth feared and was still" (v8).** The verdict came from heaven, and the whole earth fell silent, like a courtroom when the judge speaks. Compare 46:10: "Be still, and know that I am God."
- **"When God arose to establish judgment, to save all the humble of the earth" (v9).** Here is the purpose of the judgment. God's anger against the proud is the other side of his care for "the humble" (*'anvei-'erets*), the poor, meek and oppressed of the earth. Kidner, cited by Guzik, makes the point that the aim of judgment is to save the oppressed. The phrase is "all the humble of the earth," not only of Israel. Jesus said, "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5). **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If God be a consuming fire, how can chaff and stubble stand before him?" He calls God's people "the meek of the earth… the quiet in the land… that can bear any wrong, but do none," and says God "will, sooner or later, appear for their salvation, and plead their cause." JFB: "God's judgment on the wicked is His people's deliverance."

#### The Wrath of Man Shall Praise You (vv10–12)
- **"Surely the wrath of man shall praise you" (v10).** One of the boldest lines in the Psalms. Human rage against God and his people ends up serving his glory. Sennacherib's arrogance only made God's victory more famous (2 Kgs 19:21–28). Joseph told his brothers, "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Gen 50:20). Haman's plot ended on his own gallows (Esth 7:10). Above all, the fury that crucified Jesus accomplished God's plan of salvation (Acts 2:23; 4:27–28). Guzik and VanGemeren make these links. JFB: "Man's wrath praises God by its futility before His power."
- **"The remnant of wrath you will put on like a belt" (v10).** A difficult line. "Put on like a belt" is the verb "gird" (*chagar*). The ESV pictures God taking whatever wrath is left and wearing it, either the leftover rage of his enemies, now harmless as a trophy, or his own wrath, strapped on like a warrior's sword belt. The KJV reads "the remainder of wrath shalt thou restrain," meaning God limits how far human anger can go, which is how Henry, Morgan and Meyer (both cited by Guzik) take it. JFB gives both: "gird… Thyself, as with a sword… or as an ornament to Thy praise." Some modern versions read "the survivors of your wrath," and some scholars change the vowels to find place names here. The core meaning is secure: no wrath escapes God's control.
- **"Make your vows to the LORD your God and perform them" (v11).** Israel had made promises to God in the crisis, as people do (66:13–14). Now they must keep them. **"Let all around him bring gifts to him who is to be feared."** "All around him" may mean the surrounding nations. After the Assyrian defeat, "many brought gifts to the LORD to Jerusalem and precious things to Hezekiah king of Judah" (2 Chr 32:23), a verse JFB cites. "Him who is to be feared" is literally "to the Fear," a title for God (Gen 31:42, "the Fear of Isaac").
- **"Who cuts off the spirit of princes, who is to be feared by the kings of the earth" (v12).** "Cuts off" (*batsar*) is the word for harvesting grapes, cutting the clusters from the vine (Lev 25:5; Jer 6:9). God trims the pride of rulers like a vintager gathering grapes. "Spirit" may mean their arrogance or their very breath of life. JFB points to Daniel's word to Belshazzar about "the God in whose hand is your breath" (Dan 5:23). Psalm 2:10–11 gives the warning: "Now therefore, O kings, be wise… Serve the LORD with fear."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "as far as God permits the wrath of man to break forth at any time he will make it turn to his praise… and what will not turn to his praise shall not be suffered to break out." He adds, "Men must never permit sin, because they cannot check it when they will; but God can." On v11 he writes, "Our prayers and praises, and especially our hearts, are the presents we should bring." Trapp, cited by Guzik, tells the old story of a Scottish queen who was said to fear John Knox's prayers more than an army. Horne, cited by Guzik, reminds readers to be more thankful for eternal redemption than for any earthly deliverance.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 76 shows God in Zion like a lion, breaking weapons, stilling armies, and rising to judge "to save all the humble of the earth." At the cross, the wrath of man reached its peak against God's Son, and God made that very wrath praise him by turning it into the means of salvation (Acts 4:27–28). Jesus is "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" who has conquered (Rev 5:5), and he triumphed over the powers not with sword and shield but by his death and resurrection (Col 2:15). The meek who trust him will inherit the earth, and the kings of the earth will one day bow before him.
