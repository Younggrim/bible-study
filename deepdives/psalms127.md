*Setting:* Psalm 127 is the eighth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. It sits at the center of the collection, with seven songs before it and seven after. It is the only Song of Ascents with Solomon's name in the title, and one of only two psalms linked to him (with Psalm 72). Solomon was David's son, king of Israel from about 970 to 930 BC, and the builder of the first temple. Some take the title to mean the psalm was written by Solomon, others that it was written for him or about him, perhaps by David. The psalm fits Solomon well. It speaks of building a house (the word can mean a temple, a palace or a family), guarding a city, and the gift of sons, and it uses the word "beloved" that recalls Solomon's other name, Jedidiah. Pilgrims singing it on the road to Jerusalem were walking toward a house the LORD had built, in a city the LORD guarded, often with their children beside them. The psalm has two halves: work and rest (vv1–2), and children as God's gift (vv3–5). The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Unless the LORD Builds the House (vv1–2)
- **"Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain" (v1).** The verse does not tell anyone to stop building. Builders are still at work. It says that their work will not hold unless the LORD is the true builder. "House" (*bayit*) is a wide word in Hebrew. It can mean a building, the temple ("the house of the LORD"), a royal dynasty ("the house of David"), or a household and family. All of these may be in view. God had told David, "the LORD will make you a house" (2 Sam 7:11), meaning a royal line, and then said Solomon would "build a house for my name" (2 Sam 7:13), meaning the temple. Solomon's temple was later burned by Babylon, and his dynasty split under his son. Only what God builds lasts.
- **"In vain" (vv1–2).** The word *shaw'* means empty, worthless, futile. It appears three times in two verses. It is the same word used in "You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain" (Exod 20:7). The repetition hammers home the point: work done apart from God comes to nothing.
- **"Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain" (v1).** Ancient cities posted sentries on the walls at night to watch for enemies, fires and thieves (Isa 62:6; Song 3:3). The watchman's work mattered, but the city's safety finally depended on the LORD, "he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (Ps 121:4). The city of Edinburgh in Scotland took its motto from the Latin of this verse, *Nisi Dominus Frustra*, "Unless the Lord, in vain."
- **"It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest" (v2).** This describes a person working from before dawn until late at night, anxious that if they stop, everything will fall apart. The problem is not hard work. Proverbs praises diligence and warns against laziness (Prov 6:6–11). The problem is work driven by worry and done as if everything depended on oneself.
- **"Eating the bread of anxious toil" (v2).** The Hebrew is literally "the bread of pains" (*'atsabim*). It is related to the word used in the curse on Adam: "in pain you shall eat of it all the days of your life" (Gen 3:17). This is bread earned with sorrow and eaten with worry. Jesus said the same thing in another way: "do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat... your heavenly Father knows that you need them all" (Matt 6:25, 32).
- **"For he gives to his beloved sleep" (v2).** "His beloved" (*yedido*) is very close to Jedidiah, "beloved of the LORD," the name God gave Solomon at his birth through the prophet Nathan (2 Sam 12:24–25). The psalm seems to play on it: the one God loves does not have to stay up all night. Sleep is a sign of trust, as David sang, "In peace I will both lie down and sleep; for you alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety" (Ps 4:8).
- **Two readings of the last line.** The Hebrew can also be read "he gives to his beloved *while he sleeps*" (as the NASB does). On that reading, God keeps working and providing even while his people rest, so their anxious overwork adds nothing. Both readings say much the same: God, not worry, is the source of provision and rest.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "we must depend upon God's blessing and not our own contrivance," and that "bodily sleep is God's gift to his beloved." JFB states the psalm's theme as "that human enterprises only succeed by the divine blessing," and suggests Solomon wrote it with the building of the temple in mind, and that it may later have been taken up for the rebuilding of the second temple. Guzik insists that the psalm does not condemn hard work, only trust in one's own effort, and notes with Trapp and Boice the link between "his beloved" and the name Jedidiah. He also passes on Clarke's observation that the Hebrew words for son (*ben*), daughter (*bat*) and house (*bayit*) were traditionally connected to the verb "build" (*banah*). The study notes make a related point: "builders" (*bonim*) in v1 and "children" (*banim*) in v3 sound alike, tying the two halves of the psalm together.

#### Children Are a Heritage from the LORD (vv3–5)
- **"Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD" (v3).** "Children" here is literally "sons" (*banim*), the usual Hebrew way of speaking of offspring generally. "Heritage" (*nachalah*) is the word used for the land each tribe received from God as its inheritance. Children are not earned or manufactured. They are a gift entrusted by God. Rachel's cry, "Give me children, or I shall die!" was answered by Jacob, "Am I in the place of God?" (Gen 30:1–2). Hannah received Samuel as an answer to prayer (1 Sam 1:19–20).
- **"The fruit of the womb a reward" (v3).** "Reward" (*sakar*) means wages or a gift given in return. The point is not that children are payment for good behavior, but that they are a blessing from God's hand. The connection with vv1–2 is clear: building a family, like building a house, is finally God's work.
- **"Like arrows in the hand of a warrior are the children of one's youth" (v4).** Children born when parents are young will be grown and strong while the parents are still active, and able to stand with them when the parents grow old. Arrows are useful because they are shaped, straightened and aimed. A warrior did not leave his arrows lying around but kept them close and prepared. The picture suggests both the strength children give a family and the care that goes into raising them.
- **"Blessed is the man who fills his quiver with them!" (v5).** A quiver held a limited number of arrows. "Blessed" (*'ashre*) is the word that opens Psalm 1 and Psalm 128: deeply happy, favored. The psalm speaks of a large family as a blessing, as most of the ancient world did. It does not say that a small family, or a childless couple, is cursed. Scripture honors many who had no children, and Isaiah promises the eunuch who holds fast to God "a name better than sons and daughters" (Isa 56:4–5).
- **"He shall not be put to shame when he speaks with his enemies in the gate" (v5).** The city gate was where business was done, cases were judged and elders sat (Ruth 4:1–11; Prov 31:23). A man standing in court or in a dispute with grown sons beside him was not easily pushed around. The picture is legal and social, not mainly military. The sons are his support and defense in public life.
- **The irony of Solomon.** If Solomon wrote or received this psalm, his own life shows how easily its lesson is lost. He built magnificently, but in his later years his many wives "turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kings 11:3–4). Of his household, only one son, Rehoboam, is named in Scripture, and it was under him that the kingdom split (1 Kings 12).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls children "blessings and not burdens" and says a large family "is like a quiver full of arrows." He is honest, though, that "these arrows in the hand too often prove arrows in the heart, a constant grief to their godly parents." JFB reads the gate as the "place of public business," with sons defending their father in legal disputes as well as in war. Guzik notes that arrows have to be shaped, aimed and released, and passes on Trapp's point that arrows are made by skill, not by growth, so children need careful and prayerful shaping. He also mentions, with Kidner, the sad gap between this psalm and Solomon's own household.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 127 says that unless the LORD builds the house, the builders work in vain. God promised David that his son would build a house, and that God would build David a house in return, a throne that would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16). Solomon's house did not last. Jesus is the true Son of David and the true builder. "I will build my church," he said, "and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (Matt 16:18). He is the "beloved Son" with whom the Father is well pleased (Matt 3:17). Because believers are in him, they too are God's beloved, and they can lie down and sleep, trusting that the Father knows their needs. Jesus even slept in the boat in the middle of the storm (Mark 4:38). Hebrews says Christ is faithful "over God's house as a son. And we are his house" (Heb 3:6). Through him God gives a heritage greater than any family line: "to all who did receive him... he gave the right to become children of God" (John 1:12).
