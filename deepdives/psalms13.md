*Setting:* The title reads simply "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David." No occasion is given, and Matthew Henry thought that was fitting: the psalm can be prayed by any believer in a long, dark stretch. He called it "the deserted soul's case and cure." David knew many such stretches, especially the years on the run from Saul, when he once said, "I shall now perish one day by the hand of Saul" (1 Sam 27:1). Psalm 13 is one of the shortest laments in the Psalter, only six verses, and one of the most carefully built. It falls into three equal parts that climb like steps: a complaint to God (vv1–2), a petition to God (vv3–4), and a confession of trust and praise (vv5–6). The first part has four "How long?" questions. The second has three "lest" clauses. The last has three "I" statements of faith. Nothing outward changes in the psalm. What changes is where the psalmist is looking. Psalms 9–12 asked God to act against the wicked. Psalm 13 is more personal. The worst thing is not the enemy but God's apparent silence.

#### How Long, O LORD? (vv1–2)
- **"How long, O LORD?" (v1).** The question comes four times in two verses. Each one adds weight. The psalmist's trouble is not only its size but its length. Many people can bear a sharp pain for a short time. It is the long, slow ache that wears faith down. "How long?" is also one of the most faithful questions in the Bible. It assumes the trouble *will* end and that God is the one who decides when. The souls under the altar in heaven still ask it (Rev 6:10).
- **"Will you forget me forever?" (v1).** Psalm 9:12 said God "does not forget the cry of the afflicted," and 10:12 prayed "forget not the afflicted." Here the psalmist feels exactly what those verses deny. The Psalms do not cover over the gap between what we know about God and what we feel. God's own answer elsewhere is, "Can a woman forget her nursing child...? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you" (Isa 49:15).
- **"How long will you hide your face from me?" (v1).** God's face turned toward someone means favor and blessing (4:6; Num 6:25–26). His face hidden feels like abandonment (27:9; 30:7; 88:14). The second "how long" is the deepest one. Being forgotten is bad, but feeling that God has deliberately turned away is worse.
- **"How long must I take counsel in my soul" (v2).** The third "how long" turns inward. He is making plans in his head all day, thinking, worrying, going round in circles. When God seems silent, the natural thing is to try to solve everything alone, and it does not work. JFB: "The counsels or devices of his heart afford no relief."
- **"And have sorrow in my heart all the day?" (v2).** Sorrow has become the steady background of every hour.
- **"How long shall my enemy be exalted over me?" (v2).** Only in the fourth question does the enemy appear. Notice the order: God first (v1), then self (v2a), then the enemy (v2b). The real pain is distance from God. The enemy is the last and smallest of the problems.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this a real, honest complaint that God had "withdrawn from him." Guzik makes a helpful distinction: God had not actually forgotten David, but David felt forgotten, and feelings are real but are not always facts. He passes on Spurgeon that the repeated question shows "very intense desire for deliverance, and great anguish of heart," and notes that people often faint under the length of a trial more than its weight. He also says David's turning inward to "take counsel in his soul" only made things worse.

#### Consider and Answer Me (vv3–4)
- **"Consider and answer me, O LORD my God" (v3).** The complaint becomes a request. "Consider" means "look!" It answers v1 directly: you have hidden your face, so turn and look at me. "Answer" asks God to break his silence. And now he says "my God." In vv1–2 he only said "O LORD." He is still in the dark, but he holds on to the relationship.
- **"Light up my eyes" (v3).** Dim eyes were a sign of failing strength, sickness or approaching death (6:7; 38:10; Deut 34:7, where Moses' eye "was undimmed"). "Light up my eyes" means revive me, give me back life and strength. There is a picture of it in 1 Samuel 14:27–29: the exhausted Jonathan ate a little honey "and his eyes became bright." The prayer may also mean "help me see clearly," since grief clouds the way a person sees everything.
- **"Lest I sleep the sleep of death" (v3).** "Sleep" is a gentle Bible word for death (Dan 12:2; John 11:11–13; 1 Thess 4:13). He fears that if God does not act, he will not survive this.
- **"Lest my enemy say, 'I have prevailed over him'" (v4).** The three "lest" clauses build: lest I die, lest my enemy boast, lest my foes celebrate. His concern is not only for himself. If he falls, his enemy will claim that God's servant trusted God for nothing. God's honor is bound up with the fate of those who trust him (compare Moses' argument in Num 14:13–16).
- **"Lest my foes rejoice because I am shaken" (v4).** "Shaken" (*mot*) is the word the wicked man used in 10:6, "I shall not be moved." He boasted that he would never be shaken. The believer here admits that he can be. The same verb returns in 16:8, where David says that with God at his right hand "I shall not be shaken." Steadiness is not a matter of strength but of whom one is holding.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains "lighten mine eyes" as eyes dimmed by weakness, a sign of approaching death, and notes that "rejoice" in v4 literally means "shout as in triumph." Guzik sees the doubled request in v3 as the mark of a desperate prayer, and links "enlighten my eyes" to Paul's prayer that the eyes of believers' hearts would be enlightened (Eph 1:17–19). He passes on Kidner's remark that awareness of God and of the enemy "is virtually the hallmark of every psalm of David."

#### But I Have Trusted in Your Steadfast Love (vv5–6)
- **"But I have trusted in your steadfast love" (v5).** The turn comes with one word: "But." Nothing in the circumstances has changed. No enemy has fallen. God has not visibly answered. Yet the psalmist plants his feet. "I have trusted" is a perfect tense in Hebrew: I have trusted and I still do. It looks back over a lifetime. The ground of the trust is God's *chesed*, his steadfast, loyal covenant love, which does not depend on how the moment feels (see 5:7; 6:4).
- **"My heart shall rejoice in your salvation" (v5).** In v2 his heart was full of sorrow "all the day." Now the same heart is told to rejoice. This is not denial of the pain. It is faith speaking to feelings instead of letting feelings speak to faith. "Salvation" here means rescue, deliverance, though for Christians it also points to the larger salvation God brings.
- **"I will sing to the LORD" (v6).** The psalm that began with a groan ends with a song. The future tense matters: he will sing even before he sees the answer.
- **"Because he has dealt bountifully with me" (v6).** "Dealt bountifully" (*gamal*) means to deal fully with someone, to repay or reward generously. The same verb appears in 116:7: "Return, O my soul, to your rest; for the LORD has dealt bountifully with you." When he looks back, the record is one of God's generosity. Memory of past grace becomes fuel for present trust. The verb is in the past tense, but it may also be a confident statement about the answer as though it had already come. Henry reads it this way, saying David regards his deliverance as "as good as wrought."
- **An extra line in the Greek.** The ancient Greek translation adds a line at the end: "and I will sing praise to the name of the LORD Most High," borrowed from 7:17. It is not in the Hebrew, and modern translations leave it out.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "Trust is followed by rejoicing in the deliverance which God effects." Guzik notes that David moves from being ruled by his feelings to directing them, telling his own heart to rejoice. He passes on Boice: "The fact that we feel abandoned itself means that we really know God is there," and Gurnall's comment that David "begins his prayer as if he thought God would never give him a kind look more" and ends singing. Henry calls the close an "answer of peace" received by faith before it is seen.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 13 gives words for the long night when God feels far away. Jesus went into that night more deeply than anyone. On the cross he did not only feel forsaken. He bore the forsakenness that sin deserves, crying "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). Like this psalm, his cry still said "my God." And like this psalm, his story did not end in the sleep of death. God raised him, and his enemies did not get to say "I have prevailed over him." Because Jesus was forsaken in their place, those who trust him are never truly forgotten (Heb 13:5). Their "how long?" is real, but it has an answer, and their last word, like David's, will be a song of how bountifully the LORD has dealt with them.
