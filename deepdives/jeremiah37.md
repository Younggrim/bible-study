*Setting:* After two chapters set in Jehoiakim's reign, the story returns to Zedekiah and the final siege, and from here to chapter 39 it runs in order to the fall of Jerusalem. The time is 588 BC. Babylon's army has surrounded the city, and then, unexpectedly, it marches away, because Pharaoh Hophra of Egypt has sent an army north to help Judah. Jerusalem dares to hope. This chapter fills in the background to chapters 32–34: how Jeremiah came to be a prisoner in the "court of the guard," and why he and King Zedekiah keep meeting in secret. Zedekiah is one of the saddest figures in the Bible. He wants to hear from God, he half believes Jeremiah, and he never has the courage to obey.

#### Jeremiah Warns Zedekiah (vv1–10)
- **"Zedekiah the son of Josiah, whom Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon made king… reigned instead of Coniah the son of Jehoiakim" (v1).** Coniah is a shortened form of Jehoiachin (also called Jeconiah), Zedekiah's nephew, who reigned three months in 597 BC before being taken to Babylon (2 Kgs 24:8–17). Many Judeans still thought of the exiled Jehoiachin as the true king, and Babylonian records show he was still alive and receiving food rations in Babylon years later (52:31–34). Zedekiah was Babylon's appointee, and his position was weak from the start. Nebuchadnezzar renamed him from Mattaniah (2 Kgs 24:17).
- **"But neither he nor his servants nor the people of the land listened to the words of the LORD" (v2).** A one-line summary of his eleven-year reign, echoing the verdict of 2 Kings 24:19–20 and 2 Chronicles 36:12: "He did not humble himself before Jeremiah the prophet, who spoke from the mouth of the LORD."
- **"King Zedekiah sent Jehucal the son of Shelemiah, and Zephaniah the priest, the son of Maaseiah… 'Please pray for us'" (v3).** Zephaniah was the second priest, sent on a similar errand in 21:1–2, and the man who read Shemaiah's letter to Jeremiah (29:25–29). Jehucal later joined the officials who demanded Jeremiah's death (38:1–4). In 2005 a clay seal impression reading "Yehukal son of Shelemyahu son of Shovi" was found in excavations in the City of David, very likely from this official's own seal. Zedekiah wants prayer, but not the message that comes with it. Earlier God had told Jeremiah not to pray for this people (7:16; 14:11).
- **"Jeremiah was still going in and out among the people, for he had not yet been put in prison" (v4).** A note to prepare for vv11–16.
- **"The army of Pharaoh had come out of Egypt" (v5).** Pharaoh Hophra (Greek Apries) reigned about 589–570 BC (44:30). Zedekiah had rebelled against Babylon trusting in Egyptian help (Ezek 17:15). One of the Lachish Letters, written during this crisis, reports that "the commander of the army, Coniah son of Elnathan, has gone down to go to Egypt," which may reflect Judah's efforts to bring in Egyptian help. **"They withdrew from Jerusalem."** The siege was lifted to meet the threat. For a few weeks Jerusalem was free.
- **"Pharaoh's army that came to help you is about to return to Egypt, to its own land" (v7).** God's answer to the request for prayer. Egypt would not save them. Isaiah had warned Hezekiah's generation the same: "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help" (Isa 31:1). Ezekiel, prophesying in Babylon in the same year, called Egypt "a staff of reed" that splinters in the hand that leans on it (Ezek 29:6–7).
- **"The Chaldeans shall come back and fight against this city. They shall capture it and burn it with fire" (v8).** The same message Jeremiah had preached for years.
- **"Do not deceive yourselves, saying, 'The Chaldeans will surely go away from us'" (v9).** Literally "do not lift up your souls," that is, do not raise false hopes. The greatest danger was not the Babylonian army but self-deception.
- **"Even if you should defeat the whole army of Chaldeans… and there remained of them only wounded men, every man in his tent, they would rise up and burn this city" (v10).** A deliberate exaggeration to make the point. Even a defeated army of wounded men, lying in their tents, would be enough, because God himself had decided the city's fate. The outcome did not depend on military strength.
- **Commentators:** Henry observes, "It is common for those to desire to be prayed for who will not be advised," and on v9 writes that "Satan himself, though he is the great deceiver, could not deceive us if we did not deceive ourselves." JFB notes that Judah "had the misfortune to lie between the two great contending powers, Babylon and Egypt," and explains that "Coniah" is a shortened, slighting form of the name, which is a guess the text does not confirm. Guzik, citing Trapp, sees Zedekiah as one who wanted prayer without obedience, and notes, with Harrison, that the Egyptian army seems to have gone home without a major battle.

#### Jeremiah Imprisoned (vv11–21)
- **"Jeremiah set out from Jerusalem to go to the land of Benjamin to receive his portion there among the people" (v12).** Benjamin was the tribal area just north of Jerusalem, where Anathoth, Jeremiah's home village, lay. The Hebrew phrase "to receive his portion" is difficult. It may mean to take part in dividing a family inheritance, or to see to property he owned. Many connect it with the field he bought from his cousin (32:6–15), though in the book's order that purchase is told later and dated later, during the renewed siege. Either way, his errand was private family business, not flight.
- **"When he was at the Benjamin Gate, a sentry there named Irijah… seized Jeremiah the prophet, saying, 'You are deserting to the Chaldeans'" (v13).** The Benjamin Gate was in the north wall, facing Benjamin and the road the Babylonians would take. The charge was believable. Jeremiah had publicly urged people to surrender to Babylon (21:9), and many did go over to the enemy (38:19; 39:9). Irijah's grandfather Hananiah may be the false prophet who died after opposing Jeremiah (28:16–17), which would give him a personal grudge, but the name was common and the link is uncertain.
- **"It is a lie; I am not deserting to the Chaldeans" (v14).** Jeremiah's denial is short and true. Urging others to surrender as God's will was not the same as running away himself. He stayed in the doomed city to the end.
- **"The officials were enraged at Jeremiah, and they beat him and imprisoned him in the house of Jonathan the secretary, for it had been made a prison" (v15).** These officials were not the friendly ones of chapter 36. Most of those had gone into exile with Jehoiachin in 597 BC, and the men who replaced them were hostile. Jonathan's house had been turned into a jail, perhaps because the regular prisons were full.
- **"When Jeremiah had come to the dungeon cells and remained there many days" (v16).** Literally "the house of the pit and the vaults." It was likely an underground cistern with small chambers cut into its sides, damp, dark and unhealthy. Jeremiah's fear that he would die there (v20) was real.
- **"King Zedekiah sent for him and received him. The king questioned him secretly in his house" (v17).** The king who would not stand up for Jeremiah in public still wanted to know what God said. He met him in private, out of sight of the officials he feared (38:5, 24–26). **"Is there any word from the LORD?"** He hoped for a new and better word. **"There is."** Jeremiah's reply is one word in Hebrew. **"You shall be delivered into the hand of the king of Babylon."** The message had not changed. A prisoner who had been beaten and starved might have been tempted to soften the news for the king who held his life in his hands. Jeremiah did not.
- **"What wrong have I done to you or your servants or this people, that you have put me in prison?" (v18).** Jeremiah is not passive. He protests the injustice of his treatment, as Paul later did in Philippi (Acts 16:37).
- **"Where are your prophets who prophesied to you, saying, 'The king of Babylon will not come against you'?" (v19).** A pointed question. The court prophets had promised safety (28:2–4). The Babylonians had come, and would come back. Those who told lies went free while the man who told the truth sat in a pit.
- **"Let my humble plea come before you and do not send me back to the house of Jonathan the secretary, lest I die there" (v20).** Courtly language, "my lord the king," and an honest plea for his life. Faithful prophets were not superhuman. Jeremiah was afraid and said so.
- **"They committed Jeremiah to the court of the guard. And a loaf of bread was given him daily from the bakers' street" (v21).** The court of the guard was an open courtyard in the palace complex, where he could receive visitors (32:2, 8, 12). In ancient cities people of one trade often worked together on one street, so Jerusalem had a "bakers' street." A round loaf a day was a lifeline in a city under siege. **"Until all the bread of the city was gone."** That day came. By the summer of 587 or 586 BC "the famine was so severe in the city that there was no food for the people of the land" (52:6). **"So Jeremiah remained in the court of the guard."** He would stay there, apart from his time in the cistern in chapter 38, "until the day that Jerusalem was taken" (38:28).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Zedekiah asked "secretly in his house, being ashamed to be seen in his company," and JFB agrees he was ashamed before his courtiers. Guzik, drawing on Morgan, reads the secret question as fear, fear that came from knowing, despite his weakness, that God's word was true. Guzik also calls Zedekiah a coward who feared his officials more than God, and cites Maclaren's description of him as a small man in a great moment. On the false charge, Henry writes that "it is no new thing for the church's best friends to be represented as in the interest of her worst enemies." JFB thinks Irijah was the grandson of the Hananiah of chapter 28, which is possible but not stated. JFB explains the dungeon as a pit with vaulted cells around it, and notes on v21 that people of the same trade commonly lived in one street. Guzik also stresses that Jeremiah's private word to the king matched his public preaching exactly, citing Cundall that after decades of faithful preaching his message did not soften under pressure.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah was falsely accused of going over to the enemy, beaten by the officials, thrown into a pit, and questioned in secret by a ruler who knew he was innocent and lacked the courage to free him. Each detail is echoed in the trial of Jesus. He too was charged with treason against his nation (Luke 23:2), beaten by the authorities, and questioned privately by Pilate, who asked "What is truth?" and knew he had done nothing wrong, yet handed him over because he feared the crowd (John 18:33–38; 19:8–16). Jesus, like Jeremiah, spoke the same word to rulers in private as he did in public. Unlike Jeremiah, he did not plead to be spared. He went into the pit of death for his people, and God raised him up.
