*Setting:* The dedication of the temple, autumn of about 959 BC, at the Feast of Booths. In chapter 5 the ark was carried into the Most Holy Place, the singers and trumpeters praised God "as one," and the cloud of God's glory filled the house so that the priests could not stand to serve. Now Solomon speaks: first a few words to God about the cloud, then a speech to the people, then his long prayer of dedication. The chapter follows 1 Kings 8:12–50 closely, often word for word. (The deep dive on 1 Kings 8 covers the prayer's petitions in detail.) But the Chronicler makes small, telling changes, and he gives the prayer a completely different ending, drawn from Psalm 132. Those changes speak directly to his first readers, who had come home from exile to a rebuilt but humble temple and no king on David's throne.

#### Solomon Blesses the People (vv1–11)
- **"The LORD has said that he would dwell in thick darkness" (v1).** Solomon is reacting to the cloud that has just filled the house (5:13–14). "Thick darkness" (*ʿarapel*) is the dark cloud on Sinai where God met Moses (Exod 20:21; Deut 4:11). God's presence is real but veiled. No one sees him directly.
- **"I have built you an exalted house, a place for you to dwell in forever" (v2).** "Exalted house" (*bet zevul*) means a lofty, princely dwelling. "Forever" expresses Solomon's hope. The Chronicler's readers knew the first temple had been burned, and they lived beside its replacement. God's promise to dwell among his people outlasted the building.
- **"The king turned around and blessed all the assembly" (v3).** Solomon had been facing the temple. Now he faces the people.
- **"Who with his hand has fulfilled what he promised with his mouth" (v4).** God's word and God's deed match. The phrase returns in v15.
- **"I chose no city… and I chose no man as prince… but I have chosen Jerusalem… and I have chosen David" (vv5–6).** Kings has a shorter form that mentions only David (1 Kgs 8:16). Chronicles spells out both choices side by side: one city and one family. For the Chronicler they belong together. Jerusalem and the temple remained even when David's line had no throne, and the promise to David remained even when the temple lay in ruins (Ps 132:13–14; 1 Chr 17:11–14).
- **"You did well that it was in your heart" (v8).** God would not let David build the temple, since he had shed much blood (1 Chr 22:8; 28:3). But he honored David's desire. A good purpose that God redirects is not wasted.
- **"I have risen in the place of David my father" (v10).** Solomon sees himself as living proof that God keeps his word (1 Chr 17:11–12).
- **"There I have set the ark, in which is the covenant of the LORD that he made with the people of Israel" (v11).** Kings adds "when he brought them out of the land of Egypt" (1 Kgs 8:21). The Chronicler leaves out the Exodus here, as he often does. He stresses the covenant itself, the tablets in the ark, which still bound his own generation to God.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Solomon's opening words were "evidently suggested by the remarkable incident" of the cloud. Guzik notes that Solomon gives God, not David or himself, the credit for what has been done. The Tyndale notes observe that Chronicles ties together Sinai (the ark), the temple and David's line as the three pillars of Israel's life with God.

#### Solomon's Prayer of Dedication (vv12–42)
- **"Solomon had made a bronze platform… and he stood on it. Then he knelt on his knees" (v13).** Only Chronicles mentions the platform, five cubits square and three high (about 7½ by 7½ feet, 4½ feet high), set in the outer court so all could see. The word for "platform" (*kiyyor*) is the same word used for the bronze basins (4:6). It may have been shaped like an upturned basin. Kings mentions only that Solomon rose from his knees at the end (1 Kgs 8:54). Chronicles shows him kneeling from the start, a king on his knees before the whole nation. He stays in the courtyard, by the altar, and does not enter the sanctuary, which belonged to the priests.
- **"There is no God like you… keeping covenant and showing steadfast love" (v14).** The prayer begins with who God is. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, his loyal covenant love (Deut 7:9).
- **"If only your sons pay close attention to their way, to walk in my law" (v16).** Kings has "to walk before me" (1 Kgs 8:25). Chronicles makes it "walk in my law" (*torah*). After the exile, the community rebuilt its life around the written law of Moses (Neh 8). The Chronicler makes it plain that faithfulness means obeying God's written word.
- **"But will God indeed dwell with man on the earth?" (v18).** Chronicles adds "with man," which Kings lacks (1 Kgs 8:27). The question becomes not only whether God can live on earth but whether he will live with people. Solomon's answer is that he cannot be contained: **"heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you."** Yet God chooses to set his name in a place and hear there. Revelation gives the final answer in nearly the same words: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them" (Rev 21:3).
- **"Listen from heaven your dwelling place, and when you hear, forgive" (v21).** This is the refrain of the whole prayer. Where Kings says God hears "in heaven," Chronicles several times says "from heaven" (vv21, 23, 25, 30). The point is the same: the temple is where people pray, but heaven is where God hears and acts.
- **Seven situations (vv22–39).** As in Kings, Solomon walks through times when Israel will need God: a disputed oath (vv22–23), defeat in battle because of sin (vv24–25), drought (vv26–27), famine, plague and siege (vv28–31), the foreigner who comes to pray (vv32–33), war (vv34–35), and exile (vv36–39). Many of these echo the covenant curses in Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28. In almost every case the request is the same: hear, forgive, restore.
- **"Each knowing his own affliction and his own sorrow" (v29).** Kings has "the affliction (literally 'plague') of his own heart" (1 Kgs 8:38). Chronicles speaks of each person's own pain and grief. The Tyndale notes highlight the sensitivity here to private sorrow as well as public disaster. God sees both.
- **"That they may fear you and walk in your ways" (v31).** Chronicles adds "walk in your ways" (compare 1 Kgs 8:40). Forgiveness is meant to lead to a changed life.
- **"When a foreigner… comes from a far country for the sake of your great name" (v32).** Solomon prays for non-Israelites who come to Israel's God, "in order that all the peoples of the earth may know your name" (v33). This hope runs from God's promise to Abraham (Gen 12:3) to Isaiah's "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isa 56:7), which Jesus quoted when he cleared the temple (Mark 11:17).
- **"For there is no one who does not sin" (v36).** A flat statement of universal sin (Eccl 7:20; Rom 3:23). Solomon expects Israel to fail. His prayer is built for sinners.
- **"In the land of their captivity" (vv37–38).** This was the petition that mattered most to the Chronicler's readers. Israel did go into exile, and in Babylon people like Daniel prayed toward Jerusalem (Dan 6:10; 9:3–19). "Turn their heart… and repent" plays on the Hebrew *shuv*, to turn back. When they returned to God in their hearts, God returned them to the land (Ezra 1:1–5). The book's readers were the answer to this prayer.
- **"Now, O my God, let your eyes be open and your ears attentive" (v40).** Here Chronicles departs from Kings. Kings ends the prayer by appealing to the Exodus: Israel is God's heritage, brought "out of Egypt, from the midst of the iron furnace" (1 Kgs 8:50–53). Chronicles drops that ending and closes instead with a short plea, and then a psalm.
- **"Arise, O LORD God, and go to your resting place, you and the ark of your might" (v41).** These lines come from Psalm 132:8–10, a psalm about David's longing to find a home for the ark. "Arise, O LORD" was Moses' cry whenever the ark set out in the wilderness (Num 10:35). Now the ark arrives. It will not travel again. **"Resting place"** (*nuach*, rest) recalls God's promise of rest from enemies (1 Chr 22:9) and his choice of Zion: "This is my resting place forever; here I will dwell" (Ps 132:14).
- **"Let your priests… be clothed with salvation, and let your saints rejoice in your goodness" (v41).** Psalm 132:9 has "clothed with righteousness," and God's answer in Psalm 132:16 is "Her priests I will clothe with salvation." The Chronicler uses the promised answer in the prayer itself. "Saints" (*chasidim*) means God's faithful, loyal people. "Goodness" picks up the refrain just sung: "for he is good" (5:13).
- **"Do not turn away the face of your anointed one!" (v42).** To "turn away the face" of someone was to reject their request (1 Kgs 2:16). "Your anointed one" (*meshichekha*) is first of all Solomon himself, the anointed king. But the word is *mashiach*, "messiah." For readers with no king on the throne, it voiced their hope in the coming Messiah, a hope the New Testament sees fulfilled in Jesus, the Christ (the Greek form of the same title).
- **"Remember your steadfast love for David your servant" (v42).** Literally "the steadfast loves of David," meaning God's covenant kindnesses promised to David (2 Sam 7:15–16). Isaiah uses the same phrase for an everlasting covenant: "my steadfast, sure love for David" (Isa 55:3), and Paul quotes Isaiah's line as fulfilled in Jesus' resurrection (Acts 13:34). So Chronicles ends Solomon's prayer by pointing not back to Egypt but forward to God's promise to David. God's immediate answer is fire from heaven (7:1).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the platform "a sort of platform," like a "round and elevated pulpit," and notes Solomon "stood" to bless and "kneeled down" to pray. On v38 he says it "gave rise to the favorite usage" of Jews praying toward Jerusalem. On v42 JFB reads "turn not away the face of thine anointed" as "Do not reject my present petitions," and "the mercies of David" as "the mercies promised to David." Henry observes that Solomon asks first "for repentance and forgiveness, which is the chief blessing," and on vv41–42 writes that "Jehovah has made our nature his resting-place for ever," praying that the Father would "look upon us in and through his Anointed." Guzik quotes Spurgeon that the prayer is "as full and comprehensive as if it were meant to be the summary of all future prayers," and Spurgeon's line that "nothing pleases God better than to see his promises put in circulation." Guzik also connects "Arise, O LORD" with Numbers 10:35 and, citing the scholar J. Barton Payne, sees "your anointed" as Solomon himself, though later readers heard in it Israel's hope for the Messiah.

#### Pointing to Christ
Solomon asked, "Will God indeed dwell with man on the earth?" and ended by asking God not to turn away the face of his anointed and to remember his steadfast love for David. Both prayers are answered in Jesus. God dwelt with man in the flesh (John 1:14), and the Anointed One, the Messiah, is the Son of David whose prayers the Father always hears (John 11:41–42). God's "steadfast, sure love for David" was made sure when he raised Jesus from the dead (Acts 13:34). Because of him, sinners who turn back to God, in any land, pray to a Father who hears from heaven and forgives.
