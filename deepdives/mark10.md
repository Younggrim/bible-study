*Setting:* Early in AD 30, a few weeks before Passover. Jesus leaves Galilee for the last time and travels south toward Jerusalem, down the east side of the Jordan through Perea and then across to Jericho. This is the road to the cross, and Mark tells it as a journey "on the way." Chapter 9 showed the disciples arguing about who was greatest. Chapter 10 keeps up the same lesson through a string of encounters (Pharisees, parents with children, a rich man, two ambitious brothers, a blind beggar) and reaches its peak in one of the clearest sentences Jesus ever spoke about why he came: "to give his life as a ransom for many."

#### Teaching About Divorce (vv1–12)
- **"The region of Judea and beyond the Jordan" (v1).** "Beyond the Jordan" is Perea, ruled by Herod Antipas. Jewish pilgrims from Galilee often took this route to avoid Samaria.
- **"In order to test him" (v2).** The question was a trap. Herod Antipas had divorced his wife to marry Herodias, his brother's wife, and John the Baptist lost his head for objecting (6:17–18). Josephus says John died at Machaerus, a fortress in this very region. An answer against divorce could put Jesus in Herod's sights. An answer for it would make him look lax.
- **"What did Moses command you?" (v3).** The rabbis argued over Deuteronomy 24:1, which assumes a man may divorce his wife for "some indecency." The school of Shammai read that narrowly as sexual immorality. The school of Hillel read it broadly. A later Jewish writing (Mishnah Gittin 9:10) records Hillel's school allowing divorce even if a wife spoiled a meal.
- **"Moses allowed" (v4).** Jesus asked what Moses *commanded*. They answer with what Moses *allowed*. The difference matters. Deuteronomy 24 does not command divorce. It regulates it, requiring a written certificate that protected the woman's freedom to remarry, and it forbids remarrying the first husband afterward.
- **"Because of your hardness of heart" (v5).** The law tolerated divorce to limit the damage done by sin. A rule that restrains evil is not the same as God's ideal.
- **"From the beginning of creation" (vv6–8).** Jesus goes behind Moses to Genesis, quoting 1:27 ("male and female") and 2:24 ("the two shall become one flesh"). Marriage is God's design, not a human contract that can be dropped at will. "Hold fast" means to cling or be glued, the language of a bond meant to last.
- **"What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (v9).** "Joined together" is literally "yoked together." God is the one who joins.
- **"Commits adultery against her" (vv10–12).** In Jewish law a man could commit adultery against another man by taking his wife, but a husband was not thought to commit adultery against his own wife. Jesus says he does. That gave a wife a standing her culture denied her. Verse 12 speaks of a wife divorcing her husband, which Jewish law did not normally allow but Roman law did. Mark's readers in Rome needed the word, and Herodias herself had left her husband.
- **How do the Gospels fit together?** Mark and Luke (16:18) give the rule without exception. Matthew adds "except for sexual immorality" (Matt 5:32; 19:9), and Paul adds desertion by an unbelieving spouse (1 Cor 7:15). Most Protestant traditions read Mark as stating the principle and Matthew and Paul as naming exceptions. The Roman Catholic tradition holds that a valid marriage cannot be dissolved at all and handles hard cases through annulment. Some evangelicals hold that remarriage is never allowed while a former spouse lives. All agree on Jesus' main point: marriage is meant for life, and divorce always involves a breach of God's design.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lays out the Hillel and Shammai debate and stresses that Moses permitted divorce rather than commanding it. He takes Matthew's word *porneia* as broad, covering sexual sin beyond adultery in the narrow sense, and counts Paul's case of desertion as a second ground. The Tyndale notes agree that Mark states the core principle while Matthew and Paul supply exceptions. JFB's comments on vv1–31 could not be retrieved for this study.

#### Let the Children Come to Me (vv13–16)
- **"They were bringing children to him that he might touch them" (v13).** Parents often asked rabbis and elders to bless their children. The word *paidia* covers anything from infants to children of about twelve (5:39–42). Luke says some were babies (Luke 18:15).
- **"The disciples rebuked them" (v13).** They seem to think the Master is too important for children, which shows they had already forgotten 9:36–37.
- **"He was indignant" (v14).** This is the only time the Gospels use this strong word of Jesus. Mark alone records it, as he alone records many of Jesus' emotions. He was angry that his own men were blocking the way to him.
- **"To such belongs the kingdom of God" (v14).** Not to children only, but to people like them.
- **"Receive the kingdom of God like a child" (v15).** The point is not that children are innocent. Ancient people did not think so, and the Bible does not teach it. A child receives. A small child cannot earn a gift, repay it or bargain for it, and takes it with open hands. The kingdom is entered that way or not at all. The rich man in the next scene will ask what he must *do*.
- **"He took them in his arms and blessed them" (v16).** The verb for "blessed" is a strengthened form, suggesting warmth and fervor.
- **Infant baptism?** Some traditions see this passage as support for baptizing infants: if Jesus welcomed children into the kingdom, the church should too. Others reply that the passage is about blessing, not baptism. The passage itself is about Jesus' welcome, and on that all agree.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads "like a little child" as trusting and receiving, not innocence, and notes the emphatic form of "blessed." The Tyndale notes add that Mark does not say which childlike qualities he has in mind, though Matthew 18:4 points to humility.

#### The Rich Young Man (vv17–31)
- **"A man ran up and knelt before him" (v17).** Mark calls him only "a man." Matthew says he was young (Matt 19:20) and Luke says he was a ruler (Luke 18:18). Running and kneeling show real eagerness. He is no trap-setter like the Pharisees.
- **"What must I do to inherit eternal life?" (v17).** The question already shows his mindset. One does not *do* something to inherit. An inheritance is received because of who one is related to, and that is exactly the lesson of the children (v15).
- **"Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (v18).** "Good Teacher" was an unusual title. Jewish writings almost never use it of a rabbi. Jesus is not denying his own goodness. He is making the man think: if only God is truly good, what is he saying about Jesus? And what does that say about his own claim to goodness?
- **"You know the commandments" (v19).** Jesus lists the second half of the Ten Commandments, the ones about neighbors. "Do not defraud" is not one of the ten as worded. It may stand in for "do not covet," turned toward the way rich men often wronged workers (Deut 24:14; Mal 3:5; Jas 5:4). Jesus skips the first commandments, about God, for now.
- **"All these I have kept from my youth" (v20).** He means it sincerely, and outwardly it may have been true. Jesus does not argue. But Jesus had already taught that the commandments reach the heart (Matt 5:21–30).
- **"Jesus, looking at him, loved him" (v21).** Only Mark records this, and it is the only time a Gospel says Jesus loved a particular person outside the Bethany family and the beloved disciple. What follows is not harshness. It is love telling the truth.
- **"You lack one thing" (v21).** The one thing is what the skipped commandments were about. His wealth had first place in his heart, so he was breaking the first commandment while keeping the rest. "Sell… give… come, follow me": the call to give up is joined to the call to follow. Jesus does not ask him to become poor for its own sake. He asks him to trade lesser treasure for "treasure in heaven" and for Jesus himself.
- **Is this for everyone?** Jesus did not ask Zacchaeus (Luke 19:8) or Joseph of Arimathea to sell everything. The command fits this man and the idol that ruled him. But the principle is universal: nothing may stand ahead of Christ (8:34–36).
- **"Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful" (v22).** "Disheartened" can describe a sky clouding over (Matt 16:3 uses it of a gloomy sky). His face fell. He is the only person in the Gospels who is called by Jesus and goes away. Mark does not tell us whether he ever came back.
- **"How difficult it will be for those who have wealth" (vv23–24).** Most people then assumed wealth was a sign of God's favor (Deut 28:1–14). If the blessed rich could not get in, who could? "Children," Jesus calls his disciples, and then widens the warning: entering the kingdom is hard for anyone. Some later manuscripts add "for those who trust in riches" in v24, which softens the saying. The ESV follows the older, shorter reading.
- **"A camel to go through the eye of a needle" (v25).** The camel was the largest animal in the land and the needle's eye the smallest opening. The picture is deliberately absurd. Two common softenings miss the point: a supposed small gate in Jerusalem called "the Eye of the Needle" (there is no evidence it existed), and a reading of "rope" for "camel" based on a similar Greek word (found in only a few late manuscripts).
- **"Then who can be saved?" (v26).** Exactly the right question. **"With man it is impossible, but not with God" (v27).** Salvation is not hard for people. It is impossible. It is God's work from start to finish, and that is good news for rich and poor alike.
- **"We have left everything and followed you" (v28).** Peter is half proud and half anxious: what about us?
- **"A hundredfold now in this time… with persecutions, and in the age to come eternal life" (vv29–30).** Those who leave homes and families for Jesus gain a vast new family in the church, with homes open to them across the world, as Paul found in city after city. Notice two things. "Fathers" is missing from the list of what is received, perhaps because believers have one Father in heaven (Matt 23:9). And "with persecutions" is Mark's honest addition. The reward is real, but it does not mean an easy life.
- **"Many who are first will be last, and the last first" (v31).** The rich man, first by every earthly measure, walked away. Fishermen who left their nets are first.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes how earnestly the man came, running and kneeling, and warns that not seeing the spiritual depth of the law leads people to overrate themselves. He puts the danger of riches in trusting them rather than having them. Henry, Guzik and the Tyndale notes all reject the "needle gate" and "rope" softenings. Guzik points out that the man asked what he must *do*, revealing a mindset of earning, and calls his wealth his idol. The Tyndale notes add that the man's claim was naive rather than arrogant, since Jesus does not rebuke it.

#### Jesus Foretells His Death a Third Time (vv32–34)
- **"Going up to Jerusalem, and Jesus was walking ahead of them" (v32).** Jerusalem sits about 2,500 feet above sea level, and Jericho well below it, so travelers always went "up." The picture is vivid: Jesus striding out in front, set on his course (Luke 9:51), the disciples amazed and the wider group of followers afraid. They can sense something terrible ahead.
- **The most detailed prediction yet (vv33–34).** Compare 8:31 and 9:31. This time Jesus adds the Gentiles, the mocking, the spitting and the flogging. Every detail comes true in chapters 14–15: handed to the chief priests (14:53), condemned (14:64), handed to Pilate (15:1), mocked (15:16–20), spat on (14:65; 15:19), flogged (15:15) and killed (15:37). And each prediction ends the same way: "after three days he will rise."
- **"Deliver him over to the Gentiles" (v33).** Under Roman rule the Jewish council could not carry out executions without the governor's approval (John 18:31). For a Jew to be handed to Gentiles was a deep shame in itself.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the details of v32 the eyewitness touches peculiar to Mark, written "as if we ourselves were in the midst of the scenes it describes." It also notes the prediction ran "right in the teeth of their preconceived notions," which is why they kept missing it. Guzik sees in Jesus walking ahead a picture of courage, and contrasts it with the fear of his followers.

#### The Request of James and John (vv35–45)
- **"We want you to do for us whatever we ask of you" (v35).** The timing is painful. Jesus has just described his torture and death. The brothers ask for a blank check. Matthew says their mother made the request with them (Matt 20:20). She was probably Salome, who may have been the sister of Jesus' mother (compare 15:40 with Matt 27:56 and John 19:25), which would make them Jesus' cousins.
- **"One at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory" (v37).** They believe he will reign, and they want the top two seats in his cabinet. The irony is that the two places beside Jesus "in his glory" will be filled by two robbers, crucified "one on his right and one on his left" (15:27).
- **"Are you able to drink the cup that I drink, or to be baptized with the baptism?" (v38).** In the Old Testament "the cup" is often the cup of God's wrath poured out on sin (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15). Jesus will pray in Gethsemane, "Remove this cup from me" (14:36). "Baptism" here means being plunged into suffering, a figure Greek writers used for being overwhelmed by disaster and the Psalms use of floods closing over a person (Ps 42:7; 69:1–2; Luke 12:50).
- **"We are able" (v39).** Bold, sincere and still uncomprehending. Jesus tells them they will share his cup: James became the first apostle martyred (Acts 12:2), and John suffered exile on Patmos (Rev 1:9) and, according to tradition, lived to old age. They will share his suffering, but not his atoning work. Only Jesus drinks the cup of God's wrath for sin.
- **"Not mine to grant" (v40).** The Son submits to the Father's plan (13:32). The places of honor are already "prepared."
- **"The ten… began to be indignant" (v41).** Probably not because they were humbler, but because the brothers had got in first.
- **"Lord it over them… it shall not be so among you" (vv42–43).** Jesus names the way the world does power: rulers who dominate and great men who throw their weight around. Among Jesus' people the measure is turned upside down.
- **"Servant… slave of all" (vv43–44).** The words climb down: *diakonos*, one who serves, then *doulos*, a slave who belongs to another. Greatness is not escaping service. It is going lower.
- **"For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve" (v45).** The "Son of Man" in Daniel 7:13–14 receives dominion and is served by all nations. Jesus, who has every right to that, came first to serve.
- **"And to give his life as a ransom for many" (v45).** The word is *lytron*, the price paid to free a slave or a captive, or to redeem a life (Exod 21:30; Lev 25:47–52). In the New Testament it appears only here and in Matthew's parallel. "For" is *anti*, "in place of," the language of substitution. "Many" echoes the servant of Isaiah 53, who "bore the sin of many" and makes "many to be accounted righteous" (Isa 53:11–12). In Hebrew idiom "the many" stresses the great number who benefit from the one, not a limit on how many.
- **Commentators:** JFB suggests the brothers' mother voiced the request at their prompting, and admits the indignation of the ten had "a spice of the old spirit of rivalry in it." On v45 it stresses the contrast of one Son of Man for many sinners. The lexicon supports "ransom," the price of release, and the Tyndale notes connect "for many" to Isaiah 53:12. Guzik reads the cup and baptism as suffering and notes that the Greek world used baptismal language of being overwhelmed by calamity. He also accepts the tradition about John's long life as likely but not certain.

#### Jesus Heals Blind Bartimaeus (vv46–52)
- **"They came to Jericho" (v46).** Jericho was a fertile oasis town about 15 miles from Jerusalem, with Herod's winter palace. The road from there climbs about 3,500 feet through bare desert hills. Passover pilgrims crowded the road, so it was a good place for a beggar.
- **Leaving or entering?** Matthew and Mark say Jesus was leaving Jericho; Luke says he was drawing near (Luke 18:35). Matthew mentions two blind men (Matt 20:30). Many explain the first by the fact that Jericho had two sites, the ruined Old Testament town and the new Herodian city about a mile away, so Jesus could be leaving one and approaching the other. As for the number, Mark may name only the one whom the church later knew.
- **"Bartimaeus… the son of Timaeus" (v46).** *Bar* is Aramaic for "son," so Mark translates the name for his Gentile readers. He is the only person healed in Mark whose name is given, which may mean the early church knew him.
- **"Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" (v47).** This is the first time in Mark that anyone calls Jesus "Son of David," a title for the promised king from David's line (2 Sam 7:12–16; Isa 11:1). A blind man sees what the crowds have missed. The prophets said that in the days of the Messiah the eyes of the blind would be opened (Isa 35:5; 61:1).
- **"Many rebuked him… he cried out all the more" (v48).** Like the disciples with the children (v13), the crowd tries to keep the unimportant away. Bartimaeus will not be silenced.
- **"Jesus stopped" (v49).** On his way to die, with a great crowd and a city waiting, Jesus stops for one beggar. **"Take heart. Get up; he is calling you."** The crowd that silenced him now passes on the call.
- **"Throwing off his cloak, he sprang up" (v50).** A beggar's cloak was his bed, his shelter and the cloth he spread to collect coins. He leaves it on the ground, though the Tyndale notes rightly warn against loading the gesture with too much symbolism.
- **"What do you want me to do for you?" (v51).** Exactly the question Jesus asked James and John (v36). They asked for glory. Bartimaeus asks for mercy. **"Rabbi"** in the ESV is *Rabbouni* in Greek, a warmer, more personal form, "my master," which Mary Magdalene also uses at the empty tomb (John 20:16).
- **"Your faith has made you well" (v52).** The verb is *sōzō*, "saved." The healing of the body is a sign of a deeper rescue.
- **"Followed him on the way" (v52).** The rich man went away. Bartimaeus follows Jesus "on the way," the road that leads to Jerusalem and the cross. The chapter ends with a model disciple. This is also Mark's last healing story. Mark's middle section about discipleship opened with a blind man healed in stages (8:22–26) and closes with this one, a frame around chapters of disciples who have eyes but do not see.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws out Bartimaeus's persistence and humility and notes that *Rabbouni* is a strengthened, more personal form of "Rabbi." He also points out that Bartimaeus follows Jesus up the road to the cross. The Tyndale notes explain the Jericho setting and see the cry "Son of David" preparing for the crowd's shout as Jesus enters Jerusalem (11:10).

#### Pointing to Christ
In this chapter Jesus welcomes the children his disciples push away, loves a rich man who turns from him and stops for a beggar the crowd wants silenced. In the middle of it, he explains everything with one sentence: he came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many. The Son of Man who will be served by every nation first takes the place of a slave and pays the price of release for captives who could never pay it themselves. James and John wanted his right and left hands in glory. He gave them, and all who follow him, something better: a share in his cup, the gift of the kingdom received like a child, and a place on the road behind him.
