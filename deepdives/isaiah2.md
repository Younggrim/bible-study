*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably in the prosperous years of Uzziah and Jotham, around 750–735 BC. Chapter 1 was the overture to the whole book. Chapter 2 opens with a fresh heading and begins a long first collection of Isaiah's messages that runs to the end of chapter 12. Judah was rich, well armed and busy with trade, and it was also full of foreign religion and quiet idolatry. Isaiah begins with the end of the story, a vision of what Jerusalem will one day be for the whole world, and then turns to what Jerusalem actually is now. The gap between the two is the subject of the chapter.

#### The Mountain of the LORD (vv1–5)
- **"The word that Isaiah the son of Amoz saw" (v1).** An odd phrase: a word that is *seen*. The Hebrew verb is *chazah*, the same root as "vision" in 1:1. For the prophets, God's message came as something perceived and certain, not as their own opinion. The new heading suggests that chapters 2–12 once stood as their own collection.
- **"In the latter days" (v2).** Literally "in the after-part of the days." In the Old Testament it points to the future God has planned, often the time when he finally acts to put things right (Gen 49:1; Hos 3:5). The New Testament says those last days began with Christ (Acts 2:17; Heb 1:2).
- **"The mountain of the house of the LORD shall be established as the highest of the mountains" (v2).** Mount Zion, where the temple stood, is not actually high. The Mount of Olives right next to it is taller. The point is not geology but rank. In the ancient Near East, gods were imagined living on great mountains (Baal on Zaphon, the Greek gods on Olympus). Isaiah says the LORD's small hill will one day stand above them all.
- **"All the nations shall flow to it" (v2).** Water flows downhill. Here the nations flow *up*, drawn to the mountain as if by a current running the wrong way. It is a picture of something only God can cause.
- **"That he may teach us his ways" (v3).** The nations do not come for trade or to fight. They come to be taught. "Law" here is *torah*, which means instruction or teaching, not only a legal code. The tragedy, set against chapter 1, is that Judah had God's teaching and ignored it (1:10), while the nations will one day come begging for it.
- **"Out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the LORD from Jerusalem" (v3).** Jesus told his disciples that repentance and forgiveness would be preached "to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem" (Luke 24:47). Acts tells exactly that story, as the word goes out from Jerusalem to Judea, Samaria and the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8).
- **"They shall beat their swords into plowshares" (v4).** A plowshare is the iron blade of a plow, and a pruning hook is a small curved knife for vines. The weapons are not just put away but remade into farm tools, because no one will need them. Peace comes because God himself "shall judge between the nations" and settles their disputes. Joel 3:10 turns the image around, calling the nations to beat plowshares into swords for the final battle. A sculpture with these words stands in the garden of the United Nations in New York, but Isaiah's peace depends on the nations being taught by the LORD, not on human treaties.
- **Isaiah and Micah.** Verses 2–4 appear almost word for word in Micah 4:1–3, and Micah adds a line about each man sitting under his own vine and fig tree (Mic 4:4). The two prophets worked at the same time. Scholars debate whether one borrowed from the other or both used an older prophecy. The text does not say.
- **"O house of Jacob, come, let us walk in the light of the LORD" (v5).** Isaiah echoes the nations' words ("Come, let us go up," v3) and turns them on his own people. If the nations will one day walk in God's paths, why is Israel not walking in them now? John uses the same language: "if we walk in the light, as he is in the light" (1 John 1:7).
- **When is this fulfilled?** Christians read it in different ways. Many Reformed and amillennial readers see it fulfilled in the church age, as the nations stream to Christ, the true temple, and the "mountain" of God is the heavenly Zion (Heb 12:22). Premillennial readers, including many dispensationalists, expect a literal fulfilment in a future reign of Christ on earth centered in Jerusalem. Most agree it begins with the gospel going out and reaches its full form only when Christ returns.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads vv2–4 as "the glory of the gospel church," fulfilled as Gentiles were gathered in, and he notes how the world was largely at peace when Christ was born. Guzik takes the opposite view, placing it in Jesus' future thousand-year reign, and stresses that this peace is not peace by surrender but peace enforced by a righteous King (Ps 2:9). JFB argues that since Israel's curses were fulfilled literally, its blessings should be too, and holds that Micah depends on Isaiah rather than the other way round. All three agree on v5: the promise of the future is a call to walk in God's light now.

#### The Day of the LORD (vv6–22)
- **"For you have rejected your people" (v6).** Isaiah suddenly turns from speaking to the people to speaking to God. The glorious picture of vv2–4 is not happening, and he says why.
- **"Full of things from the east" (v6).** Judah had filled itself with practices from Mesopotamia and Syria, especially divination, the attempt to read the future through omens. "Fortune-tellers like the Philistines" points west to the other side. The Philistines were known for diviners (1 Sam 6:2; 2 Kgs 1:2). God's law banned all such practices outright (Deut 18:10–12). Judah was taking religion from every direction except from the LORD.
- **"They strike hands with the children of foreigners" (v6).** Striking hands sealed a deal. It probably means business partnerships and alliances that came with foreign gods attached. The issue is not foreigners as such (vv2–4 just welcomed the nations) but the idolatry that came with these deals.
- **"Filled with silver and gold… filled with horses… filled with idols" (vv7–8).** The word "filled" repeats three times. Under Uzziah, Judah grew rich and strong (2 Chr 26:6–15). Deuteronomy had warned Israel's kings not to multiply horses, silver and gold (Deut 17:16–17). Money, military power and idols are listed together because they all do the same job: they give people something to trust instead of God.
- **"To what their own fingers have made" (v8).** Isaiah mocks idols repeatedly, most sharply in 44:9–20, where a man uses half a log to cook his dinner and carves the other half into a god. The absurdity is bowing down to something you made yourself.
- **"So man is humbled, and each one is brought low" (v9).** Those who bow to idols are already lowered by it. They bend down to things beneath them.
- **"Do not forgive them!" (v9).** A startling prayer. The Hebrew is literally "do not lift [it] for them." The verb *nasa'* means "lift" or "bear," and it is a common way of saying "forgive," as when sin is lifted off and carried away (Ps 32:1). The irony runs through the chapter: these people lift themselves up, so God will not lift their guilt. Some take the line as a prediction in the form of a command ("you will not forgive them"). Either way, it is not personal spite. Isaiah is siding with God's judgment against unrepentant sin.
- **"Enter into the rock and hide in the dust" (v10).** Judah's hill country is full of limestone caves, and people hid in them during invasions (Judg 6:2; 1 Sam 13:6). But no cave can hide anyone from "the terror of the LORD, and from the splendor of his majesty." The same three-part refrain comes back in vv19 and 21.
- **"The LORD alone will be exalted in that day" (vv11, 17).** This refrain is the center of the chapter. It appears twice, framing the list of proud things in vv12–16. "That day" is the day of the LORD, the time when God steps into history to judge. The prophets used it both for nearer judgments (Babylon, the exile) and for the final day at the end of the age (2 Pet 3:10).
- **"Against all that is proud and lofty… lifted up" (vv12–16).** Eight things are listed, each one a symbol of human greatness: the tallest trees (cedars of Lebanon, oaks of Bashan), the highest land (mountains and hills), the strongest defenses (towers and walls) and the richest trade (ships of Tarshish). Uzziah had built towers and fortified walls in Jerusalem (2 Chr 26:9), so the list is close to home.
- **"Lofty and lifted up" (v13).** The same pair of Hebrew words, *rum* ("high") and *nasa'* ("lifted up"), describes the trees and hills here. Isaiah uses that pair for the LORD himself, "high and lifted up," in his temple vision (6:1), and again for God's Servant, who "shall be high and lifted up" (52:13). Only God, and the Servant he exalts, rightly hold that height.
- **"All the ships of Tarshish" (v16).** Tarshish was a distant port, probably in Spain. "Ships of Tarshish" came to mean the large ocean-going cargo ships of the day, the ancient equivalent of supertankers. Uzziah had restored the port of Elath on the Red Sea (2 Kgs 14:22), so overseas trade was part of Judah's pride. "Beautiful craft" is uncertain in Hebrew. It may mean beautiful ships or luxury objects in general.
- **"The idols shall utterly pass away" (v18).** This is the shortest verse in the chapter, as if the idols are simply gone. After the exile in Babylon, the Jewish people largely abandoned idol worship for good.
- **"To the moles and to the bats" (v20).** On that day people will throw their gold and silver idols into the dark places where burrowing rodents and bats live, as they run for the caves themselves. The gods they spent a fortune on are now junk they cannot carry fast enough. Moles and bats are blind or live in darkness, a fitting home for gods that cannot see.
- **"When he rises to terrify the earth" (vv19, 21).** "Terrify" is *'arats*, which sounds like *'erets*, "earth." Revelation picks up this scene almost directly: kings and great men hiding "in the caves and among the rocks of the mountains," begging the rocks to fall on them (Rev 6:15–16). Jesus quotes the same idea from Hosea 10:8 on his way to the cross (Luke 23:30).
- **"Stop regarding man in whose nostrils is breath" (v22).** The chapter ends with a plain command. Human beings live only as long as God keeps breath in their nostrils (Gen 2:7; Ps 146:3–4). "For of what account is he?" Do not trust people, power or wealth as if they were God. This verse is missing from the earliest Greek translation (the Septuagint), but it is in the standard Hebrew text. It pairs with v5: walk in the LORD's light, and stop leaning on man.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sums up the section in one line: "Their sin was their shame." God's judgments would humble them, and they should be "ashamed of their confidence in their idols and in an arm of flesh." JFB reads v8 as mostly private idolatry, since the kings Uzziah and Jotham did not sanction public idol worship, and says the cedars and oaks stand for "haughty nobles and princes." JFB takes v9's "forgive them not" as a threat put in the form of a command. JFB thinks v18 was fulfilled "to the letter" after the exile, while also pointing forward to Zechariah 13:2. Guzik insists that wealth and hard work are not condemned, only the trust placed in them. He calls the day of the LORD "God's time," as opposed to the present day of man, and he reads v22 as a call not to follow people into sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter sets two mountains side by side. One is Zion lifted up, where the nations come to learn God's ways. The other is every proud height of human achievement brought down. Jesus fulfills the first and suffers the second for others. He is the true temple (John 2:19–21), and through him the word of the LORD went out from Jerusalem to the nations, and people from every tribe still come to be taught. The day of the LORD that sends the proud into the caves is the day Christ returns. But Christ also humbled himself, the one truly "high and lifted up" coming down into the lowest place, so that the proud who turn to him might be forgiven. The God who would not "lift" the guilt of the unrepentant lifted it onto his Son (Isa 53:4, 12).
