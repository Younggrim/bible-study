*Setting:* About 896–895 BC, the fifteenth year of Asa, king of Judah. Asa's army has just come home from a stunning victory over Zerah's huge Cushite army, loaded with sheep, camels and spoil (14:9–15). That victory came because Asa cried out and relied on the LORD. Now, at the height of success, God sends a prophet to meet him on the road with a promise and a warning. The whole chapter is found only in Chronicles, except verses 16–18, which come from 1 Kings 15:13–15. For the Chronicler's readers after the exile, Azariah's sermon was a summary of their entire history and the one thing they most needed to hear.

#### Asa's Reforms (vv1–19)
- **"The Spirit of God came upon Azariah the son of Oded" (v1).** Azariah appears nowhere else. In Chronicles the Spirit "comes upon" or "clothes" people so they can speak God's word at a key moment (1 Chr 12:18; 2 Chr 20:14; 24:20). The prophets in these chapters rarely do miracles. Their work is to bring a word, and the king's response to the word decides his future.
- **"He went out to meet Asa" (v2).** Probably as the army returned to Jerusalem. The message comes not in defeat but in victory, when the temptation to self-confidence is greatest. Rehoboam had fallen in exactly that moment (12:1).
- **"The LORD is with you while you are with him" (v2).** The whole chapter in one sentence. God's presence is not a reward earned, but it is not automatic either. It is a relationship, and it runs both ways.
- **"If you seek him, he will be found by you, but if you forsake him, he will forsake you" (v2).** These are almost the very words David spoke to Solomon (1 Chr 28:9). The promise goes back to Moses: "you will seek the LORD your God and you will find him, if you search after him with all your heart and with all your soul" (Deut 4:29). Jeremiah repeats it to the exiles in Babylon (Jer 29:13), and Jesus says, "seek, and you will find" (Matt 7:7). The warning half is just as real, and Asa's own later years (16:7–12) will test it.
- **"For a long time Israel was without the true God, and without a teaching priest and without law" (v3).** Which period does Azariah mean? Most interpreters think of the time of the judges, when "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 21:25) and the cycle of sin, oppression, crying out and rescue repeated again and again. Some think he speaks of the northern kingdom right then, which had driven out its priests (11:14; 13:9). The Hebrew has no verb, so it can be read as a general truth about any time God's people drift. Hosea uses similar words of the north's coming exile: "the children of Israel shall dwell many days without king or prince, without sacrifice or pillar" (Hos 3:4).
- **"Without a teaching priest" (v3).** Priests were not only meant to offer sacrifices. They were Israel's teachers of God's law (Lev 10:11; Deut 33:10; Mal 2:7). Without teaching, there is no real knowledge of God. Jehoshaphat will act on exactly this by sending priests and Levites to teach the law in every town (17:7–9).
- **"When in their distress they turned to the LORD… and sought him, he was found by them" (v4).** This is the pattern of Judges (Judg 3:9; 10:10–16) and of Solomon's temple prayer (6:24–25, 36–39).
- **"No peace to him who went out or to him who came in" (v5).** Ordinary travel and daily work were unsafe. In Deborah's day "the highways were abandoned, and travelers kept to the byways" (Judg 5:6). Zechariah describes the early post-exile years the same way (Zech 8:10), so the Chronicler's readers knew this from experience.
- **"Nation was crushed by nation and city by city, for God troubled them" (v6).** Disorder is presented as God's discipline, meant to drive his people back to him.
- **"But you, take courage! Do not let your hands be weak, for your work shall be rewarded" (v7).** "Take courage" echoes Moses' and the LORD's words to Joshua (Deut 31:6–7; Josh 1:6–9) and David's to Solomon (1 Chr 22:13; 28:20). "Let your hands be weak" is the Hebrew picture of losing heart and dropping your work. "Your work shall be rewarded" is repeated almost word for word in Jeremiah 31:16. Paul says the same to the Corinthians: "your labor is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Cor 15:58).
- **"The prophecy of Azariah the son of Oded" (v8).** The Hebrew text actually reads "the prophecy of Oded the prophet," naming the father instead of the son. The ESV, with some ancient versions, supplies "Azariah the son of" to match v1. Most scholars think words dropped out in copying. Nothing important depends on it.
- **"He took courage and put away the detestable idols" (v8).** Asa obeys the very word just spoken: "take courage." "Detestable idols" (*shiqqutsim*) is a strong word of disgust for idols, used of Molech and Chemosh (1 Kgs 11:5–7). The reform of 14:3–5 had not reached everything, or idolatry had crept back in.
- **"The cities that he had taken in the hill country of Ephraim" (v8).** Probably towns Abijah captured (13:19) or that Asa took in border fighting with Baasha of Israel (1 Kgs 15:16).
- **"He repaired the altar of the LORD that was in front of the vestibule" (v8).** The great bronze altar Solomon built (4:1) was some sixty years old and needed renewal, perhaps damaged by neglect or idolatry. Reform is not only tearing down false worship but rebuilding true worship.
- **"Those from Ephraim, Manasseh, and Simeon who were residing with them" (v9).** Northerners kept coming south. Simeon's land lay inside Judah's southern territory (Josh 19:1, 9), but many Simeonites may have sided with the north or lived among them. **"When they saw that the LORD his God was with him."** People notice when God is with someone. The Chronicler loves to show all the tribes gathered at Jerusalem (11:16; 30:11, 18; 34:9).
- **"The third month of the fifteenth year" (v10).** The third month (May–June) was the time of the Feast of Weeks, or Pentecost, the harvest festival fifty days after Passover (Lev 23:15–21). The Hebrew word for "weeks" (*shabu'ot*) sounds almost the same as the word for "oaths" (*shebu'ah*, vv14–15), and some think the Chronicler meant the wordplay. The Sinai covenant was also made in the third month (Exod 19:1), so a covenant renewal at that season would echo it.
- **"700 oxen and 7,000 sheep… from the spoil" (v11).** Offered from the plunder of Zerah's army (14:15), a way of giving God the credit for the victory.
- **"They entered into a covenant to seek the LORD… with all their heart and with all their soul" (v12).** The words of the great command in Deuteronomy 6:5. This was not a new covenant but a renewal of the old one, a formal, public recommitment. Chronicles records several such renewals (23:16; 29:10; 34:31–32).
- **"Whoever would not seek the LORD… should be put to death, whether young or old, man or woman" (v13).** This troubles modern readers. It applies the law of Deuteronomy 13:6–10 and 17:2–7, which made public idolatry, leading others to worship other gods, a capital crime in Israel. Israel was a nation under a covenant with God, and idolatry was treason against its King. The text records what they swore. It does not say anyone was executed. Christians agree that the church is not a nation with a sword. In the new covenant the extreme sanction is removing someone from fellowship (1 Cor 5:13, which quotes Deut 17:7), not death, and no one can be forced to seek God.
- **"With a loud voice and with shouting and with trumpets and with horns" (v14).** A joyful, loud ceremony. "Horns" are *shofars*, rams' horns.
- **"All Judah rejoiced over the oath, for they had sworn with all their heart… and he was found by them" (v15).** The covenant was not a burden but a joy. The words of v2 come true: they sought him, and he was found. **"The LORD gave them rest all around."** Rest again, as in 14:1, 6–7.
- **"Even Maacah, his mother, King Asa removed from being queen mother" (v16).** Hebrew "mother" can mean grandmother. Maacah was Abijah's mother (11:20–22; 13:2), so Asa's grandmother. The "queen mother" (*gebirah*, literally "great lady") was an official position of honor and real influence in the court (1 Kgs 2:19; 2 Kgs 10:13; Jer 13:18). Maacah may have kept it into Asa's reign because his own mother had died, or simply because she was powerful.
- **"A detestable image for Asherah" (v16).** The word (*miphletset*) is rare and comes from a root meaning "shudder." It means something horrifying or abominable. Older writers guessed it was obscene, and it may have been, but the word itself says only that it was shocking. **"He cut down her image, crushed it, and burned it at the brook Kidron."** The Kidron Valley, east of Jerusalem, became the dumping place for idols in later reforms too (29:16; 30:14; 2 Kgs 23:4, 6, 12). Removing his own grandmother, publicly, took real courage.
- **"But the high places were not taken out of Israel. Nevertheless, the heart of Asa was wholly true all his days" (v17).** On the apparent conflict with 14:3–5, see the notes there. "Israel" here may mean the northern towns Asa held, or the whole land. "Wholly true" is *shalem*, whole, at peace, undivided, the same root as *shalom*. Asa's heart was sound even though his reform had gaps. The phrase "all his days" comes from Kings (1 Kgs 15:14), and it is striking that the Chronicler keeps it even though he will describe Asa's failures in the next chapter.
- **"The sacred gifts of his father and his own sacred gifts" (v18).** Things dedicated to God, including perhaps spoil from Abijah's victory (13:16–19) and Asa's from Zerah. They restored some of what Shishak had stripped from the temple (12:9).
- **"There was no more war until the thirty-fifth year of the reign of Asa" (v19).** The ESV adds "more." Kings says "there was war between Asa and Baasha… all their days" (1 Kgs 15:16), meaning ongoing border tension, but no major war until then. The date in this verse and in 16:1 is a famous puzzle, because Kings has Baasha dead by Asa's twenty-sixth year (1 Kgs 16:6, 8). Many scholars think the numbers here count from the division of the kingdom (about 930 BC), not from Asa's accession, which would put this war around Asa's sixteenth year, soon after this covenant. Others suspect a copying error in the numbers. The puzzle is taken up in the next chapter.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry turns v2 into a promise: "He never said, nor ever will, Seek you me in vain." On v13 he notes it was "no new law of their own making, but an order to put in execution that law of God," but adds that whether people may now "be compelled by such methods as these to seek the Lord is justly questioned." On Maacah: "This was bravely done of Asa, that he would not connive at idolatry in those that were nearest to him." And on v17, "Sincerity is something less than sinless perfection." Guzik lists the ways the people sought God: together, with sacrifice, by covenant, with all their heart, accountably, publicly and joyfully. He quotes Morgan that whoever is with God finds God with him, and cites Dilday that the word for Maacah's image means frightening rather than obscene. Guzik also passes on, via Clarke, a Jewish tradition that Maacah later repented, which is tradition, not text. JFB calls vv3–6 a picture of "spiritual destitution and ignorance" that should be "taken in a wider sense" than any one period, and regards "Oded the prophet" in v8 as "a corruption of the text."

#### Pointing to Christ
"If you seek him, he will be found by you." Azariah's promise holds because God is the one who seeks first. Jesus came "to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10), and in him the people who once sat in darkness without the true God, without a teacher and without law, have seen a great light (Matt 4:16). The covenant Judah renewed with shouting and trumpets is fulfilled in the new covenant in Christ's blood, which writes God's law on the heart (Jer 31:33; Luke 22:20). The feast of the third month, when they swore with all their heart, became, centuries later, the Pentecost when God poured out his Spirit (Acts 2:1–4), the same Spirit who came upon Azariah, now given to all God's people.
