*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Having given the royal tribe of Judah and the priestly tribe of Levi long treatment, the Chronicler now moves quickly through six of the northern tribes: Issachar, Benjamin, Naphtali, western Manasseh, Ephraim and Asher. Most of these tribes had been scattered by Assyria three centuries earlier, and few of their families came back. Including them anyway is a statement of faith. In the Chronicler's eyes Israel is still twelve tribes, and the returned community is the core of a people God has not given up on. Much of the material reads like army records, with clan heads and fighting strength, probably drawn from a census in David's time (v2). The text is also unusually rough in places. Words seem to have dropped out in copying, especially in vv12–19, and two tribes, Zebulun and Dan, are missing entirely. Two short stories break up the lists: a father's grief in Ephraim, and a woman who built towns.

#### Descendants of Issachar (vv1–5)
- **"Tola, Puah, Jashub, and Shimron, four" (v1).** The same four sons as in Genesis 46:13 and Numbers 26:23–24. Issachar's land was the fertile Jezreel Valley and the hills south of Galilee. A later judge, "Tola the son of Puah… a man of Issachar" (Judg 10:1), carried the clan names.
- **"Their number in the days of David being 22,600" (v2).** The Chronicler is working from an old military roll. David counted Israel's fighting men late in his reign (21:1–6; 27:23–24), and these figures may come from that census or from the clans' own records.
- **"All five of them were chief men" (v3).** Only four sons of Izrahiah are named, so either Izrahiah himself is counted as the fifth, as JFB suggests, or a name was lost.
- **"For they had many wives and sons" (v4).** A plain explanation for a large clan.
- **"87,000 mighty warriors" (v5).** The numbers in these lists are large. The Hebrew word for "thousand," *eleph*, can also mean a clan or a military unit, and some scholars think that sense lies behind some of these totals. The ESV itself says "units of the army" in v4. Either way, the point is strength and readiness. Issachar was a tribe that could field an army.

#### Descendants of Benjamin (vv6–12)
- **"Bela, Becher, and Jediael, three" (v6).** Benjamin's sons are listed differently in each place they appear: ten in Genesis 46:21, five in Numbers 26:38–39, five again (with different names) in 1 Chronicles 8:1–2. Some clans died out, some merged, and some were renamed. JFB notes that most of Benjamin was nearly wiped out in the civil war of Judges 20, which would have reshaped the family lines.
- **A Benjamin list in two places.** Benjamin gets a much longer record in the next chapter. Because of that, some scholars think this short list was originally Zebulun's, whose name could have been lost in copying. Others take it as a separate military roll of Benjamin. The text as it stands says Benjamin.
- **"Ehud… Anathoth, and Alemeth" (vv8, 10).** Some names in the list are also names of towns in Benjamin's territory: Anathoth and Alemeth were Levite towns there (6:60). Clans and their settlements often shared names. The Ehud here is the son of Bilhan, not the left-handed judge Ehud, who was "the son of Gera, the Benjaminite" (Judg 3:15).
- **"Shuppim and Huppim were the sons of Ir, Hushim the son of Aher" (v12).** This verse looks damaged. Ir and Aher are not introduced, and the names do not fit. *Aher* is also the Hebrew word for "another." Dan's one son in Genesis was "Hushim" (Gen 46:23), and the next verse ends with "the descendants of Bilhah," who was Dan's mother as well as Naphtali's. So many interpreters think the fragment "Hushim the son of…" is all that remains of the tribe of Dan. That is a reasonable reading, though not certain.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lays out the case that v12 hides a reference to Dan, and passes on the old view (from Poole) that Dan's name was avoided because the tribe led Israel into idolatry (Judg 18). Henry says plainly, "Here is no account either of Zebulun or Dan," and "Why they only should be omitted we can assign no reason." He adds that Dan's disgrace was that idolatry began in its northern colony, where Jeroboam later set up a golden calf (1 Kings 12:29), and notes that Dan is also left out of the sealed tribes in Revelation 7:4–8. The Tyndale notes offer a simpler possibility: the records of the far northern tribes may have been lost when Assyria deported them (2 Kings 15:29), so the Chronicler had nothing to copy.

#### Descendants of Naphtali (v13)
- **"Jahziel, Guni, Jezer and Shallum, the descendants of Bilhah" (v13).** One verse, first generation only, the same four sons as Genesis 46:24. Naphtali lived west of the Sea of Galilee and was among the first tribes swept away by Assyria (2 Kings 15:29). Its record is a stub. Yet Isaiah promised that this very region, "the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali," would see "a great light" (Isa 9:1–2), and Matthew says it did when Jesus made his home in Capernaum (Matt 4:13–16). The two tribes with the thinnest records here are the ones where Jesus spent most of his ministry.

#### Descendants of Manasseh (vv14–19)
- **"Asriel, whom his Aramean concubine bore; she bore Machir the father of Gilead" (v14).** This is western Manasseh, the half of the tribe that lived west of the Jordan, in the hills north of Ephraim (the eastern half was in 5:23–26). Machir's mother was an Aramean, a Syrian. Foreign women keep appearing in these lists. They were not erased from Israel's family record.
- **A tangled text (vv15–19).** Maacah is called Machir's sister in v15 and his wife in v16, and Huppim and Shuppim from Benjamin's list appear again. The Tyndale notes list several problems that suggest the Hebrew was damaged early. JFB calls the passage "very confused" and offers a rearranged reading. It is honest to say that the exact family links here can no longer be recovered with confidence.
- **"Zelophehad, and Zelophehad had daughters" (v15).** These are the five sisters who came to Moses because their father had died with no sons, and asked to inherit his land. God answered, "The daughters of Zelophehad are right" (Num 27:7), and the law of inheritance was changed for every family in Israel. A one-line mention recalls one of the boldest requests in the Law.
- **"Abiezer" (v18).** The Abiezrites were Gideon's clan: "Joash the Abiezrite" was his father, and Gideon called his clan "the weakest in Manasseh" (Judg 6:11, 15). **"Shechem"** (v19) was also a clan name, sharing its name with the old town in the hills (Josh 17:2).

#### Descendants of Ephraim (vv20–29)
- **"Ezer and Elead, whom the men of Gath who were born in the land killed" (v21).** A short, painful story. The "men of Gath… born in the land" were natives of the region, possibly the people of a Canaanite Gath before the Philistines held it. The Hebrew can be read two ways: either the men of Gath came down to raid Ephraim's livestock, as the ESV has it, or Ephraim's sons went down to raid Gath's. JFB favors the first, making Gath the aggressor. When it happened is also unclear. If "Ephraim" means Joseph's son himself, the event fell during Israel's years in Egypt, which raises the question of what his sons were doing in Canaan. The Tyndale notes give the main options: an early raid out of Egypt, a later event where "Ephraim" stands for the tribe, or a slip in the text.
- **"Ephraim their father mourned many days, and his brothers came to comfort him" (v22).** In the middle of a list, the Chronicler pauses for grief. Ephraim's "brothers" are his relatives and clansmen, who come and sit with him, as Job's friends did (Job 2:11) and as the Jews came to Mary and Martha (John 11:19). JFB makes the same comparisons.
- **"He called his name Beriah, because disaster had befallen his house" (v23).** A wordplay. *Beriah* sounds like *be-raah*, "in disaster" or "in evil." The new son was named for the sorrow he was born into. Compare Jabez, named for pain (4:9). The name looks back on loss, but the birth itself is new life after death.
- **"His daughter was Sheerah, who built both Lower and Upper Beth-horon, and Uzzen-sheerah" (v24).** A woman credited with founding towns, which is unique in the Old Testament. The two Beth-horons guarded the main pass from the coastal plain up into the hills toward Jerusalem. Joshua chased the Amorite kings down this road (Josh 10:10), and Solomon later fortified both towns (2 Chr 8:5). Uzzen-sheerah means "Sheerah's corner" or "Sheerah's portion," a place still named for her.
- **"Nun his son, Joshua his son" (v27).** The list builds to its goal. From Ephraim it runs about ten generations to Joshua, which fits Israel's long years in Egypt. Elishama son of Ammihud (v26) was Ephraim's tribal leader at the start of the wilderness journey (Num 1:10; 7:48). Then comes Joshua, Moses' assistant and successor, who led Israel into the land. A house that had been struck by "disaster" (v23) produced the man who led Israel into the promised land.
- **"Bethel… Gezer… Shechem" (v28).** Ephraim's towns ran from Bethel in the south, where Jacob saw the ladder to heaven and Jeroboam later placed a golden calf, to Shechem in the north, where Joshua renewed the covenant (Josh 24).
- **"Beth-shean… Taanach… Megiddo… Dor" (v29).** Manasseh's towns along the Jezreel Valley and the coast. These are the very towns Judges says Manasseh failed to take, because "the Canaanites persisted in dwelling in that land" (Judg 1:27). In time they were all brought under Israel's control. Beth-shean is where the Philistines hung Saul's body (1 Sam 31:10), a scene the Chronicler is about to retell.
- **Commentators:** Henry makes Ephraim's grief the heart of the chapter. "The sword devours one as well as another," he writes, so being Joseph's son was no protection. Of Beriah he writes, "What name more proper for man that is born of a woman than Beriah, because born into a troublesome world?" And on Sheerah: "A virtuous woman may be as great an honour and blessing to a family as a mighty man." He sees Joshua as the repair of the breach in Ephraim's family. Guzik passes on John Trapp's verdict that the Ephraimites were famous for wealth and strength, and also known as proud and quarrelsome. That fits their later quarrels with Gideon and Jephthah (Judg 8:1; 12:1) and their leading role in the northern kingdom's break from David's house.

#### Descendants of Asher (vv30–40)
- **"Imnah, Ishvah, Ishvi, Beriah, and their sister Serah" (v30).** The same family as Genesis 46:17 and Numbers 26:44–46. Serah is one of the very few granddaughters of Jacob named anywhere. Another Beriah appears here, a reminder that names were shared across tribes.
- **"Heber, and Malchiel" (v31).** Their clans appear in Numbers 26:45. **Birzaith** may be a town rather than a person, since "fathered" in these lists can mean "founded."
- **The longest line (vv32–39).** Asher's record runs several generations, mostly names with no stories attached, likely from a military register. Asher lived along the northern coast, near Phoenicia, and never fully drove out the Canaanites of Sidon and Acco (Judg 1:31–32).
- **"Approved, mighty warriors, chiefs of the princes… 26,000 men" (v40).** "Approved" translates *barur*, a word for what has been picked out or purified. Asher's force is smaller than Issachar's, but its men are described as choice and tested.
- **Commentators:** Henry suggests Asher's leaders were wise not to want a huge militia, preferring good men to many men. Guzik passes on a rabbinic tradition, via Adam Clarke, that Asher's daughters were so beautiful that they married kings and priests. That is a legend, not something the text says.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter has gaps: missing tribes, damaged verses, families whose stories survive only as names. That is what Israel's history looked like after Assyria. Yet the Chronicler writes them all down, as if to say none of them are forgotten. The New Testament carries the same hope. When the child Jesus was presented in the temple, among the first to recognize him was "Anna, a prophetess, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher" (Luke 2:36). Asher had been scattered seven hundred years earlier, yet a woman of that tribe was there to give thanks for the Redeemer. Jesus did much of his work in Naphtali's land and Zebulun's, the tribes with the thinnest records. The Messiah came to gather "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matt 15:24), and a family named for disaster, like Ephraim's after its loss, can still produce a Joshua. In Greek, Joshua's name is the same as Jesus'.
