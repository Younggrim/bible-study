*Setting:* The ark is finished. For years Noah has built it on dry ground at God's command (6:14–22), and Peter calls him "a herald of righteousness" through that time (2 Pet 2:5). Now the waiting ends. Chapter 7 tells of the flood itself: the last week of grace, the family and animals going in, the door shut by God, and the waters rising until every land creature outside the ark is gone. The story is told slowly, with repeated lists, exact dates and the same verbs again and again, as if the narrator wants the reader to feel the weight of it. Genesis 1 told of God separating the waters to make a world where life could live. Genesis 7 tells of that world being undone, the waters above and below flooding back in, with one boat floating on top.

#### Noah and the Flood (vv1–24)
- **"Go into the ark, you and all your household" (v1).** The Hebrew verb *bo* means both "come" and "go," so the KJV has "Come thou." Many preachers, Henry among them, make much of "come," as if God were inviting Noah into a place where he already was. That is a fair picture of God's presence with Noah, but it rests on the English choice more than on the Hebrew. "All your household" matters too: Noah's family is saved with him because of God's favor to him (Heb 11:7).
- **"I have seen that you are righteous before me in this generation" (v1).** God saw the world's wickedness (6:5, 12), and he sees Noah. The order of 6:8–9 still holds: Noah found grace first, and his righteousness is the life that grace produced.
- **"Seven pairs of all clean animals… and a pair of the animals that are not clean" (v2).** Chapter 6 spoke of two of every kind (6:19), the minimum to keep each kind alive. Now God adds more clean animals, needed for sacrifice after the flood (8:20) and, later, for food (9:3). The laws of clean and unclean come at Sinai (Lev 11), but the idea is already here. The Hebrew is literally "seven seven." The ESV and most modern versions read seven *pairs*. Henry and JFB read seven animals: three pairs for breeding and one extra for sacrifice. The Hebrew can be read either way.
- **Two accounts or one?** Some scholars think chapters 6–8 weave together two older sources, pointing to the two-and-seven numbers and the 40 days in v12 alongside 150 days in v24. Others answer that the numbers fit together (two of every kind in general, seven of the clean), and that the 40 days are the rain while the 150 days are the whole time the waters rose and stayed high. Many scholars, including Gordon Wenham, see the whole flood story as carefully built in a mirror pattern around a center point, "God remembered Noah" (8:1). Whatever the sources, the story as it stands is a deliberate whole.
- **"For in seven days" (v4).** A last week of grace. Henry: "these seven days were trifled away." JFB: "A week for a world to repent!" The number may also echo the seven days of creation. The world made in a week is given a week before it is unmade.
- **"Forty days and forty nights" (v4).** Forty is a number that often marks testing, judgment or a time of preparation: Moses on Sinai (Exod 24:18), Israel's forty years (Num 14:33), Elijah's journey (1 Kings 19:8), Nineveh's warning (Jonah 3:4), and Jesus in the wilderness (Matt 4:2).
- **"I will blot out" (v4).** The same verb as 6:7, repeated twice in v23. God does what he said he would do.
- **"Noah did all that the LORD had commanded him" (v5).** The refrain of 6:22 again, and again in vv9 and 16. Noah still says nothing. His obedience is the only reply the story records.
- **"Noah was six hundred years old" (v6).** Noah's three sons came when he was about 500 (5:32), so the ark-building years and the "120 years" (6:3) overlap with the raising of his family.
- **"Two and two, male and female, went into the ark" (vv8–9).** The animals "went in," as God had said they "shall come in to you" (6:20). Noah didn't have to round them up. The text doesn't say how this happened. Guzik suggests God gave them an instinct to come, like migration. The point is that the Creator is directing his creatures.
- **"In the second month, on the seventeenth day" (v11).** This is the first exact date in the Bible. The flood story is full of dates (7:11; 8:4, 5, 13, 14), and they read like a ship's log. Whether the "second month" is counted from a spring or an autumn new year is debated. The precision tells the reader this is meant as an account of something that happened, not a timeless myth.
- **"All the fountains of the great deep burst forth, and the windows of the heavens were opened" (v11).** "The deep" is *tehom*, the same word as 1:2, "darkness was over the face of the deep." The flood comes from below and above at once. In Genesis 1 God set a boundary between the waters above and the waters below (1:6–8) and gathered the seas so that dry land appeared (1:9). Now the boundaries fail and the world returns toward the watery chaos of 1:2. Jeremiah later describes judgment the same way, as creation running backward (Jer 4:23–26). "Windows" means floodgates or sluices. Some, including Guzik, think there was once a water canopy above the earth that fell as rain. Most scholars think the text uses the ordinary picture language of its time and doesn't describe a canopy.
- **"On the very same day Noah… entered the ark" (vv13–16).** The list of who went in is given again, with the "kinds" of creation language (1:21–25). The repetition slows the story down at the moment of entry.
- **"And the LORD shut him in" (v16).** One short sentence carries a great deal. The verb *sagar* means "shut" or "close," used of shutting a door behind someone (19:6, 10). JFB says the Hebrew means "covered him round about," but the lexicon gives only "shut, close." The point stands either way: God closed the door, not Noah. Noah was kept safe inside by God's own hand, and the time for anyone else to come in was over. Notice that the chapter names God with both of his main names: "God had commanded him" and "the LORD shut him in." The Creator who commands is also the covenant LORD who protects his own. Jesus' parable of the ten virgins ends the same way: "and the door was shut" (Matt 25:10).
- **"The waters increased and bore up the ark" (v17).** "Increased" (*rabah*) is the word God used in blessing, "be fruitful and *multiply*" (1:22, 28). Instead of living creatures, the waters multiply. The same waters that destroy the world lift the ark up. The judgment and the rescue happen in the same flood.
- **"The waters prevailed" (vv18–20, 24).** "Prevailed" is *gabar*, to be strong or mighty, four times in this section. It is the root of *gibborim*, the "mighty men" of 6:4. The mighty men of old are overcome by mightier waters.
- **"All the high mountains under the whole heaven were covered… fifteen cubits deep" (vv19–20).** Fifteen cubits is about 22 feet, half the ark's height of 30 cubits (6:15). Some suggest the ark, riding low in the water, would just clear the highest peaks, though the text doesn't say so. Christians read the flood's extent in two main ways:
  - **Worldwide.** "All the high mountains under the whole heaven" is very strong language, and Peter speaks of "the world that then existed" being "deluged with water" (2 Pet 3:6). JFB says the depth "argues against" a partial flood. Guzik also holds this view and points to the many flood stories found across the world's cultures.
  - **Regional.** "Earth" (*erets*) can mean "land," and "all the earth" can mean the known world (41:57). On this view the flood covered the whole region where humans then lived and destroyed all of humanity outside the ark, as the text says, without covering the whole planet.
  - Both hold that the flood really happened, that God sent it, and that it destroyed all the people outside the ark.
- **"All flesh died… in whose nostrils was the breath of life" (vv21–22).** "Breath of life" goes back to 2:7, when God breathed into Adam's nostrils. What God gave, he now takes back. The list of birds, livestock, beasts, swarming creatures and mankind runs the creation order again. The fish aren't mentioned: only creatures "on the dry land" die.
- **"Only Noah was left, and those who were with him in the ark" (v23).** "Was left" (*sha'ar*) is the root of *she'ar*, "remnant," a word the prophets use for the faithful few God saves out of judgment (Isa 10:20–22). Noah's family is the first remnant.
- **"The waters prevailed on the earth 150 days" (v24).** About five months. The next verse, "But God remembered Noah" (8:1), is the turning point of the whole story.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Christ "an ark already prepared, in whom alone we can be safe when death and judgment come." Of the final week he writes that the people were given "but one week more to turn them in," and that "In every step he took, [Noah] walked by faith, and not by sense." On v23: Noah "is, in effect, buried alive." JFB reads the clean animals as three pairs plus a seventh for sacrifice, so that worship could go on during the voyage. It sees the shut door as the end of "the season of grace" for those outside. Guzik notes that God alone shut the door, and that no servant of God gets to decide who is shut out of salvation. He links this to Christ, who "opens and no one will shut, who shuts and no one opens" (Rev 3:7). He also says Noah's seven days of waiting, after a century of work and with no sign of rain yet, were a real test of faith. Guzik takes a water canopy and a worldwide flood as most likely. As noted above, many interpreters read both differently.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus used this chapter as a warning: "As were the days of Noah, so will be the coming of the Son of Man… they were eating and drinking… until the day when Noah entered the ark, and they were unaware until the flood came and swept them all away" (Matt 24:37–39). The people of Noah's day weren't unusually busy with evil that week. They were simply living as if God would never act. The way of escape was open until God shut the door. Peter sees the ark as a picture of salvation in Christ: the same waters that judged the world carried Noah's family to safety, and baptism shows that believers have passed through judgment in Christ and come out alive (1 Pet 3:20–21). The only safe place was the one God provided, and the only safe place now is in Christ, the door that is still open (John 10:9).
