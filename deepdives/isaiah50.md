*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapter 49 ended with Zion, Jerusalem pictured as a mother, crying "The LORD has forsaken me." God answered that he could no more forget her than a mother could forget her baby. Chapter 50 continues the answer, then turns to the third Servant Song (vv4–9). Here the Servant speaks again, and for the first time we hear plainly that he will be beaten, mocked and spat on. He accepts it willingly, trusting God to vindicate him. The chapter ends with a choice: trust the LORD in the dark, or light a fire of one's own.

#### Israel's Sin and the Servant's Obedience (vv1–11)
- **"Where is your mother's certificate of divorce, with which I sent her away?" (v1).** The picture is a family. Zion is the mother, the exiles are her children, and God is the husband and father. Under Moses' law a man could divorce his wife by writing her a certificate and sending her out of the house (Deut 24:1–4). Once that was done, she could not come back to him. God asks Zion to produce the paper. There is none. Jeremiah 3:8 says God gave the northern kingdom a "decree of divorce," and the point there is the shock of Israel's unfaithfulness. Here God says Zion has not been divorced. The separation is real but not final.
- **"Or which of my creditors is it to whom I have sold you?" (v1).** A father in debt might sell his children as slaves to pay what he owed (2 Kgs 4:1; Neh 5:5). God has no creditors. He owes nobody anything and did not hand his children over because he was forced to.
- **"For your iniquities you were sold, and for your transgressions your mother was sent away" (v1).** The real reason for the exile is their sin, not God's failure or weakness. "Sold" here means handed over as a punishment, not to pay a debt. It also means the way back is open. God can take back what he never gave up.
- **"Why, when I came, was there no man; why, when I called, was there no one to answer?" (v2).** God came through his prophets, and no one answered. It is the picture of a husband coming home and nobody coming to the door (65:12; Jer 7:13).
- **"Is my hand shortened, that it cannot redeem? Or have I no power to deliver?" (v2).** A "short hand" means weakness, an arm that cannot reach. The same question is asked in Numbers 11:23, when Moses doubted God could feed Israel meat. The answer is the same here: no.
- **"By my rebuke I dry up the sea… their fish stink for lack of water" (v2).** This recalls the Red Sea and the plague on the Nile, when the fish died and "the Nile stank" (Exod 7:18, 21). The God of the Exodus has not lost his strength.
- **"I clothe the heavens with blackness and make sackcloth their covering" (v3).** Sackcloth was rough dark goat-hair cloth worn in mourning. This recalls the plague of darkness in Egypt (Exod 10:21–23). At the crucifixion darkness covered the land from noon to three (Mark 15:33). Spurgeon, whom Guzik quotes, draws that link.
- **"The Lord GOD has given me the tongue of those who are taught" (v4).** Now the Servant speaks. "Those who are taught" (*limmudim*) means disciples or learners, not scholars. The KJV's "tongue of the learned" can mislead. The Servant speaks well because he listens first. "Lord GOD" (*Adonai Yahweh*) appears four times in this song (vv4, 5, 7, 9). The Servant knows whose servant he is.
- **"That I may know how to sustain with a word him who is weary" (v4).** The purpose of his words is to hold up the weary. The LORD "does not faint or grow weary" and "gives power to the faint" (40:28–29). Here the Servant is how that comfort reaches them. Jesus said, "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).
- **"Morning by morning he awakens; he awakens my ear to hear as those who are taught" (v4).** Every day begins with God speaking and the Servant listening. Mark records Jesus rising "very early in the morning, while it was still dark" to pray (Mark 1:35). Jesus said, "I do nothing on my own authority, but speak just as the Father taught me" (John 8:28).
- **"The Lord GOD has opened my ear, and I was not rebellious; I turned not backward" (v5).** Hearing in Scripture includes obeying. Israel was deaf and rebellious (42:18–20; 48:8). The Servant hears and obeys. Some connect this with the servant in Exodus 21:5–6 whose ear was pierced with an awl as a sign he would serve his master for life, and with Psalm 40:6. The Hebrew verbs are different in each passage, so the link is a picture rather than a direct reference. But the idea is the same: a servant who chooses to obey.
- **"I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard" (v6).** The obedience costs him. "I gave" means he does it willingly. Pulling out a man's beard was a deep insult in the ancient Near East (2 Sam 10:4; Neh 13:25). Jesus was flogged (Mark 15:15), struck in the face (Matt 26:67; John 18:22) and beaten on the head (Mark 15:19). The Gospels do not mention his beard being pulled.
- **"I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting" (v6).** Spitting in someone's face was the ultimate contempt (Num 12:14; Deut 25:9). Jesus was spat on at his trial before the council and again by the soldiers (Mark 14:65; 15:19). He had foretold it: "they will mock him and spit on him, and flog him and kill him" (Mark 10:34). The Tyndale notes say the parallels are too close to be coincidence.
- **"But the Lord GOD helps me; therefore I have not been disgraced" (v7).** Outwardly he is shamed. Inwardly he is not, because God is on his side. Shame in Scripture is finally about God's verdict, not people's.
- **"Therefore I have set my face like a flint" (v7).** Flint is a very hard stone used for tools and knives. His resolve will not crack. Luke uses similar language: "he set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51), knowing what waited there. Ezekiel's forehead was also made "like emery harder than flint" for a hard mission (Ezek 3:8–9).
- **"He who vindicates me is near. Who will contend with me?" (v8).** "Vindicates" (*matsdiq*) is from the root *tsadaq*, to declare in the right, the language of the courtroom. "Justifies" is the same word. God is the judge, and he is on the Servant's side, so no accuser can win. The Servant dares any opponent: "Let us stand up together… Let him come near to me."
- **"Behold, the Lord GOD helps me; who will declare me guilty?" (v9).** Paul echoes this almost word for word for all who are in Christ: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies. Who is to condemn?" (Rom 8:33–34). What is true of the Servant becomes true of those joined to him.
- **"All of them will wear out like a garment; the moth will eat them up" (v9).** His accusers will fade away like old clothes eaten by moths (51:8). Pilate, Caiaphas and the soldiers are remembered only because of the one they condemned.
- **"Who among you fears the LORD and obeys the voice of his servant?" (v10).** The song ends with a challenge to the listeners. Fearing the LORD and obeying his Servant go together. "Let him who walks in darkness and has no light trust in the name of the LORD and rely on his God." Even the faithful sometimes walk in the dark, without seeing the way or feeling God near. The answer is not to make light for oneself but to lean on God's name. JFB puts it simply: "God never had a son who was not sometimes in the dark," and he points to Jesus' cry from the cross.
- **"All you who kindle a fire, who equip yourselves with burning torches! Walk by the light of your fire" (v11).** This is the alternative: people who refuse to wait in the dark and light their own torches, making their own plans and trusting their own wisdom (Prov 14:12). God says, in effect, "Go ahead." **"This you have from my hand: you shall lie down in torment."** The fire they light for guidance becomes the fire that burns them. The word "torment" (*ma'atsevah*) means a place of grief or pain.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the Servant as "an acceptable preacher," "a patient sufferer" and "a courageous champion," and holds that what was true of Isaiah was true far more of Christ. On v11 he compares those who light their own fire to Nadab and Abihu, who offered "unauthorized fire" (Lev 10:1). He says they "depend upon their own righteousness." Guzik makes the same comparison. JFB says of the "sparks" that self-made plans are "blazing sparks extinguished in a moment." On v1, Henry notes that husbands then put away wives "upon any little disgust," and God has done no such thing. Guzik quotes Motyer: divorce would accuse God's love of failing, and slavery would accuse his power of weakness, but the real cause was sin. On v6 JFB stresses "I gave" as proof that the sufferings were voluntary. Guzik goes further and argues from the verse that Jesus' beard must have been pulled out, but the Gospels are silent on that detail, and the song may describe abuse in general. JFB notes that "justify" in v8 means, for Christ, God's acceptance on the ground of "His own righteousness," and for believers, on the ground of his.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel would not answer when God called. The Servant listens every morning and does not turn back. Israel's sin brought shame. The Servant takes the shame of others: the blows, the beard torn, the spit. He sets his face like flint toward Jerusalem and trusts God to vindicate him, and on the third day God did (Rom 1:4). Because "he who vindicates me is near," those who belong to Jesus can say the same: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect?" For anyone walking in the dark, the call stands: do not light a torch of one's own, but trust the name of the LORD, as the Servant did.
