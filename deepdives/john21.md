*Setting:* Some days or weeks after the resurrection, about AD 30 or 33, back in Galilee. Jesus had told the disciples to go there, where he would meet them (Matt 28:7, 10; Mark 16:7). Chapter 20 ended with what sounds like the Gospel's conclusion: "these are written so that you may believe" (20:30–31). Chapter 21 is an epilogue. Most scholars think it was added after the main Gospel was finished, either by John himself or by those close to him, who vouch for his testimony in v24. No manuscript of John lacks it. It ties up two loose ends. Peter denied Jesus three times by a charcoal fire, and he has not yet been publicly restored. And a rumor had spread that the beloved disciple would never die. The chapter answers both, and it ends the Gospel where it began, with Jesus calling, "Follow me."

#### Jesus Appears to Seven Disciples (vv1–14)
- **"By the Sea of Tiberias" (v1).** This is the Sea of Galilee, a freshwater lake about 13 miles long. Herod Antipas built the city of Tiberias on its western shore around AD 20 and named it after the emperor, and by the time John wrote the lake was commonly called by that name (6:1). "Revealed himself" (*phaneroō*) frames the story at both ends (vv1, 14). Jesus appears when and where he chooses.
- **Seven disciples (v2).** Simon Peter, Thomas, Nathanael of Cana, the sons of Zebedee (James and John) and two others. Thomas is now among the believers. Nathanael appeared only in the first chapter (1:45–51), where Jesus promised he would "see greater things." This is the only place in John's Gospel where the sons of Zebedee are mentioned by name. If the beloved disciple is John son of Zebedee, as tradition holds, he is in the boat.
- **"I am going fishing" (v3).** Readers differ on what this means. Some think Peter is giving up and going back to his old trade. Others think the disciples are simply earning food while they wait for Jesus, as rabbis worked trades and Paul made tents. The text gives no rebuke. It does show them without direction, and without success.
- **"That night they caught nothing" (v3).** Night was the best time for fishing with nets on the lake. Experienced fishermen worked all night and came up empty.
- **"Just as day was breaking, Jesus stood on the shore" (v4).** The light comes with him. As with Mary (20:14) and the travelers to Emmaus (Luke 24:16), they do not recognize him at first. In the dim light and about a hundred yards off, they would see only a figure on the beach. Henry: "Christ's time of making himself known to his people is when they are most at a loss."
- **"Children, do you have any fish?" (v5).** "Children" (*paidia*) is an affectionate, informal address, something like "lads" or "boys." The question is phrased to expect "no," and they have to admit it.
- **"Cast the net on the right side of the boat" (v6).** The instruction seems pointless, since one side of a boat is barely a few feet from the other. They obey anyway, and the net fills so full they cannot haul it in. At the start of his ministry Jesus did almost the same miracle, when Peter fell at his feet and Jesus called him to catch people (Luke 5:1–11). This is a second call, a reminder of the first.
- **"It is the Lord!" (v7).** The beloved disciple recognizes him first, as he believed first at the tomb (20:8). Peter acts first. He was "stripped for work," which may mean wearing only a loincloth or a light tunic, the Greek word (*gymnos*) covering both. He pulls on his outer garment, tucks it in, and jumps into the water to reach Jesus. In Luke 5 the catch made Peter say "Depart from me." Now it makes him swim toward Jesus as fast as he can.
- **"About a hundred yards off" (v8).** Literally "about two hundred cubits." The others bring the boat in, dragging the net.
- **"A charcoal fire in place, with fish laid out on it, and bread" (v9).** The word for "charcoal fire" (*anthrakia*) appears only one other time in the New Testament: the fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter denied Jesus (18:18). The smell of a charcoal fire would bring that night back. Jesus has made breakfast. The risen Lord of glory is cooking for tired fishermen.
- **"Bring some of the fish that you have just caught" (v10).** Jesus already has fish, but he invites them to add theirs. He works, and he lets his followers share in the work.
- **"Full of large fish, 153 of them. And although there were so many, the net was not torn" (v11).** Fishermen counted their catch to divide it, and an eyewitness remembered the number. Interpreters through the centuries have searched it for symbolism. Jerome claimed Greek naturalists counted 153 kinds of fish, which would picture every nation gathered in the gospel net, though no ancient source gives that number exactly. Augustine noted that 153 is the sum of the numbers 1 to 17. Most modern commentators, along with Guzik and the Tyndale notes, take it as a literal count and see no hidden code. The unbroken net, however, may well be meant as a picture: in Luke 5:6 the nets began to break. Here the net holds every fish, as the church's mission gathers people of every kind without losing them (compare 10:16; 11:52; 17:12).
- **"Come and have breakfast" (v12).** The disciples do not dare ask "Who are you?" They know, but something about him is different, and they are in awe.
- **"Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and so with the fish" (v13).** The words echo the feeding of the five thousand, also beside this lake (6:11). The risen Jesus is the same provider.
- **"This was now the third time" (v14).** The third appearance to the disciples as a group in John's Gospel (20:19, 26). John is not counting the appearance to Mary or claiming these were the only ones (1 Cor 15:5–7).
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the return to fishing as ambiguous, either a lapse or simply provision while they waited, and he sees the empty night as a lesson that labor without Jesus produces nothing. He takes the 153 as a plain count and warns against inventing hidden meanings. JFB sees the catch as a picture of "the vast success of their now fast approaching ministry" and notes Peter's "alacrity" in leaping into the sea. Henry: "Christ manifests himself to his people by doing that for them which none else can do," and from v7, "Those that know Christ themselves should endeavor to bring others acquainted with him."

#### Jesus and Peter (vv15–19)
- **"When they had finished breakfast" (v15).** Jesus restores Peter after a meal, by a charcoal fire, with the others listening. Peter's denial was public. His restoration is public too.
- **"Simon, son of John" (v15).** Jesus does not call him Peter, "the rock," the name he gave him (1:42). He uses his original name, the one he had when Jesus first met him. It is as if Jesus is starting over with him.
- **"Do you love me more than these?" (v15).** "These" could mean these fish and boats, his old life. It could mean these other men. Or it could mean "more than these others love me." The last fits best, since Peter had boasted, "Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away" (Matt 26:33). Peter's answer does not compare himself with anyone. JFB: "Peter's silence on that point was confession enough of his sin and folly."
- **Two words for love (vv15–17).** In his first two questions Jesus uses *agapaō*. Peter answers each time with *phileō*. The third time Jesus uses *phileō*, Peter's own word. Many preachers, Guzik among them, have read *agapaō* as the higher, self-giving love and *phileō* as warm affection, so that Peter, humbled, will not claim more than friendship, and Jesus comes down to meet him. Many scholars, and the Tyndale notes, think John uses the words as near-synonyms. John uses both for the Father's love for the Son (3:35; 5:20) and for Jesus' love for Lazarus (11:3, 5). John also varies other words in this passage without any obvious difference: two words for "know," two for "feed" and two for "sheep." The lexicon allows either reading. What is certain is that the three questions answer the three denials.
- **"Feed my lambs… Tend my sheep… Feed my sheep" (vv15–17).** "Feed" (*boskō*) means to pasture, to give food. "Tend" (*poimainō*) means to shepherd: to guide, protect and rule. "Lambs" (*arnia*) and "sheep" (*probata*) are both terms of affection. Jesus is the Good Shepherd (10:11), and he entrusts his flock, still "my" lambs and "my" sheep, to a forgiven failure. Peter later writes to church elders, "shepherd the flock of God that is among you… and when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory" (1 Pet 5:2–4). JFB suggests "lambs" are the young and tender disciples and "sheep" the more mature, though the words may simply vary.
- **"Peter was grieved because he said to him the third time" (v17).** The third question touches the wound. Peter knows exactly why there are three. "Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you." Peter no longer trusts his own boasting. He appeals to what Jesus knows about him. Jesus knew Peter would deny him, and he knew Peter's love was still real.
- **"When you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will dress you and carry you where you do not want to go" (v18).** "Dress" here is literally "gird," tie a belt around. A young man girds himself and goes where he wants. An old prisoner is bound and led away. "Stretch out your hands" was a common ancient way of referring to crucifixion. Peter once said he would lay down his life for Jesus (13:37) and failed. Now Jesus tells him he will, after all.
- **"By what kind of death he was to glorify God" (v19).** John wrote after Peter's death, which early Christian writers place in Rome under Nero, about AD 64–67 (1 Clement 5; Tertullian). Tradition recorded by Origen and later Eusebius says he was crucified upside down at his own request, not feeling worthy to die as his Lord did. The phrase "glorify God" is the language John uses of Jesus' own death (12:23; 13:31). Peter's death will share in his Lord's.
- **"Follow me" (v19).** These were Jesus' first words to Peter's friend Philip (1:43) and his call to Peter at the lake (Mark 1:17). The same call comes again to the restored disciple, now knowing it leads to a cross.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the two love words as a real difference, with Peter more modest about his love than before his fall, and treats "Follow me" as the central command of the passage. He notes that Jesus restores Peter by facing his failure directly and then giving him work. JFB does not discuss the two Greek verbs. It reads the questions as a deliberate reopening of the denials and sees in vv18–19 "the honor to be conferred upon him by dying for his Master." Henry: "Those that do not truly love Christ will never truly love the souls of men," and on vv18–19, "Those that faithfully follow Christ in grace shall certainly follow him to glory."

#### Jesus and the Beloved Apostle (vv20–25)
- **"Peter turned and saw the disciple whom Jesus loved following them" (v20).** John reminds readers that this is the disciple who leaned against Jesus at the supper and asked who the betrayer was (13:23–25). The man Jesus has just told to follow sees another man already following.
- **"Lord, what about this man?" (v21).** Peter has just heard he will die a martyr's death, and he wants to know about John. Whether it was concern for his friend, curiosity or a touch of comparison, Jesus' answer redirects him.
- **"If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? You follow me!" (v22).** In Greek "you" is emphatic. Each disciple has his own path. Peter's is a cross. John's, as it turned out, was a long life. Neither is better. Comparing callings is a distraction from obeying one's own. JFB: "this was a matter which belonged to the Master of both."
- **"So the saying spread abroad… that this disciple was not to die" (v23).** Early Christians took Jesus' words as a promise that John would live until the second coming. John corrects the rumor carefully by quoting exactly what Jesus said. Jesus spoke of a possibility, not a promise. This correction makes most sense if the beloved disciple had either grown very old or had recently died, and believers were troubled. Second-century writers, especially Irenaeus, say John lived at Ephesus into the reign of Trajan (AD 98–117), outliving all the other apostles.
- **"This is the disciple who is bearing witness about these things, and who has written these things" (v24).** The Gospel identifies its own author: the beloved disciple, an eyewitness. "Has written" may mean he wrote it himself or caused it to be written. "We know that his testimony is true" adds the voice of others, probably church leaders at Ephesus who knew him and vouch for him, in the way Old Testament law required more than one witness (Deut 19:15). JFB argues the beloved disciple wrote the chapter, noting that a supplementary conclusion is not unusual in the New Testament, and reads v24 as "identifying the author of this book with all that it says of this disciple." Many scholars think the closing verses come from John's circle. Either way, the Gospel rests on an eyewitness.
- **"The world itself could not contain the books that would be written" (v25).** This is deliberate overstatement, a figure of speech familiar in ancient writing. But it is true in its own way. If 20:30 said John selected his material, v25 says the subject is inexhaustible. Every Gospel is a small window on a life too large for any book. The Gospel that opened with the Word who made all things (1:3) ends with the admission that no number of words could contain him.
- **Commentators:** Guzik applies v22 to all believers: keep the eyes on Christ's command rather than comparing callings, and note that Jesus never actually said John would survive until his return. JFB says the disciple corrects the rumor because he "is jealous for His Master's honor," and reads v25 as hyperbole signaling that the material was far from exhausted. Henry's outline treats vv24–25 as the conclusion of the whole Gospel rather than of this chapter only.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Gospel ends not in a temple or a palace but on a beach at dawn, with the risen Lord cooking breakfast for men who failed him. He fills their empty nets, as he did when he first called them. He lights a charcoal fire like the one where Peter denied him and asks three times, "Do you love me?" so that the man who denied him three times can say three times that he loves him. Then Jesus hands him the flock. Restoration is not just forgiveness. It is a commission. Jesus tells Peter he will glorify God in death, and tells the disciple he loved that his path is his own. To both, and to every reader, his last word in the Gospel is the same as his first call: "Follow me."
