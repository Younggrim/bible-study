*Setting:* Psalm 110 is titled "A Psalm of David," and Jesus himself insisted on that: "David himself, in the Holy Spirit, declared…" (Mark 12:36). It is only seven verses long, yet it is the Old Testament passage most often quoted or alluded to in the New Testament. Verse 1 alone is cited or echoed more than twenty times, and Hebrews builds its whole teaching on Christ's priesthood around verse 4. The psalm is a royal oracle: words from God to Israel's king, promising him a throne at God's right hand, victory over his enemies and, unexpectedly, a permanent priesthood. In Israel, kings came from the tribe of Judah and priests from Levi, and the two offices were kept separate. When King Uzziah tried to burn incense in the temple, he was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21). So a king who is also "a priest forever" points beyond any ordinary son of David. Before Jesus, Jewish readers already understood the psalm to speak of the Messiah, which is why Jesus' question about it silenced his opponents (Matt 22:41–46). Some modern scholars read it first as a coronation psalm for a Davidic king, later applied to the Messiah. Henry is blunt: "This psalm is pure gospel; it is only, and wholly, concerning Christ." It follows Psalm 109, which ended with God standing "at the right hand of the needy one" (109:31). Psalm 110 begins with the king seated at God's right hand. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's two oracles and their outcomes.

#### Sit at My Right Hand (v1)
- **"The LORD says to my Lord" (v1).** The first "LORD" is *Yahweh*, God's covenant name. "Says" (*ne'um*) is not the ordinary word for speaking. It is the solemn formula prophets used: "declares the LORD," "an oracle of the LORD." JFB renders it "a saying of the Lord." David is passing on a revelation.
- **"My Lord" (v1).** The second "Lord" is *'adoni*, "my lord" or "my master," the word a servant used for a master or a subject for a king (1 Sam 25:24; 1 Kgs 1:17). David was the king. No one in Israel was his master but God. So whom does he call "my lord"? That was Jesus' question to the Pharisees: "If then David calls him Lord, how is he his son?" (Matt 22:45). The Messiah would be David's descendant, yet greater than David. Only someone who is both a son of David and more than a man fits both.
- **A note on the Hebrew.** Some popular teaching says the second word is *Adonai*, the title used only for God. In the standard Hebrew text it is pointed *'adoni*, which is used of human lords as well. The argument from this verse does not depend on the word itself being divine. It depends on David calling his own descendant his lord, which is the argument Jesus made.
- **"Sit at my right hand" (v1).** The right hand of a king was the place of highest honor and shared authority (1 Kgs 2:19). To sit there is to rule with God. Jesus told the high priest, "you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power" (Mark 14:62), and the high priest tore his robes, recognizing a claim to divine authority. After the ascension, the apostles proclaimed that Jesus had taken this seat (Acts 2:33–35; Eph 1:20; Heb 1:3).
- **"Sitting" means finished work.** Hebrews draws a point from the posture. Priests in the temple always stood, because their work was never done. Christ "offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins" and then "sat down at the right hand of God" (Heb 10:11–12). His sitting means the sacrifice is complete.
- **"Until I make your enemies your footstool" (v1).** Ancient kings were shown with their feet on conquered enemies. Joshua had his commanders put their feet on the necks of the defeated kings (Josh 10:24). Egyptian royal footstools were sometimes carved with images of enemies. "Until" means the victory is certain but not yet complete. Paul explains: "he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet. The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Cor 15:25–26).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Christ "shall reign till he has put down all opposing rule, principality, and power," and that when Jesus asked about this verse, his opponents "chose rather to say nothing," for "the Jews of old, even the worst of them, so understood it" as speaking of the Messiah. JFB says the right hand speaks of position, not posture, and that this is Christ's commissioned rule as Mediator rather than his eternal rule as God. Guzik passes on Boice's count of twenty-seven New Testament quotations or allusions to this psalm, and reads v1 as God the Father speaking to God the Son. He takes the second "Lord" as *Adonai*, which the standard Hebrew does not support, though his conclusion stands on Jesus' own argument.

#### Rule in the Midst of Your Enemies (vv2–3)
- **"The LORD sends forth from Zion your mighty scepter" (v2).** A scepter was a rod symbolizing royal authority (Gen 49:10; Ps 2:9). Zion, Jerusalem, is where the king's rule begins, and from there it stretches out. The gospel spread the same way, "beginning from Jerusalem" (Luke 24:47; Acts 1:8).
- **"Rule in the midst of your enemies!" (v2).** The king does not wait for his enemies to disappear before he reigns. He rules among them now. This is the present age: Christ reigns from heaven while opposition still surrounds his people.
- **"Your people will offer themselves freely on the day of your power" (v3).** "Offer themselves freely" is a single Hebrew noun, *nedavot*, "freewill offerings," the word for voluntary gifts brought to God beyond what the law required (Exod 35:29; Lev 22:18). The king's people are not conscripts or mercenaries. They give themselves willingly, like an offering. "Day of your power" can also mean "day of your army" (*chayil* means both strength and army), the day he musters his forces. JFB calls them "free will offerings."
- **"In holy garments" (v3).** Literally "in the splendors of holiness." The king's army is dressed like priests, not just soldiers. Some Hebrew manuscripts and early translations read "on the holy mountains" instead, a difference of one letter. Either way, this is a holy army.
- **"From the womb of the morning, the dew of your youth will be yours" (v3).** This is one of the hardest lines in the Psalms to translate. Most read it as a picture of the king's young warriors, appearing as fresh and countless as dew at dawn ("youth" can be a collective noun for young men). Dew in Israel's dry summer was a sign of freshness and blessing (Hos 14:5; Mic 5:7). The Greek translation read the same consonants differently: "from the womb, before the morning star, I have begotten you," making the line about the king's birth from God (compare Ps 2:7). Early church writers often followed that reading. The ESV's reading is the more common one today.
- **Commentators:** Henry places v3 under Christ's kingly office: his people are made willing to submit to him. JFB sees in the holy garments "a nation of kings and priests" (Exod 19:6; 1 Pet 2:9), and says "the dew is a constant emblem of whatever is refreshing and strengthening." Guzik, citing Poole, says the word for "volunteers" is plural and intensive, "most willing," and that "youth" here refers to the young warriors, not to the Messiah's own youth. Meyer, cited by Guzik, adds that it is the Spirit who produces such willingness.

#### A Priest Forever After the Order of Melchizedek (v4)
- **"The LORD has sworn and will not change his mind" (v4).** God's word is always true, but an oath adds a solemn guarantee. "Will not change his mind" (*nacham*) means he will not relent or reconsider. Hebrews makes much of this: the Levitical priests became priests without an oath, but this one "was made a priest with an oath" (Heb 7:20–22), which makes Jesus "the guarantor of a better covenant."
- **"You are a priest forever" (v4).** This is the surprise of the psalm. Israel's priests came from Aaron's line in the tribe of Levi, and each one died and had to be replaced (Heb 7:23). A king from Judah could not be a priest. This king is a priest, and a priest "forever."
- **"After the order of Melchizedek" (v4).** Melchizedek appears in only three verses in Genesis (Gen 14:18–20). After Abraham rescued Lot, Melchizedek, "king of Salem" and "priest of God Most High," brought out bread and wine, blessed Abraham, and received a tenth of the spoils. Salem is almost certainly Jerusalem (Ps 76:2). So long before David, Jerusalem had a king who was also a priest. "After the order of" (*'al-divrati*) means "in the manner of" or "like." His name means "king of righteousness," and Salem means "peace" (Heb 7:2).
- **How Hebrews reads Melchizedek.** Hebrews quotes this verse five times (Heb 5:6, 10; 6:20; 7:17, 21). It argues that Melchizedek's priesthood was greater than Aaron's: Abraham, Levi's ancestor, paid him tithes and received his blessing (Heb 7:4–10). Genesis gives Melchizedek no genealogy, birth or death, so "resembling the Son of God he continues a priest forever" (Heb 7:3). The point is not that Melchizedek was literally eternal, though some have speculated that he was a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ. Most interpreters think Hebrews is drawing a lesson from what Genesis leaves unsaid. Jesus' priesthood does not rest on descent "but by the power of an indestructible life" (Heb 7:16).
- **Commentators:** Henry places this verse under Christ's office as priest, beside his offices as prophet and king. JFB says the priesthood is perpetual, royal and divinely appointed, marked by "the absence of priestly descent and succession," and so superior to Aaron's. Guzik lists the five citations in Hebrews and what each stresses, and passes on Trapp's point that the church is kept safe by both Christ's royal power and his priestly mediation. The Tyndale notes add that David and Solomon had priestly duties of a limited kind, bringing the ark and organizing temple worship, but the full union of king and priest is reserved for the Messiah.

#### He Will Shatter Kings on the Day of His Wrath (vv5–7)
- **"The Lord is at your right hand" (v5).** "Lord" here is *Adonai*, the title for God. In v1 the king sat at God's right hand. Now God stands at the king's right hand, as his helper in battle (Ps 16:8; 109:31). The picture shifts from throne to battlefield.
- **"He will shatter kings on the day of his wrath" (v5).** "Shatter" (*machats*) means to smash or crush. Psalm 2 says the same of kings who rise against the LORD's anointed: "You shall break them with a rod of iron" (Ps 2:9). "The day of his wrath" is the day of judgment (Rom 2:5; Rev 6:17).
- **Who is "he" in vv5–7?** The Hebrew moves between God and the king without always making clear which is meant. In v5 "the Lord" fights for the king. By v7 the one drinking from the brook must be the king. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes that the two act as one, and the volunteer army of v3 drops out of sight: the battle belongs to the Lord and his King together.
- **"He will execute judgment among the nations, filling them with corpses" (v6).** This is the language of a total victory. It is hard to read, but it is honest. The psalm does not pretend that God's enemies will simply fade away. Revelation pictures the final judgment the same way, with the rider on the white horse who "judges and makes war" (Rev 19:11–21).
- **"He will shatter chiefs over the wide earth" (v6).** "Chiefs" is literally "head," and it may mean one head, a chief enemy, "over the wide earth." Some read this as an echo of the promise in Genesis 3:15 that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head. That is possible, though the text does not make the link explicit.
- **"He will drink from the brook by the way; therefore he will lift up his head" (v7).** A victorious warrior, pursuing his fleeing enemies, stops for a quick drink at a stream and presses on with fresh strength, like Gideon's men at the spring (Judg 7:5–6) or Samson after his battle (Judg 15:18–19). "Lift up his head" is the opposite of hanging one's head in defeat or grief. The enemies' "head" is shattered (v6), while the king's head is raised in triumph. Some older interpreters read the brook as a picture of Christ's suffering, drinking from the stream of affliction on the way to glory. The battle context makes refreshment the more natural reading.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v7 as a picture of Christ's humiliation and exaltation, the suffering on the way and the glory after. JFB sees a victorious warrior, "weary yet still pursuing," refreshed by the brook and lifting his head in triumph. Guzik prefers the refreshment reading over the humiliation one, citing Maclaren's comparison with Gideon's men, and links vv5–6 to the final battle of Revelation 19. He passes on Maclaren's sobering line that every person will in the end either be under Christ's feet or share his throne (Rev 3:21).

#### Pointing to Christ
No psalm is more directly about Jesus. He is David's son and David's Lord, which is why the Pharisees could not answer him (Matt 22:45). He is seated at God's right hand, because his sacrifice is finished (Heb 10:12). He is the King who rules from Zion through the gospel and gathers a people who offer themselves freely to him. He is the Priest forever, not by descent from Levi but by God's oath and his own indestructible life, and because he lives forever "he is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25). And he is the coming Judge, before whom every enemy, including death itself, will be put under his feet (1 Cor 15:25–26). The only question the psalm leaves is the one Maclaren raised: whether a person will be his footstool or share his throne.
