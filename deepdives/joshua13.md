*Setting:* The second half of Joshua begins here. Chapters 1–12 told how Israel took the land, and chapters 13–21 tell how the land was shared out among the tribes. Some years have passed since the crossing of the Jordan, probably about seven (see 14:7–10), and Joshua is now an old man, probably near a hundred (he dies at 110, 24:29). The great Canaanite armies are broken, but large parts of the land are still in other hands. Chapter 13 does two things. It lists the land that "yet remains" and tells Joshua to divide it anyway, and it records the land Moses already gave to the two and a half tribes east of the Jordan.

#### Land Still to Be Conquered (vv1–7)
- **"You are old and advanced in years" (v1).** The narrator says it, and then the LORD says it to Joshua in the same words. It is not an insult. It is a reason. Joshua will not live to finish every battle, so he must do the one job only he can do: divide the land while he still can. Matthew Henry notes that Joshua and Caleb were now the only men left of all those counted at Sinai, and remarks, "It is good for those who are old and stricken in years to be put in remembrance of their being so."
- **"There remains yet very much land to possess" (v1).** Right after 11:23 said "Joshua took the whole land," this sounds like a contradiction. It is not. Chapter 11 means the power of Canaan's kings was broken across the land. Chapter 13 means many regions were not yet settled or controlled by Israel. "Possess" (*yarash*) means to take over and occupy, often by driving out the people living there. Conquest and possession are two different steps, and the second one is the work of generations.
- **"All the regions of the Philistines" (vv2–3).** The coastal plain in the southwest. Five cities, each with its own "ruler" (*seren*, a special word used only of Philistine lords, possibly from a non-Semitic language): Gaza, Ashdod and Ashkelon on the coast, Gath and Ekron a little inland. The Philistines were part of the "Sea Peoples" who came from the Aegean world (Caphtor, probably Crete; Amos 9:7). Most scholars think the main Philistine settlement came a little later than Joshua's day, so the writer may be describing the region by the name it later carried. Either way, the Philistines became Israel's great enemy through Judges and Samuel until David subdued them.
- **"From the Shihor, which is east of Egypt" (v3).** Probably the "Brook of Egypt," the wadi near El-Arish on the northern Sinai coast, the traditional border between Canaan and Egypt. JFB calls it "a small brook near El-Arish." The coastal land "is counted as Canaanite," meaning it belongs to the land promised to Israel, whoever lived there.
- **"The Geshurites… the Avvim" (vv2–3).** These Geshurites lived south of Philistia, not the Geshur in the north (v13). The Avvim were an older people of the coast whom the Caphtorim (Philistines) had displaced (Deut 2:23).
- **The north (vv4–6).** The land of the Sidonians (Phoenicians) along the northern coast, "the Gebalites" of Gebal, the famous port later called Byblos, and "all Lebanon" up to "Lebo-hamath," the entrance to the region of Hamath in Syria, the far northern limit of the promised land (Num 34:8). Israel controlled these lands only briefly, in the days of David and Solomon (1 Kings 8:65).
- **"I myself will drive them out… Only allot the land" (v6).** God's promise is emphatic: "I myself." Israel's part is to trust that promise and divide the land as though it were already theirs. That is faith in action: handing out farmland that is still in enemy hands. JFB adds that "the fulfilment of this promise was conditional." Israel would have to keep faith with God and go in and take it, as Judges 1–2 shows they often failed to do.
- **"Divide this land for an inheritance to the nine tribes and half the tribe of Manasseh" (v7).** The word "inheritance" (*nachalah*) runs through the rest of the book. It means family land, passed down from parents to children and not to be sold away permanently (Lev 25:23; 1 Kings 21:3). The land was God's, and he was granting it to Israel's families as a lasting gift. The division would be made "by lot" (14:2), so that God, not Joshua, decided who received what (Prov 16:33).
- **Commentators:** Henry has God telling Joshua, in effect, "there is as much conquered as will serve your turn for the present; divide this, and make your best of it, and wait for the remainder hereafter." JFB thinks Joshua was "probably above a hundred years old," reckoning seven years for the conquest and survey, and says the lot was "the best that could have been adopted," since it left no ground for complaints of favoritism and taught Israel that God owned the land. Guzik reads v1 as a word for every believer: however much has already been done in the Christian life, much remains. He applies v6–7 to the balance between God's work and ours. God promises to drive out the enemies, but each tribe still has to step up and claim its land. The Tyndale notes add that the unconquered areas were mostly the plains and valleys, the most populated parts of the land, and that it took generations for Israel to control them.

#### The Inheritance East of the Jordan (vv8–33)
- **"Which Moses gave them" (v8).** This land was given before Moses died, after the victories over Sihon and Og (Num 21; 32). Reuben and Gad had large herds and saw good grazing land, and asked to settle there. Moses agreed on the condition that their soldiers cross over and fight for their brothers (Num 32:20–22), which they did. The record is repeated here so the whole of Israel's land is set down in one place. JFB suggests it also served as a legal document, to settle any later dispute about boundaries.
- **The overview (vv9–12).** From Aroer on the rim of the Arnon gorge in the south, across the high flat "tableland of Medeba" (*mishor*, level ground), through Gilead, the hilly wooded land in the middle, up to Bashan and Mount Hermon in the north. The Arnon, Jabbok and Yarmuk rivers cut this land into natural sections from east to west.
- **"Yet the people of Israel did not drive out the Geshurites or the Maacathites" (v13).** The first of several notes in Joshua about peoples not driven out (15:63; 16:10; 17:12–13). Each is said without comment, but together they set up the troubles of Judges. These two small kingdoms, north and east of the Sea of Galilee, lived "in the midst of Israel to this day." Later, David married Maacah, a princess of Geshur, and their son Absalom fled to Geshur after murdering his brother (2 Sam 3:3; 13:37–38).
- **"To the tribe of Levi alone Moses gave no inheritance" (v14).** Levi, the tribe of priests and their helpers, received no territory. Instead, "the offerings by fire to the LORD God of Israel are their inheritance." The Levites lived on a share of what the people brought to God (Num 18:20–24; Deut 18:1–2). They would be given towns to live in, scattered throughout the other tribes (ch 21). This note appears again in v33 and in 14:3–4 and 18:7, framing the whole division of the land.
- **Reuben's share (vv15–23).** Reuben was Jacob's firstborn, but he lost the firstborn's place because he slept with his father's concubine (Gen 35:22; 49:3–4; 1 Chr 5:1). His tribe's land was the southern tableland, from the Arnon north to around Heshbon. It was good grazing land but exposed to Moab, and over time Moab took much of it back. The Moabite Stone, an inscription by King Mesha of Moab from about 840 BC, boasts of taking towns named in this very list, including Dibon, Medeba, Jahaz, Kiriathaim and Beth-baal-meon. Mesha mentions "the men of Gad," but not Reuben.
- **Place names with "Baal" (v17).** Bamoth-baal ("high places of Baal") and Beth-baal-meon ("house of Baal of Meon") preserve the old worship of the region. Beth-peor (v20) was near the site where Israel joined itself to "Baal of Peor" in the plains of Moab (Num 25:1–3). The land carried reminders of temptations Israel had already fallen into.
- **"The leaders of Midian, Evi and Rekem and Zur and Hur and Reba" (v21).** These five Midianite chiefs were killed in the war against Midian after the sin at Peor (Num 31:8). Here they are called "princes of Sihon," probably meaning they were his allies or vassals. Zur's daughter Cozbi was the Midianite woman killed with an Israelite man by Phinehas (Num 25:15).
- **"Balaam also, the son of Beor, the one who practiced divination" (v22).** The famous prophet-for-hire who was hired by Balak of Moab to curse Israel, but could only bless them (Num 22–24). Afterward he advised Midian to draw Israel into idolatry and sexual sin at Peor (Num 31:16; Rev 2:14). Here, he is not called a prophet but a diviner, using *qosem*, a word for forbidden fortune-telling (Deut 18:10). He died by the sword with the people he had helped. Henry notes that "he fell with those that set him on," and that the defeat of Balaam's plan to curse Israel was "the turning of that curse into a blessing."
- **Gad's share (vv24–28).** North of Reuben, from around Heshbon up through Gilead to the southern end of the Sea of Galilee ("Chinnereth," v27), including half the old Ammonite land. Mahanaim (v26) is where Jacob saw the angels of God on his return home (Gen 32:2). Succoth (v27) is where Jacob built shelters for his flocks (Gen 33:17). The land east of the Jordan is full of Jacob's footsteps.
- **The half-tribe of Manasseh (vv29–31).** The northern region, from Mahanaim through all of Bashan, the former kingdom of Og, including "the towns of Jair… sixty cities." Jair and Machir were descendants of Manasseh who had led the capture of this region (Num 32:39–41). Bashan was rich land, but open to attack from Aram (Syria) to the north and east, and it was among the first areas lost when Assyria invaded (2 Kings 15:29; 1 Chr 5:26).
- **"The LORD God of Israel is their inheritance" (v33).** The chapter ends where v14 left off, but with a stronger phrase. In v14 their inheritance is the offerings to the LORD. In v33 it is "the LORD God of Israel" himself. The other tribes received fields and towns. Levi received God. As Numbers 18:20 says to Aaron, "I am your portion and your inheritance among the people of Israel." The psalmist uses the same language for any believer: "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup" (Ps 16:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Levi's people "must be scattered in all the tribes, and their maintenance brought out of all the tribes," so that the whole nation would be within reach of teaching. On Balaam he says he was "soon after recompensed according to the wickedness of his endeavour." JFB has no comment on vv9–33, beyond its point that the eastern boundaries were written down to settle future disputes. Guzik adds background on Geshur, linking it to Absalom, and on the Levites, whose support came from the offerings while their homes were the Levitical cities of chapters 20–21. He applies v33 to all believers, whom the New Testament calls priests (1 Pet 2:5): those who are tempted to resent their place in life can remember that God himself is their inheritance. The Tyndale notes connect Reuben's weak, exposed land with his loss of the firstborn's rights.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joshua was told to divide land that was not yet fully taken. God's word made it as good as theirs, even while enemies still lived on it. The New Testament describes the Christian life the same way. In Christ believers already have "an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven" (1 Pet 1:4), and yet there is still "very much land to possess" in this life, as sin is fought and faith grows. And the strangest inheritance in the chapter turns out to be the best. Levi got no fields, only the LORD. Through Christ that portion is opened to all his people, who are "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9) and "heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ" (Rom 8:17). Every field in Canaan would eventually be lost to invaders. The inheritance that is God himself cannot be.
