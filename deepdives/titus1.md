*Setting:* Titus is one of the three "Pastoral Letters" (with 1 and 2 Timothy), written by Paul to men he had left in charge of young churches. Acts never mentions a mission on Crete, so most readers who hold that Paul wrote these letters place it after Acts ends: Paul is released from his first Roman imprisonment (about AD 62), travels again, works on Crete with Titus, and moves on, leaving Titus to finish the job. The letter probably dates to around AD 62–64, before 2 Timothy. Crete is a long, mountainous island south of the Aegean Sea, on the main sea lanes, with an old reputation for piracy, mercenaries and dishonesty, and with a sizable Jewish community. Cretans heard Peter at Pentecost (Acts 2:11), and Paul's prison ship sheltered off its south coast at Fair Havens (Acts 27:7–13). Titus himself was a Greek, never circumcised (Gal 2:3), whom Paul trusted with the hardest jobs, above all the strained relationship with the church at Corinth (2 Cor 7:6–7; 8:23). He is never named in Acts.

#### Greeting (vv1–4)
- **The longest opening of the three Pastorals.** Paul packs a whole theology into the greeting: his calling, the purpose of it, the hope behind it and God's timing. In a short letter to a trusted co-worker that may seem odd, but the letter would be read aloud to Cretan churches. The greeting establishes, for everyone listening, whose authority Titus carries.
- **"A servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ" (v1).** Paul usually calls himself a servant (*doulos*, "slave") of Christ. "Servant of God" is found only here in his letters, and it was the Old Testament title of Moses, David and the prophets. Slave and apostle together: utterly owned, and fully sent.
- **"For the sake of the faith of God's elect and their knowledge of the truth, which accords with godliness" (v1).** Paul's apostleship exists for others: to bring God's chosen people to faith and to a grasp of the truth. And this truth is never mere information. It "accords with godliness," it produces a reverent, God-centered life. That phrase sets up the whole letter, which keeps tying right teaching to right living (1:16; 2:1, 11–12; 3:8).
- **"God, who never lies" (v2).** One Greek word, *apseudēs*, "un-lying." It quietly sets up v12, where the Cretans are called "always liars." The God of the gospel stands in contrast to the island's religious lies. His promise of eternal life can be trusted because of who makes it (Num 23:19; Heb 6:18).
- **"Promised before the ages began and at the proper time manifested" (vv2–3).** The plan is older than time, but it was made public at a chosen moment (Gal 4:4; 1 Tim 2:6). Paul does not say it was manifested in a person here, though that is true (2 Tim 1:9–10). He says it was manifested "in his word through the preaching." The gospel reaches people through proclamation.
- **"The command of God our Savior" (v3).** "Command" is *epitagē*, used in inscriptions for royal or divine orders. Paul preaches under orders. "Savior" is a favorite word in these letters. Titus uses it of God (1:3; 2:10; 3:4) and of Christ (1:4; 2:13; 3:6), each three times, as if to say the saving work is one work of Father and Son.
- **"To Titus, my true child in a common faith" (v4).** "True child" (as with Timothy, 1 Tim 1:2) probably means Paul led him to Christ, or at least that Titus was a genuine son in the faith. "Common faith" matters: Paul is a Jew and Titus a Gentile, and they share one faith. That will matter when Titus faces teachers "of the circumcision party" (v10). Later manuscripts add "mercy" in v4 ("grace, mercy, and peace," as in the KJV); the oldest have "grace and peace."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The highest officers in the church are but servants," and "all gospel truth is after godliness." Guzik draws a line between truth that only informs and truth that leads to godliness, and on "the proper time" he points to the setting God prepared: a common Greek language, Roman roads and Roman peace. JFB notes that "servant of God" in this form is found nowhere else in Paul, and that the message "had lain hidden in His eternal purpose" before it was preached. The Tyndale notes make the same link Paul's wording invites, between the God who cannot lie and the Cretan liars of v12.

#### Qualifications for Elders (vv5–9)
- **"This is why I left you in Crete" (v5).** The work was begun but not finished. "Put what remained into order" is *epidiorthoō*, found only here, to set right what is still lacking. Some preachers call it a medical term for setting a broken bone; the lexicon does not support that, though the idea of straightening something is fair. The prefix stresses completing what Paul left undone.
- **"Appoint elders in every town as I directed you" (v5).** This was Paul's pattern from the first journey (Acts 14:23). Crete was known for its many towns (Homer called it the island of a hundred cities). "Elders" is plural: each town's church, likely meeting in several homes, was led by a group, not one man. Christians have organized this differently through history (bishops, elders, congregations), but all appeal to passages like this one.
- **"Elders"… "overseer" (vv5, 7).** The same men are called *presbyteros*, "elder," in v5 and *episkopos*, "overseer" (the source of "bishop" and "episcopal"), in v7. Most interpreters agree the words name one office in Paul's day. "Elder" speaks of maturity and dignity, from the Jewish synagogue. "Overseer" speaks of the job, watching over the flock, a word Greek cities used for supervisors (Acts 20:17, 28). The later pattern of one bishop over several elders came after the New Testament.
- **"Above reproach" (vv6–7).** Said twice. The word *anegklētos* means "not open to a charge," someone against whom no accusation will stick. It does not mean sinless. It means no scandal hanging over him. Paul's list in 1 Timothy 3:2 uses a different word with a similar meaning.
- **"The husband of one wife" (v6).** Literally "a one-woman man." Readers differ: some take it to exclude polygamists, some anyone remarried after divorce, some (in early church history) anyone remarried even after a spouse's death. Most now read it as faithfulness: a man devoted to his wife, with no pattern of unfaithfulness. It probably does not require that an elder be married, or Paul himself would have been unqualified.
- **"His children are believers and not open to the charge of debauchery or insubordination" (v6).** "Believers" could also be translated "faithful" (trustworthy, well behaved); the Greek word can mean either, and interpreters divide. The charges named are clear: "debauchery" (*asōtia*) is wasteful, reckless living, the same root used of the prodigal son's "reckless living" (Luke 15:13). "Insubordination" (*anupotaktos*) is the same word used of the false teachers in v10. A man whose household is out of control cannot steady God's household (1 Tim 3:4–5).
- **"As God's steward" (v7).** A steward (*oikonomos*) managed a household or estate on behalf of the owner. He was often a slave himself. The church is God's household and the elder only manages it. It is not his.
- **Five "not"s (v7).** Not "arrogant" (*authadēs*, self-willed, always insisting on his own way); not "quick-tempered" (inclined to anger, simmering rather than flaring); not "a drunkard"; not "violent" (a bully, with fists or words); not "greedy for gain," literally greedy for shameful profit. That last one points straight at the false teachers, who teach "for shameful gain" (v11), using the same words.
- **Six positives (v8).** "Hospitable" is *philoxenos*, "loving strangers." Travelling believers depended on Christian homes, since inns were often dirty and dangerous. "A lover of good," "self-controlled" (*sōphrōn*, sound-minded, a word that runs through ch 2), "upright" toward others, "holy" toward God, "disciplined" in himself. Compared with 1 Timothy 3, the list leaves out "not a recent convert," probably because every believer on Crete was recent, and it adds things that fit a rough setting: self-willed, short-tempered men are named directly.
- **Character, not credentials.** Nothing on the list is about wealth, education, speaking talent or social rank, the things that commonly qualified a man for office in the Greek world. Almost every item is asked of all believers elsewhere. The elder is to be an example of ordinary Christian maturity.
- **"He must hold firm to the trustworthy word as taught" (v9).** One skill is required: a firm grip on the apostolic teaching as he received it. He is not to invent but to hand on. And he must use it two ways: to "give instruction in sound doctrine" (literally "healthy teaching," *hygiainō*) and "to rebuke those who contradict it." A shepherd both feeds sheep and drives off wolves.
- **A strange addition in one manuscript.** A single late manuscript (minuscule 460, thirteenth century) adds a few lines after v9 forbidding the ordination of men married twice, and another after v11 about children who strike their parents. No other manuscript has them, and no modern translation includes them. They show how later readers tried to apply this chapter.
- **Commentators:** All three agree that "elder" and "overseer" name the same office. JFB: "Those called 'elders' here are called 'bishops' in Tit 1:7." On v6 JFB asks, "He who could not bring his children to faith, how shall he bring others?" Henry on v7: "How unfit are those to govern a church who cannot govern themselves, or their own turbulent and unruly passions!" Henry also explains "blameless" as "not absolutely without fault, so none are." Guzik treats the list as a target every believer should aim at, not just a checklist for leaders, says "one-woman man" does not require marriage or automatically exclude a widower who remarried, and stresses that the qualification is character and grip on the truth, not training or giftedness. The Tyndale notes suggest the list is shaped for new converts in a rough culture and that deacons are not mentioned because the churches were still small.

#### Rebuke Those Who Contradict Sound Doctrine (vv10–16)
- **"Many who are insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers" (v10).** Three descriptions. "Insubordinate" (the same word as v6) means refusing any authority. "Empty talkers" is *mataiologos*, a talker of foolishness, impressive-sounding but hollow. "Deceivers" is *phrenapatēs*, literally a "mind-deceiver," someone who misleads people's thinking, and perhaps his own.
- **"Especially those of the circumcision party" (v10).** These were Jewish believers or teachers who pressed Jewish law and tradition on Gentile Christians. Josephus mentions many Jews living on Crete. The details in this letter point to "Jewish myths," purity rules (vv14–15) and "genealogies… and quarrels about the law" (3:9). This is not quite the Galatian problem, where circumcision itself was demanded; the emphasis here is speculation and ritual rules. Paul's companion Titus, an uncircumcised Greek, was a living answer to them (Gal 2:3).
- **"They must be silenced" (v11).** The verb *epistomizō* means to put a bridle or muzzle on a mouth. Titus had no power to force anyone, so the silencing is by argument and by church discipline (3:10–11), answering them so plainly that their teaching gets no hearing.
- **"Upsetting whole families" (v11).** "Families" is "households," the churches' meeting places. Overturn a household and a house church goes with it. That is why chapter 2 turns to how each member of a household should live.
- **"For shameful gain" (v11).** The motive was money, the very thing elders must not be greedy for (v7). Paul repeatedly warns about religion as a business (1 Tim 6:5; 2 Pet 2:3).
- **"One of the Cretans, a prophet of their own" (v12).** The line is traditionally from Epimenides of Knossos, a sixth-century BC Cretan poet and seer whom Greeks regarded as a prophet; later writers such as Jerome name him. The first words also appear in the Greek poet Callimachus, who was angry that Cretans claimed to have the tomb of Zeus, an obvious lie to anyone who believed Zeus immortal. Paul may also quote Epimenides in Acts 17:28. Paul calls him a "prophet" because that is what the Cretans called him, not because his words were inspired.
- **"Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" (v12).** Greeks coined the verb *krētizō*, "to Cretanize," meaning to lie. "Evil beasts" means wild and savage. "Lazy gluttons" is literally "idle bellies," people driven by appetite who will not work. Later logicians made a puzzle of the line (if a Cretan says all Cretans lie, is he lying?), but that is not Paul's interest. He uses it as a description his readers would recognize.
- **"This testimony is true" (v13).** Paul agrees with the stereotype, which sounds harsh. But he is aiming it mainly at the false teachers, who show the island's old vices: lying, savagery and greed. And he plainly does not think Cretans are beyond help. He has planted churches among them and gives them the beautiful grace of 2:11–14. The gospel can change a Cretan.
- **"Rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith" (v13).** The rebuke is meant to heal: "that they may be sound," healthy again. Even sharp correction aims at bringing the person back.
- **"Jewish myths and the commands of people" (v14).** Probably speculative stories built around Old Testament genealogies and figures, along with man-made rules about food and purity (Col 2:20–22; 1 Tim 1:4; 4:3). Jesus said such rules were "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" (Mark 7:7, quoting Isa 29:13).
- **"To the pure, all things are pure" (v15).** Jesus declared all foods clean (Mark 7:15–19), and Paul says the same (Rom 14:14, 20; 1 Tim 4:4–5). Purity is not a matter of what a person eats or touches. "Pure" (*katharos*) is the word for ritual cleanness, but Paul moves it to the heart. To a person whose "minds and… consciences are defiled," even good things become tainted, because the problem is inside.
- **"They profess to know God, but they deny him by their works" (v16).** The chapter's verdict. Their talk was religious, but their lives contradicted it, the opposite of the truth "which accords with godliness" (v1). John says much the same (1 John 2:4). "Detestable" (*bdeluktos*) is a word the Greek Old Testament uses for what God abhors. "Unfit" is *adokimos*, a word for metal or coin that fails the test, counterfeit. And "unfit for any good work" sets up the repeated call of the rest of the letter: true believers are "zealous for good works" (2:14; 3:1, 8, 14).
- **Commentators:** On v11 Henry says their mouths must be stopped "not by outward force," but by conviction, and Guzik also says silencing means answering them with the truth. JFB traces the problem to "corrupted Judaism" and cites Josephus for the many Jews on Crete. On v12 JFB says "To Cretanize" was proverbial for lying, and Guzik makes the same point and adds that Paul did not ask Titus to find an easier place to work. Henry on v13: "The sharpest reproofs must aim at the good of the reproved," and JFB agrees the aim is restoration to sound faith. On v16 JFB: Paul does not deny their theoretical knowledge of God "but that they practically know Him." Henry: "their practice is a contradiction to their profession." Guzik reads *adokimos* as a counterfeit coin, which fits the lexicon well.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter sets a God who never lies against an island known for lying, and true shepherds against teachers who do it for money. Every quality asked of an elder is a small picture of the Good Shepherd: blameless, gentle, hospitable to strangers, never greedy, holding fast to his Father's word, feeding the flock and guarding it from wolves (John 10:11–15). The "hope of eternal life" promised before time began (v2) is not an idea the Cretans need to work out, but a promise God has kept in Jesus, "God our Savior," who turns liars and idle bellies into people "zealous for good works."
