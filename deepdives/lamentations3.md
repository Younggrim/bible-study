*Setting:* This is the third of the five laments over Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC, and the center of the book, both in position and in meaning. It is the most tightly built of the poems. Like chapters 1 and 2, it follows the Hebrew alphabet, but here each letter gets three verses, and every line of the three begins with that letter, so the chapter has 66 verses. The voice also changes. Chapters 1 and 2 pictured Jerusalem as a woman. Here a single man speaks, "the man who has seen affliction." Many readers, from the Greek translators onward, have heard Jeremiah himself, whose sufferings in a muddy cistern (Jer 38:6) seem to lie behind vv52–55. Others take the man as a representative sufferer who speaks for the whole people, the way a psalm of lament gives one voice to many. Both may be true: one person's pain becomes a pattern for everyone. The chapter moves from the darkest despair in the book (vv1–20), to its brightest hope (vv21–39), to a call for the nation to repent (vv40–47), and ends with a prayer born of answered prayer (vv48–66). Thomas Chisholm's hymn "Great Is Thy Faithfulness" (1923) takes its title from verse 23.

#### Great Is Your Faithfulness (vv1–66)
- **"I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of his wrath" (v1).** "Man" is *geber*, a word for a strong, able-bodied man. The one who should be strong has been broken. **"The rod of his wrath"**: God's rod of discipline. Isaiah called Assyria "the rod of my anger" (Isa 10:5). Notice that "his" has no name attached. God is not named until v18, but he is the subject of nearly every verb.
- **"He has driven and brought me into darkness without any light" (v2).** The exact reversal of a shepherd's care. The Shepherd of Psalm 23 leads beside still waters. This one has led into darkness.
- **"He has made my flesh and my skin waste away; he has broken my bones" (v4).** Starvation and grief take their toll on the body (compare Job 30:30; Ps 32:3). **"He has besieged and enveloped me with bitterness and tribulation" (v5)**: the speaker is like Jerusalem under siege. **"He has made me dwell in darkness like the dead of long ago" (v6)**, a line almost identical to Psalm 143:3.
- **"He has walled me about so that I cannot escape; he has made my chains heavy" (v7).** Job used the same images: God "has walled up my way, so that I cannot pass" (Job 19:8). **"Though I call and cry for help, he shuts out my prayer" (v8).** The worst part of the darkness is that prayer itself seems blocked. **"He has blocked my ways with blocks of stones; he has made my paths crooked" (v9).** The word "blocks of stones" means hewn stone, the kind used for city walls. Hosea used a similar picture of God hedging in his straying people so they would turn back (Hos 2:6–7).
- **"He is a bear lying in wait for me, a lion in hiding" (v10).** Hosea had warned that God would meet Israel "like a bear robbed of her cubs" (Hos 13:7–8). **"He bent his bow and set me as a target for his arrow" (v12).** Job too complained, "The arrows of the Almighty are in me" (Job 6:4; 16:12–13). **"He drove into my kidneys the arrows of his quiver" (v13).** "Kidneys" in Hebrew thought were the seat of the deepest feelings, as "heart" is for us.
- **"I have become the laughingstock of all my people" (v14).** Jeremiah himself said almost the same thing: "I have become a laughingstock all the day; everyone mocks me" (Jer 20:7). A few Hebrew manuscripts read "of all peoples," but most read "my people."
- **"He has filled me with bitterness; he has sated me with wormwood" (v15).** Wormwood is a plant with an intensely bitter taste, a picture of grief that poisons everything (Jer 9:15; 23:15).
- **"He has made my teeth grind on gravel, and made me cower in ashes" (v16).** Bread baked in hot ashes often had grit in it. The speaker's food is all grit. **"I have forgotten what happiness is" (v17).** The word is *tobah*, "good." He can no longer remember what good feels like.
- **"So I say, 'My endurance has perished; so has my hope from the LORD'" (v18).** This is the bottom. For the first time in the chapter he names the LORD, and it is to say that his hope in him has died.
- **"Remember my affliction and my wanderings, the wormwood and the gall!" (v19).** He turns from speaking about God to speaking to him. That alone is a sign of life. **"My soul continually remembers it and is bowed down within me" (v20).** Memory has been his enemy, replaying the pain. An ancient scribal tradition records that the original read "your soul," that is, God's soul, will remember and bow down. That reading is uncertain, but it would make the turn in v21 even more striking.
- **"But this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope" (v21).** The hinge of the chapter and of the whole book. The same faculty that has been crushing him, memory, now becomes the means of hope. He chooses what to remember. Nothing outside him has changed. The city is still in ruins. What changes is what he calls to mind.
- **"The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end" (v22).** "Steadfast love" is *ḥesed* (here plural, "loving acts"), God's loyal, covenant love, the love that keeps its promises when the other party has failed. "Mercies" is *raḥamim*, related to the word for "womb," the deep tender compassion of a mother for her child (Isa 49:15). **"Never ceases"** follows the Syriac translation and the Targum. The traditional Hebrew text reads "we are not cut off," as in the King James: "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed." The difference is one letter. Both readings are true: his love never ends, and that is why the people still exist.
- **"They are new every morning; great is your faithfulness" (v23).** Like the manna that came fresh each morning and could not be stored (Exod 16:19–21), God's mercies come daily, enough for today. **"Faithfulness"** is *'emunah*, steadiness, reliability, the root of the word "amen." The God who kept his word in judgment (2:17) will keep his word in mercy.
- **"'The LORD is my portion,' says my soul, 'therefore I will hope in him'" (v24).** When Israel divided the land, the priestly tribe of Levi received no territory. The LORD said, "I am your portion and your inheritance" (Num 18:20). The psalmists claimed the same (Ps 16:5; 73:26; 142:5). The land is gone. The speaker has lost his inheritance, but not the LORD himself. **"Hope"** here is a word for waiting, *yaḥal*, expecting with patience.
- **"The LORD is good to those who wait for him, to the soul who seeks him" (v25).** The *ṭet* stanza (vv25–27) begins all three lines with *ṭob*, "good." Three times: it is good. The man who forgot what good was (v17) now declares it.
- **"It is good that one should wait quietly for the salvation of the LORD" (v26).** Quietness here means not complaining or forcing the issue, trusting God's timing (Ps 37:7; Isa 30:15).
- **"It is good for a man that he bear the yoke in his youth" (v27).** Hardship learned early can shape a person for a lifetime. Jeremiah was called while still "only a youth" (Jer 1:6), and suffered from the start.
- **"Let him sit alone in silence when it is laid on him" (v28).** Not the bitter silence of despair, but the silence of submission before God's discipline (Ps 39:9). **"Let him put his mouth in the dust—there may yet be hope" (v29).** Lying face down was the posture of humble surrender (Josh 7:6). The words **"there may yet be hope"** are tentative. Hope here is honest, not forced.
- **"Let him give his cheek to the one who strikes, and let him be filled with insults" (v30).** Isaiah's Servant says, "I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard" (Isa 50:6). Jesus taught his disciples to turn the other cheek (Matt 5:39), and he himself was struck and mocked in silence (Matt 26:67–68).
- **"For the Lord will not cast off forever" (v31).** The *kaph* stanza (vv31–33) gives three reasons, each beginning with *ki*, "for." First, God's rejection is not permanent. Second, **"though he cause grief, he will have compassion according to the abundance of his steadfast love" (v32).** Third, **"he does not afflict from his heart or grieve the children of men" (v33).** "From his heart" means willingly, from his deepest desire. God does bring suffering, the chapter never denies it, but it is not what he delights in. Ezekiel gives the same insight: "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (Ezek 33:11). Isaiah calls judgment God's "strange work" (Isa 28:21).
- **"To crush underfoot all the prisoners of the earth… to subvert a man in his lawsuit, the Lord does not approve" (vv34–36).** The *lamed* stanza is one long sentence. God does not approve of oppression or crooked courts. This also answers a question: if God sent the Babylonians, does he approve of their cruelty? No. And Judah's own courts had done the same things (Jer 5:28; 22:3, 13–17).
- **"Who has spoken and it came to pass, unless the Lord has commanded it?" (v37).** Nothing happens outside God's rule. **"Is it not from the mouth of the Most High that good and bad come?" (v38).** "Bad" here means calamity, not moral evil. God is sovereign over disaster as well as blessing (Job 2:10; Isa 45:7; Amos 3:6). Scripture holds both truths together: he rules over everything, and he does not afflict from his heart (v33).
- **"Why should a living man complain, a man, about the punishment of his sins?" (v39).** The *geber* again, the strong man of v1. He is still alive, and life itself is mercy (v22). Complaint against God does not fit someone who is suffering for his own sins. This is not a ban on lament, since the whole book is lament. It is a ban on accusing God of injustice.
- **"Let us test and examine our ways, and return to the LORD!" (v40).** For the first time the "I" becomes "we." The individual's lesson becomes a call to the whole people. **"Return"** (*shub*) is Jeremiah's great word for repentance (Jer 3:12–14, 22). **"Let us lift up our hearts and hands to God in heaven" (v41).** Not just hands, the outward gesture of prayer, but hearts too.
- **"We have transgressed and rebelled, and you have not forgiven" (v42).** A remarkably honest prayer. They confess, and then they admit that the evidence of forgiveness has not yet come. The ruins are still there.
- **"You have wrapped yourself with anger and pursued us… you have wrapped yourself with a cloud so that no prayer can pass through" (vv43–44).** The prayer of v8 again: heaven seems sealed. **"You have made us scum and garbage among the peoples" (v45).** Paul uses similar words of the apostles: "the scum of the world, the refuse of all things" (1 Cor 4:13).
- **"Panic and pitfall have come upon us, devastation and destruction" (v47).** Two sound-pairs in Hebrew: *paḥad wapaḥat*, *hashe't wehashaber*. Isaiah uses the first: "terror and the pit and the snare" (Isa 24:17; Jer 48:43).
- **"My eyes flow with rivers of tears… until the LORD from heaven looks down and sees" (vv48–50).** The "I" returns. The weeping prophet (Jer 9:1; 14:17) will not stop crying until God answers. Tears become a form of prayer. **"The fate of all the daughters of my city" (v51)** may mean the young women, or the towns and villages around Jerusalem.
- **"I have been hunted like a bird by those who were my enemies without cause" (v52).** Bird hunters used nets and traps. **"Without cause"** echoes the psalms of the innocent sufferer (Ps 35:7, 19; 69:4). Jesus applied those words to himself: "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25).
- **"They flung me alive into the pit and cast stones on me; water closed over my head; I said, 'I am lost'" (vv53–54).** Jeremiah was lowered into a cistern by his enemies and sank in the mud (Jer 38:6). There was no water in it, only mud, so the water and stones here may be poetic pictures of near death, as in the psalms ("the waters have come up to my neck," Ps 69:1–2, 14–15). Many readers think the poet draws on Jeremiah's experience. Others see the language as a standard way to describe being near the grave.
- **"I called on your name, O LORD, from the depths of the pit; you heard my plea" (vv55–56).** Now the tense shifts. He remembers a past prayer that God answered. This is not fresh despair. It is testimony. Jonah prayed the same way from the deep (Jon 2:2).
- **"You came near when I called on you; you said, 'Do not fear!'" (v57).** "Do not fear" is the classic word God speaks to his people in the prophets when he promises rescue (Isa 41:10, 13–14; 43:1, 5). The God who seemed to wrap himself in a cloud (v44) has come near.
- **"You have taken up my cause, O Lord; you have redeemed my life" (v58).** Two legal pictures. God is his advocate in court, and his *go'el*, the kinsman-redeemer who buys back a relative's freedom (see Jer 50:34). **"You have seen the wrong done to me, O LORD; judge my cause" (v59).** Because God helped before, the speaker can ask again.
- **"You have heard their taunts… Behold their sitting and their rising; I am the object of their taunts" (vv61–63).** "Sitting and rising" means all day long, everything they do. Their mocking songs are about him (compare v14).
- **"You will repay them, O LORD, according to the work of their hands" (v64).** The ESV reads vv64–66 as statements of confidence, "You will." The Hebrew verbs can also be read as requests, "Repay them," as many translations do. Either way, the speaker hands his enemies over to God rather than taking revenge himself (Rom 12:19). **"You will give them dullness of heart" (v65)**: literally "a covering of heart," a hardness that cannot see or feel. **"From under your heavens, O LORD" (v66).** The chapter that began in darkness under God's rod ends with God enthroned in heaven as judge of all.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry captures the turn at v21: "God's compassions fail not; of this we have fresh instances every morning." On v24: "Portions on earth are perishing things, but God is a portion for ever." On v39 he writes, "what we complain of, is far less than our sins deserve." Guzik reads the speaker as Jeremiah suffering as one man who stands for the nation, and notes that corporate suffering is always felt personally. He calls v21 the first moment in the book when hope is allowed, and argues that God must be someone's portion before he can be their hope (v24). On v33 he compares God to a wise parent who disciplines to help, not for his own gratification, and he connects v30 with the abuse of Jesus at his trial. JFB sees Jeremiah's sufferings in the dungeon and the siege as "types of that of Christ," and reads v8 and v30 as fulfilled in Christ (Ps 22:2). On v24: "To have God for our portion is the one only foundation of hope." On v27 JFB adds, "The earlier the better; for the old are full of prejudices." On v41 he calls lifting the heart along with the hands "the antidote to hypocrisy," and he notes that the cistern of Jeremiah 38 had "no water," so the waters of v54 are figurative. JFB gives no separate notes on many verses here, including vv2–6, 10–13 and 45–52.

#### Pointing to Christ
The man who "has seen affliction under the rod of his wrath" speaks for a whole people, and Christians have long seen in him a picture of the one Man who bore the rod for others. Jesus was hunted "without cause" (v52; John 15:25), gave his cheek to the smiters and was filled with insults (v30; Matt 26:67), was set as a target for God's arrows (v12) and cried out when heaven seemed sealed (v8; Matt 27:46), and was laid in a pit with a stone rolled over it (v53; Matt 27:60). Then the Father heard him from the depths and raised him (v55). Because Christ bore the wrath, the hope of vv22–24 becomes certain for all who belong to him: the steadfast love of the LORD never ceases, his mercies are new every morning, and the LORD is their portion forever.
