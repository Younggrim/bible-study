*Setting:* Jonah, a prophet of the northern kingdom in the days of Jeroboam II (about 793–753 BC), was told to preach against Nineveh, the capital of brutal Assyria. He fled by ship toward Tarshish instead. God sent a storm, the pagan sailors threw Jonah overboard at his own word, and the sea went calm. As he sank, "the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah" (1:17). In Hebrew Bibles that verse is 2:1, so Hebrew verse numbers in this chapter run one higher than English ones. Chapter 2 is almost entirely a poem, a psalm of thanksgiving Jonah prays from inside the fish. It is not a cry of despair in the fish. It looks back on the moment he was drowning, when he cried out and God answered by sending the fish.

#### Jonah's Prayer (vv1–10)
- **"Then Jonah prayed to the LORD his God" (v1).** For the first time in the book, Jonah prays. The sailors cried to their gods and then to the LORD (1:5, 14) while the prophet slept and stayed silent. Now he speaks to "the LORD *his* God." Even after running away, he can still call God his own. JFB compares the returning prodigal, who still says "my father" (Luke 15:18).
- **"From the belly of the fish" (v1).** In 1:17 and 2:10 the fish is *dag*, masculine. Here it is *dagah*, the feminine form. Some old Jewish stories made much of this, saying Jonah was moved to a second, more cramped fish before he finally prayed. Most scholars think *dagah* is simply a collective or alternative form of the same word, and the text gives no second fish. It is a curiosity, not a teaching.
- **When was this prayed?** The prayer speaks of being heard and rescued in the past tense: "he answered me… you brought up my life from the pit" (vv2, 6). So most readers think Jonah is thanking God for the fish itself, which had saved him from drowning. The Tyndale notes add that its careful psalm shape suggests Jonah may have put it into final form after the event, remembering what he felt. Guzik thinks Jonah had been crying out to God throughout the three days, and that this prayer came once he had assurance he would be delivered.
- **"I called out to the LORD, out of my distress, and he answered me" (v2).** The first line sums up the whole psalm: distress, a cry, an answer. It is very close to Psalm 120:1, "In my distress I called to the LORD, and he answered me." The verb "called" (*qara'*) is the key word from chapter 1. God told Jonah to "call out against" Nineveh (1:2), and the captain told him to "call out to your god" (1:6). He refused both times. Now, at the bottom of the sea, he calls.
- **"Out of the belly of Sheol I cried" (v2).** Sheol is the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead, the grave seen as a place below. The picture of Sheol having a "belly" appears nowhere else in the Old Testament. The Tyndale notes point out the wordplay: Jonah was in the belly of death, and God rescued him through the belly of a fish. Jonah does not mean he had literally died. He means he was as good as dead, already in death's grip, as the psalmists say when they were near death (Ps 18:4–5; 30:3).
- **"For you cast me into the deep" (v3).** The sailors threw him in (1:15), but Jonah says *God* did. He sees the hand of God behind the human hands. JFB: he traces his suffering to "the hand of God," not to chance, as Job did (Job 1:21). "All your breakers and your waves passed over me" is almost word for word from Psalm 42:7. The waves are *God's* waves.
- **Jonah's prayer is made of psalms.** Nearly every line echoes the Psalter: Psalm 120:1 (v2), 42:7 (v3), 31:22 (v4), 69:1–2 (v5), 18:4–6 and 30:3 (vv2, 6), 142:3 (v7), 31:6 (v8), 116:17–18 and 3:8 (v9). JFB says Jonah "incorporates with his own language inspired utterances familiar to the Church long before." Guzik notes that Jonah had no scroll in the fish, so these words were stored in his memory. Scripture he had learned became his prayer when he had nothing else.
- **"I am driven away from your sight" (v4).** This is the bitter irony of the chapter. Jonah fled "from the presence of the LORD" (1:3, 10), and now that he seems to have gotten what he wanted, it is the thing he dreads most. The Tyndale notes highlight that irony. The line echoes Psalm 31:22: "I had said in my alarm, 'I am cut off from your sight.'"
- **"Yet I shall again look upon your holy temple" (v4).** "Your holy temple" is the temple in Jerusalem, God's chosen place of worship (1 Kings 8:29–30, 38–39). Solomon had prayed that Israelites far away or in trouble could pray "toward this house" and be heard. Jonah, at the bottom of the sea, turns his heart that way. JFB says he looks "with the eye of faith" toward it. A few translations follow a small change in one Hebrew vowel and read it as a despairing question, "How shall I ever look again upon your holy temple?" (as in the NRSV). The ESV keeps the traditional Hebrew text, which turns from despair to hope in the middle of the verse.
- **"The waters closed in over me to take my life" (v5).** Literally "the waters surrounded me up to the *nephesh*," the throat or life (Ps 69:1). The poem is vivid: water closing over him, the deep all around, "weeds wrapped about my head." This is a drowning man's memory, tangled in seaweed as he sank.
- **"To the roots of the mountains I went down" (v6).** People in the ancient Near East pictured the mountains rooted deep under the sea, with the realm of the dead beneath them. **"To the land whose bars closed upon me forever."** The grave is pictured as a city with barred gates (Job 38:17; Isa 38:10). Jonah's long descent is complete. All through chapter 1 he "went down" (*yarad*): down to Joppa, down into the ship, down into its hold (1:3, 5). Now he went down as far as anyone can go.
- **"Yet you brought up my life from the pit, O LORD my God" (v6).** The poem turns on one word. The Tyndale notes call the change striking: Jonah was sure he would die, but he was still within God's reach. "The pit" (*shachat*) is another name for the grave. Every step of Jonah's descent was his own doing. The one way up is God's doing. Hezekiah said the same after his illness (Isa 38:17).
- **"When my life was fainting away, I remembered the LORD" (v7).** "Remembered" here means more than recalling. It means he turned to God and prayed, as the Tyndale notes say. It is the same pattern as Psalm 142:3. JFB calls it "the triumph of spirit over flesh, of faith over sense." **"My prayer came to you, into your holy temple."** In v4 Jonah hoped to look toward the temple. Here his prayer has already arrived there. The temple in Jerusalem was where God's name dwelt, but his throne was in heaven (Ps 11:4; 18:6), and no sea is deep enough to keep a prayer from it.
- **"Those who pay regard to vain idols forsake their hope of steadfast love" (v8).** "Vain idols" is literally "vapors of emptiness" (*havlei-shav'*), the same phrase as Psalm 31:6. *Hevel* is the "vanity" or "breath" of Ecclesiastes. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal, covenant love. The verse can be read two ways. "Their *chesed*" may mean the faithfulness they owe God, which idol-worshipers abandon. Or it may mean the steadfast love God offers them, which they turn their backs on. The ESV's "their hope of steadfast love" takes the second sense.
- **Who is v8 aimed at?** On the surface, at pagans like the sailors, who prayed to gods that could not stop the storm. But the sailors had already turned to the LORD (1:16), and some readers hear a note of self-satisfaction here, as if Jonah were saying "*those* people… *but I*." Others, including Guzik, see Jonah applying it to himself: running from God was his own way of clinging to something worthless and leaving behind the mercy God offered. The text does not decide. It is worth noticing that the book's next two chapters show pagans receiving God's *chesed* (3:10; 4:2), and Jonah resenting it.
- **"But I with the voice of thanksgiving will sacrifice to you; what I have vowed I will pay" (v9).** This echoes Psalm 116:17–18. A "sacrifice of thanksgiving" was a fellowship offering brought in public, at the temple, to thank God for a rescue (Lev 7:11–15). Jonah promises to do exactly what the sailors had already done on the ship: sacrifice and make vows (1:16). The pagans got there first.
- **"Salvation belongs to the LORD!" (v9).** This is the climax and the thesis of the book. Literally "Salvation is to/of the LORD" (Ps 3:8). Salvation is God's to give and God's alone. It is not owned by Israel, earned by the sailors or deserved by Nineveh. Guzik notes that the line cuts both ways: it is Jonah's own testimony, and it is a statement that salvation belongs to God and not to any nation. Jonah will soon find out how far God means it.
- **"And the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited Jonah out upon the dry land" (v10).** God speaks and the fish obeys at once, which is more than the prophet did in chapter 1. The word for "vomited" (*qi'*) is blunt and not polite. The book allows itself a touch of humor: the great prophet is spat up on the beach. "Dry land" (*yabbashah*) recalls Jonah's confession that the LORD made "the sea and the dry land" (1:9). The God who made both has now moved his prophet from one to the other. The text does not say where Jonah landed. JFB guesses the coast of Palestine, and Guzik notes that Nineveh was several hundred miles inland, so it was not a short walk.
- **Was Jonah's repentance real?** Readers differ. Some see a full turning here, with chapter 3's obedience as its proof. Others note that the prayer never mentions Nineveh or confesses the particular sin of running, and chapter 4 shows his heart still at odds with God. Guzik suggests repentance is both a moment and a process, and that Jonah's had begun on the ship and was still growing. The chapter shows a real cry and a real rescue. The rest of the book shows that being rescued by grace and loving grace for others are not yet the same thing in Jonah.
- **The sign of Jonah.** Jesus pointed to this moment: "For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matt 12:40). Jonah's descent into "the belly of Sheol" and his return to the land of the living became the picture Jesus chose for his own death and resurrection. Paul's "raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Cor 15:4) may have this passage partly in mind, along with Hosea 6:2.
- **Commentators:** Henry builds the chapter around Jonah's distress, his near despair, his encouragement and his praise. Of the setting he says, "No place is amiss for prayer," and he notes that Jonah prays to God "not only as the Lord, but as his God, a God in covenant with him." Henry also reads Jonah in the fish as a type of Christ in the grave, whose "very lying there cried to God for poor sinners." JFB lists the psalms Jonah draws on, line by line, and reads v9 as thanks offered "in the believing anticipation of sure deliverance," like Jehoshaphat's singers going out before the battle (2 Chr 20:21). Guzik stresses that Jonah praised God before he was out of the fish, and that being driven from God's sight hurt Jonah more than the water did. All three agree that the fish was God's rescue, not his punishment. On v8, Henry and JFB read the "lying vanities" as idols in general, while Guzik applies them to Jonah's own flight.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jonah went down into the deep because of his own disobedience, and God brought him up again. Jesus went down into death not for his own sin but for ours, under God's waves and breakers (Ps 42:7; Luke 22:42–44), and God raised him on the third day. That is why Jesus could call this chapter "the sign of Jonah" and say that "something greater than Jonah is here" (Matt 12:41). Jonah's best line, "Salvation belongs to the LORD," is fulfilled in the one whose very name means "the LORD saves." What God did for one runaway prophet in the sea, he did for the world in an empty tomb.
