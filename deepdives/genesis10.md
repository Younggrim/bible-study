*Setting:* Chapter 9 ended with Noah's three sons and the words "from these the people of the whole earth were dispersed" (9:19). Chapter 10 shows what that looked like. It is called the Table of Nations: a map of the world as Israel knew it, drawn as a family tree. It opens a new section of Genesis ("these are the generations of the sons of Noah," v1) that runs through the tower of Babel in 11:1–9. The two belong together. Chapter 10 describes the nations spread out "each with his own language" (v5), and chapter 11 explains how that scattering came about. Genesis puts the result before the cause on purpose: the spreading of the nations is first shown as God's blessing being fulfilled ("fill the earth," 9:1), and only then as his judgment on pride. The list also sets the stage for Abram, called in chapter 12 so that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (12:3). These are the families it means.

#### Nations Descended from Noah (vv1–32)
- **"These are the generations of the sons of Noah" (v1).** "Generations" is *toledot*, the heading word that marks each new section of Genesis (2:4; 5:1; 6:9; 11:10, 27). The order of the list is reversed from the usual "Shem, Ham, and Japheth": Japheth comes first, then Ham, and Shem last. Genesis regularly deals with the side branches first and then follows the main line, the one the story will stay with. Shem is held to the end because Abram comes from him.
- **What kind of list this is.** The "sons" here are often peoples, places and cities rather than single men. Many names have plural endings, like Ludim, Anamim and Philistines (vv13–14), meaning "the Ludites," "the Anamites." Egypt (*Mizraim*, v6) is a land name. Canaan "fathered Sidon," a city, and "the Jebusites" (vv15–16). The table mixes family descent with geography and political ties, the way an ancient reader would describe who belonged with whom. That explains some puzzles. Sheba and Havilah appear under Ham (v7) and again under Shem (vv28–29), probably because related peoples lived in the same regions of Arabia. Canaanites spoke a language close to Hebrew yet are listed under Ham, because the list follows history and territory, not language or race. It is not a racial chart, and it should never be read as ranking peoples.
- **Seventy nations.** Counting the names gives seventy (fourteen from Japheth, thirty from Ham, twenty-six from Shem). Seventy, seven times ten, is a number of fullness. Jacob's family going down to Egypt is also seventy (46:27; Exod 1:5), as if Israel were a small copy of the whole world, chosen for the world's sake. Deuteronomy 32:8 says that when God "divided mankind, he fixed the borders of the peoples." The ancient Greek translation counts seventy-two here, and some suggest Jesus sending out "seventy" or "seventy-two" (manuscripts differ, Luke 10:1) looks ahead to the gospel reaching all nations. That link is possible but not stated in the text.
- **The sons of Japheth (vv2–5).** These are the peoples to the north and west of Israel: across Asia Minor (modern Turkey), around the Black Sea, and over the Mediterranean islands. **Madai** are the Medes of Iran. **Javan** is the Ionian Greeks, and *Yavan* is still the Hebrew word for Greece. **Gomer** is usually linked with the Cimmerians, and **Ashkenaz** with the Scythians. **Tarshish** is a far western port, probably in Spain, where Jonah later tried to flee (Jon 1:3). **Kittim** is Cyprus. ESV has **Dodanim**, but 1 Chronicles 1:7 and the Greek translation read **Rodanim**, Rhodes. In Hebrew, *d* and *r* look almost alike, and most think Rodanim is original.
- **Magog, Meshech and Tubal (v2).** These names return in Ezekiel 38–39, where "Gog, of the land of Magog, the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal" leads an invasion from the far north, and Magog appears again in Revelation 20:8. The Tyndale notes place these peoples in Asia Minor. Some modern prophecy teachers identify them with Russia, often linking Meshech with Moscow, but that rests on sound-alike names, not on ancient evidence. Most scholars locate all three in what is now Turkey.
- **"The coastland peoples spread in their lands, each with his own language" (v5).** "Coastlands" (*iyyim*) means shores and islands reached by sea. The prophets later promise that these distant coastlands will wait for God's law (Isa 42:4; 49:1). The phrase "each with his own language" already assumes Babel, which comes in the next chapter.
- **The sons of Ham (vv6–7).** **Cush** is the region south of Egypt (ancient Nubia, in modern Sudan), though some of Cush's sons settled across the Red Sea in Arabia. **Egypt**, **Put** (probably Libya) and **Canaan** complete the four. Many of the peoples Israel would struggle with most, Egypt, Babylon, Assyria, Canaan and the Philistines, appear in this section. **Sheba** later sends its queen to Solomon (1 Kgs 10:1).
- **"Cush fathered Nimrod; he was the first on earth to be a mighty man" (v8).** The list slows down for the only person in the chapter given a story. "Mighty man" is *gibbor*, warrior or hero. It is the word used for the "mighty men" of 6:4, before the flood, which is not a reassuring echo. "First" is literally "he began to be," the same verb used when people "began" to multiply in 6:1. Nimrod is the first empire-builder.
- **"A mighty hunter before the LORD" (v9).** This was a proverb, used of anyone famous for strength. Readers take "before the LORD" in two ways. Some read it plainly: Nimrod was a great hunter, so great that even God took notice, or a hunter "in God's sight." Others, from very early on, read it darkly. The Greek translation uses a word (*enantion*) that can mean "in front of" or "opposite," and Jewish tradition, including Josephus, made Nimrod a rebel who led the building of Babel. Ancient kings of Assyria and Babylon loved to picture themselves as hunters of lions and of men, so "mighty hunter" may describe a conqueror. The text itself stays brief and doesn't pass sentence, but the cities named next point in the darker direction.
- **"The beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar" (v10).** This is the first use of "kingdom" in the Bible. Shinar is southern Mesopotamia (modern Iraq). **Babel** is Babylon. **Erech** is Uruk, one of the oldest cities known, and **Accad** (Akkad) was the capital of Sargon, who built the first great empire in that region. Babel and Shinar come back in the next chapter as the scene of the tower (11:2, 9).
- **"From that land he went into Assyria and built Nineveh" (v11).** Nineveh became the capital of Assyria, the empire that destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel (2 Kgs 17) and the city Jonah was sent to warn (Jon 1:2). **Calah** is Nimrud, whose ruins still bear a name drawn from Nimrod. Micah calls Assyria "the land of Nimrod" (Mic 5:6). In two verses Genesis traces both of Israel's great oppressors, Babylon and Assyria, back to one proud founder. Like Cain, Nimrod is a builder of cities (4:17).
- **The sons of Egypt (vv13–14).** These are peoples of Egypt and North Africa. **Casluhim (from whom the Philistines came), and Caphtorim (v14).** Elsewhere the Philistines come from Caphtor, which is Crete (Amos 9:7; Jer 47:4). Many scholars think the note "from whom the Philistines came" originally followed Caphtorim, or that the two peoples were closely mixed. Either way, the Philistines, so important later in Judges and Samuel, are placed on the map here.
- **"Canaan fathered Sidon his firstborn and Heth" (vv15–18).** Sidon is the great Phoenician port. **Heth** gives "the Hittites" of Canaan, from whom Abraham buys a grave (ch 23). These Hittites are probably a local people, not the great Hittite empire of Turkey. **The Jebusites** held Jerusalem until David took it (2 Sam 5:6–9). **The Amorites**, **Girgashites** and **Hivites** appear again and again in the lists of the peoples of Canaan (15:19–21; Deut 7:1).
- **"The territory of the Canaanites extended from Sidon… as far as Gaza, and in the direction of Sodom, Gomorrah" (v19).** This is the only border in the chapter drawn this carefully. The reason is plain to Israel: this is the land God will promise to Abram (12:7; 15:18–21). The cities of the plain are named before their fall in chapter 19. The curse on Canaan in 9:25 is now attached to a real territory.
- **"To Shem also, the father of all the children of Eber" (v21).** Shem is introduced through a great-great-grandson, which shows what matters to the writer. **Eber** stands behind the word "Hebrew" (*ibri*), and Abram is the first person called "the Hebrew" (14:13). "The elder brother of Japheth" can also be translated "the brother of Japheth the elder." The Hebrew allows both.
- **The sons of Shem (vv22–23).** **Elam** is southwest Iran. **Asshur** is Assyria, which shows again that the table follows peoples and places, since Nimrod built Assyria's cities in v11. **Aram** is the Arameans of Syria, the people of Laban (25:20; 31:20). Israel later confessed, "A wandering Aramean was my father" (Deut 26:5). **Uz** is the land of Job (Job 1:1).
- **"Peleg, for in his days the earth was divided" (v25).** "Peleg" means "division," from the verb *palag*, to split or divide. Readers have offered several meanings. Most connect it with Babel, when the peoples were split apart by language (11:8–9). Psalm 55:9 uses the same verb for exactly that: "divide their tongues." Others think of the land being divided up among the nations (Deut 32:8), or of channels being cut for irrigation, a meaning the word has in related languages. Some modern writers suggest the splitting of the continents, but the word and the setting point to people, not geology. The two brothers split the line: **Joktan**'s sons settle in southern Arabia (vv26–30), while Peleg's line leads to Abram (11:16–26).
- **Joktan's sons (vv26–30).** Thirteen peoples of Arabia and Yemen. **Ophir** became famous for gold (1 Kgs 9:28). The list ends here, and the next chapter tells the story of Babel before taking up Peleg's line again. The chosen family is the one left hanging.
- **"From these the nations spread abroad on the earth after the flood" (v32).** The chapter closes as it opened. Every people on the list, friend or enemy, great empire or desert tribe, comes from one family that came out of the ark. Paul made this point in Athens: God "made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place" (Acts 17:26).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that no family records have survived except Israel's, kept "for the sake of the Messiah." On Nimrod he takes the darker reading: in God's account, "he was but a mighty hunter," and he suggests that tyrants over men are rebels against God. On Canaan he observes that its people were "numerous, and rich, and very pleasantly situated," yet "Canaan here has a better land than either Shem or Japheth, and yet they have a better lot, for they inherit the blessing." JFB points out that the list is "not arranged… according to seniority of birth," and reads Nimrod's move into Assyria as an invasion of Shem's territory. It takes Peleg's "division" as an ordered sharing out of the earth by God's direction, citing Deuteronomy 32:8 and Acts 17:26. JFB skips most of the names (vv2–4, 7, 9, 12–20, 22, 26–31). Guzik also reads "before the LORD" as an offense in God's face rather than praise, and he says Nimrod's name means "let us rebel." The lexicon calls the name's origin "wholly unknown," probably Babylonian, though Hebrew readers may have heard *marad*, "rebel," in it. The link is a wordplay, not a sure meaning. Guzik ties many names to modern nations, such as Meshech to Moscow, which go well beyond the evidence and should be held loosely.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is easy to skip, but the whole Bible's mission is in it. God placed every one of these seventy nations "that they should seek God" (Acts 17:27), and he called Abram from Shem's line to bring a blessing to all of them. That blessing came in Christ. At Pentecost, the scattered languages of Babel heard the gospel each "in his own language" (Acts 2:6–11), from Elamites and Medes to Egypt, Libya and Arabia, many of the very names on this list. The story ends where this chapter points: "a great multitude… from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" before the throne and before the Lamb (Rev 7:9). Nimrod built a kingdom by force. Christ builds his by gathering the nations to himself.
