*Setting:* Paul writes from Corinth around AD 57 to a church he has never visited, made up of Jewish and Gentile believers. Chapters 9–11 face the question that hangs over the whole letter: if Jesus is Israel's Messiah, why have most of Israel not believed? Chapter 9 opened with Paul's grief for his people (9:1–3) and showed that God's promises have not failed, because God has always worked by his own free choice and kept a remnant. It ended with a turn: Gentiles who were not chasing righteousness found it by faith, while Israel, pursuing a law of righteousness as if it came by works, "stumbled over the stumbling stone" (9:32), quoting Isaiah 28:16. Chapter 10 continues that thought (the ESV section began at 9:30). Where chapter 9 looked mostly at God's choosing, chapter 10 looks at the human side: Israel heard the message and did not believe. Woven through it is one of the Bible's clearest statements of how anyone, Jew or Gentile, is saved.

#### Israel's Unbelief (vv1–4)
- **"My heart's desire and prayer to God for them" (v1).** Paul has said hard things about Israel, but he writes as a man praying for his own family, not a detached critic. The KJV's "for Israel" follows later manuscripts; the older ones have "for them," with the same meaning. Paul believes firmly in God's sovereign choice (ch 9) and still prays for people to be saved. He never treats the one as a reason to stop the other.
- **"A zeal for God, but not according to knowledge" (v2).** "Zeal" (*zēlos*) is the word used in the Greek Old Testament for Phinehas's zeal (Num 25:11), the model of passionate loyalty that inspired Jewish freedom fighters in Paul's day. Paul knows this zeal from the inside. He once persecuted the church "as to zeal" (Phil 3:6; Gal 1:14). "Knowledge" here is *epignōsis*, which the lexicon gives as recognition or discernment. Israel knew the Scriptures but failed to recognize what God was doing in them.
- **"Being ignorant of the righteousness of God" (v3).** "The righteousness of God" is the theme of the whole letter (1:17; 3:21–26). It means God's own saving righteousness, his way of putting people right with himself, given as a gift. The contrast is "God's righteousness" against "their own," which they were "seeking to establish," setting it up and making it stand. The deepest failure is the last clause: "they did not submit." Receiving righteousness as a gift means giving up the hope of earning a standing before God.
- **"Christ is the end of the law for righteousness" (v4).** This is the chapter's key verse and one of the most debated in Romans. "End" is *telos*, which can mean termination ("the end of the road") or goal and purpose ("the end in view"). The lexicon covers both. Interestingly, Abbott-Smith files this verse under termination, with Christ as the one who brings the law to an end, and keeps "aim or purpose" for verses like 1 Timothy 1:5.
- **Three main readings of v4.** *Termination:* Christ ends the law as a way of gaining righteousness. This has been strong in Lutheran teaching and fits the contrast between "their own" righteousness and God's. *Goal:* Christ is what the law was aiming at all along. This is common in Reformed teaching and is the Tyndale notes' reading, which ties the verse to Jesus fulfilling the law (Matt 5:17) and to faith upholding the law (3:31). *Both:* many think the law reaches its goal in Christ and *therefore* its role as a path to righteousness is over, as a finish line is both the goal and the end of a race. Paul's qualifiers narrow the debate: Christ ends the law "for righteousness," and "to everyone who believes." All three readings agree that no one is put right with God by keeping the law, and that faith in Christ is what the law was always leading toward (Gal 3:24).
- **Commentators:** Henry presses the first verse as a lesson in prayer: "The soul of prayer is the heart's desire," and "Cold desires do but beg denials." He also commends Paul's charity in v2: "We should say the best we can even of our worst enemies." Guzik notes that Paul was describing his own former life, and that neither sincerity nor information saves without submission. On v4 Henry and JFB lean toward the goal reading, while Guzik stresses that the law is finished as the basis of a relationship with God yet still shows God's standard, close to the "both" view. JFB says Christ "contains within Himself all that the law demands for the justification of such as embrace Him." Henry puts it this way: "The moral law was but for the searching of the wound, the ceremonial law for the shadowing forth of the remedy." The lexicon allows the goal sense of *telos* in general, though it files this verse under termination, so the word alone does not settle the question.

#### The Message of Salvation to All (vv5–21)
- **"Moses writes about the righteousness that is based on the law" (v5).** Paul quotes Leviticus 18:5: "the person who does the commandments shall live by them." In Leviticus it promised Israel, already redeemed from Egypt, life and blessing in the land as they lived God's way. Paul also quotes it in Galatians 3:12. His point is that as a *way of being declared righteous*, the law's terms are doing, and doing all of it. Most readers take v5 and v6 as a contrast between two ways. A minority think v5 points to Christ as the one man who truly did the commandments and lived. The Tyndale notes take the first view: anyone relying on the law must keep every command.
- **"The righteousness based on faith says" (v6).** Paul personifies faith-righteousness and lets it speak, using Deuteronomy 30:11–14. There Moses tells Israel that God's commandment is "not too hard": no one needs to climb to heaven or cross the sea to fetch it, because "the word is very near you." Surprisingly, Paul applies a passage about the law to Christ.
- **Is Paul twisting Deuteronomy 30? (vv6–8).** It looks bold, but the passage itself gives reasons. Deuteronomy 30 looks past Israel's future exile to a time when God will "circumcise your heart" (Deut 30:6), so that his word lives in the heart by his own work, the new covenant promise (Jer 31:33). Paul sees that promise fulfilled in the gospel. Jewish writers also reused this passage freely. Baruch 3:29–30 applies it to divine wisdom, and one Aramaic paraphrase of Deuteronomy (Targum Neofiti) speaks of going "down to the depths of the sea," much like Paul's "abyss." Paul's "that is" comments follow a familiar Jewish way of explaining a text phrase by phrase.
- **"To bring Christ down… to bring Christ up from the dead" (vv6–7).** No one needs to climb into heaven to bring the Messiah down, because God already sent him in the incarnation. No one needs to go down into the depths to bring him back, because God already raised him. "Abyss" (*abyssos*) is the bottomless deep, used here of the realm of the dead. Paul has changed Moses' "beyond the sea" to fit the resurrection. Everything needed has already been done. Faith does not achieve; it receives.
- **"The word is near you… the word of faith that we proclaim" (v8).** "Word" here is *rhēma*, a spoken word or message. Paul means the gospel preached by the apostles. Some popular teaching draws a sharp line between *rhēma* (a personal word spoken by God now) and *logos* (the written word). The lexicon does not support that. *Rhēma* simply means what is said, a saying or message, and in the Greek Old Testament both words translate the same Hebrew *dabar*. Here *rhēma* is the apostolic message about Christ.
- **"If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord" (v9).** "Jesus is Lord" is probably the earliest Christian confession (1 Cor 12:3; Phil 2:11), very likely spoken at baptism. "Confess" (*homologeō*) means to acknowledge or declare openly, and its older sense is "to agree with," literally "to say the same thing." "Lord" (*kyrios*) stood for God's personal name in the Greek Old Testament, and the Roman world used it of the emperor. Decades later, Polycarp of Smyrna was urged to save his life by saying "Lord Caesar" and refused (Martyrdom of Polycarp 8). Confessing Jesus as Lord meant loyalty to him above every other claim.
- **"Believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead" (v9).** The resurrection is God's own verdict on Jesus (1:4; 4:25). In the Bible the "heart" is not just feelings but the center of a person, where thinking, choosing and trusting happen.
- **Mouth and heart, heart and mouth (vv9–10).** Verse 9 follows Deuteronomy's order, "mouth… heart." Verse 10 reverses it to "heart… mouth." The crossing pattern says the two belong together. These are not two separate steps; believing is the root and confessing its open fruit, and "justified" and "saved" are paired the same way. Traditions differ in emphasis. Some stress that saving faith will show itself in public confession (Matt 10:32–33). Others stress that Paul is not making a spoken formula a condition, since the thief on the cross and those unable to speak are saved by faith. Both agree the faith Paul means is never purely private.
- **"Everyone who believes in him will not be put to shame" (v11).** Paul quotes Isaiah 28:16 again, the stumbling-stone verse from 9:33. Neither the Hebrew nor the Greek of Isaiah has "everyone." Paul adds it, and the word "everyone" or "all" now runs through vv11–13 like a drumbeat. To be "put to shame" is to be exposed on judgment day as having trusted the wrong thing.
- **"No distinction between Jew and Greek" (v12).** The same phrase appeared in 3:22: "there is no distinction." There it meant that all have sinned. Here it means that the same Lord is generous to all. "Riches" is a favorite picture of Paul's for grace (2:4; Eph 2:4, 7).
- **"Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (v13).** This is Joel 2:32, the same verse Peter quoted at Pentecost (Acts 2:21). In Joel, "the LORD" is Yahweh, the personal name of Israel's God. In Romans the "Lord" of v12 is the Jesus confessed in v9. Paul applies a verse about calling on Yahweh to calling on Jesus, without feeling any need to defend it, which shows how early Christians placed Jesus within the identity of the one God. "Call on" (*epikaleō*) means to invoke someone for help, and in the Old Testament calling on the LORD's name meant worship and prayer (Gen 4:26; 12:8). The first Christians were known simply as "all who call on the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 1:2).
- **The chain of questions (vv14–15).** Paul works backward: calling needs believing, believing needs hearing, hearing needs a preacher, and a preacher must be sent. Read forward, it describes the gospel's journey: God sends, messengers preach, people hear, they believe, they call on the Lord, and they are saved. The Greek of v14 can mean "him whom they have never heard," not just "heard of." That suggests that when the gospel is preached, Christ himself speaks through it. The Tyndale notes take "they" as all people, while seeing Paul's focus still on Israel (v18).
- **"How beautiful are the feet" (v15).** Isaiah 52:7 pictures a runner crossing the mountains with news that the exile is ending and "Your God reigns." Feet dusty and sore from the journey look beautiful because of the news they bring. "Beautiful" (*hōraios*) first meant seasonable or timely, like fruit at the right moment, and then lovely. Both fit: the messengers come at God's appointed time. The KJV adds "that preach the gospel of peace," a phrase found in later manuscripts but missing from the oldest.
- **"But they have not all obeyed the gospel" (v16).** "Obeyed" (*hypakouō*) is built on the verb "to hear," and its basic sense is to listen and respond. Then Isaiah 53:1: "Lord, who has believed what he has heard from us?" "What he has heard" is one word, *akoē*, hearing, report or message. The wordplay: they heard, but did not truly listen and respond. In Romans, obeying the gospel and believing it are one thing seen from two angles (1:5; 16:26). Isaiah 53 is the song of the suffering Servant, and John 12:38 uses the same verse for unbelief in Jesus' ministry.
- **"Faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ" (v17).** Again *akoē*: faith comes from the message heard, and the message comes through "the word (*rhēma*) of Christ." That most likely means the message about Christ, though it may also mean Christ's own word spoken through his messengers. The oldest manuscripts read "of Christ," and later ones "of God," which the KJV follows.
- **"Have they not heard? Indeed they have" (v18).** Paul raises the first possible excuse: maybe Israel never heard. He answers with Psalm 19:4, where the heavens silently declare God's glory: "Their voice has gone out to all the earth." The Hebrew of the psalm has "their line" (*qav*). Paul follows the Greek translation, which reads "sound" (*phthongos*). Most think Paul borrows the psalm's language: as creation's witness reaches everywhere, so the gospel has now gone across the known world, especially through the synagogues of the Jewish dispersion (Col 1:6, 23). The Tyndale notes add that creation itself had already made God's message widely known (1:20). Either way the claim is not that every person had heard, but that Israel as a people could not plead ignorance.
- **"Did Israel not understand?" (v19).** The second possible excuse: maybe they heard but could not grasp it. Paul answers from Moses, then Isaiah, the Law then the Prophets. Deuteronomy 32:21 comes from the Song of Moses, a warning about Israel's future, and its logic is measure for measure. Israel made God jealous "with what is no god," so God will make Israel jealous "with those who are no people." The irony is sharp. Paul asks whether Israel did not *understand*, and Moses' answer is that a nation "without understanding" (*asynetos*, the ESV's "foolish") would grasp what Israel missed. "Make jealous" (*parazēloō*) shares its root with the "zeal" of v2. Israel's misdirected zeal would be met by a holy jealousy stirred up by Gentile believers. Paul returns to this verb in 11:11 and 11:14 and turns it into a source of hope.
- **"Isaiah is so bold as to say" (v20).** Paul quotes Isaiah 65:1: "I have been found by those who did not seek me." "So bold" means Isaiah dared to say something startling. In its own setting Isaiah 65:1 is debated. Some read it as God speaking of Gentiles, "a nation that was not called by my name." Others, including the Tyndale notes, think it first described rebellious Israel itself. Paul applies it to the Gentiles, much as he applied Hosea's words about Israel to them in 9:25–26. Either way, God is found by people who were not looking for him: grace in its plainest form.
- **"All day long I have held out my hands" (v21).** Paul ends the chapter with Isaiah 65:2. The picture is a parent standing with open arms, not for a moment but "all day long," toward a people "disobedient and contrary." "Contrary" (*antilegō*) means to talk back, to contradict. Yet the last word of the chapter is not God's rejection of Israel. It is God's patience with them. That posture sets up the question that opens chapter 11: "has God rejected his people? By no means!" (11:1).
- **Commentators:** On vv6–8 Henry contrasts the law's "Do, and live" with the plain, easy terms of the gospel, and writes that "Christ is nigh thee, for the word is nigh thee." JFB calls the phrases about heaven and the abyss "probably proverbial expressions of impossibility," and reads them as a word against despair: one does not "sigh over the impossibility of attaining to justification." Guzik defines "confess" as agreeing with what God says about Jesus, which the lexicon supports (*homologeō*, "to agree with"). Citing Barclay, he notes that *kyrios* put Jesus alongside both God and the emperor, and the lexicon confirms both uses. On v12 JFB insists the "Lord of all" is "not God… but Christ," against Calvin and others, and the Tyndale notes agree that Paul means Jesus. On vv14–15 Henry writes, "Preachers and hearers are correlates," and adds that "the holiness of ministers' lives is the beauty of their feet." Guzik says preaching is God's normal, though not only possible, way of bringing people to faith, and that preachers need sending from God and the church. On v18 JFB asks, "Can Israel, through any region of his dispersion, plead ignorance of these glad tidings?", and Guzik, citing F. F. Bruce, makes the same point: the gospel had reached the places where Jews lived. On v20, JFB and Guzik both take Isaiah 65:1 as a prophecy of the Gentiles, while the Tyndale notes think it first described Israel and see Paul extending it. On v21 Henry says simply: "He waits to be gracious."

#### Pointing to Christ
Romans 10 makes Christ the center of everything. He is the goal the law was running toward (v4). He is the one God brought down from heaven and up from the dead, so that no one has to climb or dig for him (vv6–7). He is the Lord to be confessed, the Yahweh of Joel's promise on whom anyone may call (vv9, 13), and the Servant of Isaiah 53 whose report so many did not believe (v16). The chapter also shows God's heart in two pictures: beautiful feet running with good news, and open hands held out all day long to people who keep talking back. Both are fulfilled in Jesus, who came with the good news of peace (Eph 2:17), and who stretched out his hands on the cross for those who did not seek him.
