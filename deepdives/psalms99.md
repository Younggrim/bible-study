*Setting:* Psalm 99 has no title in the Hebrew text, and its author and date are unknown. The Greek translation attributes it to David. It is the last of the psalms that open with the cry "The LORD reigns" (93:1; 97:1; 99:1), and it brings the group (93–99) to a solemn close before the short call to thanksgiving in Psalm 100. Psalms 96 and 98 rang with joy at the King's coming. Psalm 99 turns to his holiness. The refrain "Holy is he!" comes three times (vv3, 5, 9), with the last one expanded. Many have compared this to the seraphim's "Holy, holy, holy" in Isaiah 6:3. Where the earlier psalms looked to the nations and the future, this one looks back to Israel's own history, to Moses, Aaron and Samuel, and to a God who both forgave his people and disciplined them. Henry notes that while the previous psalms looked forward to gospel times, this one "seems to dwell more upon the Old-Testament dispensation." The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the ESV's three stanzas, each ending with the holiness refrain.

#### The LORD Reigns; Let the Peoples Tremble (vv1–5)
- **"The LORD reigns; let the peoples tremble!" (v1).** Psalm 97:1 followed the same cry with "let the earth rejoice." Here it is "let the peoples tremble." Both are right. The King's reign is good news, and it is also overwhelming. "Tremble" (*ragaz*) means to shake, to quiver with deep emotion, whether fear or awe.
- **"He sits enthroned upon the cherubim; let the earth quake!" (v1).** The cherubim were the two golden winged figures on the cover of the ark of the covenant, facing each other with wings spread over it (Exod 25:18–22). God was pictured as invisibly enthroned above them, and the ark as his footstool. The title "the LORD of hosts, who is enthroned on the cherubim" was used when the ark went out with Israel (1 Sam 4:4; 2 Sam 6:2; Ps 80:1). In Ezekiel's visions the cherubim are mighty living creatures bearing God's throne like a chariot (Ezek 1; 10). They are not the chubby babies of later art.
- **"The LORD is great in Zion; he is exalted over all the peoples" (v2).** Zion, the hill of the temple in Jerusalem, is where God chose to put his name. But the God who lives there is not a local god. From Zion he is exalted over every nation (Isa 2:2–3).
- **"Let them praise your great and awesome name! Holy is he!" (v3).** "Awesome" (*nora*) means fearsome, inspiring dread, as in Deuteronomy 10:17: "the great, the mighty, and the awesome God." Then comes the first refrain. "Holy" (*qadosh*) at root means set apart, different, other. God is holy because he is utterly unlike his creatures, not just bigger or better, and because he is morally pure, separate from all evil. The Hebrew can be read "it is holy" (the name) or "he is holy." The ESV takes it of God himself.
- **"The King in his might loves justice" (v4).** The Hebrew is compressed, literally "and the strength of a king loves justice." Whatever the exact grammar, the point is clear. God's power is not raw force. The strongest King in the universe uses his strength for what is right. Human rulers often love power for its own sake. This King loves justice.
- **"You have established equity; you have executed justice and righteousness in Jacob" (v4).** "Equity" (*mesharim*) is fairness, the same word as in 96:10 and 98:9. "Justice and righteousness" together are the qualities of an ideal king (2 Sam 8:15; Jer 22:15; 23:5). God has done this "in Jacob," that is, in Israel's own history, through the laws he gave and the judgments he carried out.
- **"Exalt the LORD our God; worship at his footstool!" (v5).** "Exalt" means lift him high, recognize how high he is. "Footstool" usually means the ark (1 Chr 28:2). Elsewhere the temple, Jerusalem or the whole earth is called God's footstool (Lam 2:1; Isa 66:1; Matt 5:35). To worship at someone's footstool is to bow at their feet. Even the most holy place on earth is only the place for God's feet. **"Holy is he!"** The second refrain.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that under Moses "the Old-Testament dispensation had much of terror in it," but "then he ruled more by the power of holy fear; now he rules by the power of holy love." He calls the temple "the royal palace" and the Holy of Holies "the presence-chamber." On v4 he says God's strength "is not abused for the support of any wrong, as the power of great princes often is," and that "the more we abase ourselves… the more we exalt him." JFB says God's "power is combined with justice" and reads "thy… name" as his "perfections of justice, power, &c." Guzik explains holiness as apartness: God is not a better version of humans but different in his very being, and holiness marks every attribute he has. He notes the refrain may have been sung as a solemn chorus at the end of each section.

#### Moses and Aaron Were Among His Priests (vv6–7)
- **"Moses and Aaron were among his priests" (v6).** Aaron was the first high priest (Exod 28:1). Moses was not formally a priest, but he did priestly work: he consecrated Aaron and offered sacrifices at the ordination (Lev 8:14–30), and he stood between God and the people. The point is that both were go-betweens who brought the people's needs to God.
- **"Samuel also was among those who called upon his name" (v6).** Samuel was a Levite (1 Chr 6:28, 33–34), a prophet and the last judge, who offered sacrifices and led Israel in prayer. Moses and Samuel are named together elsewhere as Israel's great intercessors: "Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my heart would not turn toward this people" (Jer 15:1).
- **"They called to the LORD, and he answered them" (v6).** Each one prayed for the people, and God listened. Moses pleaded after the golden calf (Exod 32:11–14, 30–32). Aaron stood "between the dead and the living" with incense to stop a plague (Num 16:46–48). Samuel cried to the LORD at Mizpah, and God thundered against the Philistines (1 Sam 7:8–10). Samuel even said, "far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you" (1 Sam 12:23).
- **"In the pillar of the cloud he spoke to them" (v7).** The pillar of cloud led Israel through the wilderness and stood at the door of the tent of meeting when God spoke to Moses "face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exod 33:9–11). He spoke to Aaron and Miriam from it too (Num 12:5). Samuel lived centuries later, after the pillar was gone, so the line most naturally refers to Moses and Aaron, with Samuel included in the general picture of God speaking to his servants.
- **"They kept his testimonies and the statute that he gave them" (v7).** These leaders were heard because they listened. Their prayer and their obedience went together.
- **Commentators:** JFB applies being "among… priests" and calling on the LORD to all three men, and calls the cloudy pillar "the medium of divine intercourse" (Exod 33:9; Num 12:5). He adds, "Obedience was united with worship." Henry says "they made conscience of their duty," knowing "that unless they did so they could not expect their prayers should be answered" (Prov 28:9), and that God "communed with them as one friend familiarly converses with another." Guzik notes that these three were people with weaknesses like anyone's, and that their prayers were heard both for themselves and for others.

#### O LORD Our God, You Answered Them (vv8–9)
- **"O LORD our God, you answered them" (v8).** The psalm turns to speak directly to God. The repeated "answered" (vv6, 8) underlines that the holy God, enthroned above the cherubim, is not remote. He hears and responds.
- **"You were a forgiving God to them" (v8).** Literally "a God who lifts up," that is, who carries away sin. The word (*nasa*) is the same one in God's description of himself at Sinai: "forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exod 34:7). Moses used it when he pleaded, "pardon the iniquity of this people… just as you have forgiven this people, from Egypt until now" (Num 14:19).
- **"But an avenger of their wrongdoings" (v8).** Forgiveness did not mean God ignored sin. Exodus 34:7 goes on: "but who will by no means clear the guilty." Who are "them"? It may be the people, whom God forgave in answer to prayer and yet disciplined: after the golden calf he spared the nation but a plague still came (Exod 32:14, 35), and at Kadesh he pardoned them but that generation died in the wilderness (Num 14:20–23). It may also be Moses and Aaron themselves, who were forgiven yet kept from entering the land after striking the rock at Meribah (Num 20:12). Either way the lesson is the same. God's forgiveness is real, and so is his discipline, and they do not cancel each other.
- **"Exalt the LORD our God, and worship at his holy mountain" (v9).** The refrain of v5 returns with a change. "His footstool" becomes "his holy mountain," Mount Zion. The whole hill shares his holiness.
- **"For the LORD our God is holy!" (v9).** The final refrain is longer than the first two. "Holy is he" becomes "the LORD our God is holy." It is no longer only that God is holy in himself. He is "our God," holy and yet in covenant with his people.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v8 of the people for whom Moses, Aaron and Samuel prayed: "thou wast a God that forgavest the people they prayed for; and, though thou tookest vengeance of their inventions, yet thou didst not cut them off from being a people, as their sin deserved." JFB says God "answered them as intercessors for the people, who, though forgiven, were yet chastened" (Exod 32:10, 34). Guzik leaves the question open: most commentators take the wrongdoings of v8 as Israel's, but Moses, Aaron and Samuel were each disciplined in some way too. He also points out that even men who "kept his testimonies" (v7) needed a God who forgives.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 99 shows a holy God who hears the prayers of a mediator for a sinful people, forgives them and yet does not treat sin lightly. Moses, Aaron and Samuel stood between God and Israel. Jesus is the greater mediator, "faithful over God's house as a son" where Moses was faithful as a servant (Heb 3:5–6), a high priest who "always lives to make intercession" (Heb 7:25). At the cross God's forgiveness and his justice met fully. He "passed over former sins" and yet showed himself "just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:25–26). The throne above the cherubim, once hidden behind the curtain, is now a "throne of grace" that believers may approach with confidence (Heb 4:16), and the whole church, Jew and Gentile, can sing what the seraphim sang and what Revelation still sings: "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord God Almighty" (Rev 4:8).
