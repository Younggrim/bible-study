*Setting:* Psalm 124 is the fifth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. Psalm 123 ended with God's people still looking up, full of the contempt of the proud and waiting for mercy. Psalm 124 sounds like the other side of that wait: the danger has passed, and Israel looks back and sees how close it came. The title reads "Of David," one of four Songs of Ascents with his name (with 122, 131 and 133). The psalm names no particular enemy or battle. Many readers think of the Philistine invasions early in David's reign, when the new king was nearly overrun (2 Sam 5:17–25). Others hear it as a song for any deliverance in Israel's history, from the Red Sea to the return from exile, and some scholars date it later on the basis of its language. It was written to be sung together, with a leader calling, "let Israel now say," and the people answering. It has been sung that way ever since. Its last verse, "Our help is in the name of the LORD," became the opening words of worship in many Reformed churches after Calvin's Geneva. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### If It Had Not Been the LORD (vv1–5)
- **"If it had not been the LORD who was on our side" (v1).** The psalm opens with a "what if." It imagines the story as it would have gone without God. The ESV's "who was on our side" is literally "who was for us." The covenant name, the LORD (*YHWH*), is stressed: not luck, not their own army, not an ally, but the LORD. Paul asks the same question in Romans: "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31).
- **"Let Israel now say" (v1).** A leader calls the congregation to join in, and the line is repeated in v2 as they do. The same call appears in Psalm 118:2 ("Let Israel say") and Psalm 129:1. Thanksgiving here is not only private. The whole people is meant to own the story out loud.
- **"When people rose up against us" (v2).** The Hebrew is simply *adam*, "man" or "mankind." The contrast is between the LORD and mere human beings. The enemy was dangerous, but it was only human. The LORD was the deciding difference.
- **"They would have swallowed us up alive" (v3).** The first picture is of a monster or the grave itself opening its mouth. "Swallowed" is *bala'*, the word used when the earth opened and swallowed Korah and his followers "alive into Sheol" (Num 16:30–33). The wicked in Proverbs boast, "like Sheol let us swallow them alive" (Prov 1:12). Israel was a mouthful, and nearly gone.
- **"When their anger was kindled against us" (v3).** "Kindled" is the language of fire. The enemy's fury burned hot. The psalm moves quickly from beast to fire to flood, piling up pictures of how helpless the people were.
- **"Then the flood would have swept us away" (v4).** Israel's land is full of *wadis*, dry valleys that can turn into deadly torrents within minutes after a storm in the hills. "The torrent" (*nachal*) is that kind of rushing stream. A traveler camped in a dry wadi could be swept away before he knew rain had fallen. The Psalms often use overwhelming water for overwhelming trouble (Ps 18:4, 16; 69:1–2, 15; 32:6).
- **"The torrent would have gone over us" (v4).** The ESV's "over us" is literally "over our soul" (*nephesh*). The water would have gone over their very life, the whole of them.
- **"Then over us would have gone the raging waters" (v5).** "Raging" is *zedonim*, which more literally means "proud" or "insolent," a word normally used of arrogant people. The waters are described like the enemies themselves, swelling with pride. It connects with "the proud" who end Psalm 123. Three times the word "then" sounds the alarm (vv3, 4, 5): then, then, then. Each line imagines the disaster more vividly.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the LORD "took our part, espoused our cause, and appeared for us," and that God sometimes lets enemies "prevail very far against them" so that his rescue is plainly his. JFB explains "quick" (KJV for "alive") as describing the enemy's ferocity and says "the epithet proud added to waters denotes insolent enemies," which the Hebrew *zedonim* supports. Guzik, drawing on Luther, says people tend to shrink a danger once it has passed and so forget the grace that delivered them. The psalm's "if... then" structure is meant to stop that, by making Israel look again at how bad it really was. He also notes Boice's view that the language fits a military attack better than the exile.

#### Blessed Be the LORD (vv6–8)
- **"Blessed be the LORD" (v6).** People cannot add anything to God by blessing him. To "bless" the LORD (*barak*) is to speak well of him, to praise him gratefully for what he has done. The mood turns from imagining disaster to thanking God for preventing it.
- **"Who has not given us as prey to their teeth!" (v6).** The pictures change again, now to a wild animal with its kill. The enemy is a lion or a wolf, its teeth already closing. God did not hand his people over. The study notes compare Daniel, whose God "shut the lions' mouths" (Dan 6:22).
- **"We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowlers" (v7).** The last picture is the smallest and the most tender. A bird caught in a fowler's net or spring-trap is helpless. It cannot break the trap. It can only flutter. "Bird" (*tsippor*) is the ordinary word for a small bird, often a sparrow. The bird does not free itself. Something else breaks the snare. Psalm 91:3 promises that God "will deliver you from the snare of the fowler."
- **"The snare is broken, and we have escaped!" (v7).** The words "we have escaped" open and close the verse. The joy can be heard in the repetition. The order matters too: first the snare is broken, then the bird flies. The rescue is God's work, and the escape follows from it.
- **"Our help is in the name of the LORD" (v8).** The psalm ends with confession and confidence. "The name of the LORD" means God as he has revealed himself, his character and his covenant promises (Exod 34:5–7). Israel's help did not depend on its own strength.
- **"Who made heaven and earth" (v8).** This repeats the confession of Psalm 121:2, "My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth," and Psalm 134:3 ends the Songs of Ascents on the same note. The one who made everything is greater than any enemy, any flood, any trap. Israel's neighbors trusted gods of particular places, rivers or storms. Israel trusted the Creator of all of them.
- **The psalm in later history.** Guzik retells a story from 1582 in Edinburgh: when the exiled minister John Durie returned, a crowd of about two thousand walked up the street with him singing this psalm, and one of his opponents was said to be more afraid of that sight than of anything else in Scotland. Calvin's Geneva service began with v8, and many Reformed and Presbyterian churches still open worship with it.
- **Commentators:** Henry insists that "God is the author of all our deliverances, and therefore he must have the glory of them," and notes that the word for bird "signifies a sparrow." JFB marks the change of pictures, "the figure is changed to that of a rapacious wild beast," then to the snare, where "complete escape is denoted by breaking the net." He closes with Luther's image that the danger is to God's power like "a little drop of water" to "a great fire." Guzik points out that, looking back, believers often see protection they had no idea of at the time. He encourages the habit, with Trapp, of recording God's past rescues so that remembering them can build trust for the next danger.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 124 imagines a deliverance so complete that it could only be God's work: swallowed, flooded, caught, and then suddenly free. The greatest deliverance of all looks just like that. Jesus went down into the waters that Israel feared. He entered death itself, the grave that "swallows" everyone, and "death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Cor 15:54). The snare that held humanity was broken when he rose. Hebrews says that through death he destroyed "the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil," and delivered "all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Heb 2:14–15). Paul says believers were caught in "the snare of the devil" (2 Tim 2:26), and the Son set them free (John 8:36). The confession "If it had not been the LORD who was on our side" becomes, in the gospel, "If God is for us, who can be against us? He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:31–32).
