*Setting:* Judah, around 609–605 BC, in the early reign of King Jehoiakim. In chapter 1 Habakkuk complained that God was letting violence and injustice run unchecked in Judah. God answered that he was raising up the Chaldeans, the Babylonians, to punish Judah. That answer only made things harder. How could a holy God use a nation even more wicked than Judah, a fisherman who sacrifices to his own net? Chapter 1 ended with the prophet's question hanging: will Babylon keep "mercilessly killing nations forever?" (1:17). Chapter 2 is God's second answer. It does not explain everything Habakkuk asked. Instead it gives him a vision to write down, a way to live while waiting for it, and five "woes" that show Babylon's pride carrying the seeds of its own fall. At its center is one of the most important sentences in the Bible: "the righteous shall live by his faith."

#### Habakkuk's Second Complaint (v1)
- **"I will take my stand at my watchpost and station myself on the tower" (v1).** This verse closes the complaint that began in 1:12. A watchman stood on the city wall or a tower to look out for messengers or enemies (2 Sam 18:24–27; 2 Kgs 9:17). Prophets used the picture for their own role: watching for God's word and warning the people (Isa 21:6–8; Ezek 33:2–7). Whether Habakkuk climbed a real tower or the language is figurative, the point is the same. Having asked his question, he does not walk away. He waits for an answer. Henry says standing on the tower "intimates his prudence," and JFB describes "the withdrawal of the whole soul from earthly, and fixing it on heavenly, things."
- **"And look out to see what he will say to me" (v1).** The prophet "looks" to "see" what God will "say." The vision will be seen, but it is also a word. **"And what I will answer concerning my complaint."** The Hebrew can also mean "how I will be answered when I am rebuked." Habakkuk expects that God may correct him, and he is ready for it. The Tyndale notes say he was ready to listen even if God corrected him.

#### The Righteous Shall Live by His Faith (vv2–5)
- **"And the LORD answered me" (v2).** The man who cried "how long… and you will not hear?" (1:2) is answered.
- **"Write the vision; make it plain on tablets" (v2).** Tablets were wood, clay or stone surfaces for public notices, like the law written "very plainly" on plastered stones at Mount Ebal (Deut 27:8) or Isaiah's message written as a witness "forever" (Isa 30:8). Writing makes the message permanent and public, so that when it comes true, everyone can see that God said it beforehand. Guzik turns it into a lesson for teachers: see the vision, write it down, make it plain and make it practical.
- **"So he may run who reads it" (v2).** This may mean a herald reads it and runs to spread the news (compare Jer 23:21), which is JFB's reading: so legible that a reader "may run to tell all whom he can." Or it may mean it is written so clearly that anyone can read it at a glance. Guzik points out that the familiar saying "he who runs may read" turns the verse around.
- **"For still the vision awaits its appointed time" (v3).** "Appointed time" (*mo'ed*) is the word for a set date, like the appointed feasts. God has a calendar. **"It hastens to the end."** The verb means literally "it pants," like a runner straining toward the finish. The vision is eager to be fulfilled.
- **"If it seems slow, wait for it; it will surely come; it will not delay" (v3).** It may seem slow from the human side, but it is never late from God's. Henry writes, "though it tarry longer than we expected, yet we must continue waiting for it." Peter says the same of Christ's return: "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness" (2 Pet 3:9).
- **"He who is coming" (Heb 10:37).** The Greek Septuagint translated v3 with a personal subject: not "it will come" but "he will come." The book of Hebrews quotes this Greek form, "Yet a little while, and the coming one will come and will not delay," and applies it to Christ's return (Heb 10:37). The Hebrew speaks of the vision. The Greek and the New Testament see that the vision's fulfillment is a person.
- **"Behold, his soul is puffed up; it is not upright within him" (v4).** Here are two ways to live. The first is the proud man, swollen with himself. The immediate reference is the Babylonian, but it describes anyone who trusts in himself (1:11, 16). JFB notes that interpreters differ on whether the proud one is the Chaldean or the unbelieving Jew. The Hebrew of this half-verse is difficult, and translations vary, but the contrast is clear.
- **"But the righteous shall live by his faith" (v4).** In Hebrew this is just three words: *wetsaddiq be'emunato yichyeh*. The word for faith, *'emunah*, means steadiness, firmness, faithfulness. It is used of Moses' hands held "steady" until sunset (Exod 17:12). It comes from the same root (*'mn*, the root of "amen") as the verb in "Abram believed the LORD" (Gen 15:6), as the Tyndale notes point out. So it means faith that holds on: trust in God that shows itself in steady faithfulness. The righteous person does not live by understanding everything. He lives by holding on to God while he waits for the vision.
- **"His faith" or "my faithfulness"?** The Hebrew says "by *his* faith(fulness)," the righteous person's own. The Greek Septuagint reads "by *my* faithfulness," meaning God's. Paul quotes the verse without either pronoun: "The righteous shall live by faith" (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11). Hebrews 10:38 has "my righteous one shall live by faith." The readings are not in conflict. The believer's faith rests on God's faithfulness.
- **How the New Testament uses it.** This verse is quoted three times in the New Testament, each with a slightly different emphasis. In Romans 1:17 it shows that a person is counted righteous before God by faith. In Galatians 3:11 it shows that no one is justified by keeping the law. In Hebrews 10:38–39 it calls believers to keep trusting and not shrink back while they wait for Christ. Guzik draws out all three. The Dead Sea Scrolls include an ancient Jewish commentary on Habakkuk 1–2, which applies this verse to those who are faithful to the Teacher of Righteousness. Even before the church, this verse was read as a key to how God's people live in hard times.
- **Luther.** The verse, through Romans 1:17, was at the heart of Martin Luther's discovery of justification by faith in the early 1500s, which he later described as feeling that he had been born again and entered paradise. A well-known story, passed on by Guzik, says the words came back to him as he climbed the Holy Stairs in Rome on his knees. The story comes from Luther's son and may be embellished, but Luther's own writings leave no doubt how central the verse was for him.
- **"Moreover, wine is a traitor, an arrogant man who is never at rest" (v5).** The proud man of v4 is now pictured as a drunkard, restless and treacherous. Babylon's love of wine was famous. Its final night came during a drunken feast (Dan 5:1–4, 30). Some manuscripts, including the Dead Sea Scroll of Habakkuk, read "wealth" instead of "wine," a very similar word in Hebrew. Either way, the point is insatiable appetite.
- **"His greed is as wide as Sheol; like death he has never enough" (v5).** Sheol, the realm of the dead, was pictured as an open mouth that is never full (Prov 27:20; 30:15–16; Isa 5:14). JFB: he "enlargeth his desire as hell." **"He gathers for himself all nations."** This links back to the net of 1:15–17.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v4 broadly: "Those that are made just by faith shall live, shall be happy here and for ever," and adds that in heaven "faith shall be swallowed up in vision." JFB reads v2 as written so plainly that a reader can run to spread the news. Guzik draws lessons for preaching from v2 and centers his treatment on v4, tracing its three New Testament uses and Luther's discovery. He applies it to the daily Christian life: believers live by faith, not by feelings or circumstances or even religious routine. The Tyndale notes add that God never explains why he chose Babylon. He answers instead with a promise that all violence and injustice will be judged.

#### Woe to the Chaldeans (vv6–20)
- **"Shall not all these take up their taunt against him" (v6).** "All these" are the nations Babylon has swallowed (v5). The conquered will one day sing over their conqueror. What follows are five "woes," each a short taunt song. The Tyndale notes divide them as vv6–8, 9–11, 12–14, 15–17 and 18–20. Each names a sin, announces a judgment and gives the reason. Guzik divides the chapter slightly differently and counts four, beginning at v9, but the content is the same. "Riddles" means sayings with a sting, words with a double meaning.
- **First woe: plunder (vv6–8).** **"Woe to him who heaps up what is not his own—for how long?"** Habakkuk asked God "how long?" (1:2). Now the nations ask it of Babylon. **"Loads himself with pledges"** pictures a moneylender piling up the goods taken from debtors as security (Deut 24:10–13). The KJV's "thick clay" comes from reading the rare Hebrew word as two words. JFB notes the reading "an accumulation of pledges."
- **"Will not your debtors suddenly arise?" (v7).** "Debtors" is literally "those who bite you," from *nashak*, to bite, which is also the word for charging interest. The lender bites, and his victims will bite back. **"Because you have plundered many nations, all the remnant of the peoples shall plunder you" (v8).** The punishment fits the crime exactly. In 539 BC the Persians and Medes, among others, took Babylon. **"For the blood of man and violence to the earth"** is a refrain that comes again in v17. "Violence" is *chamas*, the word Habakkuk cried out in 1:2. God has heard.
- **Second woe: the secure house (vv9–11).** **"Woe to him who gets evil gain for his house, to set his nest on high"** (v9). "House" can mean both a building and a dynasty. "Nest on high" is the picture of an eagle building out of reach (Obad 4; Jer 49:16). JFB takes it as the royal citadel. Babylon's palaces and its massive walls were meant to make it untouchable. Jeremiah speaks a similar woe against King Jehoiakim for building his own fine house by injustice (Jer 22:13–14).
- **"You have devised shame for your house… you have forfeited your life" (v10).** The schemes meant to secure the house bring disgrace on it. JFB: those who wrong others "do much greater wrong to their own souls." Guzik points to the rich fool who built bigger barns the night his life was demanded of him (Luke 12:16–21).
- **"For the stone will cry out from the wall, and the beam from the woodwork respond" (v11).** The building itself testifies. Stones and rafters, gathered by plunder, cry out against their owner like two witnesses in a lawsuit, one calling and the other answering. As Abel's blood cried from the ground (Gen 4:10), creation itself bears witness to injustice. Jesus said that if his disciples were silent, "the very stones would cry out" (Luke 19:40).
- **Third woe: building with blood (vv12–14).** **"Woe to him who builds a town with blood and founds a city on iniquity!"** (v12). Micah used almost the same words of Jerusalem's own rulers (Mic 3:10). Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt Babylon on a huge scale, with the Ishtar Gate, the great ziggurat and vast walls, much of it with captive labor and plundered wealth. His own inscriptions boast of it.
- **"Peoples labor merely for fire, and nations weary themselves for nothing" (v13).** This is a near-quotation found also in Jeremiah 51:58, in his prophecy against Babylon: "the peoples labor for nothing, and the nations weary themselves only for fire." Everything built by injustice is fuel for the fire. JFB: the Chaldeans "labor at what is to be food for the fire." And it is "from the LORD of hosts." He decides what lasts.
- **"For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD as the waters cover the sea" (v14).** In the middle of the woes comes the great promise of the chapter. Isaiah 11:9 has almost the same words, in its picture of the Messiah's peaceful kingdom. Habakkuk adds one word: "the knowledge of *the glory* of the LORD." Babylon tried to fill the earth with its own glory. In the end, the earth will be as full of God's glory as the seabed is full of water. Guzik calls this God's final triumph. Paul speaks of "the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6).
- **Fourth woe: shameful drunkenness (vv15–17).** **"Woe to him who makes his neighbors drink… in order to gaze at their nakedness!"** (v15). The picture recalls Noah lying drunk and uncovered (Gen 9:21–22). It may describe literal debauchery, but more likely it is a figure for how Babylon dealt with nations: drawing them in, making them helpless and then exposing them to shame. JFB reads it as enticing neighbors into ruin. Guzik applies it also literally, warning against drunkenness and drawing others into it (Eph 5:18).
- **"Drink, yourself, and show your uncircumcision!" (v16).** "Show your uncircumcision" means be exposed as an outsider to God's covenant. The Dead Sea Scroll and the Greek Septuagint read "and stagger!" which in Hebrew is the same letters with two swapped. Both fit the drunken picture.
- **"The cup in the LORD's right hand will come around to you" (v16).** The "cup" is a common prophetic image for God's judgment, which nations are made to drink (Jer 25:15–28; Ps 75:8). Babylon has passed the cup to others. Now it comes round to Babylon. JFB: "The cup of sorrow is now in thy turn to pass to thee." Jesus would speak of the cup his Father gave him to drink (John 18:11; Matt 26:39), the very cup of judgment, drunk for others.
- **"The violence done to Lebanon will overwhelm you" (v17).** Babylonian and Assyrian kings cut down the great cedars of Lebanon for their palaces and hunted its animals for sport. Nebuchadnezzar left an inscription in a valley of Lebanon boasting of the cedars he took. Isaiah imagines the trees rejoicing at Babylon's fall: "Since you were laid low, no woodcutter comes up against us" (Isa 14:8). JFB suggests "Lebanon" may also stand for Jerusalem or the land of Israel. Either way, God cares about violence done even to the land and its creatures. The refrain of v8 returns.
- **Fifth woe: idols (vv18–20).** **"What profit is an idol when its maker has shaped it, a metal image, a teacher of lies?"** (v18). The fifth woe begins with a question before the "woe" (v19), unlike the others. Babylon was full of gods. Marduk, its chief god, was carried in great processions. But an idol cannot teach truth. **"Speechless idols"** is a sound-play in Hebrew, *'elilim 'illemim*, "nothings that are dumb." Henry: "An image is a doctrine of vanities; it is falsehood, and a work of errors."
- **"Woe to him who says to a wooden thing, Awake; to a silent stone, Arise!" (v19).** Elijah mocked Baal's prophets in the same way: "perhaps he is asleep and must be awakened" (1 Kgs 18:27). **"It is overlaid with gold and silver, and there is no breath at all in it."** On the outside, splendor; on the inside, nothing. JFB: "Outside it has some splendor, within none." Compare Isaiah 44:9–20 and Psalm 115:4–8.
- **"But the LORD is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before him" (v20).** The contrast could not be sharper. The idols are silent because they are dead. The earth must be silent because the living God is present. The Tyndale notes point out that his "holy temple" is both his heavenly throne room and, in Habakkuk's day, the temple in Jerusalem. "Keep silence" is *has*, "Hush!", the same word as Zephaniah 1:7 and Zechariah 2:13. It is the silence of awe, the right response to God's answer. Habakkuk, who began with a complaint, falls silent before God and then, in chapter 3, prays. Henry: "Our God will make all the world silent before him."
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v11 as the very stones of a palace "built by rapine" testifying against their builder, and v14 as looking toward the restoration of Israel and the spread of God's glory. Henry and JFB both treat the idols of vv18–19 as lifeless frauds and v20 as a call to reverent submission. Guzik notes, from Campbell Morgan, that God's use of the Chaldeans never meant their power would last. On the fourth woe he applies the text to drinking, passing on Trapp's quip that drink puts out a man's ale, money and wit. He ends on v20: the majesty of the living God exposes the folly of every idol. The Tyndale notes add that the five taunts let the oppressors bring about their own fall, and that the victims themselves will sing them.

#### Pointing to Christ
God never explains to Habakkuk why he chose Babylon. He gives him something better: a promise that will "surely come," and a way to live until it does. The New Testament reads that promise as a person, "the coming one" who "will not delay" (Heb 10:37). And it reads "the righteous shall live by faith" as the heart of the gospel: people are put right with God not by building towers of their own but by trusting the faithful God who justifies the ungodly (Rom 1:16–17; 4:5). Babylon drank the cup of the LORD's right hand because of its violence. Jesus drank that cup for others, so that the earth might one day be filled with "the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6).
