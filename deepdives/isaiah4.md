*Setting:* Jerusalem in the eighth century BC, in the same message as chapters 2 and 3. Isaiah has just described the proud women of Zion stripped of their finery and the city's men killed in battle (3:16–26). Chapter 4 is short and falls in two very different parts. Verse 1 is the last scene of that judgment, and the chapter break comes in the wrong place, since it belongs with chapter 3. Then, in vv2–6, Isaiah suddenly turns to hope. The same "in that day" that brought shame will also bring a beautiful Branch, a cleansed and holy remnant, and God's own presence hovering over Zion as it did over Israel in the wilderness. The pattern of the whole book is here in miniature: judgment first, then a purified people living with God.

#### Judgment on Judah and Jerusalem (v1)
- **"Seven women shall take hold of one man in that day" (v1).** After the war of 3:25, men are so few that seven women compete for one husband. "Seven" is a round number for "many." Normally a man proposed and paid a bride-price, and a husband was bound to provide his wife with food and clothing (Exod 21:10). These women reverse it all. They offer to feed and clothe themselves if he will only marry them.
- **"Only let us be called by your name; take away our reproach" (v1).** To be called by a man's name meant to belong to his household as his wife. "Reproach" was the shame of being unmarried and childless, which in Israel felt like being cut off from the future (Gen 30:23; 1 Sam 1:6). The women who strutted proudly in 3:16 are now begging. "Called by your name" sets up a contrast with v3, where the survivors will be "called holy."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that this verse could well have been placed at the end of chapter 3, since it completes the threat of a shrinking, broken population. JFB reads the women's longing as especially sharp among Jews who hoped to bear "the seed of the woman," the promised deliverer (Gen 3:15). Guzik observes that desperation pushes people into unwise choices, and that these women would accept a marriage in name only.

#### The Branch of the LORD Glorified (vv2–6)
- **"In that day" (v2).** The same phrase as v1 and 2:20, but now it opens a promise. In Isaiah "that day" is God's time of decisive action. It brings judgment on the proud and rescue for the remnant. Both sides belong to one day.
- **"The branch of the LORD shall be beautiful and glorious" (v2).** "Branch" is *tsemach*, literally "sprout," new growth springing up from the ground. "Beautiful" (*tsevi*) and "glorious" (*kavod*) are the very things the women of chapter 3 were trying to wear. Now real beauty and glory come from what God grows.
- **Who is "the branch"?** Two main readings. Many scholars, including the Tyndale notes, think it means first the renewed growth of the land and the remnant, God making his people flourish again after judgment. The parallel line, "the fruit of the land," supports that. Many others, from the ancient Aramaic translation (the Targum, which reads "the Messiah of the LORD") through most Christian interpreters, see the Messiah. Later prophets use *tsemach* as a title for David's coming heir: "I will raise up for David a righteous Branch" (Jer 23:5; 33:15), and "my servant the Branch" (Zech 3:8; 6:12). Isaiah uses a different word for the "branch" from Jesse's stump in 11:1, but the picture is the same. The two readings need not compete, since the Messiah is the one through whom God's people flourish.
- **"The fruit of the land shall be the pride and honor of the survivors" (v2).** "Pride" here is a good word: something to rightly glory in. The people who took pride in jewelry (3:18–23) will take pride in God's gift. Fruitfulness of the land is a frequent sign of God's blessing restored (Isa 32:15; Amos 9:13–15).
- **"The survivors of Israel" (v2).** Isaiah's great theme of the remnant begins here. Not everyone will be saved. But God will keep a remnant, "those who escape," and through them his promises will go on (1:9; 10:20–22). Isaiah even names his son Shear-jashub, "a remnant shall return" (7:3).
- **"He who is left in Zion… will be called holy" (v3).** Being "called" something in Hebrew often means being it. Earlier, Jerusalem's people were called by the names of their sins (1:4). Now the survivors are called holy, set apart to God. Holiness is not something they achieve. It is the result of what God does to them in v4.
- **"Everyone who has been recorded for life in Jerusalem" (v3).** Literally "written for life." Israel kept family and tribal registers, and to be in the register meant to be a citizen. Behind it is God's own book: Moses asked to be blotted out of "your book" (Exod 32:32), and Daniel speaks of everyone "found written in the book" being delivered (Dan 12:1). The New Testament calls it the book of life, which has the names of those who belong to the Lamb (Phil 4:3; Rev 3:5; 21:27).
- **"When the Lord shall have washed away the filth of the daughters of Zion" (v4).** The word for "filth" is strong, the word used for vomit and excrement (28:8; 36:12). The daughters who perfumed themselves (3:24) were filthy before God. The "bloodstains of Jerusalem" recall the hands "full of blood" of 1:15. God himself does the washing, as he promised: "though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (1:18).
- **"By a spirit of judgment and by a spirit of burning" (v4).** "Spirit" (*ruach*) can also mean "wind" or "breath." This could be the Holy Spirit, or a "blast" of judgment and burning, like a refining wind. Either way, cleansing comes through fire. The judgment that destroys the wicked is the same fire that purifies the remnant, as a smelter burns away dross (1:25; Mal 3:2–3). John the Baptist promised that Jesus would baptize "with the Holy Spirit and fire" (Matt 3:11).
- **"Then the LORD will create… a cloud by day, and smoke and the shining of a flaming fire by night" (v5).** "Create" is *bara'*, the word in Genesis 1:1 used only of God's own creative work. This is something new, but it looks back to the exodus. When Israel left Egypt, the LORD went before them "in a pillar of cloud by day… and by night in a pillar of fire" (Exod 13:21–22). The cloud filled the tabernacle and later Solomon's temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kgs 8:10–11). God's visible glory had marked his presence, and Ezekiel would see that glory leave the temple before its destruction (Ezek 10:18; 11:23). Isaiah promises it will come back, and not just over the temple but "over the whole site of Mount Zion and over her assemblies."
- **"For over all the glory there will be a canopy" (v5).** "Canopy" is *chuppah*, the word for the bridal canopy or bridal chamber (Joel 2:16; Ps 19:5). Jewish weddings still take place under a chuppah today. God's presence will cover his cleansed people as a groom shelters his bride. Isaiah later uses the marriage picture outright: "your Maker is your husband" (54:5), and "as the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you" (62:5).
- **"A booth for shade by day from the heat, and… a shelter from the storm and rain" (v6).** "Booth" is *sukkah*, the shelter of branches Israel lived in during the Feast of Booths to remember the wilderness journey (Lev 23:42–43). In a land of scorching summer sun and sudden winter storms, shade and shelter were life-saving. God himself becomes that shelter (25:4; 32:2). The cloud that protected Israel from Egypt's army (Exod 14:19–20) now protects all of Zion.
- **A second exodus.** Verses 5–6 are the first of Isaiah's many pictures of a new exodus. God will rescue his people again, lead them again and live among them again (11:15–16; 43:16–19; 52:11–12). The return from Babylon was a partial fulfilment. The New Testament sees the full one in Christ and the new creation.
- **Commentators:** All three read the Branch as the Messiah. Matthew Henry: he is called "the branch of the Lord, because planted by his power and flourishing to his praise," and "all the graces and comforts of the gospel spring from Christ." JFB sees both sides of Christ in the parallel lines: he is "of the Lord" and also "of the earth" in his birth and death, and he is "not a dry, but a fruit-bearing branch." JFB takes the book in v3 as primarily Israel's family register and only by extension the book of life. On v4, Henry writes that "reformation-work is God's work," and JFB says the same Spirit sanctifies believers "by the fire of affliction" and condemns unbelievers. Guzik insists that holiness means being set apart from the world and to the Lord, not a kind of super-spirituality, and he notes that God's protection cannot be separated from his cleansing. Henry sums up v6: "God is a refuge to his people in all weathers."

#### Pointing to Christ
The Branch is beautiful and glorious, and those who belong to him are washed and called holy. Jesus is the Branch from David's line whom the later prophets named. He washes his people with his own blood (Rev 1:5), and he baptizes with the Spirit and fire. The glory-cloud that once filled the tabernacle came to live among us in a body: "the Word became flesh and dwelt [literally *tabernacled*] among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). The canopy over the bride points to the wedding of the Lamb, when the holy city comes "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" and God himself dwells with his people (Rev 21:2–3). There, as Isaiah promised, there will be shelter from every heat and storm (Rev 7:15–16).
