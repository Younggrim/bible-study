*Setting:* Zephaniah preached in Jerusalem "in the days of Josiah" (v1), who reigned 640–609 BC. Josiah came to the throne at eight years old, after the fifty-five-year reign of his grandfather Manasseh, the worst king in Judah's history, and the two-year reign of his father Amon, who was assassinated (2 Kgs 21). Manasseh had filled Jerusalem with altars to Baal and the stars, sacrificed his own son, and "shed very much innocent blood" (2 Kgs 21:16). The worship Zephaniah condemns in vv4–6 is exactly what Josiah tore down in his great reform of 622 BC (2 Kgs 23:4–14), so most scholars date the book a little before that, perhaps around 630–625 BC. Zephaniah may have helped prepare the ground for the reform, and Jeremiah began preaching in the same years (Jer 1:2). Assyria was weakening, and Nineveh would fall in 612 BC (Zeph 2:13). Zephaniah's great theme is "the day of the LORD," and this chapter gives the Bible's most concentrated picture of it.

#### The Coming Judgment on Judah (vv1–6)
- **"Zephaniah the son of Cushi, son of Gedaliah, son of Amariah, son of Hezekiah" (v1).** Prophets usually give only their father's name. Zephaniah goes back four generations, which is very unusual. The likeliest reason is that the last name is King Hezekiah (715–686 BC), which would make Zephaniah a distant relative of King Josiah and help explain his familiarity with the court and the city (vv8, 10–11). The Tyndale notes and Henry both take it this way, though the text does not add "king of Judah," so it cannot be certain. Another suggestion is that his father's name, Cushi ("Cushite"), might have led some to think him a foreigner, so the full line proves he was a true Israelite. That is only a guess.
- **The name Zephaniah.** It means "the LORD hides" or "the LORD has treasured," from the verb *tsaphan*, to hide something precious. Guzik suggests he may have been born during Manasseh's persecutions and that his parents named him for God's protection. That is an attractive guess but only a guess. The name does fit his message: "Seek the LORD… perhaps you may be hidden on the day of the anger of the LORD" (2:3).
- **"I will utterly sweep away everything from the face of the earth" (v2).** The Hebrew doubles the verb, *'asoph 'aseph*, "sweeping I will sweep." It is a forceful opening. With no introduction or warm-up, the book begins with the end of everything.
- **"I will sweep away man and beast… the birds of the heavens and the fish of the sea" (v3).** The list runs backward through Genesis 1, where fish and birds were made on the fifth day and animals and humans on the sixth (Gen 1:20–27). The Tyndale notes draw out this reversal. Judgment is pictured as creation being unmade. The words also echo the flood, when God said, "I will blot out man… and animals and creeping things and birds of the heavens" (Gen 6:7).
- **"And the rubble with the wicked" (v3).** The Hebrew word is unclear. It means things that cause stumbling, so many take it as idols and the objects of false worship, swept away together with their worshipers. JFB reads it that way: idols and idolaters share "a common destruction." The ESV's "rubble" follows another reading.
- **"I will cut off mankind from the face of the earth" (v3).** There is a wordplay here: *'adam*, "mankind," from the face of the *'adamah*, "ground." Adam was made from the ground (Gen 2:7), and now he is cut off from it.
- **"I will stretch out my hand against Judah and against all the inhabitants of Jerusalem" (v4).** After the whole world, the focus narrows to God's own people. Guzik notes that the move from all the earth to Judah means no one can assume the warning is for someone else. God's people are not exempt. Judgment begins at the house of God (1 Pet 4:17). "Stretched out hand" is the phrase used of God's mighty acts against Egypt in the exodus (Exod 6:6). Now it is turned on Israel.
- **"The remnant of Baal" (v4).** Baal was the Canaanite storm and fertility god. "Remnant" suggests that some cleansing had already begun or that Baal worship was in retreat, but not gone. Josiah began seeking God at sixteen and started purging idols at twenty (2 Chr 34:3), so these words may come from those early years.
- **"The idolatrous priests along with the priests" (v4).** The first word is *kemarim*, a special term used only for pagan priests (2 Kgs 23:5; Hos 10:5). The second is the ordinary word for priests, the LORD's own. Both are lumped together. The LORD's priests had become no different from Baal's.
- **"Those who bow down on the roofs to the host of the heavens" (v5).** Flat rooftops were used for worship of the sun, moon and stars, a practice that spread under Assyrian influence (2 Kgs 21:3–5; 23:12; Jer 19:13). The law had forbidden it outright (Deut 4:19).
- **"Those who bow down and swear to the LORD and yet swear by Milcom" (v5).** Milcom was the national god of Ammon, across the Jordan (1 Kgs 11:5, 33). The Hebrew can also be read *malkam*, "their king," which JFB notes, and the name is closely linked to Molech, the god of child sacrifice. The sin here is not outright atheism but mixed loyalty: the LORD on one day and Milcom on another. Elijah had asked, "How long will you go limping between two different opinions?" (1 Kgs 18:21). Jesus put it simply: "No one can serve two masters" (Matt 6:24).
- **"Those who have turned back from following the LORD, who do not seek the LORD or inquire of him" (v6).** The circle widens from open idolaters to the quietly indifferent: those who once followed and drifted, and those who never bothered to seek. Henry calls the first group "renegadoes from his service," and of the second says they are "careless people, that live without God in the world."
- **Commentators:** Henry and the Tyndale notes both take Zephaniah's ancestor as King Hezekiah, Henry noting that the name is "the very same name in the original." JFB stresses that Zephaniah preached under a reforming king, so the people had "no such excuse" for their idolatry. Guzik dates the book early in Josiah's reign, before the revival, and sees vv4–6 as fulfilled in Josiah's reform (2 Kgs 23). He turns that into an invitation: idols will be removed one way or the other, by repentance or by judgment.

#### The Day of the LORD Is Near (vv7–18)
- **"Be silent before the Lord GOD!" (v7).** The Hebrew is a single word, *has*, like "Hush!" It is the silence of awe before a king or judge (Hab 2:20; Zech 2:13). Guzik describes it as a call to stop protesting innocence and listen.
- **"For the day of the LORD is near" (v7).** "The day of the LORD" is the time when God acts decisively in history to judge and to save. Amos had already warned that it would be darkness, not light, for a sinful Israel that longed for it (Amos 5:18–20). Joel, Isaiah and Obadiah use it too. Zephaniah uses it more than any other prophet. It can mean a judgment within history, such as the fall of Jerusalem, and it also points to the final day when God judges the world. The Tyndale notes say both are in view: partly fulfilled in 586 BC, echoed in AD 70, and fully fulfilled at the end.
- **"The LORD has prepared a sacrifice and consecrated his guests" (v7).** A chilling reversal. People were invited to sacrifices and fed from the offering. Here Judah itself is the sacrifice. The "guests" made holy for the feast are the invaders, the Babylonians, set apart as God's instruments (compare Isa 13:3; Jer 46:10). Henry puts it bluntly: "these are the guests God has prepared."
- **"I will punish the officials and the king's sons" (v8).** The royal family and the court are named first, though Josiah himself was godly. Within a generation, Josiah's sons would be carried off to Egypt and Babylon, and Zedekiah's sons would be killed before his eyes (2 Kgs 25:7). Leadership carries greater responsibility (Jas 3:1).
- **"All who array themselves in foreign attire" (v8).** Fashion was a sign of loyalty. Wearing Assyrian or other foreign styles meant imitating the empire and its ways, sometimes even its religious dress (2 Kgs 10:22). Guzik sees leaders ashamed of who they were. Henry: "Pride in apparel is displeasing to God, and a symptom of the degeneracy of a people."
- **"Everyone who leaps over the threshold" (v9).** When the ark was captured, the Philistines' god Dagon fell on his temple's threshold, and ever after "the priests of Dagon… do not tread on the threshold" (1 Sam 5:5). Many think Judah had picked up the superstition of jumping over thresholds to avoid evil spirits thought to live there. The Tyndale notes and Guzik take it this way. JFB gives other possible readings, including servants rushing into houses to seize plunder. **"Those who fill their master's house with violence and fraud"** then describes officials who enrich their masters by oppressing others.
- **"A cry will be heard from the Fish Gate, a wail from the Second Quarter" (v10).** Zephaniah walks the reader through Jerusalem. The Fish Gate was in the north wall (Neh 3:3; 12:39), where fish from Tyre and Galilee were sold, and where an attacker from the north would strike first. The "Second Quarter" (*mishneh*) was a newer district on the western hill, where the prophetess Huldah lived (2 Kgs 22:14). "A loud crash from the hills" means the walls going down.
- **"Wail, O inhabitants of the Mortar!" (v11).** "The Mortar" (*Maktesh*) was probably a hollow in the city, likely in the central valley where markets stood. The name fits: they will be pounded like grain in a mortar. "Traders" is literally "the people of Canaan." The Canaanites, especially the Phoenicians of Tyre, were famous traders, so "Canaanite" came to mean "merchant." JFB sees a sting in it. Judah's merchants have become Canaanites in more ways than one. Guzik links greed to idolatry (Col 3:5).
- **"I will search Jerusalem with lamps" (v12).** Ancient houses were dark, so searching meant carrying a lamp into every corner. No one will be able to hide. Guzik passes on Baker's contrast with the Greek philosopher Diogenes, who searched Athens with a lamp for an honest man. The LORD searches Jerusalem for the guilty.
- **"The men who are complacent" (v12).** Literally "who are thickening on their dregs." Wine was left on its sediment (lees) for a time to gain strength, but if left too long it grew thick and syrupy. Jeremiah uses the same picture of Moab, who "has settled on his dregs" and never been poured from vessel to vessel (Jer 48:11). These people have been undisturbed so long they have gone stale and hard.
- **"The LORD will not do good, nor will he do ill" (v12).** Here is the root problem. They do not deny God exists. They deny he does anything. He neither rewards nor punishes, so it does not matter how they live. Guzik likens it to the "clockmaker" god of deism, who wound up the world and walked away. Psalm 10:4, 11 describes the same practical atheism. Peter warns of scoffers who say, "all things are continuing as they were" (2 Pet 3:4).
- **"Though they build houses, they shall not inhabit them" (v13).** This is a covenant curse straight from Deuteronomy 28:30, 39 (compare Amos 5:11). JFB notes the connection. Those who said God would do nothing find that he does exactly what he said.
- **"The great day of the LORD is near, near and hastening fast" (v14).** "Near" is said twice. **"The mighty man cries aloud there"** pictures even the warrior screaming in terror.
- **"A day of wrath is that day" (v15).** The verse hammers the word "day" six times in a row, each followed by a pair of words for disaster. In Hebrew some pairs rhyme, such as *sho'ah umesho'ah*, "ruin and devastation," which JFB says makes "a dreary, monotonous sound." In the Latin Vulgate the verse begins *Dies irae, dies illa*, and those words became the opening of a famous medieval hymn on the last judgment, usually credited to Thomas of Celano in the 1200s. Guzik passes on this connection. Mozart, Verdi and many others set the hymn to music.
- **"A day of darkness and gloom, a day of clouds and thick darkness" (v15).** These are the words used of Sinai, where God came down in darkness, cloud and fire (Exod 19:16–18; Deut 4:11). The God who came down to give the covenant comes again to enforce it. Joel 2:2 uses the same words.
- **"A day of trumpet blast and battle cry against the fortified cities" (v16).** The trumpet (*shofar*) that called Israel to worship becomes the enemy's signal to attack. "Lofty battlements" are the corner towers of city walls.
- **"They shall walk like the blind, because they have sinned against the LORD" (v17).** This is another covenant curse: "you shall grope at noonday, as the blind grope in darkness" (Deut 28:28–29). The Tyndale notes connect it with spiritual blindness (Rom 1:21; 1 John 2:11). **"Their blood shall be poured out like dust, and their flesh like dung"** means their lives will be treated as worthless.
- **"Neither their silver nor their gold shall be able to deliver them" (v18).** This picks up the merchants of v11. Ezekiel says the same thing in nearly the same words (Ezek 7:19). Money can buy almost anything except deliverance on that day.
- **"In the fire of his jealousy, all the earth shall be consumed" (v18).** God's jealousy is his burning zeal for his own honor and his people's faithfulness (compare Nah 1:2). The chapter ends where it began, with all the earth (vv2–3), so Judah's judgment is framed by the judgment of the world. **"A full and sudden end"** recalls 1:2's "utterly." Yet the book does not stop here. Chapter 2 opens with an invitation, "Seek the LORD" (2:3), and chapter 3 ends with God singing over his people (3:17).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the guests of v7 as the Chaldeans whom God has "prepared and invited" to the slaughter, and he hears in v12 the voice of practical atheists: "The Lord will not do good, neither will he do evil." JFB reads "Canaanites" in v11 as the "merchant people" and the complacent as "hardened by ease," and on v15 hears the dreary sound of the Hebrew. Guzik counts the "day of the LORD" over twenty-five times in Scripture, calls it God's time as opposed to man's day, and notes that the opening words of v15 in Latin gave the church the *Dies irae*. His illustration for v7 is Boice's account of notorious gangsters who insisted on their innocence to the end, a picture of people refusing to be silent before God.

#### Pointing to Christ
Zephaniah 1 is among the darkest chapters in the Bible, and it does not soften the day of the LORD. Yet the Gospels describe a day when "there was darkness over all the land" at noon (Matt 27:45), and Jesus bore that day's wrath in the place of others. Because of that, Paul can call the coming day the day of Christ, a day believers can look toward with hope rather than dread (Phil 1:6, 10). Silver and gold cannot deliver on that day, but "you were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). And the God whom the complacent said would "not do good" did the greatest good imaginable.
