*Setting:* Jerusalem, late summer of 445 BC. Despite mockery, threats of attack and an internal crisis over debt and slavery (chs 4–5), the wall is almost complete. Every gap is closed, though the gate doors are not yet hung (v1). Sanballat of Samaria, Tobiah and Geshem the Arab have failed to stop the work by ridicule or by threat. Now, with time running out, they aim at Nehemiah himself. Chapter 6 records four schemes in quick succession: a meeting that was a trap, a letter that was a smear, a prophecy that was a bribe and a web of letters between Tobiah and the nobles of Judah. In between stands one of the great verses of the book: "So the wall was finished… in fifty-two days."

#### Conspiracy Against Nehemiah (vv1–14)
- **"No breach left in it (although up to that time I had not set up the doors in the gates)" (v1).** The wall was continuous but not yet closed. The gates were the last and most difficult work, and until they were hung the city was still vulnerable. The enemies knew they had little time left.
- **"Come and let us meet together at Hakkephirim in the plain of Ono" (v2).** Ono lay near Lod, about twenty miles northwest of Jerusalem, on the edge of Judah's territory near the border with Samaria and Ashdod. "Hakkephirim" may be a place name, or it may simply mean "the villages." It looked like neutral ground for talks. It was a full day's journey from Jerusalem, far from Nehemiah's guards, and close to his enemies' territory.
- **"But they intended to do me harm" (v2).** Nehemiah saw through the friendly invitation. He does not say how he knew. Perhaps he simply knew these men.
- **"I am doing a great work and I cannot come down" (v3).** His answer is polite and firm. He does not accuse them. He simply refuses to be pulled away. "Come down" is literal, since Ono was in the lowlands far below Jerusalem, but it also carries the sense of stepping down from his work to their level. "Why should the work stop while I leave it?"
- **"They sent to me four times… and I answered them in the same manner" (v4).** Persistence is part of the pressure. Nehemiah's answer did not change either.
- **"An open letter in his hand" (v5).** Official letters were normally rolled, tied and sealed. An open letter was meant to be read along the way. Sanballat wanted the accusation in it to spread. Its contents would be public before Nehemiah even saw them.
- **"It is reported among the nations, and Geshem also says it" (v6).** The letter rests on rumor: "everyone is saying." Its source is one of the plotters himself. The charge is that the wall is a step toward rebellion and that Nehemiah means to make himself king.
- **"You have also set up prophets to proclaim concerning you in Jerusalem, 'There is a king in Judah'" (v7).** This charge was dangerous because it was believable. In Israel's history prophets had anointed kings (1 Sam 16:13; 2 Kgs 9:1–6). Prophets of the return had spoken of a coming king: "your king is coming to you" (Zech 9:9), and God's signet ring, Zerubbabel (Hag 2:23). A Persian king suspicious of Jerusalem, as Artaxerxes had been once already (Ezra 4:19–22), might act on a report like this without checking it.
- **"No such things as you say have been done, for you are inventing them out of your own mind" (v8).** Nehemiah does not argue point by point. He calls it what it is, a lie, and he goes back to work.
- **"Their hands will drop from the work" (v9).** The goal of every scheme was the same: to make the builders lose heart and stop. "Hands dropping" means losing strength and courage (Jer 38:4; Isa 13:7). Nehemiah's answer is a short prayer: "But now, O God, strengthen my hands." His enemies wanted weak hands. He asked God for strong ones. The word for "strengthen" is the same root as "repaired" all through chapter 3.
- **"Shemaiah the son of Delaiah, son of Mehetabel, who was confined to his home" (v10).** Shemaiah appears to be a priest or connected to priests, since he had access to the temple. Why he was "confined" or "shut in" is not clear. He may have been ritually unclean, under some restriction or acting out a sign as prophets sometimes did. Or he may have been pretending to be in danger himself, to make his warning more convincing. Nehemiah went to see him, perhaps at his request.
- **"Let us meet together in the house of God, within the temple. Let us close the doors" (v10).** His words are poetic in form, like a prophetic oracle. The advice sounds pious: take refuge in God's house. But "within the temple" means the holy place itself, where only priests could go. The Law said that any outsider who came near would be put to death (Num 18:7). King Uzziah was struck with leprosy for entering the temple to burn incense (2 Chr 26:16–21).
- **"Should such a man as I run away? And what man such as I could go into the temple and live?" (v11).** Two reasons. First, the leader of the city could not hide while his people stood on the wall. His flight would collapse their courage. Second, he was not a priest. To save his life by breaking God's law would cost him far more.
- **"I understood and saw that God had not sent him" (v12).** The message failed the test. A true prophet would not tell someone to break God's own law (Deut 13:1–5). Nehemiah also learned, perhaps later, that Tobiah and Sanballat had paid him.
- **"That I should be afraid and act in this way and sin, and so they could give me a bad name" (v13).** The aim was not only to scare him but to discredit him. A governor caught hiding in the sanctuary would have lost the people's respect and given his enemies a story to tell.
- **"Remember Tobiah and Sanballat, O my God… and also the prophetess Noadiah and the rest of the prophets" (v14).** Another of Nehemiah's short prayers handing his enemies over to God (compare 4:4–5). Noadiah is mentioned only here. She and other prophets had joined the campaign to frighten him. False prophets, men and women, are a recurring danger in Scripture (Ezek 13:17; Matt 7:15). Nehemiah does not take revenge. He leaves the judgment to God.
- **Commentators:** Henry turns Nehemiah's reply into a principle: "We have work to do, and must not neglect it." On the slander he writes, "We must never omit any known duty for fear it should be misconstrued; but, while we keep a good conscience, let us trust God with our good name." He calls "now, therefore, O God, strengthen my hands" "a good prayer" for any duty or temptation. On Shemaiah, Henry says, "Let us never decline a good work, never do a bad one." JFB explains that letters to persons of rank were enclosed, so an open letter was a deliberate insult. He reads "Hakkephirim" as the town Chephirah in Benjamin, which is possible though most locate the meeting in the plain of Ono itself. JFB also suggests the "temple" refuge was a strong crypt. Guzik draws out the three kinds of attack, false friendship, slander and false religion, and notes that Nehemiah met each one with discernment and kept working.

#### The Wall Is Finished (vv15–19)
- **"So the wall was finished on the twenty-fifth day of the month Elul, in fifty-two days" (v15).** Elul is the sixth month, August–September. The finishing date was about October 2, 445 BC, and the work had begun around the start of the month Ab, only a few months after Nehemiah first spoke to the king. Fifty-two days is astonishingly fast for a city wall, even allowing that some sections of the old wall still stood and that the eastern wall was built higher up the ridge on a shorter line. The Jewish historian Josephus gives two years and four months, but he likely includes all the finishing work, and the text's figure is clear.
- **"All the nations around us were afraid and fell greatly in their own esteem" (v16).** Literally "fell greatly in their own eyes." The mockers of 4:2–3 were now humbled. They had laughed at the "feeble Jews."
- **"For they perceived that this work had been accomplished with the help of our God" (v16).** This is the verdict of the chapter, spoken by the enemies themselves. They had to admit that no human explanation was enough. The wall's rapid completion was a testimony to the God of Israel among the nations. This is exactly what Nehemiah had prayed for (1:11; 2:20).
- **"The nobles of Judah sent many letters to Tobiah" (v17).** The wall was done, but the danger was not over. Inside the city, leading men kept up a steady correspondence with Nehemiah's enemy.
- **"Many in Judah were bound by oath to him" (v18).** Literally "lords of an oath," sworn allies. Tobiah had married into the family of Shecaniah son of Arah (Arah's family returned with Zerubbabel, Ezra 2:5), and his son Jehohanan had married the daughter of Meshullam son of Berechiah. Meshullam had worked hard on the wall twice over (3:4, 30). Marriages, business ties and loyalties tied leading Judeans to the very man trying to bring Nehemiah down. Later Tobiah was even given a room in the temple courts by the high priest Eliashib (13:4–9).
- **"They spoke of his good deeds in my presence" (v19).** The nobles praised Tobiah to Nehemiah. There may be a play on words: "Tobiah" means "the LORD is good," and "his good deeds" (*tovotav*) uses the same word. His name and his reputation said "good," and his actions said something else. Meanwhile the nobles "reported my words to him."
- **"And Tobiah sent letters to make me afraid" (v19).** The chapter ends not with a celebration but with intimidation still going on. The work of God is finished, but the people doing it are still under pressure. The dedication of the wall comes later (12:27–43), after the people themselves are renewed (chs 8–10).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the wall was built in fifty-two days "though they rested on the sabbaths," and that "A great deal of work may be done in a little time, if we set about it in earnest." On Tobiah's alliances he writes, "When men once became akin to Tobiah, they soon became sworn to him. A sinful love leads to a sinful league." JFB says the speed "cannot be sufficiently accounted for, except by the consideration that the builders labored with the ardor of religious zeal." He links the "troublous times" of the building to Daniel 9:25. Guzik notes that four months of prayer lay behind fifty-two days of building, and that Nehemiah let Tobiah's friends think what they liked rather than be drawn into a feud.

#### Pointing to Christ
Nehemiah was invited to come down, accused of claiming to be king, and urged by a false prophet to save his life by stepping outside God's will. Jesus faced all three. He was urged to come down from the cross (Matt 27:40), accused before the governor of making himself a king (John 19:12), and tempted, even by a friend, to avoid the suffering God had appointed (Matt 16:21–23). Like Nehemiah, he would not run: "Should such a man as I run away?" He set his face toward Jerusalem (Luke 9:51) and finished the great work he came to do (John 17:4). The nations saw in a finished wall that "this work had been accomplished with the help of our God." The empty tomb is a far greater sign that God himself has done the work of salvation.
