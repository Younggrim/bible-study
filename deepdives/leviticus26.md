*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. This chapter is the climax of the "Holiness Code" (chs 17–26), and in a sense of the whole law given at Sinai, as verse 46 says. It follows a pattern familiar from treaties of the ancient Near East, where a great king made a covenant with a lesser king and closed it with blessings for loyalty and curses for betrayal. Here the great King is the LORD and the vassal is Israel. Deuteronomy 28 is a longer parallel, given forty years later on the plains of Moab. Leviticus 26 reads in hindsight like a summary of Israel's history: blessing in the land, centuries of drift, defeat, exile, and then a door left open for return.

#### Blessings for Obedience (vv1–13)
- **"You shall not make idols… or pillar… or a figured stone" (v1).** Before the blessings, a summary of the covenant's heart: the first two commandments. A **"pillar"** was a standing stone, often set up at Canaanite shrines. A **"figured stone"** was a stone carved with images. Not every standing stone was wrong. Jacob set one up as a memorial (Gen 28:18), but stones set up "to bow down to" were forbidden.
- **"Keep my Sabbaths and reverence my sanctuary" (v2).** This repeats 19:30 word for word. Holy time and holy place guard Israel against idolatry.
- **"If you walk in my statutes" (v3).** The condition. The blessings that follow are the fruit of a relationship, not wages. God is the one who gives each of them.
- **"Your rains in their season" (v4).** Canaan depended on rain, not a river (Deut 11:10–12). The early rains in autumn softened the ground for planting, and the late rains in spring filled out the grain. God promised both on time.
- **"Your threshing shall last to the time of the grape harvest" (v5).** The harvests would be so large that threshing barley and wheat would run into the grape harvest, and the grapes into the autumn sowing. Amos uses the same picture for the age to come: "the plowman shall overtake the reaper" (Amos 9:13).
- **"You shall lie down, and none shall make you afraid" (v6).** Safety from wild animals and from war. The phrase is picked up by the prophets as a picture of the Messiah's peace (Mic 4:4; Ezek 34:25–28).
- **"Five of you shall chase a hundred" (v8).** Strength from God, not numbers. Gideon's three hundred and Jonathan with his armor-bearer are later examples (Judg 7; 1 Sam 14:6–15).
- **"I will make my dwelling among you" (v11).** "Dwelling" is *mishkan*, the word for the tabernacle itself. **"My soul shall not abhor you"** is striking language: God promises he will not reject them in disgust. The same verb returns in the curses (vv15, 30, 43–44).
- **"I will walk among you and will be your God, and you shall be my people" (v12).** This is the best blessing of all, God's own presence. The verb "walk" (*hithallek*) is the same form used of God "walking in the garden" in Eden (Gen 3:8). The promise points back to the fellowship lost there. **"Your God… my people"** is the covenant formula found from Exodus to Revelation. Paul quotes this verse to the Corinthians: "we are the temple of the living God; as God said, 'I will make my dwelling among them and walk among them'" (2 Cor 6:16). Revelation 21:3 is its final fulfilment.
- **"I have broken the bars of your yoke and made you walk erect" (v13).** A yoke bowed a slave's or an animal's neck. God snapped it, so Israel could stand up straight as free people.
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures v12 as God walking among them "with delight and satisfaction, as a man in his garden," and keeping company "as a man walking with his friend." On v13, freedom from Egypt meant they could "serve God without fear, each walking in his uprightness." JFB calls Sabbath and sanctuary "antidotes to idolatry," and notes rain in Judea fell mainly in autumn and spring. Guzik names God's presence as the greatest of the blessings and points to the parallels in Gideon and Jonathan.

#### Punishment for Disobedience (vv14–46)

**Discipline that grows stronger (vv14–39)**
- **"If you spurn my statutes, and if your soul abhors my rules" (v15).** The curses begin with Israel's heart. God promised that his soul would not abhor them (v11). Now it is Israel's soul that abhors him. **"Break my covenant"** names the sin: unfaithfulness to a relationship.
- **Five waves of judgment.** The curses come in rounds, each opened by a phrase like "if in spite of this you will not listen" (vv18, 21, 23, 27): sudden terror, disease and defeat (vv16–17), drought (vv19–20), wild animals (v22), war, plague and famine (vv25–26), then the destruction of the cities and exile (vv29–33). The pattern is not God losing patience. It is God giving Israel chance after chance to turn back. The text calls this **"discipline"** (vv18, 23, 28), the word used for a father correcting his son (Prov 3:11–12).
- **"Sevenfold for your sins" (vv18, 21, 24, 28).** Seven is the number of completeness. This means "many times over" or "in full measure," not a precise calculation.
- **"You shall flee when none pursues you" (v17).** Fear itself becomes a judgment. Proverbs 28:1 puts it simply: "The wicked flee when no one pursues." Verse 36 makes it vivid: **"The sound of a driven leaf shall put them to flight."**
- **"I will make your heavens like iron and your earth like bronze" (v19).** No rain falls from a sky like metal, and nothing grows from ground like metal. Elijah's three-and-a-half-year drought under King Ahab is the famous example (1 Kings 17:1; Jas 5:17).
- **"I also will walk contrary to you" (v24).** God walked among them (v12). Now, if they walk against him, he will walk against them. The word "contrary" (*qeri*) appears seven times in this chapter and nowhere else in the Bible. It may mean "hostility" or "by chance," as if Israel treated God's will as an accident.
- **"Ten women shall bake your bread in a single oven" (v26).** Normally each household had its own oven. In this famine there is so little flour that ten households share one, and the bread is weighed out. Ezekiel used the same picture of rationed bread for the siege of Jerusalem (Ezek 4:16).
- **"You shall eat the flesh of your sons" (v29).** The most horrifying line in the chapter. It came true: in the siege of Samaria (2 Kings 6:28–29), in the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon (Lam 2:20; 4:10), and again, Josephus reports, in the Roman siege of AD 70.
- **"I will destroy your high places… and my soul will abhor you" (v30).** "High places" were hilltop shrines where Israel later mixed the worship of the LORD with Canaanite gods. The **"incense altars"** (*hammanim*) were small altars found at such sites. **"Cast your dead bodies upon the dead bodies of your idols"** is grim irony: the "gods" are as dead as their worshipers.
- **"I will not smell your pleasing aromas" (v31).** Sacrifice could not save a people who had abandoned the covenant. The prophets said the same (Isa 1:11–15; Amos 5:21–22).
- **"I will scatter you among the nations" (v33).** Israel's ten northern tribes were exiled by Assyria in 722 BC (2 Kings 17:6), and Judah was taken to Babylon in 586 BC (2 Kings 25).
- **"Then the land shall enjoy its Sabbaths" (vv34–35).** The land had been denied its seventh-year rest (25:2–7). In exile it would rest at last. Second Chronicles quotes this as the reason for the exile's length: "until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths… to fulfill seventy years" (2 Chr 36:21, linking this chapter with Jer 25:11).
- **"Because of the iniquities of their fathers" (v39).** The sins of past generations would bear fruit in the exile, but each generation is also responsible for its own. Ezekiel later insists that a son who turns from his father's sins will live (Ezek 18:14–17).
- **Commentators:** JFB says greater privileges brought greater guilt, notes the "sevenfold" language means severe and prolonged punishment, and says the land's missed Sabbath years added up to about seventy, matching the exile. It sees the siege cannibalism fulfilled at Samaria, Jerusalem and under Rome. Guzik reads the escalating rounds as God bringing judgment slowly, hoping for repentance, and notes that God promises to discipline Israel, not to forsake them. The Tyndale notes describe God here as a perfectly righteous and loving parent, whose punishments increase step by step so that Israel can repent at any point.

**The covenant remembered (vv40–46)**
- **"But if they confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers" (v40).** Even at the bottom, the door is not shut. The way back is confession, owning both personal sin and the long history of betrayal. Daniel and Nehemiah pray exactly this way in exile and after it (Dan 9:4–19; Neh 9:16–37).
- **"If then their uncircumcised heart is humbled" (v41).** Physical circumcision was the sign of the covenant, but an "uncircumcised heart" is a stubborn heart, closed to God. Moses later says God himself will "circumcise your heart" (Deut 30:6), and Paul says true circumcision is "a matter of the heart, by the Spirit" (Rom 2:29). **"Make amends"** translates a verb (*ratsah*) that can mean "pay off" or "accept." Most translations take it as accepting their punishment as just.
- **"I will remember my covenant with Jacob… Isaac… Abraham" (v42).** The order is reversed, counting backward to the beginning. God's promise to the patriarchs came before Sinai, and it would outlast Israel's failure under the law. Paul makes this point: the law, coming 430 years later, cannot cancel a covenant God already made (Gal 3:17). **"And I will remember the land."** God has not forgotten the place either.
- **"Yet for all that… I will not spurn them, neither will I abhor them so as to destroy them utterly" (v44).** This is the turning point of the chapter. The very words of the curses, "spurn" and "abhor," are taken back. God disciplines his people, but he will not destroy them or break his covenant. Paul draws on this hope in Romans 11: "God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew" (Rom 11:1–2), and "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Rom 11:29).
- **"These are the statutes and rules and laws… on Mount Sinai" (v46).** This verse closes the main body of Leviticus, and many read chapter 27 as an appendix.
- **Commentators:** The Tyndale notes stress that the covenant itself could not be cancelled, even when Israel broke its terms. JFB reads vv40–45 as God's promise of forgiveness and return, grounded in his covenant with the patriarchs, and records the view that chapter 27 is an appendix. Guzik draws three marks of true repentance from vv40–41: confession of personal and inherited sin, a humbled heart, and acceptance of God's discipline as just. He adds that believers in Christ are not under the curse, since Christ bore it, though God still disciplines his children (Heb 12:7–11).

#### Pointing to Christ
The blessings of this chapter end with God walking among his people. The curses end with exile and death. Israel earned the curses, and so would anyone. But "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). He went into the darkness that verses 14–39 describe, cut off and forsaken, so that the blessing of verse 12 could come true for good. In him, God has "made his dwelling" (*tabernacled*) among us (John 1:14), and by his Spirit believers are now the temple where God walks (2 Cor 6:16). He also gives what verse 41 asked for, a circumcised heart (Col 2:11), and he is the proof that God does not break his covenant: "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20).
