*Setting:* Joseph is in the royal prison in Egypt, the place "where the king's prisoners were confined" (39:20), attached to the house of the captain of the guard. He was put there on a false charge of assaulting his master's wife. Even so, "the LORD was with Joseph," and the keeper put him in charge of the other prisoners (39:21–23). He is now in his late twenties. About eleven years have passed since his brothers sold him, and he is roughly two years away from standing before Pharaoh (41:1, 46). His own dreams of sheaves and stars (37:5–11) must seem very distant. This chapter is quiet and short, but it is the hinge between the prison and the palace. God brings two of Pharaoh's officials into Joseph's cell, gives them dreams, and gives Joseph the meaning. Then a man forgets, and Joseph waits.

#### Joseph Interprets Two Prisoners' Dreams (vv1–23)
- **"The cupbearer of the king of Egypt and his baker committed an offense" (v1).** The cupbearer was far more than a waiter. He served the king's wine, tasted it to guard against poison, and stood close to the king every day, so he often became a trusted adviser. Nehemiah later held this post for the king of Persia (Neh 1:11; 2:1). The chief baker was in charge of the king's food. Both were "officers" (*saris*, v2), senior court officials. Their offense is not named. Some guess at a plot to poison Pharaoh, since both handled what he ate and drank, but the text says only that "Pharaoh was angry."
- **"In the prison where Joseph was confined" (v3).** The text makes a point of it. Of all the prisons in Egypt, these two men are sent to the one where Joseph is. Nothing in the chapter says God arranged it. The whole story says he did.
- **"The captain of the guard appointed Joseph to be with them" (v4).** The captain of the guard is Potiphar's title (37:36; 39:1). JFB takes it that Potiphar himself assigned Joseph to serve these high-ranking prisoners. If so, he trusted Joseph despite the charge, which may explain why Joseph was imprisoned rather than executed. "Attended them" is the same word used of Joseph serving Potiphar (39:4). The man of the robe and the dreams is now a servant to other men's needs. **"They continued for some time"**, literally "days," probably months.
- **"One night they both dreamed… each dream with its own interpretation" (v5).** Two men, two dreams, on the same night, each fitted to the man. Egyptians took dreams very seriously. Papyri survive from ancient Egypt that list dream images and what they were thought to mean, and the court kept trained experts to interpret them (41:8). In prison these men had no access to such experts. JFB: "The influence of Providence must be seen in the remarkable fact of both officers dreaming such dreams in one night."
- **"Why are your faces downcast today?" (vv6–7).** Joseph notices. He has every reason to be absorbed in his own wrongs, but he sees two troubled faces and asks. Henry draws the lesson that it is good "to enquire into the reason of the sadness of our brethren's countenances."
- **"Do not interpretations belong to God? Please tell them to me" (v8).** Joseph's answer has two halves, and both matter. He gives the credit to God, and he offers to listen, trusting God to give the meaning. Daniel says much the same to Nebuchadnezzar: "there is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries" (Dan 2:28). Joseph has not lost faith in dreams from God, even though his own have not come true. The Tyndale notes suggest that his readiness here shows he still believed his own dreams.
- **The cupbearer's dream: the vine (vv9–11).** A vine with three branches buds, blossoms and ripens in moments. The cupbearer presses the grapes into Pharaoh's cup and puts it in Pharaoh's hand. Each step is his familiar job, done again. Egyptian tomb paintings show vineyards and wine making, and wine was the drink of the court.
- **"In three days Pharaoh will lift up your head" (vv12–13).** "Lift up the head" is a Hebrew idiom for raising someone up, honoring them, or calling them out of prison to be judged. The same phrase is used when a Babylonian king released Jehoiachin from prison (2 Kgs 25:27, where it is literally "lifted up the head"). The cupbearer will be restored to his office.
- **"Only remember me… and so get me out of this house" (v14).** This is the only time in the story we hear Joseph ask for anything for himself. He asks the cupbearer to "do me the kindness," literally to "do *hesed* with me," to show him loyal kindness. A chapter earlier, the LORD "showed him steadfast love," the same word *hesed* (39:21). Joseph asks a man for what God has already been showing him. Asking was not a lack of faith. Using honest means to seek release is not opposed to trusting God. JFB hears in it "a sadness and impatient longing for release, which not all his piety and faith in God could dispel."
- **"I was indeed stolen out of the land of the Hebrews" (v15).** "Indeed stolen" doubles the verb in Hebrew, "stolen, stolen," for emphasis. Joseph tells the truth without naming his brothers or Potiphar's wife. He does not spread blame. He simply says he did nothing to deserve this. He calls his prison **"the pit"**, the same word (*bor*) used for the empty cistern his brothers threw him into (37:24). From one pit to another.
- **The baker's dream: the baskets (vv16–17).** The baker speaks up only after he hears that the first meaning was "favorable." He has three baskets on his head, the top one full of baked goods for Pharaoh, and birds are eating from it. Egyptian art shows servants carrying baskets on their heads. "Cake baskets" may mean baskets of white bread, or, as JFB takes it, wicker baskets "full of holes." In the cupbearer's dream he is active, serving the king. In the baker's dream he does nothing while birds eat the king's food. He fails at his one task.
- **"Pharaoh will lift up your head—from you!" (vv18–19).** Joseph repeats the same idiom with a terrible twist. For the cupbearer, "lift up your head" meant restoration. For the baker it means his head will be lifted off. **"And hang you on a tree. And the birds will eat the flesh from you."** He would be executed and his body displayed on a pole or stake, left unburied for the birds. For Egyptians, who spent enormous effort preserving bodies for the afterlife, this was the worst disgrace. Later Israelite law also spoke of a body hung "on a tree" as under God's curse (Deut 21:22–23), the text Paul applies to the cross (Gal 3:13).
- **Joseph tells the hard truth.** It would have been easy to soften the meaning or keep silent. Joseph tells the baker plainly what will happen. JFB notes that it was "painful to communicate such dismal tidings."
- **"On the third day, which was Pharaoh's birthday" (v20).** Royal birthdays in the ancient world were celebrated with feasts and were natural occasions for pardons or judgments. Pharaoh "lifted up the head" of both men, calling them both out in front of his servants. Only two birthday feasts are described in the Bible: this one and Herod's, where John the Baptist was beheaded (Mark 6:21–28). Both ended in an execution.
- **Restored and hanged (vv21–22).** Everything happens "as Joseph had interpreted to them." The Tyndale notes point out that the exact fulfillment confirmed that Joseph's interpretations came from God, and with them his own dreams. The baker's death is also a reminder of how absolute Pharaoh's power was. One word from him meant life or death.
- **"Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph, but forgot him" (v23).** The narrator says it twice: did not remember, forgot. Joseph asked to be remembered (v14), and the man walked out and forgot. The next chapter begins "After two whole years" (41:1). That means two more years in prison after Joseph's hope was raised. Psalm 105 says of this time, "until what he had said came to pass, the word of the LORD tested him" (Ps 105:19). The cupbearer forgot. God did not (Isa 49:15). The forgetting was also part of God's timing: if the cupbearer had spoken up at once, Joseph might have been released and gone home to Canaan, and he would not have been in Egypt when Pharaoh needed him.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this a chapter in which "things are working, though slowly, towards Joseph's advancement." He reads Joseph's request as modest: "Only, think of me." On the birthday he notes that "the solemnizing of the birth-day of princes has been an ancient piece of respect," and on v23, "See how apt those that are themselves at ease are to forget others in distress." Guzik sees Joseph's concern for the two men as evidence that he had not grown bitter, and he commends Joseph for giving the baker the whole message, not only good news. He adds that seeking release by honest means did not take Joseph out of God's hands, and that God guides both a believer's steps and his delays. Guzik also infers, like JFB, that the captain of the guard did not fully believe the charge against Joseph. Guzik cautions that not every dream is from God. JFB's comment on v23 echoes Henry: "How prone are men to forget and neglect in prosperity, those who have been their companions in adversity." JFB has no notes on vv2, 6 or 7.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joseph, innocent and condemned, is placed between two prisoners. One will be raised up and restored. The other will die and be hanged on a tree. And the one who was restored forgets the innocent man who spoke to him. At the cross Jesus, innocent and condemned, hung between two criminals. One of them said, "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom," and Jesus answered, "Today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:42–43). Joseph asked to be remembered and was forgotten. Jesus is the one who remembers. He was himself hanged on a tree and became a curse so that people under the curse could be lifted up (Gal 3:13). Joseph's waiting was not wasted, and the long silence before God acts is often the very place he is preparing the answer.
