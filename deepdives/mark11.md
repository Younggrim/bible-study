*Setting:* Jerusalem, the Sunday to Tuesday before Passover, probably in April of AD 30 (some date it to AD 33). Jesus has climbed the desert road from Jericho with a crowd of Passover pilgrims, and Bartimaeus has just hailed him as "Son of David" (10:47). Mark has spent ten chapters in Galilee and on the road. Now he slows right down. Chapters 11–16 cover about a week, and Mark marks the days: the entry (vv1–11), "the following day" (v12), "in the morning" (v20). The whole chapter centers on the temple, which Jesus enters as its rightful Lord and finds barren.

#### The Triumphal Entry (vv1–11)
- **"Bethphage and Bethany, at the Mount of Olives" (v1).** Bethany, home of Mary, Martha and Lazarus, lay about two miles east of Jerusalem on the far slope of the Mount of Olives. Bethphage ("house of unripe figs") was nearer the summit. Jesus seems to have spent his nights in Bethany during this week (v11; 14:3). The Mount of Olives itself carried hope: Zechariah 14:4 says the LORD's feet will stand on it on the day he comes to save.
- **"A colt tied, on which no one has ever sat" (v2).** Zechariah had promised: "Behold, your king is coming to you… humble and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey" (Zech 9:9). Matthew and John quote it (Matt 21:5; John 12:15). Mark simply shows it. An animal never ridden was fit for a sacred purpose (Num 19:2; 1 Sam 6:7). Jacob's blessing of Judah also speaks of the ruler who binds "his donkey's colt to the choice vine" (Gen 49:10–11).
- **"The Lord has need of it" (v3).** It may have been prearranged, as the upper room later seems to be (14:13–16), or it may be supernatural knowledge. Either way Jesus is in control of the timing. "The Lord" in Mark's Greek can mean "the master," but Mark's readers had already heard Jesus called Lord (1:3; 2:28) and would hear more in it.
- **Riding in on purpose.** Pilgrims normally walked the last stretch into the city. Jesus, who has walked everywhere in Mark, now rides. For the first time he openly acts out a claim to be the king. But he comes on a donkey, not a warhorse. Kings rode donkeys in peace (1 Kings 1:33, 38, where Solomon rides David's mule to his coronation).
- **Cloaks and branches (vv7–8).** Spreading garments was a royal welcome: Jehu's officers did it when he was proclaimed king (2 Kings 9:13). The "leafy branches" were cut from the fields. John says palm branches (John 12:13), which had become a national symbol since the Maccabees retook the temple in 164 BC.
- **"Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!" (v9).** The crowd quotes Psalm 118:25–26, one of the psalms sung at Passover. "Hosanna" is Hebrew *hoshiah na*, "save, please." By Jesus' day it had also become a shout of praise, but the old cry for rescue was still inside it. Psalm 118 also says, "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (118:22), which Jesus will quote in the next chapter (12:10–11).
- **"Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David!" (v10).** The crowd hopes for the restored kingdom of David, and many surely pictured Rome thrown out. Jesus is that king, but his throne will be won on a cross. Within days a crowd in the same city will shout "Crucify him" (15:13–14), though it need not have been the same people.
- **"He had looked around at everything… he went out to Bethany" (v11).** The scene ends quietly. No miracle, no speech. Jesus inspects the temple like an owner checking his property and leaves. Malachi had warned: "The Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple… But who can endure the day of his coming?" (Mal 3:1–2). Tomorrow he will act.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws out the contrast with a Roman triumph, the great victory parade of a conquering general, and notes that the colt marked Jesus as a king of peace. He reads v11 as a deliberate inspection, tied to Malachi 3. The Tyndale notes take the same line, calling the entry an anticlimax that sets up the coming judgment. JFB passes over vv1–10 in Mark and only remarks that Mark disposes of the first day in a few words.

#### Jesus Curses the Fig Tree (vv12–14)
- **"He was hungry" (v12).** JFB rightly insists this was "real bodily hunger," not a staged scene.
- **"A fig tree in leaf… it was not the season for figs" (v13).** This is the puzzle of the passage. Fig trees in Judea leaf out in March and April and give their main crop in late summer. But before or with the leaves they put out small early knobs, called *taqsh* in Arabic today, which locals ate. A tree in full leaf with no knobs at all would bear nothing that year. The leaves advertised fruit that was not there.
- **"May no one ever eat fruit from you again" (v14).** This is the only miracle of judgment in the Gospels, and it is done to a tree, not a person. It is an acted parable, like the prophets' sign-acts: Jeremiah's ruined loincloth (Jer 13:1–11) or smashed jar (Jer 19:1–11). In the prophets, the fig tree is often a picture of Israel. "When I would gather them… there are no figs on the fig tree" (Jer 8:13; also Hos 9:10; Mic 7:1). Jesus came to his people looking for fruit and found leaves: busy religion without the fruit of repentance and faith.
- **"And his disciples heard it" (v14).** Mark makes a point of this. They will see the result the next morning.
- **Mark's "sandwich."** Mark splits the fig tree story in two (vv12–14, 20–25) and puts the temple scene in the middle. He does this elsewhere (5:21–43; 6:7–30) so that each half explains the other. The withered tree tells the reader how to read what Jesus does in the temple. It is not just a cleanup. It is a sign of judgment.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "interpreters are not agreed" on "not the season for figs," but says the leaves came early and promised fruit. Its comment on the curse is memorable: "That word did not make the tree barren, but sealed it up in its own barrenness." Guzik makes the same point, that the tree was condemned not for being fruitless, which was normal in spring, but for showing leaves that falsely promised fruit. Henry reads it as a picture of Christ finding the Jewish church fruitless.

#### Jesus Cleanses the Temple (vv15–19)
- **The temple courts.** Herod the Great had rebuilt and vastly enlarged the temple, beginning around 20 BC, and work was still going on. The huge outer platform, about 35 acres, was the Court of the Gentiles, the one area where non-Jews could come to pray. Stone signs warned Gentiles not to go farther on pain of death. Two of those signs have been found.
- **"Those who sold and those who bought… money-changers… those who sold pigeons" (v15).** Pilgrims needed approved animals for sacrifice, and every Jewish man paid a yearly half-shekel temple tax (Exod 30:13). Coins with the emperor's image were not accepted, so the tax was paid in Tyrian silver, and exchangers charged a fee. Pigeons were the offering of the poor (Lev 12:8; Luke 2:24). None of this was wrong in itself. Doing it inside the court for Gentiles, under the control of the high priest's family and at a profit, was the problem. Later Jewish writings complain bitterly about the greed of the high priestly families.
- **"He would not allow anyone to carry anything through the temple" (v16).** Only Mark records this. People used the outer court as a shortcut across the city. Later rabbis also forbade it (Mishnah Berakhot 9:5). For a time, Jesus shut down business as usual.
- **"A house of prayer for all the nations" (v17).** Jesus quotes Isaiah 56:7, from a passage promising that foreigners and eunuchs, once shut out, would be welcomed to God's house. Only Mark includes "for all the nations." His Gentile readers in Rome would notice. The one court where they could pray had been turned into a market.
- **"But you have made it a den of robbers" (v17).** This quotes Jeremiah 7:11, from Jeremiah's temple sermon. There God warned that people who lived wickedly and then ran to the temple for safety were treating it like a robbers' hideout, and that he would destroy it as he had destroyed Shiloh (Jer 7:12–14). A den is not where robbers rob but where they hide afterward. The choice of text is itself a warning that the temple will fall. It did, in AD 70.
- **One cleansing or two?** John places a temple cleansing at the start of Jesus' ministry (John 2:13–22), while Matthew, Mark and Luke place it in the last week. Many think there were two, one at the beginning and one at the end. Others think John moved the event for theological reasons. Both views are held by careful, believing scholars.
- **"Seeking a way to destroy him, for they feared him" (v18).** Jesus has now challenged the chief priests on their own ground. From here the plot to kill him moves quickly (12:12; 14:1–2).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the act a purification, driven by zeal for God's house, and warns that letting worldly business into worship turns a house of prayer into "a house of merchandise." Guzik stresses that the noise and trade took over the only place Gentiles could pray. JFB does not comment on these verses in Mark and refers readers to its notes on Luke. The Tyndale notes add that the action was probably limited to one part of the vast court, which may explain why the Roman garrison did not step in.

#### The Lesson from the Withered Fig Tree (vv20–26)
- **"Withered away to its roots" (v20).** Not just a few dead leaves. The whole tree is dead from below. Matthew compresses the story and has it wither at once (Matt 21:19–20). Mark spreads it over two days, as he does with the temple.
- **"Have faith in God" (v22).** The answer to the fig tree is not more religion but trust in God himself. The Greek is literally "have faith of God," but grammar and context show that God is the one in whom faith is placed.
- **"Whoever says to this mountain, 'Be taken up and thrown into the sea'" (v23).** "Moving mountains" was a Jewish figure of speech for doing what seemed impossible. Standing on the road from Bethany, "this mountain" could be the Mount of Olives, or it could be the temple mount in view across the valley. If it is the temple mount, the saying fits the context: the old temple order would be removed, and faith in God would not depend on it. From the Mount of Olives the Dead Sea is also visible in the distance.
- **"Believe that you have received it, and it will be yours" (v24).** This is not a promise that strong enough belief forces God's hand. Jesus himself prayed in Gethsemane, "Yet not what I will, but what you will" (14:36). The promise belongs to people who trust God and pray in line with his will (1 John 5:14), and it is meant to be bold.
- **"Whenever you stand praying, forgive" (v25).** Jews usually stood to pray (1 Sam 1:26; Luke 18:11, 13). Faith and forgiveness go together. People who come to God for mercy must not withhold it from others (Matt 6:12, 14–15). A temple full of transactions, but without mercy, is exactly what Jesus has just judged.
- **Verse 26 is missing.** Some later manuscripts add, "But if you do not forgive, neither will your Father who is in heaven forgive your trespasses," closely matching Matthew 6:15. The oldest manuscripts lack it, so the ESV leaves it out of the main text. The teaching is the same in Matthew.
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes "faith in God" as faith directed to God, citing Greek grammarians, and warns against putting faith in faith itself. He insists God is not bound to grant whatever is believed hard enough. JFB reads the mountain as a figure for every obstacle that cannot stand before "a confiding faith in God." Henry ties v25 to the Lord's Prayer and warns that an unforgiving heart will not be forgiven. The Tyndale notes favor the temple mount as "this mountain," which fits the judgment theme of the chapter but is not certain.

#### The Authority of Jesus Challenged (vv27–33)
- **"The chief priests and the scribes and the elders" (v27).** These three groups made up the Sanhedrin, the Jewish ruling council, the same three Jesus named in his first passion prediction (8:31). An official delegation has come.
- **"By what authority are you doing these things?" (v28).** "These things" means the temple action above all. In their eyes, only the high priest and council could say what went on in the temple. Jesus had no office, no rabbinic training under a recognized master and no license from them.
- **"Was the baptism of John from heaven or from man?" (v30).** Answering a question with a question was a normal rabbinic way of debating, and this is not a dodge. John had pointed to Jesus as the one coming after him (1:7–8), and at John's baptism heaven had opened over Jesus (1:10–11). If John's mission came from God, so did the authority of the one he announced. "Heaven" was a reverent Jewish way of saying "God."
- **"They discussed it with one another" (vv31–32).** Mark lets us overhear them. They do not ask which answer is true, only which is safe. "From heaven" condemns their unbelief. "From man" angers the crowd, who "held that John really was a prophet." Mark breaks off their sentence in the middle (v32), as if the thought were too dangerous to finish.
- **"We do not know" (v33).** The leaders of the nation, the official judges of prophets (Deut 18:20–22), will not judge the most important prophet of their time. Because they will not answer, Jesus will not either. But he does not stop there. The next parable (12:1–12) gives his answer plainly enough that they know he means them.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads Jesus' question as a decisive answer, since John's testimony settles who Jesus is, and he sees in the leaders' reply people more concerned with saving face than facing the truth. The Tyndale notes add that the three groups named made up the council of seventy-one that ruled the nation. JFB's text for this section could not be retrieved.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus rides into Jerusalem as the humble king Zechariah promised and walks into the temple as the Lord Malachi said would suddenly come. He finds leaves and no fruit, trade and no prayer, leaders who calculate and do not believe. The temple was meant to be the place where God met his people and where the nations could pray. Jesus judges it because he has come to replace it. In a few days his body will be the temple torn down and raised in three days (John 2:19–21), and through him the way to God will be opened to all the nations. The crowd's "Hosanna," "save, please," is answered, though not the way they expected.
