*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably late in Uzziah's or Jotham's reign, around 740 BC, a time of wealth and confidence in Judah. Chapters 2–4 swung between the glory God promised Zion and the pride and injustice that filled it now. Chapter 5 sums up the case. It opens with a song, perhaps sung at a harvest festival when vineyards and wine were on everyone's mind, and it turns into an indictment. Then come six "woes" that name the rotten fruit, and the chapter ends with a foreign army God himself summons. Assyria, the rising superpower to the northeast, was about to sweep west. Within a few years it would destroy the northern kingdom of Israel (722 BC) and devastate Judah (701 BC).

#### The Vineyard of the LORD Destroyed (vv1–7)
- **"Let me sing for my beloved my love song concerning his vineyard" (v1).** Isaiah begins like a singer at a wedding or a harvest feast. "Beloved" (*yadid*) and "love song" sound tender. In the Song of Songs a vineyard is a picture of a bride (Song 8:12). The listeners expect a love song. What they get is a broken heart.
- **"On a very fertile hill" (v1).** Literally "on a horn, a son of oil," a sunny ridge with rich soil. Judah's terraced hillsides were ideal for vines.
- **"He dug it and cleared it of stones… built a watchtower… hewed out a wine vat" (v2).** Every step is real farm work. The hill country is full of rocks, so stones were cleared and piled into walls. A stone watchtower guarded the crop at harvest. A wine vat was cut into the bedrock for treading grapes, and many such rock-cut presses can still be seen in Israel. The owner built for the long term, expecting a harvest for years to come.
- **"Choice vines" (v2).** The word *soreq* names a prized red grape, the same root as the Valley of Sorek where Samson met Delilah (Judg 16:4). Jeremiah uses the same word: "I planted you a choice vine… how then have you turned degenerate?" (Jer 2:21).
- **"But it yielded wild grapes" (v2).** The Hebrew *be'ushim* comes from a root meaning "to stink." The lexicon gives "stinking or worthless things." These are not merely small or sour grapes but rotten, foul ones. The problem is not that the vineyard was barren. It produced the wrong fruit.
- **"Judge between me and my vineyard" (v3).** The singer suddenly speaks as the owner, and asks the audience to rule on the case. It is a trap, like the prophet Nathan's story of the stolen lamb, which made David condemn himself (2 Sam 12:1–7).
- **"What more was there to do for my vineyard, that I have not done in it?" (v4).** The answer is nothing. God brought Israel out of Egypt, gave them a land, a law, priests, prophets and kings. The fault is not in the care but in the vine.
- **"I will remove its hedge… break down its wall" (v5).** A thorn hedge and a stone wall kept out wild animals and thieves. Without them, the vineyard is "devoured" and "trampled." God will remove his protection and let the nations in (v26).
- **"I will also command the clouds that they rain no rain upon it" (v6).** This line gives the owner away. No human farmer commands the clouds. Before v7 names him, the owner has revealed himself as God.
- **"The vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel" (v7).** The trap springs shut. The audience has just condemned the vineyard, and the vineyard is them.
- **"He looked for justice, but behold, bloodshed; for righteousness, but behold, an outcry!" (v7).** A famous wordplay that English cannot reproduce. God looked for *mishpat* (justice) and found *mispach* (bloodshed or oppression). He looked for *tsedaqah* (righteousness) and heard *tse'aqah* (a cry of distress). The words sound almost the same but mean the opposite, just as Judah's life looked like righteousness from a distance and was the reverse up close. An English attempt: he looked for right, but saw riot; for equity, but heard iniquity.
- **Jesus retells this song.** In the parable of the tenants, Jesus begins with Isaiah's words almost exactly: a man "planted a vineyard and put a fence around it and dug a winepress in it and built a tower" (Matt 21:33; Mark 12:1). Then he adds the owner's son, whom the tenants kill. The leaders "perceived that he was speaking about them" (Matt 21:45). In John 15 Jesus calls himself "the true vine," the vine that bears the fruit Israel failed to bear.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads the beloved as Christ, "God's beloved Son and our beloved Saviour," and JFB likewise calls him "Jehovah, the Second Person." Henry: "God expects vineyard-fruit from those that enjoy vineyard-privileges, not leaves only," and "wild grapes are the fruits of the corrupt nature." JFB says God is, as it were, "amazed at the unnatural fruit of so well-cared a vineyard," and explains the "fertile hill" as literally a "horn," a sunny peak. Guzik argues that God did everything short of turning people into robots, so the blame lies with human will, and he applies the removed hedge to church discipline, when God lets the unrepentant feel the loss of his protection (1 Cor 5:5). All three give the wordplay of v7, which the lexicon supports.

#### Woe to the Wicked (vv8–30)
- **Six woes.** Verses 8–23 contain six cries of "Woe" (vv8, 11, 18, 20, 21, 22). Each one names a kind of rotten grape. "Woe" (*hoy*) was the cry of mourners at a funeral. The prophet is speaking over people who are as good as dead.
- **"Woe to those who join house to house, who add field to field" (v8).** The first woe is land-grabbing. In Israel the land belonged to God, and each family's plot was to stay in that family forever (Lev 25:23). In the Year of Jubilee, land that had been sold went back to its original family (Lev 25:10–13). Naboth refused to sell his vineyard even to a king for that reason (1 Kgs 21:3). Rich men in Isaiah's day were buying up or foreclosing on small farms and building estates. Micah says the same: "They covet fields and seize them" (Mic 2:2).
- **"You are made to dwell alone in the midst of the land" (v8).** The irony is that greed ends in loneliness. The man who owns everything has no neighbors left.
- **"Ten acres of vineyard shall yield but one bath" (v10).** An "acre" here is the area a team of oxen could plow in a day. A bath was a liquid measure of roughly six gallons. Ten days' plowing of vineyard would yield a few jars of wine. A homer of seed would produce an ephah, which is one-tenth of a homer. Farmers would harvest a tenth of what they sowed. Those who grabbed land would find it worthless.
- **"Woe to those who rise early in the morning, that they may run after strong drink" (v11).** The second woe is endless partying. Drinking from morning to night was a mark of decadence (Eccl 10:16–17; Acts 2:15). "Strong drink" (*shekar*) was beer or other fermented drink, not distilled liquor.
- **"They do not regard the deeds of the LORD, or see the work of his hands" (v12).** The problem is not music or wine as such. It is that a life of pleasure has no room for God. They never ask what he is doing in their history.
- **"My people go into exile for lack of knowledge" (v13).** The prophet speaks of the future as if it has already happened. "Lack of knowledge" is not missing information but not knowing God, the same charge as 1:3 ("Israel does not know") and Hosea 4:6 ("my people are destroyed for lack of knowledge"). The feasters will end up hungry and thirsty.
- **"Sheol has enlarged its appetite and opened its mouth beyond measure" (v14).** Sheol is the place of the dead in the Old Testament. Here it is pictured as a monster with a huge throat, swallowing nobles and crowds alike. Canaanite myths pictured the god of death, Mot, with one lip on the earth and one in the heavens. Isaiah borrows the image but leaves no doubt that death serves the LORD.
- **"Man is humbled… but the LORD of hosts is exalted in justice" (vv15–16).** The refrain of 2:9, 11 and 17 returns. "The Holy God shows himself holy in righteousness": God shows he is holy not only in worship but by putting things right. The holiness Isaiah will see in chapter 6 is already the measure of everything.
- **"Then shall the lambs graze as in their pasture" (v17).** The estates of the rich will turn into open pasture where sheep graze and wandering herdsmen camp among the ruins.
- **"Woe to those who draw iniquity with cords of falsehood… as with cart ropes" (v18).** The third woe. These people are not dragged into sin. They are hauling it along behind them like a cart, harnessed to it and pulling hard. An old rabbinic saying puts the progression well: the evil impulse starts as thin as a spider's thread and ends as thick as a cart rope.
- **"Let him be quick, let him speed his work that we may see it" (v19).** Mockers dare God to act. "Be quick" and "speed" are the Hebrew *maher* and *chush*, the same words Isaiah later uses in the name of his son, Maher-shalal-hash-baz, "the spoil speeds, the prey hastens" (8:1–4). God will answer their sneer with exactly what they asked for. Peter describes the same scoffers in the last days: "Where is the promise of his coming?" (2 Pet 3:3–4). "The Holy One of Israel" is Isaiah's favorite name for God, used about twenty-five times in the book and only a handful elsewhere. Here the mockers use it as a joke.
- **"Woe to those who call evil good and good evil" (v20).** The fourth woe goes beyond doing wrong to redefining it. Darkness is labeled light, bitter is labeled sweet. A society that does this cannot even recognize its own sickness. Proverbs says, "He who justifies the wicked and he who condemns the righteous are both alike an abomination to the LORD" (Prov 17:15).
- **"Woe to those who are wise in their own eyes" (v21).** The fifth woe is self-made wisdom. "Be not wise in your own eyes; fear the LORD" (Prov 3:7). Isaiah later mocks the royal counselors who thought they could outwit God with alliances (29:14–15; 30:1–2). Paul quotes 29:14 against the wisdom of the world (1 Cor 1:19).
- **"Heroes at drinking wine… who acquit the guilty for a bribe" (vv22–23).** The sixth woe returns to drink but with bitter sarcasm. "Heroes" and "valiant men" are military words. Judah's mighty men are mighty only at mixing drinks. And these same men are the judges, letting the guilty go for a payment and denying the innocent their rights (1:23).
- **"As the tongue of fire devours the stubble" (v24).** The sentence: like dry straw in a field fire, they will burn up from root to blossom. The reason is stated plainly: "they have rejected the law of the LORD of hosts, and have despised the word of the Holy One of Israel."
- **"He stretched out his hand against them… and the mountains quaked" (v25).** God has already struck them. The earthquake may be the great quake in the days of Uzziah, remembered for centuries (Amos 1:1; Zech 14:5). Archaeologists have found signs of a major earthquake in the region around 760 BC.
- **"For all this his anger has not turned away, and his hand is stretched out still" (v25).** This refrain will return four times in 9:12, 17, 21 and 10:4. Many scholars think a block of material once ran together and has been arranged around Isaiah's account of his call (ch 6) and the Immanuel prophecies (chs 7–8). The outstretched hand that once rescued Israel from Egypt (Exod 6:6; Deut 26:8) is now raised against them.
- **"He will raise a signal for nations far away, and whistle for them" (v26).** A signal was a banner on a pole set on a hilltop to rally troops. "Whistle" is the hiss a beekeeper used to call bees, as 7:18 makes explicit. God does not need to plead with the mightiest army on earth. He whistles, and they come. Isaiah does not name Assyria yet, which makes the threat more chilling. The same banner image later turns to hope, when the root of Jesse stands "as a signal for the peoples" and the nations gather to him (11:10, 12).
- **"None is weary, none stumbles… not a sandal strap broken" (vv27–28).** The description fits Assyria's professional army, the most feared war machine of its day. Assyrian palace carvings show these troops in tight formation, archers with bows drawn and chariots with heavy wheels. Ancient horses were not shod, so "hoofs like flint" was high praise. Set this tireless army beside Judah's drunken "heroes" in v22.
- **"Their roaring is like a lion" (v29).** Assyrian kings boasted of themselves as lions and hunted lions for sport, as their palace carvings show. The vineyard without a hedge is now prey, and "none can rescue."
- **"Like the growling of the sea… behold, darkness and distress" (v30).** The chapter ends in darkness, with no light even in the sky. This sets up the next movement of the book. In chapter 6 Isaiah sees the LORD in blinding glory, and in 9:2, "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry lists the sins as covetousness "punished with famine," "rioting, revelling, and drunkenness," "presumption in sin, and defying the justice of God," and in v20 "confounding the distinctions between virtue and vice." JFB says the Jubilee law was given "as a guard against avarice," explains the measures (eight bushels of seed yielding "only three pecks"), and takes "Sheol" as the unseen world of the dead, not a place of torment. JFB is the source for the rabbinic saying on v18, and it links the earthquake of v25 to Uzziah's reign. Guzik notes the contrast between the alert, disciplined enemy army and Judah's drunken men, and closes with Spurgeon's line that God's woes are better than the devil's welcomes.

#### Pointing to Christ
God came to his vineyard looking for fruit and found rotten grapes. In Jesus' version of the story, the owner finally sends his son, and the tenants kill him. But in John 15 Jesus becomes the vineyard himself: "I am the true vine." He bore the fruit of justice and righteousness that Israel failed to produce, and those joined to him bear fruit through him. The hedge was torn down and the hand stretched out against the vineyard, but on the cross the hand of judgment fell on the true vine, crowned with thorns, the very "briers and thorns" (v6) of the ruined field. The banner God raised to summon an army against his people is raised again in Isaiah as a signal of welcome. Jesus said, "I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself" (John 12:32).
