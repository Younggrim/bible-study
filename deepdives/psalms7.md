*Setting:* The title reads "A Shiggaion of David, which he sang to the LORD concerning the words of Cush, a Benjaminite." *Shiggaion* appears only here and, in plural form, in Habakkuk 3:1. Its meaning is uncertain. Many connect it with a verb meaning "to wander" or "reel," and think of an intense, emotional song with shifting rhythm. Cush is otherwise unknown. Benjamin was Saul's tribe, and the most natural setting is the years when Saul hunted David and men around the king spread lies about him. David himself complained to Saul, "Why do you listen to the words of men who say, 'Behold, David seeks your harm'?" (1 Sam 24:9). Later, during Absalom's revolt, another Benjaminite, Shimei, cursed David as a man of blood (2 Sam 16:5–8). Psalms 3–6 were prayers under attack and in sickness. Psalm 7 is a prayer under slander. David is accused of a specific wrong, and he takes his case to the court of heaven. The psalm moves from a cry for rescue, to an oath of innocence, to an appeal for God to judge, to a picture of God as warrior-judge, and ends in praise.

#### Save Me From My Pursuers (vv1–2)
- **"O LORD my God, in you do I take refuge" (v1).** The psalm opens with trust before it asks for anything. "Take refuge" (*chasah*) is the word that closed Psalm 2 ("Blessed are all who take refuge in him," 2:12) and Psalm 5:11. It pictures running for shelter under a rock or into a stronghold. David has nowhere else to go, and that is exactly where he chooses to go.
- **"Save me from all my pursuers and deliver me" (v1).** "Pursuers" is a word for people who chase someone down to destroy them (31:15; 143:3). David spent years literally pursued through the wilderness of Judah. Saul compared the chase to hunting "a partridge in the mountains" (1 Sam 26:20).
- **"Lest like a lion they tear my soul apart" (v2).** The lion is a common picture in the Psalms for a violent enemy who drags off its prey (10:9; 17:12; 22:13, 21). David the shepherd had fought real lions (1 Sam 17:34–37). He knows what it means to be in their jaws, and he knows that the same God who rescued him "from the paw of the lion" is the one he calls on now. **"With none to deliver"** is the heart of the fear: if God does not act, no one will.
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Spurgeon's title for the psalm, "the song of the slandered saint," and his point that wounds from the tongue cut deeper and heal more slowly than wounds from a sword. Henry: "It is the glory of God to help the helpless." He sees David turning to God with the slander instead of turning to bitterness. JFB calls a Shiggaion "a plaintive song or elegy" and ties "the words of Cush" to David's protest in 1 Samuel 24:9. The lexicon is more cautious, calling the word "doubtful" and suggesting a "wild, passionate song," so neither meaning should be pressed.

#### If I Have Done This (vv3–5)
- **"O LORD my God, if I have done this" (v3).** "This" points to a specific charge. The psalm never says what it was, but the next verse gives hints: betraying a friend or robbing an enemy. In Saul's court the charge against David was treason, that he was plotting to seize the throne.
- **An oath of innocence.** Verses 3–5 follow a pattern used in Israel's courts and in Job 31: "If I have done X, then let Y happen to me." By calling a curse on himself, David puts his honesty on the line before God. It is not a claim to be sinless. It is a claim to be innocent *of this*. David elsewhere confesses his sins plainly (32:5; 51:3–4).
- **"If I have repaid my friend with evil or plundered my enemy without cause" (v4).** The first half is the betrayal of someone at peace with him. The second half is harder to read. The ESV's "plundered" is a rare verb (*chalats*) that elsewhere means "rescue" or "draw out." Some older translations render it "Yea, I have delivered him that without cause is mine enemy" (KJV), which would be a pointed reminder of the two times David spared Saul's life (1 Sam 24; 26). Most modern versions read it as "plundered," completing the list of wrongs David denies. Either reading fits David's story: he had not touched the Lord's anointed.
- **"Let the enemy pursue my soul and overtake it" (v5).** If he is guilty, let what he fears in v1–2 actually happen. **"Lay my glory in the dust"** means let his honor and life be brought down to the grave. "Glory" (*kavod*) can mean a person's dignity or even his very life, as in 16:9 and 57:8, where it stands parallel to "soul" or "heart."
- **"Selah" (v5).** A musical or liturgical marker of uncertain meaning, probably a pause. It falls right after the self-curse, letting the weight of it settle.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, drawing on Boice, says the real question is not whether David was perfect but whether he was innocent of this particular slander. He also passes on G. Campbell Morgan's reading of the charges: keeping spoil that belonged to the king, repaying good with evil and taking advantage of kindness. Henry points to David's sparing of Saul in the cave and the camp as proof of his conduct, and warns that calling a curse on oneself "has been thought as awful a form of swearing as any," never to be done lightly. JFB gives both readings of v4, "If I have injured my friend" and the alternative, and reads "mine honour" as "my personal and official dignity."

#### Arise, O LORD (vv6–9)
- **"Arise, O LORD, in your anger" (v6).** This is an old battle cry. When the ark set out in the wilderness, Moses said, "Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered" (Num 10:35). David prayed the same in 3:7. **"Awake for me"** is bold language, as if God were asleep. It is the language of urgent faith, not doubt that God is real (44:23). Psalm 121:4 makes clear that God never actually sleeps.
- **"Lift yourself up against the fury of my enemies" (v6).** The enemies are furious. David asks God to answer fury with justice. **"You have appointed a judgment"** reminds God that he has already committed himself to do right.
- **"Let the assembly of the peoples be gathered about you; over it return on high" (v7).** The scene widens from one slander to a world courtroom. The nations gather around the throne, and God takes his seat "on high" as judge. David's small case is set inside God's judgment of all peoples.
- **"The LORD judges the peoples" (v8).** This is the plain confession on which the whole psalm rests. In the Old Testament, judging is not only condemning. It is setting things right: clearing the innocent and stopping the wicked.
- **"Judge me, O LORD, according to my righteousness and according to the integrity that is in me" (v8).** This can sound proud to modern ears. But "righteousness" here is the legal standing of one in the right in a dispute, and "integrity" (*tom*) means wholeness or sincerity of heart, the word used of Job (Job 1:1, "blameless"). David is saying, "In this case I am in the right. Look and see." He is inviting God's examination, not boasting of a spotless life.
- **"Oh, let the evil of the wicked come to an end, and may you establish the righteous" (v9).** David's prayer moves beyond himself. He wants evil itself to stop, and the godly made firm. This is the same longing behind the Lord's Prayer: "your kingdom come, your will be done."
- **"You who test the minds and hearts" (v9).** Literally "hearts and kidneys." In Hebrew thought the heart was the seat of thought and will, and the kidneys stood for the deepest emotions and motives. The KJV's "reins" is the old English word for kidneys. God sees all of it, which is exactly why David is willing to be tested. Jeremiah uses the same pairing (Jer 11:20; 17:10), and the risen Christ takes it for himself: "I am he who searches mind and heart" (Rev 2:23).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that David's prayer is active, not passive: arise, lift up, awake. He warns that many dangerous fanatics have been sure God was on their side, so God's passions must not be confused with our opinions. He also notes Kidner's comment on the "great breadth of vision" in v9. Henry reads "Awake for me to judgment" as a call for God's promised justice, and sees in "return thou on high" a hint of Christ's exaltation and return. On v9 he says the prayer against the wicked is first a prayer that sin itself be destroyed. JFB explains "hearts and reins" as "affections and motives" and reads v6 as God appealed to "as if hitherto careless" of David, a figure of speech, not a doctrine.

#### God Is a Righteous Judge (vv10–13)
- **"My shield is with God, who saves the upright in heart" (v10).** "Shield" recalls 3:3, "you, O LORD, are a shield about me." "Upright in heart" (*yishre lev*) means people whose hearts are straight, not twisted. The psalm has moved from "me" to all who belong to God.
- **"God is a righteous judge" (v11).** The center of the psalm. God cannot be bribed or bent. Abraham asked, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Gen 18:25).
- **"And a God who feels indignation every day" (v11).** "Indignation" (*za'am*) is a strong word for anger that is voiced, even denounced. It is the word Balaam uses when he asks how he can "denounce" those God has not denounced (Num 23:7–8). God's anger at evil is not an occasional outburst. It is constant, because his goodness is constant. The KJV adds "with the wicked," which is not in the Hebrew but is implied by the context. The ancient Greek translation reads very differently: God is "strong and patient, not bringing on wrath every day." That likely comes from reading one small Hebrew word (*el*, "God") as the word for "not" (*al*). The standard Hebrew text, followed by the ESV, is the reading most scholars accept.
- **"If a man does not repent" (v12).** "Repent" is *shuv*, "turn back." This is the hinge of the warning. Judgment is ready but not yet released, and the door to turning is still open (Ezek 18:30–32; 2 Pet 3:9). The Hebrew is compact, and some read the subject of the next clause as the wicked man sharpening *his* sword against David. The ESV, with most translations, takes God as the one who whets his sword, which fits vv12–13 as a single picture.
- **"God will whet his sword; he has bent and readied his bow" (v12).** The language is of the Divine Warrior (Deut 32:41–42). The sword is being sharpened, and the bow already strung and drawn. The arrow has not yet been released.
- **"Making his arrows fiery shafts" (v13).** Burning arrows were used in ancient sieges to set walls and roofs alight. The image says judgment, when it falls, will be terrible.
- **Commentators:** All three stress the "if." Henry writes that while God waits "the sword is in the whetting and the bow in the drawing," and that the longer judgment is delayed, the heavier it falls on the unrepentant. He takes "angry with the wicked every day" at face value and says even the prosperity of the wicked can be a sign of God's displeasure. Guzik insists there are no small sins against a great God, and that God's delay is mercy, not indifference. He also notes Adam Clarke's preference for the "not angry every day" reading, which most do not follow. JFB prefers to read v13 as arrows "against the persecutors" rather than "for burning," arguing fire arrows suit the siege of a town more than a single enemy, though the lexicon and most modern translations support "fiery."

#### The Pit He Has Made (vv14–16)
- **"The wicked man conceives evil and is pregnant with mischief and gives birth to lies" (v14).** A three-step picture of pregnancy. Evil is conceived in the heart, grows unseen and is born as a lie. This fits the setting: a slander begins as a private grudge and comes out as a public falsehood. James uses the same image: "desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death" (Jas 1:15). Isaiah 59:4 also says the wicked "conceive mischief and give birth to iniquity."
- **"He makes a pit, digging it out, and falls into the hole that he has made" (v15).** Hunters dug pits and covered them to trap animals. The schemer works hard at his trap and then falls into it himself. The Bible is full of this pattern: Haman hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai (Esth 7:10), and Daniel's accusers thrown to the lions (Dan 6:24). Proverbs says it outright: "Whoever digs a pit will fall into it" (Prov 26:27; also Ps 9:15; 57:6).
- **"His mischief returns upon his own head, and on his own skull his violence descends" (v16).** The picture may be of a stone thrown straight up that falls back on the thrower. God's justice here is not always a lightning bolt. Often it is built into the way the world works: evil carries the seed of its own ruin.
- **Commentators:** Henry likens the sinner to a woman in labor who "conceives mischief" and finally brings forth only falsehood, and he notes how often wicked schemes recoil on those who planned them. Guzik gives Haman and Daniel's enemies as examples, and passes on Morgan's line that the pit a man digs becomes his own grave. JFB: "the devices of the wicked end in disappointment," defeating the very hopes that drove them.

#### Thanks Due to His Righteousness (v17)
- **"I will give to the LORD the thanks due to his righteousness" (v17).** The psalm began with "my righteousness" (v8). It ends with "his righteousness." David's case was only ever safe because the Judge is just. Thanks is "due," owed, not optional.
- **"I will sing praise to the name of the LORD, the Most High" (v17).** "Most High" (*Elyon*) is one of the oldest names for God, used by Melchizedek when he blessed Abraham: "God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth" (Gen 14:19). It names God as the one above every court, king and accuser. This is its first use in the Psalms, and it leads straight into Psalm 8's praise of the LORD's majestic name and Psalm 9's "I will sing praise to your name, O Most High" (9:2).
- **Praise before the verdict.** Nothing in the psalm says the slander has stopped. David praises because he has handed the case to God. Psalms 3–7 each move from lament toward trust or praise, and the Psalter as a whole moves the same way.
- **Commentators:** Henry says believers should "praise the Lord according to his righteousness," for his protection of the wronged and his justice on their oppressors. JFB links "his righteousness" to 5:8 and says it is seen when God defends his servant and punishes the wicked. Guzik draws the contrast between David's relative righteousness in this dispute and God's perfect righteousness, which is the only real ground for praise.

#### Pointing to Christ
Henry saw David here as a picture of Christ, wronged and slandered but "certainly righted at last." Jesus was falsely accused of a specific crime, of claiming a throne against Caesar, and he did not answer with revenge but "continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). Unlike David, Jesus could pray "judge me according to my righteousness" with no qualification at all, because he had no sin. And yet he did not escape the sword of v12. He took it, so that those who "turn back" find that the Judge's arrows have already been spent on him. The same Jesus is the one to whom the Father has given all judgment (John 5:22). He searches minds and hearts (Rev 2:23), and one day he will set right every slander told against his people.
