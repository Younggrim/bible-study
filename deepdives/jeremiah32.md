*Setting:* The tenth year of King Zedekiah, 588–587 BC, roughly a year before Jerusalem fell. The Babylonian army had surrounded the city since early 588 (39:1; 2 Kgs 25:1). For a short time the siege lifted when an Egyptian army marched up, and during that break Jeremiah tried to leave the city "to receive his portion" of family land in Benjamin. He was arrested as a deserter, beaten and thrown in a dungeon, and later moved to the "court of the guard" (37:11–21). That is where this chapter finds him: a prisoner in the palace compound, with the siege back on, famine in the city, and the end in sight. In that moment God tells him to buy a field. The chapter is part of the "Book of Consolation" (chapters 30–33), and it turns the promises of chapters 30 and 31 into a single, concrete act of faith. It closes with the longest prayer of Jeremiah in the book and God's answer to it.

#### Jeremiah Buys a Field During the Siege (vv1–15)
- **"In the tenth year of Zedekiah king of Judah, which was the eighteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar" (v1).** Dating by both kings ties Judah's history to Babylon's calendar. Judah no longer controlled its own story.
- **"Shut up in the court of the guard that was in the palace of the king of Judah" (v2).** Not a dungeon but an open courtyard under guard, inside the palace complex. Jeremiah could receive visitors and do business there, and people "sitting in the court" could watch (v12). It was also where Zedekiah could keep him close and ask him for a word from God in secret (37:17, 21; 38:14–16).
- **"Why do you prophesy…?" (vv3–5).** Zedekiah's complaint sums up Jeremiah's message: the city will fall, the king will not escape, and he **"shall speak with him face to face and see him eye to eye"** (v4). That came true in the worst way. Zedekiah was caught fleeing, brought before Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah, made to watch his sons executed, and then blinded (39:5–7). The last thing his eyes saw was Babylon's king. **"Until I visit him" (v5).** *Paqad*, God's attention, can mean judgment or care. The phrase leaves Zedekiah's end in God's hands.
- **"Behold, Hanamel the son of Shallum your uncle will come to you" (v7).** God warned Jeremiah before the visit, so that when Hanamel arrived Jeremiah would know the request came from God (v8). **"Buy my field that is at Anathoth."** Anathoth, Jeremiah's hometown, was a village of priests about three miles northeast of Jerusalem (1:1). In 588 it lay behind enemy lines. Its own people had once plotted to kill Jeremiah (11:21–23).
- **"For the right of redemption by purchase is yours" (v7).** The Law said land in Israel belonged ultimately to God and must stay in the family that received it (Lev 25:23). If a man fell into debt and had to sell, his nearest relative, the *go'el* or redeemer, had the right and duty to buy it so it would not pass out of the clan (Lev 25:25). Boaz did this for Naomi's land (Ruth 4:3–6). Hanamel was apparently in need, and Jeremiah was the relative next in line. The word "redemption," *ge'ullah*, is the same family of words used of God redeeming Israel (31:11).
- **"Then I knew that this was the word of the LORD" (v8).** An honest line. Jeremiah heard God first, then saw it happen, and then was sure. Faith here is not a leap into the dark but a response to God's word confirmed in events.
- **"I bought the field… and weighed out the money to him, seventeen shekels of silver" (v9).** Coins were not yet in common use in Judah, so silver was weighed on scales. A shekel was about 11.5 grams (0.4 oz), so seventeen shekels came to around 200 grams of silver (about 7 oz). It is hard to know if this was a fair price, a low wartime price, or a price for a small plot. What matters is that Jeremiah paid real money for land the enemy already held.
- **"I signed the deed, sealed it, got witnesses, and weighed the money on scales" (v10).** Every legal step is recorded. This was not a symbolic gesture done in a corner. It was a binding public contract.
- **"The sealed deed of purchase, containing the terms and conditions and the open copy" (v11).** Ancient contracts were often made in two copies on one sheet of papyrus. One copy was rolled up, tied and sealed with clay so it could not be altered. The other was left open for reference. If a dispute arose, the sealed copy could be broken open before judges. Double documents like this have been found among Jewish papyri from Elephantine in Egypt (5th century BC) and elsewhere.
- **"I gave the deed of purchase to Baruch the son of Neriah son of Mahseiah" (v12).** This is Baruch's first appearance in the book, though chapters are not in time order. He was Jeremiah's secretary and loyal friend (36:4; 45:1). His brother Seraiah was a royal official (51:59). A clay seal impression reading "Berekyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe" appeared on the antiquities market in the 1970s and was widely linked to him. Its authenticity has since been questioned, since it was not found in a controlled dig, so scholars treat it with caution.
- **"In the presence of all the Judeans who were sitting in the court of the guard" (v12).** The purchase had an audience. Everyone watching knew the prophet who said "the city will fall" had just invested in land beyond the walls.
- **"Put them in an earthenware vessel, that they may last for a long time" (v14).** Clay jars kept documents dry and safe. The Dead Sea Scrolls survived for two thousand years in jars like this near Qumran. "A long time" was a hint: the deed would not be needed soon. Jeremiah had said the exile would last seventy years (25:11; 29:10). He bought land he would never farm.
- **"Houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (v15).** The meaning of the sign. The land would not be empty forever. Normal life, buying and selling, planting and harvesting, would return. The prophet who had spent forty years announcing ruin put his own money on God's promise of renewal.
- **Matthew and the potter's field.** Matthew 27:9–10 says the purchase of the potter's field with Judas's thirty pieces of silver fulfilled "what had been spoken by Jeremiah the prophet." The words Matthew quotes are mostly from Zechariah 11:12–13, but many scholars think Matthew names Jeremiah because he is also drawing on Jeremiah's field purchase here and the potter of chapters 18–19. Both stories involve a field bought with silver in a time of judgment.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws a practical lesson: "It is good to manage even our worldly affairs in faith," and adds that buying land for the future "may be a kindness to those that come after us." JFB explains the law behind v7: "On the failure of the owner, the next of kin had the right of redeeming it," citing Leviticus 25 and Ruth 4. On v14 he says the jar kept the deeds "from the moisture of the surrounding earth." Guzik stresses how unreasonable the purchase looked by any business standard, buying land already held by the enemy. He wonders whether Hanamel was daring his cousin to put his money where his prophecies were. He compares the sealed jar to a time capsule. Henry and Guzik both note that the Law of Redemption was still being kept, even in the last days of the kingdom.

#### Jeremiah Prays for Understanding (vv16–25)
- **"After I had given the deed of purchase to Baruch… I prayed to the LORD" (v16).** Jeremiah obeyed first and prayed afterward. Earlier prayers in the book were full of anguish and complaint (12:1–4; 15:15–18; 20:7–18). This one begins in worship and ends in honest puzzlement.
- **"Ah, Lord GOD! It is you who have made the heavens and the earth by your great power and by your outstretched arm!" (v17).** "Ah" is an exclamation Jeremiah used in his first reply to God's call (1:6). The prayer starts with creation. A God who made everything can certainly keep a promise about one small field. "Outstretched arm" is the language of the exodus (Deut 4:34; 26:8).
- **"Nothing is too hard for you" (v17).** "Too hard" is *pala'*, to be extraordinary or beyond reach. It is the word of Genesis 18:14, when Sarah laughed at the promise of a son: "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" The angel says almost the same to Mary: "Nothing will be impossible with God" (Luke 1:37). God will throw the phrase back to Jeremiah in v27.
- **"You show steadfast love to thousands, but you repay the guilt of fathers to their children after them" (v18).** A quotation of God's own self-description at Sinai (Exod 20:5–6; 34:6–7). Both halves are true. God's love is lavish and his justice is real. This sits alongside 31:29–30: the proverb about sour grapes was false as an excuse, but the consequences of a nation's long sin did fall on the generation that kept on in it.
- **"Great in counsel and mighty in deed, whose eyes are open to all the ways of the children of man" (v19).** God is wise in planning and able in doing, and he sees everything. **"Rewarding each one according to his ways"** (17:10).
- **"You have shown signs and wonders in the land of Egypt… and have made a name for yourself" (v20).** Jeremiah reviews history: the exodus (v21), the gift of a land "flowing with milk and honey" (v22), and then the failure: **"They did not obey your voice or walk in your law. They did nothing of all you commanded them to do" (v23).** Jeremiah, who had argued with God about the coming judgment, now says it is just.
- **"Behold, the siege mounds have come up to the city to take it" (v24).** Babylonian armies built earthen ramps up against a city's walls so that battering rams and towers could reach them. Assyrian reliefs from the siege of Lachish show the same method. **"Because of sword and famine and pestilence."** Jeremiah's threefold warning (14:12; 21:7) has arrived. **"What you spoke has come to pass, and behold, you see it."**
- **"Yet you, O Lord GOD, have said to me, 'Buy the field for money and get witnesses'" (v25).** This is the question underneath the whole prayer. It does not accuse God. It lays the puzzle before him. God has said the city will fall, and God has told him to buy land. Jeremiah believes both and cannot see how they fit. Prayer is where he takes that tension.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes simply, "Prayer is a salve for every sore," and notes that Jeremiah obeyed before he understood. JFB observes that "God's reply exactly accords with Jeremiah's prayer," since v27 repeats v17 as a question, and links the phrase to Genesis 18:14 and Luke 1:37. On v24 he explains the "mounts" as earth ramps raised by the besiegers to bring engines up to the walls. Guzik draws on Morgan to make the point that the sign was God's command, not Jeremiah's feelings about it. He also notes that Jeremiah was honest with God about his confusion, and that the prayer itself steadied his faith.

#### They Shall Be My People; I Will Be Their God (vv26–44)
- **"Behold, I am the LORD, the God of all flesh. Is anything too hard for me?" (v27).** God answers Jeremiah's confession with the same words turned into a question. "All flesh" means all humanity. The God of Babylon's army and of Judah's exiles is one God, and he holds both judgment and restoration in his hand.
- **"Behold, I am giving this city into the hands of the Chaldeans… and he shall capture it" (v28).** First God confirms the bad news. There will be no last-minute rescue. **"Set this city on fire and burn it, with the houses on whose roofs offerings have been made to Baal" (v29).** Flat rooftops were used as living space, and also for worship of the "host of heaven" and of Baal (19:13; Zeph 1:5). The fire would fall on the very places of idolatry. In the summer of 587 or 586 BC the Babylonian commander Nebuzaradan burned the temple, the palace and "every great house" (52:12–13).
- **"Have done nothing but evil in my sight from their youth" (v30).** "From their youth" means from the nation's beginning (2:2–8; 3:24–25). **"From the day it was built to this day" (v31).** Even Jerusalem, the city God chose, had a long record of provoking him, from Solomon's shrines to foreign gods (1 Kgs 11:7–8) to Manasseh's altars in the temple (2 Kgs 21:4–7).
- **"Their kings and their officials, their priests and their prophets, the men of Judah and the inhabitants of Jerusalem" (v32).** Every level of society is named. No class could blame another.
- **"They have turned to me their back and not their face" (v33).** A vivid picture of contempt, repeated from 2:27 and 7:24. **"Though I have taught them persistently."** Literally "rising early and teaching," a phrase Jeremiah uses again and again (7:13, 25; 25:3–4). God was the diligent teacher. They were the inattentive students.
- **"They set up their abominations in the house that is called by my name" (v34).** Idols in God's own temple (7:30; Ezek 8:3–16). **"They built the high places of Baal in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom, to offer up their sons and daughters to Molech" (v35).** The worst of all. "Molech" may be the name of a god or, as some scholars argue, a term for a type of sacrifice. Either way it meant burning children (7:31; 19:5; 2 Kgs 23:10). Kings Ahaz and Manasseh had done it with their own sons (2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6). **"Nor did it enter into my mind."** God speaks in human terms to say how far this was from anything he would ever want. It was not a twisted version of true worship. It was the opposite of it (Deut 12:31).
- **"Now therefore thus says the LORD… concerning this city of which you say, 'It is given into the hand of the king of Babylon'" (v36).** The "therefore" is surprising. After vv28–35 a reader expects "therefore I will destroy it." Instead God turns to promise. The people were saying the city was finished. God agrees it will fall, and then says what he will do afterward.
- **"Behold, I will gather them from all the countries to which I drove them… and I will make them dwell in safety" (v37).** The scattering is already spoken of as done. Prophets often describe future events in the past tense to stress their certainty.
- **"And they shall be my people, and I will be their God" (v38).** The covenant formula, as in 30:22 and 31:33, which gives the section its heading.
- **"I will give them one heart and one way, that they may fear me forever" (v39).** Instead of a divided people with divided loyalties, God will give an undivided heart (Ezek 11:19; Ps 86:11). "Fear" here is reverent awe and trust, not terror. **"For their own good and the good of their children after them."** The opposite of the sour grapes of 31:29. Faithfulness will now be the family inheritance.
- **"I will make with them an everlasting covenant, that I will not turn away from doing good to them" (v40).** This is another name for the new covenant of 31:31–34. "Everlasting" (*olam*) means it will never be broken. Both sides of the bond are held by God: **"I will not turn away from doing good to them. And I will put the fear of me in their hearts, that they may not turn from me."** God promises his own faithfulness, and he promises to keep his people faithful too. This is one reason many Christians speak of God preserving his people to the end (John 10:28–29; Phil 1:6).
- **"I will rejoice in doing them good, and I will plant them in this land in faithfulness, with all my heart and all my soul" (v41).** Israel was told to love God "with all your heart and with all your soul" (Deut 6:5). Here, uniquely in Scripture, God uses that phrase of himself. He will do his people good with his whole being. "Plant" again echoes Jeremiah's call (1:10). The God who was uprooting will plant, and he will be glad to do it (Zeph 3:17).
- **"Just as I have brought all this great disaster… so I will bring upon them all the good that I promise them" (v42).** The fulfilled warnings are the proof that the promises will be fulfilled too. The fire Jeremiah could see from the guard court was evidence that God keeps his word.
- **"Fields shall be bought in this land of which you are saying, 'It is a desolation, without man or beast'" (v43).** God answers Jeremiah's question of v25 directly. **"Deeds shall be signed and sealed and witnessed" (v44).** The exact steps Jeremiah had just taken (v10). His purchase was a preview. **"In the land of Benjamin, in the places about Jerusalem, and in the cities of Judah… the hill country, the Shephelah, and… the Negeb."** Every region of the old kingdom is named, from Benjamin in the north (Anathoth's region, named first) to the low hills of the Shephelah to the west and the dry Negeb in the south (compare 17:26). **"For I will restore their fortunes."** The refrain of the Book of Consolation (30:3).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes on v35 that God's words rule out any "plea of a good intention" in worship he has not commanded. On v40 he writes, "Both the conversion and perseverance of the saints are the work of God alone," and says the promise was "never yet fully realized" in Israel's history, pointing to a fuller fulfillment. Henry says the covenant is "everlasting" because God will always be faithful to it, and adds that "teachers may put good things into our heads, but it is God only that can put them into our hearts." Guzik, like JFB, says these promises reach beyond the return under Ezra and Nehemiah to the new covenant. He also stresses, with Spurgeon, the wholehearted joy God takes in doing his people good, and notes that the passage does not explain how such a change will come, though the New Testament shows it comes through the Messiah's atoning work.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah, a prisoner, bought a field already in the enemy's hands, because God promised a future he would not live to see. Jesus did something greater. As the true Kinsman-Redeemer, he bought back what had been lost, not with seventeen shekels of silver but "with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). He paid for a world under the enemy's power, knowing the full harvest lay far ahead, and the Spirit is now the "guarantee of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it" (Eph 1:14). The everlasting covenant God promised here, in which he will never stop doing good to his people and will keep them from turning away, was sealed in Christ's blood. The God who asked "Is anything too hard for me?" raised his Son from the dead.
