*Setting:* The three days of fasting are over (4:16). Esther has decided to go to the king uninvited, at the risk of her life, to plead for her people, who are under Haman's decree of death set for the thirteenth of Adar, eleven months away (3:13). The chapter has two scenes that mirror each other. In the first, Esther approaches the throne in fear and leaves with the king's favor. In the second, Haman leaves her banquet on top of the world and goes home in a rage over one man who will not stand up. By evening he has built a stake to kill Mordecai on, and the reader goes to bed with him that night not knowing what the morning will bring.

#### Esther Prepares a Banquet (vv1–8)
- **"On the third day" (v1).** The fast began on the day of Mordecai's message, so "the third day" fits the Hebrew way of counting parts of days as days. Matthew Henry notes that the same counting is used of Christ's three days in the tomb. Many readers notice the "third day" pattern in Scripture: Abraham sees Moriah on the third day (Gen 22:4), Israel meets God at Sinai on the third day (Exod 19:11, 16), and Hosea speaks of being raised up on the third day (Hos 6:2). The text of Esther does not make the link, but it is a day of deliverance.
- **"Esther put on her royal robes" (v1).** The Hebrew is literally "Esther clothed herself with royalty" (*malkut*). She takes off sackcloth and puts on queenship. Whatever she feels inside, she comes as the queen, not as a beggar.
- **"Stood in the inner court… opposite the entrance" (v1).** The throne faced the doorway, so the king would see anyone who appeared. Reliefs at Persepolis show the Persian king on a high-backed throne with his feet on a footstool, a long scepter in his right hand, and attendants behind him. One such relief, now in the Tehran museum, shows an official bowing before the enthroned king.
- **"She won favor in his sight, and he held out… the golden scepter" (v2).** The phrase "won favor" repeats what was said when Esther first met Hegai and the king (2:9, 15, 17). Her life now depends on it. The scepter was a long golden rod, usually held upright with one end on the ground. **"Esther approached and touched the tip of the scepter"**, accepting the king's pardon and protection. Proverbs says "the king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will" (Prov 21:1). The narrator never says so, but the scene shows it.
- **"Even to the half of my kingdom" (v3).** This was a royal figure of speech for generosity, not a legal offer. Herod made the same rash promise to his stepdaughter (Mark 6:23), with a very different result. The king can see Esther has not risked her life for nothing.
- **"Let the king and Haman come today to a feast" (v4).** Esther does not answer the question. She invites him to dinner, and she invites Haman too. Readers have offered several reasons. She is choosing the right setting, private and relaxed, rather than the public throne room. She wants Haman present when she accuses him so he cannot twist the story afterward. She may also be playing on the king's jealousy or curiosity, since a queen inviting another man to her table would get his attention. The Talmud lists a dozen rabbinic guesses, among them that she wanted the king to grow suspicious of Haman.
- **"Bring Haman quickly" (v5).** The king, as always, says yes at once.
- **"As they were drinking wine after the feast" (v6).** Literally "at the banquet of wine," the part of the evening after the meal when the wine was served. JFB notes that Persian kings often ate alone and called guests to join them for the wine. The king repeats his offer, word for word. He knows there is a real request behind this.
- **"My wish and my request is…" (vv7–8).** Esther starts to speak, and then stops and asks for one more banquet tomorrow. Why the delay? The text does not say. Some readers think she lost her nerve. Others see good judgment, since a second invitation would make the king more eager to grant whatever it was, and a request made "tomorrow" was framed as obedience ("I will do as the king has said"). What matters most in the story is the result. The delay leaves one night between the banquets, and on that night everything changes (ch 6).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry finds in this scene a picture of prayer. A haughty king says, "What is thy petition?", so how much more will God hear his people? He sets out the contrast: "Esther came to a proud imperious man; we come to the God of love and grace. She was not called; we are… She had a law against her; we have a promise… She had no friend to introduce her… but we have an advocate with the Father." JFB, using Persian reliefs, describes the scepter as "a simple wand" of gold held upright, and touching its tip as the usual sign of reverence and submission. Guzik praises Esther's tact in not blurting out her request, and quotes Spurgeon's suggestion that her hesitation, whatever its cause, was God's timing. Henry also mentions the longer Greek version, where Esther faints and the king leaps down to comfort her. That is a later addition, not part of the Hebrew text.

#### Haman Plans to Hang Mordecai (vv9–14)
- **"Haman went out that day joyful and glad of heart" (v9).** The phrase "glad of heart" is the same used of the king when he was drunk at the feast (1:10, "merry"). Haman's high spirits last as far as the gate.
- **"He neither rose nor trembled before him" (v9).** Mordecai, now out of sackcloth, is back at his post. He will not even stand up. "Trembled" (*zua'*) means to shake or move in fear. Mordecai is under a death sentence and shows no fear of the man who wrote it.
- **"Haman restrained himself" (v10).** He controls his rage for now, perhaps because the decree is already in place and he can afford to wait. In the story it is also God's restraint: one hasty act by Haman that day could have changed everything.
- **"His friends and his wife Zeresh" (v10).** Haman needs an audience. He sends for them so he can boast.
- **"The splendor of his riches, the number of his sons" (v11).** Haman had ten sons (9:7–10). Herodotus says Persians counted many sons as the greatest mark of manliness after courage in battle, and that the king sent gifts each year to the man with the most. Haman lists everything he has: wealth, sons, promotions, rank. He is telling his family what they already know.
- **"Even Queen Esther let no one but me come with the king" (v12).** The reader knows what Haman does not. The invitation he is so proud of is a trap. Proverbs: "Pride goes before destruction" (Prov 16:18).
- **"Yet all this is worth nothing to me, so long as I see Mordecai the Jew" (v13).** This is one of the clearest pictures in Scripture of what pride does. Haman has everything, and one man's refusal to honor him makes it all worthless. Notice he calls him "Mordecai the Jew." It is personal and it is ethnic at the same time.
- **"Let a gallows fifty cubits high be made" (v14).** About 75 feet (23 meters). The word is again *'ets*, "tree" or "wood," and the method was almost certainly impalement on a tall stake, displaying the body (see 2:23). The height was meant to make Mordecai a public spectacle across the city. Some think the stake stood on a wall or high point, or that the number is exaggerated for effect, but the point is plain: Haman wants the whole city to see.
- **"In the morning tell the king… Then go joyfully with the king to the feast" (v14).** Zeresh and his friends offer the easy solution: kill him first thing, then enjoy dinner. They assume the king will say yes to anything Haman asks, which up to now has been true. **"This idea pleased Haman."** The same phrase was used of Memucan's advice (1:21) and the plan to find a new queen (2:4). Each time a plan "pleases," it sets something in motion that will turn around on the one who made it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Henry both see Haman as a picture of the emptiness of worldly success. Guzik says his problem was not Mordecai but the emptiness in his own heart, which no success could fill, and cites Adam Clarke's line that the soul was made for God and only God can fill it. Henry says Mordecai's slight was "the dead fly which spoiled all his pot of precious ointment" (Eccl 10:1), and that Esther, in inviting him, "did but call him to the bar." He also credits Mordecai: "Those that walk in holy sincerity may walk in holy security." JFB says little on these verses and skips most of them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Esther goes into the king's presence not knowing whether she will live, and touches the scepter he holds out. Henry rightly turns it around: believers approach a King who has already called them and who has an advocate at his right hand (1 John 2:1). Haman, meanwhile, shows the opposite way. Full of honor, he cannot rest while one man refuses to bow, and he builds a stake to put him on. The world's hatred of the righteous runs through Scripture until it reaches Christ, the one man who never bowed to evil, who was lifted up on wood for all to see. But just as Haman's stake will be turned on its maker, the cross turned out to be the defeat of the powers that put Christ there (Col 2:15).
