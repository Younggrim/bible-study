*Setting:* Moses is still preaching on the plains of Moab in the last weeks of his life. Chapter 1 retold Israel's refusal to enter Canaan from Kadesh-barnea, the oath that the unbelieving generation would die in the wilderness, and the failed attack that followed. Chapter 2 picks up the story after those lost years. It covers the journey north along the east side of the Dead Sea, past three nations Israel was told *not* to fight, and then the first great victory of the conquest: the defeat of Sihon, king of the Amorites. The same God who had held Israel back from Edom, Moab and Ammon now says "begin to take possession." This chapter parallels Numbers 20:14–21:32.

#### The Wilderness Years (vv1–25)
- **"For many days we traveled around Mount Seir" (v1).** Thirty-eight years summed up in one sentence. **Mount Seir** is the rugged highland east of the Arabah and south of the Dead Sea, the homeland of Edom. The name "Edom" means "red," perhaps for the reddish sandstone of the region, the rock in which Petra was later carved.
- **"You have been traveling around this mountain country long enough. Turn northward" (v3).** This echoes 1:6, "You have stayed long enough at this mountain." God ends both waiting periods with the same kind of word. The wandering was discipline, but it had a set end.
- **"Your brothers, the people of Esau" (v4).** Edom descended from Esau, Jacob's twin. Despite all the bad blood between the brothers (Gen 27), Israel must treat Edom as family. **"They will be afraid of you. So be very careful."** Israel now has the upper hand, and that is when restraint matters most.
- **"Not so much as for the sole of the foot to tread on, because I have given Mount Seir to Esau" (v5).** God has given land to other nations too, not just Israel. Esau had his own inheritance (Gen 36:8). Paul says the same about all nations: God "determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place" (Acts 17:26; Deut 32:8).
- **"You shall purchase food… and… buy water from them with money" (v6).** Israel is to pay its way, not plunder. Numbers 20:14–21 records Edom refusing Israel passage through its land, and Israel turning away. Deuteronomy adds that Israel bought supplies from the "sons of Esau" (v29). Most interpreters think Israel skirted Edom's heartland and traded with Edomite groups along the border, which fits both accounts.
- **"These forty years the LORD your God has been with you. You have lacked nothing" (v7).** Even during the years of judgment, God provided. Their clothes did not wear out and their feet did not swell (8:4). Israel had money to pay for water because God had blessed "the work of your hands." Discipline did not mean abandonment.
- **"Away from the Arabah road from Elath and Ezion-geber" (v8).** These twin ports sat at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba. Israel turned off the main trade road and went around Edom to the east, through the desert edge.
- **"Do not harass Moab… because I have given Ar to the people of Lot" (v9).** Moab and Ammon descended from Lot, Abraham's nephew (Gen 19:36–38). They too are relatives, and God has given them land. **Ar** was a city or district of Moab, probably near the Arnon.
- **The Emim, Anakim and Rephaim (vv10–11).** Moses adds a note about the land's earlier inhabitants. The **Emim**, a name that probably means "terrors," were "great and many, and tall as the Anakim." **Rephaim** was the general name for these ancient tall peoples. The same Hebrew word is used elsewhere for the shadowy dead (Isa 14:9; 26:14), and scholars debate whether the two senses are related, perhaps because these peoples had long since vanished. Og king of Bashan was "left of the remnant of the Rephaim" (3:11). These were the kind of people who had terrified the spies (Num 13:33).
- **"The Horites… the people of Esau dispossessed them… as Israel did" (v12).** The **Horites** are often identified by scholars with the Hurrians, a people known from many ancient texts, though some doubt the link. "As Israel did" most naturally refers to Israel's recent conquest of Sihon and Og east of the Jordan. Some think these notes in parentheses were added by a later editor. Either way, the point is clear: other nations had defeated giants with God's help. If Edom and Moab could do it, Israel had no excuse for fear.
- **"Rise up and go over the brook Zered" (v13).** The **Zered** is a deep ravine (Wadi el-Hasa) running into the south end of the Dead Sea, the border between Edom and Moab. Crossing it is a turning point.
- **"Thirty-eight years, until the entire generation… had perished" (v14).** The exact count of the wasted years. **"The hand of the LORD was against them, to destroy them" (v15).** The deaths in the wilderness were not just old age. They were the outworking of God's oath (Num 14:29–35; Ps 95:10–11). Psalm 90, titled "A Prayer of Moses," may come from this period: "we are brought to an end by your anger" (Ps 90:7).
- **"As soon as all the men of war had perished… the LORD said to me" (vv16–17).** Only now does God speak again about moving forward. The Hebrew order is striking: the death of the old generation, then the word of the LORD.
- **"Do not harass them… I have given it to the sons of Lot" (v19).** Ammon lived on the desert edge to the east, around its capital Rabbah, today Amman in Jordan.
- **The Zamzummim (vv20–21).** The Ammonites called the Rephaim there **Zamzummim**, probably the same as the Zuzim of Genesis 14:5. The name may imitate unintelligible speech, "the murmurers," but that is uncertain. Again: "the LORD destroyed them before the Ammonites." God gave giants into the hands of Lot's descendants.
- **The Avvim and the Caphtorim (v23).** The **Caphtorim** came from **Caphtor**, most likely the island of Crete, and are linked with the Philistines (Amos 9:7; Jer 47:4). They displaced the **Avvim**, who lived in villages near Gaza. God moves peoples around the map, and Israel is not his only concern.
- **"Go over the Valley of the Arnon… I have given into your hand Sihon the Amorite" (v24).** The **Arnon** is a deep canyon (Wadi Mujib) cutting into the Dead Sea's eastern shore, Moab's northern border. North of it lay Sihon's kingdom. Now the instruction changes completely: "Begin to take possession, and contend with him in battle." Unlike Edom, Moab and Ammon, Sihon's land was part of what God was giving Israel.
- **"I will begin to put the dread and fear of you on the peoples" (v25).** The fear Israel felt at Kadesh will now be felt by their enemies. Rahab later tells the spies exactly this: "we heard what you did to the two kings of the Amorites… our hearts melted" (Josh 2:9–11).
- **Commentators:** Henry tells Israel, in effect, "Meddle not with them," but rather "buy meat and water of them, and pay for what they bought," since "hitherto thou hast lacked nothing." JFB says Edom did sell supplies to Israel, calls the Emim "a race, terrible, as their name imports," and the Zamzummim "a bullying, presumptuous band of giants." JFB places Caphtor in Lower Egypt; most scholars today prefer Crete, as the Tyndale notes do. Guzik sees Israel's restraint toward weaker relatives as a test of character, and says *Rephaim* is often rendered "giants" though its sense is something like "fearsome ones." He notes the word is also used for the dead. The lexicon gives Rephaim simply as the name of an ancient race of giants, with the link to the "shades" of the dead uncertain. Guzik also notes that the name Anakim appears in Egyptian execration texts, curse lists from about 2000–1800 BC, which is widely accepted. JFB has no separate notes on vv9–12, 14–15 or 20–23.

#### The Defeat of King Sihon (vv26–37)
- **"I sent messengers… to Sihon… with words of peace" (v26).** Even with God's promise of victory in hand (v24), Moses first offers peace. This matches the later law of war: "When you draw near to a city to fight against it, offer terms of peace to it" (20:10). **Kedemoth** means "eastern places," a wilderness area north of the Arnon.
- **"I will go only by the road; I will turn aside neither to the right nor to the left" (v27).** The offer is generous: stay on the main highway (the King's Highway, Num 21:22), pay for everything, harm nothing.
- **"As the sons of Esau… and the Moabites… did for me" (v29).** Others let Israel pass. Sihon had every reason to do the same.
- **"The LORD your God hardened his spirit and made his heart obstinate" (v30).** This is the same language used of Pharaoh (Exod 4:21; 7:3). Scripture says both that people harden their own hearts and that God hardens them. The Bible holds both together without fully explaining how. Sihon was already an aggressor. He had seized this land from Moab by force (Num 21:26). God did not make a good man evil. He confirmed Sihon in the pride he had chosen, and used it to bring judgment and to give Israel the land.
- **"I have begun to give Sihon and his land over to you. Begin to take possession" (v31).** "Begin" appears again (vv24, 25, 31). Sihon is the first step of the conquest, the first fruits of what God will do in Canaan.
- **"Then Sihon came out against us… to battle at Jahaz" (v32).** Sihon attacked first. **Jahaz** was probably a fortress north of the Arnon. Numbers 21:23 records the same battle.
- **"We defeated him and his sons and all his people" (v33).** The kingdom that the spies' generation would have feared falls in a single campaign.
- **"Devoted to destruction every city, men, women, and children" (v34).** The Hebrew word is *charam*, to devote completely to God, often by destruction (see 7:1–5; 20:16–18). This is one of the hardest parts of the conquest accounts. A few things can be said honestly. It was a specific command for a specific moment, God's judgment on peoples whose sin had reached its full measure (Gen 15:16), not a general license for violence. Israel did not do this to Edom, Moab or Ammon, or keep the spoil from it for itself beyond the livestock. Scripture itself treats such commands as unique to that time, and Christians read them in light of the cross, where God bore judgment himself. Believers still feel their weight and should.
- **"There was not a city too high for us" (v36).** Literally "too high," lofty or strong. Forty years earlier the spies said the cities were "fortified up to heaven" (1:28). Now, with God fighting for them, no wall is too high. Moses wants the new generation to see what the old one missed. **Aroer** stood on the north rim of the Arnon, and the conquered land stretched "as far as Gilead."
- **"Only to the land of the sons of Ammon you did not draw near" (v37).** Israel obeyed the limits as carefully as the commands. They took what God gave and left what he withheld. The **Jabbok** River, where Jacob had wrestled with God (Gen 32:22–32), marked the edge of Ammon's land.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Moses "sends to Sihon a message of peace, and only begs a passage through his land." On the hardening, Henry says God made his heart obstinate and "hidden from his eyes the thing that belonged to his peace," echoing Jesus' words over Jerusalem (Luke 19:42). On the destruction he warns that "it ought not to be drawn into a precedent for military executions." Guzik explains the hardening as God letting Sihon's heart pursue the malice already in it, rather than turning a good heart evil, and he draws the contrast with Kadesh: when Israel moved in faith, no city was too strong. He notes the Hebrew phrase is literally "too high." JFB simply states that the Amorites "were utterly exterminated," and does not comment on God hardening Sihon's spirit.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter shows God's patience and his justice side by side. He waited thirty-eight years for a generation to pass, provided for them every day of it, and taught Israel to treat even estranged relatives with honesty and restraint. Then, when the time came, he gave the first victory, and the walls that once looked "high as heaven" fell. The New Testament speaks the same way about God's timing: "when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son" (Gal 4:4). Jesus offered peace before judgment, weeping over a city that did not know "the things that make for peace" (Luke 19:42). And the God who gave the land to Esau and Lot as well as to Israel is the God who in Christ makes one people from every nation (Eph 2:14–19; Rev 7:9).
