*Setting:* Chapter 59 ended in darkness. People groped like the blind at noon, until God put on his armor and promised, "a Redeemer will come to Zion" (59:20). Chapter 60 is what happens when he comes. Light breaks over Jerusalem, and the whole world streams toward it: scattered children coming home, camels loaded with gold, ships from the far west, kings in procession, former enemies bowing. It ends with a city that needs no sun because the LORD himself is its light. Chapters 60–62 form the bright center of Isaiah's last section, with the Spirit-anointed messenger of chapter 61 at their heart. When this was first heard, Jerusalem was ruined or barely rebuilt, small and poor. The vision is far larger than anything the returned exiles saw, and the book of Revelation draws on it heavily for the new Jerusalem (Rev 21–22). The whole chapter is one ESV section.

#### The Future Glory of Israel (vv1–22)
- **"Arise, shine, for your light has come" (v1).** Zion has been pictured as a woman sitting in the dust (52:2) and as people stumbling in gloom (59:9–10). Now she is told to get up. Guzik: darkness is for lying down, light is for rising up. "Shine" (*ʾori*) is one word, "be light." She is to reflect the light that has dawned on her, the way the moon reflects the sun. She does not produce it.
- **"The glory of the LORD has risen upon you" (v1).** "Risen" (*zarach*) is the word for the sun coming up (Gen 32:31). The glory of the LORD, his visible, weighty presence, rises over Jerusalem like dawn. Malachi says "the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings" (Mal 4:2). Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, celebrated the coming of Jesus as "the sunrise from on high" visiting "those who sit in darkness" (Luke 1:78–79).
- **"Darkness shall cover the earth, and thick darkness the peoples; but the LORD will arise upon you" (v2).** "Thick darkness" is the heavy darkness around God on Sinai (Exod 20:21). The picture also recalls the plague of darkness on Egypt, when "the people of Israel had light where they lived" (Exod 10:21–23). The contrast is the same: the world in darkness, God's people in light. Isaiah promised long before that "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (9:2), and Matthew applies that to Jesus in Galilee (Matt 4:14–16).
- **"Nations shall come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your rising" (v3).** The Servant was to be "a light for the nations" (42:6; 49:6). Here the nations come to the light themselves. Revelation 21:24 borrows this verse for the new Jerusalem: "By its light will the nations walk, and the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it."
- **"Lift up your eyes all around, and see" (v4).** These words repeat 49:18 exactly. Zion, who thought she was childless, looks up and sees her children coming. **"Your daughters shall be carried on the hip."** Small children were carried on the hip or shoulder (66:12). The nations themselves will carry Israel's little ones home (49:22).
- **"Your heart shall thrill and exult" (v5).** "Thrill" is literally "tremble," a heart pounding with wonder and joy. **"The abundance of the sea… the wealth of the nations shall come to you."** At the Exodus, Israel left Egypt carrying its silver and gold (Exod 12:35–36). Haggai promised that "the treasures of all nations shall come in" to fill God's house with glory (Hag 2:7).
- **"A multitude of camels shall cover you, the young camels of Midian and Ephah" (v6).** Midian and Ephah were Arabian tribes descended from Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:1–4), camel-traders of the desert east and south of Israel. Sheba was in southern Arabia (modern Yemen), whose queen brought Solomon spices, gold and precious stones (1 Kings 10:1–10). **"They shall bring gold and frankincense."** Frankincense is a fragrant resin from trees that grow in southern Arabia, burned as incense in worship (Exod 30:34).
- **The Magi (v6).** When wise men "from the east" came to worship the child Jesus, they brought "gold and frankincense and myrrh" (Matt 2:11). Matthew does not quote this verse, but the gold and frankincense, the eastern travelers and the light (the star) make the echo hard to miss, and churches have long read Isaiah 60 at Epiphany, the feast of the Magi. Psalm 72:10–11 adds kings of Sheba bringing gifts. The tradition that the Magi were kings comes from these passages, not from Matthew.
- **"And shall bring good news, the praises of the LORD" (v6).** "Bring good news" is *basar*, the gospel verb of 40:9 and 52:7. The Gentiles bring not just gold but praise.
- **"The flocks of Kedar… the rams of Nebaioth" (v7).** Kedar and Nebaioth were the first two sons of Ishmael (Gen 25:13), whose tribes herded sheep in the Arabian desert. The descendants of the son who was sent away (Gen 21:14) now bring offerings to God's altar, and they come "with acceptance." **"I will beautify my beautiful house."** This answers the foreigners' welcome of 56:7.
- **"Who are these that fly like a cloud, and like doves to their windows?" (v8).** The prophet sees white sails on the sea, as many as a cloud, heading for port like doves flying home to their dovecote. "Windows" means the openings of a dovecote. The ships are coming home with something precious on board.
- **"The ships of Tarshish first, to bring your children from afar" (v9).** Tarshish was a distant port in the far west, probably in Spain. Jonah tried to sail there to get away from God (Jonah 1:3). "Ships of Tarshish" came to mean the largest ocean-going ships. **"Because he has made you beautiful."** The nations come not for Zion's own glory but "for the name of the LORD your God." The beauty is his gift.
- **"Foreigners shall build up your walls, and their kings shall minister to you" (v10).** This had a small, real fulfilment. The Persian king Cyrus decreed the temple's rebuilding and paid for it (Ezra 1:1–4; 6:3–8), and Artaxerxes provided timber for Nehemiah's walls (Neh 2:8). **"For in my wrath I struck you, but in my favor I have had mercy on you."** As in 54:7–8, the wrath was real and brief. The mercy is the last word.
- **"Your gates shall be open continually; day and night they shall not be shut" (v11).** Ancient city gates were shut at night for safety (Neh 7:3). These never close, partly because the stream of people bringing gifts never stops, and partly because there is nothing to fear. Revelation: "its gates will never be shut by day, and there will be no night there" (Rev 21:25–26).
- **"The nation and kingdom that will not serve you shall perish" (v12).** Some scholars think this verse, which is prose-like and breaks the rhythm, was added as a note. But it makes a real point. The invitation is open to all nations, but refusing God's king is refusing life itself (Ps 2:10–12).
- **"The glory of Lebanon shall come to you, the cypress, the plane, and the pine" (v13).** Lebanon's forests supplied the cedar and cypress for Solomon's temple (1 Kings 5:6–10). **"I will make the place of my feet glorious."** The temple and the ark were called God's footstool, the place on earth where heaven's King rests his feet (1 Chr 28:2; Ps 132:7). Jesus said heaven is God's throne and earth his footstool (Matt 5:35).
- **"The sons of those who afflicted you shall come bending low to you" (v14).** The children of Israel's oppressors come and bow down. **"They shall call you the City of the LORD, the Zion of the Holy One of Israel."** The risen Christ uses this verse for the little, persecuted church in Philadelphia: "I will make them come and bow down before your feet, and they will learn that I have loved you" (Rev 3:9). There the ones who bow are opponents of the church, and the "Zion" is the faithful church.
- **"Whereas you have been forsaken and hated, with no one passing through" (v15).** A deserted city, with no traffic on its roads (Lam 1:4: "The roads to Zion mourn"). **"I will make you majestic forever, a joy from age to age."** Not just restored but made splendid, and permanently.
- **"You shall suck the milk of nations; you shall nurse at the breast of kings" (v16).** This is a bold picture of being nourished by the nations' wealth, as in 49:23, where kings and queens are Israel's foster parents and nurses. **"And you shall know that I, the LORD, am your Savior and your Redeemer, the Mighty One of Jacob."** The aim of all the wealth is that Zion would know God. "The Mighty One of Jacob" is Jacob's own name for God (Gen 49:24) and appears in Isaiah only here and in 49:26.
- **"Instead of bronze I will bring gold, and instead of iron I will bring silver" (v17).** This reverses decline. In Solomon's day, silver was as common as stone in Jerusalem (1 Kings 10:21, 27). After Egypt plundered the temple, Rehoboam replaced Solomon's gold shields with bronze ones (1 Kings 14:25–27). Now every cheaper material is replaced with something better.
- **"I will make your overseers peace and your taskmasters righteousness" (v17).** Even more surprising than gold for bronze. "Taskmasters" is the word for Pharaoh's slave drivers in Egypt (Exod 3:7; 5:6). Those who rule over God's people will be peace and righteousness themselves. Guzik says this is a bigger miracle than any change of metals.
- **"You shall call your walls Salvation, and your gates Praise" (v18).** City walls kept out enemies. These walls are made of God's salvation (26:1, "he sets up salvation as walls and bulwarks"). People enter the city through gates of praise. Psalm 100:4: "Enter his gates with thanksgiving, and his courts with praise!"
- **"The sun shall be no more your light by day… but the LORD will be your everlasting light" (v19).** This goes beyond anything on earth. Revelation 21:23 quotes it almost directly: "the city has no need of sun or moon to shine on it, for the glory of God gives it light, and its lamp is the Lamb." Revelation 22:5 adds, "night will be no more." In Genesis God made light before he made the sun (Gen 1:3, 14–16). In the end, God himself is the light.
- **"Your sun shall no more go down… and your days of mourning shall be ended" (v20).** The sun that never sets is joy that never ends. "God will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning" (Rev 21:4).
- **"Your people shall all be righteous; they shall possess the land forever" (v21).** This is the fulfilment of 54:13–14 and 53:11. Every citizen of this city has been made right with God. Revelation: "nothing unclean will ever enter it" (Rev 21:27).
- **"The branch of my planting, the work of my hands, that I might be glorified" (v21).** "Branch" is *netser*, the same word Isaiah used for the "branch" growing from the stump of Jesse (11:1). Many think Matthew's "He shall be called a Nazarene" (Matt 2:23) is a wordplay on *netser* and Nazareth, though that is debated. Here the "branch" is the whole people, God's own planting. Isaiah 61:3 will call them "oaks of righteousness, the planting of the LORD." Jesus: "I am the vine; you are the branches" (John 15:5).
- **"The least one shall become a clan, and the smallest one a mighty nation" (v22).** "Clan" is literally "a thousand." The tiny remnant will multiply beyond counting, as Abraham's offspring like the stars (Gen 15:5). **"I am the LORD; in its time I will hasten it."** God will do it suddenly, but only at the time he has set. JFB: in his time, "not our time; we might wish to hasten it."
- **How should this chapter be read?** Faithful Christians read it in different ways. Many dispensational and premillennial readers, including Guzik, take it as a literal future for the nation of Israel and Jerusalem in a coming thousand-year reign of Christ. Guzik rejects the view that the church simply replaces Israel. Many Reformed and historic Protestant readers, including Henry, read it as the growth and glory of the church, Jew and Gentile together, ending in the new creation. The Tyndale notes take a layered view: partly fulfilled in the return from exile, more fully in Christ and the gospel going to the Gentiles, and finally in the new Jerusalem at Christ's return. What all agree on is that Revelation 21–22 uses this chapter to describe the end of the story.
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline reads the whole chapter as about the church: it is "enlightened and shone upon," "enlarged and great additions made to it," held "in great honour," and its "glory and joy… shall be everlasting." JFB reads the chapter as an ode to Zion at Christ's second coming, says the glory is "the glory of the Lord in person," and notes Sheba was "famed for frankincense and gold." It also notes that the desolation of v15 describes Israel, not the church. Guzik holds a strictly literal millennial reading and also draws a practical application: a Christian home can have walls of salvation and gates of praise now, in place of violence.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah 60 is about light, and the New Testament knows where that light comes from. Zechariah sang of Jesus as "the sunrise from on high" (Luke 1:78). Magi came from the east with gold and frankincense to worship him. Jesus said, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12) and told his followers, "You are the light of the world" (Matt 5:14), as Zion reflects the glory risen on her. The chapter ends where the Bible ends, in a city with no need of the sun, "for the glory of God gives it light, and its lamp is the Lamb" (Rev 21:23).
