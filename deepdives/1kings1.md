*Setting:* About 970 BC, the last days of King David's forty-year reign (2:11). First and Second Kings were one book in the Hebrew Bible, picking up right where 2 Samuel ends. The author is unnamed. Jewish tradition credited Jeremiah, but most scholars think it was compiled during the exile in Babylon (after 2 Kgs 25:27–30, around 560 BC) from older court records the writer names, such as "the Book of the Acts of Solomon" (11:41). Its question is why the kingdom that began so gloriously ended in ruin. Chapter 1 opens on a crisis. David is about seventy (2 Sam 5:4), worn out and bedridden. He has never named his heir publicly, and his sons have a history of grabbing for power: Amnon raped his half-sister and was murdered by Absalom, and Absalom led a revolt that nearly cost David his throne (2 Sam 13–18). God had already chosen Solomon, the son of Bathsheba (1 Chr 22:9–10; 28:5), and loved him from birth (2 Sam 12:24–25). The chapter shows how God's choice comes to pass through very human scheming, timing and courage.

#### David in His Old Age (vv1–4)
- **"King David was old and advanced in years… he could not get warm" (v1).** The great warrior who killed Goliath now shivers under blankets. The first image of the book is of human strength fading. Years of war, flight and grief had taken their toll. JFB: military life had "prematurely… exhausted the energies" of his body.
- **"Let a young woman be sought for my lord the king" (v2).** Lying close to a healthy young person to share body heat was a known medical remedy. The Tyndale notes say it is mentioned by Josephus and the Greek doctor Galen, and Guzik notes Josephus calls the servants who suggested it physicians. It strikes modern readers as strange, but the text treats it as care, not scandal.
- **"Abishag the Shunammite" (v3).** Shunem was a town in the Jezreel Valley in the north (Josh 19:18), later home of the woman who hosted Elisha (2 Kgs 4:8). Some have identified Abishag with the "Shulammite" of the Song of Songs (Song 6:13), but that is a guess, and Guzik points out that the names are not the same.
- **"But the king knew her not" (v4).** "Knew" is the Bible's term for sexual relations (Gen 4:1). The note is there for two reasons. It shows how weak David had become, which in the ancient world meant a king was losing his grip on power. And it matters in chapter 2, when Adonijah asks to marry her (2:13–25). JFB says she was counted as a secondary wife, which explains why that request was so dangerous.

#### Adonijah Sets Himself Up as King (vv5–10)
- **"Adonijah the son of Haggith exalted himself, saying, 'I will be king'" (v5).** Adonijah was David's fourth son, born at Hebron (2 Sam 3:4). Amnon and Absalom were dead, and the second son, Chileab, is never heard of again and probably died young. So Adonijah was the oldest living son and assumed the throne was his by right. But in Israel the king was the one God chose (Deut 17:15), and "exalted himself" is the opposite of how God works (Luke 14:11). His name means "my Lord is the LORD," an irony given what he does.
- **"Chariots and horsemen, and fifty men to run before him" (v5).** This is exactly what Absalom did when he began his revolt (2 Sam 15:1). Runners before a chariot were a sign of royal status. The ESV's "horsemen" may mean the horses for the chariots.
- **"His father had never at any time displeased him by asking, 'Why have you done thus and so?'" (v6).** One of the saddest lines about David as a father. He never corrected his son. The same failure appeared with Amnon, whom David was angry with but did not punish (2 Sam 13:21), and with Absalom. Henry: "Children that are indulged learn to be proud and ambitious." He also observes that it does not say Adonijah never displeased his father, only that David never told him so.
- **"He was also a very handsome man, and he was born next after Absalom" (v6).** Like Absalom (2 Sam 14:25) and like Saul (1 Sam 9:2), he looked the part. The writer quietly reminds the reader that the last handsome prince who rode out with fifty runners ended hanging in a tree.
- **"Joab… and Abiathar the priest… followed Adonijah" (v7).** Two old and powerful allies of David. Joab had commanded the army for decades but had killed Abner and Amasa in cold blood (2 Sam 3:27; 20:10) and killed Absalom against David's orders (2 Sam 18:14). Abiathar had been with David since he fled from Saul (1 Sam 22:20–23), but his family line was under an old judgment, the curse on the house of Eli (1 Sam 2:30–36). Guzik suggests motives: Joab's resentment at being passed over before (2 Sam 19:13) and Abiathar's rivalry with Zadok.
- **"But Zadok the priest and Benaiah… and Nathan the prophet and Shimei and Rei and David's mighty men were not with Adonijah" (v8).** Zadok was the other leading priest, from the line of Aaron's son Eleazar. Benaiah, a legendary fighter who killed a lion in a pit on a snowy day (2 Sam 23:20–23), commanded the royal bodyguard. This Shimei is probably not the man who cursed David (2 Sam 16:5). Rei is otherwise unknown. Notice the pattern: the army chief and one priest on one side, the bodyguard, the other priest and the prophet on the other.
- **"By the Serpent's Stone, which is beside En-rogel" (v9).** En-rogel ("spring of the fuller" or "of the foot") is a spring just south of the city where the Kidron and Hinnom valleys meet, today's Bir Ayyub. It was a sacred site with a stone called Zoheleth, probably "the sliding one" or "the serpent." A sacrifice made the feast a religious ceremony, a coronation in all but name.
- **"But he did not invite Nathan… or Solomon his brother" (v10).** Who was left off the list shows Adonijah knew exactly who his rival was. In the ancient world a new king often killed rival claimants (15:29; 16:11), so Solomon and Bathsheba were now in danger.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws the parallel with Absalom closely and presses that David's failure to discipline is the root of Adonijah's pride. He notes that David's own failures do not excuse Adonijah. Henry: Adonijah "cannot wait till his father's head be laid low." JFB reads David's silence as taken for consent and the feast as meant "to court popularity." JFB places En-rogel "east of Jerusalem," but most today put it south of the city, at the meeting of the valleys, as the Tyndale notes do.

#### Nathan and Bathsheba Before David (vv11–27)
- **"Have you not heard that Adonijah… has become king and David our lord does not know it?" (v11).** Nathan is the prophet who brought God's covenant to David (2 Sam 7) and who confronted him over Bathsheba and Uriah (2 Sam 12). Now he works to protect Bathsheba's son, the one God named Jedidiah, "beloved of the LORD," through Nathan himself (2 Sam 12:25). He knows that if Adonijah wins, Bathsheba and Solomon may be killed.
- **"Did you not, my lord the king, swear to your servant…?" (v13).** David's oath that Solomon would reign is not recorded before this point, but David confirms it (v30), and 1 Chronicles 22:6–10 records his telling Solomon of God's choice. Some readers suspect Nathan is inventing the oath to manipulate a confused old man. The text gives no hint of that, and David's own answer treats the oath as real.
- **"I also will come in after you and confirm your words" (v14).** Two witnesses confirm a matter (Deut 19:15). Nathan's plan is careful and wise, not a trick.
- **"Bathsheba went to the king in his chamber (now the king was very old, and Abishag… was attending to the king)" (v15).** A poignant detail. Bathsheba, once the woman David wanted so badly that he committed adultery and murder for her, now finds a younger woman caring for him.
- **"The eyes of all Israel are on you" (v20).** Bathsheba's speech is skillful. She reminds him of his oath, tells him the news he hasn't heard, and lays out the stakes: if David does nothing, "I and my son Solomon will be counted offenders" (v21), treated as traitors.
- **"While she was still speaking… Nathan the prophet came in" (v22).** Right on cue. Nathan bows "with his face to the ground," and frames the matter as a question: "My lord the king, have you said, 'Adonijah shall reign after me'?" (v24). He adds the detail that tips the balance: the guests are already shouting "Long live King Adonijah!" (v25). He is careful to add that he himself, Zadok, Benaiah and Solomon were not invited (v26).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees David's warm relationship with Nathan, the man who once rebuked him, as a sign of David's humility. He also says the choice of Bathsheba's son was a remarkable act of grace, given how that marriage began. The Tyndale notes observe that Adonijah would have noticed Nathan's close ties to Bathsheba and Solomon. Henry's chapter outline simply credits Nathan and Bathsheba with securing the succession for Solomon.

#### Solomon Anointed King (vv28–53)
- **"As the LORD lives, who has redeemed my soul out of every adversity" (v29).** The old king rouses himself, and his oath is full of his life story. Every danger, from Goliath to Saul to Absalom, God brought him through. David used almost the same words when he refused to kill Saul's murderer (2 Sam 4:9).
- **"Even so will I do this day" (v30).** Not tomorrow. David acts at once. His decisiveness here is a flash of the old David.
- **"Have Solomon my son ride on my own mule" (v33).** Mules were the mounts of royalty in David's court (2 Sam 13:29; 18:9). JFB says no subject was allowed to ride the king's mule, so this was a public sign that Solomon was the heir. Guzik adds that mules had to be imported, since Israel's law forbade crossbreeding animals (Lev 19:19). The Tyndale notes point ahead to Zechariah 9:9, the king coming "humble and mounted on a donkey," fulfilled when Jesus rode into Jerusalem (Matt 21:4–7).
- **"Bring him down to Gihon" (v33).** The Gihon spring was Jerusalem's main water source, on the eastern slope of the City of David in the Kidron Valley, later fed into the city by Hezekiah's tunnel (2 Chr 32:30). It was a public gathering place, close enough to En-rogel that Adonijah's guests would hear the shouts but not see what was happening. JFB puts Gihon on the west of the city, but the spring is on the east, as the Tyndale notes say.
- **"Let Zadok the priest and Nathan the prophet there anoint him king" (v34).** Anointing with oil set a person apart for God's service. Samuel anointed Saul and David (1 Sam 10:1; 16:13). "Anoint" is *mashach*, the root of *mashiach*, Messiah. Priest and prophet together give the coronation God's seal. "Long live King Solomon!" is literally "May the king live!"
- **"I have appointed him to be ruler over Israel and over Judah" (v35).** "Ruler" (*nagid*) means leader or prince, often the one God designates (1 Sam 9:16; 13:14). The mention of both Israel and Judah is a quiet reminder that the kingdom was really two peoples, north and south, and the split comes in chapter 12. Solomon reigned alongside David for a short time before David died. The Tyndale notes suggest these co-regencies later became common in Israel and Judah.
- **"May he… make his throne greater than the throne of my lord King David" (v37).** Benaiah's prayer comes true in wealth and splendor (chs 4–10). Guzik observes that in faithfulness to God it does not.
- **"The Cherethites and the Pelethites" (v38).** David's foreign bodyguard (2 Sam 8:18; 15:18). The Cherethites were probably from Crete and the Pelethites probably Philistines, mercenaries loyal to the king personally, not to any faction. Their presence settled the matter militarily.
- **"Zadok the priest took the horn of oil from the tent and anointed Solomon" (v39).** "The tent" is the tent David had set up for the ark in Jerusalem (2 Sam 6:17). The oil was probably the sacred anointing oil of Exodus 30:22–33. "The earth was split by their noise" (v40) is an exaggeration for effect, as we might say the ground shook.
- **"What does this uproar in the city mean?" (v41).** Joab, the old soldier, is the first to notice the trumpet. Jonathan the son of Abiathar was a messenger who had served David during Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 15:36; 17:17), at the same spring of En-rogel. Adonijah greets him with "you… bring good news" (v42), and the news is the worst he could hear. The account piles up the details: the king's mule, the anointing, Solomon on the throne, the servants' blessing, David bowing in worship on his bed (vv43–48).
- **"Blessed be the LORD… who has granted someone to sit on my throne this day, my own eyes seeing it" (v48).** David sees with his own eyes the beginning of God's promise that his son would reign (2 Sam 7:12).
- **"All the guests of Adonijah trembled and rose, and each went his own way" (v49).** The party ends in minutes. A kingdom built on self-promotion collapses as soon as the true king is announced.
- **"Adonijah… took hold of the horns of the altar" (v50).** The bronze altar had a horn-like projection at each corner (Exod 27:2), the most sacred part, where blood was smeared for atonement. Grasping it was a plea for sanctuary. The law allowed this for someone who killed by accident, but not for a deliberate murderer (Exod 21:13–14). JFB says the altar was a sanctuary but "not to murderers, rebels, or deliberate perpetrators." Joab will run to the same altar in 2:28–34.
- **"If he will show himself a worthy man, not one of his hairs shall fall to the earth" (v52).** Solomon's first act as king is mercy, with a condition. He does not kill his rival, though many ancient kings would have. "Go to your house" (v53) is release, but under watch.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees in the roles of prophet, priest and king working together a picture of Jesus, who holds all three offices. He calls Solomon's mercy to Adonijah a gracious start, with a clear warning. Henry outlines the section as the anointing, "and the people's joy therein," followed by the scattering of Adonijah's party and Adonijah dismissed on condition of good behavior. JFB calls the horns of the altar symbols of grace. The Tyndale notes add that only the king could pardon treason, so Adonijah's hope lay in Solomon, not the altar.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two men claim the throne in this chapter. One "exalted himself," gathered his own supporters and threw his own party. The other did nothing to seize power. He was chosen by God, named "beloved of the LORD," anointed by priest and prophet, and rode in on the king's mule while the people shouted for joy. Jesus is the greater Son of David who did not grasp at a crown, but "humbled himself… therefore God has highly exalted him" (Phil 2:8–9). He rode into Jerusalem on a donkey to the people's shouts (Matt 21:9), and he is the anointed King, the Christ, whom God set on his throne. His rivals, like Adonijah's guests, scatter when he is revealed. And to those who run to him for mercy, as Adonijah ran to the altar, he does better than Solomon did: he does not merely spare them on condition, he gives them a place at his table.
