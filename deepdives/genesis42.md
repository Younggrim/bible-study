*Setting:* About twenty-two years have passed since Joseph's brothers sold him for twenty shekels of silver (37:28). Joseph is now around thirty-nine, the governor of Egypt, married with two sons, and in charge of the grain stored during seven good years (ch 41). The famine Joseph predicted has struck "all lands," and it is now in its first or second year (45:6). Back in Canaan, Jacob is old, still grieving Joseph, and holding tightly to Benjamin, Rachel's only other son. The brothers have lived for two decades with a lie, telling their father that a wild animal killed Joseph. Chapters 42–45 tell how God brings the family back together. It does not happen at once. Joseph tests his brothers, and through the tests their buried guilt comes to the surface. This chapter is the first of their two journeys to Egypt.

#### Joseph's Brothers Go to Egypt (vv1–38)
- **"Why do you look at one another?" (v1).** Jacob hears there is grain for sale in Egypt, and his sons just stand there looking at each other. They may simply be stuck, unsure what to do in a crisis. Guzik and others suggest something more: the word "Egypt" was the last place they ever wanted to go, since that was where Joseph was taken (37:28). The narrator does not say. Jacob, at least, acts: **"Go down and buy grain for us there, that we may live and not die" (v2).**
- **"Ten of Joseph's brothers… But Jacob did not send Benjamin, Joseph's brother" (vv3–4).** The narrator calls them "Joseph's brothers," not "Jacob's sons," because the story is about to bring them face to face with him. Benjamin is "Joseph's brother" in a closer sense, the only other son of Rachel. Jacob keeps him home "for he feared that harm might happen to him." The favoritism that once fixed on Joseph has moved to Benjamin, and the ten know it.
- **"The sons of Israel came to buy among the others" (v5).** The family of promise arrives in Egypt as just another group of hungry foreigners in a long line. They are the people God named "Israel," but here they are faceless in a crowd.
- **"Joseph's brothers came and bowed themselves before him with their faces to the ground" (v6).** This is the moment the whole story has been heading toward. "Governor" is a rare Hebrew word for someone with ruling power. The brothers bow to the ground, using the same verb as Joseph's dream of the sheaves (37:7). They sold him to stop his dreams: "we will see what will become of his dreams" (37:20). Now, without knowing it, they are fulfilling them. The Tyndale notes observe that the bowing will happen again (43:26; 44:14) and come to completion in 50:18.
- **"He recognized them, but he treated them like strangers" (vv7–8).** The Hebrew plays on one root, *nakar*. "Recognized" is from it, and "treated them like strangers" is from it too, in a form meaning "made himself unrecognizable." The same root was used when the brothers said to Jacob, "please identify" Joseph's bloodied robe (37:32), and when Tamar said to Judah, "please identify" his seal (38:25). The men who made their father recognize a false death now fail to recognize a living brother. And v8 says it twice: "Joseph recognized his brothers, but they did not recognize him." It is not surprising. He was seventeen when they last saw him. Now he is a grown man, clean-shaven, in Egyptian dress, speaking Egyptian through an interpreter, and holding a position they could never have imagined.
- **"Joseph remembered the dreams" (v9).** He sees the dreams coming true in front of him. The text does not say exactly what he felt. But his next move shows he is not after revenge. He wants to find out whether his brothers have changed.
- **"You are spies; you have come to see the nakedness of the land" (vv9–12).** "Nakedness" means the land's weak points, its unguarded borders. Egypt's northeastern frontier, facing Canaan, was the route invaders used, and Egypt kept forts along it. Ten men from one family arriving from that direction might plausibly be scouts. JFB notes that suspicion of strangers as spies was common "in all Eastern countries." There is an echo here too. Joseph was once the one who carried reports on his brothers (37:2), and they hated him for it. Now he accuses them of spying.
- **"We are all sons of one man. We are honest men" (v11).** "Honest" means upright, truthful. The irony is heavy. These men deceived their father for over twenty years. They are telling the truth now, but the word "honest" sits uneasily on them.
- **"One is no more" (v13).** They give a full account of the family, "twelve brothers… the youngest is this day with our father, and one is no more." It is a careful phrase. It does not say Joseph is dead, and it does not say what happened to him. Guzik suggests they had repeated the line so long that they half believed it. They are saying it to Joseph's face.
- **"By the life of Pharaoh" (vv15–16).** Joseph swears in the Egyptian manner, part of his disguise. His first demand is harsh: one brother goes to fetch Benjamin, and the other nine stay in prison. This is a test of whether they are telling the truth, but it also forces the issue Joseph cares about most. Is Benjamin alive and safe? Have they done to Rachel's other son what they did to him?
- **"He put them all together in custody for three days" (v17).** Joseph knew what it was to be thrown into a pit and left (37:24) and to sit in prison for years (39:20). He gives his brothers three days of it. The Tyndale notes suggest it gave their consciences time to work. JFB calls it a time for "salutary reflection."
- **"Do this and you will live, for I fear God" (v18).** On the third day Joseph changes the terms. Only one stays, and the rest take grain home "for the famine of your households." That is mercy. His reason is "I fear God," using the general word *Elohim*, which an Egyptian official might say. To "fear God" in Genesis means to live with a sense that God sees and judges (20:11; 22:12). The brothers would have heard it as a promise of fair dealing. Henry paraphrases: "high as I am, there is one higher than I."
- **"In truth we are guilty concerning our brother" (v21).** This is the turning point of the chapter. The brothers speak among themselves in Hebrew, unaware that Joseph understands. "Guilty" is a legal and religious word: bearing guilt that must be paid for. They connect their distress now with "the distress of his soul" then. The same Hebrew word, *tsarah*, is used for both, as if the brothers hear the measure being returned to them. And they reveal a detail chapter 37 left out: Joseph "begged us and we did not listen." For more than twenty years they have heard his pleading in their heads. JFB calls their talk "one of the most striking instances on record of the power of conscience."
- **"There comes a reckoning for his blood" (v22).** Reuben reminds them he warned them. "Reckoning" echoes God's words after the flood: "from his fellow man I will require a reckoning for the life of man" (9:5). Reuben still believes Joseph is dead. His "I told you so" is true, but it does nothing to help.
- **"There was an interpreter between them" (v23).** Joseph has been speaking Egyptian and using an interpreter, so the brothers do not know he understands every word. The interpreter's role also explains why they spoke freely in front of him.
- **"He turned away from them and wept" (v24).** This is the first time we see Joseph weep. He will weep again and again in the chapters ahead (43:30; 45:2, 14–15; 46:29; 50:1, 17). The Tyndale notes suggest he was hearing for the first time that Reuben had tried to save him, or that he was moved by their remorse. Whatever he felt, he does not reveal himself. The test is not over.
- **"He took Simeon from them and bound him before their eyes" (v24).** Why Simeon? Reuben, the eldest, has just shown he tried to protect Joseph, so Joseph passes over him to the next oldest. Simeon was also one of the two brothers who massacred Shechem (ch 34), and JFB suggests he may have been "the chief instigator" against Joseph. The text does not say. Binding him "before their eyes" makes the brothers watch a brother taken from them, as Joseph was.
- **"Replace every man's money in his sack" (v25).** Joseph sends them home with grain, food for the road, and their money secretly put back. It is a kindness. It is also a test. The last time these brothers came home from a journey, they came back with silver in their hands and without a brother (37:28). Now they come home again without a brother, and with silver they did not earn. Will they keep it? Will they abandon Simeon as they abandoned Joseph?
- **"He saw his money in the mouth of his sack" (vv26–27).** "Lodging place" is not a hotel but a camp or stopping place for the night. One brother opens his sack to feed his donkey and finds his silver. In 43:21 the brothers tell Joseph's steward that they all found their money at the lodging place, while 42:35 describes the rest being found at home. The steward version is probably compressed. Either way, the discovery terrifies them.
- **"What is this that God has done to us?" (v28).** "Their hearts failed them," literally "their heart went out." For the first time in the whole story the brothers name God. They do not yet know what God is doing, but they know he is in it. Their guilt has opened their eyes to God's hand.
- **The report to Jacob (vv29–34).** They tell their father what happened, mostly faithfully, but they soften it. They do not mention their three days in prison, Simeon being bound in front of them, or their conversation about guilt. They stress "the man, the lord of the land," and the demand to bring Benjamin.
- **"Every man's bundle of money was in his sack" (v35).** When the sacks are emptied at home, every brother's money is there, tied up in a small bag, and "they were afraid." They and their father are now afraid together. Money that should feel like a gift feels like a trap.
- **"You have bereaved me of my children" (v36).** Jacob's words are sharper than he knows. "Joseph is no more, and Simeon is no more, and now you would take Benjamin." He blames his sons for losing Simeon, and in fact they did lose Joseph. Whether Jacob suspected them, the text does not say. **"All this has come against me."** It is a cry of despair. In fact, every one of these things was working for him. Joseph was alive, Simeon was safe, and God was preparing to bring the whole family to food and safety. Guzik contrasts Jacob's attitude with Joseph's, who suffered far worse without this kind of despair, and points to Romans 8:28: "for those who love God all things work together for good."
- **"Kill my two sons if I do not bring him back to you" (v37).** Reuben tries to take charge again, with an offer that makes no sense. Jacob has just mourned losing sons. Reuben offers him the chance to kill two grandsons. JFB calls it a "thoughtless and unwarrantable condition." The contrast with Judah is coming: in the next chapter Judah offers not his sons but himself (43:9).
- **"His brother is dead, and he is the only one left" (v38).** Jacob refuses. "His brother" means Joseph, and "the only one left" means the only son of Rachel. Ten sons stand in front of him, one more sits in an Egyptian prison, and Jacob speaks as if Benjamin is all he has. His words must have stung, but they also show the brothers what Jacob's grief for Joseph has cost him. **"You would bring down my gray hairs with sorrow to Sheol."** He speaks again of going down to Sheol, the realm of the dead, mourning (37:35). The chapter ends with Simeon in chains, the grain running out, and Jacob refusing to move.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives two reasons for Joseph's roughness: "It was to bring them to repentance" and to complete the fulfilment of his dreams. On v18 he notes, "With those that fear God we have reason to expect fair dealing." He also draws attention to Joseph's tears and to Reuben's earlier attempt to save him. Guzik argues that a troubled conscience is a sign of God at work, and that real repentance had to come before Joseph could reveal himself and be reconciled with them. He notes that Scripture does not say why Simeon was chosen, and observes that Jacob seems less concerned about Simeon in prison than about Benjamin. JFB thinks Simeon was singled out as the likely ringleader, and explains the returned money as Joseph's "private generosity," not a cheat on Pharaoh's revenue. On Jacob's lament JFB speaks of "a painfully excited state of feeling." The Tyndale notes draw out the many mirrors: the three days in custody recall the pit, the silver recalls the sale, and the brothers are being made to face what they did. JFB has no notes on vv2–4, v16, v23, vv29–34 or v38.

#### Pointing to Christ
The brothers bow before the one they rejected, and they do not know him. He knows them completely. He speaks roughly, but only to bring them to the truth, and he weeps over them in secret while he provides their bread and pays their price. Their guilt comes out before his grace does, and yet his grace was already in their sacks before they knew it. Jesus came to his own, and they did not recognize him (John 1:10–11). He sees every buried sin and is not out for revenge. His goal, like Joseph's, is to bring people to say, "In truth we are guilty," so that he can reveal himself and say, "I am your brother." Jacob cried, "All this has come against me," when in fact everything was being worked for his good. That is often how God's saving work looks while it is still in motion.
