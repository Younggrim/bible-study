*Setting:* About AD 29, early in the journey to Jerusalem. In 9:51 Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem," was turned away by a Samaritan village, and warned would-be followers about the cost. Chapter 10 widens the mission. After the Twelve (9:1–6), Jesus now sends out seventy-two more, and their return brings a burst of joy from Jesus himself. Then a lawyer's question draws out the parable of the Good Samaritan, and a visit to the home of Martha and Mary shows what matters most. Much of this chapter is found only in Luke.

#### Jesus Sends Out the Seventy-Two (vv1–12)
- **"The Lord appointed seventy-two others" (v1).** Manuscripts are split between "seventy" and "seventy-two," with strong early witnesses on each side. The ESV follows seventy-two and footnotes seventy. The number may echo the list of nations in Genesis 10, which counts seventy in the Hebrew text and seventy-two in the Greek Old Testament Luke usually followed. If so, this mission hints at the gospel's reach to all nations. Others connect it to the seventy elders who shared Moses' Spirit (Num 11:16, 24–25), which would explain seventy-two if Eldad and Medad are added (Num 11:26).
- **"Two by two, into every town and place where he himself was about to go" (v1).** Pairs gave support and a double witness (Deut 19:15). They are advance messengers, preparing towns for his own visit.
- **"The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few" (v2).** In the prophets, the harvest is a picture of God's final gathering (Isa 27:12; Joel 3:13). The first instruction is not to go but to pray. The verb is *deomai*, to beg or plead. The harvest belongs to the Lord, and so do the workers.
- **"Lambs in the midst of wolves" (v3).** The picture is of complete vulnerability. The prophets called Israel's corrupt leaders wolves (Ezek 22:27; Zeph 3:3). The messengers are sent without defense, trusting the Shepherd.
- **"Carry no moneybag, no knapsack, no sandals, and greet no one on the road" (v4).** Middle Eastern greetings could be long and elaborate. This is not rudeness but urgency, as when Elisha sent Gehazi: "If you meet anyone, do not greet him" (2 Kings 4:29).
- **"Peace be to this house!" (v5).** The ordinary greeting, *shalom*, becomes a blessing with real weight. A "son of peace" (v6) is a Hebrew way of saying a person open to peace, someone ready to receive the message. If no such person is there, the blessing "will return to you," as if it were something that could be given and taken back.
- **"The laborer deserves his wages" (v7).** Those who preach should be supported by those who receive them. Paul quotes this line and calls it Scripture (1 Tim 5:18; compare 1 Cor 9:14). "Do not go from house to house" means do not shop for better hosts.
- **"Eat what is set before you" (v8).** This may hint at the Gentile mission, where food laws would be a question (1 Cor 10:27 uses almost the same words).
- **"The kingdom of God has come near to you" (v9).** The healings show that God's rule has arrived. The verb can mean "has come near" or "has arrived." Both are true: the kingdom is present in Jesus and still to come in fullness.
- **"Even the dust of your town that clings to our feet we wipe off against you" (v11).** As in 9:5, a solemn sign that the town has refused God's message. Even so, the message stands: "the kingdom of God has come near."
- **"More bearable on that day for Sodom than for that town" (v12).** Sodom was the Old Testament's byword for wickedness and judgment (Gen 19). A town that refuses the Messiah's messengers is in a worse position than Sodom, because it has had greater light.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the sending a sign that "Christ's interest is a growing interest." JFB ties the number to Israel's seventy elders and explains a "son of peace" as one "inwardly prepared to embrace your message of peace." Guzik lists the main proposals for the number seventy, reads the harvest as a call to pray before acting, and treats the instruction to accept support as dignity, not charity, for the worker.

#### Woe to Unrepentant Cities (vv13–16)
- **"Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida!" (v13).** Chorazin was a hill town about two miles north of Capernaum. Its black basalt ruins, including a later synagogue, can still be seen. The Gospels record no miracle there, a reminder that they tell only part of what Jesus did (John 21:25).
- **"Tyre and Sidon… would have repented long ago" (v13).** These Phoenician ports were proverbial for pagan pride, and the prophets announced their fall (Isa 23; Ezek 26–28). Sackcloth, a rough goat-hair cloth, and ashes were the signs of grief and repentance (Jonah 3:6).
- **"You, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven? You shall be brought down to Hades" (v15).** This echoes Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon, who said "I will ascend to heaven" and was "brought down to Sheol" (Isa 14:13–15). Capernaum, Jesus' own base, had seen more of his miracles than anywhere. "Hades" is the Greek name for the realm of the dead, the Hebrew *Sheol*. Today Capernaum is ruins.
- **Degrees of judgment.** Jesus speaks of judgment being more or less "bearable." Scripture teaches that responsibility rises with light received (12:47–48).
- **"The one who hears you hears me" (v16).** The messengers carry the authority of the one who sent them. To reject them is to reject Jesus, and to reject him is to reject the Father.
- **Commentators:** JFB says those who reject Christ face a judgment "less endurable" than Sodom's. Henry: "He that despiseth you doth in effect despise me." Guzik argues that greater revelation brings greater accountability and draws from these verses that judgment comes in degrees.

#### The Return of the Seventy-Two (vv17–20)
- **"Lord, even the demons are subject to us in your name!" (v17).** "Even" shows their surprise. They were sent to heal and preach (v9), and they found that the demons gave way too. They credit Jesus' name.
- **"I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (v18).** Jesus is probably not describing Satan's original fall before creation, though some read it so. The tense pictures something he was watching while they were out: as they drove out demons, Satan's power was collapsing, sudden and swift like a lightning flash. Revelation 12:7–12 pictures the same defeat, connected with Christ's victory. Isaiah 14:12, about the king of Babylon, "How you are fallen from heaven, O Day Star," lies in the background.
- **"Authority to tread on serpents and scorpions" (v19).** Snakes and scorpions were real dangers in the Judean wilderness (Deut 8:15) and pictures of evil powers. Psalm 91:13 promises the one who trusts God that he will "tread on the lion and the adder." Genesis 3:15 promised that the serpent's head would be crushed. This is not permission to take reckless risks, but a promise that no enemy power has the last word over God's servants (Acts 28:3–6).
- **"Rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (v20).** The Bible speaks of a book of life, the record of those who belong to God (Exod 32:32; Dan 12:1; Phil 4:3; Rev 20:15). Power over demons can come and go. Belonging to God is the deeper joy. Jesus later warns that some who cast out demons in his name will be told "I never knew you" (Matt 7:22–23).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Satan falls from heaven when he falls from the throne in men's hearts," and "saving graces are more to be rejoiced in than spiritual gifts." JFB renders v18 as a continuing vision, "I was beholding Satan as lightning falling from heaven," and speaks of "the joy of having their names in Heaven's register." Guzik distinguishes several falls of Satan in Scripture and warns that joy centered on success can quickly turn to pride.

#### Jesus Rejoices in the Father's Will (vv21–24)
- **"He rejoiced in the Holy Spirit" (v21).** The verb is *agalliaō*, to exult, used of Mary's spirit rejoicing in God (1:47). Here Father, Son and Spirit appear together in one sentence. What makes Jesus overflow with joy is ordinary people coming to know God.
- **"You have hidden these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to little children" (v21).** "Little children" (*nēpioi*) means infants, the simple, people with no status. God is not against intelligence. He is against pride that thinks it can reach him on its own (Isa 29:14; 1 Cor 1:26–29). "For such was your gracious will" means it pleased him.
- **"All things have been handed over to me by my Father" (v22).** This verse sounds like John's Gospel (John 3:35; 10:15; 17:2). Only the Father fully knows the Son, and only the Son fully knows the Father. Knowing God is not something people achieve. The Son reveals him to "anyone to whom the Son chooses."
- **"Blessed are the eyes that see what you see!" (v23).** The prophets and kings looked forward to the Messiah and searched for what they could not yet see (1 Pet 1:10–12). These ordinary disciples are seeing it.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Nothing rejoices the heart of the Lord Jesus so much as the progress of the gospel." JFB, comparing Matthew 11:25–27, says he "exulted in spirit." Guzik draws attention to God's preference for the humble over the self-reliant wise, and to the unity of Father and Son that v22 assumes.

#### The Parable of the Good Samaritan (vv25–37)
- **"A lawyer stood up to put him to the test" (v25).** A "lawyer" was an expert in the law of Moses. "Test" can be hostile or simply probing. Standing to speak was a sign of respect to a teacher, who sat.
- **"What shall I do to inherit eternal life?" (v25).** "Inherit" is odd, since no one does anything to receive an inheritance. "Eternal life" is the life of the age to come. A rich ruler asks the same question in 18:18.
- **"What is written in the Law? How do you read it?" (v26).** Jesus answers a lawyer with a question about his own field. Some think "How do you read it?" referred to the daily recitation of the Shema.
- **"You shall love the Lord your God… and your neighbor as yourself" (v27).** The lawyer joins Deuteronomy 6:5, part of the Shema that devout Jews recited morning and evening, with Leviticus 19:18. Elsewhere Jesus himself joins these two commands as the greatest (Mark 12:29–31). Heart, soul, strength and mind together mean the whole person.
- **"Do this, and you will live" (v28).** This echoes Leviticus 18:5. Jesus is not teaching that eternal life can be earned. He is letting the law do its work. No one loves God and neighbor perfectly. The law, taken seriously, shows the need for mercy.
- **"Desiring to justify himself… 'And who is my neighbor?'" (v29).** He wants to limit the command. Leviticus 19:18 speaks of "the sons of your own people." Many in that day took "neighbor" to mean a fellow Israelite, and some groups narrowed it further. The question is really: whom can I leave out?
- **"Going down from Jerusalem to Jericho" (v30).** The road ran about 17 miles and dropped some 3,300 feet through the barren, twisting Judean wilderness. Its caves and bends made it notorious for robbers. "Down" is literal.
- **"Stripped him and beat him… leaving him half dead" (v30).** Stripped and unconscious, he could not be identified by his clothes or his speech. No one could tell whether he was a Jew or not, or even whether he was alive.
- **"A priest… a Levite… passed by on the other side" (vv31–32).** Priests served at the temple, and many lived in Jericho. Levites assisted them. Both are going "down," away from Jerusalem, so their temple duty may be over. If the man was dead, touching him would make them unclean for seven days (Num 19:11). The text gives no reason. It just says they saw and passed by, "on the other side."
- **"But a Samaritan" (v33).** Jesus' hearers would expect the third man to be an ordinary Israelite, and the story to make a point about religious leaders. Instead he is a Samaritan, one of a people the Jews despised (see 9:52–53). About a century earlier, a Jewish ruler, John Hyrcanus, had destroyed the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim. The hatred ran both ways.
- **"He had compassion" (v33).** The same word (*splanchnizomai*) Luke uses for Jesus at Nain (7:13) and for the prodigal's father (15:20). Compassion is the hinge of the story.
- **"Bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine" (v34).** Oil soothed and wine cleaned the wound, the standard first aid of the day. He walks while the wounded man rides his animal. He pays at the inn. Each act costs him time, money, comfort and risk, since the robbers might still be near.
- **"Two denarii" (v35).** A denarius was a day's wage, so two denarii would cover perhaps several days at an inn. "Whatever more you spend, I will repay you" is an open-ended promise.
- **"Which of these three… proved to be a neighbor?" (v36).** Jesus turns the question around. The lawyer asked, "Who is my neighbor?" meaning whom must I love. Jesus asks who acted as a neighbor. The question is not whom I can exclude, but whose neighbor I will be.
- **"The one who showed him mercy" (v37).** The lawyer cannot bring himself to say "the Samaritan."
- **"You go, and do likewise" (v37).** Jesus ends with a command, not a definition. The lawyer came asking what he must do, and now he knows.
- **The Samaritan as a picture of Christ.** From Origen and Augustine on, many readers have seen Jesus in the Samaritan: an outsider despised by his own, who comes to the dying man, binds his wounds, pays his debt and promises to return. Augustine went further and gave every detail a meaning, the inn as the church, the two denarii as the two commandments. Most interpreters today keep the main point, love that crosses every boundary, while granting that the picture of a rescuing outsider fits Jesus well.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the road "a notorious haunt of robbers," says of the priest that "it was not inadvertently that he acted," explains the wine as used "to cleanse the wounds," and calls Jesus' reversal of the question "a most dexterous way of putting the question," and the whole, "O exquisite, matchless teaching!" Henry: "Christ sends him to school to a Samaritan, to learn his duty." Guzik names three errors in the lawyer: assuming he kept the first command, separating love of God from love of others, and narrowing who counts as a neighbor. He also sees in the Samaritan's costly care a reflection of Jesus' own.

#### Martha and Mary (vv38–42)
- **"Jesus entered a village… a woman named Martha welcomed him into her house" (v38).** John tells us the village was Bethany, two miles from Jerusalem on the Mount of Olives, and that they had a brother, Lazarus (John 11:1; 12:1–3). Luke does not name the village, perhaps to keep the travel story moving. Martha seems to be the head of the household, and the house is called hers.
- **"Mary, who sat at the Lord's feet and listened to his teaching" (v39).** To sit at a teacher's feet was the posture of a disciple. Paul says he was educated "at the feet of Gamaliel" (Acts 22:3). Women did not usually take that place with a rabbi. Jesus welcomes Mary there.
- **"Martha was distracted with much serving" (v40).** "Distracted" (*perispaō*) means pulled in many directions at once. Hospitality was a sacred duty, and serving a guest like Jesus, with his disciples, was a big job. Martha's work was good. Her problem was being pulled away from him by it.
- **"Lord, do you not care…? Tell her then to help me" (v40).** Martha accuses Jesus and tells him what to do in the same breath.
- **"Martha, Martha" (v41).** The doubled name is tender, as with "Simon, Simon" (22:31). "Anxious" (*merimnaō*) is the word Jesus uses for worry about life's needs (12:22) and the "cares" that choke the seed (8:14).
- **"One thing is necessary" (v42).** Some manuscripts read "few things are necessary, or only one," as if Jesus first meant "one dish will do." The shorter reading, followed by the ESV, has the better support. Either way, the point is the same: listening to Jesus comes first.
- **"Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her" (v42).** "Portion" is a share of food, a fitting pun at a dinner. A meal is soon gone. What Mary is receiving lasts. Jesus is not condemning service. The chapter has just praised the Samaritan's active mercy. Love for neighbor (vv25–37) and love for God shown in listening at his feet (vv38–42) belong together, the two halves of the lawyer's answer in v27.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "the house belonged to her" and reads "sitting at one's feet" as discipleship. It says Martha was "careful and cumbered," anxious within and bustling without, and that Mary chose the better "of two good ways of serving and pleasing the Lord." Henry: "Serious godliness is a needful thing, it is the one thing needful," and "the day will come when Martha will wish she had set where Mary did." Guzik argues that Martha's problem was not serving but being distracted from Jesus, and that service cut off from time at his feet breeds frustration.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 10 shows a Lord who sends ordinary people as lambs among wolves, watches Satan fall as they go, and rejoices in the Spirit when the simple come to know the Father. He is the Son who alone knows the Father and makes him known. The lawyer wanted to know how little love he could get away with. Jesus told a story about a despised outsider who stopped on a dangerous road, bound up a dying man's wounds and paid whatever it cost. That is a picture of what Jesus himself does for those who cannot help themselves. And he is the guest who would rather have Mary's listening heart than Martha's anxious service, because what he gives "will not be taken away."
