*Setting:* About 930–926 BC, the first years after the kingdom split. Rehoboam, Solomon's son, has just fled from Shechem back to Jerusalem after the northern tribes rejected him and stoned his labor chief (10:16–19). Ten tribes now follow Jeroboam son of Nebat. Rehoboam holds Judah and most of Benjamin, with Jerusalem at their border. Chronicles was written after the exile for a small community in Judah, and this chapter speaks straight to them: a little kingdom that seemed to have lost almost everything, yet was still the place where God's king, God's priests and God's temple were. Apart from the first four verses, which come from 1 Kings 12:21–24, everything here is found only in Chronicles.

#### Rehoboam Secures His Kingdom (vv1–12)
- **"180,000 chosen warriors, to fight against Israel" (v1).** Rehoboam's first instinct is to win back by force what he lost by pride. The number is copied from Kings (1 Kgs 12:21). Large numbers in Chronicles are often debated; the Hebrew word for "thousand" (*'eleph*) can also mean a clan or military unit, and some scholars read the figures that way. Either way, this is the whole fighting strength of Judah and Benjamin.
- **"Judah and Benjamin" (v1).** Benjamin had been Saul's tribe and had once resented David (2 Sam 16:5–8). Jerusalem sat on the Judah–Benjamin border, and the temple bound Benjamin to the south. God's promise had spoken of "one tribe" left to David's house (1 Kgs 11:13, 32); in practice that meant Judah with a good part of Benjamin folded in.
- **"The word of the LORD came to Shemaiah the man of God" (v2).** "Man of God" is an old title for a prophet (Deut 33:1; 1 Sam 9:6). Shemaiah appears again in the next chapter, when Egypt invades (12:5–7, 15), and Chronicles says he kept written records of Rehoboam's reign.
- **"To all Israel in Judah and Benjamin" (v3).** A small but telling phrase. For the Chronicler, "Israel" is not just the northern kingdom. Everyone faithful to the LORD, wherever they live, is still Israel. He uses the name this way all through the book (11:13, 16; 12:1).
- **"You shall not go up or fight against your relatives" (v4).** Literally "your brothers." The split is a judgment, but the northern tribes are still family. Civil war between brothers is not God's will.
- **"For this thing is from me" (v4).** This repeats the point of 10:15. Rehoboam's folly caused the split, and God ruled over it. To fight it would be to fight God.
- **"So they listened to the word of the LORD" (v4).** Chronicles is a book about whether kings listen to God. Rehoboam's reign opens with a real act of obedience, and it was costly: he gave up most of his kingdom on the word of one prophet. Kings and Chronicles both record that there was still "war between Rehoboam and Jeroboam continually" (12:15; 1 Kgs 14:30), so this was a halt to one invasion, not a lasting peace.
- **"He built cities for defense in Judah" (v5).** "Built" here means rebuilt or fortified. In Chronicles, building projects are a mark of God's blessing on an obedient king (compare 14:6–7; 17:12; 26:9–10).
- **The fifteen cities (vv6–10).** The list runs roughly from Bethlehem and Tekoa east and south of Jerusalem, around through Hebron and Ziph in the south, to Lachish, Mareshah, Azekah, Gath and Zorah along the western foothills (the Shephelah), the low hills between the coastal plain and the Judean highlands. Aijalon and Zorah guard the valleys coming up from the west. There are no forts on the northern border. Rehoboam was looking south and west, toward Egypt and the Philistine plain, not toward Jeroboam.
- **Why the south and west?** Jeroboam had spent years in Egypt as a guest of Pharaoh Shishak (1 Kgs 11:40). An Egyptian invasion, with Jeroboam as Egypt's friend, was a real danger. The next chapter shows it was justified: Shishak came in Rehoboam's fifth year (12:2). Some scholars think the line of forts was built after Shishak's raid rather than before it. The text does not give a date, and the forts may have been begun early and finished later.
- **Lachish (v9).** The strongest city after Jerusalem, guarding the road from the coastal highway up into Judah. Its massive gate and walls have been excavated. Two centuries later the Assyrian king Sennacherib besieged it and carved the victory on his palace walls in Nineveh (2 Kgs 18:14, 17).
- **"Commanders… stores of food, oil, and wine… shields and spears" (vv11–12).** Each fort was garrisoned and stocked to hold out under siege. **"So he held Judah and Benjamin" (v12).** The kingdom was small, but it was secure.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Payne, reads the placement of the forts as evidence that the danger came from Egypt, and Clarke adds that the stores let each city outlast a siege. Matthew Henry praises Rehoboam for laying down his arms at God's word even though it looked humiliating: "It is dangerous undertaking any thing, but especially undertaking a war, contrary to the will of God." He sums up the forts with "Those that may not be conquerors, yet may be builders." Guzik adds a caution Henry does not: stone walls were no substitute for the spiritual foundation Rehoboam would soon neglect. JFB notes that Zorah and Aijalon lay in Benjamin's territory and that Jeroboam was fortifying his own frontier at the same time.

#### Priests and Levites Come to Jerusalem (vv13–17)
- **"The priests and the Levites who were in all Israel" (v13).** The Levites had no tribal territory of their own. Joshua gave them forty-eight towns scattered through all the tribes, with "common lands," pasture around each town (Josh 21; Num 35:1–8). So when the kingdom split, many priests and Levites found themselves living in Jeroboam's territory.
- **"Jeroboam and his sons cast them out from serving as priests of the LORD" (v14).** Jeroboam feared that if his people kept going to Jerusalem to worship, their hearts would turn back to David's house (1 Kgs 12:26–27). So he built his own shrines and installed his own priests "from among all the people, who were not of the Levites" (1 Kgs 12:31). The Levites lost their jobs and their standing. **"Left their common lands and their holdings"** means they gave up homes, fields and income to stay faithful. "And his sons" suggests the exodus went on through Jeroboam's dynasty, not only in one year.
- **"The high places… the goat idols… the calves that he had made" (v15).** High places were local hilltop shrines. The calves were the golden calves at Bethel and Dan, which Jeroboam presented with the very words Aaron used at Sinai: "Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" (1 Kgs 12:28; Exod 32:4).
- **"Goat idols" (v15).** The Hebrew is *se'irim*, from a word meaning "hairy," so "he-goats." The same word is used of goat-demons or goat-idols in Leviticus 17:7, where Israel is forbidden to sacrifice to them, and of wild creatures haunting ruins in Isaiah 13:21 and 34:14. The King James Version translated it "devils." Kings does not mention goat idols. The Chronicler adds them to show how far Jeroboam's religion had slid.
- **Were the calves meant to be the LORD?** Faithful readers differ. Many think Jeroboam meant the calves as pedestals for the invisible LORD, like the cherubim over the ark, since ancient Near Eastern gods were often pictured standing on bulls. Others think he meant them as images of the LORD himself, as Aaron's calf was (Exod 32:5). Still others think he slid into worshiping other gods outright. Either way, Scripture calls it sin, and it became the standard by which every later northern king was judged: "the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, which he made Israel to sin" (1 Kgs 15:30).
- **"Those who had set their hearts to seek the LORD God of Israel came after them from all the tribes" (v16).** Ordinary worshipers followed their priests south. "Seek the LORD" is one of the Chronicler's most important phrases. It means to turn to God, depend on him and worship him as he commands (1 Chr 16:11; 2 Chr 7:14). These northerners chose the true temple over convenience, and some of them chose it over their homes.
- **"From all the tribes of Israel" (v16).** The faithful remnant is not only Judah. Every tribe is represented in Jerusalem. The Chronicler wanted his own readers, who were mostly from Judah and Benjamin, to see that the true Israel had always been gathered from all twelve tribes around God's house (compare 15:9; 30:11, 18).
- **"They strengthened the kingdom of Judah, and for three years… they walked for three years in the way of David and Solomon" (v17).** The real strength of Judah was not the fifteen forts but these faithful people. Notice the limit: **three years.** In the fourth year Rehoboam abandoned the law (12:1–2). The Chronicler counts Solomon among the faithful models here, as he does throughout, without mentioning his late idolatry.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry admires the Levites for choosing hardship over compromise: "Poverty in the way of duty is to be chosen rather than plenty in the way of sin." He adds, "Where God's faithful priests are his faithful people should be," and on the three years, "We retain our strength while we cleave to God and our duty, and no longer." Guzik observes that the north was hit twice, once by false worship and once by losing its most godly people. He cites G. Campbell Morgan that Jeroboam tried to bend religion to serve the state and so ruined both. JFB calls the priests' arrival an "accession of moral power" for Judah and describes Jeroboam's new priests as low and unworthy men. The Tyndale notes lay out the views on the calves given above.

#### Rehoboam's Family (vv18–23)
- **"Mahalath the daughter of Jerimoth the son of David" (v18).** Rehoboam married within the royal family. Jerimoth is not in David's list of sons by his wives (1 Chr 3:1–9), so JFB and others think he was a son by a concubine. **Abihail** was the daughter of Eliab, David's oldest brother (1 Sam 16:6). Mahalath was descended from Jesse on both sides.
- **"Maacah the daughter of Absalom" (v20).** "Daughter" in Hebrew can mean any female descendant. Absalom had one daughter, Tamar (2 Sam 14:27), and 13:2 names Abijah's mother as the daughter of Uriel of Gibeah. Most interpreters think Maacah was Absalom's granddaughter, the daughter of Tamar and Uriel. Josephus says the same. Absalom, of course, was the son who rebelled against David (2 Sam 15–18). His bloodline is now joined to the throne.
- **"Loved Maacah… above all his wives and concubines" (v21).** This echoes Jacob's favoring of Rachel over Leah (Gen 29:30), and the trouble that brought.
- **"Eighteen wives and sixty concubines, and fathered twenty-eight sons and sixty daughters" (v21).** The law told Israel's king not to "acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away" (Deut 17:17). Rehoboam's harem was far smaller than Solomon's seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (1 Kgs 11:3), but it was the same disobedience. These figures probably cover his whole reign, not just his first five years. In the ancient world a large family was a sign of a king's power and blessing, and Chronicles often counts children as a blessing (1 Chr 25:5; 26:4–5). Here the blessing and the warning sit side by side.
- **"Appointed Abijah the son of Maacah as chief prince among his brothers, for he intended to make him king" (v22).** Abijah was not the firstborn. The law forbade a man with two wives to pass over the firstborn of the less-loved wife in favor of the son of the favorite (Deut 21:15–17). That law was about inheritance, not crowns, and David himself chose Solomon over older sons. Still, the favoritism is plain. Naming Abijah early, perhaps as co-regent, did make the succession clear and avoided the bloody scramble that marked David's last years (1 Kgs 1).
- **"He dealt wisely and distributed some of his sons through all the districts" (v23).** Each son got a fortified city, plenty of supplies, and wives. It kept them busy, prosperous, far apart from one another, and unlikely to plot against Abijah. "Dealt wisely" (*bin*) is the language of shrewd understanding. It is a striking word to use of a man whose great failure in chapter 10 was foolish counsel. He had learned something, if late.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the harem, "though far smaller than his father's, was equally in violation of the law," and calls the choice of Abijah a breach of Deuteronomy 21:15 driven by fondness for his mother. Guzik, through Payne, says Rehoboam willfully disregarded God's law and his father's example. Matthew Henry gives him credit for not marrying foreign wives, unlike Solomon, but treats the large household as self-indulgence that weakened him. On v23 he notes Rehoboam's wisdom was "dearly bought with the loss of a kingdom," and that "After-wisdom is better than none at all." JFB and Clarke (through Guzik) both call the scattering of the sons shrewd policy, and JFB contrasts it with Eastern courts where a new king's brothers were blinded or killed.

#### Pointing to Christ
The real strength of David's little kingdom was not its forts but the worshipers who left everything to be near God's house. Jesus told a Samaritan woman whose people descended in part from Jeroboam's kingdom that the hour was coming when true worshipers would not be tied to Bethel or Jerusalem but would "worship the Father in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24). He is the true temple (John 2:19–21), and he gathers his people from every tribe and nation. Those Levites gave up their lands to stay with the LORD's altar. Jesus promised that no one who leaves house or lands for his sake will fail to receive a hundredfold, with persecutions, and eternal life (Mark 10:29–30).
