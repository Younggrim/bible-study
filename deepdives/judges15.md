*Setting:* Samson's wedding at Timnah ended in a betrayed riddle, thirty dead men at Ashkelon, and a bride given to the best man (ch 14). Samson went home angry. Some months later he comes back, and the feud that God was quietly using "to seek an opportunity against the Philistines" (14:4) flares into open conflict. Chapter 15 is a spiral of revenge: each side pays the other back, and it grows until Philistine troops march into Judah. There Samson is betrayed by his own people, and then wins his most famous victory with a donkey's jawbone. The chapter ends with Samson, for the first time in the story, calling on the LORD.

#### Samson Defeats the Philistines (vv1–20)
- **"At the time of wheat harvest" (v1).** Wheat was harvested in late spring, about May, in the dry season, when the fields were ready to burn. Samson brings "a young goat," a customary gift, and goes to see his wife. In some marriages of the time the wife stayed in her father's house and the husband visited, which may be what Samson assumed.
- **"I really thought that you utterly hated her" (v2).** The father's excuse is weak, since he gave her away within days (14:20). Offering the "more beautiful" younger sister instead treats his daughters like goods to trade. Israel's law forbade a man to marry two sisters while both were living (Lev 18:18). Jacob's marriage to Leah and Rachel shows the grief that could cause (Gen 29–30).
- **"This time I shall be innocent in regard to the Philistines" (v3).** Samson treats the insult as permission. He is thinking of private payback, not of God's people. But his personal grudge falls on the Philistines as a whole, and that is the direction God's purpose runs (13:5).
- **300 foxes and torches (vv4–5).** The Hebrew word *shu'al* covers both foxes and jackals. JFB and Guzik think jackals, which roam in packs and would be easier to catch in numbers, though the text does not say how he did it or how long it took. Tied in pairs with a torch between them, they would run zigzag in panic, spreading fire in every direction. In a dry harvest season, it destroyed the grain stacked for threshing, the standing wheat, and "the olive orchards" (the Hebrew reads "vineyard and olive"). That was a whole year's food and income for the region.
- **"The Philistines came up and burned her and her father with fire" (v6).** This is exactly what the wedding guests had threatened (14:15). She betrayed Samson to escape being burned, and was burned anyway. Matthew Henry: "The mischief we seek to escape by any unlawful practices, we often pull down upon our own heads."
- **"I swear I will be avenged on you, and after that I will quit" (v7).** Revenge never stops where people promise it will. "He struck them hip and thigh with a great blow" (v8) is a proverbial phrase for a crushing, merciless defeat, something like "thoroughly."
- **"The cleft of the rock of Etam" (v8).** Samson goes into hiding in a cave or rocky cleft in Judah's territory. The exact place is unknown. Samson, who started with family, wife and friends, is now alone in a cave.
- **"The Philistines came up and encamped in Judah and made a raid on Lehi" (v9).** Now the Philistine army comes into Israelite land. Lehi means "jaw" or "jawbone," a hint of what is coming. The place may have been named for the shape of a ridge, or for this story (v17).
- **"Do you not know that the Philistines are rulers over us?" (v11).** Three thousand men of Judah come, not to fight the Philistines, but to arrest their own deliverer. Judah was the tribe God first sent against the Canaanites (1:2). Now it sends 3,000 men against one Israelite to please its masters. The Tyndale notes say Judah missed a real chance to throw off Philistine rule. "What then is this that you have done to us?" is an echo of the Philistines' own complaint. Samson answers in the language of the whole feud: "As they did to me, so have I done to them."
- **"Swear to me that you will not attack me yourselves" (v12).** Samson lets his own people tie him up with "two new ropes," the strongest kind (v13). He will not fight fellow Israelites. They promise not to kill him, only to hand him over.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD rushed upon him" (v14).** As the Philistines come "shouting to meet him," the Spirit comes on Samson for the third time (14:6, 19). The new ropes become "as flax that has caught fire." Flax is the plant linen is made from, and its dry fibers flare up and turn to ash in an instant. The ropes did not just snap. They "melted off his hands."
- **"A fresh jawbone of a donkey" (v15).** "Fresh" means from a recently dead animal, still moist and tough, not dry and brittle. A Nazirite was not to touch a dead body, and Samson grabs one more piece of a carcass. With it he strikes down 1,000 men. Shamgar earlier killed 600 with an ox goad (3:31). Both stories show God giving victory with whatever is at hand.
- **"Heaps upon heaps" (v16).** Samson's victory song is a short Hebrew rhyme built on a pun. The word for "donkey" (*chamor*) sounds exactly like the word for "heap." It can be heard as "with the jawbone of a donkey, a donkey, two donkeys," or "a heap, two heaps," and the ESV footnote gives a third sense: "I have made donkeys of them." Notice what the song does not say: it does not mention the LORD. The Spirit gave the victory, and Samson sings about himself.
- **Ramath-lehi (v17).** "The hill of the jawbone." Samson throws the jawbone away and the place gets its name.
- **"He was very thirsty, and he called upon the LORD" (v18).** This is the first time Samson prays in the story. After fighting a thousand men he is near collapse. He calls the victory "this great salvation by the hand of your servant," which is right: it was God's salvation, and Samson was the tool. And he fears falling "into the hands of the uncircumcised." The strongest man in Israel is undone by thirst, a reminder that his strength was never his own.
- **"God split open the hollow place that is at Lehi, and water came out" (v19).** God answers at once, as he gave water from the rock to Israel in the wilderness (Exod 17:6). "His spirit returned, and he revived." The spring was named **En-hakkore**, "the spring of him who called." The name remembers Samson's prayer, not his strength. The same verb "to call" (*qara*) appears in the name and in v18.
- **"He judged Israel in the days of the Philistines twenty years" (v20).** This closing formula would normally end a judge's story. Here it comes before his last chapter, and it repeats in 16:31. "In the days of the Philistines" is telling. Samson led Israel for twenty years, but the Philistines still ruled. He began to save Israel (13:5) but did not finish.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of Judah, "Sin dispirits men, it hides from their eyes the things that belong to their peace." On the jawbone he writes, "This victory was not in the weapon, was not in the arm; but it was in the Spirit of God." On the thirst, he says those who forget to praise God may be driven to pray to him, and "Past experiences of God's power and goodness, are excellent pleas in prayer for further mercy." JFB calls the father's excuse a "mere sham," notes that the Hebrew word means jackals, and gives En-hakkore as "supplication well," a fair rendering of "the spring of the one who called." Guzik observes that revenge is a cycle no one wins, and points to Jesus' teaching against "an eye for an eye" (Matt 5:38–42). He reads Samson's letting himself be bound as faith that God would protect him, and the thirst as God humbling him right after a great victory. The Tyndale notes add that Samson and Judah settled for a small victory instead of pressing on to end Philistine rule.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah handed over its own deliverer, bound with new ropes, to the enemy, because they preferred to keep the peace with their oppressors. Centuries later the leaders of Judah bound Jesus and handed him over to the Romans (Matt 27:2; John 19:15, "We have no king but Caesar"). Samson broke his ropes and killed his enemies. Jesus let the ropes hold, went to the cross, and won his victory by dying for his enemies. And at Lehi, the man who had just won "this great salvation" found that he could not save himself from thirst. Only God could open the spring. Jesus, on the cross, said "I thirst" (John 19:28) so that he could offer to all who call on him "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14).
