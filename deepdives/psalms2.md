*Setting:* Psalm 2, like Psalm 1, has no title, but the early church prayed it as "through the mouth of our father David, your servant" (Acts 4:25). It is a royal psalm, probably first sung at the crowning of a king from David's line in Jerusalem, around 1000 BC or after. In the ancient world a new king's accession was a dangerous moment: subject nations often tested him by rebelling. The psalm answers that threat with God's own decree. Together with Psalm 1 it forms the double doorway into the Psalter. Psalm 1 shows the person who delights in God's law; Psalm 2 shows the King God has installed on Zion. The first begins with "Blessed" and the second ends with it (1:1; 2:12), and both end with someone perishing "in the way" (1:6; 2:12). No psalm is quoted more often in the New Testament as fulfilled in Jesus. It has four stanzas of three verses, and in each a different voice speaks: the rebel nations, the LORD in heaven, the King himself, and finally the psalmist with a warning.

#### The Nations Rage (vv1–3)
- **"Why do the nations rage" (v1).** The question mixes amazement with scorn. "Rage" (*ragash*) suggests a noisy, tumultuous crowd. "Nations" and "peoples" are both plural: the whole world of rulers and races, not one enemy.
- **"And the peoples plot in vain" (v1).** "Plot" is the same verb as "meditate" in 1:2 (*hagah*, to murmur or muse). The blessed man murmurs over God's law day and night; the nations murmur over schemes against God. Their plotting is "in vain," literally "emptiness," headed nowhere from the start.
- **"The kings of the earth set themselves" (v2).** They take a stand, they form a coalition. The rebellion is organized.
- **"Against the LORD and against his Anointed" (v2).** "Anointed" is *mashiach*, the word that gives us "Messiah." In Greek it is *Christos*, "Christ." Israel's kings were anointed with oil as a sign that God had chosen them (1 Sam 16:13). To rebel against God's king is to rebel against God, and the psalm sets the two side by side.
- **"Let us burst their bonds apart and cast away their cords" (v3).** The rebels see God's rule as slavery. A vassal king who threw off his overlord's yoke used just this language (compare Jer 27:2; Nah 1:13). It is the oldest human impulse: to be free of God, to make our own rules (Gen 3:5). The irony is that God's "yoke" is the one that frees (Matt 11:29–30).
- **Fulfilled at the cross.** The first church quoted these verses when Peter and John were threatened by the authorities, and applied them precisely: "Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel," gathered "against your holy servant Jesus, whom you anointed" (Acts 4:25–28). The plot reached its height in Jesus' trial and crucifixion, and it was "in vain": God turned it into the very plan he had "predestined to take place."
- **Commentators:** JFB says "nations" here means the nations generally, "not as opposed to Jews," and that the "bands" and "cords" are "the restraints of government." It sees the verse fulfilled in Christ's trial, with Pilate, Herod and the crowd. Guzik traces the pattern back to Babel, where people joined forces against God, and insists that those who think God's rule is bondage have misread him: he breaks chains rather than forging them. Henry calls the psalm evangelical: where Psalm 1 shows our duty, this one shows our Saviour.

#### The LORD Laughs (vv4–6)
- **"He who sits in the heavens laughs" (v4).** The scene changes from a frantic earth to a calm throne. The rebels are on their feet and plotting; God is seated. His laughter is not cruelty but the certainty of a King who cannot be overthrown (compare 37:13; 59:8). Next to his power, the world's armies are like children shaking their fists at the sky.
- **"The Lord holds them in derision" (v4).** Here "Lord" is *Adonai*, "Master," the title of one who rules. It answers their attempt to throw off his rule.
- **"Then he will speak to them in his wrath" (v5).** God's laughter turns to speech, and his speech is action. "Terrify" (*bahal*) means to throw into panic. The word God speaks is enough to undo every plan.
- **"As for me, I have set my King on Zion, my holy hill" (v6).** In Hebrew the "I" is emphatic: "But *I*…" Against all their schemes, God has already acted. "Set" can mean "installed" or "consecrated," as one pours out an offering. Zion was the hill in Jerusalem where David brought the ark and later where the temple stood. It is the place of God's presence and God's King together. The King is "my King." He reigns because God put him there, not because he won a vote.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws out God's calm: he sits, unhurried and unworried. He passes on the example of the emperor Diocletian, who tried to wipe out Christianity around AD 303 and is now a footnote, while Christ is worshipped across the earth. He also notes that God speaks before he strikes, which is mercy. JFB describes God as "enthroned in quiet dignities," whose laughter expresses "supreme contempt," and explains "my king" as "appointed by Me and for Me."

#### The King Speaks (vv7–9)
- **"I will tell of the decree" (v7).** Now the King himself speaks. "Decree" (*choq*) is a legal word: a fixed statute. At a coronation, the new king may have been handed a written covenant document (compare 2 Kings 11:12, where Joash is given "the testimony"). This King announces the terms God has set.
- **"You are my Son; today I have begotten you" (v7).** This rests on God's promise to David about his son: "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Sam 7:14). Other ancient kings claimed to be physically born of gods. In Israel the language is covenant adoption: on the day of his crowning, "today," God declares the king his son, his representative on earth (89:26–27). "Begotten" (*yalad*) is the ordinary word for fathering a child, used here as a royal title.
- **How the New Testament reads v7.** The verse is applied to Jesus in three settings. At his baptism and transfiguration, the Father's voice echoes it: "You are my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11; 9:7). Paul says it was fulfilled when God raised Jesus from the dead (Acts 13:33; compare Rom 1:4, "declared to be the Son of God in power… by his resurrection"). Hebrews uses it to show the Son is greater than angels and appointed high priest (Heb 1:5; 5:5). Many church fathers also read "today" as God's eternal present, so the verse speaks of the Son eternally begotten of the Father, the phrase used in the Nicene Creed. These readings fit together: the eternal Son, born as David's descendant, was publicly declared and enthroned as the messianic King at his resurrection.
- **"Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage" (v8).** Great kings invited favored vassals to make a request (compare 21:2, 4; 1 Kings 3:5). The nations who were raging in v1 become the King's inheritance. "The ends of the earth" means the whole world. No king of Judah ever ruled that far. Only the risen Christ can say, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" (Matt 28:18–19).
- **"You shall break them with a rod of iron" (v9).** A king's rod or scepter was a symbol of rule. An iron one cannot be broken. **"Dash them in pieces like a potter's vessel"** may echo an Egyptian practice: pharaohs wrote the names of rebel cities on clay pots and smashed them as a curse. A clay pot, once shattered, cannot be mended. Revelation picks up v9 three times of Christ's final victory and his people's share in it (Rev 2:26–27; 12:5; 19:15). The Greek Old Testament, followed by Revelation, reads "shepherd them with a rod of iron," since the Hebrew consonants for "break" and "shepherd" are the same. Shepherding and judging both belong to this King.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v7 as a "solemn recognition" of the Son and his installation as mediatorial King, and says this "by no means impugns the Eternal Sonship" of his divine nature. It sees the shattered pot as a picture of ruin that "cannot be mended." Guzik uses Hebrews 1:5 to show the Son is above the angels, and stresses that "begotten" is not "created": a man makes a statue but fathers a child of his own nature. Henry's outline sees in vv7–9 the confirmation and promised growth of the Messiah's kingdom, and notes that nothing in the psalm fits David alone.

#### Kiss the Son (vv10–12)
- **"Now therefore, O kings, be wise; be warned, O rulers of the earth" (v10).** The psalmist turns to the rebels and, surprisingly, offers them a way out. The same kings who plotted in v2 are invited to wise up. "Be warned" means to accept correction, to let themselves be taught. Judgment has been announced, but it has not yet fallen.
- **"Serve the LORD with fear, and rejoice with trembling" (v11).** Joy and trembling go together. Reverent fear does not cancel gladness; it is the proper shape of it. Paul says something similar: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you" (Phil 2:12–13).
- **"Kiss the Son" (v12).** Kissing a king's hand or feet was a sign of submission and loyalty (compare 1 Sam 10:1; 1 Kings 19:18). The word for "son" here is *bar*, which is the Aramaic word for son, rather than the Hebrew *ben* used in v7. That is unusual, and ancient translations handled the line in different ways. The Greek Old Testament reads "take hold of instruction," and some ancient Jewish translators and Jerome render "worship in purity." Some modern scholars suggest emending the text, for example to "kiss his feet." Most English versions keep "Kiss the Son," which fits the flow of the psalm: the Son God declared in v7 is the one the kings must honor. The lexicon lists all these options.
- **"Lest he be angry, and you perish in the way" (v12).** "The way" picks up Psalm 1:6: the way of the wicked perishes, and so will those who refuse the King. **"For his wrath is quickly kindled"** can also be rendered "for his wrath may soon flare up." The warning is urgent: there is still time, but not unlimited time.
- **"Blessed are all who take refuge in him" (v12).** The psalm, which began with raging, ends with refuge. The King whose rod shatters rebels is also a shelter for anyone who runs to him. "Take refuge" (*chasah*) is a favorite word in the Psalms, used of hiding under God's wings (36:7; 91:4). "Blessed" closes the frame that Psalm 1 opened. The happy person of Psalm 1, who loves God's word, is the same person who here shelters in God's Son.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the kiss as both submission and affection: we are to know our place before the King and also to love him. He passes on a writer who calls kissing "the token of subjugation and friendship." JFB takes "kiss the Son" to mean submitting to "the authority of the Son," explains "perish from the way" as perishing "suddenly and hopelessly," and notes that "kindled but a little" can mean "in a little time." Henry closes with the call for kings "to yield themselves the willing subjects of this kingdom," and says the psalm stands near the head of the book because we come to God only "in the name of a Mediator."

#### Pointing to Christ
This psalm is not forced onto Jesus. It is one of the main places the apostles learned to call him "the Christ" and "the Son of God." Every king of David's line was crowned with it, and every one fell short of it: none ruled the ends of the earth, and the line ended in exile. Then the nations and Israel's rulers raged together against God's Anointed and nailed him to a cross. God's answer was not panic but resurrection: "You are my Son; today I have begotten you" (Acts 13:33). He now sits enthroned, the nations are being given to him as his inheritance through the gospel, and one day his rod of iron will end all rebellion (Rev 19:15). Until then the psalm's last word is an open door. The same King who is to be feared is the one in whom anyone, ruler or ordinary person, can take refuge and be blessed.
