*Setting:* The Gospel of John was most likely written by the apostle John, "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (21:20–24), late in the first century, probably from Ephesus. By then Matthew, Mark and Luke were circulating, and John does not repeat most of what they tell. There is no birth story here, no shepherds or wise men. Instead John goes back before Bethlehem, before creation itself, and says who Jesus was "in the beginning." Then he moves quickly to John the Baptist at the Jordan and the first men who followed Jesus. John's stated purpose comes near the end: "that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (20:31). Chapter 1 lays out nearly every theme of the book: life, light, witness, belief, glory, grace and truth.

#### The Word Became Flesh (vv1–18)
- **"In the beginning" (v1).** These are the first words of the Bible in Greek (Gen 1:1). Genesis starts with creation. John starts with the one who was already there when creation began. He does not say "in the beginning the Word came to be" but "was." There was no point when the Word was not.
- **"Was the Word" (v1).** "Word" is *logos*. For Greek readers, *logos* was the reason or rational order that held the universe together, a philosopher's idea. For Jewish readers, "the word of the LORD" was God himself acting and speaking: "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made" (Ps 33:6). Jewish writers also pictured God's Wisdom as present with him at creation (Prov 8:22–31). John uses a word both audiences knew and then says something neither expected: this Word is a person, and he became human.
- **"And the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (v1).** Three short clauses. The Word existed eternally, he was "with" God (the Greek *pros* suggests face to face, in relationship), and he was God. "With God" shows he is distinct from the Father. "Was God" shows he shares the Father's very nature. This is the seed of the doctrine of the Trinity.
- **"A god"?** In the Greek, the last clause has no article before "God" (*theos ēn ho logos*). Jehovah's Witnesses translate it "the Word was a god." But Greek regularly drops the article on a noun that describes the subject, especially when it comes before the verb. The same Gospel uses *theos* without the article for the Father (1:6, 12, 18). The point of leaving it off is that "the Word" and "God" are not simply interchangeable, since the Father is also God. The Word has the full nature of God without being the Father. Scholars across traditions reject "a god," which would make John, a strict Jewish monotheist, teach two gods.
- **"In him was life, and the life was the light of men" (v4).** "Life" is *zōē*, John's word for life in its fullest sense, the life God has in himself and gives, which John often calls "eternal life" (3:16; 17:3). That life is "light": it shows people the truth about God and themselves. Later Jesus will say, "I am the light of the world" (8:12) and "I am… the life" (11:25; 14:6).
- **"The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it" (v5).** "Shines" is present tense: it keeps shining. The verb for "overcome" is *katalambanō*, which can mean "to grasp" in the sense of understand (KJV: "comprehended it not") or "to seize, overpower." John may intend both: darkness neither understood the light nor managed to put it out (compare 12:35, where the same verb means "overtake"). The whole Gospel shows darkness trying, all the way to the cross, and failing.
- **"He was not the light, but came to bear witness about the light" (v8).** Some still followed John the Baptist decades later (Acts 19:1–7). The Gospel is careful to put him in his place: a witness, and a great one, but not the light. "Witness" (*martyria* and its verb) appears over forty times in John. The Gospel is presented as testimony in a trial about who Jesus is.
- **"He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him" (v11).** In Greek the first "his own" is neuter (his own things, his own home) and the second is masculine (his own people). He came home, to Israel, and his household shut the door. Chapters 1–12 tell that story.
- **"To all who did receive him… he gave the right to become children of God" (v12).** "Right" (*exousia*) means authority or legal standing. Receiving him and believing in his name are the same thing here. John never uses the noun "faith"; he uses the verb "believe" nearly a hundred times. Faith in John is always something done.
- **"Born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God" (v13).** Three denials. Not by ancestry ("blood" is plural in Greek, perhaps meaning human descent, being a child of Abraham), not by natural desire, not by a husband's decision. Becoming God's child is God's doing. This prepares for "You must be born again" (3:7).
- **"And the Word became flesh" (v14).** This is the center of the prologue. "Flesh" (*sarx*) is a blunt word, human nature in its weakness and mortality. Greek thought tended to see the body as a prison for the soul and divinity as far from matter. Jewish faith knew God was not a man (Num 23:19). John says the eternal Word *became* flesh, not merely appeared in a body or wore one like a costume. Later teachers who denied that Jesus had a real body are answered here and in 1 John 4:2.
- **"And dwelt among us" (v14).** "Dwelt" is *skēnoō*, "pitched a tent," from *skēnē*, the Greek Old Testament word for the tabernacle. God once lived among Israel in a tent in the wilderness, and his glory filled it (Exod 25:8–9; 40:34–35). Now God's presence has come to live among people in a human body. The Greek word's consonants (s-k-n) may even echo the Hebrew *shakan*, "to dwell," from which later Jewish writers took the word *Shekinah* for God's glorious presence.
- **"We have seen his glory" (v14).** "We" is the apostles, eyewitnesses (1 John 1:1–3). In the Old Testament no one could see God's glory and live; Moses saw only his back (Exod 33:18–23). The disciples saw it in Jesus, in his signs (2:11) and finally in the cross and resurrection, which John calls his "glorification" (12:23; 17:1).
- **"The only Son from the Father" (v14).** "Only" is *monogenēs*. The KJV's "only begotten" assumed it came from the verb "to beget." Most scholars now trace it to *monos*, "only," and *genos*, "kind": "one of a kind," "unique." It is used of Isaac (Heb 11:17), who was not Abraham's only son but his unique son of promise. Jesus is the Son in a way no one else is.
- **"Full of grace and truth" (v14).** This echoes God's own self-description to Moses on Sinai: "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exod 34:6). The Hebrew pair *hesed* and *emet*, covenant love and faithfulness, lies behind John's "grace and truth." What God declared about himself in words to Moses now stands in the flesh.
- **"From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace" (v16).** Literally "grace in place of grace" (*charin anti charitos*). Most take it as one grace following another without end, like waves rolling in. Others read it as the new grace in Christ replacing the earlier grace given through the law, which fits v17.
- **"For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (v17).** This is not law bad, grace good. The law was itself a gift from God. But Moses could only receive and pass on what was given. Jesus *is* the grace and truth. This is the first time the name "Jesus Christ" appears in the Gospel.
- **"God the only Son, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (v18).** Manuscripts differ here. Many later ones read "the only Son." The oldest and best Greek manuscripts read *monogenēs theos*, "the only God" or "the unique one, himself God," and the ESV follows them. So the prologue begins and ends calling the Word God (vv1, 18). "At the Father's side" is literally "in the bosom of the Father," the place of closest intimacy, the way the beloved disciple reclined next to Jesus (13:23). "Made him known" is *exēgeomai*, from which English gets "exegesis": Jesus is the one who explains God.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that both Jewish and Greek readers had a *logos* idea, and he answers the "a god" translation at length, pointing out that the article is commonly dropped before a predicate noun and that the Watchtower itself is inconsistent here. He also distinguishes *zōē*, life in the full sense, from *bios*, mere biological life. JFB says the Word was "in substance and essence God" and stresses that "Jesus is the Son of God; He is never said to have become such." On v14 JFB renders "dwelt" as "tabernacled or pitched his tent," and says the disciples saw the glory not "by the eye of sense, which saw in Him only 'the carpenter.'" JFB takes v16 as grace given "in successive communications and larger measures." Henry: "This eternal Word, this true Light shines, but the darkness comprehends it not," and in the incarnation he "was, after another manner, God manifested in the flesh."

#### The Testimony of John the Baptist (vv19–28)
- **"The Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem" (v19).** In John's Gospel, "the Jews" often means the religious authorities in Jerusalem, not the Jewish people as a whole. Jesus, the disciples and John himself were all Jews. A popular preacher drawing crowds to the wilderness and baptizing people needed investigating.
- **"Are you Elijah?" He said, "I am not" (v21).** Malachi promised Elijah before the day of the LORD (Mal 4:5). The questioners meant Elijah returned from heaven in person. John says no. Yet Jesus later says John "is Elijah who is to come" (Matt 11:14), and the angel said he would come "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17). John was not Elijah reborn but fulfilled Elijah's role. Perhaps also, John simply refused every title that might draw attention to himself.
- **"Are you the Prophet?" (v21).** Moses promised that God would raise up "a prophet like me" (Deut 18:15–18). Some Jews identified this prophet with the Messiah, some thought him a separate figure (7:40–41). John says simply, "No."
- **"I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness" (v23).** He quotes Isaiah 40:3, the chapter that begins "Comfort, comfort my people." A voice is heard and gone. It does not point to itself but to the message. All four Gospels connect John with this verse, but only here does John apply it to himself.
- **"Then why are you baptizing?" (v25).** Washings for purity were common in Judaism, and Gentile converts were immersed. But to call *Israelites* to be baptized, as if they too were outsiders who needed cleansing, was startling. The prophets had connected the end times with God cleansing his people with water (Ezek 36:25; Zech 13:1), which may be why the questioners link baptism with the Christ, Elijah or the Prophet.
- **"The strap of whose sandal I am not worthy to untie" (v27).** Untying a master's sandals was the lowest household slave's task. A later rabbinic saying held that a disciple should do anything for his teacher that a slave does, except untie his sandals. John says he is unworthy even of that.
- **"Bethany across the Jordan" (v28).** Not the Bethany near Jerusalem where Lazarus lived (11:1), but another place east of the Jordan. Its exact location is uncertain. Some later manuscripts read "Bethabara," following Origen, who could not find a Bethany there in his day. The best manuscripts read "Bethany." A site now called Al-Maghtas, on the Jordanian side, is a traditional candidate. Some scholars think "Bethany beyond the Jordan" was the region of Batanea far to the north.
- **Commentators:** Guzik says John was Elijah "in a sense," in office and spirit, and compares his baptism to preparing a city for a royal visit. He notes that Jews normally baptized Gentile converts, so Jews accepting baptism admitted they needed cleansing too. JFB says the questioners "seem not to have been agreed whether he were the same with the Messiah or no," and defends "Bethany" from "nearly all the best and most ancient manuscripts." Henry finds it a sad sign of human blindness that the light needed a witness at all.

#### Behold, the Lamb of God (vv29–34)
- **"The next day" (v29).** John marks a series of days: v29, v35, v43 and then "on the third day" (2:1), the wedding at Cana. Counting them gives roughly a week. Some see a new creation week, matching "In the beginning" (v1). It is a suggestion, but the careful counting is deliberate.
- **"Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (v29).** What lamb did John mean? There are several Old Testament lambs, and the phrase may gather them all: the Passover lamb, whose blood saved Israel's firstborn (Exod 12); the lamb offered every morning and evening in the temple (Exod 29:38–42); the lamb God would provide, as Abraham told Isaac (Gen 22:8); and the Servant who "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" bore "the sin of many" (Isa 53:7, 12). John's Gospel later has Jesus die at the hour the Passover lambs were being killed, with none of his bones broken (19:14, 36; Exod 12:46). "Takes away" (*airō*) means lifts up and carries off. "Sin" is singular, the whole burden. "The world" means it is not only for Israel.
- **"I saw the Spirit descend from heaven like a dove, and it remained on him" (v32).** John's Gospel does not describe Jesus' baptism directly, but this refers to it (Mark 1:9–11). The key word is "remained" (*menō*), one of John's favorite words ("abide"). The Spirit came on Old Testament judges, kings and prophets for a time or a task. On Jesus he rests permanently, as Isaiah promised: "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him" (Isa 11:2).
- **"This is he who baptizes with the Holy Spirit" (v33).** John baptizes with water, an outward sign. Jesus will plunge people into God's Spirit (Ezek 36:26–27; Joel 2:28). That happens in John's Gospel after the resurrection (20:22; also 7:39).
- **"This is the Son of God" (v34).** A few early manuscripts read "the Chosen One of God," which would echo Isaiah 42:1: "my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my Spirit upon him." Most manuscripts and translations have "Son of God." Either way, John's witness is complete: Jesus is the Lamb, the one with the Spirit, the Son.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the Lamb "the one God-ordained, God-gifted sacrificial offering," says "takes away" means both "taketh up and taketh away," and notes that "of the world" means "not of Israel only." Henry connects the title to the Passover lamb and the daily sacrifices and says Christ "purchases pardon for all that repent and believe the gospel." Guzik sees the shadow of the cross falling across Jesus' ministry from its first day.

#### Jesus Calls the First Disciples (vv35–42)
- **"What are you seeking?" (v38).** These are Jesus' first words in this Gospel, and they are a question. It goes deeper than "what do you want?" He will ask it again of the soldiers in the garden (18:4) and of Mary Magdalene at the tomb (20:15).
- **"Rabbi… where are you staying?" (v38).** "Staying" is *menō* again, the word used of the Spirit remaining on Jesus (v32). They want more than an address. They want to be with him. Jesus will later say, "Abide in me" (15:4).
- **"It was about the tenth hour" (v39).** Counting from sunrise, about 4 p.m. Such a small, vivid detail suggests an eyewitness who never forgot the hour. Many think the unnamed second disciple was John himself, the author.
- **"Andrew, Simon Peter's brother" (v40).** Andrew is always introduced through his more famous brother. But Andrew brings people to Jesus: Peter here, the boy with the loaves (6:8–9), and the Greeks who wanted to see Jesus (12:22).
- **"We have found the Messiah" (v41).** *Messias* is Hebrew-Aramaic for "anointed one." John translates for his Greek readers: "which means Christ." Only John's Gospel uses the Hebrew word in Greek (here and 4:25).
- **"You shall be called Cephas" (which means Peter) (v42).** *Kepha* is Aramaic for "rock," and *Petros* is the Greek. In the Old Testament God renamed people when he gave them a new calling: Abram to Abraham, Jacob to Israel. Jesus names Simon for what he will become, not what he is yet. The Synoptic Gospels place the naming later (Matt 16:18) or at the call of the Twelve (Mark 3:16). John's account suggests the name was given early and its meaning explained later.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that Jesus taught by inviting people to live with him, that the "tenth hour" sounds like the memory of an eyewitness, and that Peter often looked more like sand than rock when he was named. JFB reckons the tenth hour as "four P.M., according to Jewish reckoning." Henry: "It is good for us to be where Christ is, wherever it be."

#### Jesus Calls Philip and Nathanael (vv43–51)
- **"He found Philip and said to him, 'Follow me'" (v43).** Others came to Jesus through someone else. Philip is sought out by Jesus himself. "Follow" is the word for a disciple walking behind his teacher, and it became the word for the whole Christian life.
- **"Him of whom Moses in the Law and also the prophets wrote" (v45).** Philip sums up the Old Testament as pointing to one person. Jesus says the same: "If you believed Moses, you would believe me; for he wrote of me" (5:46; Luke 24:27).
- **"Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" (v46).** Nazareth was a small, obscure village, never mentioned in the Old Testament. Nathanael, from Cana a few miles away (21:2), may be voicing local rivalry. Philip does not argue: "Come and see," the same invitation Jesus gave (v39).
- **"Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit!" (v47).** "Deceit" (*dolos*) is the very word the Greek Old Testament uses for Jacob tricking his father: "Your brother came deceitfully" (Gen 27:35). Jacob was renamed Israel. Nathanael is a true Israelite, without Jacob's guile. The Jacob theme continues in v51.
- **"When you were under the fig tree, I saw you" (v48).** Something happened under that fig tree that Nathanael knew no one else could know. To sit under one's own fig tree was a picture of peace (Mic 4:4; Zech 3:10), and later rabbis were known to study Scripture under fig trees. Some suggest Nathanael was praying or reading, perhaps about Jacob, but the text does not say. What matters is that Jesus saw him.
- **"Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!" (v49).** "Son of God" here likely means the Messiah, the King promised to David (2 Sam 7:14; Ps 2:6–7). Nathanael's confession is true, though he does not yet grasp all that John's readers know from v1–18.
- **"You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man" (v51).** This is Jacob's dream at Bethel: "a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven. And behold, the angels of God were ascending and descending on it!" (Gen 28:12). Jacob called the place "the house of God… the gate of heaven" (Gen 28:17). Jesus says *he* is that ladder, the meeting place of heaven and earth. "Son of Man" is Jesus' favorite name for himself, from Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds and receives everlasting dominion. It was a less political title than "Messiah," and Jesus filled it with his own meaning.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls Philip's "Come and see" a "Noble remedy against preconceived opinions," and says Nathanael was "not only no hypocrite, but with a guileless simplicity." He notes that the fig tree was a place where "retirement for meditation and prayer was not uncommon," and calls Jesus "the real Ladder" between heaven and earth. Guzik also cites the rabbinic picture of studying Scripture under a fig tree, and points to Daniel 7 behind "Son of Man." Henry warns against prejudice toward people or places, since good often comes from where we least expect it, and notes, "In Nathanael there was no guile."

#### Pointing to Christ
John 1 begins in eternity and ends under a fig tree. The Word who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made, became flesh and pitched his tent among us, full of grace and truth. John the Baptist points to him as the Lamb who takes away the sin of the world. The first disciples hear, follow, stay with him and bring others. And Jesus promises that he himself is the ladder Jacob saw, the only way between earth and heaven. No one has ever seen God, but the only Son has made him known. To see Jesus is to see the Father (14:9).
