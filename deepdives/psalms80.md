*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Lilies. A Testimony. Of Asaph, a Psalm." "Lilies" (*Shoshannim*) is probably the name of a tune (45; 69). "Testimony" (*Eduth*) may refer to God's covenant word, and JFB reads it as "a testimony of God to His people." Psalm 80 is a national lament. Its tribal names point north: Joseph, Ephraim, Benjamin and Manasseh. Many interpreters, Henry and JFB among them, connect it with the Assyrian invasions that destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC (2 Kgs 17:5–6). The Greek Old Testament adds to the title "concerning the Assyrian." Some think it was prayed in Jerusalem by people grieving for their northern brothers, perhaps in Hezekiah's day when refugees from the north came south (2 Chr 30:6–11). Others place it earlier, in a time of northern defeat. Psalm 79 mourned Judah's fall; Psalm 80 mourns Israel's. The psalm is built around a refrain repeated three times (vv3, 7, 19), and each time God's name grows longer, from "O God" to "O God of hosts" to "O LORD God of hosts," as if the prayer is gathering strength. In the middle stands one of the Bible's great pictures of God's people: a vine brought out of Egypt.

#### Shine Forth (vv1–3)
- **"Give ear, O Shepherd of Israel, you who lead Joseph like a flock" (v1).** The psalm picks up exactly where Psalms 77–79 left off: God led his people "like a flock" (77:20; 78:52), and they are "the sheep of your pasture" (79:13). The title "Shepherd of Israel" goes back to Jacob, who blessed Joseph in the name of "the God who has been my shepherd all my life long" and "the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel" (Gen 48:15; 49:24). "Joseph" stands for the northern tribes, descended from Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh. Henry: "Christ is the great and good Shepherd" (John 10:11).
- **"You who are enthroned upon the cherubim, shine forth" (v1).** The cherubim were the golden winged figures on the lid of the ark of the covenant, and God was pictured as enthroned above them (Exod 25:22; 1 Sam 4:4; 2 Kgs 19:15). "Shine forth" asks God to blaze out in glory, as at Sinai: "he shone forth from Mount Paran" (Deut 33:2; Ps 50:2).
- **"Before Ephraim and Benjamin and Manasseh, stir up your might and come to save us!" (v2).** In the wilderness these three tribes, the descendants of Rachel, camped together on the west side of the tabernacle and marched directly behind the ark (Num 2:17–24; 10:21–24). JFB: "These tribes marched next the ark." So the prayer pictures God once again rising up and going out before his people, as when Moses cried, "Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered" (Num 10:35). Benjamin is included, though it ended up mostly with Judah, perhaps as a hope that the divided family could be one again.
- **"Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved!" (v3).** "Restore us" (*hashivenu*) means "turn us back" or "bring us back." It can mean bring us back from exile, restore our fortunes, or turn our hearts back to you. The KJV has "Turn us again." "Let your face shine" quotes the priestly blessing: "the LORD make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you" (Num 6:25). God's shining face means his favor and presence. The psalm asks for the blessing every Israelite knew.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Spurgeon, reads "restore us" as a prayer for the people's own hearts to be turned back, not only their circumstances, and, with Maclaren and Trapp, links the shining face to Numbers 6. Henry: "Turn us again, O God." JFB's note on the camp order explains why these three tribes are named together.

#### How Long Will You Be Angry? (vv4–7)
- **"O LORD God of hosts" (v4).** "Hosts" means armies, heavenly and earthly. The title names God as the commander of every power in the universe, which makes Israel's defeat all the more puzzling.
- **"How long will you be angry with your people's prayers?" (v4).** Literally "will you smoke against" their prayers, as if God's anger rose like smoke and blocked their prayers from reaching him (Lam 3:44, "you have wrapped yourself with a cloud so that no prayer can pass through"). This is a bold complaint. The problem is not that they have stopped praying. It is that their prayers seem to bounce off. Henry: God seemed "angry against the prayer of his people."
- **"You have fed them with the bread of tears and given them tears to drink in full measure" (v5).** Tears have become their daily meals (42:3; 102:9). "Full measure" translates *shalish*, "a third," probably a large measure, a bowl-full. JFB: "bread of tears—still an Eastern figure for affliction." Notice that "you have fed them": God is the one who sets this table, as he once fed them manna (78:24).
- **"You make us an object of contention for our neighbors, and our enemies laugh among themselves" (v6).** "Contention" means strife: the neighbors fight over the spoils, or argue about Israel. And the enemies laugh (79:4). The Tyndale notes observe that the worst of it is not the enemy's victory but knowing that God's anger lies behind it.
- **"Restore us, O God of hosts" (v7).** The refrain returns, now with "of hosts" added.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Maclaren and Spurgeon, sees three troubles in vv4–6: an angry God, a weeping nation and mocking enemies. He stresses that the first is the deepest, because if God is restored to favor, the other two take care of themselves.

#### The Vine Out of Egypt (vv8–13)
- **"You brought a vine out of Egypt" (v8).** The vine is one of Scripture's main pictures of Israel (Gen 49:22; Isa 5:1–7; Jer 2:21; Ezek 15; 17; 19:10–14; Hos 10:1). Vines needed years of patient care before they bore fruit, and a vineyard was the farmer's pride. The picture retells the history of Psalm 78 in a single image: God dug up his vine in Egypt, carried it out, "drove out the nations and planted it." Henry: "It was brought out of Egypt with a high hand" (Exod 14:8).
- **"You cleared the ground for it; it took deep root and filled the land" (v9).** Clearing the ground meant removing stones and weeds, as in Isaiah's song of the vineyard (Isa 5:2). God did the hard work. Israel grew because God prepared the soil.
- **"The mountains were covered with its shade, the mighty cedars with its branches" (v10).** An extraordinary vine: it overshadows the hills and climbs even the "cedars of God" (literally), the great cedars of Lebanon. The picture is of Israel at its height.
- **"It sent out its branches to the sea and its shoots to the River" (v11).** The sea is the Mediterranean, and "the River" is the Euphrates. These were the borders God promised Abraham (Gen 15:18; Exod 23:31), and they were reached under David and Solomon (1 Kgs 4:21, 24; Ps 72:8). The Tyndale notes make the connection.
- **"Why then have you broken down its walls?" (v12).** Vineyards were protected by stone walls or thorn hedges against thieves and animals. Isaiah's song has God saying, "I will remove its hedge, and it shall be devoured; I will break down its wall" (Isa 5:5). The psalm does not deny that Israel deserved it. It asks why God would destroy what he himself planted. "All who pass along the way pluck its fruit": anyone may now help themselves.
- **"The boar from the forest ravages it, and all that move in the field feed on it" (v13).** A wild boar can wreck a vineyard in a night, rooting up the vines and trampling what it does not eat. Pigs were unclean to Israel (Lev 11:7), so the picture carries contempt as well as danger. JFB: "The boar—may represent the ravaging Assyrian." In 1520 Pope Leo X opened his bull against Martin Luther with this verse, calling Luther the boar in the Lord's vineyard, a story Guzik passes on.
- **Commentators:** Henry asks the psalm's question and answers it at once: "Why hast thou then broken down her hedges? There was a good reason for this change in God's way towards them," namely the people's sin. He pictures the wild boar and the beasts of the field ready to ravage the unprotected vine. Guzik, citing Horne and Boice, traces the vine image through both Testaments and to Jesus' words in John 15.

#### Turn Again, O God of Hosts (vv14–19)
- **"Turn again, O God of hosts! Look down from heaven, and see" (v14).** The refrain shifts. Instead of "restore us" (turn us), the psalmist asks God himself to "turn," to come back to his people. Both sides need turning. "Have regard for" means visit, attend to, care for.
- **"The stock that your right hand planted, and for the son whom you made strong for yourself" (v15).** "Stock" (*kannah*) is a rare word, probably the root or shoot of the vine. Then the image shifts from plant to person: "the son" (*ben*). Israel is God's son (Exod 4:22; Hos 11:1, "out of Egypt I called my son"). The Hebrew word for "son" can also mean a branch (Gen 49:22, Joseph is literally "a son of a fruitful vine"), so the vine and the son blend.
- **"They have burned it with fire; they have cut it down" (v16).** Vines that were pruned were burned (John 15:6; Ezek 15:4). Israel has been treated like dead wood. **"May they perish at the rebuke of your face!"** God's face is meant to shine on his people (v3) and to rebuke his enemies.
- **"But let your hand be on the man of your right hand, the son of man whom you have made strong for yourself!" (v17).** The center of the psalm's hope. Who is this "man of your right hand"? There are several layers. (1) Israel itself, God's son (v15), which the Tyndale notes take as the main sense. (2) The king, God's chosen ruler, who sits at God's right hand (110:1). (3) A wordplay on Benjamin, named in v2, whose name means "son of the right hand" (Gen 35:18). JFB suggests the line "may allude to Benjamin." (4) The Messiah. The ancient Aramaic paraphrase of the Psalms, the Targum, renders "son" in v15 as "King Messiah." Henry: "many interpreters, both Jewish and Christian, apply this to the Messiah, the Son of David." These need not compete. Israel was God's son, embodied in its king, and both point to the one Son who would fulfill what Israel and its kings failed to be.
- **"Then we shall not turn back from you; give us life, and we will call upon your name!" (v18).** The vow of renewed faithfulness. "Turn back" (*sug*) recalls the backsliding of 78:57. "Give us life" means revive us, make us live again. The people know they cannot keep this vow on their own, so they ask for life first. Henry: "Quicken us, put life into us, revive our dying interests, revive our drooping spirits."
- **"Restore us, O LORD God of hosts! Let your face shine, that we may be saved!" (v19).** The refrain reaches its fullest form, with God's covenant name, the LORD (*YHWH*), added. The prayer ends where it began, asking for the one thing that matters.
- **Commentators:** Guzik first takes v17 of the reigning king, then sees its full meaning in Jesus Christ. He cites Clarke and Spurgeon on the restoration that comes through this one man. Guzik, citing Morgan, closes by saying this prayer has been answered in Christ, in whom God's face shines on his people. The Tyndale notes take "son" as primarily the northern kingdom, while allowing an allusion to David's royal line.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 80 asks for a vine restored and a man at God's right hand. Jesus answers both. He said, "I am the true vine" (John 15:1). Where Israel the vine was cut down and burned, Jesus is the vine that bears the fruit God was looking for, and those who abide in him become fruitful branches. He also called himself "the Son of Man" more than any other title, and after his resurrection he was seated at the right hand of God (Mark 14:62; Acts 2:33–34; Heb 1:3). God has made him strong for himself. And through him the priestly blessing the psalm keeps asking for has come true: God has shone "in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6). The Shepherd of Israel has turned again to his flock, and in Christ his people are restored and saved.
