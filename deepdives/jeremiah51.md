*Setting:* This chapter continues, without a break, the great oracle against Babylon begun in chapter 50, and it closes the book's collection of prophecies against the nations (chs 46–51). Babylon is at its peak. Nebuchadnezzar has rebuilt the capital on a vast scale, with famous double walls, a ceremonial gateway faced with blue glazed bricks, and the great temple of Marduk with its tower. Jerusalem has either just fallen or will soon fall to this empire. The closing paragraph (vv59–64) dates the scroll to 594/593 BC, the fourth year of Zedekiah, when Judah's king made a journey to Babylon. Jeremiah's message is that the empire which looks permanent is already sentenced. Its gods are frauds, its walls cannot save it, and the God of Israel, who used Babylon as his weapon, will break it. The chapter alternates commands to the attacking armies, laments and taunts over the city, calls to the exiles to flee, and a hymn to God as Creator. It ends with a symbolic act: a scroll weighted with a stone and thrown into the Euphrates.

#### The Utter Destruction of Babylon (vv1–64)
- **"I will stir up the spirit of a destroyer against Babylon" (v1).** The Hebrew can also be read "a destroying wind," and the next verse's winnowing image (blowing chaff away) fits that reading. The same verb "stir up" is used of God moving Cyrus and the Persian king to act (v11; Ezra 1:1; Isa 45:13).
- **"Against the inhabitants of Leb-kamai" (v1).** This is a code name. In a cipher called *atbash*, the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet is swapped for the last, the second for the second-to-last, and so on. Run *leb qamai* ("heart of those who rise against me") through it and it spells *Kasdim*, "Chaldeans." The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) simply reads "Chaldeans" here. The code is not meant to hide anything, since Babylon is named in the same line. It is a scribe's or prophet's wordplay, and the meaning it yields on the surface, "the heart of my enemies," is itself a verdict.
- **"I will send to Babylon winnowers" (v2).** Winnowing meant tossing threshed grain into the wind so the chaff blew away. Babylon will be scattered like husks.
- **"Let not the archer bend his bow" (v3).** The Hebrew is difficult. The ESV takes it as a command to the defenders' archers not to bother. Others read it as a command to the attackers' archers to keep shooting. The point either way is that resistance is hopeless.
- **"Israel and Judah have not been forsaken by their God" (v5).** This is the reason for the whole oracle. The Hebrew for "forsaken" (*'alman*) is related to the word for "widow." Israel felt widowed in exile, but her Husband has not abandoned her (compare Isa 54:4–8). **"The land of the Chaldeans is full of guilt against the Holy One of Israel."**
- **"Flee from the midst of Babylon; let every one save his life!" (v6).** The call of 50:8 is repeated three times in this chapter (vv6, 45, 50). Revelation picks it up word for word: "Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins" (Rev 18:4).
- **"Babylon was a golden cup in the LORD's hand" (v7).** In 25:15–29 Jeremiah was told to make the nations drink "the cup of the wine of wrath." Babylon was that cup, splendid and rich, and the nations who drank it "went mad" under its conquests. It was a cup "in the LORD's hand": Babylon never ruled on its own authority. Revelation's Babylon holds "a golden cup full of abominations" (Rev 17:4), drawing on this verse.
- **"Take balm for her pain; perhaps she may be healed" (v8).** Gilead's balm was the famous ancient remedy (8:22; 46:11). **"We would have healed Babylon, but she was not healed" (v9).** The speakers seem to be Babylon's allies or the foreigners living in her, who give up on her and go home. **"Her judgment has reached up to heaven"** echoes the tower of Babel, whose top was meant to reach heaven (Gen 11:4). Now her guilt does.
- **"The LORD has brought about our vindication" (v10).** The Hebrew is literally "our righteousnesses." It means God has shown his people to be in the right against their oppressor, not that they were sinless. The returning exiles' first act is to **"declare in Zion the work of the LORD our God."**
- **"The LORD has stirred up the spirit of the kings of the Medes" (v11).** The Medes, from the highlands of modern northwest Iran, had once been Babylon's allies in destroying Nineveh (612 BC). Cyrus the Persian took over the Median kingdom around 550 BC and absorbed its army. **"The vengeance for his temple"** repeats 50:28.
- **"O you who dwell by many waters, rich in treasures" (v13).** Babylon sat astride the Euphrates, ringed by canals and moats. Revelation's Babylon "is seated on many waters," which it explains as peoples and nations (Rev 17:1, 15). **"The thread of your life is cut"** uses the image of a weaver cutting finished cloth from the loom (compare Isa 38:12).
- **"The LORD of hosts has sworn by himself" (v14).** There is no one greater to swear by (Heb 6:13). **"As many as locusts"**: a swarm that covers everything.
- **The hymn to the Creator (vv15–19).** These five verses repeat 10:12–16 almost exactly. In chapter 10 they mocked idols before Judah's fall. Here they stand at the heart of the oracle against the greatest idol-worshiping city on earth. **"He who made the earth by his power"**: the God who commands storm, rain and wind is not Marduk, who was hailed in Babylon's creation epic as the conqueror of the waters. **"Every goldsmith is put to shame by his idols… there is no breath in them" (v17)**: the image maker is embarrassed by his own product. **"Not like these is he who is the portion of Jacob" (v19).** Israel's share in the world is not a piece of land but God himself (Ps 16:5; Lam 3:24), and Israel in turn is "the tribe of his inheritance."
- **"You are my hammer and weapon of war" (vv20–23).** The Hebrew word is *mappets*, a war club or shattering mace, from the verb *napats*, "to shatter," which then rings out nine times: "with you I break in pieces." The pairs sweep through all of society, horse and rider, man and woman, old and young, shepherd and flock, farmer and team, governors and commanders. **Who is the hammer?** Interpreters divide. Many take it as Babylon, called "the hammer of the whole earth" in 50:23. God used it to shatter nations, and now he will repay it (v24). Others, including the Tyndale notes, take it as Cyrus or the Persian army, the new hammer God will bring down on Babylon. The verb forms can be read as either past or future, which is why both readings are possible. The lesson is the same: whoever the hammer is, the hand that swings it is God's.
- **"Before your very eyes" (v24).** The exiles will see it happen.
- **"O destroying mountain… I will make you a burnt mountain" (v25).** Babylon stood on a flat plain, so the "mountain" is a picture of its towering power, perhaps with a glance at the great temple tower (ziggurat) called Etemenanki, "house of the foundation of heaven and earth." A "burnt mountain" may be a dead volcano, black and empty. **"No stone shall be taken from you for a corner" (v26).** Babylon was built mostly of mud brick, but the point is that nothing from its ruins will be worth building with. That is exactly what happened: later builders hauled away its fired bricks for other towns, and the site itself was never rebuilt as a city.
- **"Ararat, Minni, and Ashkenaz" (v27).** Peoples of the far north, in the mountains around Lake Van and Lake Urmia (modern eastern Turkey, Armenia and northwest Iran). Ararat is Urartu, the same region where Noah's ark came to rest (Gen 8:4). Minni is the Mannaeans of Assyrian records. Ashkenaz is usually linked with the Scythians. All three came under Median control, which is why they appear with **"the kings of the Medes"** (v28). **"Horses like bristling locusts"** may picture locusts at the stage when their wings are still sheathed and look like spikes.
- **"The warriors of Babylon have ceased fighting; they remain in their strongholds" (v30).** In 539 BC the city fell without a pitched battle. **"One runner runs to meet another" (v31).** Couriers race in from different gates, each with the same news. Herodotus says the city was so large that the people in the center did not learn the outskirts were taken until long after. **"The fords have been seized, the marshes are burned with fire" (v32)**: the river crossings and the reed thickets around the city, which served as defenses, are in enemy hands.
- **"The daughter of Babylon is like a threshing floor at the time when it is trodden" (v33).** A threshing floor was packed hard before harvest. Babylon is being made ready, and harvest, the time of cutting, is coming.
- **"Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon has devoured me" (v34).** Now Zion herself speaks, the only place in these chapters where the injured city has her own voice. **"He has swallowed me like a monster"**: the word is *tannin*, a sea serpent or dragon (the same word used of Pharaoh in Ezek 29:3). He gulped down Jerusalem's riches and **"rinsed me out,"** licking the bowl clean. **"The violence done to me… be upon Babylon" (v35).** This is a prayer for justice, like the "imprecatory" psalms (Ps 137:8). Zion does not take revenge herself. She hands the case to God.
- **"Behold, I will plead your cause and take vengeance for you" (v36).** God answers at once. **"I will dry up her sea"**: Babylon's "sea" is the Euphrates with its canals and lagoons (see 50:38). **"A heap of ruins, the haunt of jackals" (v37)** is the same fate Jeremiah announced for Jerusalem (9:11). Babylon receives what it inflicted.
- **"While they are inflamed I will prepare them a feast and make them drunk" (vv38–39).** The lions who roar over their prey are given a banquet, and it is their last. **"Then sleep a perpetual sleep and not wake"**, repeated in v57. Daniel 5 tells of Belshazzar's drunken feast on the night of the city's fall, when he drank from the temple's vessels, and "that very night Belshazzar the Chaldean king was killed" (Dan 5:30). Herodotus and Xenophon also say a festival was under way when the Persians entered. **"Like lambs to the slaughter" (v40)**: the lions have become sheep.
- **"How Babylon is taken" (v41).** The Hebrew reads **"Sheshach"** here (the ESV translates it as "Babylon," and in 25:26 keeps it). It is another *atbash* code name: *Sheshach* turns into *Babel*. **"The praise of the whole earth seized!"** The city whose beauty everyone admired.
- **"The sea has come up on Babylon" (v42).** In ancient Near Eastern stories the sea stood for chaos. Babylon's own legend pictured Marduk defeating the chaos waters. Now the waves of invading armies cover Babylon.
- **"I will punish Bel in Babylon, and take out of his mouth what he has swallowed" (v44).** Bel, Marduk, is pictured as a glutton who swallowed nations' treasures. Among those treasures were the vessels of the LORD's temple, placed "in the treasury of his god" (Dan 1:2). Cyrus did give them back, 5,400 items in all (Ezra 1:7–11). **"The nations shall no longer flow to him."** The word "flow" is the river verb used in Isaiah 2:2 and Jeremiah 31:12 of nations streaming to Zion. Pilgrims will stop streaming to Marduk's city.
- **"Go out of the midst of her, my people!" (v45).** The second call to flee. **"Let not your heart faint… when a report comes in one year and afterward a report in another year" (v46).** The exiles will hear rumors and see turmoil before the end, **"ruler against ruler."** Babylon's last years were in fact unstable. After Nebuchadnezzar died in 562 BC, three kings followed in six years, two of them removed by force, before Nabonidus took the throne and then spent ten years away in Arabia, leaving his son Belshazzar in charge.
- **"The heavens and the earth… shall sing for joy over Babylon" (v48).** The whole creation celebrates when an oppressor falls. Revelation 18:20 echoes it: "Rejoice over her, O heaven." **"Babylon must fall for the slain of Israel, just as for Babylon have fallen the slain of all the earth" (v49)**: a strict balancing of the scales.
- **"Remember the LORD from far away, and let Jerusalem come into your mind" (v50).** The exiles are not to settle into Babylon in heart, even while they seek its welfare in practice (29:7). Daniel prayed three times a day with his window open toward Jerusalem (Dan 6:10). **"Foreigners have come into the holy places of the LORD's house" (v51).** The exiles' shame is real. God does not dismiss it. He answers it (v52).
- **"Though Babylon should mount up to heaven" (v53).** Again the tower of Babel stands behind the words (Gen 11:4). However high the walls rise, the destroyers come "from me."
- **"The LORD is a God of recompense; he will surely repay" (v56).** The Hebrew doubles the verb ("repaying he will repay"), a Hebrew way of saying "certainly."
- **"Declares the King, whose name is the LORD of hosts" (v57).** Babylon has a king. Over him stands the King.
- **"The broad wall of Babylon shall be leveled to the ground" (v58).** Babylon's walls were the wonder of the age. Ancient writers said a four-horse chariot could turn on top of them. Excavations found a double inner wall and a vast outer wall built by Nebuchadnezzar, and his own inscriptions boast of them. They were not torn down in 539 BC, since the city surrendered. They decayed over the following centuries. **"The peoples labor for nothing, and the nations weary themselves only for fire."** Habakkuk uses the same words (Hab 2:13). Everything built by empires for their own glory ends up as fuel.
- **"The word that Jeremiah the prophet commanded Seraiah the son of Neriah" (v59).** Seraiah was the brother of Baruch, Jeremiah's scribe, since both are "son of Neriah, son of Mahseiah" (32:12). He was **"the quartermaster,"** literally "prince of the resting place," the officer who arranged the king's lodging on the march. A clay seal impression reading "belonging to Seraiah (son of) Neriah" has been published, though, like many seals bought on the antiquities market, its authenticity is debated.
- **"When he went with Zedekiah king of Judah to Babylon, in the fourth year of his reign" (v59).** That was 594/593 BC, the same year envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon met in Jerusalem to plot revolt (27:3; 28:1). Zedekiah was probably summoned to Babylon, or went, to prove his loyalty. While the court went to pledge its submission, Jeremiah sent a quiet message that Babylon's days were counted.
- **"See that you read all these words" (vv61–62).** Probably read aloud privately, perhaps to the exiles or simply as a solemn witness before God, not in public before the Babylonians. Seraiah is to pray it back to God: **"O LORD, you have said concerning this place…"**
- **"Tie a stone to it and cast it into the midst of the Euphrates" (v63).** Jeremiah often acted out his messages: a ruined loincloth (ch 13), a smashed jar (ch 19), a yoke on his neck (ch 27). This is the last such sign in the book. **"Thus shall Babylon sink, to rise no more" (v64).** Revelation 18:21 builds directly on it: a mighty angel throws a great millstone into the sea, saying, "So will Babylon the great city be thrown down with violence, and will be found no more."
- **"Thus far are the words of Jeremiah" (v64).** An editor's note marking the end of the prophet's own words. Chapter 52 is a historical appendix, largely repeating 2 Kings 24–25, added by another hand. The note also helps explain the odd last line, **"and they shall become exhausted,"** which repeats the end of v58. It seems to mark where the Babylon oracle originally finished.
- **The Greek order.** In the Septuagint, Jeremiah is about one-seventh shorter, and the oracles against the nations stand in the middle of the book (after 25:13), in a different order. Most scholars think two editions of Jeremiah circulated, both reflected among the Dead Sea Scrolls. The Hebrew order, which the ESV follows, puts Babylon last, so the book's prophecies against the nations end with the fall of the greatest of them.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read vv1–4 as the Medo-Persian army sweeping down like a wind. Guzik explains Leb-kamai and Sheshach as *atbash* ciphers. JFB discusses the Leb-kamai wording but plays down the cipher, reasoning that Babylon is named openly in the same verse, and on v41 ties Sheshach to a Babylonian feast, an older guess modern scholars do not follow. Guzik sees a pattern in v11: God decrees judgment, uses a human instrument, the instrument acts from its own sinful motives, and then God judges the instrument too. He holds, as he did in chapter 50, that the walls and total desolation go beyond 539 BC and look to the final Babylon of Revelation 17–18, and he links v53 to the tower of Babel. JFB, on v44, sees Bel disgorging the temple vessels, fulfilled in Ezra 1:7–11, and calls Seraiah a "quiet" or docile prince, reading the old title literally. JFB also takes "Thus far are the words of Jeremiah" to mean chapter 52 was added by another inspired writer. Matthew Henry treats vv1–58 as one oracle and notes how Babylon's "destruction comes when they did not think of it." On v50 he applies the call to remember Jerusalem: "wherever we are, in the greatest depths, at the greatest distances, we are to remember the Lord our God." On the sunken scroll: "Those that sink under the weight of God's wrath and curse, sink for ever."

#### Pointing to Christ
The God of this chapter is "the portion of Jacob," the Creator who made the world and has not forsaken his people (vv5, 19). Babylon was the golden cup that made the nations drunk with wrath. In Gethsemane Jesus took the cup of wrath into his own hands and drank it (Matt 26:39), so that those who belong to him need never drink it. The command "Go out of the midst of her, my people" (v45) becomes in Revelation the call to every believer not to share in the sins of a world that sets itself against God (Rev 18:4). The stone-weighted scroll sinking in the Euphrates is Jeremiah's last word that every proud empire will fall. The kingdom that remains is the one Daniel saw as a stone cut without hands that struck the statue of the world's empires and became a great mountain filling the whole earth (Dan 2:34–35, 44), the kingdom of the Son of Man.
