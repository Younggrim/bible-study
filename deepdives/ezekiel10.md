*Setting:* The temple vision of September 592 BC continues (chs 8–11). Ezekiel, in the Spirit, stands in the courts of the Jerusalem temple. He has seen idols at the gates, elders worshiping animal images in the dark, women mourning a dead god, and priests bowing to the sun (ch 8). He has watched six executioners strike the city after a man in linen marked those who grieved over its sins (ch 9). Now the same living creatures and wheels he saw by the Chebar canal fourteen months earlier (ch 1) appear in the temple itself, and Ezekiel realizes what they are: cherubim, the guardians of God's throne. Chapter 10 shows two things at once. Fire from God's throne is thrown on the city, and the glory of the LORD rises and moves toward the exit. For a priest, nothing could be more shocking. Since the day Solomon dedicated the temple (1 Kings 8:10–11), Israel had believed that God's glory lived there. Now Ezekiel watches it leave.

#### The Glory of the Lord Leaves the Temple (vv1–22)
- **One heading, two strands woven together.** The ESV gives the chapter one heading. Ezekiel moves back and forth between the coals of judgment (vv1–8) and a description of the cherubim and wheels (vv9–17), then resumes the story of the glory leaving (vv18–22). The repetition of chapter 1 is deliberate: the God who appeared to the exiles in Babylon is the same God now departing from Jerusalem.
- **"Something like a sapphire, in appearance like a throne" (v1).** This is the throne of 1:26, above the gleaming expanse over the creatures' heads. "Sapphire" (*sappir*) was probably lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone flecked with gold. The same blue was under God's feet when the elders of Israel saw him at Sinai (Exod 24:10). In chapter 8 the "image of jealousy" sat on a pedestal at the gate. Here the true King's throne stands above all of it.
- **"Fill your hands with burning coals from between the cherubim, and scatter them over the city" (v2).** The man in linen who marked the faithful (9:4) now carries out the second half of his task. In Isaiah's vision, a coal from God's altar touched the prophet's lips to purify him: "your guilt is taken away" (Isa 6:6–7). Here the coals do not cleanse. They burn. Fire from God's own presence falls on Jerusalem, as fire fell on Sodom (Gen 19:24). Six years later the Babylonians "burned the house of the LORD and the king's house and all the houses of Jerusalem" (2 Kings 25:9). The vision shows who really lit the fire.
- **"Go in among the whirling wheels" (v2).** The word here is *galgal*, a different word from the ordinary *'ophan* used for "wheel" in chapter 1. *Galgal* suggests something rolling or whirling, like a whirlwind (Ps 77:18 uses it for the rumble of a storm). In v13 Ezekiel hears the wheels called by this name.
- **"The cherubim were standing on the south side of the house" (v3).** They were not on the north side, where the idol and the executioners had been. Some think the south side is mentioned because it was near the royal palace, others because it was simply the place from which the glory would go out. The text gives no reason.
- **"A cloud filled the inner court" (v3).** The cloud is a familiar sign of God's presence. It led Israel in the wilderness (Exod 13:21), covered Sinai (Exod 19:16), filled the tabernacle when it was finished (Exod 40:34–35), and filled Solomon's temple so thickly the priests could not stand to minister (1 Kings 8:10–11). That moment was the start of the temple's glory. Ezekiel's vision is its end. The same cloud fills the house as the glory gets ready to leave.
- **"The glory of the LORD went up from the cherub to the threshold of the house" (v4).** This repeats 9:3. The glory has moved from above the ark in the Most Holy Place to the doorway of the temple building. **"The court was filled with the brightness of the glory of the LORD."** The light spills out as God moves toward the door.
- **"The sound of the wings… like the voice of God Almighty when he speaks" (v5).** "God Almighty" is *El Shaddai*, the name by which God revealed himself to Abraham (Gen 17:1; Exod 6:3). The rushing of the cherubim's wings was heard as far as the outer court, where ordinary worshipers stood. Ezekiel described the same sound in 1:24 as "like the sound of many waters."
- **"A cherub stretched out his hand… and put it into the hands of the man clothed in linen" (v7).** The man in linen does not take the fire himself. It is handed to him from within the circle of the cherubim. Judgment comes from God's own throne, passed on by his servants, carried out by his appointed agent. Nothing about it is out of control.
- **"The form of a human hand under their wings" (v8).** Repeated from 1:8. The cherubim can act, not only fly.
- **"Like sparkling beryl" (v9).** The stone (*tarshish*) is uncertain. It may be yellow topaz, chrysolite or beryl. It was one of the stones on the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:20). The wheels glitter.
- **"As if a wheel were within a wheel" (v10).** As in 1:16. The most common explanation is two wheels set at right angles to each other, so the whole can roll in any of four directions **"without turning as they went"** (v11). God's throne-chariot is not limited the way earthly chariots are. It goes anywhere, instantly, without having to turn.
- **"Full of eyes all around" (v12).** In chapter 1 the rims of the wheels were full of eyes (1:18). Here the creatures' whole bodies are too. The elders in the dark said, "The LORD does not see us" (8:12; 9:9). The answer is a throne covered in eyes. Nothing escapes him (Prov 15:3; 2 Chr 16:9). The four living creatures around God's throne in Revelation are also "full of eyes in front and behind" (Rev 4:6, 8).
- **"The first face was the face of the cherub" (v14).** In 1:10 the four faces were human, lion, ox and eagle. Here "the face of the cherub" takes the place of the ox. Three main explanations have been offered. First, the "cherub" face may simply be the ox face, because in Mesopotamia the great guardian figures at palace and temple gates, the *lamassu*, had bull bodies, so a bull's face was what a cherub looked like. Second, Ezekiel may be naming the face he saw first, the one facing him. Third, a copyist may have changed the word. The first view fits what Ezekiel's world knew best. The Hebrew word *kerub* may also be related to the Akkadian *karibu*, a name for protective beings who stood before the gods.
- **"These were the living creatures that I saw by the Chebar canal" (vv15, 20).** Ezekiel says this twice. **"And I knew that they were cherubim"** (v20). In chapter 1 he had called them only "living creatures." Now, seeing them in the temple, he recognizes them. As a priest he knew the carved cherubim that spread their wings over the ark (1 Kings 6:23–28; 8:6–7) and the cherubim embroidered on the temple curtain (2 Chr 3:14). The God who sat enthroned between the carved cherubim in Jerusalem had appeared to him in Babylon on living ones. God was never confined to the building.
- **Cherubim in the Bible.** Cherubim first appear guarding the way to the tree of life after Adam and Eve are driven from Eden (Gen 3:24). They are guardians of God's holiness, and they carry his throne (Ps 18:10: "He rode on a cherub and flew"). They are not the chubby winged babies of later art. They are awesome and fearful.
- **"For the spirit of the living creatures was in them" (v17).** Repeated from 1:20–21. The wheels and the creatures move as one, because one spirit drives them. God's purposes are never at odds with themselves.
- **"Then the glory of the LORD went out from the threshold of the house, and stood over the cherubim" (v18).** The glory steps out of the building and mounts its living throne, like a king stepping into his chariot.
- **"They stood at the entrance of the east gate of the house of the LORD" (v19).** This is the third stage: from the Most Holy Place (9:3), to the threshold (10:4), to the east gate of the outer court. The east gate was the main entrance to the temple, facing the rising sun and the Mount of Olives. It was near here that the twenty-five men had turned their backs to the temple to worship the sun (8:16). The glory pauses at the gate. In 11:23 it will leave the city altogether. When Ezekiel later sees the glory come back, it comes in "by the gate facing east" (43:1–4), and that gate is then shut, "for the LORD, the God of Israel, has entered by it" (44:1–2).
- **The glory has departed.** Five centuries earlier, when the Philistines captured the ark, a dying mother named her newborn son Ichabod, "no glory," saying, "The glory has departed from Israel" (1 Sam 4:21–22). Ezekiel watches the same thing happen in Jerusalem. The temple building still stands, but it is empty of what made it holy. This explains how Babylon would be able to destroy it. God's people had driven him away (8:6).
- **"Each one of them went straight forward" (v22).** The chapter ends, as chapter 1's description did (1:9, 12), with the creatures moving straight on. God's purpose moves forward, and nothing deflects it, not even his people's rebellion.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the fire of judgment "is just and holy, for it is fire fetched from between the cherubim," and that the fire from God's altar, which had been slighted, is here avenged. Guzik, following Feinberg, sets the coals beside Isaiah 6: there a coal purifies, here coals destroy. JFB agrees the coals are wrath, not "the altar fire of reconciliation." On the glory's movement Henry writes, "God removes by degrees from a provoking people," and Guzik, quoting Poole, sees in the pauses God's reluctance to leave and a last window for repentance. The Tyndale notes say the same. Henry calls the wheels "wheels of Providence, which are full of eyes," moving in ways that are "steady and regular." On v14 JFB argues "cherub" cannot simply mean "ox." Guzik lists the main options and leaves it open. On v20 Henry says Ezekiel "knew assuredly, whereas before he only conjectured it," and JFB credits his recognition to what he knew as a priest of the cherubim above the ark. JFB explains *galgal* as the wheelwork in "whirlwind-like rotation."

#### Pointing to Christ
The glory that left Jerusalem in Ezekiel's vision came back in a way no one expected. John writes: "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). The word for "dwelt" means "pitched his tent," the language of the tabernacle where the glory first lived. Jesus walked into the temple through its gates, and the glory of God was there in a man. He also left the city eastward, over the Mount of Olives, but not in judgment. He went to Gethsemane and to the cross, bearing the fire that Ezekiel saw scattered over Jerusalem. And from that same Mount of Olives he ascended, with the promise that he will return the same way (Acts 1:11–12). Meanwhile his glory now dwells by the Spirit in his people, who are being "transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Cor 3:18).
