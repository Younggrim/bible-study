*Setting:* The last three verses of the book, a brief epilogue after the founding of Purim (9:20–32). The crisis is over. Haman and his sons are dead, the Jews have rest from their enemies, and the feast has been written into law. The narrator steps back from the story and closes with a short note about the king's power and Mordecai's greatness, in the style of the books of Kings ("are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles…?"). Xerxes reigned until he was murdered in 465 BC, and his son Artaxerxes I was the king who later sent Ezra and Nehemiah to Jerusalem (Ezra 7; Neh 2). The Jews of the empire were safer under that dynasty because of what happened in this book.

#### The Greatness of Mordecai (vv1–3)
- **"King Ahasuerus imposed tax on the land and on the coastlands of the sea" (v1).** The word for "tax" (*mas*) usually means forced labor in the Old Testament, the word used for Israel's slavery in Egypt (Exod 1:11) and Solomon's labor gangs (1 Kings 5:13). The lexicon notes that in this late passage it may mean tribute in money, the sense it later had. "The coastlands of the sea" are the islands and shores of the Mediterranean. There is a quiet irony here: history records that after his defeat in Greece, Xerxes lost his hold on many of the Aegean islands. The king who began the book displaying his riches for six months (1:4) ends it collecting more.
- **Why mention taxes at all?** Readers have suggested several reasons. The note may simply show that the empire went back to normal business. It may hint that the king, having lost the plunder Haman promised (3:9) and the plunder the Jews refused (9:10, 15, 16), raised the money another way. Or it may contrast with 2:18, where he remitted taxes in honor of Esther. The book does not explain, and its closing tone is matter-of-fact.
- **"All the acts of his power and might" (v2).** The phrase copies the formula that closes the reigns of Israel's and Judah's kings: "the rest of the acts of… and all his might, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel?" (1 Kings 15:23; 16:27). Using it of a Persian king, and then of Mordecai, places the story within the history of God's people.
- **"The Book of the Chronicles of the kings of Media and Persia" (v2).** The royal archive already mentioned in 2:23 and 6:1, the very book that was read to the sleepless king. It has not survived. Many Persian records have been found, including thousands of administrative tablets from Persepolis, but none so far names Mordecai's rise. Matthew Henry makes the point that the Persian chronicles "are long since lost and buried in oblivion, while the sacred writings live."
- **"Mordecai the Jew was second in rank to King Ahasuerus" (v3).** The title "the Jew" stays with him to the end. In the first chapters his Jewishness was the reason he was hated (3:4–6); now it is part of his honor. Like Joseph in Egypt (Gen 41:40–43) and Daniel in Babylon (Dan 2:48; 6:3), a faithful exile ends up second only to the foreign king. The book also sets him against Haman, who held the same office. Haman used it to destroy a people; Mordecai uses it to protect one.
- **"Great among the Jews and popular with the multitude of his brothers" (v3).** "Popular" is literally "pleasing" or "accepted." "The multitude" translates *rov*, which can mean "the majority," and the rabbis of the Talmud noticed it: most of his brothers, not all. Leadership always has its critics, and the text is honest about it. Whatever the nuance, the man who once sat unnoticed at the gate is now loved by his people.
- **"He sought the welfare of his people and spoke peace to all his people" (v3).** The last words of the book. "Welfare" is literally "good" (*tov*), and "peace" is *shalom*, which means wholeness and well-being as well as the absence of conflict. "All his people" is literally "all his seed" or offspring, the descendants of Israel. It is a quiet, fitting end. Haman sought his own glory; Mordecai sought his people's good.
- **No mention of God, to the end.** The book closes without naming God, without a prayer, without the temple or Jerusalem. That silence has troubled some readers; Esther is the only book of the Old Testament not found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and some early Jewish and Christian teachers questioned its place in Scripture. But both synagogue and church kept it. Its silence seems to be its point: God's care for his people does not depend on visible miracles. The story of Esther is God's providence written in ordinary history.
- **The Greek additions.** The Greek translation of Esther, made later, adds six long passages, including Mordecai's dream, the texts of the royal decrees, and prayers of Mordecai and Esther. These additions name God many times. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include them; Jewish and Protestant Bibles follow the shorter Hebrew text, which ends here. Matthew Henry notes that the rest of this chapter, "being found only in the Greek, is rejected as apocryphal." The additions show how early readers felt the absence of God's name and filled it in.
- **Commentators:** JFB applies Jesus' saying to Mordecai: "he that humbleth himself shall be exalted" (Luke 14:11), and compares his rise to Joseph's, where "the ordinary and natural course of things" led "to the most marvellous results." Matthew Henry praises Mordecai because "he did not seek his own wealth," did not disown his people, was "easy of access," and "did not side with any one party of his people against another." Guzik closes the book by listing every turn of the story as God's arranging: Vashti's fall, Esther's favor, the lot that gave eleven months' warning, Haman's restraint, the sleepless night, the very page read aloud. He quotes Spurgeon calling Esther "a record of wonders without a miracle," and adds, again with Spurgeon, that God's rule over all never cancels human responsibility: Haman, the king, Esther and Mordecai each acted freely and answered for it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Esther ends with a Jew at the king's right hand, seeking the good of his people and speaking peace to them. Spurgeon, quoted by Guzik, drew the obvious comfort: the Jews of Susa must have taken heart knowing one of their own was near the throne, and believers have someone nearer still. The risen Christ is seated at the right hand of the true King (Heb 1:3). He does what Mordecai did, but fully and forever: he seeks the good of his people, intercedes for them (Rom 8:34), and "came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near" (Eph 2:17). The book that never names God ends by pointing to the kind of mediator only God could provide.
