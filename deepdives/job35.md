*Setting:* This is Elihu's third speech, and his shortest. In chapter 34 he defended God's justice to the listening circle and ended harshly, wishing Job would be "tried to the end" (34:36). Job still has not answered. Now Elihu takes up two more of Job's complaints. First, Job had asked what good it does to be righteous if the righteous suffer like the wicked. Second, Job had complained that God does not answer the cries of the oppressed, or his own. Elihu's replies hold real truths: God is not a needy king who gains or loses from human behavior, and many cries for help never turn into true seeking of God. But again he uses true ideas to close the case against Job instead of opening a way for him. The chapter is also the source of one of the loveliest phrases in the book: God "who gives songs in the night" (v10).

#### Elihu Condemns Job (vv1–8)
- **"Do you think this to be just? Do you say, 'It is my right before God'?" (v2).** Elihu sums up Job's position as a claim that his case against God is just. The Hebrew is short and can be read two ways. The King James has "My righteousness is more than God's," as if Job claimed to be more righteous than God. The ESV takes it as "my right before God," Job insisting he is in the right in God's presence. Job never claimed to be more righteous than God. But Elihu's point is that Job's protest implied it: if Job is right, God must be wrong. God himself will put a similar question to Job: "Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" (40:8).
- **"What advantage have I? How am I better off than if I had sinned?" (v3).** Elihu repeats the charge from 34:9. Job did ask hard questions along these lines. He said God "destroys both the blameless and the wicked" (9:22) and asked why the wicked prosper while they say, "What profit do we get if we pray to him?" (21:15). But Job never gave up on righteousness. He held on to it fiercely: "till I die I will not put away my integrity from me" (27:5). What troubled Job was not that goodness is useless, but that it seemed to bring him no protection.
- **"I will answer you and your friends with you" (v4).** "Your friends" may mean the three friends, who also failed to answer this question, or people who think like Job (34:8, 36). Elihu again presents himself as the one who will settle what the others could not.
- **"Look at the heavens, and see; and behold the clouds, which are higher than you" (v5).** Elihu points upward. The sky is far above any person. God is far above that. The picture is the same one Zophar used ("higher than heaven," 11:8), and Eliphaz ("Is not God high in the heavens?" 22:12). Job had said it too (9:8–10). The Tyndale notes observe that Elihu adds nothing new here.
- **"If you have sinned, what do you accomplish against him?" (v6).** God is not wounded by human sin the way a king is harmed by a rebel. He does not lose power or wealth. "If you are righteous, what do you give to him?" (v7). God does not need our goodness either. Eliphaz said the same: "Can a man be profitable to God?" (22:2). This is a true point about God's self-sufficiency. Paul says God is not "served by human hands, as though he needed anything" (Acts 17:25).
- **"Your wickedness concerns a man like yourself, and your righteousness a son of man" (v8).** Sin and righteousness mainly affect people, the one who does them and those around him. So God has no reason to be unjust. He gains nothing by it. Elihu's logic is that if God cannot be bribed by goodness or threatened by sin, his judgments must be fair.
- **Where the argument falls short.** Elihu is right that God does not *need* anything from us. But Scripture also says our sin truly grieves God (Gen 6:6; Eph 4:30) and our faithfulness truly pleases him (Heb 11:6; Zeph 3:17). Joseph refused sin because it was "against God" (Gen 39:9), and David confessed, "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (Ps 51:4). The Tyndale notes say Elihu did not grasp that all sin is an offense against God. Taken too far, his picture of a distant God makes repentance pointless and prayer unnecessary, the opposite of what he wants Job to do.
- **Commentators:** Henry frames the whole chapter as Elihu answering three "improper sayings" of Job "distinctly." He reads vv1–8 as Elihu correcting the idea that religion is pointless, as if God commanded it for his own benefit rather than ours. JFB notes the irony of v5: the clouds are high above Job, and God's throne is higher still, so God has no motive for injustice, and affliction must be meant for the sufferer's good. On v2 it reads "my righteousness is before God" and notes "more than" is possible. Guzik is sharper. He passes on Mason's verdict that Elihu "grossly misrepresents" Job, and Smick's point that Job wanted vindication precisely because he trusted God's justice. Guzik also argues that Elihu traps himself: if nothing people do affects God, why should Job repent? He passes on G. Campbell Morgan's reply that Scripture shows human sin grieves God and righteousness brings him joy.

#### Elihu Condemns Job (vv9–16)
- **"Because of the multitude of oppressions people cry out" (v9).** Elihu now takes up Job's complaint about unanswered cries. Job had said, "From out of the city the dying groan... yet God charges no one with wrong" (24:12), and "I cry to you for help and you do not answer me" (30:20). Elihu grants that the oppressed do cry out "because of the arm of the mighty."
- **"But none says, 'Where is God my Maker'" (v10).** This is his explanation. People cry out from pain, but they do not seek God himself. A cry for relief is not the same as a prayer to the Maker. Israel in the wilderness complained bitterly about hunger without turning to God (Num 11:4–6; Ps 78:18–22), and Hosea says, "They do not cry to me from the heart, but they wail upon their beds" (Hos 7:14). There is truth here: suffering can make us loud without making us seek God.
- **"Who gives songs in the night" (v10).** "Songs" is *zemiroth*, a word for songs of praise. David is called "the sweet psalmist (literally, pleasant in the songs) of Israel" (2 Sam 23:1), and Psalm 119:54 says, "Your statutes have been my songs." "Night" here is both literal darkness and a picture of trouble. God is the kind of Maker who gives songs to people in their darkest hours. Paul and Silas sang hymns at midnight in a Philippian prison with their feet in stocks (Acts 16:25). The psalmist says, "By night his song is with me, a prayer to the God of my life" (Ps 42:8).
- **"Who teaches us more than the beasts of the earth" (v11).** Animals cry out in hunger and pain too (Ps 104:21; Joel 1:20). But human beings were made able to know God, to reason, pray and praise. A merely animal cry of pain does not use that gift. Job had earlier said the opposite, that animals could teach about God: "ask the beasts, and they will teach you" (12:7). Elihu points the other way: we are meant to be wiser than they are.
- **"There they cry out, but he does not answer, because of the pride of evil men" (v12).** The reason some cries go unanswered is pride. The person demands help but will not humble himself before God. James says the same: "You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly" (Jas 4:3), and "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (Jas 4:6).
- **"Surely God does not hear an empty cry" (v13).** "Empty" (*shav*) is the word for what is worthless or false, as in the commandment not to take God's name "in vain" (Exod 20:7). Some prayers are noise with nothing behind them (Prov 15:29; Isa 1:15).
- **"How much less when you say that you do not see him" (v14).** Now Elihu turns to Job. Job had said, "I go forward, but he is not there, and backward, but I do not perceive him" (23:8–9). Elihu's sharp point: if God does not answer empty cries, he is even less likely to answer a man who complains that he cannot see him. Yet the second half of the verse can also be read as advice: "the case is before him, and you are waiting for him." That is, Job's case really is in God's hands, so he should wait. Both readings appear in translations and commentaries, and the Hebrew is difficult.
- **"Because his anger does not punish... Job opens his mouth in empty talk" (vv15–16).** Elihu's final charge is that God has *not* yet punished Job as fully as he could, and has overlooked much, and Job has taken that patience as indifference and filled the air with words. The word translated "transgression" in v15 is rare and uncertain. Some take it as "folly" or "arrogance." "He multiplies words without knowledge" (v16) echoes 34:35. God will use nearly the same phrase in 38:2: "words without knowledge." So Elihu is not wholly wrong about Job's words. But God never says Job's suffering is a lighter punishment than he deserved. That was Zophar's claim (11:6), and God rejected the friends' view of Job (42:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry has the finest comment on v10. People in trouble are quick to complain and slow to ask, "Where is God my Maker?" Yet "He gives songs in the night, that is, when our condition is ever so dark, and sad, and melancholy, there is that in God, in his providence and promise, which is sufficient" to comfort. On v12 he says such sufferers "are proud and unhumbled under their afflictions, which were sent to mortify them and to hide pride from them." On v13: "God will not hear vanity, that is, the hypocritical prayer." JFB reads "songs in the night" as songs of joy at deliverance, given in calamity. It reads v12 as people crying proudly *against* God instead of humbly *to* him. On v14 JFB notes that Job did look for a Redeemer beyond death (19:25–27), so "not seeing him" cannot mean Job had no hope at all. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that believers in distress often look everywhere for help except to God, the Maker who gives songs in the night. But he also passes on Andersen's judgment that Elihu's counsel here is a cruel perfectionism that protects his theory at the sufferer's expense: God has not answered Job, so Job must be in the wrong.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elihu is right that God does not need anything from us. But the gospel shows something Elihu could not see: the God who needs nothing chose to be touched by our sin and our suffering. He did not stay above the clouds. The Son came down, "made like his brothers in every respect" (Heb 2:17), and he knows from the inside what it is to cry out and receive no answer: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). Before he went to the cross, on the night he was betrayed, Jesus and his disciples "sang a hymn" and went out to the Mount of Olives (Matt 26:30). He sang in the night. Because he bore the silence of God for his people, their cries are not empty. In him, "we have a great high priest" who sympathizes with our weaknesses, so we may "with confidence draw near to the throne of grace" (Heb 4:14–16). The Maker who gives songs in the night gave the deepest one at the cross and the empty tomb.
