*Setting:* Judah, about 835–796 BC. Joash, the boy saved from Athaliah's massacre and hidden for six years in the temple, has been crowned at seven by the priest Jehoiada (ch 23). Baal's temple has been torn down and the covenant renewed. This chapter covers his whole forty-year reign, and it falls into two halves with Jehoiada's death as the hinge. The parallel account is 2 Kings 12, which tells the temple repair in more detail but does not mention Joash's apostasy or the murder of Zechariah. Chronicles tells both, and the contrast is the point: a king who did well while a godly mentor lived, and turned bad once he was gone. It is one of the saddest portraits in the Bible of borrowed faith.

#### Joash Repairs the Temple (vv1–19)
- **"Joash was seven years old… and he reigned forty years" (v1).** The Tyndale notes date his reign 835–796 BC. His mother was **Zibiah of Beersheba**. Her name means "gazelle," and Beersheba was at Judah's far southern edge. She was not from Ahab's family, which may be why her son survived and why he was not raised as a Baal worshiper.
- **"Joash did what was right… all the days of Jehoiada the priest" (v2).** This phrase is a quiet warning. 2 Kings 12:2 says he did right "all his days, because Jehoiada the priest instructed him." Chronicles limits it: "all the days of Jehoiada." The careful reader already suspects what will happen when those days end. Kings also notes that even in his good years the high places were not removed (2 Kings 12:3).
- **"Jehoiada got for him two wives" (v3).** Jehoiada acted as a father to the orphaned king. Having more than one wife was common for kings, though the law warned against it (Deut 17:17). The Chronicler mentions it for a practical reason. David's line had nearly died out, and "he had sons and daughters" means the line was being rebuilt.
- **"Joash decided to restore the house of the LORD" (v4).** Literally "it was in the heart of Joash." The temple had been damaged and neglected during Athaliah's years. Joash had grown up inside it, and it makes sense that he cared for it.
- **"Gather from all Israel money… and see that you act quickly. But the Levites did not act quickly" (v5).** 2 Kings 12:6 says that by Joash's twenty-third year the priests still had not done the repairs, so the delay lasted years. The Tyndale notes suggest the priests and the king may have disagreed over who should pay and oversee the work. Even the godly old priest needed a push. In Kings, the priests were keeping the money for their own support.
- **"The tax levied by Moses… for the tent of testimony" (v6).** In the wilderness, each man counted in the census paid half a shekel "for the service of the tent of meeting" (Exod 30:12–16; 38:25–26). Joash appeals to an existing law, not a new tax. "Jehoiada the chief" is literally "the head," that is, the high priest.
- **"The sons of Athaliah, that wicked woman, had broken into the house of God" (v7).** Athaliah's "sons" may mean her followers or the priests of Baal, since her actual sons were killed by Arab raiders and Jehu (21:17; 22:8). They had looted the temple's dedicated things for Baal worship.
- **"They made a chest and set it outside the gate" (v8).** 2 Kings 12:9 says Jehoiada bored a hole in its lid and set it "beside the altar, on the right side as one entered." Chronicles says "outside the gate," perhaps meaning at the entrance to the inner court where ordinary worshipers could reach it. It was a public collection box that anyone could see.
- **"All the princes and all the people rejoiced and brought their tax" (v10).** Once the giving was direct and visible, people gave gladly. The Chronicler loves joyful giving (1 Chr 29:9, 17). Paul later says the same: "God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Cor 9:7).
- **The king's secretary and the high priest's officer (v11).** The money was counted jointly by a royal official and a priestly one. That is ancient accountability: no single person handled the funds alone (compare Paul's care in 2 Cor 8:20–21). 2 Kings 12:15 adds that the men who paid the workers were not asked for an accounting, "for they dealt honestly."
- **"Masons and carpenters… workers in iron and bronze" (v12).** The repair was real construction. "Restored the house of God to its proper condition" (v13) is literally "set it up according to its specification," that is, the design David and Solomon had given.
- **"With it were made utensils for the house of the LORD" (v14).** 2 Kings 12:13 says no gold or silver vessels were made from the collection. The Tyndale notes resolve it simply: during the repairs the money went to wages, and only after the work was "finished" was the surplus used for vessels.
- **"Regularly all the days of Jehoiada" (v14).** The phrase from v2 returns. The daily burnt offerings went on, while Jehoiada lived.
- **"Jehoiada grew old and full of days… 130 years old" (v15).** That is ten years older than Moses (Deut 34:7) and far beyond anyone else in this period. The Tyndale notes read it as a sign of God's blessing. "Full of days" is said of Abraham, Isaac, David and Job, men whose lives were complete.
- **"They buried him in the city of David among the kings" (v16).** A priest buried in the royal tombs is a unique honor in the Old Testament. The reason is stated: "he had done good in Israel, and toward God and his house." He had saved the royal line and restored the temple. Contrast Jehoram, who was not buried in the kings' tombs (21:20), and Joash himself, who will not be either (v25).
- **"The princes of Judah came and paid homage to the king. Then the king listened to them" (v17).** "Paid homage" means they bowed low, flattering him. Now that the old priest was gone they had the king's ear. The text does not say what they asked for, but the next verse shows the result. Many think they wanted freedom to worship at local shrines and in the old ways. Joash had always had someone to listen to, and he simply changed whom he listened to.
- **"They abandoned the house of the LORD… and served the Asherim and the idols" (v18).** The very temple Joash had repaired was abandoned. "Asherim" were wooden poles or carved images for Asherah, a Canaanite mother goddess linked with Baal. "Wrath came upon Judah and Jerusalem for this guilt of theirs." In Chronicles, God's anger is not a fit of temper but his settled response to covenant-breaking (19:2, 10).
- **"Yet he sent prophets among them to bring them back" (v19).** Before judgment comes warning. God's patience is the point of this verse (compare 36:15–16, where the Chronicler says God sent messengers "persistently" because "he had compassion on his people"). "They would not pay attention."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that Joash seemed more eager to repair the temple than the old priest was, and draws a sharp lesson: "It is easier to build temples, than to be temples to God." On v17 he warns, "Often both princes and inferior people have been flattered to their ruin." Guzik reads Joash as a weak man whose faith rested on Jehoiada rather than his own walk with God, and calls Jehoiada an example of how much one godly person can influence a nation. JFB suggests the princes "were secretly attached to idolatry" and offered a plausible request, and that the apostasy "received not only the king's toleration, but his sanction." On the honor of Jehoiada's burial, JFB notes that burial inside a city was forbidden except at Jerusalem, and even there only for eminent people.

#### Joash's Treachery (vv20–22)
- **"The Spirit of God clothed Zechariah the son of Jehoiada" (v20).** The verb is *labash*, "to put on clothing." The Spirit wore him like a garment, the same vivid picture used of Gideon (Judg 6:34) and of Amasai (1 Chr 12:18). Zechariah, "the LORD remembers," was probably a younger son, since JFB notes he does not appear among the high priests.
- **"He stood above the people" (v20).** The priests' inner court was raised above the outer court where the people stood (Jer 36:10 calls it "the upper court"). He spoke from where his father had once crowned the king.
- **"Why do you break the commandments of the LORD, so that you cannot prosper?" (v20).** "Prosper" is one of the Chronicler's key words (26:5; 31:21; 1 Chr 22:13). The message echoes the prophet Azariah's words to Asa: "If you forsake him, he will forsake you" (15:2). "Because you have forsaken the LORD, he has forsaken you" is the covenant turned around. God gave them what they chose.
- **"By command of the king they stoned him with stones in the court of the house of the LORD" (v21).** Stoning was the law's penalty for a false prophet or one who led people to other gods (Deut 13:5–10). They used the law's punishment on the man who was calling them back to the law. And the place is dreadful. Jehoiada had refused to let even Athaliah be killed in the house of the LORD (23:14). Now his own son's blood is shed there, by command of the king Jehoiada saved and crowned in that same court.
- **"Joash the king did not remember the kindness that Jehoiada… had shown him" (v22).** "Kindness" is *hesed*, the Bible's great word for loyal, covenant love. Jehoiada had shown Joash *hesed* when he hid him, raised him and made him king. Joash repaid it by killing his son. The Chronicler states it with no softening: he "killed his son."
- **"May the LORD see and avenge!" (v22).** The last verb is *darash*, "seek" or "require." It is the Chronicler's word for "seeking the LORD," used here for God seeking out blood (compare Gen 9:5, "I will require a reckoning"). The Tyndale notes compare it with David's prayers for justice in the Psalms (Ps 7:9; 28:4). It is not personal spite. It is a righteous man handing his case to God, and God answers within a year (vv23–25).
- **Jesus and Zechariah.** Jesus told the religious leaders that they would be held guilty for "all the righteous blood shed on earth, from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah the son of Barachiah, whom you murdered between the sanctuary and the altar" (Matt 23:35; Luke 11:51). In the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles is the last book, so Abel to Zechariah means the first murder to the last one in Israel's Scriptures, A to Z. Most interpreters identify this Zechariah with the one here, since the place matches exactly. Matthew's "son of Barachiah" has been explained in several ways: Barachiah may have been another name for Jehoiada, or an earlier ancestor (as "son" often means descendant), or the name may have been drawn in from the later prophet "Zechariah the son of Berechiah" (Zech 1:1). Luke gives no father's name. None of the explanations is certain, but the identity of the martyr is clear.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of Zechariah's dying words, "This came not from a spirit of revenge, but a spirit of prophecy," and JFB agrees that it may be "the utterance of a prophetic doom" rather than a curse. JFB thinks a "band of miscreants, at the secret instigation of Joash" carried out the stoning. Guzik dwells on the ironies: kindness repaid with cruelty, and a murder committed in the place where Joash was crowned. The Tyndale notes point to Jesus' use of this murder in Matthew 23:35.

#### Joash Assassinated (vv23–27)
- **"At the end of the year the army of the Syrians came up against Joash" (v23).** The new year began in spring, "the time when kings go out to battle" (1 Chr 20:1). 2 Kings 12:17–18 adds that Hazael of Syria had taken Gath and that Joash bought him off with the temple's sacred gifts and treasures. The temple Joash had restored was stripped to pay a foreign king. Chronicles focuses on the defeat itself.
- **"Destroyed all the princes of the people" (v23).** The princes who flattered Joash into idolatry (v17) are the ones the Syrians kill. Judgment falls first on those who led the way.
- **"Though the army of the Syrians had come with few men, the LORD delivered into their hand a very great army" (v24).** This is the reverse of Judah's great victories. Under Asa and Jehoshaphat, God gave small or helpless Judah victory over huge armies (14:9–15; 20:1–30). Now God gives a small foreign force victory over Judah's large army. The Tyndale notes point out that in "holy war" God's side wins regardless of numbers. Here, God is not on Judah's side. "Thus they executed judgment on Joash."
- **"Leaving him severely wounded" (v25).** The Hebrew is a plural form of the word for sickness, "in many diseases." It may mean wounds from battle or illness. Henry reads it as "great diseases, of body, or mind, or both."
- **"His servants conspired against him because of the blood of the son of Jehoiada" (v25).** The Hebrew text has "sons" of Jehoiada, which may suggest other sons died as well. The ESV follows the Greek and Latin in reading the singular. Joash had ordered Zechariah's death by conspiracy (v21). Now he dies by conspiracy, "on his bed." 2 Kings 12:20 adds that it happened "in the house of Millo," a building near the palace.
- **"They did not bury him in the tombs of the kings" (v25).** He was buried in the city of David but not in the royal tombs, the very honor the priest Jehoiada had been given (v16). The king lies outside, and the priest who made him king lies inside.
- **"Zabad the son of Shimeath the Ammonite, and Jehozabad the son of Shimrith the Moabite" (v26).** 2 Kings 12:21 gives the names as Jozacar son of Shimeath and Jehozabad son of Shomer. Only Chronicles adds that their mothers were Ammonite and Moabite. Some see a pointed irony here: the king who turned to foreign gods was killed by sons of foreign mothers. "Zabad" means "he has given" and "Jehozabad" "the LORD has given," which makes the irony sharper. Amaziah later executes them but spares their children, in obedience to Deuteronomy 24:16 (25:3–4).
- **"The Story of the Book of the Kings" (v27).** "Story" translates *midrash*, which in later Hebrew means a commentary or study. Here it probably means a written account or annals. The Tyndale notes say this source has not survived. It also recorded "the many oracles against him," so Zechariah was not the only prophet who warned Joash.
- **Commentators:** JFB says that a year after the first invasion was bought off, the Syrians defeated Judah "with a mere handful of men." Guzik stresses that Joash never repented and that the promise of his early years ended in failure, a lesson that a good start does not guarantee a good finish. Henry adds that God struck Joash "with great diseases" even before the Syrians left. JFB notes that the assassins' mothers were foreign, describing them as men "whose fathers were Jews, but their mothers aliens."

#### Pointing to Christ
Joash's faith was real enough to rebuild a temple, but it lived on another man's strength, and when Jehoiada died it died with him. Every human mediator dies, however faithful. The New Testament's answer is a priest who does not: "he holds his priesthood permanently, because he continues forever. Consequently, he is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:24–25). Zechariah's blood cried out "may the LORD see and avenge!" Jesus, killed by the leaders of his own people, prayed "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34), and his blood "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24), and better than Zechariah's too.
