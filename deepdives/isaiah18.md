*Setting:* Chapter 17 ended with the roaring nations that would vanish "before morning," Assyria's army broken at Jerusalem. Chapter 18 stays in that crisis and turns to the far south. Cush, the land along the Nile beyond Egypt's southern border (modern Sudan, called "Ethiopia" in older Bibles, though it is not today's Ethiopia), had become a great power. Around 715 BC its kings conquered Egypt and ruled it as Egypt's Twenty-fifth Dynasty, sometimes called the Nubian or Ethiopian dynasty. One of them, Tirhakah, marched against Sennacherib in 701 BC (37:9). These Cushite kings tried to build an alliance of small states against Assyria, and this chapter pictures their envoys arriving in Jerusalem. Isaiah sends them home with a message. God does not need the alliance. He will deal with Assyria himself, in his own time, and one day Cush itself will bring gifts to Zion.

#### An Oracle Concerning Cush (vv1–7)
- **"Ah, land of whirring wings" (v1).** "Ah" (*hoy*) can be a cry of woe or simply a call for attention, "Ho!" JFB prefers the second here. Cush is not threatened in this chapter, so it reads more as an address. "Whirring wings" translates a rare word that the lexicon gives as "whirring, buzzing," probably of insects. The Nile valley swarmed with flies and locusts, and some scholars link the word to the tsetse fly. Others take the wings as the sails of ships or the clatter of armies. Guzik takes them as the insects of the Nile.
- **"Beyond the rivers of Cush" (v1).** From Judah's point of view, Cush lay at the edge of the known world, past the cataracts of the Nile. The rivers are the Nile and its great branches, the Blue Nile and White Nile, and the Atbara, which divide the land (v2). Cush appears in Genesis 2:13 as one of the lands around Eden, and Moses married a Cushite woman (Num 12:1).
- **"Which sends ambassadors by the sea, in vessels of papyrus" (v2).** "The sea" here likely means the Nile itself, which was wide enough to be called a sea (19:5). Papyrus reed boats, bound in bundles and sealed with pitch, were common Nile transport. Moses was laid in a papyrus basket of the same kind (Exod 2:3). The envoys were racing about the region to organize resistance to Assyria.
- **"Go, you swift messengers" (v2).** Many interpreters read this as Isaiah's reply to the envoys: go back home. Judah does not need your treaty. Isaiah consistently opposed relying on Egypt and its allies (30:1–3; 31:1, "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help").
- **"A nation tall and smooth, a people feared near and far" (v2).** The Greek historian Herodotus described the Cushites as the tallest and most handsome of men. "Smooth" may mean their glossy skin or that they were clean-shaven. They were also respected warriors, "mighty and conquering," literally a nation "of line, line, and trampling," which may mean a people of strength who tread others down. In an age when Assyria terrified everyone, Cush was a power to reckon with.
- **"All you inhabitants of the world… when a signal is raised on the mountains, look!" (v3).** Isaiah turns from the envoys to the whole world. A signal flag on a hill and a ram's-horn trumpet were how armies were called (Judg 3:27; 6:34). When God acts against Assyria, the whole world will see and hear it. There is no need for messengers running everywhere.
- **"I will quietly look from my dwelling" (v4).** This is the heart of the chapter. While the nations rush around making plans, God is still. He watches from his dwelling, untroubled (compare Ps 2:1–4, "He who sits in the heavens laughs"). The Tyndale notes stress that God acts in his own time. Stillness is not absence. He is watching closely.
- **"Like clear heat in sunshine, like a cloud of dew in the heat of harvest" (v4).** Both images are quiet but powerful. Shimmering summer heat and morning dew both help a crop ripen silently. God lets Assyria's plans grow, apparently unhindered, until they are ripe.
- **"Before the harvest… he cuts off the shoots with pruning hooks" (v5).** Then God steps in, at exactly the right moment: just before Assyria's plans bear fruit. He prunes, not to help the vine but to destroy it, lopping off the branches and clearing them away. Assyria's campaign seemed sure to succeed until the night it ended (37:36). The Tyndale notes read the ripening as Cush's own plans and alliances, which would not help. Most commentators, including JFB and Guzik, take the vine as Assyria.
- **"They shall all of them be left to the birds of prey… and to the beasts of the earth" (v6).** The pruned branches become the bodies of the fallen army, left unburied. Birds feed on them all summer, beasts all winter. The image is grim and complete (compare Ezek 39:17–20; Rev 19:17–18).
- **"At that time tribute will be brought to the LORD of hosts from a people tall and smooth" (v7).** The chapter ends with a reversal. The same people, described with the same words as v2, now come not with treaty proposals but with tribute, gifts of honor, "to Mount Zion, the place of the name of the LORD of hosts." After Sennacherib's defeat, "many brought gifts to the LORD to Jerusalem and precious things to Hezekiah king of Judah" (2 Chr 32:23). The Tyndale notes connect v7 with that.
- **The fuller fulfillment.** The Psalms had already said, "Cush shall hasten to stretch out her hands to God" (Ps 68:31), and Zephaniah says worshipers will come "from beyond the rivers of Cush," using Isaiah's own phrase (Zeph 3:10). In Acts 8, an official of the queen of Cush (the Candace, of the kingdom of Meroë) returns from worshiping in Jerusalem, reading Isaiah, and Philip tells him the good news about Jesus (Acts 8:26–39). Christianity took root in Nubia and in Ethiopia (the Aksum kingdom) by the 300s–500s AD, among the oldest Christian churches in the world.
- **Commentators:** Henry says it hardly matters which land "shadowing with wings" is meant, since the point is that God has, on his people's account, "a quarrel with it." He sees God as one who may "seem unconcerned in the distress of his people for a time" but who "will at length appear against their enemies," and he says the outcome "shall redound very much to the glory of God." JFB identifies the land as Cush, specifically Meroë between the Nile and the Atbara, describes the papyrus boats "daubed over with pitch," and thinks Cush, Egypt and Judah were leagued together against Sennacherib. JFB points to the spread of Christianity to Abyssinia in the 300s through Frumentius, while thinking the full fulfillment of v7 is "probably still future." Guzik stresses that God does not condemn Cush here: the offer of alliance was well meant but unnecessary, because Judah should trust the Lord. For him the clearest fulfillment of v7 is the Ethiopian official of Acts 8 and the long-lasting Ethiopian church.

#### Pointing to Christ
While the nations scurry about making treaties, God sits still and watches, and then acts at the decisive moment. That is how he acted in Christ, "when the fullness of time had come" (Gal 4:4). The chapter's last line, a distant African people bringing gifts to Zion, began to come true on a desert road when an Ethiopian official heard Isaiah 53 explained and was baptized into Christ. The gospel crossed "beyond the rivers of Cush," and the nations still bring their worship to the place of the Lord's name, which is now Jesus himself (John 2:19–21; Rev 7:9).
