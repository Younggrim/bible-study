*Setting:* The division of the land west of the Jordan begins, still at Gilgal (14:6), and Judah's lot comes up first. That fits Jacob's blessing, which gave Judah the leadership among his brothers: "The scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10). Reuben, Simeon and Levi, the three older sons, had lost the firstborn's place through their sins (Gen 34; 35:22; 49:3–7), so the leadership passed to Judah and the double portion to Joseph (1 Chr 5:1–2). These two tribes are settled first, Judah in the south (ch 15) and Joseph in the center (chs 16–17). Judah's chapter is the longest and most detailed of all the tribal lists: a precise boundary line, a story about Caleb's family, and a list of more than a hundred towns.

#### The Allotment for Judah (vv1–63)
- **The southern border (vv1–4).** Judah's southern line is the southern border of the whole promised land (Num 34:3–5). It starts at the southern tip of the Dead Sea ("the bay that faces southward," literally "the tongue"), climbs the "ascent of Akrabbim" ("Scorpions' Pass"), runs past Kadesh-barnea, and ends at the "Brook of Egypt," the wadi near El-Arish on the coast. Most of this region is the Negeb, dry and thinly settled. So Judah, the strongest tribe, guarded Israel's southern frontier toward Edom and Egypt.
- **"This shall be your south boundary" (v4).** The Hebrew suddenly says "your," as though quoting the instructions God gave Moses for the whole land (Num 34:3–6).
- **The eastern and northern borders (vv5–11).** The east is the Dead Sea ("the Salt Sea"). The north runs from the mouth of the Jordan up through the hills toward Jerusalem and down to the Mediterranean. Along the way it passes several memorable places:
- **"The stone of Bohan the son of Reuben" (v6).** A landmark near the Jordan, probably a standing stone or memorial for a Reubenite named Bohan, perhaps one who fought in the conquest. Nothing else is known of him.
- **"The Valley of Achor" (v7).** "Valley of Trouble," where Achan, a man of Judah, was stoned for stealing devoted things at Jericho (7:24–26). Judah's own border runs past the place where one of its men brought disaster on Israel. Matthew Henry suggests it was there "to remind the men of Judah of the trouble which Achan… gave," so they would not be proud. Later the prophet Hosea promised that God would make "the Valley of Achor a door of hope" (Hos 2:15).
- **"The Valley of the Son of Hinnom at the southern shoulder of the Jebusite (that is, Jerusalem)" (v8).** The border runs right along the valley south of Jerusalem, leaving the city itself just north of the line, in Benjamin's territory (18:16, 28). Because Jerusalem sat on the border and belonged to neither great tribe outright, David could later make it a neutral capital for all Israel (2 Sam 5:6–9). The Valley of Hinnom later became notorious as the place where kings of Judah sacrificed children to Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31). Its Hebrew name, *Ge Hinnom*, became the Greek *Gehenna*, the word Jesus uses for hell (Matt 5:22; 10:28).
- **"The spring of the waters of Nephtoah" (v9).** Probably the spring at Lifta, just northwest of Jerusalem. Some scholars connect the name with Pharaoh Merneptah, since an Egyptian papyrus mentions "the wells of Merneptah" in the hills, but that is only a suggestion.
- **"Baalah (that is, Kiriath-jearim)" (v9).** A town about nine miles west of Jerusalem. The ark of the covenant stayed there for twenty years, after the Philistines sent it back, until David brought it to Jerusalem (1 Sam 7:1–2; 2 Sam 6:2).
- **"Beth-shemesh… Timnah… Ekron" (vv10–11).** The border drops west through the foothills past towns that appear in the Samson stories (Judg 14:1) and the ark's return (1 Sam 6), and reaches the sea near Jabneel (later Jamnia).
- **"The west boundary was the Great Sea" (v12).** The Mediterranean. In practice Judah never held the coast, which soon became Philistine land. Later some of Judah's land was given to Simeon in the south (19:1–9) and Dan in the west (19:40–48).
- **"Caleb drove out from there the three sons of Anak, Sheshai and Ahiman and Talmai" (vv13–14).** The same three names the spies reported at Hebron forty-five years earlier (Num 13:22). The very giants who terrified Israel's first generation were driven out by the one man of that generation who said they could be beaten. JFB suggests "three sons of Anak" means three clans or chiefs of the Anakim. Henry notes that the text says Caleb "drove them out," not that he killed them: they fled before him.
- **"Debir formerly was Kiriath-sepher" (v15).** *Kiriath-sepher* means "city of the book" or "city of the scribe." Verse 49 calls it Kiriath-sannah. Some have guessed it was a center of records or learning. Joshua had struck Debir in the southern campaign (10:38–39), but it seems the Canaanites retook it while Israel's army was away, so it had to be captured again. Judges 1:11–15 retells this same story.
- **"I will give him Achsah my daughter as wife" (v16).** Offering a daughter as a prize for a military feat was a known custom. Saul did the same for the man who would kill Goliath (1 Sam 17:25). Caleb, the man of faith, wants a son-in-law who will fight in faith.
- **"Othniel the son of Kenaz, the brother of Caleb" (v17).** The Hebrew can mean that Othniel was Caleb's younger brother, or that Kenaz was Caleb's brother, making Othniel his nephew. Either way, he is family. Othniel later became the first of the judges, who delivered Israel from a foreign king after Joshua's death (Judg 3:9–11). Courage he showed here as a young man served Israel for a lifetime.
- **"She urged him to ask her father for a field" (v18).** Achsah wants more than the dry land she has received. The Hebrew says she urged her husband. Some Greek manuscripts (and the Greek of Judges 1:14) reverse it, saying he urged her. Either way, Achsah is the one who speaks.
- **"She dismounted from her donkey" (v18).** The verb is rare and its sense is debated. Most take it as a respectful getting down from her donkey before her father, as Rebekah did before Isaac (Gen 24:64), or as a gesture that showed she had something to ask. Her father understands at once: "What do you want?"
- **"Give me a blessing… give me also springs of water" (v19).** "Negeb" means "dry land." A field there without water was nearly useless. Achsah calls her request a "blessing," a gift from a father to his child. Caleb gives generously: "the upper springs and the lower springs." Two springs still flowing in the hills near the likely site of Debir are often connected with this story. Like her father, Achsah asked boldly for what she knew was good, and received more than land.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that if we resist the devil, "though he fall not, yet he will flee," as the giants fled from Caleb. He commends Caleb for pushing on to Debir for the good of his tribe and not only his own family, and he draws three lessons from Achsah: it is no breach of the tenth commandment "moderately to desire those comforts" that can be had in a fair way; husbands and wives should agree in asking their heavenly Father for the best blessings; and parents should give generously to their children. He also mentions the old habit of praying for "upper springs" (spiritual blessings) and "nether springs" (blessings for this life). JFB calls Achsah's springs a lesson "that if earthly parents are ready to bestow on their children that which is good, much more will our heavenly Father" (compare Luke 11:13). Guzik sees Caleb as a bold man who made others bold, and Achsah as her father's daughter, unafraid to ask for a blessing.
- **The town list (vv20–62).** Judah's towns are listed in four regions and twelve districts, each closing with a total ("twenty-nine cities with their villages"). Many scholars think these districts reflect the way Judah was later organized for government and taxes under its kings, which shows how carefully the list was kept and updated. Many sites cannot be identified today. The four regions are:
- **"In the extreme south" (vv21–32).** The Negeb towns, including Beersheba, where Abraham and Isaac lived and dug wells (Gen 21:31; 26:33), Hormah, and Ziklag, which a Philistine king later gave to David (1 Sam 27:6). Several of these towns were later assigned to Simeon (19:1–9). Kerioth-hezron (v25) has often been suggested as the hometown of Judas Iscariot, if "Iscariot" means "man of Kerioth," though that is uncertain. The total says twenty-nine, but the names listed come to more; most likely some names that were once separate entries were later run together, or some villages were counted with their towns.
- **"In the lowland" (vv33–47).** The *Shephelah*, the rolling foothills between the hills and the coast, Judah's best farmland and its battleground. Zorah and Eshtaol were Samson's home country (Judg 13:2, 25). Socoh and Azekah frame the valley where David fought Goliath (1 Sam 17:1). Adullam's cave hid David from Saul (1 Sam 22:1). Lachish and Libnah fell in chapter 10. The last district (vv45–47) is the Philistine plain, Ekron, Ashdod and Gaza, "with their towns and their villages." Unlike the other districts, it has no total. It was promised, but Judah never really held it.
- **"In the hill country" (vv48–60).** The central ridge of Judah, from Debir north to near Jerusalem. Hebron is here (v54). So are Maon, Carmel and Ziph (v55), where David hid from Saul and met Nabal and Abigail (1 Sam 23:24; 25:2), and Jezreel (v56), the home of David's wife Ahinoam, not the northern Jezreel. Strikingly, Bethlehem, David's birthplace, is missing.
- **A missing district (after v59).** The Greek Septuagint includes an extra district of eleven towns at this point, including Tekoa, "Ephrathah (that is, Bethlehem)," and Bether. Many scholars think a copyist's eye skipped a line in the Hebrew text, so the whole district dropped out. The ESV follows the Hebrew. Either way, Bethlehem belonged to Judah (Mic 5:2; Matt 2:6).
- **"In the wilderness" (vv61–62).** The barren slopes falling east to the Dead Sea. The "City of Salt" may be the site later occupied by the Qumran community, where the Dead Sea Scrolls were found, and Secacah is named in one of those scrolls. En-gedi is the oasis with a waterfall where David spared Saul's life in a cave (1 Sam 24:1–7).
- **"But the Jebusites, the inhabitants of Jerusalem, the people of Judah could not drive out" (v63).** The chapter ends with a failure. Jerusalem sat on a ridge with steep valleys on three sides and a hidden water source, the Gihon spring. Its king had been killed (10:23–26), and Judges 1:8 records that Judah once captured and burned part of it, but the Jebusites held on to their fortress. Judges 1:21 says the same of Benjamin. The Jebusites stayed until David took the stronghold of Zion (2 Sam 5:6–9). "Could not" is honest. Elsewhere Joshua says God would drive the nations out (13:6). The problem was not God's power but Israel's faith and persistence.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks the lots were cast first between Judah and Joseph for south and north, and that the smaller tribes were fitted in later at Shiloh. He notes that Judah's boundaries were fixed so that even the strongest tribe "must live so as that their neighbours might live by them." JFB arranges the town list in four divisions, and says the best way to grasp it is "by looking at the map." Guzik says there was no good excuse for Judah's failure at Jerusalem, since God had promised help, and he applies it to believers: struggle does not excuse failing to obey. The Tyndale notes explain that Jerusalem's position between Judah and Benjamin later made it a fitting royal city for David's line.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah's lot came up first, and the long list of its towns names places that would fill the story of David: Hebron, Adullam, Ziph, En-gedi, Ziklag. The book quietly lays out the map of the coming king's life. The town it left unconquered, Jerusalem, became David's city and the place of the temple, and the town it may have dropped by accident, Bethlehem, became the birthplace of David's greater Son. "The scepter shall not depart from Judah… until tribute comes to him" (Gen 49:10). Achsah asked her father for springs of water in a dry land and received both the upper and the lower. Jesus, the Lion of the tribe of Judah (Rev 5:5), offers more: "If you, then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him!" (Luke 11:13), and "whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again" (John 4:14).
