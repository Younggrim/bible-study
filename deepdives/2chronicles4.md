*Setting:* About 966–959 BC, in Jerusalem. Chapter 3 described the temple building itself, ending with the two great bronze pillars at its entrance. Chapter 4 turns to the furnishings: first the bronze objects for the courtyard (the great altar, the huge water basin called "the Sea," and ten smaller basins), then the gold objects for inside the house (ten lampstands, ten tables, the incense altar and many small utensils). Most of this follows 1 Kings 7:23–51 closely, sometimes word for word, but the Chronicler adds the bronze altar, the tables, and the courts. The ESV section continues to 5:1, which reports the work finished and David's gifts stored in the treasury. These lists are not exciting reading, and the Chronicler does not pad them. For his readers after the exile, who had only some of the old vessels back from Babylon (Ezra 1:7–11), they recorded what God's house had been and how its worship was meant to work.

#### The Temple's Furnishings (vv1–22)
- **"An altar of bronze, twenty cubits long and twenty cubits wide and ten cubits high" (v1).** About 30 feet square and 15 feet high, four times the length and width of the tabernacle's altar (Exod 27:1). Kings mentions this altar later (1 Kgs 8:64; 9:25) but never describes it being made. Chronicles puts it first, because the altar of burnt offering was the heart of temple worship. Every approach to God began with sacrifice. The Hebrew word for altar, *mizbeach*, comes from the verb "to slaughter." An altar ten cubits high would have needed a ramp or steps (Ezek 43:17). The law forbade steps for an altar so the priest would not be exposed (Exod 20:26), so it may have been a ramp, or the priests' linen undergarments met the concern (Exod 28:42–43).
- **"The sea of cast metal" (v2).** A giant round bronze basin, 15 feet across and 7½ feet high, cast in one piece. **"A line of thirty cubits measured its circumference."** Readers sometimes notice that a 10-cubit diameter should give about 31.4 cubits, not 30. The figures are round numbers, and since the rim flared outward "like the brim of a cup" (v5), the 10 cubits may be across the outer rim and the 30 around the body below it. With a handbreadth of thickness (v5), the inner diameter would also be smaller. The text is giving a builder's measurements, not a geometry lesson.
- **"Under it were figures of gourds" (v3).** The Hebrew here reads "oxen" (*beqarim*), while 1 Kings 7:24 has "gourds" (*peqaʿim*), two words that look and sound alike. The ESV follows Kings and footnotes the Hebrew. It may be a copying slip, or the decorations may have looked like small ox heads, as JFB suggests.
- **"It stood on twelve oxen, three facing north, three facing west, three facing south, and three facing east" (v4).** The oxen probably stood for the twelve tribes, arranged like Israel's camp around the tabernacle (Num 2), three on each side. Bulls were also a symbol of strength. About two centuries later King Ahaz took the Sea down off the oxen and set it on a stone base, probably to pay tribute to Assyria (2 Kgs 16:17), and the Babylonians later broke it up and carried away the bronze (2 Kgs 25:13).
- **"Its brim was made like the brim of a cup, like the flower of a lily" (v5).** A flared rim shaped like an opening flower. **"It held 3,000 baths."** About 17,500 gallons (66,000 liters). First Kings 7:26 says 2,000 baths. Explanations include a copying error in one figure, a difference in units (a larger or smaller bath in different eras), or Kings giving its usual filling and Chronicles its full capacity, which is what Henry suggests.
- **What was the Sea for? (v6).** **"The sea was for the priests to wash in."** It replaced the bronze basin of the tabernacle, where priests washed their hands and feet before serving (Exod 30:17–21), on pain of death if they did not. It also had a symbolic weight. In the ancient Near East "the sea" often stood for chaos, the untamed deep. In Israel's temple the sea is a bronze basin standing still on twelve oxen, tamed and put to God's service, as in creation God set limits for the waters (Gen 1:6–10; Job 38:8–11; Ps 93:3–4). Some also see the waters of Eden here. Revelation's vision of the new creation has a "sea of glass" before God's throne (Rev 4:6) and, at the end, "the sea was no more" (Rev 21:1).
- **"Ten basins in which to wash… to rinse off what was used for the burnt offering" (v6).** These were smaller bronze basins on wheeled stands (1 Kgs 7:27–39 describes the stands in detail), placed five on each side of the house. They were for washing the parts of the sacrifices (Lev 1:9, 13). The priests and the offerings both had to be clean.
- **"Ten golden lampstands as prescribed" (v7).** The tabernacle had one seven-branched lampstand (Exod 25:31–40). The temple had ten, five on each side of the main hall. **"As prescribed"** (literally "according to their rule") means they were made to the pattern in the law, just multiplied. The hall would have been lit by seventy flames.
- **"Ten tables" (v8).** The tabernacle had one table for the bread of the Presence (Exod 25:23–30). Kings mentions only "the golden table" (1 Kgs 7:48). Chronicles says ten, and v19 calls them "the tables for the bread of the Presence." Either all ten held bread, or one held the bread while others held vessels. The twelve loaves stood before God every Sabbath as a sign of the covenant with the twelve tribes (Lev 24:5–9). **"A hundred basins of gold"** were probably bowls for catching and sprinkling sacrificial blood (Exod 24:6).
- **"The court of the priests and the great court" (v9).** Kings speaks of "the inner court" (1 Kgs 6:36) and "the great court" (1 Kgs 7:12). The inner court, around the house, was for the priests and the altar. The great court, outside it, was where the people gathered. The old covenant kept God's people at a distance, in graded zones of holiness, a picture the letter to the Hebrews draws on (Heb 9:6–8).
- **"He set the sea at the southeast corner of the house" (v10).** Facing east toward the entrance, to the right of the door, between the porch and the altar (1 Kgs 7:39).
- **"Hiram also made the pots, the shovels, and the basins" (v11).** Pots for carrying away ashes, shovels for clearing the altar, and bowls (Exod 27:3). Verses 11–18 repeat 1 Kings 7:40–47 almost word for word, as a summary of Huram-abi's bronze work.
- **"The two pillars, the bowls, and the two capitals… the two latticeworks… the 400 pomegranates" (vv12–13).** These are the pillars Jachin and Boaz (3:15–17). Two rows of a hundred pomegranates on each lattice gives 400 in all. Chapter 3 mentioned "a hundred" (3:16), probably counting one row.
- **"The stands also, and the basins on the stands" (v14).** The wheeled stands of 1 Kings 7:27–37, decorated with lions, oxen and cherubim.
- **"Huram-abi made of burnished bronze" (v16).** Polished bronze, bright as gold when new. The craftsman is named again as Huram-abi, as in 2:13.
- **"In the plain of the Jordan the king cast them, in the clay ground between Succoth and Zeredah" (v17).** Casting objects this large required clay molds dug into the ground and a lot of fuel. The Jordan valley had thick clay soil and was near forests. Succoth is usually placed at Tell Deir Alla, east of the Jordan, where archaeologists have found evidence of metalworking furnaces. First Kings 7:46 has "Zarethan" for "Zeredah." The site is about 40 miles from Jerusalem, so the finished pieces, some weighing many tons, had to be hauled up a steep climb of over 3,000 feet.
- **"The weight of the bronze was not sought" (v18).** Literally "was not searched out." There was so much that no one bothered to weigh it (1 Kgs 7:47). David had gathered "bronze in quantities beyond weighing" for this purpose (1 Chr 22:3, 14), and he had taken much of it from the cities of Hadadezer (1 Chr 18:8). Chronicles says that was the source of the Sea, the pillars and the bronze vessels.
- **"The golden altar, the tables for the bread of the Presence" (v19).** The golden altar was the incense altar, which stood in the main hall before the veil (Exod 30:1–10). Incense rising before God every morning and evening became a picture of prayer (Ps 141:2; Rev 8:3–4).
- **"The lampstands and their lamps of pure gold to burn before the inner sanctuary, as prescribed" (v20).** Kept burning continually (Exod 27:20–21; Lev 24:2–4).
- **"The flowers, the lamps, and the tongs, of purest gold" (v21).** The "flowers" were the flower-shaped cups of the lampstands (Exod 25:33). The tongs were for handling wicks. The detail that even the tongs were of the purest gold shows nothing in God's house was too small to matter.
- **"The sockets of the temple… for the inner doors to the Most Holy Place and for the doors of the nave… were of gold" (v22).** "Sockets" are probably the door hinges or pivots. The more inward the object, the purer the metal: bronze outside, gold inside, "purest gold" in the inner sanctuary's tools.
- **"Thus all the work… was finished" (5:1).** The ESV section ends with the first verse of chapter 5. Solomon brought in the silver, gold and vessels **"that David his father had dedicated"** (1 Chr 18:11; 26:26) and stored them in the temple treasuries. The house was David's vision and Solomon's work.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the altar was made "such a capacious scaffold" to hold the great number of sacrifices, and on the Sea, "To us there is a fountain opened for all believers" (compare Zech 13:1). On the ten basins: "As the priests must be washed, so must the sacrifices," which Henry applies to cleansing our own worship from "vain thoughts." He reads the ten lampstands as the growing light of Scripture: "The written word is a lamp and a light, shining in a dark place." JFB reads the lampstands the same way, as the "growing light" of the word. JFB gives the altar as about 35 feet square, using a longer cubit, and suggests the larger capacity of the Sea covered the extra water when it worked "as a fountain." He thinks the tabernacle's basin had probably been destroyed. Guzik notes that the altar was the "killing-place" and quotes the scholar J. Barton Payne that God may be approached only through sacrifice. He also passes on John Trapp's view of the twelve oxen as a picture of the twelve apostles carrying the water of life to the world, which is application, not exegesis. The Tyndale notes connect the Sea to the tamed waters of creation and the garden-temple imagery of Eden.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every object in this chapter is about how sinful people can come near a holy God: an altar for sacrifice, water for washing, light, bread and incense. Christ fulfils each one. He is the sacrifice offered once for all (Heb 9:26) and the one who washes his people (John 13:8–10; Titus 3:5). He is the light of the world (John 8:12) and the bread of life (John 6:35), and he "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through him (Heb 7:25). The courts kept the people at a distance. In Christ, every believer is brought near as a priest (1 Pet 2:9; Heb 10:19–22).
