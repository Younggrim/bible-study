*Setting:* Israel is at Mount Sinai. The priests have just been told that their job is "to distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (10:10). Chapters 11–15 now teach what "clean" and "unclean" mean, starting with food. This is the first of five chapters on purity: animals (ch 11), childbirth (ch 12), skin diseases and mold (chs 13–14) and bodily discharges (ch 15). Notice that God speaks here "to Moses and Aaron" (v1) together, because Aaron will now have to teach and apply these rules. A parallel list appears in Deuteronomy 14:3–21.

#### Clean and Unclean Animals (vv1–47)
- **What "clean" and "unclean" mean.** These words are not about dirt or about sin. "Unclean" (*tame'*) describes a state that kept a person or thing from coming near the sanctuary. Most uncleanness in these chapters came from ordinary life, it was not wrong in itself, and it was removed by washing and waiting "until the evening" (v24), not by a sacrifice. What *was* wrong was eating what God had forbidden, or coming to his house while unclean (7:20–21).
- **Land animals: "parts the hoof and is cloven-footed and chews the cud" (vv2–3).** Two tests, both required. Cattle, sheep, goats and deer pass both. The clean land animals are essentially the animals of the shepherd and herdsman, and the ones offered on the altar were drawn from these.
- **The camel, the rock badger and the hare (vv4–6).** Each fails the hoof test. The "rock badger" (*shaphan*) is the hyrax, a small rabbit-sized animal that lives among rocks (Ps 104:18; Prov 30:26). Neither the hyrax nor the hare is a true ruminant with a multi-chambered stomach. But both move their jaws constantly in a chewing motion, and the hare re-eats its own soft droppings to digest them a second time. Scripture describes animals as an ordinary observer would see them, the way we still say "the sun rises." The Tyndale notes make the same point, and the rule does not depend on the cud anyway: these animals are excluded for their feet.
- **"The pig, because it parts the hoof… but does not chew the cud" (v7).** The pig became the best-known unclean animal in later Jewish life. Under the Greek king Antiochus IV, Jews died rather than eat pork (2 Macc 6:18–31; 7:1). Pigs were also used in some pagan rituals (Isa 65:4; 66:17).
- **"You shall not touch their carcasses" (v8).** Living unclean animals could be handled. Donkeys and camels were work animals. It was their dead bodies that made a person unclean.
- **Water creatures: "fins and scales" (vv9–12).** That rule rules out shellfish, eels, catfish and sharks. These are called **"detestable"** (*sheqets*), a word BDB defines as "detestation, detestable thing," used in the Old Testament almost only for these forbidden creatures. It is a strong word, but here it means "off-limits for you," not "evil in itself." God made them and called them good (Gen 1:21).
- **Birds (vv13–19).** No rule is given, only a list of about twenty. Many of the Hebrew names are rare, and translations differ on several of them, so lists like "nighthawk" or "tawny owl" are scholars' best guesses. What is clear is that most are birds of prey or scavengers, birds that eat flesh with the blood still in it or feed on carcasses. Since Israel was forbidden to eat blood (7:26–27; 17:10–14), it makes sense that animals living on blood and death were unclean. **"The bat"** closes the list. It is not a bird by modern classification, but Hebrew grouped "flying things" together.
- **Winged insects (vv20–23).** Most are unclean, but four kinds that hop on **"jointed legs above their feet"** may be eaten: types of locust, cricket and grasshopper. **"Go on all fours"** is an idiom for creeping along, not a count of legs. Locusts were common food in the region. John the Baptist ate them (Matt 3:4).
- **Uncleanness by touch (vv24–28).** Touching or carrying the carcass of an unclean animal made a person unclean **"until the evening."** The Israelite day began at sundown, so the uncleanness lasted until the new day. Carrying meant also washing one's clothes. Animals that **"walk on their paws"** (v27), such as dogs, cats, lions and bears, are added to the list.
- **Swarming things on the ground (vv29–38).** Eight small creatures are named: rodents and lizards. These were the pests most likely to fall into a household's pots and grain. The rules here are very practical. Wooden items, cloth, leather and sacks touched by a dead one must be washed (v32). A clay pot must be **broken** (v33), since its porous surface could not be cleaned. Ovens likewise. But **"a spring or a cistern holding water shall be clean"** (v36), since a family could not destroy its water supply, and dry seed for planting stayed clean unless it had been soaked (vv37–38). God's law takes real life into account.
- **Clean animals that die on their own (vv39–40).** Even a cow or sheep that died naturally made a person unclean, because its blood had not been drained. Deuteronomy 14:21 allowed such meat to be given or sold to foreigners, who were not under these rules.
- **"Whatever goes on its belly" (vv41–42).** Snakes, worms and many-legged creepers are all forbidden. Some readers hear an echo of the serpent in Eden, cursed to go "on your belly" (Gen 3:14). The text does not make the link, but the wording is the same.
- **"Be holy, for I am holy" (vv44–45).** Here is the reason for the whole chapter, stated twice. This is the first time the great theme of Leviticus is spoken in these words. It returns in 19:2, 20:7 and 20:26, and Peter quotes it to Christians: "You shall be holy, for I am holy" (1 Pet 1:16). God grounds it in redemption: **"I am the LORD who brought you up out of the land of Egypt to be your God."** He saved them first, and then called them to be different.
- **Why these animals?** The text gives holiness as the reason but does not explain each choice, and several explanations have been offered. (1) *Health:* some forbidden animals carry disease or parasites. This may be a side benefit, but it cannot explain the list, since camels and horses are as healthy to eat as cattle, and the Tyndale notes say plainly that the evidence does not support health as the main reason. (2) *Separation:* a distinct diet kept Israel from easy table-fellowship with idol-worshiping nations, and later Jews like Daniel saw it that way (Dan 1:8). (3) *Order and wholeness:* many scholars note that clean animals are the "normal" ones for their realm, with fins and scales in water, hooves and cud on land, while unclean ones blur the categories or live on blood and death. (4) *Symbol:* the clean animals mirror Israel itself, set apart from the nations as these animals were set apart from the rest. Most commentators combine several of these. All agree the main reason is the one given: God's people were to be marked as his, even at the dinner table.
- **"To make a distinction" (vv46–47).** The closing summary uses the same verb as 10:10. Everyday eating became a daily lesson in telling the holy from the common.
- **What happened to these laws?** Jesus taught that food going into a person cannot defile the heart, and Mark adds, "Thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:18–19). Peter's vision of unclean animals (Acts 10:9–16) taught that the wall between Jew and Gentile had come down: "What God has made clean, do not call common." That fits the "separation" reading above. When God joined Jews and Gentiles in one church, the laws that marked them apart were no longer needed (Eph 2:14–15). Paul says the food laws were "a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ" (Col 2:16–17), and "everything created by God is good… if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Tim 4:4).
- **Commentators:** Henry gives three reasons: obedience ("it is reason enough that God would have it so"), health ("the Lord is for the body"), and separation from nations that used some of these animals in idol worship. He also offers a spiritual reading: chewing the cud may picture meditating on God's word, and the parted hoof a just and careful walk, and a clean person needs both. Guzik leans more on the health benefits than the Tyndale notes do, and adds that the clean/unclean distinction already existed in Noah's day (Gen 7:2). He stresses that uncleanness was ceremonial, not moral, and that Christians are free to eat any food (Acts 15; 1 Tim 4:1–5). JFB says the distinctions were meant "to keep them a distinct and peculiar people" and also served "sanitary as well as religious ends." It identifies the coney as the hyrax, "not the rabbit, for it is not found in Palestine or Arabia."

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel learned at every meal that God's people are set apart for him. The lesson was never really about pork or shellfish. It was about a holy God making a people holy. Jesus fulfilled what the food laws pointed to. He makes people clean from the inside, where uncleanness really lives (Mark 7:20–23), and through his cross he joined Jew and Gentile at one table. The command remains, now written on the heart rather than on the menu: "As he who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct" (1 Pet 1:15).
