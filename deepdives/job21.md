*Setting:* The second round of speeches is ending. Zophar, the bluntest of Job's three friends, has just finished his second speech (ch 20), and it was one long portrait of the wicked man. His joy is "but for a moment" (20:5). He dies young, "his bones are full of his youthful vigor" but it lies down with him in the dust (20:11). The riches he swallowed he will vomit up (20:15), "the heavens will reveal his iniquity" (20:27), and "this is the wicked man's portion from God" (20:29). Bildad had said much the same in chapter 18: the lamp of the wicked is put out, and he has "no posterity or progeny" (18:5, 19). The friends never name Job, but every reader knows whom they mean. Job has lost his wealth, his children and his health, so by their logic he must be the wicked man. Until now Job has mostly argued about his own innocence. In chapter 21 he changes ground and attacks the friends' theory head-on. Their whole case rests on one claim: that in this life the wicked always suffer and the righteous always prosper. Job says simply that it isn't true. Look around. The wicked often live long, grow rich, raise happy children and die in peace. This is the only speech in the first two rounds where Job speaks to his friends from start to finish without turning to pray, and it is the closest he comes to a calm, reasoned argument.

#### Job Replies: The Wicked Do Prosper (vv1–34)
- **"Keep listening to my words, and let this be your comfort" (v2).** "Comfort" is *tanchumot*, "consolations." The lexicon shows it is the same rare word Eliphaz used when he asked, "Are the comforts of God too small for you?" (15:11). Eliphaz meant the friends' own speeches. Job answers: if you want to console me, just listen. The Tyndale notes connect this with Job's earlier plea that his friends would keep silent (13:5). Their best comfort would be their attention, not their words. JFB hears a jab at Eliphaz's boast: the friends' quiet listening will be the only "consolations" Job gets from them.
- **"Bear with me, and I will speak, and after I have spoken, mock on" (v3).** Job expects mockery and asks only to finish first. The "mock on" is singular in Hebrew, and many take it as aimed at Zophar, who has just spoken. JFB renders the sense as "Begin your mockings."
- **"As for me, is my complaint against man?" (v4).** This is the key to the whole book. Job's real problem is not his friends, his losses or even his sores. It is God. If he were arguing with a man, he might be patient. But God, the one he trusted, seems to be against him, and God is silent. "Why should I not be impatient?" is literally "Why should my spirit not be short?" JFB puts it plainly: "Job's difficulty was not as to man, but as to God." Guzik makes the same point. At bottom Job's crisis is spiritual, not physical, financial or social.
- **"Look at me and be appalled, and lay your hand over your mouth" (v5).** A hand over the mouth was a sign of stunned silence (29:9; Mic 7:16). Job asks his friends to look honestly at what has happened to him and stop talking. Later, when God speaks, Job himself will do exactly this: "I lay my hand on my mouth" (40:4).
- **"When I remember, I am dismayed, and shuddering seizes my flesh" (v6).** Job is not saying this lightly. What he is about to say frightens him. To say the moral order does not work the way everyone thinks is to say something terrible about God's rule. The Tyndale notes compare the trembling of prophets who were overwhelmed by what God showed them (Hab 3:16; Dan 10:8–9).
- **"Why do the wicked live, reach old age, and grow mighty in power?" (v7).** Here is Job's thesis, and it is one of the great questions of Scripture. Zophar said the wicked die young (20:11). Job says they "reach old age." The question is asked again in the Psalms and the Prophets: "I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked" (Ps 73:3), "Why does the way of the wicked prosper?" (Jer 12:1), and "there is a wicked man who prolongs his life in his evildoing" (Eccl 7:15). The cross-references link the verse to all three, and to Job's earlier "the tents of robbers are at peace" (12:6). Job is not inventing a problem. He is naming one that the Bible itself takes seriously.
- **"Their offspring are established in their presence" (v8).** Bildad had said the wicked man has no descendants (18:19), and Zophar that his children will have to repay the poor (20:10). Job answers that the wicked often live to see their grandchildren grow up around them. The Tyndale notes point out the sting. Children and grandchildren are the crown of the righteous (Prov 17:6), and Job's own children are dead (1:18–19).
- **"Their houses are safe from fear, and no rod of God is upon them" (v9).** JFB notes the Hebrew is literally "peace from fear." The "rod" is God's discipline. Eliphaz had promised Job safety in his tent if he would accept God's correction (5:17, 24). Job says the wicked enjoy that safety without any correction at all.
- **"Their bull breeds without fail; their cow calves and does not miscarry" (v10).** In a herding economy, healthy breeding stock meant wealth. Job, who lost all his livestock in one day (1:14–17), watches the wicked prosper in the very things God's covenant blessings promised to the faithful (Deut 28:4).
- **"They send out their little boys like a flock, and their children dance" (vv11–12).** A picture of a happy home: children running out like lambs, music from tambourine, lyre and pipe. JFB says these are "not formal dances" but children who "skip, like lambs, in joyous and healthful play." It is painful to read beside Job's own story. His children died at a feast in their eldest brother's house (1:18–19). His own harp, he will say, "is turned to mourning" (30:31).
- **"They spend their days in prosperity, and in peace they go down to Sheol" (v13).** Sheol is the place of the dead. The word the ESV renders "in peace" is *rega*. The lexicon's usual sense is "a moment," and older versions read "in a moment they go down," a quick, painless death after a long, easy life. The lexicon notes that several scholars, following the ancient Greek and Aramaic versions, read it as "in tranquility," which is the ESV's choice. Either way the point is the same: no lingering illness, no visible judgment. JFB: death comes "not by a lingering disease." Henry adds a sober thought: "If there were not another life after this, it were most desirable to die by the quickest shortest strokes of death."
- **"They say to God, 'Depart from us!'" (v14).** Now the shocking part. These are not good people who happen to be rich. They have told God to go away. "We do not desire the knowledge of your ways." JFB and Guzik (following Adam Clarke) both say the wicked rarely say this out loud. They say it "virtually, by their conduct," as JFB puts it. Their lives tell God to leave them alone.
- **"What is the Almighty, that we should serve him? And what profit do we get if we pray to him?" (v15).** This is the creed of the practical atheist, and it echoes Pharaoh: "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?" (Exod 5:2). It is also the exact accusation Satan made in chapter 1, turned inside out. Satan claimed Job served God only for profit: "Does Job fear God for no reason?" (1:9). The wicked here won't serve God because there is no profit in it. Job, who has lost every reward, still won't let go of God. The book is quietly answering Satan through Job's own mouth. Henry captures the wicked's reasoning: "Praying will not pay debts nor portion children." Their gold, he says, serves "to steel them, to make them more insolent" in their wickedness.
- **"Behold, is not their prosperity in their hand? The counsel of the wicked is far from me" (v16).** The first line is hard. The ESV takes it as a question: don't they hold their own prosperity firmly? Many older readers take it as a statement: "their good is not in their hand." JFB and Henry read it this way, meaning the wicked did not make their prosperity themselves. It is "in the hand of God," as JFB puts it, and that is exactly what troubles Job: God gives it to them. Either way, the second line is clear. Job has just described the good life of the wicked, and he wants none of it. "The counsel of the wicked is far from me." Even while protesting, he refuses to join them. This is also the line Eliphaz will throw back at Job word for word in the next speech (22:18), as if Job had been praising the wicked.
- **"How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out?" (v17).** Job now quotes his friends back to them. Bildad said, "the light of the wicked is put out" (18:5), and the same proverb appears in Proverbs (13:9; 24:20). Job asks, "How often?" He doesn't say it never happens. He says it doesn't happen nearly as reliably as the friends claim. The Tyndale notes observe that God himself will later speak of withholding light from the wicked (38:15), so the proverb is not false. It is just not a rule that can be applied to every case.
- **"That they are like straw before the wind, and like chaff that the storm carries away?" (v18).** Chaff blown off the threshing floor was the classic picture of the wicked (Ps 1:4). Job asks how often anyone actually sees it happen.
- **"You say, 'God stores up their iniquity for their children'" (v19).** The friends had a fallback. If the wicked man escapes, his children will pay (5:4; 20:10). The idea was rooted in Israel's law, which speaks of God "visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children" of those who hate him (Exod 20:5). Job is not satisfied. "Let him pay it out to them, that they may know it." The guilty man should feel his own punishment. The Tyndale notes connect Job's protest with Ezekiel, where God himself says, "the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father" (Ezek 18:20). Guzik passes on Francis Andersen's observation that the friends' theory, far from deterring sin, almost encourages it: if someone else pays, why should the sinner care?
- **"Let them drink of the wrath of the Almighty" (v20).** The cup of God's wrath is a common Old Testament picture (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15). Guzik mentions Adam Clarke's suggestion that it may come from the cup of poison given to condemned criminals.
- **"For what do they care for their houses after them?" (v21).** Once a man is dead, what happens to his family can't touch him. Punishing his children is no punishment to him. "When the number of their months is cut off" means when his allotted time runs out (14:5).
- **"Will any teach God knowledge, seeing that he judges those who are on high?" (v22).** A turn in the argument. Job may be warning the friends: by insisting God must always punish the wicked at once, they are telling God how to run his world. JFB reads it this way, saying the friends "try to teach God how He ought to deal with men." Guzik reads it as Job checking himself, realizing he too must not lecture God. "Those who are on high" could mean angels or, as the Tyndale notes think more likely, the powerful on earth (34:17–19). Paul asks the same question: "Who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?" (Rom 11:34, quoting Isa 40:13).
- **"One dies in his full vigor... his pails full of milk" (vv23–24).** One man dies at the peak of his strength, healthy and comfortable. "Pails" translates *'atin*, a word found only here in the Old Testament. The lexicon gives it as "probably pail, bucket," and notes that related later Hebrew means a vessel. Older versions guessed "breasts" or "sides." Guzik notes the rare word and the different guesses. Either way it pictures plenty. "The marrow of his bones moist" is a picture of health. The Tyndale notes explain that people then thought of the bones as the seat of vigor.
- **"Another dies in bitterness of soul, never having tasted of prosperity" (v25).** Another man never has a good day, and the text gives no hint that he deserved it. "Bitterness of soul" is Job's own phrase for his life (7:11; 10:1).
- **"They lie down alike in the dust, and the worms cover them" (v26).** Death treats them the same. Nothing in how a person lives or dies tells you whether God approved of him. JFB gives only a cross-reference here, to Ecclesiastes: "the same event happens to the righteous and the wicked" (Eccl 9:2). Guzik, following Matthew Poole, notes how death wipes out every outward difference between people.
- **"Behold, I know your thoughts and your schemes to wrong me" (v27).** Job sees through the friends' careful speeches. Their portraits of the wicked man were really portraits of him. JFB: they "insinuate his guilt" without naming him.
- **"Where is the house of the prince? Where is the tent in which the wicked lived?" (v28).** The friends' argument in a sentence: look at the ruins of the rich man's house, and you'll know he was wicked (8:22; 15:34; 20:26–28). The implication for Job, whose house literally fell on his children (1:19), is cruel. JFB notes "prince" here has the sense of a tyrant.
- **"Have you not asked those who travel the roads?" (v29).** Job appeals to common experience. Travelers in the ancient world carried news and stories from town to town and had seen how things went in many places. Ask anyone who has been around, Job says. They'll tell you the wicked often get away with it. JFB notes that these witnesses are "no way connected with him," so they have no reason to take his side.
- **"The evil man is spared in the day of calamity" (v30).** The ESV takes this as what travelers report: the wicked man escapes when disaster strikes. Some older translations (and JFB) read it the other way, that the wicked are "reserved" for a later day of wrath. The Hebrew can bear both senses. Henry's reading is the second: "the destruction of the wicked is reserved for the other world." JFB is careful to add that Job is speaking of this life, and is not denying future punishment.
- **"Who declares his way to his face?" (v31).** The powerful wicked man is surrounded by people too afraid to tell him the truth. No one confronts him, and no one repays him. In Israel's later history it took prophets like Nathan to say to a king, "You are the man" (2 Sam 12:7).
- **"When he is carried to the grave, watch is kept over his tomb" (v32).** He doesn't even get a disgraceful burial. He gets an honored one, with a procession and a guarded tomb. Guzik, following Elmer Smick, notes how far this is from the friends' picture of the wicked man swept away and forgotten (18:17).
- **"The clods of the valley are sweet to him; all mankind follows after him" (v33).** Even the soil lies gently on him. "All mankind follows after him" may mean a long funeral procession, or that everyone eventually goes the same way. JFB takes it as "the common lot of mortals." Henry adds the sober truth Job's argument leaves room for: "what is the light, or what the perfume, to a man that is dead?" The honors cannot help him where he has gone.
- **"How then will you comfort me with empty nothings?" (v34).** Job ends where he began, with "comfort." "Empty nothings" is *hebel*, breath or vapor, the word Ecclesiastes uses for "vanity" (Eccl 1:2). The friends' comfort is a puff of air. "There is nothing left of your answers but falsehood." The word for "falsehood" is *ma'al*, which usually means treachery or unfaithfulness, often against God (Lev 5:15; Josh 22:22). The lexicon notes this is one of the few places it describes faithlessness toward a person. The friends have not just been mistaken. They have broken faith with a friend. At the end of the book God will agree: "you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (42:7). The Tyndale notes draw the same line from here to 42:7.
- **Commentators:** All three agree that Job wins this round. Matthew Henry sums it up: "in this Job was clearly in the right." He outlines the chapter as the prosperity of the wicked (vv7–16), their ruin "at length, but after a long reprieve" (vv17–26), and their destruction "reserved for the other world" (vv27–34). His closing lesson is: "Where there is not truth there is little comfort to be expected." Guzik also passes on Adam Clarke's verdict that this speech is Job's clearest victory over his friends' false doctrine, and that Job reasons well here because he reasons calmly. Guzik and JFB both stress that Job's quarrel is with God, not men (v4). On vv22–26 Guzik passes on Smick's point that wrestling with God like this is healthier than not caring. He also notes that Job does not deny that the wicked will be judged. What troubles Job is how slowly it comes. JFB says nearly the same on v30: Job "does not deny" a future judgment, only that it always falls in this life. JFB has no comment on vv1 and 25 and only cross-references on vv8 and 26. On v16 Henry and JFB read "their prosperity is not in their hand," against the ESV's question. The Hebrew allows either.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job's question, "Why do the wicked live?" is not finally answered in this chapter. Psalm 73 gives part of the answer: the psalmist saw the prosperity of the wicked and nearly slipped, "until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end" (Ps 73:17). The rest of the answer comes in Christ. The New Testament agrees with Job that this life does not settle the accounts. God has "fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed" (Acts 17:31), and the patience Job found so troubling is God's mercy: he is "not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Pet 3:9). Jesus himself warned that riches are no sign of God's favor, in the story of the rich man who feasted every day and the beggar Lazarus at his gate. The rich man was buried with honor, and the beggar was carried by angels to Abraham's side (Luke 16:19–31). The cross shows the other side of Job's complaint most clearly. If prosperity proved righteousness and suffering proved guilt, then the most guilty man who ever lived would be the one who died naked and mocked between two criminals. Jesus, the only truly righteous man, received the suffering, while the wicked around him went on in apparent peace. And he rose again, so that God's justice and mercy would be finally shown, not in a long life and an honored tomb, but in an empty one.
