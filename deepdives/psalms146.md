*Setting:* Psalm 146 opens the Psalter's grand finale: five "Hallelujah psalms" (146–150), each beginning and ending with "Praise the LORD!" (*Hallelu Yah*). Psalm 145 closed with a call for "all flesh" to bless God's name. These five psalms answer it, widening from one soul (146) to the city of Jerusalem (147), to all creation (148), to the gathered congregation (149), and finally to "everything that has breath" (150). The Hebrew text gives Psalm 146 no heading and names no author. The Greek Septuagint adds "of Haggai and Zechariah" to Psalms 146–148, linking them with the prophets who urged the Jews home from exile in Babylon to rebuild the temple around 520 BC (Ezra 5:1). That is an early tradition, not part of the original text, but many scholars agree these psalms come from after the exile. It would fit the warning against trusting "princes" (v3): the returned exiles lived at the mercy of Persian kings and governors, and the line of David was no longer on the throne. In Jewish worship, Psalms 145–150 are prayed every morning, and the early church also used them in daily prayer. The psalm is simple in shape: a vow to praise (vv1–2), a warning not to trust mortal rulers (vv3–4), a blessing on those who trust God (vv5–7), a list of what God does for the helpless (vv7–9), and his eternal reign (v10). JFB sums it up in one note: "an exhortation to praise God," who "is alone worthy of implicit trust." The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### Praise the LORD, O My Soul (vv1–2)
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v1).** *Hallelu Yah*: *hallelu* is a command, "praise," addressed to more than one person, and *Yah* is the short form of God's covenant name *Yahweh*, the name the ESV prints as "the LORD." The word came into Christian worship unchanged, in Greek and English, as "Alleluia" or "Hallelujah." In the New Testament it appears only in Revelation 19, in the songs of heaven.
- **"Praise the LORD, O my soul!" (v1).** The psalmist turns from calling others to calling himself. Psalms 103 and 104 open and close the same way: "Bless the LORD, O my soul." The Tyndale notes point out the echo. Before he can urge anyone else, he has to stir up his own heart. "Soul" (*nefesh*) means the whole living self, not just an inner part.
- **"I will praise the LORD as long as I live" (v2).** The vow covers the rest of his life. This line and the next are almost identical to Psalm 104:33.
- **"I will sing praises to my God while I have my being" (v2).** Literally "while I still am." Some readers, Clarke among them, as Guzik reports, see a hint that the praise goes on past death, since a believer's being does not end. The psalm itself is speaking of this life. Either way, praise is not a phase of life but its purpose.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If it is our delight to praise the Lord while we live, we shall certainly praise him to all eternity." Guzik explains the two parts of *Hallelujah* and passes on Horne's remark that in heaven one hallelujah ends only as another begins. He also cites Spurgeon calling this resolve the chief purpose of life.

#### Put Not Your Trust in Princes (vv3–4)
- **"Put not your trust in princes" (v3).** "Princes" (*nedivim*) means nobles, men of high rank, the powerful people others turn to for protection and favor. The warning is not against rulers as such. It is against relying on them as if they were God. Psalm 118:8–9 says the same: "It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in princes." Jeremiah puts it more sharply: "Cursed is the man who trusts in man" (Jer 17:5).
- **"In a son of man, in whom there is no salvation" (v3).** "Son of man" (*ben 'adam*) here just means a human being, a mortal. "Salvation" (*teshu'ah*) means help, rescue, deliverance. A prince may help for a while, but no human being can save in the deepest sense. For post-exilic Jews, who had seen their own kings fail and lived under foreign ones, this would have been obvious.
- **"When his breath departs, he returns to the earth" (v4).** There is a wordplay. "Man" is *'adam*, and "earth" (ground) is *'adamah*. The man of the ground goes back to the ground, as God said to Adam: "you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Gen 3:19). "Breath" (*ruach*) is also the word for spirit. When God takes it away, people "die and return to their dust" (Ps 104:29; Eccl 12:7).
- **"On that very day his plans perish" (v4).** "Plans" (*'eshtonot*) appears only here in the Bible. It means thoughts or schemes. All the promises a ruler made, all his projects, die with him. History is full of examples. Ancient Near Eastern kings boasted in inscriptions of building for eternity, and the next king often tore it down.
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that "all other sons of men are like the man from whom they sprung": mortal and unable to save. Guzik passes on Boice's note on the wordplay between *'adam* and *'adamah*, and Spurgeon's picture of a three-step decline: the breath goes, the body goes to the earth, and the plans go too.

#### Blessed Is He Whose Help Is the God of Jacob (vv5–7)
- **"Blessed is he whose help is the God of Jacob" (v5).** The warning gives way to a blessing. "Blessed" (*'ashre*) means happy, fortunate, the first word of Psalm 1. "The God of Jacob" is a striking title. Jacob was a schemer and a struggler, not a hero, and God still bound himself to him and his family (Gen 28:13–15; 32:28). Psalm 46:7 says, "the God of Jacob is our fortress." If God helped Jacob, he helps the weak and unworthy.
- **"Whose hope is in the LORD his God" (v5).** "Hope" (*sever*) means expectant waiting, looking ahead with confidence. Princes die. The LORD does not.
- **"Who made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that is in them" (v6).** Why trust God rather than princes? Because he made everything, including the princes. This line repeats the Sabbath commandment (Exod 20:11). The early church prayed with these words when the authorities threatened them: "Sovereign Lord, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them" (Acts 4:24; also 14:15).
- **"Who keeps faith forever" (v6).** Literally "who keeps truth forever." God's word does not die on the day of his death, because he has no such day. "Keeps" (*shomer*) means guards, holds fast. The contrast with v4 is exact: human plans perish, God's faithfulness is forever.
- **"Who executes justice for the oppressed, who gives food to the hungry" (v7).** Here begins a list of what God does, and every item is for people with no one else to help them. The oppressed have no power to win their case. The hungry have no food. This is the kind of king Israel's kings were meant to be (Ps 72:1–4, 12–14), and usually were not.
- **"The LORD sets the prisoners free" (v7).** With this line the list changes form. From here through v9, five lines in a row begin with the name "the LORD." The Tyndale notes say this repetition stresses that he alone heals, unburdens, loves and protects. Prisoners in the ancient world were often debtors, captives of war, or the victims of powerful enemies (Ps 107:10–14). For returned exiles, the line would recall their own release from Babylon (Isa 42:7; 61:1).
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Maclaren's remark that the warning against trusting men becomes good news only when paired with a God who can be trusted, and Trapp's contrast between God's endless faithfulness and the short life of princes. VanGemeren, as Guzik reports, admires how the psalm arranges God's works. Henry describes Christ as the one "in whom there is help," who "will not fail those that trust in him," and adds, "The God of heaven became a man that he might become our salvation."

#### The LORD Opens the Eyes of the Blind (vv8–9)
- **"The LORD opens the eyes of the blind" (v8).** Isaiah promised it for the coming age of salvation (Isa 29:18; 35:5; 42:7). Henry applies it also to the opening of "the eyes of the understanding." Blindness in the Bible is both a physical affliction and a picture of spiritual darkness (Isa 42:16–19; John 9:39–41).
- **"The LORD lifts up those who are bowed down" (v8).** The same words as Psalm 145:14. "Bowed down" (*kefufim*) describes people bent double under burdens or grief. God straightens them.
- **"The LORD loves the righteous" (v8).** In a list of the helpless, this line can seem out of place. The "righteous" in the Psalms are often the poor and wronged who still trust God, people the world ignores or tramples (Ps 34:15–19; 37:16–17). God loves them.
- **"The LORD watches over the sojourners" (v9).** "Sojourners" (*gerim*) were foreigners living in Israel without land or family ties, often poor and easily exploited. The law told Israel to love them, "for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Deut 10:19). "Watches over" (*shomer*) is the same verb as "keeps faith" in v6.
- **"He upholds the widow and the fatherless" (v9).** Widows and orphans had no man to protect them in a society where men held land and stood up in court. Again and again God names himself their defender: "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation" (Ps 68:5; also Deut 10:18; Exod 22:22–24). James later says true religion is "to visit orphans and widows in their affliction" (Jas 1:27). "Upholds" (*ye'oded*) means restores, helps up.
- **"But the way of the wicked he brings to ruin" (v9).** "Brings to ruin" (*ye'avvet*) means to bend, make crooked, twist. God twists the path of the wicked so that it leads nowhere. Psalm 1:6 makes the same contrast: "the way of the wicked will perish." God's care for the helpless includes opposing those who prey on them. Maclaren, as Guzik reports, insists that this line is necessary for a true picture of God: a God who loved the widow but did nothing about her oppressor would not be good.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sets each of these works beside the ministry of Jesus and draws the conclusion that Jesus does what the LORD does. He passes on Kidner's link to Isaiah 61 and to Jesus' words in Luke 4 and 7. Henry reads the hungry as also those who hunger for salvation, whom God feeds.

#### The LORD Will Reign Forever (v10)
- **"The LORD will reign forever" (v10).** This is the last line of the Song of Moses at the Red Sea: "The LORD will reign forever and ever" (Exod 15:18). Princes die (v4). The LORD reigns, and he will never be succeeded.
- **"Your God, O Zion, to all generations" (v10).** "Zion" is Jerusalem, God's city, and by extension his people. For a small, struggling community after the exile, with no king of their own, this was the answer to the question of who rules: their God does, and he does not die.
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v10).** The psalm ends where it began, with *Hallelujah*. The praise of one soul (v1) has become the praise of Zion for all generations.
- **Commentators:** Henry closes by urging believers to "stir up each other to praise his holy name." Guzik passes on Clarke's point that God's help to Zion's fathers is a reason for trusting him now, and Spurgeon's remark that the psalm ends but the praise does not.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 146 warns against trusting a "son of man, in whom there is no salvation." Then the Son of Man came, the one human being in whom there is salvation (Acts 4:12). He breathed his last and returned to the earth, laid in a tomb, but on the third day his plans did not perish. He rose. When John the Baptist, himself a prisoner, asked whether Jesus was the one to come, Jesus answered from this psalm and Isaiah: "the blind receive their sight… and the poor have good news preached to them" (Luke 7:22). In the synagogue at Nazareth he read that he was sent "to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovering of sight to the blind" (Luke 4:18). He fed the hungry, lifted the bowed down, welcomed the outsider and raised a widow's son (Luke 7:11–15). Every line of vv7–9 that the psalm says of the LORD, the Gospels show Jesus doing. And the God who reigns forever has given that kingdom to him (Luke 1:33; Rev 11:15).
