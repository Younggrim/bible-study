*Setting:* This is the last chapter of Judges, and it ends the book on a note of grief and confusion. Chapter 20 told how all Israel went to war against the tribe of Benjamin for shielding the men of Gibeah who raped and killed a Levite's concubine. Israel won, but then swept through Benjamin's towns and killed everyone they found. Only 600 Benjaminite men survive, hiding at the rock of Rimmon (20:47). Now the tribes wake up to what they have done: one of the twelve tribes is about to vanish. Their answer is to fix one rash oath by breaking its spirit, and to repair one slaughter with another. The chapter is a sad, almost dark-comic picture of people trying to do right with no king and no clear word from God, and the book closes with its verdict on the whole era.

#### Wives Provided for the Tribe of Benjamin (vv1–25)
- **"No one of us shall give his daughter in marriage to Benjamin" (v1).** This oath was sworn at Mizpah, before the war, though chapter 20 did not mention it. In the heat of anger it seemed like a way to shun a guilty tribe. Now, with Benjamin's women dead, it means the tribe has no future. It is the second foolish vow in Judges that traps the people who make it, after Jephthah's (11:30–31, 35).
- **"Why has this happened in Israel, that today there should be one tribe lacking?" (vv2–3).** They come to Bethel, or "the house of God," and weep bitterly, as they did after their defeats in 20:23 and 26. The question to God sounds innocent, but the answer is uncomfortable: it happened because of what they did. The twelve tribes mattered deeply. They were the sons of Jacob, the family God had promised to make a nation, and losing one would be like a wound in the covenant itself.
- **"Built there an altar and offered burnt offerings and peace offerings" (v4).** The text does not say God answered them. In chapter 20 the LORD replied every time they asked. Here, after they ask "why," there is silence, and the rest of the chapter is their own planning.
- **"He shall surely be put to death" (v5).** A second oath is now remembered: any town that failed to come to Mizpah would be put to death. Israel's war council made itself judge of life and death, binding itself with oaths God never asked for.
- **"Had compassion for Benjamin their brother" (v6).** The Hebrew word means to feel sorrow, even to relent or change one's mind. It is the same word used when God "relented" from judgment (Exod 32:14; Judg 2:18). After the slaughter of chapter 20, Benjamin is "their brother" again.
- **Jabesh-gilead (vv8–9).** A town east of the Jordan in Gilead, in the land of eastern Manasseh. Not one of its men came to the assembly. The town had close ties to Benjamin, and this story may be the reason: Saul, a Benjaminite from Gibeah, will later rescue Jabesh-gilead from the Ammonites (1 Sam 11), and its men will risk their lives to give Saul's body a decent burial (1 Sam 31:11–13). If Benjamin's new wives came from Jabesh, those were family bonds.
- **"Every male and every woman that has lain with a male you shall devote to destruction" (vv10–11).** "Devote to destruction" is *cherem*, the ban, the total destruction God commanded for certain Canaanite cities (Deut 7:2; Josh 6:17). Israel now applies it to an Israelite town for skipping a meeting, and they spare the young unmarried women only because they need them. Twelve thousand men are sent. The town is wiped out to solve a problem caused by the last town being wiped out.
- **"400 young virgins… to the camp at Shiloh, which is in the land of Canaan" (v12).** "In the land of Canaan" means west of the Jordan, as opposed to Gilead on the east. Shiloh was where the tabernacle stood (18:31; Josh 18:1).
- **"Proclaimed peace to them" (vv13–14).** Israel offers peace to the 600 at Rimmon and gives them the 400 women. "But they were not enough for them." Two hundred men are still without wives.
- **"The LORD had made a breach in the tribes" (v15).** The narrator now says the gap in Israel was the LORD's doing, a judgment he had allowed. Both things are true in this chapter: God was at work in Benjamin's defeat (20:35), and Israel was responsible for going far beyond it.
- **"There must be an inheritance for the survivors of Benjamin" (vv16–18).** The elders reason carefully. Each tribe's land was its inheritance from God (Num 26:52–56), and it had to pass to heirs. But they are bound by their own curse: "Cursed be he who gives a wife to Benjamin."
- **"The yearly feast of the LORD at Shiloh" (v19).** The elaborate directions, north of Bethel, east of the road to Shechem, south of Lebonah, read like instructions for the Benjaminites to find the place. It also suggests that for the writer's readers, Shiloh was a ruin that needed explaining (Jer 7:12). Which feast this was is not said. JFB suggests the Feast of Booths, at the end of the grape harvest, which would fit the vineyards. The opening chapter of 1 Samuel is set at a "yearly" visit to this same shrine at Shiloh (1 Sam 1:3).
- **"Snatch each man his wife from the daughters of Shiloh" (vv20–21).** The young women came out "to dance in the dances," a joyful part of worship (compare Miriam, Exod 15:20, and Jephthah's daughter, 11:34). The Benjaminites are told to hide in the vineyards and seize them. JFB compares it to the Roman legend of the Sabine women, carried off by the first Romans to populate their city.
- **"Grant them graciously to us" (v22).** The elders even script their answer to the girls' fathers and brothers: please let it go, because Israel did not capture these women in battle, and you did not *give* your daughters, so you have not broken the oath. It is a legal loophole. The oath survives on paper, while the daughters of Shiloh are taken without their consent and their families are asked to accept it.
- **"Returned to their inheritance and rebuilt the towns" (vv23–24).** Benjamin survives. It will produce Israel's first king, Saul (1 Sam 9:1–2), and later the apostle Paul, "of the tribe of Benjamin" (Phil 3:5; Rom 11:1). The tribes go home "every man to his inheritance," echoing how the book began, when Joshua sent the people out "each to his inheritance" (2:6). But the land Joshua left at peace has become a land of civil war.
- **"In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (v25).** The book's last words repeat the refrain of 17:6. Every story in chapters 17–21 has shown it: a homemade shrine, a hired priest, a stolen idol, a destroyed town, a murdered wife, a civil war, a second massacre and a mass abduction, and most of it done by people who thought they were serving the LORD. The verse points forward to the books of Samuel, where God gives Israel a king. But it also raises the question the rest of the Bible will answer: what kind of king could cure a heart that does "what is right in his own eyes"?
- **Commentators:** Henry says the oath was aimed at those who had shared in the crime, not at wiping out the tribe, and that Israel's grief was not over punishing the sin but over how far they had pushed it: "They repented for Benjamin their brother." He calls the Shiloh plan a poor way around the oath. Guzik calls the Jabesh-gilead raid a horror, one wrong committed to cover another, and says the right course was to confess that the oath was rash. He calls the Shiloh scheme a charade that let them avoid admitting their sin. JFB notes "There is no previous record of this oath," says the raid on Jabesh shows "the intemperate fury of the Israelites," and observes that "the dance was anciently a part of the religious observance." On v25 Henry ends by giving thanks for good government, since without it "every man would be his own master." The Tyndale notes add that the tribes and families still held together even when leadership failed, which is how Israel survived the era.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judges ends with God's people unable to save themselves from themselves. They had zeal, sacrifices, oaths and unity, and still they did what was right in their own eyes. The closing cry for a king is answered first by Saul, from Benjamin, and then by David, from Bethlehem, and both fail in their own ways. The true answer is the King of whom Isaiah wrote, "He shall not judge by what his eyes see" but "with righteousness he shall judge the poor" (Isa 11:3–4). Jesus does what is right in the Father's eyes, every time (John 8:29). Where Israel's leaders bound themselves with rash oaths and found loopholes to keep them, he fulfills every word his Father spoke. And unlike the elders who took brides by force, he wins his bride by giving himself for her.
