*Setting:* Jerusalem, 445 BC. The wall is rising despite mockery and threats from Sanballat, Tobiah and their allies, and the builders now work with a weapon in one hand (ch 4). The work would be finished in fifty-two days (6:15). Chapter 5 turns from enemies outside to injustice inside. In the middle of the building, poor families cry out against their own wealthy countrymen. Some scholars think the events of vv1–13 came later in Nehemiah's twelve years as governor, since v14 looks back over that whole period. But a crisis during the building fits well: men were away from their fields, food was scarce, and the threats kept farmers from bringing in supplies. The chapter ends with Nehemiah's account of how he himself governed.

#### Nehemiah Stops Oppression of the Poor (vv1–13)
- **"A great outcry of the people and of their wives against their Jewish brothers" (v1).** "Outcry" is the word used for the cry of the oppressed that reaches God, as Israel cried out in Egypt (Exod 3:7, 9) and the poor cry out under injustice (Exod 22:23). The wives are named, which is unusual. They were the ones trying to feed hungry children. The complaint is "against their Jewish brothers," not against the Persians or the neighbors.
- **Three groups, three complaints (vv2–4).** First, families with many children and no land who simply needed grain to survive (v2). Second, landowners who had mortgaged fields, vineyards and houses to buy food "because of the famine" (v3). Haggai, seventy-five years earlier, describes the same kind of failing harvests (Hag 1:6, 10–11). Third, those who had borrowed to pay "the king's tax" (v4). Persian taxes were heavy and were paid in silver, which drained coin out of the provinces and forced farmers into debt.
- **"Our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers, our children are as their children" (v5).** The heart of the complaint. "We are the same people. Our children matter as much as theirs." Yet the poor were selling their sons and daughters into debt slavery. "Some of our daughters have already been enslaved" may suggest that the girls were taken as servants or concubines in wealthy households (compare Exod 21:7–11).
- **"It is not in our power to help it, for other men have our fields and our vineyards" (v5).** They were trapped. With their land gone, they had no income to buy their children back.
- **What the Law said.** The Law of Moses allowed loans to a poor brother, but forbade charging him interest (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:35–37; Deut 23:19–20). A fellow Israelite sold for debt was to be treated "as a hired worker" and released (Lev 25:39–43; Deut 15:12–15), and his land could be redeemed (Lev 25:25). Every seventh year debts were to be released (Deut 15:1–2). The nobles were keeping the letter of nothing.
- **"I was very angry" (v6).** Nehemiah's anger was righteous. Injustice against the poor provokes God's anger too (Exod 22:22–24; Amos 2:6–7). But anger was not the end of it.
- **"I took counsel with myself" (v7).** Literally "my heart took counsel within me." He thought before he spoke. He did not act in the heat of the moment (Jas 1:19–20; Prov 14:29).
- **"You are exacting interest, each from his brother" (v7).** The word *mashsha* means lending on interest or on pledge. The lexicon gives "lending on interest, or usury." The lenders were using the crisis to profit from their neighbors' need. Note "each from his brother." Nehemiah keeps calling them brothers, the very thing they had forgotten.
- **"I held a great assembly against them" (v7).** He took the matter public. These were the most powerful men in Judah, and private words might have been ignored.
- **"We… have bought back our Jewish brothers who have been sold to the nations, but you even sell your brothers that they may be sold to us!" (v8).** The irony is sharp. Jews in exile had spent money redeeming fellow Jews from foreign masters. Now Jews in Judah were enslaving their own people, so that someone would have to buy them back again. Nehemiah had done this redeeming himself. "They were silent and could not find a word to say." There was no defense.
- **"To prevent the taunts of the nations our enemies" (v9).** The broken walls had made Jerusalem a mockery (1:3; 2:17). So did this. Strong walls would mean little if the people inside them preyed on one another. God's reputation was at stake in how his people treated the poor.
- **"I and my brothers and my servants are lending them money and grain. Let us abandon this exacting of interest" (v10).** Nehemiah includes himself. Some readers take this as an admission that he too had been taking pledges. Others read it as "we lend, but without interest, so stop yours." Either way, he does not exempt himself from the command he gives.
- **"Return to them this very day their fields, their vineyards, their olive orchards, and their houses" (v11).** Not just "stop," but "give it back," and "this very day." "The percentage" is literally "the hundredth," probably one percent a month, twelve percent a year. That was modest by the standards of the day. Documents from the Jewish colony at Elephantine in Egypt show rates of five percent a month, or sixty percent a year. But the Law forbade any interest on loans to a poor brother.
- **"We will restore these and require nothing from them" (v12).** The nobles agreed immediately and completely. Nehemiah made them confirm it with an oath before the priests, so that the promise was made before God.
- **"I also shook out the fold of my garment" (v13).** The fold of the robe served as a pocket. Shaking it empty was an acted curse: "So may God shake out every man from his house and from his labor who does not keep this promise." Prophets often acted out their messages (Jer 13:1–11; Ezek 4–5). Paul later shook out his garments as a sign against those who rejected the gospel (Acts 18:6).
- **"And all the assembly said 'Amen' and praised the LORD. And the people did as they had promised" (v13).** "Amen" means "so be it," agreement with the curse (Deut 27:15–26). And this time the promise was kept. The crisis ended in worship.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that Nehemiah "knew that, if he built Jerusalem's walls ever so high, so thick, or so strong, the city could not be safe while there were abuses," and that "Nothing exposes religion more to reproach, than the worldliness and hard-heartedness of the professors of it." He closes with "Good promises are good things, but good performances are better." JFB suggests that the earlier rains and poor harvests (Ezra 10:9; Hag 1:6–11), together with the enemy keeping supplies out of the crowded city, made the famine worse. Guzik reads the "hundredth" as twelve percent a year, as the Tyndale notes do. He praises Nehemiah for confronting the wrong directly after thinking it through, and he praises the nobles for accepting correction.

#### Nehemiah's Generosity (vv14–19)
- **"From the twentieth year to the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes the king, twelve years" (v14).** 445 to 433 BC. This is the first time the book calls Nehemiah governor. After those twelve years he went back to the king and later returned for a second term (13:6–7). This section was clearly written looking back.
- **"Neither I nor my brothers ate the food allowance of the governor" (v14).** Persian governors were supported by a tax in kind and silver collected from their province. It was legal and expected. Nehemiah did not take it.
- **"The former governors… took from them for their daily ration forty shekels of silver" (v15).** About a pound of silver a day, on top of food and wine. These earlier governors are probably the officials between Zerubbabel and Nehemiah, some of whose names may survive on seal impressions of the period, rather than Zerubbabel himself. "Even their servants lorded it over the people." Corruption spread down from the top.
- **"But I did not do so, because of the fear of God" (v15).** The same reason he gave the nobles in v9. The fear of God is not only a feeling. It shapes how a person handles power and money.
- **"We acquired no land" (v16).** A governor in a time of famine and foreclosures could easily have bought up fields cheaply. Nehemiah refused. He and his servants worked on the wall instead.
- **"At my table 150 men, Jews and officials, besides those who came to us from the nations" (v17).** A governor was expected to host officials and visitors. Nehemiah paid for it himself.
- **"One ox and six choice sheep and birds… every ten days all kinds of wine" (v18).** This was a large household, though modest next to Solomon's daily thirty cattle and a hundred sheep (1 Kgs 4:22–23). Nehemiah could afford it from his own wealth, probably earned as the king's cupbearer. "Because the service was too heavy on this people." He saw their burden and refused to add to it.
- **"Remember for my good, O my God, all that I have done for this people" (v19).** This is the first of several "remember me" prayers (13:14, 22, 31). Some readers hear self-congratulation. But Nehemiah does not ask people to remember. He asks God, and he asks for good "for this people," not for status. It is the prayer of a man who had given up what was his by right and trusted God to make it up to him (compare Heb 6:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Nehemiah mentions his conduct "not as if he had merited any favour from God, but to show that he depended upon God only, to make up to him what he had lost," like the reward promised for "a cup of cold water given to a disciple." He adds, "Let all who are in public places remember that they are so placed to do good, not to enrich themselves." JFB takes the generosity as proof that the cupbearer's post "must have been very lucrative" and works out the forty shekels as a large yearly salary in his own currency. Guzik compares Nehemiah to Paul, who gave up his right to be supported for the sake of the gospel (1 Cor 9:12–15).

#### Pointing to Christ
Nehemiah's courtroom speech turns on redemption: brothers bought back from slavery, land returned, debt canceled "this very day." That is the language of the Year of Jubilee (Lev 25), and Jesus took it up when he announced "liberty to the captives" and "the year of the Lord's favor" (Luke 4:18–19). Nehemiah gave up the governor's allowance for the people's sake. Christ "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor" (2 Cor 8:9), as Henry notes. And he did more than refuse to burden us. He paid the debt himself, "canceling the record of debt that stood against us" (Col 2:14). The church that follows him is to look like Nehemiah's renewed Jerusalem at its best, where "there was not a needy person among them" (Acts 4:34).
