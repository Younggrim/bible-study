*Setting:* Not long after David's marriage to Michal, around 1018 BC, at Saul's capital Gibeah and at Samuel's hometown of Ramah, about three miles apart in the hill country of Benjamin. Chapter 18 ended with the words "Saul was David's enemy continually" (18:29). Saul had already tried twice to pin David to the wall with his spear (18:11) and had tried to get him killed by the Philistines through a deadly bride-price (18:25). Now his hostility comes into the open. In this chapter, four people or groups stand between David and death: Jonathan, Michal, Samuel, and finally the Spirit of God himself. It marks the start of David's years as a fugitive. He leaves Saul's court here and will not return to live in a king's house until he is king.

#### Saul Tries to Kill David (vv1–24)
- **"Saul spoke to Jonathan his son and to all his servants, that they should kill David" (v1).** Saul no longer hides his plans or hopes the Philistines will do his work. He gives an open order to his son and his court. "But Jonathan, Saul's son, delighted much in David." The writer stresses "Saul's son": Jonathan's loyalty is torn between his father and his friend, and he chooses to stand with what is right.
- **"Stay in a secret place and hide yourself… I will speak to my father about you" (vv2–3).** Jonathan has a plan. He will take his father out to the field near where David is hiding, so that David can hear for himself or Jonathan can report quickly.
- **"Let not the king sin against his servant David" (v4).** Jonathan's speech is careful and brave. He uses the respectful title "the king," but he names his father's plan for what it is: sin. His argument is simple. David "has not sinned against you," and his deeds "have brought good to you."
- **"He took his life in his hand and he struck down the Philistine" (v5).** "Took his life in his hand" means risked his life (Judg 12:3; Job 13:14). Jonathan reminds Saul that "the LORD worked a great salvation," the same phrase used of Jonathan's own victory (14:45), and that Saul himself "saw it, and rejoiced." Killing David would be shedding "innocent blood," a grave crime under the law (Deut 19:10; 27:25).
- **"Saul swore, 'As the LORD lives, he shall not be put to death'" (v6).** Saul listens and swears an oath in the LORD's name. For a moment, reason and conscience win. But Saul has a history of making oaths and not keeping them well (14:24, 39, 44), and this one will be broken within a few verses.
- **"He was in his presence as before" (v7).** Jonathan acts as peacemaker and brings David back to court. It is the last time things are "as before."
- **"There was war again… David struck them with a great blow" (v8).** Another victory for David means another wave of jealousy for Saul. The pattern of chapter 18 repeats: David's success sets off Saul's torment.
- **"A harmful spirit from the LORD came upon Saul… with his spear in his hand" (v9).** The scene from 18:10–11 is repeated almost word for word, but this time it ends differently. "He struck the spear into the wall. And David fled and escaped that night" (v10). This is the third spear thrown. David does not throw it back. He leaves.
- **"Saul sent messengers to David's house to watch him, that he might kill him in the morning" (v11).** Saul plans to kill David at dawn, perhaps because killing a man in his own house at night was considered shameful or would be harder to justify publicly. The heading of Psalm 59 ties that psalm to this night: "when Saul sent men to watch his house in order to kill him." In it David describes enemies who "return, howling like dogs and prowling about the city" in the evening (Ps 59:6, 14), and he ends with a song for the morning: "I will sing aloud of your steadfast love in the morning" (Ps 59:16). The morning Saul planned for David's death became a morning of praise.
- **"Michal let David down through the window" (v12).** Rahab let Joshua's spies down from her window in Jericho (Josh 2:15), and the believers in Damascus would one day lower Saul of Tarsus over the city wall in a basket (Acts 9:25). Michal, Saul's daughter, saves David from Saul's men. Her love for him (18:20, 28) shows here at real risk to herself.
- **"Michal took an image and laid it on the bed" (v13).** The "image" is *teraphim*, the word for household idols. Rachel stole her father's teraphim (Gen 31:19, 34), Micah kept them in his private shrine (Judg 17:5), and Samuel had just named them alongside rebellion (15:23, where "idolatry" is *teraphim*). Josiah later destroyed them (2 Kgs 23:24). They could apparently be small or, as here, close to human size. Why there was one in David's house is not explained. It may have belonged to Michal, as Rachel's did. The text simply reports it and moves on, but it is a reminder that idolatry had crept even into the home of the man after God's heart. "A pillow of goats' hair" is uncertain in Hebrew. It may be a goat-hair net or quilt placed over the head to look like hair.
- **"He is sick" (v14).** Michal stalls Saul's men with a lie. Saul's answer shows how far he has gone: "Bring him up to me in the bed, that I may kill him" (v15). He would kill a sick man in his bed.
- **"Why have you deceived me thus and let my enemy go?" (v17).** Saul now openly calls David "my enemy." Michal protects herself with a second lie: David threatened her life. The Bible reports her deception without approving it. As with Rahab (Josh 2:4–6) and the Hebrew midwives (Exod 1:19), Christians have discussed whether such lies are ever right when protecting innocent life. The text's interest is elsewhere: even the king's own children are working against him. Sadly, Michal's love for David will not last (2 Sam 6:16–23).
- **"He came to Samuel at Ramah and told him all that Saul had done to him" (v18).** David goes straight to the prophet who anointed him, the one man who knew God's purpose for him. It is the first time since chapter 16 that we see David and Samuel together. They go to **Naioth**, which probably means "dwellings" or "camps." It seems to have been a compound in or near Ramah where Samuel lived with a community of prophets.
- **"The company of the prophets prophesying, and Samuel standing as head over them" (v20).** Groups of prophets appear in Samuel's time (10:5) and later under Elijah and Elisha (2 Kgs 2:3–7; 4:38). "Prophesying" here does not mean predicting the future. It means speaking or singing under the Spirit's power, likely in ecstatic praise, sometimes with music (10:5; 1 Chr 25:1). Samuel stands over them as their leader.
- **"The Spirit of God came upon the messengers of Saul, and they also prophesied" (v20).** Saul's armed men arrive to arrest David and are themselves overcome. They forget their mission and join the worship. It happens three times (v21), a complete and humorous pattern: three teams sent, three teams caught. God does not need swords to protect David.
- **"He himself went to Ramah and came to the great well that is in Secu" (v22).** When his servants fail, Saul comes himself, as he will later chase David personally through the wilderness. The location of Secu is unknown. The Greek translation reads "the well of the threshing floor on the bare height."
- **"The Spirit of God came upon him also, and as he went he prophesied" (v23).** Saul does not even reach Naioth before he is overcome. It is the same experience he had near Gibeah right after Samuel anointed him (10:10–11). Then it was a sign that God was with him. Now it is a sign that God is stopping him.
- **"He too stripped off his clothes… and lay naked all that day and all that night" (v24).** "Naked" (*'arom*) in Hebrew can mean completely naked or stripped down to the undergarment, without outer clothes (Isa 20:2–4; John 21:7). Most think Saul took off his royal robe and armor. The symbolism is strong. The robe has been a picture of the kingdom throughout these chapters: Samuel's torn robe (15:27–28), Jonathan's robe given to David (18:4). Now Saul lies before Samuel stripped of his royal clothing, helpless, all day and all night. God has stripped him of the kingdom.
- **"Is Saul also among the prophets?" (v24).** This proverb was first coined when Saul was newly anointed and rising (10:11–12). There it expressed surprise that Saul, a farmer's son, had joined the prophets. Here it takes a darker meaning: the king who opposes God is found powerless on the ground among God's servants. The same experience of the Spirit can mark a man's rise or his fall. It is also the last time Saul sees Samuel alive.
- **Commentators:** Henry praises Jonathan's speech: "How forcible are right words!" and says Saul "was, for a time, convinced of the unreasonableness of his enmity." But Henry is not soft on Michal: "her falsehood had not even the plea of necessity to excuse it," since David was already safely away. Guzik is gentler on Michal's first lie but sees self-interest in the second, and he calls Saul's words "my enemy" the saddest phrase in the chapter. He places Psalm 59 on this night. JFB explains the goat-hair pillow as a bolster covered "with a mantle, as if to foster a proper warmth in a patient." On Naioth, JFB has Samuel "superintending the school of the prophets," and reads "naked" as "divested of his armor and outer robes." Guzik draws the key lesson: it is possible to be *affected* by God's power without being *surrendered* to it. Henry says the same more sharply: "Many have great gifts, yet no grace; they may prophesy in Christ's name, yet are disowned by him" (compare Matt 7:22–23). The Tyndale notes add that both of Saul's children, Jonathan and Michal, protected David, so David's rise owed much to Saul's own family, though David never took the throne by force.

#### Pointing to Christ
An innocent man who had brought only good to the king was hunted to the death, with false charges and broken oaths. His friend pleaded, "Why then will you sin against innocent blood?" Jesus faced the same hatred without cause. Judas confessed, "I have sinned by betraying innocent blood" (Matt 27:4), and Pilate washed his hands of "this man's blood" (Matt 27:24). David escaped through a window by night. Jesus did not escape. He laid down his life of his own accord (John 10:18). At Naioth, Saul's soldiers came to seize David and were stopped by the Spirit of God. In Gethsemane, the soldiers who came to arrest Jesus "drew back and fell to the ground" at his word, "I am he" (John 18:6). And yet he gave himself up, so that his people could be kept safe: "If you seek me, let these men go" (John 18:8).
