*Setting:* The audience hall at Caesarea, about AD 59, the day after Festus briefed King Agrippa (25:23–27). Royalty in purple, Roman officers and the city's leading men are seated. Paul stands with a chain on his wrist. Technically this isn't a trial. Paul has already appealed to Caesar. It's a hearing to help Festus write his report. But it becomes Paul's fullest and most polished defense in Acts, and the third telling of his conversion (chs 9, 22, 26). Many think it is the high point of his ministry as Luke records it.

#### Paul's Defense Before Agrippa (vv1–23)
- **"Paul stretched out his hand and made his defense" (v1).** This is the gesture of a trained speaker. It is also the hand with the chain on it (v29). Agrippa presides, as the senior royal guest.
- **"I consider myself fortunate… you are familiar with all the customs and controversies of the Jews" (vv2–3).** This is genuine courtesy, not flattery like Tertullus's. Agrippa really was an expert. He controlled the temple treasury and appointed the high priests. Paul is glad to finally have a judge who understands the issues.
- **"My manner of life from my youth… spent… in Jerusalem" (v4).** This confirms 22:3: Paul was raised in Jerusalem from an early age, not just educated there. He was no Hellenistic Jew with loose habits picked up abroad.
- **"According to the strictest party of our religion I have lived as a Pharisee" (v5).** "Strictest" (*akribestatos*) means most exact, most careful. "Religion" here is *thrēskeia*, outward worship and practice.
- **"On trial because of my hope in the promise made by God to our fathers" (vv6–7).** This is the core of his defense. The "promise" is the Messiah and the resurrection that his coming would bring. **"Our twelve tribes"** pictures Israel as still one people waiting, though ten tribes had been scattered for centuries (compare James 1:1; Luke 2:36). **"For this hope I am accused by Jews, O king!"** The irony is the point. He is accused of believing exactly what every devout Jew hoped for.
- **"Why is it thought incredible by any of you that God raises the dead?" (v8).** He turns suddenly to the whole audience: Romans, Herodians, Sadducees. If God is God, resurrection is not impossible. The question hangs over everything that follows.
- **"I myself was convinced that I ought to do many things in opposing the name of Jesus of Nazareth" (v9).** Paul doesn't claim he was always open-minded. He was sincerely, zealously wrong. Sincerity is not the same as truth.
- **"I cast my vote against them" (v10).** Literally "I brought down my pebble" (*psēphos*). Ancient voting used black and white stones. This may mean Paul was a voting member of a council. Or it may be a figure of speech for his approval, like "approving" Stephen's death in 8:1. He was young for the Sanhedrin, so scholars are divided. "Many of the saints" shows that Stephen was not the only one to die.
- **"Tried to make them blaspheme" (v11).** He tried to force believers to curse Jesus (compare 1 Cor 12:3). The tense suggests repeated attempts, and Paul doesn't claim he succeeded. "Raging fury" (*emmainomai*) means madness. Paul admits that he, not Festus's version of him (v24), was the one who had been out of his mind.
- **"A light from heaven, brighter than the sun… shone around me and those who journeyed with me" (v13).** This account adds that the light surrounded everyone and that all of them fell (v14).
- **"In the Hebrew language… 'It is hard for you to kick against the goads'" (v14).** Only this account records the proverb. A goad (*kentron*) was a pointed stick used to drive an ox. An ox that kicked against it only hurt itself. It was a familiar Greek saying, used by Euripides and Aeschylus, so it was perfect for this cultured audience. The point is that Jesus had already been pressing on Saul's conscience: Stephen's face, the believers' courage, the Scriptures. Resisting was only wounding himself. The Lord who speaks Hebrew uses a Greek proverb. The gospel speaks every language.
- **"I have appeared to you for this purpose, to appoint you as a servant and witness" (v16).** Here the commission comes straight from Jesus' mouth. In ch 22 it came through Ananias. "Servant" (*hypēretēs*) is an assistant who carries out another's orders. Preachers often trace it to "under-rower," but by Paul's day it simply meant an attendant or aide (Luke 1:2 uses it of "ministers of the word"). **"To those in which I will appear to you"** promises later appearances (18:9; 22:17; 23:11).
- **"To open their eyes, so that they may turn from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God" (vv17–18).** This echoes the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah: "to open the eyes that are blind… a light for the nations" (Isa 42:6–7, 16; 49:6). The blinded persecutor is sent to open blind eyes. The gospel in one verse has three movements: eyes opened, a turning (repentance), and two gifts, **forgiveness of sins** and **a place among those who are sanctified**. Both come "**by faith in me**."
- **"I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision" (v19).** It is the turning point of Paul's life in a single understatement. The message he then preached everywhere was: "repent and turn to God, performing deeds in keeping with their repentance" (v20). That is real change, not just feeling sorry (Luke 3:8; Eph 2:8–10).
- **"For this reason the Jews seized me" (v21).** This is the real charge: offering God's mercy to Gentiles. The accusation of sedition doesn't even come up.
- **"Nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would come to pass" (vv22–23).** Paul claims no novelty. In three clauses he states what the Hebrew Scriptures foretold. The Messiah **must suffer** (Isa 53; Ps 22). He would be **the first to rise from the dead**, the "firstfruits" (1 Cor 15:20; Col 1:18). And he would bring **light both to our people and to the Gentiles** (Isa 49:6). It is the same summary Jesus gave on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:26–27, 44–47).
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that Paul could have refused to speak, since he had already appealed to Caesar, but he "cheerfully embraced the opportunity" to honor his cause. Henry adds that Paul "needed no advocate, no Tertullus." His concise commentary adds that those who were strictest before conversion later "see abundant reason for humbling themselves." JFB notes the chained hand, and that the charge of sedition is "not so much as alluded to throughout this speech. It was indeed a mere pretext." JFB on v8: Paul leaves "this pregnant question to find its answer in the breasts of his audience." Guzik points out the irony that Agrippa was an expert in Jewish customs, yet like the Sadducees he could not accept that God raises the dead. He also notes this telling of the conversion is the one shaped for Gentile ears.

#### "Almost Persuaded" (vv24–32)
- **"Paul, you are out of your mind; your great learning is driving you out of your mind" (v24).** Festus interrupts loudly at the word "rise from the dead." To a Roman, resurrection was nonsense (17:32). "Out of your mind" is *mainomai*, the root of "mania." Festus calls Paul mad, as people said of Jesus (John 10:20; Mark 3:21). "Great learning" (literally "many letters") may point to the scrolls Paul kept with him (2 Tim 4:13).
- **"I am not out of my mind, most excellent Festus, but I am speaking true and rational words" (v25).** "Rational" is *sōphrosynē*, sober-minded and sound. It is the opposite of mania. Paul answers with calm courtesy. Few replies in Scripture are so composed.
- **"This has not been done in a corner" (v26).** This is one of the great lines of the New Testament. Jesus' ministry, death and resurrection were public events in a real province under named officials. They could be checked. Agrippa's family had ruled the region through all of it. Christianity rests on history that happened in the open, not on private visions.
- **"King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know that you believe" (v27).** Now Paul presses the king personally, and Agrippa is trapped. If he says yes, Paul will say the prophets point to Jesus. If he says no, he loses face with the Jews he governs.
- **"In a short time would you persuade me to be a Christian?" (v28).** The KJV made this famous as "Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian." Most modern scholars read the Greek (*en oligō*, "in a little") as "in a short time" or "with so little." That makes the line more likely a dry deflection than a confession. Some hear sarcasm, some embarrassment, some a half-serious crack in his defenses. The ESV footnote offers "In a short time you would persuade me to act like a Christian!" "Christian" (*Christianos*) appears only three times in the New Testament (11:26; 1 Pet 4:16). In the mouth of an outsider it was not yet a compliment.
- **"Whether short or long, I would to God that… all who hear me this day might become such as I am—except for these chains" (v29).** Paul takes up Agrippa's word "short" and turns it into a prayer. Picture him lifting his chained hands. He wants for them everything he has in Christ, and nothing of what he suffers.
- **"The king rose, and the governor and Bernice" (v30).** The king ends the hearing when it becomes too personal.
- **"This man is doing nothing to deserve death or imprisonment" (v31).** The verdict is unanimous. Paul is the fourth official to declare him innocent: Lysias (23:29), Festus (25:25), and now Agrippa and the whole court. Luke's readers in Rome needed to know that Christianity was not a criminal movement.
- **"This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar" (v32).** This is a bittersweet line. Legally the appeal couldn't be withdrawn. But Paul was not trapped by a mistake. Jesus had said he "must" testify in Rome (23:11). Without the appeal he might never have left Judea alive (25:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls it "a thousand pities" that Festus cut Paul off just as he reached the death and resurrection of Christ, "the life of the cause," where "he will never know when to conclude." He sees Festus's charge of madness as a convenient excuse to escape "both from condemning Paul as a prisoner and from believing him as a preacher." His concise commentary warns: "recollect how far the being almost persuaded to be a Christian, is from being altogether such a one." JFB: "Can anything surpass this reply, for readiness, self-possession, calm dignity?" And of v29: "What unequalled magnanimity does this speech breathe! Only his Master ever towered above this." JFB imagines Paul "holding up his two chained hands," with an "electrical effect," and Agrippa rising "not over-easy, we may be sure." The Tyndale notes read Agrippa's reply as deliberately evasive, caught between Paul's argument and his Jewish subjects. That leans toward the deflection reading, not the KJV's "almost persuaded."

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul's whole defense comes down to verse 23: the Messiah must suffer, must be the first to rise, and must bring light to Israel and the nations. The "light brighter than the sun" that blinded Saul was the glory of the risen Christ (2 Cor 4:6). The man in chains is freer than the king on the throne. And the closing scene asks the reader the same question it asked Agrippa: "Do you believe?" Being almost persuaded, or persuaded "in a short time," is not the same as belonging to Christ. Paul's prayer stands open to "all who hear."
