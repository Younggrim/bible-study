*Setting:* About AD 29–30, on the journey to Jerusalem, probably still in Perea. Chapter 15 answered the Pharisees' complaint that Jesus welcomes sinners with three stories of lost things found, ending with an older brother standing outside the party. Chapter 16 turns to money, a subject Luke returns to more than any other Gospel writer. Jesus speaks first to his disciples (v1), but the Pharisees are still listening (v14), and the chapter's two stories both begin with "There was a rich man" (vv1, 19). The first is about a man who used money wisely for his future. The second is about a man who did not.

#### The Parable of the Dishonest Manager (vv1–13)
- **"He also said to the disciples" (v1).** The audience shifts, but not completely. The tax collectors, sinners and Pharisees of 15:1–2 are still nearby, and the Pharisees will react to what they hear (v14).
- **"A rich man who had a manager" (v1).** Wealthy landowners, often living away from their estates, hired a manager (*oikonomos*, "household manager," "steward") to run them. He could rent out land, make contracts and collect what tenants owed, and he acted with his master's authority.
- **"Wasting his possessions" (v1).** "Wasting" is *diaskorpizō*, the same verb used of the younger son "squandering" his inheritance (15:13). Luke places the two stories side by side on purpose. Both men scatter what belongs to someone else, but this one, at least, thinks hard about what comes next.
- **"Turn in the account of your management" (v2).** He is fired on the spot. The final accounts are for the books to be handed over. He does not argue or defend himself, which suggests the charges were true.
- **"I am not strong enough to dig, and I am ashamed to beg" (v3).** Digging was the hardest manual labor. Begging meant total disgrace. A Jewish wisdom book of the time says that it is better to die than to beg (Sirach 40:28). He has a short window before word gets out that he has been dismissed.
- **"So that... people may receive me into their houses" (v4).** His one goal is a home after he loses this one. JFB calls this "the key to the parable."
- **"A hundred measures of oil... write fifty" (vv5–6).** The measure is a *bath*, a liquid measure of perhaps six to nine gallons, so this was a large commercial debt, the yield of well over a hundred olive trees. "Sit down quickly" shows the hurry. The bill was in the debtor's own handwriting, so he rewrites it himself.
- **"A hundred measures of wheat... write eighty" (v7).** This measure is a *cor*, perhaps ten to fifteen bushels, so a thousand bushels or more. Some scholars calculate that the two reductions are worth about the same in money, roughly 500 denarii each, well over a year's wages for a laborer.
- **What was he doing?** Readers take the reductions in two main ways. The traditional view is that he simply cheated his master one last time, making the debtors grateful to him. Another view is that he cut out his own commission, or the hidden interest that the law forbade Jews to charge one another (Deut 23:19), so he gave up his own gain rather than his master's. The parable calls him "dishonest" (v8), and the first view fits that best, but either way, the debtors and the village would now praise both him and his generous master.
- **"The master commended the dishonest manager for his shrewdness" (v8).** "The master" is the rich man of the story, not Jesus. He does not praise the fraud. He admires the cleverness with a rueful shake of the head. "Shrewdness" is *phronimōs*, "prudently, sensibly," from the word Jesus uses for the "wise" man who built on rock (Matt 7:24) and the "faithful and wise manager" (12:42). The manager saw his crisis coming and acted decisively before it hit.
- **"The sons of this world are more shrewd... than the sons of light" (v8).** "Sons of" is a Hebrew way of saying "people belonging to." Worldly people plan carefully for their own short future. God's people, who know an eternal future is coming, often plan for it far less carefully. That is the sting.
- **"Make friends for yourselves by means of unrighteous wealth" (v9).** "Wealth" translates *mamōnas*, an Aramaic word for money or property, left untranslated in older English Bibles as "mammon." It is called "unrighteous" not because money is evil in itself but because it so easily serves evil, and it belongs to this passing age. Jesus' point: use the money now in hand to do lasting good, especially by giving to the poor (12:33; 14:13–14), as the manager used his master's money to secure a welcome.
- **"So that when it fails they may receive you into the eternal dwellings" (v9).** Money will fail, at death if not before. "They" may be the friends made by generosity, welcoming the giver into heaven, or it may be a reverent way of saying God will receive him. Jesus is not saying heaven can be bought. Generosity shows where a heart's treasure really is (12:34).
- **"Faithful in a very little" (v10).** Here the lesson turns. The manager was shrewd but not faithful. Jesus calls money "a very little," a small test that shows what a person will do with much.
- **"Who will entrust to you the true riches?" (v11).** The true riches are the lasting goods of God's kingdom. Trustworthiness with money is a training ground for trust in greater things.
- **"That which is another's... that which is your own" (v12).** Everything we hold in this life belongs to God and is on loan. What God gives in his kingdom will be truly ours to keep.
- **"No servant can serve two masters" (v13).** "Serve" means to work as a slave. A slave could be owned jointly, but he could not give full loyalty to two owners at once. Money is pictured as a rival master that demands love and service. A person can *have* money. The question is which one gives the orders.
- **Commentators:** All three insist the master praises the manager's foresight, not his fraud. Henry: "The lord... commended not the fraud, but the policy of the steward," and he adds that worldly people, foolish in their aims, are "often wiser than believers" in effort and perseverance. JFB passes on a vivid line from Trench: this is wisdom only "as night-birds see better in the dark than those of the day." JFB also stresses that money is "not here made the key to heaven" but is a test of character. Guzik draws the same two lessons from the manager, that he took his coming accountability seriously and used his present position to prepare for his future, and he notes that a person will make sacrifices for whatever he truly serves.

#### The Law and the Kingdom of God (vv14–17)
- **"The Pharisees, who were lovers of money" (v14).** "Lovers of money" is one Greek word, *philargyros*, used again only in 2 Timothy 3:2. Not every Pharisee was greedy, but many believed wealth was a sign of God's blessing, which made Jesus' warning sound foolish to them.
- **"They ridiculed him" (v14).** The verb is literally "they turned up their noses." It is used again only of the rulers sneering at Jesus on the cross (23:35).
- **"You are those who justify yourselves before men" (v15).** They declare themselves righteous in front of others and arrange their lives for public approval. "But God knows your hearts" echoes 1 Samuel 16:7: "The LORD looks on the heart."
- **"What is exalted among men is an abomination in the sight of God" (v15).** "Abomination" is a strong Old Testament word for what God finds disgusting, often idolatry. The status that money buys and people admire, God views the way he views an idol.
- **"The Law and the Prophets were until John" (v16).** John the Baptist stands at the turning point between the age of promise and the age of fulfilment (7:28). The Law and the Prophets pointed ahead. Now "the good news of the kingdom of God is preached."
- **"Everyone forces his way into it" (v16).** The verb *biazomai* means "to use force." Matthew's parallel saying is notoriously difficult (Matt 11:12). Some take Luke's version as positive: people of every kind, tax collectors and sinners included, are pressing eagerly into the kingdom. Others hear a warning that people are trying to force it on their own terms. The lexicon allows either. The context of chapters 15–16, with sinners crowding in while the Pharisees stand outside, favors the first, and it fits Jesus' earlier call to "strive to enter" (13:24).
- **"Easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for one dot of the Law to become void" (v17).** "Dot" is *keraia*, a "little horn," the tiny stroke that tells one Hebrew letter from another, such as *dalet* (ד) from *resh* (ר). The new age does not throw the law away. It fulfils it (Matt 5:17–18). The Pharisees, who claim to champion the law, are actually emptying it of its meaning, as the next verse shows.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus warned the Pharisees that "what they contended for as the law, was a wresting of its meaning." JFB says the Pharisees sneered because "their master sin" was struck too plainly, and "it was easier to run down than to refute such teaching." JFB takes v16 positively: publicans and sinners are eagerly pressing in, while the Pharisees let "the tide... go past" them. Guzik also reads v16 of zeal for the kingdom, comparing it to the passion of the revolutionaries of Jesus' day but without their violence.

#### Divorce and Remarriage (v18)
- **"Everyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery" (v18).** This single verse seems to come out of nowhere, but it is an example of the point in v17. The law allowed divorce in some cases (Deut 24:1–4). In Jesus' day the school of Hillel allowed a man to divorce his wife for almost any reason, even a spoiled meal, while the school of Shammai allowed it mainly for sexual unfaithfulness. Many Pharisees treated the law as permission. Jesus treats it as a limit on hardness of heart and goes back to God's purpose for marriage (Mark 10:2–12).
- **Why here?** The Pharisees "justify" themselves before people (v15) by keeping the letter of the law while ignoring its heart. Easy divorce, with all the legal paperwork in order, was a clear example. A man could send away his wife, who in that world had few ways to support herself, and call it lawful. Herod Antipas had divorced his wife to marry Herodias, and John the Baptist lost his life for saying it was wrong (3:19–20).
- **The fuller teaching.** Luke gives the saying in its shortest form. Matthew records an exception for sexual immorality (Matt 5:32; 19:9), and Paul addresses desertion by an unbelieving spouse (1 Cor 7:15). Christians differ on how to apply these texts to remarriage today, but all agree that Jesus raises the bar above the easy divorce of his day and treats marriage as a covenant before God.
- **Commentators:** JFB says that, far from weakening the law, Jesus here "sends home its high requirements with a pungency which the Pharisees would not fail to feel." Guzik mentions the rabbinic debate in which some allowed divorce over a burnt breakfast, and he warns against building a whole doctrine on one verse apart from the rest of Scripture.

#### The Rich Man and Lazarus (vv19–31)
- **"Clothed in purple and fine linen... feasted sumptuously every day" (v19).** Purple dye, made from shellfish off the Phoenician coast, was so costly that it was worn mainly by royalty. Fine linen, often from Egypt, was used for underclothes. He dressed like a king inside and out, and every day was a feast day for him. Tradition gave him the name "Dives," which is simply Latin for "rich man."
- **"At his gate was laid a poor man named Lazarus" (v20).** "Was laid" means someone carried and dropped him there each day. He could not walk. Lazarus is the Greek form of Eleazar, "God has helped." This is the only figure given a name in any of Jesus' parables. The rich man has everything but no name. The poor man has nothing but a name that says God helps him.
- **Parable or real event?** Many, including Guzik, take the naming as a sign that Jesus is describing real people. Most interpreters treat it as a parable, since it opens with Luke's usual parable phrase, "There was a rich man" (v1; 15:11), and uses familiar Jewish pictures of the afterlife. Either way, Jesus uses it to teach real truths about death, judgment and Scripture. Readers should be careful about building a detailed map of the afterlife from its images.
- **"Desired to be fed with what fell from the rich man's table" (v21).** At banquets, guests wiped their hands on pieces of bread and tossed them under the table. Lazarus longed for even those. It is the same verb used of the prodigal "longing" for pig food (15:16), and it likely means he did not get it.
- **"Even the dogs came and licked his sores" (v21).** These were street dogs, unclean scavengers. Lazarus is too weak to fend them off. It is a picture of utter misery. JFB sees "a touching act of brute pity" where no human showed any.
- **"Carried by the angels to Abraham's side" (v22).** Literally "to Abraham's bosom," picturing a banquet where guests reclined on couches and the one next to the host leaned against his chest (John 13:23). Lazarus, who longed for crumbs, now has the place of honor at the feast with the father of Israel (13:28–29). "The rich man also died and was buried," with all the ceremony money could buy. Lazarus gets no burial mentioned at all.
- **"In Hades, being in torment" (v23).** *Hades* is the Greek word for the realm of the dead, used in the Greek Old Testament for the Hebrew *Sheol*. Some Jewish writings of the time pictured Sheol with separate places for the righteous and the wicked, which fits the picture here. Christians differ on how Hades relates to final hell (Rev 20:14): some see it as a waiting place before the final judgment, others read it here as hell itself. Either way, the rich man is in conscious anguish, and the separation is real.
- **"Father Abraham, have mercy on me" (v24).** He claims Abraham as his ancestor, but John the Baptist had already warned that descent from Abraham saves no one (3:8). He asks for mercy he never showed. And even now he treats Lazarus as an errand boy: "send Lazarus."
- **"Child, remember" (v25).** Abraham calls him "child," acknowledging the family tie, but it cannot help him now. "Received your good things" echoes the woes of 6:24: "Woe to you who are rich, for you have received your consolation." The rich man is not condemned for being rich. He is condemned for a life lived only for himself, with a dying neighbor at his gate.
- **"A great chasm has been fixed" (v26).** "Fixed" means set firmly in place, and the passive verb quietly implies that God is the one who set it. After death, there is no crossing over. The choices of this life are final.
- **"Send him to my father's house—for I have five brothers" (vv27–28).** He shows concern for his family at last. But there is also a hint of complaint: if only he had been warned better, he would not be here. JFB cites Trench reading the request as "bitter reproach against God and the old economy, as not warning him sufficiently."
- **"They have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear them" (v29).** The Old Testament was full of commands to care for the poor (Deut 15:7–11; Isa 58:6–7; Amos 6:4–7). The brothers do not lack warning. They lack a willingness to listen.
- **"If someone goes to them from the dead, they will repent" (v30).** He contradicts Abraham, as if a miracle would succeed where Scripture failed.
- **"Neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead" (v31).** The last line points straight ahead. Jesus himself will rise from the dead, and many will still refuse to believe (Acts 26:8, 22–23). John tells of another man named Lazarus whom Jesus raised, and the response of the leaders was to plot to kill him too (John 11:43–53; 12:10–11). A heart that will not hear God's word will not be moved by a miracle either.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We are not told that the rich man did him any harm, but we do not find that he had any care for him," and "the sin of this rich man was his providing for himself only." Guzik makes the same point and adds that poverty did not save Lazarus any more than wealth damned the rich man. Henry also notes, against those who use this passage to justify praying to departed saints, that the only example is "the mistake of a damned sinner," whose prayer was refused. JFB observes that the rich man sees Abraham, "not God," and on v31 writes that "the greatest miracle will have no effect on those who are determined not to believe." Guzik differs from the other two in taking this as a real account and in teaching that Hades was a temporary waiting place until Christ's resurrection, a view many but not all Christians hold.

#### Pointing to Christ
The manager prepared for the day he would lose everything. Jesus calls his people to do the same, and to use what passes for what lasts. Behind that call stands the one who "though he was rich, yet for your sake... became poor" (2 Cor 8:9), who did not ignore the beggar at the gate but became like him, and whose rising from the dead is the very sign v31 says many will still refuse. Moses and the Prophets point to him (24:27), and in him the great chasm between sinners and God is crossed from God's side.
