*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Maskil of the Sons of Korah." Psalms 42–43 were the lament of one man. Psalm 44 is the lament of a nation. Israel's army has been defeated, its people killed or scattered among the nations, and its neighbors are laughing. What makes the psalm unusual is its claim: we did not deserve this. Most of Israel's defeats in the Old Testament are explained as judgment for sin (Judg 2:11–15; 2 Kgs 17:7–18). Here the people insist they have not forgotten God or broken his covenant (vv17–18), and still they suffer. The date is uncertain. JFB thinks David's reign likely, perhaps when Edom invaded while David's army was away in the north (2 Sam 8:13–14; the title of Psalm 60 mentions this). Others suggest Hezekiah's time, when Sennacherib overran Judah, or the exile, as the Tyndale notes do. Some older commentators, including Calvin, suggested the Maccabean persecution in the 160s BC, but most scholars think the Psalter was largely complete by then. The psalm moves in four stages: what God did in the past (vv1–8), what he has let happen now (vv9–16), a protest of loyalty (vv17–22), and a cry for him to wake up (vv23–26). Paul quotes v22 in Romans 8:36.

#### We Have Heard with Our Ears (vv1–8)
- **"O God, we have heard with our ears, our fathers have told us" (v1).** Israel's faith was handed down by telling. Fathers were commanded to tell their children what God had done (Exod 12:26–27; Deut 6:20–25; Ps 78:3–4). These stories are not legends to them. They are family history.
- **"What deeds you performed in their days, in the days of old" (v1).** The great "deed" is the conquest of Canaan under Joshua. JFB points to Joshua 24:12, where God reminds Israel, "it was not by your sword or by your bow."
- **"You with your own hand drove out the nations, but them you planted" (v2).** The picture is of a gardener clearing ground and planting a vine. Israel is often called God's vine (80:8–11; Isa 5:1–7). "Them you set free" may also be read "them you spread out," like a growing plant sending out shoots.
- **"For not by their own sword did they win the land" (v3).** Israel did fight, with real swords. But the victories were God's. Jericho's walls fell without a siege engine (Josh 6). The sun stood still at Gibeon (Josh 10:12–14). Gideon's army was cut to three hundred so that Israel could not boast (Judg 7:2).
- **"Your right hand and your arm, and the light of your face, for you delighted in them" (v3).** The right hand and arm are God's power. "The light of your face" is his favor, the language of the priestly blessing (Num 6:25). And the reason was not Israel's strength or goodness but God's delight in them. Deuteronomy 7:7–8 says exactly this: "It was not because you were more in number… but it is because the LORD loves you."
- **"You are my King, O God; ordain salvation for Jacob!" (v4).** The voice changes to "I," probably the king or a leader speaking for the nation. "Ordain" is literally "command." He asks God to give orders, like a general, and the victories will follow. "Salvation" is plural in Hebrew, "victories" or "deliverances." JFB: "my King" means the same covenant relation God had with "the fathers."
- **"Through you we push down our foes" (v5).** "Push down" is the word for an ox goring with its horns (Deut 33:17). JFB: an image "drawn from the habits of the ox." "Through your name": by God's authority and power.
- **"For not in my bow do I trust, nor can my sword save me" (v6).** This generation has learned the lesson of v3. They hold their weapons, but they do not trust them. David said the same to Goliath: "the LORD saves not with sword and spear" (1 Sam 17:47).
- **"In God we have boasted continually" (v8).** "Boasted" is the same root as "praise" (*halal*). Their boast was not in themselves but in God. Paul quotes Jeremiah: "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1 Cor 1:31).
- **"Selah" (v8).** A pause, and a sharp one. Everything so far has been confident. What comes next is not.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Former experiences of God's power and goodness are strong supports to faith," and on v3, "He fought for Israel, else they had fought in vain." He applies it to the early church, which grew by Christ's Spirit, not by human power. Guzik notes that Cranmer's Anglican Litany used the psalm's opening and closing lines as a prayer, and that the people received the conquest story as family history. JFB links vv1–3 to Joshua 24:12.

#### But You Have Rejected Us (vv9–16)
- **"But you have rejected us and disgraced us" (v9).** The "but" falls like a hammer. The God who went out with Israel's armies "has not gone out" with them this time. The ark had sometimes gone into battle as a sign of God's presence (Num 10:35; 1 Sam 4:3–4). JFB compares 2 Samuel 5:23, where David waited for God to go out before him.
- **"You have made us turn back from the foe" (v10).** The psalm never says "our enemies did this." It says "you" over and over: you rejected, you made us turn back, you made us like sheep, you sold us, you made us a taunt. That is not blasphemy. It is faith. They believe God is sovereign even over their defeat, and so they take their complaint to him. Guzik notes that Israel as a covenant nation stood or fell with the LORD.
- **"You have made us like sheep for slaughter" (v11).** Literally "sheep for eating," sheep raised for the butcher. Helpless, with no shepherd defending them. The phrase returns in v22.
- **"And have scattered us among the nations" (v11).** Prisoners of war were often sold as slaves abroad (Joel 3:6; Amos 1:6). The Tyndale notes take this as the exile. JFB says "the Babylonian captivity not necessarily meant," since smaller deportations happened often (1 Kgs 8:46).
- **"You have sold your people for a trifle, demanding no high price for them" (v12).** The bitterest line in the psalm. A seller normally haggles for a good price. God, they say, has let his own people go cheap, as though they were worth nothing to him. Isaiah later answers this very complaint: "You were sold for nothing, and you shall be redeemed without money" (Isa 52:3).
- **"You have made us the taunt of our neighbors" (v13).** The neighbors were Edom, Moab, Ammon and the Philistines, Israel's old rivals. The covenant had warned that unfaithfulness would make Israel "a horror, a proverb, and a byword among all the peoples" (Deut 28:37). That is what makes it so painful here: they are suffering the curse without, as far as they know, the cause.
- **"A byword among the nations, a laughingstock among the peoples" (v14).** A "byword" is a proverb, a name people use as a joke. "Laughingstock" is literally "a shaking of the head," the gesture of mockery (22:7; Matt 27:39).
- **"All day long my disgrace is before me, and shame has covered my face" (v15).** The voice is singular again, the leader feeling the nation's shame personally. JFB: "blushes in disgrace." "The enemy and the avenger" (v16): the same phrase as 8:2, where God silences them. Here they are loud.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns that the church will face times when it fears God has rejected it, and that the remedy is to look past human enemies to God, since "their worst enemies have no power against them, but what is permitted from above." Guzik stresses that the psalmist lays every defeat at God's door. JFB notes that the verb tenses in vv9–12 show cause and effect: God withdrew, so defeat and plunder followed.

#### Though We Have Not Forgotten You (vv17–22)
- **"All this has come upon us, though we have not forgotten you" (v17).** This is the heart of the psalm. They are not claiming to be sinless. They are claiming covenant loyalty: no idolatry, no national turning away. JFB: "They had not apostatized totally—were still God's people." Guzik calls this an "anti-penitential" psalm, since it denies that unfaithfulness explains the suffering.
- **"We have not been false to your covenant" (v17).** "False" is the verb for dealing treacherously. The covenant of Deuteronomy 28 promised blessing for loyalty and curse for unfaithfulness. They seem to be getting the curse for loyalty. That is the problem this psalm raises, the same problem Job raises for one man.
- **"Our heart has not turned back, nor have our steps departed from your way" (v18).** Heart and steps, inside and outside. Their loyalty, they say, has been real all the way through.
- **"Yet you have broken us in the place of jackals" (v19).** "Jackals" (*tannim*) are the scavengers that haunt ruined cities and deserts (Isa 13:22; 34:13). A "place of jackals" is a wasteland, the ruins after a battle. The KJV read "place of dragons," confusing it with a similar word for sea monster. JFB: "desolate, barren, rocky wilderness."
- **"And covered us with the shadow of death" (v19).** *Tsalmavet*, the same word as in 23:4, "the valley of the shadow of death." Deep darkness, the darkness of death itself.
- **"If we had forgotten the name of our God or spread out our hands to a foreign god" (v20).** Spreading out the hands was the posture of prayer (Exod 9:29; 1 Kgs 8:22). To spread them to another god was idolatry, the very sin that usually explained Israel's defeats.
- **"Would not God discover this? For he knows the secrets of the heart" (v21).** They appeal to God's own knowledge. If there were secret idolatry, God would know, and he could point to it. They are confident he will find none. Compare David's prayer: "Search me, O God, and know my heart!" (139:23).
- **"Yet for your sake we are killed all the day long" (v22).** Here is something new in the Old Testament. Not "because of our sin" but "for your sake." Their suffering is not a punishment. It is the cost of belonging to God in a world hostile to him. Guzik calls it a battle scar of faithfulness. JFB: their long sufferings as God's people "attests the constancy."
- **"We are regarded as sheep to be slaughtered" (v22).** Paul quotes this line in Romans 8:36, in the middle of his great passage on the love of God. He has just asked, "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or danger, or sword?" Then he quotes Psalm 44:22 to show that suffering for God's sake is nothing new. And he answers: "No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us" (Rom 8:37). JFB notes that Paul uses this language "to describe Christian steadfastness in persecution."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The church of God cannot be prevailed on by persecution to forget God," and "In afflictions, we must not seek relief by any sinful compliance." On v21 he writes, "Hearts sins and secret sins are known to God, and must be reckoned for." He connects the suffering "unto death" with those who died for Christ's testimony. Guzik links the covenant logic to Deuteronomy 28 and sees "for your sake" as a new and important idea. JFB calls vv20–21 "a solemn appeal to God to witness their constancy."

#### Awake! Why Are You Sleeping, O Lord? (vv23–26)
- **"Awake! Why are you sleeping, O Lord?" (v23).** This sounds shocking, since "he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (121:4). The psalmist knows that. It is a figure of speech: God is acting as if asleep, doing nothing. Elijah mocked Baal's prophets with the idea that their god might be asleep (1 Kgs 18:27). Here a believer uses the same words in raw prayer to the true God. JFB notes that addressing God as if indifferent "is frequent" in the Psalms (3:7; 13:1).
- **The disciples in the boat.** When a storm swamped the boat on the Sea of Galilee and Jesus was asleep, the disciples woke him: "Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38). He rose, rebuked the wind, and there was a great calm. Guzik makes the comparison. The Lord who seemed asleep was in the boat all along.
- **"Rouse yourself! Do not reject us forever!" (v23).** "Forever" is the fear behind the whole psalm. A temporary setback can be borne. Permanent rejection cannot.
- **"Why do you hide your face?" (v24).** In v3, "the light of your face" won the land. Now God's face is hidden. The opposite of the priestly blessing.
- **"For our soul is bowed down to the dust; our belly clings to the ground" (v25).** The posture of complete defeat, flat on the ground. It may also be the posture of prayer, face down before God. Genesis 3:14 uses similar words of the serpent's curse ("on your belly you shall go, and dust you shall eat").
- **"Rise up; come to our help!" (v26).** Moses' old battle cry when the ark set out: "Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered" (Num 10:35). The psalm returns to the God who went out with Israel's armies in v9.
- **"Redeem us for the sake of your steadfast love!" (v26).** The last plea is not "because we have been faithful." Their claim of loyalty was real, but it is not what they finally rest on. They rest on God's *chesed*, his loyal covenant love. "Redeem" (*padah*) is the word for buying back, used of the exodus (Deut 7:8). They were "sold for a trifle" (v12); now they ask to be bought back. The psalm ends with no answer, only the plea.
- **Commentators:** Henry says their plea rests on "Not their own merit and righteousness" but on God's mercy, which he calls "purchased, promised, and constantly flowing forth." He adds, "None that belong to Christ shall be cast off." JFB: "However low their condition, God is appealed to, on the ground, and for the honor, of His mercy." Guzik notes that the language of sleep is figurative and that the final plea is for mercy, not reward.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 44 asks a question the Old Testament could not fully answer: why do the faithful suffer "for your sake"? The answer came in a person. Jesus was the one truly faithful Israelite, who never forgot God or broke his covenant, and yet he was "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" (Isa 53:7), sold for thirty pieces of silver, mocked by shaking heads and left under the shadow of death. God did not stay asleep. He raised him up. That is why Paul can quote v22 and in the next breath say that nothing "in all creation will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Rom 8:39). Christians still suffer for his sake, but they are not "sold for a trifle." They were bought "with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Pet 1:19).
