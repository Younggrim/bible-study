*Setting:* Paul's second missionary journey, about AD 50. Paul and Silas have just been beaten, jailed and then politely asked to leave Philippi (16:39). Timothy is with them, and Luke seems to have stayed behind, since the "we" has stopped. They head west along the Via Egnatia, the Roman highway across northern Greece, to Thessalonica, then to Berea, and then Paul goes alone by sea to Athens. The chapter moves from synagogues, where Paul argues from the Scriptures, to the old intellectual capital of the Greek world, where he speaks to philosophers who have never read them. It holds the most famous sermon Paul preached to pagans.

#### Paul and Silas in Thessalonica (vv1–9)
- **Amphipolis and Apollonia (v1).** These were stops on the Via Egnatia, roughly a day's journey apart. Paul passes through them, perhaps because neither had a synagogue. Thessalonica, about 100 miles from Philippi, was the capital of the province of Macedonia and a busy port. It is Thessaloniki, Greece's second city, today.
- **"As was his custom" (v2).** Paul goes to the synagogue first wherever there is one (13:5, 14; 14:1). There he finds Jews and God-fearing Gentiles who already know the Scriptures and are waiting for the Messiah.
- **"On three Sabbath days he reasoned with them from the Scriptures" (v2).** "Reasoned" is *dialegomai*, the root of English "dialogue": a discussion with questions and answers, not a monologue. Paul's stay in the city was probably longer than three weeks. He worked "night and day" to support himself (1 Thess 2:9), and the Philippians sent him help more than once while he was there (Phil 4:16). The three Sabbaths were his time in the synagogue.
- **"Explaining and proving that it was necessary for the Christ to suffer and to rise" (v3).** "Explaining" is literally "opening," the same verb used when the risen Jesus "opened" the Scriptures to the two on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:32). "Proving" means setting the evidence alongside. The hard part for Jewish hearers was not that the Messiah would come but that he would *suffer*. Paul likely used passages such as Isaiah 53 and Psalms 16, 22 and 110. Then he made the claim: "This Jesus… is the Christ."
- **"Some of them… a great many of the devout Greeks and not a few of the leading women" (v4).** Most of the new church came from Gentiles. Paul later writes that they "turned to God from idols" (1 Thess 1:9), which fits Gentile converts. Luke often notes prominent women who believed (16:14; 17:12, 34).
- **"The Jews were jealous" (v5).** Paul was taking away the God-fearers, the synagogue's Gentile friends and often its patrons. The opponents hire "wicked men of the rabble," literally men who hang around the marketplace, to start a riot.
- **Jason (vv5–7).** Paul and Silas were staying at his house. A Jason appears with Paul in Corinth later as one of his "kinsmen" (Rom 16:21), meaning a fellow Jew, and may be the same man. Jason was a common Greek name that Jews often used for Joshua.
- **"The city authorities" (v6).** The Greek is *politarchai*, "rulers of the city." This title was once unknown outside Acts, and some scholars thought Luke had invented it. Then it turned up in inscriptions from Thessalonica, one on a Roman arch called the Vardar Gate, now in the British Museum. Luke's accuracy on small local details like this is striking.
- **"These men who have turned the world upside down" (v6).** The verb *anastatoō* means to stir up or unsettle. It is used of a rebel leader (21:38). It was meant as a charge of sedition, but Christians have long heard it as an unintended compliment. "The world" (*oikoumenē*) means the inhabited world, the Roman Empire.
- **"Acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus" (v7).** This is the most dangerous charge in Acts so far. It is close to the charge brought against Jesus himself before Pilate (Luke 23:2). Paul did preach God's kingdom and Jesus' return as Lord (1 Thess 2:12; 4:13–5:11). The opponents twisted it into rebellion against Rome. Some scholars think the "decrees" were imperial bans on predicting a change of ruler, or the oaths of loyalty that cities swore to Caesar.
- **"Taken money as security from Jason" (v9).** Jason posted a bond guaranteeing there would be no more trouble. This probably meant Paul had to leave and could not return. That may be what Paul means when he says "Satan hindered us" from coming back (1 Thess 2:18).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls Paul's preaching "chiefly expository" and points to the inscription on the arch as confirmation of Luke's word *politarchs*. It also thinks Jason was "one of Paul's kinsmen, apparently" (Rom 16:21). Guzik reads "turned the world upside down" as a backhanded compliment, and adds that the gospel really sets the world right side up. Guzik also notices that the charge of "another king" admits, without meaning to, the very thing the believers claimed about Jesus.

#### Paul and Silas in Berea (vv10–15)
- **"Sent Paul and Silas away by night to Berea" (v10).** Berea (modern Veria) lay about fifty miles southwest, off the main highway. The Roman writer Cicero called it an out-of-the-way town. Paul, just run out of town again, goes straight to the synagogue.
- **"More noble than those in Thessalonica" (v11).** The word *eugenēs* first meant "well-born," of noble birth, but came to mean noble in character: open-minded, generous. Their nobility shows in two things together. They "received the word with all eagerness," and they were "examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so." They were neither gullible nor closed. They listened warmly and checked everything against the Bible. "Examining" is the word for a legal inquiry, careful questioning to reach the truth.
- **"Many of them therefore believed" (v12).** "Therefore" ties their faith to their study. Searching the Scriptures led them to Christ, not away from him. One of them was probably Sopater of Berea, who later traveled with Paul (20:4).
- **"Agitating and stirring up the crowds" (v13).** Opponents travel fifty miles to repeat the trouble, as others had come from Antioch and Iconium to Lystra (14:19). Paul tells the Thessalonians that these same opponents "drove us out" and try to stop him speaking to the Gentiles (1 Thess 2:14–16).
- **"Sent Paul off on his way to the sea" (v14).** Paul is clearly the target. Silas and Timothy stay to care for the new believers. Those who escort Paul take him all the way to Athens, more than 200 miles south, probably by ship.
- **"A command for Silas and Timothy to come to him as soon as possible" (v15).** From 1 Thessalonians 3:1–2 it seems Timothy did come to Athens and was sent back north to Thessalonica. Paul and his team were in constant motion, and Luke gives only the main line.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the Bereans "the most gentleman-like men," and writes, "Searching the scriptures must be our daily work." He adds that those are truly noble who make the Scriptures "their oracle and touchstone." Guzik sees three marks of their study: they searched, they searched daily, and they searched to find out what was true. He stresses an open heart paired with a clear head. JFB says the comparison is between the Jews of the two cities, not the whole populations.

#### Paul in Athens (vv16–21)
- **"While Paul was waiting for them at Athens" (v16).** Athens was past its political peak, a university town living on its glorious past. It was the city of Socrates, Plato and Aristotle, and of the Parthenon on the Acropolis. Paul was not there on a planned mission. He was waiting for friends, and he could not keep quiet.
- **"His spirit was provoked within him" (v16).** The verb *paroxynō* comes from the same root as the "sharp disagreement" between Paul and Barnabas (15:39). It means stirred to deep distress or indignation. In the Greek Old Testament it is often used of God being provoked by idolatry (Deut 9:18; Isa 65:3). Paul feels something of God's own jealousy for his glory.
- **"The city was full of idols" (v16).** The word *kateidōlos* appears only here, and means "full of idols." The travel writer Pausanias, a century later, still filled pages listing them. Statues and shrines lined the streets, the marketplace and the Acropolis. Paul saw great art and great religion, and he grieved that it honored what was not God.
- **"In the synagogue… and in the marketplace every day" (v17).** Paul works two places at once: the synagogue on the Sabbath and the agora every day. The agora was the city's public square, where Socrates himself had questioned passersby centuries before. Paul takes up the Socratic method in Socrates' own marketplace.
- **The Epicureans and Stoics (v18).** These were the two great popular philosophies of the day. Epicureans, following Epicurus, taught that the gods were far away and uninvolved, that the soul dissolved at death, and that the good life was a calm one free of pain and fear. Stoics, following Zeno, taught that a divine Reason filled the universe, that one should live in line with it, accept fate and do one's duty. The Stoics would agree with parts of Paul's sermon. Neither group had room for a bodily resurrection or a final judgment.
- **"What does this babbler wish to say?" (v18).** The word is *spermologos*, literally "seed-picker," used of birds pecking at grain. In Athenian slang it meant an idler who picked up scraps of learning and passed them off as wisdom. To these philosophers Paul looked like an amateur.
- **"A preacher of foreign divinities… because he was preaching Jesus and the resurrection" (v18).** "Divinities" is plural. The Greek for resurrection, *anastasis*, is a feminine noun. The early preacher John Chrysostom suggested they thought Paul was announcing a pair of new gods, "Jesus" and "Anastasis." That is a guess, but it fits. The charge also echoes Socrates, who was condemned in Athens for bringing in new divinities.
- **"They took him and brought him to the Areopagus" (v19).** The name means "hill of Ares" (the Roman Mars, so "Mars Hill" in older English). It was also the name of the council that once met there, the oldest and most honored body in Athens. It still oversaw religion and education in Paul's day. By then it may have met in a hall in the agora. Either way, Paul is brought before the city's leading council. It was probably an inquiry, not a formal trial.
- **"Nothing except telling or hearing something new" (v21).** Luke's aside is gently ironic. The Greek orator Demosthenes had scolded the Athenians centuries before for running about asking "Is there any news?" Paul's audience was curious, but curiosity is not the same as seeking the truth.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Paul "was filled with concern for the glory of God, which he saw given to idols," and was stirred also at the philosophers who knew better and said nothing. JFB reads "wholly given to idolatry" as "covered with idols," a description of the city, which fits the lexicon's "full of idols." Guzik's picture of a city "swamped" with idols is the same idea. JFB explains *spermologos* as "a picker-up of seeds" and a term for one who retails scraps of knowledge, which the lexicon confirms. JFB also says Paul was "not here on his trial" but invited to explain himself.

#### Paul Addresses the Areopagus (vv22–34)
- **"Men of Athens… you are very religious" (v22).** The word *deisidaimōn* can mean "religious" or "superstitious," literally "fearing the gods." The King James "too superstitious" makes it an insult. Paul is about to build on their religiousness, not mock it, so the most likely sense is neutral or mildly positive: "I see how very religious you are." Paul starts where they are.
- **"To the unknown god" (v23).** Ancient writers, including the travel writer Pausanias, mention altars in and around Athens to "unknown gods." The Greeks wanted to cover every possibility. One legend says the poet Epimenides ended a plague by releasing sheep and sacrificing to an unnamed god wherever they lay down. Paul turns the altar into an admission. "What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you." He is not saying they already worship the true God without knowing it. He is saying they admit there is a God they do not know, and he can tell them who he is.
- **The sermon's shape (vv24–31).** Paul does not quote the Old Testament, which his hearers did not know, but every line is built on it. God is Creator (v24), self-sufficient (v25), the maker of all nations (v26), near (vv27–28), not an idol (v29), commanding repentance (v30), and coming as Judge through a risen man (v31).
- **"Does not live in temples made by man" (v24).** Paul says this within sight of the Parthenon. Solomon said the same at the dedication of his temple (1 Kgs 8:27), and Stephen used nearly the same words before his death (7:48–50; Isa 66:1–2).
- **"Nor is he served by human hands, as though he needed anything" (v25).** Greek temples were run on the idea that gods needed offerings, food and care. Paul turns it around: God is the giver of "life and breath and everything" (Ps 50:9–12). Epicureans would agree that the gods need nothing, and Stoics that God is the source of life, but Paul means a personal Creator.
- **"He made from one man every nation" (v26).** Athenians boasted that they had sprung from their own soil and were superior to other peoples. Paul says all nations come from one man, Adam. All peoples are one family, and the Greeks are not above the "barbarians." God also set "allotted periods and the boundaries" for nations, both times and places (Deut 32:8).
- **"That they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him" (v27).** "Feel their way" is the word for groping in the dark, like a blind man (Deut 28:29). Humanity's search for God without his revelation is real but fumbling. "Yet he is actually not far from each one of us." The problem is not God's distance but human blindness.
- **"In him we live and move and have our being" (v28).** Paul quotes the Greeks' own poets. A Christian writer of the 800s attributes this first line to the Cretan poet Epimenides (about 600 BC). Paul quotes him again in Titus 1:12. Some scholars doubt the attribution, but it is a widely held view.
- **"For we are indeed his offspring" (v28).** This line is from Aratus, a poet from Cilicia, Paul's home region (*Phaenomena* 5, about 270 BC). The Stoic Cleanthes wrote something similar in his *Hymn to Zeus*. The poets meant Zeus and a Stoic world-soul. Paul takes the line and fills it with its true meaning: every human being is made by God and in his image (Gen 1:27). He is quoting them as a point of contact, not endorsing their theology.
- **"We ought not to think that the divine being is like gold or silver or stone" (v29).** If people are God's offspring, God cannot be less than they are. A living father is not like a statue his children carved. Isaiah mocks idols the same way (Isa 44:9–20).
- **"The times of ignorance God overlooked" (v30).** "Overlooked" (*hyperoraō*, the King James "winked at") means God held back judgment. It does not mean he excused idolatry. Paul says the same in Romans 3:25: God "passed over former sins" in his patience. **"But now"** marks the turning point of history: Christ has come.
- **"He commands all people everywhere to repent" (v30).** This is not an invitation to add one more god to the shelf. It is a command to turn around. Philosophers and peasants, Jews and Greeks, all stand under it.
- **"A day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed" (v31).** Paul echoes Psalm 96:13 ("he will judge the world in righteousness"). The appointed man recalls the Son of Man who receives authority over all nations (Dan 7:13–14; John 5:27). Paul does not name Jesus here, though he had been preaching him in the agora (v18).
- **"He has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead" (v31).** "Assurance" is *pistis*, which here means proof, a pledge. The resurrection is God's public guarantee that judgment is coming and that Jesus is the Judge. This is where the Greek audience balked.
- **"Some mocked. But others said, 'We will hear you again'" (v32).** To Greeks, the body was at best a shell and at worst a prison for the soul. The idea of a dead man getting his body back sounded absurd. A line in the play *Eumenides* by Aeschylus, set at the founding of the Areopagus court itself, says that once a man dies there is "no resurrection." Paul calls the message of the cross "folly to Gentiles" (1 Cor 1:23).
- **"Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris" (v34).** Dionysius was a member of the council itself, a man of rank. Early tradition (Eusebius) says he became the first bishop of Athens. Writings in his name from about AD 500 were later shown to be by someone else. Damaris appears only here. That Luke names her at all suggests she was known to the churches.
- **Did Athens fail?** Some argue that Paul tried philosophy in Athens, saw few results, and so resolved in Corinth "to know nothing… except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Cor 2:2). Most commentators disagree. The sermon is full of Scripture's teaching, it ends with repentance, judgment and the resurrection, and Luke records real converts. Luke may give only a summary of a longer speech that said more about Christ.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the altar as "the God whom it is our unhappiness not to know," and on v24 writes, "A temple brings him never the nearer to us, nor keeps him ever the longer among us." On v31 he says God's raising Christ is "the great proof" that he is the appointed Judge. JFB prefers "in all respects extremely reverential" for v22, judging the King James wording a harsh opening that would repel his hearers. It identifies "we are his offspring" with Aratus, and explains "winked at" as God bearing with ignorance without punishing it. Guzik defends the sermon against the charge that Paul compromised or failed in Athens. He argues that the resurrection assumes the cross, and that the hearers' pride better explains the small response. He also notes that Paul quoted the poets to build a bridge, not to treat them as prophets.

#### Pointing to Christ
In the synagogue Paul shows from the Scriptures that the Messiah had to suffer and rise. On Mars Hill he starts from creation and an empty altar, and ends at the same place: a man God raised from the dead. Whether people have Bibles open like the Bereans or only an altar to an unknown god, every road in this chapter leads to Jesus, the King the mob feared, the Judge the philosophers mocked, and the living proof that God is not far from any of us.
