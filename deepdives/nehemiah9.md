*Setting:* Jerusalem, the twenty-fourth day of the seventh month, 445 BC, two days after the Feast of Booths ended (8:18). The reading of the Law in chapter 8 had brought tears, and the leaders had told the people to save their grief for another day, because a feast day was for joy. This is that day. The people gather again, this time in sackcloth and dust, to read the Law and confess. Most of the chapter is a single long prayer led by the Levites, one of the longest prayers in the Bible. It retells Israel's whole story, from creation to the Persian Empire, in a repeated pattern: God's goodness, Israel's rebellion and God's mercy. It belongs with other great prayers of confession from the same era (Ezra 9; Dan 9) and with history psalms such as Psalms 78, 105 and 106. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading, "The People of Israel Confess Their Sin." The subheadings after the first follow the prayer's own movements.

#### The People of Israel Confess Their Sin (vv1–5)
- **"With fasting and in sackcloth, and with earth on their heads" (v1).** These were the outward signs of mourning and repentance in Israel. Sackcloth was rough goat-hair cloth, the clothing of grief (Gen 37:34; Jonah 3:5–8). Dust or earth on the head pictured humiliation and loss (Josh 7:6; 1 Sam 4:12). The Day of Atonement had fallen on the tenth of the month (Lev 23:27), but it is not mentioned. This is a separate, voluntary day of confession.
- **"The Israelites separated themselves from all foreigners" (v2).** The Hebrew speaks of "the seed of Israel." This was not racial pride. Foreigners who joined themselves to the LORD were welcome in Israel (Isa 56:3–7; Ruth). The point is that Israel was confessing *Israel's* sins, its own covenant failures, so this was a family matter. The Tyndale notes connect it with the mixed marriages Ezra had confronted (Ezra 9–10), an issue that returns in 10:30 and 13:23–27.
- **"Confessed their sins and the iniquities of their fathers" (v2).** They did not blame their ancestors for their troubles while excusing themselves. They owned both. This follows the pattern God gave in Leviticus 26:40: "if they confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers," he would remember his covenant.
- **"For a quarter of the day… for another quarter of it they made confession" (v3).** The daylight hours were counted as twelve, so each quarter was about three hours. Three hours of Scripture, then three hours of confession and worship. The word led the response.
- **"On the stairs of the Levites" (v4).** This was probably a raised platform, like Ezra's in 8:4, so the leaders could be seen and heard. Two lists of eight Levites are given (vv4, 5), mostly the same men. Some names, such as Jeshua, Bani and Sherebiah, also appear among the teachers of 8:7.
- **"They cried with a loud voice to the LORD their God" (v4).** This is an earnest, public cry, not a quiet murmur.
- **"Stand up and bless the LORD your God from everlasting to everlasting" (v5).** The confession begins with praise. Before they talk about their sin, they speak about God. "From everlasting to everlasting" echoes the closing blessings of the Psalms' books (Ps 41:13; 106:48) and David's prayer (1 Chr 16:36). "Exalted above all blessing and praise" admits that even their best worship falls short of his name.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the people had feasted in joy at the right time, and now "There is a time to weep as well as a time to laugh." He assumes the Day of Atonement was kept even though the text does not mention it. He outlines the prayer as thanks for God's mercies, confession of sin, acceptance of his just judgments and a fresh promise to obey. JFB says that by "following the same evil ways" the people had made themselves "partakers of their fathers' sins," and he counts the quarters of the day as three hours each. He suggests the prayer "must have been prepared and adopted beforehand," perhaps by Ezra. Guzik sees public brokenness over sin as the first sign of revival, with Scripture leading the way. He rejects the idea of an inherited curse (citing Ezek 18), while granting that a family's sins can make the same sins easier to repeat.

#### The God Who Made and Chose (vv6–8)
- **"You are the LORD, you alone" (v6).** The prayer opens with the confession of Israel's faith, the *Shema*: "the LORD is one" (Deut 6:4). Babylonians and Persians worshiped many gods. The returnees confess that only one God made everything. The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) begins this verse with "And Ezra said," which is why many readers have taken Ezra to be the author. The Hebrew text does not have those words.
- **"Heaven, the heaven of heavens, with all their host" (v6).** "Heaven of heavens" means the highest heavens (Deut 10:14; 1 Kgs 8:27). "Host" can mean stars or angels. Here, since "the host of heaven worships you," it probably includes the angels. Many nations worshiped the stars. Here the stars and angels are worshipers, not gods.
- **"And you preserve all of them" (v6).** God did not only make the world. He keeps it alive moment by moment (compare Col 1:17; Heb 1:3).
- **"The God who chose Abram and brought him out of Ur of the Chaldeans" (v7).** "Gave him the name Abraham" recalls Genesis 17:5, where the new name means "father of a multitude." Ur was in southern Mesopotamia, in Babylonia. The returnees too had come out of the land of the Chaldeans. God had called their father out of the same region long before (Gen 11:31; 15:7).
- **"You found his heart faithful before you" (v8).** This echoes Genesis 15:6, "he believed the LORD," where the root of the word for "faithful" here is the same as "believed" (*'aman*). God made the covenant to give the land to his descendants (Gen 15:18–21). Six peoples of Canaan are named, as in many lists in the Law.
- **"And you have kept your promise, for you are righteous" (v8).** This line is the foundation of the whole prayer. God is righteous because he keeps his word. Everything that follows hangs on that.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the prayer opens with "a profound adoration of God." Guzik observes that God himself supplies the reasons for worship, beginning with creation, and that recalling his promise to Abraham reassures the people that he keeps his word. The Tyndale notes add that focusing first on who God is helped Israel see its present situation rightly.

#### Egypt, Sinai and the Wilderness (vv9–21)
- **"You saw the affliction of our fathers in Egypt and heard their cry at the Red Sea" (v9).** This echoes God's words to Moses: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people… and have heard their cry" (Exod 3:7). The God who saw then is the God they now ask to see their present hardship (v32).
- **"For you knew that they acted arrogantly" (v10).** Pharaoh's pride brought the plagues. "You made a name for yourself, as it is to this day": the Exodus made God's name known among the nations (Exod 9:16).
- **"As a stone into mighty waters" (v11).** This is a line from the Song of the Sea: "they went down into the depths like a stone" (Exod 15:5).
- **Pillar, Sinai, Sabbath, manna and water (vv12–15).** The prayer gathers the great gifts of the wilderness. God led them by the pillar of cloud and fire (Exod 13:21). He came down at Sinai and gave "right rules and true laws, good statutes." The Law is called good, not a burden. He "made known… your holy Sabbath," a reminder that the Sabbath was a gift before it was a duty (Exod 16:29; 20:8–11). He gave "bread from heaven" and "water… out of the rock" (Exod 16:4; 17:6).
- **"But they and our fathers acted presumptuously and stiffened their neck" (v16).** The turn comes. "Stiffened their neck" is a farming picture: an ox refusing the yoke. It is God's own description of Israel at the golden calf (Exod 32:9).
- **"Appointed a leader to return to their slavery in Egypt" (v17).** At Kadesh, after the spies' report, the people said, "Let us choose a leader and go back to Egypt" (Num 14:4). The Hebrew text here reads "in their rebellion" (*bemiryam*). A few Hebrew manuscripts and the Greek read "in Egypt" (*bemitsrayim*), a difference of one or two letters, and the ESV follows that reading, with a footnote. The meaning is much the same either way.
- **"But you are a God ready to forgive, gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" (v17).** This is the great self-description God gave Moses after the golden calf (Exod 34:6–7), one of the passages the Old Testament quotes most often (Num 14:18; Ps 86:15; 103:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2). "Ready to forgive" is literally "a God of forgivenesses." The word *selichot* is plural, which the lexicon calls a plural of abundance: abundant forgiveness. It appears elsewhere only in Daniel 9:9. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love.
- **"Even when they had made for themselves a golden calf" (v18).** This was the worst sin of the wilderness, committed at the foot of Sinai while the Law was being given (Exod 32). Yet "in your great mercies" God did not leave them (v19). The prayer repeats the description of the pillar from v12. The same guidance continued after the calf as before it.
- **"You gave your good Spirit to instruct them" (v20).** This probably recalls the Spirit placed on the seventy elders (Num 11:17, 25). Compare "let your good Spirit lead me" (Ps 143:10). Old Testament believers knew that God's Spirit taught and guided his people.
- **"Their clothes did not wear out and their feet did not swell" (v21).** Moses reminded Israel of this at the end of the forty years (Deut 8:4; 29:5). God's care reached even to sandals and sore feet.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the prayer sets Israel's sin against God's goodness, which makes the sin look darker, and that recognizing one's own sin is a mark of true revival. On "ready to pardon" he says the only thing that keeps anyone from forgiveness is refusing to come to God for it. He passes on Alan Redpath's observation that God's patience with his own people matches his mercy to sinners. JFB does not comment on these verses separately, treating them as part of the prayer's account of God's "forbearance and long-suffering" through Israel's "frequent rebellions."

#### The Land, the Judges and the Prophets (vv22–31)
- **"You gave them kingdoms and peoples and allotted to them every corner" (v22).** Sihon of Heshbon and Og of Bashan were the kings east of the Jordan defeated before Israel crossed into Canaan (Num 21:21–35). They were celebrated in Israel's songs (Ps 135:11; 136:19–20). "Every corner" may mean every region, or the borders of each tribe's allotment.
- **"Multiplied their children as the stars of heaven" (v23).** God's promise to Abraham (Gen 15:5; 22:17) came true.
- **"Houses full of all good things, cisterns already hewn, vineyards, olive orchards" (v25).** Almost word for word from Moses' promise and warning in Deuteronomy 6:10–12. Moses had said, "when you eat and are full, then take care lest you forget the LORD." They "ate and were filled and became fat." "Became fat" echoes the song of Moses: "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked… then he forsook God" (Deut 32:15). Blessing became the occasion for forgetting.
- **"Cast your law behind their back and killed your prophets" (v26).** "Behind their back" means deliberately refused to look at it (compare 1 Kgs 14:9). Israel's killing of prophets is recorded in 1 Kings 18:4; 19:10 and 2 Chronicles 24:20–22. Jesus would later name this pattern (Matt 23:29–37; Luke 11:47–51).
- **"You gave them saviors who saved them" (v27).** This is the cycle of the book of Judges in one verse: sin, oppression, crying out, deliverance (Judg 2:11–19; 3:9, 15). The judges are called "saviors" (*moshi'im*), from the same root as the name Joshua and, in Greek form, Jesus.
- **"Many times you delivered them" (v28).** The pattern did not happen once. It happened again and again. God's patience outlasted their relapses.
- **"Your rules, which if a person does them, he shall live by them" (v29).** This quotes Leviticus 18:5. God's law was meant to lead to life. Paul quotes the same verse to show the difference between living by law and living by faith (Rom 10:5; Gal 3:12). In the prayer, the point is that Israel turned away from the very path that would have kept it alive.
- **"Turned a stubborn shoulder" (v29).** Another ox picture: shrugging off the yoke (compare Zech 7:11, where the same phrase is used of the generation before the exile).
- **"Many years you bore with them and warned them by your Spirit through your prophets" (v30).** The prophets spoke by God's Spirit (compare Zech 7:12; 2 Pet 1:21). Hundreds of years of warnings passed before the exile came, first under Assyria and then Babylon.
- **"Nevertheless, in your great mercies you did not make an end of them" (v31).** Even the exile was not the end. The people praying this prayer are living proof. Their very presence in Jerusalem testifies that God is "a gracious and merciful God."
- **Commentators:** JFB says "corners" in v22 means the tribes' portions and calls Heshbon Sihon's "capital city." Guzik traces the cycle running through these verses: blessing, pride, rebellion, discipline, crying out, deliverance, rest, and relapse. He notes that the pattern grows worse with each turn, but that God never grows tired of hearts that turn back to him. The Tyndale notes add that the monarchy repeated the pattern of the judges, which 2 Kings 17 lays out.

#### Now, Therefore (vv32–38)
- **"Now, therefore, our God, the great, the mighty, and the awesome God, who keeps covenant and steadfast love" (v32).** These titles come from Deuteronomy 7:9 and 10:17 and from Nehemiah's own first prayer (1:5). "Now, therefore" turns from history to the present. The only petition in the whole prayer follows: "let not all the hardship seem little to you." They do not demand. They ask God to take their suffering seriously.
- **"Since the time of the kings of Assyria until this day" (v32).** Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom in 722 BC. Babylon followed, and now Persia. Almost three centuries of foreign rule are summed up in a line.
- **"Yet you have been righteous in all that has come upon us, for you have dealt faithfully and we have acted wickedly" (v33).** This is the heart of the confession. There is no complaint against God. Daniel prayed the same way (Dan 9:7, 14), as did Ezra (Ezra 9:15). Confession means agreeing with God about one's sin.
- **"Our kings, our princes, our priests, and our fathers" (v34).** Every class of leader is included. Prophets are mentioned in v32 among those who suffered, but not here among those who sinned, since the true prophets had warned the people.
- **"Even in their own kingdom, and amid your great goodness" (v35).** When Israel had its own king, its own land and plenty, it still did not serve God. Abundance did not produce faithfulness.
- **"Behold, we are slaves this day; in the land that you gave to our fathers" (v36).** This is a painful irony. They are home, and yet not free. The land God gave their fathers to enjoy now produces its harvest for the Persian king. "Slaves" here does not mean they were bought and sold. It means they were subjects who did not own their own future.
- **"They rule over our bodies and over our livestock as they please" (v37).** Persian kings demanded heavy taxes (compare 5:4) and could draft men and animals for war and public works. "We are in great distress." The prayer ends not with a neat resolution but with an honest admission of hardship.
- **"Because of all this we make a firm covenant in writing" (v38).** In Hebrew Bibles this verse is 10:1, the first line of the next chapter. "Firm covenant" is *amanah*, a sure or binding agreement, from the same root as "faithful" in v8. Abraham's heart was found faithful, and now his children make a faithful pledge. Their leaders seal it, and the names follow in chapter 10.
- **Commentators:** JFB says God's faithfulness "is prominently acknowledged," and since the people had broken the covenant they could only appeal to "the riches of divine grace." On v36 he notes that they were not, like their fathers, "free tenants of the land." He explains that their bodies could be "pressed" into the conqueror's service, and that the signed covenant would stand as "a witness against them" if they broke it. Guzik calls v33 the essence of confession: agreeing that God is right and we are wrong. He sees the written covenant as the fourth mark of revival, renewed obedience, so that repentance shapes conduct and does not remain a passing feeling. He also notes that this long prayer takes only a few minutes to read aloud, so a prayer's power does not lie in its length.

#### Pointing to Christ
The prayer is a long record of a faithless people and a faithful God. Again and again, God sent "saviors who saved them" (v27), and again and again the people fell back. The prayer ends with God's people still in distress, "slaves" in their own land, asking God not to treat their hardship as small. That ending is left open, waiting for an answer. The answer came in the Savior whose name means "the LORD saves," who did not only rescue Israel from its enemies but from its sins (Matt 1:21). He kept the law that gives life (v29) on behalf of those who could not, and in him the "God ready to forgive" (v17) has made forgiveness sure. Those who were slaves he makes children and heirs (Gal 4:4–7).
