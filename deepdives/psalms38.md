*Setting:* "A Psalm of David, for the memorial offering." The Hebrew title is one word, *lehazkir*, "to bring to remembrance." It appears again only over Psalm 70. Its exact meaning is uncertain. The ESV links it to the *azkarah*, the "memorial portion" of a grain offering that the priest burned on the altar (Lev 2:2). That word is also used of the handful of flour a very poor sinner could bring as a sin offering when he could not afford an animal (Lev 5:11–13), a fitting background for a psalm of confession. Others read it as a psalm meant to remind God of the sufferer, or to remind the sufferer of his sin. JFB puts it both ways: "remind God of His mercy and himself of his sin." No occasion is named, and suggestions (the aftermath of Bathsheba, or Absalom's revolt) are guesses. The church has long counted this among the seven penitential psalms (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143). It is one of the darkest songs in the Psalter. David is sick, in pain, guilty, abandoned by friends and hunted by enemies, and the psalm ends with no change in his circumstances, only a cry. Psalm 37 told the righteous to wait for the LORD. Psalm 38 shows what waiting looks like when the sufferer knows his own sin helped bring the trouble.

#### O LORD, Rebuke Me Not in Your Anger (vv1–8)
- **"O LORD, rebuke me not in your anger, nor discipline me in your wrath!" (v1).** This is almost word for word the opening of Psalm 6, another penitential psalm. David does not ask to escape discipline altogether. He asks not to be corrected in anger, the way a judge sentences a criminal, but as a father corrects a son (Prov 3:11–12; Heb 12:5–6). Jeremiah prays the same: "Correct me, O LORD, but in justice; not in your anger, lest you bring me to nothing" (Jer 10:24).
- **"For your arrows have sunk into me" (v2).** God is pictured as a warrior archer. The same image appears in Job's suffering ("the arrows of the Almighty are in me," Job 6:4) and in the curses of the covenant ("I will spend my arrows on them," Deut 32:23). JFB reads them as "the sharp and heavy afflictions he suffered." "Your hand has come down on me": the hand that should protect presses down instead (32:4, "your hand was heavy upon me").
- **"There is no soundness in my flesh… no health in my bones" (v3).** "Soundness" is wholeness, the same image Isaiah uses of sinful Israel: "From the sole of the foot even to the head, there is no soundness in it" (Isa 1:6). "Health" is literally *shalom*, peace. David's bones have no peace.
- **"Because of your indignation… because of my sin" (v3).** Two causes, side by side: God's anger and David's sin. He does not blame God unfairly or pretend the trouble is random. Scripture does not teach that every sickness comes from a person's own sin (John 9:2–3; the book of Job). But it does teach that some can (1 Cor 11:30; Jas 5:15–16), and David knows this one did.
- **"My iniquities have gone over my head" (v4).** Like floodwater rising above a drowning man (JFB: "as a flood"). Then the picture changes: "like a heavy burden, they are too heavy for me." Cain said almost the same: "My punishment is greater than I can bear" (Gen 4:13), where "punishment" is the same Hebrew word as "iniquity" here. Sin is a weight no one can carry alone. Jesus calls "all who labor and are heavy laden" to come to him (Matt 11:28).
- **"My wounds stink and fester because of my foolishness" (v5).** The picture is of untreated sores going bad. "Foolishness" (*'ivvelet*) in the Old Testament is not lack of intelligence but moral folly, choosing to go one's own way against God (Prov 1:7). Guzik, following Maclaren, notes how frankly the psalmist describes ugly bodily details. Prayer does not need to be polite.
- **"I am utterly bowed down and prostrate; all the day I go about mourning" (v6).** "Mourning" is literally "dark" or "dirty," as a mourner in black, unwashed, bent over. It is the same word as 35:14, where David bowed down "in mourning" for his enemies. Now he mourns for himself.
- **"For my sides are filled with burning" (v7).** "Sides" is *kesalim*, the loins, the muscles of the lower back near the kidneys. JFB: "The loins are the seat of strength." Some think of fever or kidney disease. Whether David was describing a literal illness, or using sickness as a picture of inner anguish, or both, is debated. Most commentators think there was real physical illness here.
- **"I am feeble and crushed; I groan because of the tumult of my heart" (v8).** "Groan" is literally "roar," the word for a lion's roar (KJV "I have roared"). Israel's "sweet psalmist" (2 Sam 23:1) now makes the sound of an animal in pain. God hears that kind of prayer too (Rom 8:26).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Nothing will disquiet the heart of a good man so much as the sense of God's anger," and "The guilt of sin is a burden to the whole creation, which groans under it." He links bodily suffering to sin in general: "If there were not sin in our souls, there would be no pain in our bones." Guzik notes that David admits he is being judged without accusing God, and passes on Spurgeon's picture of David as an anvil under two hammers, God's anger and his own sin. Guzik also follows Spurgeon in warning that this kind of terror is not a required stage of conversion. JFB treats the disease as "a figure" for mental anguish, though possibly with real illness behind it.

#### All My Longing Is Before You (vv9–14)
- **"O Lord, all my longing is before you; my sighing is not hidden from you" (v9).** The name changes from "LORD" (*Yahweh*) to "Lord" (*Adonai*, Master). David hides nothing. That is the opposite of Adam and Eve hiding among the trees (Gen 3:8), and the opposite of David's own silence before he confessed in Psalm 32:3–4. God knows even the sighs no one else hears.
- **"My heart throbs; my strength fails me" (v10).** "Throbs" is a rare intensive verb meaning to go round and round, the heart racing and pounding. "The light of my eyes… has gone from me": bright eyes in Hebrew are a sign of life and strength (1 Sam 14:27, where Jonathan's eyes "became bright" after eating honey). JFB: a sign of "utter exhaustion."
- **"My friends and companions stand aloof from my plague" (v11).** "Plague" is *nega'*, "stroke," a word used about sixty times in Leviticus 13–14 for the marks of leprosy and other skin disease. David's friends treat him like a leper, keeping their distance. Job knew this too (Job 19:13–19). Whether from fear of infection, embarrassment, or unwillingness to be seen with someone under God's hand, they leave him alone.
- **"My nearest kin stand far off" (v11).** At the cross, "all his acquaintances and the women who had followed him from Galilee stood at a distance watching these things" (Luke 23:49). The Greek of Luke's phrase is very close to the Septuagint of this verse.
- **"Those who seek my life lay their snares" (v12).** While friends pull back, enemies close in. They lay traps like bird-catchers and "meditate treachery all day long." JFB: "Friends desert, but foes increase in malignity." He points to 1 Samuel 20:1 and 22:23 for those who sought David's life.
- **"But I am like a deaf man; I do not hear, like a mute man who does not open his mouth" (vv13–14).** David does not answer back. "No rebukes" in v14 means no arguments or replies in his own defense. He may be too weak to answer, or he may know that with sin on his conscience he has no ground to stand on, or he may simply be leaving his case with God. When Shimei cursed David as he fled from Absalom, David refused to let his men silence him (2 Sam 16:5–14). This is the silence Isaiah gives the Servant: "like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls David "a type of Christ in his agonies, of Christ on his cross, suffering and deserted." He adds that our enemies "do us real mischief only when they drive us from God and our duty." Guzik, following Kidner, connects "plague" with the leprosy laws, which the lexicon supports. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, links the silence with Shimei, and Spurgeon with Jesus before Pilate. JFB: he "patiently submits, uttering no reproaches or replies," and cites John 19:9, where Jesus gave Pilate no answer.

#### For You, O LORD, Do I Wait (vv15–22)
- **"But for you, O LORD, do I wait; it is you, O Lord my God, who will answer" (v15).** The "but" turns the psalm. David is silent before his enemies (vv13–14) because he is waiting for God to speak. Three names for God appear in one line: *Yahweh* (the covenant name), *Adonai* (Master) and *Elohai* (my God). He does not *feel* answered. He says God *will* answer. That is faith speaking in the dark.
- **"Only let them not rejoice over me, who boast against me when my foot slips!" (v16).** David's concern is not only his own pain but that his fall would give God's enemies something to gloat over. Nathan told David after Bathsheba that his sin had given "occasion to the enemies of the LORD to blaspheme" (2 Sam 12:14).
- **"For I am ready to fall, and my pain is ever before me" (v17).** Literally "I am ready to limp" or "to stumble." He is on the edge of collapse. "Ever before me" is the language of Psalm 51:3: "my sin is ever before me."
- **"I confess my iniquity; I am sorry for my sin" (v18).** Here at last is the confession the whole psalm has been leading to. "Confess" means to declare or make known. Nothing is hidden. "I am sorry" is literally "I am anxious" or "troubled because of my sin." This is the godly grief Paul describes, which "produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret" (2 Cor 7:10). And confession is where God meets sinners: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us" (1 John 1:9).
- **"But my foes are vigorous, they are mighty" (v19).** The Hebrew word for "vigorous" is *chayyim*, "living" (KJV "lively"). JFB reads it as enemies "of life," out to take his. Many scholars suggest the original was *chinnam*, "without cause," which differs by one letter and would match "hate me wrongfully" in the next line, as in 35:19 and 69:4. Either way, David's enemies are strong and their hatred is unjust.
- **"Many are those who hate me wrongfully" (v19).** David is guilty before God, but he is not guilty before *them*. Both are true at once. Jesus applied the related words of Psalms 35:19 and 69:4 to himself: "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25).
- **"Those who render me evil for good accuse me because I follow after good" (v20).** "Accuse" is the verb *satan*, to act as an adversary. It is the root of the name Satan, "the accuser" (Zech 3:1; Rev 12:10). David's enemies play the devil's part, using his failure as a weapon against the good he still pursues. In the Hebrew Bible this is v21, because the title is counted as v1.
- **"Do not forsake me, O LORD! O my God, be not far from me!" (v21).** The worst fear is not the illness or the enemies but God's absence. The cry "be not far from me" echoes Psalm 22:11, 19, the psalm whose first line Jesus cried from the cross.
- **"Make haste to help me, O Lord, my salvation!" (v22).** The psalm ends with a plea, not a resolution. But the last word is "salvation." David calls the God whose arrows struck him (v2) "my salvation." He runs to the one who wounded him, because no one else can heal him (Hos 6:1: "he has torn us, that he may heal us").
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If we are truly penitent for sin, that will make us patient under affliction," and "The Lord will hasten to help those who trust in him as their salvation." JFB on v18: "Consciousness of sin makes suffering pungent, and suffering, rightly received, leads to confession." JFB also allows the psalm to be applied to Christ as the sufferer, but notes that this means the pains he bore, not the confessions of sin. Guzik, following Trapp, ties v20 to the name Satan, and the lexicon confirms that the verb is *satan*. Guzik stresses that the psalm ends with David's circumstances unchanged and his faith intact, and on v18 he passes on Spurgeon's point that sorrow for sin does not pay for it but is the right posture for turning to the Savior.

#### Pointing to Christ
JFB draws the right line. Jesus never prayed "I confess my iniquity," because he had none. But he bore everything else in this psalm. The arrows of God's wrath sank into him, not for his sin but for ours (Isa 53:5–6). His friends stood far off, his enemies plotted, and he was silent before his accusers. He was hated without a cause. On the cross he cried out the fear of v21, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46), so that sinners who pray this psalm need never be forsaken. Because he carried the burden "too heavy for me" (v4), the poor sinner who brings only a handful of confession can call God "my salvation."
