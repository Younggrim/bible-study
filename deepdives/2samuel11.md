*Setting:* Around 990 BC, perhaps a year after the events of chapter 10. David is at the height of his power. God has given him rest, a covenant promise of an everlasting house (ch 7) and victory on every side (chs 8, 10). The Ammonites insulted his envoys and lost their Syrian allies, but their capital, Rabbah (modern Amman), still stands. This chapter is the turning point of David's story. Before it, almost everything goes right for him. After it, the rest of 2 Samuel tells of trouble inside his own family and kingdom: rape, murder, rebellion and civil war. The writer tells the story with very little comment. God is not mentioned until the last line, and then that line says everything.

#### David and Bathsheba (vv1–27)
- **"In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle" (v1).** Winter rains made roads impassable and armies hungry, so campaigns began in spring after the harvest of barley. The Hebrew words for "kings" and "messengers" differ by one silent letter. The standard Hebrew text is written "messengers" but was traditionally read aloud as "kings," and the ESV follows that reading. The point is sharp either way: it is the season when kings go to war, and this king does not.
- **"But David remained at Jerusalem" (v1).** The verse lists who went: Joab, his servants, "all Israel." Then comes one clause that stands alone. In chapter 10 the decisive victory came when David led the army himself (10:17). The Tyndale notes observe that this is the first time a leader of Israel is said to stay off the battlefield in time of war. Whether he was lazy or simply trusted Joab, the text puts him in the wrong place.
- **"Late one afternoon, when David arose from his couch" (v2).** Literally "at the time of evening." A midday rest in the heat was normal. The picture is of a king with time on his hands, strolling on the flat roof of the palace, which stood on the high ground of the city of David and looked down over the houses below.
- **"He saw from the roof a woman bathing; and the woman was very beautiful" (v2).** Seeing her was not yet sin. What he does next is. The story moves in quick verbs that recall the first sin in Eden, where Eve "saw… took… gave" (Gen 3:6). David saw, sent and took. Jesus later taught that the look that keeps looking with desire is already adultery in the heart (Matt 5:28).
- **"Is not this Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Uriah the Hittite?" (v3).** The answer is a warning in itself. She is married. An Eliam son of Ahithophel is listed among David's mighty men (23:34), and if he is Bathsheba's father, as many think, her grandfather is Ahithophel, David's own counselor, who later joins Absalom's revolt against David (15:12). **Uriah** is one of "the Thirty," David's elite warriors (23:39). His name means "the LORD is my light," which suggests a family that had come to worship Israel's God, whatever his Hittite ancestry. The Hittites were an old people of the land (Gen 23), and some had served David since his outlaw days (1 Sam 26:6).
- **"So David sent messengers and took her" (v4).** "Took" (*lāqaḥ*) is the word Samuel used when he warned what a king would do: "he will take your sons… he will take your daughters… he will take your fields" (1 Sam 8:11–17). Nathan's parable will turn on the same verb: the rich man "took the poor man's lamb" (12:4). The king who was meant to shepherd his people is now using his power to take.
- **"She came to him, and he lay with her" (v4).** How willing was Bathsheba? The text does not say, and readers differ. She makes no recorded protest, unlike Tamar in 13:12–13. But she was summoned by the king, through his messengers, while her husband was at the front, and a subject was hardly free to refuse. The Tyndale notes lay out both sides and point out that the narrator, and Nathan in the next chapter, place the blame on David. Some older commentators assume she bathed where she knew she would be seen, but the text never suggests it. She was in her own home, and it was the king who was looking down.
- **"Now she had been purifying herself from her uncleanness" (v4).** This is the washing after her monthly period required by the law (Lev 15:19–28). The detail is there for a reason: it proves she was not already pregnant by Uriah. There is also an irony. She was carefully keeping the law of ritual cleanness at the very moment the king was breaking the law against adultery (Exod 20:14; Lev 20:10).
- **"I am pregnant" (v5).** Her only words in the chapter are two words in Hebrew. The law named death for both adulterer and adulteress (Lev 20:10). She sends the problem back to the man who caused it.
- **"Send me Uriah the Hittite" (v6).** Now the verb "send" (*shālaḥ*) takes over the chapter. David sends Joab (v1), sends to ask (v3), sends messengers (v4); Bathsheba sends (v5); David sends to Joab, Joab sends Uriah (v6); David sends a letter (v14); Joab sends a report (v18); and at the end David "sent and brought her to his house" (v27). The king who would not go is busy sending. In 12:1, "the LORD sent Nathan," and at last someone sends for David.
- **"David asked how Joab was doing and how the people were doing and how the war was going" (v7).** Three times in Hebrew the question is about *shālôm*, "peace" or "welfare." The king is making small talk about the war he should be leading, to cover what he has done.
- **"Go down to your house and wash your feet" (v8).** On the surface it means "go home and rest." Many think "feet" here is also a polite euphemism, as it sometimes is in Hebrew, so David is plainly hinting that Uriah should sleep with his wife. The "present from the king" was probably food from the royal table, a mark of honor that was also meant to set the mood.
- **"Uriah slept at the door of the king's house" (v9).** He sleeps with the palace guard. Whether he suspected anything, the text does not say. Henry sensibly asks why, if he suspected, he did not open the letter he later carried.
- **"The ark and Israel and Judah dwell in booths" (v11).** "Booths" is *sukkôt*, rough shelters of branches, the same word as the Feast of Booths. The ark may have gone with the army, as it did at times (1 Sam 4:3–4), or Uriah may mean its tent in Jerusalem. Either way, he will not take his comfort while God's people and God's ark are in the field. Israelite soldiers kept themselves from sexual relations on campaign, since the camp was to be holy (Deut 23:9–14; 1 Sam 21:4–5). The foreigner keeps Israel's law; the king of Israel breaks it.
- **"As you live, and as your soul lives, I will not do this thing" (v11).** Uriah's oath is by the king's own life. Every word he speaks is an unknowing rebuke to David.
- **"He made him drunk" (v13).** Plan two. David hopes wine will do what honor would not. Even drunk, Uriah is more faithful than the sober king: "he did not go down to his house."
- **"Sent it by the hand of Uriah" (v14).** Plan three. David writes Uriah's death warrant and has Uriah carry it. He trusts Uriah's loyalty so completely that he knows he will not read it. That trust is the cruellest detail in the story.
- **"Set Uriah in the forefront of the hardest fighting, and then draw back from him" (v15).** David once refused to lift his hand against Saul, his enemy, because he was the LORD's anointed (1 Sam 24:6; 26:9–11). Now he uses the Ammonites' swords to kill his own loyal soldier, so that his hands look clean.
- **"Some of the servants of David among the people fell. Uriah the Hittite also died" (v17).** Joab adapts the plan. A move that exposed Uriah alone would be obvious, so he sends a group near the wall, and others die too. David's cover-up costs more lives than one.
- **"Who killed Abimelech the son of Jerubbesheth?" (v21).** Joab expects David to be angry at a basic military mistake, going close to the wall, and he quotes the textbook example. Abimelech, Gideon's son, who made himself king, was killed at Thebez when a woman dropped a millstone on his head from a tower (Judg 9:50–54). "Jerubbesheth" is Jerubbaal, Gideon's other name, with *baal* replaced by *bosheth*, "shame," as in Ish-bosheth. The choice of story is grimly apt: a king brought low by a woman.
- **"Your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also" (vv21, 24).** Joab knows exactly what this news will do to the king's anger. Now Joab holds David's secret, and David will find it very hard to rein him in from here on (compare 3:39; 18:5, 14; 1 Kings 2:5–6).
- **"Do not let this matter displease you, for the sword devours now one and now another" (v25).** Literally, "Do not let this thing be evil in your eyes." David brushes off the deaths with a soldier's shrug. The phrase will come back in v27.
- **"She lamented over her husband" (v26).** The usual mourning period was seven days (Gen 50:10; 1 Sam 31:13). Bathsheba truly mourns. The text calls her "the wife of Uriah," not yet David's wife. Matthew's genealogy of Jesus will still call her that, "the wife of Uriah" (Matt 1:6).
- **"She became his wife and bore him a son" (v27).** To anyone watching, David has done a generous thing, marrying the widow of a fallen officer. The cover-up has worked. No one knows. Except one.
- **"But the thing that David had done displeased the LORD" (v27).** Literally, "the thing that David had done was evil in the eyes of the LORD." David told Joab not to let it be evil in *his* eyes (v25). The narrator answers: it was evil in God's eyes. This is the first time God is named in the chapter. He has been silent the whole time, and he has seen everything. Years later the summary of David's life in 1 Kings 15:5 still names this as the one great stain: "except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite."
- **Commentators:** All three see a steep downhill road. Matthew Henry lists the "occasions" of the sin: neglect of duty, idleness and a wandering eye. "When we are out of the way of our duty we are in the way of temptation." Guzik agrees that staying home gave the opportunity, but he argues the deeper root was older: years of taking more wives had already trained David to indulge his desires. Guzik goes further than the text by saying Bathsheba almost certainly bathed immodestly. Henry assumes she "too easily" consented. JFB pictures her as an ambitious woman. The Tyndale notes are more careful, setting out the evidence both ways and noting that the text blames only David. That is the safer reading. On Uriah, Henry calls his refusal "a generous resolution," and both he and Guzik doubt Uriah suspected anything. Henry on the letter: David made Uriah carry his own death sentence, and asks, "Is this the man whose heart smote him because he had cut off Saul's skirt?" JFB sees in the whole affair "the awful lengths to which the best of men may go." Guzik points to Psalm 32:3–4 for what the hidden year did to David inside: his bones wasted away under God's heavy hand.

#### Pointing to Christ
David, the anointed king, abuses his power, takes what is not his, and spends another man's life to protect himself. Christ, the true Son of David, does the opposite. He uses his power to give, and he lays down his own life for others (Mark 10:45; John 10:11). Uriah, the loyal soldier who would not take his ease while the army was in the field, faintly pictures the faithfulness that David lacked and that Jesus has in full. The chapter also shows how sin spreads when it is hidden: from a look to adultery, from adultery to lies, from lies to murder. The only way out is the one David will find in the next chapter, confessing the sin to the God who already sees it. Matthew names "the wife of Uriah" in Jesus' family line (Matt 1:6), so even this dark story is carried into the line of the Savior who came to deal with sins like these.
