*Setting:* Galilee, around AD 28–29. Chapter 9 ended with Jesus moved with compassion for crowds "like sheep without a shepherd" and telling his disciples to pray for laborers in the harvest (9:36–38). In chapter 10 the disciples become the answer to that prayer. This is the second of the five great teaching blocks in Matthew, often called the Mission Discourse. It begins with instructions for a short preaching tour of Galilean towns, but as it goes on it looks much further ahead, to governors, kings, Gentiles, family betrayal and death. Many scholars think Matthew has gathered sayings Jesus gave on more than one occasion, since some appear in other settings in Mark and Luke. The whole speaks to every generation of disciples sent into a hostile world.

#### The Twelve Apostles (vv1–4)
- **"His twelve disciples" (v1).** The number is deliberate. Israel had twelve tribes, and Jesus is forming the core of a renewed people of God. He later says the Twelve will sit on twelve thrones judging the tribes of Israel (19:28), and after Judas fell, the church replaced him to keep the number whole (Acts 1:15–26).
- **"Gave them authority" (v1).** The same authority Jesus has shown in chapters 8–9, over unclean spirits and every disease, he now shares. He does not just send them. He equips them.
- **"Apostles" (v2).** This is the only time Matthew uses the word. *Apostolos* means "one who is sent," an envoy who carries the authority of the sender. Jewish law had a similar idea: a man's agent is as the man himself. That is the point of v40.
- **The list (vv2–4).** The New Testament gives four lists (Mark 3:16–19; Luke 6:14–16; Acts 1:13). They fall into three groups of four, and each group always starts with the same name: Peter, Philip and James son of Alphaeus. Peter always comes first, and Matthew even says "first." Judas always comes last.
- **The men.** Two pairs of fishing brothers. Bartholomew, probably Nathanael (John 1:45–49), since John never names Bartholomew and the other Gospels never name Nathanael. Thomas, later known for doubting and then for the great confession "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28). "Matthew the tax collector," a label only this Gospel adds. Thaddaeus, who appears in Luke's lists as "Judas the son of James," likely the same man with two names. These are ordinary men, mostly from Galilee, none of them from the religious elite (Acts 4:13; 1 Cor 1:26–29).
- **"Simon the Zealot" (v4).** "Zealot" may mean a member of the movement that later led armed revolt against Rome, or simply a man zealous for the law. Either way, a man like Simon and a tax collector who had worked for Rome's client ruler were on opposite ends of Jewish politics. Jesus put them side by side.
- **"Judas Iscariot, who betrayed him" (v4).** "Iscariot" most likely means "man of Kerioth," a town in southern Judea (Josh 15:25), which would make him the only apostle not from Galilee. Other suggestions exist. Matthew mentions the betrayal at the very first mention of his name.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Peter is named first "because he was first called," and argues this gives him no authority over the others. JFB marks the note on Judas as "a note of infamy attached to his name," and with Guzik identifies Bartholomew with Nathanael. Guzik stresses that whom God calls he equips: the Twelve received power along with the call.

#### Jesus Sends Out the Twelve Apostles (vv5–15)
- **"Go nowhere among the Gentiles… but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (vv5–6).** Matthew alone records this limit, which Jesus applies to himself as well (15:24). It is a matter of order, not of exclusion. God's promises came to Israel first (Rom 1:16), and through Israel they would reach the nations (Isa 49:6). After the resurrection the limit is lifted: "make disciples of all nations" (28:19). Samaritans were descendants of northern Israelites mixed with peoples settled by Assyria (2 Kings 17:24–41), with their own temple and version of the Torah. Jews and Samaritans had been bitter rivals for centuries.
- **"Lost sheep" (v6).** This picks up the "sheep without a shepherd" of 9:36. Israel's leaders had failed them (Ezek 34:4–6).
- **"The kingdom of heaven is at hand" (v7).** This is the same message John and Jesus preached (3:2; 4:17).
- **"Heal the sick, raise the dead, cleanse lepers, cast out demons" (v8).** The list matches what Jesus has just done in chapters 8–9. The disciples' works were to show the same kingdom at work.
- **"You received without paying; give without pay" (v8).** Healing and the message of the kingdom came to them as a free gift. They must not sell them. Peter later rebuked Simon the magician for trying to buy the Spirit's power (Acts 8:18–20).
- **"Acquire no gold or silver or copper for your belts" (v9).** Money was often carried in a fold of the belt. "Acquire" (*ktaomai*) means "get" or "procure": they are not to raise funds for the trip in advance. They were to go light and depend on God and on the hospitality of those who received them.
- **"No bag… or two tunics or sandals or a staff" (v10).** Mark says they may take a staff and sandals (Mark 6:8–9). One common explanation is that Matthew's verb "acquire" forbids getting *extra* gear, while Mark allows what they already had. Either way, the point is the same: travel light and trust.
- **"The laborer deserves his food" (v10).** Paul quotes this as a principle that those who preach should be supported (1 Tim 5:18; 1 Cor 9:14). Free giving and fair support are not in conflict.
- **"Find out who is worthy in it and stay there" (v11).** They were not to shop around for better lodging. A "worthy" household was one open to God and to his messengers.
- **"Let your peace come upon it… let your peace return to you" (v13).** A greeting was "Peace" (*shalom*), and it was understood as a real blessing (Ruth 2:4), not just "hello." The disciples' peace was an offer of the kingdom. If refused, it came back to them like an undelivered letter.
- **"Shake off the dust from your feet" (v14).** Jews returning from Gentile lands sometimes shook off the dust so as not to carry the uncleanness home. For the apostles to do this in a Jewish town was a startling sign: rejecting God's messengers put that town in the position of outsiders. Paul and Barnabas did it at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:51).
- **"More bearable… for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah than for that town" (v15).** Sodom became the byword for wickedness and judgment (Gen 19). Jesus says a town that rejects the kingdom when it is offered face to face is guiltier than Sodom, because it had more light (11:23–24).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the limit to Israel applied "only in their first mission," and Guzik agrees that it was about order, Jew first and then Gentile. JFB calls "freely ye have received, freely give" a "Divine saying, divinely said!" and notes that Paul applies the laborer's wage to the support of ministers. Guzik uses the same verse to warn against preaching for profit, and explains the staff question by the difference in verbs between Matthew and Mark.

#### Persecution Will Come (vv16–25)
- **"Sheep in the midst of wolves" (v16).** The picture is alarming: the shepherd sends his sheep into a pack of wolves, on purpose. Their safety will not come from strength but from his care.
- **"Wise as serpents and innocent as doves" (v16).** The serpent was a byword for cunning (Gen 3:1). Doves were thought of as gentle and harmless. "Innocent" (*akeraios*) means "unmixed," pure, without hidden motives. Jesus wants disciples who are shrewd about danger but have nothing crooked in them. One without the other fails: shrewdness alone becomes scheming, and innocence alone becomes naivety.
- **"Courts… synagogues… governors and kings" (vv17–18).** Local Jewish councils could order flogging, up to thirty-nine lashes (Deut 25:3), and Paul received it five times (2 Cor 11:24). Governors and kings point beyond Galilee: Paul stood before Felix, Festus and King Agrippa (Acts 24–26). The goal is "to bear witness before them and the Gentiles." Persecution itself becomes a platform for the gospel.
- **"What you are to say will be given to you in that hour" (v19).** This is a promise for trials, not an excuse to skip preparing a sermon. "The Spirit of your Father speaking through you" (v20) is one of the few mentions of the Holy Spirit in Matthew outside the birth, baptism and Great Commission. Acts shows it happening (Acts 4:8–13; 6:10).
- **"Brother will deliver brother over to death" (v21).** The words echo Micah 7:6, which Jesus quotes in v35. Faith in Christ would split families, and in times of persecution relatives sometimes turned believers in.
- **"The one who endures to the end will be saved" (v22).** This is not salvation earned by endurance. It describes what real faith does: it lasts. Jesus repeats the line in 24:13.
- **"Flee to the next" (v23).** Disciples are not to court martyrdom. When one door closes, they go to the next town and keep preaching, as the church did when scattered from Jerusalem (Acts 8:1–4).
- **"Before the Son of Man comes" (v23).** This is one of the hardest verses in Matthew. Main views: (1) a coming in judgment on Jerusalem in AD 70, which many see as the most natural sense; (2) the resurrection and the giving of the Spirit; (3) the second coming, meaning the mission to Israel will not be finished before he returns. Most agree that Jesus is not mistakenly predicting the end of the world within a few weeks, since he says in 24:36 that no one knows the day.
- **"A disciple is not above his teacher" (v24).** A rabbi's student aimed to become like his teacher. That includes sharing his treatment.
- **"If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul" (v25).** Beelzebul was the name of a Canaanite god, probably meaning "Baal the prince." Jews twisted it in mockery, perhaps to "lord of dung," and the old Latin form "Beelzebub" reflects "lord of flies" (2 Kings 1:2). By Jesus' day it was a name for Satan. The Pharisees have already hinted at this charge (9:34), and they will make it openly (12:24). There may be a pun: Hebrew *zebul* can mean "lofty dwelling" (1 Kings 8:13), so *Baal-zebul* could be heard as "lord of the house," and Jesus calls himself "master of the house."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls for "a dove-like spirit, made for love, not for war," and says the serpent's policy is "only to defend themselves." Guzik reads "flee" as a warning against seeking martyrdom, and holds the AD 70 view of v23. Henry takes v23 instead as Christ's exaltation and the outpouring of the Spirit. JFB also connects the coming with judgment on Israel, and says "Beelzebul" was "the most opprobrious language which could be applied by one to another."

#### Have No Fear (vv26–33)
- **"So have no fear of them" (v26).** "Do not fear" comes three times in this section (vv26, 28, 31). The command is not to feel no fear, but not to let fear of people silence the message.
- **"Nothing is covered that will not be revealed" (v26).** In its setting this is a promise. The truth the disciples carry, though now small and opposed, will be made known, and so will the motives of their enemies.
- **"What you hear whispered, proclaim on the housetops" (v27).** Houses had flat roofs, which were natural places from which to make announcements. Jesus teaches the Twelve privately, but the message is meant for everyone. There is no secret inner teaching for an elite.
- **"Fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (v28).** The worst persecutors can do is kill the body. God alone has power over the whole person forever. "Hell" is *Gehenna*, the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, where children were once sacrificed to idols (Jer 7:31) and which became a picture of final judgment. The verse also shows that the soul is not destroyed at death, and that the final judgment involves the whole person. The right fear of God drives out the wrong fear of people (Isa 8:12–13).
- **"Two sparrows sold for a penny" (v29).** Sparrows were the cheapest birds in the market, food for the poor. The "penny" is the *assarion*, a small copper coin worth about a sixteenth of a day's wage. Luke says five sparrows sold for two pennies (Luke 12:6), so it seems a fifth was thrown in free when two pennies' worth was bought. Not even the bird thrown in for nothing is forgotten by God.
- **"Not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father" (v29).** Even a sparrow's death is within the Father's knowledge and will. That does not mean disciples will never suffer. It means suffering never happens outside his care.
- **"Even the hairs of your head are all numbered" (v30).** God knows his children in more detail than they know themselves.
- **"Everyone who acknowledges me before men, I also will acknowledge before my Father" (v32).** Jesus makes himself the one whose verdict counts on the last day. The idea of a secret disciple who never owns him is ruled out. The verse describes a life's direction, not a single moment. Peter denied Jesus three times (26:69–75) and was restored (John 21:15–19), while Judas, who confessed him outwardly, betrayed him.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "death brings the highest to the earth" and that God "takes more care of them, than they do of themselves." JFB calls v28 "a decisive proof this that there is a hell for the body as well as the soul," a reading that goes a step beyond the text, which speaks of God's power to destroy, not of how hell works. Guzik says that if fear silences the gospel, the enemy has won even if no one is harmed, and that the message is meant to be public, with no hidden inner circle.

#### Not Peace, but a Sword (vv34–39)
- **"I have not come to bring peace, but a sword" (v34).** This sounds strange from the "Prince of Peace" (Isa 9:6), who blessed peacemakers (5:9). Jesus does bring peace with God (Rom 5:1) and his own peace to his people (John 14:27). But he does not bring the false peace of everyone agreeing. A decision about him divides. Luke's parallel uses the word "division" (Luke 12:51). The sword here is not a weapon for disciples to use (26:52). It is the line Jesus draws through the world, and sometimes through a family.
- **"A man against his father, and a daughter against her mother" (v35).** Jesus quotes Micah 7:6, where the prophet laments a society so corrupt that "a man's enemies are the men of his own house." Jesus applies the words to the division his coming causes.
- **"Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me" (v37).** The law commanded honoring parents (Exod 20:12), and Jesus upholds that (15:4). But he claims a love above it. No rabbi or prophet claimed the right to be loved more than one's parents. Only God could. Luke puts it in stronger Semitic terms, "hate" (Luke 14:26), meaning to love less by comparison (Gen 29:30–31).
- **"Whoever does not take his cross and follow me" (v38).** This is the first time the cross is mentioned in Matthew. Everyone in Roman Palestine knew what it meant: a condemned man carried the crossbeam to his own execution. Taking up a cross was not a picture of a minor hardship but of going out to die. Jesus says this before he has openly told his disciples that he himself will be crucified (16:21).
- **"Whoever finds his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (v39).** "Life" (*psychē*) means one's self, one's life. The one who clings to self-preservation at all costs loses what really matters. The one who lets go for Jesus' sake finds true life. Jesus repeats this saying more often than almost any other (16:25; Mark 8:35; Luke 9:24; 17:33; John 12:25).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the sword "deadly opposition between eternally hostile principles," and says the cross means "a preparedness to go forth even to crucifixion," noting that Jesus had not yet spoken of his own death. Guzik observes how remarkable it is that Jesus ties eternal destiny to how people respond to him, and that taking up the cross means death to self, not just putting up with inconvenience. Henry sums up the section in Jesus' words: "Look not for peace, but a sword."

#### Rewards (vv40–42)
- **"Whoever receives you receives me, and whoever receives me receives him who sent me" (v40).** This is the principle behind "apostle." To welcome Christ's messenger is to welcome Christ, and to welcome Christ is to welcome the Father. The discourse that warned of rejection ends with a promise to those who receive.
- **"A prophet's reward… a righteous person's reward" (v41).** Those who help God's servants share in their work and their reward. Old Testament examples include the widow of Zarephath, who fed Elijah (1 Kings 17:8–24), and the Shunammite woman, who built a room for Elisha (2 Kings 4:8–37).
- **"Whoever gives one of these little ones even a cup of cold water because he is a disciple" (v42).** "Little ones" means Jesus' followers, small and insignificant in the world's eyes (18:6, 10, 14). Cold water in a hot land was a real kindness, but it costs almost nothing. Even that is seen and rewarded. Later Jesus says that what is done for the least of his brothers is done for him (25:40).
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "Kindnesses shown to Christ's disciples are valued in Christ's books." JFB adds that "Predicting future events was no necessary part of a prophet's office," so a prophet means a spokesman for God. Guzik, following R.T. France, says the reward comes when someone is received *because* he is a disciple, out of love for Christ rather than general goodwill.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is about the sent ones, but behind them is the Sender. Jesus has authority to give authority, to send his messengers as God sent the prophets, and to stand as the one whose acknowledgment decides a person's eternal destiny. He asks for a love higher than love for parents and children, which only God may ask. And the path he lays out for them is his own: sheep among wolves, slandered as Beelzebul, handed over to governors, carrying a cross. He went first. Those who lose their lives for his sake find them, because he laid down his life and took it up again (John 10:17–18). Even a cup of cold water given in his name is not forgotten by the Father who numbers every hair.
