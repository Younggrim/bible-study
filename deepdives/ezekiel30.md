*Setting:* Babylonia. This chapter holds two of Ezekiel's seven messages against Egypt. The first (vv1–19) is undated. It expands the judgment announced in 29:1–16 into a poem about "the day of the LORD" coming on Egypt, naming its allies and its great cities one after another. The second (vv20–26) is dated the seventh day of the first month of the eleventh year, about April 587 BC, a few months before Jerusalem fell. By then an Egyptian army under Pharaoh Hophra had marched out to relieve Jerusalem and had been driven back (Jer 37:5–11). Many in Judah still hoped Egypt would try again. God's word through Ezekiel shut that door: Pharaoh's arm was broken, and it would not heal.

#### A Lament for Egypt (vv1–19)
- **"Wail, 'Alas for the day!'" (v2).** The poem opens with a cry of mourning. The ESV heading calls it a lament. It is really a warning cry, raised before the disaster arrives.
- **"The day of the LORD is near; it will be a day of clouds, a time of doom for the nations" (v3).** "The day of the LORD" is the prophets' phrase for a time when God steps into history to judge. Amos warned Israel that it would be "darkness, and not light" (Amos 5:18–20). Joel and Zephaniah describe it as "a day of clouds and thick darkness" (Joel 2:2; Zeph 1:15). Ezekiel had applied it to Jerusalem (7:7–12; 13:5). Now Egypt's turn comes. "A time of doom for the nations" is literally "the time of the nations." Many interpreters see Egypt's day as one of a series of such days in history, each pointing ahead to a final day of the Lord.
- **The cloud reversed.** When Israel left Egypt, God led them by a pillar of cloud and stood between them and Pharaoh's army as darkness to the Egyptians (Exod 14:19–20). Now a "day of clouds" comes on Egypt itself.
- **"A sword shall come upon Egypt, and anguish shall be in Cush" (v4).** Cush was the land south of Egypt (Nubia, in modern Sudan). Its kings had ruled Egypt as its 25th dynasty about a century earlier. "Anguish" is the word for writhing in labor pains. **"Her foundations are torn down"** may mean the physical foundations of cities, or the allies and supports on which Egypt rested.
- **"Cush, and Put, and Lud, and all Arabia, and Libya, and the people of the land that is in league" (v5).** These are Egypt's allies and mercenaries. Put is Libya or part of it, and Lud may be Lydia in western Turkey, whose soldiers served Egypt in this period (compare 27:10; Jer 46:9). "All Arabia" may also be read "all the mixed people," the foreign troops in Egypt's army (Jer 25:20). "Libya" translates *Kub*, a name found nowhere else and of uncertain meaning. The Greek translation reads "Libyans." **"The people of the land that is in league"** is literally "the sons of the land of the covenant." Some think it means Egypt's allies generally. Others, Henry among them, think it refers to the Jews who had fled to Egypt against God's word (Jer 42–44), the people of the covenant land who chose to stand with Egypt. Jeremiah had warned them that the sword they fled would follow them there (Jer 42:15–17).
- **"Those who support Egypt shall fall, and her proud might shall come down" (v6).** "Proud might" is literally "the pride of her strength." **"From Migdol to Syene"** again means the whole land from north to south (29:10).
- **"When I have set fire to Egypt, and all her helpers are broken" (v8).** Fire is a common picture of war. "Then they will know that I am the LORD" appears again, here and in vv19, 25, 26. The repeated refrain is the point of the chapter. The Egyptians who once asked, "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?" (Exod 5:2) will learn the answer.
- **"Messengers shall go out from me in ships to terrify the unsuspecting people of Cush" (v9).** Isaiah once spoke of Cush sending "ambassadors by the sea, in vessels of papyrus" (Isa 18:2). Now the boats go the other way, carrying news south up the Nile that Egypt has fallen. Cush had thought itself safe behind Egypt and beyond the cataracts. The news terrifies it.
- **"I will put an end to the wealth of Egypt, by the hand of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon" (v10).** "Wealth" (*hamon*) can also mean "multitude" or "noisy crowd." The word runs through chapters 29–32, describing Egypt's vast population, army and riches (31:2; 32:12, 16). **"He and his people with him, the most ruthless of nations" (v11)** repeats the description of Babylon from 28:7.
- **"I will dry up the Nile" (v12).** "Nile" here is plural, "the Nile channels," the canals that carried water from the river to the fields. If the Nile's yearly flood failed, the canals ran dry and famine followed. Isaiah used the same threat: "the waters of the sea will be dried up, and the river will be dry and parched" (Isa 19:5–10). Pharaoh had claimed, "My Nile is my own" (29:3). God shows who controls it. **"And will sell the land into the hand of evildoers."** God sells, as an owner sells property. The land is his to give.
- **"I will destroy the idols and put an end to the images in Memphis" (v13).** Memphis (Hebrew *Noph*), near modern Cairo, was the ancient capital and a great religious center, home of the god Ptah and the sacred Apis bull. "Idols" (*gillulim*) is Ezekiel's favorite contemptuous word for false gods. It probably comes from a root meaning "dung balls." The plagues of the exodus had been a judgment "on all the gods of Egypt" (Exod 12:12). This is another. **"There shall no longer be a prince from the land of Egypt."** After the Persian conquest in 525 BC, Egypt was ruled almost entirely by foreigners for over two thousand years.
- **The tour of Egypt's cities (vv13–18).** Counting Memphis, Ezekiel names seven cities and the region of Pathros in six verses, moving back and forth from the delta to the far south to show that no part of the land will be spared.
- **"Pathros… Zoan… Thebes" (v14).** Pathros is Upper Egypt, the south (29:14). Zoan (Tanis) was a royal city in the northeastern delta, near where Israel had lived in Goshen (Ps 78:12, 43). Thebes (Hebrew *No*, "city," short for "city of Amun") was the great southern capital, with the huge temples at Karnak and Luxor. Nahum had already pointed to its fall to Assyria in 663 BC as a warning (Nah 3:8–10).
- **"I will pour out my wrath on Pelusium, the stronghold of Egypt" (v15).** Pelusium (Hebrew *Sin*) was the frontier fortress at Egypt's northeastern gate, guarding the road from Asia. Herodotus tells how the Persian king Cambyses took it in 525 BC, by one later story placing cats and other animals sacred to the Egyptians in front of his troops so the defenders would not shoot.
- **"Thebes shall be breached, and Memphis shall face enemies by day" (v16).** Attackers will come openly, in daylight, not by stealth. The Hebrew is difficult, and JFB discusses two possible readings.
- **"The young men of On and of Pi-beseth shall fall by the sword" (v17).** On (Greek Heliopolis, "city of the sun") was the center of sun worship. Joseph married the daughter of its priest (Gen 41:45). The Hebrew vowels here read *Awen*, which means "wickedness" or "nothing," a deliberate pun on the city's name, like Hosea's "Beth-aven" ("house of wickedness") for Bethel (Hos 4:15). Pi-beseth (Bubastis) in the delta was the city of the cat goddess Bastet.
- **"At Tehaphnehes the day shall be dark" (v18).** Tahpanhes (Greek Daphnae) was a royal fortress on the northeastern border. It was there that the Jews who fled after Jerusalem's fall took Jeremiah, and there he hid stones in the pavement of Pharaoh's palace and announced that Nebuchadnezzar would set his throne over them (Jer 43:7–13). "The day shall be dark" recalls the plague of darkness over Egypt (Exod 10:21–23). **"When I break there the yoke bars of Egypt."** Egypt had placed its yoke on other nations, once on Israel itself. God will break it.
- **"Thus I will execute judgments on Egypt. Then they will know that I am the LORD" (v19).** The list ends where the whole oracle aims.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes on v5, "Those that will take their lot with God's enemies shall have their lot with them, yea, though they be in profession the men of the land that is in league with God," which shows he reads the phrase as Jews in Egypt. He adds, "God often makes one wicked man a scourge to another," and "God can strike a terror upon those that are most secure." JFB identifies each city carefully: Noph as Memphis, the stronghold of idols; No as Thebes; Sin as Pelusium, "the key of Egypt"; Aven as On or Heliopolis, renamed "by a slight change of the Hebrew name… in allusion to its idolatry"; Tehaphnehes as Daphne. He notes there is no record of Nebuchadnezzar destroying the idols of Memphis, but that the Persian king Cambyses later did great damage there. Guzik, citing Block, notes how audacious the whole oracle was: an exile from a small, ruined kingdom announcing that his God would bring down the oldest power on earth. He also connects the destruction of Egypt's idols with the plagues of the exodus.

#### Egypt Shall Fall to Babylon (vv20–26)
- **"In the eleventh year, in the first month, on the seventh day of the month" (v20).** About April 587 BC, roughly three months after 29:1 and about three months before Jerusalem fell (2 Kings 25:2–4).
- **"I have broken the arm of Pharaoh king of Egypt" (v21).** The arm is the Bible's picture of strength in action. God brought Israel out of Egypt "with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm" (Deut 26:8). Egyptian art often shows Pharaoh with arm raised, striking his enemies with a sword or mace, and some scholars note that Hophra used a royal title meaning "possessor of a strong arm." "I have broken" is past tense. Most commentators connect it with Hophra's failed attempt to rescue Jerusalem a few months earlier (Jer 37:5–7). Henry takes it back further, to Egypt's defeat by Babylon at Carchemish in 605 BC (Jer 46:2), from which it never recovered.
- **"It has not been bound up, to heal it by binding it with a bandage" (v21).** The arm is not set or splinted. It will never be strong enough to "wield the sword" again. Judah's hope that Egypt would recover and come back was false.
- **"I will break his arms, both the strong arm and the one that was broken" (v22).** God goes further. Both arms will be broken. **"And I will make the sword fall from his hand."** Pharaoh will be helpless.
- **"I will strengthen the arms of the king of Babylon and put my sword in his hand" (v24).** Two kings, two pairs of arms. One is broken by God, the other strengthened by God. The sword in Nebuchadnezzar's hand is "my sword," the sword of chapter 21. Babylon wins because God gives it the victory, not because of its own power. **"He will groan before him like a man mortally wounded."**
- **"Then they shall know that I am the LORD, when I put my sword into the hand of the king of Babylon" (v25).** The refrain comes twice more in two verses.
- **"I will scatter the Egyptians among the nations" (vv23, 26).** The same words are said twice, repeating 29:12. Egypt will share the exile it could not prevent for Judah.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that the arm was broken when Babylon "routed the forces of Pharaoh-Necho at Carchemish," that "it received that fatal blow which it never recovered," and that "before Egypt's heart and neck were broken its arm was." JFB connects the broken arm with Hophra's defeat when trying to raise the siege of Jerusalem, and stresses that the meaning is "not a corporal wound, but a breaking of the power of Pharaoh." Guzik, citing Block, notes that "groan" is literally "groan groanings," and that the image implies Pharaoh himself would die. The Tyndale notes add that Nebuchadnezzar fought Egypt on several occasions, finishing with his campaign of 567 BC.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is full of arms: Pharaoh's broken arm, Babylon's strengthened arms, and behind both the arm of the LORD. Judah's mistake was to trust the wrong arm. Jeremiah put it plainly: "Cursed is the man who trusts in man and makes flesh his strength" (Jer 17:5). Isaiah asked, "To whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" and answered with the Servant who was "despised and rejected," "pierced for our transgressions," and by whose wounds we are healed (Isa 53:1, 3, 5). John quotes that verse of Jesus (John 12:38). The arm of the LORD that broke Egypt's power to set Israel free is finally revealed in a crucified man, whose hands were pierced and whose arms were stretched out on a cross. Unlike Pharaoh's arm, his wounds were not the end. They were healed in resurrection, and he still bears them as the risen Lord who saves (John 20:27).
