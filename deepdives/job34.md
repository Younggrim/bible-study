*Setting:* This is Elihu's second speech. In chapter 33 he spoke directly to Job and offered real hope: God speaks through dreams and through pain to rescue people from the pit, and there may be a mediator who finds a ransom. He ended by inviting Job to answer, and Job stayed silent. Now Elihu turns to "you wise men" (v2), the friends and perhaps others listening, and makes the case one of God's justice. Job had said God "has taken away my right" (27:2). Elihu's answer is the most forceful defense of God's justice in the book: God cannot do wrong, he owes his rule to no one, he holds every breath of life in his hand, he judges kings without partiality, and he sees everything. All of that is true. But as the chapter goes on, Elihu's tone hardens. He twists Job's words, lumps him with the wicked, and ends wishing that Job's trials would go on "to the end." The gentle counselor of chapter 33 sounds more like the friends he criticized.

#### Elihu Asserts God's Justice (vv1–9)
- **"Hear my words, you wise men" (v2).** Elihu addresses the circle, maybe with a touch of irony since he has already said the friends failed (32:12). He wants a jury: "Let us choose what is right; let us know among ourselves what is good" (v4).
- **"For the ear tests words as the palate tastes food" (v3).** This is a proverb Job himself used (12:11). Just as the tongue can tell good food from bad, a listening mind can tell sound words from rotten ones. Elihu turns Job's saying back on him.
- **"For Job has said, 'I am in the right, and God has taken away my right'" (v5).** This is close to Job's words: "I know that I shall be in the right" (13:18) and "As God lives, who has taken away my right" (27:2). It is a fair summary of Job's complaint that God was treating him unjustly.
- **"My wound is incurable, though I am without transgression" (v6).** "Wound" is literally "arrow." Job had said, "the arrows of the Almighty are in me" (6:4; compare 16:13). Job felt God was shooting at an innocent man.
- **"What man is like Job, who drinks up scoffing like water" (v7).** The friends had said hard things, but none of them quite this. Eliphaz used a similar picture of man "who drinks injustice like water" (15:16). Elihu means Job soaks up scorn against God the way a thirsty man gulps water. JFB pictures a camel drinking.
- **"Who travels in company with evildoers" (v8).** This is unfair. Elihu does not mean Job literally kept bad company, which would contradict everything known about him (1:1; 29:12–17). He means that Job's words about God put him on the side of the godless. In his view, to talk like the wicked is to walk with them.
- **"It profits a man nothing that he should take delight in God" (v9).** Here Elihu misquotes Job outright. Job never said this. He said the *wicked* talk this way: "What is the Almighty, that we should serve him? And what profit do we get if we pray to him?" (21:15), and then added, "The counsel of the wicked is far from me" (21:16). Job had said that God seems to destroy the blameless and the wicked alike (9:22), and Elihu draws out what he thinks that implies. The Tyndale notes say plainly that Elihu misrepresents Job here. He repeats the charge in 35:3.
- **Commentators:** Guzik is direct: Elihu quotes Job selectively and unfairly. Job never claimed to be sinless or said that delighting in God was useless. He only denied that some hidden sin explained his catastrophe. Guzik passes on one scholar's observation that the "drinks up scoffing" line is harsher than anything the three friends said. Henry notes that Elihu "bespeaks not only the audience, but the assistance of the company," asking the wise to judge with him. JFB reads v8 not as literal company but as Job adopting the views of the ungodly, by denying God's justice because God's reasons are hidden.

#### Elihu Asserts God's Justice (vv10–15)
- **"Far be it from God that he should do wickedness" (v10).** This is the heart of the chapter, and it is true. Abraham said it: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Gen 18:25). Moses sang it: "all his ways are justice... just and upright is he" (Deut 32:4). Paul answers the same question: "Is there injustice on God's part? By no means!" (Rom 9:14).
- **"According to the work of a man he will repay him" (v11).** Also true, and taught all through Scripture (Ps 62:12; Prov 24:12; Rom 2:6; Rev 22:12). The question is *when*. The friends assumed God settles every account in this life, so suffering proves sin. The Bible looks to a final judgment where all is put right (2 Cor 5:10). Elihu does not clearly make that distinction here.
- **"The Almighty will not pervert justice" (v12).** Bildad had asked the same thing in nearly the same words: "Does God pervert justice?" (8:3). Elihu is restating the friends' foundation, which was never wrong in itself. Their mistake was in applying it to Job.
- **"Who gave him charge over the earth?" (v13).** No one appointed God. He is not a governor who answers to a higher authority. The world is his because he made it. Elihu's point is that a God who owns everything has no motive to cheat anyone.
- **"If he should... gather to himself his spirit and his breath, all flesh would perish together" (vv14–15).** Every living creature breathes on borrowed breath. If God simply drew it back, all life would end and "man would return to dust," the sentence spoken over Adam (Gen 3:19; compare Ps 104:29–30). The fact that God keeps giving life every moment shows his goodness, not his malice. This is one of the most beautiful lines in Elihu, and it prepares for God's own speeches about his care for all creatures (chs 38–39).
- **Commentators:** Henry puts the principle simply: God "neither can nor will do a wrong thing, nor deal hardly with any man." On vv14–15 he writes, "He can soon bring us to dust; there needs not any positive act of his omnipotence to do it." He only has to stop giving. JFB calls v10 "the true answer to Job," noting that God's own speech in chapter 38 follows the same line: God's ways are right because they are his. JFB passes on Umbreit's reading of vv14–15 that God's constant sustaining of life shows he is not selfish. Guzik agrees that God is just but warns against turning "you reap what you sow" into an iron law of instant results. Applied rigidly, it would condemn everyone, and it was exactly the friends' error. He passes on Andersen's point that Elihu is trapped in the friends' logic: once every question is treated as impiety, Job must be labeled impious.

#### Elihu Asserts God's Justice (vv16–20)
- **"If you have understanding, hear this" (v16).** The "you" is now singular. Elihu turns from the group to Job.
- **"Shall one who hates justice govern?" (v17).** If the ruler of the universe were unjust, there could be no moral order at all. Even human rulers are expected to uphold justice. How much more God. God will later ask Job a similar question: "Will you even put me in the wrong? Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" (40:8).
- **"Who says to a king, 'Worthless one,' and to nobles, 'Wicked man'" (v18).** God judges the most powerful people on earth, and calls them what they are. A judge like that is not afraid of anyone and has no reason to bend justice.
- **"Who shows no partiality to princes, nor regards the rich more than the poor, for they are all the work of his hands" (v19).** Impartiality is one of Scripture's great themes: "God shows no partiality" (Acts 10:34; Rom 2:11; Deut 10:17). Rich and poor were made by the same hands (Prov 22:2). Job had defended the poor (29:12–16), so he would agree.
- **"In a moment they die; at midnight the people are shaken and pass away" (v20).** The mighty fall suddenly. "At midnight" recalls the night God struck Egypt's firstborn, from Pharaoh's house down (Exod 12:29), and Belshazzar killed the night of his feast (Dan 5:30). "By no human hand" means God needs no army. He just acts.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains "govern" as literally "bind," holding authority together, and reads v20 as a night attack on a camp, the mighty removed "without hand," with no visible human cause. Guzik observes that Elihu takes Job's laments as accusations against God, when in fact Job's anguish came from his love for God and his conviction that God is just. That is why Job was so troubled. He passes on G. Campbell Morgan's verdict on this whole section: everything Elihu says is true, but it does not explain Job's suffering. It is not the whole truth.

#### Elihu Asserts God's Justice (vv21–30)
- **"His eyes are on the ways of a man, and he sees all his steps" (v21).** Job had said almost the same thing as a complaint (31:4: "Does not he see my ways and number all my steps?"). Elihu says it as a comfort for justice: nothing is missed.
- **"There is no gloom or deep darkness where evildoers may hide" (v22).** "Deep darkness" is *tsalmaveth*, the word in "the valley of the shadow of death" (Ps 23:4). Darkness is as light to God (Ps 139:11–12).
- **"God has no need to consider a man further, that he should go before God in judgment" (v23).** Job had longed for a day in court with God (13:3; 23:3–7) and asked why God does not set times of judgment (24:1). Elihu answers that God does not need a trial. He already knows everything. That is true, but it sidesteps Job's real longing, which was not for God to learn the facts but for God to speak.
- **"He shatters the mighty without investigation and sets others in their place" (v24).** Human courts need witnesses and evidence. God does not. He raises and removes rulers (Dan 2:21).
- **"He strikes them for their wickedness in a place for all to see" (v26).** Public judgment on public evil, so that others learn (Deut 21:21). The reason is given in vv27–28: they turned from God and oppressed the poor, "so that they caused the cry of the poor to come to him, and he heard the cry of the afflicted." God hears the poor, as he heard Israel crying in Egypt (Exod 3:7) and as Abel's blood cried from the ground (Gen 4:10).
- **"When he is quiet, who can condemn? When he hides his face, who can behold him?" (v29).** This is Elihu's answer to Job's most painful complaint, God's silence and hiddenness (13:24; 23:8–9). If God chooses to be quiet, no one has the right to condemn him for it. Whether he acts or waits, toward a nation or a single person, he is still in charge. The Hebrew of vv29–30 is hard, and translations differ. The ESV sets v29 off with dashes as an aside.
- **"That a godless man should not reign, that he should not ensnare the people" (v30).** God's judgments on tyrants protect ordinary people from rulers who trap them in oppression or lead them into sin.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "in the night" (v25) as "suddenly," and v26 as an open, public execution because the wicked had hidden in darkness. Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's story of a Scottish persecutor who backed down when a poor woman told him she would take her case to God in prayer, an illustration of God as the one who hears the cry of the afflicted. Guzik also notes Andersen's warning about Elihu's logic: if whatever happens is by definition God's just judgment, evil disappears as a category, and Job's suffering must be deserved. The book's opening chapters show that is not so.

#### Elihu Asserts God's Justice (vv31–37)
- **"Has anyone said to God, 'I have borne punishment; I will not offend any more'?" (v31).** Elihu puts words in Job's mouth: this is what a sufferer *should* say. "Teach me what I do not see; if I have done iniquity, I will do it no more" (v32). In itself it is a beautiful prayer of humility (Ps 139:23–24). Job will finally pray something like it in 42:4–6, but in response to God's voice, not as a confession of hidden sin.
- **"Will he then make repayment to suit you, because you reject it?" (v33).** Does Job think God should run the world on Job's terms? "You must choose, and not I": Elihu puts the decision squarely on Job. JFB passes on two scholars' different readings of this very difficult verse, a sign of how unclear the Hebrew is.
- **"Job speaks without knowledge; his words are without insight" (v35).** Elihu claims the wise will back him. God will later say something similar to Job: "Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge?" (38:2). So Elihu is not entirely wrong. But God also says Job spoke rightly of him, unlike the friends (42:7).
- **"Would that Job were tried to the end" (v36).** This is the hardest line in the chapter. Elihu wishes Job's testing would continue until it finished its work, "because he answers like wicked men." The word for "would that" is unusual. Some, including JFB's margin, take it as "my father," Elihu calling on God. Most take it as a word of wishing. Either way, it is a brutal thing to say to a man sitting in ashes who has lost his children. Zophar had said something like it (11:6).
- **"He adds rebellion to his sin; he claps his hands among us" (v37).** Clapping hands was a gesture of scorn (27:23; Lam 2:15). Elihu accuses Job of mocking God and piling up words against him. The man who promised in 33:7 that his pressure would not be heavy now presses as hard as anyone.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v31 as the model of true repentance: "What I suffer comes justly upon me, and therefore I will bear it," and "it is not enough to be sorry for our sins, but we must go and sin no more." On v32: "Lord, give me to see it, awaken my conscience." Henry takes v36 seriously as Elihu's real wish, "My desire is that Job may be tried unto the end," and adds generously that Job "not only bore patiently, but took kindly" this rebuke, "because he saw that Elihu meant well." JFB reads "tried" as tested by calamity until the trial reaches its goal, and clapping as a sign of scorn toward God. Guzik is the most critical. He grants that Elihu means well but says his compassion runs out here, and he passes on Adam Clarke's judgment that the wish in v36 is "very harsh" and the chapter nearly without mildness. Job 1–2 shows a blameless man, but Elihu sees sin piling on sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elihu is right that God cannot do wrong, that he sees every step, that he hears the cry of the poor, and that he holds every breath in his hand. He is wrong to conclude that Job's suffering must be the proof of Job's sin. The cross shows both truths at once. Jesus was the one truly "without transgression" (v6), yet he was "counted among the transgressors" (Isa 53:12; Luke 22:37) and "tried to the end." He suffered not for his own sin but for ours, so that God could be "just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:26). God did not pervert justice at Calvary. He satisfied it. And the prayer Elihu wanted to hear, "teach me what I do not see," is answered by the Spirit Christ sends, who guides his people "into all the truth" (John 16:13).
