*Setting:* Psalm 108 is titled "A Song. A Psalm of David." It is built almost entirely from two earlier psalms of David. Verses 1–5 are the closing praise of Psalm 57:7–11, written when David hid from Saul in a cave. Verses 6–13 are the second half of Psalm 60:5–12, written after a hard war with Edom, when Joab struck down twelve thousand Edomites in the Valley of Salt (Ps 60 title; 2 Sam 8:13–14). What is left out is as telling as what is kept. Psalm 57 began with David surrounded by lions. Psalm 60 began, "O God, you have rejected us, broken our defenses." Psalm 108 drops both laments and joins the two confident halves into one song of praise and trust. Whether David himself made the combination or later temple musicians did is not known. Its placement right after Psalm 107, which celebrated the exiles' return, suggests it was sung by the restored community as a prayer that God would again give his people victory and secure their land. Henry draws a practical lesson: it teaches "that we may in prayer use the same words that we have formerly used, provided it be with new affections." The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement from praise to prayer.

#### My Heart Is Steadfast, O God! (vv1–4)
- **"My heart is steadfast, O God!" (v1).** "Steadfast" (*nakon*) means firm, fixed, settled. In Psalm 57 David said this while hiding in a cave with Saul's army hunting him. His circumstances were not steady, but his heart was, because it was fixed on God. Psalm 112:7 uses the same word of the righteous man: "his heart is firm, trusting in the LORD." Psalm 57:7 repeats the phrase twice, and Psalm 108 says it once.
- **"I will sing and make melody with all my being!" (v1).** "With all my being" is literally "even my glory." In the Psalms "my glory" can mean the soul or inner self, the noblest part of a person (Ps 16:9; 30:12). Psalm 57:8 has "Awake, my glory!" as a separate line. David means to praise God with everything he has, including the musical gift that first brought him to Saul's court (1 Sam 16:18).
- **"Awake, O harp and lyre! I will awake the dawn!" (v2).** The harp (*nevel*) and lyre (*kinnor*) were stringed instruments used in temple worship. The *kinnor* is the instrument David played for Saul. Usually the dawn wakes people up. David says he will wake the dawn, getting up before the sun to begin praising. It is a bold, joyful picture.
- **"I will give thanks to you, O LORD, among the peoples" (v3).** Psalm 57:9 has "O Lord" (*Adonai*). Psalm 108 uses the covenant name *Yahweh*. David's praise is meant to be heard by the nations. Paul quotes a similar verse to show that God always planned for the Gentiles to praise him (Rom 15:9, quoting Ps 18:49).
- **"For your steadfast love is great above the heavens; your faithfulness reaches to the clouds" (v4).** Psalm 57:10 reads "great *to* the heavens." Psalm 108 says "*above* the heavens," going one step further. God's *chesed*, his loyal covenant love, and his faithfulness are bigger than the sky. Psalm 103:11 uses the same measure: "as high as the heavens are above the earth."
- **Commentators:** Henry says David "gives thanks to God for mercies to himself," and that the psalm "begins with praise and concludes with prayer, and faith is at work in both." He adds that "it is not only allowable, but sometimes convenient, to gather some verses out of one psalm and some out of another." JFB says the differences from the earlier psalms are "verbal and trivial" and that the whole has an "altogether triumphant tone" because the laments were left out. Guzik reads "my glory" as the best of David's being, including his musical skill, and stresses that praise here is public, before the nations, not hidden.

#### Be Exalted, O God, Above the Heavens! (vv5–6)
- **"Be exalted, O God, above the heavens! Let your glory be over all the earth!" (v5).** This was the refrain of Psalm 57 (vv5, 11). David's deepest desire is not first his own rescue but God's glory filling the earth. This is the same order as the Lord's Prayer: "Hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come" comes before "give us… deliver us" (Matt 6:9–13).
- **The join (vv5–6).** Verse 5 is the last line taken from Psalm 57, and verse 6 is the first line from Psalm 60:5. The seam is where praise turns to prayer. The psalm moves from celebrating God's greatness to asking him to use it.
- **"That your beloved ones may be delivered" (v6).** "Beloved ones" (*yedidim*) is a tender word. It comes from the same root as the name David (*Dawid*, "beloved"), and as *Jedidiah*, "beloved of the LORD," the name God gave Solomon (2 Sam 12:25). God's people are not merely his subjects. They are his loved ones.
- **"Give salvation by your right hand and answer me!" (v6).** The right hand is the hand of strength and skill. God saved Israel at the Red Sea with his right hand (Exod 15:6, 12). Psalm 108 has "answer me." In Psalm 60:5 the written Hebrew reads "answer us," though the scribes' marginal reading there is "me." The prayer is both personal and national.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in the second half David "prays to God for mercies for the land, pleading the promises of God and putting them in suit," like a man presenting a legal claim. Guzik links "beloved" (*yadid*) to the meaning of David's own name and cites Kidner on the language of love poetry used here. He notes that the right hand stands for God's skill and strength.

#### God Has Promised in His Holiness (vv7–9)
- **"God has promised in his holiness" (v7).** Literally "God has spoken in his holiness," or "in his sanctuary." Either way it is God's own word, backed by his holy character. What follows is a divine speech, perhaps an oracle given through a prophet or priest. God speaks like a warrior-king dividing up his territory.
- **"With exultation I will divide up Shechem and portion out the Valley of Succoth" (v7).** Shechem lay west of the Jordan in the hill country of Ephraim, and Succoth lay east of it. Jacob camped at Succoth and then settled near Shechem when he returned to the land (Gen 33:17–18). Naming them together claims both sides of the Jordan. Dividing up land is what a conqueror does after victory.
- **"Gilead is mine; Manasseh is mine" (v8).** Gilead was the region east of the Jordan, settled by Gad, Reuben and half of Manasseh. The other half of Manasseh was west of the river. Again God claims the whole land, east and west.
- **"Ephraim is my helmet, Judah my scepter" (v8).** The two largest tribes get the highest roles. Ephraim, the strongest tribe in the north, is God's helmet, his defence and strength in battle. Judah, the tribe of David, is his scepter. Jacob had promised, "The scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor the ruler's staff from between his feet" (Gen 49:10). "Scepter" here (*mechoqeq*) is the same word as Genesis 49:10's "ruler's staff," and can also mean "lawgiver," as the KJV has it.
- **"Moab is my washbasin" (v9).** Now the surrounding enemies. Moab, east of the Dead Sea, is reduced to a basin for washing feet, a servant's task. It is a picture of humiliation, since Moab was famous for its pride (Isa 16:6; Jer 48:29).
- **"Upon Edom I cast my shoe" (v9).** Edom lay south and southeast of the Dead Sea. Throwing a shoe onto a piece of land may have been a way of claiming it as one's own, as a sandal sealed a property transfer in Ruth 4:7. Or it may be an act of contempt, tossing a dirty sandal to a slave to clean. Either way Edom becomes God's possession and servant.
- **"Over Philistia I shout in triumph" (v9).** Philistia lay along the southern coast. Psalm 60:8 reads differently, something like "over me, O Philistia, shout," which is hard to interpret. Psalm 108 makes it clear: God shouts in triumph over Philistia. JFB notes that this variant helps explain the older text.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads Ephraim as the helmet because of its large population and strength, citing Calvin, and Judah as the scepter because of its link to David and the Messiah. He notes that David did in fact defeat Moab, Edom and Philistia (2 Sam 8:1–2, 14), and cites Isaiah 16:6 and Obadiah 3 on Moab's and Edom's pride. JFB says Psalm 108:9's reading supports one interpretation of the obscure line in Psalm 60:8.

#### With God We Shall Do Valiantly (vv10–13)
- **"Who will bring me to the fortified city? Who will lead me to Edom?" (v10).** Now the speaker is David, or the king, again. God has promised Edom, but Edom is a fortress. Its capital cities, Bozrah and Sela, sat in rugged mountains, and Sela (probably near later Petra) was famous as an eagle's nest (Obad 3–4; Jer 49:16). Who can lead an army into that?
- **"Have you not rejected us, O God? You do not go out, O God, with our armies" (v11).** This is the one note of lament the psalm keeps from Psalm 60. Israel had suffered a recent setback, perhaps in the war with Edom (Ps 60:1–3, 10). The king does not hide it. But notice he brings even this complaint to God. The God who seems to have rejected them is still the only one they can ask.
- **"Oh grant us help against the foe, for vain is the salvation of man!" (v12).** "Vain" (*shav'*) means empty, worthless. Alliances, mercenaries and human strategy cannot win this war. Israel was repeatedly tempted to trust Egypt or Assyria instead of God, and the prophets warned against it (Isa 31:1; Jer 17:5). Psalm 146:3 says the same: "Put not your trust in princes, in a son of man, in whom there is no salvation."
- **"With God we shall do valiantly; it is he who will tread down our foes" (v13).** The psalm ends with confidence, not complaint. Notice both halves. "We shall do valiantly": Israel's army will really fight. "It is he who will tread down our foes": God gives the victory. Faith is not passivity. It fights while trusting God for the outcome, as Nehemiah's builders worked with a trowel in one hand and a weapon in the other while saying "Our God will fight for us" (Neh 4:17–20).
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes the "fortified city" most likely as Petra, Edom's mountain stronghold, though Scripture does not record David taking it, and suggests it may stand for any seemingly impossible obstacle. He stresses that faith does not mean passivity: Israel was to fight, but "through God," and he believes the battle was won. Henry closes, "In singing this psalm we must give glory to God and take comfort to ourselves." JFB says the psalm as a whole commemorates God's favor in his people's victories.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 108 sets God's promise beside a fortified city no human army can take. The New Testament shows that the greatest stronghold God's people faced was not Edom but sin, death and the powers of evil. Jesus, the Son of David from the tribe that holds the scepter (Gen 49:10; Rev 5:5), went into that fortress alone and broke it open by his death and resurrection, "disarming the rulers and authorities" and triumphing over them (Col 2:15). He is the beloved one, the *yadid* of the Father ("This is my beloved Son," Matt 3:17), and through him those who belong to him become God's beloved too. The psalm's last line holds both halves of the Christian life: believers are called to "do valiantly," to fight the good fight (1 Tim 6:12), and yet it is God who "will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Rom 16:20).
