*Setting:* Judah, about 735–715 BC. The Chronicler, writing for the Jews back from exile in Babylon, has just shown Jotham, a king who "ordered his ways before the LORD" and grew strong (27:6). His son Ahaz is the opposite, perhaps the worst king Judah had until his grandson Manasseh. Assyria under Tiglath-pileser III is swallowing the small kingdoms of the west. Pekah of Israel and Rezin of Syria (Damascus) have joined forces to resist Assyria, and when Ahaz refuses to join them, they attack Judah. This is the crisis of Isaiah 7, when the prophet meets Ahaz with the promise of Immanuel and Ahaz refuses to trust God. Kings tells the story in 2 Kings 16, with more detail on Ahaz's new altar from Damascus. Chronicles adds two things Kings leaves out: the heavy defeats in battle, and a remarkable scene in Samaria where the northern army sends its captives home. Within a few years of this chapter, the northern kingdom itself would be carried into exile (722 BC), though the Chronicler does not stop to tell it.

#### Ahaz Reigns in Judah (vv1–4)
- **"Ahaz was twenty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned sixteen years" (v1).** The Tyndale notes date his sole reign 731–715 BC, after some years sharing the throne with Jotham. His full name in Assyrian records is Jehoahaz, "the LORD has grasped." The Bible always shortens it to Ahaz, as if the LORD's name had been dropped from it.
- **"He did not do what was right in the eyes of the LORD, as his father David had done" (v1).** Every king of Judah is measured against David. Ahaz is one of the very few in Chronicles given no good word at all. Henry notes he had every advantage: a godly father, a good upbringing, prophets like Isaiah and Micah preaching in his own city.
- **"He walked in the ways of the kings of Israel" (v2).** This is a new low. Judah's kings had sinned before, but this verdict usually belongs to the northern kingdom, which had broken away from David's line and the Jerusalem temple. Ahaz copies the very kingdom that is attacking him. **"He even made metal images for the Baals."** Baal was the Canaanite storm and fertility god. The plural means the many local forms of Baal worship.
- **"He made offerings in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom and burned his sons as an offering" (v3).** The Valley of Hinnom runs along the south and west of Jerusalem. Here children were sacrificed in fire, usually linked with the god Molech (Lev 18:21; 20:2–5; Jer 7:31; 32:35). The law calls this one of the chief "abominations of the nations" that God drove out of the land (Deut 12:31; 18:10). Kings says he "made his son pass through the fire" (2 Kgs 16:3). Chronicles says "burned" and "sons," leaving no room to soften it. Later rabbinic tradition pictured a bronze idol heated red-hot with drums beaten to drown out the cries. How much of that detail is accurate is uncertain, but the sacrifice itself is not.
- **From Hinnom to Gehenna.** Because of what happened in this valley, its name, *Ge Hinnom* (Greek *Geenna*, Gehenna), became the Jewish word for the place of final judgment. Jesus uses it for hell (Mark 9:43–48). Ahaz turned a valley outside his capital into the picture of hell.
- **"On the high places and on the hills and under every green tree" (v4).** This is the standard phrase for Canaanite-style worship at hilltop shrines and sacred groves (Deut 12:2; Hos 4:13). The idea was that fertility gods lived in such places. Ahaz worshipped everywhere except where God had placed his name.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Ahaz "early betrayed a strong bias to idolatry" and ruled "not as a theocratic sovereign," that is, not as the LORD's deputy. He traces the slide from the north's calf worship to "the gross idolatry practised by the Canaanites." Henry observes that "did not that which was right" is an understatement for so much evil. Guzik ranks Ahaz among Judah's very worst kings and points out that he had good examples in both Jotham and David and rejected them. He traces the history of Molech worship from Solomon's toleration (1 Kgs 11:7) to Josiah's ending it in the valley (2 Kgs 23:10).

#### Judah Defeated (vv5–21)
- **"Therefore the LORD his God gave him into the hand of the king of Syria" (v5).** "Therefore" is the Chronicler's hallmark: sin leads to judgment. Note "the LORD *his* God." Ahaz had turned his back on God, but God had not stopped being his God, or his judge. Syria carried many captives to Damascus.
- **The Syro-Ephraimite war.** Kings tells how Rezin and Pekah marched on Jerusalem itself and besieged it but could not take it (2 Kgs 16:5). Isaiah says they meant to replace Ahaz with a puppet king, "the son of Tabeel" (Isa 7:6). That would have ended David's line. God sent Isaiah to tell Ahaz their plan "shall not stand" (Isa 7:7). Chronicles records the battles in the field. Both accounts fit together: heavy defeats in the countryside, Jerusalem spared.
- **"Pekah the son of Remaliah killed 120,000 from Judah in one day, all of them men of valor" (v6).** "One day" stresses how sudden and total the disaster was. As with other large numbers in Chronicles, some scholars think the Hebrew *'eleph* ("thousand") here means military units, giving far smaller figures, while others take the numbers as written. Either way it was a catastrophe, and the reason is given plainly: **"because they had forsaken the LORD, the God of their fathers."**
- **"Zichri, a mighty man of Ephraim, killed Maaseiah the king's son" (v7).** Ahaz was still young, so "the king's son" may mean a royal prince of the wider family, or a title for a court official. Azrikam ran the palace, and Elkanah was second only to the king. The attack cut down the heads of the government.
- **"The men of Israel took captive 200,000 of their relatives" (v8).** The Hebrew is "their brothers." The Tyndale notes point out that the Chronicler, for all his focus on Judah, keeps calling the northerners kin. The captives were mostly women and children, the people most vulnerable to being sold as slaves.
- **"A prophet of the LORD was there, whose name was Oded" (v9).** A true prophet in the northern capital. He is not the Oded of 15:1, who lived nearly two centuries earlier. He "went out to meet the army" as it marched home in triumph, which took real courage.
- **"Because the LORD… was angry with Judah, he gave them into your hand, but you have killed them in a rage that has reached up to heaven" (v9).** Oded's speech has three parts. First, your victory was God's doing, not yours. Second, you went too far. God can use a nation as his tool of judgment and still hold it guilty for its cruelty (compare Isa 10:5–15 on Assyria). A rage that "reached up to heaven" is a rage God has taken notice of.
- **"Have you not sins of your own against the LORD your God?" (v10).** Third, judge yourselves before you judge your brothers. The law forbade Israelites to make fellow Israelites slaves (Lev 25:39–46). The northern kingdom was itself full of idolatry and would fall to Assyria within about a dozen years (2 Kgs 17). **"The fierce wrath of the LORD is upon you"** (v11) was not a figure of speech.
- **"Certain chiefs also of the men of Ephraim… stood up" (vv12–13).** Four leaders back the prophet against their own victorious soldiers. One of them is **Jehizkiah**, which in Hebrew is exactly the same name as Hezekiah, "the LORD strengthens." Their argument is humble: "our guilt is already great." Hosea, preaching in the north in these same years, would have agreed (Hos 4:1–2).
- **"So the armed men left the captives and the spoil" (v14).** Remarkably, the army listens. Soldiers in the flush of victory give up both their prisoners and their plunder.
- **"They clothed them, gave them sandals, provided them with food and drink, and anointed them" (v15).** The Chronicler lingers over the details: clothes from the spoil for those stripped naked, sandals, a meal, oil for skin cracked by the march, donkeys for those too weak to walk. They escorted them to **"Jericho, the city of palm trees,"** on the border, back to their own people.
- **The Good Samaritan.** Many readers have noticed how closely Jesus' parable echoes this scene (Luke 10:30–35). In both, people from the north, whom Judeans despised, care for wounded Judeans on the road near Jericho, binding them up with oil, setting them on a donkey and seeing them to safety. Whether Jesus had this chapter consciously in mind cannot be proved, but the parallel is striking. In both stories the "outsiders" show the mercy God's own people failed to show.
- **"At that time King Ahaz sent to the king of Assyria for help" (v16).** The Hebrew says "kings," probably a plural of majesty for the great king (the ESV footnotes it). Kings records his message: "I am your servant and your son. Come up and rescue me" (2 Kgs 16:7). Those are words of covenant loyalty that belonged to God alone. Isaiah had just offered Ahaz any sign he cared to ask for, "deep as Sheol or high as heaven," and Ahaz refused with false piety (Isa 7:10–12). He would not trust the LORD, so he bought Assyria.
- **"The Edomites had again invaded… And the Philistines had made raids" (vv17–18).** Judah's old enemies smell weakness on every side. Edom, in the south, was released from Judah's control when Rezin took Elath (2 Kgs 16:6). The Philistines take towns in the Shephelah, the foothills between the hill country and the coast. These are famous names: **Beth-shemesh**, where the ark returned from the Philistines (1 Sam 6); **Aijalon**, where the sun stood still for Joshua (Josh 10:12); **Soco**, where David faced Goliath (1 Sam 17:1); **Timnah**, Samson's town (Judg 14). The places where God once gave victory are now lost.
- **"The LORD humbled Judah because of Ahaz king of Israel" (v19).** "Humbled" is *kana'*, one of the Chronicler's key words. God's great promise was, "if my people… humble themselves… I will forgive their sin and heal their land" (7:14). Those who refuse to humble themselves get humbled. "King of Israel" is deliberate: Chronicles often uses "Israel" for God's whole people, and here it also hints that Ahaz has become just like the northern kings (v2). **"Very unfaithful"** is *ma'al*, the sin of breaking faith with God that finally sent Judah into exile (36:14).
- **"So Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria came against him and afflicted him instead of strengthening him" (v20).** Kings says Assyria did capture Damascus and kill Rezin (2 Kgs 16:9). Chronicles looks at the outcome: Judah became an Assyrian vassal, paying heavy tribute, and Assyria would be at Jerusalem's gates in the next reign (ch 32). The help was real for a moment and ruinous in the end. "Strengthening" is *chazaq*, the root of Hezekiah's name, and the chapter ends with him (v27). Assyria could not strengthen Judah. The LORD could.
- **"Ahaz took a portion from the house of the LORD… and gave tribute to the king of Assyria, but it did not help him" (v21).** He stripped God's house to pay a pagan king. Tiglath-pileser's own inscription lists "Jehoahaz of Judah" among the kings who paid him tribute, an outside record of this very payment.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the moral of vv17–19: "Iniquity brings men low," and on the captives stripped of their clothes, "Sin strips men." He praises the Ephraimites' obedience to the prophet but notes they did not go on to turn from their own idols. JFB calls the care of the captives "a beautiful incident," and suggests the women and children were taken because they had followed the army. Guzik admires Oded's wisdom in moving the soldiers step by step, drawing on Trapp. He contrasts Ahaz with David, who cried to the LORD in distress (Ps 18:6), and with Manasseh, who humbled himself in his affliction (33:12). He says God preserved David's line in this crisis for the sake of the promise, the very promise Isaiah's Immanuel sign carried.

#### Ahaz's Idolatry (vv22–27)
- **"In the time of his distress he became yet more faithless to the LORD—this same King Ahaz" (v22).** The Hebrew is blunt: "he is King Ahaz." It reads like a finger pointing at him. Trouble can drive a person to God, as it did Manasseh (33:12–13). It drove Ahaz further away.
- **"He sacrificed to the gods of Damascus that had defeated him" (v23).** His reasoning was the ordinary logic of the ancient world: the winning side must have the stronger gods. The irony is heavy. Damascus had by now fallen to Assyria, so its gods could not even save their own city. And it was the LORD, not the gods of Syria, who had given Judah into Syria's hand (v5). **"But they were the ruin of him and of all Israel."**
- **The altar from Damascus.** Kings tells how Ahaz went to Damascus to meet Tiglath-pileser, saw an altar there, and had the priest Uriah build a copy for the temple courts, pushing the LORD's bronze altar aside (2 Kgs 16:10–16). Chronicles goes further and says what that led to.
- **"Ahaz… cut in pieces the vessels of the house of God, and he shut up the doors of the house of the LORD" (v24).** He broke up the temple furnishings, perhaps for metal to pay Assyria (2 Kgs 16:17–18). Then he shut the temple. The daily sacrifices, the lamps and the incense stopped (29:7). The Tyndale notes call this Judah's lowest spiritual point, like being in exile while still in the land. Hezekiah's first act will be to open those doors in the first month of his reign (29:3).
- **"He made himself altars in every corner of Jerusalem. In every city of Judah he made high places" (vv24–25).** Instead of one altar where God had chosen to put his name (Deut 12:5–14), altars on every street corner. **"Provoking to anger the LORD, the God of his fathers."** The Chronicler keeps naming the God of the fathers, the God Ahaz walked away from.
- **"The rest of his acts and all his ways, from first to last" (v26).** Isaiah and Micah prophesied through these years, and Isaiah dates one oracle "in the year that King Ahaz died" (Isa 14:28). Micah's charges against Jerusalem's rulers (Mic 3:9–11) and his grief over "the sins of the house of Israel" fit this reign.
- **"They did not bring him into the tombs of the kings of Israel" (v27).** He was buried in Jerusalem but not with the honoured kings, a final judgment on his life, as with Jehoram and Joash before him (21:20; 24:25). Again the royal tombs of Judah are called "the kings of Israel."
- **"And Hezekiah his son reigned in his place" (v27).** Out of the darkest reign comes one of the best kings, whose name means "the LORD strengthens." Ahaz sacrificed his sons in fire. God preserved one of them, and through him David's line went on. It is pure mercy.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Ahaz acted out of "servile fear of a heathen" power. Henry: those who will not be humbled by God's word will be humbled by his judgments. He notes that Ahaz died unrepentant and was not buried with the good kings. Guzik compares Ahaz with his grandfather Uzziah. Uzziah overstepped by worshipping the true God in a forbidden way, while Ahaz played priest at a pagan altar. He cites Spurgeon on how much worse sin becomes when a person refuses to turn back under God's discipline.

#### Pointing to Christ
At the height of this chapter's crisis, Isaiah stood before Ahaz and said God would give a sign: "the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel" (Isa 7:14). Ahaz refused God's help and bought Assyria's, and it "did not help him." But God kept his promise to David's house anyway, preserving it even through the son of a king who burned his own sons. Matthew tells us the sign was fulfilled in Jesus, "God with us" (Matt 1:22–23). Where Ahaz shut the doors of God's house, Jesus opened the way into God's presence for good (Heb 10:19–20). And in the Samaritan road to Jericho, Jesus took up this chapter's picture of mercy shown across a bitter divide and made it the shape of love for one's neighbour, the love he himself showed when he came to wounded, rebellious people and carried them home.
