*Setting:* Babylonia, in "the eleventh year" of King Jehoiachin's exile, the year Jerusalem fell (587–586 BC). The day is given ("the first day of the month") but the month is missing from the Hebrew, so the exact date is uncertain. Most commentators place it shortly before or just after the city's fall in the summer of 586 BC. Ezekiel has spoken briefly against four neighbors (ch 25). Now he gives three long chapters (26–28) to one city: Tyre, the great Phoenician port on the coast of what is now Lebanon, about 35 miles northwest of the Sea of Galilee. Tyre was the trading capital of the Mediterranean, with colonies as far away as Carthage in North Africa and Spain. It was built in two parts: an older town on the mainland, and a fortified island about half a mile offshore, where the people could retreat when attacked. In David's and Solomon's day, Tyre's king Hiram had been Israel's friend and helped build the temple (2 Sam 5:11; 1 Kings 5). By Ezekiel's day the friendship had turned to self-interest, and Amos had already condemned Tyre for selling captives as slaves (Amos 1:9–10).

#### Prophecy Against Tyre (vv1–21)
- **"Aha, the gate of the peoples is broken; it has swung open to me" (v2).** Tyre greets Jerusalem's fall with the same "Aha!" as Ammon (25:3). Its reason is business. Jerusalem sat on the inland trade routes, and with Judah gone, the traffic would flow to Tyre. **"I shall be replenished, now that she is laid waste."** Tyre does not hate Jerusalem. It simply profits from its ruin. Henry calls this "a sin that does most easily beset us, but is not thought to be such a bad thing": to be quietly glad at another's fall when we can gain by it. Proverbs warns that "he who is glad at calamity will not go unpunished" (Prov 17:5).
- **"Behold, I am against you, O Tyre" (v3).** The most frightening words a nation could hear. God himself is Tyre's opponent.
- **"I will bring up many nations against you, as the sea brings up its waves" (v3).** For an island city, the picture is exact. The sea was Tyre's wall. Now attackers will come at it like waves, one after another. "Many nations" matters for reading what follows: Ezekiel is not describing one battle but a long series of assaults.
- **"I will scrape her soil from her and make her a bare rock" (v4).** The name Tyre (Hebrew *Tsor*) means "rock." The city named "Rock" will be scraped down to bare rock. **"She shall be in the midst of the sea a place for the spreading of nets" (v5).** Fishermen spread their nets on flat rocks to dry. The market of the world will become a place where a few fishermen dry their nets.
- **"Her daughters on the mainland shall be killed by the sword" (v6).** The "daughters" are the towns and villages on the coast that depended on Tyre.
- **"Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, king of kings" (v7).** This is the first time Ezekiel names the Babylonian king. The Hebrew spelling here, *Nebuchadrezzar*, is closer to the Babylonian *Nabu-kudurri-usur*, "Nabu, protect my heir." "King of kings" was a real title of the great Mesopotamian rulers, who had many lesser kings under them. Daniel uses the same title of him (Dan 2:37).
- **The siege described (vv8–11).** Ezekiel describes a standard ancient siege: a "siege wall" around the city, an earth "mound" or ramp built up to the walls, a "roof of shields" to protect men working at the base of the walls, battering rams and axes against the towers. So many horses that "their dust will cover you" (v10). Horses trampling "all your streets" (v11). This fits an attack on the mainland city, which an army could reach by land.
- **"Your mighty pillars will fall to the ground" (v11).** The Greek historian Herodotus, who visited Tyre in the fifth century BC, described two famous pillars in the temple of Melqart, Tyre's chief god, one of gold and one of a green stone that glowed at night. Many commentators think these are the "pillars" meant here: the symbols of Tyre's gods would fall.
- **What happened under Nebuchadnezzar.** The Jewish historian Josephus, citing Phoenician records, says Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for thirteen years, roughly 585–572 BC. The mainland town was destroyed, and Tyre in the end submitted to Babylon and received a king of Babylon's choosing. But the island city was not stormed or plundered. Ezekiel himself records this later: Nebuchadnezzar's army labored hard against Tyre, "yet neither he nor his army got anything from Tyre to pay for the labor" (29:18).
- **"They will plunder your riches… Your stones and timber and soil they will cast into the midst of the waters" (v12).** Notice the shift in pronouns. Verses 7–11 say "he," meaning Nebuchadnezzar. Verse 12 says "they," the "many nations" of v3. Most commentators see this as deliberate. The prophecy reaches beyond Babylon to later attackers. In 332 BC Alexander the Great besieged island Tyre for seven months. Unable to reach it by sea, he built a causeway half a mile long from the mainland to the island, using the rubble, timber and even the soil of the old mainland city. He literally threw its stones, timber and soil into the sea. When the city fell, thousands were killed and some 30,000 sold into slavery. Silt has built up around Alexander's causeway ever since, and the island is now joined to the mainland.
- **"I will stop the music of your songs, and the sound of your lyres shall be heard no more" (v13).** Tyre was known for its festivals and music. Isaiah pictured Tyre as a forgotten prostitute singing songs to be remembered (Isa 23:15–16). The silence of a city is a mark of its death (Jer 25:10). The same image returns over the fall of "Babylon" in Revelation 18:22.
- **"You shall never be rebuilt" (v14).** This raises a fair question, since there is a city called Tyre today. Faithful readers answer in a few ways. Many note that the old mainland city that Nebuchadnezzar destroyed, and much of the island city after Alexander were never restored to what they had been. The town that stands there today is a modest successor, not the old merchant capital. Others point out that the Tyre of Ezekiel's oracle is the great merchant power, the "city renowned" (v17), and that city did come to an end. It was never again a world power. Others again note that prophetic poetry often speaks in sweeping, total terms (compare Isa 13:19–22 on Babylon) to describe the certainty and finality of judgment. Ezekiel's own later note in 29:17–20 shows that the prophets did not hide the fact that fulfilment came in stages and not always as first expected.
- **"Will not the coastlands shake at the sound of your fall?" (v15).** The "coastlands" are the islands and shores of the Mediterranean, Tyre's trading partners. When the hub of their trade falls, they all feel it.
- **"All the princes of the sea will step down from their thrones and remove their robes" (v16).** These are the merchant rulers of the coastal cities and colonies. They do what mourners did: leave the seat of honor, strip off their fine clothes and sit on the ground (Job 2:12–13; Jonah 3:6). **"They will clothe themselves with trembling."** They mourn for Tyre, and they also fear for themselves.
- **"How you have perished, you who were inhabited from the seas, O city renowned" (v17).** This is a short *qinah*, a funeral lament, with its typical limping rhythm (a longer line followed by a shorter one). "How" (*'ek*) is the standard opening cry of a lament (compare Lam 1:1; Isa 14:12). Tyre "imposed their terror," using its sea power to frighten and control others. The Tyndale notes observe that Tyre's trade seems to have been built on intimidation and exploitation (28:16, 18).
- **"When I bring up the deep over you, and the great waters cover you" (v19).** The "deep" (*tehom*) is the same word used in Genesis 1:2 and in the flood (Gen 7:11). For Israel the deep stood for chaos and death. The city that ruled the sea will be swallowed by it.
- **"I will make you go down with those who go down to the pit, to the people of old" (v20).** "The pit" (*bor*) is literally a cistern or dungeon, but it was a common way of speaking of the grave and the realm of the dead (Ps 28:1; 88:4–6; Isa 14:15). Tyre will join the long-dead nations of the past in the "world below." Ezekiel develops this picture at length for Egypt in 32:17–32.
- **"But I will set beauty in the land of the living" (v20).** The Hebrew is difficult. The ESV takes it as God setting "beauty" or "glory" (*tsevi*) in the land of the living, which JFB and others read as his glory restored in the land of Israel, in contrast to Tyre in the pit. The Greek translation reads it as a further negative, "nor shall you stand in the land of the living," and some modern translations follow that. Either reading contrasts Tyre's end in the realm of the dead with life above.
- **"Though you be sought for, you will never be found again" (v21).** The city that everyone came to find, the center of the world's trade, will be searched for and not found. Henry: "The strongest cities in this world, the best-fortified and best-furnished, are subject to decay, and may in a little time be brought to nothing."
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that the four nations of chapter 25 "were not at that time very considerable in the world," but Tyre "was known all the world over," so it gets three whole chapters. On the mourning princes he writes, "Those who have the world in their hearts lament the loss of great men more than the loss of good men." JFB explains the name as "the rock-city" and distinguishes Old Tyre on the mainland from New Tyre on the island, arguing that vv9–11 describe the mainland siege and vv3–5, 12 the island, with the fulfilment coming in stages: Nebuchadnezzar, then Alexander, then later conquerors. He reads "thou shall be no more" (v21) as meaning Tyre would no longer be "the Tyre that once was." Guzik follows the same staged reading, makes much of the shift from "he" to "they" in v12, and describes Alexander's causeway as a striking literal fulfilment. He also notes God's patience: Jesus himself ministered in the region of Tyre (Mark 7:24–31), and Paul found believers there (Acts 21:3–4).

#### Pointing to Christ
Tyre was the great marketplace of the ancient world, a city that saw everything, even the fall of God's temple, in terms of profit. Its last picture is silence where music had been, and a bare rock for drying fishing nets. Jesus later walked the region of Tyre and spoke of it with surprising mercy: "If the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago" (Matt 11:21). There a Gentile woman from that very region knelt and asked for crumbs from the Master's table, and Jesus praised her faith (Mark 7:24–30). Jesus also called fishermen who spread nets on the shores of Galilee and made them "fishers of men" (Mark 1:17). The place of nets became, in his hands, not a sign of judgment but the start of a new kingdom whose foundation is a rock that does not fall (Matt 7:24–25; 16:18).
