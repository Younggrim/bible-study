*Setting:* About 814–782 BC, back in the northern kingdom. Jehu's dynasty is on the throne, as God promised (10:30), but Jehu kept Jeroboam's golden calves, and in his last years Hazael of Syria had already taken all of Israel's land east of the Jordan (10:32–33). This chapter covers Jehu's son Jehoahaz and grandson Jehoash. The names are confusing: Israel's king Jehoash (also called Joash) reigns at the same time as Judah's king Joash (also called Jehoash) from chapter 12. The ESV uses both spellings for each. In the middle of these two short royal summaries comes the last scene of Elisha's life. He has been a prophet for over fifty years, since the days of Ahab, and has not appeared in the story since he sent a young prophet to anoint Jehu (9:1). Here he speaks one final word of hope to a weak king.

#### Jehoahaz Reigns in Israel (vv1–9)
- **"In the twenty-third year of Joash… Jehoahaz the son of Jehu began to reign… and he reigned seventeen years" (v1).** About 814–798 BC. He came to the throne in the very year Judah's Joash was rebuking his priests for not repairing the temple (12:6).
- **"He did what was evil… and followed the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat" (v2).** The verdict on every northern king. Jeroboam's golden calves at Bethel and Dan (1 Kgs 12:28–30) remained the state religion. Jehu ended Baal worship but kept the calves, and his son does the same.
- **"He gave them continually into the hand of Hazael king of Syria and into the hand of Ben-hadad the son of Hazael" (v3).** "Continually" is literally "all the days." Hazael, the man Elisha wept over (8:11–12), keeps grinding Israel down. The prophet Amos, a few decades later, condemns Damascus for having "threshed Gilead with threshing sledges of iron" (Amos 1:3–4).
- **"Then Jehoahaz sought the favor of the LORD, and the LORD listened to him" (v4).** "Sought the favor" is an idiom meaning to soften or appease someone's face, to plead for kindness. Moses used it when he pleaded for Israel after the golden calf (Exod 32:11). Some connect the verb with a word meaning "to be weak or sick," but most lexicons treat the idiom separately, and the link should not be pressed. The surprise is that God listens to an idol-tolerating king. The reason is given: **"for he saw the oppression of Israel."** It echoes Exodus 3:7, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people." God's mercy was moved by his people's suffering, not by the king's merit.
- **"The LORD gave Israel a savior" (v5).** This is the language of Judges, where God raised up "a deliverer" for his oppressed people again and again (Judg 3:9, 15). Who was it? The text does not say. Three main views: Jehoash and his son Jeroboam II, who recovered Israel's land (13:25; 14:25–27); or Adad-nirari III of Assyria, whose campaigns crushed Damascus around 805–802 BC and took the pressure off Israel; or both, since God can use a foreign king as his tool (compare Cyrus, Isa 45:1). An Assyrian stele found at Tell al-Rimah lists "Joash of Samaria" among the kings who paid Adad-nirari tribute, which fits this time.
- **"The people of Israel lived in their homes as formerly" (v5).** Literally "in their tents," an old phrase for home life. Normal life came back: no more fleeing to walled towns from raiders.
- **"Nevertheless, they did not depart from the sins of the house of Jeroboam… and the Asherah also remained in Samaria" (v6).** Verses 5–6 are a bracketed aside in the ESV, looking ahead. God delivered them, and they did not change. The Asherah, a wooden pole or image of the Canaanite mother goddess that Ahab set up (1 Kgs 16:33), was still standing in the capital. Jehu destroyed Baal's temple but apparently left this.
- **"Fifty horsemen and ten chariots and ten thousand footmen" (v7).** Compare Ahab, who sent 2,000 chariots to the battle of Qarqar against Assyria in 853 BC, according to Shalmaneser III's own records. Within forty years Israel's chariot force has fallen from two thousand to ten. **"Like the dust at threshing"** is a picture of chaff blown away from the threshing floor (Ps 1:4; Isa 41:15–16).
- **Commentators:** Henry laments, "How is its crown profaned and its honour laid in the dust!" On the king's prayer he says, "It becomes kings to be beggars at God's door." JFB says the "savior" was "neither some patriotic defender nor some signal victory" but Jehoash and Jeroboam II, who won the land back. JFB calls the threshing image "a most significant figure" for national suffering. Guzik follows Dilday in linking "sought the favor" to a word for weakness and dependence, which the lexicons do not clearly support for this idiom. Guzik also sees the Judges pattern here and quotes Spurgeon, who said the people repented of their suffering rather than of their sin.

#### Jehoash Reigns in Israel (vv10–13)
- **"In the thirty-seventh year of Joash king of Judah, Jehoash the son of Jehoahaz began to reign" (v10).** About 798–782 BC. The two kings share a name, and both reign in the same years.
- **"He also did what was evil in the sight of the LORD" (v11).** The same verdict, the same reason.
- **A summary placed early (vv12–13).** Unusually, the writer gives Jehoash's closing notice, his death and burial, before telling the stories of his reign. That clears the way for Elisha's death scene (vv14–21) and for the account of his war with Amaziah king of Judah in the next chapter (14:8–14). **"Jeroboam sat on his throne"** is Jeroboam II, the most successful king the north ever had (14:23–29).

#### The Death of Elisha (vv14–25)
- **"When Elisha had fallen sick with the illness of which he was to die" (v14).** Elisha had raised the dead and healed Naaman, and he himself grows sick and dies. God's servants are not exempt from weakness. Elijah went up in a whirlwind (2:11). Elisha, who received a double portion of his spirit, dies in bed. Both are honored, in different ways. He was likely in his eighties.
- **"Joash king of Israel went down to him and wept before him" (v14).** An idolatrous king weeps at the prophet's bedside. He knows who has really been protecting Israel.
- **"My father, my father! The chariots of Israel and its horsemen!" (v14).** These are the exact words Elisha cried as Elijah was taken up (2:12). The king means: you were worth more to Israel than an army. Strikingly, he says it with ten chariots left to his name (v7). The words honor Elisha. Whether the king knows the God behind the prophet is less clear.
- **"Take a bow and arrows… Draw the bow" (vv15–16).** Elisha gives the king an acted prophecy, a sign that makes God's word visible, as prophets often did (Jer 13:1–11; Ezek 4:1–3). **"Elisha laid his hands on the king's hands"**: the old prophet's hands over the young king's on the bow. The strength for this war will come through God's word, not from Israel's ten chariots.
- **"Open the window eastward… Shoot" (v17).** East from Samaria lay Gilead and the lands Hazael had taken (10:32–33). Shooting an arrow toward an enemy's land was a known way of declaring war. **"The LORD's arrow of victory, the arrow of victory over Syria!"** The word for "victory" is the Hebrew word for salvation or deliverance (*teshu'ah*). **"You shall fight the Syrians in Aphek."** Aphek, probably east of the Sea of Galilee, was where Ahab had won a great victory over Ben-hadad (1 Kgs 20:26–30).
- **"Strike the ground with them." And he struck three times and stopped (v18).** The first sign was explained. This one was not. The king had just been told the arrows meant victory, and he gives a few half-hearted taps and stops.
- **"You should have struck five or six times… but now you will strike down Syria only three times" (v19).** Elisha is angry. The king's lack of eagerness showed the measure of his faith. God's gift was ready; the king's expectation was small. This raises an old question: did the number of strikes change what God would do? The text presents it as a test of the king's heart, which God's foreknowledge already took into account. The king received what he reached for. James says something similar: "You do not have, because you do not ask" (Jas 4:2).
- **"So Elisha died, and they buried him" (v20).** No ascension, no fire, just a burial. His ministry had spanned the reigns of six kings of Israel.
- **"Bands of Moabites used to invade the land in the spring of the year" (v20).** Spring was raiding season, when roads were dry and crops were ripening (2 Sam 11:1). Moab lay east of the Dead Sea and had broken free of Israel after Ahab's death (3:4–5).
- **"As soon as the man touched the bones of Elisha, he revived and stood on his feet" (v21).** A burial party sees raiders coming and, in panic, throws the body into the nearest tomb, which happens to be Elisha's. Tombs were often caves or rock-cut chambers, and bodies were laid on shelves, so the corpse lands among the prophet's bones. The man revives. It is the only miracle in the Bible worked after the prophet's death. The point is not that bones have power. This never happens again, and the Bible never encourages anyone to seek it. The point is that Elisha's God is still alive, and so is Elisha's word: Israel, as good as dead (v7), will revive, just as Elisha promised.
- **Different traditions.** Roman Catholic and Orthodox writers have long cited this verse in support of honoring the relics of saints. Protestant readers generally see it as a single sign given by God, pointing to his power and confirming the prophet's last word, not a pattern to follow. Both agree that the power was God's.
- **"Hazael king of Syria oppressed Israel all the days of Jehoahaz" (v22).** The writer steps back for a summary.
- **"But the LORD was gracious to them and had compassion on them, and he turned toward them, because of his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (v23).** This is the only time in Kings the patriarchs' covenant is named. It is the reason Israel survives. Not the king's piety, not the people's repentance, but a promise God made a thousand years before (Gen 12:1–3; 15:18; Exod 2:24). **"Nor has he cast them from his presence until now."** "Until now" is a quiet warning. The writer, living after the exile, knows that day eventually came (17:18–23).
- **"Three times Joash defeated him and recovered the cities of Israel" (v25).** Hazael dies, his son Ben-hadad takes over (the third Syrian king of that name in Kings), and Jehoash wins back the cities lost under his father, probably west of the Jordan. Exactly three victories, as Elisha said, and no more. His son Jeroboam II will finish the job (14:25–28).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the writer records almost nothing of Jehoash's wars but does record his visit to Elisha: "One good action shall make a better figure in God's book than twenty great ones." On the old prophet's death he says, "The aged are most experienced and therefore can worst be spared," and, "God's grace is not tied to one hand. He can bury his workmen and yet carry on his work." He sees the bones miracle as a pledge of life beyond death. JFB says stopping at three "betrayed the weakness of his faith," and reads the miracle as given "to stimulate the king's and people of Israel's faith." Guzik applies the arrows to prayer: persistent, expectant, until God says enough. He quotes Trapp to that effect and cites Clarke's warning against using the bones as a basis for relic worship. Guzik also quotes Spurgeon on v14: live so that even ungodly people will miss us when we are gone.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elisha's bones gave life to a dead man, but only for a while. That man died again. The story is a small sign of a much greater one. Jesus did not merely touch a grave and lift another. He went into death himself and rose, never to die again, and "whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live" (John 11:25). And the chapter's quiet center, "the LORD was gracious to them… because of his covenant with Abraham," is the gospel in seed form. God's people survive not because they deserve it but because God keeps his word, the same promise to Abraham that is fulfilled in Christ (Gal 3:14, 16, 29).
