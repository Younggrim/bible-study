*Setting:* About 850–842 BC, in the reign of Joram (also spelled Jehoram) son of Ahab over Israel, the northern kingdom, from his capital Samaria. The king of Syria (Aram), based in Damascus, is almost certainly Ben-hadad, the same king Ahab had foolishly spared years before (1 Kgs 20:34, 42). The two nations are still at war, sometimes in border raids and sometimes in full campaigns. Elisha, Elijah's successor, has just healed the Syrian general Naaman and seen his own servant Gehazi struck with leprosy for greed (ch 5). This chapter moves from the smallest of Elisha's miracles, a borrowed tool, to an enemy army blinded and fed, and then to a city starving under siege. The thread running through all three scenes is sight: who sees what God is doing, and who refuses to.

#### The Axe Head Recovered (vv1–7)
- **"The place where we dwell under your charge is too small for us" (v1).** "Under your charge" is literally "sitting before you," the posture of pupils in front of a teacher. The "sons of the prophets" were not literally Elisha's children but bands of prophets in training, living in communities at places like Bethel, Jericho and Gilgal (2:3, 5; 4:38). The group is growing. In a dark time for Israel, under kings who tolerated Baal worship, the faithful school is outgrowing its building.
- **"Let us go to the Jordan and each of us get there a log" (v2).** The Jordan valley near Jericho was thick with trees and brush along the river (the "jungle of the Jordan," Jer 12:5). There is no rich patron and no hired crew. Every man cuts his own beam and they build it themselves.
- **"Be pleased to go with your servants" (v3).** They want the prophet with them, not just his permission. Elisha goes, a small detail that shows a leader who works alongside his people.
- **"Alas, my master! It was borrowed" (v5).** Iron was valuable in this period, and an iron axe head was a real asset, not something a poor student could easily replace. The Hebrew verb *sha'al* means "to ask," and can mean "ask for," "borrow" or "ask as a gift," which is why some commentators say "begged." Either way, it belonged to someone else, and losing it meant a debt he could not pay (compare Exod 22:14). His distress is honest: he grieves for the lender's loss, not just his own.
- **"He cut off a stick and threw it in there and made the iron float" (v6).** Elisha first asks "Where did it fall?" God's help comes at the actual place of loss. The stick has no power of its own (compare the salt in 2:21 and the flour in 4:41). It is a sign that the LORD is acting. Iron does not float, and the text presents it as a plain miracle.
- **"Take it up." So he reached out his hand and took it (v7).** God does the impossible, and the man still has to reach out. Grace does not make him passive.
- **Why tell such a small story?** It sits between a general healed and an army blinded, and that is the point. The God who commands chariots of fire also cares about a poor student's borrowed tool. Nothing is too small to bring to him.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the students' industry and humility and draws the lesson that "Good disciples desire to be always under good discipline." He also sees a picture of grace raising "the stony iron heart which has sunk into the mud of this world." JFB reads "borrowed" as literally "begged," and stresses that the Jordan is too deep and swift for any natural explanation: the iron swam "only by the miraculous exertion of Elisha's power." Guzik agrees that the word can mean "requested," and draws the same point as v7: God did what only he could do, and the man still had to take hold of it.

#### Horses and Chariots of Fire (vv8–23)
- **"At such and such a place shall be my camp" (v8).** The king of Syria is planning ambushes, small raiding bands hidden along roads where Israel's troops or the king himself would pass. JFB calls it "a sort of guerrilla warfare."
- **"The man of God sent word to the king of Israel" (v9).** Elisha has no love for Joram's house (3:13–14), but he warns him "more than once or twice" (v10). Israel's survival matters to God's purposes, whatever its king deserves. The Tyndale notes suggest relations between prophet and king may have warmed after Naaman's healing.
- **"Who of us is for the king of Israel?" (v11).** The Syrian king assumes a spy in his own staff. A servant knows better: Elisha "tells the king of Israel the words that you speak in your bedroom" (v12). The most private room in the palace is open to God (Ps 139:1–4; Heb 4:13). Word of Naaman's healing had probably spread through the Syrian court.
- **"Go and see where he is, that I may send and seize him" (v13).** The irony is thick. The king plans to capture a man who can hear his plans, and the plan to capture him is itself one of those plans.
- **"He is in Dothan" (v13).** Dothan was a hill town about 10–12 miles north of Samaria, on the main route through the hills. It is where Joseph's brothers sold him centuries earlier (Gen 37:17). Archaeologists have excavated it and confirmed it was occupied in this period. **"Horses and chariots and a great army"** come by night and surround it (v14): a whole army sent for one unarmed prophet.
- **"Alas, my master! What shall we do?" (v15).** This is a new servant, probably Gehazi's replacement (5:27). He uses the same cry as the student in v5. He sees the enemy clearly. What he cannot see is what is also there.
- **"Those who are with us are more than those who are with them" (v16).** Elisha does not deny the danger. He counts differently. Compare Hezekiah's words before Sennacherib's army: "there are more with us than with him" (2 Chr 32:7), and John's "he who is in you is greater than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:4).
- **"O LORD, please open his eyes that he may see" (v17).** Elisha does not pray for the army to appear. It is already there. He prays for his servant's eyes. **"The mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha."** The same fiery chariots carried Elijah away (2:11), and Elisha himself cried "the chariots of Israel and its horsemen!" (2:12). Israel's real defense was never its army but the LORD's host (Ps 34:7; 68:17). Jesus could have called "more than twelve legions of angels" (Matt 26:53).
- **"Please strike this people with blindness" (v18).** The Hebrew word, *sanwerim*, appears only here and in Genesis 19:11, where the men of Sodom are struck so they cannot find Lot's door. The lexicon calls it a sudden, miraculous blindness, and in both stories the victims can still move about but cannot recognize where they are. Elisha prays twice for opened eyes (vv17, 20) and once for closed ones. The same God gives sight and takes it away.
- **"This is not the way, and this is not the city" (v19).** Elisha's words are slippery but not quite false: he is no longer in Dothan, and he does lead them to the man they seek, himself. He walks them roughly ten miles south into Samaria, Israel's fortified capital. Readers have long debated whether this was a lie. Most see it as a lawful wartime ruse, and in any case Elisha's purpose is to spare lives, not take them.
- **"My father, shall I strike them down? Shall I strike them down?" (v21).** Joram is eager, repeating himself. "My father" is a term of respect for a prophet (2:12; 13:14), though from this king it is more excitement than devotion.
- **"Would you strike down those whom you have taken captive with your sword and with your bow?" (v22).** Even prisoners taken in battle by Joram's own hand were not usually slaughtered, so how much less these, whom God had captured. Elisha's order is startling: **"Set bread and water before them."** Joram goes further and lays on "a great feast" (v23). This is Proverbs 25:21–22 in action, and Paul quotes that proverb in Romans 12:20: "if your enemy is hungry, feed him."
- **"The Syrians did not come again on raids into the land of Israel" (v23).** Kindness ended the raiding. The small bands stopped. It did not end the war, as the next verse shows, but it shamed the raiders into staying home.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Nothing done, said, thought, by any person, in any place, at any time, is out of the reach of God's cognizance." On the captives he says, "The most glorious victory over an enemy is to turn him into a friend." JFB takes the opened eyes in v17 as "those of the Spirit, not of the body," and the blindness of v18 as a confusion of the mind rather than total physical blindness, which fits the lexicon and Genesis 19. JFB calls Elisha's words "a stratagem, which has always been deemed lawful in war." Guzik agrees it was a ruse, and draws the lesson that people who cannot see are easy to mislead. He quotes G. Campbell Morgan to the effect that faith never imagines unreal things: the chariots were there whether the servant saw them or not.

#### Ben-hadad's Siege of Samaria (vv24–33)
- **"Afterward Ben-hadad king of Syria mustered his entire army" (v24).** "Afterward" may be some time later. The raids have stopped, but now comes a full siege. Samaria sat on a hill with strong walls, almost impossible to storm, so the aim was to starve it into surrender. JFB sees this as the bitter fruit of Ahab sparing this same Ben-hadad against God's word (1 Kgs 20:42).
- **"A donkey's head was sold for eighty shekels of silver" (v25).** A donkey was unclean food under the law (Lev 11:2–7), and the head is almost the worst part of it, mostly bone. Eighty shekels is about two pounds of silver, roughly the price of a slave several times over (Exod 21:32 values a slave at 30 shekels). **"The fourth part of a kab of dove's dung for five shekels."** A kab was a small dry measure, about a quart (just over a liter), so a quarter of it is a cupful. Whether "dove's dung" means actual droppings (eaten or burned as fuel in desperate sieges, compare Isa 36:12) or is a popular name for a cheap food such as carob pods or wild bulbs, scholars disagree. Either way, the cheapest scraps cost a fortune.
- **"Help, my lord, O king!" (v26).** The king is walking the wall, inspecting defenses. A woman cries out, and he assumes she wants food: "If the LORD will not help you, how shall I help you? From the threshing floor, or from the winepress?" (v27). Both are empty. His answer is bitter and partly true: only the LORD can help. But he says it as a complaint, not a prayer.
- **"We boiled my son and ate him" (vv28–29).** This is one of the darkest verses in the Bible, and it is not there for shock. Moses warned that if Israel abandoned the LORD, a siege would come in which parents would eat their own children (Lev 26:29; Deut 28:52–57). Jeremiah saw the same thing at Jerusalem's fall (Lam 2:20; 4:10). Israel's king and people had been living under the covenant's curses for a long time, and now they are falling in full. The woman's complaint is not even about the horror. It is that the other mother broke the deal.
- **"He had sackcloth beneath on his body" (v30).** When Joram tears his robe, the people see rough sackcloth worn underneath, a sign of mourning and penitence. It looks like repentance. But hidden sackcloth on a man who in the next breath swears to murder God's prophet is grief without turning to God.
- **"May God do so to me and more also, if the head of Elisha… remains on his shoulders today" (v31).** This oath echoes his mother Jezebel's vow against Elijah (1 Kgs 19:2). Why blame Elisha? Perhaps because the prophet had urged them to hold out, or because the king thought he could end the siege and would not, or because Elisha had sent the Syrian captives home (v22). The king had sackcloth on his body and murder in his heart.
- **"Elisha was sitting in his house, and the elders were sitting with him" (v32).** The prophet is calm. "This murderer," he calls the king, "son of a murderer" in the Hebrew, a reminder that Ahab killed Naboth (1 Kgs 21). He knows the messenger is coming and the king is right behind him. "Shut the door and hold the door fast."
- **"This trouble is from the LORD! Why should I wait for the LORD any longer?" (v33).** The Hebrew has "he said," and the speaker is probably the king, who has arrived just behind his messenger (7:2, 17 shows the king and his captain were present). He gets one thing right: the trouble *is* from the LORD. But he draws the wrong conclusion. If God sent the trouble, then God is the one to turn to. Instead he gives up waiting. The answer to his despair comes in the very next verse (7:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry links the horrors to the Deuteronomy curses and presses gratitude for ordinary bread: "Learn to value plenty, and to be thankful for it." On v33 he writes, "It is an unreasonable thing to be weary of waiting for God." JFB thinks it "more than doubtful" the king was truly repentant and attributes the despairing words of v33 to him. It explains "dove's dung" as either a kind of pulse or the bulb of a wild plant and notes that the text reports famine prices, not normal ones. Guzik ties the cannibalism to Deuteronomy 28, and passes on the suggestion that five shekels was something like a month's wages. He notes that the king's anger is really aimed at God, not the prophet.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter turns on eyes. A servant's eyes are opened to see the armies of heaven, an enemy's eyes are shut and then opened in the middle of Samaria, and a king sees the trouble clearly but cannot see past it. Jesus is the one who opens blind eyes (Luke 4:18; John 9), and he did more than spare his enemies. While we were enemies he died for us (Rom 5:10) and then spread a table for us. The angels Elisha's servant saw stood ready around Jesus too, and he did not call them, so that the curse of the covenant that fell on Samaria could fall instead on him (Gal 3:13). The king said, "Why should I wait for the LORD any longer?" The next morning's news, in chapter 7, is the answer: wait for him.
