*Setting:* Moses is still on Mount Sinai, inside the cloud, during his forty days with God (24:18). Chapter 25 described the contributions and the three main pieces of furniture: the ark with its mercy seat, the table for bread and the golden lampstand. Chapter 26 describes the tent that will hold them: its four layers of coverings, its wooden frame, the veil that divides it into two rooms, and the screen at its door. The tabernacle was a portable building, designed to be taken apart and carried by the Levites as Israel traveled (Num 4). It was not large, about 45 feet long, 15 feet wide and 15 feet high (roughly 14 by 4.5 by 4.5 meters), smaller than many houses today. But it was the house of the King. This is a builder's chapter, full of measurements and joinery. What matters most is the shape of the whole: layer within layer, room within room, with God's throne hidden at the center behind a veil.

#### The Tabernacle (vv1–37)
- **How big is a cubit?** A cubit was the length from elbow to fingertip, about 18 inches (45 cm). The tent's frame was thirty cubits long, ten wide and ten high. The inner room, the Most Holy Place, was a perfect cube, ten by ten by ten cubits (about 15 feet each way). Solomon's temple later doubled every dimension (1 Kgs 6:20), and the new Jerusalem in Revelation is also a cube (Rev 21:16), as if the whole city has become the Most Holy Place.
- **"Ten curtains of fine twined linen and blue and purple and scarlet yarns… with cherubim skillfully worked into them" (v1).** This is the innermost covering, the one that gives the tabernacle (*mishkan*, "dwelling") its name. Woven with cherubim, it formed the ceiling and walls visible from inside. A priest standing in the Holy Place by lamplight would see gold walls and, above him, a ceiling of royal colors with guardian cherubim, like the sky of heaven. "Skillfully worked" means the work of a master weaver, with the pattern woven in rather than embroidered on.
- **"Twenty-eight cubits… four cubits" (v2).** Each strip was about 42 feet by 6 feet. The Tyndale notes suggest the width may have been the widest a loom could make. Five strips were sewn into one large panel, and two panels were joined.
- **"Fifty loops… fifty clasps of gold… so that the tabernacle may be a single whole" (vv4–6).** The loops were blue, the color of heaven. The gold clasps hooked the two halves together into "one tabernacle." Many readers, Guzik among them, have seen a picture of unity in diversity, like the church as one body with many members (Rom 12:5; 1 Cor 12:12). In practical terms it also allowed the curtain to be folded into two loads for travel.
- **"Curtains of goats' hair for a tent over the tabernacle; eleven curtains" (v7).** The second layer was made of dark, coarse goat-hair cloth, the material of Bedouin tents to this day. Each piece was two cubits longer than the linen ones (v8), and there was one more piece, so it overlapped the linen on every side and protected it. **"The sixth curtain you shall double over at the front" (v9).** The extra width was folded over the front edge. **"Fifty clasps of bronze" (v11).** Bronze, not gold, because this layer is further from the center.
- **"The half curtain that remains… shall hang over the back" (v12), "the cubit on the one side, and the cubit on the other" (v13).** These verses explain how the extra length hung down the sides and back. Interpreters differ on exactly how the curtains draped over the frame, whether they reached the ground or left part of the gold frames exposed. The text gives the measurements but not a drawing.
- **"A covering of tanned rams' skins and a covering of goatskins on top" (v14).** The third and fourth layers were leather. "Tanned" is literally "reddened," and JFB calls it "Turkey red leather." The outer covering (*tachash*) is uncertain: "badgers' skins" in the KJV, "goatskins" in the ESV, and other proposals include dolphin, sea cow, or fine leather. It made a weatherproof outer roof. From outside, the tabernacle looked plain, even drab. All its beauty was inside. Many have compared this to Isaiah's description of the coming servant: "he had no form or majesty that we should look at him" (Isa 53:2).
- **"Upright frames for the tabernacle of acacia wood" (v15).** The older word is "boards," but many scholars now think these were open frames, like a ladder with two uprights and crosspieces, rather than solid planks. Solid boards of this size would have been extremely heavy to carry, and open frames would let the beautiful inner curtain show through from inside. **"Ten cubits… and a cubit and a half the breadth" (v16).** About 15 feet tall and 27 inches wide.
- **"Two tenons in each frame, for fitting together" (v17).** Tenons were projections at the bottom that fit into sockets, like pegs into holes.
- **"Twenty frames for the south side… forty bases of silver" (vv18–19).** Twenty frames on each long side and six across the back, plus two corner frames (vv22–23), make forty-eight. Each stood on two silver bases, so there were ninety-six, plus four for the veil's pillars (v32), one hundred in all. Exodus 38:25–27 tells us where the silver came from: the half-shekel "ransom" each Israelite man paid when he was counted (30:11–16), and each base weighed a talent, about 75 pounds (34 kg). So the whole tent literally stood on redemption money. Every frame rested on the silver of the people's atonement, not on the desert sand.
- **"They shall be separate beneath, but joined at the top, at the first ring" (v24).** The corner frames were made to strengthen the back corners. The exact construction is not clear, and translators and builders of models have offered different solutions.
- **"Bars of acacia wood, five for the frames of the one side" (v26).** Horizontal crossbars, overlaid with gold, ran through gold rings on the frames to hold them in line. **"The middle bar, halfway up the frames, shall run from end to end" (v28).** The middle bar ran the full length of the wall. Exodus 36:33 says it ran "through the frames," that is, through them or behind them, possibly hidden. JFB says it "gave it solidity and compactness."
- **"You shall overlay the frames with gold" (v29).** Inside, everything around the priests was gold. The closer to God's presence, the more glorious the materials.
- **"Erect the tabernacle according to the plan for it that you were shown on the mountain" (v30).** The third time this point is made (25:9, 40). The design was God's, not Moses'. Hebrews builds an argument on it: the tabernacle was "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (Heb 8:5), and the true sanctuary is the one in heaven where Christ ministers (Heb 8:1–2; 9:11, 24).
- **"You shall make a veil of blue and purple and scarlet yarns and fine twined linen… with cherubim" (v31).** "Veil" is *parochet*, a curtain that shuts off or divides. It was made like the inner ceiling curtain, with cherubim woven into it. Cherubim guarded the way to the tree of life after the fall (Gen 3:24). Now woven cherubim guard the way into God's presence. The message is plain: sinners cannot simply walk in.
- **"Four pillars of acacia overlaid with gold… on four bases of silver" (v32).** The veil hung on four gold pillars, standing on silver, like the walls.
- **"Bring the ark of the testimony in there within the veil. And the veil shall separate for you the Holy Place from the Most Holy" (v33).** "Most Holy" is literally "the holy of holies," a Hebrew way of saying the holiest of all. Only the high priest could go in, once a year on the Day of Atonement, with blood and a cloud of incense (Lev 16:2, 12–15). Hebrews explains: "By this the Holy Spirit indicates that the way into the holy places is not yet opened as long as the first section is still standing" (Heb 9:8).
- **"You shall put the mercy seat on the ark… in the Most Holy Place" (v34).** God's throne sat behind the veil, in darkness, lit only by his own glory. When Pompey, the Roman general, forced his way into the temple's Most Holy Place in 63 BC, the historian Tacitus reports he found it empty, with no image of any god. That is exactly the point. Israel's God was not represented by any image.
- **"The table outside the veil, and the lampstand on the south side… opposite the table" (v35).** The Holy Place, the outer room, held the table on the north and the lampstand on the south. The golden altar of incense, described later (30:1–10), stood in front of the veil. So a priest in the Holy Place stood among bread, light and incense, facing the veil with God behind it.
- **"A screen for the entrance of the tent… embroidered with needlework" (v36).** The door curtain was made of the same colors and linen, but "embroidered" rather than woven with cherubim, a different and slightly lesser kind of craft. The entrance faced east (27:13), toward the rising sun, the direction from which Ezekiel later saw God's glory return to the temple (Ezek 43:1–4). Eden too was entered from the east (Gen 3:24).
- **"Five pillars of acacia… and you shall cast five bases of bronze for them" (v37).** At the door the bases change from silver to bronze, the metal of the courtyard and the altar outside (27:1–3). Moving inward, the materials rise from bronze to silver to gold.
- **The plan as a whole.** The tabernacle was arranged in zones of rising holiness: the camp, the courtyard, the Holy Place, and the Most Holy Place. Each step inward was more costly, more beautiful and more restricted. Many scholars note how the tabernacle recalls Eden: an entrance on the east, cherubim guarding the way, gold and precious stones (Gen 2:11–12), and a lampstand like a flowering tree. God was setting up a small garden-sanctuary in the middle of the desert, a place where, in a limited way, he could live with his people again.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the cherubim woven into the curtains signify "that the angels of God pitch their tents round about the church" (compare Ps 34:7), and that "the whole represents the person and doctrine of Christ, and the church of true Christians." On the veil he writes: "We have now boldness to enter into the holiest, in all acts of worship, by the blood of Jesus" (Heb 10:19). He puts each silver base at "about 115 pounds," an older estimate higher than most modern ones. JFB describes the inner curtains as "of elegant texture, richly embroidered," says the goat-hair layer served "to protect and conceal the richer curtains," and stresses that the frames made "a more substantial fabric than a light and fragile tent." JFB has no notes on the veil itself (vv31–35). Guzik points out that the cherubim on the inner curtain could be seen only from inside, so the glory was hidden from everyone except those who entered. He links the silver bases to redemption, noting that the silver came from the ransom money, and the veil to Christ's flesh torn at the cross (Heb 10:20). He also passes on John Trapp's warning that the woven cherubim give no license for images in worship.

#### Pointing to Christ
From outside, the tabernacle looked like any other tent of rough goat hair and leather. Inside it was gold and color, with God's throne behind a veil. The Gospels show the same mystery in Jesus: an ordinary-looking man from Nazareth in whom "the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (Col 2:9), and on the mountain of transfiguration the hidden glory showed through (Mark 9:2–3). The whole tent stood on the silver of ransom, as the church stands on the price Christ paid. And the veil, with its cherubim keeping sinners out, was torn "in two, from top to bottom" the moment Jesus died (Matt 27:51). Hebrews explains what that tear means: "we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20). What only one man could enter once a year is now open to every believer, every day.
