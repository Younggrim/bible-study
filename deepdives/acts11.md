*Setting:* About AD 40–45, a few years after Paul's conversion. Chapter 10 told how Peter, after a vision on a rooftop in Joppa, went into the house of Cornelius, a Roman centurion in Caesarea, preached Jesus, and watched the Holy Spirit fall on a roomful of Gentiles before he had even finished speaking. Peter then baptized them and stayed several days as their guest. News like that travels fast. Chapter 11 shows two things happening at once: in Jerusalem the church has to make sense of what God did in Caesarea, and three hundred miles north, in Antioch, ordinary believers are already doing the same thing on a much larger scale.

#### Peter Reports to the Church (vv1–18)
- **"The Gentiles also had received the word of God" (v1).** The headline reaches "the apostles and the brothers who were throughout Judea" before Peter does. Luke phrases it in the language of the gospel's progress (8:14 said the same of Samaria). It is a report of something God has done, but in Jerusalem it lands first as a scandal.
- **"The circumcision party criticized him" (v2).** Literally "those of the circumcision." All of the believers in the room were circumcised Jews, so the phrase probably marks out a group who held tightly to circumcision and the law as the boundary of God's people. The same label names the men who later pressured Peter at Antioch (Gal 2:12). "Criticized" is *diakrinomai*, "took issue with," "disputed." Keep that word in mind for verse 12.
- **"You went to uncircumcised men and ate with them" (v3).** Notice the complaint. It is not "you preached to Gentiles" or even "you baptized them." It is about the table. Devout Jews did not eat with Gentiles, because Gentile food and homes were not kept by the law's purity rules (10:28; Gal 2:12). Sharing a meal meant sharing life, and that is exactly what the objectors could not accept. The Tyndale notes trace this separation through the Gospels too (John 4:9; 18:28).
- **"Peter began and explained it to them in order" (v4).** "In order" is *kathexēs*, the same word Luke uses in his preface for his own "orderly account" (Luke 1:3). Peter does not argue theology first or pull rank as the leading apostle. He tells what happened, step by step, and lets the facts carry the case.
- **Why the story is told again.** Luke has just told it in full in chapter 10, and he will summarize it a third time at the Jerusalem Council (15:7–11). Ancient writers had no bold type or underlining. Repetition was how they marked what mattered most, and Luke spends more space on Cornelius than on almost any other event in Acts. The door opening to the Gentiles is the turning point of the book.
- **The vision retold (vv5–10).** Peter repeats the sheet, the animals, the command "Rise, Peter; kill and eat," his refusal, and the voice: "What God has made clean, do not call common" (v9). He adds one small, honest detail: "Looking at it closely" (v6). He studied the sheet and saw nothing he was allowed to eat. The point of the vision, as Peter came to see in 10:28, was about people more than menus: he "should not call any person common or unclean." Mark's Gospel draws the food conclusion from Jesus' own teaching (Mark 7:19).
- **"At that very moment" (v11).** The timing is the evidence. Peter did not go looking for Gentiles. They knocked on the door as the vision ended.
- **"The Spirit told me to go with them, making no distinction" (v12).** Here is the wordplay. "Making no distinction" uses the same verb, *diakrinō*, that described the critics in verse 2. They "made a distinction" against Peter, and the Spirit had told Peter *not* to make one. (In 10:20 the same verb was rendered "without hesitation.") Luke lets the vocabulary make the argument.
- **"These six brothers also accompanied me" (v12).** Now we learn the number, and we learn that they came to Jerusalem with him. They were Jewish believers from Joppa (10:23, 45). Six witnesses plus Peter made seven, well past the two or three the law required to establish a matter (Deut 19:15). Peter had seen trouble coming and brought witnesses.
- **"A message by which you will be saved, you and all your household" (v14).** Peter supplies a line from the angel that chapter 10 did not record. Cornelius was devout, generous and prayerful (10:2), but he still needed to hear the gospel to be saved. Salvation comes through a message about Christ, not through religious sincerity alone.
- **"As I began to speak, the Holy Spirit fell on them" (v15).** In 10:44 it happened "while Peter was still saying these things." Peter remembers it as barely getting started, and both are true: God interrupted the sermon. "Just as on us at the beginning" points back to Pentecost (2:1–4). This was not a lesser, Gentile version of the Spirit. It was the same gift.
- **"I remembered the word of the Lord" (v16).** Peter quotes the risen Jesus from 1:5: "John baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit." What happened in Caesarea was that promise reaching people Peter had never expected it to reach.
- **"Who was I that I could stand in God's way?" (v17).** This is the heart of his defense. God gave "the same gift" to Gentiles who believed as he gave to Jews who believed. If God has received them, refusing them baptism and fellowship would mean opposing God. Note the basis: "when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ." Faith, not circumcision, is what Jew and Gentile have in common.
- **"They fell silent. And they glorified God" (v18).** The objection ends in worship. **"Repentance that leads to life"** means a turning to God that issues in eternal life. Repentance is called something God "granted," a gift and not only a human decision (compare 5:31; 2 Tim 2:25). The peace did not last. The same question, now sharpened to "must Gentile believers be circumcised?", explodes again in chapter 15 and fills the letter to the Galatians.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that Peter "rehearsed the matter from the beginning, and laid it before them in order," answering an accusation with a plain account rather than resentment. Guzik observes that the critics' first concern was Peter's conduct, not the wonder of what God had done, and he draws the lesson that believers should look for where God is working and join in, while testing it against Scripture. He also credits Peter's foresight in bringing the six witnesses. JFB makes a useful point about authority: the objectors questioned Peter, the leading apostle, openly and without disrespect, which shows that neither side imagined him to have the final word by himself. JFB also notices that Peter never names Cornelius in his report.

#### The Church in Antioch (vv19–30)
- **"Those who were scattered because of the persecution that arose over Stephen" (v19).** Luke picks up a thread he dropped in 8:1–4. The persecution meant to stamp out the church instead spread it. Some of the scattered believers went north along the coast to **Phoenicia** (modern Lebanon, the cities of Tyre and Sidon), some across the sea to **Cyprus**, and some to **Antioch**. At first they spoke "to no one except Jews."
- **Antioch of Syria.** Founded about 300 BC by Seleucus I and named for his father Antiochus, it sat on the Orontes River about 15 miles inland from its port, Seleucia, roughly 300 miles north of Jerusalem (in modern Turkey, near the Syrian border). It was the capital of the Roman province of Syria and, after Rome and Alexandria, the third city of the empire, with perhaps a quarter to half a million people. It was cosmopolitan, wealthy and famous for loose morals, especially at the nearby pleasure grove of Daphne. It also had a large Jewish community. This is the church that will send Paul out (13:1–3).
- **"Men of Cyprus and Cyrene… spoke to the Hellenists also" (v20).** Cyrene was a city in North Africa (modern Libya) with a large Jewish population (Simon of Cyrene carried Jesus' cross, Luke 23:26). These unnamed Jewish believers, raised outside Judea and at home in Greek culture, took the step no one had planned. The manuscripts differ here. Many read *Hellēnistas*, "Hellenists," which elsewhere in Acts means Greek-speaking Jews (6:1; 9:29). Others read *Hellēnas*, "Greeks." The ESV prints "Hellenists" but footnotes "Greeks (that is, Greek-speaking non-Jews)," and the context settles the meaning: these people are set in contrast with the "Jews" of verse 19. Either way, they were Gentiles.
- **What is new here.** Cornelius was one household, brought in by a vision and an apostle. At Antioch, nameless believers simply began telling Gentiles about "the Lord Jesus," probably around the same time or not long after. Notice the title. To a Jewish hearer the good news was that Jesus is the Messiah. To Greeks who had no messianic hope, he was presented as *Kyrios*, Lord, a word they understood.
- **"The hand of the Lord was with them, and a great number… turned to the Lord" (v21).** "The hand of the Lord" is an Old Testament phrase for God's power at work (Isa 59:1; Ezra 7:9). The growth was not a technique. It was God's doing, and the people "believed" and "turned," faith and repentance together.
- **"They sent Barnabas to Antioch" (v22).** Jerusalem had sent Peter and John to check on Samaria (8:14). Now it sends Barnabas, and the choice is inspired. He was a Levite from Cyprus (4:36), so he shared roots with the men who started the work, and he was the one who had vouched for Saul when no one else would (9:27).
- **"When he came and saw the grace of God, he was glad" (v23).** Grace is something he could *see*: changed lives. Barnabas's name means "son of encouragement" (4:36), and Luke shows him living up to it: **"he exhorted them all"** uses *parakaleō*, the verb behind that name. His message was "remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast purpose," literally "with purpose of heart," a settled resolve. New believers in a pagan city needed staying power more than excitement.
- **"For he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith" (v24).** Luke rarely praises anyone this directly. It echoes the description of Stephen (6:5). Barnabas's goodness showed in what he did *not* do: he did not insist that the Gentile converts become Jews first, and he did not try to bring the work under his own name.
- **"Barnabas went to Tarsus to look for Saul" (v25).** Saul had been sent home to Tarsus about eight to ten years earlier (9:30), and he spent those years in "the regions of Syria and Cilicia" (Gal 1:21). The verb for "look for" suggests a thorough search, as if Barnabas had to track him down. Barnabas saw that the work needed a gifted teacher, and he knew the man God had called to the Gentiles (9:15). It is a humble move: he brings in someone who will soon outshine him.
- **"For a whole year they met with the church and taught" (v26).** Teaching is the first thing the two do together. A church born in a burst of evangelism was grounded through patient instruction.
- **"In Antioch the disciples were first called Christians" (v26).** The Greek *Christianoi* uses a Latin-style ending, *-ianus*, which meant "belonging to the party of," as with the Herodians (Mark 3:6) or the "Caesariani," the emperor's household men. Outsiders heard these people constantly talking about "Christos" and treated the title as a name. It was probably a label from the outside and possibly a sneer. The verb "were called," *chrēmatizō*, usually means to be divinely warned in the New Testament (Matt 2:12; Acts 10:22), but the lexicon gives a separate sense here, "to assume a name" or "be called," as in business, so nothing special should be read into it. The word appears only three times in the New Testament (26:28; 1 Pet 4:16), and never as the believers' own name for themselves. They called themselves disciples, brothers, saints, believers, and followers of "the Way." In time they took up the outsiders' label gladly.
- **"Prophets came down from Jerusalem" (v27).** Early church prophets were believers who spoke messages from God, sometimes about the future, and they ranked next to apostles in Paul's lists (1 Cor 12:28; Eph 4:11). "Came down" is geography: Jerusalem sits high in the hills, so travelers always "went up" to it and "came down" from it.
- **Agabus (v28).** He appears again years later, binding his own hands and feet with Paul's belt to show that Paul would be bound in Jerusalem (21:10–11). Here he predicts "a great famine over all the world," meaning the Roman world. Luke adds in brackets that it "took place in the days of Claudius," emperor AD 41–54. Roman writers record a string of bad harvests and food shortages in his reign, and the Jewish historian Josephus describes a severe famine in Judea around AD 45–47, when Queen Helena of Adiabene, a convert to Judaism, bought grain from Egypt for Jerusalem. Luke's note invites readers to check him against public history.
- **"Every one according to his ability" (v29).** The giving was voluntary and proportional, with no fixed amount. They "determined" to do it, a decision made ahead of time. It happened *before* the famine struck, on the strength of a prophecy. The Jerusalem church had shared with its own (2:44–45; 4:32–37). Now a largely Gentile church gives to Jewish believers it has never met. That is the unity of verse 17 put into practice with money.
- **"To the elders by the hand of Barnabas and Saul" (v30).** This is the first mention in Acts of elders in the church, leaders modeled partly on the elders of the synagogue (14:23; 15:2; 20:17). Paul later organized a far larger collection for Jerusalem from his Gentile churches (Rom 15:25–27; 2 Cor 8–9), and here the habit begins. Interpreters differ over whether this famine visit is the visit Paul describes in Galatians 2:1–10, which the Tyndale notes take it to be, or whether Galatians 2 describes the council of chapter 15. Both views have careful defenders.
- **Commentators:** All three see Barnabas as the man for the moment. JFB calls "good" here "large-hearted," above "narrow Jewish sectarianism," and Henry glosses it as "a charitable man." Henry also explains "purpose of heart": "To cleave to the Lord Jesus is to live a life of dependence upon him and devotedness to him." On the name, JFB says it "originated not within, but without, the Church" and "was not at first used in a good sense," and Guzik likewise thinks it began as mockery. Henry turns it into a challenge: "Are we Christians? Then we ought to think, and speak, and act, in every thing as becomes Christians." On verse 20 JFB is firm that "Greeks" is the true reading, which is stronger than most modern editors, who prefer "Hellenists" while agreeing that Gentiles are meant. JFB calls the Antioch giving "the pure prompting of Christian love," and Guzik suggests this may be the first recorded case of one people group raising money for another.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter turns on one sentence: God gave Gentiles "the same gift" he gave Jews "when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ" (v17). Jesus is the one who baptizes with the Spirit (v16), and the one name that made outsiders invent a new word for his people (v26). The wall at the dinner table comes down because he has made both groups one (Eph 2:14), and the first fruit of that new family is a church of Gentiles feeding hungry Jewish believers in his name.
