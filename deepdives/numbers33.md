*Setting:* The plains of Moab, across the Jordan from Jericho, in the fortieth year after the Exodus. The old generation has died in the wilderness, Aaron is dead, and Moses has only months to live. The new generation is about to cross into Canaan. Before they go, Moses sets down in writing the route they have travelled, from Rameses in Egypt to this very campsite. It is the longest list of place names in the Old Testament: forty-two stops in all. Then God gives them their marching orders for the land itself. The chapter looks back over the whole journey and then forward to what waits across the river.

#### Recounting Israel's Journey from Egypt (vv1–49)
- **"These are the stages of the people of Israel" (v1).** "Stages" is literally "pullings up," from the Hebrew word for pulling up tent pegs to break camp. The list is built on a simple, repeated rhythm: "they set out from… and camped at…" over and over. Read aloud, it sounds like what it was: forty years of packing up, walking and pitching tents again, each move at God's command (9:17–23).
- **"Moses wrote down their starting places… by command of the LORD" (v2).** This is the only place in Numbers that says Moses himself kept a written record. God wanted the journey remembered. The new generation had been children or not yet born for much of it. Now, on the edge of Canaan, they could read where God had brought them.
- **"On the day after the Passover… went out triumphantly" (v3).** "Triumphantly" is literally "with a high hand," a raised fist of victory (Exod 14:8). Israel did not sneak out of Egypt. They marched out in broad daylight, "in the sight of all the Egyptians."
- **"While the Egyptians were burying all their firstborn… On their gods also the LORD executed judgments" (v4).** The plagues were not only against Pharaoh. They were God's verdict on Egypt's gods, the Nile, the sun, the livestock deities and Pharaoh himself, who was treated as divine (Exod 12:12). The list begins with God's victory and ends on the edge of another one.
- **From Egypt to Sinai (vv5–15).** These stops follow Exodus 12–19 closely: **Succoth**, **Etham** at the desert's edge, the turn back to **Pi-hahiroth** that set the trap at the sea (Exod 14:2), the crossing, bitter water at **Marah**, the oasis at **Elim** with its twelve springs and seventy palms, the **wilderness of Sin** where manna began (Exod 16), and **Rephidim**, remembered here with a pointed note: "where there was no water for the people to drink" (v14). That was where Israel quarrelled with God and Moses struck the rock (Exod 17:1–7). **Dophkah** and **Alush** (vv12–13) are added here and appear nowhere else.
- **The wilderness of Sinai (v15).** Israel stayed nearly a year at Sinai, where God gave the law and the tabernacle was built (Exod 19–Num 10). The list gives that whole year one line. The list records where they went, and the rest of the Torah records what happened there.
- **Kibroth-hattaavah and Hazeroth (vv16–17).** "Graves of craving," where Israel's demand for meat ended in plague, and Hazeroth, where Miriam and Aaron challenged Moses (11:34–12:16). Even the place names carry memories of failure.
- **The silent years (vv18–36).** Most of the names from **Rithmah** to **Ezion-geber** appear nowhere else in Scripture. Most scholars think these belong largely to the thirty-eight years of wandering after the people refused to enter Canaan (ch 14), years Numbers otherwise passes over almost in silence. They are a generation's slow funeral march, and yet God records every campsite. **Ezion-geber** (v35) lay at the northern tip of the Gulf of Aqaba, where Solomon later built a fleet (1 Kgs 9:26). From there the list returns to **Kadesh** in the wilderness of Zin (v36), where the journey had stalled a generation earlier.
- **A puzzle about Moseroth.** Deuteronomy 10:6 says Aaron died at **Moserah** and gives the order of stops Bene-jaakan then Moserah, while here the order is Moseroth then Bene-jaakan (vv30–31) and Aaron dies at **Mount Hor** (v38). Interpreters usually suggest either that Moserah was the district in which Mount Hor stood, or that Israel passed through the same area more than once in its wanderings. The lists come from different points in the story and were not written to be harmonized step by step.
- **"Aaron the priest went up Mount Hor… and died there" (vv38–39).** This is the only death in the Pentateuch dated to the exact day: the first day of the fifth month of the fortieth year. Aaron was 123, three years older than Moses, who will die at 120 (Exod 7:7; Deut 34:7). The full account is in 20:22–29, where Aaron's priestly garments pass to his son Eleazar. The pause in the list honors the first high priest.
- **"The king of Arad… heard of the coming of the people of Israel" (v40).** A brief reminder of the attack in 21:1–3, Israel's first victory on the way to Canaan, where they vowed to devote the towns to God and he gave them the victory.
- **From Mount Hor to the plains of Moab (vv41–49).** The route curves south and east around Edom, which had refused passage (20:14–21). **Punon** (v42) is usually identified with Feinan, a major ancient copper-mining site in the Arabah, and some have suggested the bronze serpent episode (21:4–9) happened near here, though the text doesn't say so. **Dibon-gad** (v45) is Dibon, already rebuilt by Gad (32:34). **Almon-diblathaim** (v46) is probably the Beth-diblathaim named on the Mesha Stele. The final camp stretches along the Jordan "from Beth-jeshimoth as far as Abel-shittim" (v49), "the meadow of acacias," also called Shittim, from which Joshua will send the spies (Josh 2:1).
- **Forty-two stops.** Some writers see a deliberate pattern, six sets of seven. That may be so, but the list is also selective. It skips places named earlier, such as Taberah (11:3), and Deuteronomy 2 names others. The point is not a complete map. Most of the sites cannot be located with any certainty, and scholars disagree even about where Mount Sinai was. What the list does show is that the journey was real, recorded place by place, and that God led every step of it.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the chapter the "winding up" of the wilderness travels and gives three reasons it was written: to "establish the truth of the history," to "preserve a memorial of God's marvellous interpositions," and to "confirm their faith" before the invasion. JFB admits "No certainty has yet been attained" on many of the sites, and thinks the stations between Hazeroth and Kadesh belong to the long wandering, with "more than are recorded." Henry says the list was kept "to preserve in writing an account of the providences of God," because "Our memories are deceitful and need this help." He adds that "The way which God takes in bringing his people to himself is always the best way," even when it is roundabout. Guzik compares the list to an ancient king's record of conquests, the march of the LORD's victory, and applies it to the value of remembering God's faithfulness before facing new battles. JFB has no comment on Aaron's death or the stops in vv38–49.

#### Instructions for Conquering Canaan (vv50–56)
- **"When you pass over the Jordan" (v51).** God's speech is not "if" but "when." The crossing is as certain as the forty-two stops behind them.
- **"Drive out all the inhabitants… destroy all their figured stones… metal images… high places" (v52).** "Figured stones" were carved stones or stone slabs used in worship, and "metal images" were idols cast in molten metal. "High places" (*bamot*) were hilltop shrines and altars. The command is aimed at Canaanite religion, which included ritual prostitution and child sacrifice (Lev 18:21–27; Deut 12:31). The land was to be cleared of it entirely, because God knew how easily Israel would be drawn in, as Baal-peor had just shown (ch 25).
- **"For I have given the land to you to possess it" (v53).** The land is God's gift before it is Israel's conquest. They fight for what has already been promised.
- **"You shall inherit the land by lot according to your clans" (v54).** This repeats the rules of 26:52–56: larger tribes get more land, smaller tribes less, and the lot, under God's control, decides where. "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Prov 16:33).
- **"Barbs in your eyes and thorns in your sides" (v55).** A vivid picture: splinters that keep stabbing and never let you rest. Any Canaanites left in the land would keep pulling Israel toward their gods. This is exactly what happened. Judges 1 lists tribe after tribe that "did not drive out" the inhabitants, and in Judges 2:3 the angel of the LORD quotes this warning back to them: "they shall become thorns in your sides, and their gods shall be a snare to you." Joshua repeats it too (Josh 23:13). Paul's "thorn in the flesh" (2 Cor 12:7) uses similar language.
- **"I will do to you as I thought to do to them" (v56).** The most sobering line in the chapter. If Israel lives like the Canaanites, God will treat Israel like the Canaanites, and remove them from the land. That is exactly what the exiles to Assyria and Babylon would later be (2 Kgs 17:7–18). God's judgment is not favoritism. The same standard applies to his own people.
- **Is this a model for today?** No. This was a specific command to a specific nation at a specific moment, tied to God's judgment on Canaan's sin (Gen 15:16; Deut 9:4–5). The church's battle is spiritual, not against flesh and blood (Eph 6:12), and its weapons are not the sword (2 Cor 10:3–5). Christians have read the principle behind it as a call to deal ruthlessly with sin in their own lives, not with people.
- **Commentators:** Henry puts the lesson in one line: "If we do not drive sin out, sin will drive us out." JFB calls the figured stones "obelisks for idolatrous worship," takes "high places" as standing "by metonymy for all their groves and altars," and calls any survivors "troublesome and dangerous neighbors" who would entice Israel to idolatry. Guzik stresses that the command was an act of judgment on a corrupt culture and was meant to protect Israel's spiritual health, and he traces how Israel's failure to obey led step by step toward exile. He applies it to tolerating corrupt influences that seem harmless at first. JFB does not comment on v56.

#### Pointing to Christ
The list of stops is a record of God's patience: every place where Israel failed is still written down as a place God brought them. Hebrews looks back on the same journey and says that the wilderness generation did not enter God's rest because of unbelief, and that "a Sabbath rest" still remains for the people of God (Heb 3:16–4:11). Believers are "sojourners and exiles" too (1 Pet 2:11), on the way to a better country (Heb 11:13–16). Jesus, whose name is the Greek form of Joshua, is the one who leads his people all the way in. He has already gone through the wilderness himself, forty days instead of forty years, and where Israel failed at each test, he did not (Matt 4:1–11). And the warning about barbs and thorns finds its answer in him: he wore the thorns, so that sin need not rule his people (Rom 6:12–14).
