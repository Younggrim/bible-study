*Setting:* In Ezra 5 the Jews began rebuilding the temple again in 520 BC at the urging of the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, and Tattenai, the Persian governor west of the Euphrates, wrote to King Darius I to ask whether Cyrus had really authorized it. Ezra 6 gives the answer. The search of the archives turns up Cyrus's original memorandum, Darius confirms it with a decree of his own, the temple is finished in 515 BC and the people dedicate it and keep the Passover. This chapter closes the first half of the book. The story of the return under Zerubbabel and the rebuilt temple, which began in 538 BC, is complete. Chapter 7 jumps ahead almost sixty years to Ezra himself. The Aramaic section that began at 4:8 ends at 6:18, and the book returns to Hebrew for the Passover.

#### The Decree of Darius (vv1–12)
- **"Search was made in Babylonia, in the house of the archives" (v1).** Literally "the house of the books… where the treasures were stored." Persian kings kept administrative records with their treasuries. Archaeologists have found thousands of clay administrative tablets at Persepolis, another Persian capital, so a careful search of royal records was entirely realistic.
- **"In Ecbatana, the citadel that is in the province of Media" (v2).** The scroll was not in Babylon at all. Ecbatana (modern Hamadan in western Iran) was the old capital of the Medes and the cool summer residence of the Persian kings, about 300 miles northeast of Babylon in the mountains. Cyrus probably spent the summer of 538 BC there, which is where the memorandum would have been filed. The official search could have stopped when Babylon came up empty. It did not. Behind a clerk's thorough search stands God's providence.
- **"A scroll" and "A record" (v2).** Records at Babylon were often clay tablets, but this was a scroll, probably leather or papyrus, written in Aramaic. "Record" (Aramaic *dikronah*) means a memorandum, an official summary filed in the archive. That explains why it differs in form from the proclamation in 1:2–4. Chapter 1 gives the public announcement in Hebrew to the Jews. Chapter 6 gives the internal record for the treasury, with costs and measurements.
- **"Let its foundations be retained" (v3).** The meaning is uncertain. It may mean "let its foundations be firmly laid" or "let it be rebuilt on its old foundations." Either way the temple was to rise on its original site, "the place where sacrifices were offered."
- **"Its height shall be sixty cubits and its breadth sixty cubits" (v3).** That is about 90 feet by 90 feet. Solomon's temple was 60 cubits long, 20 wide and 30 high (1 Kgs 6:2), so these figures are puzzling. The length is not given, and the breadth is three times Solomon's. Some think the text has suffered in copying. Others think the numbers set the *maximum* size the treasury would pay for, not a blueprint. The builders seem not to have reached it, since the old men wept at how small the foundation was (3:12; Hag 2:3).
- **"Three layers of great stones and one layer of timber" (v4).** This matches the building method of Solomon's temple courts (1 Kgs 6:36) and the observation in Tattenai's report (5:8).
- **"Let the cost be paid from the royal treasury" (v4).** Surprising as it sounds, this was Persian policy. Persian kings funded the repair of temples in Egypt and Babylonia to win the loyalty and prayers of conquered peoples. The Cyrus Cylinder, a clay inscription from Cyrus's reign now in the British Museum, records that he returned gods to their shrines and asked them to pray for him. God used that policy for his own house.
- **"Each to its place" (v5).** Even the temple vessels were to be returned to their proper places. The detail echoes Ezra 1:7–11 and shows how carefully the memorandum was drafted.
- **"Keep away. Let the work on this house of God alone" (vv6–7).** Darius's reply turns on Tattenai's own report. The governor asked whether the Jews had permission. The answer is not only yes, but "stay out of the way." "The governor of the Jews" is Zerubbabel (Hag 1:1).
- **"The cost is to be paid… from the royal revenue, the tribute of the province" (v8).** The irony is sharp. The tax money of the very province whose officials had stopped the work (4:13, 20) would now pay for it. Haggai had promised, "The silver is mine, and the gold is mine, declares the LORD of hosts" (Hag 2:8). It came from an unexpected purse.
- **"Bulls, rams, or sheep… wheat, salt, wine, or oil… day by day without fail" (v9).** The list covers what the Law required for daily offerings: animals for burnt offerings, flour and oil for grain offerings (Lev 2:1), wine for drink offerings (Num 15:5) and salt, which went with every offering (Lev 2:13).
- **"Pray for the life of the king and his sons" (v10).** Darius's motive was partly religious self-interest. He wanted every god in his empire on his side. Yet the request fit God's own instruction to the exiles: "seek the welfare of the city… and pray to the LORD on its behalf" (Jer 29:7). Paul later tells the church to pray "for kings and all who are in high positions" (1 Tim 2:1–2). Jewish prayers and sacrifices for the ruling power continued at the temple until the war with Rome in AD 66.
- **"A beam shall be pulled out of his house, and he shall be impaled on it" (v11).** This punishment was not idle talk. In his Behistun inscription, carved on a cliff not far from Ecbatana, Darius boasts of impaling rebel leaders. The house turned into "a dunghill" (or rubble heap) was a common threat (Dan 2:5; 3:29). The KJV's "hanged" refers to the same punishment, being fixed to a stake.
- **"May the God who has caused his name to dwell there" (v12).** Darius uses, perhaps from his Jewish advisers, the language of Deuteronomy: the place "the LORD your God will choose, to make his name dwell there" (Deut 12:11). The curse on any king or people who would "destroy this house" was not kept by later kings. Antiochus IV defiled the temple in 167 BC, and Rome destroyed it in AD 70.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the section as Cyrus's decree recited and then enforced "by a new order from Darius." JFB identifies Achmetha with Ecbatana, "the Hamadan of modern times," and notes that Darius's reign is well documented in the Behistun inscriptions. He reads the warning of v11 as aimed at the Samaritans. Guzik sees God's hand in a search that could easily have been dropped and suggests the measurements were limits rather than a design. He notes that the opposition backfired: the objectors' own letter brought royal funding to the work.

#### The Temple Finished and Dedicated (vv13–18)
- **"Did with all diligence what Darius the king had ordered" (v13).** "With all diligence" repeats Darius's own words in v12. Tattenai, to his credit, obeyed promptly and fully.
- **"Built and prospered through the prophesying of Haggai… and Zechariah" (v14).** The writer will not let the reader credit Persian funding alone. The prophets' words kept the people building. Haggai's promises of blessing and Zechariah's visions of God's return to Jerusalem (Zech 1–8) carried them through four more years of work.
- **"By decree of the God of Israel and by decree of Cyrus and Darius and Artaxerxes" (v14).** The same word, "decree," is used of God and of the kings. The heavenly decree came first, and the royal decrees served it. Artaxerxes I reigned later (465–424 BC), after the temple was finished. He is named because he later sent Ezra with gifts and support for the temple's worship (7:15–24). The writer, looking back, gathers all three Persian kings who aided God's house into one line.
- **"Finished on the third day of the month of Adar, in the sixth year of the reign of Darius" (v15).** Adar is the twelfth month (February–March). Most scholars date this to March 12, 515 BC. JFB gave February 21. The work had resumed in 520, so it took about four and a half years. From the destruction of 586 BC to the completion was about seventy years, the period Zechariah mentions when the angel asks how long God would withhold mercy "these seventy years" (Zech 1:12; compare Jer 25:11–12). This second temple, later enlarged by Herod, was the temple of Jesus' day. It stood until AD 70.
- **"Celebrated the dedication… with joy" (v16).** The word "dedication" is Aramaic *chanukkah*, the same word later used for the feast that celebrated the temple's rededication in 164 BC (John 10:22, "the Feast of Dedication"). Twenty-one years after the foundation's mixed sound of joy and weeping (3:12–13), the note now is pure joy. Many of the old men who wept had probably died.
- **"100 bulls, 200 rams, 400 lambs" (v17).** At Solomon's dedication, 22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep were sacrificed (1 Kgs 8:63). The difference shows how small and poor the community was. But the offering was made with joy, and it was what they had.
- **"As a sin offering for all Israel 12 male goats, according to the number of the tribes" (v17).** Most returnees were from Judah, Benjamin and Levi. Yet they offered for all twelve tribes. They did not think of themselves as a leftover fragment but as the representatives of the whole people of God, and they looked for all Israel's restoration (Ezek 37:15–22). The sin offering came first in the dedication because a holy house needs a cleansed people. The same twelve goats were offered when the exiles of Ezra's day arrived (8:35).
- **"They set the priests in their divisions… as it is written in the Book of Moses" (v18).** The Law of Moses set apart priests and Levites (Num 3; 8), and David organized them into rotating divisions or "courses" (1 Chr 23–24). Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, was serving in one of these divisions, "the division of Abijah," when the angel appeared to him (Luke 1:5, 8). The book's concern with doing things "as it is written" runs right through this chapter. This verse is the last in Aramaic.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of the finished building, "As for God, his work is perfect," and "it may be slow work, but it will be sure work." On the small dedication he writes, "These hundreds were more to them than Solomon's thousands were to him." Guzik makes the same comparison and adds that the twelve goats affirmed that the tribes were still one people. JFB notes that most of those who had wept at the foundation would by now have died, so the dedication was kept "with joy." Guzik suggests the mention of Artaxerxes reflects the writer completing the record of Persian support.

#### Passover Celebrated (vv19–22)
- **"On the fourteenth day of the first month" (v19).** Passover fell on 14 Nisan (March–April), about six weeks after the temple was finished (Exod 12:6; Lev 23:5). It remembered the night God spared Israel's firstborn and brought them out of Egypt. The first festival in the new temple was the festival of redemption. Like the return itself, it was a story of slaves set free.
- **"The priests and the Levites had purified themselves together; all of them were clean" (v20).** At Hezekiah's great Passover, not enough priests had purified themselves and the feast had to be delayed (2 Chr 30:3, 15–17). Here all were ready. The Levites slaughtered the lambs for the whole community, as they had under Hezekiah and Josiah (2 Chr 35:6, 11).
- **"Every one who had joined them and separated himself from the uncleanness of the peoples of the land" (v21).** This verse balances chapter 4. The community had refused a building partnership with people who worshiped the LORD alongside other gods (4:2–3). But anyone who truly turned from idols to the LORD was welcome at the Passover table. The dividing line was faith and worship, not birth. The Law made the same provision: a foreigner who was circumcised could keep the Passover "as a native of the land" (Exod 12:48). Rahab and Ruth had come in the same way.
- **"The Feast of Unleavened Bread seven days with joy" (v22).** This feast followed Passover directly (Exod 12:15–20; Lev 23:6–8). Leaven was removed from every home, a picture of putting away the old life and beginning a new one.
- **"The LORD had made them joyful" (v22).** Their joy was God's gift, not a mood they produced. The word "joy" frames the end of the chapter (vv16, 22).
- **"And had turned the heart of the king of Assyria to them" (v22).** Why "Assyria" for a Persian king? Darius ruled the lands that had once been Assyria, and kings often took the titles of the empires they absorbed. Cyrus is called "king of Babylon" in 5:13 the same way. But the name also carries meaning. Assyria was the empire that first scattered Israel (2 Kgs 17:6) and oppressed God's people (Neh 9:32). Now the ruler of Assyria's lands was helping to rebuild God's house. "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will" (Prov 21:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the priests were "purified together, as one man," and draws from v21 that those who turn to God sincerely, "though strangers and foreigners, are welcome to eat of the gospel feast." He credits God with "inclining the heart of the king of Persia to favour them thus." JFB says the proselytes of v21 would have been circumcised, as Exodus 12:48 requires, and explains "king of Assyria" as the king of the Persian empire. Guzik treats the title as a mark of the account's authenticity, and he sees in v21 a correction to any idea that the community was harshly closed to outsiders.

#### Pointing to Christ
The finished temple and its first Passover bring together the two great images of salvation in the Old Testament: God dwelling among his people, and a lamb whose blood spares them. Both find their fulfilment in Jesus. He is the true temple, where God's glory dwells (John 1:14; 2:19–21), and "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7). The twelve goats offered for all Israel by a small remnant point toward the one sin offering that would gather people from every tribe and nation (Rev 7:9). And the welcome at the Passover table for any outsider who turned from idols to the LORD foreshadows the gospel feast, where Gentiles who were once "strangers to the covenants of promise" are brought near by the blood of Christ and built together into "a holy temple in the Lord" (Eph 2:12–22). The temple of Ezra 6 was finished by decrees of earthly kings. The temple Christ is building will be finished by the decree of God.
