*Setting:* This chapter is the second half of the appendix that closes 2 Samuel (chapters 21–24). Chapter 22 was a song from David's younger years as king, looking back on God's rescues. Verses 1–7 are a shorter poem from the end of his life, called his "last words." They are not his literal final words (he gives Solomon instructions later, 1 Kings 2:1–9), but his last formal word as Israel's singer and king. Then comes a roll of honor: the elite warriors who fought with David from his outlaw days on. It matches the shorter list of giant-killers in 21:15–22. A fuller version of the list, with more names, appears in 1 Chronicles 11:10–47.

#### The Last Words of David (vv1–7)
- **"The oracle of David, the son of Jesse" (v1).** "Oracle" is *neum*, a solemn word normally used for a message from God: "declares the LORD." Outside that formula it appears for only a few men, most famously Balaam's oracles about a star and scepter rising from Jacob (Num 24:3–4, 15–17). David is not just reflecting on life. He is delivering prophecy.
- **Four titles (v1).** **"Son of Jesse"** recalls where he started, the youngest son of a Bethlehem farmer, out with the sheep (1 Sam 16:11). **"The man who was raised on high"** is what God did with him. **"The anointed of the God of Jacob"** goes back to the day Samuel poured the oil (1 Sam 16:13). **"The sweet psalmist of Israel"** may also be translated "the favorite of the songs of Israel" or "the beloved of Israel's songs." Either way, David is remembered by his songs. Nearly half the psalms carry his name.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD speaks by me; his word is on my tongue" (v2).** A clear claim to inspiration. Jesus treats David's words the same way: "David himself, in the Holy Spirit, declared" (Mark 12:36), and Peter calls David "a prophet" (Acts 2:30).
- **"The Rock of Israel has said to me" (v3).** The title from the song in chapter 22 (22:2–3, 32, 47).
- **"When one rules justly over men, ruling in the fear of God" (v3).** The Hebrew is terse, with no verbs: "a ruler over men, righteous, ruling in the fear of God." It can be read as a general rule (when a king rules justly…) or as a prophecy (there shall be one who rules justly). JFB notes that "many think this is a prophecy." Both readings point to the same ideal king, and David knew he had not fully been that king.
- **"He dawns on them like the morning light… like rain that makes grass to sprout" (v4).** A just ruler is like sunrise after night and rain on dry ground. In a land that lived or died by rain, this is the picture of a reign that gives life. Psalm 72, a prayer for the king, uses the same images (Ps 72:6). The prophets later describe the Messiah as "the sunrise… from on high" (Luke 1:78) and the "sun of righteousness" (Mal 4:2).
- **"For does not my house stand so with God?" (v5).** The Hebrew is hard. It could be a confident question (yes, my house is like that) or a humble admission ("although my house is not so with God," as the KJV reads it). After Amnon, Absalom and Adonijah, David knew his house fell short of v4. Either way, the next line gives the reason for his hope.
- **"He has made with me an everlasting covenant, ordered in all things and secure" (v5).** This is the promise of 2 Samuel 7: "your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever" (7:16). David's hope at the end of his life is not his record but God's promise. "Ordered" is a legal word, set out in detail like a contract. "Secure" means guarded, kept. Isaiah calls it "my steadfast, sure love for David" and offers it to all who come (Isa 55:3), and Paul quotes Isaiah's line about the resurrection of Jesus (Acts 13:34).
- **"Worthless men are all like thorns that are thrown away" (v6).** "Worthless" is *beliyyaal* again, the word for Sheba (20:1) and for the "torrents of destruction" (22:5). Thorns are useless and painful. No one picks them up by hand. You use an iron tool or a spear shaft and then burn them (v7). The righteous king's reign brings life to the land and judgment to those who oppose it (compare Ps 1:4–6; Matt 13:30).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "David avows his Divine inspiration, that the Spirit of God spake by him," and he "comforted himself that the Lord had made with him an everlasting covenant." Henry reads the covenant as the covenant of grace in the promised Savior, the ground of David's hope despite his failures. JFB thinks the covenant would hold despite David's troubled house "until the advent of his greatest Son, the Messiah." Guzik sees David's whole life in the four titles of v1 and makes the same point as Henry: David admits his house did not measure up, and he rests on God's faithfulness. Guzik also contrasts David's troubled but praised reign with Solomon's splendid but lightly praised one, and traces the difference to David's heart for God.

#### David's Mighty Men (vv8–39)
- **What the list is.** These were David's elite warriors. "The Three" were the top rank. "The Thirty" was a larger company of heroes, a kind of order of honor whose members were replaced as they died, which explains why the total comes to more than thirty (v39). Some of these men had joined David when he was a fugitive with "everyone who was in distress… in debt… bitter in soul" (1 Sam 22:2). God made heroes out of a band of misfits.
- **"Josheb-basshebeth a Tahchemonite" (v8).** The name means "one who sits in the seat," and it looks like a copying problem. In 1 Chronicles 11:11 he is "Jashobeam, a Hachmonite." The KJV's "Adino the Eznite" comes from a hard phrase later in the verse. **"Eight hundred whom he killed at one time"** is "three hundred" in Chronicles. Many scholars, and JFB, think 300 is original, because Abishai, who "did not attain to the three," is credited with 300 (v18). The text has suffered in copying here, but the point is plain: one man stood against hundreds.
- **Eleazar the son of Dodo (vv9–10).** When Israel's army withdrew, he stayed and fought **"until his hand was weary, and his hand clung to the sword."** His hand cramped so tightly around the hilt that it would not let go. Then comes the line that matters most: **"And the LORD brought about a great victory that day."** The soldiers who ran away came back afterward "only to strip the slain." Chronicles places this at Pas-dammim, where David fought Goliath (1 Chr 11:13; 1 Sam 17:1).
- **Shammah and the field of lentils (vv11–12).** Lentils were a staple crop. A field of them was a family's food for a year. Everyone else ran. Shammah stood in the middle of the field and held it. It was not a fortress or a capital, just a field, but it was Israel's ground. Again: **"the LORD worked a great victory."** Lehi is probably the same place where Samson once struck the Philistines with a donkey's jawbone (Judg 15:9–17).
- **Water from the well of Bethlehem (vv13–17).** This comes from early in David's reign, when the Philistines had moved into the Valley of Rephaim just southwest of Jerusalem (5:17–18) and had a garrison in Bethlehem itself, David's hometown. David was at the cave of Adullam, about 12 miles southwest. **"Oh, that someone would give me water to drink from the well of Bethlehem that is by the gate!"** It was a homesick wish, the taste of the water he drank as a boy. The Tyndale notes point out that the verb for "longingly" often means strong craving, but whether David's wish was wrong is not said.
- **"The three mighty men broke through the camp of the Philistines" (v16).** They heard a passing remark and treated it as a command. They fought their way into an occupied town and out again, carrying water.
- **"But he would not drink of it. He poured it out to the LORD" (v16).** Pouring out a drink offering was an act of worship (Gen 35:14; 1 Sam 7:6). **"Shall I drink the blood of the men who went at the risk of their lives?" (v17).** The law said blood belonged to God alone, because "the life of the flesh is in the blood" (Lev 17:11–14; Deut 12:23–24). To David this water was their lives, and only God was worthy of it. It was not ingratitude. It was the highest honor he could give them. Their love for him was so costly that he could not treat it as a drink.
- **Abishai "chief of the thirty" (vv18–19).** Joab's brother, who wanted to kill Saul in his sleep (1 Sam 26:8) and Shimei on the road (16:9), and who saved David from the giant (21:17). He was the most famous of the Thirty, **"but he did not attain to the three."** There were ranks even among heroes, and the text records them honestly.
- **Benaiah the son of Jehoiada (vv20–23).** He came from Kabzeel in the far south of Judah, and was the son of a priest according to 1 Chronicles 27:5. His deeds read like legends, and they are told in one breath. He killed **"two ariels of Moab."** The word *ariel* is uncertain. It may mean "lion of God," a term for champion warriors, or it may be a name. He went down into a pit and killed **a lion "on a day when snow had fallen,"** which made the footing treacherous and the lion trapped and desperate. He faced an Egyptian, "a man of great stature, five cubits tall" according to Chronicles (1 Chr 11:23), with only a club, took the spear out of his hand, and killed him with it, much as David killed Goliath with Goliath's own sword (1 Sam 17:51). **"David set him over his bodyguard"** (the Cherethites and Pelethites, 8:18; 20:23). Under Solomon he replaced Joab as army commander (1 Kings 2:35).
- **"Asahel the brother of Joab was one of the thirty" (v24).** He heads the list though he died early, killed by Abner (2:18–23). That suggests the list was first drawn up in David's early years at Hebron. **Elhanan son of Dodo of Bethlehem** is a different man from the Elhanan of 21:19.
- **Where the men came from (vv24–39).** Most are from Judah and Benjamin: Bethlehem, Tekoa, Anathoth (Jeremiah's later hometown), Netophah near Bethlehem, Gibeah of Saul. Some are foreigners. **Zelek the Ammonite** came from a nation David conquered (ch 10). **Igal the son of Nathan of Zobah** came from Aram (8:3). **Uriah the Hittite** was descended from the old peoples of Canaan. David's inner circle was open to outsiders who joined Israel's God.
- **"Eliam the son of Ahithophel the Gilonite" (v34).** Eliam was the father of Bathsheba (11:3). If it is the same Eliam, Bathsheba was the granddaughter of Ahithophel, David's counselor who joined Absalom's revolt (15:12). That may help explain why Ahithophel turned on David. It is not stated, but the family link is hard to ignore.
- **"Uriah the Hittite: thirty-seven in all" (v39).** The list ends with a name that stops the reader. Uriah was not an ordinary soldier. He was one of the Thirty, a member of David's elite, a man of loyalty and integrity (11:11). The list in Chronicles includes him in the middle and goes on to more names (1 Chr 11:41). Samuel places him last, where the eye lands. The writer has just praised David's faithful God and his faithful men. He does not let the reader forget what David did to one of the most faithful of them.
- **"Thirty-seven in all" (v39).** The names listed come to thirty-six if counted carefully. The Tyndale notes suggest Joab, the commander, is the missing thirty-seventh, mentioned only through his brothers and his armor-bearer (vv18, 24, 37) but never named as one of the heroes. Whether that silence reflects David's troubled relationship with Joab is a guess, but Joab's name is conspicuously missing from the honor roll.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls David's longing for the water a moment of weakness, but says he "poured out the water as a drink-offering to the Lord," denying himself. Henry applies it to Christ's blood, a far greater price paid for us: "Let all beware of neglecting so great salvation." Guzik dwells on the misfits who became mighty men, holds up Eleazar and Shammah as examples of faithfulness when others run (citing Spurgeon), and notes the irony of Uriah on the list, adding that David knew Bathsheba's family ties and should have stopped right there. JFB counts the total as the Three and the second order plus thirty-one others, remarks that two of them, Asahel and Uriah, "we know, were already dead," and suggests a "legion of honor" whose places were filled as men fell. JFB and Guzik (following Clarke) both prefer Chronicles' 300 in v8.

#### Pointing to Christ
David's last words picture a king who rules justly in the fear of God, like sunrise and rain, and then admit that David's own house was not that. His hope was a covenant, not his record. That covenant found its answer in Jesus, the Son of David who rules in perfect righteousness. And the scene at Bethlehem's well points beyond itself. David would not drink water bought with his men's blood. The Son of David did the reverse: he poured out his own blood and offers it to those who were not willing to die for him, saying "Drink of it, all of you" (Matt 26:27–28).
