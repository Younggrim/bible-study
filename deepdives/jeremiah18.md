*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably early in the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC). Chapters 18–20 belong together around one image, pottery. Here God sends Jeremiah to watch a potter at work, and teaches him that God is free to reshape a nation, for disaster or for good, depending on how it responds. In chapter 19 Jeremiah will smash a finished clay flask, and in chapter 20 he is beaten and put in the stocks for it. This chapter moves from the potter's wheel to the people's flat refusal, then to a fresh plot against Jeremiah and his fiercest prayer for vengeance, another of his "confessions" (11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18).

#### The Potter and the Clay (vv1–23)
- **"Arise, and go down to the potter's house" (v2).** The temple stood on high ground, so Jeremiah would "go down" into the lower city. Potters needed water and clay, and the potters' quarter was probably near the Hinnom or Kidron valley, near the Potsherd Gate (19:2). **"There I will let you hear my words."** God speaks through an ordinary workshop. Jeremiah has to go and watch before he hears.
- **"There he was working at his wheel" (v3).** The Hebrew is literally "on the two stones." A potter's wheel had two discs on one axle: the potter spun the lower wheel with his feet while shaping clay on the upper one. Wheels like this are pictured in Egyptian art and have been found in excavations in Israel. The same Hebrew word, "two stones," is used in Exodus 1:16 for a birthing stool, the only other place it appears.
- **"The vessel he was making of clay was spoiled in the potter's hand, and he reworked it into another vessel" (v4).** "Spoiled" is the same verb used for the ruined loincloth (13:7). The clay might have had a stone or air bubble in it, or simply collapsed. While clay is still soft, the potter can squash it and begin again. He does not throw it away. He makes "another vessel, as it seemed good to the potter."
- **"Like the clay in the potter's hand, so are you in my hand, O house of Israel" (v6).** God is the potter. The Hebrew word for potter, *yotser*, comes from the verb "to form," used when God "formed" Adam from the dust (Gen 2:7) and "formed" Jeremiah in the womb (1:5). Isaiah uses the same picture: "we are the clay, and you are our potter" (Isa 64:8; 29:16; 45:9).
- **"If at any time I declare concerning a nation… that I will pluck up and break down… and if that nation… turns from its evil, I will relent" (vv7–8).** These are the very verbs of Jeremiah's call (1:10): pluck up, break down, destroy, build, plant. This is one of the clearest statements in Scripture that God's announcements of judgment are often conditional. Jonah preached, "Forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown," and when Nineveh repented, "God relented of the disaster" (Jon 3:4, 10). Years later, elders at Jeremiah's trial remembered that King Hezekiah repented after Micah's warning, and "the LORD relented" (26:18–19).
- **"I will relent of the good that I had intended to do to it" (vv9–10).** The rule works both ways. A promise of blessing made to a nation does not guarantee blessing if the nation turns to evil. Judah was relying on God's promises to David and to the temple as if they were unconditional (7:4). They are warned that they are not.
- **What does "relent" mean?** The verb *nicham* means to change course, to be moved to pity, or to hold back. Some translations say "repent." It does not mean God made a mistake. Numbers 23:19 says God is not a man "that he should change his mind" in that sense. It means God's settled character responds to a change in people. He is always against evil and always merciful to the repentant, so when people change, his dealings with them change. Scripture speaks this way so that the warnings and offers are real, not theater.
- **The potter's freedom.** Paul takes up this image in Romans 9:20–21: "Has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honorable use and another for dishonorable use?" Here in Jeremiah the emphasis falls on God's freedom to respond to a nation's repentance or rebellion, while in Romans it falls on God's freedom in mercy. Christians have long debated how these fit together, Calvinist readers stressing God's sovereign choice and Arminian readers stressing the conditional pattern here. Both agree that the clay has no right to argue with the potter, and that the call to repent is sincere.
- **"Behold, I am shaping disaster against you" (v11).** "Shaping" is *yotser*, the same word as "potter." God, the potter, is already forming the next vessel, and it is disaster. **"Return, every one from his evil way."** And yet the clay is still soft. The call to turn is real.
- **"That is in vain! We will follow our own plans" (v12).** The people's answer is chilling. "In vain" (*noʾash*) means "it's hopeless," the same word they used in 2:25: "It is hopeless, for I have loved foreigners." It may be despair, or a defiant shrug: "Don't bother. We like our ways." Jeremiah has put into their mouths what their lives were saying. **"Every one act according to the stubbornness of his evil heart."** They know exactly what they are choosing.
- **"Ask among the nations, Who has heard the like of this?" (v13).** As in 2:10–11, even pagan nations do not abandon their gods, and their gods are nothing. Israel abandoned the living God. **"The virgin Israel has done a very horrible thing."** "Virgin" makes the betrayal worse: the bride God betrothed to himself.
- **"Does the snow of Lebanon leave the crags of Sirion?" (v14).** Sirion was the Sidonian name for Mount Hermon (Deut 3:9), the snow-capped peak at the north end of Lebanon's mountains. Its snows and the cold streams that flow from them can be counted on year after year. The Hebrew is difficult, and translations differ, but the point is clear. Nature keeps faith. God's people do not.
- **"My people have forgotten me; they make offerings to false gods" (v15).** "False gods" is literally "vanity" or "emptiness." **"They made them stumble in their ways, in the ancient roads, and to walk into side roads, not the highway."** The "ancient roads" are the old paths of faithfulness, the ones God urged them to ask for in 6:16. A "highway" in the ancient world was a built-up road, banked and cleared of stones. They left it for muddy, unmade tracks where people trip.
- **"Making their land a horror, a thing to be hissed at forever" (v16).** People in the ancient Near East whistled or hissed in shock at a ruin, as we might let out a low whistle. The Hebrew word, *sheriqot*, itself sounds like a hiss. **"Shakes his head"** is a gesture of astonishment and scorn (Lam 2:15). Jesus' enemies shook their heads at him on the cross (Matt 27:39).
- **"Like the east wind I will scatter them" (v17).** The east wind is the sirocco, the hot, dry desert wind that withers plants and drives dust before it (4:11). **"I will show them my back, not my face, in the day of their calamity."** The priestly blessing asked, "The LORD make his face to shine upon you… lift up his countenance upon you" (Num 6:25–26). Judah had turned its back on God (2:27; 7:24). Now God turns his back on Judah.
- **"Come, let us make plots against Jeremiah" (v18).** "Make plots" is the same verb God used in v11 ("devising a plan"). God devises judgment, and the people devise a plot against God's messenger. **"For the law shall not perish from the priest, nor counsel from the wise, nor the word from the prophet."** These were the three recognized sources of guidance in Israel: the priests taught the Law, the wise counseled the king, and the prophets brought God's word (Ezek 7:26). The plotters are saying, "We have plenty of religious leaders. We don't need Jeremiah." Jeremiah had predicted that all three would fail (4:9; 8:8–10).
- **"Let us strike him with the tongue" (v18).** They plan to bring him down with words: slander, accusations, maybe false testimony in court, as later happened (26:8–11). **"And let us not pay attention to any of his words."** The opposite of what God asked: "Hear me" is how Jeremiah's prayer begins (v19).
- **"Should good be repaid with evil? Yet they have dug a pit for my life" (v20).** A pit was a hunter's trap, dug and covered so prey would fall in. Later Jeremiah would literally be lowered into a muddy pit (38:6). **"Remember how I stood before you to speak good for them, to turn away your wrath from them."** Jeremiah had been praying for these very people, even when God told him not to (14:7–9, 19–22). David protested the same thing: "In return for my love they accuse me, but I give myself to prayer. So they reward me evil for good" (Ps 109:4–5).
- **"Therefore deliver up their children to famine" (vv21–22).** This is the hardest prayer in Jeremiah. He asks God to bring on his enemies' families the very disasters he had warned of: famine, sword, widowhood, childlessness and sudden attack. It is stark and should not be softened.
- **How should a believer read this?** Several things help. First, Jeremiah is not taking revenge. He brings his case to God, the righteous Judge, in words (11:20; 12:3; Rom 12:19). Second, he asks God to carry out the covenant curses God himself had already announced against the unrepentant (Deut 28; Jer 15:2–3). He has stopped standing in the gap and now agrees with God's verdict. Third, Scripture records his prayers honestly, as it does the psalms of lament (Ps 35; 69; 109; 137), without asking every reader to pray the same way. The Tyndale notes treat these as prayers for vengeance that leave justice to God. Many readers also note that Jesus, under even worse betrayal, prayed for his killers' forgiveness (Luke 23:34), showing the fuller way God's people are now called to walk (Matt 5:44).
- **"Forgive not their iniquity, nor blot out their sin from your sight" (v23).** Nehemiah prayed the same about his enemies (Neh 4:5). It is the reverse of the new covenant promise that Jeremiah would later deliver: "I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (31:34). The same prophet who prayed "forgive not" would be the one to announce the greatest promise of forgiveness in the Old Testament.
- **Commentators:** JFB describes the wheel as "two round plates," and says God's "relenting" adapts to human ways of speaking: "the change is not in God, but in the circumstances," as the land seems to move when a ship sails away from it. Guzik, following Cundall, treats this as a basic rule of prophecy, that God's promises and threats are conditional, and notes from Harrison that the verb for shaping in v11 shares the root of the word for potter. He also cautions that the analogy has limits, since clay has no moral choice and Israel did. Henry notes the irony that God said, "I devise a device against you," and the plotters answered by devising devices against his prophet, "as if they resolved to be quits with him and to outwit Infinite Wisdom itself." JFB connects the potter image with the potter's field bought with Judas's money (Matt 27:9–10), noting that Matthew names Jeremiah because Zechariah drew on Jeremiah 18–19.

#### Pointing to Christ
The potter does not throw away marred clay while it is still soft. He reworks it. That is the gospel's hope: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Cor 5:17), and God "who began a good work in you will bring it to completion" (Phil 1:6). Jeremiah was repaid evil for good by people he prayed for, and he asked God not to forgive them. Jesus was repaid evil for good by those he came to save, they too dug a pit for him and struck him with the tongue, and he prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). The potter imagery follows him to the end: the thirty pieces of silver paid for his betrayal bought a potter's field (Matt 27:7–10). There, in the place of the marred vessel, the price of his blood bought a burial ground for strangers, and through that blood forgiveness is now offered to all.
