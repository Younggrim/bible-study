*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Israel is camped in Moabite territory, near the very place where the Moabite king Balak hired Balaam to curse them and where Moabite women drew Israel into idolatry (Num 22–25). That memory is fresh as Moses opens this chapter with rules about who may "enter the assembly of the LORD." The chapter then moves to keeping the war camp clean because God walks in it, and closes with a string of laws about runaway slaves, cult prostitution, interest, vows and a hungry traveler's right to a handful of grain. (In Hebrew Bibles the verse numbers here run one higher, because the last verse of ESV chapter 22 is counted as 23:1.)

#### Those Excluded from the Assembly (vv1–8)
- **What is "the assembly of the LORD"?** The *qahal* was Israel gathered as God's people, as at Sinai on "the day of the assembly" (9:10; 18:16). What "enter" meant is debated. Some take it as full membership, including the right to marry an Israelite. JFB and Guzik, following older commentators, take it as holding public office or leading the nation. Others take it as entry into the sanctuary for worship. Whichever is right, it did not mean these people were shut out from God. Foreigners could live among Israel, keep the feasts (16:11, 14) and be cared for.
- **"No one whose testicles are crushed or whose male organ is cut off" (v1).** This means men who had been castrated, often as palace servants or in pagan cults where devotees mutilated themselves. The law guarded the assembly from those practices and kept bodily wholeness as a sign of holiness, as with the priests (Lev 21:17–23). Isaiah later gave the eunuch who held fast to God's covenant "a name better than sons and daughters… an everlasting name" in God's house (Isa 56:3–5). And one of the first non-Jewish converts in Acts is an Ethiopian eunuch, reading Isaiah, baptized on the road (Acts 8:26–39).
- **"No one born of a forbidden union" (v2).** The Hebrew word, *mamzer*, appears only here and in Zechariah 9:6. Its exact meaning is uncertain. The BDB lexicon defines it as a child of incest, and Jewish law later took it as the child of incest or adultery. Others think it means a child of a mixed marriage with a pagan, or of cult prostitution (see v17). **"Even to the tenth generation"** is a way of saying "permanently" (v3 adds "forever").
- **"No Ammonite or Moabite" (v3).** Both nations came from Lot's incest with his daughters (Gen 19:30–38). But the reason given is their conduct: **"they did not meet you with bread and with water"** (v4) when Israel passed through, and Moab **"hired against you Balaam"** (Num 22:4–6). Ammon is joined with Moab here, though Numbers names only Moab hiring Balaam. **"Pethor of Mesopotamia"** is literally "Aram of the two rivers," upper Mesopotamia near the Euphrates, about 400 miles away. Moab sent a long way for its curse.
- **"The LORD your God turned the curse into a blessing for you, because the LORD your God loved you" (v5).** Balaam opened his mouth to curse three times and blessed Israel every time (Num 23–24). Nehemiah quotes this verse almost word for word (Neh 13:2). God's love, not Israel's merit, stood between them and the curse (7:7–8).
- **"You shall not seek their peace or their prosperity" (v6).** No treaties of friendship. This was not a ban on personal kindness. 2:9 and 2:19 had forbidden Israel to attack Moab or Ammon.
- **What about Ruth?** Ruth was a Moabite who joined Israel, married Boaz and became the great-grandmother of David (Ruth 4:13–22). The rabbis argued that the law excluded Moabite men, not women, since it was men who failed to bring food and water. Others note that Ruth joined herself fully to Israel's God ("your God my God," Ruth 1:16), and that the story shows God's grace reaching past the law's boundary. After the exile, Nehemiah read this law aloud and Israel separated "all those of foreign descent" (Neh 13:1–3), and Nehemiah confronted men who had married women of Ammon and Moab (Neh 13:23–27).
- **"You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (v7).** Edom came from Esau, Jacob's twin (Gen 36:1). Edom had also refused Israel passage (Num 20:14–21), but kinship counts. **"You shall not abhor an Egyptian, because you were a sojourner in his land."** This is remarkable. Egypt enslaved Israel and drowned their sons. But Egypt had also fed Jacob's family in the famine and been their home for centuries. Israel was not to hate them.
- **"Children born to them in the third generation may enter the assembly" (v8).** For Edomites and Egyptians, the door opened after three generations. The difference from Ammon and Moab is that the last two had acted with deliberate hostility against God's people on the way to the land.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "No personal blemishes, no crimes of our forefathers, no difference of nation, shuts us out under the Christian dispensation." JFB says "the tenth generation" uses "a definite… for an indefinite number," and reads "enter" as receiving public honors and offices or marrying into Israel. Guzik follows the same reading and points to Ruth as proof that a Moabite could be fully welcomed among God's people. He also notes the Ethiopian eunuch and Isaiah 56, and records that Herod the Great, an Edomite, was never accepted by many Jews.

#### Uncleanness in the Camp (vv9–14)
- **"When you are encamped against your enemies, then you shall keep yourself from every evil thing" (v9).** War camps have always been places of drunkenness, theft and sexual license, and armies of the time often took prostitutes with them. Israel's camp was to be different. JFB applies it to "the excesses incident to camp life."
- **"If any man among you becomes unclean because of a nocturnal emission" (v10).** This is not a sin. Leviticus 15:16 treats any emission of semen as making a man ceremonially unclean until evening. In the war camp, he had to step outside, **"bathe himself in water, and as the sun sets, he may come inside"** (v11). When David asked for bread from the priest at Nob, the priest's question was whether his men had kept themselves from women, and David said they always did on a mission (1 Sam 21:4–5). Uriah refused to go home to his wife while the army was in the field (2 Sam 11:11).
- **"You shall have a trowel with your tools… dig a hole… and cover up your excrement" (vv12–13).** A latrine outside the camp and a small digging tool in every soldier's kit. This is basic hygiene that protected the army from disease, well before anyone knew about germs. The armies of later centuries lost more men to camp sickness than to battle.
- **"Because the LORD your God walks in the midst of your camp" (v14).** The reason is not hygiene but holiness. The verb "walks" (*hithallekh*) is the word for God walking in the garden (Gen 3:8) and his promise, "I will walk among you and will be your God" (Lev 26:12). The camp is God's dwelling, **"to deliver you and to give up your enemies before you."** **"So that he may not see anything indecent among you and turn away from you."** "Indecent" is literally "nakedness of a thing," the same odd phrase used for the grounds of divorce in 24:1. If God saw filth in his camp, he might withdraw his help, and Israel would lose the battle.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The camp of the Lord must have nothing offensive in it," and "If there must be this care taken to preserve the body clean, much more should we be careful to keep the mind pure." Guzik separates the ceremonial uncleanness of vv10–11 from the plain sanitation of vv12–13, and quotes Spurgeon praising the wisdom of the rule. The Tyndale notes connect "indecent" with the biblical concern that nakedness be covered (Exod 20:26).

#### Miscellaneous Laws (vv15–25)
- **"You shall not give up to his master a slave who has escaped from his master to you" (v15).** This most likely means a slave who has fled from a foreign country into Israel. Ancient law codes usually did the opposite. The Code of Hammurabi made sheltering a runaway slave a crime punishable by death, and treaties between kings often required the return of fugitives. Israel was to be a refuge. **"He shall dwell with you… in the place that he shall choose… wherever it suits him"** (v16). The language echoes God choosing the place for his name (12:5). Here the escaped slave gets to choose. **"You shall not wrong him."** This is the word used for oppressing the sojourner (Exod 22:21). A nation God had freed from slavery was not to send slaves back to it.
- **"None of the daughters of Israel shall be a cult prostitute" (v17).** The words are *qedeshah* and *qadesh*, from the root *qadosh*, "holy." Canaanite religion used "holy ones" in its shrines, and sex was part of its fertility worship. God's holiness is the opposite. Israel did not keep this law well. Rehoboam's Judah had male cult prostitutes, and Asa, Jehoshaphat and Josiah each had to remove them again (1 Kgs 14:24; 15:12; 22:46; 2 Kgs 23:7). Some scholars now question how widespread temple prostitution really was in the ancient Near East, but the Bible is clear that some form of it was practiced in Israel's own land.
- **"The fee of a prostitute or the wages of a dog" (v18).** "Dog" is a term of contempt, and in this context it most likely means a male prostitute (compare Rev 22:15). God would not take money earned this way, even if it came with a vow. A gift cannot clean the way it was earned. Micah later describes Samaria's idols as bought with "the fee of a prostitute" (Mic 1:7).
- **"You shall not charge interest on loans to your brother" (v19).** "Interest" is *neshek*, which the BDB lexicon explains as "something bitten off." A loan to a fellow Israelite was meant to help someone in need, usually a poor farmer short of food or seed (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:35–37). Charging him interest would take a bite out of what little he had. Interest rates in the ancient Near East commonly ran from 20 to 50 percent. **"Interest on food"**: lending grain and demanding back more grain. Nehemiah forced the wealthy to give back the interest they had taken from their struggling brothers (Neh 5:1–13), and Psalm 15:5 describes the one who may dwell with God as one "who does not put out his money at interest."
- **"You may charge a foreigner interest" (v20).** The *nokri* was usually a foreign trader in Israel on business (see 15:3). A business loan between merchants was a different thing from relief for a struggling neighbor. Christians have long debated how this applies today. The medieval church banned most interest, and later Reformers like Calvin argued that the law forbids exploiting the poor rather than every business loan.
- **"If you make a vow… you shall not delay fulfilling it" (v21).** A vow was a voluntary promise to give God something, often in return for help (Gen 28:20–22; 1 Sam 1:11). **"But if you refrain from vowing, you will not be guilty of sin"** (v22). No one was required to vow. **"Be careful to do what has passed your lips"** (v23). Ecclesiastes says almost the same: "It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not pay" (Eccl 5:4–5). Jephthah's rash vow shows the danger of promising carelessly (Judg 11:30–40).
- **"You may eat your fill of grapes… but you shall not put any in your bag" (v24).** A hungry person walking past a vineyard could eat on the spot. He could not fill a basket. **"You may pluck the ears with your hand, but you shall not put a sickle"** (v25). The law balanced the owner's rights with the needs of the hungry. Fields were open, not fenced. This is what Jesus' disciples were doing when they plucked grain on the Sabbath (Matt 12:1–2; Luke 6:1). The Pharisees did not accuse them of theft, because this law allowed it. They objected to doing it on the Sabbath.
- **Commentators:** Henry on the runaway slave: "It is honourable to shelter and protect the weak, provided they are not wicked," and on the grapes, the law "forbids us to abuse the kindness of friends, or to take advantage of what is allowed." JFB thinks the runaway was probably a foreigner fleeing oppression or seeking the true religion, and calls the grapes and grain "a kindly privilege afforded to the poor and wayfaring man." JFB adds no note on vv17–18. Guzik argues that Jesus' teaching on oaths (Matt 5:34–37) forbids careless swearing rather than every solemn promise, since Jesus himself answered under oath (Matt 26:63–64), and he notes the Pharisees' complaint in Luke 6 was about the Sabbath, not theft.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter keeps some people out of the assembly, and the prophets saw the day God would open the doors. Isaiah promised the eunuch and the foreigner a place in God's house: "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isa 56:7). Jesus quoted those words as he cleared the temple (Mark 11:17). Through him a Moabite's descendant became king, an Ethiopian eunuch was baptized, and Gentiles who were "far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Eph 2:13). The God who "walks in the midst of your camp" came to walk among us in person (John 1:14), and now dwells by his Spirit in his people, who are called to be holy because he is there (2 Cor 6:16). And the escaped slave who finds refuge and may live "wherever it suits him" is a picture of what Christ does: "if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36).
