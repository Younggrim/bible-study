*Setting:* Jerusalem, 609–608 BC, "in the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim." Josiah had just been killed at Megiddo, his son Jehoahaz had been deported to Egypt after three months, and Pharaoh Neco had put Jehoiakim on the throne as his puppet. Josiah's reforms were coming apart. This chapter tells what happened when Jeremiah preached the temple sermon whose content is given more fully in chapter 7 (7:1–15). Chapter 7 gives the sermon, and chapter 26 gives the reaction. It also opens a new part of the book (chs 26–29) about the clash between Jeremiah and the prophets who said Jerusalem was safe. The chapter is a trial scene: a charge, a defense, a verdict, a legal precedent, and a warning of what could have happened.

#### Jeremiah Threatened with Death (vv1–15)
- **"In the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim" (v1).** This seems to be a technical phrase for a king's accession year, the months between taking the throne and the first New Year. Here that means late 609 or early 608 BC. Chapter 25, from 605 BC, is about four years later, so the book has stepped back in time.
- **"Stand in the court of the LORD's house, and speak to all the cities of Judah" (v2).** People from all over Judah came to the temple for the great festivals. Jeremiah was sent where the biggest crowd would be. **"Do not hold back a word."** Literally "do not diminish a word." It is the same verb as in Deuteronomy 4:2: "You shall not add to the word that I command you, nor take from it."
- **"It may be they will listen… that I may relent" (v3).** God knew the people's hearts, yet the offer was real. "Relent" is *nacham*, to change course or to be moved to pity. It is the principle Jeremiah learned at the potter's house: if a nation God has warned turns from its evil, he will relent (18:7–10). Nineveh is the classic example (Jonah 3:10).
- **"To walk in my law that I have set before you" (v4).** The standard is the covenant law of Sinai, not Jeremiah's own opinions.
- **"My servants the prophets whom I send to you urgently" (v5).** Literally "rising early and sending," the same idiom as 25:4.
- **"I will make this house like Shiloh" (v6).** Shiloh, about twenty miles north of Jerusalem, was where the tabernacle and the ark stood in the days of the judges (Josh 18:1; 1 Sam 1:3). When Israel carried the ark into battle as a lucky charm, the Philistines captured it, and Shiloh seems to have been destroyed (1 Sam 4; Ps 78:60–61). Excavations suggest a destruction there around the eleventh century BC, though the details are still debated. The point was clear to everyone: God's house had been destroyed before. Its presence was no guarantee (7:12–14).
- **"I will make this city a curse for all the nations" (v6).** Its name would be used in curses: "May you end up like Jerusalem" (24:9).
- **"The priests and the prophets and all the people laid hold of him, saying, 'You shall die!'" (v8).** Jeremiah's opponents are the religious establishment. The prophets here are the professional temple prophets of chapter 23. The Law ordered death for a prophet who spoke in God's name what God had not commanded (Deut 18:20). That is the charge: he must be a false prophet, because the LORD would never destroy his own house.
- **"Why have you prophesied in the name of the LORD…?" (v9).** To them, saying the temple could fall was blasphemy against the LORD himself. They believed that God's choice of Zion meant it could never fall (Ps 46:5; 132:13–14), and the rescue of Jerusalem from Sennacherib in 701 BC seemed to prove it.
- **"The officials of Judah… came up from the king's house" (v10).** The palace sat just south of and below the temple, so they "came up." These officials (*sarim*) were the king's ministers, and serious charges came under their authority. **"The entry of the New Gate."** The gate was the usual place for a trial (Deut 21:19; Ruth 4:1). This gate is mentioned again in 36:10, near the room of Gemariah son of Shaphan, the brother of Ahikam in v24.
- **"This man deserves the sentence of death" (v11).** The priests and prophets now act as prosecutors before the officials. "You have heard with your own ears." Jeremiah's own words are the evidence.
- **"The LORD sent me" (v12).** His whole defense rests on this. He does not deny what he said. He claims God's authority for it. He repeats it at the end of v15, so the defense opens and closes with the same words.
- **"Now therefore mend your ways and your deeds" (v13).** Even on trial for his life, he preaches. The sentence on the city can still be lifted.
- **"Behold, I am in your hands. Do with me as seems good and right to you" (v14).** He does not resist or run. He submits to the court, but he will not take back the message.
- **"You will bring innocent blood upon yourselves and upon this city" (v15).** Shedding innocent blood brought guilt on a whole community (Deut 19:10; 21:8–9). Jeremiah warns that killing him would not stop the judgment. It would only add to the guilt.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "God's ambassadors must keep closely to their instructions," and that "those that persecute God's ministers hurt not them so much as themselves." JFB says Jeremiah spoke "not suppressing or softening aught for fear of giving offense." He also notes that in v12 Jeremiah uses a word that can be read as "concerning" rather than "against," avoiding needless offense. That reading is possible, but the ESV's "against" is the more natural sense. Guzik, citing Kidner, sees this as a fuller account of the temple sermon in chapter 7. He compares Jeremiah's stand to Peter and John's "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 4:19–20; 5:29). He also cites Meyer, who observes that the boldness of a man who was naturally shy and sensitive shows the work of God's grace.

#### Jeremiah Spared from Death (vv16–24)
- **"This man does not deserve the sentence of death, for he has spoken to us in the name of the LORD our God" (v16).** The officials rule for the defense. Notice "all the people." In v8 "all the people" seized him and shouted "You shall die!" Now "all the people" side with the officials. Crowds are easily swayed. The religious leaders were the most hostile, and the secular officials were more open.
- **"Certain of the elders of the land arose" (v17).** Older men, probably landowners from the country towns, who knew their history. They cite a precedent, like lawyers.
- **"Micah of Moresheth prophesied in the days of Hezekiah" (v18).** Micah came from Moresheth-gath, a village in the foothills southwest of Jerusalem (Mic 1:1, 14), and prophesied about a century earlier. The elders quote Micah 3:12 word for word: **"Zion shall be plowed as a field… and the mountain of the house a wooded height."** This is one of the very few places where the Old Testament quotes an earlier prophet word for word and by name, and it shows that Micah's words had been treasured and remembered.
- **"Did Hezekiah… put him to death? Did he not fear the LORD…?" (v19).** Hezekiah responded to Micah's warning by repenting and praying, and the disaster was put off (2 Chr 32:26). The elders' argument is that a prophet who predicts Zion's ruin is not automatically a false prophet. Micah did, and he was right to. And the right response to such a word is repentance, not execution. **"But we are about to bring great disaster upon ourselves."** Killing Jeremiah would be self-destruction.
- **Did Micah's prophecy fail?** No. Like Jonah's warning to Nineveh, it was conditional. Hezekiah's repentance delayed it. Jeremiah's generation would see it fulfilled, and Lamentations 5:18 mourns "Mount Zion which lies desolate; jackals prowl over it."
- **"Uriah the son of Shemaiah from Kiriath-jearim" (v20).** He is mentioned nowhere else. Kiriath-jearim was a town about eight miles west of Jerusalem, where the ark had rested for twenty years before David brought it to Jerusalem (1 Sam 7:1–2). Uriah preached "in words like those of Jeremiah," so Jeremiah was not alone.
- **"He was afraid and fled and escaped to Egypt" (v21).** Whether this was cowardice or prudence is debated. Jesus told his disciples to flee from persecution (Matt 10:23), and Elijah fled from Jezebel. But in Egypt Uriah was in the territory of Jehoiakim's overlord.
- **"King Jehoiakim sent to Egypt certain men, Elnathan the son of Achbor" (v22).** Jehoiakim was Pharaoh Neco's vassal (2 Kgs 23:34–35), and treaties of the time often required vassals and overlords to hand over fugitives, so Egypt sent Uriah back. Elnathan's father Achbor had been one of Josiah's officials (2 Kgs 22:12). Elnathan later urged Jehoiakim not to burn Jeremiah's scroll (36:12, 25). He may also be the Elnathan whose daughter Nehushta was Jehoiakim's wife (2 Kgs 24:8). If so, the king sent his own father-in-law on this errand.
- **"Who struck him down with the sword and dumped his dead body into the burial place of the common people" (v23).** This is the "innocent blood" of 22:17. The public graves were probably in the Kidron Valley (2 Kgs 23:6). Denying a prophet an honorable burial was a final insult. Jesus later spoke of Jerusalem as the city "that kills the prophets" (Matt 23:37), and of "the blood of all the prophets" (Luke 11:50–51).
- **Why tell Uriah's story here?** It shows how serious Jeremiah's danger was. Jeremiah survived because God preserved him through human means, not because the king was gentle. It also undercuts any idea that the people "listened" (v3). They spared one prophet and killed another for the same message.
- **"But the hand of Ahikam the son of Shaphan was with Jeremiah" (v24).** Ahikam's father Shaphan was the royal secretary who read the rediscovered Book of the Law to Josiah (2 Kgs 22:8–10). Ahikam was in the delegation Josiah sent to the prophetess Huldah (2 Kgs 22:12–14). Shaphan's family stood by Jeremiah for decades. His son Gemariah's room was where Baruch read the scroll (36:10). Another son, Elasah, carried Jeremiah's letter to Babylon (29:3). Ahikam's son Gedaliah would be made governor after the fall and would protect Jeremiah (39:14; 40:5–6). A clay seal impression reading "belonging to Gemaryahu son of Shaphan" was found in the City of David. Many think it belonged to this Gemariah, though the identification is not certain.
- **Commentators:** Henry observes, "There are many that have much grace, but they have little courage, that are very honest, but withal very timorous," and that "God can, when he pleases, raise up great men to patronize good men." JFB calls the crowd "the fickle people," moved first by the priests and then by the princes. He compares the elders to Gamaliel (Acts 5:34–39). He judges Uriah "faulty in leaving his work," and adds that "the path of duty is often the path of safety." That is a harder verdict than the text gives. Guzik cites Trapp's view that fleeing was prudence rather than cowardice, so the commentators differ here. Guzik also cites Feinberg on extradition clauses in vassal treaties, and Harrison on Ahikam's family. He notes that Trapp says these same officials later turned on Jeremiah (37:15; 38:4).

#### Pointing to Christ
Six centuries later another Prophet stood in the temple courts and said it would be destroyed. Jesus too was seized by priests and brought to trial, and the charge was that he had spoken against "this temple" (Mark 14:58). Like Jeremiah, he did not defend himself by softening his words, and he placed himself in his judges' hands. Pilate, like the officials, found "no guilt in him" (John 18:38), but this time the crowd was not turned. "His blood be on us and on our children!" (Matt 27:25) is the very thing Jeremiah warned against in v15. No Ahikam stood between Jesus and death. He was given over so that the "great disaster" sinners bring on themselves could fall on him instead. And where Uriah's body was dumped among the common graves, Jesus was laid in a rich man's tomb (Isa 53:9; Matt 27:57–60) and rose from it.
