*Setting:* This is Paul's last letter, written from prison in Rome, probably around AD 64–67, not long before his execution under Nero. Unlike his first Roman imprisonment, when he rented his own lodgings (Acts 28:30), this time he is chained like a criminal (1:16; 2:9) and expects to die (4:6). Timothy is still leading the church at Ephesus, where false teachers have been upsetting the faith of some (2:17–18). Chapter 2 told Timothy to be a soldier, an athlete and a farmer, to rightly handle "the word of truth" (2:15), and, as the Lord's servant, to correct opponents gently in the hope that God would grant them repentance (2:24–26). Chapter 3 explains why that patient work will be hard: the age itself is difficult. Paul paints the false teachers and their kind, then points Timothy to two anchors that will hold him steady: Paul's own life and the Scriptures he has known since childhood.

#### Godlessness in the Last Days (vv1–9)
- **"But understand this" (v1).** Paul has just spoken of hoping opponents repent (2:25). Now he adds a sober warning so Timothy won't be surprised when many don't.
- **"In the last days" (v1).** In the New Testament the "last days" are not only the final years before Christ returns. They began with Jesus' first coming and the outpouring of the Spirit (Acts 2:16–17; Heb 1:2; 1 John 2:18). Paul is not describing a far-off future only. He tells Timothy to "avoid such people" (v5), which only makes sense if they were already around. Many readers also see the pattern growing more intense as the end draws near, as Jesus warned (Matt 24:12).
- **"Times of difficulty" (v1).** The adjective *chalepos* means hard to deal with or hard to bear. Its only other New Testament use describes the two demon-possessed men of the Gadarenes, so "fierce" that no one could pass that way (Matt 8:28). These are savage, dangerous times, and the danger Paul goes on to describe is moral and spiritual, not war or famine.
- **"Lovers of self, lovers of money" (v2).** The list of about nineteen vices opens with two "lover" words (*philautoi*, *philargyroi*) and closes with two more: "lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God" (*philēdonoi*, *philotheoi*, v4). The frame tells the story. Love has been turned in the wrong direction, away from God and neighbour and back toward self, money and pleasure. Self-love comes first because it feeds everything that follows. Jesus summed up the law as love for God and neighbour (Matt 22:37–39); this is that law turned inside out.
- **"Proud, arrogant, abusive, disobedient to their parents, ungrateful, unholy" (v2).** "Abusive" is *blasphēmoi*, people who speak evil of others and of God. "Disobedient to their parents" sounds small beside the rest, but it breaks the fifth commandment and is in Paul's similar list in Romans 1:30. "Ungrateful" and "unholy" belong together: a person who gives no thanks for what they have received has stopped recognizing anything as sacred.
- **"Heartless" (v3).** The word is *astorgos*, "without *storgē*," the natural affection of family, the love of parents for children and children for parents. Its only other use is Romans 1:31. Paul pictures people who no longer feel the ties that even pagans honoured.
- **"Unappeasable, slanderous, without self-control, brutal, not loving good" (v3).** "Unappeasable" is someone who won't make peace or keep a truce. "Slanderous" is *diaboloi*, the same word as "devil," the accuser. Paul uses it of women in 1 Timothy 3:11 and Titus 2:3. "Without self-control" and "brutal" sit side by side. JFB notes the pairing: people soft toward their own appetites, hard toward everyone else.
- **"Treacherous, reckless, swollen with conceit" (v4).** "Treacherous" is the word for a betrayer, used of Judas (Luke 6:16). "Swollen with conceit" is the verb Paul used of the would-be overseer who must not be a new convert in case he "become puffed up with conceit" (1 Tim 3:6).
- **"Having the appearance of godliness, but denying its power" (v5).** This is the sting in the list. These people are not pagans outside the church but religious people inside it. "Appearance" is *morphōsis*, outline or semblance. Its only other use is Romans 2:20, where Paul describes the Jew who has "the embodiment of knowledge and truth" in the law but doesn't keep it. The shape is there; the substance isn't. The "power" of godliness is the power of God that changes a life, the Spirit's work of new birth and holiness (1:7–8). They have the vocabulary and the rituals, but they deny by how they live the very power they claim.
- **"Avoid such people" (v5).** This seems to sit uneasily beside 2:24–26, which tells Timothy to correct opponents gently. Both are true. Timothy keeps teaching and hoping for repentance, but he does not let such people shape him or lead the church (compare 1 Cor 15:33; Rom 16:17).
- **"Those who creep into households" (v6).** "Creep" is *endynō*, to slip in or worm one's way in. The false teachers worked privately, house by house, rather than in the open assembly where elders could answer them. Churches in this period met in homes, so a household was both a family and a congregation.
- **"Capture weak women" (v6).** "Capture" is the verb for taking a prisoner of war. "Weak women" is *gynaikaria*, a diminutive of the word for woman, "little women," used with contempt: the KJV's "silly women." Paul is not saying women in general are gullible. He describes a particular group, burdened by guilt and pulled by "various passions," whose troubled consciences made them easy prey. The Pastoral Letters return to this pattern again and again: false teaching upsetting households and drawing in some younger widows (1 Tim 5:13–15; Titus 1:11). In that culture women of the household were at home during the day when traveling teachers called, and some had wealth to support them.
- **"Always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (v7).** They love new ideas and endless discussion, but never settle on the truth that would save them. "Knowledge" here is *epignōsis*, recognition and full knowledge, the same phrase Paul used in 2:25: God may grant opponents "repentance leading to a knowledge of the truth." The problem is not lack of information but an unwillingness to submit to what they learn.
- **"Just as Jannes and Jambres opposed Moses" (v8).** The Old Testament never names Pharaoh's magicians, who copied Moses' first signs by their "secret arts" (Exod 7:11, 22; 8:7). Jewish tradition gave them names. The Dead Sea Scrolls' Damascus Document speaks of Belial raising up "Yohanah and his brother" against Moses and Aaron. The Targum (an Aramaic paraphrase) of Exodus names Jannes and Jambres, and even the Roman writer Pliny the Elder mentions Jannes as a magician connected with Moses. A Jewish book about them, now surviving only in fragments, was known to early Christian writers. Paul uses names his readers recognized, the way a preacher today might use a well-known name from tradition. The comparison fits well: the false teachers of Ephesus, a city famous for magic (Acts 19:19), are like the court magicians who could imitate God's power for a while.
- **"Men corrupted in mind and disqualified regarding the faith" (v8).** "Disqualified" is *adokimos*, failing the test, like a coin rejected as counterfeit. It is the opposite of the "approved" worker of 2:15 (*dokimos*).
- **"They will not get very far" (v9).** Pharaoh's magicians kept up for a while, but by the third plague they could not copy the gnats and confessed, "This is the finger of God" (Exod 8:18–19). By the sixth plague they could not even stand before Moses because of their boils (Exod 9:11). Error has a ceiling. It may spread for a time (2:17), but God sets a limit, and in the end its emptiness is "plain to all."
- **Commentators:** Henry opens the chapter with Paul telling Timothy "how bad others would be, and therefore how good he should be." He insists that "sin makes the times perilous," not trouble from outside, and that "self-love brings in a long train of sins and mischiefs." He also sharply distinguishes the shape from the substance: "A form of godliness is a very different thing from the power of it." Guzik likewise makes self-love the root sin of the list and stresses that the danger is moral. He reads "power" as God's rightful authority over a life, and draws on 1 Corinthians 15:33 to explain why Paul says to turn away. On v6 JFB explains that a burdened conscience makes people easy prey, and that the "passions" in view include a craving for novel doctrine, not only bodily appetite. JFB traces the names Jannes and Jambres to "the unwritten teaching of the Jews," and notes the plagues that turned on the magicians themselves. Guzik adds that the magicians' signs were real works of dark power, not just tricks, a reminder that spiritual power is not automatically from God. That goes a little beyond the text, which says only that they did the same "by their secret arts."

#### All Scripture Is Breathed Out by God (vv10–17)
- **"You, however" (v10).** The Greek puts "you" first for emphasis. After the dark portrait, Paul turns to Timothy: whatever others are, he must be different. The same contrast comes again in v14 ("But as for you") and in 4:5.
- **"Have followed my teaching, my conduct, my aim in life" (v10).** "Followed" is *parakoloutheō*, to follow closely, trace out, keep company with. Luke uses it of having "followed all things closely" in his research (Luke 1:3). Timothy had not just heard Paul's sermons. He had watched how Paul lived for some fifteen years. The list mixes belief and life on purpose: teaching, conduct, purpose, faith, patience, love, steadfastness. The false teachers had a form without power. Paul offers a life where doctrine and behaviour match.
- **"At Antioch, at Iconium, and at Lystra" (v11).** These were the towns of Paul's first missionary journey in the region of Galatia (central Turkey today). At Pisidian Antioch he was driven out of the district (Acts 13:50). At Iconium there was a plot to stone him (Acts 14:5). At Lystra he was stoned and dragged out of the city for dead (Acts 14:19). Lystra was Timothy's home town (Acts 16:1–3). He may well have seen those wounds as a young man, or heard about them from his mother and grandmother, before Paul recruited him on the next journey. Paul chooses the persecutions Timothy knew best.
- **"Yet from them all the Lord rescued me" (v11).** This echoes Psalm 34:19: "Many are the afflictions of the righteous, but the LORD delivers him out of them all." Rescue did not mean avoiding suffering. Paul was stoned, but he got up and went back into the city (Acts 14:20). He will say the same about his trial in Rome: "the Lord stood by me… I was rescued from the lion's mouth," and he will be brought safely into the heavenly kingdom even through death (4:17–18).
- **"All who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted" (v12).** Not only apostles. Jesus said the same: "If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you" (John 15:20). Paul had taught these very churches that "through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God" (Acts 14:22). "Desire" matters: it is the settled will to live this way. "In Christ Jesus" matters too: the godliness that provokes opposition is life in union with Christ, not mere respectability. Persecution takes many forms, from prison and death to quieter mockery and exclusion.
- **"Evil people and impostors will go on from bad to worse" (v13).** "Impostors" is *goētes*. The word originally meant a wailer, then a sorcerer who chanted spells, and then simply a cheat or fraud. In Ephesus, the city where magic books had been burned (Acts 19:19), the word carries a hint of the occult that fits the comparison with Pharaoh's magicians. "Go on from bad to worse" is ironic: Paul uses the same verb (*prokoptō*, to advance) that he used in v9 ("will not get very far") and in 2:16. They make progress, but only downward.
- **"Deceiving and being deceived" (v13).** The deceiver is himself deceived. Sincerity is no proof of truth, and anyone who leads others astray is the first victim of their own error.
- **"Continue in what you have learned and have firmly believed" (v14).** The false teachers were always learning and never arriving (v7). Timothy is to stay where he already stands. "Continue" (*menō*, remain) is the word John uses for abiding in Christ (John 15:4).
- **"Knowing from whom you learned it" (v14).** The ESV footnote points out that "whom" is plural in Greek. Timothy learned from more than one teacher: Paul, and before Paul, his grandmother Lois and mother Eunice, whose "sincere faith" Paul praised in 1:5. The character of the teachers is a reason to trust the teaching. Timothy had seen their lives up close, which could not be said of the newcomers.
- **"From childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings" (v15).** "Childhood" is *brephos*, a word used even of a baby (Luke 2:12; 18:15). Timothy's father was a Greek (Acts 16:1), but his Jewish mother and grandmother taught him the Hebrew Scriptures from his earliest years. Jewish families were expected to teach the law to their children (Deut 6:6–7). "Sacred writings" means the Old Testament.
- **"Able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (v15).** The Old Testament itself points to Christ (Luke 24:27, 44; John 5:39). But the Scriptures do not save by mere familiarity. They make a person wise for salvation "through faith in Christ Jesus." Timothy had known the writings from infancy, but it was through faith in the one they spoke of that they became wisdom for salvation.
- **"All Scripture is breathed out by God" (v16).** The key word is *theopneustos*, made from *theos* (God) and *pneō* (to breathe). It occurs only here in the New Testament, and the lexicon gives "inspired by God" or "God-breathed." The English word "inspired" can suggest God breathing *into* human writers or making their work especially moving. Paul's word says something stronger: Scripture is breathed *out* by God, as his own speech. Peter describes the human side: "men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Pet 1:21). The writers were not typists. Their personalities, vocabulary and settings show, but what they wrote is what God says.
- **"All" or "every"? (v16).** Grammatically the phrase can be read two ways. Most translations read "All Scripture is God-breathed and profitable." A few read "Every God-breathed Scripture is also profitable," which could suggest some Scripture is not God-breathed. The Greek has no verb, and most scholars agree the first reading is more natural, since the two adjectives are joined by "and." Either way, Paul has just called these writings "sacred" (v15), so he is not hinting that some are less inspired. Paul was speaking first of the Old Testament, but the New Testament already treats apostolic writings as Scripture (1 Tim 5:18 quotes Luke 10:7 alongside Deuteronomy; 2 Pet 3:16 places Paul's letters among "the other Scriptures").
- **"Profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness" (v16).** Four uses, in a natural order. *Teaching* tells us what is true. *Reproof* (*elegmos*, conviction) shows us where we have gone wrong. *Correction* (*epanorthōsis*, used only here, literally setting upright again) puts us back on the right path. *Training* (*paideia*, the word for raising and disciplining a child, Eph 6:4; Heb 12:5–11) keeps us growing in a right life. The false teachers offered speculation and endless discussion (1 Tim 1:4). Scripture offers what actually changes people.
- **"That the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work" (v17).** "Man of God" is an Old Testament title for a prophet or messenger of God: Moses (Deut 33:1), Samuel (1 Sam 9:6), Elijah and Elisha (1 Kings 17:18; 2 Kings 4:9). The ESV footnote notes this echo. Paul gave Timothy the same title in 1 Timothy 6:11. It applies first to Timothy as a minister, though the point holds for every believer (2:21 uses the same phrase, "ready for every good work"). "Complete" is *artios*, fitted or capable, perfectly suited to its purpose. "Equipped" is *exartizō*, built on the same root: to furnish or fit out completely. Luke uses it of days "completed" (Acts 21:5), and in other Greek it was used of fitting out a ship for a voyage. The repeated root makes the point: Scripture fully fits out the servant of God for whatever good work God calls him to.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Timothy's childhood training in Scripture a great blessing, saying "children should betimes get the knowledge of the scriptures," and he sums up v17: "That which finishes a man of God in this world is the scripture." He also notes that deceivers "do but deceive themselves." On v16 JFB argues for "every Scripture" as the literal sense but refuses to let that suggest some Scripture isn't inspired: "It is useful, because God-inspired; not God-inspired, because useful." JFB stresses that inspiration is said of the writings, not only the writers, that it was not "mechanical dictation," and that the plural "whom" in v14 includes Lois and Eunice. JFB also hears in *goētes* a link to the Ephesian magic arts. Guzik makes v15 his anchor: the Bible itself doesn't save, but it reveals Jesus, who does. He then gives a long case for the Bible's reliability from its unity, fulfilled prophecy and manuscript evidence, and treats the four uses of Scripture as truth taught, sin shown, life restored and character trained. The Tyndale notes add the balance on both sides of v15: the Old Testament gives the wisdom to receive Christ, and Christ is needed to understand the Old Testament fully.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter sets two kinds of religion side by side. One has the outline of godliness without its power, keeps learning without arriving, and loves self, money and pleasure. The other is the life Timothy saw in Paul, marked by faith, love and suffering, and rooted in Scriptures that make a person wise for salvation "through faith in Christ Jesus." Christ is where the power is. He is the one Moses served while the magicians failed, the one who rescued Paul from every persecution and will rescue him through death, and the one the sacred writings were always speaking about. Timothy is not told to find something new. He is told to keep standing on a Word that God breathed out, because it leads to Christ.
