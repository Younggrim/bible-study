*Setting:* Moses is still on Mount Sinai, receiving the "Book of the Covenant" (20:22–23:33; 24:7), the case laws that spell out how Israel should live under the Ten Commandments. Chapter 22 ended with laws protecting foreigners, widows, orphans and the poor. Chapter 23 completes the collection. It begins with honest courts and kindness even to enemies, then sets out Israel's sacred calendar, the Sabbath year, the weekly Sabbath and three yearly pilgrim feasts. It ends with God's promises for the journey ahead: an angel to lead them, blessing in the land, and enemies driven out. This last section serves as the blessings that close the covenant, much as an ancient treaty closed with the great king's promises to a loyal people. In the next chapter Israel will formally accept all of it.

#### Laws for a Just Community (vv1–9)
- **"You shall not spread a false report" (v1).** "Spread" is literally "lift up" or "carry," the same verb as "take" in "You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain" (20:7). A rumor can do as much damage as testimony in court. **"Join hands with a wicked man to be a malicious witness."** "Malicious" is literally "a witness of violence" (*chamas*), meaning testimony that does violence to someone, often aiming at a death sentence. This applies the ninth commandment (20:16).
- **"You shall not fall in with the many to do evil" (v2).** Literally "You shall not be after many for evil." Crowds can push a court, or anyone, toward injustice. The Gospels show it: Pilate, "wishing to satisfy the crowd," handed Jesus over (Mark 15:15). Truth is not decided by majority vote.
- **"Nor shall you be partial to a poor man in his lawsuit" (v3).** This is surprising after so many laws protecting the poor. But justice must not be bent in either direction. A poor man's case should not win just because he is poor, any more than a rich man's should because he is rich. Leviticus puts both sides together: "You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great, but in righteousness shall you judge your neighbor" (Lev 19:15). Justice is blind to status, not blind to facts.
- **"If you meet your enemy's ox or his donkey going astray, you shall bring it back to him" (v4).** In the middle of laws about courts comes an ordinary scene in the fields. The "enemy" may be someone you are in a legal dispute with, or just someone who dislikes you. Either way, you help him. Deuteronomy 22:1–4 gives the same law about a "brother."
- **"If you see the donkey of one who hates you lying down under its burden… you shall rescue it with him" (v5).** The Hebrew is awkward and has a play on words: you shall "refrain from leaving" him, but you shall "help" (or "release") the donkey with him. The two of you, enemies, work side by side to get a fallen donkey back on its feet. It is hard to stay bitter at someone while you are lifting a donkey together. This is where Jesus' "love your enemies" (Matt 5:44) already shows itself in the law. Proverbs says the same: "If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat" (Prov 25:21; Rom 12:20).
- **"You shall not pervert the justice due to your poor" (v6).** Now the other side of v3. Courts must not tilt against the poor, who could not hire help or offer bribes. The prophets later thundered against judges who "turn aside the needy from justice" (Isa 10:2; Amos 5:12).
- **"Keep far from a false charge, and do not kill the innocent and righteous, for I will not acquit the wicked" (v7).** Human courts can make mistakes, and an innocent man can be condemned. But God sees it. A judge who condemns the innocent will answer to the Judge who will not let the guilty go free. Naboth is the classic case of an innocent man killed on a false charge (1 Kgs 21:13), and Jesus is the supreme one.
- **"You shall take no bribe, for a bribe blinds the clear-sighted" (v8).** "Clear-sighted" means those who can see, the wise and the observant. A bribe does not only buy a decision; it changes how a person sees. Deuteronomy 16:19 says it "blinds the eyes of the wise." Samuel's sons "took bribes and perverted justice" (1 Sam 8:3), and that failure led Israel to ask for a king.
- **"You know the heart of a sojourner, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (v9).** "Heart" here is *nephesh*, the inner life or soul of a person, their feelings and longings. Israel knew from the inside what it felt like to be a powerless foreigner. Memory was meant to produce empathy. This repeats 22:21, and here it closes the section on justice as it began the section on mercy.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up vv1–9: "Neither parties, friends, witnesses, nor common opinions, must move us to lessen great faults." JFB reads "put not thine hand" as "join not hands," explains "decline" in v2 as to "deviate from the straight path of rectitude," and takes v3 as a warning not to "varnish the cause even of a poor man." JFB has no notes on vv4–9. Guzik ties v1 to the rule of two or three witnesses (Deut 19:15), points out that the Hebrew behind "the heart of a sojourner" is *nephesh*, and links the enemy's donkey with Jesus' parable of the Good Samaritan, where help came from someone who might have been an enemy.

#### Laws About the Sabbath and Festivals (vv10–19)
- **"For six years you shall sow your land… but the seventh year you shall let it rest and lie fallow" (vv10–11).** The Sabbath pattern of 20:8–11 applies to the land as well. Every seventh year, fields, vineyards and olive groves were left alone. **"That the poor of your people may eat; and what they leave the beasts of the field may eat."** Whatever grew by itself belonged to the poor and to wild animals. Leviticus 25:1–7 and Deuteronomy 15:1–11 add more about this "Sabbath year," including the cancelling of debts. It required great faith, since a family had to trust God to provide enough in the sixth year (Lev 25:20–22), as he had with the double manna on the sixth day (16:22–29).
- **Did Israel keep it?** Apparently not for long stretches. Leviticus 26:34–35 warned that if Israel did not let the land rest, exile would give it the rest it was owed. Chronicles says the seventy years of exile in Babylon were "until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths" (2 Chr 36:21). Later, under Nehemiah, the people promised to keep it again (Neh 10:31).
- **"Six days you shall do your work, but on the seventh day you shall rest" (v12).** The weekly Sabbath repeated, with its purpose: **"that your ox and your donkey may have rest, and the son of your servant woman, and the alien, may be refreshed."** The emphasis here is on the least powerful. "Refreshed" is the verb for catching one's breath (*naphash*), the same word used when God "rested and was refreshed" after creation (31:17). The slave's son and the foreigner get to breathe.
- **"Make no mention of the names of other gods, nor let it be heard on your lips" (v13).** This is not about never saying the name Baal at all, since the Bible itself names other gods. It means not invoking them, swearing by them or praying to them (Josh 23:7; Ps 16:4). Before setting out the feasts, God reminds Israel whose feasts they are.
- **"Three times in the year you shall keep a feast to me" (v14).** The word for feast here is *chag*, a pilgrimage festival, a journey to the sanctuary. These are the three great pilgrim feasts of Israel: Unleavened Bread (with Passover), Weeks, and Booths. Each was tied to the farming year and to Israel's story. Leviticus 23 and Deuteronomy 16 give the fuller calendar.
- **"The Feast of Unleavened Bread… in the month of Abib, for in it you came out of Egypt" (v15).** This seven-day feast followed Passover in spring (March–April), at the start of the barley harvest (12:14–20; 13:3–10). It remembered the exodus. **"None shall appear before me empty-handed."** Worshipers brought gifts, giving back from what God had given them (Deut 16:16–17).
- **"The Feast of Harvest, of the firstfruits of your labor" (v16).** Seven weeks later (May–June), at the wheat harvest, came the feast later called the Feast of Weeks (34:22), or in Greek, Pentecost, "fiftieth," because it fell fifty days after Passover (Lev 23:15–16). In later Judaism it also came to remember the giving of the law at Sinai. On this feast, centuries later, the Holy Spirit was poured out on the church (Acts 2:1), and three thousand people became the firstfruits of a new harvest.
- **"The Feast of Ingathering at the end of the year" (v16).** In autumn (September–October), after the grape and olive harvests, came the feast later called Booths or Tabernacles (Lev 23:33–43), when Israel lived for a week in shelters made of branches to remember the wilderness journey. "End of the year" reflects the farming year, which ended with the last harvest. At this feast Jesus stood up and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37).
- **"Three times in the year shall all your males appear before the Lord GOD" (v17).** "Appear before" is literally "be seen" by him, or in a slightly different vowel reading, "see the face of" God. The Tyndale notes suggest this also kept the nation united around one sanctuary rather than many local shrines. Women and children often came too, as Hannah did (1 Sam 1:3–7) and Mary and Joseph with the boy Jesus (Luke 2:41–42), but the men were required. **"The Lord GOD"** is *Adonai Yahweh*, "the Lord, the LORD," the master who owns the land and its harvests.
- **"You shall not offer the blood of my sacrifice with anything leavened" (v18).** "My sacrifice" here likely means the Passover lamb (34:25 says so directly). Leaven, or yeast, was kept out of most offerings (Lev 2:11), partly because Passover was a feast of unleavened bread and partly because leaven, a picture of corruption working through a whole lump, became a symbol of sin (1 Cor 5:6–8). **"Or let the fat of my feast remain until the morning."** The fat was the choicest part, belonging to God, and had to be burned the same night, as the Passover meat itself had to be eaten or burned before morning (12:10).
- **"The best of the firstfruits of your ground you shall bring into the house of the LORD" (v19).** "Best" is literally "first," which in Hebrew means both the earliest and the choicest. God gets the first and the best (Prov 3:9–10). Deuteronomy 26:1–11 gives a beautiful liturgy for the farmer who brings his basket of firstfruits and recites how God rescued his fathers.
- **"You shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk" (v19).** No one knows for certain why. Three main views: it may forbid a Canaanite or other pagan ritual, as the Tyndale notes and JFB suggest, though the text once claimed as evidence from Ugarit is now read differently by most scholars; it may be a lesson in tenderness, not using the milk meant to give life to cook the young one (compare 22:30; Deut 22:6–7); or it may be a symbol of keeping life and death apart. The command appears three times (34:26; Deut 14:21). Later Jewish tradition built from it the rule of keeping meat and dairy separate, the basis of kosher kitchens with separate dishes, though the text itself only forbids this one practice.
- **Commentators:** Henry on the Sabbath year: "They must not plough or sow it; what the earth produced of itself, should be eaten, and not laid up." On coming to the feasts with gifts: "we must not come to worship God empty-hearted." JFB says the feasts kept "up a national sense of religion and a public uniformity in worship," and calls the goat-in-milk rule "a prohibition against imitating the superstitious rites of the idolaters in Egypt." Guzik links Israel's neglect of the Sabbath years to the length of the Babylonian exile, notes that later Jewish teachers excused the old, the sick and small boys from the journeys, and passes on F. B. Meyer's point that the goat rule respects the bond between a mother and her young.

#### Conquest of Canaan Promised (vv20–33)
- **"Behold, I send an angel before you to guard you on the way" (v20).** "Angel" (*malak*) means messenger. Who is this angel? He is no ordinary angel, because of what God says next. **"To bring you to the place that I have prepared."** Jesus used similar words: "I go to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2).
- **"Obey his voice… for he will not pardon your transgression, for my name is in him" (v21).** Only God can forgive or refuse to forgive sin (Mark 2:7). "My name" means God's own nature and authority. This angel carries God's very presence. He seems to be the same as "the angel of the LORD" who appeared in the burning bush (3:2) and went before Israel in the pillar of cloud (14:19), who speaks as God himself (Gen 16:10–13; 22:11–18; Judg 13:21–22). Many Christian interpreters, from the early church fathers on, have seen him as the Son of God before he became man. Others see him as a special created angel who represents God. Exodus 33:2–3 later makes a distinction: after the golden calf, God says he will send "an angel" but not go himself, and Moses pleads for God's own presence instead (33:14–15).
- **"I will be an enemy to your enemies and an adversary to your adversaries" (v22).** This is covenant language, the promise a great king made to a loyal ally. God first made it to Abraham: "him who dishonors you I will curse" (Gen 12:3).
- **"The Amorites and the Hittites and the Perizzites and the Canaanites, the Hivites and the Jebusites" (v23).** This list of peoples of Canaan appears often (3:8, 17; Deut 7:1). "Amorites" and "Canaanites" were broad names for the land's peoples; the Hittites here are probably groups related to the empire in what is now Turkey; the Jebusites held Jerusalem until David (2 Sam 5:6–9). **"I blot them out."** God says *he* will do it. The land is a gift, not something Israel earns by its own strength (Deut 9:4–6).
- **"Break their pillars in pieces" (v24).** Canaanite worship used standing stones (*matsevot*) as sacred pillars for their gods, often linked with fertility worship. Archaeologists have found many, for example at Gezer and Hazor. Israel was not to adapt pagan worship to the LORD. It was to tear it down.
- **"He will bless your bread and your water, and I will take sickness away from among you" (v25).** God had promised at Marah, "I am the LORD, your healer" (15:26). **"None shall miscarry or be barren in your land" (v26).** The Canaanites practiced fertility rituals to make their crops grow and their wombs bear children. God says he gives these gifts himself, freely, to a faithful people. **"I will fulfill the number of your days."** A full life, not cut short.
- **"I will send my terror before you" (v27).** God's reputation would go ahead of Israel. Rahab later tells the spies exactly this: "the fear of you has fallen upon us" (Josh 2:9–11).
- **"I will send hornets before you" (v28).** The word *tsirah* appears only here, in Deuteronomy 7:20 and in Joshua 24:12, which says the hornet drove out two Amorite kings, "not by your sword or by your bow." Interpreters have long disagreed what it means. Some take it literally: swarms of insects. Others take it as a picture of panic and terror, matching the parallel in v27. Some modern scholars have suggested it refers to Egyptian armies campaigning in Canaan before Israel arrived, since a bee or hornet was a royal symbol of Lower Egypt, but that remains speculation. The point is clear in every view: God did the work.
- **"I will not drive them out from before you in one year, lest the land become desolate and the wild beasts multiply" (v29).** Israel was too small to fill the land at once. If the Canaanites vanished overnight, towns and fields would go wild and dangerous animals would take over. **"Little by little I will drive them out… until you have increased and possess the land" (v30).** God's pace is deliberate and kind. Judges 2:20–23 and 3:1–4 add that the remaining nations also served to test Israel's faithfulness.
- **"From the Red Sea to the Sea of the Philistines, and from the wilderness to the Euphrates" (v31).** This is the full extent of the land promised to Abraham (Gen 15:18), from the Gulf of Aqaba to the Mediterranean, and from the southern desert to the great river in the north. Israel came closest to holding these borders under David and Solomon (1 Kgs 4:21), and only briefly.
- **"You shall make no covenant with them and their gods" (v32).** A treaty with these peoples would mean recognizing their gods too. **"Lest they make you sin against me… it will surely be a snare to you" (v33).** A snare is a hunter's trap. Israel did not listen. The Gibeonites tricked Joshua into a treaty (Josh 9), and the book of Judges is the story of the snare closing (Judg 2:1–3). The Tyndale notes add that the Canaanites' judgment had been long delayed: God told Abraham that "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Gen 15:16). By now it was.
- **Commentators:** Henry says plainly, "Christ is the Angel of Jehovah," and Guzik takes the same view, reasoning that a created angel, even Michael or Gabriel, could not claim God's name and the right to refuse pardon. JFB notes that this angel "is frequently called Jehovah and Elohim, that is, God." On v28 JFB calls the hornets "some instrument of divine judgment, but variously interpreted." On vv29–30 Henry draws a lesson for the Christian life: "Corruptions are driven out of the hearts of God's people, not all at once, but by little and little." Guzik quotes John Trapp's line that God gives "his blessings by retail," a little at a time, and notes that Israel broke v32 almost at once in its treaty with Gibeon.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter asks for justice that cannot be bought, kindness that helps an enemy's donkey up, and a heart that knows what it is to be a stranger. Jesus lived that. He was the innocent and righteous one condemned on a false charge by a crowd that perverted justice (v7; Mark 14:55–59; 15:15). The feasts Israel kept were pictures of him: he is our Passover lamb with no leaven of sin (1 Cor 5:7), he rose as "the firstfruits" of the harvest (1 Cor 15:20), he poured out his Spirit at Pentecost, and he invited the thirsty to drink at the Feast of Booths (John 7:37–38). And the angel who goes before God's people to bring them to "the place that I have prepared," bearing God's own name, finds its fullness in the one who said, "I go to prepare a place for you… I will come again and will take you to myself" (John 14:2–3). He drives out the enemies of the heart little by little, until the inheritance is fully possessed.
