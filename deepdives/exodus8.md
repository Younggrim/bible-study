*Setting:* The Nile has been turned to blood for seven days. The fish died, Egyptians dug for drinking water, and Pharaoh "did not take even this to heart" (7:22–25). Chapter 8 brings plagues two, three and four: frogs, gnats and flies. A pattern sets in. Pharaoh asks for relief, promises to let Israel go, and goes back on his word once the pressure lifts. Meanwhile the contest sharpens. Egypt's magicians fail and give up, and God begins to separate his people from Egypt. (Hebrew Bibles number this chapter differently: ESV 8:1–4 is 7:26–29 in Hebrew, so Hebrew verse numbers in this chapter run four lower.)

#### The Second Plague: Frogs (vv1–15)
- **"Go in to Pharaoh" (v1).** This is the second plague of the first set, and as the pattern holds, Moses goes to the palace instead of meeting Pharaoh at the river. The demand is the same: **"Let my people go, that they may serve me."**
- **"I will plague all your country with frogs" (v2).** Frogs were a normal part of Nile life after the yearly flood. Egyptians linked them with fertility and new life. The goddess Heqet, who helped with childbirth, was pictured with a frog's head. A sign of life and blessing becomes a plague.
- **"Into your bedroom and on your bed… into your ovens and your kneading bowls" (v3).** The description is almost comic, and deliberately so. There is no escape, not even in Pharaoh's bedroom or the bread dough. "Swarm" is *sharats*, the word used for Israel's growth in 1:7. Pharaoh tried to stop Israel from swarming. Now the frogs swarm instead.
- **"The magicians did the same" (v7).** They can add more frogs, which only makes things worse. They can't take any away. Notice who Pharaoh calls next: not his magicians, but Moses.
- **"Plead with the LORD to take away the frogs" (v8).** The king who said "Who is the LORD?" (5:2) now uses God's name and asks for prayer. **"I will let the people go"** is the first of his broken promises.
- **"Be pleased to command me when" (v9).** Literally "Have the honor over me," a courteous way of saying, "You set the time." Moses lets Pharaoh choose so that no one can say the frogs just happened to leave. **"Tomorrow" (v10).** It is a strange answer, one more night with the frogs. Perhaps Pharaoh hoped they would go on their own.
- **"So that you may know that there is no one like the LORD our God" (v10).** Again the purpose is knowledge. The phrase "no one like the LORD" will echo in Israel's song at the sea: "Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?" (15:11).
- **"Moses cried to the LORD" (v12).** "Cried" (*tsa'aq*) is the strong word for crying out, used of Israel's cry under slavery (3:7, 9). Moses prays earnestly even for the man oppressing his people. The Tyndale notes point out that this is where Moses' long work as an intercessor begins (32:11–14; Num 14:13–19).
- **"They gathered them together in heaps, and the land stank" (v14).** God answers exactly, but the relief has a cost. The frogs die where they are and the country smells of them. Once again Egypt "stinks," as it did in 7:21.
- **"When Pharaoh saw that there was a respite, he hardened his heart" (v15).** "Respite" is literally "breathing room." The word "hardened" here is *kaved*, made heavy, and Pharaoh does it to himself. **"As the LORD had said"** reminds the reader that this was foretold (7:4).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God has many ways of disquieting those that live at ease." Pharaoh, so proud before, "is now glad to be beholden to the mercy of God and the prayers of Moses." Henry also thinks Pharaoh named "tomorrow" hoping the frogs would leave anyway, so he would owe God nothing. JFB thinks the frogs were natural to the river but "miraculously multiplied to an amazing extent," and notes they left "at the very hour named by the monarch himself." Guzik connects the plague with the frog-headed goddess Heqet and sees the magicians' trick as real but limited power: they could multiply the plague but not remove it.

#### The Third Plague: Gnats (vv16–19)
- **"Strike the dust of the earth, so that it may become gnats" (v16).** This is the third plague of the first set, and it comes without warning, as the third plague of each set does (9:8; 10:21). The word *kinnim* is uncertain. The ancient Greek translation and most modern scholars take it as "gnats" or mosquitoes. Josephus, the Aramaic paraphrase and the KJV take it as "lice." Either way it is tiny, biting and everywhere, rising from the dust as the frogs rose from the water.
- **"All the dust of the earth became gnats" (v17).** The exaggeration is deliberate and makes the point. Egyptian priests prided themselves on ritual purity and shaved their bodies to stay clean. Now everyone, priest and peasant, man and beast, is covered.
- **"The magicians tried… but they could not" (v18).** For the first time the magicians fail. They could imitate a sign and add blood and frogs, but they could not make life from dust. Creating life belongs to God alone (Gen 2:7).
- **"This is the finger of God" (v19).** It is a stunning admission from Egypt's own experts. "God" here is *elohim*, which can mean the one God or "a god" depending on the speaker, so JFB suggests the magicians, speaking as polytheists, meant "a god's finger." Either way they mean, "This is beyond us." The phrase returns when God writes the Ten Commandments "with the finger of God" (31:18), and Jesus uses it of his own power over demons: "If it is by the finger of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20).
- **"But Pharaoh's heart was hardened" (v19).** Pharaoh won't listen even to his own advisers. Unbelief that refuses God's servants ends up refusing every other witness too.
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies the insects as "gnats, mosquitoes—that is the proper meaning of the original term." The lexicon agrees that gnats is the more likely meaning, though it lists "lice" as an old alternative. Guzik keeps "lice" and stresses that the magicians, who could copy evil, were stopped short at creating life. Their admission shows that Satan's power, though real, is limited. The Tyndale notes take the word broadly, as "vermin" or "bugs."

#### The Fourth Plague: Flies (vv20–32)
- **"Rise up early… as he goes out to the water" (v20).** A new set of three begins, and as with the first plague (7:15), Moses meets Pharaoh by the river in the morning.
- **"Swarms of flies" (v21).** The Hebrew *arov* means "swarm" or "mixture," and the kind of insect isn't stated. "Flies" comes from the ancient Greek version, which chose "dog-fly," a biting fly. Psalm 78:45 says the swarms "devoured them," which suggests biting insects. Egyptians honored the scarab beetle as a symbol of the sun god's daily rebirth, and some think beetles were part of the swarm. JFB lists gadflies, cockroaches and beetles.
- **"I will set apart the land of Goshen, where my people dwell" (v22).** This is new. The first three plagues seem to have touched everyone. From now on God draws a line between Egypt and Israel (9:4, 26; 10:23; 11:7). **"That you may know that I am the LORD in the midst of the earth."** The God of the Hebrews isn't a local deity of one tribe. He rules in the middle of Egypt itself.
- **"I will put a division between my people and your people" (v23).** The standard Hebrew text reads *pedut*, which elsewhere means "redemption" or "ransom" (Ps 111:9; 130:7). The ancient Greek, Latin and Syriac versions read "distinction," and the ESV follows them, perhaps reading a similar Hebrew word. The lexicon calls the Hebrew text doubtful here. Either way the meaning is clear: God makes a difference between those he is redeeming and those under judgment.
- **"The land was ruined by the swarms of flies" (v24).** It is the first time the text says the land itself was "ruined" (literally "destroyed"). The plagues are growing heavier.
- **"Go, sacrifice to your God within the land" (v25).** Pharaoh's first compromise: worship, but stay in Egypt. Compromises like this always try to keep God's people within reach.
- **"An abomination to the Egyptians" (v26).** Israel would sacrifice sheep, goats and cattle. Egyptians held some of these animals sacred: the ram was linked to the gods Amun and Khnum, the bull to Apis, the cow to Hathor. They already looked down on shepherds and would not eat with Hebrews (Gen 43:32; 46:34). Slaughtering these animals in public could cause a riot. Moses' answer is practical and firm: **"We must go three days' journey into the wilderness… as he tells us" (v27).** God, not Pharaoh, sets the terms of worship.
- **"Only you must not go very far away. Plead for me" (v28).** Pharaoh's second compromise: go, but stay close, where he can call them back. In the next chapters he will try others: only the men may go (10:11), or leave the flocks behind (10:24).
- **"Only let not Pharaoh cheat again" (v29).** Moses has learned. "Cheat" means to mock or deceive. He prays anyway, and **"not one remained" (v31).** God answers exactly, again.
- **"Pharaoh hardened his heart this time also" (v32).** Again it is *kaved*, Pharaoh making his own heart heavy. Relief without repentance leaves him harder than before.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in Goshen "the plague which annoys all their neighbours shall not approach them." He notes Israel "could not sacrifice in the land without incurring the displeasure either of their God or of their task-masters." Of Pharaoh's word in v29 he echoes Moses: "let not Pharaoh deal deceitfully any more." JFB pictures Pharaoh's spirit "like a bent bow" that "sprang back to its wonted obduracy" as soon as the pressure lifted, and says the worship of insects, especially the beetle, was "a prominent part of the religion of the ancient Egyptians." Guzik notes that the Hebrew word for the swarm is uncertain, and sees in Pharaoh's offers the pattern of every half-hearted surrender: worship God, but stay in Egypt, or at least don't go far. The Tyndale notes compare this to the double-minded person of James 1:8.

#### Pointing to Christ
Egypt's experts finally admit, "This is the finger of God." Jesus takes up that phrase for his own works: "If it is by the finger of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20). The God who drew a line around Goshen and called it a "redemption" between his people and Egypt has, in Christ, redeemed a people for himself (Titus 2:14). And where Pharaoh offered worship on his own terms, inside Egypt and not too far, Jesus calls his people out completely: "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me" (Mark 8:34). Moses kept praying for the king who kept lying to him. Jesus prayed for those who crucified him (Luke 23:34).
