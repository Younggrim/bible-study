*Setting:* Chapter 3 ended with Solomon's wedding procession and the king crowned "on the day of his wedding." Chapter 4 is the wedding night, or at least the poetry of it. From 4:8 to 5:1 the man calls her "bride" (*kallah*) six times, the only place in the Song he does. The chapter falls into three movements. First he praises her beauty from head to breast in a set-piece description (vv1–7). Scholars call this kind of poem a *wasf*, an Arabic word for "description," because Arab wedding songs in modern times follow the same pattern. Then he invites her to come away with him and tells her how she has captured his heart (vv8–11). Finally he pictures her as a locked garden and a sealed fountain (vv12–15), and she answers by opening the garden to him (v16). The Tyndale notes and Guzik both read this as the couple's wedding night and the poem's affirmation of sexual love within marriage. The language is frank but never crude. It uses landscape, fruit and fragrance to say what plain words would cheapen. For the main ways the Song is read, see the Setting of chapter 1. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song, and the headings below follow its paragraph breaks.

#### You Are Beautiful, My Love (vv1–7)
- **"Behold, you are beautiful, my love, behold, you are beautiful!" (v1).** He begins as he did in 1:15, repeating himself. "My love" is again "my companion": she is his friend as well as his bride. Guzik passes on Glickman's observation that he praises seven features, and seven in Hebrew thought is the number of completeness.
- **"Your eyes are doves behind your veil" (v1).** Brides wore veils (Gen 24:65). Her eyes, gentle and bright, shine through it. The word for "veil" (*tsammah*) is rare, and the KJV took it as "locks" of hair. JFB, following the KJV, notes the alternative and prefers "locks."
- **"Your hair is like a flock of goats leaping down the slopes of Gilead" (v1).** Gilead was the high, wooded country east of the Jordan. Middle Eastern goats are usually black, with long wavy hair. Seen from a distance, a black flock pouring down a hillside looks like a ripple of dark hair. The Tyndale notes: her dark hair flows over her lighter veil. Guzik: the image is of motion and life, not of goat-like hair.
- **"Your teeth are like a flock of shorn ewes that have come up from the washing, all of which bear twins, and not one among them has lost its young" (v2).** White, clean and even, every tooth matched with its pair and none missing. The Tyndale notes add that in a world without dentists, a full set of teeth was rare and lovely.
- **"Your lips are like a scarlet thread… Your cheeks are like halves of a pomegranate behind your veil" (v3).** Red lips, finely shaped. "Cheeks" may be "temples," the side of the face. A cut pomegranate shows a rosy blush. Rahab's scarlet cord (Josh 2:18) and the pomegranates on the high priest's robe (Exod 28:33) use the same words, but here the point is color.
- **"Your neck is like the tower of David, built in rows of stone; on it hang a thousand shields" (v4).** Not long, but straight, strong and dignified, adorned with necklaces like shields hung on a tower. "Rows of stone" translates a rare word whose meaning is uncertain. The KJV guessed "armory." Warriors hung their shields on city towers (Ezek 27:11). The tower of David is mentioned nowhere else in Scripture, as the Tyndale notes observe. JFB places it on Zion. Guzik, with Glickman, reads strength of character: she carries herself in a way that commands respect.
- **"Your two breasts are like two fawns, twins of a gazelle, that graze among the lilies" (v5).** Soft, delicate and matched. Guzik notes that many commentators shy away from the literal sense, but the comparison is gentle, not crude. Proverbs commends exactly this delight within marriage (Prov 5:18–19).
- **"Until the day breathes and the shadows flee, I will go away to the mountain of myrrh and the hill of frankincense" (v6).** These are her words from 2:17, now his. The "mountain of myrrh" is a picture of her, fragrant and desirable. He will be with her through the night. The Tyndale notes take it as his desire to be close to her. Henry suggests Mount Moriah, where the temple and its incense were, and JFB sees myrrh and Calvary, but both admit the allegory is a guess.
- **"You are altogether beautiful, my love; there is no flaw in you" (v7).** "Flaw" (*mum*) is the word for a blemish that disqualified an animal from sacrifice (Lev 22:20–21). Guzik notes the link. In his eyes she is perfect. Paul uses the same idea of the church: Christ will present her "without spot or wrinkle… holy and without blemish" (Eph 5:27).
- **Commentators:** Henry is candid about the limits of allegory here: "great mistakes are made by fanciful guesses." He still finds the point plain: "How fair and comely are believers, when justified in Christ's righteousness." JFB calls her eyes "chaste and guileless." Guzik criticizes Trapp for turning her eyes into a picture of pastors, and keeps to the plain sense.

#### Come with Me from Lebanon (vv8–11)
- **"Come with me from Lebanon, my bride; come with me from Lebanon" (v8).** This is the first time he calls her "bride." The Lebanon range is the snow-capped mountains on Israel's northern border.
- **"Depart from the peak of Amana, from the peak of Senir and Hermon, from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards" (v8).** Amana is a peak in the Anti-Lebanon range near Damascus, at the source of the Abana river (2 Kgs 5:12). Senir is the Amorite name for Mount Hermon (Deut 3:9), the highest mountain in the region at over 9,000 feet. Lions and leopards roamed these mountains in Bible times. The places are wild, remote and dangerous. He calls her away from them, to safety with him. Guzik, with Glickman, reads them as her fears: come away from anxiety into my arms. The Tyndale notes take the peaks as the heights of joy.
- **"You have captivated my heart, my sister, my bride" (v9).** "Captivated my heart" is one Hebrew verb made from the word "heart" (*libbavtini*), something like "you have hearted me." Clarke, quoted by Guzik, renders it "hearted me." **"With one glance of your eyes, with one jewel of your necklace."** A single look undoes him.
- **"My sister, my bride" (v9).** "Sister" sounds strange to modern readers. In ancient Egyptian and Near Eastern love poetry, "sister" and "brother" were common terms of endearment between lovers, and the Tyndale notes say it means close companionship, not blood relation. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line: a sister by birth, a spouse by choice. JFB: "By the term 'sister,' carnal ideas are excluded," joining a spouse's ardor with a sister's purity. The pairing says she is both family and beloved, someone he cherishes and protects.
- **"How beautiful is your love… How much better is your love than wine, and the fragrance of your oils than any spice!" (v10).** He gives back the very words she spoke in 1:2–3. She called his love better than wine and his oils fragrant. Now he says the same of hers. Love in the Song is mutual. Guzik notes she is not passive but loves richly in return.
- **"Your lips drip nectar, my bride; honey and milk are under your tongue" (v11).** "Nectar" is honey dripping straight from the comb. "Honey and milk" recalls the promised land, "flowing with milk and honey" (Exod 3:8). For him, she is a land of promise. **"The fragrance of your garments is like the fragrance of Lebanon,"** whose cedars and pines filled the air with scent (Hos 14:6).
- **Commentators:** Henry turns v8 to the call to leave the world's dangers: "These hills seem pleasant, but there are in them lions' dens." Guzik notes that "with me" is repeated: before he asks for her love, he offers his presence. JFB explains that Senir and Hermon are names for the same mountain, Senir being the Amorites' name.

#### A Garden Locked (vv12–15)
- **"A garden locked is my sister, my bride, a spring locked, a fountain sealed" (v12).** In the dry Near East, a private garden with its own spring was the height of luxury. It was walled and locked to keep out intruders and animals. Springs and cisterns were sealed with a stone or a mark to guard the water for the owner. Guzik passes on Carr's explanation that the seal kept the water for its rightful owner. The picture is of her purity: she has kept herself for her husband alone. Proverbs uses the same image: "Drink water from your own cistern… Let your fountain be blessed, and rejoice in the wife of your youth" (Prov 5:15–18). The Tyndale notes take it as her purity and virginity.
- **"Your shoots are an orchard of pomegranates with all choicest fruits" (v13).** "Orchard" is *pardes*, a word borrowed from Persian for a walled park or garden. It came into Greek as *paradeisos* and gave English the word "paradise." Jesus promised the thief on the cross, "today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). The locked garden is not barren. It is full of fruit.
- **"Henna with nard, nard and saffron, calamus and cinnamon, with all trees of frankincense, myrrh and aloes, with all choice spices" (vv13–14).** This is a catalog of the most expensive perfumes and spices of the ancient world. Nard came from the Himalayas, saffron from crocus flowers, calamus (sweet cane) from India, cinnamon from Sri Lanka or China, frankincense and myrrh from Arabia, aloes from fragrant wood. No real garden in Israel could grow them all together. The list is a poetic picture of a garden beyond any on earth. Several appear in the holy anointing oil and incense of the tabernacle (Exod 30:23–24, 34).
- **"A garden fountain, a well of living water, and flowing streams from Lebanon" (v15).** "Living water" is running water from a spring, fresh and clean, as opposed to standing water in a cistern. The same phrase is used of God, "the fountain of living waters" (Jer 2:13), and Jesus takes it up: "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink… Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water" (John 7:37–38). The sealed fountain of v12 is not stagnant but brimming, ready to flow.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws a strong application: her purity was guarded because it was precious, and virginity before marriage is worth valuing. He is also pastoral: a damaged garden can be restored, and purity can begin "from now on" through God's grace. Henry: "Saints in the church, and graces in the saints, are fitly compared to fruits and spices." JFB reads the sealed fountain as Christ sealed in the tomb, which "poured forth its full tide on Pentecost."

#### Let My Beloved Come to His Garden (v16)
- **"Awake, O north wind, and come, O south wind! Blow upon my garden, let its spices flow" (v16).** She speaks now. The north wind in Israel is cool and the south wind warm. Together they spread the garden's fragrance. She is ready for him. The Tyndale notes: she now reveals her treasures and invites him into the garden.
- **"Let my beloved come to his garden, and eat its choicest fruits" (v16).** In one line "my garden" becomes "his garden." What she guarded, she now gives. She belongs to him freely, as he belongs to her (2:16). The next verse (5:1) is his answer, and many take the chapter break to fall in the middle of the scene. Guzik warns against reading specific acts into the images. The poem is deliberately modest. Hebrews: "Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled" (Heb 13:4).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The blessed Spirit, in his work upon the soul, is as the wind," and "we can invite him to nothing but what is his own already." JFB reads the north wind as the Spirit as reprover and the south wind as the Spirit as comforter. Guzik, with Poole, sees the shift from "my" to "his" garden as a sign of their oneness, and passes on Morgan's allegorical reading of the two winds as adversity and prosperity.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter celebrates a bride kept pure and freely given, and a husband who delights in her and calls her flawless. Its first meaning is God's blessing on married love (Gen 2:24–25). It also gives the New Testament some of its language for Christ and the church. He calls his bride "altogether beautiful… no flaw in you" (v7) not because she was so by nature but because he made her so: Christ "gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her… so that he might present the church to himself… without spot or wrinkle" (Eph 5:25–27). He calls her to come away from the lions' dens to be with him (v8). The garden called *pardes* points to the paradise he opened (Luke 23:43). And the sealed fountain of living water finds its fullness in the one who promised, "whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again" (John 4:14).
