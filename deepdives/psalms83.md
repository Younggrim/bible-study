*Setting:* "A Song. A Psalm of Asaph." Psalm 83 is the last of the twelve Asaph psalms (50; 73–83) and a national lament in a time of extreme danger. Ten peoples have formed an alliance to wipe Israel off the map. Henry and JFB both connect it with the invasion in King Jehoshaphat's day, around 850 BC, when Moab, Ammon and the people of Mount Seir (Edom) came against Judah, and "Jahaziel… a Levite of the sons of Asaph" stood up in the assembly and promised victory (2 Chr 20:1–2, 14–17). That fits the psalm's stress on "the children of Lot" (Moab and Ammon, v8) and its Asaphite author. But no single known event matches the whole list, so many interpreters, as the Tyndale notes allow, take it as a poetic collage of Israel's enemies on every side, a picture of the world against God's people. The psalm moves from a cry for God to speak (vv1–4), to the list of conspirators (vv5–8), to a plea for God to do again what he did in the days of the judges (vv9–12), and finally to storm images of judgment with a surprising goal: "that they may seek your name" (vv13–18).

#### O God, Do Not Keep Silence (vv1–4)
- **"O God, do not keep silence; do not hold your peace or be still, O God!" (v1).** Three ways of saying the same urgent thing: do not be silent, do not be deaf, do not sit still. To the psalmist, God's silence feels like inaction. The psalm opens with prayer, not panic.
- **"For behold, your enemies make an uproar; those who hate you have raised their heads" (v2).** Notice the pronouns. They are "your enemies," "those who hate you." Attacking God's people is attacking God (Acts 9:4, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?"). "Make an uproar" is the roar of a mob or the surge of a sea (46:3, 6). "Raised their heads" pictures arrogant confidence, the opposite of bowing.
- **"They lay crafty plans against your people; they consult together against your treasured ones" (v3).** "Treasured ones" is *tsephunim*, literally "hidden ones," people God hides and protects like treasure (27:5, "he will hide me in his shelter"; 31:20). JFB: "hidden ones—whom God specially protects." The enemies can plot, but God's people are hidden in him (Col 3:3).
- **"Come, let us wipe them out as a nation; let the name of Israel be remembered no more!" (v4).** The goal is not conquest but erasure, the end of Israel as a people. Pharaoh had tried it (Exod 1:22), and Haman would try it (Esth 3:6, 13). The same hatred has surfaced again and again in history, which is part of why this psalm has been prayed with such feeling. Behind it lies a deeper aim: if Israel ceased to exist, God's promise to bless the world through Abraham's family would fail (Gen 12:3).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the enemies aimed to destroy not just Israel's armies but its very identity as a nation, and that their real opponent was God. Henry describes the psalm as written against those who sought to destroy the church, and he takes it of "all the confederacies of the neighbouring nations against Israel, from first to last."

#### The Conspiracy (vv5–8)
- **"They conspire with one accord; against you they make a covenant" (v5).** "With one accord" is literally "with one heart." Rival nations who rarely agreed on anything find unity in hostility to God. "Covenant" is the word for a treaty. It is a grim parody of God's covenant with Israel. Psalm 2 describes the same: "The kings of the earth set themselves… against the LORD and against his Anointed" (2:2).
- **The ten nations (vv6–8).** They surround Israel on every side:
- **"The tents of Edom and the Ishmaelites" (v6).** Edom, descended from Esau, lay southeast of the Dead Sea (Gen 36:1–8). Their hatred of Israel was a family feud going back to Jacob and Esau (Obad 10–12). The Ishmaelites, descended from Abraham's son by Hagar, were desert tribes to the south and east (Gen 25:12–18). "Tents" marks them as nomads.
- **"Moab and the Hagrites" (v6).** Moab lay east of the Dead Sea. The Hagrites (or Hagarenes) were a tribal people east of the Jordan, defeated by the tribes of Reuben and Gad in Saul's day (1 Chr 5:10, 18–22). Their name may link them to Hagar.
- **"Gebal and Ammon and Amalek" (v7).** Gebal is uncertain. It may be Byblos, the Phoenician port north of modern Beirut (Ezek 27:9), which the Tyndale notes favor, or a district called Gebal in the mountains of Edom, south of the Dead Sea, which would fit better with its neighbors in the list. Ammon lay east of the Jordan, around modern Amman. Amalek, a nomadic people of the Negev and Sinai, was Israel's first enemy after the exodus (Exod 17:8–16).
- **"Philistia with the inhabitants of Tyre" (v7).** Philistia was on the southern coast, Tyre the great Phoenician port to the north. Tyre had once been David's ally (2 Sam 5:11), but later traded Israelite captives as slaves (Amos 1:9).
- **"Asshur also has joined them; they are the strong arm of the children of Lot" (v8).** Asshur is Assyria. Here it is only a supporting player, lending its "arm" (strength) to Moab and Ammon, the "children of Lot" (Gen 19:36–38). That suggests a time before Assyria became the dominant empire of the 700s BC, though some think "Asshur" may be a desert tribe of that name (Gen 25:3). Moab and Ammon lead the alliance, which fits 2 Chronicles 20. **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** JFB: "all these united with the children of Lot, or Ammonites and Moabites (compare 2Ch 20:1)." Guzik also takes Moab and Ammon as the leaders, and says of the *Selah* that faith knows how to pause, while unbelief is always in a hurry.

#### Do to Them as You Did to Midian (vv9–12)
- **"Do to them as you did to Midian" (v9).** The psalmist reaches back to two great victories in the book of Judges, when God saved Israel against impossible odds. Both are from the time before Israel had a king, when God alone was their defender. Isaiah also remembers "the day of Midian" as the model of God's sudden rescue (Isa 9:4; 10:26).
- **"As to Sisera and Jabin at the river Kishon" (v9).** Jabin was a Canaanite king of Hazor, and Sisera his general with 900 iron chariots. Under Deborah and Barak, God routed them by the river Kishon in the Jezreel Valley. A storm seems to have turned the valley into mud: "The torrent Kishon swept them away" (Judg 4:13–16; 5:20–21). Sisera fled on foot and was killed by Jael (Judg 4:21).
- **"Who were destroyed at En-dor, who became dung for the ground" (v10).** En-dor lay near Mount Tabor, where Barak gathered his troops (Judg 4:6, 14). Judges does not name En-dor, so the psalm preserves an extra detail of the battle. "Dung for the ground" means their bodies lay unburied, fertilizing the fields (Jer 9:22).
- **"Make their nobles like Oreb and Zeeb, all their princes like Zebah and Zalmunna" (v11).** These were the leaders of Midian defeated by Gideon. Oreb ("raven") and Zeeb ("wolf") were captains, killed at a rock and a winepress that took their names (Judg 7:25). Zebah and Zalmunna were kings, captured and executed by Gideon (Judg 8:18–21). In that battle Gideon had only 300 men, and the Midianites turned their swords on one another (Judg 7:22). JFB: "They destroyed one another." That is exactly what happened in Jehoshaphat's day too: the invading armies turned on each other (2 Chr 20:22–23).
- **"Let us take possession for ourselves of the pastures of God" (v12).** The land is God's pasture, and Israel his flock (79:13; 80:1). The enemies are not only attacking a nation but trying to steal God's own property. Jehoshaphat prayed in almost these terms: they "come to drive us out of your possession, which you have given us to inherit" (2 Chr 20:11).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "let them be routed by their own fears, for so the Midianites were, more than by Gideon's 300 men." Guzik says the psalm reminds Israel of past rescues as a reason to trust God now, and notes that God calls the land his own pastures.

#### That They May Seek Your Name (vv13–18)
- **"O my God, make them like whirling dust, like chaff before the wind" (v13).** "Whirling dust" is *galgal*, a wheel or something that rolls. Many take it as tumbleweed, the dried heads of a thistle-like plant common in Israel that break off in late summer and roll across the fields in the wind. Isaiah uses the same pair: "like whirling dust before the storm" (Isa 17:13). Chaff is the light husk blown away when grain is winnowed (1:4). JFB: "like a wheel—or, whirling of any light thing (Isa 17:13), as stubble or chaff." Henry: "Those that trust in God have their hearts fixed; those that fight against him are unfixed, like a wheel."
- **"As fire consumes the forest, as the flame sets the mountains ablaze" (v14).** A wildfire racing through a dry hillside forest, unstoppable.
- **"So may you pursue them with your tempest and terrify them with your hurricane!" (v15).** Storm, fire and wind are the regular signs of God coming in judgment (18:7–15; 50:3; Isa 29:6). The Tyndale notes point this out.
- **"Fill their faces with shame, that they may seek your name, O LORD" (v16).** Here is the turn that makes this psalm more than a cry for revenge. The psalmist wants the enemies humbled, but the purpose is that they would "seek your name," that is, turn to the LORD. Shame can lead to repentance. Henry: "Let them be made to fear thy name, and perhaps that will bring them to seek thy name."
- **"Let them be put to shame and dismayed forever; let them perish in disgrace" (v17).** The psalm holds two outcomes side by side. If they will not seek him, then let their defeat be final. This is honest. Not every enemy repents, and God's justice is real.
- **"That they may know that you alone, whose name is the LORD, are the Most High over all the earth" (v18).** The psalm ends with God's covenant name, *YHWH* ("the LORD"), and the title "Most High over all the earth." The enemies wanted Israel's name "remembered no more" (v4). The psalmist wants God's name known everywhere. The Asaph psalms, which began with "the Mighty One, God the LORD" summoning the earth (50:1), close with every nation learning who he is. Henry: "that other men may know and own, if they themselves will not, that thou, whose name alone is JEHOVAH," is Lord of all.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that people often turn to God only after being brought low, and that God sometimes has to defeat people before he saves them. He reads the ending as showing that the aim of God's judgments is to humble and convert, not merely destroy. JFB on v16 notes that vv17–18 "amplify the sentiment" of v16. The Tyndale notes sum up the goal as God receiving honor among the nations.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 83 shows the nations conspiring together "against you," against God himself, just as Psalm 2 does. In the New Testament, the believers in Jerusalem saw that conspiracy reach its height when "Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel" gathered "against your holy servant Jesus" (Acts 4:25–28). Their plot to blot out his name ended in his resurrection and the spreading of his name to every nation. The psalm's surprising hope, that enemies would be humbled so that they might "seek your name," is exactly what the gospel does. Paul, who once tried to destroy the church, was brought low on the Damascus road and became an apostle to the nations (Acts 9; Gal 1:13–16). God's final aim is that every knee should bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, the name above every name (Phil 2:9–11).
