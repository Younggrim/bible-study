*Setting:* The last of the father's warnings about the forbidden woman in Proverbs 1–9 (see 2:16–19; 5:1–23; 6:20–35, and Proverbs 1 on authorship). Chapter 6 used argument: fire in the lap, a thief who can repay and a husband who cannot be bought off. Chapter 7 tells a story. The father looks out his window at dusk and watches a naïve young man walk straight into a trap. It is one of the most vivid scenes in the Old Testament, a short drama with a setting, two characters, a seduction speech and a fatal ending. Right after it, in chapter 8, Wisdom will stand at the city gates and call out, the true woman answering the false one.

#### Warning Against the Adulteress (vv1–27)
- **"Keep my words and treasure up my commandments with you" (v1).** The verb "treasure up" is the same as in 2:1, words stored away safely. "Keep" appears three times in two verses.
- **"Keep my commandments and live" (v2).** As in 4:4. Obedience is a matter of life and death, as the story will show.
- **"Keep my teaching as the apple of your eye" (v2).** "Apple of the eye" is literally "the little man of the eye" (*ʾishon*), the pupil, probably named for the tiny reflection a person sees in someone else's pupil. It is the most sensitive part of the body, the thing everyone instinctively protects. God keeps Israel "as the apple of his eye" (Deut 32:10), and David prays, "Keep me as the apple of your eye" (Ps 17:8). Guard God's word the way God guards his people.
- **"Bind them on your fingers; write them on the tablet of your heart" (v3).** Fingers and heart, outward reminder and inward memory (3:3; Deut 6:8; 11:18).
- **"Say to wisdom, 'You are my sister,' and call insight your intimate friend" (v4).** "Sister" was a term of affection for a bride in love poetry, "my sister, my bride" (Song 4:9–10). "Intimate friend" means a close relative or kinsman (Ruth 2:1). Make Wisdom the woman closest to you, and the other woman will have no room. The best protection against a false love is a true one.
- **"To keep you from the forbidden woman, from the adulteress with her smooth words" (v5).** The same phrase as 2:16. Her weapon is always words.
- **"At the window of my house I have looked out through my lattice" (v6).** Houses had small windows covered with a wooden lattice, which let a person see out without being seen. The father becomes the narrator, watching unnoticed. The Greek translation (the Septuagint) makes the woman the one peering from her window, as harlots sometimes did (compare Jezebel in 2 Kgs 9:30). The Hebrew has the father watching, and most translations follow it.
- **"Among the simple… a young man lacking sense" (v7).** The "simple" (*peti*) is the naïve, open-minded young man of 1:4 and 1:22. "Lacking sense" is literally "lacking heart." He is not wicked yet, just empty and undecided, and that is what makes him an easy target.
- **"Passing along the street near her corner, taking the road to her house" (v8).** He is not hunting for sin, exactly, but he walks where he knows it lives. He is wandering toward her corner. Proverbs 4:15 has already given the answer: "Avoid it; do not go on it; turn away from it and pass on."
- **"In the twilight, in the evening, at the time of night and darkness" (v9).** The time words pile up as the light drains away. Literally the last phrase is "in the pupil (*ʾishon*) of night," the darkest middle of it. It is the same word as "apple of your eye" in v2. He did not keep wisdom as the pupil of his eye, so he walks into the pupil of the night. Darkness is the setting for folly (4:19; John 3:19–20).
- **"The woman meets him, dressed as a prostitute, wily of heart" (v10).** She is not a professional prostitute. She is a married woman (v19). But she dresses like one, perhaps veiled or painted (Gen 38:14–15). "Wily of heart" is literally "guarded of heart." She shows her body and hides her heart. JFB: "wary" or "cunning."
- **"She is loud and wayward; her feet do not stay at home" (v11).** "Loud" is restless, turbulent. "Wayward" means stubborn or rebellious. Folly in chapter 9 is described the same way: "loud… seductive and knows nothing" (9:13).
- **"Now in the street, now in the market, and at every corner she lies in wait" (v12).** These are the same places where Wisdom calls out (1:20–21; 8:2–3). Two women compete for the same young men in the same streets. One calls openly. The other "lies in wait," the word used for the gang's ambush (1:11, 18).
- **"She seizes him and kisses him, and with bold face she says" (v13).** "Bold face" means a hardened, shameless face. She does not blush (Jer 3:3).
- **"I had to offer sacrifices, and today I have paid my vows" (v14).** "Sacrifices" here are peace offerings (*shelamim*), the one kind of sacrifice whose meat the worshiper took home and ate. The law said it had to be eaten the same day or the next (Lev 7:15–16). So she is saying: I have fresh meat at home, a feast is ready, and it has to be eaten tonight. JFB says the feast "does not necessarily imply religious profession," but the irony is plain. She uses an act of worship as the opening line of a seduction. Religion can be a cover for sin (Isa 1:11–15).
- **"I have come out to meet you, to seek you eagerly, and I have found you" (v15).** She flatters him. He is not just anyone, he is the one she wanted. Seek and find are wisdom words (2:4; 8:17, "those who seek me diligently find me"), here twisted.
- **"Colored linens from Egyptian linen… myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon" (vv16–17).** Egyptian linen was the finest and most costly, and the spices were imported luxuries. Myrrh came from Arabia and East Africa, and cinnamon and aloes probably from as far as India or Sri Lanka. Song of Songs uses these same spices for the bride's garden (Song 4:14) and Psalm 45:8 for the royal wedding. What God made beautiful in marriage she offers outside it.
- **"Come, let us take our fill of love till morning" (v18).** "Love" here is the plural *dodim*, physical lovemaking, the word in Song of Songs 1:2 and 5:1. She offers a whole night of pleasure.
- **"For my husband is not at home; he has gone on a long journey" (v19).** She does not even call him "my husband," literally just "the man." She removes the fear of being caught. **"He took a bag of money with him; at full moon he will come home"** (v20). He's away on business and won't be back for a while. If it is now dark with no moon, as v9 suggests, full moon is about two weeks away. JFB: "There is no fear of discovery."
- **"With much seductive speech she persuades him; with her smooth talk she compels him" (v21).** "Seductive speech" is literally "much teaching" (*leqach*), the same word as the father's "good precepts" in 4:2. She is a teacher too, with her own curriculum. "Compels" means she pushes him along.
- **"All at once he follows her, as an ox goes to the slaughter" (v22).** "All at once" means suddenly, without thinking. The ox walks quietly to the butcher, thinking it is going to pasture. JFB says he goes "straightway," either ignorant of the danger or unable to resist.
- **"Or as a stag is caught fast" (v22).** The Hebrew here is difficult. It reads literally "and as an anklet (or fetter) to the discipline of a fool," which the KJV renders "as a fool to the correction of the stocks." The ESV follows the ancient Greek, Latin and Syriac translations, which suggest a stag caught in a trap, an animal picture to match the ox and the bird. The ESV notes this in its footnote. Either way the picture is a creature walking unaware into capture.
- **"Till an arrow pierces its liver; as a bird rushes into a snare" (v23).** The liver was thought of as a seat of life, so this is a fatal wound. The bird rushes in for the bait and doesn't see the trap (1:17). **"He does not know that it will cost him his life."** That is the tragedy. He thinks he has found pleasure. He has found death. The father does not tell us what happened next, and he doesn't need to.
- **"And now, O sons, listen to me" (v24).** The story is over, and the father turns to his listeners.
- **"Let not your heart turn aside to her ways; do not stray into her paths" (v25).** The heart goes first and the feet follow (4:23). "Stray" is *taʿah*, to wander off course.
- **"Many a victim has she laid low, and all her slain are a mighty throng" (v26).** "Mighty throng" (*ʿatsumim*) can mean "numerous" or "strong." Not only the simple fall to her. Strong men have too. Samson and David are famous examples, and so, later, was Solomon himself (Neh 13:26). JFB: "Even the mightiest fail to resist her deathly allurements."
- **"Her house is the way to Sheol, going down to the chambers of death" (v27).** She promised a bedroom, and her house leads to the inner rooms of death. The chapter ends on the same note as 2:18 and 5:5. In chapter 9 Folly will issue the same invitation, and "her guests are in the depths of Sheol" (9:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry divides the chapter into a call to fill the mind with God's word (vv1–5), a picture of how the inexperienced are lured (vv6–23) and a warning against even the first steps (vv24–27). His aim is that "every one may possess his vessel in sanctification and honour," taking the words of 1 Thessalonians 4:4, and he turns it into a prayer: "Lord, lead us not into this temptation." Guzik says keeping God's words is not a magic shield. It means knowing, treasuring and thinking on them, so that they expose temptation's tactics. He walks through the woman's approach step by step and notes how she uses religion to cover sin and the absent husband to remove fear. JFB glosses the "apple of the eye" as the pupil, "a custody… of special value," and takes the woman's feast as meat kept from her offering (Lev 3:9). The ESV footnote, not the commentators, flags the textual problem in v22.

#### Pointing to Christ
The young man was "lacking sense" and walked toward the dark. Two women called to him from the same streets, and he listened to the wrong one. Jesus is the Wisdom who calls in the open (Matt 11:19, 28–30), "the light of the world" whose followers "will not walk in darkness" (John 8:12). He also met temptation head on and did not go the way of the simple: "It is written" was his answer every time (Matt 4:1–11). And he knows how to rescue those who were caught. He is the friend of sinners who did not turn away the woman who came to him in tears (Luke 7:36–50), and he says to people taken in adultery, "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (John 8:11).
