*Setting:* Peter is writing from Rome ("Babylon," v13) in the early 60s AD, a few years at most before his death under Nero. His readers are mostly Gentile churches scattered across five provinces of Asia Minor (1:1), slandered and squeezed out of their communities for their faith. Chapter 4 ended with the "fiery trial" and the call for sufferers to entrust their souls "to a faithful Creator while doing good" (4:19). If judgment begins "at the household of God" (4:17), the household needs good shepherds. So Peter turns to the church's leaders, then to the younger members, then to everyone, with a final word about humility, anxiety and the devil, before closing with greetings from Rome.

#### Shepherd the Flock of God (vv1–11)
- **"So I exhort the elders among you, as a fellow elder" (v1).** "So" ties this to 4:17–19: in a time of testing, leaders matter. "Elders" (*presbyteroi*) were the leaders of each local church, appointed from the start of the Gentile mission (Acts 14:23; 20:17). Peter, the leading apostle, does not pull rank. He calls himself a "fellow elder" (*sympresbyteros*, used only here) and exhorts rather than commands.
- **"A witness of the sufferings of Christ" (v1).** Peter saw Jesus arrested and tried, and he knew his own failure that night (Mark 14:66–72). Whether he saw the crucifixion itself is not recorded, but he had seen enough. "Witness" (*martys*) also came to mean one who testifies by suffering, which is how Peter would end his own life (John 21:18–19).
- **"A partaker in the glory that is going to be revealed" (v1).** Peter had already glimpsed that glory on the mountain of transfiguration (2 Pet 1:16–18), and he looks forward to sharing it. Suffering now, glory later: the same pattern as 1:11 and 4:13.
- **"Shepherd the flock of God that is among you" (v2).** The verb *poimainō* covers everything a shepherd does: feeding, guiding, guarding. It is the very word Jesus used when he restored Peter by the Sea of Galilee: "Tend my sheep" (John 21:16). Peter passes on the charge he received. The flock is "of God," not the elders' own, and "among you," a local, particular people. The word "pastor" comes from this shepherd image (Latin *pastor*, "shepherd"). In the New Testament elders, overseers and pastors are the same people seen from different angles (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7).
- **"Exercising oversight" (v2).** The verb is *episkopeō*, from which comes *episkopos*, "overseer" or "bishop." Some early manuscripts lack the word, but most modern translations keep it.
- **Three contrasts (vv2–3).** Peter frames good leadership as three pairs. *Not under compulsion, but willingly*: serving because one wants to, not because someone has to. *Not for shameful gain, but eagerly*: elders were sometimes paid (1 Tim 5:17–18), and the temptation to see ministry as income is old (1 Tim 3:3; Titus 1:11). *Not domineering, but being examples*: "domineering" (*katakyrieuō*) is the word Jesus used of Gentile rulers who "lord it over" others, adding, "It shall not be so among you" (Mark 10:42–43). Peter was in the room for that lesson.
- **"Those in your charge" (v3).** The Greek is *klēroi*, "lots" or "allotments," the word used for land shares assigned by lot (Abbott-Smith gives the sense here as the presbyter's assigned charge). Each congregation is a portion God has entrusted to its elders, not territory they own. This word later gave English "clergy," ironically, since Peter applies it to the people, not the leaders.
- **"When the chief Shepherd appears" (v4).** Jesus is the "chief Shepherd" (*archipoimēn*, used only here), the one who owns the flock and to whom every under-shepherd answers. Peter has already called him "the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls" (2:25), and Jesus called himself "the good shepherd" (John 10:11). Hebrews calls him "the great shepherd of the sheep" (Heb 13:20). Behind all three stands Ezekiel 34, where God condemns the selfish shepherds of Israel and promises to shepherd his people himself through his servant David.
- **"The unfading crown of glory" (v4).** "Crown" (*stephanos*) was the wreath given to a winning athlete or an honored citizen, usually of olive, pine or celery leaves, which withered within days. "Unfading" is *amarantinos*, literally "of amaranth," a flower that Greeks believed never faded (the name means "unwithering"). Peter used a related word in 1:4 of an inheritance that is "unfading." The reward is not power or pay but glory that lasts.
- **"You who are younger, be subject to the elders" (v5).** "Likewise" continues the call to rightly ordered submission that began in 2:13. "Younger" most likely means the younger members of the church in general, though some think of a group of younger helpers. In a church of new converts, the energetic young could easily brush aside older leaders.
- **"Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility" (v5).** Now everyone, leaders included. The verb *enkomboomai* comes from *kombos*, a knot. An *enkombōma* was a garment tied on over other clothes, especially the apron worn by slaves at work. To "tie on" humility like a work apron is to dress for service. It is hard not to think of the night Jesus "tied a towel around his waist" and washed his disciples' feet, including Peter's, while Peter protested (John 13:4–9).
- **"God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (v5).** A quotation of Proverbs 3:34 in its Greek form, also quoted by James (James 4:6). "Opposes" is a military word: God lines up against the proud.
- **"Humble yourselves, therefore, under the mighty hand of God" (v6).** "The mighty hand of God" is the Exodus phrase for God's power to rescue his people from Egypt (Exod 3:19–20; Deut 26:8). The hand that humbles is the hand that delivers. "At the proper time he may exalt you" echoes Jesus' saying that "whoever humbles himself will be exalted" (Luke 14:11). The timing is his, not ours.
- **"Casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (v7).** In Greek this is not a new command but a participle hanging on v6: humbling oneself *by* casting anxieties on God. Worry is a kind of pride, an attempt to carry what only God can. Peter echoes Psalm 55:22: "Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you." The verb "cast" (*epiriptō*) is used elsewhere in the New Testament only of throwing cloaks on the colt for Jesus to ride (Luke 19:35): a deliberate throw, not a gentle set-down. The reason is personal: "he cares for you." Peter may be remembering Jesus' teaching on anxiety and the Father who feeds the birds (Matt 6:25–34).
- **"Be sober-minded; be watchful" (v8).** Two sharp commands. "Be watchful" (*grēgoreō*) is what Jesus asked of Peter, James and John in Gethsemane: "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Mark 14:38). Peter slept, and within hours he denied Jesus three times. Satan had asked to "sift" him like wheat (Luke 22:31). Peter is writing from hard experience.
- **"Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour" (v8).** "Adversary" (*antidikos*) is a legal word for the opposing party in a lawsuit (Matt 5:25; Luke 18:3), which suits the devil's role as accuser (Rev 12:10; Zech 3:1). "Devil" (*diabolos*) means "slanderer." A lion roars to terrify, and Psalm 22:13 pictures the enemies around the suffering righteous one as "a ravening and roaring lion." For these believers the roar came through slander, social pressure and the threat of persecution, all designed to frighten them into giving up the faith. Readers in Rome may also have thought of the arena, where within a few years Christians would literally be thrown to beasts.
- **"Resist him, firm in your faith" (v9).** Resist, not run. James says the same: "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7; compare Eph 6:11–13). The resistance is "firm in your faith": standing on what God has said and done, not on willpower.
- **"Your brotherhood throughout the world" (v9).** "Brotherhood" (*adelphotēs*) is the worldwide family of believers, men and women alike (also 2:17). Suffering isolates, and the devil whispers that no one else has it this hard. Peter answers that believers in many places are going through the same kinds of suffering.
- **"After you have suffered a little while" (v10).** Compared with "eternal glory," the suffering is "a little while" (1:6; Rom 8:18; 2 Cor 4:17). It is real, but it ends.
- **"The God of all grace… will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you" (v10).** "Will himself" is emphatic: God does it personally. The four verbs pile up. "Restore" (*katartizō*) is used of fishermen mending nets (Mark 1:19), a picture Peter the fisherman knew well. "Confirm" (*stērizō*) is the word Jesus used when he told Peter, "when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers" (Luke 22:32). "Strengthen" and "establish" (*themelioō*, to set on a foundation) complete the picture of a life made firm.
- **"To him be the dominion forever and ever. Amen" (v11).** A short doxology. Some later manuscripts add "glory and," as in 4:11; the oldest have only "dominion," God's ruling might.
- **Commentators:** All three read "fellow elder" as Peter refusing to lord it over other leaders. Henry says Peter "does not command, but exhorts," and Guzik notes he claims no papal-style authority. Guzik connects the shepherd charge to John 21 and the crown to the withering wreath of the games. JFB calls the crown "a garland of victory," not a king's diadem, and reads v5 as "gird on humility as the slave dress," which the lexicon supports. Guzik makes the same apron link and ties it to Jesus washing feet. On v7 JFB writes, "Care is a burden which faith casts off the man on his God," and Henry puts v5 this way: "Humility preserves peace and order in all Christian churches and societies; pride disturbs them." On v8 Henry warns that Satan's "whole design" is "to devour and destroy souls," and Guzik adds that the lion was defanged at the cross but still devours with lies. JFB notes that the oldest manuscripts drop "because" in v8 and "glory and" in v11.

#### Final Greetings (vv12–14)
- **"By Silvanus, a faithful brother" (v12).** Silvanus is the Latin form of Silas, Paul's companion on the second missionary journey (Acts 15:40; 16:19–25; 1 Thess 1:1). He had also carried the Jerusalem council's letter to the Gentile churches (Acts 15:22–27). "By Silvanus" may mean he carried this letter to the churches, or that he helped write it as Peter's secretary, or both. Many scholars think Silvanus's help explains the letter's polished Greek, unexpected from a Galilean fisherman (Acts 4:13), though Peter may simply have been more fluent than people assume.
- **"I have written briefly to you" (v12).** The letter has 105 verses, and Peter calls it brief. Ancient letters were often long, and he has much more he could say.
- **"This is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it" (v12).** This sums up the letter. Suffering for Christ is not a sign that their faith was a mistake. The grace they received is the real thing, so they should stand in it.
- **"She who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen" (v13).** "She" is almost certainly a church, the congregation where Peter is, "chosen" like the readers (1:1). Some have thought of Peter's wife (1 Cor 9:5), but that is less likely. "Babylon" is most likely a code name for Rome. Jewish writings after AD 70 and the book of Revelation use it this way (Rev 14:8; 17:5; 18:2), and early church tradition places Peter in Rome at the end of his life. The name also fits the letter's theme: believers are "exiles" (1:1; 2:11), and Babylon was the classic place of exile. A minority have taken it literally as Babylon on the Euphrates, or as a Roman fort called Babylon in Egypt, but there is no early evidence that Peter worked in either.
- **"And so does Mark, my son" (v13).** This is John Mark, from a Jerusalem family whose house was where the church prayed for Peter's release (Acts 12:12). He had once deserted Paul (Acts 13:13) and was later restored (Col 4:10; 2 Tim 4:11). "My son" is spiritual affection, not physical. Early in the second century Papias recorded that Mark became Peter's interpreter and wrote down what Peter remembered of Jesus' words and deeds, which became the Gospel of Mark.
- **"Greet one another with the kiss of love" (v14).** A kiss on the cheek was a normal greeting among family and close friends. Paul calls it "a holy kiss" (Rom 16:16). In a church that mixed slaves and free, Jews and Gentiles, it was a striking sign that they were now one family.
- **"Peace to all of you who are in Christ" (v14).** The letter opened with "grace and peace" (1:2) and ends with peace. Some manuscripts add "Jesus" and a closing "Amen." Peace is not the absence of trouble here, since the readers are in the middle of it. It is the peace of belonging to Christ.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and the Tyndale notes take Silvanus to be Paul's companion Silas, and JFB agrees. The Tyndale notes take "Babylon" as Rome, and Guzik leans that way while allowing the literal city. JFB is the outlier: it reads Babylon as the city on the Euphrates and says "Rome is not meant," partly in reaction to Roman Catholic claims. JFB cites Papias that Mark was Peter's interpreter, and Guzik also connects Mark with Peter's memories in the Gospel. Henry draws the closing lesson that "all solid peace is founded on Christ, and flows from him."

#### Pointing to Christ
Peter's last chapter carries the story of his own restoration. The man who denied Jesus three times was asked three times, "Do you love me?" and told, "Feed my sheep" (John 21:15–17). Now he tells other leaders to do the same, under "the chief Shepherd" who will appear. He urges humility tied on like the apron Jesus wore when he washed Peter's feet, watchfulness he once lacked in Gethsemane, and firmness against the adversary who once sifted him. Every instruction points back to the Shepherd who did not give up on him, and forward to the "eternal glory in Christ" (v10) that the God of all grace has called his people to share.
