*Setting:* Jerusalem, around 976 BC, roughly the twenty-fifth year of David's reign in Jerusalem and some eleven years after Amnon violated Tamar (13:23, 38; 14:28; 15:7). Absalom has been brought home from exile and given a cold kiss of reconciliation (14:33), but nothing has been resolved. He is handsome, popular and probably now the eldest surviving son, but no one has named him heir. David is older and seems to have grown distant from the daily work of judging his people. Nathan had warned, "I will raise up evil against you out of your own house" (12:11). In this chapter that evil takes the throne, and David leaves his city on foot, weeping. Psalm 3 is headed "A Psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom his son."

#### Absalom's Conspiracy (vv1–12)
- **"Absalom got himself a chariot and horses, and fifty men to run before him" (v1).** These were the trappings of a king. Samuel had warned that a king would take sons "to run before his chariots" (1 Sam 8:11). Adonijah, the next son to grab for the throne, does exactly the same (1 Kings 1:5). Horses and chariots were still rare in Israel, so this was a public show.
- **"Absalom used to rise early and stand beside the way of the gate" (v2).** The city gate was where courts sat and business was done (Ruth 4:1–2). People from the tribes came to the king's court for justice when local courts failed them. Absalom gets there first, every morning.
- **"Your claims are good and right, but there is no man designated by the king to hear you" (v3).** He agrees with everyone before hearing the other side. He does not promise to help. He only plants the idea that the king does not care. There may have been some truth in the complaint. David had failed to do justice for Tamar (13:21), and Absalom knew it.
- **"Oh that I were judge in the land!" (v4).** "Judge" here means ruler, the one who sets things right. He is campaigning.
- **"He would put out his hand and take hold of him and kiss him" (v5).** When people bowed to him as a prince, he raised them up and embraced them like equals. He used the same kiss David had given him (14:33).
- **"So Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel" (v6).** In Hebrew, "to steal someone's heart" means to deceive them. Jacob "stole the heart of Laban" when he slipped away without telling him (Gen 31:20, where the ESV has "tricked"). So the phrase means both that he won their affection and that he did it by deceit.
- **"At the end of four years" (v7).** The standard Hebrew text reads "forty years," which cannot be right, since David reigned only forty years in all. The ESV follows the Greek, the Syriac and the historian Josephus in reading "four." Four years, probably counted from Absalom's return or from his reconciliation with David.
- **"Let me go and pay my vow… in Hebron" (v7).** Hebron was where Absalom was born (3:2–3) and where David was first crowned, over Judah and then over all Israel (2:4; 5:3). It was a natural place to launch a claim to the throne, and perhaps the men of Judah there resented that David had moved the capital to Jerusalem. The "vow" is a cover story using worship, as David himself had once used a family sacrifice as an excuse to be absent from Saul's table (1 Sam 20:6, 29).
- **"Go in peace" (v9).** These are the last words David ever speaks to Absalom. The father blesses the son who is leaving to overthrow him.
- **"As soon as you hear the sound of the trumpet, then say, 'Absalom is king at Hebron!'" (v10).** Secret agents were planted in every tribe. The trumpet would sound from hill to hill, and the cry would be taken up all over the country at once, making the revolt seem unstoppable.
- **"Two hundred men… who were invited guests… knew nothing" (v11).** Respected men from Jerusalem went along to the "sacrifice." Their presence made it look as if the court supported Absalom. They were used without their knowledge.
- **"He sent for Ahithophel the Gilonite, David's counselor" (v12).** This was the biggest blow. Ahithophel's advice was treated "as if one consulted the word of God" (16:23). Giloh was a town in the hill country of Judah near Hebron (Josh 15:51). He was probably Bathsheba's grandfather (11:3; 23:34). The text never gives his motive, but many think he had never forgiven David for what he did to her and to Uriah. Many Christians have heard David's grief over him in Psalm 41:9 and 55:12–14: "my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me." Jesus applies Psalm 41:9 to Judas (John 13:18).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry doubts there was any vow at all: "If he said he had made such a vow, nobody could disprove him." JFB thinks the real vow, if there was one, was a thanksgiving promised in exile, and that Hebron was chosen because it was a famous place of worship and his birthplace. JFB also sees genuine discontent behind the revolt: leftover loyalty to Saul, the Bathsheba scandal, Joab's arrogance and slow justice. Guzik grants that Absalom had real grievances, since David had done nothing about Tamar, but calls his deepest sin impatience: he tried to take the throne while his father lived. Guzik also notes that Absalom may well have felt spiritual and sure of God's blessing while he did it. All three agree on "four" rather than "forty" years in v7.

#### David Flees Jerusalem (vv13–37)
- **"The hearts of the men of Israel have gone after Absalom" (v13).** The stolen hearts (v6) have now followed the thief.
- **"Arise, and let us flee… lest he… strike the city with the edge of the sword" (v14).** David does not try to hold Jerusalem. He may have feared a siege trapping him with traitors inside, or he wanted to spare the city a massacre. He also knew Absalom would kill without hesitation (13:28–29).
- **"The king left ten concubines to keep the house" (v16).** A concubine was a lesser wife, a legal wife without full rights. David expects to return soon. Their fate is the fulfillment of Nathan's word in 12:11, as 16:21–22 will show.
- **"They halted at the last house" (v17).** Literally "the far house," perhaps the last building on the edge of the city. David stops there and reviews his loyal troops as they pass.
- **The Cherethites, Pelethites and Gittites (v18).** The Cherethites and Pelethites were David's bodyguard, probably Cretans and Philistines (8:18; 20:23). The six hundred Gittites were men from Gath, the Philistine city where David once lived as a refugee (1 Sam 27:2–3). At the darkest hour of his reign, foreigners stand by David while his own son and countrymen turn against him.
- **"Why do you also go with us?… You came only yesterday" (vv19–20).** Ittai of Gath was a newcomer and a foreigner. David releases him: go back and serve "the king," meaning Absalom. He prays the LORD will show Ittai "steadfast love and faithfulness" (*ḥesed* and *ʾemet*), the covenant loyalty David cannot now promise him himself.
- **"Wherever my lord the king shall be, whether for death or for life, there also will your servant be" (v21).** Ittai swears by the LORD, Israel's God. His words echo another foreigner's pledge: Ruth the Moabite's "where you go I will go… where you die I will die" (Ruth 1:16–17). Ruth was David's great-grandmother. Ittai later commands a third of David's army (18:2).
- **"All the land wept aloud… and the king crossed the brook Kidron" (v23).** The Kidron is the valley on the east side of Jerusalem, dry in summer and running only after the winter rains. It separates the city from the Mount of Olives. About a thousand years later, on the night he was betrayed, Jesus "went out with his disciples across the brook Kidron" to a garden on that same mountain (John 18:1).
- **The ark brought out (v24).** Zadok and Abiathar, David's two priests (8:17), bring the ark to go with the king. In the days of Eli, Israel had carried the ark into battle like a lucky charm, and it was captured (1 Sam 4:3–11).
- **"Carry the ark of God back into the city" (v25).** David will not use God's presence as a tool. **"If I find favor in the eyes of the LORD, he will bring me back… But if he says, 'I have no pleasure in you,' behold, here I am, let him do to me what seems good to him" (vv25–26).** This is the old David, humble before God. He accepts that this may be the LORD's discipline, and he leaves the outcome in God's hands. It echoes Joab's faith in 10:12 and Eli's in 1 Sam 3:18.
- **"Are you not a seer?" (v27).** A seer was a prophet (1 Sam 9:9). The Hebrew is a little unclear and some read it as "Do you see?" meaning "Do you understand?" Either way, David is giving Zadok a role. The priests' sons, Ahimaaz and Jonathan, will carry news from the city to the king at "the fords of the wilderness" (v28), the Jordan crossings near Jericho. David trusts God and also makes plans.
- **"David went up the ascent of the Mount of Olives, weeping as he went, barefoot and with his head covered" (v30).** These are signs of deep mourning and shame (Esth 6:12; Isa 20:2–4; Jer 14:3–4). The people with him do the same. The king who once danced before the ark (6:14) now walks barefoot up the hill in tears.
- **"O LORD, please turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness" (v31).** A short, urgent prayer. "Foolishness" may play on the name: some think Ahithophel means "brother of folly." God answers almost at once.
- **"While David was coming to the summit, where God was worshiped" (v32).** At the top of the Mount of Olives was a place of worship. As David arrives there, the answer to his prayer meets him: **Hushai the Archite** with his coat torn and dirt on his head. The Archites were a clan living near the border of Benjamin and Ephraim (Josh 16:2). Hushai may not have been an Israelite by birth.
- **"If you return to the city… then you will defeat for me the counsel of Ahithophel" (v34).** An old man would slow the flight, but as a spy in Absalom's court he can do far more. David tells him to pretend to switch sides. Hushai's reports are to go through the priests and their sons (vv35–36).
- **"So Hushai, David's friend, came into the city, just as Absalom was entering Jerusalem" (v37).** "The king's friend" was an official title for a trusted royal adviser (1 Chr 27:33; 1 Kings 4:5). The timing is tense: the rebel enters by one gate, and David's man slips in at the same moment.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says of the ark: "David's heart, like Eli's, trembles for the ark of God," and he admires David's submission to God's will. JFB holds that David also sent the ark back so the priests could watch the enemy's movements for him. All three treat the climb up the Mount of Olives as genuine humility, and JFB says David was moved by "contrition for his transgressions." Guzik draws the line to Jesus, who walked the same path from the city across the Kidron, but who suffered for others' sins, while David suffered for his own. On Hushai, Henry admits he cannot see how this deception "can be justified, as a stratagem in war." Guzik points out how quickly God answered David's prayer against Ahithophel. JFB sees in David's prayer and his plan for Hushai "fervent piety… combined… with… far-seeing policy." The Tyndale notes say the same in their own way: David trusted God's guidance and still made strategic moves.

#### Pointing to Christ
A king is betrayed by his own son and by a trusted friend who ate at his table. He leaves Jerusalem, crosses the Kidron, climbs the Mount of Olives weeping and gives himself into God's hands: "let him do to me what seems good to him." The Gospel writers saw the pattern. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus crossed the same brook to the same mountain (John 18:1), and in Gethsemane prayed, "not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). He quoted Psalm 41:9 of Judas (John 13:18). The difference is the reason. David was suffering the fruit of his own sin. Jesus, the Son of David, had no sin, and he went up that hill to bear the sins of others. A foreigner, Ittai, swore to follow David "whether for death or for life." The true King calls people from every nation to follow him like that, and promises them a share in his return.
