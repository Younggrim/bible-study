*Setting:* About 853 BC. Jehoshaphat, king of Judah, has just escaped with his life from the battle at Ramoth-gilead. He joined Ahab of Israel against the Arameans, heard the prophet Micaiah warn that Ahab would die, went anyway, and nearly died in Ahab's place when enemy chariots mistook him for the king of Israel. He cried out, "and the LORD helped him" (18:31). Ahab was killed by an arrow shot at random (18:33–34). This short chapter, found only in Chronicles, shows how Jehoshaphat responds. A prophet rebukes him, and instead of jailing the prophet as his father Asa did (16:10), he goes back to work: he travels his kingdom calling people back to God and sets up a system of courts. His name, Jehoshaphat, means "the LORD judges," and this chapter shows him living up to it.

#### Jehu the Prophet Rebukes Jehoshaphat (vv1–3)
- **"Jehoshaphat the king of Judah returned in safety to his house in Jerusalem" (v1).** "In safety" is literally "in peace" (*shalom*). It is a quiet but pointed contrast with the previous chapter. Ahab said, "until I return in peace" (18:26), and Micaiah replied, "If you return in peace, the LORD has not spoken by me" (18:27). Ahab did not return. Jehoshaphat did, by grace alone.
- **"Jehu the son of Hanani the seer went out to meet him" (v2).** A prophet from a family of prophets. His father Hanani rebuked Asa for relying on Syria and was thrown in prison for it (16:7–10). Jehu himself had delivered God's judgment on King Baasha of Israel decades earlier (1 Kgs 16:1–4, 7). Like Azariah meeting Asa (15:2), he "went out to meet" the king on the road.
- **"Should you help the wicked and love those who hate the LORD?" (v2).** Two sharp questions. "Help" is a word for military aid. "Love" and "hate" in the Old Testament are often the language of covenant and treaty loyalty, not only feelings. Ancient treaties called allies those who "love" each other. So Jehu is saying: you have made a covenant of loyalty with a man in covenant rebellion against God. Jehoshaphat had said to Ahab, "I am as you are, my people as your people" (18:3). He gave Ahab what belonged to God.
- **Does this mean believers must hate unbelievers?** No. The issue is binding alliance with those who oppose God, not ordinary kindness or neighborliness. The psalmist says, "O you who love the LORD, hate evil!" (Ps 97:10), and Paul says, "Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers" (2 Cor 6:14). Jesus commands love for enemies (Matt 5:44). Jehoshaphat's fault was joining himself to Ahab's cause, sealed by marriage and war.
- **"Because of this, wrath has gone out against you from the LORD" (v2).** "Wrath" (*qetseph*) is God's anger at sin, a key word in Chronicles for judgment on unfaithfulness (24:18; 29:8; 32:25–26). The text does not say exactly how this wrath was felt. Many think of the invasion in the next chapter (20:1), or the wrecked ships of 20:35–37. The worst result came later. The marriage alliance brought Athaliah, Ahab's daughter, into Judah's royal house, and she nearly destroyed David's line (21:6; 22:10).
- **"Nevertheless, some good is found in you" (v3).** God's rebuke comes with real encouragement. "Some good" is literally "good things," the same phrase used of Judah in Rehoboam's day (12:12). God sees what is genuine even in a compromised believer.
- **"You destroyed the Asheroth out of the land" (v3).** The wooden poles of the goddess Asherah (17:6).
- **"And have set your heart to seek God" (v3).** The exact opposite of Rehoboam, who "did not set his heart to seek the LORD" (12:14). "Set" means to prepare or fix firmly. Jehoshaphat's heart was pointed the right way, even when his choices were not. It is the same phrase used of Ezra, who "had set his heart to study the Law of the LORD, and to do it, and to teach" (Ezra 7:10).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says, "Whenever we return in peace to our houses we ought to acknowledge God's providence," and on why Jehoshaphat survived, "God is not extreme to mark what we do amiss, nor does he withdraw his protection every time we forfeit it." On the encouragement of v3: "It is good when commendations thus quicken us to our duty." Guzik, following Selman, explains "love," "hate" and "help" as treaty language, and he points to Psalm 97:10 as the principle Jehoshaphat ignored. Through Poole he adds that Jehoshaphat's prepared heart was his own act and also the work of God's grace (Prov 16:1; Phil 2:13). JFB identifies Jehu as the son of the Hanani "of whom we read in 2Ch 16:7."

#### Jehoshaphat's Reforms (vv4–11)
- **"Jehoshaphat lived at Jerusalem" (v4).** He stayed home. No more adventures in Samaria.
- **"He went out again among the people, from Beersheba to the hill country of Ephraim" (v4).** Beersheba was Judah's southern edge, and the hill country of Ephraim its northern border, including the towns Asa captured (15:8; 17:2). "From Dan to Beersheba" was the old phrase for all Israel. "From Beersheba to Ephraim" is the whole of Judah. "Again" looks back to the teaching mission of 17:7–9. This time the king goes himself.
- **"And brought them back to the LORD, the God of their fathers" (v4).** The verb is *shub*, "turn back" or "return," the Old Testament's main word for repentance. Jehoshaphat, having just turned back himself, turns others back. This is the Chronicler's picture of a shepherd-king, very different from Ahab, who left his sheep scattered (18:16).
- **"He appointed judges in the land in all the fortified cities of Judah, city by city" (v5).** Moses commanded, "You shall appoint judges and officers in all your towns" (Deut 16:18). The fortified cities were the district centers (11:5–12; 17:2, 12), so placing courts there put justice within reach of every region.
- **"Consider what you do, for you judge not for man but for the LORD" (v6).** The most important principle in the charge. A judge is not the king's servant only, and not the servant of whoever pays or pressures him. He acts on God's behalf. Moses told Israel's first judges, "the judgment is God's" (Deut 1:17).
- **"He is with you in giving judgment" (v6).** God is present in the courtroom. Psalm 82:1 pictures God standing "in the divine council," judging the judges. That is both a comfort and a warning.
- **"Let the fear of the LORD be upon you" (v7).** Awe before the God who sees everything is what keeps a judge honest when no one else is watching.
- **"There is no injustice with the LORD our God, or partiality or taking bribes" (v7).** Judges must be like God. "Partiality" is literally "lifting up faces," favoring someone because of who they are. The phrase echoes Deuteronomy 10:17, where God "is not partial and takes no bribe," and 16:19: "You shall not pervert justice. You shall not show partiality, and you shall not accept a bribe, for a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise." The prophets repeatedly condemned Israel's judges for exactly these sins (Isa 1:23; Amos 5:12; Mic 3:11).
- **"In Jerusalem Jehoshaphat appointed certain Levites and priests and heads of families of Israel" (v8).** A higher court in the capital, as Moses provided for cases "too difficult for you" (Deut 17:8–13). It combined religious experts (priests and Levites) and respected lay leaders (family heads).
- **"To give judgment for the LORD and to decide disputed cases" (v8).** Two kinds of cases, perhaps religious law and civil disputes, matching the two leaders in v11. **"They had their seat at Jerusalem."** The Hebrew could also be read "they returned to Jerusalem," and some translations take it that way. The ESV's reading makes good sense.
- **"In the fear of the LORD, in faithfulness, and with your whole heart" (v9).** Three marks of a good judge: reverence, reliability and an undivided heart. "Whole heart" is *lebab shalem*, the same phrase used of Asa's heart (15:17).
- **"Concerning bloodshed, law or commandment, statutes or rules" (v10).** "Bloodshed" refers to cases of killing, whether murder or manslaughter, as in the law of the cities of refuge (Num 35; Deut 19). The other terms cover every part of God's law.
- **"Then you shall warn them, that they may not incur guilt before the LORD" (v10).** The judges are not only to decide cases but to teach and warn, so that sin and its guilt do not spread through the nation. Guilt left unchecked brings "wrath… upon you and your brothers," the whole community. Jehoshaphat has just been told that "wrath has gone out" against him (v2). He wants to spare his people that.
- **"Amariah the chief priest is over you in all matters of the LORD; and Zebadiah… in all the king's matters" (v11).** A clear division of responsibility between religious and royal affairs. Amariah is otherwise unknown, though a priest of that name appears in the high priestly line (1 Chr 6:11). Zebadiah, "the governor of the house of Judah," was probably the head of the tribe of Judah or the king's chief official. Some see in this an early distinction between the spheres of worship and civil government, though both stood under God.
- **"The Levites will serve you as officers" (v11).** "Officers" (*shoterim*) were court officials who kept records and carried out decisions. David had appointed six thousand Levites as officers and judges (1 Chr 23:4; 26:29).
- **"Deal courageously, and may the LORD be with the upright!" (v11).** Literally "Be strong and act." Judging fairly takes courage, especially when powerful people are involved. The blessing, "the LORD be with the good," echoes the promise that "the LORD is with you while you are with him" (15:2).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry urges judges to "deal courageously, and fear not the face of man," and writes, "Wherever he finds a good man, a good magistrate, he will be found a good God." He adds that "judges, of all men, have need to be cautious, because so much depends upon the correctness of their judgment," and that those with power who fail to use it against sin "contract the guilt of sin themselves." JFB reads v4 as reappointing "the commissioners of public instruction" and describes the system as local courts "in each of the fortified cities, these being the provincial capitals," with "a supreme court, which sat in Jerusalem to review appellate cases." Guzik cites Selman that a king traveling his land to call people back to God has no real parallel in the Old Testament, the closest being Jesus' own traveling ministry. Through Adam Clarke, he adds that even the best laws are worthless without honest people to apply them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jehoshaphat told his judges, "You judge not for man but for the LORD… there is no injustice with the LORD our God, or partiality or taking bribes." Every human court falls short of that standard. Isaiah promised a Son of David on whom the Spirit would rest, who would "not judge by what his eyes see… but with righteousness he shall judge the poor" (Isa 11:3–4). The Father "has given all judgment to the Son" (John 5:22), and that Judge is also the one who bore judgment for his people. The king who came home "in peace" only by grace, and then went out to bring others back to God, is a small picture of what every forgiven sinner is called to do.
