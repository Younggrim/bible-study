*Setting:* Jerusalem, the seventh month (Tishri, September–October) of 445 BC, only days after the wall was finished on the twenty-fifth of the month before (6:15). The building is done. Now the book turns from stones to hearts. Ezra the priest and scribe, who had come from Babylon thirteen years earlier with a mandate to teach God's law (Ezra 7:6–10), appears in Nehemiah's story for the first time. Chapters 8–10 are mostly told in the third person, and many scholars think they come from Ezra's own records, woven into Nehemiah's memoir. (A minority of scholars place Ezra's arrival after Nehemiah's, but the text as it stands presents the two men working together.) The seventh month was the high point of Israel's worship year: the Feast of Trumpets on the first day, the Day of Atonement on the tenth and the Feast of Booths from the fifteenth. Chapter 8 moves through three scenes: the Law read and explained, tears turned to joy, and the Feast of Booths kept as it had not been kept in centuries.

#### Ezra Reads the Law (vv1–8)
- **"All the people gathered as one man" (v1).** The same phrase describes the first returnees gathering to rebuild the altar in the seventh month (Ezra 3:1). The writer wants the echo heard. The people are not summoned by Ezra. *They* ask him to "bring the Book of the Law of Moses." The hunger starts with the hearers.
- **"The square before the Water Gate" (v1).** The Water Gate stood on the eastern side of the city, above the Gihon Spring, Jerusalem's main water source (3:26). An open square there could hold a large crowd. The meeting was held in a public space, not in the temple courts, so that everyone could come, women included, and there was room for a crowd.
- **"The Book of the Law of Moses" (v1).** This was the Torah, the five books from Genesis to Deuteronomy, or at least the legal parts of it. It would have been a large scroll, or several.
- **"Both men and women and all who could understand what they heard" (v2).** Women and older children were included. The Law itself required this: every seventh year at the Feast of Booths, "men, women, and little ones, and the sojourner" were to gather and hear it read (Deut 31:10–13). "Understand" is a key word in the chapter, appearing in vv2, 3, 7, 8 and 12. The goal is not just hearing but grasping.
- **"On the first day of the seventh month" (v2).** This was the Feast of Trumpets, a day of rest and holy gathering announced with trumpet blasts (Lev 23:23–25; Num 29:1–6). In later Judaism it became Rosh Hashanah, the New Year.
- **"From early morning until midday" (v3).** About five or six hours. "The ears of all the people were attentive." The people stood (v5) and listened most of the morning, without complaint recorded.
- **"A wooden platform that they had made for the purpose" (v4).** The Hebrew is literally "a tower of wood," a raised stand. The KJV calls it a "pulpit." Thirteen men stood with Ezra, six on one side and seven on the other, probably leading laymen or priests, perhaps helping to hold the scroll and to share the reading.
- **"As he opened it all the people stood" (v5).** Standing for the reading of Scripture was a mark of respect, and the custom continues in synagogues and many churches today. The Tyndale notes suggest they stood through the whole reading.
- **"Ezra blessed the LORD, the great God" (v6).** Before reading, Ezra praised God. The people answered, "Amen, Amen," meaning "it is true" or "so be it," doubled for emphasis. They lifted their hands, the ancient posture of prayer (Ps 28:2; 1 Tim 2:8), then bowed with faces to the ground. The whole body joins in worship: hands up, then face down.
- **"The Levites, helped the people to understand the Law" (v7).** Thirteen Levites are named. Teaching the Law was the Levites' calling from the start: "They shall teach Jacob your rules and Israel your law" (Deut 33:10; compare Mal 2:6–7). They seem to have moved among the crowd, explaining to groups "while the people remained in their places."
- **"They read from the book… clearly, and they gave the sense" (v8).** "Clearly" is the Hebrew *meporash*, from a root meaning "to make distinct" or "to declare plainly." The same root is used in Aramaic in Ezra 4:18, where a letter is "plainly read" to the Persian king, which there probably means translated as well. Two readings are possible here, and they fit together. The Levites may have translated the Hebrew into Aramaic, which many returnees spoke as their first language after the exile. They also explained what it meant. Jewish tradition saw in this verse the beginning of the Targums, the Aramaic paraphrases of Scripture read in synagogues. "Gave the sense" means they made the meaning plain. The pattern of reading the text, explaining it and applying it is the root of Bible preaching.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in this gathering "expounding brings the reading and the preaching together." He notes that "women and children have souls to save," so the whole community heard. He also urges readers to approach worship "with solemn stops and pauses," as Ezra did when he opened the book. JFB describes the platform as "only a raised scaffold or platform, broad enough to allow fourteen persons to stand with ease." On v8 he gives both views: that Ezra read in Hebrew and the Levites "translated it sentence by sentence into Chaldee" (that is, Aramaic), or that they explained it to people who "had become very ignorant." Guzik calls this a picture of revival, God's Spirit working through God's word among God's people. He stresses that the aim of teaching is understanding, not impressing the hearers, and that "gave the sense" means conveying what the text really says, not the teacher's own agenda.

#### This Day Is Holy (vv9–12)
- **"Nehemiah, who was the governor, and Ezra the priest and scribe" (v9).** This is one of the few places where Nehemiah and Ezra are named together (compare 12:26). The civil leader and the religious teacher stand side by side. "Governor" is *Tirshatha*, the Persian title used in 7:65, 70.
- **"For all the people wept as they heard the words of the Law" (v9).** They heard what God required, saw how far they and their fathers had fallen and grieved. The Law had been ignored and broken for generations, and the exile was the result (2 Kgs 17:7–23; Dan 9:11). When King Josiah first heard the rediscovered Law, he tore his clothes for the same reason (2 Kgs 22:11).
- **"This day is holy to the LORD your God; do not mourn or weep" (v9).** The leaders did not say the tears were wrong. They said the day was wrong for them. A feast day belonged to rejoicing (Num 10:10; Deut 16:14–15). There would be a day for confession, and it comes in chapter 9. Today was for joy.
- **"Eat the fat and drink sweet wine" (v10).** "The fat" means rich, choice food, not plain bread. "Sweet wine" is literally "sweet things," sweet drinks. Meat and fine food were saved for feast days.
- **"Send portions to anyone who has nothing ready" (v10).** Joy before God had to include the poor. This is built into the feasts in Deuteronomy, which tell Israel to rejoice "you and your son and your daughter… the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow" (Deut 16:14). It shows up again at Purim (Esth 9:22).
- **"The joy of the LORD is your strength" (v10).** "Joy" here is *chedvah*, a rare, late word that appears in Hebrew only here and in 1 Chronicles 16:27: "strength and joy are in his place." "Strength" is *ma'oz*, a stronghold, a place of safety, the word in "The LORD is the stronghold of my life" (Ps 27:1). The joy is "of the LORD." It may mean the joy God gives, the joy people have in God, or even God's own joy over his people (compare Zeph 3:17). Most readers take it to include the first two. Either way, the gladness that comes from God is a fortress to shelter in, not a weakness to be outgrown.
- **"So the Levites calmed all the people" (v11).** "Be quiet" is the same word used when Caleb "quieted the people" (Num 13:30). The Levites who had explained the Law now comfort the people it had wounded.
- **"Because they had understood the words that were declared to them" (v12).** The chapter's key word appears again. Their joy rested on understanding. They now knew what God had said, including his promises and his mercy, and not only his demands.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the law "works death, and speaks terror, shows men their sins," which explains the tears, but "every thing is beautiful in its season." On v10 he says this joy strengthens worship and obedience: "Holy joy will be oil to the wheels of our obedience." It also arms believers against temptation. He warns against "a carnal sensual joy" and adds, "Even sorrow for sin must not grow so excessive as to hinder our joy in God and our cheerfulness in his service." His summary: "Holy mourning makes way for holy mirth." JFB notes their "penitential sorrow" and says sending food let the poor share the rejoicing. Guzik agrees that conviction of sin is good. He says it becomes unhealthy only when the sense of sin outweighs the sense of what God is doing to save, so that the knowledge of the Savior should always run deeper than the knowledge of sin.

#### Feast of Booths Celebrated (vv13–18)
- **"On the second day the heads of fathers' houses… came together to Ezra the scribe in order to study" (v13).** The crowd went home, but the family leaders, priests and Levites came back for more. "Study" is literally "to gain insight into." Those who would lead others had to learn first.
- **"They found it written in the Law" (v14).** Reading through the Law, they came to the command for the Feast of Booths (Lev 23:33–43; Deut 16:13–15). They did not invent a celebration. They obeyed something they found written, two weeks before it was due on the fifteenth of the month.
- **"Dwell in booths during the feast of the seventh month" (v14).** For seven days Israelites were to live in shelters made of branches, "that your generations may know that I made the people of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt" (Lev 23:43). The feast remembered the wilderness years, when God fed and sheltered his people with no house of their own. For returnees who had themselves just come out of exile into a half-empty land, the meaning was close to home.
- **"Proclaim it and publish it in all their towns" (v15).** Leviticus 23:2, 4 calls the feasts "holy convocations, which you shall proclaim." A message went out across Judah.
- **"Branches of olive, wild olive, myrtle, palm, and other leafy trees" (v15).** Leviticus 23:40 names palm, "leafy trees" and willows. The list here is a little different, perhaps because these trees grew on the hills around Jerusalem. "Wild olive" is literally "tree of oil," possibly a kind of pine. Myrtle is a fragrant evergreen. "As it is written" probably refers to the command as a whole, not to an exact list of trees.
- **"Each on his roof, and in their courts… and in the square" (v16).** Flat rooftops served as extra living space in Israel. Booths went up on roofs, in courtyards, in the temple courts and in the public squares. The whole city turned green. "The Gate of Ephraim" was on the north side of the city, facing the old territory of Ephraim (12:39).
- **"From the days of Jeshua the son of Nun to that day the people of Israel had not done so" (v17).** Jeshua is Joshua, who led Israel into the land. The feast itself had been kept since then: Solomon dedicated the temple at it (1 Kgs 8:2, 65), and the first returnees kept it in 537 BC (Ezra 3:4). The point seems to be the *way* it was kept: the whole community, actually living in booths, with daily reading of the Law and such great joy. A similar comment is made about the Passovers of Hezekiah and Josiah (2 Chr 30:26; 35:18). Not since the days of Joshua had all Israel kept this feast in quite this way.
- **"Day by day, from the first day to the last day, he read from the Book of the Law of God" (v18).** Deuteronomy required the Law to be read at the Feast of Booths every seventh year (Deut 31:10–11). Ezra read every day of the feast. This was more than the Law required.
- **"On the eighth day there was a solemn assembly, according to the rule" (v18).** The feast lasted seven days, with an extra eighth day of solemn gathering and rest (Lev 23:36, 39; Num 29:35). Centuries later, on "the last day of the feast, the great day," Jesus stood in the temple and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the second-day study and says, "Those that understand the scriptures well will still be desirous to understand them better." He adds, "Priests and Levites must be taught first and then teach." On the booths he writes, "Then we read and hear the word acceptably and profitably when we do according to what is written therein." JFB argues that the feast could not have been neglected since Joshua, since leaders like Samuel and David would never have allowed it, so v17 means "the popular feelings had never been raised to such a height of enthusiastic joy." He calls the daily reading more than was required, a sign of "exuberant zeal," and links the eighth day to John 7:37. Guzik observes that the people obeyed the command simply because they found it in Scripture, not because tradition required it. He ties their "very great rejoicing" to obedience, since real joy comes from yielding to God rather than from having one's own way.

#### Pointing to Christ
The people wept when the Law showed them their sin, and they were told to rejoice because the day was holy to the LORD. That order points beyond itself. The Law could show their guilt, but it could not take it away. Their feast looked back to God sheltering his people in the wilderness. In the fullness of time "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), a word that literally means he pitched his tent among his people. At the Feast of Booths he offered living water to the thirsty (John 7:37–39). The joy of the LORD, a stronghold for weak people, rests in the end on what Christ has done: "These things I have spoken to you, that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be full" (John 15:11).
