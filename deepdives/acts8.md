*Setting:* Jerusalem and beyond, in the early-to-mid 30s AD, right after Stephen's stoning. Until now the church has stayed in Jerusalem, worshiping at the temple and growing in the city. Jesus had said his witnesses would go "in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (1:8). In this chapter the second stage begins, and it starts with persecution. Philip, another of the seven (6:5), carries the gospel to the Samaritans and then to an African official on a desert road. Luke also introduces Saul, the persecutor whose story takes over in chapter 9.

#### Saul Ravages the Church (vv1–3)
- **"Saul approved of his execution" (v1).** The verb (*syneudokeō*) means to agree with and be pleased with. Saul was not a reluctant bystander. He later confessed it: "I myself was standing by and approving and watching over the garments of those who killed him" (22:20, the same verb).
- **"A great persecution… they were all scattered" (v1).** "Scattered" (*diaspeirō*) is the word for sowing seed. The enemy meant to stamp the church out, and instead it was sown across the country. **"Throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria"** are exactly the next places named in 1:8.
- **"Except the apostles" (v1).** Why the apostles stayed is not said. They may have felt it their duty to hold their post, or the persecution may have been aimed mostly at Greek-speaking believers like Stephen, whose teaching on the temple and the law had alarmed people, while the Aramaic-speaking apostles were still seen as loyal temple worshipers. That would explain how the Jerusalem church survived under the apostles while the Hellenists fled.
- **"Devout men buried Stephen and made great lamentation over him" (v2).** Jewish custom discouraged public mourning for someone executed under the law. These men mourned loudly anyway. They may have been believers, or devout Jews who knew he had been wronged. Either way it was a brave act.
- **"Saul was ravaging the church" (v3).** *Lymainomai* means to outrage, maltreat or wreck, and it appears only here in the New Testament. The Greek Old Testament uses it of a wild boar tearing up a vineyard (Ps 80:13). **"Entering house after house"** shows he knew where the churches met: in homes. **"Men and women"** shows how thorough he was. Paul never stopped grieving this (1 Cor 15:9; Gal 1:13; 1 Tim 1:13).
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both stress that "approved" means hearty approval, not mere consent, which the lexicon supports. Guzik paints "ravaging" as an army wrecking a city or a wild animal mauling its prey, and the Septuagint's boar in the vineyard backs up the animal picture. JFB says the apostles stayed to "cherish" the young church "where it was most needful." Matthew Henry: "Though persecution must not drive us from our work, yet it may send us to work elsewhere."

#### Philip Proclaims Christ in Samaria (vv4–8)
- **"Those who were scattered went about preaching the word" (v4).** These are ordinary believers, not apostles. "Preaching" (*euangelizomai*) is "telling good news." Wherever they fled they talked about Jesus. The church's first great missionary push was carried out by refugees.
- **"Philip went down to the city of Samaria" (v5).** "Down" because Jerusalem sits high in the hills. Philip is the deacon of 6:5, later called "the evangelist" (21:8). Samaria had been the capital of the northern kingdom, rebuilt by Herod the Great as Sebaste. Some manuscripts read "a city of Samaria," which may mean another town such as Sychar or Gitta.
- **Who were the Samaritans?** After Assyria conquered the north in 722 BC, it resettled foreigners there, who mixed with the remaining Israelites (2 Kings 17:24–41). Their descendants worshiped the LORD but accepted only the five books of Moses and had their own temple on Mount Gerizim, which the Jews destroyed around 110 BC. Jews and Samaritans had despised each other for centuries: "Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" (John 4:9). James and John once wanted to call fire down on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54).
- **"Proclaimed to them the Christ" (v5).** Samaritans also expected a coming figure, the *Taheb* or "Restorer," based on the prophet like Moses (Deut 18:15). The woman at the well said, "I know that Messiah is coming" (John 4:25). Jesus had already sown seed in Samaria (John 4:39–42). Now Philip reaps.
- **"The crowds with one accord paid attention" (v6).** The signs confirmed the message, as they had for Jesus and the apostles. Unclean spirits leave **"crying out with a loud voice"** (v7), as in Jesus' ministry (Mark 1:26).
- **"So there was much joy in that city" (v8).** Joy follows the gospel all through Luke-Acts (Luke 2:10; 24:52; Acts 8:39; 13:52).
- **Commentators:** JFB stresses that this is "the deacon of that name," not the apostle Philip, and Guzik describes the scattered believers as accidental missionaries. Guzik also traces the long hatred between Jews and Samaritans and sees Jesus' visit in John 4 as preparing this harvest. Matthew Henry notes the people "were fully convinced that it was of God."

#### Simon the Magician Believes (vv9–25)
- **"A man named Simon, who had previously practiced magic" (v9).** "Practiced magic" (*mageuō*) is related to *magos*, the word for the wise men of Matthew 2, though here in a bad sense: a sorcerer. Magicians were common and influential in the ancient world.
- **"This man is the power of God that is called Great" (v10).** The people thought Simon was a living channel of a great divine power, perhaps a god in human form. Second-century Christian writers (Justin Martyr, himself from Samaria, and Irenaeus) called "Simon Magus" the father of later heresies and said he was worshiped as a god. How much of that is reliable is uncertain, but it fits his boast to be "somebody great."
- **"When they believed Philip… they were baptized, both men and women" (v12).** Philip preached "the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ," the same content as Jesus and the apostles (1:3; 28:31). Simon's followers left him for Christ.
- **"Even Simon himself believed… he continued with Philip… he was amazed" (v13).** Note the reversal. Simon used to amaze others (vv9, 11). Now he is the one amazed, by real power. Luke says he "believed" and was baptized. Whether that faith was genuine is the question the next verses raise.
- **"They sent to them Peter and John" (v14).** The apostles check this new work. Samaritans believing was a huge step. John, who once wanted to burn a Samaritan village, now comes to pray for Samaritans to receive the Spirit.
- **"He had not yet fallen on any of them" (v16).** In Acts the Spirit usually comes with faith and baptism (2:38). Here he comes later, through the apostles' prayer and laying on of hands (v17). Why?
- **Readings differ.** Pentecostal and charismatic readers see a second experience after conversion, often called the baptism in the Spirit. Many Reformed and evangelical readers see a one-time exception: God held back the Spirit until the apostles came so that Samaritan believers would be joined to the same church as Jerusalem, not form a rival Samaritan church with a rival temple as their ancestors had. A third view holds that the Samaritans' faith was incomplete until then. Luke's wider story fits the second view well: at each new frontier, Jews (ch 2), Samaritans (ch 8), Gentiles (ch 10), the Spirit's coming is visibly linked to the apostles, showing one church, not several.
- **"When Simon saw that the Spirit was given through the laying on of the apostles' hands" (v18).** He *saw* something, which suggests visible signs, probably tongues or prophecy as elsewhere (10:46; 19:6).
- **"He offered them money, saying, 'Give me this power also'" (vv18–19).** He sees the Spirit as a technique for sale. To a magician, power was something one acquired from a more skilled master, often for a fee. Church history named the buying and selling of church offices "simony" after him.
- **"May your silver perish with you" (v20).** Very strong language, literally "your silver be with you into destruction." It amounts to a curse on the money and on the man who trusts it. **"The gift of God"** cannot be bought, because it is a gift.
- **"You have neither part nor lot in this matter" (v21).** "Part and lot" is Old Testament language for a share in the inheritance (Deut 12:12). **"Your heart is not right before God"** echoes Psalm 78:37.
- **"Repent… and pray to the Lord that, if possible, the intent of your heart may be forgiven you" (v22).** Peter's harshness has a purpose: to call Simon to repentance. "If possible" does not mean God might refuse a truly repentant man. It reflects doubt about whether Simon will truly repent.
- **"In the gall of bitterness and in the bond of iniquity" (v23).** Gall was a bitter, poisonous plant. Moses warned against "a root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit" among God's people (Deut 29:18; compare Heb 12:15). Simon is full of poison and chained by sin.
- **"Pray for me to the Lord, that nothing of what you have said may come upon me" (v24).** He asks others to pray, but not for forgiveness, only for escape from punishment. Luke ends Simon's story there, unresolved.
- **Was Simon saved?** Most readers think his faith was shallow: impressed by power, not changed in heart, like the seed on rocky ground (Luke 8:13). Others think he was a true but badly confused believer. Luke does not settle it. He shows that baptism and outward belief do not guarantee a right heart.
- **"Preaching the gospel to many villages of the Samaritans" (v25).** On their way home Peter and John preach in Samaritan villages, the very thing John once wanted to destroy.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Simon's belief was "not without a touch of real conviction," while Guzik and the Tyndale notes judge it superficial. The Tyndale notes add that his last request seems to aim at avoiding punishment rather than real repentance. JFB says his asking for prayer shows him "having no confidence in the prayer of faith." On the delayed Spirit, JFB calls the laying on of hands "clearly a superadded thing," occasional and linked to the apostles, while Guzik favors reading it as a special filling given to people already saved. Matthew Henry: "A proud and covetous heart cannot be right with God."

#### Philip and the Ethiopian Eunuch (vv26–40)
- **"Rise and go toward the south" (v26).** "Toward the south" can also mean "at noon," the hottest time of day, when few would travel. Either way, God sends Philip away from a thriving city revival to an empty road.
- **"The road that goes down from Jerusalem to Gaza. This is a desert place" (v26).** Gaza was the last town before the desert route to Egypt. "This is a desert place" could describe the road or the old city of Gaza, destroyed around 96 BC and rebuilt nearby. Either way, it was lonely country.
- **"An Ethiopian, a eunuch, a court official of Candace" (v27).** "Ethiopia" in the ancient world meant the kingdom of Kush, south of Egypt along the Nile (modern Sudan), with its capital at Meroë, not modern Ethiopia. "Candace" (*Kandakē*) was not a personal name but a royal title for the queen mother, who ruled the kingdom. This man was her treasurer, a powerful official from what Greeks and Romans thought of as the edge of the world.
- **"A eunuch" (v27).** Eunuchs served in royal courts, often castrated so they could be trusted around the royal women. The law barred a man so mutilated from "the assembly of the LORD" (Deut 23:1). He could come to the temple but not past the Court of the Gentiles. Yet Isaiah had promised a day when God would give faithful eunuchs "a name better than sons and daughters" in his house (Isa 56:3–5). That promise is fulfilled here.
- **"He had come to Jerusalem to worship" (v27).** He was a God-fearer, possibly a convert, who had made a journey of over a thousand miles to worship, and could not fully enter.
- **"He was reading the prophet Isaiah" (v28).** People in the ancient world usually read aloud, which is how Philip "heard him reading" (v30). A scroll of Isaiah was expensive. He had probably bought it in Jerusalem.
- **"Go over and join this chariot" (v29).** First an angel (v26), now the Spirit. God is orchestrating every step. **"Philip ran to him" (v30).** Eager obedience.
- **"Do you understand what you are reading?" (v30).** In Greek it is a pun: *ginōskeis ha anaginōskeis*, "Do you know what you are reading?" **"How can I, unless someone guides me?" (v31).** Scripture is clear, but God uses teachers to explain it (Rom 10:14; Eph 4:11).
- **Isaiah 53:7–8 (vv32–33).** He was reading the Suffering Servant song, quoted from the Greek Old Testament: "Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter… In his humiliation justice was denied him." Jesus was silent before his accusers (Luke 23:9) and denied justice at his trial. "Who can describe his generation?" probably means "Who can speak of his descendants, since his life was cut off?" That line would mean a lot to a man who could have no children. Isaiah 53:10 goes on: "he shall see his offspring."
- **"About himself or about someone else?" (v34).** Jewish readers then debated whether the Servant was the prophet, Israel as a nation, or the Messiah. The eunuch asks the right question.
- **"Beginning with this Scripture he told him the good news about Jesus" (v35).** As Jesus did on the Emmaus road, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets" (Luke 24:27).
- **"See, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" (v36).** "Prevents" (*kōlyō*) is a word Luke uses when barriers come down (10:47; 11:17). The man who was kept at a distance from the temple asks what could keep him from Christ. The answer is nothing.
- **Verse 37.** Some later manuscripts add: "And Philip said, 'If you believe with all your heart, you may.' And he replied, 'I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God.'" It is missing from the earliest and best manuscripts, so the ESV and most modern versions put it in a footnote. It is probably an early baptismal confession added in the margin by a scribe. It teaches nothing that the rest of the New Testament does not, since faith always comes before baptism in Acts.
- **"They both went down into the water… and he baptized him" (v38).** Going down into and coming up out of water fits immersion, though the words alone could describe stepping into water to pour it. Wadis in the area had pools after the rains.
- **"The Spirit of the Lord carried Philip away" (v39).** "Carried away" (*harpazō*) is the word for snatching or catching up (2 Cor 12:2; 1 Thess 4:17). Elijah was known for being suddenly carried off by the Spirit (1 Kings 18:12; 2 Kings 2:16). Most read it as a miraculous removal, but Luke gives few details.
- **"He went on his way rejoicing" (v39).** Without Philip, but with Christ. Irenaeus wrote that he became a missionary to his own people. The gospel has reached Africa, a first step toward "the end of the earth."
- **"Philip found himself at Azotus… until he came to Caesarea" (v40).** Azotus is old Ashdod, a Philistine coastal town about twenty miles north of Gaza. Caesarea was the Roman capital of Judea on the coast. Philip settles there, and twenty years later Paul stays at his house, where his four daughters prophesy (21:8–9).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that v37 "is wanting in the principal manuscripts and most venerable versions," and reads "Candace" as a royal family name, as Guzik also says it was a title. JFB defends Philip's removal as miraculous against natural explanations. Guzik takes the baptism as immersion and notes how foolish the desert assignment looked by human standards. Matthew Henry: "When the inquirer after salvation becomes acquainted with Jesus and his gospel, he will go on his way rejoicing."

#### Pointing to Christ
The gospel runs outward, and every barrier falls before Jesus: the hatred between Jews and Samaritans, the hold of a magician, the law that kept a eunuch outside, the distance to Africa. Philip proclaims "the Christ" to Samaria and "the good news about Jesus" from Isaiah 53 to the Ethiopian. Both are pointed to the same Lamb, led silently to the slaughter, denied justice, his life taken from the earth. The man who could have no descendants learns of a Servant who died childless and yet "shall see his offspring" (Isa 53:10), and he goes home rejoicing as one of them.
