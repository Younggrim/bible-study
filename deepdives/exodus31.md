*Setting:* This is the last of God's instructions to Moses during his forty days on Mount Sinai (24:18). Since 25:1 God has described the tabernacle, its furniture, the priests' clothing, their ordination and the daily offerings. Now he names the craftsmen who will build it all, repeats the Sabbath command, and hands Moses the stone tablets. Down at the foot of the mountain, the people are about to make a golden calf (32:1). The contrast between this chapter and the next could hardly be sharper.

#### Oholiab and Bezalel (vv1–11)
- **The seventh speech.** Chapters 25–31 are broken up by the line "The LORD said to Moses" seven times (25:1; 30:11, 17, 22, 34; 31:1, 12). Many scholars have noticed that the seventh speech is about the Sabbath (31:12–17), just as the seventh day of creation was God's rest (Gen 2:1–3). The tabernacle is presented as a new creation in miniature, a place where God lives among his people as he walked with Adam in Eden.
- **"See, I have called by name Bezalel" (v2).** To call someone by name is to choose him personally for a task (compare Isa 43:1; 45:3–4). Bezalel did not volunteer or win a competition. God picked him. His name means "in the shadow of God," that is, under God's protection.
- **"The son of Uri, son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah" (v2).** Hur is very likely the man who held up Moses' arms with Aaron during the battle with Amalek (17:10–12) and who was left in charge with Aaron while Moses was on the mountain (24:14). Bezalel came from a leading family of Judah (1 Chr 2:18–20), the tribe of the coming king.
- **"I have filled him with the Spirit of God" (v3).** This is one of the first times Scripture speaks of anyone being filled with God's Spirit, and it is said of an artist. The Tyndale notes list the later cases: judges, kings and prophets who were given power beyond their natural ability (Judg 6:34; 1 Sam 16:13; Mic 3:8). Bezalel was likely already a skilled craftsman, perhaps trained in Egypt. The Spirit took his skill and raised it to the level needed for God's house.
- **"With ability and intelligence, with knowledge" (v3).** In Hebrew these are *chokmah* (wisdom or skill), *tevunah* (understanding) and *da'at* (knowledge). The same three words appear together in Proverbs 3:19–20: "The LORD by wisdom founded the earth; by understanding he established the heavens; by his knowledge the deeps broke open." The gifts God used to build the world, he now gives to a man to build his dwelling. "Wisdom" in the Old Testament is very practical. It is the skill of a craftsman as much as the insight of a sage.
- **"To devise artistic designs… gold, silver, and bronze… cutting stones… carving wood" (vv4–5).** Bezalel was a designer as well as a worker, skilled in metal, gems and wood. In 35:34 we learn God also gave him and Oholiab the ability **to teach**. Gifts are given to be passed on.
- **"Oholiab, the son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan" (v6).** His name means "father's tent," which fits a man who helped make the tent of God. Judah was the leading tribe, and Dan was among the least. Dan camped at the very back of the march (Num 2:31; 10:25), and later it became a center of idol worship (Judg 18:30–31). Yet God set a man from the least tribe beside the man from the greatest. Centuries later, the master craftsman Solomon brought in for the temple was the son of a woman from Dan (2 Chr 2:13–14; 1 Kgs 7:14 says Naphtali, probably different ways of describing her family line).
- **"I have given to all able men ability" (v6).** Literally "in the heart of all the wise of heart I have put wisdom." Bezalel and Oholiab did not work alone. Many skilled men and women did (35:25–26). God gave the plan and also the people and gifts to carry it out. The Tyndale notes recall Augustine's prayer, which asked God to give what he commands and then command what he wills.
- **The list of everything to be made (vv7–11).** Tent, ark, mercy seat, table, lampstand, both altars, basin, garments, oil and incense. The list sums up chapters 25–30, and it will be repeated almost item by item when the work is done (39:33–43). **"According to all that I have commanded you, they shall do" (v11).** The builders were not free to improve on the pattern.
- **Commentators:** All three stress that God chose these men himself. Henry notes that pairing Judah with "the less honoured" tribe of Dan prevented rivalry and gave honor to the lesser parts of the body (1 Cor 12:23–24). He writes, "If God give the commission, he will in some measure give the qualifications, according as the service is," and "skill in common arts and employments is the gift of God." JFB likewise calls Dan "one of the least influential and honorable in Israel" and sees the choice as deliberate. JFB thinks the Spirit's filling sharpened natural talent and skills already learned in Egypt. Guzik argues that building the tabernacle was as Spirit-dependent as the work of priests and prophets, and he ties it to Paul's call to do every task "heartily, as for the Lord" (Col 3:23).

#### The Sabbath (vv12–18)
- **"Above all you shall keep my Sabbaths" (v13).** The Hebrew begins with a strong word, "surely" or "only." Why mention the Sabbath right after the building plans? Because the work was holy, urgent and exciting, and the people might think God's work excused them from God's rest. It did not. When the building begins, the Sabbath is repeated first (35:1–3). Even the tabernacle stopped for the Sabbath.
- **"This is a sign between me and you throughout your generations" (v13).** A "sign" (*'ot*) is a visible marker of a covenant. The rainbow was the sign of God's covenant with Noah (Gen 9:12–13), and circumcision the sign of his covenant with Abraham (Gen 17:11). The Sabbath is the sign of the covenant at Sinai. Every week, by stopping work, Israel showed the world whom it belonged to.
- **"That you may know that I, the LORD, sanctify you" (v13).** The Sabbath was not how Israel made itself holy. It was a reminder that the LORD makes them holy. Ezekiel echoes this verse almost word for word in his indictment of Israel's history (Ezek 20:12).
- **"Everyone who profanes it shall be put to death… that soul shall be cut off" (v14).** This seems harsh to modern readers. Under the Sinai covenant, breaking the Sabbath was rebellion against the covenant itself, like tearing up the marriage certificate. Numbers 15:32–36 records the one case: a man gathering sticks on the Sabbath, stoned at God's command. The Tyndale notes observe that this severity at the start of Israel's life taught how seriously God takes coming to him his way, much like Ananias and Sapphira at the start of the church (Acts 5:1–11).
- **"Six days shall work be done" (v15).** The Sabbath command is also a command to work. Rest has meaning because of work. **"A Sabbath of solemn rest"** is a doubled phrase in Hebrew (*shabbat shabbaton*), "a Sabbath of complete rest."
- **"A covenant forever… a sign forever" (vv16–17).** The Sabbath was to last as long as the covenant it marked.
- **"In six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth, and on the seventh day he rested" (v17).** The reason is creation, as in the fourth commandment (20:11). In Deuteronomy the reason given is the rescue from slavery (Deut 5:15). Both are true. Israel rested because God rested, and because God had set them free.
- **"And was refreshed" (v17).** This is a bold phrase. The verb *naphash* means "take breath, refresh oneself," and it is used elsewhere of a tired worker getting his breath back (23:12; 2 Sam 16:14). God does not grow tired (Isa 40:28). The phrase speaks in human terms of God's satisfaction and delight in a finished work, and it invites his people to share that delight.
- **How does this apply to Christians?** Faithful traditions differ. Many Reformed and Presbyterian Christians see the Lord's Day, Sunday, as the Christian Sabbath, to be kept as a day of rest and worship. Many others, including Lutherans, most Baptists and many evangelicals, see the Sabbath as a sign fulfilled in Christ, so that no day is binding (Col 2:16–17; Rom 14:5), though the pattern of weekly rest and worship is still wise and good. Seventh-day Adventists and some others keep Saturday. All agree that Jesus is "lord of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28) and that "the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). Hebrews says "there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God," a rest entered by faith (Heb 4:9–10).
- **"The two tablets of the testimony, tablets of stone, written with the finger of God" (v18).** "Testimony" means the covenant terms, God's solemn witness to his will. The tablets were written on both sides (32:15). Some think the two tablets held the commandments split between duties to God and to neighbor. Others think they were two copies of the whole covenant, since ancient treaties gave each party a copy. If so, both copies went into the ark (25:16), because God's copy and his people's were kept in the same place, where he dwelt among them.
- **"The finger of God."** Egypt's magicians said "This is the finger of God" when they could not copy his plagues (8:19). Jesus said he cast out demons "by the finger of God" (Luke 11:20). Here the phrase means God wrote the law himself, directly (Deut 9:10). Paul later contrasts these stone tablets with a greater work, a letter "written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts" (2 Cor 3:3; Jer 31:33).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the Sabbath "the hem and hedge of the whole law," and stresses that not even work on the tabernacle could break it. He says the tablets were written "by his will and power immediately, without the use of any instrument," and sees in this a picture of God writing his law on the heart. JFB thinks the eager zeal of the builders created a real danger of breaking the Sabbath, which is why the warning comes here. Guzik also sees the placing as deliberate: even urgent work for God must not crowd out God's rest. He believes Christians are not bound to keep a Sabbath day (Rom 14:5; Col 2:16–17), but that the principle remains. He uses the picture of an eclipse, where the small moon blocks the great sun, and warns that our work for Jesus can hide his finished work for us, which he sees behind much burnout in ministry.

#### Pointing to Christ
God gave the plan, the Spirit, the skilled people and the tablets, and then commanded rest. The tabernacle would be built by people God had filled with his Spirit, and it would point to the one in whom "the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (Col 2:9). Jesus, the carpenter's son, is the true builder of God's house (Heb 3:3–6), and he builds it with living stones (1 Pet 2:5) gifted by the same Spirit. And the rest that the Sabbath pictured is found in him: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28). On the seventh day God rested from a finished creation. On the cross Jesus said, "It is finished" (John 19:30), and his people now rest in what he has done.
