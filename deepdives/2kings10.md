*Setting:* 841 BC, in the days right after Jehu's coup. In a single afternoon at Jezreel he has killed Joram king of Israel, mortally wounded Ahaziah king of Judah, and had Jezebel thrown from a window (ch 9). But Jehu holds only Jezreel and the army. Samaria, the fortified capital, is still in the hands of Ahab's officials, and dozens of Ahab's descendants live there. A prophet anointed Jehu with a commission to end the house of Ahab and avenge the blood of the prophets (9:6–10). This chapter shows him carrying it out, with ruthless thoroughness and no little cunning. It also ends with a verdict that is mixed: God commends what Jehu did, and then the writer says his heart was never fully the LORD's. The prophet Hosea, a century later, will look back on "the blood of Jezreel" as something God would punish (Hos 1:4).

#### Jehu Slaughters Ahab's Descendants (vv1–17)
- **"Ahab had seventy sons in Samaria" (v1).** "Sons" in Hebrew can include grandsons and other male descendants. Seventy may be a round number for a large royal family (compare Gideon's seventy sons, Judg 8:30; 9:5). They were being raised by "guardians," nobles responsible for bringing up princes, a costly honor that tied those families to the dynasty.
- **"Select the best and fittest of your master's sons and set him on his father's throne and fight" (vv2–3).** Jehu's letter is a dare. He lists their assets, chariots, horses, fortified cities, weapons, and invites them to fight. He knows they will not.
- **"The two kings could not stand before him. How then can we stand?" (v4).** Two kings are dead in a day. The officials fold at once. "We are your servants… Do whatever is good in your eyes" (v5). The palace steward, the city governor, the elders and the guardians all switch sides together.
- **"Take the heads of your master's sons and come to me at Jezreel tomorrow" (v6).** The Hebrew "heads" is ambiguous on purpose. It can mean "chief men" or literal heads. Jehu may have phrased it so that the officials would do the killing and carry the blame. They take it literally.
- **"Put their heads in baskets and sent them to him at Jezreel" (v7).** There is a grim echo. Jezreel was where Ahab's palace stood beside Naboth's vineyard. Baskets once carried grapes from the stolen vineyard. Now they carry Ahab's sons.
- **"Lay them in two heaps at the entrance of the gate until the morning" (v8).** Assyrian kings boasted in their records and wall reliefs of piling enemies' heads at city gates. It was a public warning: the old dynasty is finished.
- **"You are innocent. It was I who conspired against my master… but who struck down all these?" (v9).** Jehu's speech is clever. He admits his own killing of Joram, then points to the heaps: these were killed by Samaria's own leaders. The whole nation is now implicated. Then he puts it under God's word: "there shall fall to the earth nothing of the word of the LORD" (v10). That much is true. Elijah's prophecy (1 Kgs 21:21–24) is being fulfilled to the letter.
- **"All his great men and his close friends and his priests, until he left him none remaining" (v11).** Here Jehu goes beyond the prophecy. God's word was against Ahab's male descendants (9:8). Jehu also kills Ahab's officials, friends and priests, anyone who could organize a return. Many readers see this as the point where obeying God shades into securing his own power.
- **"Beth-eked of the Shepherds" (v12).** The name means something like "house of binding of the shepherds," probably a place where sheep were tied for shearing (hence the KJV's "shearing house"). Its location is uncertain, somewhere on the road between Jezreel and Samaria.
- **"We are the relatives of Ahaziah king of Judah" (v13).** Forty-two princes of Judah, cousins and nephews of the dead king, are on their way north to visit the royal family. They have not heard the news. They mean "the sons of the queen mother," Jezebel. Their family tie to Ahab came through Athaliah, Ahab's daughter, who married into Judah's royal house (8:18, 26).
- **"Take them alive." … and slaughtered them at the pit of Beth-eked, forty-two persons (v14).** Jehu had no command concerning Judah's royal family. Chronicles treats their deaths as part of God's judgment on the house of Ahab and its allies (2 Chr 22:7–8), but the slaughter at a well or pit, with no trial and no mercy, looks like a man removing anyone who might ever claim his throne. It had a terrible side effect. With Judah's princes dead, Athaliah would soon seize Jerusalem and try to kill the rest of David's line (11:1).
- **"Jehonadab the son of Rechab" (v15).** Jehonadab led a clan of Kenites (1 Chr 2:55) who lived by strict rules: no wine, no houses, no fields, living in tents as a protest against the settled, Baal-soaked culture of Canaan. Two and a half centuries later Jeremiah held up his descendants, the Rechabites, as a model of faithfulness (Jer 35:6–10). He was respected as a man of God. Jehu wants him seen at his side.
- **"Is your heart true to my heart as mine is to yours?" … "Give me your hand" (v15).** Clasping hands sealed an agreement. Jehu pulls him up into the royal chariot in public view.
- **"Come with me, and see my zeal for the LORD" (v16).** Zeal for the LORD was a good thing. Elijah used the same word of himself (1 Kgs 19:10, 14), and Phinehas was praised for it (Num 25:11–13). But the phrase "see my zeal" is telling. Jehu wants it watched. Jesus warned against righteousness done "in order to be seen" (Matt 6:1).
- **"He struck down all who remained to Ahab in Samaria" (v17).** The narrator again notes it was "according to the word of the LORD that he spoke to Elijah." Both things are true at once: God's word is fulfilled, and Jehu's methods are his own.
- **An image of Jehu.** The Black Obelisk of the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III, now in the British Museum, records tribute from "Jehu son of Omri" in 841 BC, the very year of this chapter. One panel shows an Israelite king, or his envoy, bowing to the ground before the Assyrian king. It is the only picture of an Israelite king known from ancient times. The Assyrians called him "son of Omri" because they named Israel after its famous dynasty, the one Jehu had just destroyed. The Bible does not mention this tribute, but it fits the weak position Israel was in (v32).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jehu "did not do it deceitfully, or by halves," and that "God is not the author of any man's sin," though he works his judgment through men's own choices. He admits the killings were harsh and should not be treated as a pattern. JFB calls the murder of rival claimants "the barbarous practice of a successful usurper" and Jehu's speech "artfully framed." Guzik quotes Trapp on the grim irony of the baskets, and Knapp to the effect that the sword went beyond its allotted limits. On v16 he cites G. Campbell Morgan, who saw a proud spirit in Jehu's boast.

#### Jehu Strikes Down the Prophets of Baal (vv18–27)
- **"Ahab served Baal a little, but Jehu will serve him much" (v18).** It is a lie, and a bold one. Baal worship had been the royal religion in Israel since Ahab married Jezebel (1 Kgs 16:31–33), and its priests would expect a new king to keep it. Jehu promises even more.
- **"I have a great sacrifice to offer to Baal" (v19).** The word "sacrifice" carries a grim double meaning. The narrator lets the reader in at once: **"Jehu did it with cunning in order to destroy the worshipers of Baal."** The word for "cunning" means trickery or deceit.
- **"Sanctify a solemn assembly for Baal" (v20).** He uses the language of a holy festival. Every worshiper of Baal in Israel comes, "so that there was not a man left who did not come" (v21), and the temple is packed from wall to wall. Ahab had built this house of Baal in Samaria (1 Kgs 16:32).
- **"Bring out the vestments for all the worshipers of Baal" (v22).** Special robes were worn for worship. Here they also serve a practical purpose: everyone in the temple is marked.
- **"Search, and see that there is no servant of the LORD here among you" (v23).** Jehu has Jehonadab with him, and they make sure no worshiper of the LORD is caught in the trap. In the strange logic of the scene, Baal's priests make sure of it for him.
- **"Jehu had stationed eighty men outside" (v24).** Any guard who lets one escape will pay with his life. When the burnt offering is finished, Jehu gives the order: "Go in and strike them down; let not a man escape" (v25).
- **"They brought out the pillar that was in the house of Baal and burned it" (v26).** Sacred standing stones (*matsevot*) marked Canaanite shrines as the god's dwelling. The law commanded Israel to break them (Exod 23:24; Deut 7:5). There seems to have been both a wooden object that was burned and a stone pillar that was smashed (v27).
- **"Made it a latrine to this day" (v27).** The temple was turned into a public toilet, a deliberate desecration that made it permanently unclean and useless for worship (compare Ezra 6:11; Dan 2:5, where houses are made dunghills). "To this day" means it was still that way when the writer's source was written.
- **How to read the trick.** The Bible reports Jehu's deceit without praising it. Some readers note that Baal worship was a capital offense under the law (Deut 13:6–10; 17:2–5), and that Elijah himself had Baal's prophets executed (1 Kgs 18:40). Others point out that Elijah did it openly, after God answered by fire, while Jehu did it through a lie about worship itself. Both points stand, and the writer seems content to let the reader weigh them.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks Jehu's motives were mixed, but defends the outcome on the ground of God's jealousy for his honor: "A man may hate cruelty and yet love justice." JFB calls the gathering "a deep-laid plot" and thinks Jehu acted from "purely political motives." It notes that the vestments were probably white linen robes kept in the temple wardrobe. Guzik, following Dilday, says Baal's priests must have been thrilled by the apparent conversion of their new king.

#### Jehu Reigns in Israel (vv28–36)
- **"Thus Jehu wiped out Baal from Israel" (v28).** This was a real achievement. Elijah's contest on Carmel (1 Kgs 18) had won a day. Jehu ended the state cult of Baal in the northern kingdom. It never again became Israel's official religion, though Baal worship lingered among the people (Hos 2:13, 17).
- **"But Jehu did not turn aside from the sins of Jeroboam… the golden calves that were in Bethel and in Dan" (v29).** Jeroboam I set up these calves at the founding of the northern kingdom to keep his people from going to Jerusalem's temple (1 Kgs 12:26–30). They were probably meant as pedestals for an invisible LORD, but they broke the second commandment and cut Israel off from the place God chose. Every northern king kept them. Jehu did too. Getting rid of Baal cost him nothing politically. Getting rid of the calves would have sent his people south to worship in another king's capital.
- **"Because you have done well… your sons of the fourth generation shall sit on the throne of Israel" (v30).** God commends Jehu for carrying out the judgment on Ahab's house, and rewards him. His dynasty became the longest in Israel's history: Jehoahaz, Jehoash, Jeroboam II and Zechariah, nearly a century in all. The promise came true exactly, and Zechariah was assassinated after six months (15:8–12).
- **"But Jehu was not careful to walk in the law of the LORD… with all his heart" (v31).** "Not careful" is literally "did not take heed" or "did not guard." He was zealous for a cause but not devoted to God. The verdict on his whole life comes down to this one verse.
- **Hosea 1:4.** About a century later, during Jeroboam II's reign, God tells Hosea to name his son Jezreel, "for in just a little while I will punish the house of Jehu for the blood of Jezreel." How can God commend the act (v30) and then punish it? Most readers answer that God approved the judgment Jehu carried out but not the spirit in which he did it, his going beyond his orders, and his use of God's cause for his own power. A man can be God's instrument and still be accountable for what he brought to the work.
- **"In those days the LORD began to cut off parts of Israel" (v32).** The verb is "to cut short" or "trim off." Hazael, the king Elisha wept over (8:12), takes all the land east of the Jordan: Gilead and Bashan, the territory of Gad, Reuben and half of Manasseh, south to Aroer on the Arnon gorge, Moab's border (v33). This was the first great shrinking of Israel's land, and the writer says plainly who was doing it: the LORD. Losing land was one of the covenant curses (Deut 28:25, 36).
- **"Jehu reigned over Israel in Samaria… twenty-eight years" (v36).** About 841–814 BC. "All his might" (v34) is noted, but Kings records nothing of the rest of his reign. The man who made his name in a week of blood left little else behind.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "No services done for God shall go unrewarded," but "true conversion is not only from gross sin, but from all sin." JFB says Jehu "had no intention of carrying his zeal for the Lord beyond a certain point," and kept the calves because he thought it unwise to let his people travel to Jerusalem. Guzik draws on Spurgeon, who judged that Jehu obeyed God where it paid and went no further, and on G. Campbell Morgan, who observed that a man can be an instrument in God's hand without any fellowship with God. Guzik sets verse 30 beside Hosea 1:4 and concludes that Jehu was both good and bad.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jehu was zealous, and his zeal destroyed Baal, but it did not change his heart. He cleaned out the house of Baal and left the golden calves standing. Jesus showed a different zeal. When he drove the money changers from the temple, his disciples remembered, "Zeal for your house will consume me" (John 2:17), and it did, all the way to the cross. Jehu shed the blood of others to secure his throne. Jesus shed his own to win his people. And where Jehu "was not careful to walk in the law of the LORD… with all his heart," Jesus kept it wholly (Matt 5:17) and gives his people new hearts to walk in it (Ezek 36:26–27).
