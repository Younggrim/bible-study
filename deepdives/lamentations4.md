*Setting:* The fourth lament over Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC returns to the opening cry of chapters 1 and 2, *'Ekah*, "How!" It is again an alphabetic acrostic of twenty-two stanzas, but each stanza is now two lines instead of three, and the poem feels quieter and more numb, as if the grief has burned down to ash. Where chapter 3 climbed to hope, chapter 4 goes back to the street and looks hard at the details of the siege: starving children, fallen nobles, mothers driven to unthinkable acts, priests and prophets shunned as unclean, a captured king. A narrator speaks in vv1–16, and the people speak as "we" in vv17–20, describing their last days. The chapter closes with two short addresses, to Edom and to Zion (vv21–22), and the last one contains the first plain promise in the book that Zion's exile will end. As in chapters 2 and 3, the letter *pe* comes before *'ayin* (vv16–17).

#### The Holy Stones Lie Scattered (vv1–22)
- **"How the gold has grown dim, how the pure gold is changed!" (v1).** Gold does not rust or tarnish, so the image is shocking. It may be the temple's gold, stripped from walls and doors and trampled (Jer 52:19). Verse 2 shows the poet also means the people of Zion. **"The holy stones lie scattered at the head of every street."** These may be the temple's stones, its jewels, or the gems on the high priest's breastplate. Zechariah later calls God's restored people "the jewels of a crown" (Zech 9:16), and verse 2 likely explains the image: the holy stones are Zion's children, now lying in the streets.
- **"The precious sons of Zion, worth their weight in fine gold, how they are regarded as earthen pots, the work of a potter's hands!" (v2).** Clay pots were cheap and easily broken, and thrown away when they cracked. Jeremiah had smashed a clay jar before the elders as a sign of the city's coming ruin (Jer 19:1, 10–11). God's treasured people (Exod 19:5) are now treated as throwaway pottery.
- **"Even jackals offer the breast; they nurse their young; but the daughter of my people has become cruel, like the ostriches in the wilderness" (v3).** The Hebrew spelling could also read "sea monsters," which is why the King James has "sea monsters draw out the breast." Either way, even wild animals care for their young. The ostrich had a reputation for laying eggs in the sand and leaving them (Job 39:13–16). Famine has made Zion's mothers worse than beasts.
- **"The tongue of the nursing infant sticks to the roof of its mouth for thirst; the children beg for food, but no one gives to them" (v4).** No milk, no water, no bread. The adults themselves have nothing to give.
- **"Those who once feasted on delicacies perish in the streets; those who were brought up in purple embrace ash heaps" (v5).** Purple dye was costly, worn only by the rich. Now the wealthy cling to the town dump, the place where beggars and lepers sat (1 Sam 2:8; Job 2:8). Famine has erased the difference between rich and poor.
- **"The chastisement of the daughter of my people has been greater than the punishment of Sodom" (v6).** The words for "chastisement" and "punishment" both normally mean "iniquity" (*'awon*) and "sin" (*ḥaṭṭa't*). Hebrew often uses the same word for a sin and its consequence. So the line may mean Zion's punishment was greater, or her sin was. Both are in view: Jerusalem had the law, the temple and the prophets, and Sodom did not (Ezek 16:46–52; Matt 11:23–24). **"Overthrown in a moment, and no hands were wrung for her"**: Sodom's end was over in an instant (Gen 19:24–25). Jerusalem's dragged on for eighteen months of starvation. The last phrase is hard in Hebrew, but probably means no human hands did it, or no long agony came first.
- **"Her princes were purer than snow, whiter than milk; their bodies were more ruddy than coral, the beauty of their form was like sapphire" (v7).** "Princes" here is a word normally meaning "Nazirites" (*nezireha*), people consecrated to God by a vow (Num 6). Many take it here to mean "her consecrated ones" or "her nobles," since the root also means "crowned" or "set apart." The description is of health and beauty: radiant skin, ruddy cheeks (the same word used of young David, 1 Sam 16:12), and a beauty like "sapphire," probably lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone, perhaps for blue veins showing through fair skin.
- **"Now their face is blacker than soot; they are not recognized in the streets; their skin has shriveled on their bones" (v8).** Starvation darkens and dries the skin. The noble young men have become unrecognizable.
- **"Happier were the victims of the sword than the victims of hunger" (v9).** Death in battle at least came quickly. Starvation was slow, **"pierced by lack of the fruits of the field."** The fields outside the walls were held by the enemy.
- **"The hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children; they became their food" (v10).** The most terrible line in the book, stated plainly. Moses had warned that under siege the most tender and refined woman would eat her own children (Deut 28:56–57). It happened in Samaria's siege (2 Kgs 6:28–29), Jeremiah had foretold it of Jerusalem (Jer 19:9), and the historian Josephus records it again in the Roman siege of AD 70. The word **"compassionate"** (*raḥmaniyyot*) is related to *raḥamim*, the "mercies" of 3:22. The women who should be the picture of mercy have been reduced to this.
- **"The LORD gave full vent to his wrath… and he kindled a fire in Zion that consumed its foundations" (v11).** The poet names the cause again. These are not random horrors. This is God's judgment, foretold for centuries (Deut 32:22). Nebuzaradan's fire burned the temple and the houses (Jer 52:13).
- **"The kings of the earth did not believe… that foe or enemy could enter the gates of Jerusalem" (v12).** In 701 BC the Assyrian army of Sennacherib had surrounded Jerusalem and then withdrawn after God struck its camp (2 Kgs 19:35–36; Isa 37). The psalms sang of Zion as the city God would never let fall (Ps 46:5; 48:3–8). The people came to believe Jerusalem was untouchable, whatever they did (Jer 7:4). Its fall stunned the region.
- **"This was for the sins of her prophets and the iniquities of her priests, who shed in the midst of her the blood of the righteous" (v13).** The religious leaders bore special guilt. False prophets promised peace, priests profited, and both opposed God's true messengers. A prophet named Uriah was hunted down and executed for preaching what Jeremiah preached (Jer 26:20–23), and priests and prophets demanded Jeremiah's own death (Jer 26:8, 11). Jesus spoke of the same pattern when he wept over Jerusalem, "the city that kills the prophets" (Matt 23:29–37).
- **"They wandered, blind, through the streets; they were so defiled with blood that no one was able to touch their garments" (v14).** The irony is sharp. Priests were supposed to stay ritually clean and teach the people the difference between clean and unclean (Lev 10:10). Now they are so stained with blood that people step aside from them. **"Blind"** may mean groping like the blind, or spiritually blind (Isa 56:10).
- **"'Away! Unclean!' people cried at them. 'Away! Away! Do not touch!'" (v15).** This was the cry a person with a skin disease had to shout to warn others away (Lev 13:45–46). The priests are treated as lepers. **"They shall stay with us no longer"**: even foreign peoples would not let them settle among them.
- **"The LORD himself has scattered them; he will regard them no more" (v16).** Literally "the face of the LORD has scattered them." The face of God, which shone on his people in blessing (Num 6:25), now turns against them. **"No honor was shown to the priests, no favor to the elders."** The Babylonians executed the chief priest and the city's leaders at Riblah (Jer 52:24–27).
- **"Our eyes failed, ever watching vainly for help; in our watching we watched for a nation which could not save" (v17).** The voice changes to "we," the people looking back on the siege. The nation is Egypt. Pharaoh Hophra's army marched out, the Babylonians briefly lifted the siege, and then Egypt went home (Jer 37:5–10). Isaiah had called Egypt's help "worthless and empty" (Isa 30:7). The watchmen on the walls kept looking south for an army that never came.
- **"They dogged our steps so that we could not walk in our streets" (v18).** The enemy's siege towers and archers could shoot into the city. **"Our end drew near; our days were numbered, for our end had come."** Amos had used the same word: "The end has come upon my people Israel" (Amos 8:2), and so had Ezekiel (Ezek 7:2–6).
- **"Our pursuers were swifter than the eagles in the heavens; they chased us on the mountains; they lay in wait for us in the wilderness" (v19).** Moses warned of a nation "swooping down like the eagle" (Deut 28:49), and Habakkuk described the Babylonian cavalry that way (Hab 1:8). The people who fled with Zedekiah were caught in the Judean wilderness on the way to Jericho (Jer 52:7–8).
- **"The breath of our nostrils, the LORD's anointed, was captured in their pits" (v20).** "The LORD's anointed" (*meshiaḥ YHWH*) is the king of David's line, here Zedekiah, captured on the plains of Jericho, blinded and taken to Babylon (Jer 52:8–11). **"The breath of our nostrils"**: the king was the people's life, as breath is. Similar language for a king appears in letters to Pharaoh found at Amarna in Egypt. **"Under his shadow we shall live among the nations"** describes the protection a king was meant to give (Judg 9:15; Ps 91:1). It is painful to hear such words spoken of Zedekiah, who failed so badly. But the people mourn not so much the man as the office: the line of David, God's promised king, seemed to have been snuffed out.
- **"Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom, you who dwell in the land of Uz" (v21).** This is irony. Edom, Israel's brother nation through Esau, had cheered Jerusalem's fall and helped the Babylonians catch fugitives (Ps 137:7; Obad 10–14; Ezek 25:12). **"Uz"** was the home of Job (Job 1:1), a region east or southeast of Judah, linked with Edom here and in Jeremiah 25:20–21. **"To you also the cup shall pass; you shall become drunk and strip yourself bare."** Edom was on Jeremiah's list of nations who must drink the cup of God's wrath (Jer 25:15–21). Drunken exposure is a picture of shame (Gen 9:21; Hab 2:15–16).
- **"The punishment of your iniquity, O daughter of Zion, is accomplished; he will keep you in exile no longer" (v22).** The first direct word of hope to Zion in the whole book. **"Accomplished"** (*tam*) means complete, finished. The debt has been paid. Isaiah announces the same news: "her warfare is ended… her iniquity is pardoned" (Isa 40:2). The return from exile began in 538 BC, under Cyrus (Ezra 1:1–4). **"But your iniquity, O daughter of Edom, he will punish; he will uncover your sins."** Zion's sins are covered at last, Edom's laid bare. The two sisters trade places. Edom was eventually driven from its homeland by Arab tribes and disappears from history as a nation. Its remnant, known as the Idumeans, was absorbed into the Jewish people centuries later.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reflects on v1: "Sin tarnishes the beauty of the most exalted powers and the most excellent gifts." On v13: "Nothing ripens a people more for ruin, nor fills the measure faster, than the sins of priests and prophets." He reads v20 forward to Christ: "Our anointed King alone is the life of our souls." Guzik reads the "Nazirites" of v7 as people of distinction rather than those under the Nazirite vow, and explains the comparison with Sodom by its length: Sodom's end was instant, Jerusalem's prolonged and agonizing. He identifies the nation of v17 as Egypt, the false hope the prophets and politicians clung to, and the anointed of v20 as Zedekiah, weak and treacherous, in whom the people's hopes were bitterly disappointed. JFB takes v7 more literally, of actual Nazirites, whose healthy looks came from abstaining from strong drink, and he notes that "No prophets had been sent to Sodom, as there had been to Judea." On v12, JFB argues that since the city seemed impregnable, "It therefore could only have been the hand of God." On v20 he says the language seems first to have suited Josiah, but here applies to Zedekiah as David's heir and a "type of Messiah," though as a description of Zedekiah himself it is "too favorable." JFB sees the full fulfilment of v22 in a final restoration of the Jews, since the Romans later sent them into exile again.

#### Pointing to Christ
The cry of v20 is the cry of a people who have lost their anointed king: "The breath of our nostrils, the LORD's anointed, was captured in their pits." Six centuries later the Anointed One, the true Son of David, was captured, mocked and executed, and his disciples mourned, "We had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel" (Luke 24:21). But this Anointed did not stay in the pit. He rose, and under his shadow his people truly live among the nations. The priests of v14 were too defiled for anyone to touch. Jesus touched the unclean (Mark 1:40–42), and as the great High Priest he makes the defiled clean. And the word of v22, "The punishment of your iniquity… is accomplished," finds its deepest meaning in his cry from the cross, "It is finished" (John 19:30). The debt of sin is paid in full for all who trust him.
