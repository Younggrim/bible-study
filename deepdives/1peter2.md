*Setting:* The apostle Peter writes from "Babylon," most likely Rome, in the early 60s AD, shortly before Nero's persecution, to churches scattered across what is now northern and central Turkey. Most of the readers are Gentile converts who have lost their place in society by turning from the gods of their families and towns. Neighbors slander them, some masters beat Christian slaves, and some husbands resent Christian wives. Chapter 1 reminded them that they are born again to a living hope, ransomed by Christ's blood and called to be holy. Chapter 2 tells them who they now are: living stones in God's new temple, a royal priesthood, the people of God. Then it begins the letter's long practical section: how such people live under the emperor, under harsh masters, and in the footsteps of the Christ who suffered unjustly for them.

#### A Living Stone and a Holy People (vv1–12)
- **"So put away all malice and all deceit and hypocrisy and envy and all slander" (v1).** "So" ties this to 1:22–25: people born again by God's word to love one another must strip off whatever kills love. "Put away" is the word for taking off clothes (Eph 4:22; Col 3:8). Each sin on the list wrecks a community. Malice wishes harm, deceit and hypocrisy hide the truth, envy resents another's good, and slander runs people down. Slander is exactly what outsiders were doing to these believers (v12). They must not do it to each other.
- **"Like newborn infants, long for the pure spiritual milk" (v2).** A baby does not need to be taught to want milk. It cries for it. "Long for" is *epipotheō*, intense longing, the word the Greek Old Testament uses in Psalm 42:1 for the deer panting for water. "Newborn" picks up the new birth of 1:3, 23. Peter is not calling them immature. Elsewhere milk stands for basic teaching (1 Cor 3:2; Heb 5:12–13), but here it is simply what all believers need to grow: God's word, which they have just been told "remains forever" (1:25).
- **"Pure spiritual milk" (v2).** "Pure" is *adolos*, "without deceit," a deliberate echo of the "deceit" (*dolos*) of v1. Put away deceit, and drink what has no deceit in it. "Spiritual" is *logikos*, a word built on *logos*, "word" or "reason." Its only other use is Romans 12:1, "spiritual worship." The ESV and most modern versions render it "spiritual." The King James has "milk of the word," and the lexicon allows that the play on *logos*, coming straight after "the word of the Lord" in 1:25, is probably intended. Either way, the milk is God's word.
- **"That by it you may grow up into salvation" (v2).** Some late manuscripts leave out "into salvation," and the King James follows them. The oldest manuscripts include it. Salvation, as in 1:5, is the goal still ahead. Believers grow toward it.
- **"If indeed you have tasted that the Lord is good" (v3).** From Psalm 34:8, "Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good!" Peter will quote this psalm again at length in 3:10–12. In the psalm "LORD" is God's covenant name. Peter applies it to Jesus, a quiet statement of who Jesus is. "Good" is *chrēstos*, which in Greek of the time sounded almost exactly like *Christos*, "Christ." Some readers would have caught the echo.
- **"As you come to him, a living stone" (v4).** Peter's own name means "rock" or "stone," and he never once mentions that here. He points away from himself to the true Stone. Jesus is "living" because he is risen. "Rejected by men" recalls the leaders who handed him over. "Chosen and precious" in God's sight anticipates the quotations of vv6–7.
- **"You yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house" (v5).** The temple in Jerusalem was still standing when Peter wrote, and it would be destroyed in AD 70. But God is already building another temple, made of people. Each believer is a stone, alive because joined to the living Stone. Paul says the same (1 Cor 3:16; Eph 2:19–22), and Jesus called his own body the temple (John 2:19–21). Stones are not meant to lie alone in a field. They are built together.
- **"To be a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices" (v5).** In Israel only descendants of Aaron could be priests, and only they could offer sacrifices. Now every believer is a priest. The sacrifices are no longer animals but praise, prayer, good deeds and generous giving (Heb 13:15–16), and the whole self (Rom 12:1). "Acceptable to God through Jesus Christ." No offering is accepted on its own merit, only through him. The Reformers drew heavily on this verse for what came to be called "the priesthood of all believers."
- **"Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a cornerstone chosen and precious" (v6).** From Isaiah 28:16, where God answers Jerusalem's leaders who were trusting in political deals. The cornerstone was the first, carefully placed stone in a foundation. It set the lines for every wall. Some scholars think the word can also mean a capstone at the top of an arch, but in Isaiah's picture it is a foundation stone. "Will not be put to shame" means will not be disappointed in the end, a promise that matters to people being shamed now.
- **"The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (v7).** From Psalm 118:22. Jesus applied this verse to himself after telling the parable of the tenants (Matt 21:42), and Peter quoted it to the Sanhedrin: "This Jesus is the stone that was rejected by you, the builders" (Acts 4:11). He heard it from Jesus and used it for the rest of his life.
- **"So the honor is for you who believe" (v7).** Literally "to you who believe, the preciousness." The stone God calls precious (v6) is precious to believers too, and they share its honor. People who are shamed by their neighbors are honored by God.
- **"A stone of stumbling, and a rock of offense" (v8).** From Isaiah 8:14. The same stone that is a foundation for some is something others trip over. Paul combines Isaiah 8:14 and 28:16 the same way (Rom 9:33), which suggests these stone texts were gathered together very early in the church to explain Jesus. Simeon told Mary her child was "appointed for the fall and rising of many" (Luke 2:34).
- **"They stumble because they disobey the word, as they were destined to do" (v8).** A hard line, and Christians read it differently. Some, especially in the Reformed tradition, take it to mean God appointed certain people to unbelief. Others, including JFB and many Arminian readers, take it to mean God appointed the *consequence*: those who refuse to believe will stumble. The Tyndale notes say plainly that the text does not settle which. What the verse clearly says is that the stumbling is their responsibility ("because they disobey") and nothing happens outside God's purpose.
- **"But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession" (v9).** Almost every phrase comes from God's words to Israel at Mount Sinai: "you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples… a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:5–6), and from Isaiah 43:20–21, "my chosen people, the people whom I formed for myself that they might declare my praise." Peter applies Israel's titles to a mostly Gentile church. This does not mean God is finished with Israel (Rom 11:1–2). It means Gentile believers are not second-class. Through Christ they are fully part of God's one people.
- **"Royal priesthood" (v9).** In Israel kings and priests were separate. King Uzziah was struck with leprosy for trying to burn incense (2 Chr 26:16–21). Only Melchizedek was both (Gen 14:18), and Jesus is priest "after the order of Melchizedek" (Heb 7). In him his people are both too (Rev 1:6).
- **"That you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (v9).** The privileges have a purpose: to tell what God has done. "Excellencies" (*aretai*) means his mighty deeds and praiseworthy acts, echoing Isaiah 43:21. Gentile converts knew what darkness meant: idol temples, fear of spirits, despair at death.
- **"Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people" (v10).** From Hosea 1–2. Hosea named his children *Lo-ruhamah*, "No Mercy," and *Lo-ammi*, "Not My People," as a sign of God's judgment on unfaithful Israel. Then God promised to reverse it: "I will have mercy on No Mercy, and I will say to Not My People, 'You are my people'" (Hos 2:23). Paul applies the same verses to Gentiles (Rom 9:25–26). People with no family, no nation and no claim on God now have all three.
- **"I urge you as sojourners and exiles" (v11).** "Beloved" opens the second half of the letter. "Sojourners" (*paroikos*) means resident aliens, people living legally in a place that is not their home. "Exiles" (*parepidēmos*) is the word of 1:1, used of Abraham in Genesis 23:4. Abraham called himself both. Many of Peter's readers may literally have been outsiders, but every believer is spiritually one.
- **"Abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul" (v11).** "Flesh" here is not the body as bad. It is human nature turned away from God. These desires are not harmless. They are soldiers on campaign against the soul. James used the same military picture (James 4:1).
- **"Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable" (v12).** "Gentiles" here means unbelievers, a sign of how fully Peter sees his Gentile readers as the new Israel. "Honorable" (*kalos*) means good and attractive, beautiful goodness others can see.
- **"When they speak against you as evildoers" (v12).** Early Christians faced strange rumors. Because they ate "the body and blood" at communion, they were accused of cannibalism. Because they called each other "brother" and "sister" and held "love feasts," they were accused of incest. Because they had no idols, they were called atheists. Because they skipped civic festivals, they were called haters of humanity. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing about Nero's persecution, calls Christians a group "hated for their abominations."
- **"They may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation" (v12).** An echo of Jesus: "let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father" (Matt 5:16). The "day of visitation" is the day God comes. In the Old Testament it usually means judgment (Isa 10:3). It may mean that on judgment day even opponents will have to admit God was at work in Christians' lives. It may mean the day God "visits" an unbeliever in mercy and brings them to faith (Luke 19:44). Commentators divide, and both fit Peter's hope.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Evil-speaking is a sign of malice and guile in the heart; and hinders our profiting by the word of God," and "Christ is the chief Corner-stone, that unites the whole number of believers into one everlasting temple." Guzik makes Christ's preciousness a test of real faith and notes that Spurgeon, at sixteen, preached his first sermon on v7. He observes that a cornerstone fixes two walls at once, so Christ joins Jew and Gentile in one house. JFB reads *adolos* as "guileless," contrasting it with the guile of v1, and holds firmly to "milk of the word." He argues that the stumbling of v8 is God's judgment on unbelief, "not a decree to sin." He also notes that Peter calls every believer a living stone, which he takes as weighing against reading Matthew 16:18 as making Peter alone the rock. On v12 JFB takes the "day of visitation" as the day God shows mercy and converts the slanderers, while Guzik leans toward a final meeting with God.

#### Submission to Authority (vv13–25)
- **"Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution" (v13).** "Be subject" (*hypotassō*) means to take one's place under an order. It runs through the next section (2:18; 3:1, 5, 22; 5:5). "Human institution" is literally "human creation," authority structures people set up. Peter does not call them divine, but believers honor them "for the Lord's sake": because Jesus himself submitted to Pilate's court, and because faithful citizenship commends the gospel.
- **"To the emperor as supreme" (v13).** When Peter wrote, the emperor was Nero. In his early years Nero ruled reasonably well, but by AD 64 he was blaming Christians for the great fire of Rome and burning them as torches. Peter is not saying the emperor is good. He is saying God has given government a role. Paul says the same in Romans 13:1–7, also written to Christians in Rome under Nero.
- **"To punish those who do evil and to praise those who do good" (v14).** This is the purpose of government. When it does the opposite, it betrays its calling. Peter knew limits. When the Sanhedrin ordered him to stop preaching, he said, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Submission does not mean obeying an order to sin. It means respecting the office and living as good citizens.
- **"Put to silence the ignorance of foolish people" (v15).** "Put to silence" is literally "muzzle," the same word Jesus used to silence a demon and still the storm (Mark 1:25; 4:39). Slander is answered best not by argument but by a life that makes it look ridiculous.
- **"Live as people who are free… as servants of God" (v16).** Believers are free. They belong to no emperor in their souls. But that freedom is not a "cover-up for evil," a mask to hide wrongdoing behind. They are free because they now belong to God as his *douloi*, slaves (Gal 5:13). Peter writes this just before addressing actual slaves (v18): the slave of God is free even in chains, and the free citizen is still God's slave.
- **"Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor" (v17).** Four short commands in a careful pattern. Everyone gets honor, including the despised slave and the pagan neighbor. Fellow believers get love. Only God gets fear. And the emperor, at the top of the Roman world, gets *honor*, the same as everyone else, not fear and not worship. In a world where emperors were treated as gods, that was a bold distinction. It echoes Jesus: "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Mark 12:17).
- **"Servants, be subject to your masters with all respect" (v18).** "Servants" here is *oiketai*, household slaves, often educated people who worked as cooks, tutors, accountants and doctors. Slavery in the Roman Empire was not based on race, and many slaves eventually gained freedom. But slaves had no legal rights, and masters could beat them freely. Peter speaks directly to slaves as moral persons who make real choices, which the culture did not usually do. Notice he gives no instructions to Christian masters, perhaps because there were few among his readers.
- **"Not only to the good and gentle but also to the unjust" (v18).** "Unjust" is *skolios*, "crooked" or "twisted," the same word Peter used on the Day of Pentecost: "Save yourselves from this crooked generation" (Acts 2:40). A slave could not leave a cruel master. Peter is not approving the system. He is telling slaves how to live faithfully inside a situation they cannot change.
- **"For this is a gracious thing, when, mindful of God, one endures sorrows while suffering unjustly" (v19).** "Gracious thing" is *charis*, "grace," used here to mean something God looks on with favor. "Mindful of God" is literally "because of conscience toward God." A Christian slave could be beaten for refusing to take part in household idol worship or to lie for a master.
- **"When you sin and are beaten for it" (v20).** "Beaten" (*kolaphizō*) means struck with the fist. It is the word for what the soldiers and guards did to Jesus at his trial (Matt 26:67; Mark 14:65). Peter may have watched it happen from the courtyard. There is no credit in bearing punishment you deserve. There is honor with God in bearing punishment you do not.
- **"For to this you have been called, because Christ also suffered for you" (v21).** Peter widens his words beyond slaves to every believer. Suffering unjustly is part of the Christian calling, because it was Christ's path. "For you" means more than an example. He suffered on our behalf.
- **"Leaving you an example, so that you might follow in his steps" (v21).** "Example" is *hypogrammos*, used only here in the New Testament. It was a teacher's model for a child learning to write: letters traced lightly for the student to write over. "Follow in his steps" pictures walking in someone's footprints. Peter, told by Jesus at the start, "Follow me" (Mark 1:17), and again at the end, "You follow me!" (John 21:22), now says the same to every believer.
- **"He committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (v22).** From Isaiah 53:9. Peter lived with Jesus for three years and could say this from experience. "Deceit" (*dolos*) is the word of v1. Jesus never once spoke falsely, even under torture.
- **"When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten" (v23).** At his trials Jesus was mocked, spat on and struck, and "he gave him no answer, not even to a single charge" (Matt 27:14; Isa 53:7). On the cross he prayed for those who crucified him (Luke 23:34). Peter, by contrast, drew a sword in Gethsemane (John 18:10).
- **"But continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (v23).** The verb tense pictures him handing himself over again and again. Jesus did not stay silent because he did not care about justice. He trusted God to deliver it. His last words on the cross: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46). That frees a suffering believer from the need to get even.
- **"He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree" (v24).** Now Peter moves past example to something no one can copy. "Bore" is the word of Isaiah 53:12, "he bore the sin of many," and the language of sacrifice, where a priest carried an offering up onto the altar. "Tree" (*xylon*) was Peter's own word for the cross in his preaching (Acts 5:30; 10:39). It recalls Deuteronomy 21:23, "a hanged man is cursed by God," which Paul applies to Jesus (Gal 3:13).
- **"That we might die to sin and live to righteousness" (v24).** The cross does not only forgive. It changes how we live. A person who has died to sin is no longer at its beck and call (Rom 6:11).
- **"By his wounds you have been healed" (v24).** From Isaiah 53:5. "Wounds" is singular in Greek, *mōlōps*, the welt or bruise left by a whip. The whole of Jesus' body was one wound. Peter is writing to slaves who knew exactly what such welts felt like. The healing here is mainly from sin, as the next verse shows. Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus' healing of bodies (Matt 8:17), and Christians differ on how much physical healing the atonement promises now. Most agree it is fully realized at the resurrection.
- **"For you were straying like sheep, but have now returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls" (v25).** Still Isaiah 53: "All we like sheep have gone astray" (v6). Jesus called himself the good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). After his denial, Peter was told three times, "Feed my sheep" (John 21:15–17). "Overseer" is *episkopos*, the word later used for church leaders ("bishops"). Peter will call himself a "fellow elder" and Jesus "the chief Shepherd" (5:1–4). The shepherd who leads the church is the one who was wounded for it.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The sinful misconduct of one relation, does not justify sinful behaviour in the other," and "Sinners, before their conversion, are always going astray; their life is a continued error." On v16 he warns believers not to "abuse and affront the free grace of God." Guzik notes that Peter wrote under an empire that was no friend to Christians, yet still honored its authority, and he insists this stops where government commands what God forbids (Acts 4:19). He reads v24 as substitution, not only example. Guzik takes "by his wounds" mainly of spiritual healing while allowing physical healing, partly now and fully at the resurrection. JFB calls the example "a copy" set for pupils to imitate, as the lexicon confirms (*hypogrammos*, "a writing-copy"). He reads "bore" as a sacrificial term, to carry up and offer, and calls the cross "the proper place for One on whom the curse was laid." He notes the Greek "stripe" is singular and calls "ye were healed" a paradox, "yet true."

#### Pointing to Christ
Every image in this chapter is Christ. He is the "living stone" the builders threw out and God made the cornerstone, the foundation of a temple made of people. He is the true King-Priest whose people share his royal priesthood. He is the sufferer who did not revile or threaten, the one whose life is the model letters every believer traces over, and the Lamb of Isaiah 53 who "bore our sins in his body on the tree." And he is the Shepherd who went after the straying sheep and brought them home. Peter, who once stumbled over the idea of a suffering Messiah, and who denied him in a courtyard while Jesus was struck, now calls slaves and outsiders to walk in the footprints of that same Jesus, because those footprints lead through the cross to glory.
