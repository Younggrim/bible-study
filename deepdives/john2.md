*Setting:* John's Gospel was most likely written by the apostle John late in the first century, probably from Ephesus. Chapter 1 ended with a string of days: John the Baptist's testimony, the first disciples following Jesus, and Philip and Nathanael called in Galilee, where Jesus promised Nathanael he would "see greater things than these" (1:50). Chapter 2 shows the first of those greater things. It pairs two scenes from the center of Jewish life, a village wedding in Galilee and the Passover at the temple in Jerusalem. In both, Jesus takes something Israel already had, water for purification and a house for sacrifice, and shows that he himself is what they were pointing to. John calls Jesus' miracles "signs" (*sēmeia*). They are not only acts of power but pointers that show who Jesus is, and this chapter gives the first one.

#### The Wedding at Cana (vv1–12)
- **"On the third day" (v1).** Counted from the call of Philip and Nathanael (1:43), this rounds off a week of days John has been numbering since 1:29 ("the next day," 1:29, 35, 43). Some readers see a deliberate new-creation week ending in the first sign. Others take it simply as a travel note. Either way, readers who know the end of the book will hear an echo of another "third day."
- **"A wedding at Cana in Galilee" (v1).** Cana was a village in the hills of lower Galilee, a few miles north of Nazareth. Its exact site is debated: Khirbet Qana, about nine miles north of Nazareth, is favored by many archaeologists, while the traditional site, Kafr Kanna, is closer. It was Nathanael's hometown (21:2). A village wedding could last a week. The groom's family hosted, and the whole community came.
- **"The mother of Jesus was there" (v1).** John never names Mary in his Gospel. She appears only here and at the cross (19:25–27), at the first sign and at the "hour" itself. Joseph is not mentioned, and most think he had died by now.
- **"When the wine ran out" (v3).** This was more than awkward. Hospitality was a matter of family honor in a village, and running out of wine would shame the groom's family for years. The Tyndale notes suggest the arrival of Jesus with several disciples may have stretched the supply. Wine in Scripture is also a picture of joy and of the abundance of the messianic age (Isa 25:6; Amos 9:13–14). A wedding without wine is a feast that has lost its gladness.
- **"Woman, what does this have to do with me?" (v4).** "Woman" (*gynai*) sounds cold in English, but it was a polite form of address. Jesus uses it for Mary again from the cross, in one of the tenderest moments of the Gospel (19:26). The phrase "what does this have to do with me?" is literally "what to me and to you?", an idiom that puts distance between two parties (Judg 11:12; 2 Kings 3:13). Jesus is gently saying that his actions are set by his Father, not by family. Even his mother now relates to him as one of his followers.
- **"My hour has not yet come" (v4).** This is the first mention of Jesus' "hour" in John. It runs through the book as a countdown (7:30; 8:20) until Jesus finally says, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified" (12:23), meaning his death, resurrection and return to the Father. The wine at Cana is a small preview of what that hour will pour out.
- **"Do whatever he tells you" (v5).** These are the last words of Mary recorded in John, and they are a good summary of discipleship. She doesn't argue or tell him how to solve the problem. She leaves the matter with him and prepares the servants to obey. Her words echo Pharaoh's instruction about Joseph: "Go to Joseph. What he says to you, do" (Gen 41:55).
- **Six stone water jars (v6).** Stone was used because, under the purity rules of the day, stone vessels did not become ritually unclean the way clay pots did (Lev 11:33). Archaeologists have found many such jars in first-century Galilee and Jerusalem. The water was for washing hands and utensils before meals (Mark 7:3–4). "Twenty or thirty gallons" each means 120–180 gallons in all, an enormous amount of wine for a village feast. The Greek measure is the *metrētēs*, about nine gallons, and each jar held "two or three."
- **"They filled them up to the brim" (v7).** There is no room left to add anything. The servants obey fully, and only they know what happened (v9). Jesus speaks no prayer and no word over the water. He simply tells them to draw and serve.
- **"The master of the feast" (vv8–10).** This was the head steward or toastmaster in charge of the banquet. His remark is a piece of common wisdom: hosts served the best wine first, while guests could still tell the difference, and the cheaper wine later. "When people have drunk freely" translates a verb that can mean "become drunk," but here it simply describes the later stage of a long feast. The point is the quality of the new wine, not the guests' condition. It was real wine, and better than anything served before.
- **"You have kept the good wine until now" (v10).** The steward unknowingly speaks a deeper truth. God's best gift has come late in Israel's story, in the Messiah. The water of the old purification rites is not thrown out. It is transformed into something far richer.
- **"The first of his signs… and manifested his glory" (v11).** "First" (*archē*) can mean "beginning" or "chief," the first of a series John has selected (20:30–31). John's first chapter said, "We have seen his glory" (1:14). Here it is seen for the first time in action. The result is that "his disciples believed in him": they already followed him, and now their faith deepens.
- **"He went down to Capernaum" (v12).** Capernaum, a fishing town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, became Jesus' base for his Galilean ministry (Matt 4:13). "Down" is accurate: Cana is in the hills, and Capernaum is about 700 feet below sea level. "His brothers" are named in Mark 6:3. Catholic and Orthodox traditions understand them as cousins or Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage, while most Protestants take them as later children of Joseph and Mary. John notes that at this stage they "did not believe in him" (7:5).
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Matthew Henry both read the miracle as a contrast between the Law and the gospel. Moses' first sign in Egypt turned water into blood (Exod 7:20), and Jesus' first sign turns water into wine. Henry: "the blessing of the gospel turns water into wine." Both see honor given to marriage in Jesus' presence at a wedding. On "Woman," JFB calls it "no term of disrespect in the language of that day," and reads "mine hour" as a hint that he would act, but in his own time. Henry sees in the gentle correction a warning against giving Mary a place of influence over Christ, a point Guzik also makes when he notes that Mary's words point away from herself to her Son. On the steward's remark, JFB says it shows "that it was real wine, but better than any at the feast." Guzik stresses that the word good here speaks of quality rather than strength.

#### Jesus Cleanses the Temple (vv13–22)
- **"The Passover of the Jews was at hand" (v13).** Passover was the spring feast remembering the exodus from Egypt (Exod 12). Jewish men were to go up to Jerusalem for it (Deut 16:16), and the city's population swelled many times over. John mentions three Passovers (2:13; 6:4; 11:55), which is the main reason many think Jesus' public ministry lasted about three years.
- **"In the temple" (v14).** John uses *hieron* here, the whole temple complex with its courts. The selling probably took place in the vast outer court, the Court of the Gentiles, the only place non-Jews could come to pray. Pilgrims traveling long distances needed animals that would pass inspection, so a market had grown up to supply them. Money-changers swapped Roman and Greek coins, which carried images of emperors and gods, for the Tyrian shekel used for the annual temple tax. A fee was charged on every exchange.
- **"Making a whip of cords" (v15).** Jesus takes time to braid it. This is deliberate action, not a loss of temper. The text says he drove "them all out… with the sheep and oxen." Many read the whip as used on the animals, though the merchants left too. Scattering coins and overturning tables would have caused a scene in the busiest week of the year.
- **"Do not make my Father's house a house of trade" (v16).** "My Father's house" is a striking claim. Jesus speaks as the Son with authority over God's house, not as a pilgrim. "House of trade" may echo Zechariah 14:21, which says there will "no longer be a trader in the house of the LORD" on the day the Lord comes. The Synoptic Gospels record different words, "a den of robbers" (Mark 11:17, from Jer 7:11), which focus on corruption. Here the problem is that worship has become a marketplace at all.
- **One cleansing or two?** Matthew, Mark and Luke place a temple cleansing in Jesus' last week (Mark 11:15–17), while John puts one at the start. There are two main views. Many evangelical scholars hold that Jesus cleared the temple twice, once at the beginning and once at the end, and the details and wording differ between the accounts. Others think there was one event and John placed it here on purpose, for theme rather than strict sequence, to pair it with the purification jars at Cana. Both views take John's account as true. The first is the more natural reading of John's Passover framework.
- **"Zeal for your house will consume me" (v17).** This is Psalm 69:9, a psalm of the righteous sufferer quoted elsewhere of Jesus' passion (Ps 69:21; John 19:28–29; Rom 15:3). The verb shifts to future tense: zeal "will consume" him. The disciples see that this passion for God's house will cost Jesus his life, and indeed it is this kind of act that sets the temple leadership against him.
- **"What sign do you show us?" (v18).** "The Jews" in John usually means the Jewish religious authorities in Jerusalem, not the Jewish people as a whole (Jesus and all his disciples were Jews). Their question is fair on its face: a man who acts like a prophet should show prophetic credentials. But they have just seen the act and ask only for proof.
- **"Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (v19).** Here John switches words. "Temple" is now *naos*, the sanctuary building itself, the dwelling place of God, rather than the whole complex. The lexicon confirms that difference and lists John 2:19–21 among the places where *naos* is used of Christ's body. "Destroy" is a command with the force of a challenge: "go ahead and destroy it." "Raise up" (*egeirō*) is the ordinary verb for raising the dead. Jesus says he will raise it, which John affirms elsewhere: "I lay it down, and I take it up again" (10:17–18).
- **"It has taken forty-six years to build this temple" (v20).** Herod the Great began rebuilding the second temple in his eighteenth year, about 20/19 BC, according to Josephus. Forty-six years later brings us to about AD 27/28, which fits the start of Jesus' ministry. The main building had been finished long before, but work on the courts went on until AD 63/64, only a few years before the Romans destroyed it in AD 70. To his hearers, Jesus' words sounded absurd. At his trial they were twisted into a threat to destroy the temple (Mark 14:58), and they were thrown at him on the cross (Mark 15:29).
- **"He was speaking about the temple of his body" (v21).** John has already said the Word "dwelt" (literally "pitched his tent," *skēnoō*) among us (1:14), the language of the tabernacle where God's glory lived. Jesus is the true place where God meets his people. The temple's sacrifices pointed to him, and after his death and resurrection, the stone building's role comes to an end (4:21–24). Later the New Testament extends the picture to the church as God's temple (1 Cor 3:16; Eph 2:21).
- **"His disciples remembered" (v22).** This is the second time disciples "remembered" (v17). John is honest that they didn't understand at the time. Understanding came after the resurrection, when the Spirit reminded them of Jesus' words (14:26). "The Scripture" probably refers to passages like Psalm 16:10 that they came to see fulfilled.
- **Commentators:** All three treat the whip as controlled and purposeful. Henry says Jesus used it only on the animals and "aimed no further than at reformation." JFB says the work was done "not by this slender whip but by divine majesty." JFB heads the section "First Cleansing of the Temple," holding to two cleansings, and Guzik agrees, pointing to the Synoptics' later one. Guzik adds that the merchants filled the one court where Gentiles could pray, so their trade crowded out the nations' worship, and he notes the pattern of this chapter: first a conversion of water, then a cleansing of the house. On v17 JFB calls zeal "a glorious feature in the predicted character of the suffering Messiah." On v20 JFB counts the forty-six years from Herod's eighteenth year, following Josephus, as most scholars still do. On v19 Guzik uses Jesus' "I will raise it up" against teaching that denies Jesus raised himself, citing 10:18.

#### Jesus Knows What Is in Man (vv23–25)
- **"Many believed in his name when they saw the signs" (v23).** John mentions other signs in Jerusalem without describing them. Nicodemus will refer to them in the next verse (3:2). This belief was real in a sense but shallow. It rested on what people saw, and John will show over and over how quickly sign-based faith can turn away (6:26, 66; 8:31, 59).
- **"But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them" (v24).** In Greek this is a wordplay. "Believed" in v23 and "entrust" in v24 are the same verb, *pisteuō*. They "trusted" in him, but he did not "trust" himself to them. Jesus welcomed them but did not hand himself over to a crowd's enthusiasm.
- **"He himself knew what was in man" (v25).** Jesus doesn't need anyone's report on a person. He has already read Nathanael (1:47–48), and in the next chapters he will know Nicodemus's heart and the Samaritan woman's life story (4:18). In the Old Testament this is something God alone does: "I the LORD search the heart" (Jer 17:10; 1 Kings 8:39). The word "man" also leads straight into the next verse: "Now there was a man of the Pharisees named Nicodemus" (3:1). These three verses introduce the conversations that follow.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus "knew them too well to put any confidence in them." JFB says many believed only "superficially," struck by the miracles, and ties Jesus' knowledge of the heart to Jeremiah 17:9–10, where God alone searches it. Guzik draws a practical point: knowing what is in people did not stop Jesus from loving them, and believers can love people without placing blind trust in them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Both scenes in this chapter point to the same truth. At Cana, the water of Jewish purification becomes the best wine, poured out when the old supply has run dry. At the temple, the house of sacrifice is exposed as a marketplace, and Jesus names the real temple: his own body, to be destroyed and raised on the third day. The rites of cleansing and the place of meeting with God were never the goal. They pointed to him. The "hour" Jesus mentions at the wedding is the hour of the cross, where the true temple was torn down and raised up, and from which the new wine of the kingdom flows to every guest who will come.
