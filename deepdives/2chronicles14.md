*Setting:* About 911–896 BC. Abijah has died after a short reign, and his son Asa takes the throne of Judah. Asa reigned forty-one years (16:13), longer than any king of Judah before him except his great-great-grandfather David's son Solomon. Chronicles gives him three chapters (14–16), against sixteen verses in Kings (1 Kgs 15:9–24), and arranges them as a story in two halves: years of faithfulness, reform, peace and a great victory won by relying on the LORD (chs 14–15), then a late turn to relying on a foreign king instead (ch 16). This chapter is the bright beginning. In the Hebrew Bible, verse 1 here is the last verse of chapter 13, so Hebrew chapter 14 is numbered one verse behind English.

#### Asa Reigns in Judah (vv1–8)
- **"In his days the land had rest for ten years" (v1).** "Rest" is a loaded word in Chronicles. God promised David that his son would be "a man of rest," and named him Solomon, from *shalom*, peace (1 Chr 22:9). Rest from enemies is God's gift to a king who seeks him. The ten years probably run from Asa's accession to Zerah's invasion.
- **"Asa did what was good and right in the eyes of the LORD his God" (v2).** Kings says he did right "as David his father had done" (1 Kgs 15:11). "Father" there means ancestor. Asa's actual father and grandfather were mixed at best.
- **"He took away the foreign altars and the high places and broke down the pillars and cut down the Asherim" (v3).** Four kinds of false worship. *Foreign altars* were altars to other gods. *High places* were local hilltop shrines. *Pillars* (*matstsebot*) were standing stones linked to Canaanite gods, often Baal. *Asherim* were wooden poles or carved trees standing for Asherah, the Canaanite mother goddess, often paired with Baal. Moses commanded Israel to destroy all of these (Deut 7:5; 12:2–3).
- **Did Asa remove the high places?** Here he takes away "the high places" (vv3, 5). In 15:17 and 1 Kings 15:14 "the high places were not taken out of Israel." The usual explanation is that Asa removed the shrines to foreign gods but left local altars where people still worshiped the LORD in the wrong way. Another is that 15:17 refers to "Israel," meaning the northern towns Asa later captured. A third is simply that a reform early in a reign did not last, and the shrines came back. The texts are honest that his reform was real but not complete.
- **"Commanded Judah to seek the LORD, the God of their fathers, and to keep the law and the commandment" (v4).** A king cannot make anyone love God, but he can lead, remove obstacles and call the nation back. "Seek the LORD" is the phrase that measures every king in Chronicles. It runs right through Asa's story (14:4, 7; 15:2, 4, 12–15; 16:12).
- **"The incense altars" (v5).** The Hebrew word *hammanim* is uncertain. Older translations rendered it "images" or "sun-images," linking it to the sun. Excavations have turned up small stone altars used for burning incense at Canaanite and Israelite shrines, and most modern translations follow that sense.
- **"He built fortified cities in Judah, for the land had rest" (v6).** Peace is not wasted. In Chronicles a king who seeks God builds (11:5; 17:12; 26:9–10; 27:4). **"For the LORD gave him peace."** The Chronicler is clear about who gave it.
- **"The land is still ours, because we have sought the LORD our God" (v7).** Literally "the land is still before us," open and in our power. Asa urges the people to build while they can. **"We have sought him, and he has given us peace on every side."** The connection between seeking and rest is the whole lesson of these chapters. It was also the hope of the post-exile readers, who had a small piece of land and a temple, and were rebuilding Jerusalem's walls (Neh 2:17–18; 4:6).
- **"300,000 from Judah… 280,000 men from Benjamin" (v8).** Judah's troops carried the large body shield (*tsinnah*) and spear of heavy infantry. Benjamin's carried the small round shield (*magen*) and bow. Benjamin was famous for its archers and slingers (1 Chr 8:40; 12:2). The large numbers raise the same questions as in chapter 13. Either way, Asa's army is large and well equipped, and yet the coming victory will not be credited to it.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says that "times of rest from war should be employed in work, for we must always find ourselves something to do," and, more memorably, "When the sword is sheathed take up the trowel." Guzik draws on 1 Kings 15:12–13 for details Chronicles saves for later: Asa banished the male cult prostitutes and removed his grandmother Maacah as queen mother. He cites Knapp that faithfulness is tested most in a man's own family. Guzik reconciles the high places by saying Asa removed shrines to foreign gods but not those used for the LORD, the first of the views above. He also cites Trapp that Asa prepared for war though he had none. JFB takes the "images" of v3 as images of Baal.

#### Zerah Attacks (vv9–15)
- **"Zerah the Ethiopian" (v9).** "Ethiopian" is *Cushite*. Cush normally means the Nile lands south of Egypt (Nubia, modern Sudan). Zerah is not named in any Egyptian record. The most common view is that he was a Nubian commander serving Pharaoh Osorkon I, Shishak's son, sent to repeat his father's raid (12:2–9). The mention of Libyans alongside Cushites in 16:8 supports a link with Egypt. A second view is that he led a tribe of nomads in the Negev near Gerar, since "Cushan" can also refer to a people near Midian (Hab 3:7) and the spoil in vv14–15 is tents, sheep and camels. JFB holds this second view.
- **"An army of a million men and 300 chariots" (v9).** Literally "a thousand thousands," the largest army in the Old Testament. Whether taken literally, as a round figure meaning a countless horde, or as a thousand units, the point is that Asa faced overwhelming odds, nearly double his own army. Three hundred chariots is a modest, realistic number beside it.
- **"Came as far as Mareshah" (v9).** Mareshah, in the foothills (Shephelah) about 25 miles southwest of Jerusalem, was one of Rehoboam's forts (11:8). It guarded a main pass up into the hill country. The forts built in faith were doing their work.
- **"The Valley of Zephathah at Mareshah" (v10).** The name is uncertain and appears only here. The Greek translation reads "the valley north of Mareshah," and many think the location lay along the valley that runs up past modern Beit Jibrin.
- **"O LORD, there is none like you to help, between the mighty and the weak" (v11).** Asa's prayer is one of the great short prayers of the Bible. Literally "there is none beside you to help between the many and the one without strength." It means: it makes no difference to you whether the side you help is strong or weak. Jonathan said the same before his attack on the Philistines: "nothing can hinder the LORD from saving by many or by few" (1 Sam 14:6).
- **"Help us, O LORD our God, for we rely on you" (v11).** "Rely" is *sha'an* again, to lean your weight on. Abijah's army won because they "relied on the LORD" (13:18). Asa will later be rebuked because he "relied on the king of Syria, and did not rely on the LORD" (16:7). This word is the hinge of Asa's whole story.
- **"In your name we have come against this multitude" (v11).** Like David against Goliath: "I come to you in the name of the LORD of hosts" (1 Sam 17:45).
- **"O LORD, you are our God; let not man prevail against you" (v11).** Asa makes the battle God's. "Man" is *'enosh*, a word for humankind that often stresses weakness and mortality (Ps 8:4; 103:15). Mortal man, however many, against the living God.
- **Solomon's prayer answered.** At the temple's dedication Solomon asked that when Israel went out to battle and prayed, God would "hear in heaven their prayer… and maintain their cause" (6:34–35). This is that prayer answered.
- **"So the LORD defeated the Ethiopians before Asa and before Judah" (v12).** The verb is the same one used of God defeating Jeroboam (13:15). The Chronicler is building a pattern: when Judah cries out and relies on the LORD, the LORD himself fights.
- **"They were broken before the LORD and his army" (v13).** "His army" (literally "his camp") could mean Judah's army fighting as God's host, or the heavenly army fighting alongside them, as at Dothan (2 Kgs 6:17). Either way, the army is the LORD's.
- **"As far as Gerar" (v13).** Gerar lay on the edge of the Negev, about 25 miles beyond Mareshah toward Gaza. It was where Abraham and Isaac had lived among the Philistines (Gen 20:1; 26:1, 6).
- **"For the fear of the LORD was upon them" (v14).** The towns around Gerar did not resist, gripped by a terror God sent. The same phrase appears in Jehoshaphat's days (17:10; 20:29).
- **Tents, sheep and camels (v15).** The spoil of herdsmen. The towns around Gerar were perhaps allied with Zerah or provided his base. Judah went out outnumbered and came home rich.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says of the prayer, "It is nothing with thee to help and save by many or few," and on v11's last line, "The enemy is a mortal man; make it to appear what an unequal match he is for an immortal God." He adds that "He that sought God in the day of his peace and prosperity could with holy boldness cry to God in the day of his trouble," a key to the whole chapter. Guzik follows Selman in identifying Zerah as a Nubian general under Osorkon I, and points out that Asa's prayer asks God to defend his own honor, not just to give Judah a win. He cites F. B. Meyer that prayer can remind God of his full responsibility for his people. Adam Clarke, through Guzik, suspects a mistake in the numbers. JFB is sure Zerah "could not have been from Ethiopia south of the cataracts of the Nile" and makes him the chief of "a nomad horde" camped near Gerar. The Tyndale notes, like Guzik, lean the other way, toward an Egyptian-backed force.

#### Pointing to Christ
"There is none like you to help, between the mighty and the weak." Asa's prayer is the cry of every believer who has faced something far bigger than their resources. The help he found was not in his walls or his 580,000 men but in God's name. Paul learned the same: "my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Cor 12:9). On the cross Jesus faced the greatest enemy, sin and death, alone and apparently powerless, and won the victory God's people share as spoil (Col 2:15). Those who rely on him are, as Paul says, "more than conquerors through him who loved us" (Rom 8:37).
