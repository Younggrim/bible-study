*Setting:* Directly after David spared Saul for the second time and the two men parted for good, with Saul's blessing ringing in the air: "You will do many things and will succeed in them" (26:25). David has just told Saul that being driven out of Israel would be like being told, "Go, serve other gods" (26:19). Now, in the very next verse, he decides to go to the Philistines anyway. After years on the run he is worn out. This chapter tells of the sixteen months David spent living among Israel's enemies. It is a story of a tired man's choice made without asking God, and of the lies and violence that followed. God still protects him, but the narrator does not hide the cost.

#### David Flees to the Philistines (vv1–12)
- **"Then David said in his heart" (v1).** David talks to himself instead of to God. In chapter 23 he inquired of the LORD again and again (23:2, 4, 9–12). Here there is no prayer, no ephod and no prophet. Gad had told him to stay in Judah (22:5), and there is no word that God changed that instruction.
- **"Now I shall perish one day by the hand of Saul" (v1).** This is fear speaking, not faith. God had anointed David as king (16:13). Jonathan had told him he would reign (23:17). Abigail had said the LORD would keep his life "bound in the bundle of the living" (25:29). Saul himself had just said David would succeed (26:25). And twice God had delivered Saul into David's hands. Yet David concludes there is "nothing better" than to run to the Philistines.
- **"Then Saul will despair of seeking me" (v1).** In one sense David is right. It works. "He no longer sought him" (v4). The plan succeeds on human terms, which does not make it the right plan.
- **"He and the six hundred men… every man with his household" (vv2–3).** This is no longer a lone fugitive. Six hundred fighters with their wives and children, perhaps two or three thousand people, move into Philistine territory. David's decision affects everyone who follows him. His two wives go too, Ahinoam and Abigail.
- **"Achish the son of Maoch, king of Gath" (v2).** This is the same king David fooled by pretending to be mad (21:10–15), or possibly a relative with the same name. This time Achish welcomes him. A famous Israelite commander who has broken with Saul, bringing six hundred battle-hardened men, was a valuable asset. An Achish "son of Maacah" is still king of Gath in Solomon's early years (1 Kings 2:39), which would mean a very long reign or a grandson with the same name.
- **"Why should your servant dwell in the royal city with you?" (v5).** David's request is humble on the surface and shrewd underneath. In Gath he would be watched. In a country town he could do as he pleased. He calls himself Achish's "servant," which he now is.
- **"Achish gave him Ziklag" (v6).** Ziklag was a town in the far south, in the Negeb near the edge of the desert. It had been allotted to Judah and then to Simeon (Josh 15:31; 19:5), but the Philistines held it. Its exact location is debated among archaeologists. Now it becomes David's base, and Achish has gained a buffer town on his southern frontier.
- **"Therefore Ziklag has belonged to the kings of Judah to this day" (v6).** The narrator steps out of the story to speak to his own readers. "The kings of Judah" suggests he wrote after the kingdom split in two at Solomon's death (around 930 BC), when there were separate kings of Judah. It is one of several clues that the books of Samuel were finished well after the events they record.
- **"A year and four months" (v7).** Sixteen months. David's later reign in Hebron lasted seven and a half years (2 Sam 2:11), so this was a short, intense chapter of his life. Commentators have long noted that no psalm title is linked to this time, which fits the impression that David was not living close to God here.
- **"Raids against the Geshurites, the Girzites, and the Amalekites" (v8).** These were desert peoples living south of Judah toward the Sinai and Egypt. "Shur" was the wilderness on Egypt's eastern frontier (15:7; Gen 16:7). The Amalekites were Israel's oldest enemy, the people God had said would be wiped out (Exod 17:14–16; Deut 25:17–19), the people Saul failed to destroy (ch 15). The Geshurites here are a southern group (Josh 13:2), different from the Geshur in the north whose princess David later married (2 Sam 3:3). The Girzites are not mentioned anywhere else.
- **"Would leave neither man nor woman alive" (v9).** This is grim. Some interpreters connect it to God's command to destroy Amalek (15:3), which David is carrying out where Saul failed. But the text gives a different reason in v11: David killed everyone so that no one could tell Achish the truth. He kept the livestock and goods as plunder, which is exactly what Saul was condemned for doing with Amalek (15:9, 19). The Bible does not dress this up.
- **"Against the Negeb of Judah" (v10).** The Negeb is the dry southern region. David tells Achish he has been raiding his own people, the southern parts of Judah, and the clans of the Jerahmeelites and Kenites, who were friendly with Israel (1 Chr 2:9; Judg 1:16; 1 Sam 15:6). In fact he was protecting them by attacking their enemies. He will later send gifts from his plunder to these very towns (30:26–31).
- **"Lest they should tell about us" (v11).** One lie needs another, and here the lie needs bloodshed to keep it hidden. "Such was his custom all the while." It became a pattern, not a single slip.
- **"He has made himself an utter stench to his people Israel" (v12).** Achish believes David's lies completely. "Stench" is a vivid word: Achish thinks David has made himself hateful to Israel, so he has nowhere else to go. "He shall always be my servant." The trap is closing. In the next chapter Achish will ask David to march with him against Israel (28:1–2).
- **How to read David here.** Interpreters differ. Some see a clever survivor who outwitted Israel's enemies and protected Judah while he waited for God's timing. Others see a man drifting from God, relying on deception and violence, the same pattern of hidden sin that will later lead to Uriah's death (2 Sam 11). The Tyndale notes point out that the text neither praises nor condemns the lies outright. But the story that follows (chs 29–30) suggests the narrator sees danger here. Only God's intervention gets David out of the mess he has made.
- **Commentators:** Henry is direct: "Unbelief is a sin that easily besets even good men," and "When we forget the Lord's past mercies, and his gracious assurances, we shall be overwhelmed with desponding fears." On David's answers to Achish, he will not excuse them: "an equivocation which serves the purpose of a lie, is as like to it as a hypocrite is to a profane person." JFB calls David's resolution "in every respect, wrong," and says the deceit and slaughter "exhibit an unfavorable view" of this part of his life. It suggests Achish may have invited David for political reasons, which the text doesn't say. Guzik stresses that what David said "in his heart" set his course, and that discouragement did what Saul could not, driving him out of Israel. He sees David's raids as a step down from fighting for God's honor to fighting for plunder, and connects the habit of covering up with the later sin against Uriah. He adds that David had not burned his bridge with God, who was still able to bring him back.

#### Pointing to Christ
David, worn out by suffering, takes the easier road. He leaves God's land, lives by lies and secures his own safety by the sword. It works for a while. Jesus faced the same pressure, and greater, and did not take the shortcut. In the wilderness he refused to save himself by any means but trust in his Father (Matt 4:1–11), and on the cross he refused to come down (Matt 27:40–42). David's story shows that even the man after God's own heart could drift. Christ is the King who never did, "who committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22). That is why he can be trusted, and why his grace reaches those who, like David, have run.
