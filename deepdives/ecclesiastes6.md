*Setting:* Chapter 5 ended on a bright note: when God gives someone wealth *and* the power to enjoy it, "this is the gift of God" (5:19). Chapter 6 shows the dark side of the same truth. Here is a man who has wealth and honor, but God does not give him the power to enjoy them. The chapter is short and bleak. It continues the ESV section that began at 5:8, "The Vanity of Wealth and Honor." Its picture of a man with a hundred children and a long life who would be better off stillborn is one of the starkest lines in the Old Testament. The last three verses (vv10–12) close the first half of the book with a string of questions: who knows what is good for man, and who can tell him what comes after? Chapter 7 begins to answer with a set of "better" proverbs. For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### The Vanity of Wealth and Honor (vv1–12)
- **"There is an evil that I have seen under the sun, and it lies heavy on mankind" (v1).** "Lies heavy" may mean "is common" (KJV) or "is great upon" people. JFB gives both. Either way it is not rare.
- **"A man to whom God gives wealth, possessions, and honor… yet God does not give him power to enjoy them, but a stranger enjoys them" (v2).** This is the reverse of 5:19. He has everything he could want, three times over: wealth, goods and honor, the very things God gave Solomon (2 Chr 1:11–12). But the power to enjoy it is a separate gift, and God has withheld it. Something cuts the enjoyment short: illness, war, death or a restless heart. A "stranger," not even a family member, gets it all. The Tyndale notes compare 4:7–8 and 5:13–17 and call it the tragedy of success without enjoyment. "Grievous evil" is literally "an evil sickness," from the same root as the "grievous evil" of 5:13.
- **"If a man fathers a hundred children and lives many years" (v3).** In Israel, many children and long life were the two great signs of blessing (Ps 127:3–5; 128:3–6; Prov 3:16). A hundred is an exaggeration for effect, though some kings came close: Ahab had seventy sons (2 Kgs 10:1), Rehoboam eighty-eight children (2 Chr 11:21).
- **"But his soul is not satisfied with life's good things, and he also has no burial" (v3).** Two things undo his blessings. First, he never enjoys them. Second, he dies without honor. To lie unburied was a deep disgrace in Israel, the fate of Jezebel (2 Kgs 9:35–37) and the curse pronounced on King Jehoiakim, "buried with the burial of a donkey" (Jer 22:19). JFB calls it "the greatest degradation." It may mean his own family did not bother to mourn him, or that no one remembered him enough to bury him. Kidner, cited by Guzik, sums him up: unnoticed, unlamented and unfulfilled.
- **"I say that a stillborn child is better off than he" (v3).** A shocking comparison. A miscarried or stillborn baby never lives at all, but it is spared what this man went through. Job wished the same in his agony: "Why did I not die at birth?" (Job 3:11, 16). Guzik draws the link to Job.
- **"For it comes in vanity and goes in darkness, and in darkness its name is covered" (v4).** The stillborn child arrives as *hebel*, a breath, and leaves in silence. It is not even named. JFB notes the subject is the child, not the man.
- **"It has not seen the sun or known anything, yet it finds rest rather than he" (v5).** "Rest" is the key. The child never saw the sun, never knew pain or disappointment. The man saw the sun for many years and never found rest. Henry draws the contrast: the stillborn's loss is "only negative," while the miser "lives a great while to see himself miserable."
- **"Even though he should live a thousand years twice over, yet enjoy no good — do not all go to the one place?" (v6).** No one lived even a thousand years. Methuselah, the longest, reached 969 (Gen 5:27). Double that and it changes nothing, if there is no good in it. And in the end both the long-lived man and the stillborn child go to "the one place," the grave (3:20). Guzik notes that the Preacher, like much of the Old Testament, sees the afterlife dimly.
- **"All the toil of man is for his mouth, yet his appetite is not satisfied" (v7).** "Appetite" is *nephesh*, often translated "soul." It means the whole living, desiring self, and literally the throat. He works to feed his mouth, but what is deeper than his mouth stays hungry. Guzik connects this to "man does not live by bread alone" (Deut 8:3; Matt 4:4).
- **"What advantage has the wise man over the fool? And what does the poor man have who knows how to conduct himself before the living?" (v8).** Both need to eat. Both feel the hunger of v7. Wisdom does not fill the stomach better, and a poor man's good manners do not feed him. Clarke, cited by Guzik, notes that the wise and the fool share the same needs, illnesses and death. The Tyndale notes and JFB find the wise man's real advantage in knowing how to be content with what God gives.
- **"Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the appetite" (v9).** What is in front of you, which you can see and have, is better than restless desire that wanders after more. A bird in the hand. It is the same contentment as "a handful of quietness" (4:6). JFB: "the wise man's godly enjoyment of present seen blessings" against the fool's insatiable desire. Even so, "this also is vanity," because contentment does not answer the deeper question.
- **"Whatever has come to be has already been named, and it is known what man is" (v10).** To name something in the Bible is to define it and to have authority over it. God named day and night (Gen 1:5), and Adam named the animals (Gen 2:19–20). What man is has already been fixed. The word for "man" is *adam*, which echoes *adamah*, "ground": he is made of dust (Gen 2:7). JFB draws out the name. The Tyndale notes connect the verse to God's foreknowledge (Ps 139:15–16; Rom 8:29).
- **"He is not able to dispute with one stronger than he" (v10).** The "one stronger" is God. People cannot argue their way out of being human. Job tried to take God to court and finally put his hand over his mouth (Job 9:32–33; 40:3–5). Paul asks, "Who are you, O man, to answer back to God?" (Rom 9:20), and the Tyndale notes, Guzik and JFB all cite that verse. Wright, cited by Guzik, finds something close to fatalism here. Read alongside the rest of the book, it is more a call to humility than a shrug.
- **"The more words, the more vanity, and what is the advantage to man?" (v11).** Talking about it, arguing with God, theorizing, multiplies the vapor without changing anything. The same lesson as 5:2–3, 7. Morgan, cited by Guzik, puts a twist on it: the more a man has under the sun, the more he feels its emptiness.
- **"Who knows what is good for man while he lives the few days of his vain life, which he passes like a shadow?" (v12).** The first half of the book ends on two questions. The first is about the present: what is good? Life is "few days," a "vain life" (literally "his life of *hebel*"), and it passes "like a shadow" that lengthens and is gone at evening. The image is common in the Psalms and Job (Ps 102:11; 144:4; Job 8:9; 14:2), and the Tyndale notes cite several.
- **"For who can tell man what will be after him under the sun?" (v12).** The second question is about the future. No one can see past his own death, either to what happens on earth after him or to what becomes of him. Chapters 7–12 take up these two questions, though the second is answered fully only beyond the book (12:7, 14).
- **Commentators:** Henry's verdict on the chapter: worldly things, even fully enjoyed, "leave a man but a man still," and "Our satisfaction must be in another life, not in this." JFB, quoting Henry on v3: "Better the fruit that drops from the tree before it is ripe than that left to hang on till rotten." JFB also calls the miser "fit neither for life, nor death, nor eternity." Guzik passes on Kidner's comment that the chapter ends in a depressing uncertainty, which fits man on his own. He reminds readers that the clearer light comes in the New Testament, where Christ "brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10).

#### Pointing to Christ
"Who can tell man what will be after him?" (v12). The Preacher could not, and he was honest enough to say so. But the one who could tell came. Jesus spoke plainly of what lies beyond death: "In my Father's house are many rooms… I go to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2). He told a rich fool much like the man of v2 that the question is not how much he has stored up but whether he is "rich toward God" (Luke 12:20–21). Where the soul of v7 is never satisfied, he said, "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger" (John 6:35). And the man who cannot "dispute with one stronger than he" (v10) finds that the Stronger One is not his enemy. The Son of God took on our *adam*, our dust, and passed through the shadow of death himself, so that "through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death" (Heb 2:14–15).
