*Setting:* Days after Jericho fell. Israel has crossed the Jordan, kept the Passover, and watched a walled city collapse at a shout. Every sign says God is with them. Joshua's next target is Ai, a town in the hills about ten miles west of Jericho and more than 3,000 feet higher, on the edge of the central plateau. Taking it would give Israel a foothold in the hill country and open the road to Bethel and the heart of Canaan. The chapter turns on one sentence the reader learns before Joshua does: someone has taken what belonged to God. Chapter 6 ended with "the LORD was with Joshua, and his fame was in all the land." Chapter 7 begins with "But."

#### Israel Defeated at Ai (vv1–5)
- **"But the people of Israel broke faith in regard to the devoted things" (v1).** "Broke faith" is *ma'al*, a word for treachery or unfaithfulness, used of a wife betraying her husband (Num 5:12) and of sacrilege against holy things (Lev 5:15). One man did it, but the verse says "the people of Israel" broke faith, and "the anger of the LORD burned against the people of Israel." In God's eyes Israel is one body, and the sin of one member is counted against the whole.
- **"Achan the son of Carmi, son of Zabdi, son of Zerah, of the tribe of Judah" (v1).** The full family line is solemn, the way a legal charge would read. Achan was from Judah, the leading tribe, the tribe of the future kings. Zerah was Judah's son by Tamar (Gen 38:30). The irony is sharp: Rahab, an outsider, is brought inside Israel by faith (6:25), while Achan, an insider from the royal tribe, puts himself outside by greed. 1 Chronicles 2:7 calls him "Achar, the troubler of Israel," a play on his name.
- **"Ai, which is near Beth-aven, east of Bethel" (v2).** Ai means "the ruin," usually with the article, "the Ai." Bethel means "house of God." Beth-aven means "house of wickedness" or "house of nothingness." Hosea later uses Beth-aven as a mocking name for Bethel when it had become a center of idol worship (Hos 4:15; 10:5).
- **"Do not make the whole people toil up there, for they are few" (v3).** The spies' advice sounds sensible, and it is the opposite of the Kadesh report: there the spies said the enemy was too strong, here they say the enemy is too weak. Both reports left God out of the calculation. Nobody asked the LORD. Joshua sought God before Jericho, or rather God came to him (5:13–6:5). Before Ai, the text records no prayer at all.
- **Where was Ai?** Most scholars have identified Ai with et-Tell (Arabic for "the mound"), a large ruin near Bethel. Excavations there found a great city destroyed around 2400 BC and nothing but a small later village, with no city in Joshua's day. Some explain this by saying Ai in Joshua was a ruin used as a fortified outpost. Others argue et-Tell is the wrong site. Recent excavations at Khirbet el-Maqatir, about a mile away, found a small fortress of the right period destroyed by fire, and some propose it as Joshua's Ai. The question remains open.
- **"They fled before the men of Ai" (v4).** This is the first and only recorded defeat in the conquest. About thirty-six men were killed (v5), a small number in battle terms, but this was supposed to be the army no one could stand against (1:5).
- **"As far as Shebarim… at the descent" (v5).** Shebarim means "breaks" or "quarries." The Israelites were chased down the steep slope they had climbed.
- **"The hearts of the people melted and became as water" (v5).** The phrase used of the Canaanites (2:11; 5:1) is now used of Israel. When Israel is faithless, it becomes like the people it was sent to judge.

#### The Sin of Achan (vv6–26)
- **"Joshua tore his clothes and fell to the earth on his face before the ark" (v6).** Torn clothes and dust on the head were the standard signs of grief and disaster in the ancient Near East (1 Sam 4:12; Job 2:12). Joshua and the elders lie before the ark "until the evening," a day of fasting and mourning.
- **"Why have you brought this people over the Jordan at all?" (v7).** Joshua's prayer sounds dangerously like the complaints of the wilderness generation: "Would that we had died in Egypt!" (Num 14:2). "Would that we had been content to dwell beyond the Jordan" is close to what the tribes east of the Jordan chose. Joshua is honest in his distress, but he assumes the problem is God's faithfulness, not Israel's.
- **"What will you do for your great name?" (v9).** Here Joshua's prayer rises to its best point, and it echoes Moses' prayers at Sinai and Kadesh: if Israel is destroyed, the nations will say the LORD could not keep his promise (Exod 32:12; Num 14:13–16). God's reputation and his people's survival are bound together.
- **"Get up! Why have you fallen on your face?" (v10).** God's reply is abrupt. This is not the time for lying on the ground. There is a reason for the defeat, and it is not on God's side.
- **"Israel has sinned; they have transgressed my covenant" (v11).** God piles up the verbs: they have sinned, transgressed, taken, stolen, lied, and hidden it among their own belongings. Each step made it worse. "Lied" may mean the deception of carrying on as if nothing were wrong.
- **"They have become devoted for destruction" (v12).** This is the startling reversal. Whoever takes what is under the ban comes under the ban himself (6:18; Deut 7:26). Israel has made itself like Jericho. "I will be with you no more, unless…" The promise of 1:5 was never a blank check.
- **"Consecrate yourselves for tomorrow" (v13).** The same words used before the Jordan crossing (3:5). Then they prepared to see God's wonders. Now they prepare to face his judgment.
- **"The tribe that the LORD takes" (vv14–18).** The sinner was found by narrowing down: tribe, clan, household, man. The text does not say how, but most think it was by sacred lot, perhaps the Urim and Thummim kept by the high priest (Exod 28:30; Num 27:21; compare 1 Sam 14:38–42). "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Prov 16:33). Achan had a whole night and morning to step forward while the lot moved closer: Judah, then Zerah, then Zabdi. He said nothing until he was named.
- **"My son, give glory to the LORD God of Israel" (v19).** Joshua speaks tenderly ("My son"), and he uses a formula that meant "tell the truth before God." The Pharisees use the same words to the man born blind, "Give glory to God" (John 9:24). Confessing glorifies God because it agrees with his judgment and shows that he saw what was hidden.
- **"I saw… I coveted… I took… they are hidden" (v21).** Achan's confession traces the anatomy of sin in four steps, the same pattern as Eve in the garden: she "saw," the fruit was "desired," and she "took" (Gen 3:6), and then she and Adam hid. The Hebrew word for "coveted" (*chamad*) is the word of the tenth commandment: "You shall not covet" (Exod 20:17). David followed the same path with Bathsheba: he saw, he sent, he took (2 Sam 11:2–4).
- **"A beautiful cloak from Shinar, and 200 shekels of silver, and a bar of gold weighing 50 shekels" (v21).** Shinar is the plain of Babylonia (Gen 11:2), so this was an expensive imported robe. The silver weighed about five pounds and the gold about a pound and a quarter. Coins did not exist yet, and metal was weighed out as money. "A bar of gold" is literally "a tongue of gold," an ingot shaped like a tongue. It was a fortune for an ordinary soldier, and nothing he could use in the open without being caught.
- **"They ran to the tent" (v22).** The messengers hurry. The goods are exactly where Achan said, and they are laid out "before the LORD" (v23), returned to their true owner.
- **"His sons and daughters… and all that he had" (v24).** Everything connected to Achan is taken to the Valley of Achor. This is the hardest part of the chapter. The law said children were not to be put to death for their fathers' sins (Deut 24:16). Interpreters answer in different ways. Some think the family must have known, since the goods were buried under the floor of the tent they shared, and so they shared the guilt. Others note that what is under the ban, and all it touches, falls under the ban (Deut 13:15–17), so this case was unique. Some read the text as saying the family were brought out as witnesses and that only Achan was stoned, pointing to "all Israel stoned *him*" (v25) and "a great heap of stones… over *him*" (v26), though v25 also says "they burned *them*." The text does not settle it. What is clear is that sin against God is never only private.
- **"Why did you bring trouble on us? The LORD brings trouble on you today" (v25).** The verb is *'akar*, "to trouble," which gives the valley its name, Achor, "trouble." Joshua had warned that whoever took the devoted things would "bring trouble" on Israel (6:18). Achan's name, Achar in Chronicles, becomes a byword.
- **"A great heap of stones that remains to this day" (v26).** A heap of stones memorialized the Jordan crossing (4:20–24). Now another heap memorializes sin. Israel was to remember both God's mighty acts and the cost of disobedience.
- **"Then the LORD turned from his burning anger" (v26).** With the sin removed, God's anger turns away and the way forward opens again.
- **The Valley of Achor later.** The story does not end in judgment. Through Hosea, God promises that he will take his unfaithful people back into the wilderness and "make the Valley of Achor a door of hope" (Hos 2:15). Isaiah says it will become a resting place for herds (Isa 65:10). The valley of trouble becomes a door of hope.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that through Achan "guilt was brought upon the whole society of which he was a member," and "many a careful tradesman has been broken by a careless partner." On the confession: "He saw these fine things, as Eve saw the forbidden fruit," and "it was not the looking, but the lusting that ruined him." Henry thinks the children did die with Achan and suggests they "were aiders and abettors in the villany." JFB agrees that Achan "could scarcely have dug a hole within his tent without his family being aware of it." Guzik reads the text the other way, arguing it more likely means only Achan was stoned. JFB reads Joshua's prayer as the cry of "a deeply humbled and afflicted mind," not irreverence. The Tyndale notes are blunter, calling it an angry prayer and adding that Joshua and the elders had also failed to ask God before attacking Ai. Guzik observes that Achan confessed only after he was caught, which is not the same as repenting.

#### Pointing to Christ
Achan took what belonged to God and hid it, and the whole people suffered for one man's sin. The New Testament has its own Achan story in Ananias and Sapphira, who held back what they claimed to give to God (Acts 5:1–11). But the gospel turns the pattern around. Paul writes that as one man's disobedience brought condemnation on many, one man's obedience brings righteousness to many (Rom 5:18–19). Jesus is the one Israelite who took nothing that was not his, yet he bore the curse that sin deserves, outside the camp (Gal 3:13; Heb 13:12). Because he did, the Valley of Achor can become, as Hosea promised, a door of hope.
