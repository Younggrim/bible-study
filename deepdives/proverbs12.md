*Setting:* The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 12 keeps the two-line contrasts between the righteous and the wicked, and two threads run through it: honest work (the farmer, the sluggard, the hunter) and the power of words (lies, rash words, a good word, honest testimony). Nearly half the verses are about speech. The chapter ends with one of the most striking lines in the book, a path of righteousness in which "there is no death."

#### Discipline, a Good Wife and Deep Roots (vv1–7)
- **"Whoever loves discipline loves knowledge, but he who hates reproof is stupid" (v1).** "Discipline" (*musar*) is training, including correction. "Stupid" (*baʿar*) is a blunt word, related to the word for cattle: brutish, like an animal that only feels the stick and never learns (Ps 73:22). JFB: "brutish," that is, careless of one's own welfare. Guzik adds that being offended by correction reveals a lack of grace. The difference between wise and foolish people shows less in how much they know than in how they take correction.
- **"A good man obtains favor from the LORD, but a man of evil devices he condemns" (v2).** "Evil devices" (*mezimmot*) are schemes, plans. Guzik notes that God judges intentions even if they never become actions.
- **"No one is established by wickedness, but the root of the righteous will never be moved" (v3).** Wickedness can bring a quick rise, but it cannot give a person roots. The righteous are like a tree planted by water (Ps 1:3; Jer 17:8), which JFB cites. Storms come to both, but only one is still standing (10:25).
- **"An excellent wife is the crown of her husband" (v4).** "Excellent wife" is *ʾeshet chayil*, a woman of strength, worth and ability, the phrase used of Ruth (Ruth 3:11) and of the wife of noble character in 31:10. The Tyndale notes observe that the crown elsewhere in Proverbs stands for wealth, gray hair and grandchildren (14:24; 16:31; 17:6). She is his honor.
- **"But she who brings shame is like rottenness in his bones" (v4).** "Rottenness" is decay eating from the inside out, the same word as envy in 14:30. JFB calls it "an incurable evil." Bones were the core of a person's strength. Shameful conduct in a marriage weakens a man from the inside, where no one sees it.
- **"The thoughts of the righteous are just; the counsels of the wicked are deceitful" (v5).** It starts in the mind. JFB: "are right" is literally "are judgment," fair decisions. Guzik notes the righteous think rightly even in private.
- **"The words of the wicked lie in wait for blood, but the mouth of the upright delivers them" (v6).** The gang of 1:11 said, "Let us lie in wait for blood." Words can be weapons, setting traps through false testimony or manipulation. The upright use words to rescue people instead.
- **"The wicked are overthrown and are no more, but the house of the righteous will stand" (v7).** "Overthrown" is the word used for Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 19:25). "House" means the household and family line. The Tyndale notes: the wicked may succeed briefly, but the righteous family lasts for generations.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read v1 as a test of character: how a person takes correction shows what they love. JFB adds that the wife of v4 is "virtuous" in the broad sense, disposed to every moral duty. Guzik notes that a wife who brings shame drains her husband's strength.

#### Work and Its Fruit (vv8–14)
- **"A man is commended according to his good sense, but one of twisted mind is despised" (v8).** "Good sense" (*sekel*) is insight, prudence. The Tyndale notes define it as thinking about consequences before acting. A "twisted mind" acts without thinking.
- **"Better to be lowly and have a servant than to play the great man and lack bread" (v9).** This is the first "better than" saying in the collection, a form that will become common (15:16–17; 16:8, 19; 17:1). Having a servant implies modest but real means. "Play the great man" means to act important, to put on airs. The Tyndale notes: what is real matters more than appearances. Better a plain life that pays its bills than a show of status with an empty pantry.
- **"Whoever is righteous has regard for the life of his beast" (v10).** "Has regard" is literally "knows" (JFB), knowing what the animal needs. God's law cared for animals: rest on the Sabbath (Exod 20:10; 23:12), no muzzling the ox as it threshed (Deut 25:4), help for a fallen donkey even if it belonged to an enemy (Exod 23:4–5). God himself cares for "much cattle" in Nineveh (Jonah 4:11). Henry points to the law's care for oxen and to Balaam, who "was checked for beating his ass" (Num 22:28–32). A person's character shows in how they treat creatures that cannot pay them back.
- **"But the mercy of the wicked is cruel" (v10).** Even the wicked man's kindness has a hard edge. JFB says his compassion is given ungraciously. Guzik says it is self-serving. JFB's summary is sharp: "The righteous more regards a beast than the wicked a man."
- **"Whoever works his land will have plenty of bread, but he who follows worthless pursuits lacks sense" (v11).** "Worthless pursuits" are empty things, get-rich-quick schemes, idle company. The saying is repeated in 28:19 with a sharper ending: "will have plenty of poverty." Faithful, ordinary work feeds a family.
- **"Whoever is wicked covets the spoil of evildoers, but the root of the righteous bears fruit" (v12).** The Hebrew of the first line is difficult. Literally it may be "the wicked desires the net (or stronghold) of evil men." The ESV takes it as wanting what crooks have taken. The contrast is clear: the wicked want to grab what someone else caught, while the righteous have roots that produce their own fruit. JFB allows that the second line may mean God makes the root firm.
- **"An evil man is ensnared by the transgression of his lips" (v13).** Liars trip over their own stories. JFB: liars and flatterers fall by their own words. The righteous, with nothing to cover up, get out of trouble.
- **"From the fruit of his mouth a man is satisfied with good, and the work of a man's hand comes back to him" (v14).** Words and work both bear fruit, and both come back to their owner. Paul: "whatever one sows, that will he also reap" (Gal 6:7). Words are as real an investment as labor.
- **Commentators:** Henry uses v10 to show that a good man's mercy reaches even his animals, drawing on the law and Balaam. JFB on v9 reads "honoureth himself" as being self-conceited. The Tyndale notes on v12 explain that thieves are unstable and can turn violent at any moment, while the righteous are rooted.

#### Truthful Lips and Rash Words (vv15–23)
- **"The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to advice" (v15).** The fool never thinks he is the fool. The Tyndale notes: fools don't grasp what the wise know, that listening is how one learns. Judges ends with the nation's whole problem in these words: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 21:25).
- **"The vexation of a fool is known at once, but the prudent ignores an insult" (v16).** "Known at once" is literally "on the same day," immediately. The fool flares up on the spot. "Ignores an insult" is literally "covers shame," the same verb as love covering offenses in 10:12. The prudent person absorbs the insult and does not hit back. JFB: he "is slow to denounce his insulters." The Tyndale notes add that staying calm keeps the mind clear. Compare 19:11: "it is his glory to overlook an offense."
- **"Whoever speaks the truth gives honest evidence, but a false witness utters deceit" (v17).** This is the language of the law court, the ninth commandment in practice (Exod 20:16). Israel's courts depended on witnesses (Deut 19:15–19). The Tyndale notes connect 14:5. JFB has no note on this verse.
- **"There is one whose rash words are like sword thrusts, but the tongue of the wise brings healing" (v18).** "Rash words" (*boteh*) means speaking thoughtlessly, blurting out, the word used of rash vows (Lev 5:4; Num 30:6). Careless words cut like stabbing wounds. JFB: like an angry man who retorts harshly. The wise tongue is "healing" (*marpeʾ*), medicine. JFB: "by soothing and gentle language." The same word appears in 15:4: "A gentle tongue is a tree of life."
- **"Truthful lips endure forever, but a lying tongue is but for a moment" (v19).** "For a moment" is literally "while I wink," the blink of an eye. JFB: lies are soon exposed. Guzik: lies may seem to win for a time, but measured against eternity they last a moment.
- **"Deceit is in the heart of those who devise evil, but those who plan peace have joy" (v20).** Both groups make plans, but the planners of peace get the joy. JFB connects Jesus' "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matt 5:9).
- **"No ill befalls the righteous, but the wicked are filled with trouble" (v21).** Read alone, this sounds untrue. The righteous suffer many things (Ps 34:19). JFB adds "within God's wise limits," and Guzik says no devastating disaster finally overtakes them. "Ill" (*ʾawen*) often means trouble that comes from wrongdoing. The righteous are not spared hardship, but they are spared the ruin the wicked bring on themselves. Romans 8:28 is the New Testament depth of it.
- **"Lying lips are an abomination to the LORD, but those who act faithfully are his delight" (v22).** The same pairing as 11:1 and 11:20. "Act faithfully" (*ʾemunah*) means acting with firmness and reliability, the word behind "amen." JFB: acting "according to promises." God hates lying because he is the God of truth (Num 23:19; Titus 1:2), and "a lying tongue" is on the list of things he hates (6:17).
- **"A prudent man conceals knowledge, but the heart of fools proclaims folly" (v23).** This is not hiding truth to deceive. It is knowing when to speak and how much. The prudent do not show off what they know. Fools cannot wait to tell everyone what they think, and what they announce is folly. JFB: concealment from modesty.
- **Commentators:** JFB and Guzik agree that v16 is about self-control under insult. Guzik says the prudent person covers the offense with wisdom. On v21 both commentators add the qualification the verse needs, that God sets limits but does not promise an easy life. Guzik reads v19 against eternity.

#### Diligence, Anxiety and the Path of Life (vv24–28)
- **"The hand of the diligent will rule, while the slothful will be put to forced labor" (v24).** "Forced labor" (*mas*) is the word for the labor gangs Solomon conscripted to build the temple and his cities (1 Kgs 5:13; 9:21). Diligence leads to leadership. Laziness ends with others making the decisions. JFB reads it as dependence, not legal taxation.
- **"Anxiety in a man's heart weighs him down, but a good word makes him glad" (v25).** "Anxiety" (*deʾagah*) is worry, the restless fear of what might happen. "Weighs him down" is literally "makes it bow." Worry pushes the heart down. A "good word" lifts it. JFB: "one of comfort." Guzik makes this a key verse: an encouraging word costs almost nothing and can give a great deal, and Jesus spoke such words constantly ("Take heart," Matt 9:2; 14:27). Peter gives the deeper answer: "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Pet 5:7).
- **"One who is righteous is a guide to his neighbor, but the way of the wicked leads them astray" (v26).** The first line is hard Hebrew. It may mean the righteous "searches out" (or "guides") his neighbor, or chooses his friends carefully, or, by a small change, "is spared from harm." JFB gives two readings, that the righteous are more successful or that they guide their friends. Guzik reads it as a call to choose friends carefully. Whatever the exact wording, the contrast is plain: the righteous lead others well, and the wicked lead them astray.
- **"Whoever is slothful will not roast his game" (v27).** "Roast" (*charak*) appears only here, and its meaning is uncertain. It may mean to roast, or to start a hunt, or to catch. The picture is a lazy hunter who either never goes out or catches something and can't be bothered to cook it. The sluggard does not finish what he starts (19:24). Guzik: he wastes what is already in his hands. "Precious wealth" is diligence itself, as JFB allows.
- **"In the path of righteousness is life, and in its pathway there is no death" (v28).** The chapter ends on a high note. "No death" in Hebrew is *ʾal-mawet*, using a particle normally found with commands and wishes. Some translations, following the Greek and a slight change of vowels, read "but the way of error leads to death" (*ʾel-mawet*, "to death"), a normal two-way contrast. The ESV and many others keep the Hebrew as it stands, which makes v28 one of the clearest Old Testament hints that the righteous path leads beyond death (compare 14:32; Ps 16:10–11; 49:15). Guzik reads it as life now and forever. JFB calls it a common principle stated positively, then negatively.
- **Commentators:** Guzik singles out v25 for its practical weight. JFB and Guzik differ on v26, JFB offering two readings and Guzik reading a warning about friends, which matches the uncertain Hebrew. On v28 Guzik is willing to see eternal life in the verse, while JFB stays general.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 12 pairs rash words that wound with a tongue that heals, and it ends with a path where "there is no death." Jesus is that healing word. He spoke the good word that lifts anxious hearts ("Let not your hearts be troubled," John 14:1), and he was never caught in "the transgression of his lips" (1 Pet 2:22). When he was insulted, he did not insult in return (1 Pet 2:23), covering shame rather than returning it (v16). The righteous man who "has regard for the life of his beast" points to the Good Shepherd who knows his sheep by name (John 10:3, 11). And the "path of righteousness" where there is no death is finally opened by him: "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), and "everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die" (John 11:26).
