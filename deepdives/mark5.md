*Setting:* The Sea of Galilee, around AD 28. At the end of chapter 4 Jesus calmed a storm with a word, and the terrified disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (4:41). Chapter 5 continues the answer with three more mighty acts. On the eastern shore, in Gentile country, he frees a man held by a legion of demons. Back on the western shore, he heals a woman who had been bleeding for twelve years and raises a twelve-year-old girl from death. Together with the storm, these show Jesus' authority over nature, demons, disease and death. They also show three very different kinds of people coming to him: a wild man from the tombs, a respected synagogue leader and a woman who had been shut out for years.

#### Jesus Heals a Man with a Demon (vv1–20)
- **"The country of the Gerasenes" (v1).** The manuscripts differ here. Mark's best manuscripts read "Gerasenes," Matthew has "Gadarenes" (Matt 8:28), and some copies read "Gergesenes." Gerasa (modern Jerash) was a large city over thirty miles from the lake, and Gadara about six miles away. Both may simply name the wider region, as people still speak of a big city's countryside. Many think the event happened near Kursi on the eastern shore, possibly the Gergesa of some manuscripts, where steep hills come down close to the water and a large Byzantine church and monastery later marked the spot. This was the Decapolis (v20), a league of Greek-speaking cities. It was Gentile land, as the pigs make plain.
- **"A man with an unclean spirit" who "lived among the tombs" (vv2–3).** Tombs were often caves cut into hillsides, which could serve as shelters. To a Jew, contact with the dead made a person unclean (Num 19:11). Here is a man unclean in every way: an unclean spirit, living among the dead, near a herd of unclean animals, in Gentile territory. Isaiah describes a rebellious people "who sit in tombs… who eat pig's flesh" (Isa 65:3–4). Matthew mentions two men (Matt 8:28). Mark and Luke focus on the one who spoke, likely the one the eyewitnesses remembered best.
- **"No one could bind him anymore, not even with a chain" (vv3–4).** Mark piles up the words: bind, chain, shackles, wrenched apart, broke in pieces, no one strong enough. People had tried to control him and failed. "No one had the strength" is the same word family as the "strong man" Jesus spoke of in 3:27. The strong man's captive is about to be set free by the one who is stronger.
- **"Always crying out and cutting himself with stones" (v5).** Evil seeks not only to control but to destroy. The man was harming the very image of God in himself.
- **"He ran and fell down before him" (v6).** Something in him runs toward Jesus. The word for "fell down" can mean to worship or bow down. Whether this is the man's own longing for help or the demons forced to bow, he ends up at Jesus' feet.
- **"Son of the Most High God" (v7).** "Most High" was a title for God often used by or among Gentiles (Gen 14:18–20; Dan 3:26). The demon knows exactly who Jesus is. **"I adjure you by God, do not torment me."** A demon tries to put Jesus under oath, using the language of an exorcist against the exorcist. Matthew adds, "Have you come here to torment us before the time?" (Matt 8:29). Demons know that a day of judgment is coming.
- **"What is your name?" "My name is Legion, for we are many" (v9).** A Roman legion was a military unit of about five or six thousand soldiers, though Jesus' hearers would have used the word loosely for a huge number. The word also carried the sting of foreign occupation. The legion stationed in the region later, the Tenth (*Legio X Fretensis*), had a wild boar among its emblems, and some scholars see a pointed irony in a "Legion" ending up in a herd of pigs, though that link is not certain. Jesus' question is not a magic technique for gaining power over the demon. It brings the full extent of the man's bondage into the open.
- **"Begged him earnestly not to send them out of the country" (v10).** Luke says they begged not to be sent "into the abyss" (Luke 8:31). The demons are not bargaining from strength. They are begging.
- **"A great herd of pigs" (v11).** Pigs were unclean to Jews (Lev 11:7), so this was a Gentile herd, or Jews breaking the law. "About two thousand" (v13), a detail only Mark gives, was a large and valuable herd.
- **"So he gave them permission… and drowned in the sea" (v13).** Why did Jesus allow it? The text does not say directly, and readers have offered several reasons. It showed the man, and everyone, that the demons really had left him. It revealed what the demons are: destroyers, who wreck whatever they enter. It showed that one human life is worth more than a herd of animals. And the final judgment of demons was not yet; their full defeat waits for the cross and the end (Col 2:15; Rev 20:10). The sea, a picture of chaos in the Old Testament, swallows the destroyers.
- **"Sitting there, clothed and in his right mind" (v15).** Three words to describe what Jesus has done. Luke says he was "sitting at the feet of Jesus" (Luke 8:35), the posture of a disciple. He had been naked (Luke 8:27); now he is clothed. He had been out of control; now he is "in his right mind" (*sōphroneō*, sound-minded, self-controlled).
- **"And they were afraid" (v15).** Not of the wild man, but of the one who had set him free. **"They began to beg Jesus to depart from their region" (v17).** The demons begged to stay. The people begged Jesus to leave. They valued the pigs and their peace more than what Jesus had done. Jesus does not stay where he is not wanted. He leaves.
- **"Begged him that he might be with him" (v18).** The healed man wants what the twelve were called to: "to be with him" (3:14). It is the right desire.
- **"Go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you" (v19).** Jesus refuses, kindly. The man has a mission where everyone knew him and had feared him. Elsewhere in Mark Jesus tells people to keep quiet (1:44; 5:43). Here, in Gentile territory where people were not looking for a political Messiah, he tells the man to speak. Note the words: "how much *the Lord* has done." In v20 the man tells "how much *Jesus* had done." Mark lets the two stand side by side.
- **"He… began to proclaim in the Decapolis" (v20).** The Decapolis ("ten cities") stretched across the region east and south of the lake. The man becomes the first missionary to the Gentiles in Mark's Gospel. When Jesus returns to the Decapolis later, crowds come to him (7:31–37; 8:1–10), perhaps because of this man's witness.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads "Legion" as a picture of demons who are many, united and "war against God and his glory." He notes the people "wished him further off" because they loved their swine more than their Savior. Guzik suggests the villagers first bound the man and then drove him out, and says Jesus allowed the pigs' destruction because the final defeat of demons was reserved for the cross. He also observes that people sometimes prefer their familiar troubles to Jesus' presence. JFB defends Jesus' permission: if the owners were Jews "they drove an illegal trade; if heathens, they insulted the national religion: in either case the permission was just." That is one view; Mark himself makes no point of the owners. JFB says witnessing at home, where the man "was so well known and so long dreaded," was "a far nobler calling" than going with Jesus.

#### Jesus Heals a Woman and Jairus's Daughter (vv21–43)
- **Two stories, one sandwich.** Mark begins the story of Jairus, interrupts it with the story of the woman, and then finishes it. He often does this (3:20–35; 11:12–25; 14:1–11). The two stories light up each other. Both involve a "daughter" (vv23, 34, 35). Both involve "twelve years" (vv25, 42): the girl had lived as long as the woman had suffered. Both involve uncleanness under the law, blood and a corpse, and Jesus touches both. And both turn on faith in the face of fear.
- **"One of the rulers of the synagogue, Jairus by name" (v22).** A synagogue ruler was a respected layman who organized the services and chose who would read and preach. In Mark's story so far, synagogues have been places of conflict (3:1–6), and the religious leaders have been plotting against Jesus. Jairus is a leader who comes to Jesus anyway. **"He fell at his feet"**, an act of public humility for a man of his standing.
- **"My little daughter is at the point of death… that she may be made well and live" (v23).** "Little daughter" is a tender word. "Made well" is *sōzō*, the usual word for "save." In Mark it covers both rescue of the body and salvation of the whole person. Jairus believes Jesus' touch can heal. **"And he went with him" (v24).**
- **"A woman who had had a discharge of blood for twelve years" (v25).** Probably a constant menstrual-type bleeding. Under the law of Leviticus 15:25–27, such a woman was ceremonially unclean the entire time, and anyone or anything she touched became unclean too. For twelve years she had been unable to worship at the temple, and likely kept at a distance from family and friends. She was also probably weak and anemic.
- **"Suffered much under many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was no better but rather grew worse" (v26).** The Talmud later lists remedies for this condition, from drinking wine mixed with rubber-tree gum to carrying the ash of an ostrich egg. Luke, a physician, gently softens Mark's description: she "could not be healed by anyone" (Luke 8:43). Mark lets the doctors take the blame.
- **"Came up behind him in the crowd and touched his garment" (v27).** She should not have been in the crowd at all, since touching people made them unclean. So she comes from behind, secretly. Matthew and Luke say she touched "the fringe" of his garment (Matt 9:20), likely one of the tassels the law told every Israelite man to wear on the corners of his cloak (Num 15:38–40). Some connected Malachi 4:2, "the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings," with the corners ("wings") of the garment.
- **"If I touch even his garments, I will be made well" (v28).** Her faith has a touch of superstition in it, and it is directed at Jesus. Jesus honors faith that is real even when it is imperfect.
- **"Immediately the flow of blood dried up, and she felt in her body that she was healed" (v29).** What twelve years of doctors could not do happens at once. Mark uses the verb "healed" in a perfect tense: she is healed, and stays healed.
- **"Jesus, perceiving in himself that power had gone out from him" (v30).** "Power" is *dynamis*. Healing was not automatic or impersonal. It came from him, and he knew it. **"Who touched my garments?"** He knows. He asks so that she will come forward.
- **"You see the crowd pressing around you, and yet you say, 'Who touched me?'" (v31).** The disciples' question is close to mockery. Many people bumped into Jesus. Only one touched him in faith.
- **"He looked around to see who had done it" (v32).** Mark often notes Jesus' looking (3:5, 34). The woman expected to slip away unnoticed. Jesus will not let her be healed in body and still be left alone in her shame.
- **"Came in fear and trembling and fell down before him and told him the whole truth" (v33).** She has made Jesus unclean, by the rules, in front of everyone. She may expect a rebuke. Instead:
- **"Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your disease" (v34).** She is the only person the Gospels record Jesus addressing as "daughter." A woman who has been an outsider for twelve years is publicly welcomed into the family of God (3:35). "Made you well" is again *sōzō*, "saved you." "Go in peace" (*shalom*) is wholeness, not just calm. Jesus' public words restore her to her community: everyone now knows she is clean. The delay that seemed so costly to Jairus brought her far more than healing.
- **"Your daughter is dead. Why trouble the Teacher any further?" (v35).** While Jesus stopped for the woman, Jairus's daughter died. The messengers think it is over. Death is the one thing no teacher can fix.
- **"Do not fear, only believe" (v36).** "Overhearing" can also mean "ignoring." Jesus refuses to accept the messengers' verdict. "Believe" is a present-tense command: keep on believing. The woman has just shown what that looks like.
- **"Peter and James and John" (v37).** The first time Jesus takes only these three. They will be with him again on the mountain of transfiguration (9:2) and in Gethsemane (14:33), moments of glory and of agony.
- **"People weeping and wailing loudly" (v38).** Mourning began right away in that culture, and burial was usually the same day. Even a poor family was expected to hire at least two flute players and a professional wailing woman for a funeral. A synagogue ruler's household would have had more (Matt 9:23).
- **"The child is not dead but sleeping" (v39).** Jesus does not mean she is in a coma. The mourners know she is dead, which is why they laugh. He means that her death, in his presence, is like sleep: something from which she will soon wake. The New Testament often calls the death of believers "sleep" (John 11:11–14; 1 Thess 4:13–14; 1 Cor 15:20), not because the soul sleeps, but because death is not the end.
- **"And they laughed at him" (v40).** The verb is imperfect: they kept laughing. **"He put them all outside."** The same word used for casting out demons (*ekballō*). Jesus clears the room of unbelief. He takes in only the parents and his three disciples.
- **"Taking her by the hand" (v41).** Touching a dead body made a person unclean for seven days (Num 19:11). Once again Jesus' touch goes the other way: life flows into what is dead.
- **"Talitha cumi" (v41).** Aramaic, the everyday language Jesus spoke. Mark keeps the actual words, as he does in several places (7:34; 14:36; 15:34), perhaps because Peter could never forget them. *Talitha* is a tender word for a little girl, like "little lamb." *Cumi* (some manuscripts read *koum*) means "arise." Mark translates and adds "I say to you" to bring out the force. These are not magic words. They are a father's kind of words to a sleeping child, spoken by the Lord of life.
- **"Immediately the girl got up and began walking (for she was twelve years of age)" (v42).** She is not weak or bedridden. She is up and walking. **"Overcome with amazement"** is literally "amazed with great amazement," the strongest wording Mark can find.
- **"He strictly charged them that no one should know this" (v43).** With a crowd of mourners outside who knew she had died, the news could not stay hidden forever. But Jesus did not want to be known chiefly as a wonder-worker, and he did not want popular excitement to force events before the time.
- **"And told them to give her something to eat" (v43).** A beautiful, practical ending. The Lord who has just defeated death remembers that a twelve-year-old girl who has been ill is hungry. It is also proof that she is truly alive, not a ghost (compare Luke 24:41–43).
- **Commentators:** Henry stresses that Christ knew her hidden faith and drew her out because it was right for her to confess openly what he had done. On Jesus' word to Jairus: "Faith is the only remedy against disquieting grief and fear at such a time." He notes that Christ, having raised the girl "by a word of power," still "took care something should be given her to eat." JFB says that among the crowd only "the voluntary, living contact of faith" drew power from Christ. It calls "Daughter… go in peace" the royal seal on her cure, calls "Be not afraid, only believe" words of "unchanging preciousness and power," and reads the mourners' laughter as "an important testimony" that she was really dead. JFB has no comment on v25 itself. Guzik lists reasons Jesus called the woman out: so she would know she was healed, so others would know she was clean, so she would know it came through faith in him, so Jairus would be encouraged, and so he could call her "Daughter." He follows Robertson that the imperfect verb in v40 means the mourners kept mocking.

#### Pointing to Christ
In this chapter Jesus goes where no respectable teacher would go: to a graveyard in Gentile country, into contact with a bleeding woman, into a room with a corpse. Each place was unclean, and each person was beyond human help. Chains could not hold the demoniac, doctors could not heal the woman, and mourners could only weep for the girl. Jesus' holiness does not shrink from uncleanness. It overcomes it. The demons are cast out, the bleeding stops, and the dead girl wakes. These are previews of what he came to do for all his people: to take on himself the uncleanness and death that belonged to them, and to speak the word over them that one day will raise them from the dust: "I say to you, arise."
