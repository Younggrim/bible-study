*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Chapter 17 began the part of Leviticus many scholars call the "Holiness Code" (chs 17–26), which moves from the sanctuary into everyday life. Chapter 18 turns to the family and sexual conduct. Chapters 18 and 20 cover much the same ground: chapter 18 gives the commands and chapter 20 the penalties, with chapter 19's call to everyday holiness between them. Israel is standing between two cultures, the Egypt they have left and the Canaan they are about to enter, and God tells them to be like neither.

#### Unlawful Sexual Relations (vv1–30)
- **"I am the LORD your God" (v2).** The chapter opens and closes with these words (vv2, 30), and "I am the LORD" punctuates it (vv4, 5, 6, 21). Ancient treaties began by naming the great king who made them, and this phrase does the same: it calls up the covenant of Exodus 20:2, where God introduced himself as the one "who brought you out of the land of Egypt." These laws come from the God who rescued Israel, not from a stranger.
- **"You shall not do as they do in the land of Egypt… or… Canaan" (v3).** Egypt was known for royal brother-sister marriage, and some of the practices in this chapter appear in Canaanite religion and myth. **"You shall not walk in their statutes."** Every culture has its own "statutes," its accepted ways. Israel's were to come from God.
- **"If a person does them, he shall live by them" (v5).** In the setting of Leviticus, this is a promise. God's ways are the path of life and blessing in the land (Deut 30:15–20; Ezek 20:11). Paul quotes this verse twice (Rom 10:5; Gal 3:12) to describe the way of the law, in contrast to righteousness by faith. His point is not that the law was bad, but that no sinner has kept it perfectly enough to win life by it. Jesus said much the same to the lawyer who summed up the law: "do this, and you will live" (Luke 10:28), and then told the story of the good Samaritan to show how far short we fall.
- **"None of you shall approach any one of his close relatives to uncover nakedness" (v6).** "Uncover nakedness" is a Hebrew idiom for sexual relations. "Close relatives" is literally "flesh of his flesh." The verse states the principle, and vv7–18 list the cases. The commands are addressed to men, who held more power in the household and were more often the ones pressing such relationships, but chapter 20 makes clear that women were equally responsible.
- **Parents, stepmother, sisters and grandchildren (vv7–11).** A man may not have relations with his mother, with **"your father's wife"** (a stepmother), with a full or half sister, or with a granddaughter. Several reasons are given in the text itself: **"it is your father's nakedness"** (v8), **"their nakedness is your own nakedness"** (v10). Husband and wife are "one flesh" (Gen 2:24), so to violate one is to dishonor the other. Reuben lost his birthright for sleeping with his father's concubine (Gen 35:22; 49:4), and Paul was appalled that the Corinthian church tolerated "a man… with his father's wife" (1 Cor 5:1).
- **Aunts, daughter-in-law and sister-in-law (vv12–16).** The list widens to relatives by marriage. Relations with **"your brother's wife"** (v16) are forbidden, with one exception given later: if a brother died childless, his brother was to marry the widow to raise up an heir (Deut 25:5–10). John the Baptist condemned Herod Antipas for marrying his brother Philip's wife while Philip was alive, citing this very law (Mark 6:17–18).
- **Before the law was given.** Some patriarchs did things this chapter forbids. Abraham married his half-sister Sarah (Gen 20:12). Jacob married two sisters (Gen 29). Moses' own parents were nephew and aunt (Exod 6:20). The text does not hide this. These rules were new standards for a holy nation, and the stories of Genesis, especially Jacob's household of rival wives, show the pain such relationships could cause.
- **"A woman and… her daughter" (v17).** Marrying a woman and her daughter or granddaughter is called **"depravity"** (*zimmah*), a strong word for shameful, planned wickedness.
- **"A rival wife to her sister… while her sister is still alive" (v18).** The word "rival" recalls Rachel and Leah's bitter competition (Gen 30:1–8) and Hannah's "rival" Peninnah (1 Sam 1:6). Readers have debated whether this verse forbids polygamy altogether or only marrying two sisters. JFB thinks it refers to a sister during the wife's lifetime. Either way, the Bible consistently shows that polygamy brings rivalry and grief, and Jesus points back to the beginning, where one man and one woman become one flesh (Matt 19:4–6).
- **Why these laws?** The Tyndale notes stress that the family was meant to be a safe place, and sexual relationships inside it destroy trust, create rivalry and confuse the roles of parent, child and sibling. Many readers also note the real genetic danger of close-relative unions, though the text gives the reason in terms of honor and relationship, not health.
- **During menstruation and with a neighbor's wife (vv19–20).** Verse 19 turns the ritual rule of 15:24 into a firm prohibition. Verse 20 forbids adultery, the seventh commandment (Exod 20:14), which was a capital crime in Israel (20:10).
- **"You shall not give any of your children to offer them to Molech" (v21).** Molech was a god linked to the Ammonites (1 Kings 11:7). His worship involved making children "pass through the fire," most scholars think as burnt sacrifices. Kings Ahaz and Manasseh did this in the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6), and Josiah later desecrated the site (2 Kings 23:10). That valley, *Ge-Hinnom*, gave its name to "Gehenna," Jesus' word for hell. Child sacrifice appears in a chapter on sexual sins perhaps because it was tied to fertility religion, and because both destroy the family. It **"profane[s] the name of your God,"** dragging his reputation into the mud before the nations.
- **"You shall not lie with a male as with a woman; it is an abomination" (v22).** "Abomination" is *to'evah*, a strong word for something detestable. BDB shows it has a broad range: Egyptians found eating with Hebrews a *to'evah* (Gen 43:32), but it is also used of idolatry, child sacrifice, dishonest scales and lying lips (Deut 12:31; Prov 11:1; 12:22). In this chapter it is clearly moral, applied to all the practices listed (vv26–30). The Christian church has historically read this verse as forbidding all same-sex intercourse, and the New Testament repeats that teaching (Rom 1:26–27; 1 Cor 6:9–10; 1 Tim 1:10). Some recent interpreters argue it addressed only exploitative or cultic acts, but most scholars and most Christian traditions read it as a general command. The New Testament sets it alongside many other sins, including greed, drunkenness and slander, and right after it says, "such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified" (1 Cor 6:11). No sin listed here is beyond God's forgiveness.
- **"It is perversion" (v23).** Bestiality is forbidden for men and women alike. "Perversion" (*tevel*) means confusion, mixing what God made separate (Gen 1:24–27).
- **"The land vomited out its inhabitants" (vv24–25).** A vivid picture: the land is like a body that cannot keep down what poisons it. The Canaanites were not driven out because Israel was better (Deut 9:4–5), but because their sins had reached their full measure, as God told Abraham centuries earlier: "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Gen 15:16).
- **"Lest the land vomit you out" (vv26–28).** The same standard applied to Israel and to **"the stranger who sojourns among you."** God has no favorites. When Israel later did these things, the land did vomit them out, the north to Assyria in 722 BC (2 Kings 17:6–23) and Judah to Babylon in 586 BC.
- **"Cut off from among their people" (v29).** Chapter 20 sets out the specific penalties. **"Keep my charge… I am the LORD your God"** (v30). The chapter ends where it began.
- **Ceremonial or moral?** Christians do not keep the food laws of chapter 11, so why keep this chapter? The difference is in the text. Uncleanness in chapters 11–15 was usually temporary and washed away by evening. These acts are called "abominations" that defile the land itself and brought judgment even on nations who never received the law of Moses (vv24–25). That marks them as moral commands for all people. The New Testament repeats them (1 Cor 5:1; 6:9–11; Heb 13:4), while it explicitly sets aside the food laws (Mark 7:19).
- **Commentators:** Henry grounds the whole chapter in "I am the Lord your God," and says keeping Egypt's ways after God rescued them from Egypt would be both absurd and ungrateful. On v5 he notes that under the gospel, life comes not by our own perfect obedience but by faith in Christ. On vv24–30 he warns that "the way of sin is downhill," and that once one evil is allowed, many more follow. JFB calls the incest laws wise and notes that they became the basis of marriage law in much of Christendom. It reads v5 as a promise of blessing in the land with a higher spiritual meaning (Luke 10:28; Rom 10:5). Guzik stresses that Christians too easily take their sexual standards from the surrounding culture rather than from God, and that God's boundaries are for human good, not arbitrary limits. He compares the sacrifice of unwanted children to Molech with modern abortion.

#### Pointing to Christ
"If a person does them, he shall live by them." No one but Jesus has ever done them fully. Paul quotes this verse to show that life by law-keeping is out of reach, and then points to the one who kept the law and "redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:12–13). God's design for marriage and family in this chapter points beyond itself to Christ and the church, the "profound mystery" of one flesh (Eph 5:31–32). To people from every background this chapter describes, the gospel offers what Leviticus could not: washing, a new heart, and a place in God's family. "Such were some of you. But you were washed."
