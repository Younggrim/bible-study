*Setting:* Judah, 640–622 BC. Manasseh ruled fifty-five years, filling the land with idols and the city with innocent blood, and repented only late in life (ch 33). His son Amon returned to the idols and was murdered after two years. "The people of the land" executed the assassins and crowned Amon's eight-year-old son Josiah (33:25). Assyria, which had dominated Judah for a century, was weakening after Ashurbanipal's death around 627 BC, and Babylon was rising. Jeremiah was called to prophesy in Josiah's thirteenth year (Jer 1:2), and Zephaniah also preached in his reign (Zeph 1:1). 2 Kings 22–23 tells the same story. Chronicles adds the early stages of Josiah's faith and tells the events in a different order: Kings puts the whole purge after the book was found, while Chronicles puts most of it before.

#### Josiah Reigns in Judah (vv1–7)
- **"Eight years old… thirty-one years" (v1).** About 640–609 BC. A boy king had to be guided by others, and some of the officials in this chapter, like Shaphan and his family, were probably among the good influences around him.
- **"He did not turn aside to the right hand or to the left" (v2).** No other king gets this phrase. It comes from Deuteronomy, where Moses tells Israel, and the future king, not to turn aside "to the right hand or to the left" from God's commands (Deut 5:32; 17:20). It was also God's charge to Joshua (Josh 1:7). Josiah is the king who fits Deuteronomy's description.
- **"In the eighth year of his reign, while he was yet a boy, he began to seek the God of David his father" (v3).** He was about sixteen. Kings does not mention this. "Seek" is the Chronicler's great word for real faith, a turning of the whole life toward God (7:14; 15:2). JFB notes that sixteen was three years past the age when a royal son was considered of age. He sought "the God of David," not the gods of his father and grandfather. He went back past two generations to find the faith of his ancestor.
- **"In the twelfth year he began to purge Judah and Jerusalem" (v3).** About age twenty, around 628 BC. Faith came first, and four years later came action. JFB says the abolition of idolatry "began in the twelfth and was completed in the eighteenth year."
- **"The high places, the Asherim, and the carved and the metal images" (v3).** Every kind of false worship his grandfather restored. "Carved" images were wood or stone; "metal" images were cast in molds.
- **"They chopped down the altars of the Baals in his presence" (v4).** The king did not just give orders. He was there watching it done. "The incense altars that stood above them" may have been small stands for burning incense to the sun.
- **"Made dust of them and scattered it over the graves of those who had sacrificed to them" (v4).** Graves were ritually unclean (Num 19:16). The idols were turned into defilement on the very graves of their worshipers. JFB says Josiah treated the graves "as guilty of the crimes of those who were lying in them." Moses did the same with the golden calf, grinding it to powder (Exod 32:20).
- **"He also burned the bones of the priests on their altars" (v5).** Three hundred years earlier an unnamed prophet stood at Jeroboam's altar in Bethel and announced: "a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name, and he shall sacrifice on you the priests of the high places… and human bones shall be burned on you" (1 Kings 13:2). 2 Kings 23:15–20 tells how that prophecy was fulfilled at Bethel itself. JFB says "a greater defilement could not have been done to the altars of idolatry."
- **"In the cities of Manasseh, Ephraim, and Simeon, and as far as Naphtali" (v6).** These were the old territories of the northern tribes, now an Assyrian province. Naphtali was far north, near the Sea of Galilee. With Assyria collapsing, no one stopped him, as the Tyndale notes say. Simeon's territory had been in the south, inside Judah's lands (Josh 19:1), but some Simeonites had apparently moved north and been counted with Israel (15:9).
- **"In their ruins all around" (v6).** The ESV footnotes that the meaning of the Hebrew is uncertain. JFB mentions the alternative "in their deserted suburbs." Either way, the north was still scarred by the Assyrian conquest a century earlier.
- **"Throughout all the land of Israel. Then he returned to Jerusalem" (v7).** Josiah treats the whole land of the twelve tribes as one, as Hezekiah had with his Passover (30:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls these "some glorious years, while good Josiah sits at the helm," a last season in which God tried Judah "yet once more" before its ruin. Guzik observes that the sheer number of idols shows how deep idolatry had gone, and argues from v5 that reform must deal with those who lead people astray, not only with the objects. JFB says Josiah's authority in the north probably rested on ties with the Israelite remnant, and Assyria was too weak to object.

#### The Book of the Law Found (vv8–21)
- **"In the eighteenth year of his reign, when he had cleansed the land and the house" (v8).** About 622 BC, when Josiah was twenty-six. Cleansing came first; now repair.
- **Shaphan, Maaseiah and Joah (v8).** Shaphan the secretary was the senior official whose family stood with God's word for forty years. His son Ahikam later saved Jeremiah from a mob (Jer 26:24), and his grandson Gedaliah became governor after the fall of Jerusalem (2 Kings 25:22). Maaseiah, "the governor of the city," appears only here. Joah the recorder was the royal herald or keeper of records.
- **"Collected from Manasseh and Ephraim and from all the remnant of Israel and from all Judah and Benjamin" (v9).** Chronicles adds that northerners gave too. The money came from all Israel, the Chronicler's favorite theme. The collection chest at the temple entrance went back to Joash's day (24:8–11).
- **"Buildings that the kings of Judah had let go to ruin" (v11).** Manasseh and Amon had neglected and abused the temple. This needed timber and quarried stone, real structural work.
- **"The men did the work faithfully" (v12).** The same honest workmen Kings praises (2 Kings 22:7).
- **"The Levites, all who were skillful with instruments of music, were over the burden-bearers" (vv12–13).** Musicians as construction foremen. The Tyndale notes say ancient texts sometimes mention music as part of building work, perhaps to keep the rhythm of labor. It also shows the Chronicler's delight in the Levites, who were trusted with the work in every way: overseers, scribes, officials and gatekeepers.
- **"Hilkiah the priest found the Book of the Law of the LORD given through Moses" (v14).** The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew: "by the hand of" Moses. Some older writers took this to mean Moses' own handwritten copy, which Guzik notes from Clarke, but the phrase normally means "through Moses," as the ESV has it. The Tyndale notes suggest the book was Deuteronomy, or at least centered on it, which is the most common view. Josiah's reforms follow Deuteronomy closely: one place of worship (Deut 12), Passover at the chosen place (Deut 16), and the covenant curses (Deut 28). Deuteronomy itself told the Levites to keep a copy "by the side of the ark" (Deut 31:26). It had probably been lost or hidden during Manasseh's reign.
- **A modern theory.** Many critical scholars since the 1800s have argued that Deuteronomy was actually written in Josiah's time and "found" to support his reform. The text presents it as an old book that had been lost, and that is how the Chronicler, Kings and the whole Jewish and Christian tradition have read it. Guzik notes Selman's discussion of this debate.
- **"Hilkiah gave the book to Shaphan. Shaphan brought the book to the king, and further reported" (vv15–16).** The order is telling. Shaphan first reports on the money and the workers (vv16–17), and only then mentions, almost as an afterthought, "Hilkiah the priest has given me a book" (v18). Nobody yet knew what it was worth.
- **"When the king heard the words of the Law, he tore his clothes" (v19).** Tearing clothes was a sign of grief and alarm. Josiah, who had already been reforming for six years, heard the law and realized how far short Judah still fell. Guzik reads this as real conviction of sin, the kind of awakening the Spirit brings (John 16:8).
- **"Inquire of the LORD for me and for those who are left in Israel and in Judah" (v21).** Chronicles adds "those who are left in Israel," the northern remnant. Josiah prays for all Israel.
- **"Great is the wrath of the LORD that is poured out on us, because our fathers have not kept the word of the LORD" (v21).** He takes the curses as addressed to his own generation, not just a past one.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that much of this story was already told in 2 Kings 22 and refers his readers there. Guzik links the book to the copy Deuteronomy 31 placed beside the ark, and passes on Payne's suggestion that it had simply been misplaced during the years of neglect. He tells the story of Peter Waldo, a merchant of Lyon around 1170, whose reading of Scripture in his own language started a movement, to illustrate how a recovered Bible can spark renewal. On the difference in order between Kings and Chronicles, Guzik cites Wiseman: Chronicles may reflect the Chronicler's own emphasis. JFB says the two books simply differ in arrangement and gives no separate comment on vv9–18.

#### Huldah Prophesies Disaster (vv22–33)
- **"Huldah the prophetess" (v22).** Jeremiah and Zephaniah were both active, but the king's officials went to Huldah. She lived "in the Second Quarter," a newer district of Jerusalem probably on the western hill, inside the expanded walls from Hezekiah's day. Her husband Shallum was "keeper of the wardrobe," in charge of the royal or priestly garments. Few women prophets are named in the Old Testament: Miriam (Exod 15:20), Deborah (Judg 4:4), Isaiah's wife (Isa 8:3) and Huldah. Her word is accepted without question by the high priest himself. Guzik passes on Clarke's observation that a humble woman may know God's word better than great officials. The ESV footnote says the Hebrew lacks the words "had sent" in v22.
- **"Tell the man who sent you to me" (v23).** She speaks of the king simply as "the man." Before God's word, he is one more hearer.
- **"All the curses that are written in the book" (v24).** These are the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 27–28, which end with exile among the nations (Deut 28:36, 64). The Tyndale notes explain that covenant curses spell out what happens to those who break the covenant.
- **"Will not be quenched" (v25).** The decision is final. Judgment is coming because the nation has forsaken God, not only because of Manasseh. The reform cannot reverse it.
- **"Because your heart was tender and you humbled yourself before God" (v27).** "Tender" means soft, responsive. "Humbled yourself" comes twice, again the word of 7:14. Josiah's response is the reverse of Manasseh's early years and of Amon, who "did not humble himself" (33:23). Guzik reads "tender heart" two ways: quick to respond to God's word, and quick to fear his warning.
- **"You shall be gathered to your grave in peace" (v28).** Josiah was killed in battle (35:23–24), so in what sense was it "in peace"? The Tyndale notes explain it as the city being at peace and the exile not yet coming. He was buried in Jerusalem before Babylon ever reached it, and his eyes did not see the disaster. Guzik passes on Selman's similar reading.
- **"He read in their hearing all the words of the Book of the Covenant" (v30).** Kings lists "the priests and the prophets" (2 Kings 23:2). Chronicles says "the priests and the Levites." Both were there. The king reads it himself, as Deuteronomy pictured (Deut 17:18–19; 31:10–13).
- **"The king stood in his place and made a covenant" (v31).** "In his place" is probably the royal platform or pillar in the temple court (23:13; 2 Kings 23:3). Guzik notes Dilday's point that the Hebrew idiom is to "cut" a covenant, from the old ceremony of passing between divided animals (Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18). The words "with all his heart and all his soul" echo Deuteronomy 6:5. The Tyndale notes call this covenant renewal the most important event of the reform.
- **"He made all who were present in Jerusalem and in Benjamin join in it" (v32).** Literally "stand to it." The king led and the people followed. Whether their hearts went with them is the question. Jeremiah, preaching in these years, said Judah "did not return to me with her whole heart, but in pretense" (Jer 3:10).
- **"All his days they did not turn away from following the LORD" (v33).** That is the reform's success and its limit in one line: all *his* days. As soon as Josiah died, his sons went back to evil (36:5, 9, 12).
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline ends with "the public reading of the law" and the people's renewing of the covenant, and he sets Josiah's reign as the last time God offered Judah mercy before its fall, which they might have taken had they known "the day of their visitation." Guzik quotes Morgan: knowing the outcome was fixed, Josiah kept on doing right anyway. Guzik also notes that the people stayed faithful while Josiah lived. JFB refers its readers to the notes on 2 Kings 22–23 for this whole section.

#### Pointing to Christ
Josiah sought God as a teenager, cleansed the land, repaired the temple, and wept when God's word was read. He is the king who did not turn aside to the right or the left. Yet he could not change the heart of his nation. The people kept the covenant only "all his days." In the same years, Jeremiah heard God promise something Josiah could not give: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jer 31:33). Jesus is the King who keeps God's word perfectly and, through his death and the Spirit he sends, writes that word on his people's hearts. His reign does not end, so "all his days" means forever.
