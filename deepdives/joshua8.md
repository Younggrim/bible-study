*Setting:* The day after Achan's judgment in the Valley of Achor. Israel has been beaten at Ai, a small hill town about ten miles west of Jericho, and thirty-six men are dead (7:5). The sin that caused the defeat has been dealt with, and "the LORD turned from his burning anger" (7:26). Now God speaks again, and Israel returns to Ai with God's plan instead of its own. The chapter ends far to the north, at Mount Ebal near Shechem, where Joshua carries out the covenant ceremony Moses had commanded for Israel's first days in the land (Deut 27).

#### The Fall of Ai (vv1–29)
- **"Do not fear and do not be dismayed" (v1).** These are the words of 1:9, spoken again after failure. God does not just give Joshua orders. He restores his courage first. "See, I have given" is the same completed promise as at Jericho (6:2).
- **"Take all the fighting men with you" (v1).** The opposite of the spies' advice that only a few thousand were needed (7:3). Ai was small, but this is not about Ai's strength. It is about not trusting in clever shortcuts.
- **"Only its spoil and its livestock you shall take as plunder for yourselves" (v2).** Here is the irony of Achan's story. Had he waited one battle, he could have taken plunder legally. Jericho was the firstfruits and belonged wholly to God. At Ai the people may keep the goods. God's way was never meant to leave his people empty-handed.
- **"Lay an ambush against the city, behind it" (v2).** This time God gives the strategy himself, and unlike at Jericho, it is an ordinary military tactic. God works through miracle and through good planning, and both are his.
- **30,000 and 5,000 (vv3, 12).** Verse 3 says Joshua sent 30,000 men by night to set an ambush; verse 12 says he set about 5,000 in ambush. There are several explanations. Some think there were two groups: 30,000 as the main force and 5,000 in the ambush. Others think v3 summarizes the whole operation and v12 gives the detail. Others note that the word for "thousand" (*'eleph*) can also mean a military unit, which would make both numbers smaller and closer together. JFB takes the 5,000 as one detachment within the larger 30,000. The plan itself is clear even if the numbers are hard to fit together.
- **"They will say, 'They are fleeing from us, just as before'" (v6).** The plan uses Israel's earlier defeat as bait. Ai's confidence from the first battle becomes its downfall. God turns even Israel's failure into part of the victory.
- **"You shall do according to the word of the LORD" (v8).** Joshua stresses that this is God's plan, not his own.
- **"Between Bethel and Ai, to the west of Ai" (v9).** The ambush hid in the rough ground between the two towns. "Joshua spent that night among the people." The leader stayed with his troops.
- **"With a ravine between them and Ai" (v11).** The main army camped openly to the north across a valley, in full view. The point was to be seen.
- **"Joshua spent that night in the valley" (v13).** He moved down into the valley, closer to the city, perhaps to draw the enemy's eye.
- **"He… hurried and went out early" (v14).** The king of Ai rushes out, eager to repeat his earlier victory, to "the appointed place toward the Arabah," a spot overlooking the Jordan valley, east of the city. He has no idea there is an ambush behind him.
- **"Pretended to be beaten" (v15).** Israel flees as it did before, toward the wilderness, the barren slopes running down toward the Jordan valley.
- **"Not a man was left in Ai or Bethel" (v17).** Bethel was nearby and seems to have joined Ai's army. "They left the city open." The ancient Greek translation lacks "or Bethel," and some scholars think it was added later, but most translations keep it.
- **"Stretch out the javelin that is in your hand toward Ai" (v18).** The word *kidon* is a javelin or short spear, distinct from the longer thrusting spear (1 Sam 17:6–7, 45). Holding it out was the signal to the ambush. But the writer also intends a picture. Moses held out his staff over the Red Sea (Exod 14:16), and during the battle against Amalek, while Moses held up his hands, Joshua prevailed (Exod 17:11–13). Now Joshua is the one with the outstretched hand.
- **"The smoke of the city went up to heaven" (v20).** The men of Ai look back and see their home burning. "They had no power to flee" is literally "they had no hands," no strength to go either way. The retreating Israelites turn, and the men of Ai are trapped between two armies.
- **"The king of Ai they took alive" (v23).** He is kept for judgment rather than killed in battle.
- **"Both men and women, were 12,000, all the people of Ai" (v25).** The whole population of the town. Ai was "few" (7:3), and 12,000 seems large for a small hill town, which is another reason some read *'eleph* as a smaller unit. The ban (*cherem*) is carried out as at Jericho, but this time with the spoil allowed.
- **"Joshua did not draw back his hand" (v26).** He held the javelin out until the work was finished, as Moses' hands were held up "until the going down of the sun" (Exod 17:12). This verse returns to the scene of v18 after the battle account, framing the whole victory with Joshua's outstretched hand.
- **"Made it forever a heap of ruins" (v28).** The Hebrew is *tel 'olam*, "a mound forever." A *tel* is the hill formed by layer upon layer of an ancient city's ruins (Deut 13:16). Ai's name already meant "the ruin," and now it lives up to it. Where Ai stood is debated (see the notes on chapter 7): et-Tell, the traditional site, was in ruins long before Joshua, and Khirbet el-Maqatir has been proposed instead.
- **"He hanged the king of Ai on a tree until evening" (v29).** The king was killed first and his body hung up publicly, as 10:26 makes clear for the five kings. The law required the body to be taken down before night, "for a hanged man is cursed by God" so that the land would not be defiled (Deut 21:22–23). Joshua keeps that law exactly. A heap of stones over the king's body at the city gate made a third stone memorial in this book, after the Jordan stones and Achan's grave.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that God gave the general strategy while Joshua worked out the details, and that the outstretched javelin echoes Moses' raised hands. He also sees in chapters 7–8 the cycle that will mark Judges: obedience, blessing, pride, defeat, repentance and restoration. JFB likewise compares Joshua's unwithdrawn hand to Moses at the battle with Amalek, and treats the 5,000 as part of the 30,000. JFB also notes that "or Beth-el" is missing from the Septuagint. The Tyndale notes add that the "30,000" may mean 30 units, and that Ai's king rushed out with the same careless confidence that had cost Israel the first battle.

#### Joshua Renews the Covenant (vv30–35)
- **"At that time Joshua built an altar to the LORD… on Mount Ebal" (v30).** Moses had commanded this in detail: once Israel crossed the Jordan, they were to set up large plastered stones on Mount Ebal, write the law on them, build an altar there, and recite the blessings and curses (Deut 11:29; 27:1–26). Ebal and Gerizim are twin mountains about 20 miles north of Ai, on either side of the pass where the town of Shechem stood. Shechem was where God first promised the land to Abraham and where Abraham built his first altar in Canaan (Gen 12:6–7).
- **When did this happen?** It is surprising to find all Israel 20 miles north, in territory not yet conquered, right after Ai. The text does not explain how they got there, and no battle for Shechem is recorded. Perhaps the region was friendly, or the march was unopposed. The ancient manuscripts also show some uncertainty about where this passage belongs. The Greek Septuagint places it after 9:2, and one Dead Sea Scrolls fragment of Joshua seems to put the altar right after the Jordan crossing. Most translations follow the standard Hebrew order. Either way, the writer places the covenant ceremony here deliberately: after sin and restoration, Israel recommits to God's law.
- **"An altar of uncut stones, upon which no man has wielded an iron tool" (v31).** This is the law of the altar from Exodus 20:25 and Deuteronomy 27:5–6. Human craftsmanship was not to be added to God's altar, perhaps to prevent carved images or to make clear that worship was God's gift, not human achievement. "Burnt offerings" were wholly given to God. "Peace offerings" were shared meals in which the worshipers ate part, celebrating fellowship with God.
- **An altar on Mount Ebal?** In the 1980s the archaeologist Adam Zertal excavated a structure on Ebal from about the right period, a large rectangular stone platform with a ramp and many burned animal bones, mostly from animals that were clean under Israel's law. Zertal identified it as Joshua's altar. Others think it was a watchtower or a farm building. The Tyndale notes take it as an altar. The identification is still debated.
- **"He wrote on the stones a copy of the law of Moses" (v32).** Moses had said to coat large stones with plaster and write "all the words of this law very plainly" on them (Deut 27:2–8). Large public inscriptions of laws were known in the ancient world. The best-known is the Code of Hammurabi, set up on a stone pillar. How much of the law was written, whether all of Deuteronomy or a core of it, is not said.
- **"Half of them in front of Mount Gerizim and half of them in front of Mount Ebal" (v33).** Moses had named which tribes stood on each mountain (Deut 27:12–13). The ark stood in the valley between them, with the priests. The two slopes form a natural amphitheater, and travelers have long noted that a voice carries well across the valley.
- **The mountain of the curse.** Moses' instructions put the altar on Ebal, the mountain where the curses were spoken (Deut 27:4, 13). The Samaritan version of the Pentateuch reads "Gerizim" in Deuteronomy 27:4, because the Samaritans later built their temple on Gerizim (compare John 4:20). The standard Hebrew text reads Ebal, and Joshua 8 agrees. The placement fits the gospel: the altar of sacrifice stands on the mountain of the curse.
- **"Sojourner as well as native born" (v33).** Foreigners living among Israel, such as the "mixed multitude" who left Egypt with them (Exod 12:38) and, now, Rahab's family, stood with Israel under the covenant. The covenant was not a matter of blood but of belonging to the LORD.
- **"The blessing and the curse" (v34).** The blessings and curses of Deuteronomy 27–28 spelled out life for obedience and disaster for rebellion. Israel had just lived through both, at Jericho and at Ai.
- **"There was not a word… that Joshua did not read" (v35).** Joshua had been told to keep the Book of the Law on his lips (1:8), and here he reads all of it, publicly. "The women, and the little ones, and the sojourners" were all there. God's word was not for priests or elders alone.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the altar stood on Ebal, "the mount on which the curse was put," and sees it as pointing to Christ, through whose sacrifice "we have peace with God" where the law threatened a curse. He also writes that "every Israelite was present, even the women and the little ones, that all might know and do their duty." JFB thinks the reading was done by priests or Levites under Joshua's direction, and that the law was written "on the plaster," not on the bare stones. Guzik sees the uncut stones as a sign that the glory belongs to God, not to human skill, and the public reading as a picture of a people shaped by the Word who must also act on it.

#### Pointing to Christ
After defeat and judgment, God restores his people, gives them victory, and leads them to renew the covenant. And he does it at an altar on the mountain of the curse. The law's curse was real, and Israel had just felt it. But the altar stood there, with sacrifices and a shared meal of peace, in the very place the curses rang out. That is the gospel's shape: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us, for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'" (Gal 3:13, quoting Deut 21:23). The king of Ai hung on a tree under God's curse for his own sin. Jesus hung on a tree under the curse for ours, and like the king of Ai, his body was taken down before evening (John 19:31).
