*Setting:* Abraham is about 140 years old. Isaac married at 40 (25:20), and Abraham was 100 when Isaac was born (21:5). Sarah has just died at 127 and been buried in the cave of Machpelah, the first piece of the promised land Abraham legally owned (ch 23). That means Isaac has been without his mother for about three years. Just before that, after Isaac was spared on Mount Moriah, news reached Abraham that his brother Nahor back in Mesopotamia had a large family, and the list quietly ended with a granddaughter named Rebekah (22:20–23). Now the promise that runs through Genesis faces a practical question. God has promised Abraham a nation through Isaac, but Isaac has no wife. This is the longest chapter in Genesis, and in it God never speaks aloud or appears. He works through a servant's prayer, a young woman's kindness and a family's consent, and the narrator lets the characters say again and again that the LORD is behind it.

#### Isaac and Rebekah (vv1–67)
- **One heading for the whole chapter.** The ESV keeps all 67 verses under a single heading. It moves in stages: Abraham's charge (vv1–9), the meeting at the well (vv10–28), the servant's speech (vv29–49), the family's consent and Rebekah's choice (vv50–61), and the meeting with Isaac (vv62–67).
- **"His servant, the oldest of his household" (v2).** He is never named in this chapter. Many readers assume he is Eliezer of Damascus, whom Abraham once described as the heir of his house (15:2), and the Tyndale notes take that as probable. The text never says so, and some sixty years have passed since chapter 15.
- **"Put your hand under my thigh" (v2).** Jacob asks Joseph for the same gesture when he makes him swear to bury him in Canaan (47:29). The Hebrew *yarek* means "thigh," but the lexicon notes it is also used for the loins as the source of offspring, as in Exodus 1:5, where Jacob's descendants are literally those who "came out of" his *yarek*. So the oath was probably sworn on Abraham's line of descendants, and possibly on the mark of circumcision, the covenant sign (17:10–11). That second idea is an interpretation, not stated in the text. The Tyndale notes simply describe it as a very solemn oath in which the servant took the full weight of the mission on himself.
- **"Not… from the daughters of the Canaanites" (v3).** The concern is not ethnicity for its own sake. The Canaanites' sin was ripening toward judgment (15:16), and marriage would draw Isaac into their gods and ways. Marrying within the clan was the preferred custom of the time, but the reason Genesis gives is spiritual. The same grief comes later when Esau marries Hittite women, and it makes "life bitter" for Isaac and Rebekah (26:34–35; 27:46).
- **"See to it that you do not take my son back there" (v6).** Abraham says it twice (vv6, 8). God called Abraham *out* of that land (12:1), and the promise is tied to *this* land. Isaac must not undo his father's obedience. Notice that Isaac is the only patriarch who never leaves Canaan in his whole life.
- **"He will send his angel before you" (v7).** Abraham's confidence rests on what God has already said and sworn: "To your offspring I will give this land" (12:7). If God promised the land to Isaac's offspring, God will provide the wife. These are the last recorded words of Abraham in Genesis. They are words of faith.
- **"You will be free from this oath" (v8).** Abraham trusts God's guidance but does not make the servant responsible for the woman's free choice.
- **"Ten of his master's camels… choice gifts" (v10).** The caravan carries the gifts that went with a marriage proposal. Scholars debate how common domesticated camels were this early. Some argue they were rare in Canaan until around 1000 BC. Others point to earlier evidence of camels used in small numbers, especially by the very wealthy, which is exactly how Abraham is described (12:16; 24:35).
- **"Mesopotamia to the city of Nahor" (v10).** The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew, *Aram-naharaim*, "Aram of the two rivers," the land between the upper Euphrates and its tributaries. It is called Paddan-aram in 25:20. The "city of Nahor" is probably Haran, where Abraham's family settled (11:31) and where Laban later lives (27:43). The Tyndale notes estimate the trip at about 450 miles, roughly two weeks of travel each way.
- **"At the time of evening, the time when women go out to draw water" (v11).** The servant goes where he will meet the young women of the town. Wells are where several great betrothals in the Bible begin: Jacob meets Rachel at a well (29:1–12) and Moses meets Zipporah at one (Exod 2:15–21).
- **"Please grant me success today" (v12).** This is one of the first recorded prayers for guidance in the Bible. The verb *qarah* (here in a causative form) means "make it happen" or "cause it to meet me." The lexicon notes the same root in Ruth 2:3, where Ruth "happened" to come to Boaz's field. What looks like chance in both stories is God's quiet arranging.
- **"Show steadfast love to my master Abraham" (v12).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal, covenant kindness. The word runs through the chapter (vv12, 14, 27, 49) and becomes its theme. The servant prays to "the God of my master Abraham."
- **"Drink, and I will water your camels" (v14).** The servant does not ask for a sign out of nowhere, like fire from the sky. He asks for a test of character. Giving a stranger a drink was ordinary courtesy. Offering to water ten camels without being asked would show unusual generosity and a willingness to work hard. The ESV footnote offers "by her" as an alternative to "by this."
- **Is this a model for seeking God's will?** Christians have read this prayer differently. Some see a pattern for asking God for specific signs. Others note that the servant's mission was already defined, so he asked *which* woman, not *whether* to obey. The text doesn't make it a rule for every decision, but it does show a man praying specifically, setting his criteria in advance, and then watching carefully (v21) instead of jumping to conclusions.
- **"Before he had finished speaking, behold, Rebekah" (v15).** The answer is already walking toward him. Isaiah later says, "Before they call I will answer; while they are yet speaking I will hear" (Isa 65:24). The narrator gives her family line right away, so the reader knows before the servant does that this is Abraham's own family: Nahor's granddaughter (22:23).
- **"A maiden whom no man had known" (v16).** The word here is *betulah*, which the lexicon defines as "virgin," and the narrator adds the clarifying phrase. In his retelling the servant calls her an *almah* (v43), which the lexicon defines as a young woman of marriageable age. The two words overlap here, since the same woman is described with both. That matters for discussions of Isaiah 7:14, which uses *almah*.
- **"Drink, my lord… I will draw water for your camels also" (vv18–19).** She does exactly what the servant prayed for, without knowing it. The Tyndale notes estimate ten thirsty camels could drink around 250 gallons. Guzik suggests a lower figure but still at least an hour of hard work. Either way, this is many trips down to the spring and back with a heavy jar. "Quickly" and "ran" (vv18, 20) are the same words used for Abraham's eager hospitality in 18:2, 6–7. Rebekah serves strangers the way Abraham did.
- **"The man gazed at her in silence" (v21).** He doesn't announce his conclusion too soon. "Prospered" is *tsalach*, which the lexicon defines as "advance, prosper." It recurs in vv21, 40, 42 and 56, tying the whole journey together: the servant wanted to know whether the LORD had made his way succeed.
- **"A gold ring weighing a half shekel, and two bracelets… ten gold shekels" (v22).** The ESV footnote puts a shekel at about 11 grams, so the bracelets alone weigh about 110 grams of gold. The ring is a *nezem*, which the lexicon says is a nose ring when worn by a woman. That is how the servant describes it later (v47).
- **"The man bowed his head and worshiped the LORD" (vv26–27).** His first response is worship, right there by the well, and he will bow twice more (vv48, 52). *Chesed* and *emet*, steadfast love and faithfulness, are the pair God uses to describe himself to Moses (Exod 34:6). The Tyndale notes call this verse the heart of the chapter's message. "As for me, the LORD has led me in the way" is the servant's own testimony. He walked, and God led.
- **"Told her mother's household" (v28).** This may reflect the women's sphere of the home, where women had real authority. Rebekah's mother is involved again in vv53 and 55.
- **"Laban ran out… as soon as he saw the ring and the bracelets" (vv29–30).** This is the first appearance of Laban, Rebekah's brother. The narrator says he ran out "as soon as he saw" the gold, a small detail that foreshadows the grasping man who will later cheat Jacob for twenty years (29:21–27; 31:7, 41). His greeting, "Come in, O blessed of the LORD" (v31), is warm and even uses the LORD's name.
- **"I will not eat until I have said what I have to say" (v33).** Hospitality expected a guest to eat first, then talk business. The servant reverses that. His mission matters more than his comfort.
- **"I am Abraham's servant" (v34).** He introduces himself only by whom he serves. He never gives his own name.
- **The retelling (vv35–48).** The servant repeats almost the whole story, and the small changes show his tact. He stresses Abraham's wealth and that Isaac is the sole heir, "to him he has given all that he has" (v36; compare 25:5). He speaks of Abraham's "father's house" and "clan" (v38), which flatters the relatives he is speaking to. He leaves out Abraham's firm "do not take my son back there," which might have sounded unfriendly to them. And he tells the order slightly differently: in v47 he says he asked her family first and then gave the ring, while vv22–23 show the gift came before the question. The effect is to show a careful diplomat who wants the family to see God's hand, not a stranger's money.
- **"Who had led me by the right way" (v48).** The ESV footnote gives "faithfully" as an alternative. The Hebrew is literally "the way of *emet*," the same word for "faithfulness" as in v27, so the servant may be saying that God led him in a true and faithful way.
- **"If you are going to show steadfast love and faithfulness to my master, tell me" (v49).** Now *chesed* and *emet* are asked of people. God has shown his loyal love. Will this family respond with the same? "That I may turn to the right hand or to the left" means "so I know where to go next."
- **"The thing has come from the LORD; we cannot speak to you bad or good" (v50).** Laban and Bethuel recognize God's hand. "Bad or good" is a Hebrew way of saying "anything at all": there is nothing left to argue. This is Bethuel's only appearance in the story. Laban speaks first, and in v55 it is "her brother and her mother" who make requests, so some think Bethuel was old or not the real head of the household. The text doesn't explain.
- **"Let the young woman remain with us a while, at least ten days" (v55).** A natural request from a family losing a daughter they may never see again. The servant refuses gently, again pointing to God: "since the LORD has prospered my way" (v56).
- **"Will you go with this man?" (v58).** The family asks Rebekah herself. Her answer in Hebrew is a single word, *elek*, "I will go." Like Abraham, who left "your country and your kindred and your father's house" (12:1) for a land he had not seen, Rebekah leaves the same family for a husband she has not seen. She never returns.
- **"May your offspring possess the gate of those who hate him" (v60).** Without knowing it, Rebekah's family echoes the LORD's own promise to Abraham on Mount Moriah: "your offspring shall possess the gate of his enemies" (22:17). To "possess the gate" meant to control the city. The ESV footnote offers "hate them" as an alternative. "Thousands of ten thousands" anticipates the multitude promised to Abraham.
- **"Isaac had returned from Beer-lahai-roi" (v62).** This is the well where the LORD saw and spoke to Hagar (16:13–14), whose name means "the well of the Living One who sees me." Isaac settles there after Abraham's death (25:11).
- **"Isaac went out to meditate in the field" (v63).** The verb (*suach*) appears only here in the Bible, and its meaning is uncertain. The ESV, KJV and many others render it "meditate." Some scholars suggest "to walk" or "to stroll." Jewish tradition (in the Talmud) read it as prayer and credited Isaac with starting the afternoon prayer. The lexicon only says "perhaps to muse." Either way, Isaac is alone in the field at evening, probably still grieving, when the camels appear.
- **"She took her veil and covered herself" (v65).** A bride was veiled when she was brought to her husband. That custom explains how Laban later deceives Jacob with Leah (29:23–25). Rebekah's veil marks this meeting as her wedding.
- **"The servant told Isaac all the things that he had done" (v66).** Abraham does not appear again in this chapter, and the servant now calls Isaac "my master" (v65). Some readers have wondered whether Abraham had died. Genesis says he lived another 35 years and married again (25:1, 7). The shift simply shows the family's future passing to Isaac.
- **"Into the tent of Sarah his mother" (v67).** Rebekah takes Sarah's place as the mother of the promised line.
- **"He loved her. So Isaac was comforted" (v67).** This is the first time the Bible says a husband loved his wife. "Comforted" is *nacham*, which the lexicon defines as "be comforted, console oneself." The same verb is used when Jacob refuses to be comforted over Joseph (37:35). The marriage that began with Abraham's faith, a servant's prayer and a young woman's courage ends with a grieving son finding love and comfort.
- **Commentators:** All three see the servant as a model of faithful, prayerful service. Henry opens with "Marriages and funerals are the changes of families," linking Sarah's funeral with this wedding. On Abraham's charge, Henry says that Isaac should "rather want a wife than expose himself to that temptation," meaning better to go without a wife than to go back. JFB calls Abraham's concern "natural to his position as a pastoral chief," and adds that "the servant appears worthy of the master he served." On the prayer, Henry writes, "Those that would have good speed must pray for it," and on the servant's worship, "What we win by prayer we must wear with praise." Guzik warns that reading circumstances alone is risky because people tend to see what they want, and he commends the servant for fixing his test before anything happened. Henry praises Rebekah, who "did not stand to gaze upon the strange man and his camels, but minded her business." On Laban, Henry is blunt that he would not have been so welcoming "if he had not hoped to be well paid for it," and JFB says "the sight of the dazzling presents increased both his haste and his invitation." JFB thinks the father was "probably dead" because the brothers lead the negotiations, but v50 names Bethuel as speaking, and Guzik takes Bethuel as alive with Laban as the family's spokesman. Guzik calls Rebekah's "I will go" a real word of faith. On Isaac in the field, Henry reads "meditate, or pray" and adds, "Holy souls love retirement." He sees the veil as a sign of "humility, modesty, and subjection," and JFB likewise reads it as respect. Guzik says the name Eliezer means "God of help," and the lexicon gives "God is help," so the claim holds, though the chapter never names the servant. JFB has no separate comment on vv4–9, 11, 13–14, 51–52, 54–63 or 66.

#### Pointing to Christ
Genesis 24 is first of all a true story about God keeping his promise from one generation to the next. Isaac's marriage keeps the line of promise alive, and through it comes Jacob, then Judah, then David, and in the end Jesus (Matt 1:2). The New Testament never treats this chapter as an allegory, but many Christian readers, Henry and Guzik among them, have seen a picture in it: a father sends his servant to a far country to find a bride for his beloved son, who once was offered up on a mountain and given back. Henry puts it this way: "The spouse of Christ must not be of the Canaanites, but of his own kindred, born again from above." Some go further and see the unnamed servant, who speaks only of his master and his master's son, as a picture of the Holy Spirit, who "will glorify me" (John 16:14). That is a devotional reading, not something the text claims, and it is best held lightly. What the text plainly shows is a God of *chesed* who leads his people step by step, and a bride who hears about a bridegroom she has not seen and says, "I will go." Peter writes of Jesus, "Though you have not seen him, you love him" (1 Pet 1:8). And at the well where Rebekah gave water to a stranger, the same kind of scene comes back in John 4, when a weary traveler asks a woman for a drink and then offers her living water.
