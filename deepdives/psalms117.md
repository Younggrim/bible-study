*Setting:* Psalm 117 is the shortest psalm and the shortest chapter in the Bible, just two verses and about seventeen words in Hebrew. Counted by chapters, it also sits almost exactly in the middle of the Protestant Bible. It belongs to the "Egyptian Hallel" (Psalms 113–118), the praise psalms sung at Passover, and is one of the group sung after the meal, so it was very likely sung by Jesus and his disciples on the night of the Last Supper (Matt 26:30). It has no title, author or date. Some ancient manuscripts join it to the psalm before or after it, and JFB calls it "a doxology, suitable to be appended to any Psalm of similar character." Yet it stands on its own, and for its size it reaches very far. Israel's Passover songs, sung to remember a rescue that belonged to Israel alone, suddenly call every nation on earth to praise Israel's God. Paul quotes it in Romans 15:11 as proof that God always meant the Gentiles to be included. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's two lines.

#### Praise the LORD, All Nations! (v1)
- **"Praise the LORD, all nations!" (v1).** "Praise" is *halelu*, the same word as in *Hallelu-Yah*. "Nations" is *goyim*, the usual word for the Gentiles, the peoples outside Israel. That is the surprise. The psalms around it call on Israel, the priests and the God-fearers to praise (115:9–11; 118:2–4). This one calls the whole world.
- **"Extol him, all peoples!" (v1).** "Extol" is *shabach*, a less common word for praise that means to laud or commend, to speak highly of someone. It is a loan from Aramaic and appears mostly in later Hebrew. "Peoples" (*'ummim*) is a rarer word than "nations," and the two together cover everyone: every nation, every tribe, every ethnic group. Hebrew poetry says the same thing twice with different words, and here the doubling stretches the circle as wide as it will go.
- **This was God's plan from the start.** It is not a late afterthought. God told Abraham, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). Solomon prayed that foreigners would come to the temple and pray, "in order that all the peoples of the earth may know your name and fear you" (1 Kings 8:41–43). Isaiah said God's house would be "a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isa 56:7), words Jesus quoted when he cleared the temple (Mark 11:17). Psalm 117 is one of many places where that hope breaks through in Israel's worship (22:27; 67:3–5; 96:7–10).
- **Paul's use of v1 (Rom 15:11).** At the end of Romans, Paul strings together four Old Testament texts, from the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms, to show that Christ came "in order that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy" (Rom 15:8–12). Psalm 117:1 is one of them. For Paul, a church of Jews and Gentiles worshiping together is not a strange new idea. It is what the Psalms already sang about.
- **A missionary psalm.** The call has a logic to it. The nations cannot praise a God they have never heard of (Rom 10:14). A psalm that calls every people to praise implies that someone must go and tell them.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls it "a solemn call to all nations to praise the Lord" and "a gospel psalm," noting that God was long known only in Judah but the gospel brings the distant near. JFB reads it as "prophetical of the prevalence of God's grace in the world," pointing to Paul's quotation. Guzik ties the call to the promise to Abraham and observes that Jesus, singing this psalm on the eve of the cross, had the nations in mind as he went to die for a people from every tribe. He draws the missionary application plainly: those who sing this psalm should also work to make God known.

#### Great Is His Steadfast Love Toward Us (v2)
- **"For great is his steadfast love toward us" (v2).** The reason for praise is given in one line. "Great" here is not the usual Hebrew word for "big." It is *gabar*, a verb meaning to be strong, to prevail, to overpower. It is used of the floodwaters that "prevailed" over the earth in Noah's day (Gen 7:18–19, 24) and of God's love in Psalm 103:11: "so great is his steadfast love toward those who fear him." The picture is of God's love rising over his people like a flood, stronger than anything against them.
- **"Steadfast love" (v2).** *Chesed* again, the covenant love that runs through the whole Hallel (118:1–4, 29). It is love that commits and keeps its commitments.
- **"Toward us" (v2).** "Us" most naturally means Israel: the people God rescued from Egypt and bound to himself. That is what makes the psalm striking. The nations are called to praise God for what he has done for Israel. Israel's story becomes good news for the world, because the God who keeps faith with Israel is a God the nations can trust as well. In the light of the New Testament, "us" widens to include all who are joined to Israel's Messiah by faith (Eph 2:11–13).
- **"And the faithfulness of the LORD endures forever" (v2).** "Faithfulness" is *'emet*, truth, reliability, the quality of being true to one's word. Steadfast love and faithfulness are paired often in the Old Testament, beginning with God's self-description at Sinai, "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exod 34:6). Psalm 115:1 just used the pair. John's "grace and truth" in Jesus (John 1:14, 17) is the same pairing in Greek. Love without faithfulness would not last, and faithfulness without love would not help. Together they are the whole reason for praise.
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v2).** The psalm ends with *Hallelu-Yah*, as it began with *halelu*. Two lines of reasons sit between two calls to praise.
- **Short is not small.** A psalm this short can be memorized in a minute. It shows that worship does not have to be long to be complete. It needs a true call, a true reason and a true God.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "is great toward us" can be rendered "prevailed over" or "protected us," which the lexicon supports: *gabar* with "over" means to prevail over, the same construction as Psalm 103:11. Guzik, citing Adam Clarke, makes the same point, that the word means strong and prevailing, not just large. He also passes on Morgan's observation that mercy without truth, or truth without mercy, would leave no hope, but the two together are the gospel. Guzik, following Kidner, suggests "us" may already draw Israel and the nations together. Henry urges believers to bless God for gospel grace and pray for the day when all nations praise him.

#### Pointing to Christ
On the night before the cross, Jesus very likely sang this psalm, calling every nation to praise the God of Israel. The next day he died to make that call possible. Paul says Christ became "a servant to the circumcised to show God's truthfulness, in order to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs, and in order that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy" (Rom 15:8–9). That is Psalm 117 in one sentence: faithfulness kept to Israel, mercy opened to the nations. After he rose, Jesus sent his followers to "make disciples of all nations" (Matt 28:19). The psalm's final picture comes in Revelation: "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages," crying out praise to God and to the Lamb (Rev 7:9–10).
