*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. Of David, the servant of the LORD." Only one other title calls David "the servant of the LORD": Psalm 18, his great song of victory. Henry says no one can explain why these two, but the title is an honor, the same one given to Moses and Joshua (Josh 1:1; 24:29). No occasion is named. Henry thinks of Saul or Absalom, and of Saul especially, who seemed to welcome David as a son-in-law while plotting his death (1 Sam 18:17–25). The psalm is short and built on a striking contrast. It opens in the dark, inside the heart of a wicked man who has stopped fearing God (vv1–4). Then, without warning, it lifts its eyes to the heavens and sings of God's steadfast love, faithfulness, righteousness and justice (vv5–9). Morgan, cited by Guzik, pictures David on a height looking out over the whole landscape: heavens, clouds, mountains, the deep, the river, and over everything the light. It ends with a short prayer and a glimpse of the evildoers fallen (vv10–12). Paul quotes v1 in Romans 3:18 as part of his proof that all people, Jew and Gentile alike, are under sin. Psalm 35 ended with David's tongue telling of God's righteousness "all the day long." Psalm 36 shows the other kind of tongue, and then shows the righteousness David promised to praise.

#### Transgression Speaks to the Wicked (vv1–4)
- **"Transgression speaks to the wicked deep in his heart" (v1).** The Hebrew word for "speaks" (*ne'um*) is unusual. It is almost always used in the phrase "declares the LORD," the formula prophets use for a word from God (Isa 1:24; Jer 1:8). Here it is "the oracle of transgression." Sin is pictured as a false prophet whispering in the sinner's heart, as if it had divine authority. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, imagines it speaking "in the secret heart of the wicked, as in some dark cave."
- **"His heart" or "my heart"?** The standard Hebrew text reads "in *my* heart," which would mean something like "Transgression's message to the wicked is clear to my heart" or, as JFB renders it, "such is my reflection on men's transgressions." A few Hebrew manuscripts and the ancient Greek and Syriac versions read "in *his* heart," and the ESV follows them. JFB calls it a "difficult verse," but the general sense is clear either way: the wicked man has stopped fearing God.
- **"There is no fear of God before his eyes" (v1).** This is the root of everything that follows. The "fear of God" is the reverent awe that keeps a person honest because he knows God sees (34:11). Without it, nothing holds a person back. Paul quotes this line as the last in his chain of Old Testament texts about human sin (Rom 3:10–18). Boice, cited by Guzik, thinks Paul had this whole psalm in mind. Henry notes that old English law charged a criminal with acting "not having the fear of God before his eyes."
- **"For he flatters himself in his own eyes" (v2).** "Before his eyes" there is no fear of God; "in his own eyes" there is only flattery. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, puts it sharply: he who makes little of God makes much of himself, and those who forget adoration fall into adulation. "Flatters" is literally "makes smooth." He smooths things over with his own conscience.
- **"That his iniquity cannot be found out and hated" (v2).** The Hebrew is compressed and difficult. JFB gives it as "for finding his iniquity for hating," meaning he persuades himself that God will never find his sin and hate it, that is, punish it. The KJV reads "until his iniquity be found to be hateful," meaning he goes on flattering himself until the truth breaks through. Horne and Poole, cited by Guzik, read it that way. The two readings fit together: the wicked man believes he will never be found out, and one day he will be.
- **"The words of his mouth are trouble and deceit" (v3).** "Trouble" (*'aven*) is wickedness that causes harm, and "deceit" (*mirmah*) is fraud. What begins as self-deception in the heart comes out as lies and harm in the mouth (Luke 6:45). Henry: "to whom will those be true who are false to their own souls?"
- **"He has ceased to act wisely and do good" (v3).** "Act wisely" (*sakal*) is the root of "Maskil" in Psalm 32's title and "instruct" in 32:8. Once he had at least some good sense. He has given it up.
- **"He plots trouble while on his bed" (v4).** The bed is where the godly meditate on God (4:4; 63:6) and the night is when the righteous man of Psalm 1 meditates on God's law (1:2). This man lies awake planning harm. Micah describes the same: "Woe to those who devise wickedness and work evil on their beds!" (Mic 2:1). Boice, cited by Guzik, notes that he is not drifting into evil. He is inventing it.
- **"He sets himself in a way that is not good; he does not reject evil" (v4).** Psalm 1 described a man walking, then standing, then sitting with the wicked. Here the man has "set himself," taken a stand on a road that leads nowhere good. "He does not reject evil" sounds mild, but JFB notes that these negatives "affirm more emphatically their contraries." He does not merely fail to reject evil. He embraces it.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "sin in its causes and sin in its colours, in its root and in its branches." The root is the lack of the fear of God; the branches are self-flattery, deceitful speech and plotting. "The sinner defies God, and even deifies himself." He notes that some think David means Saul, "but we are under no necessity of limiting ourselves so… there are too many among us to whom the description agrees." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's comment that "iniquity and deceit" are a pair of dogs that hunt together.

#### Your Steadfast Love, O LORD (vv5–6)
- **The sudden turn (v5).** With no transition at all, David turns from the wicked man's heart to God. Henry: having "looked round with grief upon the wickedness of the wicked, [he] here looks up with comfort upon the goodness of God." The contrast is the message. However dark human hearts are, God is not like them.
- **"Your steadfast love, O LORD, extends to the heavens" (v5).** Steadfast love (*chesed*) is God's loyal, covenant love, the love that keeps its promises. Boice, cited by Guzik, calls it the most important word in this psalm. It comes three times (vv5, 7, 10). It reaches as high as the sky, beyond human measuring (103:11: "as high as the heavens are above the earth, so great is his steadfast love").
- **"Your faithfulness to the clouds" (v5).** Faithfulness (*'emunah*) is God's reliability. The wicked man's words are deceit (v3); God's are faithful. Steadfast love and faithfulness are often paired (25:10; 57:10; Exod 34:6).
- **"Your righteousness is like the mountains of God" (v6).** "Mountains of God" is a Hebrew way of saying "the mightiest mountains." Trapp, cited by Guzik, notes that Hebrew adds God's name to describe something as great. God's righteousness is as solid and immovable as the highest peaks.
- **"Your judgments are like the great deep" (v6).** "The great deep" (*tehom rabbah*) is the vast ocean depths, the waters of creation (Gen 1:2) and the flood (Gen 7:11). God's decisions are as deep as the sea, beyond our sounding. Paul says the same: "How unsearchable are his judgments" (Rom 11:33).
- **"Man and beast you save, O LORD" (v6).** God's care reaches every living creature. He provided for animals in the ark (Gen 8:1) and feeds the young lions and the birds (104:21, 27; Matt 6:26). He spared Nineveh partly for the sake of "much cattle" (Jonah 4:11). Henry: "he that feeds the young lions will not starve his own children."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Whatever is missing, or amiss, in the world, we are sure there is nothing missing, nothing amiss, in him that governs it." JFB: these qualities are "set forth, by the figures used, as unbounded." Guzik passes on Clarke's note that "clouds" may mean the highest skies, above all visible space, and Morgan's picture of David looking out over the whole landscape.

#### How Precious Is Your Steadfast Love (vv7–9)
- **"How precious is your steadfast love, O God!" (v7).** Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the change of scale. In v5 God's love is immense, too big to grasp. Here it is "precious," intimate, too good to let slip. The same love is as high as heaven and as close as a mother hen's wing.
- **"The children of mankind take refuge in the shadow of your wings" (v7).** The picture is of chicks running under a mother bird's wings (91:4; Deut 32:11). Jesus used it of himself over Jerusalem: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (Matt 23:37). Boaz blessed Ruth, a Moabite, as one who had come "to take refuge" under the wings of the God of Israel (Ruth 2:12). Clarke, cited by Guzik, suggests an allusion to the wings of the cherubim over the ark. Notice "the children of mankind," not only Israel. The refuge is open to all who come.
- **"They feast on the abundance of your house" (v8).** "Abundance" is literally "fatness" (*deshen*), the richest part. It is the language of the sacrificial meals at the sanctuary, where worshippers ate part of their peace offerings in God's presence (Lev 7:15; Deut 12:7). God is the host, and his guests eat the best. Psalm 23:5 has the same picture: "You prepare a table before me." Henry: the world's pleasures "will surfeit, but never satisfy," but God's favor "will satisfy, but never surfeit."
- **"And you give them drink from the river of your delights" (v8).** "Delights" (*'adanim*) is the plural of the word *'eden*, the same as the garden of Eden. It is literally "the river of your Edens." JFB: "may allude to Eden." A river flowed out of Eden to water the garden (Gen 2:10). Ezekiel saw a river flowing from the temple bringing life wherever it went (Ezek 47:1–12), and Revelation ends with "the river of the water of life… flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb" (Rev 22:1). To be in God's house is to drink from paradise.
- **"For with you is the fountain of life" (v9).** Life does not just come from God once, at creation. He is its ever-flowing source. Jeremiah calls God "the fountain of living waters" and condemns Israel for digging "broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jer 2:13). Jesus told the Samaritan woman he would give "living water," a spring "welling up to eternal life" (John 4:10–14).
- **"In your light do we see light" (v9).** One of the most loved lines in the Psalms. We cannot see anything rightly except in God's light. The wicked man "flatters himself in his own eyes" (v2) and sees nothing truly. The one who lives in God's presence sees God, the world and himself as they really are. John's Gospel takes up both images of this verse: "In him was life, and the life was the light of men" (John 1:4). Jesus: "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life" (John 8:12).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those know not God that do not admire his loving-kindness; and those know not themselves that do not earnestly covet it." He pictures God's people "as the chickens under the wing of the hen." JFB reads "fatness" as "richness" and "thy house" as the privileges of communion with God (23:6; 27:4), and says that "light is an emblem of all blessings, given of God as a means to gain more." Guzik passes on Trapp's picture of chickens hiding under the hen when a hawk threatens.

#### Oh, Continue Your Steadfast Love (vv10–12)
- **"Oh, continue your steadfast love to those who know you" (v10).** "Continue" is literally "draw out" or "prolong," as one draws out a cord or keeps a kindness going. To "know" God means more than knowing about him. It is a personal, covenant relationship (Jer 31:34; John 17:3). JFB: "right knowledge of God is the source of right affections and conduct."
- **"And your righteousness to the upright of heart!" (v10).** The wicked man's heart was full of the oracle of transgression (v1). The upright of heart are the opposite. David prays not only for himself but for all of them.
- **"Let not the foot of arrogance come upon me, nor the hand of the wicked drive me away" (v11).** The foot of a proud conqueror trampled the defeated (Josh 10:24). The hand of the wicked could drive David into exile, away from his home and from God's house, the very house of v8. JFB: "all kinds of violent dealing." Henry reads "drive me away" as being removed both from integrity by temptation and from peace by trouble.
- **"There the evildoers lie fallen" (v12).** "There" is pointed, as if David can see the very spot. JFB: "in the acting of violence, they are overthrown." Henry passes on the view that "there" may also point to the final judgment, where the wicked fall and the saints stand.
- **"They are thrust down, unable to rise" (v12).** The man who "set himself" on a bad road (v4) is thrown down and cannot get up. The psalm ends with the end of the wicked, as Psalm 1 did: "the way of the wicked will perish" (1:6).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those that have experienced the pleasure of communion with God cannot but desire that nothing may ever remove them from him." He adds that "we are not to rejoice when any particular enemy of ours falls; but the final overthrow of all the workers of iniquity will be the triumph of the saints." JFB calls v12 "a signal defeat."

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul used the first verse of this psalm to show that every human heart, not just the obviously wicked, lacks the fear of God (Rom 3:18). Then, in the very next verses, he turns, as the psalm does, from human sin to God's righteousness: "But now the righteousness of God has been manifested… through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe" (Rom 3:21–22). The love that reaches to the heavens came down in Jesus. He is the one who longed to gather Jerusalem under his wings. He invites sinners to a feast in his Father's house (Luke 14:15–24; 15:23). He offers living water from the fountain of life (John 7:37–38). And he is the light in whose light we see light. Because of him, the river of God's Edens flows again, and at the end of the Bible those who belong to him drink from it freely: "Let the one who is thirsty come" (Rev 22:17).
