*Setting:* Egypt, a few weeks after the hailstorm. Seven plagues have fallen. The hail wrecked the flax and barley but missed the wheat, which had not yet come up (9:31–32), so the fields are green again. Pharaoh has confessed "I have sinned" and then hardened his heart once more, and some of his own officials have started to fear the LORD's word (9:20). Chapter 10 brings plagues eight and nine, locusts and darkness. Pharaoh's court begins to break with him, he offers his last two bargains, and the chapter ends with Moses and Pharaoh parting for good.

#### The Eighth Plague: Locusts (vv1–20)
- **"For I have hardened his heart and the heart of his servants" (v1).** God tells Moses ahead of time that Pharaoh will not yield, so Moses is not to be discouraged when he refuses. The hardness serves a purpose: **"that I may show these signs of mine among them."**
- **"That you may tell in the hearing of your son and of your grandson" (v2).** This is the first time the plagues are said to be for Israel's children. The story is meant to be retold in families, and it was: the Passover meal is built around a child's questions (12:26–27), and Psalms 78 and 105 sing these plagues for later generations. **"How I have dealt harshly"** is literally "how I made a fool of" or "made sport of" Egypt, the same verb Balaam uses when he complains that his donkey has "made a fool" of him (Num 22:29). Egypt's might has been made to look ridiculous.
- **"How long will you refuse to humble yourself before me?" (v3).** The Hebrew verb is *anah*, "to be bowed down" or "afflicted." It is the same root used for Egypt's affliction of Israel: the taskmasters were set "to afflict them" (1:11–12). Pharaoh has bowed Israel down for years. Now God asks how long Pharaoh will refuse to bow.
- **"Tomorrow I will bring locusts" (v4).** The desert locust is a grasshopper that, under the right conditions, gathers into swarms of billions. A swarm can cover many square miles and eat its own weight in green plants every day. The word *arbeh* is related to the verb for "to multiply."
- **"They shall eat what is left to you after the hail" (v5).** The wheat and emmer that escaped the hail are now in danger. Egypt's food supply for the whole year is at stake.
- **"Then he turned and went out" (v6).** Moses does not wait for an answer. He has said what God told him to say.
- **"How long shall this man be a snare to us?" (v7).** Pharaoh's officials use his own words back at him: "how long" (v3). A "snare" (*moqesh*) is a trap for birds. **"Do you not yet understand that Egypt is ruined?"** For the first time Pharaoh's court openly pushes him to give in. The servants who feared God's word in 9:20 are no longer a few.
- **"But which ones are to go?" (v8).** Pharaoh is ready to bargain, but only on his terms. Moses answers that everyone goes: **"our young and our old… our sons and daughters… our flocks and herds" (v9).** Worship of the LORD involves the whole household.
- **"The LORD be with you, if ever I let you and your little ones go!" (v10).** Pharaoh's words drip with sarcasm. In effect: "God help you if you think I'll ever let your children go." **"Look, you have some evil purpose in mind."** The word for "evil" is *ra'ah*. Some scholars hear a pun on the name of Ra, the sun god, as if Pharaoh were saying, "Ra is against you." That is possible, but uncertain.
- **"Go, the men among you" (v11).** Pharaoh's third compromise. Earlier he said worship in the land (8:25), then don't go far (8:28). Now only the men may go, and their wives and children stay behind as hostages. The Tyndale notes point out that Moses never said Israel would come back, and Pharaoh knew that no freed slaves would willingly return. **"They were driven out from Pharaoh's presence."**
- **"Moses stretched out his staff… and the LORD brought an east wind" (v13).** God uses natural means, a wind blowing "all that day and all that night" from the Arabian desert, but the timing, scale and target are his.
- **"Such a dense swarm… as had never been before, nor ever will be again" (v14).** It is the worst locust plague in Egypt's history. **"The land was darkened" (v15).** A thick swarm blocks out the sun, a foretaste of the next plague. **"Not a green thing remained."** Egypt honored Osiris as god of the harvest and plant life that returned each spring, and Seth was sometimes credited with protecting crops. Neither saved a blade of grass.
- **"I have sinned against the LORD your God, and against you" (v16).** Pharaoh calls Moses **"hastily."** His confession goes further than before: he admits sin against Moses as well as God, and asks for forgiveness, **"only this once" (v17).** He calls the locusts **"this death."** The Tyndale notes observe that his theology is improving, but correct words are not a changed heart.
- **"A very strong west wind… drove them into the Red Sea" (v19).** The Hebrew is literally "sea wind," a wind off the Mediterranean. The **"Red Sea"** is *yam suph*, often rendered "Sea of Reeds," the body of water Israel will soon cross (13:18; 15:4). The same God who brought the locusts sweeps them away completely: **"Not a single locust was left."** The sea that swallows the locusts will soon swallow Pharaoh's army.
- **"But the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart" (v20).** The verb is *chazaq*, "made strong." From the sixth plague on, the text more and more says God did the hardening, confirming what Pharaoh had chosen again and again.
- **Locusts elsewhere in Scripture.** Moses later warns that disobedient Israel will face locusts too (Deut 28:38). Joel describes a locust plague as the army of the LORD and a picture of his coming day (Joel 1–2), and Revelation uses locusts as an image of judgment (Rev 9:3–11).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the plagues were recorded "for the generations to come as undeniable proofs" of God's power, and on v3: "Those that will not humble themselves God will humble." He notes that Pharaoh would allow the men to go, "pretending that this was all they desired." JFB describes the locust as "a large, spotted, red and black, double-winged grasshopper," says locusts are "natives of the desert and are only brought by an east wind into Egypt," and pictures v11 as an Eastern court custom of "seizing the obnoxious suppliant by the neck" and dragging him out. JFB has no comment on vv3, 5–6 or 12. Guzik names Pharaoh's root problem as pride and sees in Pharaoh's offers a man trying to bargain with God as an equal. God does not need to bargain, because he holds every card.

#### The Ninth Plague: Darkness (vv21–29)
- **"Stretch out your hand toward heaven" (v21).** As with the gnats and the boils, the third plague of the set comes without warning.
- **"A darkness to be felt."** The verb is *mashash*, "to feel" or "grope," the word used for groping in the dark (Deut 28:29). It is a darkness so thick that people feel their way through it.
- **"Pitch darkness in all the land of Egypt three days" (v22).** Some scholars connect the plague with the *khamsin*, a hot spring wind that can fill the sky with sand and dust for days. If God used such a storm, its strength and its limits were still beyond anything natural: **"all the people of Israel had light where they lived" (v23).**
- **A blow at the sun god.** The sun was Egypt's greatest god. Under the name Amun-Ra, he was king of the gods and the source of life, and Pharaoh was called the son of Ra. For three days the sun is gone. The Tyndale notes stress that the LORD switched it off over Egypt while it went on shining in Goshen.
- **"Nor did anyone rise from his place" (v23).** Egypt is frozen. Daily life, work, temple rites, even moving across a room all stop.
- **"Your little ones also may go… only let your flocks and your herds remain behind" (v24).** Pharaoh's fourth and final compromise. He gives in on the children but tries to keep the animals. Without flocks Israel could not survive in the desert or offer sacrifices, and the livestock would draw them back to Egypt.
- **"Not a hoof shall be left behind" (v26).** Moses will not give an inch. **"We do not know with what we must serve the LORD until we arrive there."** God, not Pharaoh, will say what worship requires. Moses also knows God promised Israel would not leave empty-handed (3:21–22).
- **"Take care never to see my face again, for on the day you see my face you shall die" (v28).** Pharaoh cannot win the argument, so he threatens the messenger. The Tyndale notes compare this to the leaders in Jesus' day who, after the raising of Lazarus, could not deny his signs and so planned to kill him (John 11:47–53).
- **"As you say! I will not see your face again" (v29).** Moses' calm reply closes the negotiations. Moses does speak once more in 11:4–8 before he leaves, probably in this same meeting, and then walks out "in hot anger" (11:8). After that, Pharaoh will be the one who sends for Moses (12:31).
- **Darkness elsewhere in Scripture.** The prophets speak of darkness as a sign of God's judgment on "the day of the LORD" (Amos 5:18–20; Joel 2:2), and in Revelation the fifth bowl plunges the beast's kingdom into darkness (Rev 16:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls it "darkness which might be felt," while "the children of Israel, at the same time, had light in their dwellings," and says Pharaoh wanted the cattle "left in pawn." He notes that "after this interview, Moses came no more, till he was sent for." JFB lists the natural explanations others have offered, "thick clammy fogs and vapors," a sandstorm or the khamsin, but describes a darkness thick enough to be "almost perceived by the organs of touch." He calls Pharaoh at the end "frantic with disappointment and rage." Guzik sees the darkness as a judgment on the sun god Ra and a picture of what it means for God to withdraw his light, and he notes that we are not told how Israel's homes stayed lit.

#### Pointing to Christ
Egypt sat in a darkness that could be felt while God's people had light in their homes. That contrast runs all through Scripture: "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (Isa 9:2), and Jesus says, "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness" (John 8:12). At the cross, darkness fell over the land for three hours (Mark 15:33) as Jesus took God's judgment in the place of his people, so that they could be brought out of darkness into his marvelous light (1 Pet 2:9). And Moses' "not a hoof shall be left behind" echoes Jesus' own words: of all the Father has given him he will lose nothing (John 6:39).
