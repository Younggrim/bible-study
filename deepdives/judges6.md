*Setting:* After Deborah's victory "the land had rest for forty years" (5:31). Then the cycle turns again, and this time the oppressor is not a king in a city but waves of raiders from the desert. Gideon's story runs through three chapters (6–8), the longest of any judge so far, and it marks a turning point. Gideon begins as a frightened man threshing in hiding and becomes one of Israel's great deliverers, but his story ends with an idol in his hometown (8:27). Chapter 6 tells how God called him, starting at home with his father's altar to Baal, and how God patiently answered his fears.

#### Midian Oppresses Israel (vv1–10)
- **"The LORD gave them into the hand of Midian seven years" (v1).** Midian was descended from Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:1–2). Moses lived among the Midianites and married one (Exod 2:15–21), but Midian later joined Moab against Israel and led Israel into sin at Peor, and Israel fought a war against them (Num 25; 31). The Tyndale notes say the Midianites were not only nomads. They controlled much of northwest Arabia and the land east of the Jordan at this time.
- **"Dens that are in the mountains and the caves and the strongholds" (v2).** Israel was driven into hiding. The hill country of Israel is full of limestone caves, and people fortified them. JFB says they were "not, of course, excavating them," but "making them fit for habitation." Israel will hide in caves again under the Philistines (1 Sam 13:6).
- **"Whenever the Israelites planted crops" (vv3–4).** The raids came at harvest. Midian, the Amalekites, and "the people of the East" (desert tribes from east of the Jordan) swept through the Jezreel Valley and down the coast "as far as Gaza," taking crops and animals. Israel could plant, but not eat.
- **"Like locusts in number—both they and their camels could not be counted" (v5).** This is one of the earliest descriptions in history of camels used on a large scale in war. Camels let raiders cover long distances quickly and carry away plunder. To farmers who had never faced them, they were terrifying. The locust picture fits: they came in swarms and left nothing.
- **"Israel was brought very low… and cried out for help to the LORD" (v6).** Seven years of this broke Israel. Then they cried out.
- **"The LORD sent a prophet" (vv7–8).** This time God answers the cry not first with a deliverer but with a preacher. The prophet is unnamed. He and Deborah are the only prophets mentioned in Judges.
- **"I led you up from Egypt… But you have not obeyed my voice" (vv8–10).** His message is short, and it follows the pattern of the angel at Bochim (2:1–3): what God has done, what God commanded, how Israel failed. "You shall not fear the gods of the Amorites" is a striking phrase. Israel's problem was not only that they worshiped Baal, but that they were afraid of the local gods and tried to keep them happy. The prophet names the cause before God sends the cure.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the calamity "is authoritatively traced to their infidelity as the cause." Guzik notes that Israel cried to God only after everything else failed, and that the prophet was sent to show them the real problem was their disobedience, not Midian's strength. He also points out that crying out is not the same as repenting. Henry's concise commentary connects the oppression directly to Israel's renewed sin.

#### The Call of Gideon (vv11–27)
- **"The angel of the LORD came and sat under the terebinth at Ophrah" (v11).** Ophrah was a town of the clan of Abiezer in Manasseh, probably west of the Jordan near the Jezreel Valley. Its exact site is unknown. JFB says the angel "appeared in the character and equipments of a traveller," simply sitting under a tree.
- **"Beating out wheat in the winepress to hide it from the Midianites" (v11).** Wheat was normally threshed on an open hilltop threshing floor, where the wind could blow away the chaff. A winepress was a pit cut in rock, small and hidden. Gideon is threshing a little wheat by hand, in secret. JFB: "This incident tells emphatically the tale of public distress."
- **"The LORD is with you, O mighty man of valor" (v12).** The greeting sounds almost ironic, spoken to a man hiding in a hole. The phrase *gibbor hayil* can mean a mighty warrior or a man of standing and wealth (2 Kings 15:20). It describes what Gideon will become. Henry quotes Bishop Hall: "While God calls Gideon valiant, he makes him so."
- **"If the LORD is with us, why then has all this happened to us?" (v13).** Gideon's reply is honest and bitter. He has heard the old stories of the exodus, and he sees none of it now. He even says, "the LORD has forsaken us." The prophet of vv8–10 has already answered his question: Israel had forsaken God first. JFB says "Gideon's language betrays want of reflection."
- **"Go in this might of yours and save Israel… do not I send you?" (v14).** The text now says "the LORD" turned to him, not just the angel. The angel speaks as God himself (see 2:1). "This might of yours" is whatever strength Gideon has, which is little. The real strength is in the words "do not I send you?"
- **"My clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house" (v15).** Like Moses (Exod 3:11), Saul (1 Sam 9:21) and Jeremiah (Jer 1:6), Gideon protests his smallness. "Clan" is literally "thousand," a unit of the tribe. God's answer does not argue with him. It simply promises: "But I will be with you" (v16), the same words God said to Moses at the burning bush (Exod 3:12).
- **"You shall strike the Midianites as one man" (v16).** As easily as if they were a single man, or as if all Israel fought together as one.
- **"Show me a sign that it is you who speak with me" (v17).** Gideon wants proof that the speaker is really from God. He asks the visitor to wait while he brings a "present," the normal word for a gift or offering.
- **"A young goat and unleavened cakes from an ephah of flour" (v19).** An ephah was about 20 liters or more, a large amount, in a time when Midian left no food. Gideon brings a generous meal, the kind of hospitality Abraham showed his three visitors (Gen 18:6–8).
- **"Fire sprang up from the rock and consumed the meat" (v21).** The rock becomes an altar, and the angel touches the food with the tip of his staff. Fire from God consuming an offering was a sign of God accepting it (Lev 9:24; 1 Kings 18:38). JFB says it "betokened the acceptance of his person." Then the angel vanishes.
- **"Alas, O Lord GOD! For now I have seen the angel of the LORD face to face" (v22).** Gideon is terrified. It was believed that no one could see God and live (Exod 33:20). Jacob (Gen 32:30) and later Samson's parents (13:22) say the same.
- **"Peace be to you. Do not fear; you shall not die" (v23).** God answers his fear directly. "Peace" (*shalom*) means wholeness and well-being, not just calm.
- **"Called it, The LORD Is Peace" (v24).** In Hebrew *YHWH Shalom*. The altar was still standing at Ophrah when the book was written. In a land at war, Gideon names the altar for what God had just given him. Peace with God comes before victory over Midian.
- **"Pull down the altar of Baal that your father has" (v25).** That same night God gives Gideon his first job, and it is at home. His own father, Joash, kept an altar to Baal and an Asherah, a sacred wooden pole or tree, for the town. Gideon's name probably means "hewer" or "one who cuts down," which fits what he is about to do.
- **"The second bull seven years old" (vv25–26).** The Hebrew is unclear about how many bulls are involved, which is why translations differ. The bull's age matches the seven years of Midian's oppression (v1), perhaps a sign that the years of judgment were ending. JFB guesses that the first and best bull "may have been fattened for the service of Baal." The new altar is to be built "with stones laid in due order," and the wood of the Asherah will burn the sacrifice. The idol becomes firewood for the LORD's altar.
- **"He was too afraid… to do it by day, he did it by night" (v27).** Gideon obeys, but in fear. The text does not hide it. JFB: "the work had to be done under cover of night." He is a man of valor in the making, not yet in full.
- **Commentators:** Henry's concise commentary says Gideon "was weak in faith," but "the Angel answered his objections," and it stresses the order of events: "It was needful he should make peace with God, before he made war on Midian." Guzik reads the angel as a pre-incarnate appearance of God the Son, and takes "this might of yours" as what Gideon did have: humility, concern for his people and a teachable spirit, along with real weakness. He also distinguishes between asking God for guidance where Scripture is silent, which is reasonable, and demanding signs of God's love, which the cross has already given. JFB compares Gideon's hesitation to Moses, noting that he "wishes to be better assured that the mission was really from God."

#### Gideon Destroys the Altar of Baal (vv28–35)
- **"Who has done this thing?" (vv28–29).** The town wakes to find the altar wrecked, the Asherah cut down, and a bull burning on a new altar. They search and find out quickly.
- **"Bring out your son, that he may die" (v30).** The irony is sharp. By God's law, it was the Baal worshipers who deserved death (Deut 13:6–10; 17:2–5). Israelites are ready to execute an Israelite for honoring the LORD.
- **"Will you contend for Baal?… If he is a god, let him contend for himself" (v31).** Joash, the owner of the altar, turns and defends his son. His logic is simple: a god who needs people to defend him is no god. It is the same challenge Elijah will later make on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18:27). Henry's concise commentary credits God with turning Joash's heart. The Tyndale notes count him as the first to follow Gideon's lead.
- **"Jerubbaal, that is to say, 'Let Baal contend against him'" (v32).** The name comes from *rib*, "to contend" or "to bring a lawsuit." It was a mocking dare: let Baal sue him if he can. Baal never did. The name became a badge of honor, and Gideon is called Jerubbaal again and again (7:1; 8:29, 35). Later scribes, unwilling to say "Baal," sometimes changed it to Jerubbesheth, "let shame contend" (2 Sam 11:21).
- **"Came together… and encamped in the Valley of Jezreel" (v33).** The raiders gather for the season's invasion. They cross the Jordan into the broad Jezreel Valley, the same plain where Sisera fell.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD clothed Gideon" (v34).** A vivid verb. The Spirit "put on" Gideon like a garment. Gideon did not take hold of the Spirit. The Spirit wrapped himself in Gideon. The same word is used of the Spirit with Amasai (1 Chr 12:18) and Zechariah son of Jehoiada (2 Chr 24:20). Paul uses the same picture when he tells believers to "put on the Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 13:14).
- **"He sounded the trumpet" (vv34–35).** Gideon's own clan answers first, the Abiezrites. Then messengers go to the rest of Manasseh, to Asher, Zebulun and Naphtali, the northern tribes nearest the valley. Asher, who sat still in Deborah's day (5:17), now comes.

#### The Sign of the Fleece (vv36–40)
- **"If you will save Israel by my hand, as you have said" (vv36–37).** Gideon twice says "as you have said." He knows what God promised. He asks for a sign anyway. He lays a sheep's fleece on the threshing floor overnight: dew on the fleece alone, dry ground around it.
- **"He wrung enough dew from the fleece to fill a bowl" (v38).** It happens. But a fleece naturally soaks up dew, and the ground dries faster, so Gideon may have thought it was not convincing enough.
- **"Let not your anger burn against me; let me speak just once more" (v39).** He echoes Abraham pleading for Sodom (Gen 18:30, 32). This time the harder test: dry fleece, wet ground. "And God did so that night" (v40). JFB: "the divine patience and condescension were wonderfully manifested in reversing the form of the miracle."
- **Is "putting out a fleece" a good model?** The phrase has become a common way of describing a request for God's guidance through a sign. But Gideon was not asking what God wanted. God had already told him plainly, and given him a sign of fire (vv14–21). He was asking God to confirm what God had already said. The Tyndale notes say the fleece shows God's patience with doubt, not a method for decisions. Guzik judges the fleece a sign of weak faith, though God graciously answered. Others read it more gently, as a frightened man's honest prayer before a battle he knew he could not win. Either way, God did not rebuke him. "He bears with the infirmities of His people," as JFB puts it.
- **Commentators:** Henry's concise commentary sees a picture in the fleece and the ground: God's grace can rest on some and not on others, as dew fell on one and not the other. Guzik contrasts God's patience here with Zechariah, who was struck silent for asking for a sign (Luke 1:18–20). He notes that God answered Gideon anyway, and that Gideon still appears in Hebrews 11:32 among the heroes of faith. JFB calls the fleece miracle "a very remarkable one" given how heavy the dew is in that region, and says Gideon's repeated request may have displeased God, but God bore with him.

#### Pointing to Christ
God came to Gideon in his hiding place, called him by a name he did not yet deserve, and promised, "I will be with you." Before Gideon fought Midian, he had to deal with the idol in his own house, and before that, God gave him peace: "Peace be to you… you shall not die." That order is the order of the gospel. In Christ, God first makes peace with sinners "by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20), and then sends them out. Jesus is called "Immanuel," God with us (Matt 1:23), and his last promise to his fearful disciples was the same one Gideon heard: "I am with you always" (Matt 28:20). And the Spirit who clothed Gideon is poured out on all who belong to Christ, who are told to "put on the Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 13:14).
