*Setting:* Paul is still a prisoner, most likely under guard in Rome around AD 60–62, and this is the last chapter of his warmest letter. Chapter 3 ended looking up: "our citizenship is in heaven," and the Lord Jesus will one day transform our lowly bodies (3:20–21). Chapter 4 brings that hope down to earth. It speaks to a quarrel between two women, to anxiety, to the thought life, and finally to money. Behind the whole letter is a gift. Epaphroditus had carried money from Philippi to Rome, nearly died on the job, and is now carrying this letter home (2:25–30). Paul has saved his thank-you for the end.

#### Exhortation, Encouragement, and Prayer (vv1–9)
- **"Therefore" (v1).** It points back to 3:20–21. Because Christ is coming to finish what he started, the Philippians have reason to hold their ground now. The hope of heaven is not an escape from the present but the reason to stay steady in it.
- **"Whom I love and long for, my joy and crown" (v1).** Paul piles up affection: "brothers," "love," "long for," "beloved" (twice in one verse). The word for crown is *stephanos*. It was the wreath given to a winning athlete, a festival garland, or a public honour for distinguished service, and is different from the *diadēma* of royalty. Paul uses the same picture of the Thessalonians, "our crown of boasting before our Lord Jesus at his coming" (1 Thess 2:19). These people are the evidence that his race was not run in vain (2:16).
- **"Stand firm thus in the Lord" (v1).** "Thus" means in the way chapter 3 just described: pressing on, imitating Paul, refusing the teachers who trust in the flesh. "Stand firm" is a soldier's word, picking up 1:27, "standing firm in one spirit."
- **"I entreat Euodia and I entreat Syntyche" (v2).** Paul repeats the verb for each woman, so neither can think the appeal is aimed only at the other. We know nothing about what they disagreed over, and Paul does not take sides.
- **"To agree in the Lord" (v2).** Literally "to think the same thing," the same phrase as 2:2, "being of the same mind," just before the great hymn about Christ's humility (2:5–11). The cure for the quarrel is the mind of Christ, who "humbled himself." "In the Lord" is the common ground: they need not agree about everything, but they share the same Master.
- **Women in the work.** Philippi's church began with women at a riverside place of prayer, and Lydia was its first convert (Acts 16:13–15). That a quarrel between two of them was worth naming in a letter read aloud to the whole church shows how prominent they were.
- **"True companion" (v3).** The Greek is *syzygos*, "yokefellow," one who pulls in the same harness. Nobody knows who he is. Suggestions include Epaphroditus (who carried the letter), Timothy, Silas, Luke, or a leading elder in Philippi. The lexicon also allows it as a proper name, Syzygus, and then "true" makes a pun: "Syzygus, true to your name." The Tyndale notes leave both options open, and so should we.
- **"Labored side by side with me" (v3).** The verb is *synathleō*, "to struggle together as athletes," the same rare word as 1:27, "striving side by side for the faith of the gospel." It occurs only in these two places in the New Testament. These women were not on the edges of Paul's mission. They had fought beside him.
- **Clement (v3).** He is otherwise unknown. Some early writers identified him with Clement, the leader of the church in Rome who wrote to the Corinthians about AD 96, but the name was very common and most scholars think the link is unlikely.
- **"Whose names are in the book of life" (v3).** This is the register of God's people, an image that runs from Moses (Exod 32:32) through Daniel (12:1) to Revelation (3:5; 20:12, 15). Jesus told his disciples to rejoice more in this than in any success (Luke 10:20). A city proud of its citizen rolls would feel the point: the names that matter are enrolled in heaven (3:20).
- **"Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice" (v4).** This is the letter's refrain (1:18; 2:17–18; 3:1). The repetition is deliberate, almost as if Paul hears the objection, "Always? Even in a cell?" and answers it by saying it again. The joy is "in the Lord," so it rests on someone who does not change, not on circumstances that do.
- **"Let your reasonableness be known to everyone" (v5).** The word is *epieikeia* (here as the adjective *epieikes*). The lexicon gives "fairness, moderation, gentleness," and one translator famously rendered it "sweet reasonableness." It is the opposite of insisting on every right. Paul uses the noun of Christ himself, "the meekness and gentleness of Christ" (2 Cor 10:1). It is to be "known to everyone," including the outsiders who were making life hard for the church (1:28).
- **"The Lord is at hand" (v5).** This can be read two ways, and both are true. It may mean Christ's return is near, the hope of 3:20, so believers need not grab for their rights now (James 5:8–9). Or it may mean the Lord is close by, near to all who call on him (Ps 145:18).
- **"Do not be anxious about anything" (v6).** The verb is *merimnaō*. Jesus uses it six times in Matthew 6:25–34: "do not be anxious about your life." There is an irony here. In 2:20 Paul praised Timothy for being "genuinely concerned" for them, with the same verb. Caring about people is good. Being torn apart with worry, as if everything depended on us, is what Paul forbids.
- **"In everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving" (v6).** "Anything" and "everything" are set side by side: worry about nothing, pray about everything. "Prayer" is the general word for talking to God, and "supplication" is asking for specific needs. **Thanksgiving** keeps prayer from turning into complaint and reminds the one praying of what God has already done.
- **"The peace of God… will guard your hearts and your minds" (v7).** Paul does not promise that every request will be granted. He promises peace. "Surpasses all understanding" means it is more than anyone could work out or explain, and also better than any plan we could have come up with. **"Guard"** is *phroureō*, a military word for posting a garrison. In a garrison town like Philippi, with Roman soldiers watching the gates, the picture was vivid. And the guard is posted "in Christ Jesus." Isaiah promised the same: "You keep him in perfect peace whose mind is stayed on you" (Isa 26:3), and Jesus left it as his parting gift (John 14:27).
- **"Finally, brothers, whatever is true…" (v8).** Eight qualities follow. Several of them, especially "lovely," "commendable" and "excellence" (*aretē*, the great word for virtue in Greek moral thought, found only here in Paul), were the language of the best pagan ethics. Paul borrows his neighbours' vocabulary and claims all that is good for Christ.
- **"Think about these things" (v8).** The verb is *logizomai*, "to reckon, calculate, consider." It is the same word used for God counting righteousness to Abraham (Rom 4:3). Paul means more than a passing thought. He means taking these things into account, weighing them, and letting them shape choices. The peace that guards the mind in v7 goes with a mind deliberately filled in v8.
- **"What you have learned and received and heard and seen in me—practice these things" (v9).** "Received" is the language of passing on teaching (1 Cor 15:3). But Paul adds "heard and seen": the Philippians had watched him in their jail (Acts 16:25). Truth was taught and also lived, as in 3:17, "join in imitating me."
- **"And the God of peace will be with you" (v9).** In v7 the peace *of God* guards them. In v9 the God *of peace* himself is with them. The gift and the Giver come together.
- **Commentators:** On the crown, Guzik draws the line between the victor's *stephanos* and the royal *diadēma*. The lexicon supports the distinction, though it notes the Greek Old Testament sometimes uses *stephanos* for a king's crown too, so it should not be pressed too hard. Henry: "They were his crown as well as his joy." He judges it "uncertain" who the yokefellow was, and JFB lists Timothy, Silas, or a Philippian bishop, and allows Synzygus as a proper name. JFB gives the best definition of "reasonableness": "not urging one's own rights to the uttermost, but waiving a part." It also calls Christ's return "the grand motive to every Christian grace." On v6 it puts it memorably: "Care and prayer are as mutually opposed as fire and water." Henry adds a balance: careful thought and planning are right, but anxious distrust of God is not. Guzik distinguishes peace *with* God (the settled matter of reconciliation, Rom 5:1) from the peace *of* God, which is the calm that comes from trusting him day by day. He also connects v8 to the renewed mind of Romans 12:2.

#### God's Provision (vv10–20)
- **"I rejoiced in the Lord greatly" (v10).** Now Paul comes to the thank-you. He is grateful, but he does not want to sound like he is fishing for more.
- **"Now at length you have revived your concern for me" (v10).** "Revived" is *anathallō*, a gardening word: "to sprout again, flourish again," as a tree puts out new leaves in spring. Their care had not died. It had been dormant, like a tree in winter. Paul quickly adds that they had no **opportunity** to send help, so they should not hear a rebuke.
- **"I have learned… to be content" (v11).** The word is *autarkēs*. In Greek philosophy, especially among the Stoics, it was a key ideal: "self-sufficient," needing nothing from anyone or anything outside yourself. Paul takes the word and changes what it means. His sufficiency is not in himself but in Christ (v13). And it was **learned**. Paul was not born content. He learned it in shipwrecks, beatings and prisons (2 Cor 11:23–27).
- **"I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound" (v12).** "Brought low" (*tapeinoō*) is the verb used of Christ, who "humbled himself" (2:8). And Paul notices that plenty is as much a test as hunger. Many people who stay faithful in hard times are undone by comfortable ones (Deut 8:11–14; Prov 30:8–9).
- **"I have learned the secret" (v12).** This is a single striking word, *myeō*, used only here in the New Testament. The lexicon gives its basic meaning as "to initiate into the mysteries." It was the language of the mystery religions, where initiates were let in on secret rites. Paul's "secret" is not a hidden ritual but a person, as the next verse says.
- **"I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (v13).** This is one of the most quoted verses in the Bible and one of the most misused. In context, "all things" means facing all *circumstances*: plenty and hunger, abundance and need. It is not a promise of success at anything we set our hearts on. It is strength to be content and faithful whatever comes. The Greek has "him who strengthens me." The KJV's "through Christ" comes from later manuscripts that spelled out the "him." The meaning is the same either way, but the ESV follows the older text. Jesus said the other side of it: "apart from me you can do nothing" (John 15:5).
- **"Yet it was kind of you to share my trouble" (v14).** Paul does not want his talk of contentment to sound cold or ungrateful. They did not just send money. They took a share in his suffering. "Share" is from the *koinōnia* word family, "partnership," that runs through the letter (1:5, 7).
- **"In the beginning of the gospel" (v15).** This means when Paul first brought the gospel to them, about AD 49–50 (Acts 16–17). When he left Macedonia for Athens and Corinth, only Philippi supported him.
- **"Partnership with me in giving and receiving" (v15).** This is the language of business accounts, debits and credits. The Philippians had opened an account with Paul. They gave money, and they received spiritual blessing (compare Rom 15:27). The money language continues through v18.
- **"Even in Thessalonica… once and again" (v16).** Thessalonica was about 100 miles west along the Via Egnatia, the great Roman road. Paul was there only a few weeks (Acts 17:2), yet the Philippians sent help more than once. He also worked night and day there so as not to burden anyone (1 Thess 2:9). He refused money from the Corinthians to avoid any appearance of preaching for profit (1 Cor 9:12–18), but he gladly accepted it from Philippi, a church that understood the gospel was free.
- **"Not that I seek the gift, but I seek the fruit that increases to your credit" (v17).** "Credit" is still bookkeeping language: "interest accumulating to your account." Paul is more excited about what their giving does for them, in God's eyes, than about what it did for him.
- **"I have received full payment, and more" (v18).** The verb is *apechō*. The lexicon notes it was the standard word written on receipts in Paul's day, meaning "paid in full." Paul writes them a receipt: account settled, and then some.
- **"A fragrant offering, a sacrifice acceptable and pleasing to God" (v18).** Now Paul changes from business language to temple language. In the Old Testament, the burnt offerings were "a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (Lev 1:9, 13, 17). Paul uses the same phrase of Christ's self-giving: "a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2). Money given to a missionary in prison was, in God's eyes, worship. It went to Paul, but it rose to God.
- **"And my God will supply every need of yours" (v19).** Notice "my God." Paul cannot pay them back, but his God can, and he speaks of God as one he knows personally. **Every need** is the scope, not every wish. And the measure is "according to his riches in glory." God gives not merely *from* his wealth, as a rich man might drop a coin, but in proportion to it. All of it comes "in Christ Jesus."
- **"To our God and Father be glory forever and ever. Amen" (v20).** Paul cannot talk about God's riches without breaking out in praise. The glory goes to God, not to Paul or to the givers.
- **Commentators:** JFB loves the picture in "revived": "Ye have flourished again," like trees in spring. It contrasts Paul's contentment with the Stoic's self-sufficiency. Paul learned it "by the teaching of the Holy Spirit, and the dealings of Providence." It renders v13 "I have strength for all things," and notes the older manuscripts lack "Christ." On v19 it gives Paul's voice: "My Master will fully repay you; I cannot." Henry agrees that prosperity can be the harder test, with its pride and worldliness: "Let us pray for patient submission and hope when we are abased." Guzik warns against waving v13 as a slogan for success and says it is about endurance, and he stresses that God promises needs, not every want. He compares God's supply to the widow's oil in 2 Kings 4, which kept flowing as long as there were empty jars to fill. All three agree the Philippians' gift was really given to God.

#### Final Greetings (vv21–23)
- **"Greet every saint in Christ Jesus" (v21).** Not "all the saints" but "every saint," one by one. After a letter that had to name a quarrel, Paul greets everyone individually, leaving no one out. "Saint" means one set apart for God. It is the normal word for every believer, not a title for a spiritual elite (1:1).
- **"The brothers who are with me" (v21).** These are Paul's co-workers in Rome. Timothy was among them (1:1; 2:19–22).
- **"Especially those of Caesar's household" (v22).** This does not mean the emperor's family. "Caesar's household" was the huge staff of slaves and freedmen who ran the imperial palace and government across the empire: clerks, accountants, messengers, cooks and administrators. Nero was emperor. Some of them had come to faith, very likely through Paul's imprisonment, as he said in 1:13 ("the whole imperial guard"). For a Roman colony like Philippi, proud of its ties to Rome, this would have been thrilling news: the gospel had reached the heart of the empire.
- **"The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit" (v23).** The letter began with grace (1:2) and ends with grace. "Your" is plural, but "spirit" is singular. Whether or not Paul meant it, it fits a letter that has pleaded for the church to be "of one spirit" (1:27; 2:2). The Tyndale notes take "spirit" as the inner person, the deepest part of each believer, where Christ's grace does its work. Some later manuscripts add "Amen." The older ones do not.
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies Caesar's household as "the slaves and dependents of Nero," probably won while Paul was held near the Praetorian guard. It notes the oldest manuscripts read "with your spirit" and omit the closing "Amen." Guzik sees the greeting as evidence that the gospel was spreading in the very center of Roman power, among some of the least likely people. Henry says the grace that ends the letter is called "the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ" because it was "purchased for us by Christ's merit."

#### Pointing to Christ
Every part of this chapter is held together by the phrase "in the Lord." The women are to agree in the Lord. The church stands firm in the Lord and rejoices in the Lord. Peace guards hearts in Christ Jesus. Paul's secret of contentment is "him who strengthens me," and God supplies needs "in Christ Jesus." This is the Christ of chapter 2 who emptied himself, became poor, was brought low, and was raised to glory. Because he walked the road from plenty to want and back to glory, those who are in him can walk it too, content in both. And because he gave himself as "a fragrant offering" (Eph 5:2), even a small gift sent to a prisoner in Rome becomes a sacrifice pleasing to God.
