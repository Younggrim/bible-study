*Setting:* Babylonia, around 591 BC. Chapter 17 ended with God's promise to plant a tender twig from David's cedar. Chapter 19 goes back to the royal house as it actually was and sings a funeral song over it. Judah's last kings had been a sorry line. After good King Josiah fell in battle against Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo in 609 BC, four of his family reigned in turn: his son Jehoahaz (three months, then taken to Egypt), his son Jehoiakim (eleven years, a vassal first of Egypt and then of Babylon), his grandson Jehoiachin (three months, then taken to Babylon in 597 BC), and his son Zedekiah, still on the throne as Ezekiel spoke (2 Kings 23:29–24:20). The poem pictures this dynasty first as a lioness and her cubs, then as a vine. Zedekiah was alive, and Jerusalem still stood, but Ezekiel sings the lament as if the funeral had already happened.

#### A Lament for the Princes of Israel (vv1–14)
- **"Take up a lamentation for the princes of Israel" (v1).** A lamentation (*qinah*) was a funeral song, sung by mourners at a burial, like David's lament over Saul and Jonathan (2 Sam 1:17–27). In Hebrew it had its own limping rhythm, a longer line followed by a shorter one, like a sob. Ezekiel's hearers would have recognized the beat at once. Normally a dirge praised the dead. This one recounts how the kings went wrong. **"Princes"** rather than "kings" is Ezekiel's habit (12:10), a quiet reminder that Judah's rulers now reigned on someone else's terms.
- **"What was your mother? A lioness!" (v2).** The lion was Judah's emblem. Jacob had blessed Judah as "a lion's cub… he couched as a lion and as a lioness" and promised that "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:9–10). Ezekiel turns that blessing into a dirge. The mother is most likely Judah, or Jerusalem and the royal house, raising her cubs among the lions, the kings of the nations. Some take the mother literally as Hamutal, Josiah's wife, who was the mother of both Jehoahaz and Zedekiah (2 Kings 23:31; 24:18). That fits the first cub and the last branch, but the poem works best as a picture of the nation and its dynasty.
- **"She brought up one of her cubs… he learned to catch prey; he devoured men" (v3).** This is Jehoahaz, also called Shallum (Jer 22:11). When Josiah died, "the people of the land" made him king, passing over his older brother (2 Kings 23:30–31). He reigned only three months, and 2 Kings says he "did what was evil in the sight of the LORD." To "devour men" is a picture of a ruler who preyed on his own people instead of protecting them (22:25, 27).
- **"He was caught in their pit, and they brought him with hooks to the land of Egypt" (v4).** Lions were hunted by driving them into a pit or a net. Assyrian kings boasted of lion hunts, and palace reliefs from Nineveh show captured lions and caged ones. Captives too were sometimes led with hooks or rings through the nose or lip (2 Kings 19:28; Amos 4:2). Pharaoh Neco deposed Jehoahaz at Riblah and took him to Egypt, where he died (2 Kings 23:33–34). Jeremiah mourned him: "he shall return here no more… he shall never see this land again" (Jer 22:10–12).
- **"When she saw that she waited in vain… she took another of her cubs" (v5).** The mother hoped the first cub would come home. When that hope died, she raised another. Which king is meant is disputed. Jehoiakim followed Jehoahaz directly, but he was put on the throne by Pharaoh, not chosen by Judah, and he died in Jerusalem rather than being taken to Babylon (2 Kings 23:34; 24:6). Jehoiachin, like Jehoahaz, reigned three months and was carried away, in his case to Babylon (2 Kings 24:8–15), and the details of vv8–9 fit him exactly. Most modern commentators and Guzik favor Jehoiachin. JFB favors Jehoiakim, pointing to his long and violent reign, and 2 Chronicles 36:6 says Nebuchadnezzar bound Jehoiakim in chains to take him to Babylon. The Tyndale notes leave the question open. Ezekiel may have meant the cub to stand for the whole line of failed kings.
- **"And seized their widows" (v7).** The Hebrew is difficult. Read as it stands, it says "he knew their widows," or perhaps "their strongholds" (the word for widows and for palaces or fortresses looks almost the same). The ESV takes it as the king seizing the widows of the men he devoured. Others read "he ravaged their strongholds." Either way, the cub has become a destroyer. **"He laid waste their cities, and the land was appalled… at the sound of his roaring."** Jehoiakim's reign fits this picture of violence. He built a palace with forced labor (Jer 22:13–17) and killed the prophet Uriah (Jer 26:20–23).
- **"They spread their net over him; he was taken in their pit… brought him to the king of Babylon" (vv8–9).** In 597 BC Babylon came with troops from its subject peoples, the "provinces on every side" (2 Kings 24:2), and took the young king away. **"That his voice should no more be heard on the mountains of Israel."** Jehoiachin lived the rest of his life in Babylon. Babylonian ration tablets found in the ruins of the city list oil given to "Yaukin, king of the land of Yahud" and his five sons. Decades later, he was released from prison and given a place at the king's table (2 Kings 25:27–30), but he never went home.
- **"Your mother was like a vine in a vineyard planted by the water" (v10).** The second picture changes the image but tells the same story. The ESV's "in a vineyard" follows a slight correction of the Hebrew, which reads "in your blood" (the KJV's "in thy blood"). The two words differ by a letter or two. **"Fruitful and full of branches by reason of abundant water."** This was the dynasty at its height under David and Solomon, given every advantage (17:5–8).
- **"Its strong stems became rulers' scepters; it towered aloft" (v11).** The vine produced kings. "Stems" or "rods" (*matteh*) can mean a branch or a staff of office. The word plays on both. But **"it was seen in its height"** hints at pride. The vine grew tall and wanted to be seen.
- **"But the vine was plucked up in fury, cast down to the ground" (v12).** The poem switches to the past tense as if the end had already come. **"The east wind dried up its fruit"** is Babylon again (17:10). **"As for its strong stem, fire consumed it."**
- **"Now it is planted in the wilderness, in a dry and thirsty land" (v13).** This is the exile. Babylon was well watered, but for Judah's royal house it was a desert. There was no land, no throne and no future that anyone could see.
- **"Fire has gone out from the stem of its shoots, has consumed its fruit" (v14).** The fire comes from inside the vine, from one of its own branches. This is Zedekiah, whose broken oath brought Babylon's army back and burned Jerusalem (17:15–21). The dynasty was destroyed by its own last king. **"There remains in it no strong stem, no scepter for ruling."** After Zedekiah, no descendant of David ever sat on the throne of Judah as an independent king. Governors such as Zerubbabel came later (Hag 1:1), but not kings. The scepter of Genesis 49:10 seemed to be gone.
- **"This is a lamentation and has become a lamentation" (v14).** When Ezekiel first sang it, the song looked ahead. Within a few years it described what had happened. A song written as prophecy became a song sung in grief.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads both pictures as a dynasty that turned on its own people: "they were cruel and oppressive to their own subjects." He adds that "advancement to authority discovers the ambition and selfishness of men's hearts." On the vine he notes, "her own branches serve as fuel to burn her," but also that it is "destroyed, though not plucked up by the roots," leaving room for hope. JFB identifies the second cub as Jehoiakim. It takes the obscure word in v7 as "widowed palaces," and reads the fire of v14 as "God's wrath kindled by the perjury of Zedekiah." It adds that the song "shall be so to distant posterity." Guzik identifies the second cub as Jehoiachin, for the reasons given above, and sees the lament as the Lord's own sorrow over failed leaders. He notes that David's royal line went underground after Zedekiah until the Messiah came.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel's lament ends with "no scepter for ruling," which is a funeral for the house of David. But Jacob's blessing had said that the scepter would not depart from Judah "until tribute comes to him; and to him shall be the obedience of the peoples" (Gen 49:10). God had not forgotten. Out of the stump of this burned vine came the Lion of the tribe of Judah (Rev 5:5). Unlike the cubs of Ezekiel 19, he did not devour men. He laid down his life for them. He was not caught in a pit and led away with hooks against his will. He went to the cross freely, "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" (Isa 53:7). And where the vine of David withered in a dry and thirsty land, Jesus calls himself the true vine (John 15:1) and holds the scepter of a kingdom that will never end (Luke 1:32–33).
