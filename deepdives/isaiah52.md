*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC, more than a century after Isaiah's own day. Chapter 51 ended with Jerusalem lying in the street, drunk on the cup of God's wrath, and with God's promise to take that cup from her hand and give it to her tormentors (51:17–23). Chapter 52 picks up the same cry, "Awake, awake," and turns it into a wake-up call for a wedding rather than a funeral. A messenger runs over the hills with good news, watchmen shout, the ruins sing, and the exiles are told to leave Babylon clean. Then, at v13, the tone changes completely. God says "Behold, my servant," and the most famous passage in Isaiah begins. That poem runs from 52:13 to 53:12, so the last three verses of this chapter are really its opening stanza.

#### The LORD's Coming Salvation (vv1–12)
- **"Awake, awake, put on your strength, O Zion" (v1).** This is the third double "awake" in a row. The people cried to God's arm, "Awake, awake" (51:9). God answered, "Wake yourself, wake yourself" to a Jerusalem sprawled under judgment (51:17). Now he calls her to rise and dress. The cup is gone. It is time to stop lying in the dust.
- **"Put on your beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, the holy city" (v1).** Mourners wore sackcloth. Brides and priests wore fine clothes (49:18; 61:10; Exod 28:2). Zion is told to change clothes because her situation has changed. "Holy city" is a title that only makes sense if God has come back to live in her.
- **"There shall no more come into you the uncircumcised and the unclean" (v1).** In context this means foreign conquerors who trampled the temple (Lam 1:10). Babylon had marched in. That will not happen again. The promise reaches past anything the returned exiles saw, since Jerusalem was conquered again by Greeks and Romans. Revelation picks it up for the new Jerusalem: "nothing unclean will ever enter it" (Rev 21:27).
- **"Shake yourself from the dust and arise; be seated" (v2).** Captives sat in the dust and mourners sat on the ground (3:26; 47:1). The picture flips the one in chapter 47, where proud Babylon was told to come down and "sit in the dust." Now Babylon goes down and Zion gets up and takes her seat, perhaps on a throne. **"Loose the bonds from your neck"** pictures the slave's yoke or a prisoner's chain coming off.
- **"You were sold for nothing, and you shall be redeemed without money" (v3).** God did not "sell" his people to Babylon because he needed the money. He handed them over because of their sin (50:1). So he owes Babylon nothing, and he will buy them back without paying their captors a cent. "Redeem" (*gaʾal*) is the word for a kinsman-redeemer buying back a relative (Lev 25:47–49; Ruth 4). Cyrus later released the exiles without asking a ransom (45:13). Peter seems to echo this verse: "you were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19).
- **Egypt, Assyria and now Babylon (vv4–5).** God reviews history. Israel "went down at the first into Egypt to sojourn," as guests, and was enslaved. Assyria "oppressed them for nothing," without any right to. Now, he asks, "what have I here?" Same story again: "my people are taken away for nothing." Three oppressors, one pattern. The question "what have I here?" is God looking at the situation and deciding to act.
- **"Their rulers wail… and continually all the day my name is despised" (v5).** "Rulers" probably means the Babylonian overlords, howling in triumph, though some take it of Israel's own leaders wailing in captivity. Either way, God's name is mocked because his people are in chains: the nations assume Israel's God was too weak to save them. Paul quotes the Greek form of this verse against his fellow Jews: "the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you" (Rom 2:24). In Isaiah the blame falls on the oppressors. Paul turns it around and says that sin among God's people does the same damage.
- **"Therefore my people shall know my name… it is I who speak; here I am" (v6).** God acts for the sake of his name (48:9–11). Before the first Exodus, God told Moses his name (Exod 3:13–15). Before this new exodus, his people will learn it again by watching him act. "Here I am" (*hinneni*) is normally what a servant says when his master calls (Gen 22:1; 1 Sam 3:4; Isa 6:8). Here God says it to his people. He is the one answering their call.
- **"How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news" (v7).** Picture a city under siege, watchmen on the walls, and a lone runner coming over the hills. A runner who is fleeing looks different from one carrying victory news (2 Sam 18:24–27). These feet are dusty and sore, but to the watchmen they are beautiful because of what they carry. Nahum uses nearly the same line for the news of Nineveh's fall (Nah 1:15).
- **"Who brings good news" (v7).** The verb is *basar*, to carry good tidings, used twice in this verse. Its Greek equivalent in the Septuagint is *euangelizō*, the root of "evangelize" and "gospel." Isaiah uses it for the herald of 40:9 and for the anointed one who will "bring good news to the poor" (61:1). The good news has four parts here: peace (*shalom*), good (*tov*, "happiness"), salvation (*yeshuʿah*), and the headline, **"Your God reigns."** The exile looked like proof that Babylon's gods ruled. The message is that the LORD is king after all.
- **Paul and the beautiful feet.** In Romans 10:15 Paul quotes this verse to show that people cannot believe a message no one brings them. Preachers of the gospel are these messengers. Jesus himself came "proclaiming the gospel of God… the kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:14–15), which is v7's "Your God reigns" in Jesus' words.
- **"Eye to eye they see the return of the LORD to Zion" (v8).** The watchmen on the walls see it first and burst into song together. "Eye to eye" is a Hebrew idiom for seeing something up close and plainly (Num 14:14, "face to face"). The thing they see is not mainly the exiles coming home but the LORD coming home. Ezekiel saw God's glory leave the temple before its destruction (Ezek 10–11) and later return (Ezek 43:1–5).
- **"Break forth together into singing, you waste places of Jerusalem" (v9).** Even the rubble sings. **"The LORD has comforted his people; he has redeemed Jerusalem."** Both verbs are in the perfect tense, the "prophetic perfect" that speaks of a future act as already done because God has decided it. "Comforted" goes back to the keynote of 40:1.
- **"The LORD has bared his holy arm" (v10).** A warrior pulled back his cloak and rolled up his sleeve to fight or to work. God's arm, the people's plea in 51:9, is now stripped for action "before the eyes of all the nations." Verse 53:1 will ask who has believed it, "and to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" The answer will be surprising. **"All the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God."** Psalm 98:2–3 says almost the same, and Luke quotes the related line of 40:5 about John the Baptist (Luke 3:6). Simeon, holding the baby Jesus, says, "my eyes have seen your salvation" (Luke 2:30).
- **"Depart, depart, go out from there; touch no unclean thing" (v11).** Now the exiles are told to leave Babylon, a city full of idols. Leaving is not enough. They must leave clean. **"You who bear the vessels of the LORD"** refers to the priests and Levites who would carry the temple's gold and silver vessels, which Nebuchadnezzar had looted and Cyrus returned (Ezra 1:7–11). Holy things must be carried by purified hands (Num 4:15). Paul quotes this verse in 2 Corinthians 6:17 to tell believers not to be bound together with idolatry, and Revelation 18:4 sounds the same call to come out of "Babylon."
- **"You shall not go out in haste… for the LORD will go before you, and the God of Israel will be your rear guard" (v12).** At the first Exodus, Israel left in a hurry with bread still unrisen (Exod 12:11, 33–39; Deut 16:3, where "haste" is the same rare word). This exodus will be calm. The front and back of a column on the march were the most exposed places. God takes both. That recalls the pillar of cloud that went ahead of Israel and then moved behind them to stand between them and Pharaoh's army (Exod 13:21; 14:19–20).
- **Near and far fulfilment.** The return from Babylon in 538 BC was real but small, and it did not look like this. Many stayed in Babylon, the returned community struggled, and God's glory did not visibly fill the second temple. Most Christian readers, ancient and modern, see the return from exile as a first, partial fulfilment pointing to a greater deliverance in the gospel and its completion when Christ returns. The New Testament's use of vv7, 11 and 15 supports that reading.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that "redeemed without money" does not mean redemption is free. It costs, but someone else pays. JFB agrees that God pays the oppressors nothing and calls v1's promise of no more unclean invaders "a prophecy never yet fulfilled," which it places in the future. On v7 Henry says, "When bad news is abroad this is good news, and when good news is abroad this is the best news," and that the best news is that "the Lord Jesus reigns." Guzik, citing an older writer, explains the bared arm as rolling up sleeves for a big job, and JFB reads "eye to eye" as "close at hand, and so clearly," linking it to seeing face to face (1 Cor 13:12). On v11 Henry says simply, "Babylon is no place for Israelites," and on v12, "Those that are in the way of their duty are under God's special protection." Guzik applies v11 to Christian holiness through 2 Timothy 2:21, a cleansed person as a vessel for honor.

#### He Was Pierced for Our Transgressions (vv13–15)
- **The poem begins here.** The ESV heading for 52:13–53:12 comes from 53:5. This is the fourth of Isaiah's "Servant Songs" (42:1–9; 49:1–13; 50:4–11). The Tyndale notes describe it as five stanzas of three verses each. It opens with the Servant exalted (52:13), sinks to his humiliation and death (52:14–53:9), and rises again to his exaltation (53:10–12). The first stanza, here, gives the whole story in brief: lifted up, disfigured, then astonishing the world.
- **"Behold, my servant shall act wisely" (v13).** The verb is *sakal*, to have insight and act with it, and by extension to succeed because one acts wisely. The KJV's "deal prudently" and the NIV's "act wisely" both fit, and others translate "prosper." The same verb describes David, who "had success in all his undertakings" (1 Sam 18:14), and the coming righteous Branch who will "reign as king and deal wisely" (Jer 23:5). The Servant wins by wise obedience, not by force.
- **"He shall be high and lifted up, and shall be exalted" (v13).** These words echo Isaiah's vision of the Lord "high and lifted up" on his throne (6:1) and the "High and Lofty One" of 57:15. Isaiah uses this pairing for God himself. Here it is given to the Servant. John seems to connect them: he quotes 53:1 and 6:10 side by side and says Isaiah "saw his glory and spoke of him," meaning Jesus (John 12:38–41). John also uses "lifted up" for the cross itself (John 3:14; 12:32–33).
- **"As many were astonished at you" (v14).** "Astonished" means appalled, horrified, struck dumb (the same word is used of people staring at a ruined land, Lev 26:32). The sudden "you" is a little jarring. It may address the Servant directly, or Zion. Several ancient versions read "at him."
- **"His appearance was so marred, beyond human semblance" (v14).** The Hebrew is strong: his look was disfigured until it was hardly human. The word *mishkhat* is from a root meaning "ruin" or "corrupt." Before the cross Jesus was beaten in the face, spat on, struck with a reed, crowned with thorns and flogged (Matt 26:67; 27:26–30; John 19:1–3). Roman flogging could leave a man barely recognizable.
- **"So shall he sprinkle many nations" (v15).** This is the most debated word in the passage. The verb *nazah* in this form is used throughout the Law for a priest sprinkling blood, oil or water to cleanse and consecrate (Lev 4:6; 14:7; 16:14–15; Num 19:18–19). If that is the meaning here, the disfigured Servant does a priest's work, cleansing not just Israel but "many nations." The Greek Septuagint, however, reads "so shall many nations marvel at him," and many modern versions follow a similar sense, "startle" (NIV, NRSV). The lexicon lists "startle" as a possible second root meaning "leap," but marks it dubious. The ESV keeps "sprinkle," which matches the word's ordinary use and the priestly language of 53:10–12 ("an offering for guilt"). Both readings make good sense in the parallel line: as many were horrified at him, so many will be either cleansed by him or stunned by him.
- **"Kings shall shut their mouths because of him" (v15).** People put a hand over their mouth in awe or shame (Job 29:9; 40:4; Mic 7:16). Rulers who expected to judge this broken figure will find themselves speechless before him.
- **"That which has not been told them they see, and that which they have not heard they understand" (v15).** Gentiles who never had the prophets will see and understand. Paul quotes this line (in the Greek) to explain why he preaches where Christ has not been named: "Those who have never been told of him will see, and those who have never heard will understand" (Rom 15:21).
- **Who is the Servant?** Jewish interpretation from the Middle Ages onward has often read the Servant as the nation of Israel suffering among the nations, and Israel is called God's servant in these chapters (41:8; 44:1). But this Servant suffers for others' sins and is himself innocent (53:4–9), and earlier songs give him a mission *to* Israel (49:5–6). Some older Jewish sources read the passage as messianic. The New Testament applies it to Jesus repeatedly (Matt 8:17; Luke 22:37; Acts 8:30–35; 1 Pet 2:22–25).
- **Commentators:** Guzik points out that the exaltation is announced before the suffering, so the reader knows how the story ends before the hard part begins. He ties the marred appearance to the mocking soldiers (Luke 22:63–64) and reads "sprinkle" as cleansing, with Exodus 24:8 and Ezekiel 36:25. JFB rejects "startle" in favor of "sprinkle," saying the Servant cleanses the nations "as the high priest makes an expiation." It also reads "marred" as "not only disfigured, but disfigurement itself," and thinks these verses should begin chapter 53. Henry agrees on "sprinkle" and applies it to Christ's blood, which "shall be applied to their consciences, to purify them," and to the gospel going out to all nations. The Tyndale notes, by contrast, follow "startle," so here the commentators part company with the notes. The lexicon allows both, with "sprinkle" as the ordinary meaning.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter holds two kinds of news side by side. First, a runner on the mountains cries "Your God reigns," and the LORD bares his arm and comes home to Zion. Then the arm turns out to belong to a Servant so disfigured that people looked away. Paul applies the beautiful feet to every preacher of Christ, and the speechless kings to every nation that hears of him for the first time. The good news is that God reigns, and the cross is how he reigns: through a Servant marred beyond recognition, lifted up, and sprinkling many nations clean.
