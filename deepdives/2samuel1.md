*Setting:* About 1010 BC, a few days after the battle on Mount Gilboa. 1 and 2 Samuel were one book in the Hebrew Bible, and the split here is only for length, so this chapter picks up the story directly. While Saul and his sons were dying in the north (1 Sam 31), David was in the far south, chasing down the Amalekites who had burned Ziklag and carried off his family (1 Sam 30). He has won that fight and come home to a burned-out town. He does not yet know that Saul is dead. For about ten years he has been hunted by Saul, and twice he refused to kill him when he could have (1 Sam 24; 26). Now the news arrives, and how David receives it shows what kind of king he will be.

#### David Hears of Saul's Death (vv1–16)
- **"When David had returned from striking down the Amalekites" (v1).** The chapter opens by tying two stories together. David has just defeated Amalekites. Saul lost his kingdom over his failure to destroy the Amalekites (1 Sam 15). And the man who now arrives to say Saul is dead is an Amalekite.
- **"With his clothes torn and dirt on his head" (v2).** These were the standard signs of mourning and bad news (1 Sam 4:12, where a Benjaminite runs from the battle at Aphek in the same state). The man plays the part well. He also "fell to the ground and paid homage," bowing to David as if to the next king.
- **"Saul and his son Jonathan are also dead" (v4).** He names only Jonathan of the three sons who died (1 Sam 31:2). The Tyndale notes suggest he may not have known about the others, or he mentioned the one son who could have stood between David and the throne.
- **"By chance I happened to be on Mount Gilboa" (v6).** His story begins to sound rehearsed. An Amalekite wandering a battlefield "by chance" is far more likely to have been stripping the dead.
- **Saul "leaning on his spear" (v6).** Here is the hard question. In 1 Samuel 31:4–5 Saul fell on his own sword, and his armor-bearer saw that he was dead. Now the Amalekite says Saul was still alive, leaning on his spear, and begged to be killed. There are two main readings. Most commentators think the Amalekite lied: he found Saul's body before the Philistines did, took the crown and armlet, and made up a story to win a reward. A minority think both accounts are true: Saul's fall on his sword did not kill him at once, and the Amalekite finished him. The narrator gave the reliable account in the previous chapter and lets the reader judge this one. Either way, the man's own claim condemns him.
- **"Anguish has seized me, and yet my life still lingers" (v9).** The word for "anguish" is rare and its meaning uncertain. Some translate it "cramp" or "dizziness." The point is the same: a dying man who cannot finish dying.
- **"I took the crown… and the armlet" (v10).** The crown here is *nezer*, from a root meaning "to set apart" or "consecrate." It is the same word used for the high priest's holy crown (Exod 29:6) and linked to the Nazirite vow. Saul's crown marked him as set apart by God. The Amalekite brings it to David as a gift, expecting thanks for clearing the way to the throne.
- **"David took hold of his clothes and tore them" (v11).** David does not celebrate. He and all his men tear their clothes, weep and fast until evening. They mourn "for Saul and for Jonathan his son and for the people of the LORD and for the house of Israel" (v12). David grieves for the king who hunted him, for his closest friend, and for the nation now defeated and exposed.
- **"I am the son of a sojourner, an Amalekite" (v13).** David asks again, "Where do you come from?" This time the answer matters. A "sojourner" was a resident foreigner who lived among Israel and was expected to know its ways. This man had lived in Israel long enough to know what the king was.
- **"How is it you were not afraid to put out your hand to destroy the LORD's anointed?" (v14).** This is exactly the line David himself would not cross. "Who can put out his hand against the LORD's anointed and be guiltless?" (1 Sam 26:9). David had left Saul's end to God: "the LORD will strike him, or his day will come to die, or he will go down into battle and perish" (26:10). That last option is just what happened. A foreigner claims the honor of doing what David refused to do.
- **"Go, execute him" (v15).** This can seem harsh. But the man confessed, by his own testimony, to killing the king, and David treats it as a capital crime. **"Your blood be on your head, for your own mouth has testified against you" (v16).** Whether his story was true or a lie, he was condemned by his own words (compare Luke 19:22). The execution also made it plain to all Israel that David had no part in Saul's death and that he would not reward anyone who raised a hand against the king. David will act the same way when men bring him the head of Saul's son Ish-bosheth (4:9–12).
- **Commentators:** All three treat the Amalekite's tale as false. JFB calls it "a fiction of his own" told to win David's favor, and Henry notes that whatever the truth, "the Amalekite confessed the crime." Guzik draws a picture from the Amalekites, whom Saul spared against God's command: what Saul would not put to death ends up claiming credit for his death. Henry and Guzik both stress that David's grief was real. Henry: "David was sincere in his mourning for Saul." Guzik adds that David chose not to be bitter toward the man who had made his life so hard. On the execution, Guzik argues that ending Saul's reign was God's business, not any man's, even when Saul was failing and asked to die. JFB describes the crown as "a small metallic cap or wreath, which encircled the temples."

#### David's Lament for Saul and Jonathan (vv17–27)
- **"David lamented with this lamentation" (v17).** The Hebrew word *qinah* means a funeral song, usually sung with a limping rhythm, a long line followed by a shorter one. David was a gifted musician and poet (1 Sam 16:18), and this is one of the few poems in Samuel that the text says he wrote. It is a public poem, not a private prayer. Strikingly, it never mentions God. It is pure grief.
- **"It should be taught to the people of Judah" (v18).** The Hebrew reads literally "to teach the sons of Judah the bow." Many take "The Bow" as the song's title, perhaps because of Jonathan's bow in v22. Others think it means the song was to be taught along with training in archery. The ESV leaves the word out of its main text.
- **"Written in the Book of Jashar" (v18).** *Jashar* means "upright." The book is also cited for Joshua's long day (Josh 10:13). It was probably a collection of Israel's national poems and songs, and it has not survived. Books mentioned in the Bible are not necessarily Scripture. The inspired text gives us what God meant us to have.
- **"Your glory, O Israel, is slain on your high places!" (v19).** "Glory" is *tsevi*. Hebrew has two words with that spelling: one means "beauty" or "glory," the other "gazelle." Some read "the gazelle of Israel," a picture of a swift, graceful warrior. The lexicon places this verse under "beauty, honor," and the ESV follows that. Either way it means the pride of the nation, Saul and Jonathan. "High places" here simply means the heights of Gilboa.
- **"How the mighty have fallen!" (v19).** This refrain comes three times (vv19, 25, 27) and frames the poem. It has become a proverb in English.
- **"Tell it not in Gath, publish it not in the streets of Ashkelon" (v20).** Gath and Ashkelon were two of the five main Philistine cities. David knew Gath very well, because he had lived there as Achish's servant (1 Sam 27). He does not want the enemy to rejoice. Victory songs were often sung by women (Exod 15:20–21), and "the daughters of the Philistines" would do what the women of Israel once did for David (1 Sam 18:6–7). Micah later echoes the line (Mic 1:10).
- **"You mountains of Gilboa, let there be no dew or rain upon you" (v21).** David curses the place where they died, in poetry, not as a literal prayer. The Tyndale notes point to a Canaanite text from Ugarit, from around the 1300s BC, where a grieving father calls down drought on the place where his son was killed. "Fields of offerings" may mean fields so rich they produced grain for offerings. The text is difficult, and some scholars suggest a slightly different reading.
- **"The shield of Saul, not anointed with oil" (v21).** Leather shields were rubbed with oil to keep them from cracking and to make blows slide off (Isa 21:5). Saul's shield lies on the mountain uncared for. The word "anointed" may carry a double sense, because Saul had been the LORD's *anointed* (*mashiach*, v14). The shield is unoiled, and its anointed owner is dead.
- **"The bow of Jonathan turned not back, and the sword of Saul returned not empty" (v22).** David praises them as fighters. Jonathan's bow is the one he used to send David his warning signal (1 Sam 20:20, 35–40), and the one he gave David as a sign of their covenant (18:4).
- **"Saul and Jonathan, beloved and lovely! In life and in death they were not divided" (v23).** This is remarkable grace. Saul and Jonathan were often at odds, and Saul once threw a spear at his own son over David (1 Sam 20:33). But Jonathan never abandoned his father. He fought beside him to the end. David chooses to remember the best of Saul. "Swifter than eagles… stronger than lions" were the highest praises of a warrior.
- **"You daughters of Israel, weep over Saul" (v24).** Saul's victories brought plunder home, and the women wore its fine cloth and gold. The women who once sang "Saul has struck down his thousands" (1 Sam 18:7) are now told to weep.
- **"I am distressed for you, my brother Jonathan" (v26).** Now the poem becomes personal. Jonathan was not David's blood brother, but he was his covenant brother (1 Sam 18:3; 20:8, 42). "Very pleasant have you been to me."
- **"Your love to me was extraordinary, surpassing the love of women" (v26).** Some modern readers have claimed this means a sexual relationship. The text gives no support for that. "Love" (*ahavah*) in the Old Testament is used for loyalty between friends, allies and covenant partners (1 Sam 18:1, 3; 1 Kgs 5:1). The point is the depth and cost of Jonathan's loyalty. He gave up his own claim to the throne for David (1 Sam 23:17), risked his life for him and defied his father for him. No one in David's life, not even his wives, had given him that kind of self-giving love. It is also worth remembering that David's marriages so far were shaped by politics and danger more than devotion (1 Sam 18:20–27; 25:42–43).
- **"And the weapons of war perished!" (v27).** The final refrain. "Weapons of war" is probably a poetic way of speaking of Saul and Jonathan themselves, Israel's great warriors, now gone.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the lament as proof of David's generous spirit, noting that "Jonathan was a dutiful son, Saul an affectionate father, therefore dear to each other," which is why David says they were not divided. Guzik observes that David speaks no evil of Saul, a living example of love covering a multitude of sins (1 Pet 4:8), though he also notes that Saul had fallen spiritually long before he fell on Gilboa. On v26 Guzik says plainly that the friendship was godly and not sexual. JFB reads v19 as "literally, 'the gazelle' or 'antelope of Israel,'" though the lexicon lists this verse under the other word, "beauty" or "glory." On v21 JFB explains that "to cast away the shield was counted a national disgrace," so Saul's abandoned shield on the mountain is part of the shame David grieves. Guzik cautions against treating the Book of Jashar as a lost book of Scripture.

#### Pointing to Christ
David refuses to grab the throne. He waits for God to give it, he will not reward the man who claims to have cleared his path, and he honors his enemy in death. That is a picture of a greater Son of David, who did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped and who received his kingdom from the Father's hand, not by seizing it (Phil 2:6–9). David's love for the man who hunted him points toward Jesus, who prayed for those who crucified him (Luke 23:34). And Jonathan's love, which gave up a crown so that his friend could have it, points to the deepest love of all: "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends" (John 15:13).
