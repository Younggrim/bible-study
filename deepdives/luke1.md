*Setting:* Luke, a Gentile doctor and Paul's travel companion (Col 4:14; 2 Tim 4:11), wrote this Gospel and its sequel, Acts, probably in the 60s or 70s AD. The story opens around 7–6 BC, late in the reign of Herod the Great, an Idumean king installed by Rome who rebuilt the Jerusalem temple on a grand scale. Israel has heard no prophet for about four hundred years. The last prophetic words in the Christian Old Testament promised a messenger, "Elijah the prophet," who would turn hearts before the day of the LORD (Mal 4:5–6). Luke 1 begins where Malachi left off: in the temple, with a priest, a promise of a forerunner, and the angel who last appeared to Daniel. Through chapters 1–2 Luke tells two birth stories side by side, John's and Jesus', and at every step Jesus is greater.

#### Dedication to Theophilus (vv1–4)
- **"The things that have been accomplished among us" (v1).** The verb means "fully carried out" or "fulfilled." Luke is writing about events that brought God's promises to completion (4:21; 24:44).
- **"Having followed all things closely… an orderly account" (v3).** Luke investigated. In Acts the "we" passages put him in Judea for about two years while Paul was held at Caesarea (Acts 21:17; 27:1), plenty of time to interview people, perhaps even Mary. "Orderly" means well arranged, not necessarily in strict time order.
- **"Most excellent Theophilus" (v3).** The name means "loved by God" or "friend of God," but it was a real personal name. "Most excellent" (*kratistos*) is a title of rank Luke also uses for the governors Felix and Festus (Acts 23:26; 26:25). Theophilus was probably a well-placed patron who paid for the copying of Luke's work, as was common for books then.
- **Luke's Greek.** These four verses are one long sentence in polished literary Greek, in the style of historians such as Thucydides. Then at v5 the style shifts abruptly into the flavor of the Greek Old Testament. Luke is signaling that this story belongs in both worlds: real history, and the continuation of Israel's Scriptures.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the preface is one sentence in the original, in formal academic style, and that Theophilus was probably a Roman official. JFB agrees that "most excellent" is "a title of rank" and thinks Theophilus may have been a magistrate. Henry observes that Luke writes of things "most surely believed," and "will not write of things about which Christians may safely differ."

#### Birth of John the Baptist Foretold (vv5–25)
- **"A priest named Zechariah, of the division of Abijah" (v5).** David organized the priests into twenty-four divisions (1 Chr 24:1–19), and Abijah's was the eighth (1 Chr 24:10). Each division served in the temple for one week, twice a year, plus the great festivals. Zechariah's name means "the LORD remembers." His wife was also of priestly descent, "from the daughters of Aaron," and her name, Elizabeth (Hebrew *Elisheba*), was the name of Aaron's own wife (Exod 6:23). It means "my God is an oath."
- **"Both righteous before God, walking blamelessly" (v6).** This does not mean sinless. Zechariah is about to doubt an angel. It means they lived faithfully under the covenant, offering the sacrifices and keeping the commands. They are pictures of the godly remnant Malachi spoke of (Mal 3:16).
- **"But they had no child" (v7).** In that culture childlessness brought shame, and people often assumed it was God's judgment. Luke deliberately recalls the barren women of Israel's story whose sons God gave: Sarah (Gen 18), Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother and Hannah (1 Sam 1). Elizabeth stands in that line. "Advanced in years" echoes Abraham and Sarah (Gen 18:11).
- **"He was chosen by lot to enter the temple of the Lord and burn incense" (v9).** There were thousands of priests, so the privilege of offering incense in the Holy Place was assigned by lot, and Jewish tradition says a priest could do it only once in his life. This was the high point of Zechariah's career. The incense altar stood just before the curtain that hid the Most Holy Place. Incense was offered every morning and evening, and its rising smoke pictured prayer going up to God (Ps 141:2; Rev 8:3–4).
- **"You shall call his name John" (v13).** *Yohanan* means "the LORD has been gracious." God names him before he is conceived.
- **"He must not drink wine or strong drink" (v15).** This echoes the Nazirite vow of special dedication (Num 6:1–4). Like Samson and Samuel, John is set apart for life (Judg 13:4–5; 1 Sam 1:11). Instead of wine, he "will be filled with the Holy Spirit, even from his mother's womb" (compare Eph 5:18).
- **"In the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children" (v17).** This quotes Malachi 4:5–6 almost word for word. The angel is saying: the messenger Malachi promised is about to be born. "To make ready for the Lord a people prepared" echoes Malachi 3:1 and Isaiah 40:3. "The Lord" whom John goes before is the LORD God himself (v16), and the rest of Luke shows him to be Jesus.
- **"How shall I know this?" (v18).** Abraham asked almost the same question (Gen 15:8). Zechariah wants a sign, and gets one. His question and Mary's (v34) sound alike, but they differ. He asks for proof that it will happen. She asks how it will happen, given that she is a virgin. The angel treats one as unbelief (v20) and the other as faith (v45).
- **"I am Gabriel. I stand in the presence of God" (v19).** Only two angels are named in the Protestant Bible: Gabriel and Michael. Gabriel's earlier appearances were to Daniel, explaining visions of the end and the "seventy weeks" leading to "an anointed one" (Dan 8:16; 9:21–27). His return signals that the long countdown is closing. "Good news" here is the verb *euangelizō*, the root of "gospel."
- **"You will be silent and unable to speak" (v20).** The discipline is also the sign he asked for. The word for "mute" in v22 (*kōphos*) can also mean deaf, and in v62 people make signs to him, which suggests he could not hear either. The priest was supposed to come out and pronounce the blessing over the people (Num 6:24–26). Zechariah comes out and cannot say a word.
- **Commentators:** Guzik describes the lot and the incense in detail and observes that Zechariah's unbelief did not cancel God's promise, though it robbed him of the joy of speaking about it. He also suspects Zechariah had long ago stopped asking for a son. Henry, in his concise commentary, notes that the couple were "sinners as all are, and were justified and saved in the same way as others," and that "Zacharias heard all that the angel said; but his unbelief spake." JFB notes Abijah was "the eighth of the twenty-four" courses and that incense was "the most distinguished part of the service." On v17 JFB adds that "John did no miracle" (John 10:41), so Elijah's "power" here is the power of his preaching, not of signs. On Zechariah's silence JFB says dryly, "He asked a sign, and now he got it."

#### Birth of Jesus Foretold (vv26–38)
- **"A city of Galilee named Nazareth" (v26).** The contrast with the previous scene is deliberate. Then: a priest, a holy man, in the temple in Jerusalem, at the hour of prayer. Now: a young woman, in an unremarkable village in the north that the Old Testament never mentions, in an ordinary house. "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" (John 1:46).
- **"A virgin betrothed to a man whose name was Joseph, of the house of David" (v27).** Betrothal was a binding legal agreement, broken only by divorce (Matt 1:19). The couple did not yet live together. Girls were usually betrothed in their early to mid teens, so Mary was probably young. Luke says "virgin" twice in one verse. Joseph's descent from David gives Jesus his legal right to David's throne.
- **"Greetings, O favored one, the Lord is with you!" (v28).** "Favored one" is *kecharitōmenē*, from *charitoō*, "to endow with grace." The lexicon's sense here is "to endue with divine favor." The word appears only once more in the New Testament, where Paul says God's grace was "freely given us in the Beloved" (Eph 1:6). The Latin Vulgate rendered it *gratia plena*, "full of grace," which became the opening of the Hail Mary. The form is passive: Mary has *received* grace. She is favored because God chose her, not because she earned it.
- **Different traditions.** Roman Catholic and Orthodox Christians honor Mary highly here, and Catholic teaching sees in this greeting support for her preservation from sin. Protestants generally read it as God's unearned favor shown to a humble believer, as at Ephesians 1:6, and point to Mary calling God "my Savior" (v47). All agree that the text presents her as uniquely chosen and a model of faith, and that "all generations will call me blessed" (v48).
- **"You shall call his name Jesus" (v31).** *Yeshua*, a form of Joshua, means "the LORD saves" (Matt 1:21).
- **"Son of the Most High… the throne of his father David… of his kingdom there will be no end" (vv32–33).** Nearly every phrase comes from God's covenant with David: "I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever. I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Sam 7:13–16; also Isa 9:7). Gabriel is announcing the promised King. "The house of Jacob" is Israel.
- **"How will this be, since I am a virgin?" (v34).** Literally, "since I do not know a man," the Bible's usual way of speaking of sexual relations. She is not doubting. She is asking how, since the child is to come now, not after the wedding. Some older interpreters read her words as a vow of lifelong virginity, but the text does not say that.
- **"The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you" (v35).** "Overshadow" (*episkiazō*) is the word used of the cloud covering the mountain at the Transfiguration (9:34), and in the Greek Old Testament of the glory-cloud settling on the tabernacle (Exod 40:35). God's presence will rest on Mary as it rested on the tabernacle. The language is reverent and restrained. There is no hint of the crude stories of gods and women found in pagan myth. This is creation, not union.
- **"The child to be born will be called holy—the Son of God" (v35).** Jesus did not *become* God's Son at his conception. John 1 says the Word was with God in the beginning. But his human life began in a unique way, by the Spirit, and he is holy from the first moment. Isaiah 7:14 promised that "the virgin shall conceive and bear a son," and Matthew quotes it (Matt 1:23).
- **"For nothing will be impossible with God" (v37).** This echoes the LORD's words to Abraham about Sarah: "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" (Gen 18:14). The Greek reads literally "no word (*rhēma*) will be impossible from God," that is, no word God speaks will fail.
- **"Behold, I am the servant of the Lord; let it be to me according to your word" (v38).** "Servant" is *doulē*, a female slave, the KJV's "handmaid." She accepts at great cost: a pregnancy before marriage could mean shame, the loss of Joseph and, under the law, even death (Deut 22:20–21). Her answer is the model of faith in Luke: hearing the word and submitting to it (8:21; 11:28).
- **Commentators:** Guzik says Gabriel's description of Mary as favored and blessed is accurate and worth honoring, but that the grace was received, not something Mary dispenses to others. He also notes that "overshadow" recalls the cloud of God's glory. JFB is blunter: he calls the Vulgate's "full of grace" a mistake which "the Romish Church" has "taken abundant advantage of," and says *charitoō* means "a favoured one," as in Ephesians 1:6. The lexicon supports the meaning "endued with divine favor." On v35 JFB calls the overshadowing "the immediate energy of the Godhead conveyed by the Holy Ghost." Henry calls Mary's reply "the language of faith and humble admiration," and notes that she, unlike Zechariah, asked for no sign.

#### Mary Visits Elizabeth (vv39–45)
- **"Went with haste into the hill country, to a town in Judah" (v39).** The town is not named. Traditions place it at Ein Karem, just west of Jerusalem. JFB suggests Hebron, a priestly city. Either way it was a journey of about 80–100 miles from Nazareth, several days on foot. Mary goes to the one person who could understand.
- **"The baby leaped in her womb" (v41).** The word *skirtaō* is used in the Greek Old Testament of Jacob and Esau struggling within Rebekah (Gen 25:22). Before he is born, John is already doing his work, pointing to Jesus. As promised, he is "filled with the Holy Spirit, even from his mother's womb" (v15).
- **"The mother of my Lord" (v43).** An older woman, wife of a priest, honors a young girl from Galilee. And she calls the unborn child "my Lord." In Luke "Lord" is a title that belongs to God (1:16–17). Elizabeth, by the Spirit, recognizes who Jesus is before he is born.
- **"Blessed is she who believed" (v45).** This is the key to the section. Mary is blessed not only for bearing the Lord but for trusting his word. The contrast with Zechariah, Elizabeth's own husband, who sits mute beside her, is unspoken but clear.
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that Mary's faith acted: she went, she said yes, she believed God's promise would be fulfilled. Henry, in the concise commentary, says it is "very good for those who have the work of grace begun in their souls, to communicate one to another." JFB names Hebron as the likely town, citing its status as a priests' city (Josh 21:11).

#### Mary's Song of Praise: The Magnificat (vv46–56)
- **Hannah's song echoes through it.** Hannah, also given a child by God against all odds, sang: "My heart exults in the LORD… The LORD makes poor and makes rich; he brings low and he exalts" (1 Sam 2:1–10). Mary's song is full of that and of the Psalms and the prophets. A young woman from a village knew her Scriptures so well that her praise came out in their words.
- **"My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior" (vv46–47).** Soul and spirit are Hebrew parallelism, two ways of saying "my whole self." "Magnify" means to make great, not to make God bigger than he is but to declare his greatness. "God my Savior" (*sōtēr*) is notable. Mary, by her own words, needs a Savior.
- **"He has looked on the humble estate of his servant" (v48).** "Humble estate" (*tapeinōsis*) is the word Hannah used in her prayer in the Greek Old Testament: "if you will look on the affliction of your servant" (1 Sam 1:11). Mary sees herself as low and God as the one who stoops.
- **The great reversals (vv51–53).** The verbs are in the past tense (Greek aorist), though they describe what God is beginning now. The prophets often spoke of future acts as already done because God's word is that certain. God has "scattered the proud," "brought down the mighty from their thrones," "exalted those of humble estate," "filled the hungry," and "sent away" the rich "empty." This upside-down kingdom runs all through Luke: the Beatitudes and woes (6:20–26), the rich man and Lazarus (16:19–31), the tax collector justified rather than the Pharisee (18:9–14). "His arm" is a picture of God's power, used of the exodus (Exod 6:6; Ps 98:1).
- **"He has helped his servant Israel… as he spoke to our fathers, to Abraham and to his offspring forever" (vv54–55).** Gabriel's words recalled the covenant with David. Mary's song goes further back, to the covenant with Abraham (Gen 12:1–3; 17:7). God is keeping promises two thousand years old.
- **Commentators:** Guzik counts at least twelve Old Testament allusions beyond Hannah's song, evidence of how deeply Mary knew Scripture. He argues that "God my Savior" shows Mary herself needed saving, against the Catholic teaching of the Immaculate Conception. Henry agrees: "She knew herself to be a sinner who needed a Saviour." JFB says "the strain of Hannah's ancient song, in like circumstances, is caught up" and slightly modified, so that Mary's song, like Hannah's, ends pointing to God's anointed King.

#### The Birth of John the Baptist (vv57–66)
- **"He asked for a writing tablet and wrote, 'His name is John'" (v63).** A writing tablet was a wooden board coated with wax. Notice the wording: not "his name *will be* John," but "his name *is* John." God named him, and the matter is settled. This is Zechariah's act of faith, his obedience to the angel's word nine months late.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the naming as Zechariah's second chance to believe, and notes that the people treated him as deaf. JFB agrees that the signs show "he was deaf, as well as dumb." Henry says the name means "Gracious," fitting because John would "bring in the gospel of Christ, wherein God's grace shines most bright."

#### Zechariah's Prophecy (vv67–80)
- **"He has visited and redeemed his people" (v68).** "Visited" is a Bible word for God coming to act, in rescue or judgment. He "visited" Israel in Egypt (Exod 4:31). "Redeemed" means set free by paying a price, the language of the exodus. Zechariah speaks in the past tense of what God is doing through a child not yet born. The song begins with Jesus, not John.
- **"A horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" (v69).** A horn was the symbol of an animal's strength, and in Scripture of a king's power (1 Sam 2:10; Ps 132:17). "I will make a horn to sprout for David" (Ps 132:17). Jesus, still in Mary's womb, is that horn. Zechariah, a priest of Levi, rejoices in a king from Judah.
- **"To remember his holy covenant, the oath that he swore to our father Abraham" (vv72–73).** Listen to the names. Zechariah means "the LORD remembers." Elizabeth means "my God is an oath." John means "the LORD is gracious," and "mercy" (v72) is close kin to grace. The family's names are being fulfilled before their eyes. The oath is God's sworn promise to Abraham after the near-sacrifice of Isaac: "in your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed" (Gen 22:16–18).
- **"And you, child, will be called the prophet of the Most High" (v76).** Zechariah turns to the baby in his arms. Jesus is "Son of the Most High" (v32). John is "prophet of the Most High." The difference is the point. "You will go before the Lord to prepare his ways" quotes Malachi 3:1 and Isaiah 40:3.
- **"To give knowledge of salvation to his people in the forgiveness of their sins" (v77).** This is what salvation finally means. John would preach "a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins" (3:3).
- **"The sunrise shall visit us from on high" (v78).** "Sunrise" is *anatolē*, the dawn. In the Greek Old Testament the same word translates the Hebrew *tsemach*, "Branch," the messianic title in Jeremiah 23:5 and Zechariah 3:8 and 6:12. Luke's word may carry both pictures: the royal Branch of David and the dawning light. It also echoes Malachi's "sun of righteousness" (Mal 4:2) and Isaiah's promise: "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (Isa 9:2).
- **"The child grew and became strong in spirit, and he was in the wilderness" (v80).** Luke compresses about thirty years into one verse. The wilderness of Judea lies east of Jerusalem toward the Dead Sea. Some scholars have wondered whether John had contact with the Qumran community there, who also lived in the desert to "prepare the way of the Lord" (Isa 40:3). It is possible but unproven. The text says only that God kept him there "until the day of his public appearance to Israel." Compare 2:40 and 2:52, where Luke says almost the same of Jesus, and again Jesus is greater.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that a prophecy given at the birth of Zechariah's son is mostly about someone else's Son, which is a mark of the Spirit's work. JFB explains the "horn of salvation" as "mighty Salvation," the Savior himself, from animals whose strength is in their horns. On the "dayspring" JFB allows that it means either Christ as the rising light or "the light which He sheds," and adds that "the sense, of course, is one." JFB thinks John went to the wilderness to be free from rabbinic influence. Henry calls the song "a prophecy concerning the kingdom and salvation of the Messiah."

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 1 is a chapter of answered prayer and kept promises. God remembers his covenant with Abraham, his oath to David, Malachi's word about Elijah. A priest's long-silent prayer is heard, a barren woman bears a son, and a virgin conceives by the Holy Spirit. Everything points past John to the child in Mary's womb, the Son of the Most High whose kingdom will have no end. He is the horn of salvation, the dawn from on high, the Lord before whom even an unborn prophet leaps for joy. And the right response is Mary's: "let it be to me according to your word."
