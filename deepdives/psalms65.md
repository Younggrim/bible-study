*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David. A Song." After a long run of David's prayers from danger, the mood lifts completely. There are no enemies in this psalm, no complaints, no requests. It is pure thanksgiving, and the subject widens from forgiveness in the temple courts to the oceans, the mountains, the nations, and finally the rain on newly plowed fields. The occasion is not stated. Because it ends with grain, flocks and the "crowned" year, many readers, Guzik among them, think it was sung at one of Israel's harvest festivals, perhaps the Feast of Tabernacles in the autumn, when the year's crops were gathered in (Lev 23:39–43). Some think of a year when rain came after drought (2 Sam 21:1, 10; 24:25). The phrase "your temple" (v4) has made some scholars date the psalm later than David, since Solomon built the temple, but the same word (*hekal*) was used of the tabernacle at Shiloh (1 Sam 1:9; 3:3), so it does not settle anything. Henry divides the psalm into two kingdoms: God's goodness in "the kingdom of grace" (vv1–5) and in "the kingdom of Providence" (vv6–13). Psalms 65–68 form a group of joyful songs, each called both "a Psalm" and "a Song" in its title

#### Praise Is Due to You, O God, in Zion (vv1–4)
- **"Praise is due to you, O God, in Zion" (v1).** The Hebrew text reads literally, "To you silence is praise," or "To you there is silence, praise." The word is *dumiyyah*, the same "silence" that opened Psalm 62. The ESV follows the ancient Greek and Syriac translations, which read a closely related word meaning "fitting" or "due." The King James has "Praise waiteth for thee." JFB offers two senses: praise waits like a servant, or silence itself is praise. Henry: "As there are holy groanings which cannot be uttered, so there are holy adorings which cannot be uttered." Morgan and Kidner, cited by Guzik, note that the deepest worship sometimes runs out of words (Hab 2:20). Both readings are true: praise is owed to God, and sometimes the best praise is awed silence.
- **"In Zion" (v1).** Zion was the hill in Jerusalem where David brought the ark (2 Sam 6:12–17), and later the temple mount. It was the place where God chose to put his name (Deut 12:5). Henry: praise is due from all the world, "but it waits for him in Zion only, in his church."
- **"And to you shall vows be performed" (v1).** Worshipers who had made vows in time of need came to the sanctuary to keep them, usually with a thank offering (Lev 7:16; Ps 66:13–15). If the psalm marks a harvest after hard times, these are the vows made while praying for rain. JFB links praise and vows to 50:14: "Offer to God a sacrifice of thanksgiving, and perform your vows to the Most High."
- **"O you who hear prayer, to you shall all flesh come" (v2).** A beautiful title for God, "Hearer of prayer." The gods of the nations had ears that "do not hear" (115:6). Israel's God does. "All flesh" means all humanity, not just Israel, as the Tyndale notes say. Isaiah looks ahead to the day when "all flesh shall come to worship before me" (Isa 66:23), and Solomon prayed that foreigners would come to the temple and be heard (1 Kgs 8:41–43). Henry links it to the day when God's house would be "a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isa 56:7).
- **"When iniquities prevail against me, you atone for our transgressions" (v3).** Before anyone can enjoy God's house, sin has to be dealt with. "Prevail" means to be too strong for, to overpower. JFB: literally "words of iniquities," the record of sins, like charges read out in court. The singer speaks personally ("against me"), then for everyone ("our transgressions"). "Atone" (*kipper*) is the great word of Israel's sacrificial system, used for the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:30). It means to cover, to wipe clean, to make reconciliation by a payment. Guzik says David looked beyond animal sacrifice to the perfect atonement God would provide. Trapp, cited by Guzik, notes that no one is ever turned away for confessing how bad they are.
- **"Blessed is the one you choose and bring near, to dwell in your courts!" (v4).** God takes the initiative twice: he chooses, and he brings near. The verb "bring near" (*qarav*) is the word used for bringing an offering near the altar, and for priests drawing near to God (Num 16:5: "the one whom he chooses he will bring near to him"). Some think the verse is about priests, but Guzik notes that the courts were open to ordinary worshipers, and the Tyndale notes include people from all nations. Henry: we come "not recommended by any merit of our own… but by God's free choice," and those whom God chooses "he causes to approach." Jesus said, "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (John 6:44).
- **"We shall be satisfied with the goodness of your house, the holiness of your temple!" (v4).** Again the change from "the one" to "we." JFB notes the shift: it is a blessing for all God's people. The picture is a feast in a great house where God is the host (23:5–6; 36:8). Maclaren, cited by Guzik, describes God as a host who shelters and feeds his guests. Henry: "God keeps a good house… enough for all, enough for each… without money and without price."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v1–5 as God's glory in hearing prayer, pardoning sin, satisfying souls, and protecting his people. He insists that thanks are not accepted "unless we make conscience of paying the vows which we made when we were in pursuit of the mercy." JFB: "God's mercy alone delivers us from the burden of iniquities, by purging or expiating by an atonement." Guzik connects the performing of vows to Christ, who kept the greatest vow of all in giving his life (40:6–8).

#### The Hope of All the Ends of the Earth (vv5–8)
- **"By awesome deeds you answer us with righteousness, O God of our salvation" (v5).** "Awesome deeds" (*nora'ot*) are things that inspire fear and awe, like the plagues and the crossing of the sea (Deut 10:21; 2 Sam 7:23). God answers his people's prayers in ways that make the world tremble. JFB takes it as judgments on enemies that go with mercy to his people. Henry allows both: sometimes God answers his own people with "terrible things" that wake them up, "but always in righteousness."
- **"The hope of all the ends of the earth and of the farthest seas" (v5).** A remarkable claim for a small nation's God. He is the hope, the confidence, of people everywhere, even sailors on distant seas who do not yet know him. JFB: "deservedly such, whether men think so or not." Henry: he is "the God of the Gentiles as well as of the Jews."
- **"The one who by his strength established the mountains, being girded with might" (v6).** The mountains are the most permanent things people can see. God set them in place. "Girded with might" pictures God as a warrior with his belt fastened, ready for action (93:1). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, criticizes explanations of the mountains that leave out the hand that set them there.
- **"Who stills the roaring of the seas, the roaring of their waves, the tumult of the peoples" (v7).** To the peoples of the ancient Near East the sea was chaos, wild and threatening, home of monsters. Israel's neighbors told stories of gods battling the sea. Israel knew the LORD simply rules it (89:9; 104:6–9). VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, notes the contrast. The sea's roar is then placed alongside "the tumult of the peoples," rebellious nations (2:1–3; 46:6). The Tyndale notes draw the parallel: the same God who calms one calms the other. Isaiah makes the same comparison: "Ah, the thunder of many peoples; they thunder like the thundering of the sea!" (Isa 17:12).
- **"So that those who dwell at the ends of the earth are in awe at your signs" (v8).** "Signs" are God's great works in nature and history, which speak about him (19:1–4; Rom 1:20).
- **"You make the going out of the morning and the evening to shout for joy" (v8).** "The going out" is where the sun rises and sets, east and west. JFB: "all people from east to west." It may also mean dawn and dusk themselves, the daily rhythm God keeps going. Kidner, cited by Guzik, suggests either the glory of day and night or the whole earth from east to west. Every sunrise and sunset is a shout of joy at what God has made.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that seeing God's power "as the God of nature" helps us be "the more affected with the wonderful condescensions of the God of grace." The God who sets mountains in place stoops to forgive sins. Guzik illustrates God's power over the sea with the vast energy of a single hurricane. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says a troubled believer should run to the one who stills the seas.

#### You Crown the Year with Your Bounty (vv9–13)
- **"You visit the earth and water it" (v9).** "Visit" (*paqad*) means to come and attend to, to take care of. It is often used of God coming to help (Gen 21:1; Ruth 1:6, where God "visited his people and given them food"). JFB: "in mercy." Moses told Israel that Canaan was not like Egypt, which was watered by the Nile and irrigation ditches. It was "a land of hills and valleys, which drinks water by the rain from heaven, a land that the LORD your God cares for" (Deut 11:10–12). Without rain, Israel starved.
- **"The river of God is full of water" (v9).** "River" here is *peleg*, a channel or water-course, the kind of ditch farmers cut to water fields. God's channel is in the sky. Job 38:25 asks, "Who has cleft a channel for the torrents of rain?" JFB: "His exhaustless resources." The Tyndale notes connect it with the river in God's city (46:4) and the river of life in Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22:1.
- **"You provide their grain, for so you have prepared it" (v9).** The farmer plows and sows, but God prepares the grain. Morgan, cited by Guzik, notes that the psalm assumes human work but focuses on God's part. Luther, cited through Spurgeon by Guzik, calls God the true farmer, working while the farmer sleeps (compare Mark 4:26–29).
- **"You water its furrows abundantly, settling its ridges, softening it with showers, and blessing its growth" (v10).** A detailed picture of a plowed field after rain. The furrows fill, the ridges of hard soil settle and soften, the seed sprouts. In Israel, the "early rain" in autumn softened the ground for plowing and sowing, and the "latter rain" in spring filled out the grain (Deut 11:14; Jas 5:7).
- **"You crown the year with your bounty" (v11).** The harvest is the crown on the head of the year, the finishing glory. Clarke, cited by Guzik, calls a plentiful harvest the crown of the year, and a gift of undeserved goodness.
- **"Your wagon tracks overflow with abundance" (v11).** A vivid picture. God is imagined driving his chariot (or harvest cart) across the land, and wherever its wheels go, abundance drips out behind. "Abundance" is literally "fatness," richness. The King James has "thy paths drop fatness." JFB reads it as the ways of God's providence.
- **"The pastures of the wilderness overflow, the hills gird themselves with joy" (v12).** Even the "wilderness," the grazing land on the dry edges, turns green. JFB: places not lived in but fit for pasture. The hills put on joy like a belt. God was "girded with might" in v6; the hills are "girded with joy" here.
- **"The meadows clothe themselves with flocks, the valleys deck themselves with grain" (v13).** The land puts on new clothes. Meadows are dressed in white flocks of sheep, valleys in golden grain. Kidner, cited by Guzik, calls it the earth putting on its best clothes. JFB notes the Hebrew for "meadows" can also mean "flocks" or "lambs," so "the flocks are clothed with lambs," a picture of abundant increase.
- **"They shout and sing together for joy" (v13).** All creation joins the praise that began in silence in v1. The Tyndale notes link it to other psalms where nature praises God (96:11–13; 98:7–9) and to Isaiah: "the mountains and the hills before you shall break forth into singing, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands" (Isa 55:12). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes that it is not only a shout but a song.
- **Commentators:** Guzik links v9–10 to Isaiah 55:10–11: as rain makes the earth fruitful, so God's word does not return empty. Trapp, cited by Guzik, notes how easily God could starve us if he withheld the rain. JFB's comments are brief, noting that God's power and goodness are shown "in His fertilizing showers, causing the earth to produce abundantly for man and beast."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 65 moves from the temple to the whole world, and from atonement to harvest. In Jesus both come together. He is the atonement for our transgressions (Rom 3:25; 1 John 2:2), and through him those God chooses are brought near (Eph 2:13) and made a "house" where God dwells (Eph 2:19–22). He is also the one who "stills the roaring of the seas." On the Sea of Galilee he rebuked the wind and said to the sea, "Peace! Be still!" and his disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:39–41). Psalm 65 had already answered: only God. Jesus called the gospel's spread a harvest (John 4:35), and through him "all flesh" is coming to the God who hears prayer. One day the whole creation, "set free from its bondage to corruption" (Rom 8:21), will put on its best clothes and shout and sing together for joy.
