*Setting:* Jesus is still in Jerusalem, probably soon after the Feast of Booths in the autumn, about six months before the cross. Chapter 8 ended with Jesus claiming "before Abraham was, I am," the crowd picking up stones, and Jesus leaving the temple. In 8:12 he had said, "I am the light of the world." Chapter 9 shows what that means. A man who has never seen light is given sight, and the people who claim to see clearly are shown to be blind. The chapter is one of the most carefully told stories in John. It plays out almost like a drama in scenes: Jesus and the disciples, the neighbors, the Pharisees, the parents, the Pharisees again, and finally Jesus and the man. As the man's understanding of Jesus grows, the leaders' blindness deepens.

#### Jesus Heals a Man Born Blind (vv1–41)
- **"As he passed by, he saw a man blind from birth" (v1).** Jesus sees him first. The man doesn't ask for anything. "From birth" matters: this was no injury or illness that might improve. He had never seen anything. In the ancient world a blind man had almost no way to earn a living except begging (v8).
- **"Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents?" (v2).** The disciples assume suffering is always punishment for a specific sin. That was a common view (compare Job's friends). Some rabbis taught that a child could sin in the womb (they pointed to Jacob and Esau struggling, Gen 25:22), and the Law spoke of iniquity visited on children (Exod 20:5). The disciples see a theological puzzle. Jesus sees a person.
- **"It was not that this man sinned, or his parents" (v3).** Jesus doesn't say the man and his parents were sinless. He denies that this blindness was caused by their particular sin. Suffering in a fallen world is real and often has no traceable link to a person's own wrongdoing (Luke 13:1–5). Elsewhere Jesus does allow that sin can lead to suffering (5:14), so the point is not that sin never matters, only that it doesn't explain every case.
- **"But that the works of God might be displayed in him" (v3).** Jesus moves from cause to purpose. The question is no longer "whose fault?" but "what will God do here?" Some translators repunctuate the Greek so the sentence reads: "Neither this man sinned nor his parents. But so that the works of God might be displayed in him, we must work…" (vv3–4). This avoids suggesting God made him blind for decades just to heal him. Either way, the blindness becomes the place where God's work is seen.
- **"We must work the works of him who sent me while it is day" (v4).** "We" includes the disciples in Jesus' work. "Day" is the time of Jesus' earthly ministry, and "night" is coming: his death, and also the general truth that opportunity does not last.
- **"As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world" (v5).** This repeats 8:12 and introduces the miracle as a picture of it.
- **"He spit on the ground and made mud with the saliva" (v6).** Ancient people did regard saliva as having healing power, and Jesus used it in two other healings (Mark 7:33; 8:23). Many readers also see an echo of creation, when God formed man from the dust of the ground (Gen 2:7). The man was born without sight, and Jesus, the Word through whom all things were made (1:3), finishes the work with earth in his hands. "Anointed" (*epichriō*) is a word related to *christos*, "anointed one," though John doesn't press the link.
- **"Go, wash in the pool of Siloam" (v7).** The Pool of Siloam was at the south end of the city of David, fed by Hezekiah's tunnel from the Gihon spring (2 Kings 20:20). In 2004 workers repairing a sewer pipe uncovered the steps of a large first-century pool there, which most archaeologists identify with the Siloam of Jesus' day. It was also the pool from which priests drew water for the ceremony at the Feast of Booths, so it may have been fresh in everyone's mind (7:37–39).
- **"(Which means Sent)" (v7).** *Siloam* comes from the Hebrew *Shiloach*, from *shalach*, "to send" (Isa 8:6). John points out the meaning because Jesus is the one sent by the Father, a phrase John uses dozens of times. The man is sent to "Sent" and returns seeing. The healing comes through obedience to Jesus' word. He has to walk across the city, mud on his eyes, before anything happens.
- **"So he went and washed and came back seeing" (v7).** It is a short sentence for a life-changing moment. Healing the blind was expected of the messianic age: "the eyes of the blind shall be opened" (Isa 35:5; compare 29:18; 42:7). In the Old Testament no one gives sight to the blind except God (Ps 146:8; Exod 4:11).
- **The neighbors (vv8–12).** Some recognize him. Others think he only looks similar. It's understandable: a man who has been sitting by the road for years is now walking around, looking at things. "I am the man" (v9) is literally "I am" (*egō eimi*), the same words Jesus uses. Here they simply mean "it's me," but John's readers may notice the echo. The man tells the story plainly. He knows only "the man called Jesus" (v11) and doesn't know where he is (v12).
- **"They brought to the Pharisees the man" (v13).** Why bring him to the Pharisees? Perhaps because they were recognized authorities, or because people sensed trouble: "it was a Sabbath day" (v14).
- **"Now it was a Sabbath day when Jesus made the mud" (v14).** According to the Mishnah's list of thirty-nine kinds of forbidden work, kneading was banned on the Sabbath (Shabbat 7:2), and making mud could count as kneading. Some rabbinic teachers also forbade anointing eyes with saliva on the Sabbath, and others allowed healing only when a life was in danger. A man blind for decades could have waited a day. Jesus deliberately didn't wait. As in chapter 5, he acts as Lord of the Sabbath.
- **"This man is not from God… How can a man who is a sinner do such signs?" (v16).** The Pharisees split. One side reasons from their rules to Jesus: he broke the Sabbath, so he can't be from God. The other reasons from the sign to Jesus: he did this, so he must be from God. "Division" (*schisma*) again, as in 7:43 and 10:19.
- **"He is a prophet" (v17).** This is the man's first step of understanding. He started with "the man called Jesus" (v11). Now Jesus is a prophet, like Elijah and Elisha, who did wonders by God's power.
- **"The Jews did not believe that he had been blind" (v18).** The leaders now try to deny the miracle happened at all, so they call his parents. Their three questions, "Is this your son? Was he born blind? How does he now see?", are meant to break the story.
- **"Ask him; he is of age" (vv20–21).** The parents answer the first two questions and dodge the third. "Of age" meant over thirteen, old enough to give legal testimony. They are protecting themselves.
- **"If anyone should confess Jesus to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue" (v22).** "Put out of the synagogue" is one Greek word, *aposynagōgos*, found only in John (9:22; 12:42; 16:2). The synagogue was the center of Jewish community life: worship, education, law and social ties. Expulsion meant losing family and community, almost a social death. Many scholars connect this with a later Jewish prayer against heretics (the *Birkat ha-Minim*), added around AD 85–90, and think John is describing the situation of his own readers. Others point out that Jewish communities already used local exclusion in Jesus' day (Luke 6:22), and that Jesus warned his disciples of exactly this (16:2). The text presents it as an agreement already made by the Jerusalem leaders.
- **"Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner" (v24).** "Give glory to God" was a solemn oath formula meaning "tell the truth before God." Joshua said it to Achan before he confessed (Josh 7:19). The leaders are pressuring him to confess that the story is false or that Jesus is a sinner. There is an irony here too: the man will give glory to God, by worshiping Jesus (v38).
- **"One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see" (v25).** The man won't argue theology with experts. He sticks to what he knows. It's one of the most famous testimonies in Scripture, echoed in John Newton's hymn "Amazing Grace" ("was blind, but now I see").
- **"Do you also want to become his disciples?" (v27).** The man is growing bolder, and his wit is sharp. "Also" suggests he now counts himself a disciple. The Greek question expects the answer "no," which makes it more pointed: surely they don't want to join him?
- **"You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses" (v28).** They insult him and set Moses against Jesus. But Jesus has already said Moses wrote about him (5:46). They claim Moses without hearing him.
- **"As for this man, we do not know where he comes from" (v29).** In 7:27 the crowd said they knew where Jesus came from. Now the leaders say they don't. Both are wrong, because he comes from the Father.
- **"Why, this is an amazing thing!" (v30).** The beggar now teaches the teachers. His argument is simple: God does not listen to sinners but to those who worship him and do his will (v31; compare Ps 66:18; Prov 15:29; Isa 1:15). "Never since the world began has it been heard that anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind" (v32). Nowhere in the Old Testament does anyone give sight to a person born blind. "If this man were not from God, he could do nothing" (v33). That's the very conclusion the leaders refuse.
- **"You were born in utter sin, and would you teach us?" (v34).** They answer with the disciples' original assumption (v2): his blindness proves he was steeped in sin from birth. And they condemn themselves, admitting he had been born blind after all. "They cast him out" probably means the formal expulsion of v22, not just throwing him out of the room. He has lost his place in the community for telling the truth.
- **"Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him" (v35).** Jesus seeks him out, as he did the man at Bethesda (5:14), but this time with a far different result. The good shepherd goes after the sheep that has been thrown out (10:3–4, 16), which is why chapter 10 follows directly.
- **"Do you believe in the Son of Man?" (v35).** "You" is emphatic. Jesus asks for the man's own decision, whatever the others decide. Later manuscripts read "Son of God," which the KJV follows. The earliest manuscripts read "Son of Man," and most modern translations follow them. "Son of Man" is Jesus' title for the heavenly figure of Daniel 7 who receives authority to judge (5:27), which fits the judgment saying in v39.
- **"You have seen him, and it is he who is speaking to you" (v37).** The man has never actually seen Jesus. He went away blind and came back to find Jesus gone. "You have seen him" may be the first time he looks at Jesus' face. The phrase echoes Jesus' words to the Samaritan woman: "I who speak to you am he" (4:26).
- **"'Lord, I believe,' and he worshiped him" (v38).** "Worshiped" (*proskyneō*) means to bow down in reverence. In John this word is used only of worshiping God (4:20–24; 12:20). Jesus accepts it. When people bowed to Peter or tried to worship an angel, they were stopped (Acts 10:25–26; Rev 19:10). The man's journey is complete: "the man called Jesus" (v11), "a prophet" (v17), a man "from God" (v33), "Lord," and worship (v38). (A few early manuscripts lack vv38–39a, but most scholars accept them.)
- **"For judgment I came into this world" (v39).** This seems to contradict 3:17 ("not to condemn the world"). But Jesus' coming inevitably divides people. His purpose is to save, but his light also exposes, and those who refuse it are left in darkness by their own choice (3:19). "That those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind." This is the great reversal of the chapter, and it echoes Isaiah's commission (Isa 6:9–10, which John quotes in 12:40).
- **"Are we also blind?" (v40).** The Greek expects "no." They can't imagine it.
- **"If you were blind, you would have no guilt; but now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains" (v41).** Someone who knows they can't see can be healed. Those who insist they see perfectly well refuse the cure, so their sin "remains" (*menei*, the same verb as in 3:36, the wrath of God "remains"). The most dangerous blindness is blindness that thinks it sees.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the disciples' question "uncharitably censorious," and Guzik contrasts the disciples, who treated the man as a riddle, with Jesus, who treated him as a person in need. Guzik and Henry both see an echo of creation in the clay, and both say Jesus varied his methods so no one would tie his power to a formula. Henry: "The power of God often works by contraries." JFB denies that the clay had any real healing effect, and with Henry and Guzik sees the pool's name "Sent" as pointing to Christ, the one sent by the Father. Guzik notes that making mud counted as kneading under the rabbis' Sabbath rules. Henry observes that a blind beggar judged more clearly than the "masters in Israel," and that the parents show how fear of people leads to disowning Christ. On v24, Henry and Guzik both give two readings: a solemn call to tell the truth (Josh 7:19), or a pious way of denying Christ the credit. JFB reads v27 as spoken "in a vein of keen irony." On v25, Henry says firsthand knowledge of grace makes for the most durable faith, and Guzik adds that experience supports faith but Scripture is its foundation. On v35, JFB follows the reading "Son of God," while Guzik notes the manuscript difference and says both point to the Messiah. JFB calls the man's worship "the unstudied, resistless expression, probably of SUPREME faith and adoration." Guzik traces the man's growing understanding step by step and stresses that Jesus accepted worship no angel or apostle would accept. On v41, JFB says the Pharisees' claim to see "seals you up in the guilt of unbelief."

#### Pointing to Christ
The light of the world meets a man who has never seen light. With mud, a word and a walk to the pool called "Sent," the man sees, and then step by step sees more: a man, a prophet, one from God, the Son of Man, his Lord. He loses his place in the synagogue and finds the good shepherd waiting outside. Meanwhile the men who know the Law, guard the Sabbath and claim Moses grow blinder with every question. The chapter's warning is for anyone who thinks they can see well enough without him. Its hope is for anyone willing to say, "One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see."
