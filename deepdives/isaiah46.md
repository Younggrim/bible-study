*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapters 44–45 named Cyrus and ended with every knee bowing to the LORD. Chapter 46 shows Babylon's own gods bowing. It is a short chapter built on one picture: who carries whom? Babylon's gods have to be carried, on the shoulders of worshipers or on the backs of tired animals. Israel's God carries his people from birth to old age. Every spring at the New Year festival (the *Akitu*), Babylon paraded its gods' statues through the streets in glory. Isaiah pictures the same statues loaded like baggage and hauled away.

#### The Idols of Babylon and the One True God (vv1–13)
- **"Bel bows down; Nebo stoops" (v1).** Bel means "lord," related to Baal. In Isaiah's day it was the title of Marduk, Babylon's chief god, whose great temple Esagila stood beside the ziggurat many think lies behind the tower of Babel. Nebo (Nabu) was Marduk's son, god of writing and wisdom, with his temple in nearby Borsippa. Their names were built into royal names: Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar (Nabu-kudurri-usur, "Nabu, protect my heir") and Nabonidus. Belshazzar's name honors Bel. Isaiah names the gods of the empire directly.
- **"Bows down… stoops" (v1).** These are the words of 45:23, where every knee bows to the LORD. Here the gods themselves bow, not in worship but under their own weight, like statues tipping over in a cart.
- **"Their idols are on beasts and livestock… borne as burdens on weary beasts" (v1).** In the festival procession the gods rode in state. Now they are freight. The animals are "weary" from hauling gods. Isaiah's word "burdens" (*massa'*) is also the word for a prophet's oracle (13:1). The burden God lays on Babylon is the weight of its own idols.
- **"They cannot save the burden, but themselves go into captivity" (v2).** Gods were supposed to protect their cities. When a city fell, its images were often carried off as trophies, as the Philistines took the ark (1 Sam 5) and Assyria boasted of the gods it had captured (36:18–20). The prophecy is a picture of Babylon's helpless gods, not a detailed forecast of one day. In fact Cyrus honored Marduk when he took the city. But Babylonian records say that before Cyrus arrived, King Nabonidus had the statues of other cities' gods brought into Babylon for safety. The gods were literally loaded on carts, and they saved no one. Later Persian kings did plunder Babylon's temples.
- **"Listen to me, O house of Jacob… who have been borne by me from before your birth, carried from the womb" (v3).** The contrast is deliberate. The same verbs, "borne" (*'amas*) and "carried" (*nasa'*), used of the idols in v1 are now used of God. Babylon's people carry their gods. Israel's God carries his people. Moses used the same picture: "the LORD your God carried you, as a man carries his son, all the way" (Deut 1:31). Isaiah later says, "he lifted them up and carried them all the days of old" (63:9).
- **"All the remnant of the house of Israel" (v3).** The exiles were a remnant, what was left after the northern kingdom fell to Assyria and Judah to Babylon. They were few, but God speaks to them as his family.
- **"Even to your old age I am he, and to gray hairs I will carry you" (v4).** God carried Israel at its birth as a nation, in Egypt and the wilderness. He will carry them still now that they are old and worn out in exile. It is also one of the most personal promises in Scripture for anyone growing old (Ps 71:9, 18). "I am he" (*'ani hu'*) is the near-name of God again (41:4; 43:10).
- **"I have made, and I will bear; I will carry and will save" (v4).** Four strong verbs, each with an emphatic "I." Idols are made, lifted and carried, and save nobody. God makes, bears, carries and saves. Guzik puts the chapter's question simply: does a person carry their god, or does their God carry them?
- **"To whom will you liken me and make me equal?" (v5).** This is the question of 40:18, 25. Nothing can be compared with God, least of all a statue.
- **"Those who lavish gold from the purse… hire a goldsmith, and he makes it into a god; then they fall down and worship!" (v6).** The worshipers pay for their god, weigh out the silver, and hire a craftsman to make it. Then they bow to what they paid for. Henry and JFB both remark that idolaters' lavish spending shames believers who give God what costs them little.
- **"They lift it to their shoulders, they carry it, they set it in its place, and it stands there; it cannot move" (v7).** It has to be carried, then it has to be set down, and then it stays put. "If one cries to it, it does not answer." Psalm 115:5–7 says the same: "They have mouths, but do not speak."
- **"Remember this and stand firm, recall it to mind, you transgressors" (v8).** The ESV's "stand firm" translates a rare Hebrew verb (*hit'oshashu*) found only here. Its meaning is uncertain. Older versions linked it to *'ish*, "man," and translated "show yourselves men" (KJV), meaning "be grown-ups, stop the childish folly of idols." Others connect it to a word for being firmly founded. Either way it calls for steady thinking. The exiles were tempted by Babylon's splendid gods. God calls them "transgressors" and tells them to think it through.
- **"Remember the former things of old; for I am God, and there is no other" (v9).** Memory is the cure. Israel's history, from the Exodus on, showed what God alone could do.
- **"Declaring the end from the beginning" (v10).** This is one of the Bible's great statements of God's knowledge of the future. He announces how things will end before they begin. **"My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose."** "Counsel" (*'etsah*) is a settled plan. God does not just foresee history like a spectator. He directs it (14:24, 27; Ps 33:11; Eph 1:11).
- **"Calling a bird of prey from the east, the man of my counsel from a far country" (v11).** This is Cyrus again (41:2, 25), swooping down from Persia as fast as an eagle. The Greek writer Xenophon says Cyrus's royal standard was a golden eagle on a spear. Cyrus is "the man of my counsel," the man who carries out God's plan. He is not a free agent who happens to help Israel.
- **"I have spoken, and I will bring it to pass; I have purposed, and I will do it" (v11).** Two pairs: spoken and done, purposed and done. God's word and his action are never separated.
- **"Listen to me, you stubborn of heart, you who are far from righteousness" (v12).** "Stubborn of heart" is literally "mighty of heart," hard and unyielding. These are the exiles who would not believe the promise. "Far from righteousness" may mean far from doing right, or far from expecting God to put things right, or both.
- **"I bring near my righteousness; it is not far off, and my salvation will not delay" (v13).** The answer to "far" is "near." They were far from righteousness, so God brings his righteousness near to them. Here "righteousness" (*tsedaqah*) is God's saving faithfulness, his act of putting things right, paired with "salvation." Paul draws on this pattern: "the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law" (Rom 3:21), and "the word is near you" (Rom 10:8).
- **"I will put salvation in Zion, for Israel my glory" (v13).** The chapter ends in Zion, Jerusalem. Babylon's gods are hauled off to exile, and God's people come home. The ESV reads "for Israel my glory," meaning Israel will display God's glory. Some translate "I will give my glory to Israel." In either reading, the glory belongs to God and shows up in his people. The Tyndale notes point out the contrast with 44:13. A craftsman gives an idol its "beauty," but God gives his people real honor.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains "carriages" (KJV v1) as the old English word for things carried, meaning the idols as baggage, not the wagons. He calls God's care in vv3–4 that of "a nurse tenderly carrying a child." Henry says that when Israel grows old and weak, "you will need help as much as in your infancy," and God gives it. All three follow the KJV in v8, "show yourselves men." JFB adds, "In order to be manly we must be godly." Henry says sinners would become saints if they would only "act with reason." That reading is possible, but the Hebrew word is uncertain, as the ESV's different rendering shows. JFB and Guzik both take the bird of prey as Cyrus, and JFB cites Xenophon's eagle standard. Guzik says God is not watching the parade of history but directing it. Henry suggests that Bel and Nebo were deified kings and prophets, an old theory that modern study of Babylonian religion does not support.

#### Pointing to Christ
Religion that has to be carried wears out its worshipers. The gospel is the reverse: God carries his people. Jesus invited the "weary" and "heavy laden" to come to him for rest (Matt 11:28). He is the shepherd who finds the lost sheep and "lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing" (Luke 15:5). At the cross he carried the heaviest load of all: "he himself bore our sins in his body on the tree" (1 Pet 2:24). The God who says "I have made, and I will bear; I will carry and will save" did that in person.
