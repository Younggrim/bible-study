*Setting:* Still at Sinai, with the camp ordered (chs 1–4) and cleansed (ch 5). Chapter 5 dealt with what defiles the camp. Chapter 6 turns to the opposite: a way for any ordinary Israelite, man or woman, to choose a season of special holiness to the LORD. Only Aaron's family could be priests, and only Levites could serve at the tabernacle, but the Nazirite vow opened a priest-like devotion to anyone. The chapter then ends with the words the priests were to use in blessing the whole people, the best known blessing in the Bible. One section is about what an Israelite could offer God; the other is about what God gives his people.

#### The Nazirite Vow (vv1–21)
- **"When either a man or a woman makes a special vow" (v2).** "Special" translates a verb meaning to do something extraordinary or wonderful (*pala'*). The vow was voluntary, unusual and serious. **"Either a man or a woman"** is worth noticing. In a world where the priesthood was male, this path of devotion was open to women as well.
- **"The vow of a Nazirite" (v2).** *Nazir* comes from a verb meaning "to separate" or "to consecrate." The lexicon notes it was also used of a prince "set apart from his brothers" (Joseph in Gen 49:26), and of an "untended vine," left unpruned in the sabbath year like a Nazirite's uncut hair (Lev 25:5, 11). A Nazirite was not withdrawing from the world as a hermit. He or she was living among ordinary people under visible marks of being set apart.
- **"He shall separate himself from wine and strong drink" (v3).** The first mark. Not only wine but anything from the vine: vinegar, juice, grapes, raisins, **"not even the seeds or the skins"** (v4). Priests already had to avoid wine when they served in the sanctuary (Lev 10:9). The Nazirite extended that for the whole period of the vow, everywhere. Wine itself was not evil. Psalm 104:15 calls it God's gift "to gladden the heart of man." The point was to give up something good, a pleasure of settled life in the land, as an act of devotion.
- **"No razor shall touch his head" (v5).** The second mark, and the most visible. Long hair announced the vow to everyone who saw it. In v7 the vow itself is called **"his separation to God… on his head."** The word is *nezer*, from the same root as *nazir*, and it is also the word for the high priest's gold "crown" (Exod 29:6; 39:30) and a king's crown (2 Sam 1:10). A Nazirite's hair was a kind of crown of consecration.
- **"He shall not go near a dead body… not even for his father or for his mother" (vv6–7).** The third mark, and the hardest. Ordinary priests could make themselves unclean to bury close relatives (Lev 21:1–3). Only the high priest was forbidden even that (Lev 21:11). The Nazirite took on the high priest's level of separation from death. Death is the great sign of sin's curse (Gen 2:17; Rom 6:23), and the one set apart to the living God kept away from it.
- **"He is holy to the LORD" (v8).** The vow did not make the person morally better. It marked them as belonging, for a time, to God in a special way.
- **Accidental defilement (vv9–12).** If someone **"dies very suddenly beside him"** (v9), the vow is broken even though the Nazirite did nothing wrong. He must wait the seven days of cleansing (19:11–12), shave his head, bring two birds for a sin offering and a burnt offering (the offering of the poor, Lev 5:7), and a lamb as a guilt offering. Then he starts over: **"the previous period shall be void"** (v12). It is a sobering rule. Holiness is not a matter of good intentions only, and a vow to God is taken seriously.
- **Completing the vow (vv13–20).** At the end of the period, the Nazirite brings every major kind of offering: a burnt offering, a sin offering and a peace offering, with bread, grain and drink offerings. It was expensive. Then the Nazirite shaves the consecrated hair at the entrance to the tabernacle and burns it **"on the fire that is under the sacrifice of the peace offering"** (v18). The hair belonged to God, so it went to God. The peace offering was a shared meal, and the vow ends in fellowship. **"And after that the Nazirite may drink wine"** (v20).
- **"As he can afford… in exact accordance with the vow" (v21).** A Nazirite could promise more than the minimum, but having promised, must keep it (Eccl 5:4–5).
- **Nazirites in the Bible.** Most Nazirite vows were for a set time, often thirty days in later Jewish practice. The famous exceptions were lifelong and set by God or a parent before birth. **Samson** was a Nazirite "from the womb" (Judg 13:5–7), though he broke every rule: he touched a lion's carcass, held a drinking feast, and let his hair be cut (Judg 14:8–10; 16:17–19). **Samuel** was dedicated by his mother Hannah with the promise that "no razor shall touch his head" (1 Sam 1:11). **John the Baptist** would "not drink wine or strong drink" (Luke 1:15), probably a lifelong Nazirite. Amos lists Nazirites, with prophets, as God's gifts that Israel despised by making them drink wine (Amos 2:11–12). In Acts, **Paul** cut his hair at Cenchreae "for he was under a vow" (Acts 18:18), and later paid the costs of four men completing their Nazirite vows in the temple (Acts 21:23–26).
- **Was Jesus a Nazirite?** No. Matthew says he would be called a "Nazarene" (Matt 2:23), meaning a man from Nazareth, likely a play on the Hebrew *netser*, "branch" (Isa 11:1), not on *nazir*. Jesus drank wine (Luke 7:34), and he touched the dead to raise them (Luke 7:14; 8:54). His holiness did not need distance from death, because he overcame it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the vow gave any Israelite a chance at a priest-like consecration, and he reads the grape restriction as giving up a genuine blessing for God. He observes that the vow laid down no further rules, only that the person be "holy to the LORD." JFB thinks wine was forbidden because it "tended to inflame the passions," and calls the uncut hair "a symbol of the purity he professed." On the sin offering at the end of a completed vow, JFB writes, "Sin mingles with our best and holiest performances." Even a vow kept perfectly needed atonement. Henry calls vv9–12 "a remedial law in case a Nazarite happened to be polluted by the touch of a dead body."

#### Aaron's Blessing (vv22–27)
- **"Thus you shall bless the people of Israel" (v23).** Blessing the people was one of the priests' main tasks (Deut 10:8; 21:5). Leviticus 9:22–23 records Aaron lifting his hands and blessing the people the day the sanctuary began operating. Here God gives the exact words. The priests were not to invent a blessing. They were to speak God's own.
- **The shape of the blessing.** In Hebrew it is a short poem of three lines that grow: three words, then five, then seven. Each line has two verbs, and each names **"the LORD,"** *YHWH*, the covenant name. The blessing builds like a rising tide.
- **"The LORD bless you and keep you" (v24).** "Bless" covers every kind of good God gives: life, children, crops, health. "Keep" is *shamar*, to guard, the same word used throughout chapters 3–4 for the Levites guarding the tabernacle. The Tyndale notes point to Psalm 121, which uses this verb six times: "The LORD will keep you from all evil" (Ps 121:7). Note the "you": singular in Hebrew all six times. The blessing was spoken over the nation, but it lands on each person.
- **"The LORD make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you" (v25).** A shining face is a smile, the look of a king pleased with his subject (Prov 16:15). It is the opposite of God "hiding his face" in displeasure (Ps 30:7; Deut 31:17). Moses' own face shone after he had been with God (Exod 34:29). "Be gracious" asks God to treat his people with undeserved kindness. Psalm 67:1 and Psalm 80:3, 7, 19 echo this line ("let your face shine, that we may be saved").
- **"The LORD lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace" (v26).** To "lift up the face" toward someone is to give them full, favorable attention. The blessing ends in **"peace,"** *shalom*: not just the absence of trouble but wholeness, health and well-being in every part of life, with God and with others.
- **"So shall they put my name upon the people of Israel" (v27).** This is the purpose of the blessing. Israel would carry God's name, marked as his people. **"And I will bless them."** The priests speak, but God blesses. The power is his, not theirs.
- **The oldest biblical text yet found.** In 1979 archaeologists working at Ketef Hinnom, a burial site just southwest of Jerusalem's Old City, found two tiny rolled silver amulets dating to about 600 BC, before the Babylonian exile. When they were unrolled, they contained a version of this blessing, the oldest known written words from the Bible, centuries older than the Dead Sea Scrolls. People wore God's blessing around their necks.
- **Still in use.** The blessing has been spoken in synagogues and churches for more than two thousand years, and it is used in Christian services to close worship to this day.
- **The threefold name.** Many Christian writers have seen a hint of the Trinity in the three repetitions of the LORD's name, with each line suited to the Father, the Son and the Spirit. The text itself does not say that, and it would be too much to call it a proof. But Paul's closing blessing in 2 Corinthians 13:14, "the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ and the love of God and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit," has the same threefold shape.
- **Commentators:** Henry and JFB both connect the threefold name to the Trinity. JFB speaks of "three persons, and yet one God," assigning the first line to the Father, the grace to the Son, and the peace to the Holy Spirit. Henry says the persons are "not three Lords, but one Lord," and that "the favour of God is all in all in this blessing," "the fountain of all good." He calls the blessing "a type of Christ's errand into the world," and says ministers who are God's mouth to teach should also be his mouth to bless. Guzik is more cautious, saying the threefold name illustrates rather than proves the Trinity. He draws a point from where the blessing sits: right after the Nazirite law, it shows that God's blessing is not earned by special acts of devotion but flows from his desire to bless.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Nazirite was set apart for a season, kept away from death, and ended the vow in a fellowship meal. Jesus was set apart wholly and forever, yet he did not keep his distance from death. He walked straight into it and drank the cup the Father gave him (John 18:11), and at the Last Supper he said he would not drink wine again until the kingdom comes (Luke 22:18), a kind of vow of his own until his work was finished. And he is the true High Priest who blesses. Luke's Gospel ends with the risen Jesus lifting up his hands and blessing his disciples as he was carried up into heaven (Luke 24:50–51). In him the LORD's face shines on his people: God has shone "in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6). He is our peace (Eph 2:14), and his name is the one placed on his people (Rev 22:4).
