*Setting:* Right after the southern campaign of chapter 10. Israel's base camp is still at Gilgal by the Jordan. The south has fallen in one sweep, and now the scene moves about 100 miles north, to Galilee and the lands below Mount Hermon. The book has been building up step by step: one city (Jericho), then a second (Ai), then an alliance of five kings in the south, and now the largest alliance of all, led by the most powerful city in Canaan. After the victory, the chapter steps back to sum up years of fighting and closes with the words the whole first half of Joshua has been moving toward: "the land had rest from war."

#### Conquests in Northern Canaan (vv1–23)
- **"When Jabin, king of Hazor, heard of this" (v1).** "This" is the fall of the south. Hazor sat on a great mound north of the Sea of Galilee, beside the main trade road between Egypt and Mesopotamia. At about 200 acres it was by far the largest city in Canaan, many times the size of Jericho or Jerusalem. Verse 10 calls it "the head of all those kingdoms." Hazor is named in Egyptian records and in the Mari letters from Mesopotamia (1700s BC), which mention a king of Hazor called Ibni-Addu. Many scholars think "Ibni" is the same name as Jabin, which would make Jabin a family or royal name used by more than one king. That fits Judges 4, where another "Jabin king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor" oppresses Israel generations later.
- **The guest list (vv1–3).** Jabin summons the kings of Madon, Shimron and Achshaph, then the whole north: the hill country of Galilee, the Jordan valley ("the Arabah") south of Chinneroth (the Sea of Galilee), the lowlands, and Naphoth-dor on the coast. Then six peoples are named: Canaanites, Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Jebusites and Hivites. It is the list of nations God promised to drive out (Exod 3:8; Deut 7:1). Everyone the LORD named has now come out to fight.
- **"The Hivites under Hermon in the land of Mizpah" (v3).** Mizpah means "lookout" or "watchtower," a common place name. This one is the region on the lower slopes of Mount Hermon, the highest peak in the promised land, at the far northern edge.
- **"Like the sand that is on the seashore" (v4).** The phrase God used of Abraham's descendants (Gen 22:17) is now used of the army against them. Josephus, the Jewish historian of the first century, put their number at 300,000 foot soldiers, 10,000 horsemen and 20,000 chariots, but that is his estimate, not the text's.
- **"Very many horses and chariots" (v4).** This is the first time Israel faces chariots in Joshua. In this period horses were used to pull chariots, not ridden by cavalry. A chariot carried a driver and an archer or spearman, and on open, flat ground a massed chariot charge could crush foot soldiers. Israel had none. Later, in Judges 1:19, Judah cannot drive out the lowland peoples "because they had chariots of iron."
- **"The waters of Merom" (v5).** The site is uncertain. Most now place it in Upper Galilee a few miles west of Hazor, near springs at a town called Merom (Meirun). Older commentators, including JFB, identified it with Lake Huleh, the small marshy lake north of the Sea of Galilee. Either way, it was a gathering point with water for horses and room for chariots to move.
- **"Do not be afraid of them" (v6).** The LORD speaks the same words he spoke before Ai and Gibeon (8:1; 10:8). The promise is precise: "tomorrow at this time." It also hints that Joshua was afraid. Courage in this book is never the absence of fear. It is trust in a word from God.
- **"You shall hamstring their horses and burn their chariots" (v6).** To hamstring (*'aqar*) is to cut the tendon at the back of a horse's hind leg, which leaves the animal unable to run or pull, so it is useless for war. The same verb is used of Simeon and Levi crippling oxen in Genesis 49:6, and of David hamstringing the chariot horses of Hadadezer (2 Sam 8:4). The command keeps Israel from building its own chariot force out of the spoil. The law of the king had warned that Israel's king must not "acquire many horses for himself" (Deut 17:16), and the psalmist sang, "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (Ps 20:7). Solomon would later ignore this and build a great chariot army (1 Kings 10:26–29).
- **"Joshua… came suddenly against them" (v7).** Gilgal was several days' march from Merom, so Israel must have moved north first and then struck without warning. The Tyndale notes suggest Jabin may have planned to move to better ground for his chariots. A sudden attack, perhaps in the hill country before the chariots could deploy, took that advantage away. As in 10:9, God's promise and Joshua's hard, swift march go together.
- **"As far as Great Sidon and Misrephoth-maim, and eastward as far as the Valley of Mizpeh" (v8).** The routed army scattered in different directions: northwest to Sidon on the Phoenician coast, and east toward Hermon. Israel chased each group. *Misrephoth-maim* may mean "burnings of water" (perhaps hot springs, salt pans or glass works; JFB suggests "glass-smelting houses"). The exact meaning is unknown.
- **"Joshua did to them just as the LORD said to him" (v9).** The verse repeats the command almost word for word. It would have been tempting to keep the finest military equipment in the land. Joshua obeys exactly.
- **"For Hazor formerly was the head of all those kingdoms" (v10).** Having broken the field army, Joshua turns back to the capital. Its king, Jabin, is struck down.
- **"He burned Hazor with fire" (v11).** Archaeologists have dug at Hazor since the 1950s (Yigael Yadin, and later Amnon Ben-Tor). They found a massive destruction layer with signs of intense fire from the Late Bronze Age, along with Canaanite statues of gods and kings that had been deliberately defaced, heads and hands cut off. Ben-Tor argues that Israel is the most likely destroyer, since Egyptians would not have mutilated images of their own kind of gods and Canaanites would not have defaced their own. Not all scholars agree. Some credit Egyptians or other peoples, and scholars who hold the earlier date for the exodus (about 1446 BC) point to an earlier destruction at Hazor as well. On every view, the city the Bible says was burned did burn.
- **"Just as Moses the servant of the LORD had commanded" (v12).** The total destruction follows the command of Deuteronomy 7:1–2 and 20:16–17. As in chapter 10, this is presented as God's judgment on the long-ripened sin of Canaan (Gen 15:16), carried out under God's command and not for Israel's own gain.
- **"None of the cities that stood on mounds did Israel burn, except Hazor alone" (v13).** The mounds are *tels*, the hills built up by layer upon layer of ruined and rebuilt towns on the same good site. Israel left these cities standing, to live in them later, just as God had promised Israel would receive "great and good cities that you did not build" (Deut 6:10). Hazor, the head of the alliance, was the exception, as Jericho and Ai had been (6:24; 8:28). The KJV's "cities that stood still in their strength" is an older, less likely rendering.
- **"The spoil… the people of Israel took for their plunder" (v14).** Unlike Jericho, where everything was devoted to God (6:17–19), the livestock and goods of these cities could be kept, as at Ai (8:2, 27). The difference is not Israel's choice but God's instruction in each case.
- **"Just as the LORD had commanded Moses his servant, so Moses commanded Joshua, and so Joshua did" (v15).** A chain of faithful handing-on: God to Moses, Moses to Joshua, Joshua into action. "He left nothing undone" is high praise. Compare Saul, who later did leave things undone and lost his kingdom (1 Sam 15).
- **"So Joshua took all that land" (vv16–17).** A summary of the whole conquest, south and north. The regions listed are the hill country, the Negeb in the far south, the land of Goshen (a district in southern Judah, not the Goshen in Egypt), the lowland (the *Shephelah*), the Jordan valley, and "the hill country of Israel." The limits run from Mount Halak ("the bare mountain") in the far south, rising toward Seir, the hills of Edom, to Baal-gad under Mount Hermon in the north. Baal-gad means "Baal of fortune." JFB connected it with Baalbek in Lebanon, though most now place it nearer Hermon.
- **"Joshua made war a long time with all those kings" (v18).** The quick, dramatic battles in chapters 10 and 11 could give the impression of a lightning war. The writer corrects that. Caleb's words in 14:7–10 suggest the main conquest took about seven years: he was forty when Moses sent him as a spy, the conquest began about 38 years later, and he is 85 when the land is divided. Breaking enemy armies took days, but taking and holding cities took years.
- **"Not a city that made peace… except… Gibeon" (v19).** Gibeon stands out as the one city that sought peace, even if it did so by trickery (ch 9). The door had been open: Rahab and Gibeon prove that anyone in Canaan who turned to Israel's God could be spared. Deuteronomy 20:10 even required Israel to offer peace to distant cities.
- **"It was the LORD's doing to harden their hearts" (v20).** The verb is *chazaq*, "to make strong" or "firm." It is the same word God used three times to tell Joshua "be strong" (1:6, 7, 9), and the same word used for God hardening Pharaoh's heart (Exod 14:4, 17). Here it means God made the kings stubborn, firm in their resolve to fight. As with Pharaoh, the Bible holds two things together: these kings chose to fight (they "heard" and "came out," vv1, 4), and God confirmed their choice as judgment. The Canaanites had centuries to repent (Gen 15:16), and they had heard what God did at the Red Sea and the Jordan (2:10–11; 5:1). Most of them hardened themselves in response. God gave them over to it. Paul describes the same pattern in Romans 1:24–28 ("God gave them up") and 9:17–18.
- **"Joshua… cut off the Anakim" (vv21–22).** The Anakim were the giants whose size terrified the ten spies forty years before: "we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers" (Num 13:33). That fear kept the first generation out of the land (Num 14). Now the giants who once stopped Israel are wiped out of the hill country, from Hebron, Debir and Anab. Chapter 15:13–14 tells how Caleb drove out three Anakite chiefs from Hebron. The two passages may describe the same events from two angles, or Caleb may have had to finish the job after the Anakim returned.
- **"Only in Gaza, in Gath, and in Ashdod did some remain" (v22).** Three cities on the coastal plain, later Philistine cities. A small detail with a long shadow: some three centuries later, a giant named Goliath comes out of Gath to defy Israel (1 Sam 17:4). Israel's incomplete conquest left room for future trouble.
- **"So Joshua took the whole land… And the land had rest from war" (v23).** This verse sums up the first half of the book and opens the second. "Took the whole land" means the power of the Canaanite kings was broken in every region. Chapter 13 will say plainly that "there remains yet very much land to possess" (13:1). Both are true: the land was conquered, but not yet fully occupied. Each tribe would still need to claim its share. "Rest" fulfills what God promised (1:13, 15), though Hebrews 4:8 later says this rest was not the final one.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the kings' unity in a bad cause and suggests it should shame God's people into healing their own divisions. On the horses and chariots he writes, "Let Israel look upon their chariots but as rotten wood designed for the fire," and on the Anakim, "Giants are dwarfs to Omnipotence." He also stresses that Joshua "left nothing undone," contrasting him with Saul. JFB explains hamstringing as "cutting the sinews" of the hind legs and gives three reasons for it: trust in God rather than military force, the hill country's unsuitability for horses, and Israel's calling to be a farming people rather than a trading power. JFB notes that "cities that stood still in their strength" means in Hebrew "on their heaps," and calls Merom the northern counterpart of the battle of Beth-horon. JFB has no comment on vv18–22. Guzik sees a steady rise in the size of each challenge, from Jericho to Ai to the southern kings to this coalition, with each victory preparing Israel for the next. He applies the burned chariots to believers who are tempted to fight with the world's weapons instead of trusting God. On v20 he reads hardening, with Romans 1, as God giving people over to the sin already in their hearts. He also notes that Israel met the Anakim last, after long training in battle, and draws the lesson that God orders our battles wisely. The Tyndale notes say that this was likely Israel's first encounter with chariots, and that the destruction layer at Hazor fits this period.

#### Pointing to Christ
The giants that had kept Israel out of the land for forty years were finally defeated, not by Israel growing taller, but by the LORD fighting for them. The horses and chariots that looked unbeatable became fuel for the fire. Joshua's name is the Hebrew form of Jesus, and the book has been showing what kind of victory God gives: one that leaves his people trusting him, not their own weapons. Yet the "rest" Joshua won was partial. Gath still had giants, and the land still needed to be possessed. Hebrews 4 picks up this very word: "if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on" (Heb 4:8). The greater Joshua has defeated the enemy that no army could touch, and "there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Heb 4:9), a rest that will never be broken by war again.
