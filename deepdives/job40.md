*Setting:* God has spoken out of the whirlwind for two full chapters, walking Job through creation from the foundations of the earth to the eagle's nest (chs 38–39). Now he pauses and gives Job the chance to answer. Job, who for thirty chapters wanted nothing more than a hearing, has almost nothing to say. Then God speaks again in a second speech (40:6–41:34). The first speech was about God's wisdom in running the natural world. The second is about his justice and power in ruling it, and especially in dealing with pride and evil. It centers on two great beasts, Behemoth in this chapter and Leviathan in the next.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv1–2)
- **"And the LORD said to Job" (v1).** A short break separates the summing-up from the long speech before it. God turns from questions about animals to a direct question to Job.
- **"Shall a faultfinder contend with the Almighty?" (v2).** "Faultfinder" is one who corrects or reproves, someone who thinks he can set God straight. "Contend" is the language of a lawsuit. Job had wanted to take God to court (9:3; 13:3; 23:4). "He who argues with God, let him answer it." Job wanted to put God on trial. Now God asks him to respond to the evidence.
- **The Greek version.** The Tyndale notes mention that the ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) leaves out the end of God's first speech here. The Hebrew text, which the ESV follows, includes it.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the LORD here is "JEHOVAH" in the Hebrew, the covenant name, and that "answer it" means answer God's questions. Henry sees God pressing Job for a reply after the long series of questions.

#### Job Promises Silence (vv3–5)
- **"Behold, I am of small account" (v4).** The word is *qalal*, "to be light" or "slight," the opposite of *kabod*, the word for being heavy, weighty, honored, and the word for "glory." Job had once pictured himself approaching God "like a prince" (31:37). Now he feels weightless. The KJV's "I am vile" makes it sound like a confession of wickedness. It is not. The Tyndale notes compare Abraham's "I am but dust and ashes" (Gen 18:27). Job is not admitting the friends were right about hidden sin. He is admitting his size.
- **"What shall I answer you? I lay my hand on my mouth" (v4).** Job once told his friends to "lay your hand over your mouth" in shock at his suffering (21:5). Now he does it himself, stunned to silence. Covering the mouth was a sign of respect or awe before someone greater (29:9; Prov 30:32; Mic 7:16).
- **"I have spoken once, and I will not answer; twice, but I will proceed no further" (v5).** "Once... twice" is a Hebrew way of saying "more than enough" (33:14; Ps 62:11). Job has nothing more to add, no more charges against God. But silence is not yet the same as full surrender. God has more to say, and Job's fuller response comes in 42:1–6.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this a humble silence: Job submits. JFB reads v4 as "I am (too) vile (to reply)," and draws a careful line: Job could rightly defend himself before men, but not before God. It takes "once... twice" to mean "oftentimes." Guzik agrees that "vile" means "of no weight" rather than a confession of moral failure, and notes that Job's change came from being in God's presence again, not from any change in his circumstances. Nothing about his sores, his losses or his grief has changed yet.

#### The LORD Challenges Job (vv6–14)
- **"Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" (v6).** God speaks again from the storm. Job's silence is not enough. God wants not just a quiet Job but a Job who understands.
- **"Dress for action like a man" (v7).** The same challenge as in 38:3. Round two begins.
- **"Will you even put me in the wrong? Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" (v8).** This is the heart of the second speech, and perhaps the most important verse God says to Job. Job had insisted so hard on his own innocence that he had come close to accusing God of injustice: "God has put me in the wrong" (19:6), "he has taken away my right" (27:2). Elihu was angry because Job "justified himself rather than God" (32:2). The Tyndale notes trace this line through Job's speeches (10:7; 27:2; 31:35–37). God does not say Job was a hypocrite or that his suffering was deserved. He says Job was wrong to think that for one of them to be right, the other had to be wrong. Both can be true: Job did not deserve his suffering, and God is not unjust.
- **"Have you an arm like God, and can you thunder with a voice like his?" (v9).** The "arm" in Scripture is a picture of strength to act and save (Exod 6:6; Isa 52:10). The thunder is the voice Job has just heard from the storm. To judge the world, you need the power to put things right. Job does not have it.
- **"Adorn yourself with majesty and dignity; clothe yourself with glory and splendor" (v10).** God invites Job to try on God's royal robes. These are the words used of God himself: "you are clothed with splendor and majesty" (Ps 104:1). It is a gentle irony: put on the throne room clothes, Job, and see how they fit.
- **"Pour out the overflowings of your anger, and look on everyone who is proud and abase him" (vv11–12).** Job had complained that God lets the proud and wicked flourish (21:7–34; 24:1–12). God's answer is: you do it, then. Take your anger and bring every proud person down. "Tread down the wicked where they stand." The point is not that God does nothing about evil. It is that judging the whole world rightly takes a power and wisdom Job does not have. Scripture says God does bring down the proud (Isa 2:11–12; Jas 4:6; 1 Pet 5:5), in his time.
- **"Hide them all in the dust together; bind their faces in the world below" (v13).** Bury them, and shut them up in the grave. JFB suggests "bind their faces" pictures the cloth put over the face of a condemned man.
- **"Then will I also acknowledge to you that your own right hand can save you" (v14).** If Job could rule the moral universe and destroy all evil, then he could save himself. But he cannot. This is the theological bottom line of the second speech: no human being can save himself. Salvation must come from God's right hand (Ps 98:1; Isa 59:16).
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines this section as God challenging Job "to vie with him, if he durst, for justice," then in power and majesty, then in bringing down the proud. JFB reads v8 as Job wanting to "set aside" God's judgment and declare God unrighteous so that Job might appear innocent. It says "acknowledge" in v14 should be "extol," and it sees in "your own right hand can save you" a pointer to the truth that eternal salvation comes only through Jesus Christ. Guzik observes that Job had been more concerned to defend his own integrity than God's, and he stresses the gulf between Creator and creature: if Job cannot do what God does, he cannot save himself by his own strength.

#### The LORD Challenges Job (vv15–24)
- **"Behold, Behemoth" (v15).** *Behemoth* is the plural of *behemah*, the ordinary word for "beast" or "cattle." The plural here probably means "the beast of beasts," the greatest beast, a common Hebrew way of expressing size or importance. Some older scholars thought it came from an Egyptian word for "water ox," though that is uncertain. The word appears only here in the Bible as a name.
- **"Which I made as I made you" (v15).** This is an important phrase. Behemoth is a creature, made by God on the same terms as Job. It is not a god or a rival power. It is part of the creation Job has just been touring.
- **"He eats grass like an ox" (v15).** For all its terrifying size, it is a plant-eater. The Tyndale notes count this, with the phrase "which I made," among the reasons to take Behemoth as a real animal rather than a mythical monster.
- **"His strength in his loins, and his power in the muscles of his belly" (v16).** A massive body, all muscle.
- **"He makes his tail stiff like a cedar" (v17).** This line causes the most discussion. A hippo's tail is short. Some read "tail" here as the trunk of an elephant or as a poetic exaggeration. Others think the word may refer to the animal's whole hindquarters or is a polite euphemism. Still others, especially in young-earth creationist circles, see a long-tailed dinosaur like a sauropod. JFB thought it might be an extinct species. "The sinews of his thighs are knit together": tightly woven muscle.
- **"His bones are tubes of bronze, his limbs like bars of iron" (v18).** Bronze and iron were the hardest materials known. The beast is built like armor.
- **"He is the first of the works of God" (v19).** "First" can mean "chief," the greatest of land creatures. The same word is used of Wisdom, which the LORD possessed at "the beginning of his work" (Prov 8:22). "Let him who made him bring near his sword": only his Maker can approach him with a weapon. JFB suggests another reading: God "furnished him with his sword," meaning the hippo's great tusks, used like sickles to cut grass.
- **"The mountains yield food for him where all the wild beasts play" (v20).** Even though he is huge, the other animals play near him without fear. He is no predator.
- **"Under the lotus plants he lies, in the shelter of the reeds and in the marsh" (vv21–22).** The picture of a hippo half-sunk in a river among water plants. The "lotus" is probably a thorny shrub that grows near water. "The willows of the brook surround him."
- **"If the river is turbulent he is not frightened; he is confident though Jordan rushes against his mouth" (v23).** A flood does not bother him. "Jordan" is probably used here as a name for any rushing river, as JFB says. Hippos did live in parts of the eastern Mediterranean coast in ancient times, and bones have been found in Israel's coastal plain, so the image would not have seemed strange.
- **"Can one take him by his eyes, or pierce his nose with a snare?" (v24).** The question expects the answer no. No one captures this animal by facing it head-on. If Job cannot master Behemoth, how could he master its Maker?
- **Who or what is Behemoth?** Most commentators, ancient and modern, take Behemoth as the hippopotamus, and the Tyndale notes agree: a large plant-eater that rests among reeds and lotus in the river. Some have suggested the elephant, or a blend of the two. Others note that in the ancient world the hippo was also a symbol. In Egypt the king ritually hunted the hippo as a picture of conquering chaos. So some scholars think Behemoth is a real animal that also stands for the untamable forces of the world, which only God can master. Later Jewish writings (1 Enoch 60; 2 Esdras 6) turned Behemoth and Leviathan into great end-time monsters, but that is a later development, not the text of Job. Christians hold these views in good faith, and the main point does not depend on the identification: God made and controls a creature Job could never subdue.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Behemoth "an instance of his power in one particular animal." JFB says the description fits partly the hippopotamus and partly the elephant but neither exactly, and treats it as a poetic picture of the great river beasts, with the hippo predominant. It notes the Egyptian "water-ox" etymology and, because hippos were not known in the Jordan in its day, suggests the author was not a Hebrew, a guess most modern scholars would not follow. Guzik says the identity is debated but most think God had the hippopotamus in mind. His point is that if Job cannot handle this creature, he certainly cannot contend with its Creator.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's challenge in v14 is the key to the chapter: if Job could bring down every proud person and tread down the wicked, "then... your own right hand can save you." No one can. The Bible's answer is that God's own right hand did. Isaiah says God "saw that there was no man... then his own arm brought him salvation" (Isa 59:16), and "to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" introduces the Suffering Servant (Isa 53:1). Jesus is that arm. He did what God dares Job to do in vv11–13: he "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame" (Col 2:15), and God "has highly exalted him" after he humbled himself (Phil 2:8–9). Job's silence before God (v4) also points beyond him. Job laid his hand on his mouth because he had spoken too much. Jesus, who had every right to speak in his own defense, "opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7) and was condemned so that we, who had put God in the wrong, could be declared in the right.
