*Setting:* The sermon to weary Jewish Christians, probably written in the 60s before the temple fell, moves from teaching to its first warning. Chapter 1 showed the Son above the prophets and the angels: he made the world, he is the radiance of God's glory, he made purification for sins, and he sat down on the throne while angels are sent out to serve. Chapter 2 first draws the lesson: if the Son is that great, his message cannot be shrugged off (vv1–4). Then it faces the obvious objection. If Jesus is so far above the angels, why did he become a man, suffer and die? The answer turns from the Son's deity to his humanity, and it introduces for the first time the title the rest of the book will unfold: Jesus our "merciful and faithful high priest" (v17).

#### Warning Against Neglecting Salvation (vv1–4)
- **"Therefore" (v1).** The warning grows straight out of chapter 1. Hebrews never teaches for its own sake. This is the first and shortest of five warning passages in the book (with 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:19–39; 12:14–29), and the writer says "we," putting himself under the same warning.
- **"Lest we drift away from it" (v1).** The verb *pararreō* appears only here in the New Testament. The lexicon gives "to flow by, drift away, slip away," and in classical Greek it could be used of something slipping from memory. The picture is a boat carried past its harbor by the current. The danger is not a dramatic rejection of Christ but a slow, quiet slide, the kind that happens when no one is paying attention. Drifting needs no effort. Holding course does.
- **"The message declared by angels" (v2).** This is the law of Moses. The Old Testament does not say outright that angels gave it, but Deuteronomy 33:2 in the Greek Old Testament speaks of angels with the LORD at Sinai, and Stephen and Paul both say the law came through angels (Acts 7:53; Gal 3:19). Chapter 1 set the Son above the angels. So if the message spoken through angels was binding, the message spoken through the Son weighs far more.
- **"Proved to be reliable" (v2).** The word is *bebaios*, "firm, sure," and the lexicon notes its common use for a legally guaranteed security. The law was not a suggestion. "Every transgression or disobedience received a just retribution": Israel's history is full of it, from the golden calf to the wilderness generation (3:16–19).
- **"How shall we escape if we neglect such a great salvation?" (v3).** The argument runs from lesser to greater, a common Jewish way of reasoning. "Neglect" is *ameleō*, "to be careless of, not to care." Jesus uses the same verb for the guests invited to the king's wedding feast who "paid no attention and went off" to their farms and businesses (Matt 22:5). They did not hate the king; they simply had other things to do. The danger here is not rebellion but indifference.
- **"Declared at first by the Lord" (v3).** The law came through angels; the gospel came from the Lord himself, preached by Jesus in Galilee and Judea (Mark 1:14–15). Then it was "attested to us by those who heard." This is the line that most scholars think rules out Paul as the writer. Paul insisted he received his gospel directly from the risen Christ, not from others (Gal 1:11–12). This writer belongs to the second generation, who heard it from the apostles.
- **"Signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will" (v4).** God added his own testimony. The three words for miracles look at them from different angles: signs point beyond themselves, wonders cause amazement, and "miracles" (literally "powers") show God's might. Peter and Paul use the same set (Acts 2:22; 2 Cor 12:12). "According to his will" matters. The Spirit gives gifts as God chooses (1 Cor 12:11), not on demand. Christians differ on whether such gifts continue in the same way today, but here they are God's seal on the original message.
- **Commentators:** Henry on v1: "Our minds and memories are like a leaky vessel, they do not without much care retain what is poured into them," and "inattentive hearers will soon be forgetful hearers." JFB gives the verb in v1 as literally "flow past," which the lexicon supports. Guzik stresses that drifting is gradual and needs no decision at all, and he applies the warning to mature believers who have grown used to the truth, not mainly to outsiders. He ties "neglect" to Matthew 22:5, as the lexicon does. Henry: "It is a great salvation that the gospel discovers, for it discovers a great Saviour." JFB notes that the law was confirmed by penalties and the gospel "by spiritual gifts," and on "those who heard" he points to Luke 1:2, where Luke says the same of his sources.

#### The Founder of Salvation (vv5–18)
- **"It was not to angels that God subjected the world to come" (v5).** "World" here is *oikoumenē*, the inhabited earth, the same word used in 1:6. The lexicon notes that with "to come" it means the messianic age, the new world God is bringing. Some Jewish writers taught that God had placed the nations under angelic rulers (Deut 32:8 in the Greek Old Testament; Dan 10:13, 20). The world to come will not be theirs. It belongs to a man.
- **"It has been testified somewhere" (v6).** This is not vagueness. Hebrews almost never names the human writer of a passage, because for this preacher the real speaker of Scripture is God (1:5–13; 3:7). The quotation is Psalm 8:4–6, from David.
- **"What is man, that you are mindful of him?" (v6).** In Psalm 8 David looks at the moon and stars, astonished that God notices human beings at all and made them rulers of his creation (Gen 1:26–28). "Son of man" in the psalm is the Hebrew *ben adam*, simply "a human being," parallel to "man." Hebrews does not press the phrase, though it was Jesus' favorite title for himself.
- **"You made him for a little while lower than the angels" (v7).** In Hebrew Psalm 8:5 reads "a little lower than *elohim*," which can mean God or heavenly beings (the ESV's Psalm 8:5 has "the heavenly beings"). The Greek Old Testament read it as "angels," and Hebrews follows it. The Greek *brachy ti* can mean "a little" in degree or "for a little while" in time, and the lexicon allows both. The ESV chooses time, which suits the way Hebrews uses it of Jesus in v9: his lowering was temporary.
- **A textual note (v7).** The ESV footnotes that some manuscripts add "and set him over the works of your hands." That line is in Psalm 8:6, and a scribe could easily have added it to complete the quotation. Others think it was left out by accident. Nothing in the meaning turns on it.
- **"At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" (v8).** Here is the honest problem. The psalm says God put all things under man's feet, yet humans plainly do not rule creation. Since Adam's sin, the crown God gave humanity has slipped (Gen 3:17–19; Rom 8:20–22). Hebrews does not pretend otherwise. "Not yet" is one of the key phrases of the New Testament.
- **"But we see him… namely Jesus" (v9).** The Greek holds the name to the end of the clause, for emphasis: we see the one who was made lower than the angels for a little while, Jesus. Psalm 8 is not yet true of the human race, but it is already true of one man. He is "crowned with glory and honor," and where he has gone, his people will follow (v10). Paul reads Psalm 8 the same way (1 Cor 15:25–27; Eph 1:20–22).
- **"Because of the suffering of death" (v9).** The order is suffering first, then the crown (Phil 2:8–9; Isa 52:13–53:12). The cross was not a detour on the way to glory. It was the way.
- **"That by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone" (v9).** "Taste death" is a Jewish idiom for actually dying (Matt 16:28), and the lexicon notes a matching phrase in the Talmud. It does not mean he only sampled it. He went through the whole of it. "For everyone" has been read in different ways: many see an atonement offered for all people (1 John 2:2), while Reformed readers often take it as every one of the "many sons" of v10. Both agree that no one who comes to Christ is outside its reach. A few ancient witnesses read "apart from God" instead of "by the grace of God," a reading Origen knew. Almost all manuscripts read "by the grace of God," and English versions follow them.
- **"For it was fitting" (v10).** The verb *prepō* means "to be fitting, suitable, becoming." This answers the scandal of the cross. To first-century Jews a crucified Messiah was a contradiction (1 Cor 1:23). The writer says the cross was not beneath God. It fits who he is, the one "for whom and by whom all things exist."
- **"In bringing many sons to glory" (v10).** The Son (1:2, 5) brings many sons with him. "Sons" includes daughters. It is the language of heirs, since in the ancient world sons inherited.
- **"The founder of their salvation" (v10).** The word is *archēgos*. The lexicon gives two senses, "founder, author" and "prince, leader," and lists Hebrews 2:10 under both. It is used of Jesus only four times: "the Author of life" (Acts 3:15), "Leader and Savior" (Acts 5:31), here, and "the founder and perfecter of our faith" (12:2). The KJV's "captain" catches the leading; the ESV's "founder" catches the beginning. Jesus does both: he opened the road to glory and goes ahead of his people on it.
- **"Perfect through suffering" (v10).** This is a startling phrase about the sinless Son, but *teleioō* means "to bring to completion, to bring to the goal," not to fix something faulty. In the Greek Old Testament it is used of consecrating priests (Exod 29:9, literally "fill their hands"), which fits where the chapter is heading. Jesus was never morally imperfect (4:15). Through suffering he was fully qualified for his work as Savior and priest, and his obedience was tested to the end (5:8–9).
- **"He who sanctifies and those who are sanctified all have one source" (v11).** The Greek is literally "all are of one," as the ESV footnotes. Of one what? Most take it as one Father, God. Others suggest Abraham (v16), Adam, or a shared human nature. The Tyndale notes leave it open. Either way, the Son and his people belong to the same family. "Sanctifies" is temple language, to make holy; Jesus does it by his own blood (10:10, 14).
- **"He is not ashamed to call them brothers" (v11).** Hebrews has just set the Son high above the angels. Now the same Son calls weak, struggling believers his brothers and sisters, as the ESV footnote explains *adelphoi* can mean. The risen Jesus did exactly this: "Go to my brothers" (Matt 28:10; John 20:17).
- **"I will tell of your name to my brothers" (v12).** Psalm 22:22. This is the psalm Jesus prayed on the cross, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Ps 22:1; Mark 15:34). At verse 22 the psalm turns from agony to praise. Having been rescued, the sufferer stands among his brothers in the congregation and sings. Hebrews hears the risen Christ in that turn. "Congregation" in the Greek Old Testament is *ekklēsia*, the word for church.
- **"I will put my trust in him… Behold, I and the children God has given me" (v13).** Both lines come from Isaiah 8:17–18. In the days of King Ahaz, when Judah was panicking at the Assyrian threat, Isaiah refused to join the nation's fear. He would wait for the LORD and trust him, he and his children, whose names were living signs of God's message (Isa 7:3; 8:3). Hebrews puts the words in Jesus' mouth. He too lived by trust in his Father, as a true man must, and he stands with the children God has given him (John 6:37; 17:6).
- **"He himself likewise partook of the same things" (v14).** The children "share in flesh and blood," a state they were born into. The Son "partook" of it, a single deliberate act. He took a real human body. Very early some Christians, later called Docetists, taught that Jesus only seemed to have a body. Hebrews will not allow it. A Savior who could not die could not save (John 1:14; 1 John 4:2).
- **"That through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil" (v14).** "Destroy" is *katargeō*, "to make idle, render inoperative." The devil still exists, but his hold over death has been broken, like a weapon taken from an enemy's hands. His power over death was the accusation that sinners belong to death (Rom 6:23; Rev 12:10). When Jesus died for sin, the charge was answered. Jesus defeated death by dying (Gen 3:15; Col 2:15; 2 Tim 1:10).
- **"Deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (v15).** "Slavery" is *douleia*, the word for bondage, used in the Greek Old Testament of Egypt (Exod 13:3). Ancient writers knew this fear well. The Jewish philosopher Philo, writing in this same century, said that nothing enslaves the mind like the fear of death. For readers tempted to retreat into a safer life to avoid persecution, this was pointed. Death has lost its sting (1 Cor 15:54–57).
- **"It is not angels that he helps, but he helps the offspring of Abraham" (v16).** The verb is *epilambanomai*, "to lay hold of," used of Jesus grabbing sinking Peter by the hand (Matt 14:31). The lexicon lists v16 as a figurative use. The KJV, following an older reading, made it "he took not on him the nature of angels," as if it described the incarnation. Most modern versions and JFB read it as help: Christ reaches out and takes hold of his people. He did not come to rescue fallen angels. "Offspring of Abraham" fits Jewish readers, though all who believe are Abraham's children (Gal 3:7).
- **"Made like his brothers in every respect" (v17).** Born, raised, tired, tempted, dying. "In every respect" has one limit, which the writer adds later: "yet without sin" (4:15).
- **"A merciful and faithful high priest" (v17).** This is the first time Hebrews calls Jesus high priest, and the book's central theme starts here (4:14–10:25). A priest stands between God and people, so he must belong to both. Jesus is merciful toward people, because he knows their weakness, and faithful toward God, because he did all his Father gave him to do. Hebrews 3:1–6 takes up "faithful," and 4:14–5:10 returns to "merciful."
- **"To make propitiation for the sins of the people" (v17).** The verb is *hilaskomai*. The lexicon lists its classical meaning as "to appease, propitiate," its common biblical sense as "to be merciful," and for this verse "to expiate, make propitiation for" sins. Translations divide. "Propitiation" (ESV) stresses turning away God's just anger. "Make atonement" or "expiation" (other versions) stresses removing the sin. The two belong together, and both point to the Day of Atonement, when the high priest sprinkled blood on the mercy seat (Lev 16:15–16; Rom 3:25; 1 John 2:2). The love that provided the sacrifice comes from God himself.
- **"Because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted" (v18).** "Tempted" (*peirazō*) means both tested by trials and enticed toward sin, and the readers faced both: the pressure of persecution and the pull to give up. Jesus was tested in the wilderness, in Gethsemane and on the cross. "Help" is *boētheō*, built from words for "a cry" and "to run": to come running to someone's aid. The Canaanite mother used it, "Lord, help me" (Matt 15:25).
- **Commentators:** On v5 JFB defines "the world to come" as "the new dispensation brought in by Christ, beginning in grace here, to be completed in glory hereafter." On v9 JFB mentions the reading "without God" but says it "is not supported by the manuscripts." Henry on v10 says the writer moves to "remove the scandal of the cross," and adds, "He found his way to the crown by the cross, and so must his people too." Guzik and JFB both stress that "perfect" means completion, not correction, and JFB prefers the sense of a finished sacrifice; the lexicon supports "bring to completion." On v11 JFB says "of one" means God the Father, "not 'of one father Adam,' or 'Abraham,' as Bengel and others suppose," while Henry finds a shared Father, nature and Spirit. JFB's "render powerless" for *katargeō* (v14) matches the lexicon. Guzik answers the idea that the devil had a rightful claim Christ had to pay off: Jesus laid down his life freely, and death had no claim on a sinless man. On v16 Henry follows the KJV's "nature of angels," while JFB reads "It is not angels that He is helping," which matches the lexicon better. Guzik recalls the tribes' names on the high priest's breastplate and says Christ's wounds speak for his people more eloquently still. Henry calls him "a sympathizing physician, tender and skilful," and warns, "let us never count upon an absolute freedom from temptations in this world."

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 1 showed the Son on the throne. Chapter 2 shows how he got there and why it matters: he came down. Psalm 8 promised humanity a crown it lost, and Jesus, as one of us, has worn it first. He tasted death, broke the devil's hold on it, freed slaves who lived afraid, and became a priest who knows temptation from the inside. That is why drifting away makes no sense. The one the readers were tempted to leave is the greatest message God ever sent, and he is also the brother who is not ashamed of them.
