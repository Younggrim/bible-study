*Setting:* Chapters 28–33 are a run of six "woes" spoken in Jerusalem around 705–701 BC, while King Hezekiah's advisers were pinning their hopes on a treaty with Egypt and the Assyrian king Sennacherib was preparing to crush Judah's revolt. Chapters 30 and 31 condemned that trust in Egypt and promised that God himself would defend Zion. Chapter 32 has no "woe" of its own. It is a bright interlude that lifts the eyes past the crisis to a righteous king and a transformed people, then turns sharply to warn the comfortable women of Jerusalem that disaster is closer than they think, and ends with the Spirit poured out and a land at peace.

#### A King Will Reign in Righteousness (vv1–8)
- **"Behold, a king will reign in righteousness, and princes will rule in justice" (v1).** After chapters full of drunken priests (28:7), scoffing rulers (28:14) and counselors who plot without God (29:15; 30:1), this is what Judah lacked. "Righteousness" and "justice" are the pair that describe the Messiah's rule in 9:7 and 11:4–5. The "princes" are the officials under the king, the people who actually carry out his government day to day.
- **Who is the king?** Readers have taken three main lines. Some see Hezekiah, a genuinely godly king whose reforms (2 Kings 18:3–6) partly matched this picture. Most Christian readers, including the Tyndale notes, see the Messiah, the shoot from Jesse's stump of 11:1. Many hold both: Hezekiah as a small, real picture of the greater King to come. The text itself points beyond any ordinary reign, because what follows is a change in people's hearts, eyes and tongues, not just better administration.
- **"Each will be like a hiding place from the wind, a shelter from the storm" (v2).** The Hebrew begins "and a man (*'ish*) will be," which can mean "each one" (each prince, as the ESV takes it) or one particular man. The images are Isaiah's words for God himself: "a shelter from the storm and a shade from the heat" (25:4). Good rulers become the channel of God's own protection.
- **"Like streams of water in a dry place, like the shade of a great rock in a weary land" (v2).** Anyone who has walked the Judean wilderness at midday knows these pictures are not decoration. Water in a dry gully and the shade of a big rock can mean the difference between collapse and survival. A good king is that kind of relief to his people.
- **"The eyes of those who see will not be closed" (v3).** This reverses the judgment of Isaiah's call: "make their ears heavy, and blind their eyes" (6:10), and the spiritual stupor of 29:9–10. Under this king, seeing and hearing come back.
- **"The heart of the hasty will understand… the tongue of the stammerers will hasten to speak distinctly" (v4).** "Hasty" means rash, people who jump to conclusions. They will learn discernment. Those whose speech about God was halting will speak clearly. Compare 35:5–6, where the deaf hear and "the tongue of the mute sing for joy."
- **"The fool will no more be called noble, nor the scoundrel said to be honorable" (v5).** "Fool" is *nabal*, the word that names Abigail's husband (1 Sam 25:25) and describes the one who says "There is no God" (Ps 14:1). In the Old Testament the *nabal* is not slow-witted but morally and spiritually senseless. "Noble" is *nadiv*, which BDB gives as both "noble in rank" and "willing, generous" in heart (Exod 35:5, "a willing heart"). "Scoundrel" is *kilay*, a rare word found only here and in v7, meaning a knave or swindler. The point is that people will be called what they are. A society that honors scoundrels as great men is a society under judgment (5:20).
- **What the fool does (v6).** He "speaks folly," schemes in his heart, practices "ungodliness" (the word means profane, godless), and spreads "error concerning the LORD." The test of his folly is social as much as religious: he leaves "the craving of the hungry unsatisfied" and deprives "the thirsty of drink." For Isaiah, how a person treats the hungry shows what he really believes about God (1:17; 58:6–7).
- **"As for the scoundrel—his devices are evil" (v7).** There is a wordplay in Hebrew: the *kelay* (scoundrel) has *kelayw* (his tools, his weapons). He works the legal system "to ruin the poor with lying words, even when the plea of the needy is right." Isaiah has seen this in Jerusalem's courts (10:1–2).
- **"But he who is noble plans noble things, and on noble things he stands" (v8).** The section closes on the *nadiv*, now three times in one verse. A generous person thinks generously, and generosity is what holds him up. Character shows in what a person plans, and it lasts.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv1–8 as a reformation at the start of Hezekiah's reign, and he applies v2 to the good magistrate as "a covert to the subject from the tempest of injury and violence," and then to Christ. JFB argues it cannot be Hezekiah, since he was already on the throne and a future time is in view. He also prefers to read v2 as "the man," the Son of Man himself, rather than each prince. Guzik takes the middle road: Hezekiah fulfilled this in part, and Jesus fulfills it completely, with his people as the princes who reign with him (Rev 5:10). JFB rightly corrects the KJV's "vile person" and "liberal" in v5 to "fool" and "noble-minded," which matches the lexicon.

#### Complacent Women Warned of Disaster (vv9–20)
- **"Rise up, you women who are at ease… you complacent daughters" (v9).** Isaiah has rebuked the wealthy women of Jerusalem before, with their anklets and perfumes (3:16–4:1). Here the charge is not vanity but complacency. "At ease" is *sha'anan*, which BDB says can mean simply secure, but often carries the sense of careless and arrogant (Amos 6:1, "Woe to those who are at ease in Zion"). "Complacent" is from *batach*, "trust," here a false confidence. Vitringa, cited by JFB, read the "daughters" as the towns of Judah, but most take them as real women.
- **The same words, turned around (v18).** Both words come back at the end of the chapter: God's people will live in "secure (*mibtach*) dwellings, and in quiet (*sha'anan*) resting places." The words are the same, but the ground is different. Rest built on luxury and denial is about to be shattered. Rest that God gives will last. Guzik draws this out: there is nothing wrong with being secure, if the security rests on the Lord. The lexicon supports the link.
- **"In little more than a year… the grape harvest fails" (v10).** Literally "days upon a year," an idiom for a year and some days. An invading army arriving before harvest would strip the vineyards and orchards, and the next year's crop would never be gathered. Most connect this with Sennacherib's invasion of 701 BC, when he claimed to have taken forty-six of Judah's walled cities (37:30 shows the farms took two years to recover).
- **"Strip, and make yourselves bare, and tie sackcloth around your waist" (v11).** Exchange the fine clothes for rough goat-hair cloth, the dress of mourning (Ps 30:11). **"Beat your breasts" (v12)** is the gesture of grief, here for the lost fields and the "fruitful vine."
- **"The soil of my people growing up in thorns and briers" (v13).** God still calls them "my people" even as he announces judgment. Thorns and briers are Isaiah's repeated picture of a land ruined and left untended (5:6; 7:23–25). "The exultant city" is a sad echo of 22:2, where Jerusalem partied while disaster approached.
- **"The palace is forsaken… the hill and the watchtower will become dens forever" (v14).** "The hill" is *'ophel*, the Ophel, the raised part of Jerusalem between the temple mount and the old City of David where the royal buildings stood. JFB makes this identification. Wild donkeys and grazing flocks will wander where the palace stood. "Forever" here means for a long age, not without end, because v15 sets a limit: "until." The Tyndale notes observe that Jerusalem was not destroyed by Assyria, as chapter 37 shows, but by Babylon over a century later (39:6).
- **"Until the Spirit is poured upon us from on high" (v15).** This is the hinge of the chapter. The ruin lasts *until* God acts from heaven. Renewal does not grow up from below. The Spirit is "poured," the same picture as in 44:3 ("I will pour my Spirit upon your offspring"), Ezekiel 39:29 and Joel 2:28. Peter quoted Joel at Pentecost to explain what had just happened (Acts 2:17).
- **"The wilderness becomes a fruitful field, and the fruitful field is deemed a forest" (v15).** "Fruitful field" is *karmel*, garden land or orchard. The wording repeats 29:17. Everything moves up a level: desert becomes farmland, and farmland grows as tall and rich as forest. In Isaiah, renewed people and renewed land go together (35:1–2).
- **"Justice will dwell in the wilderness, and righteousness abide in the fruitful field" (v16).** The same pair from v1. What the king brings at the top, the Spirit spreads through the whole land, into its wild places and its farms.
- **"The effect of righteousness will be peace, and the result of righteousness, quietness and trust forever" (v17).** This is one of the great verses of Isaiah. "Peace" is *shalom*, which means wholeness and well-being, not just the absence of war. Righteousness comes first, and peace follows. Isaiah has already told Judah, "In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength. But you were unwilling" (30:15). Here the quietness and trust they refused is given as a gift. James seems to echo this: "a harvest of righteousness is sown in peace" (Jas 3:18), and Paul puts it in gospel terms: "since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God" (Rom 5:1).
- **"My people will abide in a peaceful habitation" (v18).** These are covenant blessings (Lev 26:5–6), the very things Judah's sin had forfeited.
- **"It will hail when the forest falls down, and the city will be utterly laid low" (v19).** This is the hardest verse in the chapter, and it seems to break the mood. Hail is often a picture of God's judgment (28:2, 17; 30:30). Most commentators think the "forest" is the enemy's army, as in 10:33–34, where the Assyrian forces are a forest cut down. The "city" may be the enemy's capital (JFB says Nineveh) or the proud city of this world generally (24:10; 25:2). The Tyndale notes read it as judgment falling all around while God's people stay safe. Either way, the peace of v18 exists alongside judgment on everything set against God.
- **"Happy are you who sow beside all waters, who let the feet of the ox and the donkey range free" (v20).** The chapter closes with a farm scene. Land well watered can be sown anywhere, and there is so much pasture that the animals can be turned loose without fear of them eating the crops or being stolen. It is a picture of plenty and security after the ruin of vv10–14. "Happy" (*'ashre*) is the word that opens Psalm 1: "Blessed is the man."
- **Commentators:** Henry sees vv15–20 as better times late in Hezekiah's reign stretching forward "as far forward as the days of the Messiah," and he insists the Spirit is the only source of real renewal: "then, and not till then, there will be good times, when God by his grace gives men good hearts." On v19 he makes a homely point, that the low city sheltered by hills rides out the hailstorm, so "those are most safe… that are humble, and are willing to dwell low." JFB places the full outpouring of the Spirit at Pentecost and in the future, and reads v20 as sowing "upon" the waters, like Ecclesiastes 11:1. Guzik stresses that the Spirit comes "from on high," not from human effort, and that peace, quietness and assurance are fruits to ask God for.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah's king is the shelter from the storm, water in the desert and shade in a weary land, and Jesus invites the weary to come to him for rest (Matt 11:28) and the thirsty to come and drink (John 7:37–39). John adds that Jesus said this "about the Spirit," who would be poured out once Jesus was glorified, which is exactly the "until" of v15. Under this King, fools stop being called noble, the blind see and stammering tongues speak plainly. And the order of v17 is the order of the gospel: righteousness first, given in Christ, and then peace, quietness and trust forever.
