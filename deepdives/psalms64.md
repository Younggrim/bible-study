*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David." No occasion is given, and Henry says there is no need to guess one, since David faced slanderers and plotters "almost all his days": Saul's court, Doeg the Edomite, the men of Ziph, Absalom and Ahithophel. The danger in this psalm is not an army but a conspiracy: people meeting in secret, sharpening words, setting traps, and confident no one is watching. It follows Psalms 62 and 63, which also ended with liars (62:4; 63:11). The psalm has a clean, almost symmetrical shape. In vv1–6 the wicked shoot their arrows "suddenly" at the innocent. In vv7–10 God shoots his arrow "suddenly" at them, and everyone sees it. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes how much shorter God's answer is than their scheming: six verses of plotting, then one arrow.

#### Hide Me from the Secret Plots of the Wicked (vv1–6)
- **"Hear my voice, O God, in my complaint" (v1).** "Complaint" (*siach*) is not grumbling. It means an earnest outpouring, a troubled musing spoken aloud. The King James has "in my prayer," and the same word is "complaint" in the title of Psalm 102: "when he is faint and pours out his complaint before the LORD." David brings his worry straight to God. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, says even a dungeon always has an opening in the roof for prayer to rise through.
- **"Preserve my life from dread of the enemy" (v1).** David asks for more than safety. He asks to be kept from the *fear* of the enemy. Henry: "not only from the thing itself which I fear, but from the disquieting fear of it." Fear can eat away at a person long before the enemy ever arrives. Morgan, cited by Guzik, says we should pray to be delivered from fear even more than from our foes. Henry adds that it is no shame to admit fear: Jacob prayed, "Please deliver me from the hand of my brother… for I fear him" (Gen 32:11).
- **"Hide me from the secret plots of the wicked, from the throng of evildoers" (v2).** "Secret plots" (*sod*) is the word for a private council, people talking things over in confidence. "Throng" (*rigshah*) is a noisy crowd. JFB: "uproar, noisy assaults, as well as their secret counsels." The quiet meeting turns into a loud mob. Henry: "The secret counsel ends in an insurrection." The verb "rage" from the same root opens Psalm 2: "Why do the nations rage?"
- **"Who whet their tongues like swords" (v3).** To "whet" is to sharpen a blade on a stone. They practice their words in advance, getting them sharp. Words as swords is a common picture in these psalms (57:4; 59:7). James warns that "the tongue is a fire" (Jas 3:6). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, calls the slanderer more dangerous than a viper.
- **"Who aim bitter words like arrows" (v3).** Literally "they tread their arrow, a bitter word." An archer bent a heavy bow by bracing it with his foot. JFB explains that the verb for bending a bow is here applied to the arrows themselves. A sword works up close, and an arrow from far off. These enemies attack both ways.
- **"Shooting from ambush at the blameless" (v4).** "Ambush" is "hiding places." They hit from cover, so the target never sees who shot. Kidner, cited by Guzik, suggests this could be a framed accusation or an anonymous rumor. "Blameless" (*tam*) means whole, of integrity, not sinless. JFB: "one innocent of the charges made." It is the word used of Job (Job 1:1). Henry: "The better any man is the more he is envied by those that are themselves bad."
- **"Shooting at him suddenly and without fear" (v4).** "Suddenly" (*pit'om*) will come back in v7. Boice, cited by Guzik, calls it the key word of the psalm. "Without fear" means they do not fear God (55:19). Henry: "they fear not the wrath of God, which will be the portion of a false tongue."
- **"They hold fast to their evil purpose" (v5).** Literally "they strengthen for themselves an evil word." They encourage each other. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes how the wicked keep up each other's spirits. Henry quotes a Latin proverb: slander boldly and something will stick. He adds, "It is bad to do a wrong thing, but worse to encourage ourselves and one another in doing it."
- **"They talk of laying snares secretly, thinking, 'Who can see them?'" (v5).** The same question as in 59:7 ("Who will hear us?"). The root of it all is practical atheism: they believe no one, God included, is watching. Henry: "A practical disbelief of God's omniscience is at the bottom of all the wickedness of the wicked."
- **"They search out injustice, saying, 'We have accomplished a diligent search'" (v6).** The Hebrew is compressed and hard. One reading: they dig hard for something wrong in David they can use against him. Another: they work hard at inventing new ways to do wrong. Henry offers both. The ESV puts their words in quotation marks: they boast of a well-laid plan. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, reads it as a "perfect" plot. Henry: "Half the pains that many take to damn their souls would serve to save them."
- **"For the inward mind and heart of a man are deep" (v6).** "Deep" (*'amoq*) means unfathomable. On one level it says these plotters are cunning, their plans hidden far down. On another, it is a general truth about every human heart. Jeremiah says the same: "The heart is deceitful above all things… who can understand it?" (Jer 17:9). The Tyndale notes say human beings can sink to unthinkable depths. Boice, cited by Guzik, says the heart's capacity for evil is almost bottomless. What is too deep for people is not too deep for God (139:1–4).
- **Commentators:** Henry compares the enemies to archers aiming at "the harmless bird that apprehends not herself in any danger," and calls bitter words "the fiery darts of the wicked one" (Eph 6:16). JFB says v5 shows "their deliberate malice" and v6 their "diligent efforts and deeply laid schemes." The Tyndale notes suggest the deadly words of v3 may refer to curses or magical spells as well as lies.

#### But God Shoots His Arrow at Them (vv7–10)
- **"But God shoots his arrow at them; they are wounded suddenly" (v7).** The turning point is a single word, "But." The verb "shoot" (*yarah*) and the word "suddenly" (*pit'om*) both repeat from v4. They shot from hiding, suddenly. God shoots, suddenly, and no hiding place is deep enough. JFB: "The contrast is heightened by representing God as using weapons like theirs." Their many arrows are words. God needs only one. Henry: "God's arrows will hit surer, and fly swifter, and pierce deeper, than theirs do or can." Psalm 7:12–13 also pictures God as an archer: "he has bent and readied his bow… making his arrows fiery shafts."
- **"They are brought to ruin, with their own tongues turned against them" (v8).** Literally "they make him stumble, their tongue against them." The weapon they sharpened becomes the thing that trips them. Their own words become the evidence against them. Proverbs says, "a fool's lips walk into a fight… his lips are a snare to his soul" (Prov 18:6–7). Jesus said, "by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned" (Matt 12:37). Henry: "Many have cut their own throats… with their tongues."
- **"All who see them will wag their heads" (v8).** The verb (*nud*) can mean to shake the head, in shock or mockery, or to flee. The King James has "all that see them shall flee away," and JFB takes it that their partners in evil will run in terror. The ESV takes it as shaking heads. The men who asked "Who can see them?" (v5) are now seen by everyone.
- **"Then all mankind fears" (v9).** The fear that the wicked lacked (v4), all people now have. When God's judgment comes, people take God seriously. Guzik links this to Isaiah 26:9: "when your judgments are in the earth, the inhabitants of the world learn righteousness."
- **"They tell what God has brought about and ponder what he has done" (v9).** "Ponder" (*sakal*) means to consider wisely, to understand. The King James: "they shall wisely consider of his doing." The plotters' "diligent search" (v6) found only evil. Now people search out God's deeds and find wisdom. The Tyndale notes say the godly grow in wisdom as they reflect on God's mighty acts.
- **"Let the righteous one rejoice in the LORD and take refuge in him!" (v10).** The covenant name *YHWH* appears only here in the psalm, at the end. The righteous do not rejoice in their enemies' fall as such, but "in the LORD," in his justice and faithfulness. Their response is to trust him more. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says that a God who keeps his warnings will not forget his promises.
- **"Let all the upright in heart exult!" (v10).** "Upright in heart" is set against the "deep" heart of v6. Two kinds of heart, two endings. Kidner, cited by Guzik, describes the joy here as sober: it has faced the worst and found God's best greater.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "it is observable how the punishment answers the sin." He says that when God brings on men the harm they wished on others, "he makes their own tongues to fall upon them; and it is weight enough to sink a man… like a talent of lead." Guzik says the answer to "Who can see them?" turns out to be "everyone." JFB: "Men, generally, will acknowledge God's work, and the righteous… shall be encouraged to trust Him" (58:10). Horne, cited by Guzik, reads the psalm of Christ and his enemies.

#### Pointing to Christ
Henry, in his opening note, says the psalm shows "the old enmity" between the seed of the serpent and the seed of the woman (Gen 3:15). It reached its height in Jesus. His enemies met in secret council to plot how to arrest him "by stealth and kill him" (Matt 26:3–4). They searched diligently for false testimony against the blameless one (Mark 14:55–56), and they sharpened bitter words at his trial and at the cross, where "those who passed by derided him, wagging their heads" (Matt 27:39). They thought they had succeeded. But God's arrow struck where they never expected: the cross they planned became the defeat of the serpent (Col 2:15), and the resurrection exposed their plot before the world. Now the words of v9 are being fulfilled as all nations "tell what God has brought about" in Christ, and "the righteous one" rejoices in the LORD and takes refuge in him.
