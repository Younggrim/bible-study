*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, near the end of the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapter 24 was full of laws protecting the weak: borrowers, day laborers, widows and gleaners. Chapter 25 continues the run of short laws: a limit on how hard a guilty man may be beaten, an ox allowed to eat while it works, a widow's right to a family name for her dead husband, honest weights in the market. It closes with a command to remember Amalek, the people who attacked Israel's stragglers in the wilderness. Next comes chapter 26, the closing ceremony of the law section. The chapter's laws seem unrelated, but they share one concern: the dignity of those who are vulnerable, whether a beaten man, a working animal, a childless widow or a tired straggler at the back of the line.

#### Miscellaneous Laws (vv1–4)
- **"If there is a dispute between men and they come into court" (v1).** The ESV heading from 24:5 runs on through these verses. Judges were to **"acquit the innocent and condemn the guilty."** The Hebrew is literally "declare righteous the righteous and declare wicked the wicked." This is the legal language Paul later uses for justification. God's justice is that he "justifies the ungodly" (Rom 4:5), which no human judge was allowed to do (Prov 17:15). Only the cross explains how God can do it and remain just (Rom 3:26).
- **"The judge shall cause him to lie down and be beaten in his presence" (v2).** Beating with a rod or whip was the normal punishment for crimes short of death. The judge had to be present, so the punishment stayed under the court's control, not left to an angry crowd or a cruel officer. **"In proportion to his offense"**: the number of blows had to fit the crime.
- **"Forty stripes may be given him, but not more" (v3).** There was a fixed upper limit. Other ancient law codes allowed sixty blows or more, and Egyptian and Assyrian records show men beaten until crippled. Later Jewish practice stopped at thirty-nine, in case of a miscount. Paul received "forty lashes less one" five times from the synagogues (2 Cor 11:24).
- **"Lest… your brother be degraded in your sight" (v3).** This is the reason, and it is striking. The guilty man is still "your brother." Punishment could correct him but must not destroy his dignity. The same word for "degraded" (*qalah*) is used for treating parents with contempt (27:16).
- **"You shall not muzzle an ox when it is treading out the grain" (v4).** Grain was threshed by driving oxen in circles over the cut stalks on a hard threshing floor, so their hooves knocked the grain loose. A muzzle would keep the ox from eating the grain it was working in all day. God says let it eat. Proverbs says, "Whoever is righteous has regard for the life of his beast" (Prov 12:10).
- **Paul's use of this verse.** Paul quotes it twice to argue that ministers of the gospel should be paid by those they serve (1 Cor 9:9–11; 1 Tim 5:17–18). "Is it for oxen that God is concerned? Does he not certainly speak for our sake?" (1 Cor 9:9–10). Paul is not denying that God cares about oxen. He is arguing from the lesser to the greater: if God provides for an animal that works, how much more for a person who works for the gospel. The verse sits among laws about human dignity, which supports the idea that its principle reaches beyond animals. In 1 Timothy Paul sets it beside Jesus' saying, "The laborer deserves his wages" (Luke 10:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the muzzle law "a charge to husbandmen," which "teaches us to make much of the animals that serve us," and applies it to supporting teachers. JFB says the forty-stripe limit set Israel apart from Egyptian and other rulers who beat people to death, and that later practice used a three-cord whip, thirteen strokes counting as thirty-nine. Guzik notes that the law did not require forty, only forbade more, and stresses that a guilty person keeps his dignity before God.

#### Laws Concerning Levirate Marriage (vv5–10)
- **"If brothers dwell together, and one of them dies and has no son" (v5).** This custom is called "levirate" marriage, from the Latin *levir*, "husband's brother." The Hebrew verb *yabam* means to do the duty of a husband's brother. The case is limited: brothers living together, probably on the same family land, and a brother who dies with no son.
- **"Her husband's brother shall go in to her and take her as his wife" (v5).** The widow was not to be married off to an outsider, which would take her and her husband's land out of the family. The brother would marry her, even if he was already married.
- **"The first son whom she bears shall succeed to the name of his dead brother, that his name may not be blotted out of Israel" (v6).** The child would legally be the dead man's son and heir. This mattered deeply in Israel. Land was tied to a family name and given by God, and a man's name living on through his sons was a sign of God's blessing. It also gave the widow a home, a husband and a son to care for her old age, in a world where a childless widow could be left with nothing.
- **The custom is older than Moses.** Judah's sons were expected to do this for Tamar. Onan took Tamar as his wife but deliberately refused to give her a child, because the child "would not be his," and the LORD put him to death (Gen 38:8–10). Tamar's son Perez was in the line of David and of Jesus (Matt 1:3).
- **"If the man does not wish to take his brother's wife" (v7).** He could refuse. This was not forced. But the widow could take him to the elders at the gate, and if he still refused, **"his brother's wife shall go up to him… and pull his sandal off his foot and spit in his face"** (v9). The sandal stood for his right to walk on and claim the land (compare Josh 1:3; Ps 60:8), which he was giving up. Spitting in the face was a deep public insult (Num 12:14). The Tyndale notes call it a nearly universal sign of contempt.
- **"So shall it be done to the man who does not build up his brother's house" (v9).** "Build up his brother's house" means give him descendants. **"The house of him who had his sandal pulled off"** (v10) would follow his family as a nickname. The law gave the man a choice but made selfishness costly.
- **Ruth and Boaz.** The book of Ruth shows a related custom. A nearer relative refused to redeem the land of Ruth's dead husband when he learned he would have to marry Ruth and raise a son who would inherit it, "lest I impair my own inheritance" (Ruth 4:6). He took off his own sandal and gave it to Boaz to confirm the transfer (Ruth 4:7–8), with no spitting. Boaz was not a brother but a more distant relative, a *go'el* or "redeemer." He married Ruth, and their son Obed was counted as Naomi's family line. Obed was the grandfather of David (Ruth 4:13–17).
- **The Sadducees' question.** The Sadducees, who denied the resurrection, used this law to mock it. They told Jesus of a woman married in turn to seven brothers, each dying childless, and asked whose wife she would be in the resurrection (Matt 22:23–28). Jesus answered that in the resurrection people "neither marry nor are given in marriage," and that they knew "neither the Scriptures nor the power of God" (Matt 22:29–30).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the custom came before Moses (Gen 38:8) and that Moses made it binding in law, tying it to keeping a family's name and property. Henry explains it as a way of keeping family inheritances distinct, and notes that it no longer applies. Guzik points out from Kalland that the Hebrew word "son" can mean "child," so a daughter could also count, since daughters could inherit when there was no son (Num 27:1–8). He also follows Clarke in suggesting the spitting may have been on the ground before the man rather than in his face, though the Hebrew "in his face" is plain.

#### Miscellaneous Laws (vv11–19)
- **"When men fight… and the wife of the one… seizes him by the private parts" (v11).** A wife sees her husband losing a fight and grabs the other man's genitals. Her act could leave him unable to have children, the very thing the levirate law above was protecting. **"You shall cut off her hand. Your eye shall have no pity"** (v12). This is the only penalty of mutilation in the law of Moses apart from "eye for eye." It is severe, and the reason seems to be the seriousness of destroying a man's ability to have descendants. Jewish interpreters from early times did not apply it literally. The rabbis read it as requiring payment for the shame and injury done, in line with how they treated "eye for eye." Others have suggested the Hebrew word for "hand" (*kaph*) here could mean a different part of the body, which would make the penalty mirror the crime more exactly. The ESV's reading is the plain and common one.
- **"Two kinds of weights, a large and a small" (v13).** The Hebrew is literally "stone and stone." Weights were stones of different sizes kept in a merchant's bag. A dishonest trader kept a heavy set for buying, so he got more, and a light set for selling, so he gave less. **"Two kinds of measures"** (v14) is literally "ephah and ephah," the basket used to measure grain. Archaeologists have found many ancient weights that do not match the size marked on them.
- **"A full and fair weight you shall have" (v15).** "Full" is *shelemah*, "complete, whole," related to *shalom*. "Fair" is *tsedeq*, "righteous." Honest weights were a matter of righteousness. **"That your days may be long in the land"**: the same promise attached to honoring parents (5:16). God's people were not to be holy at the altar and crooked at the market.
- **"All who act dishonestly, are an abomination to the LORD" (v16).** The same strong word used for idolatry. Proverbs repeats the point three times: "A false balance is an abomination to the LORD, but a just weight is his delight" (Prov 11:1; compare 16:11; 20:10, 23). The prophets condemned merchants who made "the ephah small and the shekel great" (Amos 8:5; Mic 6:10–11).
- **"Remember what Amalek did to you" (v17).** The Amalekites were desert raiders descended from Esau's grandson Amalek (Gen 36:12). Shortly after the Red Sea, they attacked Israel at Rephidim. Joshua fought them while Moses held up his hands on the hill, and God swore to "utterly blot out the memory of Amalek" (Exod 17:8–16).
- **"He attacked you on the way when you were faint and weary, and cut off your tail" (v18).** Exodus does not give this detail. "Cut off your tail" is a single Hebrew verb, *zinnev*, which the BDB lexicon defines as "attack in the rear." Amalek did not meet Israel's fighting men. They picked off the stragglers at the back: the old, the sick, mothers with children, the exhausted. **"And he did not fear God."** That is the heart of the charge. Attacking the helpless showed contempt for the God who defends them.
- **"You shall blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven; you shall not forget" (v19).** The command waits until Israel had **"rest from all your enemies."** In Samuel's day God sent Saul to carry it out. Saul spared King Agag and the best of the livestock, and God rejected him as king (1 Sam 15). Saul's death was later reported by an Amalekite (2 Sam 1:8–10). David fought Amalekite raiders who had burned Ziklag (1 Sam 30), and in Hezekiah's day the last of them were struck down (1 Chr 4:43). Many see one more echo in the book of Esther: Haman, who plotted to wipe out every Jew, is called "the Agagite" (Esth 3:1), probably a descendant of Amalek's king Agag. The command to destroy Amalek belongs, like the Canaanite command in chapter 20, to God's judgment on one nation at one time for its deliberate cruelty. It is not a pattern for hatred of any people today.
- **Commentators:** Henry on the weights: "Dishonest gain always brings a curse on men's property, families, and souls." On Amalek: "The longer it is before judgement comes, the more dreadful will it be at last," and "Amalek may remind us of the foes of our souls." JFB calls Amalek's attack "an unprovoked outrage on the laws of nature and humanity" and points out that Exodus 17 did not record the attack on the stragglers. JFB has no note on vv11–12. Guzik, like Henry, notes that many readers see Amalek as a picture of the sinful nature that wars against the Spirit (Gal 5:17) and must not be spared, and he follows Clarke in connecting Haman's downfall to this command.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter sets a limit on the beating a guilty man may receive, so that "your brother" is not degraded. Jesus, who was not guilty, was flogged by the Romans, who had no such limit, and mocked and spat upon (Mark 14:65; 15:15–19). He took the shame so that the guilty could be "declared righteous." He is also the true kinsman-redeemer, the brother who does "build up his brother's house." Where the nearer relative in Ruth's story refused to risk his own inheritance, Christ gave his own life to raise up a family that bears his name (Heb 2:11–13). Amalek preyed on the weary at the back of the line. Jesus does the opposite: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).
