*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Chapter 28 set out the blessings and curses of the covenant. Chapter 29 begins Moses' last great appeal (chapters 29–30), a covenant renewal ceremony for the generation about to enter Canaan. Almost everyone who had stood at Mount Sinai (also called Horeb) forty years earlier had died in the wilderness (2:14–15). Their children now stand before the LORD to take the covenant on themselves. Moses reviews what God has done, gathers the whole community, warns against the one person who thinks he can quietly keep his idols, and pictures a future generation looking at a ruined land and asking why. A small numbering note: the ESV's 29:1 is the last verse of chapter 28 in Hebrew Bibles, so Hebrew verse numbers in this chapter run one lower than English ones.

#### The Covenant Renewed in Moab (vv1–29)
- **"Besides the covenant that he had made with them at Horeb" (v1).** This is not a new or different covenant. It is the Sinai covenant renewed for a new generation, in new circumstances, on the edge of the land. JFB says it was "substantially the same; but it was renewed now, in different circumstances." Many scholars take v1 as the closing summary of chapters 5–28, which is how the Hebrew Bible arranges it. Others read it as the heading for what follows.
- **"You have seen all that the LORD did before your eyes in the land of Egypt" (v2).** Most of these listeners were children or not yet born at the Exodus. Moses speaks to them as if they were there. The nation is one people across the generations, and the story belongs to all of them.
- **"But to this day the LORD has not given you a heart to understand or eyes to see or ears to hear" (v4).** One of the most striking verses in Deuteronomy. Israel saw the plagues, the sea, the manna and the water from the rock, and still did not truly understand. Miracles did not change their hearts. Moses does not excuse them. They are fully responsible (9:6–7). But he admits that a new heart is something only God can give. In the next chapter he promises God will do just that: "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart" (30:6). Paul quotes this verse, joined with Isaiah 29:10, to explain Israel's unbelief in his own day (Rom 11:8). Jesus speaks of people who "seeing… do not see, and hearing… do not hear" (Matt 13:13–15).
- **"Your clothes have not worn out on you, and your sandals have not worn off your feet" (v5).** Notice that Moses suddenly speaks in God's voice ("I have led you"). Forty years of walking in the desert, and God kept their clothes and sandals from falling apart (compare 8:4; Neh 9:21). Some take this to mean a direct miracle. Others think God provided through trade and flocks, so nothing ran out. The text presents it as God's care either way.
- **"You have not eaten bread, and you have not drunk wine or strong drink" (v6).** In the wilderness they did not live on farmed grain and wine but on manna and water from the rock. The purpose: **"that you may know that I am the LORD your God."** The same lesson Jesus quoted in the wilderness: "man does not live by bread alone" (8:3; Matt 4:4).
- **Sihon and Og (vv7–8).** The two Amorite kings east of the Jordan, defeated in chapters 2–3. Their land was already given to Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh. God's promises were not just words. Part of the land was already Israel's.
- **"Therefore keep the words of this covenant" (v9).** The pattern of the whole book: first what God has done, then what Israel must do. Grace comes first, and obedience is the response.
- **"You are standing today, all of you, before the LORD your God" (v10).** Everyone is listed: tribal heads, elders, officers, men, **"your little ones, your wives, and the sojourner… from the one who chops your wood to the one who draws your water"** (v11). Woodcutters and water carriers did the lowest jobs in the camp. Later the Gibeonites were given this role (Josh 9:21–27). From the highest leader to the lowest servant, and including foreigners, all stood equally before God. Henry says the covenant reached "down to the meanest drudge they had among them."
- **"The sworn covenant" (v12).** The Hebrew combines "covenant" (*berith*) with "oath" or "curse" (*'alah*). Entering the covenant meant swearing an oath that called the curses of chapter 28 down on oneself if it was broken. The aim, though, is relationship: **"that he may establish you today as his people, and that he may be your God"** (v13), the heart of every covenant from Abraham onward (Gen 17:7; Exod 6:7; Jer 31:33; Rev 21:3).
- **"With whoever is standing here… and with whoever is not here with us today" (v15).** The covenant reaches future generations not yet born. Every later Israelite stood, in a sense, on the plains of Moab. This explains why later prophets could hold people to promises made centuries before.
- **"Their detestable things, their idols of wood and stone" (v17).** "Detestable things" is *shiqqutsim*, things to be loathed. "Idols" is *gillulim*. Many have taken this word to mean "dung-pellets," a mocking name, and the Tyndale notes favor that. The BDB lexicon lists that view alongside others ("logs, blocks, shapeless things," or standing stones), so the insult is possible but not certain. Ezekiel uses the word almost forty times.
- **"A man or woman or clan or tribe whose heart is turning away today" (v18).** The danger starts with one heart. It can spread to a family, then a clan, then a tribe.
- **"A root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit" (v18).** "Poisonous" is *rosh*, a bitter, poisonous plant, and "bitter" is *la'anah*, wormwood. Hidden idolatry is a root under the ground that grows into a poisonous plant. Hebrews 12:15 picks up this verse: "that no 'root of bitterness' springs up and causes trouble, and by it many become defiled."
- **"Blesses himself in his heart, saying, 'I shall be safe, though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart'" (v19).** "Safe" is *shalom*, peace. He hears the curses and quietly tells himself they are not for him. He thinks he can belong to the covenant people and keep his own way. "Stubbornness" (*sherirut*) is a word Jeremiah uses often for Israel's hard hearts (Jer 7:24; 9:14). Guzik compares this false peace to that of a passenger who does not know there is a bomb on his plane: the calm comes from ignorance, not safety.
- **"This will lead to the sweeping away of moist and dry alike" (v19).** A hard phrase, literally "to sweep away the watered with the thirsty." It seems to be a proverb for total destruction: everything, good and bad, green and dry, swept away together. One person's secret sin can bring ruin on many (compare Achan in Josh 7).
- **"The LORD will not be willing to forgive him… the LORD will blot out his name from under heaven" (v20).** This is aimed at the person who presumes on God's grace while refusing to turn from sin. **"His jealousy will smoke"** is a picture of anger like a fire. God's jealousy is the rightful zeal of a husband for his wife's faithfulness (5:9).
- **"The whole land burned out with brimstone and salt… like that of Sodom and Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim" (v23).** Sodom and Gomorrah were destroyed in Abraham's day (Gen 19). Admah and Zeboiim were nearby cities destroyed with them (Gen 14:2; Hos 11:8). The area around the Dead Sea, with its salt flats and sulfur deposits, was a picture everyone knew.
- **"Why has the LORD done thus to this land?" (v24).** Moses imagines the future. Foreigners will look at Israel's ruined land and ask the question, and the answer will be plain: **"because they abandoned the covenant of the LORD"** (v25). Solomon (1 Kgs 9:8–9) and Jeremiah (Jer 22:8–9) echo this almost word for word. **"Gods… whom he had not allotted to them"** (v26) recalls 4:19 and 32:8: the nations had their gods, but Israel belonged to the LORD alone.
- **"Cast them into another land, as they are this day" (v28).** The verb "cast" pictures throwing something away. "As they are this day" is spoken from the viewpoint of the future speakers, people living in exile. In the Hebrew text the word "cast" is written with an unusually large first letter, a scribal feature whose meaning is uncertain.
- **"The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever" (v29).** One of the best-known verses in Deuteronomy. After describing a future that is hard to grasp, with the chosen people in exile, Moses draws a line. God's hidden purposes (how and when he will judge and restore, why he does what he does) are his. What he has made known, his law, is ours to keep. **"That we may do"** is the point. In the Hebrew text a series of dots is written above "to us and to our children," one of a handful of such dotted passages in the Hebrew Bible. Rabbis debated what it meant, but it does not change the reading.
- **Commentators:** On v4, Henry: "The hearing ear, the seeing eye, and the understanding heart, are the gift of God." Guzik makes the same point: miracles alone do not produce faith, only God's work in the heart does, and he draws on Spurgeon for why people stay blind. Guzik also notes Paul's use of v4 in Romans 11:8. On v19, Henry warns that "there are many who are under the curse of God and yet bless themselves," and on v25 says, "God never forsakes any till they first forsake him." JFB comments on vv10–29 as a single block. It says some would think God "would wink at such liberties," and it finds that v29 has "no apparent connection with the thread of discourse," linking it with Romans 11:33, "How unsearchable are his judgments." Guzik sees a closer link: after describing judgment no one can fully explain, Moses turns the people back to what they can and must do. Henry also reads the verse as a warning against prying into God's secret counsels and a call to study and practise what he has revealed.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses admits in v4 that the LORD had not yet given Israel "a heart to understand." That is the problem the whole Old Testament leaves open, and the gospel answers it. The new covenant that Jeremiah foresaw is one where God writes his law on the heart (Jer 31:33), and Jesus announced that covenant in his blood (Luke 22:20). He opened his disciples' minds to understand the Scriptures (Luke 24:45). The "secret things" of v29 included God's plan of salvation, which Paul calls "the mystery hidden for ages and generations but now revealed to his saints" (Col 1:26). And the covenant made "with whoever is not here with us today" reached further than Moses could see: to Gentiles who were once "strangers to the covenants of promise," now "brought near by the blood of Christ" (Eph 2:12–13).
