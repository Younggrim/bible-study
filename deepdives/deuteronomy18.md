*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Since 16:18 Moses has been describing the offices that will lead Israel in the land. Chapter 16 dealt with local judges, and chapter 17 with the central court and the king. Chapter 18 finishes the list with the priests and Levites, and then the prophet. In between, Moses forbids every Canaanite way of trying to learn hidden things or control the future. That placement is the point: Israel is not to look for guidance from diviners or the dead, because God will speak to them through a prophet he raises up. Moses was about to die, and the people would soon cross the Jordan without the man through whom God had spoken to them for forty years. This chapter answers the question they must have been asking: who will speak for God when Moses is gone?

#### Provision for Priests and Levites (vv1–8)
- **"The Levitical priests, all the tribe of Levi" (v1).** Deuteronomy often speaks of "the Levitical priests" as one group. Strictly, only Aaron's descendants served as priests, and the other Levites assisted them (Num 3:5–10; 18:1–7). Deuteronomy's point here is that the whole tribe shared one position: **"no portion or inheritance with Israel."** When the land was divided, Levi received only towns to live in and pasture around them (Num 35:1–8; Josh 21).
- **"The LORD is their inheritance" (v2).** The other tribes would have fields and vineyards. Levi would have God. The Tyndale notes observe the beautiful reversal: Israel is the LORD's special possession (7:6), and the LORD is Levi's. The psalmist borrows the language for every believer: "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup… I have a beautiful inheritance" (Ps 16:5–6; compare 73:26). **"As he promised them"** points back to Numbers 18:20.
- **"The shoulder and the two cheeks and the stomach" (v3).** When an Israelite killed an ox or sheep for a sacrifice, these cuts went to the priest. The Tyndale notes say they were among the choice cuts in Israel's diet. Leviticus 7:31–34 names the breast and right thigh of the peace offering. Whether this is a different portion, perhaps from animals slaughtered at the feasts, or an addition, is not clear, and JFB says so honestly. Eli's sons grabbed meat with a fork before the fat was even burned, and the Lord judged them for despising his offering (1 Sam 2:12–17).
- **"The firstfruits… and the first fleece of your sheep" (v4).** Grain, wine and oil were the three staples of the land (7:13). The first fleece is mentioned only here. The first of everything went to God through his servants.
- **"Chosen him out of all your tribes to stand and minister in the name of the LORD" (v5).** "Stand" is the posture of a servant waiting on his master. The priesthood was God's choice, not something a man could take for himself (Heb 5:4).
- **"If a Levite comes from any of your towns… when he desires" (v6).** Levites lived scattered in towns across Israel. Any Levite whose heart moved him to serve at the central sanctuary could come and serve, and he was to receive **"equal portions to eat"** (v8), not be treated as a newcomer by the Levites already there. Once worship was centered in one place, the country Levites could easily have been pushed aside, and this law protected them.
- **"Besides what he receives from the sale of his patrimony" (v8).** The Hebrew is difficult, literally "besides his sales according to the fathers." Most take it to mean family property or income he already had. A Levite did not have to give up private property to serve. Jeremiah, from a priestly family in the Levite town of Anathoth, later bought a family field there (Jer 32:6–9).
- **Commentators:** Henry says ministers should not be entangled in worldly business, since "they have better things to mind," and the other side of that is plain: "The people must provide for them." Paul makes the same argument from these laws (1 Cor 9:13–14). JFB says of the country Levite that "as he was admitted to a share of the work, so also to a share of the remuneration." Guzik treats the Levite who comes "when he desires" as a picture of someone moved by a God-given desire to serve, and draws on Clarke to show that Levites could still own land, as Abiathar and Jeremiah did.

#### Abominable Practices (vv9–14)
- **"You shall not learn to follow the abominable practices of those nations" (v9).** The danger was not that Israel would invent these things. It was that they would *learn* them from their new neighbors. Moses names nine practices, the fullest list of forbidden occult practices in the Bible. They fall roughly into three groups: offering children, trying to read hidden knowledge, and trying to control spirits or reach the dead.
- **"Anyone who burns his son or his daughter as an offering" (v10).** Literally "makes his son or daughter pass through the fire." Child sacrifice was practiced in the region and is linked in Scripture with the god Molech (Lev 18:21; 20:2–5). It heads the list because it was the worst, and because it was tied to the others: people offered children to buy favor or knowledge from the gods. Kings Ahaz and Manasseh did it in the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem (2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6), and Jeremiah said such a thing had never entered God's mind (Jer 7:31). The valley's name, *Ge Hinnom*, became "Gehenna," Jesus' word for hell.
- **"Divination… tells fortunes… interprets omens" (v10).** *Qosem qesamim*, "one who divines," uses a general word for seeking hidden knowledge. Balaam was one (Josh 13:22), and Nebuchadnezzar consulted arrows and a liver to choose his road (Ezek 21:21). The Tyndale notes explain that ancient diviners often read the livers and organs of sacrificed animals. *Me'onen*, "fortune-teller," may come from a word for clouds or for a low humming. *Menachesh*, "one who interprets omens," uses the same root as Joseph's silver cup, by which, his steward said, he "practices divination" (Gen 44:5).
- **"A sorcerer or a charmer" (vv10–11).** A *mekhashsheph* practiced sorcery, the word used for Pharaoh's magicians (Exod 7:11) and for the sorceress in Exodus 22:18. A *chover chever* is literally "one who binds a binding," someone who casts spells to bind or harm others. Balak hired Balaam to curse Israel in exactly this way (Num 22:6).
- **"A medium or a necromancer or one who inquires of the dead" (v11).** An *'ov* was a medium who claimed to call up the spirits of the dead. The BDB lexicon notes that the word can also mean a ghost, and Isaiah mocks mediums "who chirp and mutter" (Isa 8:19). A *yidde'oni*, "necromancer" or "spiritist," is literally a "knowing one," someone claiming secret knowledge from the spirit world. Saul banned them and then, desperate, consulted the medium of Endor (1 Sam 28:3–25). It did not save him (1 Chr 10:13–14). Isaiah's question still stands: "Should they inquire of the dead on behalf of the living?" (Isa 8:19).
- **"Because of these abominations the LORD your God is driving them out" (v12).** The conquest was not favoritism. God was judging Canaan's sin, which he had told Abraham would one day be "complete" (Gen 15:16). If Israel copied those sins, they would face the same judgment, and 2 Kings 17:17–18 records that this is exactly what happened to the northern kingdom.
- **"You shall be blameless before the LORD your God" (v13).** *Tamim* means whole, complete, wholehearted: the word used for an unblemished sacrifice and for Noah and Abraham (Gen 6:9; 17:1). Here it means undivided loyalty. Israel was not to trust God and keep a fortune-teller on the side.
- **"But as for you, the LORD your God has not allowed you to do this" (v14).** The other nations "listen" to diviners. Israel will "listen" to a prophet (v15). The same verb, *shama'*, connects the two paragraphs. The question is not whether people will seek guidance but whom they will listen to.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls diviners "mere impostors who blind and cheat their followers." Guzik groups the practices much as above (foretelling, controlling, contacting the dead), names their modern forms (horoscopes, tarot, Ouija boards, psychics), and urges Christians to renounce any past involvement, pointing to the Ephesians who burned their magic books (Acts 19:19). He follows older writers in tracing "sorcery" to a root meaning "to cut," but the BDB lexicon links *kashaph* instead to an Assyrian word that simply means "practice sorcery," so that derivation should not be pressed. JFB says the Canaanites, and especially the Philistines (Isa 2:6), were "a constant snare and stumbling block" in this area.

#### A New Prophet like Moses (vv15–22)
- **"The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me" (v15).** The word "prophet" is singular. In the first place this promises that God would keep sending true prophets, like Samuel, Elijah and Isaiah, so Israel would never need diviners. But the words go further. **"Like me"** is a high standard: Deuteronomy itself ends by saying "there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face" (34:10). By Jesus' day, Jews were waiting for "the Prophet" as a figure of the end times. The priests asked John the Baptist, "Are you the Prophet?" (John 1:21), and after the feeding of the five thousand the crowd said, "This is indeed the Prophet who is to come into the world!" (John 6:14; 7:40). The Dead Sea Scrolls include a list of messianic proof-texts that quotes these very verses. The Samaritans, too, looked for a prophet like Moses (compare John 4:25).
- **"From among you, from your brothers—it is to him you shall listen" (v15).** He would be an Israelite. At the transfiguration, with Moses himself standing there, the Father's voice from the cloud said of Jesus, "listen to him" (Matt 17:5), echoing this verse.
- **"Just as you desired… at Horeb… 'Let me not hear again the voice of the LORD'" (v16).** At Sinai the people heard God speak the Ten Commandments from the fire and begged Moses to go between them and God (5:23–27; Exod 20:18–21). God's answer: **"They are right in what they have spoken"** (v17). Sinful people cannot stand in the blazing presence of God without a mediator. The prophet was God's gracious answer to that fear.
- **"I will put my words in his mouth, and he shall speak to them all that I command him" (v18).** This is the definition of a true prophet: he says what God gives him, no more and no less. God said almost the same to Jeremiah (Jer 1:9). Jesus said of himself, "I have not spoken on my own authority, but the Father who sent me has himself given me a commandment—what to say and what to speak" (John 12:49).
- **"Whoever will not listen… I myself will require it of him" (v19).** Refusing God's spokesman is refusing God. Peter preached this verse in the temple, saying that this prophet was Jesus, and that everyone who would not listen to him would be "destroyed from the people" (Acts 3:22–23). Stephen quoted the promise too (Acts 7:37).
- **"The prophet who presumes to speak a word in my name that I have not commanded… shall die" (v20).** Two kinds of false prophets are named: one who speaks in the LORD's name what the LORD did not say, and one who speaks for other gods. Chapter 13 already dealt with the second. Jeremiah faced the first kind, men like Hananiah who promised peace in God's name. Jeremiah told him he would die within the year, and he did (Jer 28:15–17).
- **"How may we know the word that the LORD has not spoken?" (v21).** Moses gives a practical test: **"if the word does not come to pass or come true, that is a word that the LORD has not spoken"** (v22). A real prophet's predictions come true. This test works alongside the test in 13:1–5: even if a sign does come true, a prophet who leads people to other gods is false. Together they mean a true prophet must be both accurate and faithful to the God already revealed. Some prophecies were conditional, depending on how people responded, as Jonah's warning to Nineveh was (Jonah 3:4–10; Jer 18:7–10), so the test applies to plain predictions spoken in God's name.
- **"You need not be afraid of him" (v22).** The false prophet's threats carry no weight. Israel could ignore him.
- **Commentators:** All three read vv15–19 as finally fulfilled in Christ, and Guzik and the Tyndale notes both allow that the line of true prophets fulfilled it in part first. JFB lists ways the Messiah is "like unto Moses" and notes Peter and Stephen's use of the text. Guzik observes that chapters 17 and 18 between them describe king, priest and prophet, the three offices Christ fills, and that Hebrews 3:1–6 shows Jesus as greater than Moses. On the test of a prophet, Guzik stresses that accuracy is the first test but not the only one, and that the New Testament still calls the church to weigh prophecy (1 Cor 14:29; 1 John 4:1). Henry: "It highly concerns us to have a right touchstone wherewith to try the word we hear."

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses promised Israel a prophet like himself, and Jesus is that prophet in a fuller way than Moses could have imagined. Like Moses he was spared as a baby from a king who killed children, came out of Egypt, fasted forty days, fed God's people in the wilderness, gave God's word on a mountain, and stood between a sinful people and a holy God. But "Moses was faithful in all God's house as a servant… but Christ is faithful over God's house as a son" (Heb 3:5–6). Moses spoke the words God put in his mouth. Jesus is the Word (John 1:1). Israel at Sinai begged not to hear God's voice from the fire. In Jesus, God speaks to us face to face, without the fire, "in these last days… by his Son" (Heb 1:2). The nations listen to diviners and the dead. God's people are told to listen to him.
