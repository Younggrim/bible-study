*Setting:* Solomon's fourth year, about 967 BC, in Jerusalem. The timber from Lebanon and the stone from the quarries are ready (ch 5). Now the building begins. Chapters 6–8 are the center of Solomon's story: the temple is built (ch 6), furnished (ch 7) and dedicated (ch 8). The writer gives measurements in detail, much as Exodus did for the tabernacle (Exod 25–31; 35–40), because this was the place God chose to put his name and meet his people. Second Chronicles 3 tells it more briefly and names the site: Mount Moriah, on the threshing floor of Araunah where David built an altar to stop the plague (2 Chr 3:1; 2 Sam 24:18–25). Tradition holds this is the same Moriah where Abraham offered Isaac (Gen 22:2). The site is now the Temple Mount, where the Dome of the Rock stands. No physical trace of Solomon's temple has been securely identified, because of later rebuilding and the political sensitivity of excavating there. A cubit was about 18 inches (45 cm), the length from elbow to fingertip, so the building was roughly 90 feet long, 30 feet wide and 45 feet high, not huge by modern standards. Its glory was in its materials and meaning, not its size.

#### Solomon Builds the Temple (vv1–38)
- **"In the four hundred and eightieth year after the people of Israel came out of the land of Egypt" (v1).** This is one of the key dates of the Old Testament. Taken as literal years, it puts the Exodus about 1446 BC. Many scholars instead read 480 as twelve generations of forty years, a round number, which fits a later Exodus around 1270 BC, under Ramesses II. The Tyndale notes give both views. Either way, the writer links the temple to the Exodus on purpose. God brought his people out of Egypt so that he could dwell among them (Exod 29:45–46), and the temple is the next great step in that story.
- **"In the month of Ziv, which is the second month" (v1).** Ziv ("brightness," the month of flowers) is an old Canaanite month name, April–May. The writer has to explain it, which suggests it was no longer in common use when he wrote. Later Israel used the Babylonian name Iyyar.
- **"Sixty cubits long, twenty cubits wide, and thirty cubits high" (v2).** These are exactly double the tabernacle's dimensions (30 by 10 cubits). The Tyndale notes and Guzik both make this point. The temple was not a new idea but the tabernacle made permanent. JFB says the cubits are the older, longer standard (2 Chr 3:3), so the building may have been slightly larger.
- **"The vestibule… twenty cubits long… and ten cubits deep" (v3).** A porch across the front, facing east. The bronze pillars Jachin and Boaz stood before it (7:15–22). Worshipers faced west toward God, with their backs to the rising sun that pagans worshiped (Ezek 8:16).
- **"Windows with recessed frames" (v4).** Probably narrow windows high in the wall, set into frames, letting out smoke from the incense and letting in some light. JFB: "windows with lattices." Henry draws a lesson: "The narrowness of the lights intimated the darkness of that dispensation," compared to "the gospel day."
- **Side chambers (vv5–6, 8, 10).** Three stories of small rooms ran along the outside of the walls, on both sides and the back, used for storing temple treasures and supplies (7:51; Neh 13:4–9). Each story was wider than the one below, because the temple wall stepped inward as it rose. The beams rested on those ledges and were "not inserted into the walls of the house." Nothing was cut into God's house itself.
- **"Neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron was heard in the house while it was being built" (v7).** The stones were cut and shaped at the quarry and fitted together at the site in silence. It may echo God's law that an altar was to be built of uncut stones, with no iron tool lifted on it (Exod 20:25; Deut 27:5–6). The silence was a kind of reverence. Henry: "It was to be the temple of God of peace, and therefore no iron tool must be heard in it," and "Quietness and silence both become and befriend religious exercises."
- **"The entrance for the lowest story was on the south side… one went up by stairs" (v8).** The word for "stairs" is uncertain, possibly a winding staircase or trapdoors.
- **"The ceiling of the house of beams and planks of cedar" (v9).** Fragrant cedar roofing. The building was "finished" in its outer shell, and the writer pauses here for God's word before going inside.
- **"Now the word of the LORD came to Solomon" (v11).** In the middle of the building report, God speaks. The Tyndale notes suggest it came through a prophet, since God's direct appearances to Solomon are in chs 3 and 9.
- **"If you will walk in my statutes… then I will establish my word with you, which I spoke to David" (v12).** The message is a warning as much as a promise. The building does not guarantee God's presence. Obedience does. JFB: God spoke to guard him from "the pride and presumption of supposing that after the erection of so magnificent a temple" God's favor was secured. Jeremiah would later preach against people who chanted "the temple of the LORD" as if the building made them safe (Jer 7:4–11).
- **"I will dwell among the children of Israel and will not forsake my people Israel" (v13).** God promises to live *among* his people, not to be shut up in a building. That was the point from the start: "let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell in their midst" (Exod 25:8). Guzik draws the distinction: the temple was a meeting place, not a pagan-style house for a god.
- **"He lined the walls of the house on the inside with boards of cedar… the floor… with boards of cypress" (v15).** All the stone was hidden. "All was cedar; no stone was seen" (v18). And then the cedar itself was covered with gold.
- **"Twenty cubits of the rear of the house… as an inner sanctuary, as the Most Holy Place" (v16).** The building had two main rooms. The nave (the "Holy Place") in front was 40 cubits long (v17). The inner sanctuary at the back was 20 cubits. The older English word for it was "oracle" (KJV), from a Hebrew word (*debir*) that probably means "the back room" or "innermost part," though older readers linked it to the verb "to speak," the place where God spoke.
- **"The cedar within the house was carved in the form of gourds and open flowers" (v18).** The carvings call to mind a garden. With the palm trees and cherubim of v29, the temple's decoration echoes Eden, where God walked with man and cherubim guarded the way back (Gen 3:24).
- **"The inner sanctuary was twenty cubits long, twenty cubits wide, and twenty cubits high" (v20).** A perfect cube, about 30 feet each way, like the tabernacle's Most Holy Place (10 cubits). It was probably raised on a platform, since the main building was 30 cubits high. The new Jerusalem in Revelation is also a perfect cube (Rev 21:16). The whole city becomes the Most Holy Place, where God lives with his people.
- **"He overlaid it with pure gold. He also overlaid an altar of cedar" (v20).** This altar is the golden altar of incense, which stood in the nave just in front of the inner sanctuary (v22: "the altar that belonged to the inner sanctuary"; Exod 30:1–6). Hebrews describes it the same way (Heb 9:3–4).
- **"He drew chains of gold across, in front of the inner sanctuary" (v21).** A barrier, along with a curtain (2 Chr 3:14). No one entered the Most Holy Place except the high priest, once a year on the Day of Atonement, with blood (Lev 16).
- **"He overlaid the whole house with gold" (v22).** Gold everywhere: walls, floor (v30), doors, cherubim. In the ancient world gold was associated with heaven and with gods, because it does not tarnish. David had gathered immense quantities for this (1 Chr 22:14; 29:4). JFB: "neither wood nor stone was seen."
- **"Two cherubim of olivewood, each ten cubits high" (v23).** Cherubim were heavenly beings who guard God's holiness and surround his throne (Ezek 1; 10). They are not chubby babies, as later art has it. Ancient Near Eastern art shows winged figures with animal bodies and human faces guarding thrones and temples. These two stood about 15 feet tall, side by side, their wings stretching wall to wall (v27). They faced the entrance (2 Chr 3:13) and stood over the ark with its own two small cherubim on the lid (Exod 25:18–22). Together they formed God's throne room. The LORD is called the one "enthroned on the cherubim" (1 Sam 4:4; Ps 80:1), and the ark was his footstool (1 Chr 28:2).
- **"Around all the walls… cherubim and palm trees and open flowers" (v29).** A garden guarded by cherubim. Ezekiel's vision of the future temple has the same carvings (Ezek 41:18–20).
- **"For the entrance to the inner sanctuary he made doors of olivewood; the lintel and the doorposts were five-sided" (v31).** The meaning of "five-sided" is uncertain. It may describe the shape of the frame or its recessed steps. The doors to the nave had "square" doorposts (v33), and folding double doors of cypress (v34). As one moved inward, the materials became more precious and the shapes more special.
- **"He built the inner court with three courses of cut stone and one course of cedar beams" (v36).** The inner court around the temple was for the priests (2 Chr 4:9), where the bronze altar and the great basin stood (7:23–39). The layering of stone with wood beams was a known building technique, perhaps to absorb earthquake shocks.
- **"In the eleventh year, in the month of Bul, which is the eighth month, the house was finished… He was seven years in building it" (v38).** Bul is October–November, another old Canaanite name. From Ziv of year four to Bul of year eleven is seven and a half years, which JFB says is "reckoned here in round numbers." Solomon's own palace took thirteen (7:1), a contrast the writer leaves the reader to notice.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the temple "one of the wonders of the world," built "chiefly because it was to be a shadow of good things to come" (Heb 9:9). Guzik sees in the silent building a picture of the church: God shapes his "living stones" out of sight, through trials, and fits them together. He also notes that the doubled tabernacle plan shows continuity, and warns that Israel later trusted the building more than the God of the building. Within five years of Solomon's death, the Egyptian king Shishak carried off its treasures (14:25–26). JFB stresses that God's word in vv11–13 was meant to keep Solomon humble. The Tyndale notes describe the cherubim as guards and say the gold everywhere reminded the priests of God's presence.

#### Pointing to Christ
The temple was a house of cedar and gold built so that the holy God could live among his people. Yet the gold chains and curtain said that sinners could not come in. Only one man, once a year, could enter the Most Holy Place, with blood. Jesus is the true temple, God dwelling among us (John 1:14; 2:19–21). When he died, "the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (Matt 27:51), and the way into God's presence was opened. "We have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20). And as Solomon's stones were shaped far away and set in place in silence, God is now building his people "into a holy temple in the Lord… a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" (Eph 2:21–22). The garden-temple, with its cherubim, palms and gold, points finally to the new creation, where "the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3), and the city itself is the cube-shaped Most Holy Place.
