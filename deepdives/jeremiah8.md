*Setting:* Jerusalem, early in the reign of King Jehoiakim (609–598 BC). Chapter 7 was Jeremiah's temple sermon: the temple will not save a people who break the covenant, and the Valley of Hinnom, where they burned their children, will become the Valley of Slaughter. Chapter 8 continues straight on. It begins with an even darker picture of the dead (vv1–3), then asks why God's people, unlike every other creature, will not turn back (vv4–17). It ends with one of the most personal passages in the prophets, where Jeremiah's grief and God's grief run together: "Is there no balm in Gilead?"

#### The Valley of Slaughter (vv1–3)
- **"The bones of the kings… officials… priests… prophets… and the inhabitants of Jerusalem shall be brought out of their tombs" (v1).** The ESV section that began at 7:30 ends here. Conquering armies sometimes broke open royal tombs to loot the gold and jewelry buried with the dead, or simply to disgrace a defeated people. The Assyrian king Ashurbanipal boasted of doing this to the kings of Elam. The list of five groups is the same list of leaders who failed in 2:26, now named in death.
- **"Spread before the sun and the moon and all the host of heaven, which they have loved and served" (v2).** Worship of the sun, moon and stars came in from Assyria. Manasseh built altars to "all the host of heaven" in the temple courts, and people burned incense to them on their rooftops (2 Kings 21:5; 23:5, 11; Zeph 1:5). Five verbs pile up their devotion: loved, served, gone after, sought, worshiped. The bones will lie face up before the gods they adored, and those gods will do nothing.
- **"They shall not be gathered or buried. They shall be as dung on the surface of the ground" (v2).** To be left unburied was the worst fate an Israelite could imagine (7:33; 16:4). Being "gathered to one's fathers" was the hope of a peaceful death (Gen 25:8). Here there is no gathering.
- **"Death shall be preferred to life by all the remnant that remains" (v3).** The survivors in exile will envy the dead. Revelation 9:6 uses the same idea.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the scene plainly: "Before these lights of heaven, which they had courted, shall their dead bodies be cast, and left to putrefy." JFB explains that conquerors robbed tombs for plunder as well as to bring the "greatest dishonor" on the dead, and that the survivors would be "still worse off than the dead." Guzik notes how deliberate the disgrace was in that culture.

#### Sin and Treachery (vv4–17)
- **"When men fall, do they not rise again? If one turns away, does he not return?" (v4).** Common sense: a man who trips gets back up, and a traveler who takes a wrong turn comes back. The Hebrew plays on *shuv* ("turn, return") again, as in chapter 3, over and over in vv4–6.
- **"Why then has this people turned away in perpetual backsliding?" (v5).** "Backsliding" is *meshuvah*, "turning away." Their turning away has become permanent. **"They hold fast to deceit; they refuse to return."** They cling to the lie as tightly as a drowning man to a rope.
- **"I have paid attention and listened, but they have not spoken rightly" (v6).** God pictures himself listening at the door for a word of repentance, and hearing nothing. **"No man relents of his evil, saying, 'What have I done?'"** Repentance begins with that simple question, and no one asks it.
- **"Like a horse plunging headlong into battle" (v6).** A war horse charges straight at spears and arrows without thinking of danger (Job 39:19–25). The verb pictures a flood bursting out. They rush on in their own course without a moment's thought of where it ends.
- **"Even the stork in the heavens knows her times" (v7).** Israel lies on one of the world's great bird migration routes, between Europe and Africa. Every spring and autumn hundreds of thousands of storks fly over the Jordan valley, and doves, swifts and cranes pass in season. The ESV footnote notes that the exact identity of one of the birds is uncertain. Their timing never fails. **"But my people know not the rules of the LORD."** "Rules" (*mishpat*) can also mean "just decrees" (ESV footnote). Birds obey the order God built into creation. His people ignore the order he spoke to them. Isaiah makes the same point with the ox and donkey (Isa 1:3).
- **"How can you say, 'We are wise, and the law of the LORD is with us'?" (v8).** They had the written law, the Book of the Law found under Josiah only a few years earlier (2 Kings 22:8). They took pride in owning it.
- **"The lying pen of the scribes has made it into a lie" (v8).** The scribes were the trained experts who copied, studied and explained the law. The charge is either that they twisted it in their teaching and interpretation, or that they wrote new rulings that contradicted it while claiming its authority. Either way, the law in their hands has become *sheqer*, a lie. Jesus brought a similar charge against scribes who "made void the word of God" by their tradition (Mark 7:13).
- **"They have rejected the word of the LORD, so what wisdom is in them?" (v9).** Wisdom begins with the fear of the LORD (Prov 1:7). Wise men who reject his word have no wisdom left. They will be **"put to shame… dismayed and taken"**, caught like animals in a trap.
- **"Therefore I will give their wives to others and their fields to conquerors" (v10).** Verses 10–12 repeat 6:12–15 almost word for word. Jeremiah's messages were preached more than once and gathered later into the book, so a key oracle could appear in two settings. Its repetition here marks it as one of his central charges: greed **"from the least to the greatest"**, falsehood **"from prophet to priest"**.
- **"They have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace" (v11).** The same words as 6:14. In 6:14 they were followed by the call to the ancient paths. Here they come right after the lying scribes. False teachers and false prophets give the same comfort.
- **"They did not know how to blush" (v12).** Shame would have been the first sign of life. There is none.
- **"When I would gather them… there are no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree" (v13).** God comes to his vineyard at harvest and finds nothing. "Gather" here could also mean "make an end of them." The ESV footnote says the Hebrew is uncertain. **"Even the leaves are withered."** Jesus acted out this picture when he cursed a leafy fig tree with no fruit, on the way to clearing the temple (Mark 11:12–21). Mark sandwiches the two scenes together, as Jeremiah joins the temple sermon (ch 7) and the fruitless fig tree (8:13).
- **"Why do we sit still? Gather together; let us go into the fortified cities and perish there" (v14).** The people speak. In 4:5 they ran to the fortified cities to be safe. Now they go there knowing they will die. **"The LORD our God… has given us poisoned water to drink."** The Hebrew is literally "water of *rosh*," a bitter, poisonous plant (Deut 29:18; Lam 3:15, 19). For once they admit, **"because we have sinned against the LORD."** But they confess without turning. They resign themselves to fate.
- **"We looked for peace, but no good came; for a time of healing, but behold, terror" (v15).** This repeats in 14:19. The false promise of "peace, peace" has run out.
- **"The snorting of their horses is heard from Dan" (v16).** Dan was Israel's northernmost town, where invaders from the north first entered the land (4:15). The war horses can be heard snorting from there. **"They come and devour the land and all that fills it."**
- **"I am sending among you serpents, adders that cannot be charmed" (v17).** Snake charmers were well known in the ancient Near East (Ps 58:4–5; Eccl 10:11). Some snakes could be handled by a skilled charmer, but these cannot. No clever words or diplomacy will stop the Babylonians. The picture recalls the fiery serpents in the wilderness (Num 21:6), but this time no remedy is offered.
- **Commentators:** Henry turns v4 into a question everyone understands: "If men happen to fall to the ground, to fall into the dirt, will they not get up again as fast as they can?" On v6 he says, "They did not so much as take the first step towards repentance." JFB explains the horse "pours himself forth," like a bursting flood. Henry and JFB both draw the contrast of v7: the birds return in their seasons, but "God's people will not return to Him" (JFB). On v8 JFB calls the scribes "copyists" and cites the rendering "The false pen of the scribes hath converted it." Guzik reads the verse as false teachers corrupting Scripture with their pens. The Tyndale notes suggest the scribes were presenting the new policies of Jehoiakim's court as if they were God's word. JFB suggests the "water of gall" in v14 may be a poppy-like poisonous plant and says Dan lay on Nebuchadnezzar's road into the land.

#### Jeremiah Grieves for His People (vv18–22)
- **"My joy is gone; grief is upon me; my heart is sick within me" (v18).** The ESV footnote explains that the Hebrew of the first line is uncertain, so the translators followed the Greek Septuagint. The sense is clear: Jeremiah is sick at heart. He is not a detached judge but a man who loves the people he must condemn.
- **"The cry of the daughter of my people from the length and breadth of the land" (v19).** Jeremiah hears ahead of time the voices of exiles in a far country (KJV "from a country that is far off"; the Hebrew can mean either). **"Is the LORD not in Zion? Is her King not in her?"** They still think God's presence in the temple guarantees their safety, the same belief Jeremiah attacked in 7:4.
- **"Why have they provoked me to anger with their carved images?" (v19).** God breaks in to answer their question. The problem is not that the King has left Zion. It is that they brought other gods in.
- **"The harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved" (v20).** In Israel the grain harvest came in late spring and the fruit harvest in late summer. If both failed, there was nothing to live on until the next year. The line sounds like a proverb: every season of hope has come and gone and no rescue came. It may also look back on years of waiting for Egypt's help. Many preachers have used it as a warning that the day of opportunity can pass (2 Cor 6:2; Luke 19:44).
- **"For the wound of the daughter of my people is my heart wounded" (v21).** "Wounded" is literally "broken." Jeremiah is broken by the brokenness of his people. **"I mourn"** means literally "I go about in black," the dark clothing of mourning. The "I" here is the prophet, but his grief is so close to God's that the two voices blend. The Tyndale notes point out that God himself feels his people's pain (compare Hos 11:8).
- **"Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?" (v22).** Gilead was the hill country east of the Jordan, famous for its balm (*tsori*), a fragrant resin from trees or shrubs, used to soothe wounds and sold widely. The Ishmaelite traders who bought Joseph were carrying balm from Gilead down to Egypt (Gen 37:25). Gilead had healing, and presumably healers. So **"why then has the health of the daughter of my people not been restored?"** The healing exists. The patient will not take it. Jeremiah returns to the image in 46:11 and 51:8.
- **The chapter division.** In the Hebrew Bible this chapter continues for one more verse: the ESV's 9:1, "Oh that my head were waters, and my eyes a fountain of tears," is 8:23 in Hebrew (ESV footnote). The cry of v22 leads straight into that weeping.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of v20, "there is a great deal of opportunity lost," and applies the balm of v22 directly: "The blood of Christ is balm in Gilead." JFB calls v20 "proverbial" and notes that Gilead was so rich in balm that "many 'physicians' established themselves" there. Guzik reads Jeremiah's grief as a picture of the compassion God's messengers must have for those they warn. The Tyndale notes recall the African American spiritual "There Is a Balm in Gilead," which answers Jeremiah's question by naming Jesus as the balm.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah asked, "Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?" The gospel answers yes. Jesus called himself the physician who came "not to call the righteous but sinners" (Mark 2:17), and by his wounds the wounded are healed (Isa 53:5; 1 Pet 2:24). Where the scribes turned God's law into a lie, Jesus fulfilled it (Matt 5:17). Where Jeremiah's heart was broken for his people, Jesus wept over Jerusalem and then went to the cross for it (Luke 19:41). The people said, "The harvest is past… and we are not saved." In Christ, "now is the day of salvation" (2 Cor 6:2), and the balm is offered freely to anyone who will turn and receive it.
