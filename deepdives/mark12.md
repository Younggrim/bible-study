*Setting:* The temple courts in Jerusalem, Tuesday of the last week, a few days before Jesus' death. The day before, he drove the traders out of the court of the Gentiles (11:15–17). Now the leaders of the nation have come to demand where his authority comes from, and he has refused to say until they answer about John (11:27–33). The whole of chapter 12 takes place in the temple, and it reads like a series of public tests. Group after group (priests and elders, Pharisees with Herod's supporters, Sadducees, a scribe) brings a question meant to trap or measure him. He answers each one, then asks a question no one can answer, warns against the scribes and ends by pointing to a poor widow.

#### The Parable of the Tenants (vv1–12)
- **"He began to speak to them in parables" (v1).** "Them" is the chief priests, scribes and elders of 11:27. This parable is Jesus' answer to their question about his authority.
- **"A man planted a vineyard… built a tower" (v1).** Every listener would have heard Isaiah 5:1–7, the Song of the Vineyard, where God plants a vineyard, clears it, builds a tower and hews a wine vat, and then finds only wild grapes. Isaiah explains: "The vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel" (Isa 5:7). Jesus changes one thing. The trouble is not the vines but the tenants. The fault lies with Israel's leaders.
- **Tenant farming.** Absentee landlords were common in Galilee, and many owned estates worked by tenants who paid a share of the crop as rent. A new vineyard took about four years to produce. Bad feeling between tenants and owners was common, and stories of tenants refusing to pay would not have sounded far-fetched.
- **The servants (vv2–5).** Beaten, struck on the head, treated shamefully, killed: this is Israel's history with its prophets. God sent "all my servants the prophets… yet they did not listen" (Jer 7:25–26). Elijah was hunted (1 Kings 19:10), Zechariah son of Jehoiada stoned in the temple court (2 Chr 24:20–22) and Jeremiah beaten and jailed (Jer 20:2; 37:15). Jesus has just been asked about John the Baptist, the latest in that line.
- **"He had still one other, a beloved son" (v6).** "Beloved son" is the very phrase the Father used at Jesus' baptism (1:11) and on the mountain (9:7). Jesus is telling the leaders who he is, in the form of a story. He is not one more servant. He is the son and heir.
- **"This is the heir. Come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours" (v7).** Under some customs, land with no living heir could be claimed by those who held it. The tenants' logic is brutal and foolish. They think that removing the son will make the vineyard theirs. The leaders feared losing their place and nation to Rome (John 11:48) and decided Jesus had to die.
- **"Threw him out of the vineyard" (v8).** Unburied, a final disgrace. Jesus was crucified outside the city wall (Heb 13:12–13).
- **"He will come and destroy the tenants and give the vineyard to others" (v9).** In Isaiah, the listeners are asked to judge the case (Isa 5:3–4). Here Jesus answers his own question. The leaders will lose their stewardship of God's people. Matthew's version makes it explicit: "the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits" (Matt 21:43). Many read "others" as the apostles and the church of Jews and Gentiles. The fall of Jerusalem in AD 70 followed within a generation.
- **"The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (vv10–11).** This is Psalm 118:22–23, from the same psalm the crowds sang as Jesus entered the city (11:9–10). The image shifts from vineyard to building, but the point is the same. The "builders," Israel's leaders, will reject the stone, and God will make it the most important stone in the building. In Hebrew, "son" (*ben*) and "stone" (*eben*) sound alike, a wordplay that may lie behind the move from the son to the stone. The cornerstone might be the foundation stone that sets the lines of the whole building or the capstone that completes it. Either way, it is the stone everything depends on. Peter later uses this verse before the same council (Acts 4:11; 1 Pet 2:7).
- **"They perceived that he had told the parable against them" (v12).** They understood it perfectly, and it made them want to arrest him. Only fear of the crowd held them back for now.
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes the vineyard as Israel and the tenants as its leaders, and stresses that the son is the owner's last messenger: reject him and there is no one left to send. The Tyndale notes read the parable as an allegory with clear points of reference, the servants as prophets, the son as Jesus and the others as the church. They also note that the cornerstone could be either the foundation stone or the capstone.

#### Paying Taxes to Caesar (vv13–17)
- **"Some of the Pharisees and some of the Herodians" (v13).** An odd alliance. The Pharisees were popular teachers who resented Roman rule. The Herodians supported Herod's family, which ruled with Rome's backing. Mark has paired them before, plotting Jesus' death (3:6). "To trap him" uses a hunting word, to snare.
- **"We know that you are true… you are not swayed by appearances" (v14).** Flattery meant to make him feel he must give a bold answer. Ironically, every word of it is true.
- **"Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?" (v14).** The tax was the poll tax, a head tax every adult in Judea paid directly to Rome since AD 6. That year Judas the Galilean led a revolt over it, teaching that paying tribute to Caesar meant accepting a human master in place of God (Acts 5:37; Josephus, Antiquities 18.1.1). The question is a perfect trap. "Yes" makes Jesus look like a collaborator to the crowds. "No" makes him a rebel the Romans would crush.
- **"Bring me a denarius" (v15).** A silver coin, about a day's wage for a laborer. Jesus seems not to carry one. Its image almost certainly showed Tiberius, who reigned AD 14–37. Surviving coins read "Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus, Augustus," and the reverse calls him "high priest." For many Jews, carrying such a coin into the temple courts was offensive in itself, since it bore an image and a claim to divinity. Yet his questioners have one ready to hand.
- **"Whose likeness and inscription is this?" (v16).** "Likeness" is *eikōn*, "image," the word the Greek Old Testament uses in Genesis 1:26–27: people are made in the image of God.
- **"Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (v17).** "Render" means "give back," as one pays a debt that is owed. The coin bears Caesar's image, so give it back to him. Paying taxes is a legitimate duty (Rom 13:6–7). But Caesar is not God, and his claim is limited. Human beings bear God's image, so they owe God themselves. The answer escapes the trap and turns it back on the questioners, who are as guilty as the tenants of the parable of keeping back from God what is his.
- **Church and state.** Christians have drawn very different conclusions from this verse, from close cooperation between church and state to a sharp separation. What the verse clearly does is rule out both extremes in Jesus' day: refusing all authority to Caesar, and giving Caesar what belongs to God alone. When those two clash, the apostles answer, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the answer was framed so that "it was impossible for sedition itself to dispute it," and praises its "fulness, brevity, clearness, weight." Guzik describes the coin's claims to divinity and priesthood and draws out the image theme: Caesar's image is on the coin, and God's image is on people, so believers owe their money to Caesar and themselves to God. The Tyndale notes add that the answer is neither pro-zealot nor simply pro-Roman, because God stands over Caesar.

#### The Sadducees Ask About the Resurrection (vv18–27)
- **"Sadducees… who say that there is no resurrection" (v18).** This is their only appearance in Mark. The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy, wealthy and allied with Rome, and they controlled the temple. They accepted only the five books of Moses as binding, and they denied resurrection, angels and spirits (Acts 23:8). Because they were tied to the temple, they disappeared from history after AD 70.
- **"Moses wrote for us" (v19).** They cite the law of levirate marriage (Deut 25:5–6). If a man died childless, his brother was to marry the widow, and their first son would carry the dead man's name, so his family line would not be lost. Ruth and Boaz show a version of this custom (Ruth 4), and Genesis 38 shows it abused. The Latin *levir* means "brother-in-law."
- **Seven brothers (vv20–23).** A story built to make resurrection look ridiculous. If all seven rise, whose wife is she? The Sadducees likely used it often against the Pharisees.
- **"You know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God" (v24).** Two errors. They do not understand the Bible, and they do not believe God can do anything truly new. They picture resurrection as the old life continued, with the same arrangements and the same problems.
- **"They neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (v25).** In the resurrection there is no more death, so marriage's role of carrying on the family line ends. People are "like angels" in this respect, not in becoming angels. Jesus does not say human love or relationships are lost. He says the age to come is not a copy of this one. The comparison to angels also corrects the Sadducees, who did not believe in angels at all.
- **"In the passage about the bush" (v26).** There were no chapter and verse numbers, so passages were named by their subject. Jesus answers the Sadducees from Moses, the part of Scripture they accepted. He could have cited clearer texts from the Prophets and Writings (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2), but he meets them on their own ground.
- **"I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (v26).** This is Exodus 3:6, spoken to Moses centuries after the patriarchs died. God did not say "I was their God." Jesus' argument does not rest only on the tense, since the Hebrew has no verb there. It rests on what it means for God to be someone's God. God bound himself in covenant to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob and promised them a future. If death ended them for good, God would be the God of corpses and his promises would have failed. The covenant faithfulness of God requires that they live, and finally that they rise.
- **"He is not God of the dead, but of the living. You are quite wrong" (v27).** Only Mark has the blunt last line. Luke adds, "for all live to him" (Luke 20:38).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the Sadducees "the materialists of the day" and says the angel comparison concerns only the immortal life, not becoming angels. Guzik explains the term *levir* and notes that Jesus used Exodus because the Sadducees accepted only the Torah. He leans on the present tense of "I am." The Tyndale notes make the stronger version of the argument: God's covenant with the patriarchs cannot be broken by death.

#### The Great Commandment (vv28–34)
- **"One of the scribes" (v28).** Unlike the others, this man seems to come sincerely. He heard the debate and liked Jesus' answer. In Matthew he asks "to test him" (Matt 22:35), but Mark shows his better side.
- **"Which commandment is the most important of all?" (v28).** Later rabbis counted 613 commandments in the Law, and teachers often tried to sum them up. A famous story tells of Hillel, a generation before Jesus, summing up the law as "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor" (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a).
- **"Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one" (v29).** This is the *Shema*, named for its first Hebrew word, "Hear" (Deut 6:4–5). Devout Jews recited it every morning and evening, and still do. It confesses that Israel's God is the only God. Jesus begins with who God is before he says what God requires.
- **"With all your heart… soul… mind… strength" (v30).** Deuteronomy has heart, soul and strength. Mark adds "mind," perhaps to make clear for Greek readers that the Hebrew "heart" included thinking. The point is not four separate parts but the whole person, held back in nothing.
- **"You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (v31).** This is Leviticus 19:18, from a chapter full of practical laws about the poor, the stranger, wages and honest weights. Jesus was asked for one commandment and gives two, because love for God cannot be separated from love for people (1 John 4:20–21). Neither is new. Joining them as the summit of the Law is Jesus' own work.
- **"Much more than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices" (v33).** A remarkable thing to say in the temple courts, standing among the altars. The prophets had said it first (1 Sam 15:22; Hos 6:6; Mic 6:6–8). In a few decades the sacrifices would end, and this scribe had already grasped what mattered more.
- **"You are not far from the kingdom of God" (v34).** Encouragement and warning at once. He sees the truth. But understanding the commandment is not keeping it, and the kingdom is entered by coming to the king. Mark leaves his story open.
- **"No one dared to ask him any more questions" (v34).** The testers have run out of tests.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the scribe as a sincere inquirer and comments: "There is hope of those who make a good use of the light they have." He adds that many come near the kingdom and never enter. JFB likewise takes "not far" as a gentle warning and notes that the man may never have entered. Guzik calls love first both in time and in priority, since obedience without love is empty.

#### Whose Son Is the Christ? (vv35–37)
- **Now Jesus asks the question (v35).** After the tests, Jesus puts one of his own. Everyone agreed the Messiah would be the son of David (2 Sam 7:12–16; Isa 11:1; Jer 23:5). Jesus does not deny it. Bartimaeus had just hailed him as "Son of David" (10:47). He asks what that title leaves out.
- **"The Lord said to my Lord" (v36).** This is Psalm 110:1, the Old Testament verse most often quoted in the New. In Hebrew, the first "Lord" is *YHWH*, God's personal name, and the second is *adoni*, "my lord" or "my master." In the psalm David speaks of a king greater than himself, whom God seats at his right hand. Jesus takes for granted that David wrote it, "in the Holy Spirit," and that it speaks of the Messiah, as Jews in his day generally agreed.
- **"David himself calls him Lord. So how is he his son?" (v37).** In that culture a father did not call his son "lord." The son honored the father. If David calls his descendant "Lord," the Messiah must be far more than a human heir. Jesus leaves the riddle unanswered. The answer is that he is both: David's son by human birth and David's Lord as the Son of God (Rom 1:3–4). At his trial Jesus will quote this same psalm about himself (14:62).
- **"The great throng heard him gladly" (v37).** The crowd enjoyed seeing the experts silenced.
- **Commentators:** JFB states the answer the passage implies: Messiah is "at once inferior to David as his son according to the flesh, and superior to him as the Lord." Guzik connects it to Revelation 22:16, where Jesus calls himself both "the root and the descendant of David."

#### Beware of the Scribes (vv38–40)
- **"Long robes… greetings… best seats… places of honor" (vv38–39).** Scribes were experts in the law, and many were respected and godly. Jesus warns against a type: the robed scholar who loved to be noticed. The "best seats" in a synagogue were on a bench at the front, facing the congregation. Matthew 23 gives the full speech; Mark gives the core.
- **"Who devour widows' houses" (v40).** Scribes were not supposed to charge for teaching, so many depended on gifts. Some may have taken advantage of widows' trust, managing their estates for a fee, accepting gifts beyond what the widows could afford or using legal skill to seize property. Exploiting widows is one of the sins the prophets condemned most fiercely (Isa 10:1–2; Mal 3:5).
- **"For a pretense make long prayers" (v40).** Public piety used as a cover. The judgment is heavier on those who claim to know God's will best: "greater condemnation."
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the mix of vanity and exploitation and the special seriousness of hiding greed behind religion. JFB, writing in 1871, calls v40 "a lifelike description" of the Roman Catholic clergy of its day, a polemic that tells more about JFB's time than about the text. The warning applies to any religious leader in any tradition.

#### The Widow's Offering (vv41–44)
- **"Opposite the treasury" (v41).** In the Court of the Women, open to all Israelites, there were thirteen trumpet-shaped offering boxes for different purposes. Jesus sits and watches, as a teacher would.
- **"Two small copper coins, which make a penny" (v42).** The coin is the *lepton*, the smallest coin in circulation. The word means "small" or "thin." Mark explains for his Roman readers that two of them make a *quadrans*, the smallest Roman coin, about one sixty-fourth of a day's wage. Note "two." She could have kept one.
- **"This poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing" (v43).** God measures a gift by what it costs the giver and what is left over. The rich gave from surplus. She gave her whole living.
- **"Everything she had, all she had to live on" (v44).** Literally "her whole life" (*bios*). She is a living picture of loving God with all one's strength (v30). She is also a quiet contrast to the scribes who "devour widows' houses" just before. Some read the scene as a lament: a corrupt system has taken even this widow's last coins. Most read it as praise of her devotion. The two readings can stand together. The temple she gives to is about to be condemned (13:1–2), but God sees her gift.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Christ watches not only what is given but how and why, and adds: "Those that are rich, ought to give richly." He notes that they gave "of their abundance, but she of her want." Guzik suggests the widow might have been one whose house had already been devoured, linking her to v40, and says a gift's value lies in what it costs the giver.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every question in this chapter is turned back toward Jesus himself. He is the beloved son the tenants kill and the rejected stone God makes the cornerstone. He knows the Scriptures and the power of God that the Sadducees lack, and in three days he will rise. He is the Lord David called Lord, seated at God's right hand. And he is the one who loved God with all his heart and his neighbor as himself, perfectly. In the end he will do what the widow did on a far greater scale: give everything he had, his whole life, for others.
