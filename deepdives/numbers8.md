*Setting:* Still at Sinai, in the weeks before departure. Chapter 7 ended with Moses hearing God's voice from above the mercy seat, and chapter 8 records what that voice said next. It covers two things. First, a short instruction about the golden lampstand, the only light inside the tabernacle. Then the formal ceremony that set the Levites apart for their work. Chapters 3–4 told what the Levites would do and why God took them in place of Israel's firstborn. This chapter shows how they were made ready: cleansed, presented by the whole nation, offered to God, and handed over to the priests. Henry neatly calls the two halves "the burning lamps" and the "living lamps."

#### The Seven Lamps (vv1–4)
- **"When you set up the lamps, the seven lamps shall give light in front of the lampstand" (v2).** The lampstand (*menorah*) stood on the south side of the Holy Place, facing the table of the bread of the Presence on the north (Exod 26:35). There were no windows. Without these lamps the room was dark. The lamps were to be set so their light fell forward, across the room, onto the table and toward the altar of incense in front of the veil. The lampstand existed to light up the place where God met his people, not to draw attention to itself.
- **"Aaron did so" (v3).** Tending the lamps was the high priest's own task, every evening and morning, so that they burned "regularly" through the night (Exod 27:20–21; Lev 24:1–4). JFB says Aaron lit the middle lamp from the altar fire and then the others from it, a detail from later Jewish tradition, which JFB reads as a picture of all heavenly light coming from Christ.
- **"Hammered work of gold. From its base to its flowers" (v4).** The lampstand was not cast in a mold or built from parts. It was beaten out of a single talent of pure gold, about 75 pounds (Exod 25:31–39), with branches shaped like an almond tree in blossom. Every other major piece of tabernacle furniture was wood overlaid with gold. The lampstand was solid gold throughout.
- **"According to the pattern that the LORD had shown Moses" (v4).** Moses had been shown a pattern on the mountain (Exod 25:9, 40). Hebrews 8:5 says the earthly sanctuary was "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things," and the lampstand was made to match.
- **Why is this here?** The lampstand was described in Exodus, so why repeat it? Most likely because the tabernacle is now in use, and Numbers is moving from building to daily service. Chapter 7 recorded the tribal leaders' gifts for the altar. Aaron's tribe gave no gift there, and some Jewish interpreters suggested this command was Aaron's share: the daily work of keeping the light burning.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes "It was Aaron's personal duty, as the servant of God, to light His house." Guzik stresses that the lampstand itself produced no light. It held the lamps, which burned only as long as they had oil, and he applies this to the church, which does not make its own light but holds out the light of Jesus (Rev 1:20). The Tyndale notes describe the lampstand as a stylized tree of lamps.

#### Cleansing of the Levites (vv5–22)
- **"Take the Levites… and cleanse them" (v6).** The verb is "cleanse" (*taher*), not "consecrate" or "make holy" (*qadash*), the word used for the priests (Exod 29:1; Lev 8:12). The difference is deliberate. Priests were anointed with oil and dressed in new holy garments. Levites were sprinkled with water and washed their own clothes. Both were God's servants, but the priests were closer to the holy things, and the ceremonies showed it.
- **"Sprinkle the water of purification upon them" (v7).** The Hebrew is literally "water of sin," meaning water that removes sin's defilement. This exact phrase appears only here. Many think it was the water mixed with the ashes of the red heifer (19:9, 17), though that chapter has not yet been given. Hebrews 9:13 refers to that cleansing, and Ezekiel 36:25 promises a future one: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean."
- **"Let them go with a razor over all their body" (v7).** Shaving all the body hair was otherwise done by a leper on being declared clean (Lev 14:8–9). It was a fresh start, the old removed. Guzik adds a thought: the Levites stand in for the firstborn, and newborns come into the world nearly hairless.
- **"Wash their clothes and cleanse themselves" (v7).** Washing clothes accompanies nearly every purification in the law (Lev 15:5–27). Before Sinai, all Israel had washed their garments to meet God (Exod 19:10).
- **Two bulls (v8).** One for a burnt offering, with its grain offering, and one for a sin offering. A bull was the sin offering required for the high priest or the whole congregation (Lev 4:3, 14), the most costly kind. These men were being set apart to serve the whole nation.
- **"Assemble the whole congregation" (v9).** The ceremony was public. All Israel stood around the tabernacle to watch.
- **"The people of Israel shall lay their hands on the Levites" (v10).** Probably done by the tribal leaders on behalf of the rest. Laying hands on an animal before sacrifice identified the worshiper with it as a substitute (Lev 1:4; 4:15). Here Israel lays hands on people. The firstborn sons belonged to God, and Israel now hands over the Levites in their place. In the New Testament, laying on hands marks out people for service (Acts 6:6; 13:3; 1 Tim 4:14).
- **"Aaron shall offer the Levites before the LORD as a wave offering" (v11).** A "wave offering" (*tenufah*) was normally a portion of a sacrifice lifted or moved toward the altar and back, showing it was given to God and then received back by the priests for their use (Lev 7:30–34). Here the offering is people, thousands of them. Exactly how they were "waved" is not described. Perhaps they were led toward the altar and back. The meaning is clear: the Levites are given to God, and God gives them back to the priests to serve. The Tyndale notes connect this with Romans 12:1, presenting our bodies "as a living sacrifice."
- **"The Levites shall lay their hands on the heads of the bulls" (v12).** Now the Levites themselves need a substitute. The ones offered for Israel must have atonement made for them. Everyone in the chain needs grace.
- **"The Levites shall be mine" (v14).** The heart of the ceremony. **"Wholly given to me"** (v16) repeats the doubled phrase of 3:9, "given, given."
- **"Instead of all who open the womb" (vv16–18).** For the third time (3:12–13, 41), God explains why the Levites are his: the firstborn were spared on Passover night. Each time the Levites served, they were a reminder of that night.
- **"To make atonement for the people of Israel, that there may be no plague" (v19).** The Levites guarded the sanctuary and handled the holy things so that ordinary Israelites would not come too near and die. "Atonement" here means protecting the people from God's wrath by standing between them and the sanctuary. The word "plague" anticipates later chapters, when plague does break out (16:46–50).
- **"Thus did Moses and Aaron and all the congregation" (vv20–22).** Everything was done exactly as commanded, and **"after that the Levites went in to do their service"** (v22). The ceremony was not the goal. Real work followed it.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the Levites "burning and shining lights" (borrowing Jesus' words about John the Baptist, John 5:35) and says they were presented "in lieu of the firstborn" and "consigned to Aaron and his sons, to be ministers to them." JFB reads the shaving and washing as reminders to put off worldly desires, notes that "the imposition of hands was a ceremony used in consecrating persons to holy offices," and observes that the Levites were "first devoted as an offering to God," then given to the priests. Guzik lists the differences between the priests' consecration and the Levites' cleansing, and contrasts Israel's careful obedience here with the rebellions that begin in chapter 11.

#### Retirement of the Levites (vv23–26)
- **"From twenty-five years old and upward" (v24).** Chapter 4 set the working age at thirty to fifty. There are two main explanations. One is that twenty-five to thirty was a time of training or apprenticeship (JFB says they began "as probationers"). The other is that the age changed as the need for workers changed. Later, David lowered it to twenty (1 Chr 23:24–27). The Tyndale notes allow both.
- **"From the age of fifty years they shall withdraw… and serve no more" (v25).** The heavy lifting of carrying the tabernacle was for men in their physical prime. The "duty of the service" uses the military word *tsaba* again (see 4:3), which Guzik notes suggests that serving God is a kind of warfare.
- **"They minister to their brothers… by keeping guard" (v26).** Retired Levites were not cast aside. They could still help their brothers and stand guard, a lighter but real job. Wisdom and experience were still needed. Psalm 84:10 shows how highly a doorkeeper's post in God's house was valued: "I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness."
- **Commentators:** JFB says at thirty "they were admitted to the full discharge of their official functions," and after fifty continued with lighter duties such as instructing and supervising the young. Guzik sees mercy toward aging bodies in the retirement age.

#### Pointing to Christ
The lampstand's light fell on the bread of the Presence, and the Levites were cleansed, offered and given so that others might draw near without dying. Jesus fulfils both. He is "the light of the world" (John 8:12) and "the bread of life" (John 6:35), and he tells his followers that they are lights too, set on a stand so that others see and give glory to the Father (Matt 5:14–16). More than that, he is the true substitute, not one offered by others with hands laid on him for a ceremony, but one who offered himself (Heb 9:14) and needed no sacrifice for his own sin. Through him, those he cleanses are presented to God as living sacrifices (Rom 12:1), and every one of them is given work to do.
