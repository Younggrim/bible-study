*Setting:* Jerusalem, 640–622 BC. For more than half a century Manasseh filled Judah with idols, star altars, occult practices and innocent blood (ch 21), and his son Amon followed him for two years before his own officials murdered him. "The people of the land" put Amon's eight-year-old son Josiah on the throne (21:24). Three hundred years earlier an unnamed prophet had stood at the northern kingdom's altar in Bethel and announced, "a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name," who would burn the bones of its priests on it (1 Kings 13:2). Now that son is king. In the wider world, Assyria's long grip was weakening after Ashurbanipal's death (about 627 BC), which gave Josiah room to act. Jeremiah began prophesying in Josiah's thirteenth year (Jer 1:2), and Zephaniah also preached in his reign (Zeph 1:1). Chapters 22–23 tell Josiah's story; 2 Chronicles 34–35 gives a fuller parallel.

#### Josiah Reigns in Judah (vv1–2)
- **"Josiah was eight years old when he began to reign, and he reigned thirty-one years" (v1).** About 640–609 BC. Like Joash, who was crowned at seven (11:21), he was a boy king, and someone must have guided him well. JFB says he had "better guardians" who trained him in piety. Chronicles says that at sixteen, "while he was yet a boy, he began to seek the God of David his father," and at twenty began purging the land of idols (2 Chr 34:3).
- **"His mother's name was Jedidah the daughter of Adaiah of Bozkath" (v1).** Jedidah means "beloved," related to Jedidiah, "beloved of the LORD," the name God gave Solomon (2 Sam 12:25). Bozkath was a town in the Judean lowlands near Lachish (Josh 15:39). A godly mother may be part of the story, though the text does not say.
- **"He did what was right… and walked in all the way of David his father, and he did not turn aside to the right or to the left" (v2).** Only Josiah gets this phrase among the kings. It comes straight from Deuteronomy, where Moses tells Israel and its future king not to turn aside "to the right hand or to the left" from God's commands (Deut 5:32; 17:11, 20; 28:14), as the Tyndale notes observe. It also echoes God's charge to Joshua (Josh 1:7). Josiah is the king who finally fits the job description. Guzik notes that v2 sums up his whole reign rather than describing him at age eight. Henry says his goodness "shines the brighter because it came just after so much wickedness."

#### Josiah Repairs the Temple (vv3–7)
- **"In the eighteenth year of King Josiah" (v3).** 622 BC, when he was 26. By then, according to Chronicles, he had already been purging idols for six years (2 Chr 34:3–8). The Tyndale notes count about ten years of following the Lord. Kings arranges things differently, putting the whole reform after the discovery of the book (ch 23). Both may be true: an early, partial reform, then a much deeper one after the book was read. JFB says the narrative "turns on one interesting incident" in this year.
- **"Shaphan the son of Azaliah… the secretary" (v3).** The royal secretary or scribe was a senior official handling records and correspondence. Shaphan's family appears for the next forty years. His son Ahikam (v12) later protected Jeremiah from being killed (Jer 26:24). Another son, Gemariah, had a room in the temple where Baruch read Jeremiah's scroll (Jer 36:10), and a clay seal impression reading "Gemaryahu son of Shaphan" has been found in the City of David. Ahikam's son Gedaliah became governor of Judah after the fall of Jerusalem (25:22). This family stood with God's word through Judah's darkest years.
- **"Go up to Hilkiah the high priest, that he may count the money… which the keepers of the threshold have collected" (v4).** The "keepers of the threshold" were senior priests or Levites who guarded the temple entrances, where a collection chest stood (12:9). The system was set up by Joash about two centuries earlier (12:9–15), and Josiah follows the same pattern, as the Tyndale notes say. Some think Hilkiah was the father of Jeremiah, whose father had the same name (Jer 1:1), as the Tyndale notes mention and Guzik states. But Jeremiah's father was a priest of Anathoth, outside Jerusalem, and Hilkiah was a common name, so most scholars think they were different men.
- **"The carpenters, and… the builders, and… the masons… buying timber and quarried stone" (v6).** After decades of neglect and pagan altars, the temple needed real structural repair, not just cleaning. Chronicles adds that Manasseh and Amon "had let go to ruin" the buildings (2 Chr 34:11).
- **"No accounting shall be asked from them… for they deal honestly" (v7).** The same words describe the workmen under Joash (12:15). In a nation where so much had gone wrong, here was a crew you could trust with the money. "Honestly" (*beʾemunah*) is literally "in faithfulness."
- **Commentators:** Henry says Josiah showed his respect first "to God's house, which he repaired." Guzik notes the two stages in Chronicles, a commitment at sixteen and a purge at twenty, and the care Josiah gave to organizing and funding the work. JFB notes the reform had already begun before this year.

#### Hilkiah Finds the Book of the Law (vv8–20)
- **"I have found the Book of the Law in the house of the LORD" (v8).** A scroll of God's law, found during the repairs. Moses had commanded that the book of the law be placed "by the side of the ark" as a witness (Deut 31:24–26). The king was to have his own copy and read it all his life (Deut 17:18–20), and the law was to be read aloud to all Israel every seven years (Deut 31:10–13). Under Manasseh all of that had stopped. Guzik observes that very few public readings are recorded in Israel's history.
- **What book was it?** The text calls it "the Book of the Law" (v8) and later "the Book of the Covenant" (23:2). Most scholars, ancient and modern, think it was Deuteronomy or a core of it. The reasons: Josiah reacts to curses like those of Deuteronomy 28; his reforms follow Deuteronomy closely, especially worship at one central place (Deut 12) and the Passover (Deut 16; 2 Kings 23:21–23); and "the Book of the Covenant" fits Deuteronomy's covenant language. JFB thinks it was the whole Pentateuch, the temple copy kept beside the ark, with Deuteronomy 28–30 the part read to the king. The Tyndale notes allow either.
- **Was it found or written?** Many critical scholars since the 1800s have argued that Deuteronomy was written in Josiah's time, or shortly before, and "found" to give the reform Moses' authority. Conservative scholars reply that the text presents it as an old, recognized book that everyone, king, priests and prophetess, accepted as genuine and as condemning them. A newly written book would hardly have announced doom on its own discoverers. Its form also resembles treaties from centuries earlier. The text itself plainly treats it as a rediscovery.
- **How could it be lost?** Easily, after fifty-seven years of Manasseh and Amon. Scrolls were few and handwritten. Manasseh had persecuted the faithful (21:16), and copies may have been hidden or destroyed. JFB suggests it was lost under Manasseh and Amon, or even under Ahaz. It may have been hidden in a wall or a storeroom. The tragedy is not only that a scroll went missing but that no one noticed.
- **"Hilkiah the priest has given me a book" (v10).** Hilkiah says "the Book of the Law"; Shaphan tells the king only "a book." Guzik passes on the observation that Shaphan reports the money first and the book almost as an afterthought. The most important discovery of the reign is mentioned after the accounts.
- **"And Shaphan read it before the king" (v10).** Reading aloud was the normal way to "read" in the ancient world. Chronicles says he read "from it," perhaps a portion (2 Chr 34:18).
- **"When the king heard the words of the Book of the Law, he tore his clothes" (v11).** Torn clothes meant grief and horror, as with Hezekiah (19:1). JFB: the reading produced "grief and terror" in him. Josiah does not argue, delay or explain it away. He takes God's word as addressed to him. Contrast his son Jehoiakim, who listened to Jeremiah's scroll and cut it up and burned it piece by piece, and "neither the king nor any of his servants… tore their garments" (Jer 36:23–24). Guzik sees this kind of conviction of sin as the beginning of every true revival (John 16:8).
- **"Go, inquire of the LORD for me, and for the people, and for all Judah" (vv12–13).** He sends five senior men, the high priest, the secretary and three officials. Achbor's son Elnathan appears later in Jeremiah (Jer 26:22; 36:12). "Inquire of the LORD" means seeking a word from God through a prophet (1 Kings 22:5–8). **"Great is the wrath of the LORD… because our fathers have not obeyed the words of this book."** Josiah understands the curses of the covenant apply to them right now. The Tyndale notes point to Deuteronomy 28.
- **"Huldah the prophetess, the wife of Shallum… keeper of the wardrobe" (v14).** Huldah stands with Miriam, Deborah and Isaiah's wife (Isa 8:3) as women the Old Testament calls prophetesses, as the Tyndale notes say. Her husband kept the royal or priestly garments. A Shallum was Jeremiah's uncle (Jer 32:7), and some connect them, but it is uncertain. Her name means "weasel" or "mole," and Guzik passes on the curious point that Shaphan ("rock badger") and Achbor ("mouse") also have animal names, which was ordinary in Israel.
- **Why Huldah and not Jeremiah?** The text does not say. Jeremiah was young, perhaps in his twenties, and may have been at home in Anathoth; JFB suggests both that and that Zephaniah was young. Huldah lived near the palace and was evidently well known and trusted. Guzik leaves the reason open, "perhaps spiritual, perhaps practical." What is clear is that the king's chief men went to her without hesitation and took her word as God's word. Centuries later, the southern gates of the temple mount were known as the Huldah Gates, and Jewish tradition remembered her with honor.
- **"Now she lived in Jerusalem in the Second Quarter" (v14).** The *Mishneh*, a newer district of Jerusalem, probably the area on the western hill enclosed by Hezekiah's expanded wall (2 Chr 32:5). Zephaniah mentions it (Zeph 1:10). The KJV's "in the college" comes from an old Jewish tradition that she taught there; JFB correctly says it means "a particular suburb of Jerusalem."
- **"Tell the man who sent you to me" (v15).** Not "the king" but "the man." Before God's word, the king is a man like any other. When she turns to his personal answer, she calls him "the king of Judah" (v18).
- **"I will bring disaster upon this place and upon its inhabitants, all the words of the book" (v16).** The curses written in the scroll will happen. Guzik stresses that God's faithfulness shows in his warnings as much as his promises. **"Because they have forsaken me and have made offerings to other gods… my wrath will be kindled against this place, and it will not be quenched" (v17).** The same verdict as 21:10–15. Josiah's reform could not reverse the judgment Manasseh's generation had earned, as 23:26 states plainly.
- **"Because your heart was penitent, and you humbled yourself before the LORD" (v19).** "Penitent" is literally "your heart was soft" (*rakh*), tender, able to be moved, the opposite of a hard heart (Ezek 36:26). God notices three things: his soft heart, his humbling himself, and his tears. **"That they should become a desolation and a curse"** echoes covenant curses like Deuteronomy 28:37. **"I also have heard you."** Like Hezekiah (20:5), Josiah's tears are seen.
- **"You shall be gathered to your grave in peace, and your eyes shall not see all the disaster" (v20).** The judgment would not fall in his lifetime, as JFB and the Tyndale notes explain. God had done the same for Ahab when he humbled himself (1 Kings 21:29). But Josiah died in battle at Megiddo in 609 BC, shot by Pharaoh Neco's archers (23:29; 2 Chr 35:23). How was that "in peace"? Most interpreters answer that "in peace" here means before the catastrophe, in God's favor, and buried in his own tomb in Jerusalem, rather than a promise of a quiet death in bed. Twenty-three years later Jerusalem burned, and he did not see it. Guzik gives these three senses. Others note that his death came after he ignored a warning and went to battle against Neco (2 Chr 35:21–22), which may have forfeited part of the promise.
- **"And they brought back word to the king" (v20).** The answer is not what anyone hoped: judgment is coming anyway. Josiah's response, in the next chapter, is to obey God's word fully regardless. He does not reform in order to avoid judgment. He reforms because God's word is true.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as Josiah's respect "to God's book, which he was much affected with the reading of," and "to God's messengers, whom he thereupon consulted," after which "he set about that glorious work of reformation." JFB identifies the book as the temple copy of the Mosaic law, with Deuteronomy 28–30 read to the king, and suggests Jeremiah was away at Anathoth. Guzik thinks the book was Deuteronomy, traces how the law was meant to be read and how rarely it was, and explains "in peace" in three senses while admitting Josiah's violent death.

#### Pointing to Christ
Josiah is the king who did not turn aside to the right or the left, the closest Judah ever came to the king Deuteronomy describes. When God's word was read, his heart broke, and God heard him. But even Josiah could not stop the judgment his people had earned; he could only be spared from seeing it. The chapter leaves Judah waiting for a king who would not only keep God's law perfectly but take its curse on himself. Jesus is that King. He did not turn aside from his Father's will, and "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). The book that condemned Judah finds its fulfilment in him, and the hearts it softens are the ones he makes new.
