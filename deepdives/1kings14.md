*Setting:* The years after the kingdom split in about 931 BC. Chapters 12–13 showed the break: Rehoboam's harsh answer lost him ten tribes, and Jeroboam set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan to keep his people from going to Jerusalem. A man of God from Judah denounced the Bethel altar, and still "Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way" (13:33). Chapter 14 shows both kings coming to the end of their reigns. In the north, Jeroboam's son falls sick and the prophet who once promised Jeroboam the kingdom now announces his family's ruin. In the south, Judah sinks into Canaanite worship and an Egyptian army strips Jerusalem of Solomon's gold. From here on, Kings moves back and forth between the two kingdoms, giving each king a standard opening and closing summary and a verdict on how he stood before God.

#### Prophecy Against Jeroboam (vv1–18)
- **"Abijah the son of Jeroboam fell sick" (v1).** His name means "my father is the LORD" (*Yah*), a sign that Jeroboam still thought of himself as a worshiper of Israel's God. JFB thinks Abijah was old enough to be seen as heir to the throne.
- **"Disguise yourself... and go to Shiloh" (v2).** Shiloh, about 20 miles north of Jerusalem, was where the tabernacle had stood in the days of the judges (Josh 18:1; 1 Sam 1:3). The Philistines had destroyed it (Jer 7:12), and it was now a small place. Ahijah the prophet lived there. He had torn his cloak in twelve pieces and promised Jeroboam ten tribes (11:29–39). Jeroboam turns to the true prophet of the true God, not to the priests of his calves. But he does not come himself, and he sends his wife in disguise. He wants God's information without God's rebuke.
- **"Ten loaves, some cakes, and a jar of honey" (v3).** People normally brought a gift to a prophet (1 Sam 9:7–8; 2 Kgs 8:8–9). These are a farmer's gifts, not a queen's, to match her disguise. JFB says the gift was "in unison with the peasant character she assumed." "He will tell you what shall happen to the child." Jeroboam asks what will happen, not for prayer or healing. Guzik passes on Henry's line that people would rather hear their fortune than their faults.
- **"Ahijah could not see, for his eyes were dim because of his age" (v4).** The irony is rich. A disguise for a blind man. JFB calls it "Strange infatuation" to think God "could not penetrate a flimsy disguise." Before she arrives, "the LORD said to Ahijah, 'Behold, the wife of Jeroboam is coming'" (v5).
- **"Come in, wife of Jeroboam. Why do you pretend to be another?" (v6).** He knows her by "the sound of her feet." She has not said a word. "I am charged with unbearable news for you." The word is literally "hard," harsh news.
- **"Because I exalted you from among the people" (v7).** God reminds Jeroboam of everything he did for him. He took a man of no royal family and made him "leader" (*nagid*, the title used of Saul and David when God chose them, 1 Sam 9:16; 13:14). He "tore the kingdom away from the house of David" (v8), the same verb as Ahijah's torn cloak (11:30–31).
- **"You have not been like my servant David" (v8).** David sinned gravely, but he repented and never worshiped idols. The Tyndale notes say David truly repented for his sins (Ps 51). Jeroboam had been promised a house like David's if he obeyed (11:38). David becomes the measure for every king from here on.
- **"You have done evil above all who were before you" (v9).** "Above all" means worse than Saul, David and Solomon. Solomon allowed idols for his wives. Jeroboam made idol worship the national religion. "Other gods and metal images" refers to the golden calves (12:28). Whatever Jeroboam claimed they stood for, God calls them "other gods."
- **"And have cast me behind your back" (v9).** A picture of contempt, like tossing something worthless over the shoulder. Ezekiel uses the same phrase (Ezek 23:35). Guzik passes on Dilday's note that the same image is used in reverse of God's grace: Hezekiah praised God, "you have cast all my sins behind your back" (Isa 38:17).
- **"I will... cut off from Jeroboam every male, both bond and free" (v10).** "Every male" is literally a crude phrase, "every one who urinates against the wall," an insult as much as a description (also 1 Sam 25:22; 1 Kgs 16:11). "Bond and free" probably means "everyone, without exception." "As a man burns up dung until it is all gone" is a deliberately ugly image. Jeroboam's dynasty will be burned away like refuse.
- **"The dogs shall eat... the birds of the heavens shall eat" (v11).** To lie unburied was the worst disgrace in the ancient world. This fulfills the curse of the covenant (Deut 28:26). The same sentence will fall on Baasha's house (16:4) and Ahab's (21:24).
- **"When your feet enter the city, the child shall die" (v12).** Her own footsteps, the sound that gave her away, will now mark her son's death.
- **"He only of Jeroboam shall come to the grave, because in him there is found something pleasing to the LORD" (v13).** This is a tender and surprising line. The child is the one member of the family who will be properly buried and mourned. "Something pleasing" is literally "a good thing." We are not told what it was, only that God saw it. JFB says only that he alone would receive burial. Henry draws a sad lesson: "It is often a bad sign for a family, when the best in it are buried out of it." Isaiah says, "The righteous man is taken away from calamity" (Isa 57:1). His early death spares him the slaughter that is coming.
- **"The LORD will raise up for himself a king over Israel who shall cut off the house of Jeroboam today" (v14).** That king is Baasha, who will kill Jeroboam's son Nadab in only the second year of his reign and wipe out the family (15:27–30). The Hebrew of the end of v14 is hard, literally "this day, and what? even now." It means "it is starting already."
- **"The LORD will strike Israel as a reed is shaken in the water" (v15).** The prophecy widens to the whole northern kingdom. A reed in a river bends with every current, unstable and helpless. "Root up Israel out of this good land... and scatter them beyond the Euphrates" was fulfilled about 200 years later when Assyria deported the northern tribes in 722 BC (2 Kgs 17:6, 23). The reason given: "they have made their Asherim." The Asherim were wooden poles or trees set up for Asherah, the Canaanite mother goddess, believed to be the consort of the chief god El (Deut 16:21; Judg 3:7).
- **"Because of the sins of Jeroboam, which he sinned and made Israel to sin" (v16).** This is the first appearance of a refrain that runs through the rest of Kings like a drumbeat. Jeroboam's sin did not stay personal. He led a nation into it.
- **"As she came to the threshold of the house, the child died" (v17).** Jeroboam's capital has moved to Tirzah, a lovely town a few miles northeast of Shechem (Song 6:4 uses it as a picture of beauty). The child dies exactly as foretold, "and all Israel buried him and mourned for him" (v18). The quick fulfillment confirms the rest of the prophecy. Guzik makes the point.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God will judge men according to what they are, not by what they seem to be," and "If great men do wickedly, they draw many others, both into the guilt and punishment." Guzik notes that Jeroboam treated Ahijah like a fortune-teller rather than a man of God who could pray for the boy. The Tyndale notes add that the "eaten by dogs" sentence is drawn straight from the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28:26.

#### The Death of Jeroboam (vv19–20)
- **"The Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel" (v19).** This is not the biblical book of Chronicles but an official court history, now lost, that the writer of Kings used and pointed readers to. The Tyndale notes say it is cited seventeen times. The Bible's writer is telling readers where they can find more, and he chooses what matters for his purpose: how each king stood before God.
- **"How he warred" (v19).** Chronicles records one of those wars: a crushing defeat by Rehoboam's son Abijah, after which "Jeroboam did not recover his power" and "the LORD struck him down, and he died" (2 Chr 13:20).
- **"Jeroboam reigned twenty-two years" (v20).** About 931–910 BC. Nadab his son succeeds him, and he will last two years (15:25–28).

#### Rehoboam Reigns in Judah (vv21–31)
- **"Rehoboam was forty-one years old when he began to reign, and he reigned seventeen years in Jerusalem" (v21).** About 931–913 BC. Forty-one means he was born before Solomon became king, so Solomon married his Ammonite mother early. "The city that the LORD had chosen... to put his name there" is a reminder: Judah has the true temple. That makes its sin worse, not better.
- **"His mother's name was Naamah the Ammonite" (v21).** Judah's kings are introduced with their mothers' names. The queen mother was a powerful figure (15:13; 2 Kgs 24:12). Naamah is mentioned twice, here and at the end (v31), which seems deliberate. The Ammonites worshiped Milcom (Molech), whose altar Solomon had built for his wives (11:5–7). JFB blames "her heathen extraction and her influence as queen mother" for Rehoboam's lapse.
- **"Judah did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, and they provoked him to jealousy" (v22).** God is jealous for his people the way a faithful husband is jealous for his wife (Exod 20:5; 34:14). This is not petty envy but the right anger of covenant love betrayed. The Chronicler adds that Rehoboam began well for three years, then "abandoned the law of the LORD, and all Israel with him" (2 Chr 11:17; 12:1).
- **"High places and pillars and Asherim on every high hill and under every green tree" (v23).** High places were local hilltop shrines. Pillars were standing stones, often linked with male fertility gods. Asherim were the poles of the goddess. "Under every green tree" became the prophets' stock phrase for Canaanite worship spreading everywhere (Jer 2:20; Hos 4:13).
- **"There were also male cult prostitutes in the land" (v24).** Canaanite religion included ritual sex at shrines, a fertility rite meant to make the land and the herds fruitful. The law forbade it outright (Deut 23:17–18). "According to all the abominations of the nations that the LORD drove out" is a stark warning: Judah is becoming the very thing God removed from the land.
- **"In the fifth year of King Rehoboam, Shishak king of Egypt came up against Jerusalem" (v25).** This is about 926 BC. Shishak is Shoshenq I, founder of Egypt's Twenty-second Dynasty, the pharaoh who sheltered Jeroboam (11:40). It is one of the earliest points where Egyptian records and the Bible meet. On the Bubastite Portal of the great temple of Amun at Karnak (Thebes), Shoshenq carved a list of more than 150 places he conquered in Judah, Israel and the Negev. A fragment of a victory stela with his name has been found at Megiddo. Jerusalem does not appear on the surviving portion of the list, which fits Chronicles: Rehoboam paid tribute and the city was not destroyed. Chronicles adds that Shishak came with 1,200 chariots and 60,000 horsemen, took Judah's fortified cities, and that Rehoboam and the princes humbled themselves at Shemaiah's warning, so God limited the damage (2 Chr 12:2–12).
- **"He took away everything. He also took away all the shields of gold that Solomon had made" (v26).** Solomon made 500 gold shields, 200 large and 300 small, and hung them in the House of the Forest of Lebanon (10:16–17). They were for display, a dazzling show of wealth. Gold is too soft to stop a spear. Only five years after Solomon died, all of it went to Egypt. It had taken a lifetime to gather.
- **"King Rehoboam made in their place shields of bronze" (v27).** Bronze shines in the sun and could pass for gold from a distance. It is a perfect picture of the kingdom: still keeping up appearances, but the real glory is gone. The bronze shields were locked in the guardroom and carried out only when the king went to the temple (v28), a parade of what used to be. Guzik passes on Knapp's comparison to a soul or church that keeps the outward forms after losing its strength.
- **"There was war between Rehoboam and Jeroboam continually" (v30).** Not necessarily full war, since Shemaiah had forbidden that (12:24), but constant border fighting. JFB says "frequent skirmishes would take place on the borders." The two halves of God's people bled each other for decades.
- **"Abijam his son reigned in his place" (v31).** Chronicles calls him Abijah, "my father is the LORD." Kings has Abijam, "my father is the sea." The two spellings are probably just variants of one name. Some think Kings uses the less flattering form on purpose.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Sin exposes, makes poor, and weakens any people," and on the shields, "Sin makes the gold become dim, changes the most fine gold, and turns it into brass" (compare Lam 4:1). Guzik passes on Payne's remark that Solomon's court despised silver (10:21) and his son's court had to make do with bronze. He contrasts the two kings: Rehoboam, a hard ruler who humbled himself late, and Jeroboam, a man of the people who began with promise and ended badly. JFB calls Shishak "the instrument in the hand of Providence for punishing the national defection."

#### Pointing to Christ
Two kingdoms, one story of decline. Jeroboam had God's promise and would not trust it. Rehoboam had the temple and the city God chose and filled the land with idols. Within five years, Solomon's gold was in Egypt and bronze stood in its place. And yet in the darkest house in Israel there was one child in whom God found "something pleasing" (v13), and God saw it. The Bible's story keeps moving toward the King in whom the Father would find everything pleasing: "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" (Matt 3:17). He is the Son of David who succeeded where David's sons failed, and he brings a glory that cannot be carried off to Egypt or replaced with bronze. His people's inheritance is "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven" (1 Pet 1:4).
