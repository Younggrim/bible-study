*Setting:* A few months after Jerusalem fell in 587 or 586 BC. The Babylonians have left a governor, Gedaliah son of Ahikam, at Mizpah, about eight miles north of the ruined city. Soldiers have come in from the hills, refugees have come home from Moab, Ammon and Edom, and the summer harvest has been plentiful (ch 40). Chapter 40 ended with a warning: Baalis king of Ammon had sent Ishmael son of Nethaniah to kill Gedaliah. Gedaliah refused to believe it. This chapter tells what happened next. It is one of the darkest stories in the Old Testament: a murder at a shared meal, a massacre of worshipers, and the collapse of the remnant's one hope of a quiet life in the land. 2 Kings 25:25–26 tells the same events in two verses. Jeremiah gives the full account, and the prophet himself is not mentioned once.

#### Gedaliah Murdered (vv1–18)
- **"In the seventh month" (v1).** Tishri, in September–October. Jerusalem's walls were breached in the fourth month and the city burned in the fifth (39:2; 52:12), so this is probably only about two months later. Some scholars think it could be a year or more later, since the text gives no year, and they link it to a further Babylonian deportation in 582 BC (52:30). Jews later kept a fast in the seventh month (Zech 7:5; 8:19), and Jewish tradition holds that it mourned Gedaliah. The Fast of Gedaliah is still observed on the day after the Jewish New Year.
- **"Ishmael the son of Nethaniah, son of Elishama, of the royal family, one of the chief officers of the king" (v1).** Ishmael was a member of David's house, perhaps from a side branch. Some think his grandfather was Elishama the royal secretary of 36:12, but the name was common and that is uncertain. The phrase "one of the chief officers of the king" is missing from the parallel in 2 Kings 25:25 and from the Greek translation, and it may be a later addition that explains his rank. Either way, he saw a commoner from a family of officials ruling where a son of David should be.
- **"As they ate bread together there at Mizpah" (v1).** In the ancient Near East a shared meal created a bond. A host owed his guest protection, and a guest owed his host loyalty. To kill a host at his own table was among the worst betrayals imaginable. David grieved over the same kind of treachery: "Even my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me" (Ps 41:9). Jesus quoted that psalm about Judas at the Last Supper (John 13:18).
- **"Struck down Gedaliah… with the sword, and killed him, whom the king of Babylon had appointed governor in the land" (v2).** The phrase at the end of the verse is the whole problem. This was not just a murder. It was a direct act of rebellion against Nebuchadnezzar, and the whole remnant would pay for it. **"Ishmael also struck down all the Judeans who were with Gedaliah… and the Chaldean soldiers who happened to be there" (v3).** The killing of Babylonian soldiers made reprisal certain. Most commentators take "all the Judeans" to mean Gedaliah's attendants and guard, since many other people at Mizpah were still alive the next day (v10).
- **"On the day after the murder of Gedaliah, before anyone knew of it" (v4).** The killers kept it quiet. Mizpah was a small town, and many people were out in the fields and vineyards at harvest time.
- **"Eighty men arrived from Shechem and Shiloh and Samaria" (v5).** These were towns in the old northern kingdom of Israel, which Assyria had conquered more than a century earlier (722 BC). Many Israelites still lived there, and King Josiah's reforms had reached into the north (2 Kgs 23:15–20; 2 Chr 34:6–9). Each town carries memories. Shechem was where Joshua renewed the covenant (Josh 24). Shiloh was where the tabernacle once stood before God let it be destroyed, the very example Jeremiah used in his temple sermon (7:12–14). Samaria had been the capital of the northern kings. Now worshipers from all three walk south toward Jerusalem.
- **"With their beards shaved and their clothes torn, and their bodies gashed" (v5).** All three are signs of mourning, almost certainly for the burned temple. Shaving the beard and tearing clothes were ordinary Israelite customs of grief. Cutting the body was forbidden in the law because pagan worshipers did it (Lev 19:28; Deut 14:1), and Jeremiah himself had mentioned it as part of the land's grieving (16:6; 47:5). These men mixed true sorrow with a forbidden custom, as people with partial teaching often do.
- **"Bringing grain offerings and incense to present at the temple of the LORD" (v5).** The temple was in ashes, but the site was still holy to them. They brought no animals, only grain and incense (the Hebrew is *lebonah*, frankincense), perhaps because no altar was standing for blood sacrifices. The Feast of Booths fell in the seventh month (Lev 23:33–36), and these pilgrims may have been coming to keep it at the ruins. Their faith is touching: they came to worship at a ruin. Psalm 79 and Lamentations 1–2 show the kind of grief they carried.
- **"Ishmael… came out from Mizpah to meet them, weeping as he came" (v6).** The tears were a mask. He matched their grief so they would trust him. **"Come in to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam."** He uses the name of the man he has just murdered as bait.
- **"Slaughtered them and cast them into a cistern" (v7).** The text gives no motive. Perhaps Ishmael feared they would report the murder, or he wanted their offerings and goods, or he simply enjoyed killing. Some suggest he saw northerners loyal to Gedaliah's peace as enemies. The text leaves it unexplained, which makes it more chilling.
- **"We have stores of wheat, barley, oil, and honey hidden in the fields" (v8).** In unsettled times farmers hid grain in underground pits and dry cisterns, and many such pits have been found in Israel. The "honey" may be bee honey or a thick syrup made from dates or grapes. Ten men buy their lives with food. Ishmael was planning to flee and needed supplies. His greed proved stronger than his cruelty.
- **"The large cistern that King Asa had made for defense against Baasha king of Israel" (v9).** About three centuries earlier, King Baasha of Israel fortified Ramah to choke off Jerusalem. King Asa of Judah hired the Arameans to attack Baasha, then carried off Baasha's building stones to fortify Geba and Mizpah (1 Kgs 15:16–22; 2 Chr 16:1–6). Those passages do not mention a cistern, but a fortress needed water. Excavations at Tell en-Nasbeh, the likely site of Mizpah, have found many cisterns, though no one can say which was Asa's. The Hebrew at the start of the verse is awkward. It may read "by the hand of Gedaliah" (the ESV footnote has "Hebrew *by the hand of*"), and the Greek translation reads "a great cistern," which the ESV follows. A reservoir built to save a city in a siege is now filled with corpses, which also fouls the town's water.
- **"Ishmael took captive all the rest of the people… the king's daughters" (v10).** "The king's daughters" probably means women of the royal household, Zedekiah's daughters or other royal women, who had been spared when the princes were killed at Riblah (39:6). They will appear again in the group taken to Egypt (43:6). Jeremiah and Baruch were probably among these captives too, since they are with the group later (42:2; 43:6). **"Set out to cross over to the Ammonites."** Ishmael headed east for the Jordan and the protection of Baalis, who had sent him. He may have meant to sell the captives as slaves or use the royal women as a political prize.
- **"When Johanan the son of Kareah and all the leaders of the forces with him heard" (v11).** Johanan was the man who had warned Gedaliah and offered to kill Ishmael first (40:13–16). He and his soldiers were not at Mizpah when the attack came.
- **"They came upon him at the great pool that is in Gibeon" (v12).** Gibeon (modern el-Jib) lay a few miles southwest of Mizpah. Ishmael may have gone that way to avoid being followed on the direct road east. In 1956–57 archaeologists found a huge round shaft there, about 37 feet across, cut into the rock with a spiral staircase leading about 80 feet down to the water. Many scholars think this is the pool where Joab's and Abner's men met centuries earlier (2 Sam 2:12–17).
- **"They rejoiced… turned around and came back" (vv13–14).** As soon as the captives see Johanan, they turn and go to him. Ishmael's men give him no real loyalty.
- **"But Ishmael the son of Nethaniah escaped from Johanan with eight men" (v15).** He came with ten and leaves with eight, so two were probably killed or captured in the clash. He reaches the Ammonites and disappears from the Bible. The prince of David's house who could have joined the remnant's peace ends as a hired killer for a foreign king.
- **"Soldiers, women, children, and eunuchs" (v16).** "Eunuchs" (*sarisim*) may mean court officials in general, not only men who were castrated. Either way they were part of the royal household that had been at Mizpah.
- **"They went and stayed at Geruth Chimham near Bethlehem" (v17).** *Geruth* means "lodging place." It probably took its name from Chimham, the son of old Barzillai, whom David took to Jerusalem as a reward for his father's loyalty (2 Sam 19:31–40). Tradition holds that David gave him land near Bethlehem, though Scripture does not say so. By now it was likely a stopping place for travelers on the road south, the road to Egypt.
- **"Intending to go to Egypt because of the Chaldeans. For they were afraid of them" (vv17–18).** Their fear was not foolish. Babylon would not ask carefully who had killed its governor and its soldiers. But the decision is already half made before anyone asks the LORD, and that is the problem chapter 42 takes up. Egypt was the place God had led Israel out of. Going back meant undoing the exodus. The law even warned that Israel's king must not "cause the people to return to Egypt" (Deut 17:16).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says it is hard to tell which is more astonishing, that God permits such crimes or that men commit them. He adds, "Let it inspire us with an indignation at the wickedness of men and an awe of God's righteousness." On v8: "Ishmael saved them, not for the love of mercy, but for the love of money." On v15: "The most cruel are often the most cowardly." All three stress the broken bond of the shared meal. Guzik cites commentators who link it to Psalm 41:9 and John 13, and JFB calls it a violation of "the sacred right of hospitality." JFB thinks the pilgrims were killed because Ishmael suspected they came to live under Gedaliah, and that the captives were taken to be sold as slaves. Guzik quotes Kidner's verdict that Ishmael disgraced the name of David, acting less like David than like the violent Jehu. On v18 Henry and JFB both suspect the fear of Babylon was partly an excuse. JFB says the wiser course was to clear themselves before the Babylonians and name the real culprit. Henry writes that those who "excuse themselves in sin with pretended fears" will justly lose their comfort in real ones.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is what life looks like when there is no righteous king. A son of David murders a man at his own table, weeps false tears to lure worshipers to their deaths, and flees to a foreign king. The pilgrims mourning at a burned temple had nowhere left to bring their offerings. Jesus is the true Son of David who reverses all of it. When he was betrayed at a table by one who ate his bread (John 13:18), he did not take life but gave his own. He wept real tears over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41). He made himself the temple that would be destroyed and raised in three days (John 2:19–21), so that worshipers from every region, Samaria included, would no longer depend on a place at all (John 4:21–24).
