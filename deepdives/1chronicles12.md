*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Chapter 11 told how all Israel made David king at Hebron and listed his greatest warriors. This chapter steps back to show how that support grew. The first half (vv1–22) goes back to the years before Saul's death, when David was an outlaw. He spent about sixteen months living among the Philistines at Ziklag, a town in the Negev given to him by Achish, king of Gath (1 Sam 27:6–7). Even then, fighting men were slipping away from Saul to join him, including men from Saul's own tribe. The second half (vv23–40) jumps to the great gathering at Hebron, where warriors from every tribe came to crown David, followed by a three-day feast. Only Chronicles records any of this. The chapter's key word is "help." It appears again and again (vv1, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 33), and it carries the Chronicler's message: God was helping David, and those who saw that came to help him too.

#### The Mighty Men Join David (vv1–40)
- **"The men who came to David at Ziklag, while he could not move about freely because of Saul" (v1).** Literally "while he was still shut away from Saul." David was a hunted man with a price on his head. Joining him then cost something. JFB suggests the three groups in vv1–22 are arranged in the order they arrived.
- **"Bowmen… with either the right or the left hand; they were Benjaminites, Saul's kinsmen" (v2).** Benjamin's name means "son of the right hand," yet the tribe was famous for left-handed and two-handed fighters. Ehud the judge was left-handed (Judg 3:15), and Benjamin had 700 left-handed slingers who "could sling a stone at a hair and not miss" (Judg 20:16). That the first group to come over to David were Saul's own kin shows how clearly God's favor had shifted. JFB sees it as a protest against Saul's unjust hounding of David.
- **"Jehu of Anathoth, Ishmaiah of Gibeon" (vv3–4).** Towns of Benjamin near Saul's home at Gibeah. **"Elkanah, Isshiah, Azarel, Joezer, and Jashobeam, the Korahites"** (v6) is a surprise in a Benjaminite list. These may be Levites of Korah's family living in Benjamin, or a different family with the same name.
- **"From the Gadites there went over to David at the stronghold in the wilderness" (v8).** Now men from east of the Jordan. "The stronghold in the wilderness" is probably one of David's hideouts in the Judean wilderness, perhaps Adullam or En-gedi, earlier than Ziklag (1 Sam 22:4; 24:22).
- **"Whose faces were like the faces of lions and who were swift as gazelles upon the mountains" (v8).** Fierce and fast, the two great virtues of an ancient foot soldier. Deuteronomy 33:20 pictures Gad as a lion, and David's lament praised Saul and Jonathan as "swifter than eagles… stronger than lions" (2 Sam 1:23).
- **"The least was a match for a hundred men and the greatest for a thousand" (v14).** The Hebrew can mean this, a proverb of courage (compare Lev 26:8), or it can mean the least of them commanded a hundred and the greatest a thousand. Either way these were officers, not raw recruits. JFB takes the first reading as a vivid way of describing their valor.
- **"Crossed the Jordan in the first month, when it was overflowing all its banks" (v15).** The first month, Nisan (March–April), was flood season, when snowmelt from Mount Hermon swelled the river. It was in the same month and the same flood that Israel crossed under Joshua, on dry ground (Josh 3:15; 4:19). These men had no miracle. They forded it anyway, and then cleared their enemies from the valleys on both banks. JFB calls it "a remarkable feat," and Henry calls them "daring men, that could break through the greatest difficulties."
- **"Some of the men of Benjamin and Judah came to the stronghold" (v16).** A third group. David had reason to be cautious. The men of Keilah, whom he had rescued, were ready to hand him over to Saul, and the Ziphites, men of Judah, betrayed his hiding place twice (1 Sam 23:12, 19; 26:1).
- **"If you have come to me in friendship… my heart will be joined to you; but if to betray me… may the God of our fathers see and rebuke you" (v17).** David is open but not naïve. He offers friendship, and he leaves any treachery for God to judge rather than taking revenge himself. **"Although there is no wrong in my hands"** was David's steady claim during these years, that he had never harmed Saul (1 Sam 24:11).
- **"Then the Spirit clothed Amasai" (v18).** A vivid Hebrew picture. The verb *labash* means "to put on clothing," so the Spirit of God put Amasai on like a garment and spoke through him. The same expression is used of Gideon (Judg 6:34) and of the priest Zechariah (2 Chr 24:20). Amasai may be Amasa, David's nephew, who later commanded Absalom's army and then David's (2 Sam 17:25; 19:13), but that is only a suggestion.
- **"We are yours, O David, and with you, O son of Jesse!" (v18).** A short poem, and the heart of the chapter. It is a pledge of total loyalty. When the northern tribes later broke away from David's grandson, they shouted the opposite: "What portion do we have in David?… To your tents, O Israel!" (2 Chr 10:16).
- **"Peace, peace to you, and peace to your helpers! For your God helps you" (v18).** *Shalom* three times: wholeness, well-being, safety. Then the reason they are joining: "your God helps you." The word for "help" (*azar*) links helpers and God. People were helping David because God was. Henry and Guzik both make that the point of the verse. Guzik adds that Amasai's loyalty rested on seeing God's hand on David, not on David's prospects.
- **"Some of the men of Manasseh deserted to David when he came with the Philistines for the battle against Saul" (v19).** This refers to the moment just before Gilboa, when David had marched out with King Achish's army. The Philistine commanders distrusted him and sent him home (1 Sam 29:4). The Chronicler includes their exact fear, **"At peril to our heads he will desert to his master Saul,"** which is darkly ironic after Saul's own head was taken to Dagon's temple (10:10).
- **"They helped David against the band of raiders" (v21).** While David was away, Amalekites had burned Ziklag and carried off the families of David's men, including his own wives. David pursued them and recovered everyone (1 Sam 30). These seven commanders from Manasseh joined him just in time for that fight.
- **"From day to day men came to David to help him, until there was a great army, like an army of God" (v22).** Literally "like the camp of God." It is the phrase Jacob used when angels met him: "This is God's camp!" and he named the place Mahanaim, "two camps" (Gen 32:1–2). JFB takes it as a Hebrew way of saying "a very great army." It may also suggest that David's camp was what God's own army looked like on earth. Henry: "When the promise comes to the birth, leave it to God to find strength to bring forth."
- **"Who came to David in Hebron to turn the kingdom of Saul over to him, according to the word of the LORD" (v23).** Now the scene is Hebron, after Ish-bosheth's death (2 Sam 5:1–3). "Turn over" echoes 10:14, "the LORD… turned the kingdom over to David." The people are doing on earth what God had already decided in heaven.
- **The tribal muster (vv24–37).** Every tribe sends men. All twelve are here, plus Levi, including **Dan** (v35), which was missing from the genealogies (chapter 7). The totals add up to about 340,000. As elsewhere, some scholars think *eleph*, "thousand," here means a military unit, which would make the numbers much smaller. The order runs from south to north and then across the Jordan, as JFB notes. The nearer tribes sent fewer men and the distant ones far more, perhaps because Judah had already been David's for seven years.
- **"Jehoiada, of the house of Aaron… Zadok, a young man mighty in valor" (vv27–28).** Priests came armed. Jehoiada was probably Benaiah's father (27:5), the man in 11:22. Zadok, still young, later became David's high priest and the head of the priestly line (6:8; 1 Kings 1:39). Guzik, following Selman, notes that Levites were not barred from fighting.
- **"Benjaminites, the kinsmen of Saul, 3,000, of whom the majority had to that point kept their allegiance to the house of Saul" (v29).** An honest note. Benjamin sent the fewest men, because loyalty to Saul's family died hard. Years later a Benjaminite named Shimei would curse David as "a man of blood" (2 Sam 16:5–8).
- **"Of Issachar, men who had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do, 200 chiefs" (v32).** The most quoted verse in the chapter. These leaders read the situation rightly: Saul's house was finished, God's hand was on David, and Israel needed to unite. The phrase recalls the wise men of Persia "who knew the times" (Esth 1:13). JFB passes on a Jewish tradition that the tribe was skilled in astronomy and the calendar. Guzik, following Poole and Trapp, takes it as practical wisdom about when to act. Henry: "A stander-by sees sometimes more than a gamester."
- **"Of Zebulun… to help David with singleness of purpose" (v33).** Literally "not with heart and heart," that is, with no double heart. The same expression in Psalm 12:2 describes liars who "speak with a double heart." Zebulun came undivided. Henry: "They were, in this matter, Israelites indeed, in whom was no guile."
- **"All these… came to Hebron with a whole heart to make David king over all Israel… all the rest of Israel were of a single mind" (v38).** "A whole heart" is *lev shalem*, a complete or undivided heart, one of the Chronicler's favorite phrases (28:9; 29:9, 19). "Single mind" is literally "one heart." The Tyndale notes point out that the succession of a king from a new family was usually bitterly contested, and the Chronicler's emphasis is that this time the tribes were united.
- **"They were there with David for three days, eating and drinking" (v39).** A coronation feast. The covenant of 11:3 was sealed with a shared meal, as covenants often were (Gen 31:54; Exod 24:11).
- **"Bringing food on donkeys and on camels and on mules and on oxen… for there was joy in Israel" (v40).** Even the far northern tribes, Issachar, Zebulun and Naphtali, sent supplies more than a hundred miles south. Figs, raisins, wine, oil, cattle and sheep, loaded on every animal that could carry them. The chapter ends with the word "joy." JFB estimates that about 300,000 armed men had to be fed.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees God's providence at work while David was still in hiding: support was gathering before David could see it. On the feast: "Thus, when the throne of Christ is set up in a soul, there is, or ought to be, great joy in that soul." Guzik, quoting Selman, says the real heroes of the chapter are the people of God themselves, and draws the lesson that unity comes when God's people are wholly committed to his chosen King. He cites F. B. Meyer: the church's unity comes not from organization but from each heart giving Christ the throne. JFB notes that only 3,000 Benjaminites came, evidence that David's cause was still unpopular in Saul's tribe.

#### Pointing to Christ
Men came to David while he was still an outlaw, "shut away" and rejected by the reigning king. They saw God's hand on him before most of Israel did, and they risked everything to join him. Hebrews calls believers to do the same with Jesus: "Let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured" (Heb 13:13). Amasai's words, spoken by the Spirit, are fitting words for anyone who comes to Christ: "We are yours… and with you." And Jesus, like David, greets those who come to him with "Peace" (John 20:19). The chapter ends with all Israel gathered around its king for a feast "for there was joy in Israel." Scripture closes with a greater gathering, a multitude "from every nation, from all tribes," and a marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 7:9; 19:9).
