*Setting:* Abraham is about 99 or 100 and Sarah about 89 or 90, probably sometime around 2000 BC, though dates for the patriarchs are approximate. In chapter 18 the LORD promised that Sarah would bear a son "about this time next year" (18:10), and in chapter 19 Sodom and Gomorrah were destroyed while Abraham watched the smoke rise from the hills near Hebron (19:27–28). Now he moves south, out of the hill country, into the dry grazing land of the Negeb. Isaac's birth is only months away (21:1–2), and the promised mother of the promised son is about to be put at risk by the man who received the promise. Readers of Genesis have seen this before. Abraham told the same half-truth in Egypt, and Pharaoh took Sarah until God struck his house with plagues (12:10–20). Chapter 20 replays that story, and it is not flattering to Abraham. It shows a believer falling back into an old sin, and a God who keeps his promise anyway.

#### Abraham and Abimelech (vv1–18)
- **"Between Kadesh and Shur; and he sojourned in Gerar" (v1).** Kadesh (Kadesh-barnea) lies far south in the wilderness, and Shur is the desert on the road to Egypt (16:7). Gerar was a town in the western Negeb, inland from the coast near Gaza and roughly fifty miles southwest of Hebron. Many archaeologists identify it with Tel Haror, though that is not certain. "Sojourned" (*gur*) means living as a resident alien, someone with no land rights who depends on the goodwill of the local ruler. The text gives no reason for the move. Some suggest Abraham wanted fresh pasture, or to get away from the view of the ruined plain, but that is a guess.
- **"She is my sister" (v2).** No conversation is reported this time. In chapter 12 Abraham explained his plan to Sarah first, and here the narrator gives it in a single flat sentence, as if it has become routine. Verse 13 will confirm that it had. The Tyndale notes point out that Isaac will later tell the same lie to a king of Gerar also named Abimelech (26:1–11), probably having learned it at home.
- **Abimelech king of Gerar (v2).** The name means "my father is king." The Tyndale notes suggest it may have been a royal title in Gerar, like "Pharaoh" in Egypt, which would explain why an Abimelech still rules there in Isaac's day (26:1). Others think the two are father and son, or the same long-lived man. The text doesn't say. Genesis later links Gerar with the Philistines (21:32, 34; 26:1), but this chapter never uses that word.
- **Why take a 90-year-old woman? (v2).** The question is fair, and readers answer it in different ways. Some think God had renewed Sarah's body to bear Isaac, and her beauty with it. Many point out that royal marriages in the ancient Near East were often alliances: marrying the "sister" of a wealthy chieftain with hundreds of men (14:14) bound his household to the king. Both can be true. The narrator is not interested in her looks, only in the danger to the promise.
- **"God came to Abimelech in a dream by night" (v3).** God speaks directly to a ruler outside the covenant family. Dreams are how God often reveals himself in Genesis, to outsiders like Laban (31:24) and Pharaoh (ch 41) as well as to Jacob and Joseph. Through this whole chapter the narrator calls God *Elohim*, the general word for God, which suits a story set among people who do not know him by his covenant name. Only at the very end (v18) does the name "the LORD" (*YHWH*) appear, and it is the LORD who has been guarding Sarah all along.
- **"You are a dead man… for she is a man's wife" (v3).** God's opening sentence is a death sentence. Adultery was a capital crime in Israel's law (Lev 20:10), and the Tyndale notes add that it was treated as a grave offense across the ancient Near East. Several scholars have noted that Egyptian and Ugaritic texts call adultery "the great sin," the very phrase Abimelech uses in v9.
- **"Now Abimelech had not approached her" (v4).** The narrator settles the key fact before anything else is said. Sarah has not been touched. Whatever else happens, there can be no question about who fathers Isaac.
- **"Lord, will you kill an innocent people?" (v4).** The Hebrew is *goy gam tsaddiq*, literally "a nation, even a righteous one." This sounds like an echo of chapter 18, where Abraham asked, "Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?" (18:23). There Abraham pleaded for Sodom on the basis of God's justice. Here a pagan king pleads the same case, and this time the man of God is the one who has put an innocent people in danger. The Tyndale notes take the echo as a quiet rebuke of Abraham's lapse in faith.
- **"In the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands" (v5).** "Integrity" is *tom*, which the lexicon defines as completeness, innocence or integrity. "Integrity of heart" refers to motives, and "innocence of hands" refers to actions. Abimelech points out that both Abraham and Sarah told him the same story. He is not claiming to be sinless, only that he acted in good faith on what he was told.
- **"It was I who kept you from sinning against me" (v6).** God agrees with Abimelech's plea and then shows him something he did not know. His innocence was not entirely his own achievement. God himself held him back. The way God did it is explained in vv17–18: an affliction on the king's household that prevented the marriage from going any further. Note also that adultery against another man's wife is called sin "against me," against God. Joseph will say the same when tempted (39:9), and so will David after his sin (Ps 51:4).
- **"Return the man's wife, for he is a prophet" (v7).** This is the first time the word *nabi*, "prophet," appears in the Bible. The lexicon gives its basic sense as "spokesman." Here the role is not mainly predicting the future but standing between God and others in prayer. "Pray" here is *palal*, which the lexicon says means specifically to intercede on someone's behalf, and this is its first appearance in the Bible. Moses later intercedes in the same way for Miriam (Num 12:13), and Job for his friends (Job 42:8). The irony is sharp. The one man in the story who has acted wrongly is the one whose prayer the innocent king needs. Psalm 105 looks back on the patriarchs' wanderings and hears God telling kings, "Touch not my anointed ones, do my prophets no harm!" (Ps 105:14–15).
- **"Abimelech rose early in the morning" (v8).** He obeys at once, and he tells his whole court. "The men were very much afraid." In v11 Abraham will claim there was "no fear of God" in Gerar. The narrator has already shown otherwise.
- **"What have you done to us?" (v9).** It is the same question God asked Eve (3:13) and Pharaoh asked Abraham (12:18). A pagan king rebukes the father of the faithful, and he is right. "A great sin" (*chata'ah gedolah*) is how he describes what Abraham nearly brought on his kingdom. "Things that ought not to be done" is a phrase the Bible uses for deeds that break the basic moral order (compare 34:7). Abimelech knows right from wrong without having received any covenant.
- **"What did you see, that you did this thing?" (v10).** In other words, what had he observed in Gerar to justify this? The honest answer is nothing.
- **"There is no fear of God at all in this place" (v11).** Abraham's first excuse is an assumption he never checked. Having just watched Sodom burn, he may have expected every Canaanite town to be like it. Yet the king proved more God-fearing than Abraham expected, and in this moment more honest than Abraham. "The fear of God" will return at the height of Abraham's story, when the angel says, "now I know that you fear God" (22:12). Genesis is honest that the man who will pass that test failed this one.
- **"She is indeed my sister" (v12).** Abraham's second excuse is a technicality. Sarah was the daughter of his father Terah by a different mother. This is the only place the Bible tells us this. Such a marriage was later forbidden in Israel's law (Lev 18:9; 20:17), but it was before that law was given. Jewish tradition offered another explanation, identifying Sarah with Iscah, Abraham's niece (11:29), and taking "sister" loosely, but most readers follow v12 at face value. Either way, the statement was true in its words and false in its purpose. Abraham said "sister" so that Abimelech would conclude "not his wife," which is a lie.
- **"When God caused me to wander" (v13).** Abraham's third excuse is to explain that this had been the plan all along, "at every place to which we come," ever since leaving Haran. The verb is *ta'ah*, to wander or go astray. The lexicon uses it both for physical wandering, such as Hagar in the wilderness (21:14) or Joseph in the field (37:15), and for straying morally. It is an odd way to describe God's call, which in 12:1 was to go to "the land that I will show you." Oddly, the verb is plural, literally "God, they caused me to wander." The word for God (*Elohim*) is plural in form, and occasionally takes a plural verb, especially when someone is talking to people who worship many gods. Abraham may be speaking in terms Abimelech would understand, but it is still a strange, vague way to describe the God who had called him by name.
- **"This is the kindness you must do me" (v13).** The word for kindness is *chesed*, the Bible's great word for loyal, covenant love. Abraham uses it for asking his wife to put herself at risk for his safety. The word that will later describe God's faithfulness to Abraham's family (24:27) is used here for a cover story.
- **Sheep, oxen and servants (v14).** Abimelech returns Sarah and gives Abraham generous gifts, just as Pharaoh did (12:16). The wronged party pays the one who wronged him. Abraham once refused anything from the king of Sodom, "lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich'" (14:23). This time he takes the gifts, and the story does not comment.
- **"My land is before you; dwell where it pleases you" (v15).** Pharaoh expelled Abraham (12:19–20). Abimelech invites him to stay. The relationship begun here leads to the treaty and the well at Beersheba (21:22–34).
- **"A thousand pieces of silver" (v16).** This was a very large amount. These were not coins but weighed pieces of silver, probably shekels. A single shekel could be several days' wages, so this was a public, costly settlement. Notice Abimelech calls Abraham "your brother" when speaking to Sarah, which many readers hear as a pointed jab.
- **"A sign of your innocence in the eyes of all who are with you" (v16).** The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew as "a covering of eyes for all." The lexicon explains *kesut einayim* as a gift that covers the eyes so that others can no longer see the wrong, in other words compensation that settles the matter publicly. The last word, "vindicated," is a form of *yakach*, a verb whose range runs from "decide" and "prove right" to "rebuke." The ESV, along with most modern versions, reads it as Sarah being publicly cleared. The KJV's "thus she was reproved" reads it as a rebuke, which is possible within the word's range but fits the setting less well. The point of the payment is that no one in Abraham's camp or in Gerar can whisper about Sarah.
- **"Abraham prayed to God, and God healed Abimelech" (v17).** The intercession promised in v7 now happens, and it comes from a man who has just been rebuked. God does not set Abraham aside because of his failure. He uses Abraham's prayer, exactly as he told Abimelech he would. The healing restored the women of the household "so that they bore children."
- **"For the LORD had closed all the wombs" (v18).** This explains how God "kept" Abimelech from sin (v6): some affliction kept the household from normal family life, including the king. The Tyndale notes suggest this shows some time had passed, enough for childlessness in the household to be noticed. Scripture often presents God as the one who opens and closes the womb (29:31; 30:22; 1 Sam 1:5–6). The irony is plain: Abimelech's wives are healed so they can have children, while Sarah, barren all her life, is about to conceive. The chapter that endangered the promise ends with God's covenant name and with Sarah, "Abraham's wife," named one last time.
- **Why tell this story twice?** Some scholars think chapters 12, 20 and 26 are three versions of one old story. Others, and the Tyndale notes, read them as separate events, pointing out that v13 says this was Abraham's habit "at every place." The book as it stands presents them as separate events, and the repetition is part of the point. Abraham, who has just interceded so boldly for Sodom, slips back into the same fear that tripped him nearly twenty-five years earlier. Faith grows, but old sins can come back.
- **Commentators:** All three are frank about Abraham's sin. Henry opens: "The fairest marbles have their flaws," and notes that "a good man may, not only fall into sin, but relapse into the same sin." JFB is sterner: "His conduct was highly culpable. It was deceit, deliberate and premeditated," and adds that it was the second offense of its kind. Henry suggests Sarah may already have been pregnant, or about to be, which made the risk worse. The Tyndale notes place the episode shortly *before* she conceived (21:1–2). On v6 Henry writes, "It is not from him that there is sin, but it is from him that there is not more sin." On v7 he adds that "Ignorance will excuse no longer than it continues." Guzik points out that Abimelech is right and Abraham, the man of God, is wrong, and JFB says much the same: "What a dignified attitude is that of the king." On v11 JFB suggests that "From the horrible vices of Sodom" Abraham had assumed every city in Canaan was as corrupt. Henry draws a warning against judging others: "Men would not do ill if they did not first think ill." On v12 all three call the half-truth a lie in substance. JFB says Abraham "had told a moral untruth because there was an intention to deceive," and Guzik that a half-truth meant to deceive is a whole lie. On v13 Guzik passes on Barnhouse's claim that the verb "wander" occurs fifty times, is never used in a positive sense, and was the worst word Abraham could have chosen. The lexicon confirms the count of fifty, but several uses are plain physical wandering (21:14; 37:15; Exod 23:4), so it is not as dark a word as Barnhouse claims. Guzik also notes that Abraham now takes gifts he would once have refused (14:23). On v16 Henry reads the "covering of the eyes" as advice about marriage ("Yoke-fellows must be to each other for a covering of the eyes"), a moral lesson that goes beyond what the phrase means here. Guzik, citing *yakach*, allows that Sarah could have been both set right and shown right. Henry closes, "The prayers of good men may be a kindness to great men." JFB has no comments on vv4–8, 10 or 13–18.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter has no hero but God. Abraham fails, and the promise survives anyway, because God himself guards the mother of the promised line and makes sure no one can doubt whose son Isaac is. That same care runs through the generations to "the son of David, the son of Abraham" (Matt 1:1), and it shows that the promise always rested on God's faithfulness, not Abraham's. "If we are faithless, he remains faithful" (2 Tim 2:13). Abraham, a flawed prophet, prays for a man his own sin endangered, and God heals. Jesus is the prophet without flaw, who never lied to save himself (1 Pet 2:22) and who prayed even for those who wronged him: "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34).
