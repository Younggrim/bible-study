*Setting:* God is still speaking to Moses from the tent of meeting at Sinai. Chapter 4 set out the sin offering for unintentional sins, scaled from the high priest down to an ordinary Israelite. Chapter 5 finishes that law with some everyday cases and a cheaper option for the poor (vv1–13), then begins the fifth and last of the main offerings, the guilt offering, for wrongs that cost someone something (vv14–19). The guilt offering law runs on into 6:1–7. In Hebrew Bibles those seven verses are numbered 5:20–26, so the Hebrew chapter ends at a more natural place.

#### Laws for Sin Offerings (vv1–13)
- **"He hears a public adjuration to testify… yet does not speak" (v1).** When a crime was investigated, a public call could go out under oath for anyone who knew anything to come forward (compare Judg 17:2; Prov 29:24). Keeping quiet was not neutral. It let the guilty go free. **"He shall bear his iniquity"** means he carries the guilt and is liable for it. Justice in Israel depended on ordinary people telling the truth.
- **Touching something unclean (vv2–3).** Being "unclean" was not the same as sinning. It meant being temporarily unfit to come to the sanctuary, and chapters 11–15 explain the cases and how to be cleansed, usually by washing and waiting until evening. The problem here is **"it is hidden from him"**: the person did not realize and so did not deal with it, and the uncleanness spread. The Tyndale notes explain that the sin lay in neglecting it once known.
- **"A rash oath to do evil or to do good" (v4).** "Rash" means thoughtless, words blurted out with God's name attached. Jephthah's vow is the tragic example (Judg 11:30–31, 34–35). Scripture takes words spoken before God seriously: "It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not pay" (Eccl 5:5). Jesus later told his followers to let their yes be yes and their no be no (Matt 5:37).
- **"When he realizes his guilt… and confesses the sin he has committed" (v5).** This is the first time Leviticus says confession is required. "Confess" (*yadah* in the reflexive form) means to acknowledge openly. A sacrifice without owning the sin would be an empty ritual. Henry says confession must name the particular sin, because "deceit lies in generals." Compare David: "I acknowledged my sin to you… and you forgave the iniquity of my sin" (Ps 32:5).
- **"As his compensation" (v6).** The word is *'asham*, the same word used for the guilt offering in vv14–19. Here the ESV translates it "compensation," and the offering itself is clearly called a sin offering. The overlap is one reason scholars have long found this section hard to classify. It sits at the seam between the two offerings.
- **"But if he cannot afford a lamb… two turtledoves or two pigeons" (v7).** The first bird was a sin offering, its blood sprinkled on the altar. The second was a burnt offering (vv8–10). Taken together they did what one larger animal would have done. Mary brought two birds after Jesus' birth (Luke 2:22–24, following Lev 12:8), so Jesus grew up in a family that could not afford a lamb.
- **"A tenth of an ephah of fine flour" (v11).** About two quarts (two liters). For the very poor, God accepted flour in place of blood. Normally blood was required for atonement (17:11; Heb 9:22, "*almost* everything is purified with blood"). This exception shows how far God went so that no one was shut out. The Tyndale notes add that it was burned on top of the food offerings already on the altar (v12), so it was joined to the blood sacrifices burning there.
- **"He shall put no oil on it and… no frankincense" (v11).** This sets it apart from the grain offering of chapter 2, which was a joyful gift. A sin offering was not a celebration. Henry reads it as showing "how loathsome sin is."
- **Commentators:** Guzik applies v1 to Christian witness: God expects his people not only to avoid lies but to speak the truth when it matters, including the truth about Jesus. On v4 he lists the kind of vows believers often make and break, more prayer, more Bible reading, and calls for honest confession instead of quiet forgetting. Henry: "no man's poverty shall ever bar the way of his pardon." JFB says the confession in v5 was to be "a voluntary acknowledgment of his sin from the impulse of his own conscience," before anyone else found out. JFB has no separate note on v3.

#### Laws for Guilt Offerings (vv14–19)
- **"If anyone commits a breach of faith" (v15).** "Breach of faith" is *ma'al*, BDB "unfaithful, treacherous act." It is the word for betraying a trust, used of a wife's unfaithfulness (Num 5:12) and of Israel's unfaithfulness to God (Lev 26:40). Here it means misusing **"the holy things of the LORD,"** things set apart for God, such as eating holy food by mistake or holding back a tithe (22:14; 27:30–31).
- **"A guilt offering" (v15).** The word is *'asham*, "guilt," and also "compensation." BDB explains the difference from the sin offering. The sin offering dealt with sin as defilement and cleansed it at the altar. The guilt offering dealt with sin as a debt, for wrongs that could be measured and paid back. Some translations call it the "reparation offering," which captures the idea.
- **"A ram without blemish… valued in silver shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary" (v15).** The ram had a set value, measured by the standard weight kept at the sanctuary so no one could cheat. Some scholars think the worshiper could pay the equivalent in silver. A ram cost more than the female goat or lamb of the sin offering. There is no cheaper option here for the poor, since this was about paying a debt.
- **"He shall also make restitution… and shall add a fifth to it" (v16).** First he repays what he took or damaged, then he adds 20 percent, and then he brings the ram. Repentance in Leviticus is not only feeling sorry. It means repairing the damage. Zacchaeus went further than the law and offered to restore fourfold (Luke 19:8).
- **"Though he did not know it, then realizes his guilt, he shall bear his iniquity" (v17).** Guilt is real even before a person knows about it. Ignorance does not erase it. JFB applies this to doubtful cases "where conscience suspects" a wrong but is not sure. David prays, "Who can discern his errors? Declare me innocent from hidden faults" (Ps 19:12).
- **"He has indeed incurred guilt before the LORD" (v19).** The section ends by repeating the word *'asham* in a strong form, "he is truly guilty." Even a wrong done against property or another person is, first of all, a wrong done before God (compare Ps 51:4).
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on C. A. Coates's point that confession alone was not enough when a wrong had been done; it had to be put right. He also says plainly that ignorance can itself be sin. Henry says that because God's law is broad and our hearts are prone to wander, believers need to "fear always, and to pray always." JFB describes the guilt offering as for those who "had either done evil unwittingly, or were in doubt as to their own criminality."

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah describes the suffering Servant making "his soul an offering for guilt" (Isa 53:10), using this very word, *'asham*. BDB comments that the Servant offers himself as compensation for the sins of the people, standing in their place. Christ paid the debt that sin owes to God, a debt no one could repay. And the gospel keeps this chapter's practical edge. Forgiven people still go back and set things right, as Jesus said: "first be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift" (Matt 5:24).
