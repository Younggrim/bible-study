*Setting:* Chapter 9 ended with a parable and a warning: a poor wise man saved a city and was forgotten, and "one sinner destroys much good" (9:18). Chapter 10 picks up that last line and runs with it. It reads more like Proverbs than any other chapter in the book: short, punchy sayings about how folly spoils wisdom, how foolish people end up in power, how ordinary work carries risk, how fools talk too much, and how a land rises or falls with its leaders. The ESV keeps the whole chapter under the heading that began in 9:11. The sayings are loosely grouped, not tightly argued, and the theme that holds them together is that wisdom is better than folly, but wisdom is fragile and folly is everywhere. For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### Wisdom Better Than Folly (vv1–20)
- **"Dead flies make the perfumer's ointment give off a stench" (v1).** In the ancient world perfumed oil was costly, carefully blended and used for anointing, burial and celebration. A few flies drowned in the jar would rot and ruin the whole batch. The verse continues the thought of 9:18. One small thing ruins something large and precious.
- **"So a little folly outweighs wisdom and honor" (v1).** "Outweighs" is literally "is heavier than" or "is weightier than." A lifetime of good sense can be undone in a moment. Scripture has many examples: Moses striking the rock (Num 20:10–12), David with Bathsheba (2 Sam 11), Solomon himself turning to other gods in old age (1 Kgs 11:4). Guzik passes on Kidner's list of prizes lost in one reckless moment, among them Esau's birthright. JFB adds that "the more delicate the perfume, the more easily spoiled is the ointment." The more honored the person, the more a lapse costs.
- **"A wise man's heart inclines him to the right, but a fool's heart to the left" (v2).** The right hand in Scripture is the side of strength, skill and favor (Ps 16:8; 110:1; Matt 25:33). The left is the weaker side. The proverb has nothing to do with left-handed people or modern politics. It means the wise person's inner bent leads him toward what is good and skillful, and the fool's leads him astray. "Heart" in Hebrew is the center of thinking and choosing, not just feeling.
- **"Even when the fool walks on the road, he lacks sense, and he says to everyone that he is a fool" (v3).** The fool cannot even manage a walk down the road without showing what he is. The Hebrew could mean he announces that everyone else is a fool (the Latin Vulgate takes it so, and JFB mentions it). The ESV reads it as his behavior giving him away. Either way, folly does not stay hidden. Henry says the fool shows his folly to everyone he meets as plainly as if he had said so.
- **"If the anger of the ruler rises against you, do not leave your place" (v4).** This repeats advice from 8:3. Do not quit in a rage or storm out when the boss is angry. "Your place" means your post or position. Stay, keep calm and keep doing your work.
- **"For calmness will lay great offenses to rest" (v4).** "Calmness" (*marpe*) is the word for healing, as in "a gentle tongue is a tree of life" (Prov 15:4, where the same word stands behind "gentle"). A calm response heals a tense situation. Proverbs says it directly: "A soft answer turns away wrath" (Prov 15:1). Abigail calming David's anger is a picture of it (1 Sam 25:23–35).
- **"An error proceeding from the ruler" (v5).** The word for "error" (*shegagah*) is the one used in the law for unintentional sins (Lev 4:2). The Preacher is not accusing every ruler of malice. He is describing what happens when those in power make bad appointments, whether by carelessness or worse. The Tyndale notes name favoritism, nepotism, extortion and bribery as ways reckless people rise to power.
- **"Folly is set in many high places, and the rich sit in a low place" (v6).** "The rich" here seems to mean the capable and noble, those with the means and standing to lead well. JFB takes it as the wise and generous, and gives Haman raised above Mordecai as an example (Esth 3:1–2).
- **"I have seen slaves on horses, and princes walking on the ground like slaves" (v7).** Horses were for the powerful and wealthy, and a king's officials rode them. The picture is a world turned upside down. Proverbs says it is not fitting "for a slave to rule over princes" (Prov 19:10), and Proverbs 30:21–22 lists "a slave when he becomes king" among things the earth cannot bear. The point is not that people of low birth should never rise. Joseph rose from slavery to rule Egypt. The point is that putting the unqualified in charge is a disaster. JFB points to David leaving Jerusalem on foot while Absalom's men took over (2 Sam 15:30).
- **"He who digs a pit will fall into it" (v8).** A pit could be a trap for animals or for an enemy. The saying appears elsewhere as a moral proverb: the one who sets a trap for others gets caught in it (Ps 7:15; Prov 26:27). Haman was hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai (Esth 7:10). In this context it may also be simply practical: digging is dangerous work.
- **"A serpent will bite him who breaks through a wall" (v8).** Stone walls in Israel were built of loosely fitted stones, and snakes nested in the cracks. Someone tearing down a wall could put his hand on one. Amos uses the same picture: a man "leaned his hand against the wall, and a serpent bit him" (Amos 5:19).
- **"He who quarries stones is hurt by them, and he who splits logs is endangered by them" (v9).** Ordinary work has real dangers. The law even made provision for a man killed by a flying axe head (Deut 19:5). The Tyndale notes say wisdom takes safety precautions, but some risk always remains. JFB reads all of vv8–9 morally: those who violate wisdom find it "recoil on the perpetrators."
- **"If the iron is blunt, and one does not sharpen the edge, he must use more strength, but wisdom helps one to succeed" (v10).** This is one of the most practical verses in the book. Working with a dull axe means more effort and less result. Taking time to sharpen it is wisdom. "Helps one to succeed" is literally "has the advantage of making successful." The word for "advantage" (*yitron*) is the book's key word for "gain" or "profit" (1:3). Here, finally, is something with real profit: skill. JFB connects blunt force with Rehoboam's rash choice to rule harshly (1 Kgs 12:13–14).
- **"If the serpent bites before it is charmed, there is no advantage to the charmer" (v11).** Snake charmers were known in the ancient Near East (Ps 58:4–5; Jer 8:17). The "charmer" is literally "the master of the tongue," which leads neatly into the next section on speech. Skill used too late is no use. Again the word is *yitron*, "advantage." Wisdom has to be applied at the right time.
- **"The words of a wise man's mouth win him favor, but the lips of a fool consume him" (v12).** "Win him favor" is literally "are grace." Wise words are gracious and bring goodwill. A fool's own lips "swallow him up," the same verb used of the earth swallowing Korah (Num 16:30). His mouth destroys him. The Tyndale notes list foolish promises, false testimony and angering the wrong people as ways a fool's words ruin him.
- **"The beginning of the words of his mouth is foolishness, and the end of his talk is evil madness" (v13).** His talk gets worse as it goes. It starts as nonsense and ends as something harmful.
- **"A fool multiplies words, though no man knows what is to be" (v14).** The fool talks endlessly, especially about the future, as if he knows it. But no one does (6:12; 8:7). James says the same to merchants making confident plans: "You do not know what tomorrow will bring" (James 4:13–16). Proverbs: "When words are many, transgression is not lacking" (Prov 10:19).
- **"The toil of a fool wearies him, for he does not know the way to the city" (v15).** The road to the city would be the most obvious road there is. The fool wears himself out and cannot even find it. Guzik passes on Maclaren's note that this was a proverb for missing the obvious. JFB reads the city spiritually as the heavenly city, an application beyond the plain sense but echoed in Hebrews 11:16.
- **"Woe to you, O land, when your king is a child" (v16).** "Child" is *na'ar*, a boy or young man, but also the word for a servant. It describes immaturity more than age. JFB takes it as one given to pleasure and "childish frivolity." Guzik, citing Eaton, agrees it is about maturity. Solomon himself, newly king, prayed, "I am but a little child. I do not know how to go out or come in," and asked for wisdom (1 Kgs 3:7–9). His son Rehoboam, forty-one when he took the throne, acted like a child anyway (1 Kgs 12; 14:21).
- **"And your princes feast in the morning" (v16).** Morning was for work and for judging cases (Jer 21:12). Officials who start drinking at breakfast neglect their duties. Isaiah pronounced woe on "those who rise early in the morning, that they may run after strong drink" (Isa 5:11).
- **"Happy are you, O land, when your king is the son of the nobility" (v17).** "Son of the nobility" is literally "son of free men," someone raised to lead with dignity and responsibility. JFB says the point is noble character, not just birth. His princes eat "at the proper time, for strength, and not for drunkenness." The Tyndale notes say wise leaders relax after the work is done, not before it starts.
- **"Through sloth the roof sinks in, and through indolence the house leaks" (v18).** Houses had flat roofs of beams covered with packed earth, which had to be rolled and repaired after the rains. Neglect them and the beams sag and the rain comes in. The proverb works for a household and for a kingdom. The Tyndale notes and JFB both apply it to government corruption.
- **"Bread is made for laughter, and wine gladdens life, and money answers everything" (v19).** In context this may be the leaders' attitude: feasting and money solve all problems. JFB reads it as rulers buying favor and taxing heavily while the house falls in. It may also be a plain observation: money gets things done in this world (7:12). The Tyndale notes take it that way. Either way, "answers everything" is true only "under the sun." Money "will neither feed nor clothe" of itself, Henry says, and it cannot buy what matters most (5:10; Matt 16:26).
- **"Even in your thoughts, do not curse the king" (v20).** Even private grumbling is dangerous, because thoughts become words and words travel. "In your bedroom" is the most private room in the house. The king of Syria learned that Elisha could tell Israel's king "the words that you speak in your bedroom" (2 Kgs 6:12).
- **"For a bird of the air will carry your voice" (v20).** Our saying "a little bird told me" has a long history, and Guzik passes on Eaton's note that versions of it turn up in many cultures. Secrets get out. JFB applies it further to God, who knows every thought, and Guzik makes the same turn: if a king may find out, how much more the Lord (Ps 139:4; Luke 12:2–3).

- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv4–11 as teaching subjects to stay loyal and dutiful even when rulers err, and on v4 advises, "wait awhile, and thou wilt find that yielding pacifies great offences." He warns that those "who make a profession of religion, should keep from all appearances of evil," since their lapses are noticed most. On the fool's talk: "The babbler's tongue is an unruly evil, full of deadly poison" (compare James 3:8), and on v16, "the people cannot be happy when their princes are childish, and lovers of pleasure." Guzik passes on Meyer's application of the dull axe: a worn-out worker should seek God's strength rather than simply push harder. Through Kidner he links the fool who cannot find the city to people "always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (2 Tim 3:7), and he turns v20 toward God: if a king may find out a secret, how much more the Lord. JFB reads vv8–11 morally, as schemes that "recoil on the perpetrators," and applies the way to the city to the heavenly city.

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 10 is full of what goes wrong when the wrong people rule: fools in high places, childish kings, princes drinking at dawn, a roof sinking in. Israel learned it the hard way, beginning with Solomon's own son. The longing behind "Happy are you, O land, when your king is the son of the nobility" (v17) is answered in the King of whom Isaiah said, "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him, the Spirit of wisdom and understanding" (Isa 11:2). He "came not to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45), was never idle at his Father's work (John 5:17), and his words were gracious (Luke 4:22), the very mark of the wise in v12. He also turned v7's upside-down world on its head in a good way. The Prince walked the ground like a slave, took "the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7), and rode into his city on a borrowed donkey, so that the lowly might be raised up with him.
