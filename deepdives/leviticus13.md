*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai. Chapters 11–15 teach the priests "to distinguish… between the unclean and the clean" (10:10). After food (ch 11) and childbirth (ch 12), chapters 13–14 deal with *tsara'at*, usually translated "leprosy" or "leprous disease." Matthew Henry remarked that hardly anything in the whole Levitical law takes up so much room. Chapter 13 is the diagnosis: how a priest decides whether a person, or even a piece of cloth, is unclean. Chapter 14 is the cure: how someone healed is brought back.

#### Laws About Leprosy (vv1–59)
- **What was *tsara'at*?** The Hebrew word covers more than modern leprosy (Hansen's disease). BDB defines it simply as "leprosy," but the chapter applies it to a range of skin conditions: swellings, scaly patches, infected boils and burns, scalp disease, and even mold on cloth (vv47–59) and walls (14:33–53). BDB calls the meaning in cloth and houses "dubious, perhaps some fungus or mould." The Greek Old Testament translated it *lepra*, which in Greek meant a condition "which makes the skin scaly," and that is where our word "leprosy" comes from. Most scholars think classic Hansen's disease, which attacks nerves and causes numbness and disfigurement, is not what these symptoms describe, though it may have been included later. That is why newer translations often say "a serious skin disease." The ESV keeps "leprous disease" but makes clear the term was broad.
- **"He shall be brought to Aaron the priest" (v2).** The priest was not a doctor. He did not treat the condition. His job was to judge whether a person could come near God's sanctuary and live among the people. A priest's word, "clean" or "unclean," decided whether someone stayed in the camp.
- **The two key signs (v3).** The priest looked for **hair turned white** in the patch and whether it seemed **"deeper than the skin,"** probably sunken or reaching below the surface. Both together meant a true case.
- **"The priest shall shut up the diseased person for seven days" (vv4–8).** When signs were unclear, the person was isolated and checked again after a week, then after another week if needed. The rule was patience, not snap judgment. If the patch faded and stopped spreading, **"it is only an eruption,"** a rash. The person washed his clothes and was clean. If it spread, he was unclean. Spreading was the decisive sign throughout the chapter.
- **"A chronic leprous disease" (vv9–11).** A white swelling, white hair and **"raw flesh"** meant an established case. No waiting period was needed.
- **"It has all turned white, and he is clean" (vv12–17).** This is one of the most puzzling rules in Leviticus. If the condition covered the whole body, the person was clean. But **"when raw flesh appears on him, he shall be unclean"** (v14). The most likely explanation is that a condition turning the whole skin white and flaking had run its course and was no longer active, while raw, open flesh meant active disease. The Tyndale notes suggest the white may mean lost pigment, which is harmless. What made someone unclean was not how much skin was affected but whether the condition was open and spreading.
- **Boils and burns (vv18–28).** An old boil or a burn could become infected. The same signs applied: white hair and depth meant unclean, while a patch that **"remains in one place and does not spread"** was only **"the scar."** The law kept healed people from being wrongly shut out.
- **Disease of the head or beard (vv29–37).** Here the sign was **"yellow and thin"** hair, probably a scaly fungal infection like ringworm hidden by the hair. The person shaved around the patch, though **"the itch he shall not shave"** (v33), so the priest could watch its edges. **Black hair** growing back meant healing (v37).
- **Harmless conditions (vv38–41).** **"Dull white"** spots are **"leukoderma… he is clean"** (v39). Ordinary baldness, at the back or the front of the head, is **"clean"** (vv40–41). The law took care to say what was *not* a problem as well as what was. Only a **"reddish-white"** sore on a bald head, perhaps an infection, made someone unclean (vv42–44).
- **"He shall wear torn clothes… cover his upper lip and cry out, 'Unclean, unclean'" (v45).** These were the signs of mourning (10:6; Ezek 24:17). The person with *tsara'at* lived as if in permanent grief, mourning a kind of living death. Covering the lip and crying out warned others to keep their distance and not become unclean themselves. When Miriam was struck with this condition, Aaron pleaded, "Let her not be as one dead" (Num 12:12).
- **"He shall live alone. His dwelling shall be outside the camp" (v46).** The camp was arranged around God's dwelling. To be outside it was to be cut off from worship, family and community, for as long as the disease lasted. The same place outside the camp received the dead (10:4–5), sin offerings to be burned (4:12) and executions (24:14). Later in Israel's history, lepers lived on the edges of towns (2 Kings 7:3), and King Uzziah, struck for entering the temple to burn incense, lived "in a separate house" until he died (2 Chr 26:19–21).
- **Mold in cloth and leather (vv47–59).** *Tsara'at* could also appear as **"greenish or reddish"** patches in wool, linen or leather. The steps match those for people: isolate the item for a week, wash it, examine it again. If the mark spread, the item was **burned**. If it faded, the spot was torn out. If it never came back, the item was washed again and was clean. In Israel's damp winter rainy season, mold on fabric and leather was a real problem.
- **Is *tsara'at* a picture of sin?** The chapter itself is about a physical condition, and those who had it were not necessarily guilty of anything. Job suffered skin disease while blameless (Job 2:7). At the same time, the Old Testament sometimes sends this condition as judgment, on Miriam (Num 12:10), Gehazi (2 Kings 5:27) and Uzziah (2 Chr 26:19), and Christians have long seen in it a vivid picture of sin. Sin also starts small, spreads, defiles and cuts us off from God and from others. The picture can teach without suggesting that a sick person is a worse sinner (John 9:2–3).
- **Jesus and the lepers.** By Jesus' day, rabbis sometimes treated healing a leper as being as hard as raising the dead. Jesus did not cry "unclean" and back away. "Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him," and instead of Jesus becoming unclean, the man became clean (Matt 8:2–3). Then Jesus sent him to "show yourself to the priest" (Matt 8:4), the priest of Leviticus 13–14, as proof. Jesus also healed ten lepers who "stood at a distance," just as verse 45 required (Luke 17:12–14).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that priests, not physicians, judged leprosy, because it was a matter of ceremonial cleanness. He reads it as a picture of sin's defilement: the priest "could only convict the leper," but Christ cleanses. On the puzzling vv12–13 he offers a doctor's analogy from his own time: a rash that "comes out well" all over, like smallpox, shows the body throwing off the disease, and spiritually, sin openly confessed is less dangerous than sin hidden. JFB likewise says a condition that spread over the whole body without open sores "lost its contagious power by degrees." It thinks the disease's roots "must have been laid in Egypt." Guzik sees the priests as something like public health officers using careful criteria. He draws a detailed comparison between leprosy and sin, which both begin small, grow slowly and numb a person to their own condition. His description assumes Hansen's disease, a narrower view than the Tyndale notes, which stress how broad the Hebrew term is.

#### Pointing to Christ
The priest in Leviticus 13 could look, wait, and pronounce, but he could not heal. All he could do was say who had to live outside the camp. Jesus is the priest who touches the unclean and makes them clean. Hebrews adds a final turn: "Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood. Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured" (Heb 13:12–13). He went to the place of the unclean so that the unclean could be brought home.
