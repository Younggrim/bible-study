*Setting:* More of the father's talks in Proverbs 1–9 (see Proverbs 1 on authorship). Chapter 5 warned against the forbidden woman and celebrated faithful married love. Chapter 6 opens with something different: four short warnings about rash debts, laziness, troublemakers and the things God hates (vv1–19). These read more like the short proverbs later in the book. Then the father returns to adultery (vv20–35), this time with the jealous husband in view. The chapter is a reminder that wisdom covers everything from money and work to speech and sex, the ordinary places where life goes right or wrong.

#### Practical Warnings (vv1–19)
- **"If you have put up security for your neighbor, have given your pledge for a stranger" (v1).** "Security" means guaranteeing someone else's debt, what we would call cosigning. "Given your pledge" is literally "struck your palms," the handshake that sealed a deal (17:18; 22:26). The "stranger" may be an outsider, or the same neighbor described from a distance. Proverbs warns against this repeatedly (11:15; 17:18; 20:16; 22:26–27; 27:13). The danger is promising what you cannot control: another person's ability or willingness to pay.
- **"If you are snared in the words of your mouth" (v2).** The repeated phrase shows that a few rash words can trap a person. Promises are binding (Num 30:2; Eccl 5:4–6).
- **"Go, hasten, and plead urgently with your neighbor" (v3).** "You have come into the hand of your neighbor" means the man you vouched for now holds your fate. The father's advice is to swallow pride and get released. "Plead urgently" pairs two strong verbs: humble yourself, perhaps literally "let yourself be trampled," and "press," "badger." Guzik, following the Puritan Matthew Poole, reads it as offering yourself to be trodden on. Do whatever it takes.
- **"Give your eyes no sleep… save yourself like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter" (vv4–5).** Don't sleep on it. Escape fast, the way a trapped animal fights to get free. There is an irony coming: the next verse turns to the person who sleeps too much.
- **Is cosigning always wrong?** Israel's law encouraged generous lending to the poor without interest (Exod 22:25; Deut 15:7–11), so the issue is not generosity. It is binding yourself to a debt whose outcome you cannot control, often for someone you do not know well. Judah pledged himself as security for Benjamin out of love and was ready to pay with his own freedom (Gen 43:9; 44:32–33). Proverbs warns against foolish pledges, not against costly love.
- **"Go to the ant, O sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise" (v6).** "Sluggard" (*ʿatsel*) is one of Proverbs' great comic characters, the lazy man (19:24; 22:13; 26:13–16). He is sent to school with an insect. Harvester ants are common in Israel and gather seeds into underground stores. Agur also praises the ants as "a people not strong, yet they provide their food in the summer" (30:25).
- **"Without having any chief, officer, or ruler" (v7).** No one makes the ant work. The lesson is self-motivation: doing what needs to be done without a boss watching. Modern study of ants agrees. A colony has a queen, but she does not give orders. The work is organized without a chain of command.
- **"She prepares her bread in summer and gathers her food in harvest" (v8).** The ant plans ahead. Wisdom sees the winter coming while it is still summer (10:5).
- **"How long will you lie there, O sluggard?" (v9).** The father mimics the sluggard's mumble in v10: **"A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest."** Each request sounds small and reasonable. The disaster is built out of littles. The same lines appear in 24:33–34.
- **"Poverty will come upon you like a robber, and want like an armed man" (v11).** "Robber" is literally "one who walks about," a vagrant or highwayman. "Armed man" is literally "a man with a shield." Poverty does not knock. It arrives suddenly and can't be fought off.
- **"A worthless person, a wicked man" (v12).** "Worthless person" is *ʾadam beliyyaʿal*, literally "a man of Belial," "a man of no worth." The word became a name for wickedness itself, and Paul uses "Belial" as a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15). He goes about with **"crooked speech."**
- **"Winks with his eyes, signals with his feet, points with his finger" (v13).** He speaks in code: a wink, a shuffle, a gesture, signals to his partners behind someone's back (10:10; Ps 35:19). JFB says such signs were still used in the East in his day. Body language can lie as well as words.
- **"With perverted heart devises evil, continually sowing discord" (v14).** It all starts in the heart (4:23). His real product is "discord," quarrels between people. The word returns as the climax of the next list (v19).
- **"Calamity will come upon him suddenly; in a moment he will be broken beyond healing" (v15).** He who broke others is broken. God is not named, but the sudden ruin implies his judgment.
- **"There are six things that the LORD hates, seven that are an abomination to him" (v16).** This is a common Hebrew pattern, "x, and x+1," that introduces a list and often puts weight on the last item (30:15–31; Amos 1:3–2:6; Job 5:19). "Abomination" (*toʿevah*) means something God finds detestable.
- **The list moves down the body (vv17–19).** **"Haughty eyes"** (literally "eyes raised high," pride, which comes first, Ps 101:5). **"A lying tongue."** **"Hands that shed innocent blood"**, the gang of 1:11–16. **"A heart that devises wicked plans."** **"Feet that make haste to run to evil"** (1:16). Eyes, tongue, hands, heart, feet: from head to foot, the whole person can be turned to evil. Then two people complete the seven: **"a false witness who breathes out lies"** (in court, breaking the ninth commandment, Exod 20:16) and **"one who sows discord among brothers."**
- **Why discord comes last.** It ties back to v14 and is probably the point of the list. All the other sins feed it: pride, lies, violence, schemes. And it destroys community, the "brothers" who should live together in unity (Ps 133:1). Henry: "The God of love and peace hates him that sows discord among brethren, for he delights in concord." Notice that two of the seven are about lying. Henry again: "lying involves two of them; he hates it, and doubly hates it."
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls rash suretyship folly but does not say all cosigning is sin. He notes that Paul offered to cover what Onesimus already owed, a past debt, not an open-ended future one (Philem 18–19). He follows Bruce Waltke's count of at least fourteen proverbs that tie laziness to poverty. On the list of seven, Guzik follows G. Campbell Morgan in seeing discord as the climax, the result of all the others. He also notes that most items are parts of the body, recalling Paul's call not to present our members to sin as instruments of unrighteousness (Rom 6:13). JFB says the sluggard is one who often needs sureties, which ties the first two warnings together. He glosses the "worthless person" as "a man of Belial," and says the six-and-seven form is a way of speaking meant to arrest attention.

#### Warnings Against Adultery (vv20–35)
- **"Keep your father's commandment, and forsake not your mother's teaching" (v20).** This is almost word for word the opening of the first talk (1:8). Both parents speak.
- **"Bind them on your heart always; tie them around your neck" (v21).** The same picture as 3:3 and Deuteronomy 6:8.
- **"When you walk… when you lie down… and when you awake, they will talk with you" (v22).** This echoes Deuteronomy 6:7: talk of God's words "when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise." The parents' teaching becomes a companion that guides by day, guards by night and talks first thing in the morning.
- **"For the commandment is a lamp and the teaching a light" (v23).** Psalm 119:105: "Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path." **"The reproofs of discipline are the way of life."** Even correction is a road to life.
- **"To preserve you from the evil woman, from the smooth tongue of the adulteress" (v24).** The purpose of all that teaching is protection. Again her weapon is her speech (2:16; 5:3).
- **"Do not desire her beauty in your heart, and do not let her capture you with her eyelashes" (v25).** The battle begins in the heart and the eyes. Jesus makes the same point: "everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matt 5:28). The tenth commandment forbids coveting a neighbor's wife (Exod 20:17). "Eyelashes" may refer to the painted eyes of ancient cosmetics, or simply to flirtatious glances.
- **"The price of a prostitute is only a loaf of bread, but a married woman hunts down a precious life" (v26).** The Hebrew of the first line is compressed and has been read in different ways, but the contrast is clear. Visiting a prostitute costs money and leaves a man poor. Adultery with another man's wife may cost his very life. This is not approval of prostitution. Proverbs condemns it elsewhere (23:27; 29:3). It is an argument from the greater danger.
- **"Can a man carry fire next to his chest and his clothes not be burned?" (v27).** Two plain questions: fire in the lap, bare feet on hot coals (v28). Of course he gets burned. There is a play on sound here. "Fire" is *ʾesh*, and "man" and "woman" (*ʾish*, *ʾishshah*) sound close to it. Some consequences are built into the act itself.
- **"None who touches her will go unpunished" (v29).** "Unpunished" (*naqah*) means held innocent. No one who does this is innocent, however he explains it.
- **"People do not despise a thief if he steals to satisfy his appetite when he is hungry" (v30).** The father draws a comparison. Even a desperate thief is punished, **"he will pay sevenfold"** (v31). The law required double, fourfold or fivefold repayment (Exod 22:1–4). "Sevenfold" here means in full, whatever it costs, "all the goods of his house." But at least a thief can repay. Stolen bread can be replaced.
- **"He who commits adultery lacks sense; he who does it destroys himself" (v32).** "Lacks sense" is literally "lacks heart." The adulterer has no excuse of hunger, and what he takes cannot be given back. He "destroys himself," his own soul.
- **"Wounds and dishonor, and his disgrace will not be wiped away" (v33).** The shame lasts. Even after forgiveness, consequences remain. David's family bore the effects of his sin with Bathsheba for years (2 Sam 12:10–14).
- **"For jealousy makes a man furious, and he will not spare when he takes revenge" (v34).** The final reason is the wronged husband. Jealousy here is a husband's fierce, rightful anger at the betrayal of his marriage.
- **"He will accept no compensation" (v35).** Literally "he will not lift up the face of any ransom." Under the law adultery could bring the death penalty (Lev 20:10; Deut 22:22), and a husband might accept a payment instead. This one will not, however many gifts are offered. The thief can repay sevenfold. The adulterer may find there is no price high enough.
- **Commentators:** Guzik uses the fire and coals to argue that the man who touches another's wife cannot claim to be innocent, and he presses the comparison with theft: a thief can make restitution, but an adulterer cannot really undo what he has done. He also stresses that adultery damages the soul even if no one finds out. JFB on v26 prefers the reading "The price of a whore is a piece of bread," and says the adulteress aims at "precious life." He also takes "sevenfold" to mean "ample restitution." The Tyndale notes say that "sevenfold" is more than the law required, which supports the reading of it as full repayment.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter opens with a man who has pledged himself for another's debt and closes with a debt no payment can cover. Both point to the gospel. Jesus became "the guarantor of a better covenant" (Heb 7:22), the Greek word for a surety. Like Judah offering himself in Benjamin's place, he pledged himself for a debt he did not owe, and he paid it in full. Where the wronged husband refuses every ransom, the wronged God provides the ransom himself (Mark 10:45). The God who hates the one who sows discord sent the Peacemaker, who broke down "the dividing wall of hostility" (Eph 2:14) and makes brothers out of enemies.
