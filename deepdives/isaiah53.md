*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC, promising a new exodus and a God who comes home to Zion. Running through these chapters is a mysterious figure called "my servant." In earlier songs he brings justice to the nations (42:1–9), is called to restore Israel and be a light to the Gentiles (49:1–13), and takes beatings and spitting without turning away (50:4–11). The fourth and last song began at 52:13 with the Servant exalted, then horribly disfigured, then sprinkling many nations. Chapter 53 is the rest of that poem, and the most quoted Old Testament passage in the New Testament on the death of Jesus. A complete scroll of Isaiah found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied more than a century before Jesus was born, contains this chapter almost exactly as it stands in our Bibles. Most of the chapter is spoken by "we," people looking back in shock at how wrong they were about him.

#### He Was Pierced for Our Transgressions, continued (vv1–12)
- **The shape of the poem.** The Tyndale notes describe 52:13–53:12 as five stanzas of three verses: the Servant exalted (52:13–15); despised (53:1–3); bearing our sins (vv4–6); silent, condemned and buried (vv7–9); and vindicated (vv10–12). The middle stanza, vv4–6, is the heart of it, and the heart of that stanza is v5.
- **"Who has believed what he has heard from us?" (v1).** "What he has heard from us" is one Hebrew word meaning "report" or "message," the thing heard. Who is "us"? Some think the prophet speaks for a believing remnant. Others hear the nations' kings from 52:15 still speaking. Most likely it is Isaiah speaking with and for Israel, confessing that they did not believe. John quotes this verse to explain why so many rejected Jesus despite his signs (John 12:37–38). Paul quotes it to show that not all accepted the good news (Rom 10:16), right after quoting the "beautiful feet" of 52:7.
- **"To whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?" (v1).** God's arm has been the hope of these chapters: it rules (40:10), it will judge the peoples (51:5), and the people begged it to wake up (51:9). In 52:10 God bared it before all nations. Now comes the shock: the mighty arm of the LORD is this weak, rejected Servant. Paul says God chose what is weak to shame the strong (1 Cor 1:27).
- **"He grew up before him like a young plant, and like a root out of dry ground" (v2).** "Young plant" is a suckling shoot, a sprout that springs up from a stump. Isaiah has already promised "a shoot from the stump of Jesse" and "the root of Jesse" (11:1, 10), a king from David's family after the family tree has been cut down. Dry ground is the worst place for a plant: hard, unpromising, likely to kill it. Jesus grew up in Nazareth, a village of no reputation (John 1:46), in a land under Roman rule, in a family of modest means (Luke 2:24 shows his parents giving the poor family's offering).
- **"He had no form or majesty that we should look at him" (v2).** Kings were expected to look the part. Saul stood head and shoulders above everyone (1 Sam 9:2), and David was handsome (1 Sam 16:12). Nothing about the Servant's appearance would draw a crowd. The Gospels never describe Jesus' looks at all.
- **"Despised and rejected by men" (v3).** "Rejected" can mean "lacking men," forsaken or shunned by others. People of importance kept away from him. **"A man of sorrows and acquainted with grief."** The words are "pains" (*makʾobot*) and "sickness" (*choli*), which the ESV footnotes. JFB notes that Jesus is never said to have been ill himself. Rather, he knew pain and sickness closely because he spent his life among the sick and took their burdens on himself, as v4 will say. Being a man of sorrows does not mean he was never joyful (Luke 10:21; John 15:11).
- **"As one from whom men hide their faces" (v3).** People turned away from lepers and from the badly disfigured (52:14). The line ends with the confession: "we esteemed him not." We did not think he was worth anything.
- **"Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows" (v4).** The same two words from v3 come back: sicknesses and pains. But now they are "*ours*." He knew grief because he picked up ours. "Borne" (*nasaʾ*) and "carried" (*sabal*) are words for lifting and hauling a heavy load. Matthew quotes this line after Jesus heals crowds of the sick: "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases" (Matt 8:16–17). Peter applies the next verse to sin (1 Pet 2:24). Both belong together. Sickness is one fruit of the fall, and Jesus' healings were a preview of the full healing the cross wins. Guzik notes that the complete healing of the body waits for the resurrection, so this verse does not promise any believer perfect health now.
- **"Yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted" (v4).** This is what the onlookers assumed. Anyone who suffered like that must be under God's curse for something he did. Job's friends thought the same about Job. "Stricken" (*nagua*) is the word used for being struck with a disease, especially leprosy (Lev 13). At the cross, passers-by mocked Jesus on exactly these terms: "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (Matt 27:43). They were right that God was involved. They were wrong about why.
- **"But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities" (v5).** "He" and "our" are both emphatic in Hebrew. "Pierced" (*mecholal*) means wounded through, a fatal wound (51:9 uses it of piercing the dragon). "Crushed" (*dakaʾ*) means beaten to pieces, the same verb as v10. Zechariah speaks of the people looking "on me, on him whom they have pierced" (Zech 12:10), and John sees that fulfilled when the soldier pierced Jesus' side (John 19:34–37).
- **"Upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace" (v5).** "Chastisement" (*musar*) is discipline or punishment. The punishment that produced our *shalom*, our wholeness and our peace with God, fell on him. Paul: "we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 5:1). JFB reads *musar* as a parent's discipline rather than a judge's penalty, on the grounds that the Servant was guiltless. Most commentators take it more strongly, as the penalty that should have fallen on us.
- **"With his wounds we are healed" (v5).** "Wounds" is singular in Hebrew, *chaburah*, the welt or bruise left by a whip (KJV "stripes"). It points to a beating like the Roman flogging Jesus received (John 19:1). Peter, writing to Christian slaves who were themselves beaten unjustly, quotes this: "By his wounds you have been healed. For you were straying like sheep" (1 Pet 2:24–25).
- **"All we like sheep have gone astray" (v6).** The verse begins and ends with "all": "all we… the iniquity of us all." Sheep wander off not out of malice but by drifting after the next patch of grass, and they cannot find their way back. **"We have turned, every one, to his own way."** Sin is a shared condition and also personal. Each person chooses a road of his own.
- **"The LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (v6).** The verb is *pagaʿ*, "to meet" or "to fall upon," and in this form "to cause to land on." God made the guilt of all of us strike him. On the Day of Atonement the high priest laid both hands on a goat's head, confessed Israel's sins over it, and sent it away "bearing all their iniquities" (Lev 16:21–22). Here a person, not an animal, bears them. Paul: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin" (2 Cor 5:21). Henry puts it plainly: "The laying of our sins upon Christ implies the taking of them off from us."
- **"He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth" (v7).** Twice this verse says he did not open his mouth. Jesus stayed silent before the high priest (Matt 26:62–63), before Pilate (Matt 27:12–14) and before Herod (Luke 23:9). Pilate "was greatly amazed" (Matt 27:14). "Afflicted" here can mean "he humbled himself," and many translations take it that way: he let it happen.
- **"Like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" (v7).** The sheep that wandered in v6 are now matched by a lamb that goes quietly where it is led. Jeremiah used the same picture of himself (Jer 11:19). John the Baptist called Jesus "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29), and the Passover lamb lies behind both (1 Cor 5:7). His silence was not helplessness. He said no one took his life from him; he laid it down of his own accord (John 10:18).
- **The Ethiopian's question (vv7–8).** An Ethiopian official was reading these verses aloud in his chariot when Philip ran up. He asked, "About whom, I ask you, does the prophet say this, about himself or about someone else?" Philip "beginning with this Scripture… told him the good news about Jesus" (Acts 8:32–35). Luke quotes the Greek version, which differs a little from the Hebrew in v8.
- **"By oppression and judgment he was taken away" (v8).** This describes a legal process that was really an act of oppression, a show trial. Jesus' trials were held at night, with false witnesses whose stories did not agree (Mark 14:55–59), before a governor who said he found no guilt in him (John 18:38).
- **"As for his generation, who considered…" (v8).** The Hebrew is hard. "Generation" (*dor*) can mean his contemporaries ("who among his generation considered…?"), his life span, or his descendants ("who can speak of his descendants?", since he died young and childless). The ESV takes it as his contemporaries: none of them stopped to think that he was dying for the sins of God's people. JFB prefers the same, while listing the others.
- **"Cut off out of the land of the living, stricken for the transgression of my people" (v8).** "Cut off" means a violent and early death. "My people" suggests God himself or the prophet speaks here. Daniel uses a similar phrase, with a different Hebrew verb: "an anointed one shall be cut off" (Dan 9:26).
- **"They made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death" (v9).** Executed criminals in Judea were often thrown into a common grave. That is what the plan would have been. Instead, Joseph of Arimathea, "a rich man," asked for Jesus' body and laid it in his own new tomb (Matt 27:57–60). The two lines are probably meant to stand side by side, not to say the same thing: condemned as a criminal, buried as an honored man. "Death" is plural in Hebrew, which may simply mean a violent death. JFB says the word for "rich" never means "ungodly," against those who make the two lines identical.
- **"Although he had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth" (v9).** This is why the honorable burial fits. He was innocent in deed and word. Peter quotes it word for word: "He committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22). Hebrews says he was tempted in every way, "yet without sin" (Heb 4:15).
- **"Yet it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (v10).** This is the hardest line in the chapter. "Will" means "pleasure" or "purpose." God was not cruel and did not enjoy pain. Rather, the cross was not an accident or a defeat. It was God's plan for saving sinners. Peter preached the same at Pentecost: Jesus was delivered up "according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God," and yet "you crucified and killed" him (Acts 2:23). Both are true at once. Jesus himself prayed, "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42).
- **"When his soul makes an offering for guilt" (v10).** The word is *ʾasham*, the guilt offering of Leviticus 5–7. The guilt offering was for wrongs that could be measured and needed paying back: it came with restitution, repaying what was owed plus a fifth (Lev 5:14–6:7). The lexicon notes that the Servant offers himself as an *ʾasham*, as compensation for the people's sins, standing in as their substitute. The older "sin offering" of the KJV is a different sacrifice. The point is that he pays the debt. Jesus said he came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45), a likely echo of this chapter's "many."
- **"He shall see his offspring; he shall prolong his days" (v10).** This comes right after his death and burial. A dead man does not see his children or live long. The poem itself demands a resurrection. His "offspring" are not physical children (v8 suggests he had none) but the many who are made righteous through him (v11). Hebrews pictures Christ saying, "Behold, I and the children God has given me" (Heb 2:13). **"The will of the LORD shall prosper in his hand."** The same word, "will," appears twice in v10. The purpose that crushed him now succeeds through him.
- **"Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied" (v11).** The ESV footnotes that the Dead Sea Scroll of Isaiah and the Greek version read "he shall see light," that is, he will see life again. Either way, the Servant looks at the result of his suffering and is satisfied. Hebrews says Jesus "for the joy that was set before him endured the cross" (Heb 12:2).
- **"By his knowledge shall the righteous one, my servant, make many to be accounted righteous" (v11).** "By his knowledge" can mean the knowledge he has (his understanding of God's will, compare 52:13's "act wisely") or the knowledge of him that others gain (knowing him by faith). JFB takes the second. Both may be in view. "Make… to be accounted righteous" is the language of a court declaring someone in the right. This is the Old Testament seed of justification. Paul: "by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (Rom 5:19). **"And he shall bear their iniquities."** The reason they can be declared righteous is that he carries their guilt.
- **"The many."** The word *rabbim* runs through the poem like a drumbeat: "many were astonished" (52:14), "many nations" (52:15), "make many to be accounted righteous" (v11), "a portion with the many" and "the sin of many" (v12). It does not mean "a few." It stresses the huge numbers who benefit from one Servant. Jesus at the Last Supper: "my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many" (Mark 14:24).
- **"Therefore I will divide him a portion with the many, and he shall divide the spoil with the strong" (v12).** The picture is of a victorious king sharing out the plunder after a battle. The defeated victim turns out to be the conqueror. Paul says Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities… triumphing over them" in the cross (Col 2:15), and "therefore God has highly exalted him" (Phil 2:9).
- **Four reasons he is rewarded (v12).** "Because he poured out his soul to death" (all the way, holding nothing back), "and was numbered with the transgressors" (counted among criminals), "yet he bore the sin of many, and makes intercession for the transgressors." Jesus quoted the second phrase about himself the night before he died: "this Scripture must be fulfilled in me: 'And he was numbered with the transgressors'" (Luke 22:37). He was crucified between two criminals (Mark 15:27–28).
- **"Makes intercession for the transgressors" (v12).** The verb is *pagaʿ* again, the same word as "laid on" in v6. The LORD made our sin *fall on* him, and he *steps in* for us. "Transgressors" (*poshʿim*) are rebels, people in willful defiance, the strongest word for sinners. On the cross Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). He still "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through him (Heb 7:25).
- **Who is the Servant?** Since the Middle Ages, much Jewish interpretation has read the Servant here as Israel, suffering among the nations, and Israel is called "my servant" in these chapters (41:8; 44:1). But this Servant is innocent (v9), suffers for "my people" (v8), and dies for others' sins willingly and silently. Israel in Isaiah is anything but innocent (42:24–25; 43:24). Some ancient Jewish writings, including the Targum (an Aramaic paraphrase), read the passage as about the Messiah, though they explain away the suffering. The New Testament quotes or alludes to nearly every verse of this chapter about Jesus.
- **Commentators:** All three read the chapter as a picture of Christ's substitution. Henry organizes it around Christ's shame and the honor of his suffering: he did his Father's will, made atonement "for the sin of man," suffered for "not… any sin of his own," endured with "invincible and exemplary" patience, and his sufferings ended "in his immortal honour." Guzik warns against trying to make Jesus attractive through style or presentation, since v2 says he had no outward appeal and only the Spirit opens eyes to his beauty. Guzik also says the lamb led to the slaughter was never out of control (John 10:18), and that the Father's pleasure in v10 was in the reconciliation achieved, not in the suffering itself. JFB notes that "grief" in v3 is literally "disease," says the grave of v9 shows "He was with a rich man," and reads "knowledge" in v11 as knowing him "experimentally," by experience. Guzik links the guilt offering of v10 to Leviticus 5, which the lexicon supports. JFB's gentler reading of "chastisement" as a parent's discipline sits uneasily with v6 and v10, where the Servant bears guilt that is not his own, and most readers take it as penalty.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter needs no forcing. Written centuries before Jesus, it describes a Servant who grows up unnoticed, is rejected, bears others' sins, stays silent before his accusers, dies with criminals, is buried with a rich man, and then lives again to see his offspring and share the spoil. The New Testament writers keep coming back to it because it explains the cross as no other passage does: he was pierced for our transgressions, and the LORD laid on him the iniquity of us all. The Ethiopian's question still stands, "about himself or about someone else?" Philip's answer was Jesus, and the whole New Testament agrees.
