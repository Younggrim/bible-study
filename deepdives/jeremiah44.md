*Setting:* Some time after 586 BC, perhaps a few years. The remnant of Judah has fled to Egypt against God's word, taking Jeremiah and Baruch with them (ch 43). They have spread out across the country, from the frontier forts in the delta to the far south. This chapter is Jeremiah's last recorded message and the last scene of his forty-year ministry. It is set at a large gathering, possibly a festival, where the Judeans are openly worshiping the "queen of heaven." Jeremiah preaches once more, and the people answer him to his face. They are not confused or sorry. They argue, from their own experience, that idolatry worked and obeying the LORD did not. The chapter ends with the question it has been building toward: "whose word will stand, mine or theirs" (v28).

#### Judgment for Idolatry (vv1–30)
- **"All the Judeans who lived in the land of Egypt, at Migdol, at Tahpanhes, at Memphis, and in the land of Pathros" (v1).** In a short time the refugees have scattered across Egypt. Migdol ("tower") and Tahpanhes were border forts in the northeast delta. Memphis (Hebrew *Noph*) was Egypt's ancient capital near modern Cairo. Pathros is Upper Egypt, the long southern valley of the Nile (the name comes from Egyptian for "the southland"). So the message reaches north and south. Some Judeans had been in Egypt before Jerusalem fell, perhaps as soldiers in Pharaoh's service. Aramaic papyri from the island of Elephantine, far up the Nile, show a Jewish military colony living there by the 400s BC.
- **"You have seen all the disaster that I brought upon Jerusalem" (v2).** God begins with what they saw with their own eyes. **"This day they are a desolation, and no one dwells in them."** The land they left is empty. **"Because of the evil that they committed… to make offerings and serve other gods that they knew not" (v3).** "Make offerings" here translates a verb that usually means to burn incense or send up sacrificial smoke. It runs through the whole chapter.
- **"Yet I persistently sent to you all my servants the prophets" (v4).** Literally "rising early and sending," one of Jeremiah's favorite phrases for God's patience (7:13, 25; 25:3–4; 2 Chr 36:15). **"Oh, do not do this abomination that I hate!"** It is almost a cry. God is not only a judge giving a verdict. He pleads.
- **"Why do you commit this great evil against yourselves" (v7).** Literally "against your souls." Idolatry does God no harm. It destroys the worshiper. **"Leaving you no remnant."** They are the last of Judah, and they are throwing their future away.
- **"Making offerings to other gods in the land of Egypt where you have come to live" (v8).** They had fled to Egypt for safety. Now they pick up Egypt's gods as well. **"A curse and a taunt among all the nations."**
- **"Have you forgotten the evil of your fathers, the evil of the kings of Judah, the evil of their wives, your own evil, and the evil of your wives" (v9).** Five times "evil." The list includes the queens. Solomon's foreign wives led him to build shrines to Ashtoreth (1 Kgs 11:1–8), and Jezebel's influence reached Judah through her daughter Athaliah (2 Kgs 8:18, 26). The wives are mentioned because, as the chapter will show, they led this worship.
- **"They have not humbled themselves even to this day" (v10).** The verb (*daka'*) means "crushed." The BDB lexicon gives "made humble, contrite" for this verse. It is the same form Isaiah uses of the Servant, "crushed for our iniquities" (Isa 53:5). Judah had been crushed by Babylon but not crushed in heart. Suffering broke their city but not their pride.
- **"Behold, I will set my face against you for harm" (v11).** To "set the face" is resolve. They "set their faces to come to the land of Egypt" (v12). Now God sets his face against them. The words of 42:15–17 are coming true.
- **"An oath, a horror, a curse, and a taunt" (v12).** Their name will be used in curses: "May you end up like the Judeans in Egypt." **"I will punish those who dwell in the land of Egypt, as I have punished Jerusalem" (v13).**
- **"None of the remnant of Judah… shall escape or survive or return to the land of Judah, to which they desire to return" (v14).** They meant to wait out the trouble in Egypt and then go home. Literally they "lift up their soul" to return, their deepest longing. **"For they shall not return, except some fugitives."** A small number will escape (v28). The irony is sharp. The exiles in Babylon, whom everyone pitied, were the ones God promised to bring home (24:5–7; 29:10). The ones who ran to Egypt to stay close to home are the ones who will never see it again.
- **"All the men who knew that their wives had made offerings to other gods, and all the women who stood by, a great assembly" (v15).** The crowd is large and includes "all the people who lived in Pathros," so it may have been a festival or a gathering in the south. The men know and allow it. The women stand by in agreement.
- **"As for the word that you have spoken to us in the name of the LORD, we will not listen to you" (v16).** This is the plainest refusal in the book. Earlier the people dodged, blamed Baruch or called Jeremiah a liar (43:2–3). Now they say it outright.
- **"We will do everything that we have vowed, make offerings to the queen of heaven" (v17).** The "queen of heaven" was a goddess of love, war and fertility, known in Mesopotamia as Ishtar and in Canaan as Astarte (the Old Testament's Ashtoreth). She was linked with the planet Venus. Some Hebrew manuscripts and old Jewish tradition read the words as "the work of heaven," meaning the starry host, but most scholars take it as a title. Jeremiah had condemned this same worship in Jerusalem decades earlier: "The children gather wood, the fathers kindle fire, and the women knead dough, to make cakes for the queen of heaven" (7:18). An Aramaic letter from Egypt from about a century later mentions a temple of the "Queen of Heaven" at Syene, near Elephantine.
- **"For then we had plenty of food, and prospered, and saw no disaster" (v17).** Their argument is from experience. They remember good years under kings like Manasseh, when every kind of worship was allowed (2 Kgs 21:3–7). **"But since we left off making offerings to the queen of heaven… we have lacked everything" (v18).** They probably mean King Josiah's reform in 622 BC, when such worship was stamped out (2 Kgs 23:4–14). After it, things went badly: Josiah was killed in battle in 609 BC, Egypt and then Babylon ruled Judah, and finally the city fell. They read the evidence and conclude that the reform caused the disaster. Hosea had described the same blindness: "she did not know that it was I who gave her the grain, the wine, and the oil" (Hos 2:8). The reform was real on the surface, but Jeremiah had said that Judah did not return to God "with her whole heart, but in pretense" (3:10).
- **"Was it without our husbands' approval that we made cakes for her bearing her image" (v19).** The ESV footnote says the Hebrew lacks "And the women said," which is supplied from the Syriac. Under the law, a wife's vow could be cancelled by her husband (Num 30:6–8). The women say, in effect, "Our husbands knew and agreed, so the vows stand." The word for "cakes" (*kawwan*) appears only here and in 7:18. The BDB lexicon calls it probably a Babylonian loan word for a cake used in the worship of Ishtar. "Bearing her image" may mean the cakes were shaped like the goddess or stamped with her figure. Clay moulds found at Mari in Syria, shaped like a woman's figure, may have been used for cakes like these.
- **"Did not the LORD remember them? Did it not come into his mind?" (v21).** Jeremiah turns their argument around. Yes, they made offerings in the old days, and God remembered every one. **"The LORD could no longer bear your evil deeds" (v22).** The disaster did not come because they stopped. It came because God's patience with years of idolatry finally ran out. Judgment is often delayed. That does not mean it is cancelled.
- **"It is because you made offerings and because you sinned against the LORD… that this disaster has happened to you" (v23).** The three words "law," "statutes" and "testimonies" together sum up the whole covenant they had broken.
- **"Then confirm your vows and perform your vows!" (v25).** This is bitter irony. Go ahead, keep your promises to her. They had made a solemn vow to obey the LORD (42:5–6) and broke it within days. Their vows to the goddess they keep faithfully. God gives them over to what they have chosen (compare Rom 1:24).
- **"I have sworn by my great name… that my name shall no more be invoked by the mouth of any man of Judah in all the land of Egypt, saying, 'As the Lord GOD lives'" (v26).** "As the LORD lives" was the standard oath of Israel. God swears by himself, since there is no one greater (Gen 22:16; Heb 6:13). The people still used his name while serving another god, and he will not let them go on mixing the two (compare Ezek 20:39). The faith of Israel in Egypt will die out with them.
- **"I am watching over them for disaster and not for good" (v27).** This reverses a promise. God had said, "As I have watched over them to pluck up and break down… so I will watch over them to build and to plant" (31:28). The same verb opened Jeremiah's ministry, when God showed him an almond branch and said, "I am watching over my word to perform it" (1:11–12). God's watchful care can be for good or for harm. It is never absent.
- **"Those who escape the sword shall return from the land of Egypt to the land of Judah, few in number" (v28).** Not quite none, then. A handful will go back. **"And all the remnant of Judah… shall know whose word will stand, mine or theirs."** This is the question of the whole book. Kings, priests and false prophets have contradicted Jeremiah for forty years. History will settle it. "The word of our God will stand forever" (Isa 40:8).
- **"This shall be the sign to you… I will give Pharaoh Hophra king of Egypt into the hand of his enemies" (vv29–30).** Hophra (Apries in Greek sources) ruled about 589–570 BC. He was the pharaoh whose army briefly lifted the siege of Jerusalem (37:5). The Greek historian Herodotus tells how an army revolt made his general Amasis king in his place. Hophra was defeated in a civil war and later killed, apparently strangled. Amasis was then attacked by Nebuchadnezzar in about 568–567 BC (43:10–13). **"As I gave Zedekiah king of Judah into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar."** The refugees had seen what happened to their own king. Egypt's king would fare no better. The protector they trusted would fall to "those who seek his life," not to Babylon itself but to his own people.
- **The end of Jeremiah's story.** The book records nothing more of Jeremiah after this. Chapter 45 goes back to an earlier date, and chapters 46–51 gather his oracles against the nations. Jewish and Christian tradition holds that he died in Egypt, perhaps stoned by his own people. Another tradition, in 2 Maccabees 2:1–8, says he hid the tent, the ark and the altar of incense in a cave on Mount Nebo, but that book is not part of the Protestant canon and the story is legend.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry pictures the household of v19: "the fathers kindled the fire while the women kneaded the dough." On v18 he says the people blamed their suffering on giving up idols, when the truth was that they had kept their idols in their hearts. On v23: "Whatever evil comes upon us, it is because we have sinned against the Lord." On v28: "The contest between God and sinners is whose word shall stand… God's word shall stand, and not the sinner's." JFB says it is "the curse of impiety not to perceive the hand of God in calamities," and notes that hardships are often God's way "to do His people good at their latter end" (Deut 8:16). JFB calls v25 "ironical praise" and points out the reversal of the exiles: those sent to Babylon were cured of idolatry and restored, while those who chose Egypt hardened and perished. Guzik draws out the reasoning of vv17–18, which credits the goddess and blames the reform, and passes on Kidner's comment that it is a revealing picture of a mind turned upside down. He also notes the Elephantine papyri, which show a later Jewish community in Egypt mixing Israel's faith with other worship. On v10 Guzik cites the word for "humbled" as "crushed" or "bruised," and the lexicon supports him.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah was crushed by Babylon but never crushed in heart (v10). It would not humble itself, and it kept score by its harvests. The same Hebrew word appears in Isaiah's song of the Servant: "he was crushed for our iniquities" (Isa 53:5). The one who had no pride to break let himself be crushed for people too proud to bend. The question of v28, "whose word will stand, mine or theirs," was finally answered at an empty tomb. Jesus said, "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35). Everyone who trusts the word that stands is spared the end of those who trusted the queen of heaven.
