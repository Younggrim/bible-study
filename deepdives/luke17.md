*Setting:* About AD 29–30, late in the journey to Jerusalem. Chapter 16 ended with a rich man who ignored a beggar at his gate and learned too late that Moses and the Prophets had been warning him. Chapter 17 opens with short sayings for the disciples about sin, forgiveness, faith and humble service. Then Luke reminds us where Jesus is heading (v11), tells of ten lepers healed and one who came back, and closes with Jesus' first long teaching in Luke about the coming of the kingdom and the day the Son of Man is revealed. Chapter 21 will give the second.

#### Temptations to Sin (vv1–4)
- **"Temptations to sin are sure to come" (v1).** The word is *skandalon*, originally the trigger stick of a trap and then anything that trips someone up. Here it means something that leads another person into sin or out of faith. In a fallen world such things are certain, but that does not excuse the person who causes them.
- **"Woe to the one through whom they come!" (v1).** "Woe" is a cry of grief and warning, the word Jesus used over the rich and the Pharisees (6:24–26; 11:42–52). Coming right after the Pharisees mocked him (16:14), the warning has the religious leaders in view, but it is spoken to disciples.
- **"A millstone... hung around his neck" (v2).** The word here is for an ordinary household millstone, heavy enough that a man thrown into the sea with one tied on would never come up. Jesus says even that would be better than leading "one of these little ones" into sin. "Little ones" means not only children but humble, vulnerable believers, like the tax collectors and sinners just welcomed in (15:1).
- **"Pay attention to yourselves!" (v3).** This hinges both ways. Disciples must guard against tripping others up, and they must guard their own hearts when others sin against them.
- **"If your brother sins, rebuke him, and if he repents, forgive him" (v3).** Rebuke is not revenge. It is honest confrontation for the other person's good (Lev 19:17; Matt 18:15). And when he repents, the matter is to be settled.
- **"Seven times in the day" (v4).** Seven is a round number for "again and again" (Ps 119:164). Some rabbis taught that a person should be forgiven up to three times. Jesus goes well past that, and in Matthew he tells Peter "seventy-seven times" (Matt 18:21–22). A disciple does not keep score.
- **Commentators:** Guzik traces *skandalon* to the bent stick that springs a trap and says love is what keeps believers from tripping one another up (1 John 2:10). He adds that forgiveness in the full sense waits on repentance, but the believer can release bitterness before then. Henry: "It is no abatement of their guilt by whom an offence comes... that offences will come." JFB says "seven times" is not a lower standard than "seventy times seven," since Jesus means to forgive however often sincere repentance comes.

#### Increase Our Faith (vv5–6)
- **"The apostles said to the Lord, 'Increase our faith!'" (v5).** Luke calls them "apostles" and Jesus "the Lord," which gives the request weight. Faced with a standard of forgiveness that high, they know they cannot meet it on their own. JFB notes this is the only time the Twelve ask Jesus for a change in their own souls.
- **"Faith like a grain of mustard seed" (v6).** The mustard seed was proverbially tiny (13:19). The point is not that they need more faith, but that even a little faith, placed in God, can do what seems impossible. What matters is the one trusted, not the size of the trust.
- **"This mulberry tree" (v6).** This is the black mulberry (*sykaminos*), known for a deep, wide root system and for living for centuries. Rooting it up and planting it in the sea is a picture of the impossible, like moving a mountain (Matt 17:20; Mark 11:23). It is not an invitation to perform tricks. Jesus has just been talking about forgiving the same person seven times in a day, and bitterness can feel as deep-rooted as any tree.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the request as wise: forgiving difficult people takes great faith, and some of the greatest miracles of faith are restored relationships. He stresses that the object of faith matters more than its amount. Henry: "Faith in God's pardoning mercy, will enable us to get over the greatest difficulties in the way of forgiving our brethren."

#### Unworthy Servants (vv7–10)
- **"A servant plowing or keeping sheep" (v7).** This is a small farmer with one slave who does everything: fields, flocks and kitchen. Jesus asks his hearers to imagine themselves as the master. No master would tell such a slave to sit down and be served first.
- **"Dress properly, and serve me while I eat and drink" (v8).** Literally "gird yourself," tie up the long outer robe for work. After a day in the field, the slave's duties are not finished.
- **"Does he thank the servant because he did what was commanded?" (v9).** "Thank" here means "Is he in the servant's debt?" A slave did not earn credit for doing his job.
- **A different picture elsewhere.** In 12:37 Jesus describes a master who comes home and serves his own servants, and at the Last Supper he says, "I am among you as the one who serves" (22:27). Both pictures are true. God in grace serves his people beyond anything they deserve. But no servant can ever put God in his debt.
- **"We are unworthy servants; we have only done what was our duty" (v10).** "Unworthy" is *achreios*, "unprofitable" or "useless." It is not a claim that the servants are worthless people, but that their service has added nothing to God and earned no reward from him. The disciples have just asked for faith to do great things. Jesus guards them against pride in whatever faith accomplishes.
- **Commentators:** JFB connects the parable to what goes before: once faith has grown enough to forgive and do the impossible, "be not puffed up as though you had laid the Lord under any obligations to you." JFB says *achreios* here means, "We have not... profited or benefited God at all" (Job 22:2–3; Rom 11:35). Henry: God "cannot be in debt to them for their services." Guzik says the phrase is not false modesty or an excuse for doing nothing. It means believers are always in God's debt, so their service should come from gratitude, not a sense of having earned anything.

#### Jesus Cleanses Ten Lepers (vv11–19)
- **"On the way to Jerusalem" (v11).** Luke reminds the reader again of the journey (9:51; 13:22). "Between Samaria and Galilee" means along the border between the two regions, likely heading east toward the Jordan valley route that pilgrims took south. The border setting explains why Jews and a Samaritan are found together.
- **"Ten lepers, who stood at a distance" (v12).** "Leprosy" in the Bible covered a range of skin diseases, not only modern Hansen's disease. The law required those with it to live outside the community, tear their clothes and call out "Unclean, unclean" (Lev 13:45–46). Shared misery had erased the old hatred between Jew and Samaritan. Four lepers in 2 Kings 7:3 had also banded together outside a city.
- **"Jesus, Master, have mercy on us" (v13).** "Master" (*epistatēs*) is a word only Luke uses, and elsewhere only the disciples use it. "Have mercy" is the cry of the blind man near Jericho (18:38) and of countless psalms.
- **"Go and show yourselves to the priests" (v14).** A priest had to inspect a healed leper and declare him clean before he could return to society, then offer sacrifices (Lev 14:2–32). Earlier, Jesus touched a leper and healed him on the spot (5:12–14). This time he sends them off still diseased. They have to start walking before anything changes. Naaman was told something similar (2 Kgs 5:10–14).
- **"As they went they were cleansed" (v14).** Their obedience was a kind of faith. All ten trusted Jesus enough to go.
- **"One of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back" (v15).** "Turned back" is the same verb for returning that runs through Luke (2:20; 8:39). He praises God "with a loud voice," as loud now as when he called out from a distance.
- **"He fell on his face at Jesus' feet, giving him thanks" (v16).** He thanks God and he thanks Jesus, and he does it at Jesus' feet. Luke lets the reader see that the two belong together.
- **"Now he was a Samaritan" (v16).** Luke saves this detail for last for effect. Samaritans were descendants of northern Israelites mixed with foreign settlers, with their own temple on Mount Gerizim and their own version of the five books of Moses. Most Jews considered them heretics. Like the good Samaritan (10:33), the outsider is the one who does what is right.
- **"Where are the nine?" (v17).** The nine Jews were probably doing exactly what Jesus told them: going to the priests at the temple. The Samaritan would not have been welcome there anyway. But his return shows he recognized where his healing had really come from. The nine got what they wanted from Jesus. He came back for Jesus.
- **"This foreigner" (v18).** The word is *allogenēs*, "of another race," used only here in the New Testament. The same word was carved on the stone warning notices in the Jerusalem temple courts, forbidding foreigners to enter on pain of death. The man barred from the temple gives praise to God at the feet of Jesus.
- **"Your faith has made you well" (v19).** The verb is *sōzō*, which can mean "heal" or "save." All ten were cleansed. Only to this one does Jesus say that his faith has saved him, the same words he spoke to the sinful woman (7:50) and the woman who touched his robe (8:48). Many readers take this as more than physical healing.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The others only got the outward cure, he alone got the spiritual blessing." JFB agrees, saying his faith made him whole "not as the others, merely in body," and quotes Trench: their common misery made them "forget the fierce national antipathy of Jew and Samaritan." JFB also notes that Jesus' question is literally "Were not the ten cleansed?" Guzik stresses that Jesus sent them before they were healed, and he applies it to living out a new identity before old feelings catch up. He recounts the story of Matthew Henry, robbed one day, writing down reasons to be thankful: that he was never robbed before, that they took his money and not his life, and that it was he who was robbed and not he who robbed.

#### The Coming of the Kingdom (vv20–37)
- **"Being asked by the Pharisees when the kingdom of God would come" (v20).** Many Jews expected the kingdom to arrive with clear signs: the Messiah defeating Rome, judging the nations and ruling from Jerusalem. The question may be sincere or may be a challenge: if he is the Messiah, where is the kingdom?
- **"Not coming in ways that can be observed" (v20).** The word *paratērēsis* means "observation," and can carry the sense of close watching or surveillance. The kingdom does not come in a way that can be tracked by watching for signs and calculating dates.
- **"The kingdom of God is in the midst of you" (v21).** The Greek *entos hymōn* can mean "within you" (KJV) or "among you" (ESV, with "within" in the footnote). The lexicon allows both. "Among you" fits best here. Jesus is speaking to Pharisees who reject him, so he is hardly saying the kingdom is in their hearts. He means the kingdom is already present in their midst, in the King himself and his works (11:20). It also fits the next words, "Behold," which point to something in front of them.
- **"You will desire to see one of the days of the Son of Man" (v22).** Now Jesus turns to the disciples. Hard days are coming when they will long for even one day of his presence again, or for the day of his return, and not see it. Waiting will be painful.
- **"Do not go out or follow them" (v23).** False messiahs were common in the first century (Acts 5:36–37; 21:38), and they have appeared ever since. The disciples are told not to chase rumors.
- **"As the lightning flashes" (v24).** His return will not be a local event that needs announcing. It will be sudden, public and unmistakable, like lightning across the whole sky.
- **"But first he must suffer many things and be rejected by this generation" (v25).** This is the third time Jesus foretells his suffering (9:22, 44), and it is placed in the middle of a teaching on glory. There is no crown without the cross first. "Must" (*dei*) is Luke's word for God's plan.
- **"As it was in the days of Noah" (vv26–27).** Eating, drinking and marrying are not sins. The point is that people went on with ordinary life, ignoring God's warnings, "until the day" the flood came. Noah had preached and built for years (2 Pet 2:5).
- **"As it was in the days of Lot" (vv28–29).** Sodom was wicked (Gen 19), but Jesus' list is again ordinary activity: buying, selling, planting, building. The danger is not only gross sin but comfortable indifference. In both stories, God brings his people out first, then judgment falls, "and destroyed them all."
- **"The one who is on the housetop... not come down" (v31).** Houses had flat roofs reached by an outside stair, so a man could flee without going inside. In Mark and Matthew this saying is about fleeing the fall of Jerusalem (Mark 13:15–16). Here Luke applies it to the Son of Man's day. In both cases, the lesson is to let nothing in this world hold a person back.
- **"Remember Lot's wife" (v32).** Three words. She was rescued from Sodom by the hand, but "looked back" and became a pillar of salt (Gen 19:16, 26). Her body was leaving, but her heart had stayed behind.
- **"Whoever seeks to preserve his life will lose it" (v33).** Jesus has said this before (9:24). Clinging to this life costs the next one.
- **"One will be taken and the other left" (vv34–35).** People side by side, in bed at night or grinding grain at dawn, will be separated in an instant. Two women grinding together with a hand mill was a daily chore. Readers differ on what "taken" means. Many see the gathering of believers to Christ (1 Thess 4:16–17), sometimes called the rapture. Others note that in the stories of Noah and Lot those "taken" were taken by judgment, and the vultures of v37 fit judgment, so being "left" may be the safer place. The text's main point is clear either way: the separation will be sudden, and close companions may not share the same end.
- **The missing verse 36.** Some manuscripts add, "Two men will be in the field; one will be taken and the other left." The ESV leaves it out because the earliest manuscripts lack it, and it seems to have been copied in from Matthew 24:40. Nothing is lost by its absence.
- **"Where, Lord?" "Where the corpse is, there the vultures will gather" (v37).** The disciples want a location. Jesus answers with a proverb. The word can mean "eagle" or "vulture," and here vultures circling a carcass fit best. Judgment will come wherever there is spiritual death, as surely as vultures find a dead body. It is not a map but a warning.
- **Commentators:** JFB says it is "not their wickedness, but their worldliness, their unbelief and indifference to the future" that Noah's and Lot's generations are held up for, and of Lot's wife, "Her heart was in Sodom still." JFB reads v23 as "a warning to all so-called expositors of prophecy and their followers" who cry "here" and "there" at every war. Henry and JFB both see the warning fulfilled first in the fall of Jerusalem and finally at Christ's return. Guzik says the word for "observation" suggests hostile scrutiny, and the lexicon gives "close observation" and "surveillance," so it allows that sense. He rejects reading v21 as a teaching that the kingdom is an inner spiritual feeling, since Jesus was speaking to Pharisees. He applies vv34–35 to the rapture, while the Tyndale notes favor the final judgment. Henry, by contrast, takes v21 as a spiritual kingdom set up "in the heart," though he hedges: it was among them, "or rather within some of them."

#### Pointing to Christ
The King stands in the middle of the Pharisees, and they ask where the kingdom is. The Samaritan sees it and falls at his feet. Between the healing and the glory comes the cross: "first he must suffer" (v25). The one who will come like lightning is the same one who gave up his life, and the one who tells disciples to forgive again and again is the one who will pray for those who crucify him (23:34).
