*Setting:* Paul is writing to Timothy in Ephesus in the early 60s AD. Having charged Timothy personally (ch 4), Paul now tells him how to deal with different groups in "the household of God" (3:15): older and younger members, widows, and elders. The ESV section runs on to 6:2, where slaves are addressed. In the Roman world a widow without a husband, sons or property could fall into real poverty quickly. Israel's law had always commanded care for "the fatherless and the widow" (Deut 10:18; 24:19–21), and the Jerusalem church had organized daily food for its widows from the start (Acts 6:1). By now the Ephesian church seems to have kept an official list of widows it supported, and some were abusing it. The false teaching was also unsettling households, and some elders had gone wrong. The chapter is practical church life: who to support, how to honour leaders and how to correct them.

#### Instructions for the Church (vv1–25)
- **"Do not rebuke an older man but encourage him as you would a father" (v1).** "Rebuke" here is *epiplēssō*, found only here in the New Testament, which originally meant to strike at. Paul isn't forbidding correction; he is forbidding harsh, attacking correction. "Encourage" is *parakaleō*, to appeal, urge, come alongside. A young leader must correct older men the way a respectful son would speak to his father.
- **"Younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, younger women as sisters" (vv1–2).** The whole church is a family, and every relationship takes its tone from that. Treating others as family is not a sentimental phrase. It sets the boundaries of respect, warmth and propriety.
- **"In all purity" (v2).** Added for the younger women. A young unmarried leader visiting homes needed to guard both his conduct and his reputation. The false teachers were already slipping into households and taking advantage of women (2 Tim 3:6).
- **"Honor widows who are truly widows" (v3).** "Honor" (*timaō*) includes respect, but here it clearly includes material support, as v4 and vv16–18 show. Jesus used "honor your father and mother" the same way, of providing for them (Mark 7:10–13). "Truly widows" means those with no family to support them (v5).
- **"Let them first learn to show godliness to their own household" (v4).** Children and grandchildren come first. "Make some return to their parents" means paying back what they received: their parents fed and raised them, and now it's their turn. This is the fifth commandment in practice, and "pleasing in the sight of God."
- **"She who is truly a widow… has set her hope on God" (v5).** The picture is like Anna in the temple, a widow who "did not depart from the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day" (Luke 2:36–37). With no one else to lean on, she leans on God.
- **"She who is self-indulgent is dead even while she lives" (v6).** The word means living in luxury and pleasure (James 5:5). Some widows in Ephesus were not in need at all, or were living carelessly. Spiritually, such a life is death in the middle of life (compare Rev 3:1).
- **"If anyone does not provide for his relatives… he has denied the faith" (v8).** One of the strongest lines in the letter. Even unbelievers in the Greco-Roman world held that children must care for their aged parents. A Christian who refuses this falls below the standard of the pagans around him. Faith is not just what we believe. It is how we live (James 2:15–17).
- **"Let a widow be enrolled if she is not less than sixty years of age" (v9).** "Enrolled" means put on a list. This seems to be a recognised group of older widows supported by the church, who in turn gave themselves to prayer and service. In Paul's day sixty was old age, and a widow that age was unlikely to remarry. Whether this was a formal order with vows (as it later became) or simply a support list is debated. By the second century, writers like Ignatius and Polycarp mention widows as a recognised group in the churches.
- **"Having been the wife of one husband" (v9).** The same phrase as for overseers and deacons, reversed (3:2, 12). It most likely means a faithful wife, not that a widow who had remarried was barred, since Paul tells younger widows to remarry in v14.
- **"Brought up children… shown hospitality… washed the feet of the saints… cared for the afflicted" (v10).** A record of service. Foot-washing was a slave's task and a basic act of hospitality to travellers (Gen 18:4), and Jesus made it a picture of humble love (John 13:14–15). "Brought up children" may include taking in abandoned infants, which Christians were known for in a culture where unwanted babies were often left outside to die.
- **"Refuse to enroll younger widows" (v11).** Not because they are less worthy, but because the commitment was too heavy for them. Enrolment seems to have involved some kind of pledge to devote themselves to the Lord's service. "When their passions draw them away from Christ" uses a picture of growing restless against a yoke or bridle.
- **"Incur condemnation for having abandoned their former faith" (v12).** "Faith" here likely means their pledge, their first promise of devotion. Breaking it would bring judgment on them, a kind of broken vow. Others suggest Paul means marrying an unbeliever and drifting from Christ altogether (v15). Paul is not condemning remarriage itself (v14; 1 Cor 7:39).
- **"Idlers, going about from house to house… gossips and busybodies" (v13).** With church support and no household of their own, some younger widows were drifting from home to home. "Saying what they should not" may mean spreading the false teaching, which travelled through households (2 Tim 3:6–7; Titus 1:11).
- **"So I would have younger widows marry, bear children, manage their households" (v14).** Against false teachers who forbade marriage (4:3), Paul encourages it. "Manage their households" is a strong word: to be the head of a household, the ruler of the home. Paul gives women real authority in that sphere.
- **"Some have already strayed after Satan" (v15).** This was not a hypothetical. Some had already been led away, probably by the false teachers.
- **"If any believing woman has relatives who are widows, let her care for them" (v16).** Some manuscripts read "believing man or woman." Paul may have in mind well-off Christian women who had widowed relatives or household members. Family support frees the church to care for those with no one.
- **"The elders who rule well" (v17).** "Rule" (*proistēmi*) means to lead, manage, take care of (3:4–5). Elders seem to have shared oversight, with some giving themselves especially to "preaching and teaching." "Labor" is the hard-work word, toil to the point of weariness (4:10).
- **"Worthy of double honor" (v17).** Probably both respect and material support, or simply generous honour. "Honor" (*timē*) in the lexicon here means esteem, but the same word can mean a price, and v18 shows money is in view.
- **"You shall not muzzle an ox… The laborer deserves his wages" (v18).** The first is Deuteronomy 25:4, which Paul also uses for the same point in 1 Corinthians 9:9. The second is word for word the saying of Jesus in Luke 10:7 (compare Matt 10:10). What's striking is that "the Scripture says" seems to cover both. If so, Jesus' words, probably already written down in a Gospel, are being quoted alongside Moses as Scripture. Some scholars think Paul is quoting a familiar saying of Jesus rather than a written Gospel. Compare 2 Peter 3:16, where Paul's letters are called Scripture.
- **"Except on the evidence of two or three witnesses" (v19).** Deuteronomy 19:15 required this for any charge. Jesus applied it to church life (Matt 18:16). Leaders attract criticism, and in a church divided by false teaching, accusations would fly. A charge must be established, not just whispered.
- **"Those who persist in sin, rebuke them in the presence of all" (v20).** "Persist" is present tense: those who keep on sinning. When an elder's sin is proven and continues, it is handled openly, "so that the rest may stand in fear." "The rest" may mean the other elders or the whole church. The aim is both justice and a healthy fear of sin.
- **"In the presence of God and of Christ Jesus and of the elect angels I charge you" (v21).** One of the most solemn oaths in Paul's letters. Angels are pictured as witnesses at God's judgment (Luke 9:26; Matt 25:31). "Elect" angels probably means the faithful angels, as opposed to those who fell (2 Pet 2:4; Jude 6). Timothy's dealings with elders take place before heaven's court.
- **"Without prejudging, doing nothing from partiality" (v21).** No verdict before the facts, and no favouritism for friends or the powerful. James says the same about partiality in the church (James 2:1–4).
- **"Do not be hasty in the laying on of hands" (v22).** Most read this as ordination of elders (4:14). Rushing to appoint unproven men had probably helped cause Ephesus's troubles. A minority view, held by some early writers, reads it as restoring repentant sinners to fellowship, which also involved laying on of hands. "Nor take part in the sins of others": whoever approves a man for leadership shares responsibility for what he then does.
- **"No longer drink only water, but use a little wine" (v23).** A personal aside in the middle of church business. Timothy may have been abstaining completely, perhaps as a witness against drunkenness (3:3, 8) or to set himself apart from the false teachers, who were not drinkers but ascetics (4:3). Paul doesn't want "keep yourself pure" (v22) to be misheard as a rule against wine. Water in ancient cities was often unsafe, and wine was a common medicine (Luke 10:34). It is a glimpse of Paul's fatherly care.
- **"The sins of some people are conspicuous… the sins of others appear later" (vv24–25).** Back to choosing leaders. Some men's sins are obvious and walk ahead of them to judgment. Others come out only in time. Good works are the same: some are plain, and those that are hidden will come to light. So be patient in choosing, and leave final judgment to God (1 Cor 4:5).
- **Commentators:** On v1 Guzik notes the word for "rebuke" means to strike at, which the lexicon confirms, and JFB says Timothy should treat younger men as equals, "not lording it over them." Henry: "Respect must be paid to the dignity of years and place." On widows Henry writes: "Widowhood is a desolate state; but let widows trust in the Lord, and continue in prayer." JFB reads v6 as "dead in the Spirit while alive in the flesh." On v14 JFB says Paul "does not impose a law, but points out a remedy." On v17 Guzik follows Clarke in reading "honor" as wages and argues churches should pay pastors where they can; the lexicon lists 5:17 under esteem, so the financial sense comes more from v18 than from the word itself. JFB takes "double" as meaning generous, not literally twice, and Henry says "care must be taken that ministers are maintained." All three, like the Tyndale notes, see v18 as quoting Luke 10:7 as Scripture. On v23 Guzik and JFB both see Paul correcting an unhealthy asceticism, and Henry gives the balance: "As we are not to make our bodies masters, so neither slaves." On vv24–25 Henry: "God will bring to light the hidden things of darkness."

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is full of ordinary needs: lonely widows, ageing parents, tired preachers, a young leader with a bad stomach. Christ is behind all of it. He is the Lord who "watches over the sojourners; he upholds the widow and the fatherless" (Ps 146:9), who from the cross gave his own mother into John's care (John 19:26–27), and who is the true and impartial Judge in whose presence every hidden sin and every hidden good work will come to light. His church is a household where no one is left alone, because he did not leave us alone.
