*Setting:* Jacob's family is settled in the Valley of Hebron in southern Canaan, where Isaac lived and died (35:27–29). Jacob is now old, perhaps around 108. Rachel, the wife he loved, has died giving birth to Benjamin (35:16–19). Of her two sons, Joseph is seventeen and Benjamin is a small child. Jacob has twelve sons by four women, and the rivalry between Leah and Rachel (chs 29–30) has passed down to their children. The older brothers have already shown what they are capable of: Simeon and Levi slaughtered the men of Shechem (ch 34), and Reuben slept with his father's concubine (35:22). Esau's family has grown into a nation with chiefs and kings (ch 36), while Jacob still lives in tents as a foreigner in the land. Chapter 37 opens the story of Joseph, which runs, with one break, to the end of Genesis. It is the longest single story in the book, and in it God's hand is seldom seen but never absent.

#### Joseph's Dreams (vv1–11)
- **"Jacob lived in the land of his father's sojournings" (v1).** The contrast with chapter 36 is deliberate. Esau "settled" in Seir with kings and chiefs. Jacob is still a sojourner, a resident foreigner, in the land God promised him. The Tyndale notes put it well: worldly greatness often comes faster than spiritual greatness.
- **"These are the generations of Jacob" (v2).** This is the last of the ten "generations" (*toledot*) headings in Genesis. It names Jacob, but the story that follows is mostly about his sons, especially Joseph and Judah. The account of a man is the account of what came from him.
- **"A boy with the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah" (v2).** Joseph works under the sons of the two servant wives, Dan, Naphtali, Gad and Asher, probably the lowest-ranked brothers in the family. **"Joseph brought a bad report of them."** The word for report, *dibbah*, usually means something negative: whispering or slander, as with the spies' "bad report" of the land (Num 13:32). The Hebrew dictionary takes it here as a true report of real wrongdoing. Readers have disagreed about Joseph. The Tyndale notes and JFB see him as faithful, "not as a gossiping telltale, but as a 'faithful steward,'" in JFB's words. Others think he comes across as a young informer who did not mind being his father's eyes. The text tells us what he did and leaves us to weigh it.
- **"Israel loved Joseph more than any other of his sons" (v3).** Jacob was the favored son of his mother (25:28), and he married for love and openly preferred Rachel (29:30). Now he repeats the pattern with Rachel's son. "Son of his old age" fits Joseph, born when Jacob was about ninety-one, even though Benjamin is younger.
- **"A robe of many colors" (v3).** The Hebrew is *ketonet passim*. The dictionary takes *passim* as "palms and soles," a tunic reaching to the wrists and ankles. "Many colors" comes from the old Greek translation and the King James tradition. Either way it was not work clothing. You cannot herd sheep in long sleeves. The only other robe called by this name was worn by King David's daughter Tamar, "for thus were the virgin daughters of the king dressed" (2 Sam 13:18). The robe marked Joseph as the son of honor, perhaps as the one Jacob intended to treat as firstborn in place of Reuben.
- **"They hated him and could not speak peacefully to him" (v4).** "Peacefully" is *shalom*. The brothers could not wish him *shalom*, the everyday greeting. Their hatred grows in three steps: they hated him (v4), "hated him even more" (v5), and "hated him even more" (v8).
- **The first dream: the sheaves (vv5–8).** In the dream the brothers' sheaves of grain bow to Joseph's sheaf. The picture is fitting, since in the end it is grain that brings the brothers to Joseph's feet in Egypt (42:6). The brothers understand it immediately: "Are you indeed to reign over us?" The Hebrew doubles each verb for emphasis, something like "Will you really reign over us?"
- **The second dream: sun, moon and eleven stars (vv9–10).** Now the whole family bows, including Jacob. Pharaoh later learns that a dream given twice means "the thing is fixed by God" (41:32). Revelation uses similar imagery for God's people: a woman "clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars" (Rev 12:1).
- **"Shall I and your mother…?" (v10).** Rachel has already died (35:19). Some think the dream was told before her death and the account is not in strict order. Others think "your mother" means Leah, or the family's mothers generally, or that Jacob is simply mocking the dream's logic. The family did bow, though Jacob himself never bowed to Joseph in the way the dream suggests. He did bow toward him at the head of his bed (47:31), or as Hebrews reads it, "bowing in worship over the head of his staff" (Heb 11:21).
- **"His father kept the saying in mind" (v11).** Jacob rebukes Joseph in public but wonders in private. Jacob himself had received dreams from God (28:12; 31:10–13) and knew God might choose a younger son. Luke uses the same kind of language of Mary, who "treasured up all these things, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19).
- **Was Joseph wise to tell the dreams?** He told them twice, to brothers who already hated him. Most commentators think he was naive or proud, a seventeen-year-old who did not yet know how to carry what God had shown him. Others note that the dreams were from God and Joseph simply told the truth. Both can be true. God gave the dreams; Joseph had a lot to learn.
- **Commentators:** Henry lists three reasons for the brothers' hatred: Joseph reported their wrongdoing, his father loved him most, and "he dreamed of his dominion over them." Guzik is blunt that telling the dreams showed a lack of tact and some self-focus, and he adds that not everything God shows a person is meant to be broadcast. He also sees the sheaves as a hint of the food Joseph would one day provide. JFB defends Joseph's report as faithful, and calls the coat "a dress of distinction," made in early times by sewing patches of colored cloth together. The lexicon supports a long-sleeved, full-length tunic rather than a patchwork, and English Bibles still differ. JFB has no note on v10.

#### Joseph Sold by His Brothers (vv12–36)
- **"Pasture their father's flock near Shechem" (v12).** Shechem is about fifty miles north of Hebron, a long way to move flocks, and a strange place for this family to go after what Simeon and Levi did there (ch 34). Jacob does not seem to see the danger to Joseph, any more than he saw it before.
- **"Here I am" (v13).** Joseph answers with *hinneni*, the same ready word Abraham used to God (22:1, 11) and Jacob to the angel (31:11). He goes willingly, knowing how his brothers feel about him. **"See if it is well with your brothers" (v14)** is literally "see the *shalom* of your brothers," the very thing they refused him (v4).
- **"A man found him wandering in the fields" (vv15–17).** This nameless man has no reason to be in the story except to show that the road to Egypt was not a matter of chance. Without him Joseph would have gone home. Rashi and other Jewish interpreters said he was an angel. The text only says "a man." **Dothan** was about fifteen miles farther north, near a main trade route that ran from Gilead across to the coast and down to Egypt. Its name probably means "two wells." Centuries later the prophet Elisha was at Dothan when an army surrounded the town and God opened his servant's eyes to see the horses and chariots of fire (2 Kgs 6:13–17).
- **"Here comes this dreamer" (v19).** Literally "this master of dreams," a sneer. **"We will see what will become of his dreams" (v20).** This is the irony that drives the whole story. The brothers act to kill the dreams, and their act becomes the very road by which the dreams come true.
- **Reuben: "Shed no blood" (vv21–22).** The narrator lets us into Reuben's mind: he meant "to restore him to his father." As eldest, he would answer to Jacob for Joseph. He may also have wanted to win back his father's favor after his sin with Bilhah (35:22). But he does not stand openly against his brothers. He suggests a delay and then leaves (v29).
- **"They stripped him of his robe" (v23).** The robe that marked him out is the first thing they take. Changes of clothes mark the turning points of Joseph's story: stripped here, his garment left in the hand of Potiphar's wife (39:12), shaved and changed for Pharaoh (41:14), and dressed in fine linen as ruler of Egypt (41:42).
- **"The pit was empty; there was no water in it" (v24).** A pit (*bor*) was a cistern cut into the rock to store rainwater, often shaped like a bottle, narrow at the top and wide below, so a person could not climb out. Dry, it was a natural prison, as it was for Jeremiah (Jer 38:6). Joseph cried out. Genesis does not say so here, but the brothers later remember it: "we saw the distress of his soul, when he begged us and we did not listen" (42:21).
- **"Then they sat down to eat" (v25).** They eat their meal within earshot of the pit. That one line says more about their hearts than a paragraph could. JFB speaks of the "fiendish satisfaction" with which they sat down to their food.
- **"A caravan of Ishmaelites coming from Gilead" (v25).** Gilead, east of the Jordan, was famous for its "balm," a healing resin (Jer 8:22). Gum, balm and myrrh were valuable aromatic resins, used in Egypt for medicine, incense and embalming. Joseph is sold to men carrying perfume to Egypt, and Egypt is where he will one day embalm his father (50:2).
- **Judah: "What profit is it…?" (vv26–27).** Judah steps forward for the first time. His argument mixes conscience and calculation: killing Joseph gains nothing, and "he is our brother, our own flesh," so sell him instead. It is better than murder, but it is still selling a brother into slavery. Judah's journey from this moment to his offer to become a slave in Benjamin's place (44:33) is one of the great threads of the story.
- **Ishmaelites and Midianites (vv25–28, 36).** The caravan is called Ishmaelite (vv25, 27, 28; 39:1) and Midianite (vv28, 36). Both peoples descended from Abraham, Ishmael through Hagar and Midian through Keturah (25:2). The two names seem to overlap: in Judges 8:24 the Midianites defeated by Gideon are said to have worn gold earrings "because they were Ishmaelites." So "Ishmaelites" may be a general term for desert traders, and "Midianites" the specific group. Some scholars think two older versions of the story have been woven together. Others read v28 to mean the Midianites pulled Joseph out of the pit before the brothers could sell him, which would explain Reuben's surprise and Joseph's later words that he was "stolen" (40:15). But Joseph also says plainly to his brothers, "you sold me here" (45:4–5), and most readers take "they" in v28 as the brothers.
- **"Twenty shekels of silver" (v28).** A shekel was a weight, not a coin; coins came much later. Twenty shekels was the price later set in Moses' law for a boy between five and twenty years old (Lev 27:5). Ancient records from the early second millennium BC give about the same price for a slave, which fits the period of the story. Kidnapping and selling a person later became a capital crime in Israel (Exod 21:16).
- **"The boy is gone, and I, where shall I go?" (vv29–30).** Reuben tears his clothes, the sign of grief. His half-measure has failed. His first thought, notice, is of himself.
- **The goat and the robe (vv31–33).** The brothers slaughter a goat and dip Joseph's robe in its blood. Years before, Jacob deceived his own blind father with the skins of young goats and his brother's garments (27:9, 15–16). Now Jacob is deceived by his sons with a goat and his son's garment. **"Please identify whether it is your son's robe" (v32).** The Hebrew phrase, *hakker-na*, "identify, please," will come back word for word in the next chapter, when Tamar sends Judah his own seal and staff (38:25). Even here the brothers say "your son," not "our brother."
- **"Joseph is without doubt torn to pieces" (v33).** The brothers never say Joseph is dead. They let Jacob reach the conclusion himself. The lie works by suggestion.
- **"I shall go down to Sheol to my son, mourning" (vv34–35).** Torn garments and sackcloth were the usual signs of deep mourning. "All his daughters" probably includes daughters-in-law as well as Dinah. **Sheol** appears here for the first time in the Bible. It is the Old Testament name for the realm of the dead, the place where all the dead go, pictured as down, dark and silent. The Old Testament reveals only gradually what lies beyond it. JFB calls it "the place of departed souls, where Jacob expected at death to meet his beloved son." Jacob's grief is real. So is the hypocrisy of the sons who "rose up to comfort him."
- **"Sold him in Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard" (v36).** Potiphar's name is Egyptian, probably "he whom Ra gave." "Officer" is *saris*. It often means "eunuch," but the dictionary notes the word can be a general title for a court official, and Potiphar is married (39:1, 7). "Captain of the guard" is literally "chief of the butchers" or "executioners," likely the head of Pharaoh's bodyguard. The chapter ends not in Canaan with the grieving father but in Egypt with the son. While Jacob mourns, God's plan is already in motion. The story pauses for chapter 38 and picks up in 39:1.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the parallel the New Testament invites: "As Joseph was sold by the contrivance of Judah for twenty pieces of silver, so was our Lord Jesus for thirty," and by a man named Judas. On the meal he notes how a hardened conscience can eat in peace, and on Judah's plan he paraphrases Judah: "it will be less guilt, and more gain, to sell him." On the bloody coat, Henry says they sent Jacob the robe "with one colour more than it had had, a bloody colour," and he calls Jacob "an obstinate mourner." Guzik judges Reuben's stand weak and fruitless, and links the brothers' meal to their later confession in 42:21. He quotes F. B. Meyer's line that though Joseph was stripped of his coat, he was not stripped of his character. JFB explains that such caravans traded the goods of Arabia and India "in the regular course of traffic" to Egypt, and that the price would have been small. The Tyndale notes add that the Ishmaelites who now enslave Joseph were descended from the son once cast out for mocking Isaac (21:9). JFB has no notes on v24 or v36.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joseph is the beloved son sent by his father to see the welfare of his brothers, who "saw him from afar" and "conspired against him to kill him." He is stripped of his robe, thrown into a pit, sold for silver by brothers led by Judah, and counted as dead by his father. Jesus too was sent by the Father to his own, "and his own people did not receive him" (John 1:11). He was hated without a cause, sold for silver by Judas (Matt 26:15), stripped of his garments (John 19:23), and handed over to Gentiles. The New Testament draws the Joseph line itself: the patriarchs, "jealous of Joseph, sold him into Egypt; but God was with him" (Acts 7:9). The brothers meant to end the dreams. Instead their crime set them in motion. In the same way, the cross that was meant to finish Jesus became the way God saved his people.
