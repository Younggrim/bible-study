*Setting:* This song sits at the center of the appendix that closes 2 Samuel (chapters 21–24), with a list of warriors on either side and a national crisis at each end. It almost certainly comes from earlier in David's life than its place here suggests. The heading ties it to the day "the LORD delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul," which points to the years after David became king and God gave him rest (7:1), before the Bathsheba affair. The same song appears, with small differences, as Psalm 18. It also forms a pair with Hannah's song at the start of 1 Samuel (1 Sam 2:1–10). Both praise God as the Rock, speak of a lifted "horn," and end with God's "king" and "anointed." The books of Samuel open and close with praise. Like Moses, whose life ends with a song about God the Rock (Deut 32), David's story ends with a song, followed by a shorter set of last words (23:1–7, like Moses' blessing in Deut 33).

#### David's Song of Deliverance (vv1–51)
- **"David spoke to the LORD the words of this song" (v1).** He sings *to* God, not just about him. The Psalm 18 heading adds that David was "the servant of the LORD," the title given to Moses and Joshua.
- **Two versions.** Psalm 18 has a different opening line ("I love you, O LORD, my strength") and dozens of small differences in wording. The likeliest explanation is that David's song was used and lightly revised for public worship, as Henry and JFB both suggest. The two copies support each other, and nothing important hangs on the differences.
- **"The LORD is my rock and my fortress and my deliverer" (v2).** Nine pictures pile up in two verses: rock, fortress, deliverer, rock again, shield, horn, stronghold, refuge, savior. Most of them come straight from David's years as a fugitive. He hid among real rocks, at the "Rock of Escape" where Saul nearly caught him (1 Sam 23:28) and the "Wildgoats' Rocks" (1 Sam 24:2). He lived in a "stronghold" (1 Sam 22:4; 24:22). The places he hid in became pictures of the God who hid him.
- **Two words for rock (vv2–3).** Verse 2 uses *sela*, a crag or cliff, a high place hard to reach. Verse 3 uses *tsur*, a great rock or boulder, the word Moses used of God: "The Rock, his work is perfect" (Deut 32:4). Hannah sang, "there is no rock like our God" (1 Sam 2:2). The title runs through the whole song (vv32, 47).
- **"The horn of my salvation" (v3).** A horn was the symbol of an animal's strength, like a bull's horns lifted in power. Hannah sang, "my horn is exalted in the LORD" (1 Sam 2:1). Zechariah, at the birth of John the Baptist, praised God for raising up "a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" (Luke 1:69). That line looks back to this song.
- **"I call upon the LORD, who is worthy to be praised" (v4).** The whole song in one sentence: call, and be saved.
- **"The waves of death… the torrents of destruction… the cords of Sheol… the snares of death" (vv5–6).** Four pictures of drowning and trapping. "Destruction" is *beliyyaal*, the same word used for the "worthless" Sheba (20:1), here meaning something like ruin, a flood of chaos. Sheol is the grave, the realm of the dead. To the Hebrew mind deep waters stood for chaos and death. David felt pulled under and tied down at once, like an animal in a hunter's snare.
- **"From his temple he heard my voice" (v7).** There was no temple yet. This is God's heavenly dwelling (Ps 11:4). A single cry from a man going under reaches the throne of heaven.
- **"Then the earth reeled and rocked" (v8).** Now comes a theophany, a description of God showing up in power. The images are earthquake, smoke, fire, thick darkness, a storm. They come from Sinai, where "the whole mountain trembled greatly" and the LORD "descended on it in fire" (Exod 19:16–18), and from the old songs of God marching out for Israel (Judg 5:4–5). David is not describing a literal storm on the day Saul missed him. He is saying that every rescue he experienced was the God of Sinai coming down for him.
- **"He bowed the heavens and came down" (v10).** As though heaven were a curtain God pulled down as he stepped through it. Isaiah later prays, "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down" (Isa 64:1).
- **"He rode on a cherub and flew" (v11).** Cherubim were the mighty winged beings who guarded God's presence (Gen 3:24) and whose carved figures sat on the ark (Exod 25:18–22). The LORD was called the one "enthroned on the cherubim" (6:2). Here the throne becomes a chariot. **"Seen on the wings of the wind"** (Psalm 18 has "came swiftly"; in Hebrew the two verbs differ by one similar letter).
- **"He sent out arrows… lightning" (v15).** Lightning as God's arrows. Canaanite poems pictured Baal as the storm god with thunderbolts in his hand. David's song quietly takes the image back: the LORD, not Baal, rides the storm.
- **"The channels of the sea were seen" (v16).** The sea floor laid bare by the blast of God's breath recalls the Red Sea, when "at the blast of your nostrils the waters piled up" (Exod 15:8).
- **"He drew me out of many waters" (v17).** The verb *mashah*, "draw out," is rare. It is the verb that gave Moses his name: "Because I drew him out of the water" (Exod 2:10). God did for David what he did for Moses.
- **"They were too mighty for me" (v18).** The giant-killer admits he was outmatched, just as the warrior lists in chapter 21 admitted he "grew weary" (21:15).
- **"He brought me out into a broad place; he rescued me, because he delighted in me" (v20).** From the narrow trap of vv5–6 to open space. The reason given is God's delight, not David's skill. The same word is used when Jonathan "delighted" in David (1 Sam 19:1) and when Saul pretended to (1 Sam 18:22).
- **"The LORD dealt with me according to my righteousness" (vv21–25).** This troubles readers who remember Bathsheba and Uriah. Three things help. First, the song comes from before that sin (see the setting). Second, "righteousness" and "blameless" (*tamim*, complete, whole, sound) in the Old Testament mean wholehearted loyalty to God's covenant, not sinless perfection. Noah and Job are called *tamim* too (Gen 6:9; Job 1:1). Third, in context David is comparing himself with Saul and his other enemies. He had refused to lift a hand against the LORD's anointed even when he could have (1 Sam 24; 26), and God vindicated him. Psalm 51 shows how the same man spoke after his fall.
- **"With the merciful you show yourself merciful… with the crooked you make yourself seem tortuous" (vv26–27).** God meets people as they come to him. "Merciful" is *chasid*, a person loyal in covenant love (from *chesed*, steadfast love, v51). To the twisted, God seems twisted: he outmaneuvers them and lets their crooked paths lead where crooked paths go. Saul's scheming against David kept turning back on Saul.
- **"You save a humble people, but your eyes are on the haughty to bring them down" (v28).** Hannah's theme exactly: "The LORD… brings low and he exalts" (1 Sam 2:7). Mary sings it again (Luke 1:52).
- **"For you are my lamp, O LORD" (v29).** In the previous chapter David's men called him "the lamp of Israel" (21:17). David knows where his own light comes from.
- **"By you I can run against a troop… leap over a wall" (v30).** Pictures of a soldier's daring: charging a raiding band (the word used for the Amalekite raiders, 1 Sam 30:8) and scaling a city wall, as at Jerusalem (5:6–8).
- **"This God—his way is perfect; the word of the LORD proves true" (v31).** "Proves true" means "refined," like metal tested in fire. God's promises have been put through the fire and come out pure (Ps 12:6; Prov 30:5).
- **"For who is God, but the LORD? And who is a rock, except our God?" (v32).** The center of the song is a statement of God's uniqueness, echoed in Isaiah (Isa 44:8, "Is there a God besides me? There is no Rock").
- **"He made my feet like the feet of a deer" (v34).** Mountain deer and ibex could leap across crags without slipping. David had watched them in the wilderness of Engedi (1 Sam 24:2). God gave him the same sure footing on the heights.
- **"He trains my hands for war, so that my arms can bend a bow of bronze" (v35).** A bow fitted with bronze, or a bow of great strength. The point is strength beyond normal.
- **"Your gentleness made me great" (v36).** One of the loveliest lines in the song. In Psalm 18:35 the Hebrew word is *anvah*, meaning humility or condescension: God's stooping down. Here in Samuel the Hebrew is spelled slightly differently and may come from the verb "to answer," giving "your answering" or "your help made me great." The lexicon lists both, and both are true to the song: the high God stooped down and answered.
- **"I pursued my enemies and destroyed them" (vv37–43).** Hard words to modern ears. David speaks as Israel's king defending God's people against nations that attacked them (8:1–14; 10:1–19), not as a man settling private scores. The Tyndale notes point out that David credits these victories to God rather than boasting. **"They cried to the LORD, but he did not answer them"** (v42) contrasts sharply with v7. David cried and was heard. His enemies cried to the same God, too late and without trust.
- **"You kept me as the head of the nations" (v44).** David's rule reached beyond Israel to Edom, Moab, Ammon and Aram (ch 8). **"Delivered me from strife with my people"** may look back to the civil war with Saul's house (chs 2–4). **"Foreigners came cringing to me"** (v45): "cringing" is literally "lying," meaning they flattered and feigned submission.
- **"The LORD lives, and blessed be my rock" (v47).** The song returns to where it began, God as the rock. Unlike the idols of the nations, the LORD is alive.
- **"For this I will praise you, O LORD, among the nations" (v50).** Paul quotes this verse in Romans 15:9 as proof that God always meant the Gentiles to praise him for his mercy. David's song carries the mission of the gospel inside it.
- **"Great salvation he brings to his king, and shows steadfast love to his anointed, to David and his offspring forever" (v51).** The last line rests everything on God's covenant promise in 2 Samuel 7: "your throne shall be established forever" (7:16). "Anointed" is *mashiach*, Messiah. Hannah's song ended the same way: God "will exalt the horn of his anointed" (1 Sam 2:10). The two songs frame the books of Samuel, and both end looking for the king God will raise up.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the Psalm 18 form "a second edition with some amendments," made for public worship, and urges believers to "look as far back as we can" when giving thanks. On vv21–25 he says God rewarded David's integrity "though not for his righteousness, as if that had merited any thing." JFB agrees that "the perfection does not relate to degree" but to sincerity, and points to Psalm 51 as the other side of David's story. Guzik treats the righteousness lines as the words of a forgiven man whose hands God had cleansed, and admits some lines are hard to read. Henry says God's "gentleness, his grace and tender mercy, made him great." JFB reads the theophany as figures "drawn from His appearance on Sinai." Guzik adds that the God who loves his people moves heaven and earth when they cry out. All three read the ending past David. Henry: "all his joys and all his hopes terminate, as ours should, in the great Redeemer." JFB sees David's victories as types of "the more illustrious triumphs of David's greater Son," and Guzik points to Paul's use of v50.

#### Pointing to Christ
The song ends with "his anointed… David and his offspring forever," and the New Testament hears Christ in it. Jesus is the horn of salvation from David's house (Luke 1:69). He went down further than David into the waters of death and the cords of the grave, and God drew him out (Acts 2:24, where Peter speaks of God loosing "the pangs of death"). He alone could say "I was blameless before him" without qualification. And through him the nations now join David's song (Rom 15:9).
