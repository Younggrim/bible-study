*Setting:* Psalm 111 has no title and no named author or occasion. It opens a small group of psalms marked by "Praise the LORD!" (*Hallelu-Yah*, Pss 111–117), sung at Israel's festivals. It is an acrostic: after the opening "Praise the LORD," its twenty-two lines each begin with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from *aleph* to *tav*. That shape cannot be seen in English, but it would have helped worshippers memorize the psalm, and it suggests completeness, praise "from A to Z." Psalm 111 is the first of a matched pair. Psalm 112 has exactly the same length and pattern and uses many of the same phrases, but Psalm 111 describes God and his works while Psalm 112 describes the person who fears God. Psalm 111 ends with "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (v10), and Psalm 112 begins, "Blessed is the man who fears the LORD." Because it praises God's "works" from creation through the exodus, the wilderness and the gift of the land, it reads as a short summary of Psalms 104–106. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement from praise, to God's works, to the wisdom they teach.

#### Great Are the Works of the LORD (vv1–4)
- **"Praise the LORD! I will give thanks to the LORD with my whole heart" (v1).** The psalmist starts with himself. Before calling anyone else to praise, he commits to do it, and with his "whole heart." A divided heart cannot praise God properly (Ps 86:11–12). Henry says the psalmist "sets himself for an example."
- **"In the company of the upright, in the congregation" (v1).** Two settings. "Company" (*sod*) means a close circle, a council of friends who share confidences (Ps 25:14, "the friendship of the LORD"). "Congregation" (*'edah*) is the whole assembly of Israel. Praise belongs both in small groups of the faithful and in the great gathering of God's people.
- **"Great are the works of the LORD, studied by all who delight in them" (v2).** "Works" (*ma'asim*) includes creation (Ps 104:24) and God's acts in history. "Studied" (*darash*) means sought out, searched into, investigated. God's works are not just admired from a distance. They reward careful study by people who enjoy them. In 1874 the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge, where the electron would later be discovered, had this verse carved over its door in Latin at the request of James Clerk Maxwell. Christians in science have often seen their work as obeying it.
- **"Full of splendor and majesty is his work" (v3).** "Splendor and majesty" (*hod vehadar*) are royal words (Ps 104:1, where God is "clothed with splendor and majesty"). Here the singular "work" (*po'al*) may point to God's providence, his ongoing activity in the world (Deut 32:4, "his work is perfect").
- **"And his righteousness endures forever" (v3).** This line appears again, word for word, in Psalm 112:3 and 112:9, but there it is said of the righteous man. God's righteousness is eternal in itself. A godly person's righteousness lasts because it reflects his.
- **"He has caused his wondrous works to be remembered" (v4).** Literally "he has made a memorial for his wonders." God did not leave his great acts to fade from memory. He built remembering into Israel's life through festivals. The Passover was called "a memorial day" (*zikkaron*, Exod 12:14), the stones from the Jordan were a memorial (Josh 4:7), and the feasts retold the exodus every year. Jesus did the same at the Last Supper: "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19).
- **"The LORD is gracious and merciful" (v4).** This is a short form of God's self-description to Moses (Exod 34:6), the same words at the heart of Psalm 103:8. Psalm 112:4 will say the same of the godly man.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the psalm "furnishes us with matter for praise from the works of God," beginning with "the greatness of his works and the glory of them." JFB gives "carefully studied" for v2 and says "honourable and glorious" in the KJV of v3 is literally "honor and majesty." Guzik, citing Kidner, explains the difference between the small, intimate "assembly" and the larger congregation, and takes v2 as a charter for science, with Kidner's warning that it has to be read with v10, so that study of God's works does not turn into foolishness claiming to be wisdom (Rom 1:22). He notes the Cavendish inscription, citing Boice.

#### He Remembers His Covenant Forever (vv5–9)
- **"He provides food for those who fear him" (v5).** "Food" here is an unusual word, *tereph*, which usually means prey, food torn by a hunting animal (Ps 104:21, where young lions roar for their *tereph*). It may recall the quail and manna in the wilderness (Ps 105:40), or simply mean daily provision (Prov 31:15; Mal 3:10). The word is a vivid one: God provides for his people as surely as a lion finds its prey.
- **"He remembers his covenant forever" (v5).** This is the theme of Psalm 105 (105:8, 42). God made a memorial of his works so his people would remember (v4), and he himself remembers his covenant.
- **"He has shown his people the power of his works, in giving them the inheritance of the nations" (v6).** The gift of Canaan was proof of God's power (Ps 105:44). The Tyndale notes add that it was also an act of justice against nations whose sin had ripened (Gen 15:16), and a warning that Israel too could lose the land if it lived like them.
- **"The works of his hands are faithful and just; all his precepts are trustworthy" (v7).** The psalm moves from God's works to God's words. "Precepts" (*piqqudim*) are his instructions, the word used often in Psalm 119. What God does and what God says match: both are faithful, true and right.
- **"They are established forever and ever, to be performed with faithfulness and uprightness" (v8).** God's commands are not temporary rules that change with fashion. They rest on his unchanging character. They are also meant to be "performed," done, not merely admired.
- **"He sent redemption to his people" (v9).** "Redemption" (*peduth*) means ransom, release by payment. The psalmist is probably thinking of the exodus, when God redeemed Israel from Egypt (Deut 7:8). Zechariah, John the Baptist's father, echoes this line: "Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has visited and redeemed his people" (Luke 1:68).
- **"He has commanded his covenant forever" (v9).** God does not merely offer his covenant. He decrees it, like a king.
- **"Holy and awesome is his name!" (v9).** "Awesome" (*nora'*) means to be feared, held in reverent awe. The KJV has "holy and reverend is his name," and older writers like Clarke noted that this is the only place "reverend" occurs in the KJV, and that it belongs properly to God, not to ministers. Mary's song has the same ring: "holy is his name" (Luke 1:49).
- **Commentators:** Henry lists among God's works "the conformity of them to his word of promise," showing that what God does keeps what he has said. JFB says God's works and precepts are "reliable and of permanent obligation," and that the deliverance of v9 matches the covenant he established. Guzik, citing Clarke, notes that the word for "food" can mean prey or spoil, and, citing Maclaren, sees two proofs of God's kindness in vv5–6: food in the wilderness and the gift of the land. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says God is no fickle ruler who changes his laws.

#### The Fear of the LORD Is the Beginning of Wisdom (v10)
- **"The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (v10).** This is one of the Bible's key sayings, repeated in Job 28:28, Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10. "Fear" is not terror but reverent awe that takes God seriously, trusts him and obeys him. "Beginning" (*re'shith*) can mean both the starting point and the first or chief part, as in "the firstfruits" (Prov 3:9). Wisdom starts with fearing God, and fearing God is also its heart.
- **Why end with wisdom?** After nine verses on God's works, the psalm says what to do with them. Studying God's works (v2) should lead to fearing God. Knowledge of the world without reverence for its Maker is not yet wisdom. This brings the psalm round to the warning Kidner draws from v2.
- **"All those who practice it have a good understanding" (v10).** "Practice it" is literally "do them," referring to God's precepts (v7). Understanding is not only gained by thinking but by doing. Jesus said the wise man is the one who "hears these words of mine and does them" (Matt 7:24).
- **"His praise endures forever!" (v10).** The psalm ends where it began, with praise. This is the fifth "forever" in the psalm (vv3, 5, 8, 9, 10). God's righteousness, covenant, precepts and praise all last.
- **Commentators:** Henry presents reverent fear and obedience as "the most acceptable way of praising God." JFB calls loving and fearing God "the chief element of true wisdom." Guzik lists the parallels in Job, Proverbs and Ecclesiastes, passes on a saying, cited by Spurgeon, that the fear of God is not only the beginning of wisdom but its whole substance, and notes Morgan's point that this closing line prepares for Psalm 112.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 111 praises God for works worth studying, a covenant he remembers, and a redemption he "sent" to his people. In the fullness of time, God sent redemption in person: "God sent forth his Son… to redeem those who were under the law" (Gal 4:4–5). Jesus is the one in whom "all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" are hidden (Col 2:3), the Word through whom the great works of creation were made (John 1:3). He gave his people a new memorial meal so that his greatest work, his death for sinners, would always be remembered (1 Cor 11:24–26). And in him the God who is "gracious and merciful" is seen most clearly, for "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us… full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).
