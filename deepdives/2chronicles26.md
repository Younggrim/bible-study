*Setting:* Judah, about 792–740 BC. The Chronicler is writing for the Jews who came back from exile in Babylon, probably in the 400s BC, retelling the history of David's line to show what faithfulness and unfaithfulness lead to. King Amaziah (ch 25) began well, turned to the gods of Edom, was crushed by Israel and finally murdered. His son Uzziah inherits a battered kingdom: Jerusalem's wall broken down, treasuries plundered and hostages taken (25:23–24). Kings covers Uzziah, whom it mostly calls Azariah, in seven verses (2 Kgs 15:1–7) and simply says "the LORD touched the king." The Chronicler gives the whole story: fifty-two years of blessing, and then the day pride carried him into the temple with a censer in his hand. Uzziah reigned at the same time as Jeroboam II in the north. It was the last great age of both kingdoms, and the age of the prophets Amos, Hosea, Jonah and the young Isaiah.

#### Uzziah Reigns in Judah (vv1–15)
- **"All the people of Judah took Uzziah, who was sixteen years old, and made him king" (v1).** "The people" chose him, just as they had stepped in at other moments of crisis (2 Chr 22:1; 33:25). His father had been assassinated in a conspiracy (25:27). Many scholars think Uzziah had already been made co-regent years earlier, when Amaziah was captured by Joash of Israel (25:23), and that the fifty-two years count from then.
- **Two names.** Kings mostly calls him Azariah, "the LORD has helped." Chronicles calls him Uzziah, "the LORD is my strength." The Chronicler plays on both names through the chapter: God "helped" him (vv7, 13, 15) until he was "strong" (vv8, 15, 16). The helped man became the strong man, and the strong man forgot who helped him.
- **"He built Eloth and restored it to Judah" (v2).** Eloth (Elath), on the Gulf of Aqaba, was Judah's gateway to the Red Sea trade. Solomon had used the port next to it, Ezion-geber (8:17–18). Getting it back from Edom was a major achievement. It was lost again under Ahaz (2 Kgs 16:6). A seal from this era reading "belonging to Jotham" has been found in the area, possibly connected with Uzziah's son.
- **"He did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, according to all that his father Amaziah had done" (v4).** The comparison is a warning in itself. Amaziah did right "yet not with a whole heart" (25:2), and his reign ended badly. Uzziah would follow the same curve.
- **"He set himself to seek God in the days of Zechariah, who instructed him in the fear of God" (v5).** "Seek" (*darash*) is the Chronicler's favourite word for real faith: turning to God in worship, prayer and obedience (1 Chr 28:9; 2 Chr 7:14). Nothing else is known of this Zechariah. He is not the later prophet of that name. The standard Hebrew text reads "who understood the visions of God"; the ESV follows other manuscripts and early translations with "the fear of God." The pattern recalls Joash, who did right "all the days of Jehoiada the priest" and fell away after his mentor died (24:2, 17–18).
- **"As long as he sought the LORD, God made him prosper" (v5).** This is the key to the chapter, and to Chronicles. "As long as" quietly hints that it will not last. Blessing follows seeking. God's word to Asa sums it up: "If you seek him, he will be found by you, but if you forsake him, he will forsake you" (15:2).
- **"Broke through the wall of Gath and the wall of Jabneh and the wall of Ashdod" (v6).** These were Philistine cities on the coastal plain to the west. Gath had been a major city, home of Goliath; Amos, preaching in Uzziah's reign, mentions its fall (Amos 6:2). Jabneh was later Jamnia, where Jewish scholars gathered after AD 70. Uzziah also built settlements in Philistine territory to secure the coast road.
- **"God helped him against the Philistines and against the Arabians… and against the Meunites" (v7).** The Arabians and Meunites were desert peoples to the south, probably around the Negev and Edom. "Helped" is *'azar*, the root of his name Azariah. **"The Ammonites paid tribute to Uzziah"** (v8): east of the Jordan. **"His fame spread even to the border of Egypt."** His kingdom reached nearly the size of Solomon's in the south.
- **"Uzziah built towers in Jerusalem at the Corner Gate and at the Valley Gate and at the Angle" (v9).** The Corner Gate was the stretch of wall Joash of Israel had broken down in Amaziah's day (25:23). Uzziah repaired and fortified it. There was also a great earthquake in his reign, remembered for centuries (Amos 1:1; Zech 14:5), and some of this rebuilding may have followed it.
- **"He built towers in the wilderness and cut out many cisterns… for he loved the soil" (v10).** Archaeologists have found forts, watchtowers and cisterns in the Negev and the Judean wilderness from this period, including at Qumran. The Shephelah is the low foothills between the Judean hills and the coastal plain. "The fertile lands" is literally "Carmel," which here probably means "garden land" rather than the mountain in the north. **"He loved the soil"** is a lovely and unusual note in a king's biography. Henry: "He did not delight in war, nor addict himself to sports, but delighted in husbandry."
- **"An army of soldiers… 2,600… 307,500" (vv11–13).** The army was organised by clan, with family heads as officers. Large numbers in Chronicles are a recognised puzzle. The Hebrew word for "thousand" (*'eleph*) can also mean a military unit or clan group, so some scholars read much smaller figures. Others take them at face value. Either way the point is a strong, well-ordered force, raised **"to help the king,"** the "help" word again.
- **"Shields, spears, helmets, coats of mail, bows, and stones for slinging" (v14).** Earlier soldiers usually supplied their own weapons. Uzziah equipped the whole army from the royal arsenal, a sign of a strong central state.
- **"In Jerusalem he made machines, invented by skillful men… to shoot arrows and great stones" (v15).** The Hebrew is *chishshevonot*, "inventions" or "devices," from the verb "to think, devise." JFB calls this "the first notice that occurs in history of the use of machines" for throwing missiles. Modern historians are more cautious. True catapults are first recorded in the Greek world around 400 BC, so many think these were protective structures on the walls that let archers and slingers shoot and drop stones safely. Assyrian carvings of the siege of Lachish (701 BC) show just such shielded platforms on Judah's walls.
- **"For he was marvelously helped, till he was strong" (v15).** The section ends on a carefully balanced line. "Marvelously" (*hiphli'*) comes from the word for God's wonders. "Till he was strong" marks the turning point. The very strength God gave became the soil for pride.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "As long as Uzziah sought the Lord, and minded religion, God made him to prosper." JFB calls Zechariah "a wise and pious counsellor" and says Uzziah "loved and encouraged all branches of agriculture." He thinks Uzziah was "marvellously helped till he was strong" because he acted as God's viceroy. Guzik notes the throne came to Uzziah amid the wreckage of his father's reign, and he admires the king's care for the land and for ordinary people. He also discusses whether the "machines" were early catapults, more carefully than JFB. Guzik passes on Selman's point that the names Uzziah and Azariah come from closely related Hebrew roots.

#### Uzziah's Pride and Punishment (vv16–23)
- **"But when he was strong, he grew proud, to his destruction" (v16).** Literally "his heart was lifted up." Moses warned that this was the great danger of prosperity: "Then your heart be lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God" (Deut 8:14). The same was said of Hezekiah later (32:25). Proverbs puts it simply: "Pride goes before destruction" (Prov 16:18).
- **"For he was unfaithful to the LORD his God" (v16).** "Unfaithful" is *ma'al*, one of the Chronicler's key words. It means breaking faith, trespassing on what belongs to God. It is the sin that cost Saul his kingdom (1 Chr 10:13) and the sin that finally sent Judah into exile (2 Chr 36:14). For the Chronicler's readers, newly back from that exile, it was a loaded word.
- **"Entered the temple of the LORD to burn incense on the altar of incense" (v16).** The altar of incense stood inside the Holy Place, just before the curtain of the Most Holy Place (Exod 30:1–10). Only priests from Aaron's family could go in and offer incense, morning and evening (Exod 30:7–8). Anyone else who came near was to die (Num 18:7). The story of Korah, whose followers were burned up while offering incense, was the classic warning (Num 16:35, 40).
- **Why would a good king do this?** Perhaps he wanted to be a priest-king like the rulers of surrounding nations, whose kings regularly acted as high priests. Perhaps he pictured himself like David or Solomon, who led worship and offered sacrifices at dedications (2 Sam 6:17–18; 1 Kgs 8:62–64). But neither entered the Holy Place to burn incense. Whatever the reason, the Chronicler calls the root of it pride.
- **"Azariah the priest went in after him, with eighty priests of the LORD who were men of valor" (v17).** Note the name: the priest who stands against King Azariah-Uzziah is also called Azariah, "the LORD has helped." **"Men of valor"** is the same phrase used of soldiers (v12). It took courage to confront the most powerful king Judah had had in two hundred years. Compare Uriah the priest, a generation later, who meekly built Ahaz's pagan altar (2 Kgs 16:10–16).
- **"It is not for you, Uzziah… but for the priests, the sons of Aaron, who are consecrated to burn incense" (v18).** The priests do not argue his skill or his sincerity. They point to God's word. The two offices, king and priest, were kept separate in Israel. Even the greatest king had his limits. **"It will bring you no honor from the LORD God."** He wanted glory and would get disgrace.
- **"Then Uzziah was angry. Now he had a censer in his hand" (v19).** The censer was a fire pan holding coals, onto which incense was sprinkled. Picture the king, red-faced with rage, holding it up. **"Leprosy broke out on his forehead in the presence of the priests."** The judgment came in the very moment and the very place of his sin, by the altar, and on the most visible part of his body. The Hebrew word for leprosy (*tsara'at*) covered a range of skin diseases, but whatever its exact form, it made him ritually unclean (Lev 13).
- **Miriam and Gehazi.** Uzziah is the third person in the Old Testament struck with leprosy as a direct judgment. Miriam was struck for challenging Moses' God-given authority (Num 12:10), and Gehazi for greed and lying (2 Kgs 5:27). In each case the sin was grasping at what God had not given.
- **"They rushed him out quickly, and he himself hurried to go out, because the LORD had struck him" (v20).** The priests now had a second reason to remove him: an unclean person in the holy place defiled it. The king himself hurried out. His rage turned to fear when he realised God had answered.
- **"King Uzziah was a leper to the day of his death, and… lived in a separate house" (v21).** The "separate house" is literally "a house of freedom," probably meaning freedom from duties, though some ancient parallels suggest confinement. **"He was excluded from the house of the LORD."** The king who forced his way in to the Holy Place could now not even enter the courts. **"Jotham his son was over the king's household"**: his son ruled as regent, probably from about 750 BC. Uzziah may have lived on for ten years as a king in name.
- **"The rest of the acts of Uzziah, from first to last, Isaiah the prophet the son of Amoz wrote" (v22).** Isaiah wrote a history of Uzziah's reign that has not survived. It is not the book of Isaiah. Isaiah began prophesying "in the days of Uzziah" (Isa 1:1), and his call came "in the year that King Uzziah died" (Isa 6:1). In that vision Isaiah saw the true King "high and lifted up" in the temple, with seraphim crying "Holy, holy, holy." He saw an altar from which a coal was taken to cleanse his own lips (Isa 6:6–7). Uzziah had grasped at the altar and gone out a leper. Isaiah confessed he was unclean and was made clean from it.
- **"They buried him… in the burial field that belonged to the kings, for they said, 'He is a leper'" (v23).** He was laid near the royal tombs but not in them. In 1931 a stone plaque was found in Jerusalem, from around the first century AD, in Aramaic: "Here were brought the bones of Uzziah, king of Judah. Do not open." Centuries later his bones were moved, and still his grave was marked as different. His epitaph, in effect, was "He is a leper."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Pride of heart was at the bottom of his sin; a lust that ruins many," and "Those that covet forbidden honours, forfeit allowed ones." He ends: "Let all that read say, The Lord is righteous." JFB puts the sin down to "the intoxicating influence of overweening pride and vanity," and says God acted to "vindicate the sacredness of the priestly office." He thinks Uzziah was buried apart because a leper's body would defile the royal tombs. Guzik argues that prosperity can be more dangerous than hardship, and that God may wisely hold back blessings some people could not handle. He points out that the offices of king and priest were kept apart in Israel until they were joined in Christ. Drawing on Spurgeon, he warns that mature believers are as open to self-trust as young ones, and that a late fall can colour a whole life. Guzik and JFB both connect the king's death to Isaiah's vision.

#### Pointing to Christ
Uzziah wanted to be both king and priest, and it was the one thing God had not given him. The Old Testament kept the two offices apart, but it also looked forward to one who would hold both: "You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek," spoken to David's Lord, who sits at God's right hand (Ps 110:1, 4), and "he shall sit and rule on his throne. And there shall be a priest on his throne" (Zech 6:13). Jesus is that King-Priest (Heb 7:1–3, 24–26). He did not grasp at the honour but "did not exalt himself to be made a high priest" and was appointed by the Father (Heb 5:5). He entered the true holy place, not with a censer in his hand but with his own blood (Heb 9:11–12). Where Uzziah went in proud and came out a leper, Jesus went in for us, and he touches lepers and makes them clean (Mark 1:41).
