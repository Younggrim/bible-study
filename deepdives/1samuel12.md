*Setting:* Around 1050 BC at Gilgal, near Jericho, where Israel first camped after crossing the Jordan. Saul has just rescued Jabesh-gilead from the Ammonites (ch 11), and the whole nation has gathered to "renew the kingdom," confirming him as king with peace offerings and great joy (11:14–15). Samuel has led Israel as prophet, priest and judge since his boyhood at Shiloh. The people had asked for a king "like all the nations" (8:5, 20), and the LORD had told Samuel, "they have rejected me from being king over them" (8:7). Now, in the middle of the celebration, Samuel stands up to speak. Chapter 12 is his farewell address as Israel's judge. He clears his own record, reviews the LORD's faithfulness, calls a storm out of a clear sky, and then gives the nation a choice that will hang over the whole monarchy: follow the LORD, king and people together, or be swept away.

#### Samuel's Farewell Address (vv1–25)
- **"I have obeyed your voice… and have made a king over you" (v1).** Samuel begins by putting the responsibility where it belongs. "Obeyed your voice" is the phrase the LORD used in 8:7, 9 and 22: "Obey the voice of the people." The king was the people's request, which God granted. Samuel did what he was told, even though it grieved him.
- **"The king walks before you, and I am old and gray" (v2).** "Walk before" is the language of a leader going ahead of the people, like a shepherd in front of the flock. The baton is being passed. Samuel is not retiring from prayer or prophecy (v23), but from governing.
- **"My sons are with you" (v2).** A painful line. Samuel's sons, Joel and Abijah, had taken bribes as judges at Beersheba, and their corruption was one reason the elders demanded a king (8:1–5). "With you" probably means they now stand among the people as ordinary citizens. Samuel does not hide them or defend them.
- **"I have walked before you from my youth until this day" (v2).** Samuel's public life began as a boy serving at the tabernacle (2:18; 3:19–21). Everyone in the crowd had grown up with him as their leader.
- **"Testify against me before the LORD and before his anointed" (v3).** This is the language of a courtroom. "His anointed" (*meshiach*, the word behind "Messiah") is Saul, anointed in 10:1. It is the first time Saul is called by this title. Hannah had prophesied that the LORD would "exalt the power of his anointed" (2:10) long before Israel had any king.
- **"Whose ox have I taken? Or whose donkey?" (v3).** Samuel's questions answer, point by point, the warning he gave about kings in chapter 8. A king "will take" (8:11–17, the verb repeated again and again) your sons, daughters, fields, servants and donkeys. Samuel has taken nothing. The words also echo Moses, who said in a similar crisis, "I have not taken one donkey from them" (Num 16:15). "Bribe to blind my eyes" recalls the law against bribery (Exod 23:8; Deut 16:19), which his own sons had broken (8:3).
- **"You have not defrauded us or oppressed us" (v4).** The people agree without hesitation. For a man who had held power for decades, it is a remarkable verdict. Paul could make a similar appeal: "we have wronged no one, we have corrupted no one, we have taken advantage of no one" (2 Cor 7:2; compare 1 Thess 2:10).
- **"The LORD is witness against you, and his anointed is witness" (v5).** Samuel turns the testimony around. If the old government was honest, then the people cannot blame the change on Samuel's failure. The fault for rejecting God's way of ruling lies with them. "He is witness," the people reply, binding themselves to their own words.
- **"The LORD… who appointed Moses and Aaron" (v6).** The Hebrew is literally "who *made* Moses and Aaron." Israel's leaders have always been the LORD's choice and the LORD's gift. The point is aimed at a nation that thinks it has chosen its own king.
- **"Stand still that I may plead with you" (v7).** "Plead" is courtroom language again (the verb *shaphat*, "to judge" or "to enter into judgment"). The prophet now brings a case against Israel, and the evidence is "the righteous deeds of the LORD." The Hebrew word is *tsidqot*, "righteousnesses," the LORD's saving acts that proved him faithful to his covenant. Deborah's song uses the same word for the LORD's victories (Judg 5:11).
- **From Egypt to Canaan (v8).** Samuel retells Israel's history in a few strokes: Jacob went into Egypt, the Egyptians oppressed them, the people cried out, the LORD sent Moses and Aaron, and the LORD settled them "in this place." Every main verb in the rescue belongs to God.
- **"But they forgot the LORD their God. And he sold them" (v9).** "Sold" is the vivid word from the book of Judges (Judg 2:14; 3:8; 4:2). Samuel names three oppressors: Sisera, commander of Hazor's army (Judg 4), the Philistines (Judg 13; 1 Sam 4), and Eglon king of Moab (Judg 3:12–30). This is not a full list. It is a sampling of the pattern.
- **"We have sinned… we have served the Baals and the Ashtaroth" (v10).** The confession quotes Judges 10:10. Baal was the Canaanite storm god, believed to send rain and fertility, and Ashtoreth (plural Ashtaroth) was a goddess of fertility and war. Israel kept looking to them for the very things the LORD had always given. That detail makes the storm in v18 especially pointed.
- **"Jerubbaal and Barak and Jephthah and Samuel" (v11).** Jerubbaal is Gideon's other name, meaning "let Baal contend" (Judg 6:32). The Hebrew text actually reads "Bedan," a name found in no list of judges. The ESV follows the Greek and Syriac translations, which read "Barak," and footnotes "Bedan." Others guess it is a short form of Abdon (Judg 12:13) or a nickname for Samson. Some ancient copies also read "Samson" instead of "Samuel," since it seems odd for Samuel to list himself, though it fits that he is giving a historical summary in which the LORD sent him too (7:3–14). No major teaching depends on the choice.
- **"You lived in safety" (v11).** The point of the list: every time Israel was in danger and turned back, the LORD raised up a rescuer. They never needed a king to be safe.
- **"When you saw that Nahash… you said, 'No, but a king shall reign over us,' when the LORD your God was your king" (v12).** This adds something chapter 8 did not tell us. The Ammonite threat was already brewing when the elders asked for a king, so the request was partly driven by fear. Instead of crying out to the LORD as their fathers did, they reached for a human solution. That is the heart of the sin: they had a King and wanted another.
- **"Behold the king whom you have chosen… behold, the LORD has set a king over you" (v13).** Both sides are true at once. Israel chose Saul and asked for him, and yet the LORD gave him. God takes a request born of unbelief and still works through it. The name Saul (*Sha'ul*) means "asked," and the verb "asked" (*sha'al*) runs like a refrain through the chapter (vv13, 17, 19).
- **"If you will fear the LORD and serve him and obey his voice… it will be well" (v14).** This is the covenant from Sinai, applied to the new situation (Exod 19:5; Deut 28; 30:15–20). The Hebrew of v14 is actually an unfinished sentence, literally "If you fear the LORD… and both you and the king follow the LORD your God," and the ending is left hanging. Most translations, like the ESV, supply "it will be well." Either way, the king is not above the covenant. He is one more Israelite who must follow the LORD (Deut 17:18–20).
- **"Then the hand of the LORD will be against you and your king" (v15).** Having a king will not shield Israel from God's discipline. The "hand of the LORD" that struck the Philistines in chapters 5–6 can turn against his own people.
- **"Is it not wheat harvest today?" (v17).** Wheat was harvested around late May and June, in the dry season when it almost never rains in Israel. Proverbs uses the image for something out of place: "like snow in summer or rain in harvest" (Prov 26:1). A storm in harvest could also damage the standing grain and the gathered sheaves. Samuel announces it in advance so no one can call it chance.
- **"The LORD sent thunder and rain that day" (v18).** Samuel had once prayed and the LORD thundered against the Philistines at Mizpah (7:10). Now the thunder is a warning to Israel. It also answers Baal worship directly. The so-called storm god never did anything like this. The LORD alone commands the weather. Psalm 99:6 remembers Samuel as one of those "who called upon his name," and Jeremiah 15:1 ranks him with Moses as a great intercessor.
- **"All the people greatly feared the LORD and Samuel" (v18).** The same words describe Israel after the Red Sea: they "feared the LORD, and they believed in the LORD and in his servant Moses" (Exod 14:31). Samuel is presented as a second Moses.
- **"Pray for your servants to the LORD your God, that we may not die" (v19).** Notice "*your* God," not "our God." The people feel far from the LORD and ask the prophet to stand between them, as Israel asked Moses at Sinai (Exod 20:19). They also finally name their sin: "we have added to all our sins this evil, to ask for ourselves a king."
- **"Do not be afraid; you have done all this evil. Yet do not turn aside" (v20).** Samuel neither waves away the sin nor leaves them crushed by it. The past cannot be undone, and the king will not be taken back. What they can do is follow the LORD from here on, "with all your heart." That is the gospel shape of repentance: real sin, real fear, and then a real way forward.
- **"Empty things that cannot profit or deliver, for they are empty" (v21).** "Empty" is *tohu*, the word for the earth "without form" in Genesis 1:2 and for trackless wasteland (Deut 32:10). Isaiah uses it for idols and the people who make them (Isa 41:29; 44:9). Samuel says it twice. Idols are not a neutral alternative. They are a void. The word may also glance at the new king: a king, too, cannot "deliver" if he replaces the LORD.
- **"The LORD will not forsake his people, for his great name's sake" (v22).** Here is the reason for hope, and it rests on God, not on Israel. "For his name's sake" means for the sake of his own reputation and character, which he has staked on this people before the nations (Josh 7:9; Ps 23:3; Ezek 36:22). "It has pleased the LORD to make you a people for himself": he chose them because he wanted to, not because they deserved it (Deut 7:7–8). Paul echoes this verse when he asks, "has God rejected his people? By no means!" (Rom 11:1–2).
- **"Far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you" (v23).** This is one of the great verses on prayer in the Old Testament. The people had rejected Samuel's leadership, yet he calls it *sin* to stop praying for them. Prayerlessness for God's people is not merely a missed opportunity. It is disobedience. And he adds teaching: "I will instruct you in the good and the right way." Prayer and the word belong together, as the apostles later said (Acts 6:4).
- **"Consider what great things he has done for you" (v24).** Obedience flows from remembering. The whole speech has been an exercise in remembering, and Samuel ends on gratitude, not threat.
- **"You shall be swept away, both you and your king" (v25).** "Swept away" (*saphah*) is the word used of Sodom (Gen 18:23; 19:15). David later uses it of Saul's death in battle (26:10). The warning hangs over the whole history of the kings and comes true when Judah and its king are carried into exile (2 Kgs 25).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the chapter Samuel's "farewell sermon to that august assembly and Saul's coronation sermon," and says that by clearing his own name Samuel "set an example before Saul." On the storm he writes, "Some will not be brought to see their sins by any gentler methods than storms and thunders." Guzik points out that Baal was thought to control rain and thunder, so the sign answered Israel's idolatry. He explains the timing too: Samuel waited until Saul was established, so no one could say he acted out of wounded pride. On v23, Henry: "We sin if we restrain prayer, and in particular if we cease praying for the church," and he notes that the people only asked for prayer, but Samuel promises "to teach them" as well. Guzik makes the same point, that failing to pray for God's people is sin for a leader. JFB stresses that by affirming Samuel's honesty the people admitted "the blame of desiring a change of government rested with themselves," and calls the harvest storm as clear "a proof of a divine mission" as could be given. On v11 JFB prefers the reading "Samson" for "Samuel," while Guzik lists Barak, Samson and Jair as possibilities for "Bedan." The Tyndale notes add the detail that the people say "your God" in v19, a sign of distance.

#### Pointing to Christ
Samuel stands before the nation with clean hands, asks, "Whose ox have I taken?", and no one can accuse him. One day a greater Prophet would ask, "Which of you convicts me of sin?" (John 8:46), and no one could answer. Israel wanted a king to save them from their enemies, when they already had a King. The LORD gave them Saul, and the story of the monarchy shows that no human king could carry the covenant's weight. Only the LORD's true Anointed could keep it perfectly and bear its curse for those who broke it. Samuel refused to stop praying for a people who had rejected him. Jesus prayed for those who crucified him (Luke 23:34), and "he always lives to make intercession" for his people (Heb 7:25). The ground of hope is the same in both covenants: the LORD will not forsake his people, "for his great name's sake."
