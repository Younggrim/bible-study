*Setting:* About 835–796 BC in Judah. Joash (also spelled Jehoash), the boy rescued from his grandmother Athaliah's massacre and crowned at seven in the temple courts (ch 11), now reigns for forty years. For most of that time Jehoiada the priest, who hid him and made him king, guides him. Chapter 12 gives the official account of his reign in Kings. 2 Chronicles 24 tells the same story at greater length and adds a darker turn that Kings only hints at: after Jehoiada's death, Joash abandoned the LORD and had Jehoiada's son stoned in the temple courts. Reading the two together explains why a reign that began so well ends with Joash paying off an enemy and dying at the hands of his own officers.

#### Jehoash Reigns in Judah (vv1–3)
- **"In the seventh year of Jehu, Jehoash began to reign, and he reigned forty years" (v1).** About 835–796 BC. Kings now dates Judah's kings by Israel's, and the reverse, as it moves back and forth between the two kingdoms. **"His mother's name was Zibiah of Beersheba."** Kings names the mothers of Judah's kings because the queen mother held real influence. Beersheba is at Judah's far southern edge, a long way from Jezebel's Phoenicia, and the name may hint that this king's mother was a better influence than Ahaziah's.
- **"Jehoash did what was right in the eyes of the LORD all his days, because Jehoiada the priest instructed him" (v2).** The Hebrew can be read two ways: "all his days in which Jehoiada instructed him," or "all his days, because Jehoiada had instructed him." Chronicles settles it: "all the days of Jehoiada the priest" (2 Chr 24:2). Joash's faith leaned on another man's. That is right for a child, and dangerous for a grown king. Guidance from a godly mentor is a gift, but it is not a substitute for one's own walk with God.
- **"Nevertheless, the high places were not taken away" (v3).** "High places" were local hilltop shrines. Some were used for worship of other gods, and some for worshiping the LORD outside the temple, contrary to the law's command to worship at the one place God chose (Deut 12:2–14). Even good kings of Judah left them standing (1 Kgs 15:14; 22:43) until Hezekiah and Josiah (18:4; 23:8). The people loved them, and removing them would have been costly. The writer marks it every time.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that a young ruler with good counsel is greatly blessed, and that Joash "did that which was right as long as Jehoiada lived to instruct him." He suggests even the priest may have tolerated the high places while the temple lay in poor repair. JFB credits Joash's right conduct "more to the salutary influence of his preserver and tutor, Jehoiada," and says popular attachment to the high places was too strong to break. Guzik calls this a half-way reform and, citing Patterson and Austel, ties v2 to Joash's later fall under ungodly advisors.

#### Jehoash Repairs the Temple (vv4–16)
- **Why the temple needed repair.** Solomon's temple was now about 125 years old. Chronicles explains the damage: "the sons of Athaliah, that wicked woman, had broken into the house of God, and had also used all the dedicated things of the house of the LORD for the Baals" (2 Chr 24:7). The house of God had been plundered and neglected for years.
- **Three kinds of money (v4).** "The money of the holy things" was the general term. Within it Joash names "the money for which each man is assessed," probably the half-shekel census tax that Moses set for the tabernacle (Exod 30:12–16; 2 Chr 24:6, 9); "the money from the assessment of persons," money paid to redeem a vow (Lev 27:1–8); and "the money that a man's heart prompts him to bring," freewill gifts (Exod 35:5, 29). Each priest was to take the money from his own "donor," probably the people he served, and use it to repair the house.
- **"By the twenty-third year of King Jehoash, the priests had made no repairs" (v6).** Joash was now about thirty. Twenty-three years into his reign, and maybe many years after the order was given, nothing has been done. Money had come in, but it was going into the priests' own upkeep. It was not necessarily theft, since the priests had to live, but the repairs never got done.
- **"Why are you not repairing the house?" (v7).** The king rebukes Jehoiada himself, the man who crowned him, now an old man (2 Chr 24:15 says he lived to 130). Joash takes the work out of the priests' hands. The priests agree: they will stop collecting, and they will not be responsible for the repairs (v8).
- **"Jehoiada the priest took a chest and bored a hole in the lid of it" (v9).** This is the first collection box in history that we know of. It was set "beside the altar on the right side as one entered the house," where every worshiper would pass it. Chronicles adds that it was placed "outside the gate," that a proclamation went out across Judah, and that "all the princes and all the people rejoiced and brought their tax and dropped it into the chest until they had finished" (2 Chr 24:8–10). The problem had never been unwilling givers.
- **"The king's secretary and the high priest came up and they bagged and counted the money" (v10).** Before coins (which came later), silver was weighed and tied in bags, sometimes sealed and labeled. A royal official and a priest counted it together. Two keys, two witnesses: the system was built so no one person handled the money alone (compare Paul's care in 2 Cor 8:20–21).
- **Carpenters, builders, masons, stonecutters, timber and quarried stone (vv11–12).** The money went straight to the work: the supervisors paid the craftsmen and bought the materials.
- **"There were not made for the house of the LORD basins of silver, snuffers, bowls, trumpets" (v13).** The structure came first, and the ornaments waited. Chronicles adds that when the repairs were finished, the leftover money was used for vessels of gold and silver (2 Chr 24:14). The two accounts fit: none of the repair money went to vessels while the work was going on.
- **"They did not ask for an accounting from the men… for they dealt honestly" (v15).** "Honestly" is literally "in faithfulness" (*be'emunah*). After all the careful counting at the top, the foremen were trusted. The same phrase is used of the workmen in Josiah's day (22:7). Godly work needs both good systems and trustworthy people.
- **"The money from the guilt offerings and the money from the sin offerings… belonged to the priests" (v16).** The law gave the priests a share of certain offerings (Lev 5:16; 7:7; Num 5:8–10). The reform did not take away their living. It only kept the repair money separate.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws two lessons that still apply to church finances: "When public distributions are made faithfully public contributions will be made cheerfully," and, on v16, "Let not the servants of the temple be starved under colour of repairing the breaches of it." JFB lists the three sources of money as the half-shekel offering, redemption payments and voluntary gifts, and observes that sealed bags of money were still a common form of currency in the East in its day. Guzik blames the delay on priestly neglect, not a lack of money. Following Dilday and Wiseman, he notes that a visible box and a clear purpose brought out generous giving.

#### The Death of Joash (vv17–21)
- **What Kings leaves out.** Chronicles fills the gap between vv16 and 17. After Jehoiada died, the princes of Judah came and bowed to Joash, and he listened to them. They abandoned the temple and served Asherah poles and idols. God sent prophets, and they would not listen. Then the Spirit came on Zechariah, Jehoiada's son, and he warned, "Because you have forsaken the LORD, he has forsaken you." At the king's command they stoned him "in the court of the house of the LORD," the same courts where Jehoiada had crowned Joash. Zechariah died saying, "May the LORD see and avenge!" (2 Chr 24:17–22). Jesus refers to this murder in Matthew 23:35 and Luke 11:51. Most scholars think he means this Zechariah, and that he names him as the last murder in the Hebrew Bible, since Chronicles comes last in its order, as Abel is the first.
- **"Hazael king of Syria went up and fought against Gath and took it" (v17).** Hazael, whom Elisha wept over (8:12), had already taken Israel's land east of the Jordan (10:32–33). Now he marches down the coastal road. Gath was the Philistine city of Goliath, near Judah's western border. Some think a smaller town called Gittaim is meant, but most take it as the Philistine city. Excavations at Tell es-Safi, the likely site of Gath, have found a siege trench and a destruction layer from the late 800s BC, which many scholars connect with Hazael.
- **"When Hazael set his face to go up against Jerusalem" (v17).** "Set his face" means he made up his mind. Chronicles adds that a small Syrian army defeated a much larger army of Judah, killed the princes and wounded Joash (2 Chr 24:23–25). It says plainly why: "because Judah had forsaken the LORD."
- **"Jehoash… took all the sacred gifts that Jehoshaphat and Jehoram and Ahaziah his fathers… had dedicated… and sent these to Hazael" (v18).** The temple Joash had spent years restoring, he now strips to pay off an enemy. Asa had done the same (1 Kgs 15:18), and later kings would too (16:8; 18:15–16). It bought time. **"Then Hazael went away from Jerusalem."** But it was a sad picture: the king who once restored God's house now uses God's house to buy his safety instead of turning to God.
- **"His servants arose and made a conspiracy and struck down Joash in the house of Millo" (v20).** "Millo" means "filling," probably the terraces built to support buildings on the slope of the City of David (1 Kgs 9:15). Silla is unknown. Chronicles says Joash was lying wounded in bed and that the conspirators acted "because of the blood of the son of Jehoiada the priest" (2 Chr 24:25).
- **"Jozacar the son of Shimeath and Jehozabad the son of Shomer" (v21).** Chronicles adds that Shimeath was an Ammonite woman and Shomer (or Shimrith) a Moabite woman (2 Chr 24:26). Both assassins had foreign mothers, which may be a quiet comment on how far Judah's court had drifted. Amaziah, Joash's son, later executed them, but not their children, following the law (14:5–6; Deut 24:16).
- **"They buried him with his fathers in the city of David" (v21).** Chronicles adds, "but they did not bury him in the tombs of the kings" (2 Chr 24:25). He lay in David's city but not with David's honored sons. His guardian, Jehoiada the priest, *was* buried among the kings, "because he had done good in Israel, and toward God and his house" (2 Chr 24:16).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the assassination as God's justice working even through men acting wrongly, and adds, "God usually sets marks of his displeasure upon apostates, even in this life." His summary of the reign: "Thus fell Joash, who began in the spirit and ended in the flesh." JFB has no comment here beyond cross-references to Chronicles. Guzik reads the surrender of the treasures as a failure to trust God, and notes there is no record that Joash ever repented. Citing Knapp, he draws the lesson of sowing and reaping.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joash's life is a warning in two parts. He was faithful while a godly man held him up, and when that support was gone, his faith showed itself borrowed. He also restored the temple and then stripped it. No king of Judah could be counted on to guard God's house to the end. Jesus is the King whose zeal for God's house never cooled (John 2:17), who said he would raise the true temple in three days (John 2:19–21), and who builds his people into a living temple that cannot be plundered (Eph 2:19–22; 1 Pet 2:5). And the blood of Zechariah, who cried "May the LORD see and avenge!", is answered by "the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24): the blood of the One who prayed for his murderers.
