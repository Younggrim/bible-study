*Setting:* Directly after Saul's massacre of the priests at Nob (22:6–19). Abiathar, the one surviving priest, has fled to David with the ephod (22:20–23), and the prophet Gad has told David to leave Moab and return to Judah (22:5). David now leads about six hundred men (v13), most of them debtors and outcasts (22:2). He is living in the rough country of southern Judah, where the hills fall away eastward toward the Dead Sea. This chapter shows David acting like the king Saul should be, defending an Israelite town from the Philistines, and asking God before each step. It also shows how alone he is. Even the people he saves and the people of his own tribe are ready to hand him over. Only Jonathan comes to him, and for the last time.

#### David Saves the City of Keilah (vv1–14)
- **"The Philistines are fighting against Keilah and are robbing the threshing floors" (v1).** Keilah was a walled town in the lowlands of Judah, a few miles south of Adullam and close to the Philistine border (Josh 15:44). The threshing floors were open, flat places where the harvested grain was beaten out and winnowed. Raiders struck at harvest time to steal a year's food (compare Gideon hiding his threshing in Judg 6:11). Defending Israel from the Philistines was the very reason Israel had asked for a king (9:16), but Saul is busy chasing David.
- **"David inquired of the LORD" (v2).** This becomes the chapter's refrain (vv2, 4, 9–12). David asks before acting, which is exactly what Saul failed to do at Gilgal (13:8–14) and in the war with Amalek (15). The Tyndale notes point out that David may have prayed directly or used the ephod. The ephod did not arrive until Abiathar fled (v6), so the first two questions may have come through Gad or through prayer.
- **"We are afraid here in Judah; how much more then if we go to Keilah" (v3).** David's men have a point. They are already fugitives in their own country. Now he wants them to fight the Philistines too, with Saul at their backs.
- **"Then David inquired of the LORD again" (v4).** David does not override his men by force of personality, and he does not give in to their fear either. He asks God a second time, and the answer is stronger: "I will give the Philistines into your hand." Gideon also asked twice (Judg 6:36–40), and God was patient with him.
- **"So David saved the inhabitants of Keilah" (v5).** The word "save" (*yasha'*) is the same one used of Saul's early victories (11:13; 14:23). The anointed one is doing the work of a king before he wears the crown. He even brings away the Philistines' livestock, food for his six hundred men.
- **"He had come down with an ephod in his hand" (v6).** This is the high priest's ephod, the vest that held the Urim and Thummim, the sacred lots used to receive a yes-or-no answer from God (Exod 28:30; 1 Sam 14:41). Saul killed the priests and lost the ephod. In slaughtering Nob he cut himself off from God's guidance, and the means of guidance went straight to David.
- **"God has given him into my hand, for he has shut himself in" (v7).** Saul speaks in God's name, but he is wrong. A walled town with gates and bars was safe against raiders but a trap against a siege. Saul now plans to besiege an Israelite town to catch one man, after doing nothing to protect it from the Philistines.
- **"Bring the ephod here" (v9).** David asks two precise questions (vv10–12). Will Saul come? Will the men of Keilah hand him over? God answers both: "He will come down." "They will surrender you."
- **What God knows (vv11–12).** God tells David what *would* happen if he stayed, and because David leaves, it never does. Saul does not come, and Keilah never betrays him. Christians have drawn on this verse in debates about how God knows the future. Some see it as evidence that God knows not only what will happen but what would happen under other choices. Others see it simply as God reading the intentions of Saul and the townspeople. All agree on the plain lesson: God's knowledge is complete, and he used it to protect David.
- **"They will surrender you" (v12).** The people David has just rescued would give him up. They had heard what happened to Nob for helping David (22:19). Fear of Saul was stronger than gratitude.
- **"About six hundred" (v13).** The band has grown from four hundred (22:2). They leave Keilah and go "wherever they could go," a phrase that captures how unsettled their life is. Saul calls off the march.
- **"Saul sought him every day, but God did not give him into his hand" (v14).** This is the summary of the whole wilderness period. Saul says God gave David into his hand (v7). The narrator says God did not. The wilderness of Ziph was the dry, broken country southeast of Hebron, full of caves and natural rock strongholds. Ziph itself lay about four miles southeast of Hebron.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws a pointed lesson from Keilah's danger: "The way for any country to be quiet, is to let God's church be quiet in it." On David's careful questions, he applies it plainly: "We have the Scriptures in our hands, let us take advice from them in doubtful cases." JFB says of Saul's claim in v7, "The wish was father to the thought," and suggests the first inquiries came "most probably through Gad," which fits the order of the text. Guzik observes that Saul neglected his duty to Keilah while chasing David, and stresses that knowing God's will is only half of it, since David then had to obey. He reads God's answers about Keilah as conditional: they were true if David stayed.

#### Saul Pursues David (vv15–29)
- **"David was in the wilderness of Ziph at Horesh" (v15).** *Horesh* means "wood" or "thicket." The place was a small wooded area in the desert hills.
- **"Jonathan… went to David at Horesh, and strengthened his hand in God" (v16).** Jonathan finds David when Saul cannot, perhaps because David was willing to be found by him. "Strengthened his hand" means he encouraged him, put courage back into him. He did it "in God": he did not just cheer David up, he pointed him back to God's promise. Later, at his lowest moment, David "strengthened himself in the LORD his God" (30:6), the same idea.
- **"You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you" (v17).** This is the clearest statement yet from Jonathan. The heir to the throne says out loud that David will be king. He hopes to serve as David's second-in-command. That hope never comes true, since Jonathan dies with his father on Mount Gilboa (31:2). "Saul my father also knows this" is a remarkable line. Saul knows, and fights anyway (compare 24:20).
- **"The two of them made a covenant before the LORD" (v18).** This is the third time they make or renew a covenant (18:3; 20:16–17, 42). This is the last time they see each other. David keeps the covenant years later by caring for Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth (2 Sam 9).
- **"Then the Ziphites went up to Saul at Gibeah" (v19).** These are men of Judah, David's own tribe, offering to betray him. They give Saul exact directions: the strongholds at Horesh, "on the hill of Hachilah, which is south of Jeshimon." *Jeshimon* means "wasteland," the barren desert above the Dead Sea. Psalm 54 is titled "when the Ziphites went and told Saul, 'Is not David hiding among us?'" It opens, "O God, save me by your name," and ends with confidence: "he has delivered me from every trouble" (54:1, 7). The Ziphites will betray him again (26:1).
- **"May you be blessed by the LORD, for you have had compassion on me" (v21).** Saul's self-pity again (compare 22:8). He calls a betrayal "compassion" and blesses it in the LORD's name.
- **"It is told me that he is very cunning" (v22).** Saul wants more surveillance: where David's foot is, who has seen him, every hiding place. "I will search him out among all the thousands of Judah" (v23) shows how obsessed he has become. The king of Israel is now spending his army hunting one loyal man.
- **"The wilderness of Maon, in the Arabah" (v24).** Maon was a town about eight to ten miles south of Hebron, the home of Nabal in chapter 25. "The Arabah" here is the rift valley along the Dead Sea. David keeps moving south and east into drier country.
- **"Saul went on one side of the mountain, and David and his men on the other side" (v26).** This is the closest Saul has come. The two forces are circling the same hill, and Saul's men are "closing in." The picture is of a hunt about to end.
- **"A messenger came to Saul… the Philistines have made a raid" (v27).** At the last possible moment, the Philistines, Israel's enemies, become David's rescuers. The text does not say God sent them, but the timing says it for the reader. Saul must go and defend the land, which he should have been doing all along.
- **"The Rock of Escape" (v28).** In Hebrew, *Sela-hammahlekoth*. The second word comes from a root meaning "divide" or "separate," so the name may mean "Rock of Parting," the place where the two armies were pulled apart. Others connect it to a root for "slippery," the rock where David slipped away. Israelites often named places to remember what God did there (Gen 22:14; 1 Sam 7:12).
- **"The strongholds of Engedi" (v29).** En-gedi, "spring of the young goat," is an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea, with a waterfall and caves in the cliffs above. It is still a nature reserve with ibex today. It sets the scene for the next chapter.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Jonathan "a constant friend" who "renewed his friendship with him," and notes that David "kept God's way, waited God's time." On the Philistine raid he writes, "Sooner than his promise shall fail, he will commission Philistines to effect our escape." Guzik makes the point that Jonathan strengthened David in God by encouragement rather than rescue. He could not end David's troubles, but he could point him to God's promise. JFB explains En-gedi as "the spring of the wild goats or gazelles," which matches the Hebrew. The Tyndale notes add that neither Keilah nor Ziph, both towns of Judah, gave David shelter, so his rise did not come from his own tribe's loyalty but from God.

#### Pointing to Christ
David saves a town that would hand him over, and is betrayed by men of his own tribe. Jesus "came to his own, and his own people did not receive him" (John 1:11). He healed and fed crowds who later shouted for his death, and he was handed over by one of his own. David survived because God kept him: "God did not give him into his hand" (v14). Jesus was handed over, by the will of God, so that he could save the very people who rejected him (Acts 2:23). And Jonathan, who gave up a throne to stand with God's chosen king, shows what it looks like to "strengthen one another's hand in God," the work every believer can do for another (Heb 10:24–25).
