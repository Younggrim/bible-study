*Setting:* About AD 30, in the temple courts during the last week before the crucifixion. Chapter 20 ended with Jesus warning the crowd about scribes who "devour widows' houses" (20:47). Chapter 21 opens with a widow, and then turns to the temple itself. When people admire its beauty, Jesus says it will be thrown down. The disciples ask when, and he answers with his second long teaching in Luke on the future (the first was 17:20–37). Matthew and Mark place this teaching on the Mount of Olives, so it is often called the Olivet Discourse (Matt 24; Mark 13). Luke's version keeps two events in view: the fall of Jerusalem, which came in AD 70, forty years later, and the coming of the Son of Man at the end of the age. Telling the two apart, and seeing how they relate, is the main challenge of the chapter.

#### The Widow's Offering (vv1–4)
- **"Jesus looked up and saw the rich putting their gifts into the offering box" (v1).** In the Court of the Women stood thirteen trumpet-shaped collection chests for different temple offerings. Gifts were made in public, and a large gift of coins clattering into the metal horn would be noticed. Mark says Jesus sat down opposite them and watched (Mark 12:41).
- **"A poor widow put in two small copper coins" (v2).** These were two *lepta*, the smallest coins in use in Judea, each worth about one hundred-twenty-eighth of a denarius, a day's wage. Together they would buy almost nothing. The word translated "poor" here is a word for someone who must work for a living with nothing to spare. She is the kind of widow the scribes devour (20:47).
- **"This poor widow has put in more than all of them" (v3).** God does not count the amount but the cost. The rich gave what they would never miss. She gave "all she had to live on," literally "her whole life" (*bios*). She could have kept one coin. She gave both.
- **How to read it.** Most readers take this as Jesus praising her devotion. Some recent scholars read it as a lament: the temple system, run by men who devour widows, has taken her last coins, and that very temple is about to be destroyed (v6). The text allows both to be true. Jesus honors her gift, and he condemns those who exploit people like her.
- **Commentators:** JFB quotes Bengel: "She might have kept one," and states the lesson that Christ's standard "is not our superfluity, but our deficiency." Henry turns the two coins into a prayer: the poorest have two mites, "a soul and a body," and should offer both. Guzik connects her gift to David's refusal to offer God what cost him nothing (2 Sam 24:24).

#### Jesus Foretells Destruction of the Temple (vv5–9)
- **"Adorned with noble stones and offerings" (v5).** Herod the Great began rebuilding the temple around 20 BC, and work continued until about AD 63, only seven years before it was destroyed. Some of its white limestone blocks were over 30 feet long. Josephus says the gold plating on the front blazed in the sun, and from a distance the building looked like a snow-covered mountain. "Offerings" were gifts dedicated by worshipers and displayed in the temple, such as the golden vine over the entrance to which donors added gold leaves and grapes.
- **"Not... one stone upon another" (v6).** In AD 70 the Roman army under Titus burned the temple during the siege. Josephus says the fire was started against Titus's orders, and that soldiers later pried the stones apart to get at gold that had melted into the cracks. What is left today, such as the Western Wall, is part of the retaining walls around the temple platform, not the temple building.
- **"When will these things be, and what will be the sign?" (v7).** In Luke the question is about the temple's destruction. Matthew's version adds "the sign of your coming and of the end of the age" (Matt 24:3). The disciples probably assumed the two would happen together.
- **"See that you are not led astray" (v8).** Jesus' first concern is not timing but deception. "Many will come in my name, saying, 'I am he!'" Josephus tells of a number of would-be deliverers in the years before AD 70, and about sixty years later Simon bar Kokhba was hailed by some as Messiah before his revolt was crushed. Jesus adds a second warning: "The time is at hand!" Do not follow people who announce the date.
- **"When you hear of wars and tumults, do not be terrified" (v9).** Wars "must first take place," since they are part of God's plan for a fallen world, "but the end will not be at once." Jesus is telling them not to read every crisis as the final one.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus answers "as far as was necessary to teach them their duty; for all knowledge is desirable as far as it is in order to practice." JFB reads "Do not go after them" with Stier's paraphrase, "I come not so very soon." Guzik retells Josephus's account of the fire and the melted gold, and he suggests that the temple had become an idol to many, and God tears down idols, even good things that have become idols.

#### Jesus Foretells Wars and Persecution (vv10–19)
- **"Nation will rise against nation... earthquakes... famines and pestilences" (vv10–11).** Jesus describes the normal troubles of the age between his ascension and return. Matthew and Mark call them "the beginning of the birth pains" (Mark 13:8). The decades before AD 70 had their share: a severe famine under Claudius (Acts 11:28), earthquakes in Asia Minor, and the "year of four emperors" with civil war in AD 69. "Terrors and great signs from heaven" are found only in Luke. Josephus reports strange signs in the sky before the fall of Jerusalem, including what looked like a sword-shaped comet over the city. Such things have happened in every age since.
- **"But before all this they will lay their hands on you" (v12).** The disciples' own suffering comes first. "Synagogues and prisons... kings and governors" is a summary of the book of Acts: Peter and John before the council (Acts 4), Paul flogged in synagogues (2 Cor 11:24), Paul before Felix, Festus and King Agrippa (Acts 24–26).
- **"This will be your opportunity to bear witness" (v13).** Arrest becomes a platform. Paul's chains in Rome advanced the gospel (Phil 1:12–13).
- **"Settle it therefore in your minds not to meditate beforehand" (v14).** "Meditate beforehand" was a technical term for rehearsing a speech in court. Jesus is not forbidding preparation in general (1 Pet 3:15). He is telling them not to panic about what to say.
- **"I will give you a mouth and wisdom" (v15).** In 12:12 Jesus promised the Holy Spirit would teach them. Here he says, "I will give." Stephen's opponents "could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit with which he was speaking" (Acts 6:10).
- **"Delivered up even by parents and brothers" (v16).** Following Jesus divided families, as he warned in 12:51–53. "Some of you they will put to death," and James the brother of John was the first apostle killed (Acts 12:2).
- **"But not a hair of your head will perish" (v18).** This seems to contradict v16, which says some will be killed. The point is that nothing can destroy them. Their bodies may die, but their lives are safe in God's keeping, and they will be raised (12:4–7).
- **"By your endurance you will gain your lives" (v19).** "Endurance" is *hypomonē*, steadfast perseverance under pressure, literally "remaining under" a load. "Gain your lives" means to secure their souls. Endurance does not earn salvation. It is the mark of those who truly belong to Christ (8:15; Matt 24:13).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the contrast between vv16 and 18 shows this promise is "far above immunity from mere bodily harm," and gives the key to reading Psalm 91. JFB also says "the book of Acts verifies all this." Henry: "Though we may be losers for Christ, we shall not, we cannot be losers by him, in the end," and "it is by Christian patience we keep possession of our own souls." Guzik stresses that *hypomonē* is not passive waiting but strong, active endurance that God himself builds in believers.

#### Jesus Foretells Destruction of Jerusalem (vv20–24)
- **"When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies" (v20).** Matthew and Mark at this point speak of "the abomination of desolation" from Daniel (Matt 24:15; Mark 13:14). Luke explains it plainly for Gentile readers: Roman armies around the city. "Its desolation has come near."
- **"Let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains" (v21).** Normally people fled into a walled city when an army came. Jesus says the reverse: get out. The fourth-century historian Eusebius records that the Christians of Jerusalem, warned by a prophecy, left before the siege and settled at Pella, a town east of the Jordan. When Cestius Gallus besieged the city in AD 66 and then unexpectedly withdrew, there was a window to escape. Tradition holds that the church was spared.
- **"These are days of vengeance, to fulfill all that is written" (v22).** The prophets warned that God would judge Jerusalem for breaking his covenant (Deut 28:49–57; Jer 6:1–8; Dan 9:26). Jesus wept over this in 19:41–44. "Vengeance" here is God's just judgment, not personal revenge.
- **"Alas for women who are pregnant and for those who are nursing infants" (v23).** Flight is hardest for them, and siege worst for children. Josephus describes the famine in the city so terribly that it is painful to read. He tells of a mother who ate her own child, the curse of Deuteronomy 28:53–57 fulfilled.
- **"They will fall by the edge of the sword and be led captive among all nations" (v24).** Josephus puts the dead at over a million, which many historians think is too high, and the captives at 97,000. Many were sold as slaves across the empire. After a second revolt in AD 132–135, the emperor Hadrian rebuilt Jerusalem as a pagan city, Aelia Capitolina, and barred Jews from entering it.
- **"Until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled" (v24).** Only Luke has this line. Jerusalem will be under Gentile control for a set period, and that period has an end. Christians differ on what it means. Many dispensational readers, Guzik among them, see a future restoration of Jerusalem to Israel and a final period of God's dealing with Israel in the last days. Others connect it to Paul's words that "a partial hardening has come upon Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in" (Rom 11:25), the age of Gentile mission, which will end with Christ's return. Both agree the word "until" holds out hope.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the Christians fled to Pella "being 'prophetically directed,'" and reads "until" as implying that one day Jerusalem will cease to be trodden down, after the Gentiles have had "their full time" in the church and the Jews are "grafted into their own olive tree" (Rom 11). Henry says Jerusalem's ruin is set before us "to show that sins will not pass unpunished," and asks readers to pray for the day when Jews and Gentiles alike turn to the Lord. Guzik says Israel took control of Jerusalem in 1968 (it was in 1967) while the Temple Mount remains under Muslim administration, and he sees the times of the Gentiles leading into the final seven years of Daniel 9.

#### The Coming of the Son of Man (vv25–28)
- **"Signs in sun and moon and stars" (v25).** The prophets used this language for God's great acts of judgment (Isa 13:10; Joel 2:10, 31). Some take it as picture language for the fall of nations and powers. Others take it more literally of the end of the age. Either way, the whole created order is shaken when God acts.
- **"The roaring of the sea and the waves" (v25).** In the Bible the sea is often a picture of chaos and of restless nations (Ps 65:7; Isa 17:12). "People fainting with fear" (v26): what terrifies the world does not terrify believers, as v28 shows.
- **"The Son of Man coming in a cloud with power and great glory" (v27).** This is Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of Days and is given everlasting dominion. The angels at the ascension promised that Jesus would return in the same way he was taken up, in a cloud (Acts 1:9–11).
- **"Straighten up and raise your heads, because your redemption is drawing near" (v28).** Others shrink in fear. Disciples stand tall and look up. "Redemption" (*apolytrōsis*) means release, as of a slave or captive set free by a payment. It looks to the full freedom of God's people, including the resurrection of the body (Rom 8:23).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the grandeur of the language carries the mind to Christ's second coming, though nearly every expression is used in the Old Testament of God's judgments on nations, and concludes the passage's "immediate reference was to the destruction of Jerusalem, though its ultimate reference beyond doubt is to Christ's final coming." Henry also sees the fall of Jerusalem as "a type and figure of Christ's second coming." Guzik reads vv25–27 as the great tribulation of Revelation 6–18 at the end of the age, followed by Christ's return with his church.

#### The Lesson of the Fig Tree (vv29–33)
- **"Look at the fig tree, and all the trees" (v29).** In Israel most trees stay green in winter, but the fig loses its leaves. When new leaves appear in spring, summer is close. Only Luke adds "and all the trees," which makes it a general lesson from nature, not a special code. Some readers take the fig tree as a symbol of the nation of Israel, but the text does not say so.
- **"When you see these things taking place, you know that the kingdom of God is near" (v31).** The signs are not for calculating a date. They are for recognizing the season and staying ready.
- **"This generation will not pass away until all has taken place" (v32).** This is one of the hardest verses in the Gospels. The word *genea* usually means the people alive at one time, and the lexicon lists this verse under that sense. The main views: (1) "all" refers to the fall of Jerusalem and the signs leading up to it, which did happen within that generation, forty years later. (2) "This generation" means the generation alive when the final signs begin, whenever that is. (3) *genea* means "race" or "kind," either the Jewish people or wicked humanity, which will last until the end. Many evangelical scholars hold the first view, often combined with the idea that Jerusalem's fall was a preview and guarantee of the final coming.
- **"Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (v33).** In Isaiah 40:8 it is "the word of our God" that stands forever. Jesus says the same of his own words. No prophet ever spoke that way.
- **Commentators:** JFB rejects reading "generation" as "this nation," calling it "very unnatural here," and links the verse to 9:27. Henry, by contrast, takes the line to mean the Jewish race, which "shall survive as a nation... when the Son of man shall be revealed." Guzik holds that the generation that sees the final signs will also see the end, and he says the fig tree is only an illustration, not a symbol of Israel. Guzik also notes that the claim in v33 is one no mere man could truthfully make.

#### Watch Yourselves (vv34–38)
- **"Lest your hearts be weighed down with dissipation and drunkenness and cares of this life" (v34).** "Dissipation" is *kraipalē*, a rare word for drunken nausea, the morning-after headache. The danger is not only wild living but ordinary worry. Both make the heart heavy and dull. "Cares of this life" are the thorns that choke the seed in the parable of the sower (8:14).
- **"That day come upon you suddenly like a trap" (v34).** An animal walks calmly into a snare until it springs. Paul uses the same image (1 Thess 5:2–3).
- **"It will come upon all who dwell on the face of the whole earth" (v35).** Unlike the fall of Jerusalem, which one could flee, the final day cannot be escaped by moving away. The only safety is readiness.
- **"Stay awake at all times, praying" (v36).** "Stay awake" is *agrypneō*, to keep watch through the night. The disciples will fall asleep instead of praying in Gethsemane within days (22:45–46).
- **"To stand before the Son of Man" (v36).** To stand means to stand approved, not to fall in judgment (Ps 1:5; Mal 3:2). The question in Malachi is "who can stand when he appears?" Jesus says: those who watch and pray.
- **"Every day he was teaching in the temple, but at night he went out and lodged on the mount called Olivet" (vv37–38).** Luke closes with a picture of Jesus' last days. He spent nights on the Mount of Olives, probably at Bethany or in the open, since Jerusalem was packed with pilgrims. The people came "early in the morning" to hear him. The leaders still cannot arrest him in public. They will need a traitor, and the next chapter begins with one.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls watching and praying "the two great duties which in prospect of trial are constantly enjoined," and says the whole discourse points to the day when all safety lies in being able "to stand before the Son of Man." Henry: "Those shall be accounted worthy to live a life of praise in the other world, who live a life of prayer in this world." Guzik notes that *kraipalē* means the hangover after drinking, which the lexicon confirms, and he draws the parallel that those who heeded Jesus escaped Jerusalem's fall, and those who heed him now will escape the greater judgment.

#### Pointing to Christ
The temple, with its gleaming stones, will be thrown down, but the words of Jesus will never pass away. Within days, the true temple, his body, will be destroyed and raised on the third day (John 2:19–21). The judgment he foretold on Jerusalem fell on him first at the cross, and because it did, those who belong to him can lift up their heads when he comes, "because your redemption is drawing near."
