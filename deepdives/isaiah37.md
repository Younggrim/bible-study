*Setting:* It is 701 BC. Sennacherib of Assyria has overrun Judah, taken its fortified cities and set up headquarters at Lachish. His field commander, the Rabshakeh, has just stood outside Jerusalem's wall and mocked King Hezekiah, the people's trust in the LORD, and the LORD himself, lumping him in with the gods of the nations Assyria had already crushed (ch 36). Hezekiah's officials have come back with their clothes torn. This chapter, which matches 2 Kings 19 almost word for word, is the climax of the first half of Isaiah: everything Isaiah has said about trusting God rather than Egypt or Assyria now comes to the test.

#### Hezekiah Seeks Isaiah's Help (vv1–13)
- **"He tore his clothes and covered himself with sackcloth and went into the house of the LORD" (v1).** The contrast with his father is sharp. When Ahaz faced a smaller threat, Isaiah had to go looking for him, and he refused even to ask for a sign (7:3–12). Hezekiah goes straight to God. Henry: "The more Rabshakeh reproaches God the more Hezekiah studies to honour him."
- **"To the prophet Isaiah the son of Amoz" (v2).** This is the first time in the book that a king sends to Isaiah for a word. He sends his highest officials and the senior priests, all in sackcloth.
- **"Children have come to the point of birth, and there is no strength to bring them forth" (v3).** A proverb for a crisis at its most dangerous moment, with no strength left to get through it. In the ancient world a birth like this often meant death for both mother and child. JFB calls it "a proverbial expression for, We are in the most extreme danger and have no power to avert it." The day is one of "distress, of rebuke, and of disgrace," and Hezekiah does not pretend otherwise.
- **"It may be that the LORD your God will hear the words of the Rabshakeh… sent to mock the living God" (v4).** Hezekiah's hope does not rest on Judah's goodness. It rests on God's own honor. The Assyrians have mocked "the living God," a title that sets him against the dead idols of v19. **"Lift up your prayer for the remnant that is left."** Only Jerusalem remained of all Judah's cities. "Remnant" is a key word in Isaiah, the name of his son Shear-jashub, "a remnant shall return" (7:3; 10:20–22).
- **"Do not be afraid because of the words that you have heard" (v6).** Isaiah's first word to Hezekiah echoes his first word to Ahaz: "Be careful, be quiet, do not fear" (7:4). **"The young men of the king of Assyria."** God belittles the Assyrian officials, calling them "lads" or servant boys.
- **"I will put a spirit in him, so that he shall hear a rumor and return to his own land, and I will make him fall by the sword in his own land" (v7).** God announces the whole outcome in one sentence: a report, a retreat, and a death at home. Every part comes true by the end of the chapter (vv9, 37, 38).
- **"Found the king of Assyria fighting against Libnah" (v8).** Libnah was a town in the lowlands a few miles north of Lachish, so Lachish had fallen and the Assyrians were mopping up.
- **"Tirhakah king of Cush" (v9).** Cush was the kingdom of Nubia, south of Egypt (modern Sudan). Its kings ruled Egypt as the 25th Dynasty. Tirhakah (Taharqa) became pharaoh around 690 BC, so in 701 he was probably a young prince commanding the army, and the narrator calls him by the title he later held, as we might say "President Lincoln" of his younger years. Some scholars instead propose a second, later campaign by Sennacherib. Either way, news of an Egyptian army on the move pushed Sennacherib to try to scare Jerusalem into surrender quickly.
- **"Do not let your God in whom you trust deceive you" (v10).** In ch 36 the Rabshakeh said Hezekiah was deceiving the people. Now the letter says God is deceiving Hezekiah. The attack has gone straight at God's truthfulness.
- **Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, Eden, Telassar, Hamath, Arpad, Sepharvaim, Hena, Ivvah (vv12–13).** A roll call of places in Mesopotamia and Syria that Assyrian kings had conquered over the previous century. Haran was where Abraham's family had settled (Gen 11:31), and Gozan was where Israelites from Samaria had been deported (2 Kings 17:6). The list is meant to say: no god has ever stopped us.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees Hezekiah turning an insult into an occasion for worship, and he calls prayer "the midwife of mercy, that helps to bring it forth," fitting the birth picture of v3. Guzik notes that Isaiah's first answer (vv6–7) says nothing yet about saving Jerusalem. It deals only with Sennacherib's fall. JFB takes the "rebuke" of v3 as the LORD's rebuke of his people's sins, which makes the day both a judgment and a call to repent.

#### Hezekiah's Prayer for Deliverance (vv14–20)
- **"Hezekiah went up to the house of the LORD, and spread it before the LORD" (v14).** The letter was likely a scroll or a written tablet, and Hezekiah literally unrolls it in the temple, as if to say, "Read this." Henry: "Let the thing speak itself; here it is in black and white." It is a picture of what Peter tells believers to do: "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Pet 5:7).
- **"O LORD of hosts, God of Israel, enthroned above the cherubim" (v16).** "LORD of hosts" means the commander of heaven's armies, the answer to Sennacherib's "many chariots" (v24). "Enthroned above the cherubim" refers to the ark in the Most Holy Place, whose lid was guarded by two gold cherubim, the place where God was pictured as invisibly seated (Exod 25:22; Ps 80:1). Hezekiah prays toward the throne room of the real King.
- **"You are the God, you alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth; you have made the heavens and the earth" (v16).** The prayer begins not with the problem but with who God is. Sennacherib is a king over many kingdoms. The LORD is the God of all of them, and the maker of heaven and earth besides. Isaiah 40–48 will develop this theme at length.
- **"Incline your ear, O LORD, and hear; open your eyes, O LORD, and see" (v17).** This is the language of the psalms (Ps 31:2; Dan 9:18). Hezekiah asks God to take notice of the mockery against him.
- **"They were no gods, but the work of men's hands, wood and stone. Therefore they were destroyed" (v19).** Hezekiah agrees with the Assyrian's facts and reverses his conclusion. Yes, Assyria burned the gods of the nations, but that only proved they were not gods. The LORD is in a different category altogether. This is the theme Isaiah will mock at length in 44:9–20.
- **"Save us from his hand, that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you alone are the LORD" (v20).** The request is for rescue, but the goal is God's name among the nations. The Tyndale notes connect it with God's purpose in the Exodus, "that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth" (Exod 9:16). This is a prayer God loves to answer.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the plea "for thy glory's sake" as the strongest argument in the prayer. Guzik calls spreading the letter a model of 1 Peter 5:7 and notes how the prayer moves from God's titles to his power as Creator to the contrast with lifeless idols. JFB says the word for "spread" means he "unrolled the scroll of writing," and he adds that God welcomes his people laying out their needs before him.

#### Sennacherib's Fall (vv21–38)
- **"Because you have prayed to me concerning Sennacherib king of Assyria" (v21).** God's answer is explicitly linked to the prayer. Scripture holds together God's sovereign plan (v26) and the real effect of prayer. Guzik presses the point that some blessings wait for God's people to ask (Jas 4:2).
- **"She despises you, she scorns you—the virgin daughter of Zion" (v22).** God answers with a taunt song. Jerusalem is pictured as a young woman whom the attacker could not touch, "virgin" meaning still unconquered. She "wags her head behind you," a gesture of contempt, as he turns and leaves.
- **"Whom have you mocked and reviled?… Against the Holy One of Israel!" (v23).** This is Isaiah's favorite title for God, used about 25 times in the book. Sennacherib thought he was insulting a small king. He was insulting the Holy One.
- **"With my many chariots I have gone up the heights of the mountains, to the far recesses of Lebanon" (v24).** God quotes Sennacherib's boasting back to him. The Tyndale notes observe how closely this matches the style of real Assyrian royal inscriptions, which bragged about taking chariots into impossible mountain terrain and felling the cedars of Lebanon for royal buildings.
- **"I dug wells and drank waters, to dry up with the sole of my foot all the streams of Egypt" (v25).** A boast that no desert or river could stop him. The irony is that it was God who dried up the sea for Israel (Exod 14:21; Isa 51:10).
- **"Have you not heard that I determined it long ago?" (v26).** This is the heart of God's answer. Everything Sennacherib boasted of, God had planned and permitted. Assyria was the axe in God's hand, as Isaiah said in 10:15: "Shall the axe boast over him who hews with it?" Its victims were "like grass on the housetops," which sprouts quickly in the shallow soil of flat roofs and withers before it grows (Ps 129:6).
- **"I know your sitting down and your going out and coming in, and your raging against me" (v28).** Nothing about this emperor is hidden from God, the same language David uses of God's knowledge in Psalm 139:2–3.
- **"I will put my hook in your nose and my bit in your mouth" (v29).** Assyrian reliefs really do show captive kings led by ropes attached to rings through their lips or noses. JFB mentions such carvings. God will do to Sennacherib what Sennacherib did to others, and lead him home like a tamed animal "on the way by which you came."
- **"This shall be the sign for you" (v30).** The Assyrian army had wrecked the farmland, so there could be no proper sowing. God promises that the people will live on what grows by itself this year and the next, and in the third year plant and harvest normally. The Tyndale notes suggest the timing: after the rescue it would be too late to plant that year, so the first full harvest would come in the third. It is a sign that requires trust over time, and it was also a promise that the Assyrians would not be back.
- **"The surviving remnant… shall again take root downward and bear fruit upward" (v31).** A tree cut down to a stump that grows again, Isaiah's picture from 6:13 and 11:1. **"The zeal of the LORD of hosts will do this" (v32)** is the same phrase that closes the great promise of the Messiah in 9:7. God's passionate commitment to his people is the guarantee.
- **"He shall not come into this city or shoot an arrow there or come before it with a shield or cast up a siege mound" (v33).** These are the four stages of an Assyrian siege, all shown in the Lachish reliefs. Jerusalem would face none of them. Sennacherib's own annals agree: he boasts of shutting Hezekiah up "like a bird in a cage," and of the tribute Hezekiah paid, but never of taking the city or even of a siege battle against it.
- **"For I will defend this city to save it, for my own sake and for the sake of my servant David" (v35).** Not for Jerusalem's sake. God acts for his own name and for his promise to David of an everlasting throne (2 Sam 7:12–16). That promise points to the Messiah.
- **"The angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians" (v36).** One verse, stated with stunning plainness. The same angel of judgment appears at the Exodus (Exod 12:23) and in David's time (2 Sam 24:16). Isaiah had predicted it: "The Assyrian shall fall by a sword, not of man" (31:8). Many have asked *how*. Some think God used a sudden plague. The Greek historian Herodotus (2.141) preserves an Egyptian tradition that when Sennacherib's army camped near Egypt, field mice swarmed the camp overnight and gnawed through their bowstrings and shield straps, and many scholars think this is a garbled memory of the same disaster, possibly hinting at disease spread by rodents. JFB argues for a violent storm instead. The text simply says the angel did it.
- **"Sennacherib king of Assyria departed and returned home and lived at Nineveh" (v37).** The great king went home, exactly as v7 and v34 said. He reigned another twenty years and never returned to Judah.
- **"As he was worshiping in the house of Nisroch his god, Adrammelech and Sharezer, his sons, struck him down with the sword" (v38).** This was in 681 BC, and Babylonian and Assyrian records confirm that Sennacherib was murdered by a son, whose name appears in Assyrian as Arda-Mulissu, very likely the Adrammelech of this verse. The god Nisroch has not been identified with certainty. The irony is pointed. Sennacherib mocked the LORD for being unable to save, and his own god could not save him in his own temple from his own sons. Henry: "his own sons, who should have guarded him, sacrificed him to his idol, whose protection he sought." **Esarhaddon**, another son, took the throne and ruled until 669 BC. The murderers fled to Ararat, the kingdom of Urartu in what is now eastern Turkey and Armenia.
- **Commentators:** Henry stresses that God's answer is personal and full, and that "one angel shall, in one night, lay a vast army of men dead upon the spot," linking the event to Psalm 76, a psalm many think celebrates this rescue. He also ties the sign of v30 to God's provision: "The blessing of the Lord can, when he pleases, make rich without the hand of the diligent." Guzik stresses that God saved the city "for My own sake," not because Jerusalem deserved it. He treats Herodotus's mice as a possible garbled version of the event. JFB connects the hook of v29 to Assyrian carvings of captives led by rings and argues the disaster was a storm rather than a plague, since the Judeans afterwards plundered the bodies.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hezekiah, the son of David, went into the temple, laid out the enemy's threat before God and prayed for God's glory, and a single night of God's power saved the city without one arrow being shot. Jesus, the greater Son of David, faced a far greater enemy. He prayed in the garden, and on the cross he won the victory alone, disarming "the rulers and authorities" and "triumphing over them" (Col 2:15). God defended Jerusalem "for the sake of my servant David." He defends his people now for the sake of David's greater Son, and the root that grows again from the stump in v31 is the "shoot from the stump of Jesse" (11:1).
