*Setting:* Samson has been born to Manoah and his wife, set apart as a Nazirite from the womb, and "the Spirit of the LORD began to stir him" (13:25). The Philistines rule Israel, and Israel seems content to live under them. Zorah, Samson's home, sat on a ridge above the Sorek Valley, the natural road down from the hills of Judah and Dan to the Philistine plain. Timnah lay a few miles down the same valley, on the border where Israelite and Philistine villages mixed. Chapter 14 is the first of Samson's adventures. It is full of strength, wit and appetite, and every step he takes breaks something holy. Yet the narrator tells us, unexpectedly, that God is at work in the middle of it.

#### Samson's Marriage (vv1–20)
- **"Samson went down to Timnah" (v1).** "Down" is literal, from the hills to the lowlands. It also becomes a pattern. Samson keeps going "down" toward the Philistines (vv5, 7, 10, 19; 15:8; 16:21), and in the end the Philistines bring him "down" to Gaza in chains.
- **"Get her for me as my wife" (v2).** In Israel, parents arranged marriages, and Samson is ordering his father to do it. His parents object: "Is there not a woman among… our people, that you must go to take a wife from the uncircumcised Philistines?" (v3). God's law forbade marriage with the Canaanite nations because it would draw Israel to their gods (Deut 7:3–4; Josh 23:12–13). The Philistines were not among the seven nations named there, as JFB notes, but the principle plainly applied. "Uncircumcised" was the standard Israelite insult for Philistines, who unlike most of Israel's neighbors did not practice circumcision.
- **"She is right in my eyes" (v3).** Literally, "she is right in my eyes," repeated in v7. This is the key phrase of the book's closing chapters: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (17:6; 21:25). Samson is the judge who embodies the problem he was meant to help solve.
- **"It was from the LORD, for he was seeking an opportunity against the Philistines" (v4).** This is one of the most surprising verses in Judges. Samson's choice is selfish and against God's will, and yet God is using it. The Philistines ruled Israel, and Israel had stopped resisting. God would provoke the conflict Israel was too comfortable to start. This does not make Samson's sin good. It shows God working through it, as with Joseph's brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Gen 50:20).
- **"They came to the vineyards of Timnah" (v5).** A Nazirite was not to touch anything from the grapevine (Num 6:3–4; Judg 13:14). What is Samson doing in the vineyards? Notice also that his parents seem to be elsewhere when the lion attacks, since he does not tell them about it (v6).
- **"A young lion came toward him roaring" (v5).** Lions lived in the thickets and hills of Israel in Bible times (1 Sam 17:34; 1 Kgs 13:24). This was a young lion in its full strength.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD rushed upon him" (v6).** The verb *tsalach* means to rush in, come mightily. It is used three times of Samson (14:6, 19; 15:14), and later of Saul and David (1 Sam 10:6; 16:13). With his bare hands Samson "tore the lion in pieces as one tears a young goat," the way a man might pull apart a kid for cooking. His strength was not natural muscle but God's Spirit. David later tells Saul he killed lions and bears the same way (1 Sam 17:34–37).
- **"He turned aside to see the carcass of the lion" (v8).** Some time later, perhaps a year later at the wedding, Samson leaves the road to look. In that dry heat a carcass soon dried out, and a swarm of bees had made a hive inside. A Nazirite was not to go near a dead body (Num 6:6). Samson reaches into the carcass, scrapes out the honey and eats it as he walks.
- **"He did not tell them that he had scraped the honey from the carcass" (v9).** He gives some to his parents too, and keeps quiet about where it came from. Touching a carcass made a person unclean (Lev 11:24–28), and he has made his parents unclean without their knowing. The secret shows he knew it was wrong.
- **"Samson prepared a feast there" (v10).** The word for feast, *mishteh*, comes from the verb "to drink." It was a seven-day wedding drinking party. A Nazirite was not to drink wine. The text does not say Samson drank, but he chose to host one.
- **"Thirty companions" (v11).** The Philistines supply thirty young men as wedding companions. They were probably meant to keep an eye on this strong Israelite as much as to celebrate with him.
- **The riddle and the wager (vv12–14).** Riddles were a favorite entertainment at feasts. Samson offers thirty "linen garments" (fine linen undergarments or wraps) and thirty "changes of clothes" (festive outer garments), an expensive bet. "Out of the eater came something to eat. Out of the strong came something sweet." In Hebrew it is a short, rhyming couplet. It was not really fair, since no one could solve it without knowing about the lion.
- **"On the fourth day" (v15).** The Hebrew says "seventh day," but the Greek and Syriac read "fourth," which the ESV follows to fit v17 (she wept all seven days). Either way, after several days the Philistine men threaten the bride: tell us, "lest we burn you and your father's house with fire." It is a threat they will carry out anyway (15:6).
- **"You only hate me; you do not love me" (v16).** She weeps and accuses him. He answers that he has not even told his parents. "She wept before him the seven days… and on the seventh day he told her, because she pressed him hard" (v17). The scene previews Delilah almost word for word (16:15–17). Samson can tear a lion apart but cannot resist a woman's tears.
- **"What is sweeter than honey? What is stronger than a lion?" (v18).** They answer just before sunset on the last day, in a matching rhymed couplet. Samson's reply is bitter and crude: "If you had not plowed with my heifer, you would not have found out my riddle." A heifer is a young cow, and to plow with another man's heifer meant using what was his. He is accusing them of using his wife against him.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD rushed upon him, and he went down to Ashkelon" (v19).** Ashkelon was one of the five Philistine cities, on the coast more than twenty miles away. Samson kills thirty Philistines, strips their clothes and pays his debt with them. It is a strange use of the Spirit's power, driven by anger and a lost bet. But it is also the first blow against the Philistines that 13:5 promised. God's purpose (v4) moves forward through Samson's temper.
- **"Samson's wife was given to his companion, who had been his best man" (v20).** Samson goes home "in hot anger" without finishing the marriage, and her father gives her to the best man. The Tyndale notes say this was likely a breach of custom and deceitful. It sets up the next round of revenge in chapter 15.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says Samson "is only guided by his eye, and governed by his fancy," and adds that children should not marry "without the advice and consent of their parents." Guzik also points to the Hebrew "right in my eyes" and says Samson cared about his own view, not God's. He applies it to believers marrying unbelievers (2 Cor 6:14). JFB reads the same phrase as meaning she was "fit for his purpose," which pushes toward the idea that Samson himself knew this was for God's plan. That is a stretch, since v4 says even his parents "did not know," and the phrase elsewhere in Judges describes self-will. JFB does argue, fairly, that the Philistines were not among the seven nations the law named. On v4 Guzik says God brings good out of evil without excusing it, and that obedience would have brought more good with less pain. All three notice the Nazirite boundaries: Guzik names the vineyard, the carcass and the drinking feast. Henry: "A worldly wife, or a worldly friend, is to a godly man as an enemy in the camp," and "No union can be comfortable or lasting, where secrets cannot be intrusted." JFB calls the bride's being given away "an act of base treachery." The Tyndale notes say Israel's hero, like Sisera, was beaten by a woman.

#### Pointing to Christ
Samson's riddle has a deeper answer than he knew: "Out of the eater came something to eat. Out of the strong came something sweet." In the gospel, life comes out of death. Jesus, "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" (Rev 5:5), went into death, the great devourer, and out of his death comes the sweetest food there is: "I am the living bread… the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh" (John 6:51). Samson was set apart to God and broke his vows one by one. Jesus was perfectly holy and kept every command, "holy, innocent, unstained" (Heb 7:26). And where Samson's angry strength began to save Israel almost in spite of himself, Christ's strength saves on purpose, by love. God worked through Samson's sin; he works for us through Christ's righteousness.
