*Setting:* About AD 33–36, a year or so after Stephen's death. The church in Jerusalem has scattered across Judea and Samaria, and Saul of Tarsus, a young Pharisee trained under Gamaliel (22:3), is hunting believers from house to house (8:3). Saul was born in Tarsus in Cilicia, held Roman citizenship from birth, and spoke Greek and Aramaic. By his own account he was "as to zeal, a persecutor of the church" (Phil 3:6). This chapter tells how the risen Jesus stopped him. It is so central that Luke tells it three times (here, 22:1–21 and 26:9–23). Then the story turns back to Peter, whose ministry on the coast prepares for the opening to the Gentiles in chapter 10.

#### The Conversion of Saul (vv1–19a)
- **"Still breathing threats and murder" (v1).** "Breathing" pictures someone whose every breath is rage. Paul later admits he voted for believers' deaths and was "raging" against them, pursuing them "even to foreign cities" (26:10–11).
- **"Letters to the synagogues at Damascus" (v2).** Damascus was a major city in Syria, about 135 miles north-east of Jerusalem, perhaps a week's journey on foot, with a large Jewish population. The high priest had no civil authority there, but synagogues respected his letters in religious matters, and Rome allowed Jewish communities a measure of self-discipline. The believers there may have been refugees from Jerusalem.
- **"Belonging to the Way" (v2).** This is the first time Acts uses "the Way" for the Christian faith (19:9, 23; 22:4; 24:14, 22). It may come from Jesus' saying "I am the way" (John 14:6), or from Isaiah's "prepare the way of the LORD" (Isa 40:3), which the Dead Sea community also used of itself. It describes a way of life, not only a set of beliefs.
- **"A light from heaven shone around him" (v3).** Paul later says it was midday and the light was brighter than the sun (22:6; 26:13). In Scripture, blinding light marks the glory of God (Ezek 1:28). Paul always counted this as a real appearance of the risen Jesus, the last of the resurrection appearances: "Last of all… he appeared also to me" (1 Cor 15:8; 9:1).
- **"Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" (v4).** God called "Abraham, Abraham," "Jacob, Jacob," "Moses, Moses" at turning points (Gen 22:11; 46:2; Exod 3:4). Jesus does not say "my followers" but **"me."** To persecute the church is to persecute him. The seed of Paul's later teaching that the church is the body of Christ (1 Cor 12:12, 27) is here.
- **"Who are you, Lord?" (v5).** Saul may mean only "Sir," but before a light from heaven "Lord" is more likely the awed address to a heavenly being. The answer turns his world upside down: **"I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting."** The crucified man he thought was cursed (Deut 21:23) is alive and speaking from heaven's glory.
- **The goads.** The KJV adds in v5, "it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks," and more in v6. Those words are missing here from the Greek manuscripts and were added later from Paul's own retelling (26:14), where they belong. A goad was a pointed stick for driving oxen. An ox that kicked back only hurt itself. It was a Greek proverb for useless resistance against a god.
- **"Rise and enter the city, and you will be told what you are to do" (v6).** The man who came with orders to bind others now waits to be told what to do.
- **"Hearing the voice but seeing no one" (v7).** In 22:9 Paul says his companions "did not understand the voice of the one who was speaking." The two accounts fit. They heard a sound but not the words. Greek grammar can mark the difference (hearing a sound versus hearing with understanding), and the same thing happened when God spoke to Jesus and some heard only thunder (John 12:29).
- **"Although his eyes were opened, he saw nothing" (v8).** The man who thought he saw clearly is blind and led by the hand. Paul later wrote that the god of this age "has blinded the minds of the unbelievers" (2 Cor 4:4).
- **"For three days he was without sight, and neither ate nor drank" (v9).** A fast of shock and repentance. Three days of darkness before new life is a pattern worth noticing, though Luke does not draw it out.
- **"A disciple at Damascus named Ananias" (v10).** Not the Ananias of 5:1 or the high priest of 23:2. Paul later calls him "a devout man according to the law, well spoken of by all the Jews" there (22:12). **"Here I am, Lord"** is the answer of Abraham, Samuel and Isaiah (Gen 22:1; 1 Sam 3:4; Isa 6:8).
- **"The street called Straight" (v11).** A long colonnaded street running east-west through Damascus. It still exists, now called Straight Street (*Via Recta*) or Midhat Pasha Souq. **"For behold, he is praying."** The persecutor is on his knees.
- **"Lord, I have heard… how much evil he has done to your saints" (vv13–14).** Ananias objects, understandably. "Saints" (*hagioi*, holy ones) appears here for the first time in Acts for believers. It means people set apart for God, not specially good people.
- **"A chosen instrument of mine" (v15).** "Instrument" is literally "vessel" or tool. Saul is chosen, not by his own goodness. Paul says God "set me apart before I was born, and… called me by his grace" (Gal 1:15). **"Before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel."** That is the outline of the rest of Acts: Paul preaches to Gentiles across the empire, stands before governors and a king (chs 24–26), and always begins with the synagogue.
- **"How much he must suffer for the sake of my name" (v16).** Saul had made others suffer for that name. Now he will suffer for it. Paul's list in 2 Corinthians 11:23–28 shows how much: beatings, stoning, shipwreck, hunger. "Must" is the same divine necessity used of Christ's own suffering (Luke 24:26).
- **"Brother Saul" (v17).** The first word Saul hears from a Christian is "brother." Ananias's fear has turned to welcome.
- **"Regain your sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit" (v17).** Here the Spirit comes through an ordinary disciple, not an apostle, which shows the apostles' role in Samaria (8:14–17) was not a fixed rule.
- **"Something like scales fell from his eyes" (v18).** Luke, the physician, says "something like." English has the saying "the scales fell from his eyes" from this verse.
- **"Then he rose and was baptized" (v18).** Before food. Ananias later urges him, "Rise and be baptized and wash away your sins, calling on his name" (22:16).
- **When was Saul converted?** On the road, when he called Jesus "Lord" and surrendered? Or in Damascus, when he received the Spirit and was baptized? Many say the road; others say the house of Judas. Luke tells it as one event over three days.
- **Commentators:** JFB says "No language can express the affecting character of this question" (v4), and harmonizes v7 with 22:9 by saying the men "heard not the articulate words." Guzik notes that the goad saying and the KJV's "what will you have me do?" are not part of Luke's original text here and were brought in from chapters 22 and 26, which agrees with the manuscripts. Matthew Henry: "Let us not despair of renewing grace for the conversion of the greatest sinners," and of Ananias, "even eminent disciples… sometimes stagger at the commands of the Lord." JFB calls Ananias's obedience "beautifully childlike."

#### Saul Proclaims Jesus in Synagogues (vv19b–22)
- **"For some days he was with the disciples" (v19b).** The people he came to arrest became his family.
- **"Immediately he proclaimed Jesus in the synagogues, saying, 'He is the Son of God'" (v20).** This is the only time in Acts that the title "Son of God" appears in preaching. It means more than "Messiah": it claims Jesus' unique relation to the Father (Rom 1:3–4). Saul goes to the very synagogues where he had letters to arrest believers.
- **"Is not this the man who made havoc in Jerusalem?" (v21).** "Made havoc" (*portheō*) means to destroy or sack, as an army does a city. Paul uses the same word of himself (Gal 1:13, 23).
- **"Confounded the Jews… by proving that Jesus was the Christ" (v22).** "Proving" (*symbibazō*) means to put things together, to bring texts side by side to make a case. Saul knew the Scriptures well and now saw where they all pointed.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the oldest manuscripts read "Jesus," not "Christ," in v20, which the ESV follows. Guzik explains that "Son of God" was heard as a claim to deity, and Matthew Henry describes Saul trying "to recommend the anointed Saviour, the Son of God, to his former companions."

#### Saul Escapes from Damascus (vv23–25)
- **"When many days had passed" (v23).** Luke compresses. Paul says he went from Damascus into Arabia and then returned to Damascus, and only "after three years" went to Jerusalem (Gal 1:17–18). "Arabia" was the Nabataean kingdom east and south of Damascus. Whether he went there to preach or to pray and think through the Scriptures is debated. The "many days" probably include that time.
- **"The Jews plotted to kill him" (v23).** The hunter becomes the hunted. Paul adds that the governor under King Aretas of Nabataea was also guarding the city to seize him (2 Cor 11:32), perhaps because of trouble he had stirred up in Arabia.
- **"Let him down through an opening in the wall, lowering him in a basket" (v25).** Houses were built into city walls, with windows on the outside (compare Rahab in Josh 2:15). The basket (*spyris*) was a large woven hamper. Paul lists this humiliating escape among his "weaknesses" (2 Cor 11:30–33). The man who came to Damascus as the high priest's agent leaves it in a laundry basket.
- **"His disciples" (v25).** Saul already has disciples of his own, people who came to faith through his preaching.
- **Commentators:** JFB, drawing on Galatians, says "a period of three years elapsed" between Saul's conversion and his return to Jerusalem, and notes that "such overhanging windows in the walls of Eastern cities were common." Guzik dates the conversion to about AD 34–36. Others put it a year or two earlier, and the exact dates are uncertain.

#### Saul in Jerusalem (vv26–31)
- **"They were all afraid of him" (v26).** It had been three years, but the Jerusalem believers remembered him dragging their friends off to prison. Was this a trick to infiltrate the church?
- **"But Barnabas took him" (v27).** "Son of encouragement" (4:36) lives up to his name. He risks his own reputation for a man no one trusted. Barnabas will do the same later for Saul in Antioch (11:25–26) and for John Mark (15:37–39). Paul says he stayed with Peter fifteen days and also saw James, the Lord's brother (Gal 1:18–19), so "the apostles" here means those two.
- **"He went in and out among them" (v28).** An idiom for living and moving freely among them as one of them.
- **"He spoke and disputed against the Hellenists" (v29).** The Greek-speaking Jews of Jerusalem, Stephen's own group and the people who had debated and killed Stephen (6:9). Saul takes up the debate where Stephen left off, with the same result: **"they were seeking to kill him."**
- **"Sent him off to Tarsus" (v30).** His hometown in Cilicia, a university city. Paul says that in a vision in the temple, Jesus told him to leave Jerusalem because they would not accept his testimony, and "I will send you far away to the Gentiles" (22:17–21). He spends perhaps ten years in Syria and Cilicia (Gal 1:21) before Barnabas brings him to Antioch.
- **"So the church throughout all Judea and Galilee and Samaria had peace" (v31).** "Church" is singular: one church spread across the regions, not separate churches. This is the only mention of Galilee in Acts. The peace came partly from the persecutor's conversion and perhaps partly because the Jewish leaders were soon distracted by the emperor Caligula's attempt to set up his statue in the temple (AD 39–40). **"In the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit."** Reverence and encouragement together.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Believers are apt to be too suspicious of those against whom they have prejudices." JFB suggests Saul argued with the Hellenists "as being of his own class," the people he knew best. On v31 JFB credits the peace "not so much to the conversion of Saul" as to the Caligula crisis, while Guzik also mentions both Caiaphas's removal and Caligula as part of the background.

#### The Healing of Aeneas (vv32–35)
- **"As Peter went here and there among them all" (v32).** The apostle visits the scattered churches. **Lydda** (Old Testament Lod) was on the plain about 25 miles north-west of Jerusalem, near the road to the coast.
- **"Aeneas, bedridden for eight years" (v33).** His Greek name, the same as the hero of Virgil's *Aeneid*, suggests he was a Greek-speaking Jew. Luke does not say he was a believer.
- **"Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you; rise and make your bed" (v34).** Peter does not say "I heal you." The verb is present: Jesus is healing him right now. "Make your bed" may mean "spread your own couch," or perhaps "set your table," either way doing for himself what others had done for him.
- **"All the residents of Lydda and Sharon saw him, and they turned to the Lord" (v35).** Sharon is the coastal plain from Joppa north to Mount Carmel. Luke uses "all" loosely to mean very many, but the effect was huge.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Peter does not pretend to heal by any power of his own, but directs Eneas to look up to Christ for help," and Guzik makes the same point. JFB adds only cross-references to the lame man (3:6) and Jesus' healing at Bethesda (John 5:8).

#### Dorcas Restored to Life (vv36–43)
- **"In Joppa a disciple named Tabitha, which, translated, means Dorcas" (v36).** Joppa (modern Jaffa, beside Tel Aviv) was the old port of Jerusalem, about ten miles from Lydda. Jonah sailed from here, running from God's call to preach to Gentiles (Jonah 1:3). Tabitha (Aramaic) and Dorcas (Greek) both mean "gazelle." This is the only place in the New Testament where the feminine form of "disciple" (*mathētria*) is used.
- **"Full of good works and acts of charity" (v36).** Her faith showed in her hands, making clothes for widows, the poor of the poor in that society.
- **"When they had washed her, they laid her in an upper room" (v37).** Washing the body was the first step of a Jewish burial. Burial normally happened the same day, but they delayed, perhaps hoping Peter could do something.
- **"Please come to us without delay" (v38).** They had heard about Aeneas.
- **"All the widows stood beside him weeping and showing tunics and other garments" (v39).** A moving scene. These may have been the very clothes they were wearing.
- **"Peter put them all outside, and knelt down and prayed" (v40).** Peter had watched Jesus do exactly this with Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:40). **"Tabitha, arise."** In Aramaic it would be *Tabitha qumi*, one letter away from Jesus' *Talitha qumi*, "little girl, arise" (Mark 5:41). Peter copies his Lord, but he prays first, because the power is not his.
- **"He gave her his hand and raised her up… he presented her alive" (v41).** Elijah and Elisha raised children (1 Kings 17:17–24; 2 Kings 4:32–37), and Jesus raised three people. This was a restoration to ordinary life. Dorcas would die again, unlike Jesus, who rose never to die (Rom 6:9).
- **"He stayed in Joppa for many days with one Simon, a tanner" (v43).** Tanners worked with the skins of dead animals, which made them ritually unclean, and the smell meant they often lived on the edge of town. Some rabbis said a woman could divorce a man who became a tanner. That Peter, a law-keeping Jew, lodged with one shows that walls were already starting to come down in him. He is about to have a vision about unclean things on that tanner's roof (10:9–16).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Tabitha was a great doer, no great talker." Guzik and JFB both explain that the two names mean "gazelle" and connect the tanner's trade with ritual uncleanness, JFB calling it "regarded by the Jews as half unclean." Guzik points out the near echo of Jesus' "Talitha, cumi." He also calls this a resuscitation rather than resurrection, since she would die again, and warns against treating it as a promise for every case.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Why are you persecuting me?" The risen Jesus is so joined to his people that their suffering is his. He turns his chief enemy into his chosen vessel, and he does it by grace alone, through a blinding light and a fearful disciple who says "Brother." Then the same Jesus goes on healing and raising through Peter: "Jesus Christ heals you," "Tabitha, arise." The chapter's two halves meet in one truth. Jesus is alive and Lord, and no persecutor, paralysis or death is beyond his reach.
