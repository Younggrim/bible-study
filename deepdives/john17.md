*Setting:* The last words before the arrest, late on Thursday night, about AD 30 or 33. Jesus has finished his farewell teaching (13:31–16:33), ending with "take heart; I have overcome the world." Now he turns from speaking to the disciples to speaking to the Father, aloud, in their hearing. Right after this "he went out with his disciples across the brook Kidron" to the garden where Judas would find him (18:1). Matthew, Mark and Luke record Jesus' agonized prayer in Gethsemane. John records this one instead, the longest prayer of Jesus in the Bible. Since the sixteenth century, starting with the Lutheran theologian David Chytraeus, it has been called the High Priestly Prayer, because in it Jesus intercedes for his people like a priest and consecrates himself like a sacrifice. It moves in widening circles: Jesus prays for himself (vv1–5), for the eleven (vv6–19), and for all who will believe through them (vv20–26). The ESV keeps the whole chapter under one heading.

#### The High Priestly Prayer (vv1–26)
- **"He lifted up his eyes to heaven" (v1).** Jews commonly prayed standing with eyes and hands raised (Ps 123:1; John 11:41). Jesus prays in front of the disciples on purpose. They are meant to overhear how the Son speaks to the Father about them.
- **"Father, the hour has come" (v1).** Jesus addresses God simply as "Father" six times in the prayer (vv1, 5, 11, 21, 24, 25). The "hour" announced in 12:23 has arrived: arrest, cross, resurrection and return to the Father.
- **"Glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you" (v1).** This is not a selfish request. The Son's glory and the Father's glory are bound together. In John, the cross is where this glory is shown, which looks to the world like shame (12:23–28; 13:31–32).
- **"Authority over all flesh, to give eternal life" (v2).** "All flesh" is a Hebrew way of saying all humanity. The Son rules over everyone, and his rule has a saving purpose: giving life to "all whom you have given him." This phrase, the Father's gift of people to the Son, runs through the prayer (vv2, 6, 9, 24). Before believers ever chose Christ, the Father gave them to him (6:37, 39; 10:29).
- **"This is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent" (v3).** The only definition of eternal life in the Bible. It is not mainly a length of life but a relationship. "Know" (*ginōskō*) in the Hebrew sense means personal, intimate knowledge, as a husband knows his wife, not mere information (Jer 31:34; Hos 6:6). "The only true God" holds to Israel's confession of one God (Deut 6:4), yet Jesus Christ is named alongside him as the one to be known with him. John's Gospel as a whole makes clear the Son is not a second god but one with the Father (1:1; 10:30).
- **"I glorified you on earth, having accomplished the work that you gave me to do" (v4).** Jesus speaks as if the work is finished, though the cross is still ahead. "Accomplished" (*teleioō*) shares a root with "It is finished" (*teleō*, 19:30).
- **"Glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had with you before the world existed" (v5).** A man cannot ask for glory he had before creation unless he existed before creation. This is the prologue in a prayer: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God" (1:1). The Son does not ask for a new glory but to return to the old one, now as the one who has become flesh. God says in Isaiah 42:8, "my glory I give to no other." The Son shares it.
- **"I have manifested your name to the people whom you gave me out of the world" (v6).** In the Bible a "name" stands for the person and his character. When God proclaimed his name to Moses, he described himself: merciful, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love (Exod 34:5–7). Jesus has shown the disciples who the Father truly is. "Out of the world" means taken from the world and set apart.
- **"They have kept your word" (v6).** These are the men who argued about greatness at dinner, who will sleep in the garden, and who will run. Jesus still speaks of them generously. They had truly believed his words (v8), even if their faith was weak, and he sees what grace will make of them.
- **"I am not praying for the world but for those whom you have given me" (v9).** This does not mean Jesus has no concern for the world. God loves the world (3:16), and the prayer goes on to ask that "the world may believe" (v21). Here Jesus is praying specifically for his own as he leaves them, the way a priest bore the names of Israel's tribes on his chest (Exod 28:29).
- **"All mine are yours, and yours are mine" (v10).** Anyone can say "all mine is yours" to God. Only the Son can say "all yours is mine" (16:15).
- **"Holy Father, keep them in your name" (v11).** "Holy Father" occurs only here in the New Testament. Holiness is the theme of what follows: the holy Father is asked to keep and to sanctify, that is, to make holy, his people. "Keep them in your name" means keep them safe by his power and in loyalty to his revealed character. The first purpose named is "that they may be one, even as we are one."
- **"Not one of them has been lost except the son of destruction" (v12).** There is wordplay in Greek: "not one has been *lost* (*apōleto*) except the son of *destruction* (*apōleia*)." "Son of" in Hebrew idiom means "belonging to" or "destined for." The same phrase is used of the man of lawlessness in 2 Thessalonians 2:3. "That the Scripture might be fulfilled" probably points to Psalm 41:9, already quoted in 13:18 (compare Ps 109:8, applied in Acts 1:20). Judas's loss is not Jesus' failure. Christians have long asked whether Judas was ever truly one of those given to the Son. JFB argues he was not, and that the wording does not imply it. Others see Judas as a real disciple who fell away. The text stresses both Judas's responsibility and that Scripture foresaw it.
- **"That they may have my joy fulfilled in themselves" (v13).** Jesus prays aloud so that the disciples can hear and be glad. Believers who know Jesus is praying for them have reason for joy (Heb 7:25).
- **"The world has hated them because they are not of the world" (v14).** Receiving Christ's word puts a person at odds with the world, just as it did Jesus (15:18–19).
- **"I do not ask that you take them out of the world, but that you keep them from the evil one" (v15).** Jesus does not pray for escape but for protection. Elijah once prayed to be taken away (1 Kings 19:4). The disciples have a mission in the world. "The evil one" may be masculine (Satan) or neuter (evil), as in the Lord's Prayer. John's letters favor the personal reading (1 John 2:13–14; 5:18–19).
- **"They are not of the world, just as I am not of the world" (v16).** The church's classic summary of this passage is "in the world, but not of it." Believers live in the world, are sent into it (v18), and do not draw their identity or values from it.
- **"Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth" (v17).** "Sanctify" (*hagiazō*) means to make holy or set apart for God. In the Old Testament priests, altars and sacrifices were sanctified, set apart from ordinary use and made fit for God's service (Exod 28:41; 29:1, 36–37). God's word is the means. Jesus is the truth (14:6), the Spirit is the Spirit of truth (16:13), and the Father's word is truth. The three work together to make God's people holy.
- **"As you sent me into the world, so I have sent them into the world" (v18).** Holiness is not withdrawal. They are set apart in order to be sent. After the resurrection Jesus repeats this almost word for word as he breathes the Spirit on them (20:21–22).
- **"For their sake I consecrate myself" (v19).** "Consecrate" is the same verb, *hagiazō*. Jesus is already holy, so he does not need to be made morally pure. He sets himself apart as a priest offers a sacrifice to God, and he is both the priest and the sacrifice (Heb 9:14; 10:10). The phrase "for their sake" (*hyper*) is the language of substitution John uses for Jesus' death (10:11; 11:51–52). He offers himself so that they "may be sanctified in truth."
- **A high priest's prayer.** On the Day of Atonement the high priest made atonement "for himself and for his house and for all the assembly of Israel" (Lev 16:17). This prayer moves in a similar pattern: Jesus, then his disciples, then all believers. The difference is that the high priest needed atonement for himself. Jesus prays for his own glory, not his own cleansing.
- **"I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word" (v20).** Here the prayer reaches forward through every century. Every believer since has come to faith through the apostles' word, written in the New Testament. Jesus prayed for them by name, as it were, that night.
- **"That they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you" (v21).** The model of Christian unity is the unity of the Father and the Son: a oneness of life and love, not merely agreement or organization. It is also a unity *in* them: "that they also may be in us." Unity is not first something believers produce but something they share by being joined to God. This verse has inspired the modern ecumenical movement. Christians differ on how far visible, institutional unity is required by it. Evangelical readers often stress spiritual unity in the truth (v17), while Catholic and Orthodox readers stress a visible church. All agree that division among believers damages the witness this verse describes.
- **"So that the world may believe that you have sent me" (v21).** The unity has a missionary purpose. A loving, united people are evidence that the Father sent the Son (13:35).
- **"The glory that you have given me I have given to them" (v22).** What glory? JFB thinks of the present glory of the indwelling Spirit. Others think of the glory of sonship, of being loved by the Father, or of the humble glory of the cross that Jesus shares with his people. The aim is again unity: "that they may be one even as we are one."
- **"That they may become perfectly one… and loved them even as you loved me" (v23).** "Perfectly one" is literally "perfected into one" (*teteleiōmenoi*), from the same root as "accomplished" in v4. The most astonishing phrase comes at the end: the Father loves believers "even as" he loves the Son. The world is meant to see that too.
- **"Father, I desire that they also… may be with me where I am" (v24).** The verb is *thelō*, "I will, I want." Elsewhere Jesus asks. Here, as the Son who shares the Father's will, he states his desire. This is the prayer behind the promise of 14:3. Heaven, in Jesus' words, is being with him and seeing his glory.
- **"Because you loved me before the foundation of the world" (v24).** Before anything was made, there was love: the Father loving the Son. This is why "God is love" (1 John 4:8). Love is not something God began doing when he made creatures. It is what he eternally is.
- **"O righteous Father, even though the world does not know you, I know you" (v25).** "Righteous" here answers the world's injustice. The world is about to condemn the Son. The righteous Father will vindicate him.
- **"I made known to them your name, and I will continue to make it known" (v26).** "Will continue" points to the cross and resurrection, which will reveal the Father's character more fully than anything yet, and to the Spirit's ongoing work. The prayer's final goal is "that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them." The last words of the prayer, and of Jesus' teaching before his arrest, are "I in them."
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls this the greatest prayer recorded in Scripture and reads Jesus' request for glory as unselfish, since the Son's glory serves the Father's. He stresses that the unity Jesus seeks is personal and spiritual, rooted in the Father and Son, not uniformity or institutional merger, and he notes that Jesus prayed for future believers, every reader included. JFB calls eternal life "not mere conscious and unending existence, but a life of acquaintance with God in Christ." On v12 JFB argues: "It is not implied here that the son of perdition was one of those whom the Father had given." On v19 it says the word means "to consecrate with the additional idea of previous sanctification," and on v21 it calls the indwelling Spirit "the one perfect bond of union," not "a merely formal, mechanical unity of ecclesiastical machinery." JFB gives only a cross-reference on v23. Henry draws a lesson from Jesus praying for those he had taught: "Those we preach to we must pray for." On v14 he adds, "Those that receive Christ's good will and good word must expect the world's ill will and ill word."

#### Pointing to Christ
On the night he is betrayed, Jesus does not pray first for his own rescue. He prays for the Father's glory, for frightened disciples, and for believers not yet born. He is the high priest who carries his people's names before God, and the sacrifice who sets himself apart "for their sake." He asks for the glory he had before the world began, and in the same breath asks that those the Father gave him may be kept, made holy, made one, and brought to be with him. He still lives to make intercession for them (Heb 7:25). The love the Father had for the Son before the foundation of the world is the love Jesus prays will be in them, and with it, himself.
