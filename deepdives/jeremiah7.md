*Setting:* The temple in Jerusalem, very likely in 609/608 BC, "in the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim" (26:1). Good King Josiah had just been killed in battle by Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo (2 Kings 23:29). Neco removed Josiah's son Jehoahaz after three months and set up his brother Jehoiakim, a greedy and violent king, as an Egyptian puppet. Josiah's reform was collapsing. Chapter 7 is Jeremiah's famous "temple sermon." Chapter 26 tells what happened after he preached it: the priests and prophets seized him and demanded his death, and only the intervention of some officials saved him. Chapters 7–10 collect material on the theme of false worship. The people believed the temple was a guarantee of safety. Jeremiah tells them it is not.

#### Evil in the Land (vv1–29)
- **"Stand in the gate of the LORD's house" (v2).** Jeremiah is placed at one of the gates into the temple courts, probably at a festival when crowds from all over Judah came "to worship the LORD." He preaches to worshipers on their way into worship.
- **"Amend your ways and your deeds, and I will let you dwell in this place" (v3).** The offer comes first. "This place" means the temple and the land around it. The promise of the land had always been tied to keeping the covenant (Deut 4:40). The Hebrew could also be read "I will dwell with you in this place." Either way, God's presence and their staying in the land depend on a changed life.
- **"Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD'" (v4).** The threefold chant was something like a slogan. A century earlier, in 701 BC, the Assyrian army had surrounded Jerusalem, and God had struck it down in one night (2 Kings 19:32–36). Isaiah had promised that God would defend his city. Over time that promise hardened into a belief that God would never let Jerusalem or the temple fall, whatever the people did. Jeremiah calls the slogan **"deceptive words"** (literally "words of falsehood," *sheqer*, his key word for lies).
- **"If you truly amend your ways… execute justice… do not oppress the sojourner, the fatherless, or the widow" (vv5–6).** Real repentance means fair dealing with neighbors and care for the vulnerable: the foreigner, the orphan and the widow, the three groups the law protected most often (Exod 22:21–24; Deut 24:17–22). **"Or shed innocent blood in this place"** may point to Jehoiakim's murders in the city (22:17; 26:20–23). **"Then I will let you dwell in this place… forever"** (v7). The promise is still open.
- **"Will you steal, murder, commit adultery, swear falsely, make offerings to Baal, and go after other gods" (v9).** The list runs through the Ten Commandments: the eighth, sixth, seventh and ninth, then the first and second (Exod 20:3–16). Hosea 4:2 has a similar list. In Hebrew each verb is a short infinitive, rapid-fire, like a charge sheet read out in court.
- **"And then come and stand before me in this house… and say, 'We are delivered!'" (v10).** The people break the law all week, then come to the temple and declare themselves safe. **"Only to go on doing all these abominations?"** They treat the temple as a license to keep sinning, not a place to stop.
- **"Has this house… become a den of robbers in your eyes?" (v11).** A den was a cave where bandits hid between raids. The temple had become their hideout, the safe place they ran back to after their crimes. **"Behold, I myself have seen it."** God sees what goes on outside the temple as well as inside it. Jesus quoted this verse when he drove the money changers out of the temple (Matt 21:13; Mark 11:17), together with Isaiah 56:7. He spoke them in the rebuilt temple, about forty years before Rome destroyed it.
- **"Go now to my place that was in Shiloh, where I made my name dwell at first" (v12).** Shiloh, about twenty miles north of Jerusalem, was where the tabernacle stood in the days of Joshua, the judges and Eli (Josh 18:1; 1 Sam 1:3). Israel once carried the ark into battle as a lucky charm, and the Philistines captured it (1 Sam 4). Shiloh itself was later destroyed, probably by the Philistines around 1050 BC. Psalm 78:60 says God "forsook his dwelling at Shiloh." Excavations there have found a destruction layer from about that time. Jeremiah's hearers could walk to the ruins in a day and see what God's house looked like when he left it.
- **"When I spoke to you persistently you did not listen" (v13).** "Persistently" is literally "rising early and speaking," a Hebrew idiom for doing something eagerly and again and again. Jeremiah uses it often (7:25; 25:3–4; 35:14–15). God had not been silent.
- **"I will do to the house that is called by my name… as I did to Shiloh" (v14).** The unthinkable. The temple of Solomon, God's own house, will be destroyed. In chapter 26 this line almost cost Jeremiah his life: the priests and prophets cried, "You shall die! Why have you prophesied… 'This house shall be like Shiloh'?" (26:8–9). Babylon burned the temple in 586 BC (52:13).
- **"As I cast out all your kinsmen, all the offspring of Ephraim" (v15).** Ephraim stands for the northern kingdom, which went into exile in 722 BC. Shiloh was in Ephraim's territory. Both examples point north, and both are warnings.
- **"As for you, do not pray for this people" (v16).** A shocking command to a prophet, since praying for the people was part of a prophet's work (1 Sam 12:23). Moses and Samuel had turned God's anger aside by prayer (Exod 32:11–14; Ps 99:6). God repeats this command in 11:14 and 14:11, and in 15:1 says that even Moses and Samuel could not change his mind now. It does not mean God has stopped caring. It means the people's hearts are set, and judgment is fixed.
- **"The children gather wood, the fathers kindle fire, and the women knead dough, to make cakes for the queen of heaven" (v18).** The whole family joins in. The "queen of heaven" was most likely the Mesopotamian goddess Ishtar, known in Canaan as Astarte or Ashtoreth, a goddess of love, war and fertility, linked with the planet Venus. Chapter 44 shows that the women of Judah baked cakes "bearing her image" and kept up her worship even in Egypt, and insisted that life had gone better when they served her (44:15–19). Clay moulds for shaped cakes have been found in excavations elsewhere in the region.
- **"Is it I whom they provoke?… Is it not themselves, to their own shame?" (v19).** They think they are hurting God's feelings. They are really harming themselves. Sin always injures the sinner.
- **"My anger and my wrath will be poured out on this place" (v20).** The judgment reaches "man and beast… trees… fruit." Creation suffers with its stewards (Rom 8:20–22). **"It will burn and not be quenched"** echoes 4:4.
- **"Add your burnt offerings to your sacrifices, and eat the flesh" (v21).** Biting irony. Burnt offerings were burned whole for God. Other sacrifices were shared and partly eaten by the worshiper. God says: put them together and eat them all yourselves, since they mean nothing to me.
- **"I did not speak to your fathers or command them concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices" (v22).** This does not deny that God gave the laws of sacrifice in Leviticus. A Hebrew way of speaking puts two things side by side as "not this, but that" to mean "not mainly this, but above all that" (compare Hos 6:6, "I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice"). At Sinai the first thing God said was "if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my covenant, you shall be my treasured possession" (Exod 19:5), before any instructions for sacrifice. Sacrifice was meant to serve a relationship of obedience, never to replace it (1 Sam 15:22; Ps 51:16–17).
- **"Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people" (v23).** The covenant formula, repeated across Scripture (Lev 26:12; Jer 11:4; 31:33; Rev 21:3). God's aim was always a relationship.
- **"Went backward and not forward" (v24).** They followed "the stubbornness of their evil hearts" (a phrase Jeremiah uses eight times) and made no progress at all.
- **"I have persistently sent all my servants the prophets to them, day after day" (v25).** From Moses to Jeremiah, the prophets came. **"They did worse than their fathers"** (v26). Each generation outdid the last.
- **"You shall call to them, but they will not answer you" (v27).** God tells Jeremiah ahead of time that his preaching will fail. He is to preach anyway. Faithfulness, not results, is the prophet's job (Ezek 2:5–7).
- **"Truth has perished; it is cut off from their lips" (v28).** "Truth" is *emunah*, faithfulness. It has gone from the nation's speech as well as its conduct.
- **"Cut off your hair and cast it away" (v29).** The command is feminine, spoken to Jerusalem as a woman. Shaving the head was a sign of mourning (Job 1:20; Mic 1:16). The word for "hair" is *nezer*, which is related to the word Nazirite and can also mean "consecration" or "crown." It may suggest that Jerusalem, once consecrated to God like a Nazirite, has lost her consecration and must cut it off. **"Raise a lamentation on the bare heights"**, where she once worshiped idols (3:2, 21). **"The generation of his wrath"** means the generation that has earned it.
- **Commentators:** JFB dates the sermon to the beginning of Jehoiakim's reign and links it with chapter 26, as most modern scholars do. Henry captures the people's thinking on v10: "It is as if you should say, We are delivered to do all these abominations." On v11 he asks whether the temple was built to be "not only a rendezvous of, but a refuge and shelter to, the vilest of malefactors." On Shiloh JFB comments that "God's favor is not tied down to localities," and Henry widens the lesson: "Remember Lot's wife; remember Shiloh and the seven churches of Asia." Guzik compares the temple chant to treating church attendance or a religious label as proof of faith. On v22 JFB reads the verse as a contrast and not a denial: God "did not require sacrifices, unless combined with moral obedience," and Henry sums up v23: "Let God's will be your rule and his favour shall be your felicity." JFB links the cakes of v18 with a moon goddess, while Guzik and the Tyndale notes identify the queen of heaven with Ishtar or Ashtoreth. Most scholars today favor Ishtar/Astarte.

#### The Valley of Slaughter (vv30–34)
- **"They have set their detestable things in the house that is called by my name" (v30).** Idols inside the temple itself. Manasseh had put an image of Asherah in the temple and altars to the stars in its courts (2 Kings 21:4–7). Josiah removed them (2 Kings 23:4–7), but they came back after him (Ezek 8:3–16 describes idol worship in the temple some years later).
- **"The high places of Topheth, which is in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom" (v31).** The Valley of Hinnom curves around the west and south sides of Jerusalem. Topheth was a shrine there. Its name may come from an Aramaic word for "fireplace," and the Hebrew scribes may have given it the vowels of *boshet*, "shame." Here parents **"burn their sons and their daughters in the fire."** Kings Ahaz and Manasseh had both sacrificed their own sons there (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6), and Josiah had defiled the place to stop it (2 Kings 23:10). The god most associated with it was Molech (Lev 18:21; 32:35).
- **"Which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind" (v31).** Some people may have thought they were honoring the LORD by giving him their most precious thing, perhaps misreading the law of the firstborn (Exod 22:29) or the story of Abraham and Isaac. God says flatly that he never asked for it and never would have thought of it. The law called it detestable (Deut 12:31; 18:10).
- **"It will no more be called Topheth… but the Valley of Slaughter" (v32).** The place where they killed their children will fill with their own dead during the siege. **"They will bury in Topheth, because there is no room elsewhere."** Burying bodies in a place of worship defiled it, which is exactly what Josiah did to Topheth. Now it will be defiled by the people themselves.
- **"Food for the birds of the air, and for the beasts of the earth" (v33).** In Israel an unburied body was the final disgrace (Deut 28:26; 1 Kings 14:11). **"None will frighten them away"**: no survivors will be left to drive the birds off, as Rizpah did for her sons (2 Sam 21:10).
- **"The voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" (v34).** The sounds of a wedding, music and laughter in the streets, will stop. The phrase returns in 16:9 and 25:10, and in 33:11, where God promises these voices will be heard again. Revelation 18:23 uses the same words for the fall of the final Babylon.
- **The valley's later name.** In Hebrew, *Ge Hinnom* (Valley of Hinnom) became *Gehenna* in Greek. Because of its history of child sacrifice and Jeremiah's prophecy of slaughter there, later Jewish writers used the name for the place of final judgment. Jesus used it the same way (Matt 5:22; 10:28), and English Bibles usually translate it "hell." The tradition that the valley was a burning rubbish dump in Jesus' time is often repeated, but it appears only in medieval sources and is uncertain.
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies Topheth's high places with altars to Molech south of the city and sees fit justice in the valley becoming a graveyard: there will be "no place" to bury the dead. Guzik calls child sacrifice one of Judah's most abhorrent sins, made worse by the people's claim that it was worship. The Tyndale notes connect the valley with Gehenna and with Jesus' teaching about hell.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah stood in the temple gate and warned that God's house had become a den of robbers. Six centuries later Jesus stood in a rebuilt temple and quoted his words, then said that this temple too would fall (Mark 13:2). He spoke of his own body as the true temple (John 2:19–21). The people of Jeremiah's day trusted a building and a slogan. The gospel offers a person, and calls for real faith, not a chant. Where they offered sacrifices without obedience, Jesus offered the one perfect sacrifice joined with perfect obedience: "I have come to do your will, O God" (Heb 10:5–10). And the valley where parents gave their children to the fire stands in contrast to the Father who gave his only Son, not to an idol's flames, but to bear judgment for sinners so that the voice of the bridegroom and the bride would be heard again (Rev 19:7).
