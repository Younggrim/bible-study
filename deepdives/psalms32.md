*Setting:* "A Maskil of David." This is the first psalm in the Psalter with that title. The word probably comes from the Hebrew verb *sakal*, "to have insight, to instruct." Henry's KJV margin reads "a psalm giving instruction," and v8 uses the same verb: "I will *instruct* you." David is teaching from his own experience. Most readers connect the psalm with David's sin against Bathsheba and Uriah, his long silence afterwards, and the day Nathan confronted him and he finally said, "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Sam 11–12). Psalm 51 is the prayer he prayed then, and Psalm 32 reads like the testimony that came afterwards. JFB makes this connection, though the title does not. Paul quotes the opening verses in Romans 4:6–8 to show that God counts people righteous apart from their works. The early church counted it among the seven "penitential psalms" (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143), and Augustine's biographer Possidius says that as Augustine lay dying, he had those psalms written out on the wall where he could read them. Psalm 31 ended with David trusting God in danger from enemies. Psalm 32 is about a danger from within: his own hidden sin.

#### Blessed Is the One Whose Transgression Is Forgiven (vv1–2)
- **"Blessed is the one" (v1).** Literally "Oh, the blessednesses of," plural, the same word that opens the whole book of Psalms (1:1). Psalm 1 called blessed the man who never walks in the counsel of the wicked. Psalm 32 calls blessed the man who did, and was forgiven. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, calls it "double joys."
- **Four words for sin.** David piles them up. "Transgression" (*pesha'*) is rebellion, a deliberate crossing of a line, like a subject rebelling against his king. "Sin" (*chata'ah*) is missing the mark, falling short. "Iniquity" (*'avon*) is twistedness, a bending of what is straight, and it often includes the guilt that comes with it. "Deceit" (*remiyyah*) in v2 is the self-deception that hides it all. Between them they cover every angle of what had gone wrong in David.
- **Three pictures of forgiveness.** "Forgiven" (*nasa'*) is literally "lifted," a burden taken off one's back and carried away. On the Day of Atonement the goat "shall bear all their iniquities on itself to a remote area" (Lev 16:22). "Covered" (*kasah*) means hidden from sight, so that God no longer looks at it. "Counts no iniquity" (*lo' yachshov*) is a bookkeeping word: God does not enter it in the account against him. JFB: forgiven is "literally, 'taken away'"; covered, "so that God no longer regards the sin."
- **Paul's use (Rom 4:6–8).** Paul quotes vv1–2 as David's description of "the blessing of the one to whom God counts righteousness apart from works." The word for "count" is the same one Paul has just used of Abraham: "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness" (Gen 15:6; Rom 4:3). David the adulterer and murderer could not point to good works. He received forgiveness as a gift. Henry: this psalm, "though it speaks not of Christ… has yet a great deal of gospel in it."
- **"And in whose spirit there is no deceit" (v2).** This is not a second requirement that earns forgiveness. It describes the forgiven person. The blessed man is not someone with no sin but someone who no longer lies about it. Henry: "He does not say, 'There is no *guilt*'… but no *guile*." Jesus said of Nathanael, "Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit!" (John 1:47).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the forgiveness of sin "that article of the covenant which is the reason and ground of all the rest" (Heb 8:12), and describes the guileless man as one who does not "repent with a purpose to sin again, and then sin with a purpose to repent again." JFB links "no guile" with "no false estimate of himself." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's observation that after his sin with Bathsheba, David lost the open, truthful character he had shown before.

#### When I Kept Silent (vv3–5)
- **"For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away" (v3).** Silence here means refusing to confess. If the psalm reflects the Bathsheba story, this could have been the better part of a year, from the sin until Nathan came after the child was born (2 Sam 11:27–12:1). David kept ruling, worshipping and carrying on, but inside he was wasting away. "Bones" in Hebrew stand for the whole frame, the deepest part of a person (6:2; 31:10).
- **"Through my groaning all day long" (v3).** Silent toward God, but not silent. He groaned like a wounded animal. Keeping a secret from God does not bring peace. It brings misery.
- **"For day and night your hand was heavy upon me" (v4).** David recognizes that the pressure came from God. The Tyndale notes read it as God disciplining David (compare 38:2). It was not cruelty. It was mercy that would not let him go on lying. Hebrews says, "the Lord disciplines the one he loves" (Heb 12:6). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: better to carry the world on your shoulders like Atlas than to have God's hand pressing on your heart.
- **"My strength was dried up as by the heat of summer" (v4).** "Strength" is a rare Hebrew word (*leshad*) that probably means moisture, sap, the juice of life. JFB: "vital juices of the body." Like a plant in the burning heat of an Israelite summer, when no rain falls for months, David withered.
- **"Selah" (v4).** A musical or liturgical marker, probably calling for a pause or an instrumental interlude. It comes three times in this psalm (vv4, 5, 7), each at a turning point. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, thinks the pause here underlines this "terrible picture of a soul gnawing itself."
- **"I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity" (v5).** The same word "cover" as v1. When David covered his sin, God could not. When David uncovered it, God covered it. Proverbs says the same: "Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy" (Prov 28:13).
- **"I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,' and you forgave the iniquity of my sin" (v5).** The speed is the point. David says "I will confess," and the very next words are "you forgave." When Nathan confronted him, David said, "I have sinned against the LORD," and Nathan answered immediately, "The LORD also has put away your sin" (2 Sam 12:13). John's letter says it plainly: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins" (1 John 1:9). "Confess" (*yadah*) is the same verb used for giving thanks and praise. To confess sin is to agree with God, to say about it what he says.
- **"You" is emphatic (v5).** In Hebrew, "you" stands out: "and *you*, you forgave." Forgiveness is God's act, not the result of David's confession being good enough.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God is more ready to pardon sin, upon our repentance, than we are to repent in order to the obtaining of pardon." He pictures David "put to the rack before he was brought to" confess, but when he finally did, "see how quickly, how easily, he obtained good terms: 'I did but say, I will confess, and thou forgavest.'" He compares the father of the prodigal son, who saw him "when he was yet afar off" and ran to meet him (Luke 15:20). JFB: "A prompt fulfilment of the purposed confession is followed by a prompt forgiveness." Guzik passes on Kidner: "If David's symptoms are exceptional, his stubbornness is common enough." He also passes on Spurgeon's warning that confession from a hard heart, without real sorrow, only adds to guilt.

#### You Are a Hiding Place for Me (vv6–7)
- **"Therefore let everyone who is godly offer prayer to you" (v6).** David's experience becomes a lesson for others. "Godly" (*chasid*) means one who belongs to God's covenant love. Even the godly sin, and they too need to bring it to God.
- **"At a time when you may be found" (v6).** Literally "at a time of finding." Isaiah says, "Seek the LORD while he may be found; call upon him while he is near" (Isa 55:6). There is urgency here. Don't wait, as David did. The longer sin is hidden, the harder the heart grows (Heb 3:13). JFB: "when God's Spirit inclines us to seek pardon, He is ready to forgive."
- **"Surely in the rush of great waters, they shall not reach him" (v6).** Floods are the Bible's picture of overwhelming danger and judgment (18:16; 69:1–2; Isa 43:2). The person who has made peace with God will find the floodwaters stop short of him. The image recalls Noah, safe in the ark while the flood rose.
- **"You are a hiding place for me" (v7).** Notice the reversal. Earlier, David was hiding his sin from God. Now he hides himself in God. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: the same man who was crushed by God's presence in v4 now finds shelter in him. The previous psalm ended with God hiding his people "in the cover of your presence" (31:20).
- **"You surround me with shouts of deliverance" (v7).** Instead of the groaning of v3, David is surrounded by songs. "Shouts" (*ronne*) are ringing cries of joy. "Surround" will come back in v10.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "All godly people are praying people… You may as soon find a living man without breath as a living Christian without prayer." He adds that after forgiveness we still need God's keeping, because otherwise "we shall soon run as far in debt again as ever." JFB takes v7 as David's experience illustrating the promise of v6. Guzik passes on Horne: if angels came down to comfort a despairing sinner, they could say nothing better than v5.

#### I Will Instruct You (vv8–9)
- **"I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go" (v8).** "Instruct" (*sakal*) is the same root as "Maskil" in the title. Who is speaking? Many, including the Tyndale notes, hear God himself interrupting the psalm with a promise. Others, including JFB ("as most likely") and Henry, hear David turning to teach others what he has learned, as he promised in Psalm 51:13: "Then I will teach transgressors your ways." Both are possible, and in the end the teaching is God's either way.
- **"I will counsel you with my eye upon you" (v8).** Literally "I will counsel, my eye upon you." The picture is of a guide or parent who keeps an eye on the one he is leading. JFB: "My eye shall be on thee, watching and directing thy way." The KJV's "I will guide thee with mine eye" suggests guidance by a look, the way a close friend can direct you with a glance. Either way, the guidance is personal and attentive.
- **"Be not like a horse or a mule, without understanding" (v9).** A horse or mule has no "understanding" (*bin*). It has to be steered with a bit in its mouth and a bridle over its head. James uses the same picture (Jas 3:3). David had been like that, needing God's heavy hand (v4) to bring him round. The call is to be the kind of person who comes near when God speaks, not the kind who has to be dragged.
- **"Which must be curbed with bit and bridle, or it will not stay near you" (v9).** The Hebrew of the last line is difficult. JFB gives it as "in that they come not near thee," meaning the animal will not come unless forced. Henry notes that some read it the other way: the bridle is to keep the animal from coming near to hurt you. The ESV's reading fits the point of the psalm best: God wants his people near him, willingly.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "When Solomon became a penitent he immediately became a preacher," and "those are best able to teach others the grace of God who have themselves had the experience of it." Kidner, cited by Guzik, puts it simply: "If forgiveness is good, fellowship is better." Guzik passes on Clarke's warning not to force God to keep using hard providences to steer us. JFB lays out both possible speakers.

#### Steadfast Love Surrounds the One Who Trusts (vv10–11)
- **"Many are the sorrows of the wicked" (v10).** David knew those sorrows firsthand during his silence. They are not only punishments from outside. They are the misery of a life that will not be honest with God.
- **"But steadfast love surrounds the one who trusts in the LORD" (v10).** "Surrounds" is the same verb as in v7. God's people are surrounded by songs of deliverance and by steadfast love (*chesed*). The forgiven sinner lives inside God's covenant love the way a city lives inside its walls. Henry: mercy "shall keep them in" and "keep it out," so that they do not wander from God and no real evil breaks in.
- **"Be glad in the LORD, and rejoice, O righteous" (v11).** Who are "the righteous"? In this psalm, they are not people who never sinned. They are people whose sin has been forgiven and who no longer hide it. The guilty man of vv3–4 is now among the righteous.
- **"Shout for joy, all you upright in heart!" (v11).** The psalm that began with "blessed" ends with shouts of joy. The "upright in heart" are the same as those "in whose spirit there is no deceit" (v2).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this joy "that present bliss which the upright in heart, and they only, are entitled to." He urges God's people to "be so transported with this holy joy as not to be able to contain themselves." JFB: those "conforming to the divine teaching for securing the divine blessing, may well rejoice with shouting."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 32 says God "covers" sin and "counts no iniquity," but it does not say how a holy God can do that and stay just. The New Testament answers. Paul quotes these verses in Romans 4 right after explaining that God put Christ forward "as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith… so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:25–26). David's sin was not counted against him because it would be counted against another: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Cor 5:21). Jesus is the one in whose spirit there was truly no deceit (1 Pet 2:22), and he carried the iniquity of others, like the goat that bore Israel's sins away (Isa 53:6). Because of him, the way out of the misery of hidden sin is the same short step David took: "I will confess," and "you forgave."
