*Setting:* Jerusalem, 622 BC, then Megiddo in 609 BC. In his eighteenth year Josiah repaired the temple, and the Book of the Law was found. When it was read he tore his clothes, the prophetess Huldah confirmed the coming judgment and promised him he would die before it came, and he led the whole nation in renewing the covenant (ch 34). This chapter shows the high point of his reign, a Passover kept exactly by the book, and then jumps thirteen years to its sudden end. 2 Kings 23:21–23 gives the Passover three verses and his death two (23:29–30). Chronicles tells both at length. By 609 BC Assyria's capital Nineveh had fallen to Babylon (612 BC), and Egypt was marching north to prop up what was left of Assyria.

#### Josiah Keeps the Passover (vv1–19)
- **"On the fourteenth day of the first month" (v1).** The date the law set (Exod 12:6; Lev 23:5). Hezekiah's Passover had to be kept a month late because the priests were not ready (30:2–3). Guzik notes that this time everything was ready on time.
- **"He appointed the priests to their offices and encouraged them" (v2).** Guzik passes on Spurgeon's comment: first each person must be in the right place, then have the right spirit in it.
- **"The Levites who taught all Israel and who were holy to the LORD" (v3).** Teaching was part of the Levites' calling (Deut 33:10; 17:7–9). Here they are honored both as teachers and as men set apart.
- **"Put the holy ark in the house that Solomon… built. You need not carry it on your shoulders" (v3).** This is the last time the ark is mentioned in the Old Testament's history. The command implies it had been moved out of the Most Holy Place, perhaps by Manasseh when he put an idol in the temple (33:7), or by faithful priests to protect it, or during the repairs. The Tyndale notes and JFB suggest one or other of these. The Levites had once carried it on poles through the wilderness (Num 4:15; 1 Chr 15:15). Now that it has a permanent home, their job changes: "Now serve the LORD your God and his people Israel." What happened to it after this, Scripture does not say. Guzik passes on a Jewish tradition that it was hidden in Shallum's house; there are others. Jeremiah, preaching in these very years, said the day would come when no one would even miss it (Jer 3:16).
- **"As prescribed in the writing of David… and the document of Solomon" (v4).** David organized the priests and Levites into divisions (1 Chr 23–26), and Solomon put the system into practice at the temple (8:14). Josiah follows both, and Moses above them: "according to the word of the LORD by Moses" (v6). The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew there as "by the hand of" Moses.
- **"Stand in the Holy Place according to the groupings of the fathers' houses" (v5).** Each group of Levites served a set of lay families, so the huge crowd moved through in order. JFB describes the people entering the court family by family.
- **"Slaughter the Passover lamb… and prepare for your brothers" (v6).** In the first Passover each household killed its own lamb (Exod 12:6), and Deuteronomy assumed the same (Deut 16:5–6). As at Hezekiah's Passover (30:17), the Levites did it for the people. The Tyndale notes suggest the crowd was too large and many were not ritually ready. Josiah turns Hezekiah's emergency measure into the standard practice.
- **"Josiah contributed… 30,000, and 3,000 bulls; these were from the king's possessions" (v7).** The king gave the animals for the lay people from his own herds, as Hezekiah had (30:24), and his officials followed (vv8–9). JFB suggests the lambs went mainly to the poor, who could not afford their own. The Tyndale notes add that the totals were nearly double Hezekiah's, though less than at Solomon's dedication (7:5).
- **Hilkiah, Zechariah and Jehiel (v8).** Hilkiah is the high priest who found the book (34:14). The Levite leaders in v9 include a Conaniah, the name of Hezekiah's chief officer over the gifts (31:12), probably a descendant.
- **"The priests threw the blood… while the Levites flayed the sacrifices" (v11).** Everyone has a role. Only priests could handle the blood at the altar. The Levites killed and skinned. JFB notes this division of labor differed from Hezekiah's Passover.
- **"They set aside the burnt offerings that they might distribute them… to offer to the LORD" (v12).** The burnt offerings were portions to be burned wholly on the altar. "The bulls" were probably peace offerings for the feasting that followed, as Guzik passes on from Payne.
- **"They roasted the Passover lamb with fire according to the rule; and they boiled the holy offerings" (v13).** The law said the Passover lamb must be roasted, not boiled (Exod 12:8–9). Other offerings were boiled (1 Sam 2:13–15). The same Hebrew verb is used for both cooking methods here, so the Chronicler adds "with fire" to make clear the lamb was roasted as Moses commanded. Guzik passes on Trapp's reading of the roasting as a picture of Christ enduring the fire of God's wrath. "Carried them quickly to all the lay people": the Tyndale notes connect the haste to the first Passover, eaten "in haste" (Exod 12:11).
- **"Afterward they prepared for themselves and for the priests" (v14).** The Levites served everyone first, then the priests, who were at the altar "until night," and only then themselves. That is a picture of servant leadership.
- **"The singers… were in their place… and the gatekeepers were at each gate. They did not need to depart from their service" (v15).** The Levites brought food to the singers and gatekeepers so they did not have to leave their posts. The law said nothing about singing at Passover, as Guzik notes, but David had appointed singers for temple worship. JFB says they sang the Hallel, Psalms 113–118, which later Jewish practice tied to Passover. The text does not say which songs they sang, but that is what was sung at Passover in Jesus' day.
- **"No Passover like it had been kept in Israel since the days of Samuel the prophet" (v18).** Kings says "since the days of the judges" (2 Kings 23:22). Samuel was the last judge, so the two mean the same. But what about Hezekiah's Passover, which was also called unmatched "since the time of Solomon" (30:26)? Hezekiah's was unequaled for joy and length. Josiah's was unequaled for exact obedience and organization: on the right date, with the priests ready and every rule followed. Guzik gives three reasons for its greatness: its size, drawing north and south; its strict obedience to Moses; and the darkness of the years around it. JFB credits the people's devotion and careful keeping of the law.
- **"All Judah and Israel who were present" (v18).** Again the Chronicler includes the north.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes Selman's point that the phrase "the service was prepared" (vv10, 16) is rare and appears also at the completion of Solomon's temple and at Hezekiah's restoration (8:16; 29:35), marking a work finished as God required. JFB explains the ark's absence as possibly due to earlier kings or the repairs, and describes the singers working in relays. Henry's chapter-level comments focus more on Josiah's death.

#### Josiah Killed in Battle (vv20–27)
- **"After all this, when Josiah had prepared the temple" (v20).** Thirteen years pass in one phrase. Chronicles has just shown that the temple was "prepared" (vv10, 16). Now Josiah goes out to battle unprepared in another sense.
- **"Neco king of Egypt went up to fight at Carchemish on the Euphrates" (v20).** Pharaoh Neco II (610–595 BC). Carchemish was a fortress city on the Euphrates, in what is now the Turkey–Syria border region. In 609 BC Neco marched north to help the last Assyrian king retake Haran from Babylon. The Babylonian Chronicle confirms Egypt was Assyria's ally. Four years later, in 605 BC, Nebuchadnezzar would crush Egypt at Carchemish (Jer 46:2).
- **"Josiah went out to meet him" (v20).** Why? The text does not say. The Tyndale notes suggest he may have been allied with Babylon, or trying to keep Egypt from controlling his land. He may simply have hated Assyria, which had dominated Judah for a century.
- **"What have we to do with each other, king of Judah?" (v21).** Neco's message is reasonable: I'm not here to fight you, but "the house with which I am at war," meaning Babylon. Then he adds, "God has commanded me to hurry. Cease opposing God, who is with me, lest he destroy you."
- **"The words of Neco from the mouth of God" (v22).** This is the striking part. The Chronicler himself says Neco's words came "from the mouth of God." God can speak through a pagan king, as he did through Balaam (Num 22–24), and he later called Nebuchadnezzar "my servant" (Jer 25:9). Neco may have meant his own god. Guzik compares it to Caiaphas, who spoke God's truth without knowing it (John 11:49–52). JFB says it is unclear whether Neco's claim was sincere, and suggests Josiah's opposition may have been a sin of ignorance. A later apocryphal retelling (1 Esdras 1:28) says the warning came through the prophet Jeremiah, but Chronicles says nothing of that.
- **"Josiah did not turn away from him, but disguised himself" (v22).** Here is irony. Josiah is the king who "did not turn aside to the right hand or to the left" from God's word (34:2). Now he will "not turn away" from a fight God told him to avoid. And the disguise recalls Ahab, Israel's most wicked king, who disguised himself before the battle where an archer's arrow killed him (18:29–34).
- **"The plain of Megiddo" (v22).** Megiddo guarded the pass from the coast into the Jezreel Valley, one of the most fought-over places in the ancient world. Its name lies behind "Armageddon," *Har-Megiddo*, "the mountain of Megiddo" (Rev 16:16).
- **"The archers shot King Josiah. And the king said… 'Take me away, for I am badly wounded'" (v23).** Ahab said nearly the same words: "Carry me out of the battle, for I am wounded" (18:33). Guzik passes on Selman's sad point that the best king since David died the way a king who hated the LORD died. The Chronicler wants readers to feel it.
- **"Carried him in his second chariot and brought him to Jerusalem. And he died" (v24).** JFB takes the "second chariot" as his ordinary carriage, more comfortable than a war chariot. 2 Kings 23:29 says Neco killed him at Megiddo; Chronicles says he died after reaching Jerusalem, or on the way. Both may describe the same thing: a mortal wound at Megiddo, and death soon after.
- **"Buried in the tombs of his fathers" (v24).** This is how Huldah's promise came true: "you shall be gathered to your grave in peace, and your eyes shall not see all the disaster" (34:28). He died in battle, but he was buried in Jerusalem with honor before any Babylonian army arrived.
- **"Jeremiah also uttered a lament for Josiah" (v25).** Jeremiah, who began preaching in Josiah's thirteenth year, honored him. He later contrasted Josiah with his son Jehoiakim: "Did not your father eat and drink and do justice and righteousness? Then it was well with him. He judged the cause of the poor and needy… Is not this to know me? declares the LORD" (Jer 22:15–16).
- **"They made these a rule in Israel; behold, they are written in the Laments" (v25).** The mourning for Josiah became a yearly custom. The Tyndale notes say this collection of laments is lost and is not our book of Lamentations, which mourns the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC. A century later Zechariah pictured Israel's future mourning for "him whom they have pierced" as "the mourning for Hadad-rimmon in the plain of Megiddo" (Zech 12:10–11). Many, including JFB, take that as a reference to the grief over Josiah.
- **"His good deeds according to what is written in the Law of the LORD" (v26).** The Chronicler's summary is generous. One bad decision at the end did not cancel a life lived by the book. "Good deeds" (*chasadim*) is literally "acts of devotion," from *chesed*, steadfast love, the same word used of Hezekiah (32:32).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Josiah was "cut off in the midst of his days and usefulness," and blames him for not seeking God before going to war: "His not doing that was his great fault, and of fatal consequence." He also observes about the nation's mourning that people are often more easily persuaded "to lament the miseries that are coming upon them" than to give up the sins that bring them. Guzik notes that, unlike his godly ancestors, Josiah is not recorded praying before the battle, and passes on Selman's remark that sanctified common sense would have been enough. He also quotes Morgan, who suspects the people's devotion was more to Josiah than to God. JFB links the lament to Zechariah 12:11 and treats Josiah's fault gently, as possibly a sin of ignorance.

#### Pointing to Christ
Josiah's Passover was the most faithful ever kept: the lamb roasted with fire, the blood at the altar, the Levites serving everyone before themselves, the singers in their place. Then the faithful king died of an arrow at Megiddo, and the nation mourned him for generations. Zechariah took that mourning and turned it into a promise: one day Jerusalem will look "on me, on him whom they have pierced," and mourn as they mourned at Megiddo, and on that day "a fountain" will be opened "to cleanse them from sin" (Zech 12:10–13:1). John saw that promise begin at the cross (John 19:37). Jesus kept the Passover in Jerusalem, sang the hymns with his disciples (Mark 14:26), and then became the Passover lamb himself (1 Cor 5:7). Unlike Josiah, he did not die for his own mistake. He was pierced for our transgressions (Isa 53:5).
