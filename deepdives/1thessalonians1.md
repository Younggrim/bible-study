*Setting:* Paul writes from Corinth around AD 50–51, only a few months after founding this church, which makes 1 Thessalonians one of his earliest letters and possibly the earliest (some scholars put Galatians first). Thessalonica was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia (northern Greece today), a busy port on the Via Egnatia, the great Roman road running east to west across the Balkans. Acts 17:1–10 tells the story: Paul preached in the synagogue for three Sabbaths, a number of Jews, many God-fearing Greeks and "not a few of the leading women" believed, and then a jealous mob dragged Paul's host Jason before the city officials with the charge that these men "have turned the world upside down." Paul and Silas left by night. He worried about the young church he had been forced to abandon, sent Timothy back to check on them (3:1–5), and wrote this letter when Timothy returned with good news (3:6). Chapter 1 is almost all thanksgiving: Paul tells them what God has plainly done among them.

#### Greeting (v1)
- **"Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy" (v1).** All three had been in Thessalonica together. Silvanus is the Latin form of Silas, Paul's partner since the split with Barnabas (Acts 15:40). He was beaten and jailed with Paul at Philippi just before they reached this city (Acts 16:19–24; compare 2:2). Timothy, the youngest, had joined them at Lystra (Acts 16:1–3). Paul names them because the letter really is from a team, and "we" runs through nearly the whole letter.
- **No title.** Paul does not call himself "apostle" here, as he does in most of his letters. Nobody in Thessalonica was questioning his authority, so he writes as a friend more than as an official.
- **"To the church of the Thessalonians" (v1).** "Church" is *ekklēsia*, the ordinary Greek word for a gathered assembly of citizens. Thessalonica had its own civic assembly. This one is different because of where it lives: "in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." It is located in a city, but its real address is God.
- **One preposition for Father and Son (v1).** In Greek a single "in" governs both "God the Father" and "the Lord Jesus Christ." In what may be his first letter, about twenty years after the cross, Paul already sets Jesus beside the Father as the sphere in which the church lives. No ordinary man could be named that way.
- **"Grace to you and peace" (v1).** Greek letters normally opened with *chairein*, "greetings." Paul turns it into *charis*, "grace," and adds the Hebrew greeting *shalom*, "peace." Grace is God's undeserved kindness, and peace is the wholeness it brings. The order never reverses: grace first, then peace.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both note that Paul drops the title "apostle" because his standing with this church needed no defense. Guzik draws out the theological weight of joining Father and Son under one preposition, and he points out that Paul had gathered new converts into a real community, not just an audience. JFB adds that "in" here implies "vital union." Henry treats the greeting briefly and moves to the thanksgiving.

#### The Thessalonians' Faith and Example (vv2–10)
- **"We give thanks to God always for all of you" (v2).** This is the first of three thanksgivings in the letter (2:13; 3:9). Paul thanks *God*, not the Thessalonians. What happened in them was God's work. "All of you" matters too. Later chapters show the church was not perfect: there were questions about sexual purity (4:3–8), idleness (4:11–12; 5:14) and grief over believers who had died (4:13). Paul still thanks God for every one of them.
- **"Constantly mentioning you in our prayers" (v2).** "Constantly" does not mean nonstop praying. It means regular, never-dropped. They come up again and again whenever Paul prays.
- **"Your work of faith and labor of love and steadfastness of hope" (v3).** Here is Paul's favorite triad, faith, love and hope, in what may be its first appearance in his letters (compare 5:8; 1 Cor 13:13; Col 1:4–5). Each one is shown by what it produces: faith produces *work*, love produces *labor*, and hope produces *steadfastness*. Faith that does nothing is not what Paul means by faith (compare Gal 5:6; Jas 2:17).
- **"Labor of love" (v3).** The word is *kopos*, toil that leaves a person tired. Greek writers used it to stress the fatigue more than the effort. This is love that costs something: late nights, sore hands, worn-out patience. Paul uses the same word for his own work in Thessalonica (2:9).
- **"Steadfastness of hope" (v3).** *Hypomonē* is endurance, the ability to keep standing under a load. It is not passive waiting. The Thessalonians were enduring persecution (v6; 2:14), and what held them up was hope "in our Lord Jesus Christ," the expectation of his return that closes this chapter (v10).
- **"Before our God and Father" (v3).** Paul is not only remembering them fondly. He brings them into God's presence as he remembers.
- **"Brothers loved by God… he has chosen you" (v4).** "Brothers" (*adelphoi*) includes the sisters too, the whole church family. Former pagans, a few months old in the faith, are told they are loved by God and chosen by him. "Chosen" (*eklogē*, "election") speaks of God's initiative: they did not stumble into faith by luck.
- **How Paul knows they are chosen (vv4–5).** Paul does not claim a look into God's hidden decrees. He reasons from what he saw: the gospel came "in power" and the Thessalonians welcomed it "with joy" under persecution. Election is known by its fruit. Christians have long disagreed on how election works: Reformed readers see God choosing individuals unconditionally, while Arminian and Wesleyan readers see God choosing those he foreknew would believe, or choosing the church as a body in Christ. All agree on Paul's point here: salvation begins with God, and the evidence of it is a changed life.
- **"Not only in word, but also in power and in the Holy Spirit and with full conviction" (v5).** The gospel came as words, of course: Paul reasoned from the Scriptures for three Sabbaths (Acts 17:2–3). But the words came loaded. "Power" (*dynamis*) may include miracles, but Paul's main point is the power that changed people (Rom 1:16). "Full conviction" is *plērophoria*, "full assurance, certainty." It probably describes both the preachers, who spoke with complete confidence, and the hearers, whom the Spirit persuaded (compare 1 Cor 2:4–5).
- **"You know what kind of men we proved to be among you" (v5).** This sentence opens the theme of chapter 2. Paul's life backed up his message. He will need to make that case, because after he left, his enemies apparently said he was one more traveling talker after money (2:3–6).
- **"You became imitators of us and of the Lord" (v6).** The Greek *mimētai* gives English "mimic." The order is striking: "of us and of the Lord." New believers learned to follow Christ by watching people who already followed him. Paul says the same elsewhere: "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ" (1 Cor 11:1; Phil 3:17).
- **"In much affliction, with the joy of the Holy Spirit" (v6).** "Affliction" is *thlipsis*, literally "pressure" or "crushing," used in the New Testament for distress and persecution. The riot of Acts 17:5–9 did not end when Paul left. The believers lost friends, standing and perhaps work (2:14). The joy did not come from the circumstances. It came from the Spirit, which is why it could sit right beside pain (Acts 5:41; 16:25).
- **"An example to all the believers in Macedonia and in Achaia" (v7).** Macedonia was the northern province (Philippi, Berea) and Achaia the southern one (Athens, Corinth), where Paul was writing. So the whole of Greece had heard. The word "example" is *typos*, the mark left by a blow, a stamp or pattern. The imitators (v6) became the pattern for others (v7) in a few months.
- **"The word of the Lord sounded forth from you" (v8).** The verb *exēcheō* occurs only here in the New Testament. It means "to sound forth" like a trumpet or a roll of thunder. Thessalonica sat on a major road and a busy harbor, so traders and travelers carried the news everywhere. Paul says he no longer has to tell people about them. People were telling him.
- **"How you turned to God from idols" (v9).** This tells us most of the church were Gentiles, former worshipers of the Greek gods, the local mystery cult of Cabirus and the cult of the Roman emperor. Leaving the gods was costly. In the ancient world, the city's gods were part of civic life, family life and trade guilds. Refusing them looked disloyal and could cost a job (compare Acts 19:25–27). Note the direction: they turned *to God from idols*. They were drawn to someone better before they let go of what they had.
- **"To serve the living and true God" (v9).** "Serve" (*douleuō*) means to serve as a slave. They traded one set of masters for another, and the new Master is "living," unlike lifeless images (Ps 115:4–8), and "true," the real God and not a counterfeit (Jer 10:10).
- **"And to wait for his Son from heaven" (v10).** The verb *anamenō*, used only here, means to wait for someone whose coming is expected, with patience and confidence. This is the "steadfastness of hope" of v3. Every chapter of this letter ends with the Lord's return (2:19; 3:13; 4:13–18; 5:23), and this verse introduces that theme.
- **"Whom he raised from the dead" (v10).** The resurrection is the proof that Jesus will come again. In Athens a few weeks later, Paul said the same thing: God "has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead" (Acts 17:31).
- **"Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come" (v10).** "Delivers" (*rhyomai*) means to rescue, to pull someone to oneself out of danger. The Greek is a present participle: Jesus is "the one who rescues us." "The wrath to come" is God's final judgment on sin (Rom 2:5; 5:9). It is coming, but believers do not wait for it in fear, because their Rescuer is the one they are waiting for. Paul returns to this in 5:9: "God has not destined us for wrath." Readers who hold a pre-tribulation rapture often see this verse as pointing to rescue before a coming tribulation, while most others take "the wrath to come" as final judgment. Either way the comfort is the same.
- **Verses 9–10 as a summary.** Many scholars think these lines condense Paul's preaching to Gentiles: turn from idols, serve the living God, wait for his Son, raised from the dead, who rescues from judgment. Compare Paul's speeches at Lystra (Acts 14:15) and Athens (Acts 17:24–31).
- **Commentators:** All three see faith, love and hope as the root of the Thessalonians' visible life. Henry: "Wherever there is a true faith, it will work." JFB notes that "work" is singular, one continuous outworking of "a realizing, working faith," and that "labour" means toil. On election, Henry says it "may be known by the fruits thereof," and Guzik passes on Spurgeon's evidences of election drawn from vv5–6: the gospel coming with power, deep assurance, a desire to imitate Christ, and joy in suffering. Henry: "Wherever the gospel comes in power, it is to be attributed to the operation of the Holy Ghost." Guzik and JFB both hear a trumpet in "sounded forth" (v8), which the lexicon supports, and JFB pictures merchants carrying the report. Guzik notes the Thessalonians had good examples before they became examples, and he warns that a church only spreads a message that has first changed it. JFB stresses that "delivers" is present tense: "He is our Deliverer ALWAYS." Henry calls waiting for the Son "one of the peculiarities of our holy religion."

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole chapter rests on Jesus. The church lives "in" him alongside the Father (v1), its hope is "in our Lord Jesus Christ" (v3), believers become imitators "of the Lord" (v6), and the chapter ends with him: God's Son, raised from the dead, coming from heaven, rescuing his people from the wrath they deserved (v10). A young church in a hostile city had little else, but it had a living Lord who was coming back for it, and that was enough to make it ring out across Greece.
