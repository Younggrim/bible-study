*Setting:* The fortieth year after the exodus. Miriam and Aaron are dead (ch 20), Edom has refused passage, and Israel must take the long way south and around to approach Canaan from the east, across the Jordan. This chapter is a turning point. For the first time in Numbers, Israel wins battles. It begins with a victory at the very place of their earlier defeat, has one more outbreak of grumbling, answered by the bronze serpent, and then moves quickly through a string of camps to the plains above the Dead Sea, defeating two Amorite kings on the way. The new generation is starting to look like an army that trusts God. Many of the place names are hard to locate today, and the route is debated, but the direction of travel is clear.

#### Arad Destroyed (vv1–3)
- **"The Canaanite, the king of Arad, who lived in the Negeb" (v1).** Arad lay in the Negeb, the dry southern region of Canaan, roughly fifty miles north of Kadesh. The modern Israeli town of Arad preserves the name. Excavations at Tell Arad have found no settlement from the time of Moses, so some scholars place the ancient city at a nearby site, and others think "Arad" named a whole district. The question is not settled.
- **"By the way of Atharim" (v1).** The meaning is uncertain. Some old translations read it as "the way of the spies," the route the twelve had taken forty years earlier (13:21). **"Took some of them captive."** The new generation's first encounter with the Canaanites starts badly.
- **"Israel vowed a vow to the LORD" (v2).** This time they do not charge ahead in their own strength, as their parents did (14:40–45). They turn to God first. **"I will devote their cities to destruction"** uses *haram*, to give something over wholly to God, often by destruction, so that no one profits from the plunder. Israel promises to keep nothing for themselves.
- **"The LORD heeded the voice of Israel" (v3).** God answers the prayer and gives the victory.
- **"So the name of the place was called Hormah" (v3).** *Hormah* comes from the same root as *haram*, "devoted to destruction." This is the very place where their parents had been routed (14:45). Where the first generation was destroyed in disobedience, the second wins in faith.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads Arad's attack as a test of Israel's faith, which this time they passed by turning to God. Guzik points out that Hormah was the site of the earlier defeat and that taking no plunder marked the battle as God's judgment, not Israel's gain. JFB thinks the lesson of the early captives was to teach Israel to rely on God, not their own valor, and suggests the full destruction may have come later, after the conquest (compare Judg 1:16–17).

#### The Bronze Serpent (vv4–9)
- **"By the way to the Red Sea, to go around the land of Edom" (v4).** Edom's refusal meant a long, hot detour south toward the Gulf of Aqaba through the Arabah, the desert rift valley south of the Dead Sea, then north again along Edom's eastern edge. After thirty-eight years in the wilderness and a fresh start, they were walking away from the land again.
- **"The people became impatient on the way" (v4).** Literally "the soul of the people became short," the opposite of patience. They ran out of endurance.
- **"Spoke against God and against Moses" (v5).** Earlier complaints were aimed at Moses and Aaron. This one is aimed directly at God, the first time Numbers says so this bluntly.
- **"There is no food and no water, and we loathe this worthless food" (v5).** Two complaints that cancel each other out: "there is no food," and "we hate the food we have." "Worthless" (*qeloqel*) means contemptible or miserable, a word found only here. They are talking about the manna, "the bread of the angels" (Ps 78:25), which had fed them every day for forty years.
- **"The LORD sent fiery serpents among the people" (v6).** "Fiery" is *saraph*, "burning." It probably describes the burning pain and inflammation of the bite, or perhaps the snakes' coppery color. The plural, *seraphim*, is the same word as the burning angelic beings of Isaiah 6:2. The Arabah and Sinai are home to dangerous vipers, such as the carpet viper and the horned viper (Deut 8:15; Isa 30:6). **"Many people of Israel died."**
- **"We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD and against you" (v7).** Unlike earlier generations, this one confesses quickly and plainly. **"Pray to the LORD."** They ask the man they have just insulted to pray for them, and Moses does, without a word of reproach. **"So Moses prayed for the people."**
- **"Make a fiery serpent and set it on a pole" (v8).** God does not take the snakes away, as the people asked. He gives a remedy while the danger remains. And the remedy is strange: an image of the very thing that was killing them. **"Everyone who is bitten, when he sees it, shall live."**
- **"Moses made a bronze serpent" (v9).** There is a wordplay in Hebrew: "serpent" is *nachash* and "bronze" is *nechoshet*, a *nachash nechoshet*. Bronze or copper also has the reddish color of the "fiery" serpents. The Timna valley north of Aqaba, in exactly this region, had ancient copper mines, and archaeologists found a small copper snake in a shrine there, which shows that snake images were known in this area.
- **Was this an idol?** No. The second commandment forbids making images to worship (Exod 20:4–5). This image was commanded by God and was not worshiped. It was a sign to look at in faith. But it later did become an idol. Centuries afterward, Israelites were burning incense to it in Jerusalem, and they called it *Nehushtan*. King Hezekiah broke it in pieces (2 Kgs 18:4). A good gift from God can become a snare when people honor the object instead of the God who gave it.
- **"He would look at the bronze serpent and live" (v9).** The cure was simple: look. No ritual, no payment, no medicine, no effort. Even a dying person could turn their eyes to the pole. But they had to look, which meant trusting God's word that looking would save them. The apocryphal Wisdom of Solomon, written before Christ, already saw this: the one who turned toward it "was saved, not by what he saw, but by you, the Savior of all" (Wis 16:7).
- **Jesus' own reading (John 3:14–15).** Jesus took this event as a picture of himself: "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life." The serpent pictured the deadly thing, sin, judged and lifted up. Christ, who knew no sin, was "made to be sin" for us (2 Cor 5:21) and lifted up on a cross, and life comes by looking to him in faith.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "those that are of a fretful discontented spirit will always find something or other to make them uneasy," and that "justly are those made to feel God's judgments that are not thankful for his mercies." He sees "much of gospel in this appointment," and stresses that the serpent "would not cure if it was not looked upon," while adding that whoever believes in Christ, "though as yet but weak in faith, shall not perish." Guzik sees the serpent as a picture of evil judged, since serpents often represent evil in Scripture and bronze is associated with judgment. He notes that healing came by looking, not by touching or ritual, and connects it to Isaiah 45:22, "Turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth," the verse through which Spurgeon came to faith. JFB explains "light bread" as bread "without substance or nutritious quality," and says "fiery" may refer to "their bright, vivid color" or to the inflammation of the bite. The Tyndale notes add that this region still has extremely poisonous snakes.

#### The Song of the Well (vv10–20)
- **The route north (vv10–13).** Israel camps at Oboth, then **Iye-abarim**, "ruins of the regions beyond," then the **Valley of Zered**, the stream bed that formed the border between Edom and Moab, and then beyond the **Arnon**, the great canyon (today Wadi Mujib) that cuts into the plateau east of the Dead Sea. Deuteronomy 2:13–14 marks the crossing of the Zered as the moment the last of the first generation had died. The Arnon was the border between Moab and the Amorites. Israel was told not to attack Moab, since they were descendants of Lot (Deut 2:9).
- **"The Book of the Wars of the LORD" (v14).** An ancient collection of war songs, now lost, much like "the Book of Jashar" (Josh 10:13; 2 Sam 1:18). The quoted lines are fragmentary and hard to translate, and the places **Waheb** and **Suphah** are unknown. The point of the quotation is to confirm the Arnon as Moab's border. Quoting another source does not mean the Bible is incomplete. It means the biblical writers used and cited earlier records, as Luke says he did (Luke 1:1–3).
- **"Beer… the well of which the LORD said… 'Gather the people together, so that I may give them water'" (v16).** *Beer* simply means "well." For once, the water comes without complaint. God provides before they ask.
- **"Spring up, O well!—Sing to it!" (vv17–18).** This is the first song Israel has sung since the song at the sea forty years earlier (Exod 15). It is a work song, the kind people sang as they dug. **"The well that the princes made… with the scepter and with their staffs."** The leaders join in the digging with their staffs of office, a picture of a ceremonial dig in which everyone shares the joy. The contrast with Meribah (20:2–13) is complete: there, quarreling and a struck rock; here, a song.
- **"To the valley… by the top of Pisgah that looks down on the desert" (v20).** Pisgah is a ridge at the edge of the plateau overlooking the Jordan valley and the Dead Sea. Moses will view the land from there before he dies (Deut 34:1). The list of camps brings Israel to the edge of the promised land. Because the battle with Sihon (vv21–30) cleared this region, many think vv21–35 describe events that happened before the arrival at Pisgah, told separately.
- **Commentators:** Henry emphasizes the people's joyful gratitude at Beer. Guzik notes that this is the first recorded song since the Red Sea, and rejects the idea that mentioning a lost book makes Scripture incomplete. He cites Maclaren's picture of the leaders digging with their staffs. JFB thinks the well may have been hidden by brush or sand and that the song suits caravan life, where water brings "prayer and thanksgiving." JFB calls Ar "the capital of Moab" and identifies the Arnon as "now El-Mojib, a deep, broad, and rapid stream."

#### King Sihon Defeated (vv21–30)
- **"Israel sent messengers to Sihon king of the Amorites" (v21).** The Amorites were a people found across the ancient Near East. These Amorites had pushed into the region east of the Jordan, between Moab and Ammon. Israel's request is almost word for word the request to Edom (20:17), a peaceful offer to stay on **"the King's Highway"** and touch nothing.
- **"Sihon would not allow Israel to pass" (v23).** He gathers his whole army and attacks at **Jahaz**. Deuteronomy 2:30 adds that God "hardened his spirit," not making a good man evil, but confirming Sihon in the hostility he had chosen, so that the land would come to Israel. Unlike Edom, Moab and Ammon, the Amorites were not Israel's relatives, and their time of judgment had come (Gen 15:16).
- **"Israel defeated him… and took possession of his land from the Arnon to the Jabbok" (v24).** The Jabbok is the river where Jacob once wrestled with God (Gen 32:22–30), and now his descendants hold the land up to it. **"The border of the Ammonites was strong"** may mean it was well defended, or that Israel respected it as God had commanded (Deut 2:19).
- **"Israel settled in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon" (v25).** Heshbon, about sixteen miles north of the Arnon, was Sihon's capital. This territory became the home of Reuben and Gad (ch 32). Israel now has land, the first piece of the inheritance.
- **"Sihon… had fought against the former king of Moab and taken all his land" (v26).** This explains why Israel could take the land: it was no longer Moab's. Centuries later, when Moab claimed it back, Jephthah used exactly this argument (Judg 11:12–27). The Moabite Stone (Mesha Inscription, about 840 BC) records a Moabite king retaking some of these same towns from Israel, including Dibon and Medeba.
- **"Therefore the ballad singers say" (vv27–30).** "Ballad singers" are those who speak in proverbs or taunt songs (*moshelim*). The song seems originally to be an Amorite victory song celebrating Sihon's conquest of Moab: **"fire came out from Heshbon… It devoured Ar of Moab."** Israel takes it over and turns it into a taunt. Sihon beat Moab, and now Israel has beaten Sihon. **"You are undone, O people of Chemosh!"** (v29). Chemosh was Moab's national god, and the song mocks him for failing to protect his people. Jeremiah later echoes these lines in his prophecy against Moab (Jer 48:45–46).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes from Deuteronomy 2:30 that God hardened Sihon's heart without making him evil, giving him over to what he wanted. He cites Wenham that Israel reused the Amorite song to support its claim, and Allen that the chapter sets up the story of Balak and Balaam that follows. JFB calls the song "an extract from an Amorite song exultingly anticipating an extension of their conquests to Arnon," with vv28–29 as Israel's mocking reply. The Tyndale notes add that Heshbon is usually identified with Tell Hesban, which has not yielded evidence from this period, so the question of its location remains open.

#### King Og Defeated (vv31–35)
- **"Moses sent to spy out Jazer" (v32).** This time the spies bring back a good report, and Israel takes the towns. The contrast with chapter 13 is deliberate.
- **"Went up by the way to Bashan" (v33).** Bashan was the rich plateau east of the Sea of Galilee, famous for its cattle and oaks (Ps 22:12; Amos 4:1; Isa 2:13). **"Og the king of Bashan came out against them… to battle at Edrei."**
- **Who was Og?** Deuteronomy 3:11 says he was the last of the Rephaim, a race of giants, and that his iron bed, or possibly coffin, was nine cubits long, about thirteen feet. He was exactly the kind of enemy who had terrified the spies forty years before (13:32–33).
- **"Do not fear him, for I have given him into your hand" (v34).** This is the word their parents never believed. Note the past tense: "I have given." The victory is already settled in God's mind before the battle begins.
- **"They defeated him and his sons and all his people… And they possessed his land" (v35).** The giant falls. Israel now holds the whole region east of the Jordan from the Arnon to Bashan. The victories over Sihon and Og became one of Israel's favorite memories of God's faithfulness, sung in worship for centuries (Ps 135:10–12; 136:17–22; Neh 9:22). Rahab in Jericho had heard about them (Josh 2:10).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls Og "a giant, an Amoritish prince," and says God's assurance was needed because Og's size would inspire terror. Guzik notes Og's reputation as a giant and that his land became part of the eastern tribes' inheritance. He closes by noting that many challenges remained, but Israel had begun to trust God.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus chose this chapter to explain the heart of the gospel to Nicodemus (John 3:14–16). Bitten, dying people were told to look at a serpent lifted on a pole, and those who looked lived. In the same way, the Son of Man was lifted up on a cross, bearing the curse of sin, so that "whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life." The cure was not earned or worked for. It was received by looking. And the giants who once made Israel feel like grasshoppers fell before a people who finally believed God's word: "Do not fear him, for I have given him into your hand." The same promise stands behind every believer, for "in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us" (Rom 8:37).
