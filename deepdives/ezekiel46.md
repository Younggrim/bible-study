*Setting:* The temple vision of chapters 40–48 continues (573 BC). Chapter 45 gave the prince his own land so he would not grab anyone else's, and it set out the offerings he provides for the year's great feasts. Chapter 46 completes the picture of worship in the restored temple with the weekly and monthly rhythm: which gate opens when, where the prince stands, how the crowds move through the courts at the festivals, and the lamb offered every morning. It adds one more safeguard for the people's land, and then the tour of the temple ends where it started, in the outer court, this time in the kitchens where sacrifices were cooked for the priests and for the worshipers' own meals before God. After this, the vision turns to a river flowing out from the temple (ch 47).

#### The Prince and the Feasts (vv1–18)
- **"The gate of the inner court that faces east shall be shut on the six working days" (v1).** This is the inner east gate, between the outer and inner courts, not the outer east gate that stays shut forever (44:1–2). "Working days" is literally "days of work," the language of the Sabbath command (Exod 20:9). The week has its own shape: six days of ordinary life, then a day when the gate stands open.
- **"On the Sabbath day it shall be opened, and on the day of the new moon" (v1).** The Sabbath was the sign of God's covenant with Israel (Exod 31:13; Ezek 20:12), and its neglect was one of the sins Ezekiel listed against Jerusalem (22:8, 26). New moons marked the start of each month with offerings and trumpets (Num 10:10; 28:11–15). Both are restored here.
- **"The prince shall enter by the vestibule of the gate from outside, and shall take his stand by the post of the gate" (v2).** The prince comes in from the outer court and stands at the inner end of the gateway, looking into the inner court where the altar stands. That is as far as he goes. The Tyndale notes see both sides here: he is honored above the common people, but he still cannot stand in God's presence without the priests.
- **"The priests shall offer his burnt offering… and he shall worship at the threshold" (v2).** "Worship" is *shachah*, to bow low, to lie face down. The ruler of God's people is first a worshiper. **"The gate shall not be shut until evening"** gives the people time to come and look in.
- **"The people of the land shall bow down at the entrance of that gate" (v3).** Ordinary Israelites could not enter the inner court, but on Sabbaths and new moons they could stand at the outer end of the open gate and see through to the altar. The prince bows at the threshold, the people at the entrance, all before the same LORD.
- **"Six lambs without blemish and a ram" (v4).** Moses required two lambs on the Sabbath (Num 28:9–10). Ezekiel's Sabbath offering is much larger. **"As much as he is able"** (v5) leaves the amount of grain with the lambs to the prince's generosity. The new moon offering (vv6–7) is one bull, a ram and six lambs, where Moses set two bulls, a ram and seven lambs (Num 28:11). The differences from the law of Moses are another sign that the vision is describing a new order, not simply copying the old.
- **"He shall enter by the vestibule of the gate, and he shall go out by the same way" (v8).** The prince on his own uses the gateway like a private entrance and leaves the way he came.
- **"He who enters by the north gate… shall go out by the south gate" (v9).** At the great pilgrim feasts thousands crowded the courts. A one-way flow kept the crowds from crushing into one another, as JFB notes. But many readers from Henry onward also see a lesson: no one should leave the presence of God exactly as he came in. The Tyndale notes add a detail: the people move on the north–south line, while the priests' service runs along the sacred east–west line from the gate to the altar to the Most Holy Place.
- **"No one shall return by way of the gate by which he entered, but each shall go out straight ahead" (v9).** "Straight ahead" is literally "in front of him." The repetition makes the rule emphatic.
- **"When they enter, the prince shall enter with them, and when they go out, he shall go out" (v10).** At the festivals the prince does not use his special entrance. He walks in the crowd. David danced before the ark among the people (2 Sam 6:14–15), and the psalmist remembered leading the throng "to the house of God… a multitude keeping festival" (Ps 42:4). A ruler among his people at worship was a picture of what Israel's kings should have been (Deut 17:20: "that his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers").
- **"At the feasts and the appointed festivals" (v11).** The grain offerings are restated: an ephah with each bull and ram, and with lambs as much as one is able. The wording shifts from "he" to "one," widening the generosity rule to anyone giving.
- **"When the prince provides a freewill offering… the gate facing east shall be opened for him" (v12).** A freewill offering was a gift beyond what the law required, given out of gratitude (Lev 22:18–23; Deut 16:10). The gate opens even on an ordinary day for a gift of love, then closes when he leaves.
- **"A lamb a year old without blemish… daily; morning by morning" (v13).** This is the "regular" or continual burnt offering (*tamid*), the heartbeat of temple worship (Exod 29:38–42). Under Moses there were two lambs, morning and evening (Num 28:3–8). Ezekiel names only the morning. Some, including Poole as Guzik notes, assume the evening offering is implied. Others think the omission is deliberate, part of the vision's many changes. Elijah prayed "at the time of the offering of the oblation" in the evening (1 Kings 18:36), and Daniel in exile still prayed at that hour (Dan 9:21).
- **"One sixth of an ephah, and one third of a hin of oil" (v14).** These amounts are also more than in Numbers 28:5 (a tenth of an ephah and a quarter of a hin). Throughout the chapter, worship is more lavish than before. **"A perpetual statute"** marks the morning lamb as a fixed part of the new order.
- **"If the prince makes a gift to any of his sons as his inheritance, it shall belong to his sons" (v16).** The prince's land is his family's to keep, like any Israelite family's inheritance.
- **"If he makes a gift… to one of his servants, it shall be his to the year of liberty. Then it shall revert to the prince" (v17).** The "year of liberty" (*deror*) is the year of jubilee, every fiftieth year, when land returned to the family that originally received it (Lev 25:10–13: "proclaim liberty throughout the land"). Kings rewarded loyal officials with land (1 Sam 8:14; 22:7). Under this rule a prince could not build up a class of royal favorites with permanent estates, and the crown lands would stay intact so he would never be tempted to take anyone else's.
- **"The prince shall not take any of the inheritance of the people, thrusting them out of their property" (v18).** This is the point of vv16–18. "Thrusting them out" is a strong word, used of oppression and violence (compare 45:8–9 and 22:29). **"So that none of my people shall be scattered from his property."** God calls them "my people" and the land his to give. The exile had scattered Israel among the nations (36:19). This law guards against a ruler scattering them again from their own fields.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the people worship at the door of the east gate "where the prince does," so prince and people stand before God together. On the morning lamb he writes, "Every morning, as duly as the morning came, they must offer a lamb," and on v18 that the prince must not take the people's land "under pretence of having many children to provide for." On v9 he notes that "God is the God of order, and not of confusion," and urges worshipers to keep "pressing forward" and leave "more holy, and heavenly, and spiritual." JFB says the gate opens on the Sabbath "to mark its peculiar sanctity," and that at the feasts the prince is "not isolated as at other times" but leads the throng, as David did. He explains vv16–18 as removing any "temptation to spoil his people of their inheritance." Guzik, citing Wiersbe, says the prince does not set himself apart, and passes on F.B. Meyer's picture of Christ going before his people. He follows Feinberg that the offerings here are enlarged beyond the law, and Morgan that they would look back to Christ rather than forward.

#### Boiling Places for Offerings (vv19–24)
- **"He brought me through the entrance… to the north row of the holy chambers for the priests" (v19).** The guide, silent since chapter 43, takes over the tour again. These are the priests' rooms of 42:1–14. At their far western end is a separate place.
- **"Where the priests shall boil the guilt offering and the sin offering, and… bake the grain offering" (v20).** The priests ate their share of these "most holy" offerings (Lev 6:25–29; 7:6), and they needed a kitchen inside the holy area. **"In order not to bring them out into the outer court and so transmit holiness to the people"** repeats the reason given for the priests' garments in 44:19. Holy food, like holy clothing, stayed in its holy place.
- **Eli's sons are the background.** The worst abuse of priestly portions in Israel's history was at Shiloh, where Eli's sons grabbed meat from the pot with a fork before the fat was offered, and even demanded raw meat (1 Sam 2:12–17). The text does not mention them, but well-ordered kitchens were a guard against exactly that kind of greed.
- **"In each corner of the court there was another court" (v21).** Four enclosures sat in the four corners of the outer court, each **"forty cubits long and thirty broad"** (v22), about 68 by 51 feet. The word translated "small" is uncertain. It may mean "enclosed" or "roofed," or possibly "smoke-filled," which would suit kitchens.
- **"A row of masonry, with hearths made at the bottom" (v23).** Ledges of stone ran around the inside walls, with fireplaces underneath for cooking pots.
- **"These are the kitchens where those who minister at the temple shall boil the sacrifices of the people" (v24).** "Those who minister" are the Levites (44:11). The "sacrifices of the people" are their peace offerings, the fellowship meals in which part of the animal was burned for God, part given to the priest, and the rest eaten by the worshiper with family and friends (Lev 7:11–21; Deut 12:7, 18). Hannah's family ate such meals at Shiloh (1 Sam 1:4–5). The vision of God's holy house ends in a place full of smoke, steam and families sharing a meal.
- **The tour comes full circle.** The Tyndale notes point out that the temple tour began in the outer court (40:17) and, after moving in to the center and out again, ends here. The last thing seen in the house of the holy God is a kitchen. Holiness in Ezekiel's temple is not cold. It makes room for God's people to eat and rejoice before him.
- **Commentators:** Henry simply describes the places where "they were to boil the trespass-offering and the sin-offering," and remarks that using the corners for kitchens made good use of space, for "It is a pity that holy ground should be waste ground." JFB draws out the principle: "no longer are the common and unclean to be confounded with the sacred and divine," and distinguishes the priests' kitchens for the most holy offerings from the corner kitchens for peace offerings shared by priests and people. Guzik follows Block that the corner kitchens were for the people's sacrificial meals, and quotes writers who see in them the fellowship of God and his people. He passes on Taylor's point that Ezekiel's temple holds worship and ordinary social life together.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel's prince worships at the threshold and walks in the crowd of pilgrims, but he cannot go further in. Jesus is the King who went all the way: he "entered once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12), and he walks among his people, "in the midst of the lampstands" (Rev 1:13). The morning lamb, offered "morning by morning," points to "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29), whose mercies to his people "are new every morning" (Lam 3:23). The jubilee "year of liberty" is the very thing Jesus announced in Nazareth (Luke 4:18–19). And the kitchens where worshipers ate in God's house anticipate the table he sets for his people, and the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:9).
