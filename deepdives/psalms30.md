*Setting:* "A Psalm of David. A song at the dedication of the temple." The Hebrew is literally "the dedication of the house," and which house is a real question. Henry and JFB take it as David's own palace of cedar, built with help from Hiram king of Tyre (2 Sam 5:11), and Israelites did dedicate a new house to God (Deut 20:5). Others connect it with the site of the future temple. After David's proud census, a plague struck Israel, and David and the elders put on sackcloth. God stopped the angel at the threshing floor of Araunah, David built an altar there, and he declared, "Here shall be the house of the LORD God" (1 Chr 21:1–22:1). That story fits the psalm closely: pride in prosperity, God's anger, sackcloth, a quick end to the anger. JFB objects that "house" alone never means the temple. The Tyndale notes suggest David wrote the psalm in advance for the temple's dedication, making his own rescue a picture of the nation's. Whatever the first occasion, later Jewish worship used it at Hanukkah, the feast that remembers the rededication of the temple in 164 BC. The psalm itself never mentions a building. It is a thanksgiving for being brought back from the edge of death, and it answers the fears of the last few psalms: "lest… I become like those who go down to the pit" (28:1).

#### I Will Extol You, O LORD (vv1–3)
- **"I will extol you, O LORD, for you have drawn me up" (v1).** "Extol" means to lift up high. God lifted David up, so David lifts up God's name. "Drawn me up" (*dalah*) is the verb for drawing water from a well, used when Moses drew water for Jethro's daughters (Exod 2:16, 19). David pictures himself as a bucket hauled out of a deep well. JFB: "as one is drawn from a well (Ps 40:2)."
- **"And have not let my foes rejoice over me" (v1).** David's enemies were waiting for him to fall. Part of the rescue was that they never got their celebration (25:2; 41:11).
- **"I cried to you for help, and you have healed me" (v2).** "Healed" (*rapha'*) may mean a literal recovery from illness, and many readers think David had been seriously sick. Hezekiah's thanksgiving after his sickness is very similar (Isa 38:10–20). But the word is also used for national and spiritual restoration (2 Chr 7:14; Ps 41:4). JFB: "Affliction is often described as disease… and so relief by healing." "The LORD your healer" (Exod 15:26) heals all kinds of ruin.
- **"You have brought up my soul from Sheol" (v3).** Sheol is the Hebrew name for the place of the dead, the grave seen from the inside. "The pit" is its partner word (28:1). David was not literally raised from the dead. He means he was so near death that he was as good as gone, and God pulled him back. "Soul" (*nephesh*) means his whole self, his life.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes David says "thou hast lifted me up," not only out of the pit "but up to the throne of Israel." He finds three things that made the rescue sweeter: his enemies were disappointed, it answered prayer, and it was "very great": "from the grave." Guzik, passing on VanGemeren, pictures a bucket raised from a well. JFB says the terms "describe extreme danger."

#### Joy Comes with the Morning (vv4–5)
- **"Sing praises to the LORD, O you his saints" (v4).** David cannot keep the thanksgiving to himself. "Saints" (*chasidim*) means those who have received God's steadfast love (*chesed*) and live loyally in it. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: he could not praise God enough himself, so he recruited others.
- **"Give thanks to his holy name" (v4).** Literally "to the memorial of his holiness." God's name is how he is remembered (Exod 3:15). JFB: holiness here is "the sum of God's perfections."
- **"For his anger is but for a moment, and his favor is for a lifetime" (v5).** This is one of the best-loved lines in the Psalter. It does not say God is never angry with his people. It says his anger with them is brief and his favor lasts. Isaiah has the same contrast: "For a brief moment I deserted you, but with great compassion I will gather you" (Isa 54:7–8). The Hebrew is very compact, literally "a moment in his anger, a life in his favor," and "a lifetime" may also be read "life." Either way, God's favor is the long story and his anger the short one. JFB: "These precious words have consoled millions."
- **"Weeping may tarry for the night" (v5).** "Tarry" (*lun*) means to lodge overnight, as a traveler stays at an inn. Weeping is pictured as a guest who comes in the evening, stays the night, and is gone by daybreak.
- **"But joy comes with the morning" (v5).** "Joy" (*rinnah*) is a ringing shout of joy. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, notes the contrast: the voice that was breaking into sobs at night is shouting in the morning. Morning in the Bible is often the time of God's help (46:5; 90:14; Lam 3:22–23). And the greatest morning of all came early on the first day of the week (Mark 16:2).
- **Commentators:** Henry: the saints should "give thanks at the remembrance of his holiness," because holiness is what the angels "fasten most upon" in their praise (Isa 6:3). Guzik passes on Boice's warning that v5 is not a slogan like "every cloud has a silver lining." It is a claim about God: for his people, his favor outweighs his displeasure. He also passes on Horne's comment that the verse is a picture of Christ's night of death and morning of resurrection.

#### I Said in My Prosperity (vv6–7)
- **"As for me, I said in my prosperity, 'I shall never be moved'" (v6).** Now David tells how he got into trouble. "Prosperity" (*shalvah*) is ease, security, the sense that all is well. In that comfort he said the very words the wicked say in 10:6: "I shall not be moved." The same words can be faith or pride. In 16:8 and 21:7, "I shall not be moved" rests on God. Here it rested on David's own success. The Tyndale notes call it presumption. If the census story lies behind the psalm, this is exactly the attitude that made David count his soldiers (2 Sam 24:1–10).
- **"By your favor, O LORD, you made my mountain stand strong" (v7).** David admits that even his strong position was God's gift. A "mountain" is a picture of stability and of a stronghold. David's own fortress, the stronghold of Zion, stood on a hill (2 Sam 5:7–9). Henry has a sharp line: David did not look on his prosperity "as his heaven… only his mountain; it is earth still, only raised a little higher than the common level."
- **"You hid your face; I was dismayed" (v7).** One line is enough to bring down the mountain. All God had to do was turn his face away. "Dismayed" (*bahal*) is a strong word: terrified, thrown into panic. It is used of Saul when he heard from the medium at Endor that he would die (1 Sam 28:21). The security David felt was really God's favor, and when that seemed to leave, nothing was left.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If God hide his face, a good man is certainly troubled… when the sun sets night certainly follows, and the moon and all the stars cannot make day." JFB suggests the prosperity was perhaps David's accession to the throne, and notes that "in his self-complacent elation he was checked by God's hiding His face." Guzik passes on Morgan: "Self-satisfaction cannot praise Jehovah," so it had to be corrected by discipline.

#### What Profit Is There in My Death? (vv8–10)
- **"To you, O LORD, I cry, and to the Lord I plead for mercy" (v8).** David now quotes the prayer he prayed in the crisis. He did not run from God when God hid his face. He ran toward him. Henry: "let us learn to pray in the dark."
- **"What profit is there in my death, if I go down to the pit?" (v9).** Literally "in my blood." This is a bold kind of prayer: David argues with God, giving God reasons to save him. Moses prayed like this too (Exod 32:11–13). David's argument is that his death would cost God a worshipper.
- **"Will the dust praise you? Will it tell of your faithfulness?" (v9).** The same argument appears in 6:5, 88:10–12 and Hezekiah's prayer: "Sheol does not thank you… those who go down to the pit do not hope for your faithfulness" (Isa 38:18). This does not mean believers in the Old Testament had no hope beyond death (16:10–11; 49:15). It means that the grave is not the place where God's faithfulness is told to the living, in the congregation, on earth. A dead man cannot stand in the assembly and testify. David wants to live so he can keep praising.
- **"Hear, O LORD, and be merciful to me! O LORD, be my helper!" (v10).** Short, desperate, and enough. Henry: he prays "for mercy to pardon… and for grace to help in time of need," the same two errands Hebrews 4:16 invites believers to bring to the throne of grace.
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures David asking whether God will "sell one of thy own people for nought," and adds that "the sanctified spirit, which returns to God, shall praise him," but "the dust… shall not." JFB groups this with Psalms 6:5 and 88:10 and Isaiah 38:18: the appeal rests on the loss of his "agency in praising God here." Guzik admits the words sound strange next to Paul's "to die is gain" (Phil 1:21), and explains that the Old Testament had only partial light on the afterlife until Christ "brought life and immortality to light" (2 Tim 1:10). He passes on Spurgeon's point that this is an argument pleaded with God, not a statement of doctrine.

#### You Have Turned My Mourning into Dancing (vv11–12)
- **"You have turned for me my mourning into dancing" (v11).** "Mourning" (*mispad*) is loud wailing, the kind done at funerals (Amos 5:16). "Dancing" (*machol*) is the dancing of celebration, done with tambourines at festivals and victories (149:3; 150:4; Jer 31:4, 13). JFB says the word means "a lute," but the lexicon (BDB) gives "dance" and nothing else. The reversal is complete: from the funeral to the festival.
- **"You have loosed my sackcloth and clothed me with gladness" (v11).** Sackcloth was a rough garment of goat or camel hair worn in grief and repentance. Kings wore it too (1 Chr 21:16; Isa 37:1). God unties the sackcloth like a belt and wraps David in joy instead. JFB: "gladness," used for a garment, "shows the language to be figurative." Isaiah uses the same picture: "a garment of praise instead of a faint spirit" (Isa 61:3).
- **"That my glory may sing your praise and not be silent" (v12).** "My glory" (*kavod*) here means the inner self, the soul, as in 16:9 and 57:8. The rescue has a purpose. God did not lift David out of the pit only for David's comfort, but so that he would sing. The "silence" of the grave (v9) is answered by a voice that will not be silent.
- **"O LORD my God, I will give thanks to you forever!" (v12).** "Forever" answers v9. The dust cannot praise. David will, and not just once.
- **Commentators:** Henry: his joy "was made his ornament, was made his strength, and seemed to cleave to him, as the girdle cleaves to the loins." He adds a balanced lesson: because trouble may come suddenly, "rejoice as though we rejoiced not," and because relief may come suddenly, "weep as though we wept not." JFB: "The ultimate end of God's mercies to us is our praise to Him." Guzik stresses the purpose: God's rescue was not mainly to give David a palace but so that he would praise and not be silent. JFB takes "glory" as the tongue, "the organ of praise."

#### Pointing to Christ
David was drawn up from the pit, and the dust did not get him. His greater Son went all the way down. Jesus did not just come near Sheol. He died and was buried, and Peter says it was "not possible for him to be held by it" (Acts 2:24). His friends wept through the night, and joy came in the morning, when the women found the tomb empty "very early on the first day of the week, when the sun had risen" (Mark 16:2). David's prosperity made him proud, and God corrected him. Jesus, who had every right to say "I shall never be moved," humbled himself to death on a cross (Phil 2:8), and God exalted him. Because of that, the anger that believers deserve was taken on by him, and what is left for them is favor for a lifetime. Jesus promised his disciples the same reversal: "you will be sorrowful, but your sorrow will turn into joy" (John 16:20). And on the last morning, God will take off his people's sackcloth for good: "he will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more" (Rev 21:4).
