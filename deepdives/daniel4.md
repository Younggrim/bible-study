*Setting:* This chapter is unique in the Bible: most of it is a public letter written by a pagan emperor, telling the world how the God of Israel humbled him. It is set late in Nebuchadnezzar's long reign (605–562 BC), after his great building projects were finished, and probably after Jerusalem's destruction in 586 BC. Daniel, now an older man, is chief of Babylon's wise men (2:48). Nebuchadnezzar has twice been shown the power of Daniel's God. After the dream of the statue he called him "God of gods" (2:47), and after the fiery furnace he forbade anyone to speak against him (3:29). But both times he simply added Israel's God to his own. In chapter 4 he learns the lesson personally and painfully: "the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will" (v17). In the Aramaic text, vv1–3 are the last verses of chapter 3, so Aramaic and Hebrew Bibles number the rest of the chapter one verse lower than English Bibles.

#### Nebuchadnezzar Praises God (vv1–3)
- **"King Nebuchadnezzar to all peoples, nations, and languages" (v1).** The chapter opens like a royal letter or proclamation. Nebuchadnezzar uses the same formula he used to command worship of his golden image (3:4). Now the whole empire is told to hear about a different king. **"Peace be multiplied to you!"** was a standard greeting, and the same blessing opens Peter's letters (1 Pet 1:2; 2 Pet 1:2).
- **"The signs and wonders that the Most High God has done for me" (v2).** "Signs and wonders" is the language of the exodus (Deut 4:34; 6:22). The Pharaoh of Moses' day saw signs and wonders and hardened his heart. This king saw them and was humbled.
- **"His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom" (v3).** The emperor of the world's greatest kingdom confesses another kingdom that outlasts his. He echoes what Daniel told him years earlier: "a kingdom that shall never be destroyed" (2:44). The same words return at the end of his letter (v34), framing the whole chapter.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the lesson that "We ought to show to others God's dealings with us," and notes that the king's letter is a public confession of God's sovereignty. JFB calls the greeting "the usual salutation in the East." Guzik reads the chapter as a Gentile king's testimony and sees it as a model of telling others what God has done.

#### Nebuchadnezzar's Second Dream (vv4–18)
- **"I, Nebuchadnezzar, was at ease in my house and prospering in my palace" (v4).** "Prospering" is literally "flourishing," like a green tree, which anticipates the dream. The king was at the height of his success, with no wars and no threats. The danger came from within, from pride.
- **"I saw a dream that made me afraid" (v5).** As in chapter 2, a dream disturbs the most powerful man on earth.
- **"I told them the dream, but they could not make known to me its interpretation" (v7).** This time he tells the wise men the dream, and they still cannot explain it. The Tyndale notes point out that their manuals were no help. Guzik suggests they may have understood it well enough but lacked the courage to tell the king. The text does not say.
- **"At last Daniel came in before me" (v8).** Why "at last"? Daniel was head of the wise men (2:48), yet he is called only after the others fail. Perhaps the king feared what Daniel might say. **"Named Belteshazzar after the name of my god"** confirms that Daniel's Babylonian name honored the king's god (1:7). JFB links it to Bel.
- **"In whom is the spirit of the holy gods" (v8).** The king still speaks as a polytheist. Pharaoh said something similar of Joseph (Gen 41:38). The king recognizes something divine in Daniel without yet knowing the one God.
- **"O Belteshazzar, chief of the magicians" (v9).** The king gives Daniel his official title. Daniel held the post without practicing the arts of Babylon's magicians, as Joseph served in Pharaoh's court without serving Egypt's gods.
- **"A tree in the midst of the earth, and its height was great" (v10).** Ancient peoples sometimes pictured a great world-tree at the center of the earth. In the Bible, great trees stand for great empires and kings. Ezekiel pictured Assyria as a cedar of Lebanon, with birds in its branches and beasts under it, cut down for its pride (Ezek 31:3–14). **"Its top reached to heaven"** (v11) recalls Babel's tower, "with its top in the heavens" (Gen 11:4). Babylon is the city of Babel.
- **"In it was food for all… the birds of the heavens lived in its branches" (v12).** The tree is not evil. It gives shade, food and shelter. Nebuchadnezzar's empire really did bring order and provision to many nations. His sin was not ruling but taking the credit. Jesus later uses this picture for the kingdom of God, which starts as a mustard seed and grows until "the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches" (Matt 13:31–32).
- **"A watcher, a holy one, came down from heaven" (v13).** "Watcher" (*'ir*) means "one who is awake," an angel who never sleeps and keeps watch over the world. The word appears in the Bible only in this chapter (vv13, 17, 23), though it became common in later Jewish writings. JFB notes that only one angel is meant here, and a holy one, not a fallen one.
- **"Chop down the tree… But leave the stump of its roots in the earth, bound with a band of iron and bronze" (vv14–15).** The stump is the note of hope. A stump can sprout again (Job 14:7–9; Isa 11:1). The metal band may picture protection, or restraint, or both. Some think it was meant to keep the stump from splitting.
- **"Let him be wet with the dew of heaven" (v15).** Partway through the sentence, the tree becomes a man: "him." The picture and its meaning begin to merge.
- **"Let his mind be changed from a man's, and let a beast's mind be given to him" (v16).** Literally "let his heart be changed." In Aramaic, as in Hebrew, the "heart" is the center of thought and will. The man who thought like a god will think like an animal.
- **"Let seven periods of time pass over him" (v16).** "Periods of time" (*'iddanin*) is a vague word. Most take it as seven years, as in 7:25, where "time, times, and half a time" seems to mean three and a half years. Seven is also the number of completeness: the discipline would last exactly as long as needed. JFB calls it "a complete revolution of time accompanying a complete revolution in his state of mind."
- **"The sentence is by the decree of the watchers… to the end that the living may know that the Most High rules the kingdom of men" (v17).** The heavenly council announces God's decision (compare 1 Kings 22:19–22). The purpose is stated three times in the chapter (vv17, 25, 32): that all may know God rules. **"And sets over it the lowliest of men."** God can raise anyone. Nebuchadnezzar's own father was, by his own inscriptions, "the son of a nobody," and God had raised David from the sheepfold (Ps 78:70–71).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the tree is a picture "fitly representing him who reigned in Babylon," and calls the watchers' decree a judgment on which "God has determined it, as a righteous Judge; he has signed this edict." JFB sees the tree's place at the center as "the center whence the imperial authority radiated in all directions," and on v17 says that "It is not one's talents, excellency, or noble birth, but God's will, which elevates to the throne." Guzik, citing Clarke, notes how often trees picture great rulers in Scripture, and observes that the king had been impressed by Daniel's God without being converted.

#### Daniel Interprets the Second Dream (vv19–27)
- **"Daniel… was dismayed for a while, and his thoughts alarmed him" (v19).** Daniel understands immediately, and it horrifies him. He has served this king for decades. The king himself has to reassure him: **"Let not the dream or the interpretation alarm you."**
- **"May the dream be for those who hate you" (v19).** This is not flattery. Daniel genuinely wishes the king well. Jeremiah had told the exiles to "seek the welfare of the city" and pray for it (Jer 29:7). Daniel shows what that looks like toward a ruler who destroyed his homeland.
- **"It is you, O king" (v22).** Guzik compares this to Nathan's words to David: "You are the man!" (2 Sam 12:7). Daniel speaks clearly and personally, with respect, but without softening the message.
- **"It is a decree of the Most High, which has come upon my lord the king" (v24).** Daniel corrects the king's language. The king spoke of "the decree of the watchers" (v17), and Daniel names who stands behind it. Angels carry out God's word. They do not make the decision.
- **"You shall be driven from among men… made to eat grass like an ox" (v25).** The judgment is spelled out plainly. **"Till you know that the Most High rules the kingdom of men"** gives its goal. The punishment has a purpose and an end.
- **"Your kingdom shall be confirmed for you from the time that you know that Heaven rules" (v26).** This is the only place in the Old Testament where "Heaven" stands in for God's name. Later Jewish speech did this often, out of reverence, which is why Matthew's Gospel usually says "kingdom of heaven" where Mark and Luke say "kingdom of God."
- **"Break off your sins by practicing righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed" (v27).** Daniel does not only announce judgment. He calls for repentance. "Break off" (*peraq*) can mean to tear away, or to redeem or ransom. Older Latin and Catholic translations read it as "redeem your sins with alms," and the verse was debated at the Reformation over whether good works can atone for sin. The context supports "break off": Daniel calls the king to turn from his sins and show it in justice and mercy. The Tyndale notes point out that Babylon's own kings claimed to protect the weak. Hammurabi's famous law code says the gods called him to keep the strong from oppressing the weak. Daniel holds Nebuchadnezzar to the standard his own throne claimed.
- **"That there may perhaps be a lengthening of your prosperity" (v27).** "Perhaps" is honest. Daniel cannot promise that God will relent, but he knows God's character. Jonah's warning to Nineveh brought a reprieve (Jonah 3:9–10), and God told Jeremiah that if a nation turns from its evil he may relent (Jer 18:7–8).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Daniel urged the king "to break off a course of sin that he was in, to reform his life." JFB on v19 describes Daniel as "overwhelmed with awe at the terrible import of the dream," and on v27 says repentance must be shown "by works of justice and charity." Guzik compares Daniel's counsel to Jonah's preaching in Nineveh, where a warning of judgment was followed by repentance and reprieve.

#### Nebuchadnezzar's Humiliation (vv28–33)
- **"All this came upon King Nebuchadnezzar" (v28).** The narrator gives the outcome before the story. The letter switches here from first person ("I") to third person ("he"), and switches back in v34. Many think this reflects the fact that the king could not tell this part himself, since he was not in his right mind.
- **"At the end of twelve months he was walking on the roof of the royal palace of Babylon" (v29).** A whole year passed. God's patience gave time to respond to Daniel's counsel (compare 2 Pet 3:9). Flat palace roofs were places to walk and look out (2 Sam 11:2).
- **"Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power… for the glory of my majesty?" (v30).** From the roof he could see one of the wonders of the ancient world: walls so thick that chariots could drive on them, the Ishtar Gate glazed in blue with dragons and bulls, Marduk's temple and its ziggurat, and the palaces. Tradition says he built the Hanging Gardens for his wife. His own building inscriptions sound remarkably like this verse. Thousands of bricks have been dug up stamped with his name, and long inscriptions record his boasts of rebuilding the city. The words "I," "my mighty power," "my majesty" say it all.
- **"While the words were still in the king's mouth, there fell a voice from heaven" (v31).** Judgment comes instantly. **"The kingdom has departed from you."** The king who credited himself with everything loses everything.
- **"Immediately the word was fulfilled… he… ate grass like an ox" (v33).** Many link his condition to a rare mental illness called boanthropy, in which a person believes he is an ox and behaves like one. Guzik mentions a twentieth-century case of this disorder. **"Till his hair grew as long as eagles' feathers, and his nails were like birds' claws."** Left outside, unwashed and uncut, he looked like a beast.
- **Is there outside evidence?** No Babylonian record mentions the king's madness, and royal inscriptions rarely recorded a king's humiliations. A Greek writer, Abydenus, preserved later by Eusebius, tells a strange story of Nebuchadnezzar being "possessed by some god" on his palace roof before disappearing, which Guzik notes. A Dead Sea Scrolls text called the Prayer of Nabonidus tells of Babylon's last king, Nabonidus, struck with a disease for seven years in Arabia until a Jewish exile told him of the true God. Some scholars think the story in Daniel grew out of traditions about Nabonidus. Others think the scroll shows how a well-known story about Nebuchadnezzar was retold. The Tyndale notes mention a gap in Babylonian records in the 570s BC that might fit this period, though that is uncertain.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls pride and self-conceit "sins that most easily beset great men," and describes the fallen king as "a perfect brute in the shape of a man." JFB says of v30 that "thousands of bricks on the plain, the inscriptions of which have been deciphered" bear the king's name, and calls the illness "hypochondriacal madness," the king "fancying himself a wild beast." Guzik, drawing on the British Museum's inscriptions, argues that a writer living centuries later would hardly have known how closely the boast matches Nebuchadnezzar's own records.

#### Nebuchadnezzar Restored (vv34–37)
- **"I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted my eyes to heaven, and my reason returned to me" (v34).** The first person returns, and so does his sanity. The order is striking: he looks up first, and then his reason comes back. For years he had looked down like an animal grazing. The moment he looks up to God, he becomes fully human again.
- **"I blessed the Most High, and praised and honored him who lives forever" (v34).** The court greeting was "O king, live forever!" (2:4; 3:9). The king now knows who really lives forever.
- **"His dominion is an everlasting dominion" (v34).** The same words as v3, which close the frame of the letter.
- **"All the inhabitants of the earth are accounted as nothing" (v35).** This echoes Isaiah 40:15–17: "the nations are like a drop from a bucket." **"None can stay his hand or say to him, 'What have you done?'"** Literally "strike his hand," a picture of slapping a child's hand to stop it. No one restrains God. Job and Isaiah say the same (Job 9:12; Isa 45:9), and Paul uses this idea in Romans 9:20.
- **"My majesty and splendor returned to me… and still more greatness was added to me" (v36).** God restored the kingdom as promised (v26). The stump sprouted. Like Job, he ends with more than before (Job 42:10, 12).
- **"Now I, Nebuchadnezzar, praise and extol and honor the King of heaven" (v37).** Three verbs of praise. He calls God "King of heaven," a title found only here in the Old Testament. The king of Babylon bows to the King above all kings.
- **"For all his works are right and his ways are just; and those who walk in pride he is able to humble" (v37).** The letter ends with the lesson of his whole life. He does not complain that God was harsh. He calls God's ways just.
- **Was Nebuchadnezzar truly converted?** Faithful readers have disagreed. Some see real saving faith here: he humbled himself, praised God alone, and called God's ways just. Others note that he never renounces his other gods, and that Babylonian kings commonly praised different gods at different times. Henry says simply, "Whether he continued in the same good mind that here he seems to have been in we are not told." JFB is more hopeful, noting that "It is a mark of true contrition to condemn one's self, and justify God." Guzik calls him a true witness to the true God without claiming to know his heart. The text gives the testimony and leaves the rest to God.
- **Commentators:** JFB writes that "Praise is a sure sign of a soul spiritually healed," and links v35 to Isaiah 40. Henry follows the outline of the king's own letter, from the humbling to the restoration, and ends with his praise of God as Lord of all. Guzik draws from v34 that a right view of God leads to a right view of oneself, and that this produces worship. He quotes Spurgeon on adoring God's sovereignty and applies the chapter to pride with James 4:6: "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble."

#### Pointing to Christ
The proud king tried to lift himself to heaven, and God brought him down to the dust until he learned to look up. Paul describes Christ moving the opposite way. Though he was "in the form of God," he "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself… and became obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him" (Phil 2:6–9). Nebuchadnezzar's empire was a great tree that fell. Jesus is the shoot that comes "from the stump of Jesse" (Isa 11:1), whose kingdom is the mustard seed that grows into the tree where the birds of the air find shelter (Matt 13:31–32). And the king's last line is the gospel's warning and promise at once: those who walk in pride God is able to humble, and those who humble themselves he will exalt (Luke 14:11).
