*Setting:* The same night as the end of chapter 5. Esther has had her first banquet with the king and Haman and has asked them back tomorrow, without yet saying what she wants (5:8). Haman has gone home furious that Mordecai still will not stand for him, and on his wife's advice has had a stake set up seventy-five feet high to kill Mordecai on in the morning (5:14). Everything now hangs on what happens before breakfast. Chapter 6 is the turning point of the whole book, and it is full of reversal and comedy. It turns on a king's sleepless night, and God, still unnamed, seems to be behind every detail.

#### The King Honors Mordecai (vv1–13)
- **"On that night the king could not sleep" (v1).** The Hebrew is "the king's sleep fled." The narrator does not say why. It could have been the wine, or a restless mind wondering what Esther wanted, or why Haman was always with her. The Greek version adds "the Lord took sleep from the king." The Hebrew leaves God out, but the point is the same. Compare Daniel 6:18, where another Persian king lies awake while Daniel is in the lions' den, and the sleepless Pharaoh whose dreams brought Joseph out of prison (Gen 41).
- **"The book of memorable deeds, the chronicles" (v1).** Literally "the book of remembrances." Persian kings kept official records of their reign, including those who had done the king a service. Herodotus says these "benefactors of the king" were written down by name, and Ezra 4:15 mentions the same kind of royal archive. Malachi uses nearly the same phrase for God's "book of remembrance" of those who fear him (Mal 3:16).
- **"It was found written how Mordecai had told" (v2).** Of all the pages in the record, this is the one read aloud. The narrator names the eunuchs again, slightly differently spelled (Bigthana, compare Bigthan in 2:21). Five years have passed since then (2:16; 3:7).
- **"What honor or distinction has been bestowed on Mordecai?" (v3).** Persian kings prided themselves on rewarding loyalty, so leaving a man unrewarded was an embarrassment. **"Nothing has been done for him."** The delay that seemed so unjust in chapter 2 now proves to have been perfectly timed. If Mordecai had been rewarded five years earlier, there would be nothing to remember tonight.
- **"Who is in the court?" (v4).** At dawn, the hour when kings did business, the first man waiting in the outer court is Haman, who has come to ask permission to impale Mordecai. The timing is almost absurd, and the narrator enjoys it. Haman came to ask for Mordecai's death and will leave having given him a parade.
- **"What should be done to the man whom the king delights to honor?" (v6).** The king does not say whom he means. Haman does not ask. **"Whom would the king delight to honor more than me?"** We hear his private thought, as earlier we heard him boast to his family. The irony rests on what each man knows: the king knows the name, the reader knows the name, and only Haman does not.
- **Royal robes, the king's horse and a crown (vv7–9).** Haman does not ask for money. He already has money. He asks to look like the king: to wear the king's own robe, ride the king's own horse, and be led through the city square by one of the highest nobles while someone shouts his praise. Plutarch tells of a Persian courtier who asked for the king's robe; the king gave it but forbade him to wear it, because wearing the king's clothes came close to claiming the throne. **"On whose head a royal crown is set"** most naturally refers to the horse. Persian reliefs show royal horses with a crested topknot, and the ESV word order supports this. Some take it as a crown for the man.
- **"Hurry… do so to Mordecai the Jew, who sits at the king's gate" (v10).** The king names him: Mordecai the Jew. He knows his name and race, yet seems not to connect him with the people he has sentenced to die. **"Leave out nothing that you have mentioned."** Every honor Haman designed for himself, he must now give his enemy, and he must do it in person.
- **"Thus shall it be done to the man whom the king delights to honor" (v11).** Haman must lead the horse and shout the words himself, through the same square where the decree against the Jews was read. The narrator records it without comment. There is no word of what Mordecai said or felt. Compare Joseph, robed by Pharaoh and paraded in his chariot while servants shouted before him (Gen 41:42–43).
- **"Mordecai returned to the king's gate" (v12).** He goes straight back to his post. The honor changes nothing for him, and the decree of death still stands. **"But Haman hurried to his house, mourning and with his head covered."** Covering the head was a sign of grief and shame (2 Sam 15:30; Jer 14:3–4). Now it is Haman who mourns, the reversal of Mordecai in sackcloth (4:1).
- **"If Mordecai… is of the Jewish people, you will not overcome him but will surely fall before him" (v13).** The same wife and friends who told him to build the stake yesterday now predict his ruin. Notice they are now called "his wise men." The verb "fall" (*naphal*) appears three times in this one verse. They seem to know something of Israel's history, perhaps the old prophecy that Amalek would perish (Num 24:20; Exod 17:14), or simply the reputation of the Jews' God. Even pagans can read the signs. The verb "begun to fall" is the key: it is already happening.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry, Guzik and JFB all see this chapter as the clearest picture of providence in the book. JFB says that in the king's sleeplessness, his choice of book and the passage read, "the immediate interposition of Providence is distinctly visible." Henry: "He that commanded 127 provinces could not command one hour's sleep," a line Guzik credits in slightly different words to Spurgeon. Henry also says: "Satan put it into the heart of Haman to contrive Mordecai's death… God put it into the heart of the king to contrive Mordecai's honour." Guzik walks through each "coincidence" in turn, the sleepless night, the book, the page, Haman's arrival, and argues none of it was luck, pointing to Romans 8:28 and to Proverbs 26:27 on the proud falling into their own pit. JFB explains that a robe once worn by a king was the most honorable of gifts. Henry passes on a Jewish tradition that the pages of the book kept flying back to the place about Mordecai. That is legend, not text.

#### Esther Reveals Haman's Plot (v14)
- **"While they were yet talking with him, the king's eunuchs arrived" (v14).** The ESV places its heading for chapter 7's banquet here, because this verse begins the scene that follows. Haman has no time to think, plan or recover. JFB notes that it was the custom to send servants to bring the invited guests when the meal was ready.
- **"Hurried to bring Haman to the feast" (v14).** The word "hurry" runs through the chapter: the king tells Haman to hurry (v10), Haman hurries home in shame (v12), and now he is hurried off to the banquet. Yesterday he went to Esther's table as the most honored man in the empire. Today he goes as a man whose own household has just told him he will fall.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the timing as God's wisdom: Haman hoped the banquet would revive "his drooping spirits," but because "his spirits being broken by this sore disappointment, he might the more easily be run down by Esther's complaint." On v13 he calls Zeresh and the wise men "miserable comforters," who "did not advise him to repent" but only foretold his fall, and he contrasts the two men: Mordecai went straight back to work, while "that will break a proud man's heart which would not break a humble man's sleep."

#### Pointing to Christ
Haman designed honor for himself and had to give it to the man he wanted dead. That reversal runs through the whole Bible: "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52). It reaches its center in Christ, who did not seek honor but "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death," and so God "highly exalted him" (Phil 2:8–9). The king's book of records is a faint picture of the God who forgets nothing done for him (Heb 6:10; Mal 3:16), and the robe given to Mordecai is a picture, too, of the robe of righteousness the King gives to those who did not earn it (Isa 61:10).
