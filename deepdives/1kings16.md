*Setting:* About 886–874 BC in the northern kingdom of Israel. Chapter 15 showed Baasha wiping out the house of Jeroboam and then walking in Jeroboam's sins himself (15:29, 34). Chapter 16 races through the next thirty years in the north: a prophet's sentence on Baasha, a drunken king murdered at a party, a usurper who lasts seven days, a civil war, a powerful new dynasty with a new capital at Samaria, and finally Ahab and Jezebel, who bring the worship of Baal into the heart of Israel. Meanwhile, in Judah, Asa's long reign goes on, and every northern king is dated by it. The chapter is the dark backdrop for Elijah, who bursts onto the scene in the very next verse.

#### Baasha Reigns in Israel (vv1–7)
- **"The word of the LORD came to Jehu the son of Hanani against Baasha" (v1).** Not the later king Jehu (2 Kgs 9), but a prophet. His father Hanani was the prophet Asa jailed for rebuking him (2 Chr 16:7–10). The son is just as brave. Jehu will still be prophesying decades later, rebuking Judah's King Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 19:2), and he wrote a history of that king (2 Chr 20:34).
- **"Since I exalted you out of the dust and made you leader over my people Israel" (v2).** Baasha came from nowhere. "Out of the dust" is the language of Hannah's song: God "raises up the poor from the dust" (1 Sam 2:8). Baasha was "leader" (*nagid*), the title God gave Saul, David and Jeroboam. He could have been the king who turned Israel back. Instead, "you have walked in the way of Jeroboam."
- **"I will make your house like the house of Jeroboam" (v3).** The same sentence, almost word for word, including the dogs and the birds (v4; compare 14:10–11). The man who carried out God's judgment on Jeroboam's house will suffer the same judgment for the same sins.
- **"Baasha slept with his fathers and was buried at Tirzah" (v6).** Baasha himself dies peacefully. Henry notes that he "dies in peace, and is buried with honour." The judgment falls on his house, through his son. God is patient, but he is not mocked.
- **"And also because he destroyed it" (v7).** This line matters. God had foretold the end of Jeroboam's house, and Baasha did it. But he did it out of his own ambition, not obedience, and God holds him guilty for the bloodshed. Hosea makes the same point about the later king Jehu: God would "punish the house of Jehu for the blood of Jezreel" (Hos 1:4), though Jehu too had carried out a prophesied judgment. God can use a person's sin to accomplish his purpose without approving the sin. Guzik makes the point.
- **Commentators:** The Tyndale notes say God charged Baasha with murder as well as idolatry. JFB says Baasha owed his rise to "the appointment or permission" of God, and notes this is the only incident recorded of this prophet in Kings.

#### Elah Reigns in Israel (vv8–14)
- **"Elah the son of Baasha began to reign over Israel in Tirzah, and he reigned two years" (v8).** About 886–885 BC. Again, parts of years count as whole years. JFB says "not fully two years."
- **"His servant Zimri, commander of half his chariots, conspired against him" (v9).** Israel's chariot force was split between two commanders. Zimri held one half. His name may mean "my praise" or "my protection."
- **"Drinking himself drunk in the house of Arza, who was over the household" (v9).** Arza was the palace steward, the chief official of the royal household. While the army was in the field besieging Gibbethon (v15), the king was getting drunk at his steward's house. The Tyndale notes call it a sign of poor character. Arza may have been part of the plot. Henry: "Let Elah be a warning to drunkards, who know not but death may surprise them." Proverbs says, "It is not for kings to drink wine" (Prov 31:4).
- **"He struck down all the house of Baasha. He did not leave him a single male of his relatives or his friends" (v11).** "Single male" is again the crude phrase from 14:10. "Relatives" is literally "redeemers" (*go'alim*), the kinsmen whose duty it was to avenge a murdered relative (Num 35:19). Zimri kills everyone who might take revenge. The Tyndale notes say he acted from self-interest, though he fulfilled Jehu's prophecy.
- **"Provoking the LORD God of Israel to anger with their idols" (v13).** "Idols" here is literally *hebel*, "vapors" or "vanities," the key word of Ecclesiastes. The same word is used of idols in Deuteronomy 32:21 and Jeremiah 10:15. Idols are breath, nothing.

#### Zimri Reigns in Israel (vv15–20)
- **"Zimri reigned seven days in Tirzah" (v15).** The shortest reign in Israel's history. The army was encamped at Gibbethon, the same Philistine-held town where Baasha had killed Nadab twenty-five years earlier (15:27). History is repeating itself.
- **"All Israel made Omri, the commander of the army, king over Israel that day in the camp" (v16).** The army did not accept a palace coup by a chariot commander. They chose their own general. Omri's background is not given, not even his father's name, which is unusual. Some think he was not an Israelite by birth.
- **"He went into the citadel of the king's house and burned the king's house over him with fire and died" (v18).** "Citadel" is the fortified inner tower of the palace. Zimri chose death by fire over capture. JFB compares him to Sardanapalus, the Assyrian king of legend who burned himself with his palace. The Bible records the act without approving it. The Tyndale notes read it as cowardice. Guzik notes that Scripture does not commend suicide, nor does it treat it as unforgivable. Jezebel later throws the name in Jehu's face: "Is it peace, you Zimri, murderer of your master?" (2 Kgs 9:31). By then "Zimri" had become a byword for a traitor.
- **"Because of his sins... walking in the way of Jeroboam" (v19).** In only seven days, he still managed to be judged by the same verdict. Either he had time to commit himself to the calf worship, or the narrator means his whole career showed the same heart.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "When men forsake God, they will be left to plague one another." Guzik points to God's slowness to anger as well as the certainty of his judgment. Baasha's dynasty, like Jeroboam's, ended with the son's second year on the throne.

#### Omri Reigns in Israel (vv21–28)
- **"Half of the people followed Tibni the son of Ginath... and half followed Omri" (v21).** A civil war. Tibni is otherwise unknown. Comparing the dates in vv15 and 23 shows the struggle lasted about four years (the 27th to the 31st year of Asa). JFB makes the calculation. The Greek Septuagint adds that Tibni's brother Joram died with him. "So Tibni died" (v22). The Hebrew doesn't say how. JFB notes it is unclear whether his death "was violent or natural."
- **"Omri began to reign over Israel, and he reigned for twelve years" (v23).** About 885–874 BC, counting from the start of the civil war. The Tyndale notes suggest the twelve years may include a period of shared rule with Ahab.
- **"He bought the hill of Samaria from Shemer for two talents of silver" (v24).** A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this was about 150 pounds of silver. Omri bought the land rather than seizing it, as David bought the threshing floor for the temple (2 Sam 24:24). The hill stood alone, about 300 feet above the surrounding valleys, with steep sides, good farmland and a view to the Mediterranean. It was much easier to defend than Tirzah. Samaria (*Shomron*) took its name from Shemer, and may also play on the word *shamar*, "to watch" or "guard," fitting for a watchtower hill. It remained Israel's capital until Assyria destroyed it in 722 BC (2 Kgs 17:5–6), and "Samaria" became the name for the whole region (13:32).
- **What archaeology adds.** Excavations at Samaria found the royal acropolis with fine stonework from Omri's and Ahab's time, and hundreds of carved ivory plaques, the kind of luxury Amos condemned (Amos 3:15; 6:4; compare "the ivory house that he built," 22:39).
- **"Omri did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, and did more evil than all who were before him" (v25).** Each northern king is worse than the last. Micah later speaks of "the statutes of Omri" being kept (Mic 6:16), which suggests Omri made Jeroboam's worship a matter of law. Guzik, via Knapp, draws this out.
- **"The might that he showed" (v27).** Kings gives Omri only eight verses, but outside the Bible he is one of Israel's best-known kings. The Moabite Stone (the Mesha Stele, about 840 BC) says "Omri was king of Israel, and he oppressed Moab many days." For more than a century, Assyrian records called Israel "the house of Omri" (*Bit-Humri*). Shalmaneser III's Black Obelisk even calls Jehu, who wiped out Omri's family, "son of Omri." The Bible's writer is not interested in Omri's might. He cares about his heart.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Omri made himself infamous by his wickedness." JFB notes the Assyrian name for the capital, "Beth-khumri—the house of Omri." The Tyndale notes suggest Omri arranged Ahab's marriage to Jezebel to cement an alliance with Phoenicia, a common move for kings of the day.

#### Ahab Reigns in Israel (vv29–34)
- **"Ahab the son of Omri reigned over Israel in Samaria twenty-two years" (v29).** About 874–853 BC. His name means "brother of the father," probably meaning God is like a father's brother, a close protector. The irony is heavy. "Ahab the son of Omri" is repeated three times in two verses, as if to stress the family line.
- **"Did evil in the sight of the LORD, more than all who were before him" (v30).** Omri was worse than all before him (v25). Ahab is worse still. He is the low point of the northern kingdom. Later Kings says, "There was none who sold himself to do what was evil in the sight of the LORD like Ahab, whom Jezebel his wife incited" (21:25).
- **"As if it had been a light thing for him to walk in the sins of Jeroboam" (v31).** Jeroboam's sin was worshiping the LORD in a forbidden way, with calves. Ahab goes further: he worships another god altogether. Guzik, citing Dilday, makes this the key difference.
- **"He took for his wife Jezebel the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians" (v31).** "Sidonians" means the Phoenicians, whose great cities were Tyre and Sidon on the coast of modern Lebanon. Ethbaal ("with Baal") is known from the Jewish historian Josephus, who quotes a Phoenician history saying he was a priest of Astarte who seized the throne of Tyre by murder. Jezebel's name probably means "Where is the Prince (Baal)?", a cry from Baal worship. She was not a quiet foreign queen. She was a committed missionary for her god, and she brought hundreds of his prophets with her (18:19).
- **"Went and served Baal and worshiped him" (v31).** Baal ("lord" or "master") was the Canaanite storm god, believed to send rain and make the land fertile. The Tyrian form was Baal-Melqart, the city's patron god. To an Israelite farmer, Baal's promise was rain, crops and children. This is exactly why the next chapter begins with a drought. The LORD will show who really controls the rain.
- **"He erected an altar for Baal in the house of Baal, which he built in Samaria" (v32).** A temple to a foreign god in Israel's capital. Solomon built shrines to his wives' gods on a hill outside Jerusalem (11:7). Ahab builds a temple in the middle of his city. It will stand until Jehu tears it down and makes it a latrine (2 Kgs 10:27).
- **"And Ahab made an Asherah" (v33).** The sacred pole of Asherah, the mother goddess, often paired with Baal. JFB says the altar was for Baal, "that is, the sun," an older guess, since Baal was mainly a storm god.
- **"Hiel of Bethel built Jericho" (v34).** After Jericho fell, Joshua had pronounced a curse: "Cursed before the LORD be the man who rises up and rebuilds this city, Jericho. At the cost of his firstborn shall he lay its foundation, and at the cost of his youngest son shall he set up its gates" (Josh 6:26). Jericho had been lived in at times since then (Judg 3:13; 2 Sam 10:5), but no one had rebuilt it as a fortified city. Hiel did, and lost his oldest son when he laid the foundation and his youngest when he hung the gates. JFB says the curse "was fulfilled more than five hundred years after it was uttered."
- **How did the sons die?** The text does not say. The Aramaic Targum and some scholars think Hiel sacrificed them as "foundation offerings," a practice known in the ancient world. Others think they died by sickness or accident as God's judgment. The Tyndale notes give both views and add a third: firstborn and youngest may be a figure of speech for all his sons. Either way, the point is "according to the word of the LORD, which he spoke by Joshua." In Ahab's day, God's word was mocked, and it came true anyway. That leads straight into Elijah's first words: "As the LORD, the God of Israel, lives" (17:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Ahab did evil above all that reigned before him, and did it with a particular enmity both against Jehovah and Israel." He closes: "Let the reading of this chapter cause us to mark the dreadful end of all the workers of iniquity." Guzik reads v34 as a warning to Ahab that God's word still stands, a warning he ignored. JFB says Jezebel pressed Tyrian worship on Israel with all her energy.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is the darkest stretch yet: five kings in about thirty years, three assassinations, a suicide, a civil war, and at the end a king who brings Baal into Israel's capital. Kings rise "out of the dust" (v2) and fall back into it. Through all of it, God's word does not fail. What Jehu says comes true. What Joshua said five centuries earlier comes true. And when Israel has sunk lowest, God sends not a new king but a prophet, Elijah, to call them back. The story points beyond the long line of failed kings to the one who was truly raised from the dust of death and made "both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:36). He is the King who never conspires, never betrays and never lies. He is the true "Lord" (*Baal* means "lord"), who actually sends the rain and gives life (Matt 5:45; John 10:10).
