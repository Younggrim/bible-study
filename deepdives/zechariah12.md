*Setting:* Chapter 12 begins the second "oracle" of Zechariah 9–14, which runs to the end of the book. The first oracle (chs 9–11) ended with the good shepherd rejected and a worthless shepherd in his place. The second looks further ahead, to "that day," a phrase repeated again and again in chapters 12–14 for the final Day of the LORD. Its frame is the same at both ends: all nations gather against Jerusalem, and God fights for his city (12:1–9; 14:1–15). In between is the most surprising turn in the book. The people of Jerusalem, rescued by God, look on one "whom they have pierced," and the whole land breaks into mourning. These words were spoken to a small, weak community in Judah, probably in the decades after 500 BC, with no army and no king. They promised that God would one day defend Jerusalem against the whole world and then do something deeper: pour out his Spirit and bring his people to grieve over what they had done to him. John's Gospel and Revelation both apply the pierced one to Jesus (John 19:37; Rev 1:7).

#### The LORD Will Give Salvation (vv1–9)
- **"The oracle of the word of the LORD concerning Israel" (v1).** The same heading as 9:1 and Malachi 1:1. "Israel" here means the whole people of God, Judah and Jerusalem included.
- **"Who stretched out the heavens and founded the earth and formed the spirit of man within him" (v1).** Before promising the impossible, God names himself as Creator. The language echoes Isaiah (Isa 42:5; 44:24; 51:13). The God who spread the sky like a tent and shaped the human spirit can certainly defend a city and change hearts. Both halves of the chapter are in view: power over nations (vv2–9) and power over the "spirit of man" (v10).
- **"I am about to make Jerusalem a cup of staggering to all the surrounding peoples" (v2).** The prophets often pictured God's judgment as a cup of strong wine that makes those who drink it reel (Isa 51:17, 22; Jer 25:15–28; Hab 2:16). Jerusalem had drunk that cup. Now the nations who try to swallow her will stagger. Jesus spoke of his own suffering as a cup he must drink (Matt 26:39).
- **"The siege of Jerusalem will also be against Judah" (v2).** The Hebrew is unclear, literally "and also upon Judah it will be in the siege." The ESV takes it to mean Judah's countryside is caught up in the attack too. Some older interpreters read it as Judah being forced to join the attackers, but vv5–7 show Judah on Jerusalem's side.
- **"I will make Jerusalem a heavy stone for all the peoples. All who lift it will surely hurt themselves" (v3).** Jerome, who lived near Bethlehem around AD 400, described a custom in Judean villages of young men testing their strength by lifting heavy stones. Jerusalem is a stone too heavy for the nations. Those who try to lift it will "hurt themselves," literally "be gashed" or "scratched." History has often seemed to illustrate this, but the full picture is of a final gathering: **"all the nations of the earth will gather against it."**
- **"I will strike every horse with panic, and its rider with madness… with blindness" (v4).** Panic, madness and blindness are the curses Moses warned would fall on a disobedient Israel (Deut 28:28, almost the same three words). Now they fall on Israel's enemies instead. Cavalry was the most feared force in ancient war, and God disables it. **"I will keep my eyes open"** for Judah, a picture of watchful care.
- **"The inhabitants of Jerusalem have strength through the LORD of hosts, their God" (v5).** The clans of Judah look at the city and recognize where its strength comes from. The ESV's "clans" (*'allufim*) can also mean "leaders" or "chiefs" (KJV "governors").
- **"Like a blazing pot in the midst of wood, like a flaming torch among sheaves" (v6).** A firepot was a pan of live coals carried to start a fire (Exod 27:3). Dropped into a woodpile or a field of cut grain, it sets everything ablaze. Weak Judah becomes a fire that consumes its attackers, while **"Jerusalem shall again be inhabited in its place."**
- **"The LORD will give salvation to the tents of Judah first" (v7).** "Tents" suggests the unwalled villages of the countryside, the most exposed people. God saves them first, so that the city and the royal house will not boast over them. Pride in Jerusalem's walls or David's line is ruled out. Salvation starts with the vulnerable.
- **"The feeblest among them on that day shall be like David" (v8).** The word for "feeblest" means one who stumbles or totters. David was the hero of Israel's memory, the giant-killer and the man of whom it was sung, "Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands" (1 Sam 18:7). The weakest believer will fight like him.
- **"The house of David shall be like God, like the angel of the LORD, going before them" (v8).** A breathtaking line. The angel of the LORD went before Israel in the exodus (Exod 14:19; 23:20) and is often identified with God himself (Gen 16:13; Exod 3:2–6; Zech 3:1–2). The text itself explains "like God" with "like the angel of the LORD." The house of David will lead God's people with God's own presence. Christians see this fulfilled in the son of David who is God with us.
- **"I will seek to destroy all the nations that come against Jerusalem" (v9).** "Seek" expresses settled purpose. The section ends where it began: Jerusalem's attackers are judged.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads Jerusalem as the church, "a cup of trembling to all that lay siege to it" and "a burdensome stone" to all who try to carry it away, and says weak believers will become not just "good soldiers, but great soldiers, like David." JFB explains the stone by the old custom of testing strength by lifting stones and says the clans of Judah are saved first so that Jerusalem's glory does not outshine theirs. Guzik reads the chapter as finally fulfilled at the end of the age, with all nations gathered against Jerusalem, while allowing that there may be earlier fulfilments. The Tyndale notes point out that the curses of Deuteronomy 28:28 are here turned on Israel's enemies.

#### Him Whom They Have Pierced (vv10–14)
- **"I will pour out on the house of David and the inhabitants of Jerusalem a spirit of grace and pleas for mercy" (v10).** After the military rescue comes the deeper work. "Pour out" is the language Joel and Ezekiel use for God's Spirit (Joel 2:28–29; Ezek 39:29). "Grace" (*chen*) is the favor God gives. "Pleas for mercy" (*tachanunim*) are the prayers that grace produces, cries for pity. God gives the grace that makes people ask for grace. It begins with the leaders, the house of David, and reaches the whole city.
- **"When they look on me, on him whom they have pierced" (v10).** The Hebrew is literally "they will look to me, whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn for him." The speaker is the LORD, who has been speaking throughout the chapter. So the LORD himself says he has been pierced. Then the pronoun changes from "me" to "him." The shift has puzzled readers for centuries. A few Hebrew manuscripts read "to him," and some scholars prefer that. But "me" is the reading of almost all manuscripts and of the ancient Greek translation, and it is the harder reading, the one a scribe would be less likely to invent. Christians have long seen in the "me… him" the mystery of the Father and the Son: God says he is pierced, and the one pierced is distinct from the speaker.
- **"Pierced" (v10).** The verb *daqar* means to run through with a sword or spear. It is used of Phinehas driving a spear through the couple in the tent (Num 25:8) and of Saul begging his armor-bearer to run him through (1 Sam 31:4). It is not a figure for being insulted. It means a violent, fatal wound. The same verb appears in 13:3.
- **The fulfilment in John.** When the soldier pierced Jesus' side with a spear, John writes, "these things took place that the Scripture might be fulfilled… 'They will look on him whom they have pierced'" (John 19:34–37). At the start of Revelation he widens it: "Behold, he is coming with the clouds, and every eye will see him, even those who pierced him, and all tribes of the earth will wail on account of him" (Rev 1:7). Jesus himself used the wording when he spoke of his return: "all the tribes of the earth will mourn" (Matt 24:30).
- **"They shall mourn for him, as one mourns for an only child, and weep bitterly over him, as one weeps over a firstborn" (v10).** The death of an only child, or of the firstborn who carried the family's hope, was the deepest grief known (Jer 6:26; Amos 8:10). The Egyptians wailed over their firstborn on Passover night (Exod 12:29–30). Here God's own people grieve that way over the one they pierced. Notice the order: first they look, then they mourn. The sight produces the sorrow.
- **"As great as the mourning for Hadad-rimmon in the plain of Megiddo" (v11).** Two main views. Most interpreters take this as the mourning for King Josiah, Judah's most godly king, killed in battle against Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo in 609 BC. "All Judah and Jerusalem mourned for Josiah," Jeremiah wrote laments for him, and the laments were still sung years later (2 Chr 35:22–25). Hadad-rimmon would then be a place near Megiddo, which Jerome identified with a village of his own day. Others note that Hadad and Rimmon were names of the Syrian storm god (2 Kgs 5:18) and think of a pagan ritual of weeping for a dying god, like the weeping for Tammuz (Ezek 8:14). The first fits the context much better: a national grief for a good king cut down before his time.
- **"The land shall mourn, each family by itself… and their wives by themselves" (v12).** The mourning is not a crowd's excitement but personal and household grief, everyone facing their own guilt. Men and women mourned separately, as was the custom in public mourning and at worship. No one hides in the crowd.
- **"The house of David… the house of Nathan… the house of Levi… the Shimeites" (vv12–13).** Two royal and two priestly families. David is the royal house. Nathan is most likely David's son (2 Sam 5:14), a branch of the royal family, and Luke traces Jesus' line through him (Luke 3:31). Levi is the priestly tribe, and Shimei was Levi's grandson through Gershon (Num 3:17–18), a branch of the priestly family. Some instead see Nathan the prophet, giving king, prophet and priest. Either way, the leaders who should have been shepherds are the first to grieve.
- **"And all the families that are left" (v14).** Every household, from the highest to the lowest. The repentance is total.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God will "pour upon them the Spirit of grace and supplication," and that the mourning is for Christ's death and for the sins that caused it: "They shall look on me whom they have pierced, and shall mourn for him." He stresses that true repentance is private and family-based as well as public. JFB ties the mourning to true repentance and to the lament for Josiah at Megiddo. Guzik points out that the speaker who says "me" is the LORD himself, which he takes as evidence for the Trinity, and draws from Spurgeon that saving repentance starts with looking at the crucified one. He links the national mourning to Paul's hope that "all Israel will be saved" (Rom 11:26). The Tyndale notes favor the pagan-ritual background for Hadad-rimmon, but most commentators prefer the mourning for Josiah, and the families listed in vv12–13 make the royal-priestly reading attractive.

#### Pointing to Christ
This is one of the clearest windows in the Old Testament onto the cross. The LORD speaks of himself as pierced, and the Gospel of John stands at the foot of the cross and says, "this is it" (John 19:37). God's people did not recognize their king, and he was run through with a spear outside their city. Zechariah saw what would follow. God would pour out "a spirit of grace and pleas for mercy," and people would look and mourn. That began at Pentecost, when the Spirit was poured out and Peter told the crowd in Jerusalem, "God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified." They were "cut to the heart" and asked what to do (Acts 2:36–37). It goes on whenever anyone sees what their sin cost the Son of God and grieves for it, and Paul expects it for Israel as a whole (Rom 11:25–27). The look comes first, then the grief, and then, as the next verse says, a fountain opened for cleansing (13:1).
