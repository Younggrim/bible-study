*Setting:* Babylonia, around 586 BC. This is the third and last of Ezekiel's oracles against Tyre (chs 26–28). Chapter 26 announced the city's fall, and chapter 27 sang over it as a sinking ship. Chapter 28 turns from the city to its ruler. According to Josephus, citing Phoenician records, the king of Tyre during Nebuchadnezzar's siege was Ithobaal, a name meaning "Baal is with him." The chapter has four parts: a charge against the ruler's pride (vv1–10), a lament that pictures him in Eden and then cast out (vv11–19), a short oracle against Sidon (vv20–24), and a promise that Israel will be gathered home in safety (vv25–26). The lament in vv11–19 is one of the most debated passages in the prophets, because many Christians have read it as describing the fall of Satan.

#### Prophecy Against the Prince of Tyre (vv1–10)
- **"Because your heart is proud, and you have said, 'I am a god'" (v2).** "Proud" is literally "lifted up." Ancient kings often claimed divine status or a special closeness to the gods. Tyre's kings served as priests of Melqart, the city's god. **"I sit in the seat of the gods, in the heart of the seas."** The island fortress seemed untouchable, rising out of the sea like a throne. **"Yet you are but a man, and no god."** God's reply is short. Isaiah made the same point to Egypt: "The Egyptians are man, and not God" (Isa 31:3).
- **"You are indeed wiser than Daniel; no secret is hidden from you" (v3).** This is irony. Ezekiel has already named Daniel, with Noah and Job, as a model of righteousness (14:14, 20). Most readers take him to be Ezekiel's contemporary, the Daniel taken to Babylon in 605 BC, whose God-given wisdom in explaining secrets became famous (Dan 2:47; 5:11–12). The Hebrew spelling here (*Dan'el*) lacks one letter found in the book of Daniel, and some scholars instead link it with Danel, a wise and just king in older Canaanite poems found at Ugarit, a name that would have been known in Phoenicia. Either way, the point is the same: the king of Tyre thinks himself wiser than the wisest man anyone knew.
- **"By your wisdom and your understanding you have made wealth for yourself" (vv4–5).** Tyre's wisdom was real. It was business sense, shrewd trading, skill in shipbuilding and navigation. Guzik, citing Block, notes that the prophet does not condemn the king for being clever or rich. The problem is what wealth did to his heart: **"your heart has become proud in your wealth."** Moses had warned Israel of the same danger: when your silver and gold multiply, "then your heart be lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God… Beware lest you say in your heart, 'My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth'" (Deut 8:13–17).
- **"I will bring foreigners upon you, the most ruthless of the nations" (v7).** The Babylonians are "the most ruthless of the nations" here and again in 30:11, 31:12 and 32:12. **"They shall draw their swords against the beauty of your wisdom and defile your splendor."** "Defile" (*chalal*) means to profane or treat as ordinary. The king who claimed to be holy, a god, will be treated like any common man.
- **"They shall thrust you down into the pit… in the heart of the seas" (v8).** The one who sat enthroned "in the heart of the seas" (v2) dies "in the heart of the seas." The pit is the grave, the realm of the dead (26:20).
- **"Will you still say, 'I am a god,' in the presence of those who kill you?" (v9).** This is the decisive test. Gods do not die. The king will meet his death and prove he was a man all along. Herod Agrippa learned this at Caesarea when the crowd shouted, "The voice of a god, and not of a man!" and he died soon after (Acts 12:21–23).
- **"You shall die the death of the uncircumcised" (v10).** The Phoenicians, like many peoples of the region, practiced circumcision, so to die "uncircumcised" meant to die in shame, treated as an outsider and a barbarian. For Israel it also meant dying outside God's covenant (Gen 17:14). The phrase returns many times in chapter 32.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "Men must be made to know that they are but men," and "Knowledge puffeth up; it is hard to know much and not to know it too well and to be elevated with it." He adds, "It is just with God that our enemies should make that their prey which we have made our pride." JFB identifies the prince as "Ithbaal II," a name implying his close connection with Baal, whose representative he was, and reads the comparison with Daniel as irony aimed at the king's opinion of himself. Guzik lists other rulers whose heads were turned by success: Sennacherib, Nebuchadnezzar, Pharaoh and Herod.

#### A Lament over the King of Tyre (vv11–19)
- **"Raise a lamentation over the king of Tyre" (v12).** The title changes from "prince" (*nagid*, v2) to "king" (*melek*). Some readers see a different figure here. Others see the same ruler, with "king" fitting the royal lament. The lament form praises before it mourns, and the praise here is extraordinary.
- **"You were the signet of perfection, full of wisdom and perfect in beauty" (v12).** A signet was a seal ring or cylinder seal, used to sign documents with the owner's authority. It was precious and was kept close (Hag 2:23; Song 8:6). The Hebrew is unusual. JFB renders it "thou sealest up the sum," the finished pattern. The king is pictured as the model of perfection.
- **"You were in Eden, the garden of God" (v13).** The setting moves to the beginning of the world. The king is placed in the garden, covered with precious stones.
- **"Every precious stone was your covering" (v13).** The nine stones named here are all among the twelve on the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:17–20), and the Greek translation lists all twelve. Some English names are guesses, since ancient gem names are hard to match with modern ones. The picture is of a figure dressed like a priest, in splendor given by God. **"Crafted in gold were your settings and your engravings."** These two words are uncertain. The KJV took them as musical instruments ("tabrets and pipes"), which led some to imagine the king or Satan as a leader of heaven's music. Most modern versions read them as jewelry settings.
- **"You were an anointed guardian cherub" (v14).** Cherubim were the mighty heavenly beings who guarded Eden after Adam was driven out (Gen 3:24), whose figures spread their wings over the ark (Exod 25:20), and whom Ezekiel saw carrying God's throne (ch 1, 10). "Guardian" is literally "covering," like the wings over the ark. The Hebrew can also be read differently: "With an anointed guardian cherub I placed you." The Greek translation takes it that way, as do some modern versions (NRSV). On that reading, the king is a human in Eden *with* the cherub, not the cherub himself.
- **"You were on the holy mountain of God; in the midst of the stones of fire you walked" (v14).** Eden is pictured as a mountain sanctuary, the place of God's presence (compare 28:16; Isa 14:13). The "stones of fire" are mysterious. They may be the gleaming gems of v13, fire around God's throne (1:13), or burning coals of the altar.
- **"You were blameless in your ways from the day you were created, till unrighteousness was found in you" (v15).** "Blameless" (*tamim*) is the word used of Noah (Gen 6:9) and of what God asked of Abraham (Gen 17:1). This is a fall story. A created being began innocent and then fell.
- **"In the abundance of your trade you were filled with violence in your midst, and you sinned" (v16).** The lament comes back to earth. The sin is described in Tyre's own terms: trade and violence. Tyre's commerce was built on force and exploitation (26:17; 27:13). **"So I cast you as a profane thing from the mountain of God, and I destroyed you, O guardian cherub."** On the other reading of v14, the last phrase becomes "and the guardian cherub drove you out," which matches Genesis 3:24, where a cherub guards the way to the garden after the man is expelled.
- **"Your heart was proud because of your beauty; you corrupted your wisdom for the sake of your splendor" (v17).** Pride is the root again. Beauty and wisdom, both gifts, became reasons to exalt himself. **"I cast you to the ground; I exposed you before kings, to feast their eyes on you."** The one who sat above the nations becomes a spectacle for them.
- **"You profaned your sanctuaries… so I brought fire out from your midst; it consumed you" (v18).** Tyre was famous for its temples. The fire comes "from your midst": the judgment grows out of the sin itself. **"You have come to a dreadful end and shall be no more forever" (v19).** The same closing words as 26:21 and 27:36 end each of the three oracles.
- **Who is being described?** Faithful Christian interpreters have read vv11–19 in three main ways.
- **The first view: Satan behind the king.** Many church fathers (Tertullian, Origen) and many evangelical teachers since read the passage as looking past the human king to the spiritual power behind him, Satan, who was created good, filled with beauty and wisdom, placed near God's throne, and fell through pride. They point out that the language of Eden, a cherub, the holy mountain and being "blameless… from the day you were created" seems to go beyond any human king. They read it alongside Isaiah 14:12–15 and Revelation 12:7–9, and note that Scripture elsewhere speaks of spiritual powers behind earthly kingdoms (Dan 10:13, 20).
- **The second view: the king pictured as a new Adam.** Others read the passage as describing the human king of Tyre in the terms of the first man in Eden. Adam was created good, placed in the garden, given glory and wisdom, fell, and was driven out with a cherub guarding the way. The king of Tyre, in his pride, saw himself as a perfect, god-like figure, and God tells his story as a second fall. This reading notes that the passage is explicitly addressed to "the king of Tyre," that the sins named are trade and violence (v16), and that the Greek translation of v14 puts the king *with* the cherub rather than making him one.
- **The third view: satire on royal myth.** Some scholars see Ezekiel taking up the kind of exalted language ancient kings used of themselves, borrowing images from Eden and perhaps from Phoenician myth, and using it as satire. The king claimed a place among the gods. Ezekiel grants the claim in his lament only to show how far he will fall.
- **Holding the views together.** These readings are not entirely opposed. Many who take the second or third view still see in the king's pride the same pattern as the devil's: the creature who wants to be God. Many who take the first view agree that the passage is first of all about a real king of Tyre. The New Testament does not quote this passage directly. Paul warns that a new believer appointed to leadership may "become puffed up with conceit and fall into the condemnation of the devil" (1 Tim 3:6), which supports the idea that pride is the devil's own sin.
- **Commentators:** Here the commentators differ clearly. Guzik takes the first view firmly: the "king of Tyre" is Satan, the spiritual ruler behind the human prince, and he draws from the passage Satan's original glory, his place near God's throne and his fall through pride. He acknowledges that others read it as Adam or as borrowed mythology, but argues that the "anointed cherub" does not fit Adam. Henry takes the first sense to be the reigning prince, who saw himself as a guardian angel to his people, and notes that some link the cherub to Eden and others to the cherubim over the ark: "he thought himself as bright as one of them." JFB takes the second view: the king is described "under images drawn from the primeval man in Eden," and like the first man "hast fallen by abusing God's gifts." The Tyndale notes also take the historical view, warning that the Satan reading overlooks the passage's real setting, while allowing that proud rulers can act as agents of Satan's opposition to God. Henry also adds a practical note on v16: "Those that have much to do in the world are in great danger of doing much amiss."

#### Prophecy Against Sidon (vv20–24)
- **"Set your face toward Sidon" (v21).** Sidon lay about 25 miles north of Tyre. It was the older of the two cities. Genesis names Sidon as the firstborn of Canaan (Gen 10:15), and "Sidonians" was a general name for the Phoenicians (1 Kings 5:6). Jezebel, who brought Baal worship into Israel, was the daughter of "Ethbaal king of the Sidonians" (1 Kings 16:31). By Ezekiel's day Tyre had outgrown it.
- **"I will manifest my glory in your midst… and manifest my holiness in her" (v22).** No specific sin is named. The emphasis falls on God's glory and holiness, shown in judgment. Henry: "When God is sanctified he is glorified, for his holiness is his glory."
- **"Pestilence… blood into her streets… the sword" (v23).** These are the familiar judgments of siege and war that Ezekiel announced against Jerusalem (5:12, 17). Sidon submitted to Babylon. Much later, in 351 BC, it rebelled against Persia and was destroyed, with many of its people burning themselves in their homes rather than surrender.
- **"For the house of Israel there shall be no more a brier to prick or a thorn to hurt them" (v24).** This verse sums up all six oracles against the neighbors in chapters 25–28. Moses had warned that if Israel let the Canaanites remain, they would be "barbs in your eyes and thorns in your sides" (Num 33:55; Josh 23:13). Sidon was one of the cities Israel never drove out (Judg 1:31). God promises that these "neighbors who have treated them with contempt" will trouble them no more.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the name Sidon as from a root meaning "to fish" and notes that Tyre was originally a colony of Sidon, so the mother city shared the daughter's fate. He links the brier and thorn of v24 to the nations left in Canaan that first led Israel into sin and then were used to punish them. Guzik notes that Tyre and Sidon are usually named together (Isa 23; Joel 3:4), but here Sidon is given its own oracle.

#### Israel Gathered in Security (vv25–26)
- **"When I gather the house of Israel from the peoples among whom they are scattered" (v25).** In the middle of the oracles against the nations, while Jerusalem is falling, comes the first clear promise of return. It looks ahead to chapters 34–37. **"And manifest my holiness in them in the sight of the nations."** God's holiness is shown in judgment on Sidon (v22) and in mercy toward Israel. Both reveal who he is.
- **"They shall dwell in their own land that I gave to my servant Jacob" (v25).** The promise reaches back to the patriarchs (Gen 28:13; 35:12). The exile does not cancel it.
- **"They shall build houses and plant vineyards" (v26).** These are signs of settled peace. People build and plant only when they expect to stay. Jeremiah told the exiles to build houses and plant gardens in Babylon (Jer 29:5) and promised that houses and vineyards would again be bought in Judah (Jer 32:15). Isaiah pictures the new creation the same way: "They shall build houses and inhabit them; they shall plant vineyards and eat their fruit" (Isa 65:21). **"They shall dwell securely"** is said twice.
- **"Then they will know that I am the LORD their God" (v26).** The nations will know that he is "the LORD" (vv22–24). Israel will know that he is "the LORD *their* God," the covenant Lord. The Tyndale notes point out the difference.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that God takes care that his people under his correcting hand "shall not seem vile to those that are about them, and therefore takes it ill of those who despise them." The promise here is the reverse side of every oracle against the nations.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel 28 is about a creature who said, "I am a god," and was shown to be a man who could die. The gospel tells the opposite story. The one who truly was God "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself… being born in the likeness of men," and "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death" (Phil 2:6–8). The king of Tyre grasped at heaven and was cast from the mountain of God. Christ came down from heaven and was raised and given "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9). Whether the lament looks back to Adam or behind the king to Satan, Jesus is the answer to both. He is the last Adam, who did not fall under temptation (1 Cor 15:45; Matt 4:1–11), and he came "to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8). And the promise at the end of the chapter, that God's scattered people will be gathered and live securely, is the promise Christ fulfils as the Good Shepherd who gathers his flock (John 10:16; 11:52).
