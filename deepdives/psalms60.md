*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Shushan Eduth. A Miktam of David; for instruction; when he strove with Aram-naharaim and with Aram-zobah, and when Joab on his return struck down twelve thousand of Edom in the Valley of Salt." The scene has changed completely from the last few psalms. David is no longer a fugitive in a cave. He is king over all Israel, probably around 1000–990 BC, and his armies are fighting on several fronts at once. 2 Samuel 8 and 1 Chronicles 18 record the wars: against Hadadezer king of Zobah, an Aramean (Syrian) kingdom north of Damascus, whose allies reached to the Euphrates; and in the south against Edom, where a great victory was won in the Valley of Salt (2 Sam 8:3–14; 1 Chr 18:3–13). Those histories tell only of victories. This psalm reveals what they leave out: at some point things went badly wrong. Perhaps while the army was in the far north, Edom invaded from the south, and Israel suffered a defeat that felt like God himself had turned away. The psalm moves from lament (vv1–5) to God's own answer (vv6–8) to renewed prayer and confidence (vv9–12). Verses 5–12 were later reused as the second half of Psalm 108, joined to Psalm 57:7–11.

#### The Title
- **"According to Shushan Eduth" (title).** "Lily of the Testimony," probably a tune name. The Tyndale notes say the tune is unknown today. Other psalm titles mention lilies too (45, 69, 80). JFB suggests the phrase could describe the psalm itself, a beautiful poem bearing witness to God's faithfulness.
- **"A Miktam of David; for instruction" (title).** This is the last of the run of Miktam psalms (56–60). "For instruction" (*lelammed*, "to teach") is found only here in a psalm title. The Tyndale notes take it to mean the psalm was taught to young people. David's lament for Saul and Jonathan was likewise to be "taught to the people of Judah" (2 Sam 1:18). Henry: in a day of public rejoicing "we have need to be taught to direct our joy to God."
- **"Aram-naharaim and… Aram-zobah" (title).** "Aram of the two rivers" is the land of upper Mesopotamia between the Euphrates and the Tigris region, Abraham's family's homeland (Gen 24:10). Zobah was an Aramean kingdom in Syria. Its king Hadadezer had allies from "beyond the Euphrates" (2 Sam 10:16).
- **"Joab on his return struck down twelve thousand of Edom in the Valley of Salt" (title).** The Valley of Salt was probably near the salt flats south of the Dead Sea, on the border with Edom. 2 Samuel 8:13 says David struck down 18,000 Edomites there, and 1 Chronicles 18:12 credits Abishai, Joab's brother, with 18,000. Commanders often received credit for what their troops did, and the king for all of them. The difference in numbers is usually explained as different parts of one campaign: Joab's part of the victory, or one stage of a longer war. 1 Kings 11:15–16 says Joab stayed in Edom six months. Guzik notes that the histories record the victories but not the setback this psalm laments.

#### You Have Rejected Us (vv1–5)
- **"O God, you have rejected us, broken our defenses" (v1).** A shocking opening for a victory psalm. The word "rejected" (*zanach*) means to cast off, spurn, as in 44:9 and 74:1. "Broken our defenses" is literally "broken through us," like a flood bursting a wall. It is the word David used when God "broke through" the Philistines at Baal-perazim (2 Sam 5:20), as JFB notes. Now it feels like God has broken through Israel.
- **"You have been angry; oh, restore us" (v1).** David does not blame the enemy first. He sees God's hand behind the defeat. Henry: "whoever are the instruments of it, we must own the hand of God, his righteous hand, in it." That is uncomfortable, but it is also hopeful. If God sent the trouble, God can end it.
- **"You have made the land to quake; you have torn it open; repair its breaches, for it totters" (v2).** The defeat is pictured as an earthquake that splits the ground. JFB: physical images for "great civil commotions." Henry adds that the breaches may include divisions inside the nation, still recovering from years of civil war between the houses of Saul and David (2 Sam 3:1).
- **"You have made your people see hard things" (v3).** "See" means experience, live through.
- **"You have given us wine to drink that made us stagger" (v3).** The cup of God's wrath is a common Old Testament picture: the nation drinks and reels like a drunk man, dazed and unable to stand (75:8; Isa 51:17, 22; Jer 25:15). The Tyndale notes say wine became a symbol of God's judgment. JFB: literally "wine of staggering."
- **"You have set up a banner for those who fear you" (v4).** The tone turns. A banner (*nes*) was a pole with a flag set on a hill as a rallying point for troops. After the victory over Amalek, Moses built an altar and named it "The LORD Is My Banner" (Exod 17:15), a link Guzik makes. Even in defeat, God has not abandoned "those who fear" him. He has raised a standard for them to rally around. Isaiah uses the word of the coming Messiah: "the root of Jesse, who shall stand as a signal (*nes*) for the peoples" (Isa 11:10).
- **"That they may flee to it from the bow. Selah" (v4).** The King James has "that it may be displayed because of the truth." The difference comes from one Hebrew word, *qoshet*, which can be read as "truth" (as in Prov 22:21) or as a form of "bow." Most modern translations take "bow": the banner is a refuge when the arrows fly. The verb can also mean either "rally" or "flee." Either way, the banner is a place to run to.
- **"That your beloved ones may be delivered" (v5).** "Beloved" (*yedidim*) is a tender word. It is related to Jedidiah, "beloved of the LORD," the name God gave David's son Solomon (2 Sam 12:25), and to David's own name, which probably means "beloved." Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes that the word comes from the language of love. A defeated nation is still God's beloved.
- **"Give salvation by your right hand and answer us!" (v5).** The right hand is the hand of power (17:7; 20:6). Psalm 108 begins its borrowing here.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives three reasons to remember past disasters in a day of victory: to keep their good lessons, to make God's goodness "more magnified," and so "that we may not be secure, but may always rejoice with trembling." He reads the banner as David's government, which united the scattered tribes, and ultimately Christ, "given for an ensign of the people." Guzik, quoting VanGemeren, says abandonment by God is the most miserable condition there is, and that David's answer to defeat is deeper loyalty to God, not less. JFB reads vv4–5 as the people rallying to God's banner and praying to be "led and sustained by His power."

#### God Has Spoken in His Holiness (vv6–8)
- **"God has spoken in his holiness" (v6).** Now God answers. It may be a word given through a prophet in the sanctuary, or David may be recalling God's promises. JFB: God spoke "on the pledge of His attributes," meaning God swore by his own holiness (89:35; Amos 4:2), and he cannot lie. The answer to "you have rejected us" is not an explanation. It is a promise.
- **"With exultation I will divide up Shechem and portion out the Vale of Succoth" (v6).** God speaks like a warrior who has won the land and is now dividing it up. Shechem lay west of the Jordan, in the hill country of Ephraim. Succoth lay east of the Jordan. Together they stand for the whole land on both sides of the river (so JFB and the Tyndale notes). There is a lovely echo. These were the two places Jacob settled when he came home to Canaan after meeting his brother Esau, the father of Edom (Gen 33:16–18). God is reclaiming Jacob's land, and Esau's descendants are about to be humbled.
- **"Gilead is mine; Manasseh is mine" (v7).** Gilead was the highland east of the Jordan. Manasseh's tribe held land on both sides of the river. "Mine" is repeated. The land is God's before it is Israel's (Lev 25:23). Kidner, cited by Guzik, says Israel holds it more securely because God owns it.
- **"Ephraim is my helmet" (v7).** Ephraim was the largest and strongest northern tribe. Moses compared Joseph's descendants to a wild ox with horns (Deut 33:17). Here Ephraim is the helmet on God's head, his defense in battle. JFB: Ephraim stands for "the military."
- **"Judah is my scepter" (v7).** The scepter is the symbol of royal rule, and it was promised to Judah: "The scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10). The King James has "lawgiver," and the Hebrew word (*mechoqeq*) can mean the ruler's staff or the one who decrees. JFB: Judah is "the civil power." David, of the tribe of Judah, holds the scepter, but God holds David.
- **"Moab is my washbasin" (v8).** Now the enemy nations. Moab, east of the Dead Sea, becomes the basin a warrior washes his feet in when he comes home from battle. It is the lowest household vessel, used for the dirtiest job. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, calls it servant status. David had conquered Moab (2 Sam 8:2).
- **"Upon Edom I cast my shoe" (v8).** Throwing down a shoe was a sign of taking possession. In Ruth 4:7 a man handed over his sandal to transfer a property right. It was also a gesture of contempt, the way a master tosses his sandals to a slave to carry. JFB: "as a slave." Either way, Edom, the very enemy that had just defeated Israel, will belong to God. Balaam had prophesied it centuries earlier: "Edom shall be dispossessed… while Israel is doing valiantly" (Num 24:18).
- **"Over Philistia I shout in triumph" (v8).** Philistia lay on the southwest coast. The Hebrew here reads "over me shout, Philistia," which the King James renders "Philistia, triumph thou because of me." Psalm 108:9 has the clearer "over Philistia I shout in triumph," and the ESV follows it. JFB reads it as Philistia being forced to acknowledge its subjection. The old enemy of Goliath's day is under God's feet.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God's word of promise, being a firm foundation of hope, is a full fountain of joy to all believers," and he applies the verse to the believer: "pardon is mine, peace mine, grace mine, Christ mine, heaven mine." Guzik notes that these are real places, not symbols, and that God speaks like a lord dividing his estate. JFB reads Ephraim and Judah as the military and civil powers of the kingdom.

#### Who Will Lead Me to Edom? (vv9–12)
- **"Who will bring me to the fortified city? Who will lead me to Edom?" (v9).** Back to David's voice. God has promised Edom, but Edom was a land of cliffs and mountain strongholds, extremely hard to invade. The Tyndale notes suggest the fortified city was Bozrah, Edom's capital. Boice, cited by Guzik, thinks of Sela, the rock city later called Petra. Obadiah, centuries later, describes Edom's pride in exactly these terms: "you who live in the clefts of the rock, in your lofty dwelling, who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'" (Obad 3).
- **"Have you not rejected us, O God? You do not go forth, O God, with our armies" (v10).** David answers his own question, and the answer is honest. The only one who can lead Israel into Edom is the very God who seemed to have rejected them. JFB reads it as "Is it not Thou?" The recent defeat came because God did not go out with the armies (44:9). Victory will come only when he does.
- **"Oh, grant us help against the foe, for vain is the salvation of man!" (v11).** "Vain" is *shav'*, emptiness, worthlessness. No alliance, no army, no general can save if God does not act. Trapp, cited by Guzik, points out that Israel had learned this under Saul, the king they asked for in order to be like the nations, who could not save them from the Philistines. Psalm 146:3 says the same: "Put not your trust in princes, in a son of man, in whom there is no salvation."
- **"With God we shall do valiantly" (v12).** "Do valiantly" is *'asah chayil*, literally "do strength" or "make valor." It is the very phrase in Balaam's prophecy about Edom: "while Israel is doing valiantly" (Num 24:18). David may be claiming that ancient word. Trust in God is not passive. The army still has to fight. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says God's working is the best motive for our courage, not an excuse for laziness.
- **"It is he who will tread down our foes" (v12).** The last word belongs to God. Israel fights, but God treads down. Kidner, cited by Guzik, says God's foot on the enemy finishes what human courage begins.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v9 as possibly "who has led me," as if the conquest had already begun, and sees vv11–12 as "a prayer for success, and an assurance of a hearing." Henry, in his opening comments, notes how the psalm balances joy and care: the Edomites are beaten but the Syrians not yet, "therefore let not him that girds on the harness boast as if he had put it off" (1 Kgs 20:11).

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 60 holds together defeat and promise. God's people have been shaken, made to drink a staggering cup, and seem rejected. Yet God has raised a banner and spoken an unbreakable word. Jesus is that banner, the "root of Jesse" raised as a signal for the nations to rally to (Isa 11:10; Rom 15:12). He also drank the cup that made Israel stagger. In Gethsemane he prayed, "let this cup pass from me" (Matt 26:39), and on the cross he cried out the words of real rejection, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46), so that his people would never be finally cast off. The Son of David is the King who holds Judah's scepter (Gen 49:10), and in him the promise of v12 is fulfilled for all believers: "the God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Rom 16:20). "Vain is the salvation of man." The salvation of God is not.
