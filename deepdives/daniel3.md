*Setting:* No date is given in the Aramaic text, but the event probably comes some years after chapter 2. The ancient Greek translation dates it to Nebuchadnezzar's eighteenth year (about 587 BC), the year before he destroyed Jerusalem, and JFB places it about nineteen years after the dream, though neither date is certain. In chapter 2 Nebuchadnezzar dreamed of a statue with a head of gold and heard that his kingdom, like all others, would be swept away. He confessed Daniel's God as "God of gods" (2:47) and promoted Daniel's three friends to govern the province of Babylon (2:49). Now he builds a statue of his own, gold from head to foot, and commands the whole empire to bow to it. Daniel does not appear in the chapter. The test falls on Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, and their answer to the king, "But if not" (v18), has become one of the most famous statements of faith in Scripture.

#### Nebuchadnezzar's Golden Image (vv1–7)
- **"King Nebuchadnezzar made an image of gold" (v1).** "Image" (*tselem*) is the same word used for the statue in the dream (2:31). The dream gave Babylon only the head of gold. Nebuchadnezzar answers with an image that is all gold, as if to say his kingdom will not be replaced. A statue that large was almost certainly wood or another core overlaid with gold, like many idols (Isa 40:19; Jer 10:3–4). The text does not say whom it represented: the king, his god Nabu or Marduk, or the empire itself. Refusing it is described as refusing to "serve your gods" (v12), so it had religious meaning.
- **"Sixty cubits and its breadth six cubits" (v1).** About 90 feet high and 9 feet wide. The shape, ten times taller than wide, is odd for a human figure, so many think the height included a tall pedestal, or that it was an obelisk-like pillar with a figure on top. Babylon counted in sixties (our 60 minutes and 360 degrees come from Babylonian mathematics), so the numbers sound distinctly Babylonian.
- **"On the plain of Dura, in the province of Babylon" (v1).** *Duru* was a common Babylonian word for a walled place, and several sites bore the name. Some point to a plain a few miles south of Babylon where a large brick base was found, perhaps the pedestal of a colossal statue, which Guzik mentions. The Tyndale notes suggest it may mean the plain within Babylon's great outer wall. An open plain allowed a huge crowd to gather.
- **"The satraps, the prefects, and the governors…" (vv2–3).** The list of eight ranks of officials is repeated, a feature of the chapter's style. Lists of officials, instruments, and the phrase "the image that King Nebuchadnezzar had set up" repeat again and again (vv2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 14, 18). The repetition has a mocking tone. Everything turns on what the king has set up. "Satrap" is a Persian title, one of several later Persian-era words in the book, which may show that the story was written or updated in Persian times.
- **"You are commanded, O peoples, nations, and languages" (v4).** This was a formula for the whole empire (vv7, 29; 4:1; 6:25). The dedication was a loyalty test for every official class from every conquered people. Bowing to the image was a political pledge as much as a religious act. Guzik points out that refusal would be read as treason.
- **"The horn, pipe, lyre, trigon, harp, bagpipe, and every kind of music" (v5).** The orchestra signaled the moment to bow. Three of these words are borrowed from Greek: the lyre (*qitharos*, related to "guitar"), the harp (*psanterin*, from "psaltery"), and the "bagpipe" (*sumponyah*, related to "symphony"). The last may mean a kind of instrument or simply "in unison." Some scholars use these Greek words to argue for a late date for the book. Others note that Greek traders and mercenaries were already present in the Near East in Nebuchadnezzar's time, and that musical instruments often travel with their foreign names.
- **"Whoever does not fall down and worship shall immediately be cast into a burning fiery furnace" (v6).** Babylon's kilns, used for baking bricks for its vast building projects, were large and fierce. Jeremiah mentions that Nebuchadnezzar "roasted in the fire" two false prophets (Jer 29:22). The threat was not theoretical.
- **"All the peoples, nations, and languages fell down and worshiped" (v7).** Almost everyone conformed. For most of the empire, bowing to one more god cost nothing. Polytheists could add a god without giving up any. Only those who worshiped one God alone had a problem.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls it plainly "a golden image set up to be worshipped," and on the crowd's response, "Thus beset with temptation, they all yielded." JFB thinks the frame was wood overlaid with gold, and distinguishes this statue on the plain of Dura from the golden image in Babylon's temple that Herodotus describes. Guzik estimates its size at about ninety by nine feet and sees in the scene the old pattern of politics using religion to bind people to the state, which he illustrates from modern totalitarian regimes.

#### The Fiery Furnace (vv8–30)
- **"Certain Chaldeans came forward and maliciously accused the Jews" (v8).** "Maliciously accused" is a vivid Aramaic idiom: they "ate the pieces of" the Jews, that is, they tore them apart with words (the same phrase in 6:24). These Chaldeans were probably members of the wise men's class, the same group Daniel's friends now outranked (2:48–49). Henry suggests envy was behind it.
- **"Certain Jews whom you have appointed over the affairs of the province" (v12).** The accusers stress three things: they are Jews, they are the king's own appointees, and they "pay no attention to you." The charge is ingratitude and disloyalty. **"They do not serve your gods or worship the golden image."** The refusal was public and deliberate.
- **Where was Daniel?** The text does not say. He may have been away on royal business, as JFB suggests, or his rank at court may have excused him from the gathering of provincial officials, or the accusers may have feared to touch him. Some think the story was deliberately told to show the three friends' faith on its own. Daniel's own test comes in chapter 6.
- **"Nebuchadnezzar in furious rage" (v13).** This king's temper has been shown before (2:12). **"Is it true?" (v14).** The Aramaic word is rare and may mean "Is it on purpose?", as the Tyndale notes suggest. The king offers them a second chance (v15). He respects these men and would rather they simply bowed.
- **"And who is the god who will deliver you out of my hands?" (v15).** This is the question the chapter answers. Assyria's field commander had taunted Jerusalem in almost the same words: "Who among all the gods of the lands have delivered their lands out of my hand?" (Isa 36:20). By the end of the chapter Nebuchadnezzar gives the answer himself (v29).
- **"We have no need to answer you in this matter" (v16).** This is not rudeness. It means they do not need to argue their case or plead for time. Their minds are made up. The KJV's "we are not careful to answer thee" means "we are not anxious."
- **"Our God whom we serve is able to deliver us… and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king" (v17).** The first clause, "If this be so," is hard to translate. It may mean "If it is so that you throw us in," or "If our God exists, as he does." Either way, they declare God's ability to rescue and their expectation that he will.
- **"But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods" (v18).** This is the heart of the chapter. Their faithfulness does not depend on rescue. They believe God *can* save them, but they do not claim to know he *will*, and their obedience is the same either way. Job said, "Though he slay me, I will hope in him" (Job 13:15). In 1940, British forces trapped at Dunkirk are said to have sent home the three-word message "But if not," and the country understood the reference.
- **"The expression of his face was changed" (v19).** Literally "the image of his face changed," the same word *tselem*. The king's own face is now twisted with rage. **"Heated seven times more"** means as hot as possible. JFB comments that passion defeats itself, since a hotter fire would only end their pain sooner.
- **"Bound in their cloaks, their tunics, their hats, and their other garments" (v21).** The exact meaning of several of these clothing words is uncertain. Their official robes, which would burn quickly, went in with them. The detail matters later (v27).
- **"The flame of the fire killed those men who took up Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego" (v22).** The soldiers who threw them in died from the heat. The fire was real.
- **"Fell bound into the burning fiery furnace" (v23).** At this point the ancient Greek translation, followed by Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, adds 68 verses: the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men. These are not in the Aramaic text, and Protestant Bibles place them in the Apocrypha. The Song is still used in Christian worship as the *Benedicite*.
- **"Did we not cast three men bound into the fire?" (v24).** The king jumps up in alarm. His advisers confirm it. **"But I see four men unbound, walking in the midst of the fire, and they are not hurt" (v25).** The fire burned their ropes and nothing else. They are walking, not running: God's people at peace in the place of judgment. Isaiah's promise is acted out: "When you walk through fire you shall not be burned, and the flame shall not consume you" (Isa 43:2).
- **"The appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods" (v25).** The Aramaic is *bar elahin*, "a son of gods," meaning a divine being. The KJV's "like the Son of God" reads a Christian meaning into a pagan king's words. In v28 Nebuchadnezzar calls him God's "angel." Christians have read the fourth figure in two main ways. Many, including the Tyndale notes and JFB, take him as an angel sent by God, as in 6:22. Many others, including Guzik and many early Christian writers, see a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ, like "the angel of the LORD" in the Old Testament (Gen 16:7–13; Exod 3:2–6). The text itself does not decide. What is clear is that God was with them in the fire, not merely watching from outside.
- **"Servants of the Most High God, come out" (v26).** "Most High" (*'illaya*) is an ancient title for the God who rules above all (Gen 14:18–20), and it becomes a key title in Daniel (4:2, 17, 34; 7:18–27). The king who asked "Who is the god?" now names him.
- **"The fire had not had any power over the bodies of those men" (v27).** The officials who came to watch the loyalty ceremony become witnesses. The details pile up: hair not singed, cloaks not harmed, **"no smell of fire had come upon them."** Anyone who has stood near a campfire knows how smoke clings. They came out as if they had never been there.
- **"Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who has sent his angel" (v28).** The king praises the men for exactly what he had condemned: they **"set aside the king's command, and yielded up their bodies rather than serve and worship any god except their own God."** Faithful disobedience to an unjust command is honored here, as later with the apostles: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
- **"Any people… that speaks anything against the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego shall be torn limb from limb" (v29).** The king threatens the same brutal punishment he used in 2:5. He still does not worship this God alone. He simply adds him to the list of protected gods. **"There is no other god who is able to rescue in this way"** answers his own question in v15. The true conversion comes in chapter 4.
- **"Then the king promoted Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego" (v30).** Like Joseph, whom the LORD made to prosper (Gen 39:2–3, 23), they end in higher honor than before.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the fire "burnt the cords wherewith they were bound, and set them at liberty." On v18 he paraphrases their resolve: "we will not serve these gods, though they are thy gods." JFB on v16: "Hesitation, or parleying with sin, is fatal; unhesitating decision is the only safety." On v18, their loyalty was not mercenary: "Though He slay them, they will still trust in Him." JFB takes the fourth figure as an angel in the king's mind, yet also as "the 'messenger of the covenant,'" foreshadowing Christ. He adds that "Decision for God at last gains the respect even of the worldly." Guzik identifies the fourth figure as Christ present with his people in the fire, reads "But if not" as faith that submits to God's will without presuming to know it, and warns against the small compromises that prepare the way for large ones. He also reads the chapter as a type of Israel preserved through the Great Tribulation, a reading many others do not share.

#### Pointing to Christ
Three young men walked into a fire they did not deserve, and they were not alone there. Whether the fourth figure was an angel or the Lord himself, he points to the one who did not merely visit the fire but entered the full judgment for sin in his people's place. Jesus' prayer in Gethsemane, "if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39), is "But if not" at its deepest. Unlike the three, he was not spared. Because he went through it, those who belong to him can say, "Neither death nor life… will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Rom 8:38–39), and can face whatever furnace comes knowing he walks with them in it.
