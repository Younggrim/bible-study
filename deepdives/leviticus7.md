*Setting:* This chapter finishes the priests' handbook on the offerings that began at 6:8 ("Command Aaron and his sons"). Chapter 6 covered the burnt, grain and sin offerings from the priest's side. Chapter 7 does the same for the guilt offering and the peace offering, then adds rules about fat and blood, sets out the priests' share and closes the whole first section of Leviticus (chs 1–7) with a summary. The peace offering comes last in this handbook, though it was third in chapters 1–5. It ends the list because it ends in a meal, the happiest of the offerings.

#### The Priests and the Offerings (vv1–38)
- **"This is the law of the guilt offering. It is most holy" (v1).** The procedure follows the sin offering: killed on the north side where the burnt offering was killed, the fat and kidneys burned (vv2–5), the meat eaten by the priests in the courtyard (v6). **"There is one law for them"** (v7). The difference between the two was in what they dealt with (5:14–6:7), not in how they were done at the altar. One difference stands out: in the guilt offering the blood was **"thrown against the sides of the altar"** (v2), not put on its horns as in the sin offering (4:25).
- **The priest's share (vv7–10).** The priest who did the work received the meat of the guilt offering, the **skin** of a burnt offering (v8), and baked grain offerings (v9). Raw grain offerings were **"shared equally among all the sons of Aaron"** (v10). Those who served at the altar lived from it (1 Cor 9:13).
- **"This is the law of the sacrifice of peace offerings" (v11).** Chapter 3 described the altar's part. Now the meal itself is explained. There were three kinds of peace offering: **thanksgiving** (v12), a **vow** offering, and a **freewill** offering (v16).
- **"If he offers it for a thanksgiving" (vv12–15).** "Thanksgiving" is *todah*, which BDB says can also mean a song of thanks or a thank offering. Several psalms were written for this moment: "I will offer to you the sacrifice of thanksgiving and call on the name of the LORD. I will pay my vows to the LORD in the presence of all his people" (Ps 116:17–18; compare Ps 107:22). A worshiper God had rescued brought an animal, loaves and wafers, and told the story of what God had done.
- **"Loaves of leavened bread" (v13).** Leaven was banned from the altar (2:11), but these loaves were not burned. They were eaten at the meal, with one loaf given to the priest (v14). A thanksgiving feast had ordinary festive bread on the table.
- **"Shall be eaten on the day of his offering" (v15).** The thank offering had to be eaten the same day, the vow and freewill offering by the second day, and anything left on the third day burned (vv16–17). A whole animal is a lot of meat for one day, so the worshiper had to invite family, friends, servants and the poor to the feast (compare Deut 12:17–18). Thanksgiving became a party. The time limit also kept a holy meal from becoming ordinary leftovers.
- **"It is tainted" (v18).** The word is *piggul*, which the Tyndale notes say appears only four times and always means unfit meat. Eaten on the third day, the offering **"shall not be accepted, neither shall it be credited to him."** A sacrifice handled carelessly undid its own purpose.
- **"While an uncleanness is on him… that person shall be cut off from his people" (vv19–21).** Anyone ceremonially unclean could not eat a holy meal. "Cut off" is serious. It may mean God himself would cut the person off by early death, or that he lost his place in the community. The Tyndale notes set out both views. Paul gives a New Testament parallel at the Lord's Supper: eating "in an unworthy manner" brings judgment (1 Cor 11:27–30).
- **"You shall eat no fat… you shall eat no blood whatever" (vv22–27).** This expands 3:17. The fat banned is the fat of animals that could be sacrificed, ox, sheep and goat (v23). The fat of an animal that died naturally or was killed by beasts could be **"put to any other use,"** such as lamp oil or leatherwork, but not eaten (v24). Blood of any animal or bird was never to be eaten (v26). The reason comes in 17:11: "the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood that makes atonement."
- **"His own hands shall bring the LORD's food offerings" (v30).** The worshiper did not hand the animal over and walk away. He carried God's portion himself.
- **"That the breast may be waved as a wave offering" (v30).** "Wave offering" is *tenuphah*. BDB gives "a swinging, waving" and also simply "offering." The worshiper, probably with the priest's help, held the breast up and moved it toward the altar and back, a public act of presenting it to God. God then gave it to the priests. Some scholars think "elevation offering" fits the motion better. The **right thigh** was a **"contribution"** (*terumah*, from "to lift up," the old "heave offering") and went to the priest who did the work (vv32–33).
- **"A perpetual due from the people of Israel" (vv34–36).** The priests' share was not a tip. God had **"taken"** it from the people and **"given"** it to the priests. Sadly, Eli's sons later took more than this, grabbing meat with forks before the fat was even burned (1 Sam 2:12–17).
- **"This is the law of the burnt offering, of the grain offering…" (vv37–38).** The summary lists six laws, adding **"the ordination offering,"** which was mentioned in 6:19–23 and will be carried out in chapter 8. It ends where Leviticus began: these were the commands **"the LORD commanded Moses on Mount Sinai… in the wilderness of Sinai."**
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the worshiper got no share of the guilt offering, since it expressed repentance and so "fasting" suited it better than feasting. He says the peace offering spoke of joy and fellowship after pardon, and that Christ is the peace offering on which believers now feed. He also says the waving showed the offerer giving God his portion cheerfully. Guzik makes the same point about the time limits that the meal was fellowship that should not be allowed to go stale, and says being clean before the meal pictures receiving God's cleansing before enjoying his peace. He closes the section by showing how Christ fulfills each of the five offerings, citing Ephesians 5:2, Romans 5:1, 2 Corinthians 5:21 and Isaiah 53:10. JFB thinks the shared priestly meals "would tend to promote brotherly harmony," and it reads "cut off" as exclusion from Israel's privileges, a kind of excommunication. JFB has no notes on vv2–7, 9–10, 19 and 28.

#### Pointing to Christ
These seven chapters have shown five offerings, and Christ fulfills all of them. He is the burnt offering wholly given to God (Eph 5:2), the sin offering that cleanses (2 Cor 5:21; Heb 13:11–12), the guilt offering that pays sin's debt (Isa 53:10), and the peace offering that makes a meal with God possible (Rom 5:1). The many offerings were brought again and again. "We have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all" (Heb 10:10). What remains for believers is the thanksgiving offering, which now needs no altar: "let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15).
