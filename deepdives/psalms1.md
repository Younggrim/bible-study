*Setting:* Psalm 1 has no title and no named author. It was almost certainly placed first on purpose, as the gateway to the whole book of Psalms. The Psalter was gathered over centuries, from David's time (around 1000 BC) to after the exile, and whoever arranged it set this short wisdom poem at the door. It does not pray or praise. It describes two kinds of people and two roads, and asks which one the reader is on. Psalm 2 then stands with it as a second doorway: Psalm 1 is about the person who loves God's word, Psalm 2 about the King God has set on Zion. Psalm 1 opens with "Blessed" and Psalm 2 closes with "Blessed are all who take refuge in him" (2:12), so the two are framed as a pair. Everything that follows in the Psalter, lament and praise alike, is sung by people who have chosen the first road.

#### The Blessed Man (vv1–2)
- **"Blessed is the man" (v1).** The Hebrew word is *ashre*, a plural form, almost an exclamation: "Oh, the happinesses of the man…" It is not the word for being blessed by someone (*barak*). It describes a state of real well-being, the kind of life that is truly flourishing. It is the word behind Jesus' Beatitudes, "Blessed are the poor in spirit…" (Matt 5:3–12). "The man" is the ordinary Hebrew way of speaking of any person, and the psalm means anyone who walks this way.
- **The psalm starts with what this person does *not* do.** Before anything positive, there is a turning away. Faith in Scripture always includes a "no" to something.
- **Walk, stand, sit (v1).** Three verbs, three groups, three nouns, each a step further in. To **walk** in the *counsel* of the wicked is to take their advice, to let them shape how you think. To **stand** in the *way* of sinners is to stop and linger on their road, to take up their habits. To **sit** in the *seat* of scoffers is to settle down among them, to belong there and join in their mockery. The movement slows from walking to standing to sitting, and that is how drift into sin usually happens: thinking, then behaving, then belonging.
- **Wicked, sinners, scoffers (v1).** The words also deepen. The "wicked" (*reshaim*) are people who live without regard for God. "Sinners" are those who make a habit of missing the mark. "Scoffers" (*letsim*) appear often in Proverbs: people who mock wisdom and hate correction (Prov 9:7–8; 21:24). The end of the slide is not just doing wrong but laughing at what is right.
- **"But his delight is in the law of the LORD" (v2).** The negatives make room for a positive. The answer to bad influence is not empty space but a better love. "Law" is *torah*, which means more than legal rules. It is God's instruction, his teaching for life. In the psalmist's day that meant especially the books of Moses, but the word grew to cover all God's revealed word. "Delight" (*chefets*) means desire and pleasure. This person does not merely tolerate God's word. They want it. Psalm 119 is this verse expanded into 176 verses.
- **"On his law he meditates day and night" (v2).** "Meditate" is *hagah*. It is a sound word: the lexicon gives "moan, growl, utter, muse," and it is used for a lion growling over its prey (Isa 31:4) and a dove's moaning (Isa 38:14). People in the ancient world usually read aloud, so meditation meant murmuring the words over and over, turning them in the mouth and mind. It is the opposite of emptying the mind. God told Joshua the same: "This Book of the Law shall not depart from your mouth, but you shall meditate on it day and night" (Josh 1:8). "Day and night" is a figure for all the time, at home and on the road, lying down and getting up (Deut 6:7).
- **A link to Psalm 2.** The same verb comes back in 2:1: "Why do the nations rage and the peoples *plot* in vain?" The righteous murmur over God's word; the nations murmur over empty schemes against him. Everyone meditates on something.
- **Commentators:** All three read v1 as a downward progression. Henry says the path begins with "casting off the fear of God" and adds, "Thus is the way of iniquity down-hill." JFB calls the final "seat" a sign of "the boldest impiety," and renders "Blessed" as "oh, the happiness," an exclamation. Guzik's summary of the three verbs as thinking, behaving and belonging fits the text well. He also stresses that Christian meditation fills the mind with Scripture rather than emptying it, and he passes on an old picture of meditation as a cow chewing the cud, drawing nourishment out slowly. JFB takes "law" to mean "all of God's word then written, especially the books of Moses."

#### Tree and Chaff (vv3–4)
- **"He is like a tree planted by streams of water" (v3).** In a dry land, a tree's life depends on water. "Planted" (*shatul*) usually means transplanted, set in place on purpose, like a tree moved to a well-watered spot. The tree did not get there on its own. "Streams" (*palge mayim*) are channels or irrigation canals, so the picture is of a cultivated orchard where water never runs dry. Jeremiah uses the same picture for the one "who trusts in the LORD": "He is like a tree planted by water, that sends out its roots by the stream, and does not fear when heat comes" (Jer 17:7–8).
- **"That yields its fruit in its season" (v3).** Fruit comes in due time, not every day. A tree is not anxious about producing. It is fed, and fruit follows. Jesus said the same: "Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit" (John 15:5).
- **"And its leaf does not wither" (v3).** Even in drought, when other plants fade, this tree stays green. Its life is drawn from a source deeper than the weather.
- **"In all that he does, he prospers" (v3).** Again this echoes Joshua 1:8: meditate on the law, "for then you will make your way prosperous." The verb means to succeed or to carry something through to its goal. The rest of the Psalms makes clear that this is not a promise of wealth or an easy life. The righteous suffer, are slandered and cry out to God, sometimes for years (Ps 22; 73; 88). The promise is that a life rooted in God reaches its true end, and nothing done in him is wasted.
- **"The wicked are not so" (v4).** In Hebrew this is two blunt words, *lo-ken*, "not so." Everything just said of the tree is denied.
- **"But are like chaff that the wind drives away" (v4).** At harvest, farmers threw crushed grain into the air on a hilltop threshing floor. The heavy grain fell back, and the wind blew the light husks away. A tree is rooted, alive, fruitful and lasting; chaff is dead, rootless, weightless and gone. John the Baptist used the same picture of Christ's coming judgment: "the chaff he will burn with unquenchable fire" (Matt 3:12).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the godly man grows from "the planting of the Lord," so his flourishing is God's work, and that whatever he does in line with God's law "shall prosper," in the ways that matter spiritually. JFB glosses "planted" as "settled, fast" and the rivers as "canals for irrigation," and explains the chaff from winnowing against the wind. Guzik warns that "prosper" is not a promise that everything a believer touches turns to gold, but that God brings good fruit out of every season. He also passes on an older saying: "There are no barren trees in God's orchard."

#### Two Ways, Two Ends (vv5–6)
- **"Therefore the wicked will not stand in the judgment" (v5).** To "stand" in a court means to hold your ground, to be acquitted. The wicked, light as chaff, will not survive the weighing. The psalm looks beyond this life to God's final verdict. Note the reversal of v1: the righteous refused to *stand* in the way of sinners, and now sinners cannot *stand* before God.
- **"Nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous" (v5).** In this life the two kinds of people live side by side, even in the same worshipping community. In the end they will be separated. Jesus told the parable of the weeds and the wheat growing together until the harvest (Matt 13:24–30, 36–43), and of the sheep and goats divided before the King (Matt 25:31–46).
- **"For the LORD knows the way of the righteous" (v6).** "Knows" (*yada*) is more than awareness. God sees everything, including the wicked. Here it means to know with care and approval, to watch over. It is the knowing of a shepherd who knows his sheep (John 10:14), and the opposite of the terrible words, "I never knew you" (Matt 7:23). The righteous are safe not because they are strong but because God knows their road.
- **"But the way of the wicked will perish" (v6).** Notice it is the *way* that perishes. A road that leads nowhere simply ends in ruin, and everyone on it goes down with it. Psalm 2 ends the same way: those who refuse the King "perish in the way" (2:12). The psalm closes on this sober note, and the reader is left to choose.
- **Two ways.** The teaching of two paths runs through Scripture: Moses set before Israel "life and good, death and evil" (Deut 30:15–20), Proverbs contrasts the path of the righteous and the way of the wicked (Prov 4:18–19), and Jesus spoke of the narrow gate and the broad road (Matt 7:13–14). One of the earliest Christian writings outside the New Testament, the *Didache*, opens, "There are two ways, one of life and one of death."
- **Commentators:** Henry says the wicked will be found guilty and "shall not stand in the judgment," and that though hypocrites may mix among believers now, Christ cannot be deceived. He takes "the Lord knows their way" to mean that God chose, guides and approves them. JFB reads "stand in the judgment" as being acquitted and points to Matthew 25:45–46. Guzik notes that the book of Acts calls the Christian faith "the Way" (Acts 9:2; 19:9, 23; 24:14), and that God's knowing means his protective care even when the road is dark.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 1 describes a person no one has fully been. Israel did not live this way, and even David's best sons failed. The Tyndale notes observe that the apostles saw Jesus as the one who fulfils the ideal of the righteous man in the Psalms. He never walked in the counsel of the wicked. His food was to do the Father's will (John 4:34), he answered temptation with Scripture (Matt 4:1–11), and he was the true tree planted by the waters, whose fruit was perfect in its season. Yet the psalm's sentence on sinners still stands over everyone else. The good news is that the righteous one took the place of the unrighteous (1 Pet 3:18). He was cut off so that people blown like chaff could be transplanted beside the streams. Those joined to him are "the planting of the LORD" (Isa 61:3), and the river that waters them flows from God's throne, with the tree of life on its banks (Rev 22:1–2).
