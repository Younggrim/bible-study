*Setting:* The debate between Job and his three friends is over in all but name. Bildad managed only six verses (ch 25), and Zophar never took his third turn. Job answered Bildad (ch 26), and then, with no one replying, "Job again took up his discourse" (27:1). Chapter 27 opened with an oath: "As God lives, who has taken away my right" (27:2). Job swore he would not lie by agreeing with his friends: "till I die I will not put away my integrity from me" (27:5). Then, surprisingly, he described the doom of the wicked man in terms that sound much like the friends' own speeches: his children fall to the sword, his silver passes to the innocent, and the east wind sweeps him away (27:13–23). Some scholars think those verses were Zophar's missing third speech; others read them as Job's warning to his accusers (27:7). Either way, the arguments have run dry. Now comes chapter 28, and the tone changes completely. There is no anger, no complaint and no reply to anyone. Instead there is a calm, carefully built poem on one question: "where shall wisdom be found?" (v12). No new speaker is named, so as the text stands it continues Job's discourse from 27:1, and 29:1 repeats the same formula. Even so, readers have long asked whether these are Job's words or a pause in which the author steps forward. The poem stands between the failed debate and Job's final speech (chs 29–31), and it looks ahead to God's answer out of the whirlwind (ch 38).

#### Job Continues: Where Is Wisdom? (vv1–28)
- **Who speaks the poem? (vv1–28).** Three main views exist. The first, held by Matthew Henry, JFB and Guzik, takes the text as it stands: Job is still speaking, and in a quieter moment he reaches past the debate to the truth beneath it. Guzik passes on Mike Mason's suggestion that Job, having worn himself out with protest, may simply have grown calm for a while. Henry says Job "forgets his sores, and all his sorrows, and talks like a philosopher." The second view, held by many modern scholars and noted by Guzik through Francis Andersen, is that the poem is an interlude from the narrator, something like a chorus in a play. It sums up the debate and prepares for God's speeches. The Tyndale notes mention this view and point to one piece of evidence: the name *Adonai* ("Lord") in v28 is found nowhere else in Job. A third group thinks a later editor added the poem, though no ancient manuscript lacks it or places it elsewhere. The main objection to Job as speaker is that the poem ends in calm trust (v28), while in chapters 29–31 Job goes back to lament and to demanding a hearing. Those who hear Job's voice answer that a person can know the right answer and still ache for God to show up. On either reading, the poem says what the book wants understood before God speaks.
- **"Surely there is a mine for silver, and a place for gold that they refine" (v1).** The poem opens underground. Mining was hard, dangerous work in the biblical world. Copper was mined at Timna and Faynan in the Arabah, south of the Dead Sea, and Egypt took gold from Nubia. JFB explains that the word "mine" means literally a place from which silver "goes forth," and that the gold of v1 is ore that must be refined, unlike gold washed out of rivers. Watch the word "place," *maqom*. It appears here ("a place for gold"), again in v6 ("the place of sapphires"), in the refrain "where is the place of understanding?" (vv12, 20), and at the climax: God "knows its place" (v23). Metals have a place people can find. Only God knows wisdom's place.
- **"Iron is taken out of the earth, and copper is smelted from the ore" (v2).** JFB notes that copper was worked long before iron, since it is easier to smelt, which fits what archaeology calls the Bronze Age and Iron Age. Precious or useful, human skill can get at all of them.
- **"Man puts an end to darkness and searches out to the farthest limit" (v3).** Miners carried lamps into places no light had ever reached. "Searches out" is *chaqar*, a verb the lexicon defines as "search through, explore," and it lists this verse under its sense for mining. The same verb returns at the end of the poem: God "established it, and searched it out" (v27). The miner searches out ore. Only God has searched out wisdom.
- **"He opens shafts in a valley away from where anyone lives… they swing to and fro" (v4).** This is one of the hardest verses in Job to translate. The word the ESV renders "shafts" is *nachal*, which normally means a torrent or a wadi, a stream bed. The King James reads "the flood breaketh out," and JFB explains the verse as a stream suddenly bursting in on the miner. The ESV pictures miners lowered on ropes into shafts far from any town. Either way, the scene is isolation and danger. Henry: "Those that dig in the mines have their lives in their hands."
- **"Out of it comes bread, but underneath it is turned up as by fire" (v5).** The surface of the earth grows grain, while deep below, miners tear the rock apart. The Tyndale notes explain that ancient miners built fires against a rock face for long periods to crack it, and sometimes threw cold water on the heated stone to split it. JFB also says fire was used in mining. The ground that quietly feeds people above is violently overturned below.
- **"Its stones are the place of sapphires, and it has dust of gold" (v6).** The ESV footnote offers "lapis lazuli" for "sapphires," and the lexicon agrees that *sappir* may mean either. Lapis lazuli is a deep blue stone often flecked with gold-colored specks of pyrite, which may be the "dust of gold" here. JFB also takes it as lapis lazuli. The same word describes the pavement under God's feet in Exodus 24:10.
- **"That path no bird of prey knows… the lion has not passed over it" (vv7–8).** The falcon sees farther than any creature, and the lion goes wherever it likes. Neither has found the miner's path. Henry describes these roads as "untrodden by the lion's whelps, which traverse all the paths of the wilderness." Human daring goes where no creature's eye has been.
- **"Man puts his hand to the flinty rock and overturns mountains by the roots" (vv9–11).** The poem praises human engineering: breaking hard rock, cutting channels (JFB says these drained the water away), damming underground streams, and bringing "the thing that is hidden" into the light. Job once said God overturns mountains (9:5); here the miner seems to do it too. Guzik passes on Elmer Smick's observation that human cleverness achieves amazing technical feats and still cannot find wisdom on its own. The praise of vv1–11 is sincere, and it is also a setup.
- **"But where shall wisdom be found? And where is the place of understanding?" (v12).** Here the poem turns. "Wisdom" is *chokmah*. The lexicon shows its range: skill in craftsmanship (Exod 31:3), skill in war, shrewdness in government, and finally the ethical and religious wisdom of living rightly before God. The lexicon lists this verse under God's own wisdom, which no human search can find. "Understanding" is *binah*, insight or discernment. In Job's situation, wisdom is the knowledge of how God runs the world: why the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper, which is exactly what the friends claimed to know. Zophar had wished God would show Job "the secrets of wisdom" (11:6). The poem's answer: no one in the debate has it.
- **"Man does not know its worth, and it is not found in the land of the living" (v13).** "Worth" is *'erek*, a word for valuation or estimate, used of priests setting a price on an offering (Lev 5:15). The lexicon notes that many scholars follow the Greek Old Testament here and read *darkah*, "its way," a small difference in Hebrew letters. That reading would fit v23, "God understands the way to it." The ESV keeps the Hebrew text. Both readings make sense: humans neither know what wisdom is worth nor how to get to it.
- **"The deep says, 'It is not in me,' and the sea says, 'It is not with me'" (v14).** The poem lets the deepest parts of creation speak. The "deep" (*tehom*) is the word for the primal waters of Genesis 1:2. If wisdom is not under the earth with the gems, perhaps it lies at the bottom of the sea. It does not. Henry: "It can never be got either by trading on the waters or diving into them." JFB has no note on v14.
- **Wisdom cannot be bought (vv15–19).** Having shown that wisdom cannot be dug up, the poem shows it cannot be bought either. It is one of the fullest lists of riches in the Old Testament. Hebrew uses four different words for gold in these verses. *Segor* (v15) is so rare that the lexicon says it must mean fine gold, if the reading is correct. JFB notes that it is "Not the usual word for 'gold.'" *Ketem* (vv16, 19) is a poetic word, used for the famous gold of Ophir. Ophir was the source of Solomon's gold (1 Kgs 9:28), though no one knows where it was, and southern Arabia, East Africa and India have all been proposed. *Zahav* (v17) is ordinary gold, and *paz* (v17) is refined, pure gold. "Silver cannot be weighed as its price" (v15) reflects a world before coins, when payment meant weighing silver on scales (Gen 23:16), as JFB notes.
- **The jewels (vv16–19).** "Onyx" (*shoham*) is a stone the lexicon calls uncertain. It was found near Eden (Gen 2:12) and set on the high priest's ephod (Exod 28:9). "Glass" (*zekokith*, v17) appears only here. In the ancient world glass was rare and costly; the lexicon calls it "precious," yet of less value than wisdom. JFB suggests "vessels" rather than "jewels" in v17, and the lexicon supports him: the word is *keli*, "article, vessel," so "vessels of fine gold." "Crystal" (*gabish*, v18) also appears only here. JFB says it literally means "what is frozen," and the lexicon compares the Hebrew word for hail and the Greek word *krystallos*, which also meant ice. "Coral" is *ramoth*, and "pearls" is *peninim*, which the lexicon thinks may also be red coral. The King James renders it "rubies." It is the word Proverbs uses: wisdom "is more precious than jewels" (Prov 3:15; 8:11). "The topaz of Ethiopia" (v19) is *pitdah*, a yellow or greenish stone on the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:17), from Cush, the land south of Egypt along the upper Nile (modern Sudan more than modern Ethiopia). The list gathers the costliest things known and sets them all aside.
- **Wisdom's value in the rest of Scripture (vv15–19).** Proverbs repeats this teaching often: "How much better to get wisdom than gold!" (Prov 16:16), and "if you seek it like silver and search for it as for hidden treasures" (Prov 2:4). Proverbs uses the picture to urge the search; Job 28 shows its limit. Wisdom must be sought with all one's heart, but it is finally received as a gift. Henry: "Gold is another's, wisdom our own; gold is for the body and time, wisdom for the soul and eternity."
- **"From where, then, does wisdom come? And where is the place of understanding?" (v20).** The refrain of v12 returns, almost word for word. It divides the poem into three movements: what people can find (vv1–11), what they cannot buy (vv12–19), and who alone knows the way (vv20–28).
- **"It is hidden from the eyes of all living and concealed from the birds of the air" (v21).** The falcon's sharp eye from v7 returns. Birds see farther than anything living, but they cannot see this.
- **"Abaddon and Death say, 'We have heard a rumor of it with our ears'" (v22).** First the deep and the sea spoke (v14). Now the realm of the dead speaks. Abaddon means "Destruction." The lexicon says it occurs only five times, all in the wisdom books, and here it is paired with Death (*mavet*). Job has just said that Sheol and Abaddon lie open before God (26:6). The dead do slightly better than the living: JFB says they know "the report of her" but have not seen her. Henry imagines Death telling the seeker, "I will fetch thee shortly to a place where even this wisdom will be found."
- **"God understands the way to it, and he knows its place" (v23).** This is the turning point of the whole poem. "God" here is emphatic in the Hebrew word order: *God* understands the way. JFB: "God hath, and is Himself, wisdom." Job had said as much before: "With God are wisdom and might" (12:13).
- **"For he looks to the ends of the earth and sees everything under the heavens" (v24).** The miner sees "every precious thing" in his small tunnel (v10). God sees everything everywhere. The Tyndale notes observe that when God surveys the whole earth, he sees his wisdom built into what he has made, and that later God will show Job this very wisdom by taking him on a tour of creation (chs 38–41).
- **"When he gave to the wind its weight and apportioned the waters by measure" (vv25–26).** God's wisdom shows itself in creation's order. Wind seems weightless and free, yet God has set its force. Henry says God "gives it out by weight." Rain too follows his "decree" (v26). God will use nearly the same words when he speaks to Job: "Who has cleft a channel for the torrents of rain and a way for the thunderbolt?" (38:25), and "Who can number the clouds by wisdom?" (38:37). The poem quietly prepares Job for the whirlwind.
- **"Then he saw it and declared it; he established it, and searched it out" (v27).** Four verbs describe God's relationship to wisdom at creation. He saw it, declared it, established it, and searched it out. The last verb is *chaqar* again, the miner's word from v3. JFB warns that "established" (King James "prepared") means "not created, for wisdom is from everlasting." Here the poem comes close to Proverbs 8, where Wisdom herself speaks: "The LORD possessed me at the beginning of his work" (Prov 8:22). JFB goes further and says that Divine Wisdom is pictured here as a person, and that "The Almighty Word, Jesus Christ, we know now, is that Wisdom." Henry notes that some read it the same way, but he himself focuses on the wisdom of God's counsel. Most modern readers see poetry personifying God's wisdom rather than a direct portrait of the Son, while the New Testament makes the link to Christ explicit (1 Cor 1:24).
- **"And he said to man, 'Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom'" (v28).** After all the searching, the answer is given, not found. "He said" means this wisdom is revealed. "Man" is *adam*, and Henry notes that some read it as God's word to Adam on the day he was created. The deep wisdom by which God runs the universe stays with God. The wisdom human beings need is something else: to fear him. This is Henry's key distinction. One kind of wisdom is hidden in God, and the other is revealed to us. "Our enquiries into the former must be checked, into the latter quickened." He links the verse to Deuteronomy 29:29: "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us." "Fear" is *yir'ah*. The lexicon shows it ranges from terror to reverence and piety, and it lists this verse with the Proverbs sayings that the fear of the Lord is "the beginning of wisdom" (Prov 9:10; Ps 111:10). Here it is the awe and trust of a creature who knows who God is.
- **"The Lord" (v28).** The Tyndale notes, JFB and the lexicon all confirm that this is *Adonai*, "Lord" or "Master," the only place in Job where this name appears. Elsewhere the speakers almost always use *El*, *Eloah*, *Shaddai* ("the Almighty") or *Elohim*, while the narrator uses *Yahweh*, the LORD. JFB notes that *Adonai* is often applied to the Messiah, and he finds it fitting here, where the subject is the Word or Wisdom "by whom the world was made." Many scholars take the unusual name as evidence for the narrator view.
- **"And to turn away from evil is understanding" (v28).** This is the final surprise, and it reaches back to the very first verse of the book. Job was "blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil" (1:1), and God described him with the same words to Satan (1:8). The two phrases of v28, fearing the Lord and turning from evil, are exactly the two things said of Job at the start. So the wisdom no miner can dig up and no fortune can buy is something Job already had before his suffering began. The friends thought wisdom meant knowing why things happen. The poem says it means fearing God and turning from evil when no explanation comes. Job lacks the answers, but he has wisdom. Henry: "We must first cease to do evil, or we shall never learn to do well."
- **Commentators:** All three read the poem as Job's words. Henry and JFB both connect it to the debate. JFB says that in chapter 27 Job admitted the friends were often right that the wicked are punished, "but still the affliction of the godly remained unexplained," and chapter 28 explains why: the divine wisdom that governs such things is beyond human reach. Henry says Job answers Zophar's demand for "the secrets of wisdom" (11:6) with Deuteronomy 29:29, and he adds a pastoral word on the debate itself: "When disputes grow hot, better lose the question than lose our temper." He also turns the miners into a lesson: "Go to the miners then, thou sluggard in religion." Guzik is the most open about the speaker question. He reports Andersen's view that most scholars cannot imagine Job reciting this poem, then sides with Job as speaker. He reasons that because wisdom comes by revelation, not effort, a right relationship with God is the only way to it. JFB is the most helpful on details, and his notes on *segor*, "vessels" (v17) and "what is frozen" (v18) hold up against the lexicon. He goes beyond Henry and Guzik in identifying the personified Wisdom of vv12–27 with Christ. JFB has no note on v14. Guzik makes no Hebrew word claims in this chapter.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job 28 asks where wisdom is and answers that only God knows the way to it. The poem never says that God himself would come and show the way. The New Testament does. Paul calls Christ "the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24), and says that Christ Jesus "became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption" (1 Cor 1:30). The language of hidden treasure in Job 28 comes back almost exactly in Colossians, which speaks of Christ, "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3). The poem's search through mines, oceans and death ends in a person, not a place. JFB's identification goes further than the poem itself says, but the movement is real: the wisdom that was with God at creation (Prov 8:22) is the Word through whom "all things were made" (John 1:3), and that Word became flesh. Job did not get the explanation he wanted, and neither do believers in every trial. What they are given is what Job 28:28 offers in seed form: to know and fear the Lord, and to turn from evil, now through the Son who is wisdom in person.
