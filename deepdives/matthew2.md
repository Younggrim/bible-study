*Setting:* Bethlehem and Jerusalem, around 6–4 BC, in the last years of Herod the Great. Chapter 1 showed that Jesus is the son of David and the son of Abraham, conceived by the Holy Spirit and named "God with us." Chapter 2 shows how the world receives him. Gentile scholars travel hundreds of miles to worship him. The king of the Jews tries to kill him. The religious experts know exactly where he was to be born and do not go to see. The chapter is built around four places, each tied to an Old Testament text: Bethlehem (Micah), Egypt (Hosea), Ramah (Jeremiah) and Nazareth ("the prophets"). Again and again Matthew shows Jesus reliving the story of Israel and fulfilling its Scriptures.

#### The Visit of the Wise Men (vv1–12)
- **"After Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea in the days of Herod the king" (v1).** "Of Judea" distinguishes it from a Bethlehem in Galilee. Herod, an Idumean (Edomite) whose family had been forced to convert to Judaism, was made "king of the Jews" by the Roman Senate in 40 BC and ruled from 37 BC until his death in 4 BC. He was a brilliant builder: the vast temple renovation, the port of Caesarea, the fortresses of Masada and the Herodium (visible from Bethlehem). He was also ruthlessly suspicious. He executed his favorite wife Mariamne, her mother, and three of his own sons.
- **"Wise men from the east" (v1).** The Greek is *magoi*, from a Persian word for a priestly caste who studied the stars and interpreted dreams. The same word is used of the "magicians" in Babylon in the Greek Old Testament of Daniel, where Daniel himself was made chief over them (Dan 2:48; 5:11). That may explain how men from the east knew of a coming Jewish king: Jews had lived in Babylon and Persia since the exile, and Daniel's writings and the hope of a Messiah were known there. They came from Persia, Babylon or Arabia. The text does not say they were kings, or that there were three, or give their names. Those traditions came later, the number three from the three gifts and the idea of kings from Psalm 72:10–11 and Isaiah 60:3.
- **"Where is he who has been born king of the Jews?" (v2).** To Herod this was a dangerous question. He was "king of the Jews" by appointment. This child was *born* to it. The same title appears next over Jesus' cross (27:37). Matthew's Gospel opens and closes with Gentiles recognizing Jesus: the magi here and the centurion at the cross (27:54).
- **"We saw his star when it rose" (v2).** The phrase can mean "in the east" (KJV) or "at its rising." Balaam, another wise man from the east, had prophesied: "a star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel" (Num 24:17). Suggested explanations include a conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 7 BC, a comet, or a nova. But this star "went before them" and "came to rest over the place" (v9), which no ordinary star does. Matthew presents it as God's sign. God met astrologers through the stars they watched, and then led them past the stars to Scripture and to Christ.
- **"Have come to worship him" (v2).** "Worship" (*proskyneō*) means to bow down in homage. Matthew uses it of Jesus more than any other Gospel (2:11; 8:2; 14:33; 28:9, 17).
- **"He was troubled, and all Jerusalem with him" (v3).** Herod was about seventy and dying. A rival king, even a baby, was a threat. Jerusalem was troubled because a troubled Herod meant bloodshed.
- **"He inquired of them where the Christ was to be born" (v4).** Herod connects "king of the Jews" with "the Christ" at once. The chief priests (the high priest and leading priestly families) and the scribes (experts in the law) knew the answer instantly.
- **"In Bethlehem of Judea, for so it is written by the prophet" (vv5–6).** The quotation combines Micah 5:2 and 2 Samuel 5:2. Micah said Bethlehem was "too little to be among the clans of Judah." Matthew's form says Bethlehem is "by no means least," because the Ruler has now come from it. "Who will shepherd my people Israel" is God's word to David (2 Sam 5:2). The Messiah is a king who shepherds. Micah goes on to say his "coming forth is from of old, from ancient days" (Mic 5:2).
- **The scribes did not go.** They knew the text and the place, five or six miles away, and stayed in Jerusalem. The Gentiles who knew far less traveled hundreds of miles. Knowing the Bible is not the same as seeking the Lord of the Bible.
- **"Herod summoned the wise men secretly and ascertained from them what time the star had appeared" (v7).** He was already planning. The timing would set the age limit in v16.
- **"That I too may come and worship him" (v8).** The lie is chilling. Herod uses the language of worship to cover murder.
- **"The star… went before them until it came to rest over the place where the child was" (v9).** Bethlehem lies south of Jerusalem. Whatever the star was, it guided them to a particular house.
- **"They rejoiced exceedingly with great joy" (v10).** Matthew piles up words in Greek: "they rejoiced a joy great exceedingly." Contrast "troubled" in v3.
- **"Going into the house, they saw the child with Mary his mother" (v11).** A house, not a stable, and a "child" (*paidion*), not a newborn (*brephos*, Luke 2:12). The magi probably arrived some weeks or months after the birth, after the presentation in the temple (Luke 2:22–39). Herod's "two years old or under" (v16) suggests the star had appeared up to two years before. Christmas scenes that put the shepherds and magi together compress the story. Notice also "the child with Mary his mother": Joseph is not mentioned, and the child comes first, as throughout the chapter (vv13, 14, 20, 21).
- **"They fell down and worshiped him" (v11).** They worship the child, not his mother.
- **"Gold and frankincense and myrrh" (v11).** Gold was the gift for a king. Frankincense was a fragrant resin burned in worship (Exod 30:34). Myrrh was a costly resin used in perfume, anointing oil and burial (John 19:39). Since the early church, Christians have read them as gifts for a king, for God and for one who would die. That is a fitting reflection, though the magi may simply have been bringing the most precious goods of their homeland, as Isaiah foresaw: "They shall bring gold and frankincense, and shall bring good news, the praises of the LORD" (Isa 60:6). The gifts may also have paid for the journey to Egypt.
- **"Being warned in a dream not to return to Herod" (v12).** God guides Gentiles by dreams too. "By another way" is simply the road home that avoided Jerusalem.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the magi "of the learned class who cultivated astrology," admits that nothing definite is known of them, and suggests the star may have been "a luminous meteor, which appeared under special laws and for a special purpose." JFB also calls the symbolic readings of the gifts "precarious suppositions." Guzik also doubts the magi intended the symbolism, and notes that the chief priests and scribes knew the answer but had no interest in going. Henry: "Those who live at the greatest distance from the means of grace often use most diligence," and he thinks "Providence sent these as a seasonable relief to Joseph and Mary in their present poor condition."

#### The Flight to Egypt (vv13–15)
- **"Rise, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt" (v13).** Egypt was outside Herod's reach and had large Jewish communities, especially in Alexandria, perhaps a million Jews by this time. The journey was roughly 75 miles to the border and further to any Jewish settlement. It was a long, dangerous trip for a young family with a small child.
- **"He rose and took the child and his mother by night" (v14).** Joseph obeys at once, that same night. As in 1:24, he says nothing. He simply does what God says.
- **"Remained there until the death of Herod" (v15).** Herod died in the spring of 4 BC, painfully, after ordering the execution of yet another son just days before. Josephus records that he ordered prominent men arrested so they could be killed when he died, to make sure the nation mourned. The order was not carried out. The family's stay in Egypt was probably months, not years.
- **"Out of Egypt I called my son" (v15).** This is Hosea 11:1. In Hosea it is not a prediction but a memory: "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son." It looks back to the exodus, when God called Israel "my firstborn son" (Exod 4:22). Matthew is not misreading Hosea. He sees a pattern. Israel, God's son, went down to Egypt and was brought out. Jesus, God's Son, goes down to Egypt and is brought out. Where Israel failed (Hosea's next verses tell of their idolatry), Jesus will be the faithful Son. Chapter 4 continues the pattern: water, then forty days in the wilderness. This kind of reading, where events in Israel's history foreshadow the Messiah, is called typology.
- **A second Moses.** Pharaoh ordered Hebrew baby boys killed, and Moses was saved (Exod 1–2). Herod orders Bethlehem's boys killed, and Jesus is saved. Later God told Moses, "all the men who were seeking your life are dead" (Exod 4:19). The angel tells Joseph, "those who sought the child's life are dead" (v20). Matthew's Jewish readers would not have missed it.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Egypt, once the place of Israel's bondage, becomes the Savior's refuge: "God, when he pleases, can make the worst of places serve the best of purposes." JFB points out that Matthew follows the Hebrew of Hosea, since the Septuagint reads "his children," and reads Christ's return from Egypt as the deeper fulfilment of what was said of Israel. Guzik draws the same parallel between Israel as a nation and Jesus as the true Son.

#### Herod Kills the Children (vv16–18)
- **"He had been tricked by the wise men" (v16).** The verb (*empaizō*) means "mocked" or "made a fool of." Herod's pride is wounded.
- **"Killed all the male children in Bethlehem and in all that region who were two years old or under" (v16).** Bethlehem was a village of perhaps a thousand people or fewer. Scholars estimate that a dozen to two or three dozen boys died. No other ancient writer mentions it, but that is not surprising: Josephus records far larger atrocities by Herod, and a small village massacre would hardly register. It fits everything known of the man. Augustus is said to have joked that it was better to be Herod's pig than his son, since a Jewish king would not eat pork. The joke works in Greek, where "pig" (*hys*) and "son" (*huios*) sound alike.
- **"Then was fulfilled what was spoken by the prophet Jeremiah" (v17).** Notice that Matthew does not say "this took place *in order to* fulfill," as elsewhere (1:22; 2:15). He does not say God planned the murders. He says the prophecy was fulfilled in them.
- **"A voice was heard in Ramah… Rachel weeping for her children" (v18).** This is Jeremiah 31:15. Rachel was Jacob's beloved wife, who died in childbirth near Bethlehem (Gen 35:16–20). Ramah, about five miles north of Jerusalem, was where Babylon gathered the captives of Judah for deportation (Jer 40:1). Jeremiah pictures Rachel, mother of the nation, weeping from her grave as her children are led away. Now she weeps again, for the children of Bethlehem.
- **The hope that follows.** Matthew's readers knew the rest of Jeremiah 31. The next verse says: "Keep your voice from weeping… there is hope for your future" (Jer 31:16–17), and the chapter goes on to promise the new covenant (Jer 31:31–34). The weeping of Ramah was not the last word then, and it is not now. The child who escaped would come back and pour out his blood for the new covenant (26:28).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this the product of "unbridled wrath, armed with an unlawful power," and sees the children as early martyrs for Christ. JFB argues that Bethlehem was small and the number of children few, which helps explain why Josephus does not mention it. JFB says Jeremiah's words "undoubtedly relate to the Babylonish captivity" and are applied here by a deeper fulfilment. Guzik makes the same point and repeats the pig-and-son saying.

#### The Return to Nazareth (vv19–23)
- **"When Herod died" (v19).** The great king is gone. The child is alive.
- **"Go to the land of Israel" (v20).** "The land of Israel" appears only here in the New Testament. It recalls God bringing his people into the promised land.
- **"Archelaus was reigning over Judea in place of his father Herod" (v22).** Herod's kingdom was divided among three sons. Archelaus received Judea, Samaria and Idumea, with the lesser title "ethnarch." He began his reign by having about three thousand people killed in the temple courts at Passover. He was so brutal that a delegation of Jews and Samaritans complained to Rome, and Augustus deposed and banished him in AD 6. Judea then came under Roman governors, of whom Pontius Pilate was the fifth. Jesus' parable of a nobleman who went to a far country to receive a kingdom, whose citizens sent word, "We do not want this man to reign over us," may recall Archelaus (Luke 19:12–14).
- **"He withdrew to the district of Galilee" (v22).** Galilee was ruled by another son, Herod Antipas, who later beheaded John the Baptist (14:1–12). For now he posed no threat. Luke tells us that Nazareth had been Joseph and Mary's home before (Luke 2:4, 39). Matthew emphasizes God's guidance, Luke the family's history. The two fit together.
- **"He would be called a Nazarene" (v23).** No Old Testament verse says this, and Nazareth is never mentioned in the Old Testament. Notice that Matthew says "the prophets" (plural) and does not use his usual "saying." He may be summarizing a theme rather than quoting a verse. Two main links are offered. One is the Hebrew *netser*, "branch," in Isaiah 11:1: "There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse, and a branch (*netser*) from his roots shall bear fruit." The name Nazareth probably comes from the same root. Other prophets also call the Messiah "the Branch," though with a different Hebrew word (Jer 23:5; Zech 3:8; 6:12). The other link is that Nazareth was obscure and looked down on (John 1:46), so to be "a Nazarene" was to be despised, fitting the prophets' picture of a rejected Messiah (Isa 53:3; Ps 22:6). Both may be in view. A link with the "Nazirite" vow (Judg 13:5) is less likely, since Jesus was not a Nazirite (11:19).
- **"Nazarene" later.** Jesus was called "Jesus of Nazareth" all his life, and the sign on his cross read "Jesus of Nazareth" (John 19:19). Early Christians were called "the sect of the Nazarenes" (Acts 24:5), and the Hebrew word for Christians today is still *Notzrim*.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the link with *netzer* "the best explanation," the word for "the small twig, sprout, or sucker," and notes that Nazareth appears in neither the Old Testament nor Josephus, which fits the lowly sense of the name. JFB also explains that Augustus gave Archelaus only the title of ethnarch and banished him after ten years. Guzik weighs the Nazirite and Branch suggestions and leans toward the view that Matthew is summing up the prophets' theme of a humble, rejected Messiah. Henry says Egypt "may serve to sojourn in, or take shelter in, for awhile, but not to abide in," and that sharing the reproach of Nazareth is part of following Christ.

#### Pointing to Christ
Matthew 2 shows the King of the Jews worshiped by Gentiles and hunted by his own king. In his first years Jesus relives Israel's story, down into Egypt and back out, sheltered from a murderous ruler like Moses, and settling in a despised town as the Branch from Jesse's stump. The world's power tries to destroy him and fails. His own people's experts know where he is and do not come. Strangers from far away bring their treasures and fall down before him. The chapter asks the question every reader must answer: will we be like Herod, the scribes, or the magi?
