*Setting:* About AD 29, on the journey to Jerusalem. Chapter 11 ended at a Pharisee's dinner with six woes, after which the scribes and Pharisees began "lying in wait for him" (11:54). Chapter 12 opens with a crowd so huge that people trample each other. Jesus turns first to his disciples, then to the crowd, then back again. The chapter is a long string of teaching held together by one question: what should God's people fear, and what should they trust? Jesus warns against hypocrisy, the fear of people, greed and worry, and calls for courage, generosity and readiness for his return. He ends with a warning to the crowds to read the times and settle with God before it is too late.

#### Beware of the Leaven of the Pharisees (vv1–3)
- **"So many thousands… that they were trampling one another" (v1).** Literally "myriads," tens of thousands. Popularity is at its height just as official hostility hardens. Jesus speaks "to his disciples first," within earshot of all.
- **"The leaven of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy" (v1).** Leaven was a bit of old fermented dough worked into new dough. A little spreads through the whole batch, which is why the Bible often uses it for corruption that spreads (Exod 12:15; 1 Cor 5:6–8). "Hypocrisy" (*hypokrisis*) comes from the word for an actor playing a role. The disciples are not immune. Hypocrisy is a danger for anyone admired by a crowd.
- **"Nothing is covered up that will not be revealed" (v2).** God sees every heart, and one day everything will be brought into the open (Eccl 12:14; 1 Cor 4:5). Pretending is pointless.
- **"Proclaimed on the housetops" (v3).** Flat roofs were where announcements were shouted to a village. What the disciples say privately now will one day be proclaimed openly. This is both a warning about secret sin and an encouragement: the gospel taught in small rooms will be preached to the world.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The day is coming when hypocrites will be stripped of their fig-leaves." JFB notes that Jesus had been "speaking out more plainly than ever before," and puts v2 bluntly: "'Tis no use concealing anything, for all will one day come out." Guzik observes that the pull toward hypocrisy is strongest when popularity is greatest, as it was here.

#### Have No Fear (vv4–7)
- **"I tell you, my friends" (v4).** This is the only place in the first three Gospels where Jesus calls his disciples "friends" (compare John 15:14–15). The tender word comes right before a warning about martyrdom.
- **"Do not fear those who kill the body" (v4).** Persecutors can take life, and many of these disciples would die for their faith. But that is the limit of what they can do.
- **"Fear him who, after he has killed, has authority to cast into hell" (v5).** "Hell" is *Gehenna*, from the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem. There, in the days of the kings, children were sacrificed to the god Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31–32). It became a picture of final judgment. Jesus speaks of God, not Satan, as the one with authority over final judgment. The right fear of God drives out the fear of people (Isa 8:12–13).
- **"Five sparrows sold for two pennies" (v6).** Sparrows were the cheapest birds in the market, food for the poor. Matthew 10:29 has two for a penny. Luke's five for two suggests that if a buyer took four, a fifth was thrown in free. Even that extra bird "is not forgotten before God."
- **"Even the hairs of your head are all numbered" (v7).** God's care goes down to the smallest detail. "Fear not" follows straight after "fear him." The one to be feared is also the Father who counts every hair.
- **Commentators:** JFB on v4: "My friends, there their power ends," and on the repeated "fear him," "how striking the repetition here!" It notes Jesus says "more value than many sparrows," not "than millions of sparrows," an example of his plain speech. Guzik gives the background of Gehenna and tells the story of the English martyr Rowland Taylor, who went calmly to the stake in 1555.

#### Acknowledge Christ Before Men (vv8–12)
- **"Everyone who acknowledges me before men" (v8).** "Acknowledge" (*homologeō*) means to say the same thing, to agree openly, to confess. Public allegiance to Jesus on earth will be matched by his acknowledgment in God's court, "before the angels of God." The Son of Man is pictured as an advocate in the heavenly court (Dan 7:9–14; Acts 7:56).
- **"The one who denies me before men will be denied" (v9).** Peter would deny Jesus three times (22:54–62) and be restored (John 21:15–19). The warning is about a settled refusal to own Jesus, not one failure repented of.
- **"The one who blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven" (v10).** Speaking against the Son of Man, as many did before they understood who he was, can be forgiven. Paul had once been a "blasphemer" (1 Tim 1:13). Blasphemy against the Spirit is a persistent, hardened refusal of the Spirit's testimony to Jesus, calling God's work evil, as some did in 11:15. It is unforgivable not because God's mercy is too small but because it rejects the only way of forgiveness. Those who fear they have committed it show by that fear that they have not.
- **"Before the synagogues and the rulers and the authorities" (v11).** Acts shows exactly this: apostles before the Sanhedrin (Acts 4–5) and Paul before governors and a king (Acts 24–26).
- **"Do not be anxious about how you should defend yourself" (v11).** "Defend" is *apologeomai*, to make a defense, the root of "apologetics." The point is not that preparation is wrong (1 Pet 3:15), but that the Spirit gives the words when it counts (Acts 4:8; 6:10).
- **Commentators:** JFB says that for those who confess him, "the Lord holds Himself bound to repay it in kind." Guzik treats blasphemy against the Spirit as a settled rejection of Christ, not a single lapse, and connects *apologeomai* to apologetics. The Tyndale notes add that Acts records both kinds of persecution, Jewish and Gentile.

#### The Parable of the Rich Fool (vv13–21)
- **"Teacher, tell my brother to divide the inheritance with me" (v13).** Rabbis were often asked to settle legal disputes. Under the law, the oldest son received a double share (Deut 21:17). This man wants Jesus on his side. He has just heard about martyrdom and the judgment, and all he can think about is his share of the estate.
- **"Man, who made me a judge or arbitrator over you?" (v14).** The words echo the question thrown at Moses: "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (Exod 2:14). Jesus refuses to be used as a tool in a family quarrel. He came to deal with the heart behind it.
- **"Be on your guard against all covetousness" (v15).** "Covetousness" (*pleonexia*) is literally "having more," the craving for more. "All" means every form of it. The tenth commandment forbids it (Exod 20:17), and Paul calls it idolatry (Col 3:5).
- **"One's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (v15).** Life is a gift from God, not a sum of things owned. A person may have much and still miss life.
- **"The land of a rich man produced plentifully" (v16).** The harvest came from God's sun and rain. The man did nothing wrong in farming well. What follows is what he does with the blessing.
- **"What shall I do?… I will… I will… my crops… my barns… my grain… my goods… my soul" (vv17–19).** The man talks to himself, and the words "I" and "my" fill his speech. He never mentions God, and he never thinks of the hungry. Storing grain for years in a land where many lived on the edge was a choice to keep it for himself.
- **"Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry" (v19).** The phrase was proverbial (Eccl 8:15; Isa 22:13: "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die"). He thinks his soul can be fed with grain.
- **"Fool! This night your soul is required of you" (v20).** Here God himself speaks in the story, which is rare in Jesus' parables. In Scripture a "fool" is not someone unintelligent but someone who lives as if there were no God (Ps 14:1). "Required" was used for calling in a loan: his life was only on loan, and the lender wants it back. He planned for "many years" and had one night.
- **"Whose will they be?" (v20).** The man who sought to secure his inheritance (v13) now hears that the things he hoarded will go to someone else (Ps 39:6; Eccl 2:18–21).
- **"Rich toward God" (v21).** The parable does not condemn wealth or saving. It condemns laying up treasure "for himself" while being poor toward God. The next section explains what it means to be rich toward God: generosity, trust and seeking his kingdom (vv31–34).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the request an inopportune intrusion and says v14 is "a question literally repudiating the office which Moses assumed." It says the rich man took "a life of secure and abundant earthly enjoyment" as the summit of happiness, and that he "lives and dies a beggar!" toward God. Guzik suggests the man's demand for fairness may have been greed in disguise, and cites John Wesley's rule to earn all you can, save all you can and give all you can. Henry draws the lesson that a man's life does not lie in what he owns.

#### Do Not Be Anxious (vv22–34)
- **"Therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life" (v22).** "Therefore" links worry to greed. Both come from trusting possessions instead of God. One grasps for more, and the other frets about having enough. The verb *merimnaō* means to be pulled apart, divided in mind.
- **"Consider the ravens" (v24).** Matthew 6:26 says "birds of the air." Luke names ravens, which were unclean under the law (Lev 11:15). Even these God feeds (Job 38:41; Ps 147:9). They do not store up barns, unlike the rich fool, and God still provides.
- **"Add a single hour to his span of life" (v25).** The phrase can mean add a cubit (about eighteen inches) to one's height or one's span of life. Either way the point is that worry achieves nothing.
- **"Consider the lilies… even Solomon in all his glory" (v27).** The word may cover the wildflowers of Galilee generally, such as anemones that color the hillsides each spring. Solomon's splendor was famous (1 Kings 10:4–7). God clothes flowers more beautifully than a king.
- **"Thrown into the oven" (v28).** In a land with little wood, dried grass and stalks were used to heat clay bread ovens. Something here today and fuel tomorrow, God still clothes with beauty.
- **"O you of little faith" (v28).** Worry is not just a feeling. It shows a small view of the Father.
- **"Do not seek what you are to eat… nor be worried" (v29).** "Worried" (*meteōrizomai*) appears only here in the New Testament. It comes from a word for something hanging in midair, like a ship tossed on the waves.
- **"All the nations of the world seek after these things, and your Father knows" (v30).** "The nations" means those who do not know God. Their anxiety makes sense if there is no Father in heaven. Believers have one.
- **"Seek his kingdom, and these things will be added" (v31).** The kingdom comes first. Food and clothes are "added," given along the way.
- **"Fear not, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom" (v32).** One of the tenderest lines in Luke. The disciples are few and vulnerable, but God is their shepherd (Ps 23:1; Isa 40:11). The kingdom is not something they must strive to earn. The Father delights to give it.
- **"Sell your possessions, and give to the needy" (v33).** This is not a rule that every believer must sell everything. Elsewhere disciples keep homes and goods (10:38; 19:8–9). It calls for an open hand. The earliest church took it seriously (Acts 2:44–45; 4:34–35).
- **"Moneybags that do not grow old… where no thief approaches and no moth destroys" (v33).** Wealth was often stored as fine clothing, which moths could ruin, or in coins that thieves could dig out of a mud-brick wall. Treasure in heaven cannot be lost.
- **"Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" (v34).** The heart follows the money. What people invest in is what they come to love.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the "worried" of v29 as an "unsettled mind; put off your balance," and calls the "little flock" of v32 "a sublime and touching contrast" to all the striving of the world. Henry urges believers to "sit loose to this world." Guzik links worry and greed as twin failures of trust, notes that birds still work even though they do not worry, and stresses that seeking the kingdom shapes everything, not one item on a list.

#### You Must Be Ready (vv35–48)
- **"Stay dressed for action and keep your lamps burning" (v35).** Literally "let your loins be girded." Long robes were tucked up into the belt for work or travel, as Israel ate the first Passover ready to leave (Exod 12:11). A burning lamp means servants still awake at night.
- **"Waiting for their master to come home from the wedding feast" (v36).** A wedding feast could last for days, and no one knew when the master would leave. Faithful servants stay ready to open at the first knock.
- **"He will dress himself for service and have them recline at table, and he will come and serve them" (v37).** Here the story turns upside down. No master served his slaves. But this Master will. Jesus did exactly this at the Last Supper (22:27, "I am among you as the one who serves"; John 13:4–5), and the promise points to the messianic banquet (Isa 25:6; Rev 19:9).
- **"In the second watch, or in the third" (v38).** Jews often divided the night into three watches, Romans into four. The second and third watches covered roughly 10 p.m. to 6 a.m., the hours when servants would most want to sleep.
- **"If the master of the house had known at what hour the thief was coming" (v39).** The picture changes from a welcome master to an unwelcome thief, to stress the surprise. Paul and Peter both use it of the Lord's return (1 Thess 5:2; 2 Pet 3:10).
- **"Lord, are you telling this parable for us or for all?" (v41).** Peter's question is not answered directly. Jesus answers by describing the special responsibility of those placed over others.
- **"The faithful and wise manager… to give them their portion of food at the proper time" (v42).** Wealthy households often put a trusted slave in charge of the other servants and the supplies. The application is to leaders in the church, who feed God's people (Acts 20:28; 1 Pet 5:2–3).
- **"My master is delayed in coming" (v45).** The danger is not denying the master's return but assuming there is plenty of time. Then the manager abuses others and indulges himself.
- **"Cut him in pieces and put him with the unfaithful" (v46).** Shocking language, but such punishments were known in the ancient world. The point is the severity of judgment on a leader who betrays his trust. Interpreters differ on whether this pictures a false servant who was never truly the master's, or the loss facing an unfaithful believer.
- **"A severe beating… a light beating" (vv47–48).** Punishment is in proportion to knowledge. The law already distinguished sins done "with a high hand" from unintentional ones (Num 15:27–31). Ignorance lessens guilt but does not erase it.
- **"Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required" (v48).** This is Luke's summary of stewardship. Gifts, knowledge, time and money all come with responsibility.
- **Commentators:** JFB on v37 quotes Bengel's picture of "the Bridegroom entertain his friends (nay, servants)," and on v42 says "fidelity is the first requisite in a servant." It notes that the unfaithful servant acts "in the confidence that his Lord's return will not be speedy," and says the degrees of punishment match the knowledge sinned against. Guzik applies vv41–48 especially to leaders. Henry stresses both watchfulness and the rule that much is required where much is given.

#### Not Peace, but Division (vv49–53)
- **"I came to cast fire on the earth, and would that it were already kindled!" (v49).** Fire in Scripture can mean judgment or purifying. John the Baptist said Jesus would baptize "with the Holy Spirit and fire" (3:16), and at Pentecost tongues of fire appear (Acts 2:3). Jesus longs for the work he came to start.
- **"I have a baptism to be baptized with, and how great is my distress until it is accomplished!" (v50).** "Baptism" means to be overwhelmed, plunged under. The Old Testament pictures suffering as waters closing over a person (Ps 42:7; 69:1–2). Jesus means his death (Mark 10:38). "Distress" (*synechō*) means to be pressed in, held tight. He is not calm about the cross. He feels its weight already.
- **"Do you think that I have come to give peace on earth? No… but rather division" (v51).** The angels sang "on earth peace" (2:14), and Jesus does bring peace with God. But his coming forces a choice, and choices divide.
- **"Father against son… mother-in-law against her daughter-in-law" (v53).** This is from Micah 7:6, where the prophet describes the breakdown of loyalties in a corrupt time. Jewish teachers later linked that verse with the troubles before the Messiah's coming. Many early believers found that following Jesus cost them their families.
- **Commentators:** JFB quotes Olshausen's view that the fire is "the higher spiritual element of life which Jesus came to introduce," and on v50, "what a pressure of spirit is upon Me." It says Jesus brings "the reverse of peace, in the first instance." Guzik lists the main views of the fire, judgment, the Spirit and the spread of the gospel. The Tyndale notes connect the "baptism" with the Old Testament picture of overwhelming floods.

#### Interpreting the Time (vv54–59)
- **"A cloud rising in the west… the south wind blowing" (vv54–55).** In Israel, clouds from the west come off the Mediterranean and bring rain (1 Kings 18:44). A south wind blows from the desert and brings scorching heat. Farmers read the sky with skill.
- **"You hypocrites!… why do you not know how to interpret the present time?" (v56).** "Time" is *kairos*, a decisive moment. They can read the weather but not the arrival of the Messiah, though the signs are all around them: the healings, the preaching to the poor, the kingdom breaking in (7:22).
- **"Make an effort to settle with him on the way" (v58).** A debtor on the way to court still has a chance to settle. Once before the judge, it is too late, and the officer hands him over to prison. In the Roman world, a debtor could be jailed until the debt was paid.
- **"Until you have paid the very last penny" (v59).** The "penny" (*lepton*) was the smallest coin in circulation, the coin the poor widow gave (21:2). The lesson is urgency: reconcile with God now, while the way is open. Roman Catholic tradition has sometimes read this verse as pointing to purgatory, though most interpreters, including many Catholics, take it in context as a warning to settle accounts with God before judgment.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls v54 "a word of special warning to the thoughtless crowd," says they were "unable to perceive what a critical period that was," and that their own conscience should be "enough to show them who He was." Henry urges making peace with God in time. Guzik reads the court scene as a call to be reconciled to God before judgment.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 12 shows the Son of Man as the one whose verdict finally matters, who will acknowledge before the angels those who acknowledged him. He is the shepherd of a little flock, telling them their Father counts their hairs and delights to give them the kingdom. He is the master who comes home from the wedding and puts on an apron to serve his servants, as he did on the night he was betrayed. And he is the one who had a baptism to undergo, a flood of suffering he felt pressing on him all the way to Jerusalem. The rich fool tried to secure his soul with barns. The disciples' souls are secured by a Savior who went under the waters for them, so that there need be no last penny left to pay.
