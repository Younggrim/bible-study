*Setting:* Directly after David and Jonathan parted in the field, kissing and weeping, with Jonathan's parting words, "Go in peace" (20:41–42). David is now a fugitive with no home, no position and no protection. He has lost his post at court, his wife Michal (19:11–17), his mentor Samuel (19:18) and now his closest friend. This chapter shows him on the run for the first time, and it is not his finest hour. In two short scenes David lies to a priest at Nob and then plays the madman before a Philistine king. Both times God protects him, but the first lie will cost innocent people their lives in the next chapter (22:18–22). Two psalms are linked by their titles to these days, Psalm 34 and Psalm 56.

#### David and the Holy Bread (vv1–9)
- **"Then David came to Nob, to Ahimelech the priest" (v1).** Nob sat on a hill just north of Jerusalem, a short distance from Saul's home at Gibeah. After the Philistines destroyed Shiloh (Jer 7:12), the priests seem to have moved the tabernacle and its furniture here, which is why the city is later called "the city of the priests" (22:19). Ahimelech was a great-grandson of Eli (14:3; 22:9, 20), from the family God had said would be cut down (2:31–33). He is probably the same man as "Ahijah" in 14:3, since the names are close.
- **"Ahimelech came to meet David, trembling" (v1).** A famous commander turning up alone and unannounced was alarming. The elders of Bethlehem trembled the same way when Samuel arrived unexpectedly (16:4). Anyone caught helping a man out of favor with Saul could pay with his life, and that is exactly what happens.
- **"The king has charged me with a matter" (v2).** This is a plain lie. David says he is on a secret royal mission, with soldiers waiting at "such and such a place." He may have meant to spare Ahimelech from knowing the truth, but the lie leaves the priest with no chance to choose with his eyes open. David later owns the result: "I have occasioned the death of all the persons of your father's house" (22:22). Scripture records the lie without endorsing it.
- **"Give me five loaves of bread, or whatever is here" (v3).** David has fled with nothing. He is hungry, and he says he has men to feed.
- **"I have no common bread on hand, but there is holy bread" (v4).** This was the bread of the Presence, twelve loaves set out on a golden table in the Holy Place before the LORD and replaced fresh every Sabbath (Exod 25:30; Lev 24:5–9). The old loaves were for Aaron and his sons to eat "in a holy place." Ahimelech is willing to stretch the rule, but only if the men are ceremonially clean, meaning they have not had sexual relations recently (Exod 19:15; Lev 15:18).
- **"The vessels of the young men are holy" (v5).** "Vessels" probably means their gear and packs, though some take it as a polite word for their bodies. David says that on campaign his men always keep themselves from women, which matches the rules for holy war (Deut 23:9–11; 2 Sam 11:11). Whether there were any young men at all is unclear. Jesus speaks as though there were (Mark 2:25–26).
- **"So the priest gave him the holy bread" (v6).** The note that the bread had just been "removed from before the LORD" and replaced with hot bread suggests it was the Sabbath, the day the loaves were changed. Jesus uses this scene to answer the Pharisees who complained that his hungry disciples picked grain on the Sabbath (Matt 12:1–8; Mark 2:23–28; Luke 6:1–5). If the priest could give holy bread to hungry men, then God's laws were never meant to be used against human need. Jesus adds, "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hos 6:6), and "the Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath."
- **"In the time of Abiathar the high priest" (Mark 2:26).** Mark names Abiathar, while 1 Samuel names his father Ahimelech. Abiathar was at Nob and soon became David's priest, the better-known name of the two. Mark's phrase can mean "in the passage about Abiathar" or "in Abiathar's days," and some think the two names were used loosely in the family (compare 2 Sam 8:17, where they appear reversed). Interpreters offer these explanations. None of them changes Jesus' point.
- **"Doeg the Edomite, the chief of Saul's herdsmen" (v7).** One sentence drops a shadow over the scene. Doeg was a foreigner from Edom, Israel's old rival descended from Esau, and a senior servant of Saul. He was "detained before the LORD," perhaps to fulfill a vow, to wait out the Sabbath, or for some ritual purification. Whatever the reason, he was at the house of God, and he watched everything. Psalm 52, titled for the moment Doeg reported this to Saul, describes him as a man whose "tongue plots destruction, like a sharp razor."
- **"Have you not here a spear or a sword at hand?" (v8).** David tells a second lie: the king's business "required haste." He is a soldier without a weapon.
- **"The sword of Goliath… wrapped in a cloth behind the ephod" (v9).** After killing Goliath, David had taken the giant's armor to his tent (17:54), and the sword had ended up at the sanctuary as a trophy dedicated to God, much as Saul's armor later ended up in a Philistine temple (31:10). The "ephod" here is the priest's garment used to seek God's will. It would soon go with Abiathar to David (23:6, 9).
- **"There is none like that; give it to me" (v9).** The irony runs deep. David won his first great victory with a sling and a stone, saying "the LORD saves not with sword and spear" (17:47). Now, frightened and alone, he takes the biggest sword in Israel. It is not wrong to carry a weapon, but the scene shows how far his confidence has slipped since the Valley of Elah.
- **Commentators:** All three call David's words a lie. Henry is blunt: "David told Ahimelech a gross untruth," and "it was ill done, and proved of bad consequence; for it occasioned the death of the priests of the Lord." JFB calls it "a direct falsehood, extorted through fear." Guzik sees David starting well by running to the house of the LORD and then stumbling there by trusting his own cleverness instead of telling the truth and asking the priest to pray. On the bread, JFB says "the ritual ordinance would have to yield to the great law of necessity and mercy," and Henry draws the same lesson from Jesus: "mercy is to be preferred to sacrifice." JFB also suggests God himself granted the exception through the priest, which goes further than the text says. Henry notes that Doeg "set his foot as far within the tabernacle as David did," a warning that outward devotion proves nothing. Guzik contrasts the sword with the faith that first won it.

#### David Flees to Gath (vv10–15)
- **"Went to Achish the king of Gath" (v10).** Gath was one of the five chief Philistine cities, and Goliath's hometown (17:4). Archaeologists identify it with Tell es-Safi, about 25 miles southwest of Jerusalem at the edge of the coastal plain. David may have hoped that the enemy of his enemy would take him in, or that no one in Gath would know his face. Walking into Goliath's city carrying Goliath's sword was a strange plan either way.
- **"Is not this David the king of the land?" (v11).** The Philistine servants know the victory song: "Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands" (18:7; 29:5). That song first made Saul jealous. Now it puts David in danger in a foreign court. "King of the land" may be loose talk for a powerful leader, but it is also one more voice, this time from outsiders, saying what Saul refuses to accept.
- **"David took these words to heart and was much afraid" (v12).** This is the first time the text says David was afraid. Psalm 56, whose title places it "when the Philistines seized him in Gath," shows what he did with the fear: "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you. In God, whose word I praise, in God I trust; I shall not be afraid" (Ps 56:3–4).
- **"He changed his behavior before them and pretended to be insane" (v13).** "Changed his behavior" is literally "changed his taste" or "his judgment" (Hebrew *ta'am*). "Pretended to be insane" is a form of *halal*, to act madly or like a fool. He scratched or scribbled marks on the gate doors and let saliva run into his beard. In that culture a man's beard was a mark of dignity, and to spoil it was shameful (2 Sam 10:4–5). It was a convincing act. Many people in the ancient world thought madness was a touch of the gods and left mad people alone.
- **"Do I lack madmen…?" (v15).** Achish's line is dry and almost comic: he has enough lunatics already. The great warrior of Israel leaves Gath not as a hero but as a joke, and alive.
- **Psalm 34.** Its title reads, "Of David, when he changed his behavior before Abimelech, so that he drove him out, and he went away." It uses the same Hebrew phrase as v13. "Abimelech" ("my father is king") was probably a royal title among the Philistines, as it seems to be in Genesis 20 and 26, with Achish as the king's personal name. The psalm is David's praise afterward: "I sought the LORD, and he answered me and delivered me from all my fears" (Ps 34:4), and "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted" (34:18). It also contains the line John applies to Jesus at the cross: "not one of them is broken" (34:20; John 19:36).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the whole chapter as a lesson that "justly are troubles called temptations, for many are by them drawn into sin," and he contrasts David with Jesus: "the example of the suffering Jesus was a copy without a blot, that of David was not so." On the madness he says "it was not a downright lie," which partly excuses it, though he will not justify it. Guzik leaves room for the view that God guided David into a humbling escape, and stresses that God delivered him even though he did not deserve it, which is why Psalm 34 overflows with praise. JFB thinks the protection he got implies he had been "directed by the divine oracle," which the text does not say. JFB also mentions a suggestion that the fit was "an attack of epilepsy," but the text says plainly that David pretended.

#### Pointing to Christ
David, the anointed king, is hungry, hunted and alone, and receives holy bread from the priest. Jesus took this very story to show that he, David's greater Son, is lord of the Sabbath and gives mercy to the hungry. The difference is just as plain. David lied and played the fool to save himself, and others paid for it. Jesus, rejected and hunted, never lied and never saved himself, and he laid down his life so that others would live. The psalm David wrote after this escape promises that the LORD "redeems the life of his servants" (Ps 34:22). Its line about unbroken bones is fulfilled at Jesus' cross (John 19:36).
