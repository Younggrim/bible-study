*Setting:* "A long time afterward" (v1). The battles of chapters 6–12 are years in the past, the land has been divided, and the eastern tribes have gone home (ch 22). Joshua is now near the end of his life. He will die at 110 (24:29), and JFB reckons this assembly came about fourteen years after the conquest. Like Jacob (Gen 49), Moses (Deut 31–33) and later David (1 Kings 2) and Samuel (1 Sam 12), Joshua gives a farewell address. This chapter and the next are his last words. Here he speaks to Israel's leaders, and the place is not named (Henry and JFB both think it was Shiloh). In chapter 24 he gathers all the tribes at Shechem. His theme is simple: God has kept every promise, so cling to him, because his warnings are just as sure.

#### Joshua's Charge to Israel's Leaders (vv1–16)
- **"When the LORD had given rest to Israel from all their surrounding enemies" (v1).** The rest promised in 21:44 has lasted. The war is over, but Joshua knows that peace brings its own danger. Moses warned that when Israel was settled and full, it would be tempted to forget the LORD (Deut 6:10–12; 8:11–14).
- **"Its elders and heads, its judges and officers" (v2).** Joshua summons the leaders, the people who would carry his words to every tribe and town. Elders led clans and towns. "Officers" (*shoterim*) were administrators, perhaps record-keepers, who had already served under Moses (Deut 1:15) and organized the crossing of the Jordan (Josh 1:10; 3:2). There is no successor named. Moses passed the leadership to Joshua, but Joshua leaves it with the elders, and that gap explains much of what happens in Judges.
- **"I am now old and well advanced in years" (v2).** He uses the same words God used of him in 13:1, "You are old and advanced in years, and there remains yet very much land to possess." Joshua's age is the reason to speak now.
- **"It is the LORD your God who has fought for you" (v3).** Joshua was a great general, but he takes no credit. God fought for Israel, as he promised in Exodus 14:14 and Deuteronomy 1:30, and as Joshua had seen when hailstones fell and the sun stood still (10:11–14, 42).
- **"Those nations that remain" (vv4–5).** Joshua admits plainly that not all the Canaanites are gone. He has already allotted their land, and God promises to "push them back" and "drive them out." The Tyndale notes give three reasons the conquest was gradual: God said he would drive them out "little by little," so that wild animals would not overrun an empty land (Exod 23:29–30; Deut 7:22); some peoples were militarily too strong for Israel yet (Judg 1:19); and God left some nations to test Israel's obedience (Judg 2:20–3:4).
- **"Be very strong to keep and to do all that is written in the Book of the Law of Moses" (v6).** These are nearly the words God spoke to Joshua at the beginning: "Only be strong and very courageous, being careful to do according to all the law… Do not turn from it to the right hand or to the left" (1:7). The book begins and ends with the same charge. Courage in Joshua is not only for battle. It takes courage to obey.
- **"That you may not mix with these nations" (v7).** The Hebrew is "not go in among" them, joining their life and customs. Joshua lists four steps that lead deeper into idolatry: to "make mention of the names of their gods," to "swear by them," to "serve them," and to "bow down to them." It starts with speech. Exodus 23:13 says, "make no mention of the names of other gods, nor let it be heard on your lips."
- **"You shall cling to the LORD your God just as you have done to this day" (v8).** "Cling" is *dabaq*, as in 22:5: holding fast, as a husband holds to his wife (Gen 2:24). Joshua credits them with faithfulness so far. Judges 2:7 confirms it: "the people served the LORD all the days of Joshua, and all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua."
- **"One man of you puts to flight a thousand" (v10).** This echoes Moses' promise, "five of you shall chase a hundred, and a hundred of you shall chase ten thousand" (Lev 26:8), and Moses' song, where the reverse happens to a disobedient Israel: "How could one have chased a thousand… unless their Rock had sold them?" (Deut 32:30). The numbers depend on who fights for whom. Shamgar (Judg 3:31), Gideon's three hundred (Judg 7), and Samson with the jawbone (Judg 15:15–16) later show it in action.
- **"Be very careful, therefore, to love the LORD your God" (v11).** Literally "guard yourselves greatly, for your souls." Love for God is something to keep watch over. In the Bible, love for God is not mainly a feeling. It is loyalty chosen and kept, which is why it can be commanded (Deut 6:5) and why it has to be guarded.
- **"If you turn back and cling to the remnant of these nations" (v12).** The same verb as v8. Israel would cling either to the LORD or to the Canaanites. Marriage is the clearest case. Deuteronomy 7:3–4 forbade marrying the Canaanites, "for they would turn away your sons from following me, to serve other gods." The concern was faith, not race: Rahab of Jericho (6:25) and Ruth the Moabite (Ruth 4:13) both married into Israel after turning to the LORD.
- **"A snare and a trap for you, a whip on your sides and thorns in your eyes" (v13).** Four pictures, from hunting and from pain. A snare (*moqesh*) catches a bird by surprise. A whip on the sides and thorns in the eyes torment someone who cannot get away. Numbers 33:55 had said the same: "barbs in your eyes and thorns in your sides." Judges 2:3 shows God's word coming true: "they shall become thorns in your sides, and their gods shall be a snare to you."
- **"Until you perish from off this good ground" (v13).** The land was a gift, and it could be lost. Joshua repeats the phrase "the good land that the LORD your God has given you" three times in vv13–16, as if to say, "Look at what you would be throwing away."
- **"I am about to go the way of all the earth" (v14).** A Hebrew way of saying "I am about to die." David uses the same words to Solomon (1 Kings 2:2). Everyone walks that road.
- **"Not one word has failed of all the good things that the LORD your God promised" (v14).** The words of 21:45, now spoken by Joshua as a witness. He appeals to their own experience: "you know in your hearts and souls, all of you." Faith rests on things God has actually done, in history, which they had seen.
- **"So the LORD will bring upon you all the evil things" (v15).** The turn of the speech. The same faithfulness that kept every good promise will keep every warning. The covenant came with blessings and curses (Lev 26; Deut 28), and the curses included losing the land: "you shall be plucked off the land that you are entering" (Deut 28:63). God's faithfulness is good news only for those who keep faith with him.
- **"If you transgress the covenant… and go and serve other gods" (v16).** The covenant is the agreement made at Sinai and renewed in Moab. To "transgress" (*'abar*) is literally to "cross over" it, the same verb Joshua used again and again for crossing the Jordan. The warning came true. Judges begins with Israel failing to drive out the Canaanites (Judg 1), Kings ends with both kingdoms exiled (2 Kings 17:7–23; 25:21), and Kings explains the exile in the terms of this verse.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry pictures Joshua "jealous over Israel with a godly jealousy," like a dying father anxious for his children. He has Joshua giving all the credit to God ("we were only instruments in God's hand") and urging love "not by constraint or from a slavish fear of God, but of choice and with delight." He traces apostasy downhill, from friendship with idolaters to marriage to worship, because "the way of sin is down-hill," and he says God's warnings are as real as his promises: "his threatenings are not bugbears." Guzik also stresses that Joshua points to what God has done in real places and times rather than to feelings, that the best defense against false religion is knowing the true God rather than becoming an expert in error, and that compromise usually begins small and harmless-looking before it becomes a snare. He reminds Christian readers that Christ has borne the law's curse (Gal 3:13), while God still disciplines his children (Heb 12:7). JFB praises "the modesty and humility of Joshua" in v3 and sums up the speech in v11: "the sum of his exhortation is comprised in the love of God." All three read v13 and v15 as warning, not threat for its own sake.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joshua could remind Israel that God had fought for them, but he could not change their hearts, and within a generation they turned away (Judg 2:10–12). Moses had said that one day "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart… so that you will love the LORD your God with all your heart" (Deut 30:6). That is what the new covenant in Christ promises: God's law written on the heart (Jer 31:33; Heb 8:10). Jesus, whose name is the same as Joshua's, does what the first Joshua could not. He "redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13), and he keeps his people: "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28). The call to "cling to the LORD" still stands for Christians (Acts 11:23), but it rests now on a Savior who holds on to them.
