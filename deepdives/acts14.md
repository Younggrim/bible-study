*Setting:* About AD 47–48, the second half of Paul and Barnabas's first missionary journey. They have just been driven out of Antioch in Pisidia after a great response among the Gentiles and fierce opposition from the synagogue leaders (13:44–51). Now they head east along the Roman road across the high plateau of central Asia Minor (modern Turkey) into the regions of Phrygia and Lycaonia, then part of the Roman province of Galatia. Most scholars think these are the churches Paul later wrote to in his letter to the Galatians. The chapter follows the same pattern three times, preaching and then opposition, then turns back to strengthen the young churches before the missionaries sail home.

#### Paul and Barnabas at Iconium (vv1–7)
- **"At Iconium they entered together into the Jewish synagogue" (v1).** Iconium (modern Konya, still a major city) lay about 90 miles east-southeast of Pisidian Antioch, at a junction of trade routes in a well-watered plain. Paul and Barnabas begin where they always do, in the synagogue, and **"spoke in such a way"** that many believed. Luke is not praising technique. He notes that how the gospel is spoken matters, and here it was clear and compelling.
- **"Both Jews and Greeks believed" (v1).** The same gospel won both groups. There was not a Jewish version and a Gentile version.
- **"Poisoned their minds against the brothers" (v2).** The verb is literally "made their souls evil," embittered them. The opposition works by rumor and suspicion before it turns to stones.
- **"So they remained for a long time, speaking boldly for the Lord" (v3).** The "so" is surprising: the opposition was a reason to stay, not leave. They spoke boldly "for," or literally "upon," the Lord, relying on him. The gospel is called **"the word of his grace,"** and the Lord himself **"bore witness"** to it with signs and wonders. In Acts miracles back up the message. They are not the message.
- **"Some sided with the Jews and some with the apostles" (v4).** This is the first time Luke calls Paul and Barnabas "apostles" (also v14). The word means "sent ones," and here it probably means they were sent by the church at Antioch and the Spirit (13:2–4). Paul elsewhere insists that he was an apostle in the full sense, commissioned by the risen Christ himself (Gal 1:1; 1 Cor 9:1). Barnabas is not counted among the Twelve, but Luke uses the word of both.
- **"They learned of it and fled" (vv5–6).** A plot by Gentiles and Jews together, with their rulers, to "mistreat them and to stone them." Stoning was the Jewish penalty for blasphemy, so the Jewish leaders likely saw the message that way. Fleeing was not cowardice. Jesus had told his disciples, "When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next" (Matt 10:23). There is a time to stay (v3) and a time to go (v6).
- **"Lystra and Derbe, cities of Lycaonia" (v6).** Luke's phrasing implies that crossing from Iconium to Lystra meant crossing into a different region. Some older scholars thought Luke had blundered, since Iconium was sometimes counted as part of Lycaonia. William Ramsay's research showed that in this period Iconium belonged to Phrygia, and Lystra and Derbe to Lycaonia, so Luke was exactly right. **Lystra** lay about 20 miles south-southwest of Iconium. Augustus had made it a Roman colony with a garrison. **Derbe** lay about 60 miles further southeast, on the road toward Paul's home region of Cilicia.
- **"There they continued to preach the gospel" (v7).** Fleeing changed the address, not the work.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the pattern as God using persecution to move his messengers on, not as a sign that God had stopped working. He also cites Ramsay's work on the borders of Lycaonia as support for Luke's accuracy. JFB, writing in 1871, says the exact positions of Lystra and Derbe "has not yet been discovered." Both have since been identified from inscriptions: Lystra in 1885 and Derbe in the mid-twentieth century. JFB calls "grace" the whole burden of the gospel, the point of Luke's phrase "the word of his grace."

#### Paul and Barnabas at Lystra (vv8–18)
- **"A man… who could not use his feet. He was crippled from birth and had never walked" (v8).** Luke stacks three phrases to stress that this case is hopeless. The scene closely mirrors Peter's first healing at the temple gate (3:1–10): a man lame from birth, an apostle who looks intently at him, a command, and the man leaping up and walking. Luke often sets Peter and Paul side by side so readers see that the same Lord is at work through both, among Jews and among Gentiles.
- **"Seeing that he had faith to be made well" (v9).** "To be made well" is *sōthēnai*, "to be saved," the ordinary Greek word for both healing and salvation. The man had been listening to Paul preach. Whatever he heard about Jesus produced faith, and Paul, by the Spirit, saw it in his face.
- **"Stand upright on your feet." And he sprang up (v10).** Unlike Peter's healing, there is no mention of Jesus' name in the command, though Paul had just been preaching him. The crowd misses the point entirely.
- **"The gods have come down to us in the likeness of men!" (v11).** The crowd shouts in **Lycaonian**, their local language. Paul and Barnabas probably did not understand it, which explains why they only reacted when the oxen arrived (v14). Educated people in the region spoke Greek, but at home and in excitement the crowd fell back on their mother tongue.
- **A local legend.** The Roman poet Ovid, writing a generation before Paul, tells a story set in the nearby hills of Phrygia: Zeus and Hermes visited disguised as men, were turned away from a thousand homes, and were welcomed only by a poor old couple, Baucis and Philemon. The gods flooded the rest of the region and turned the couple's cottage into a temple. Whether or not the people of Lystra knew Ovid, inscriptions from the area show Zeus and Hermes worshiped together there. The people of Lystra were not going to miss the gods a second time.
- **"Barnabas they called Zeus, and Paul, Hermes, because he was the chief speaker" (v12).** Zeus was king of the gods. Hermes was their messenger and the god of eloquence. Barnabas was perhaps the more dignified, quieter figure, and Paul did the talking. Paul's own enemies later sneered that his "bodily presence is weak" (2 Cor 10:10), so the crowd's choice may say something about how he looked.
- **"The priest of Zeus, whose temple was at the entrance to the city" (v13).** The local temple stood before the city gates. Oxen were the costliest sacrifice, and the wool garlands were draped on the animals before sacrifice. The town was preparing an official festival.
- **"They tore their garments and rushed out into the crowd" (v14).** Tearing clothes was the Jewish response to blasphemy and grief (Mark 14:63). Paul and Barnabas react to being worshiped the way a Jew reacted to hearing God's name dishonored. Contrast Herod, who accepted divine honors and was struck down (12:22–23), and Peter, who told Cornelius, "I too am a man" (10:26).
- **"We also are men, of like nature with you" (v15).** The phrase is *homoiopatheis*, "with the same nature and feelings." James uses it of Elijah, "a man with a nature like ours" (Jas 5:17).
- **A sermon to pagans (vv15–17).** This is the first gospel sermon in Acts to a purely pagan audience, and it is different from the synagogue sermon in chapter 13. There are no Old Testament quotations and no history of Israel, because these hearers did not know them. Paul starts with creation and the God who made "the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them," wording from Exodus 20:11 and Psalm 146:6. He calls idols **"vain things,"** *mataia*, the Greek Old Testament's word for idols as empty and useless (Jer 10:3–15). He sets against them **"a living God."** Paul will preach along the same lines in Athens (17:22–31).
- **"In past generations he allowed all the nations to walk in their own ways" (v16).** God was patient with the nations, not indifferent to them. Paul says the same in Athens: "The times of ignorance God overlooked, but now he commands all people everywhere to repent" (17:30).
- **"He did not leave himself without witness" (v17).** God's goodness in nature is a witness to him, what theologians call "general revelation" (Rom 1:19–20; Ps 19:1). The detail is pointed. Zeus was the sky god, believed to send the rain. Paul tells the priests of Zeus that the rain and the harvests and the gladness at the table come from the living God, not from the god at their gate.
- **"They scarcely restrained the people" (v18).** Even this sermon barely stopped the sacrifice. A miracle can draw a crowd without producing faith, and people readily fit new wonders into old beliefs.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of the lame man that "God will not disappoint the desires that are of his own kindling." He pictures the crowd thinking Paul and Barnabas had "dropped down to them out of the clouds," and praises the apostles for "being more concerned for God's honour than their own." On verse 17: "It is not the heaven that gives us rain, but God that gives us rain from heaven." Guzik points out that the apostles preached first and the miracle came second, and that the healing drew attention but did not by itself save anyone. He notes that Paul met these hearers where they were, with creation and common grace. JFB thinks the Lycaonian speech may have been a corrupt Greek dialect or a survival of an older tongue, and calls the apostles' reaction "How unlike either imposture or enthusiasm": no fraud would refuse such honor.

#### Paul Stoned at Lystra (vv19–23)
- **"Jews came from Antioch and Iconium" (v19).** Pisidian Antioch was over 100 miles away. These opponents were determined. **"Having persuaded the crowds"** shows how quickly a crowd can swing: the people ready to worship Paul one day help stone him the next. Henry: "Popular breath turns like the wind."
- **"They stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing that he was dead" (v19).** Years before, Paul had stood guarding the coats of those who stoned Stephen (7:58). Now he takes the stones himself. Paul later lists it among his sufferings: "Once I was stoned" (2 Cor 11:25). He probably also had it in mind when he wrote to these same churches, "I bear on my body the marks of Jesus" (Gal 6:17).
- **Was Paul dead?** Luke says the crowd "supposed" he was dead, which suggests they were wrong. Some think he really died and was raised, and some link this to the vision of being "caught up to the third heaven" (2 Cor 12:2–4). But Paul dates that vision "fourteen years ago" in a letter written around AD 55–56, which places it a few years before this event. Most readers take it that Paul was knocked unconscious and left for dead, and that his recovery was itself a miracle.
- **"He rose up and entered the city, and on the next day he went on" (v20).** He does not run from Lystra. He walks back into it. The next day he travels 60 miles to Derbe. Among "the disciples" who gathered round him may have been a young man named **Timothy**, from Lystra (16:1). Paul later reminds Timothy that he saw "my persecutions and sufferings that happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium, and at Lystra" (2 Tim 3:10–11).
- **"They returned to Lystra and to Iconium and to Antioch" (v21).** From Derbe the shortest way home lay east through the Taurus Mountains past Paul's hometown of Tarsus. Instead they turned back through the very towns that had attacked them. New churches needed care more than the missionaries needed safety. Some scholars suggest new magistrates had taken office by then, which may explain why these visits seem to have been quieter.
- **"Through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God" (v22).** "Tribulations" is *thlipsis*, pressure or affliction. "Must" is the same word for divine necessity Luke uses of Christ's sufferings (Luke 24:26). Note the "we": Paul says it with fresh scars on his body. The kingdom here is the future kingdom believers will enter at the end. Suffering is not a sign that the gospel has failed but part of the road, as Jesus said (John 16:33).
- **"When they had appointed elders for them in every church" (v23).** "Appointed" is *cheirotoneō*, originally "to vote by stretching out the hand," as in the Athenian assembly, and then simply "to appoint." The lexicon notes that in 2 Corinthians 8:19 it involves a vote by the churches but here means appointment without a vote. JFB argues instead that it means the apostles oversaw a choice made by the people. Christians have organized church leadership in different ways since, and both sides have appealed to this verse. Note that there were *elders*, plural, in every church, and they were new believers only months in the faith. Paul and Barnabas entrusted them, with prayer and fasting, **"to the Lord in whom they had believed."** That is the real security of any church (compare 20:32).
- **Commentators:** Henry draws from Paul's recovery that God's servants "shall not die as long as he has work for them to do," and on verse 22 that tribulation is real "but this is encouraging": it ends in the kingdom. He thinks "every particular church should have one or more" elders to lead it. Guzik suggests Paul may truly have died and been raised, but he calls the link to 2 Corinthians 12 "only conjecture," and he thinks Paul's courage at Lystra may have helped win young Timothy. JFB also thinks Timothy was probably won to Christ during this visit and that Paul's recovery was miraculous. Guzik passes on F. F. Bruce's observation that appointing elders from such young converts would look risky by later missionary standards, which shows how much the apostles trusted the Spirit's work.

#### Paul and Barnabas Return to Antioch in Syria (vv24–28)
- **"They had spoken the word in Perga" (v25).** On the way in they passed through Perga without recorded preaching (13:13–14), and this time they preach there. Then they go down to **Attalia** (modern Antalya), the main port on that coast, and sail for Syria.
- **"Where they had been commended to the grace of God for the work that they had fulfilled" (v26).** The journey ends where it began. "The work" echoes the Spirit's words in 13:2, "the work to which I have called them." The circle is complete. The whole trip, well over a thousand miles by land and sea, probably took a year and a half to two years.
- **"They declared all that God had done with them" (v27).** Not "what we did for God," but what God did *with* them. Paul and Barnabas report as servants, and the report centers on one great fact: God **"had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles."** The door is opened by God, and it is a door of *faith*, not of circumcision or law-keeping. That last phrase is the fuse for the controversy that breaks out in Antioch soon after (15:1–2): if Gentiles enter by faith, must they also become Jews?
- **"They remained no little time with the disciples" (v28).** "No little time" is Luke's understatement for a long stay, probably a year or more. Many scholars think Paul wrote Galatians from Antioch in this period, to the very churches he had just planted, but the dating of that letter is debated.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the "work" was finished only in one sense, since the new churches would need ongoing care, and Paul later returns to them twice (16:1–5; 18:23). He also uses the report to warn against hero-worship: the missionaries told what God had done. JFB fastens on the same point in verse 27: God himself is credited with opening "the door of faith." Henry's overview sums the chapter up as a gospel that "goes on conquering and to conquer."

#### Pointing to Christ
A town tried to make Paul and Barnabas into gods, and they tore their clothes in horror. The one man who truly was "God come down in the likeness of men" (Phil 2:7) is the one they preached, and he too was first welcomed by crowds and then killed by them. Paul's scars at Lystra followed the path of his Lord's, and his words to the young churches are true because Jesus walked them first: the road into the kingdom runs "through many tribulations." The rain, the harvest and the gladness that the people of Lystra credited to Zeus were witnesses all along to the living God, who has now opened the door of faith through his Son.
