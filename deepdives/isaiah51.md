*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. In chapter 50 the Servant spoke of his suffering and his trust in God, and called those "who fear the LORD" to trust God in the dark (50:10). Chapter 51 speaks to those faithful listeners. It is full of urgent repeated calls. Three times God says "Listen to me" (vv1, 4, 7). Then the people cry to God, "Awake, awake" (v9), and God answers by calling Jerusalem, "Wake yourself, wake yourself" (v17). The chapter moves from Abraham and Eden, through the Exodus, to a city that has drunk God's wrath down to the dregs, and to the promise that the cup will be taken from her hand. The whole chapter is one long ESV section.

#### The LORD's Comfort for Zion (vv1–23)
- **"Listen to me, you who pursue righteousness, you who seek the LORD" (v1).** This time God speaks to the faithful, those who want to do right and seek God, not to the stubborn. They are a minority, and they feel small and discouraged.
- **"Look to the rock from which you were hewn, and to the quarry from which you were dug" (v1).** The picture is a stone-cutter's quarry. Every stone in a building was cut from somewhere. The next verse names the quarry: Abraham and Sarah. The point is not mainly humility, as the verse is often quoted, but hope. God made a nation out of very unpromising rock.
- **"Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you; for he was but one when I called him" (v2).** Abraham was one man, old and childless, married to a barren woman (Gen 11:30; 15:2). God called him and made him a great people (Gen 12:1–3). The exiles were a small remnant. If God could do it with one man, he can do it again with them. Ezekiel records the exiles' boast turned the wrong way: "Abraham was only one man… but we are many" (Ezek 33:24). Isaiah turns it into a promise. Paul says Abraham believed the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (Rom 4:17–19).
- **"The LORD comforts Zion… and makes her wilderness like Eden, her desert like the garden of the LORD" (v3).** This goes back even further than Abraham, to the garden of Genesis 2. The ruined land of Judah will be like paradise restored. "Comforts" is the keynote of these chapters (40:1; 49:13). "Joy and gladness… thanksgiving and the voice of song" fill the place where there were ruins. Revelation ends with the river of life and the tree of life in the new Jerusalem (Rev 22:1–2).
- **"Give attention to me, my people… for a law will go out from me, and I will set my justice for a light to the peoples" (v4).** "Law" is *torah*, instruction (ESV footnote "teaching"). This echoes 2:3, where the nations stream to Zion, "for out of Zion shall go the law." The Servant was to bring justice to the nations (42:1–4) and be their light (49:6). Here God says it is his own justice going out.
- **"My righteousness draws near, my salvation has gone out… the coastlands hope for me, and for my arm they wait" (v5).** "Righteousness" and "salvation" are paired again (46:13). God's righteousness is his saving faithfulness in action. "My arm" is God's power to save (40:10). The next verse asks the arm to wake up, and 53:1 will ask, "to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?"
- **"The heavens vanish like smoke, the earth will wear out like a garment" (v6).** The universe that seems permanent is not. It will wear thin like an old cloak. Psalm 102:25–27 says the same, and Hebrews applies that psalm to the Son (Heb 1:10–12). "They who dwell in it will die in like manner." The ESV footnote gives an alternative, "like gnats," which depends on how one Hebrew word is read. **"But my salvation will be forever."** Jesus says, "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35).
- **"Listen to me, you who know righteousness, the people in whose heart is my law" (v7).** The law written on the heart is the promise of the new covenant (Jer 31:33). Psalm 37:31 says of the righteous, "the law of his God is in his heart." It is not just known but loved.
- **"Fear not the reproach of man, nor be dismayed at their revilings" (v7).** Those who belong to God will be mocked. Jesus said, "Blessed are you when others revile you… on my account" (Matt 5:11–12).
- **"The moth will eat them up like a garment, and the worm will eat them like wool" (v8).** This repeats the picture of 50:9. The mockers are as fragile as a wool cloak in a chest full of moths. God's righteousness lasts "to all generations."
- **"Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD; awake, as in days of old" (v9).** Now the people speak, in a bold prayer. God does not sleep (Ps 121:4), but in times of disaster it can feel as if he does. The Psalms pray the same way: "Awake! Why are you sleeping, O Lord?" (Ps 44:23). This is the faithful remnant reminding God of what he has done before.
- **"Was it not you who cut Rahab in pieces, who pierced the dragon?" (v9).** This Rahab is not the woman of Jericho (the names are spelled differently in Hebrew). It is a poetic name for a monster of the sea, used in the Bible for Egypt (30:7; Ps 87:4; 89:10). The "dragon" (*tannin*) is a sea creature, also used of Pharaoh (Ezek 29:3). Israel's neighbors told myths about a god fighting a sea monster at creation. In the Babylonian creation story, Marduk splits the sea goddess Tiamat in two. The exiles would have heard that story in Babylon. Isaiah borrows the picture and gives it a real place in history: the LORD's victory over the "monster" Egypt at the Red Sea.
- **"Was it not you who dried up the sea… who made the depths of the sea a way for the redeemed to pass over?" (v10).** The monster is the sea, and the sea is Egypt. The Exodus was God's great act of redemption (Exod 14:21–22; 15:13). The prayer asks for it again.
- **"And the ransomed of the LORD shall return and come to Zion with singing" (v11).** This line is repeated word for word from 35:10. It reads like the answer to the prayer. **"Sorrow and sighing shall flee away."** Revelation promises the same: "he will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more" (Rev 21:4).
- **"I, I am he who comforts you; who are you that you are afraid of man who dies" (v12).** God answers with a gentle rebuke. The repeated "I" is emphatic. Why fear a mortal who is "made like grass" (40:6–8) when the eternal God is the one comforting them?
- **"And have forgotten the LORD, your Maker, who stretched out the heavens" (v13).** Fear of people grows when God is forgotten. "You fear continually all the day because of the wrath of the oppressor." Then God asks, **"And where is the wrath of the oppressor?"** It is a question asked from the future. Babylon's fury will soon be gone (Ps 37:10).
- **"He who is bowed down shall speedily be released; he shall not die and go down to the pit" (v14).** "Bowed down" is the captive bent under chains or in a cell. "The pit" can mean a dungeon or the grave. Release is coming soon, with bread enough.
- **"I am the LORD your God, who stirs up the sea so that its waves roar" (v15).** The God who stirs the sea is the God who once split it. The phrase is found again almost exactly in Jeremiah 31:35. "The LORD of hosts is his name."
- **"I have put my words in your mouth and covered you in the shadow of my hand" (v16).** These are the very images used of the Servant in 49:2. Here they are spoken to Israel, or to the Servant who represents it. JFB reads it as the Messiah, "Israel's ideal and representative Head." The purpose is cosmic: "establishing the heavens and laying the foundations of the earth, and saying to Zion, 'You are my people.'" The work of new creation and the renewal of the covenant go together (65:17–18).
- **"Wake yourself, wake yourself, stand up, O Jerusalem" (v17).** The people told God to wake up (v9). Now God tells Jerusalem to wake up. The problem was never that God was asleep. It was that his people were lying in a stupor.
- **"You who have drunk from the hand of the LORD the cup of his wrath… to the dregs" (v17).** The "cup of wrath" is a common Old Testament picture of judgment (Ps 75:8; Jer 25:15–28; Hab 2:16). It is like being forced to drink strong wine until you stagger. "To the dregs" means to the bitter sediment at the bottom. Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC was that cup.
- **"There is none to guide her among all the sons she has borne" (v18).** A drunk person needs someone to take her by the hand and lead her home. Jerusalem's children are no help. They are lying in the streets.
- **"These two things have happened to you… devastation and destruction, famine and sword" (v19).** Two pairs: what happened to the land (devastation and destruction) and to the people (famine and sword). Lamentations describes exactly this (Lam 2:11–12, 19–21; 4:9–10). The ESV here follows the Dead Sea scroll and early translations, "who will comfort you?" The traditional Hebrew reads "how shall I comfort you?" Either way, no human comforter is found.
- **"Your sons have fainted; they lie at the head of every street like an antelope in a net" (v20).** An antelope caught in a hunter's net thrashes until it is exhausted. Jerusalem's young men lie in the streets just as helpless, "full of the wrath of the LORD."
- **"You who are afflicted, who are drunk, but not with wine" (v21).** Their staggering is not from drinking but from judgment (29:9).
- **"Your God who pleads the cause of his people" (v22).** God is now Jerusalem's lawyer, defending her case (Jer 50:34; Mic 7:9).
- **"Behold, I have taken from your hand the cup of staggering; the bowl of my wrath you shall drink no more" (v22).** This is the turn of the whole chapter. God takes the cup out of her hand. The judgment is over.
- **"I will put it into the hand of your tormentors, who have said to you, 'Bow down, that we may pass over'" (v23).** Conquerors sometimes put their feet on the necks of defeated enemies or made them lie down to be walked over (Josh 10:24). Ancient Near Eastern royal inscriptions and carvings show it. Jerusalem had been made to "make your back like the ground." Now the cup passes to Babylon (47:6; Jer 25:26).
- **Commentators:** JFB insists that the "rock" and "quarry" in v1 are not mainly a call to humility about our origins, as the verse is often quoted, but a call to hope based on what God did with Abraham. Guzik and the Tyndale notes take it the same way. JFB identifies Rahab as Egypt and the dragon as Pharaoh. Guzik explains Rahab as "pride," which fits the root of the word, and also links the dragon with the serpent defeated since Eden. That is a broader theological reading, while the parallel with v10 points to Egypt and the Red Sea. Henry says that when believers pray "Awake," they are asking God to show his power, not suggesting he sleeps. He reads God's call in v17 as a call to wake "out of the stupor of despair." Guzik and the Tyndale notes both link the cup of wrath to Jesus' prayer in Gethsemane (Matt 26:39). On v18, JFB argues that "none to guide her" cannot describe the Babylonian exile, which had Daniel, Ezekiel, Ezra and Nehemiah, and applies it to Israel's later dispersion. Most other interpreters read it more simply as a poetic picture of Jerusalem's helplessness in 586 BC.

#### Pointing to Christ
The cup of God's wrath was taken out of Jerusalem's hand, but it was not poured out on the ground. Someone drank it. In Gethsemane Jesus prayed, "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). He drank it to the dregs on the cross, so that "the bowl of my wrath you shall drink no more" could be said to everyone who trusts him. The arm of the LORD that cut through the sea is revealed in the Servant (53:1). Through him the ransomed come to Zion singing, the desert becomes Eden, and God says to his people, "You are my people."
