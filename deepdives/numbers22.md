*Setting:* The fortieth year after the exodus, about 1406 BC on the traditional dating (many scholars prefer the 1200s BC). Israel has just defeated Sihon and Og, the two Amorite kings east of the Jordan (21:21–35), and now camps on the plains of Moab, the flat land north of the Dead Sea across the river from Jericho. They will stay here through the rest of Numbers and all of Deuteronomy. Chapters 22–24 are a story within the story. Israel does not even know it is happening. On the hills above the camp, a frightened king hires the most famous diviner of the age to curse them, and God turns every curse into a blessing. The story is full of irony and comedy, and it carries one of the Old Testament's great themes: no one can curse those whom God has blessed.

#### Balak Summons Balaam (vv1–21)
- **"Camped in the plains of Moab beyond the Jordan at Jericho" (v1).** "Beyond the Jordan" is written from the point of view of someone in Canaan: the east side. This is Israel's last camp before crossing (Josh 3:1).
- **"Balak the son of Zippor saw all that Israel had done to the Amorites" (v2).** Balak is known only from this story and later references to it (Josh 24:9; Judg 11:25; Mic 6:5). Sihon had already taken much of Moab's land (21:26), and Israel had crushed Sihon. Balak assumed he was next. He did not know that God had told Israel not to attack Moab, since the Moabites were descendants of Lot (Deut 2:9).
- **"Moab was in great dread… overcome with fear" (v3).** This fulfils the song Israel sang at the Red Sea: "trembling seizes the leaders of Moab" (Exod 15:15).
- **"The elders of Midian" (v4).** The Midianites were desert tribes descended from Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:1–2). Moses' father-in-law Jethro was a Midianite priest, but these Midianites now join Moab against Israel, the start of a long hostility (25:16–18; ch 31; Judg 6–8). **"As the ox licks up the grass of the field."** A farmer's picture: a huge animal working steadily across a pasture until nothing is left.
- **"Balaam the son of Beor at Pethor, which is near the River" (v5).** "The River" in the Old Testament means the Euphrates. Pethor is probably Pitru, a town on the Euphrates south of Carchemish known from Assyrian records, some 400 miles from Moab, a journey of about three weeks each way (Deut 23:4 places him in "Mesopotamia"). The ESV's "the land of the people of Amaw" follows one reading of the Hebrew; other versions read "the land of his people." Balaam was a famous professional, worth sending a long way for.
- **Balaam outside the Bible.** In 1967 archaeologists at Deir Alla, in the Jordan valley, found an inscription in ink on plaster, dated to around 800 BC, that begins "the book of Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods." In it he receives a night vision from the gods and announces disaster. It does not mention Israel, but it shows that Balaam was remembered in this region as a great seer centuries later. He was a real figure, and his reputation was exactly what Balak counted on.
- **"Come now, curse this people for me… he whom you bless is blessed, and he whom you curse is cursed" (v6).** In the ancient world a curse spoken by a skilled specialist was thought to unleash real power. Kings hired diviners before battle. Balak's words are a flattering pagan echo of God's promise to Abraham: "I will bless those who bless you, and him who dishonors you I will curse" (Gen 12:3). Balak is about to find out whose word actually works that way.
- **"With the fees for divination in their hand" (v7).** The word for divination (*qesem*) is a practice the law forbids in Israel (Deut 18:10–14). Balaam was a diviner who read omens for pay (Josh 13:22 calls him "the one who practiced divination"), not a prophet of Israel. The messengers bring his fee up front.
- **"I will bring back word to you, as the LORD speaks to me" (v8).** Balaam uses God's personal name, *Yahweh* (printed "the LORD"). He knows about Israel's God, perhaps because news of the exodus had spread (Josh 2:10), or because knowledge of the true God lingered in Mesopotamia where Abraham came from. He calls him "the LORD my God" in v18. Whether he truly feared God or simply treated him as one more power to consult is the question the whole story raises.
- **"Who are these men with you?" (v9).** God asks, as he asked Adam "Where are you?" (Gen 3:9), not for information but to make Balaam face what he is doing.
- **"You shall not go with them. You shall not curse the people, for they are blessed" (v12).** The answer could not be plainer: don't go, don't curse, and here is why. Israel's blessedness is a settled fact.
- **"The LORD has refused to let me go with you" (v13).** Balaam reports the "don't go" but leaves out the "they are blessed." His answer sounds like "I would, but I'm not allowed." Balak hears it as a bargaining position.
- **"More in number and more honorable" (v15).** Balak raises the stakes: more important envoys, and a blank cheque, "whatever you say to me I will do" (v17).
- **"Though Balak were to give me his house full of silver and gold, I could not go beyond the command of the LORD" (v18).** Fine words, and Balaam does keep them in the end (24:13). But he asks the men to **"stay here tonight, that I may know what more the LORD will say to me" (v19).** God had already spoken. Asking again on a settled question is how a heart that wants something different keeps the door open.
- **"If the men have come to call you, rise, go with them; but only do what I tell you" (v20).** God lets him go, with a firm limit. Readers differ on why: some see a genuine permission that Balaam then abused, and many (Henry and Guzik among them) see God giving Balaam over to what he wanted, as happens elsewhere when people insist on their own way (Ps 81:12; Rom 1:24).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Balaam "desired a re-hearing of that cause which was already so plainly determined," and that "the searcher of hearts knew that he loved the wages of unrighteousness." Guzik makes the same reading: asking twice was itself the sin, like a child going back to a father hoping for a different answer. The Tyndale notes are more generous, arguing that Balaam's later refusal (24:13) weighs against reading v18 as haggling, and that he seems resolved to obey. JFB records three views of Balaam held in its day, "a saint," "an irreligious charlatan," or "a novice in the faith," and leans toward a man who knew the true God but was ruled by greed. JFB calls the fee "the fee of a fortune teller." The New Testament settles the question of his motive: he "loved gain from wrongdoing" (2 Pet 2:15; Jude 11).

#### Balaam's Donkey and the Angel (vv22–41)
- **"God's anger was kindled because he went" (v22).** This puzzles readers right after v20. The story itself gives the answer: God looks at the heart, and Balaam set out wanting Balak's reward and hoping to find a way to earn it. Permission to go was not permission to go *like that*. The angel says so in v32.
- **"The angel of the LORD took his stand in the way as his adversary" (v22).** "Adversary" is the Hebrew *satan*, here an ordinary noun meaning "opponent," not the name of the devil. The same word appears in v32 ("I have come out to oppose you"). God's own messenger blocks the road. The angel of the LORD speaks as God himself in v35 ("speak only the word that I tell you"), as in other appearances (Gen 16:7–13; Exod 3:2–6). Many Christian interpreters, Guzik among them, see these appearances as the Son of God before his incarnation. Others read the angel as a messenger who speaks with God's full authority. The text does not settle it.
- **"The donkey saw the angel of the LORD" (v23).** Balaam's whole profession was "seeing," yet the donkey sees what the famous seer cannot. The story is deliberately funny, and the joke is on the diviner.
- **Three narrowing places (vv23–27).** First an open road with a field to turn into. Then **"a narrow path between the vineyards, with a wall on either side"** (v24), where the donkey crushes Balaam's foot against the stone wall. Then **"a narrow place, where there was no way to turn either to the right or to the left"** (v26). Each time the space shrinks, and each time Balaam's anger grows. He is running out of road, and he cannot see it.
- **"The LORD opened the mouth of the donkey" (v28).** The only other talking animal in Scripture is the serpent in Eden (Gen 3:1). There, an animal speaks to lead a person into sin. Here, an animal speaks to stop one. The text presents it as a real event, and Peter treats it so: "a speechless donkey spoke with human voice and restrained the prophet's madness" (2 Pet 2:16).
- **"I wish I had a sword in my hand, for then I would kill you" (v29).** Balaam doesn't even seem surprised that his donkey is talking. He is too angry. And the irony is sharp: he wants a sword, while an angel with a drawn sword stands a few feet away, ready to kill *him* (v33). The man who claims his words can destroy a nation cannot kill a donkey without a weapon.
- **"Am I not your donkey, on which you have ridden all your life long?" (v30).** A faithful animal asks a reasonable question, and Balaam has to answer "No." The diviner loses an argument to his donkey.
- **"The LORD opened the eyes of Balaam" (v31).** The same verb as v28: God opened the donkey's mouth and then the seer's eyes. True sight is a gift from God, not a skill a professional can sell.
- **"Your way is perverse before me" (v32).** The Hebrew verb (*yarat*) is rare, found only here and in Job 16:11, and its meaning is uncertain. The lexicon suggests "precipitate" or "headlong": Balaam was rushing recklessly down a road that led to ruin. **"If she had not turned aside… I would have killed you and let her live" (v33).** The donkey he beat three times had saved his life three times.
- **"I have sinned… if it is evil in your sight, I will turn back" (v34).** A confession with a sword in front of him. And even now there is an "if," as though it were still unclear what God wanted.
- **"Go with the men, but speak only the word that I tell you" (v35).** The angel repeats v20, word for word in substance. Balaam will go, but as a mouthpiece, not a hired gun. Nothing God has said has changed. Balaam has simply been shown that he cannot get around it.
- **"Am I not able to honor you?" (v37).** Balak meets him at the border on the Arnon, the gorge that marked Moab's northern edge. The king's opening words are about payment. **"Have I now any power of my own to speak anything? The word that God puts in my mouth, that must I speak" (v38).** Balaam has learned the donkey's lesson, at least in what he says.
- **"Balak sacrificed oxen and sheep" (v40).** This was both a sacrificial meal and a welcome feast for the guests. **Kiriath-huzoth** (v39), "town of streets," is unknown.
- **"Bamoth-baal" (v41).** The name means "high places of Baal," hilltop shrines on the ridge overlooking the plains of Moab (compare "Bamoth" in 21:19). A high place was the natural spot for a ritual curse: near the gods, and with the target in view.
- **"He saw a fraction of the people" (v41).** Literally "the end of the people," the nearest edge of the camp. Balak may have thought that seeing only part of Israel would make the curse easier. He will try two more viewpoints (23:13, 27), as if changing the angle could change God's mind.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The sin of sinners is not to be thought the less provoking to God because he permits it," and "As God sometimes denies the prayers of his people in love, so sometimes he grants the desires of the wicked in wrath." On the beating he writes, "Nothing besots men worse than unbridled anger," and he adds that "the holy angels are adversaries to sin, and perhaps are employed more than we are aware of in preventing it." Guzik reads the permission of v20 as judgment, notes that the donkey was more spiritually perceptive than the prophet, and doubts the depth of a repentance offered with a sword at one's throat. He also suspects Balaam's "I can only speak what God says" (v38) was partly self-protection, so he could blame God if the curse failed. JFB locates God's displeasure in Balaam's secret purpose of going against God's charge for "the wages of unrighteousness," and insists the text "strongly favors the literal view" of the donkey speaking, against those who made it a vision. JFB describes the high places as "eminences consecrated to the worship of Baal-peor" or Chemosh, Moab's god. That detail matters, because Baal of Peor is where Israel will fall into sin in chapter 25.

#### Pointing to Christ
A pagan king pays to have God's people cursed, and God will not allow it. Behind the comedy is a promise that runs from Abraham to the cross: those whom God has blessed cannot be cursed by anyone. Paul says the same thing of everyone in Christ: "If God is for us, who can be against us?… Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies" (Rom 8:31–33). The curse that should have fallen on God's people did fall, but Christ took it himself, "becoming a curse for us," so that "the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles" (Gal 3:13–14). Balaam's story also stands as a warning: it is possible to speak God's name, know his will and even carry his words, while the heart goes after reward. The donkey saw the angel; the seer did not. God gives sight to those who will receive it, not to those who sell it.
