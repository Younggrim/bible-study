*Setting:* Spring of AD 44, or shortly before. Chapter 11 ended with Barnabas and Saul carrying famine relief from Antioch to the elders in Jerusalem, and chapter 12 ends with them heading home (v25), so this chapter is told in the middle of that trip. The king is Herod Agrippa I, grandson of Herod the Great, who had tried to kill the infant Jesus, and nephew of Herod Antipas, who beheaded John the Baptist and mocked Jesus at his trial. Agrippa grew up in Rome among the imperial family, was a friend of the emperors Caligula and Claudius, and by AD 41 had been given his grandfather's whole kingdom. For three years Judea had a Jewish king again, and he worked hard to keep his Jewish subjects happy. Until now the church's opponents were the temple leaders. Here, for the first time, the state itself turns its sword on the apostles.

#### James Killed and Peter Imprisoned (vv1–5)
- **"Herod the king laid violent hands on some who belonged to the church" (v1).** "About that time" ties this to the famine years of chapter 11. "Laid violent hands" is literally "laid hands on to harm." Luke does not say why Agrippa started, but verse 3 hints at it: this was politics.
- **"He killed James the brother of John with the sword" (v2).** This is James son of Zebedee, one of Jesus' inner three with Peter and John, present at the transfiguration and in Gethsemane. When he and John asked for the best seats in the kingdom, Jesus asked whether they could drink his cup, and said, "The cup that I drink you will drink" (Mark 10:38–39). Here that word comes true. Execution "with the sword" was beheading, a Roman method. James is the first of the Twelve to die for Christ, and Luke gives it one verse. A tradition passed on by Clement of Alexandria and recorded by Eusebius holds that the man who brought James to trial was so moved by his witness that he confessed Christ and was beheaded with him.
- **No replacement.** When Judas fell, the apostles chose Matthias to keep the Twelve complete (1:15–26). When James dies, no one replaces him. Judas abandoned his office; James finished his. The Twelve were witnesses to Jesus' life and resurrection, a foundation laid once (Eph 2:20), not an office handed down.
- **"When he saw that it pleased the Jews" (v3).** "The Jews" in Acts often means the leaders and people of Jerusalem who rejected the gospel, not every Jewish person. Josephus describes Agrippa as careful to keep the law and eager to be popular in Jerusalem. Killing a leader of the sect that ate with Gentiles (11:3) was a cheap way to win favor. Guzik contrasts this with Saul's persecution, which was misguided but sincere. Herod's was simply calculated.
- **"During the days of Unleavened Bread" (v3).** Passover was followed by the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread, and Jerusalem was packed with pilgrims. Herod waited until "after the Passover" (v4) to bring Peter out for a public trial and execution, since a killing during the feast could stir the crowds. The chief priests had made the same calculation about Jesus (Mark 14:1–2). The parallels to Jesus' last Passover are deliberate: a festival, an arrest, a king, and a plan to bring the prisoner "out to the people."
- **"Four squads of soldiers" (v4).** A squad was four men, so sixteen soldiers rotated through the four watches of the night, four at a time: two chained to Peter and two guarding the door (v6). Herod remembered that the apostles had walked out of prison before (5:17–25), and he was taking no chances.
- **"Earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church" (v5).** The verse turns on its little word "but": Peter was kept in prison, *but* the church prayed. "Earnest" is *ektenōs*, from a root meaning "stretched out," so prayer that strains forward. The same word describes the believers' love in 1 Peter 1:22, and a related form describes Jesus praying "more earnestly" in Gethsemane (Luke 22:44). The Tyndale notes gather the promises that God answers such prayer (Luke 18:1–8; Jas 5:16). Prayer was the only weapon the church had against a king, and Luke wants readers to see it was enough.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that James's death ended any idea that the apostles were untouchable, and asks why God rescued Peter but not James. He gives no tidy answer, and neither does Luke. Guzik also calls *ektenōs* a medical word for a muscle stretched to its limit. The lexicon supports "stretched" in the root but gives the word's meaning simply as "fervently, earnestly," so the medical picture is a preacher's illustration rather than the word's sense. Henry: "prayers and tears are the church's arms; therewith she fights, not only against her enemies, but for her friends." JFB thinks the church met "in little groups in private houses," since gathering publicly "was evidently not safe," which fits the house of Mary in verse 12.

#### Peter Is Rescued (vv6–19)
- **"On that very night, Peter was sleeping between two soldiers" (v6).** This is the last night before his execution, and Peter is asleep. He had once been told that he would live to be old and die with his hands stretched out (John 21:18), so he may have known his time had not yet come. Either way, this is the man who could not stay awake in Gethsemane and then denied Jesus three times out of fear. Now he sleeps in chains. Psalm 4:8 fits: "In peace I will both lie down and sleep." Henry marvels at it: "sleep in the midst of his enemies—sleep when, it may be, they were awake."
- **"He struck Peter on the side and woke him" (v7).** Peter is so soundly asleep that the angel has to hit him. The verb is *patassō*, "strike." Luke will use the same verb in verse 23, when an angel "struck" Herod. One angelic blow wakes God's servant to freedom; the other ends a proud king's life.
- **"Get up quickly… Dress yourself and put on your sandals… wrap your cloak around you" (vv7–8).** There is no rush, despite "quickly." The angel waits for Peter to dress properly. It also echoes the first Passover night, when Israel was told to eat with "sandals on your feet" and leave in haste (Exod 12:11). It is Passover week, and God is bringing a prisoner out again.
- **"He thought he was seeing a vision" (v9).** Peter had seen visions recently (10:10–16), and this felt like one. Luke stresses that it was real. Chains really fell, guards were really passed, a gate really opened.
- **"The iron gate… opened for them of its own accord" (v10).** "Of its own accord" is *automatē*, which gives English "automatic." Mark 4:28 uses it of the earth producing grain "by itself." One manuscript tradition (Codex Bezae) adds that they "went down the seven steps," a vivid detail that may preserve local memory, though most scholars think it a later addition. The angel goes as far as Peter needs and no further: one street, and "immediately the angel left him." God did what Peter could not do, and left Peter to do what he could.
- **"Now I am sure that the Lord has sent his angel and rescued me" (v11).** "Rescued me from the hand of Herod" again sounds like the exodus, where God came down to "deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians" (Exod 3:8). Peter is delivered from both Herod "and from all that the Jewish people were expecting," the public execution they had been promised.
- **"The house of Mary, the mother of John whose other name was Mark" (v12).** Mary owned a house with a gateway and a servant, so she was well off, and her home was a meeting place for the Jerusalem believers. John was his Jewish name and Mark (*Marcus*) his Roman one. He was a cousin of Barnabas (Col 4:10), he will join Barnabas and Saul at the end of this chapter (v25), and tradition identifies him as the writer of the second Gospel, working from Peter's preaching (1 Pet 5:13). Some suggest the upper room of Jesus' last supper was in this house, but that is only a guess.
- **Rhoda (vv13–14).** Her name means "rose," and she is a slave girl. She recognizes Peter's voice and is so overjoyed that she runs to tell everyone, leaving the man the king wants dead standing in the street. It is one of the funniest moments in Acts, and Luke clearly enjoys it.
- **"You are out of your mind" (v15).** The church has been praying for Peter all night, and when the answer knocks on the door they refuse to believe it. It mirrors Easter morning, when the women brought news of the empty tomb and the apostles thought it "an idle tale" (Luke 24:11). The people praying were not faithless; they simply could not imagine God answering so fully. Luke does not scold them. He lets the scene teach.
- **"It is his angel!" (v15).** Many Jews believed each person had a guardian angel (compare Matt 18:10), and some thought that angel could look or sound like the person. Their guess may even mean they thought Peter was already dead and his angel had come. JFB calls the remark plain unbelief, much like the disciples' first response to the risen Jesus.
- **"Tell these things to James and to the brothers" (v17).** With the son of Zebedee dead, this James is almost certainly James the brother of Jesus, who came to faith after the resurrection (1 Cor 15:7), whom Paul calls a "pillar" (Gal 1:19; 2:9), and who leads the Jerusalem church at the council (15:13) and when Paul last visits (21:18). JFB instead thinks the apostle James son of Alphaeus is meant, a view few now hold. The verse quietly marks a handover: Peter is about to leave Jerusalem, and James will lead there.
- **"Then he departed and went to another place" (v17).** Luke will not say where, perhaps to protect people who sheltered him. Some Roman Catholic tradition has placed Peter's first journey to Rome here, but the text is silent, and Peter is back in Jerusalem for the council in chapter 15. From here on, Acts follows Paul.
- **"Ordered that they should be put to death" (v19).** Under Roman practice a guard who lost a prisoner could suffer the prisoner's penalty (compare 16:27; 27:42). Herod, robbed of his show trial, takes it out on the soldiers and leaves for **Caesarea**, the Roman capital on the coast and the city where Cornelius lived.
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes Peter's sleep as a picture of a man who had genuinely handed his life to God, and from the angel stopping after one street he draws the lesson that God does what only he can do and leaves to believers what they can do. He enjoys the church's disbelief at Rhoda's news as little faith in a great God, and he takes the James of verse 17 to be the Lord's brother, as do most modern scholars, against JFB. Henry says Peter's arrival was "both surprising and pleasing to them in the highest degree." JFB estimates the escape came in the fourth watch, between three and six in the morning, and calls Herod's execution of the guards "Impotent vengeance!"

#### The Death of Herod (vv20–25)
- **"Herod was angry with the people of Tyre and Sidon" (v20).** Tyre and Sidon were the great Phoenician ports north of Galilee, with plenty of trade but little farmland. Since Solomon's day they had bought grain from Israel (1 Kings 5:9–11; Ezek 27:17). Luke does not say what started the quarrel, but Herod had cut them off, and in famine years that was serious. They won over **Blastus, "the king's chamberlain,"** the official in charge of the royal bedchamber, a trusted insider. Bribery is likely but not stated.
- **"On an appointed day Herod put on his royal robes" (v21).** Josephus gives a fuller, independent account of the same day (Antiquities 19.343–350). Agrippa was at Caesarea for games in honor of the emperor. At daybreak he entered the theater in a robe woven entirely of silver, which blazed in the morning sun. His flatterers cried out that he was more than mortal, and he did not rebuke them. Immediately he was seized with violent pains in his stomach, was carried out, and died five days later, at about fifty-four years old.
- **"The voice of a god, and not of a man!" (v22).** The Phoenician delegation, eager to make peace, piled on the flattery. In the Roman world rulers were often honored as gods. Herod, a Jew who knew the first commandment, accepted it.
- **"Because he did not give God the glory" (v23).** Luke gives the reason in plain terms. Contrast Peter refusing Cornelius's worship, "Stand up; I too am a man" (10:26), and Paul and Barnabas tearing their clothes when Lystra wants to worship them (14:14–15). **"Eaten by worms"** is a single Greek word, *skōlēkobrōtos*, used only here in the New Testament. Some readers think of a parasitic infection or an intestinal obstruction, but Luke is not diagnosing. Jewish writers told the same kind of end for the proud king Antiochus IV (2 Macc 9:9), and the point is that God brought low a man who took glory that was his. Luke and Josephus agree on the place, the occasion, the royal splendor, the cry of "god," the sudden collapse and the speed of death. Josephus adds an owl as a bad omen; Luke names the angel of the Lord.
- **"But the word of God increased and multiplied" (v24).** This is one of Luke's progress reports (6:7; 19:20). The chapter opened with Herod killing an apostle and jailing another. It closes with Herod dead and the word growing. The verbs echo God's first blessing, "be fruitful and multiply" (Gen 1:28), and the growth of Israel in Egypt under a hostile king (Exod 1:7).
- **"Barnabas and Saul returned from Jerusalem when they had completed their service" (v25).** The "service" is the famine gift of 11:29–30. The best manuscripts actually read "returned *to* Jerusalem," which is hard to make sense of, since chapter 13 opens with them in Antioch. Some copies read "from," and the ESV follows that sense. One possibility is to read the Greek as "returned, having completed their service in Jerusalem." The meaning is clear either way: their errand done, they head back to Antioch. **"Bringing with them John, whose other name was Mark"** sets up chapter 13, where Mark will go with them, and turn back (13:13).
- **Commentators:** All three line Luke up with Josephus. JFB says "Josephus' account of his death is remarkably similar to this," and Guzik draws from the worms a picture of the inner corruption of pride. Henry's outline sums the scene as "the cutting off of Herod in the height of his pride by the stroke of an angel." Guzik ends with the chapter's own lesson: those who set themselves against God's word do not outlast it.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter opens at Passover with a king, an arrest and a plan to bring a prisoner out to the people, and Peter walks out of it free. The pattern belongs first to Jesus, who at Passover was handed over to death and came out of the tomb that no guard could hold. Herod takes the glory of a god and is struck down; Jesus, who truly is God, "humbled himself" to the cross and is now given "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:8–9). The church's only weapon in this chapter is prayer to him, and its quiet confidence is the last line before Paul's journeys begin: kings die, but "the word of God increased and multiplied."
