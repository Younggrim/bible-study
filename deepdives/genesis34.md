*Setting:* Jacob is back in Canaan. He has made peace with Esau (ch 33), spent some time at Succoth east of the Jordan, and now camps outside the city of Shechem, in the hill country between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim. He bought land there and built an altar to "God, the God of Israel" (33:18–20). But he has not gone on to Bethel, where he vowed to worship (28:20–22; 31:13). Dinah, his daughter by Leah (30:21), is probably in her teens. Her full brothers include Simeon and Levi, Leah's second and third sons. Dates are approximate, somewhere in the early second millennium BC. Chapter 34 is one of the darkest in Genesis. A young woman is raped, her attacker's family tries to settle the matter with a marriage deal, and her brothers answer with deceit and a massacre. God is not named anywhere in the chapter. Genesis tells the story plainly and lets the reader feel its weight, and the verdict on the brothers comes later, from their own father (49:5–7).

#### The Defiling of Dinah (vv1–31)
- **"Dinah the daughter of Leah… went out to see the women of the land" (v1).** Dinah is introduced through her mother, which explains why Leah's sons Simeon and Levi take the lead later. Why she went out is not explained. Some older commentators, Henry among them, blame her for curiosity or vanity. The text does not. It simply says she went to see the women of the area, and places the guilt squarely on the man who attacked her.
- **"He seized her and lay with her and humiliated her" (v2).** Shechem is the son of Hamor, "the Hivite, the prince of the land." The city and the man share a name. The narrator uses three short verbs in a row: "seized" (literally "took"), "lay with," and "humiliated." The last verb, *anah*, is defined in the lexicon as "humble, mishandle, afflict." It is used of Sarai's harsh treatment of Hagar (16:6) and of Egypt's oppression of Israel (15:13; Exod 1:11). The same verb describes Amnon forcing his half-sister Tamar (2 Sam 13:14) and appears in the later laws about sexual assault (Deut 22:24, 29). Most translators and interpreters read v2 as rape, and the ESV's "seized" reflects that. A few scholars have argued the verbs could describe sex without the family's consent rather than force. But the sequence of verbs, the brothers' reaction, and the word used in v2 all point to violence.
- **"His soul was drawn to Dinah… he loved the young woman and spoke tenderly to her" (v3).** The verb "drawn" is *dabaq*, "cling, cleave." The lexicon groups this verse with 2:24, where a man "shall hold fast to his wife." "Spoke tenderly" is literally "spoke to her heart," the phrase used of comforting someone (compare Isa 40:2; Hos 2:14). Shechem's desire does not undo his crime. The order matters. He took her first, then claimed to love her. The Tyndale notes call his "love" selfish and impetuous. The contrast with Amnon is telling: Amnon hated Tamar after he raped her (2 Sam 13:15), while Shechem wants to keep Dinah. But wanting to keep her is not the same as honoring her.
- **"Get me this girl for my wife" (v4).** He speaks like a man ordering a purchase. Marriages were normally arranged between fathers, and Shechem asks his father to do it. Dinah herself never speaks in the chapter. Her silence is one of the saddest features of the story.
- **"Jacob held his peace until they came" (v5).** Jacob hears that Shechem has "defiled" his daughter. The verb is *tame*, "be or become unclean," usually used of ritual impurity in Leviticus. Here it describes a moral defilement. The narrator says nothing about Jacob's grief, anger, or plans. He waits for his sons. Henry is kind to him: "The good man held his peace, as one astonished, that knows not what to say." The Tyndale notes are more critical. Jacob, the head of the family, does nothing, and that silence leaves the way open for his sons to act without wisdom. Compare David, who was "very angry" when Amnon raped Tamar but did nothing either (2 Sam 13:21), with similar results.
- **"An outrageous thing in Israel… such a thing must not be done" (v7).** The brothers come in from the fields "indignant and very angry," and rightly so. "Outrageous thing" is *nebalah*, which the lexicon defines as "disgraceful folly," especially of sexual sins, "a thing disgraceful according to Israel's standard." The same phrase appears in the law (Deut 22:21), in the horror of Judges 19–20 (Judg 20:6, 10), and in Tamar's plea to Amnon: "such a thing is not done in Israel; do not do this outrageous thing" (2 Sam 13:12). The Tyndale notes observe that this is the first time the name "Israel" refers to Jacob's family as a people. The family that received a new name at the Jabbok is now expected to live by a different standard from its neighbors.
- **Hamor's offer (vv8–10).** Hamor speaks to Jacob and his sons together. The ESV footnote says "your" in v8 is plural. He offers a marriage, then much more: intermarriage between the two peoples, land to "dwell and trade in," and the chance to "get property." He never apologizes. He never mentions what his son did. And Dinah is still in Shechem's house (v26) while the talks go on. There is an irony in "the land shall be open to you" (v10). God had already promised Jacob this land (28:13; 35:12). Now a Canaanite prince offers it in exchange for absorbing Jacob's family into his people. The Tyndale notes point out that this is exactly what later law forbade: Israel was not to intermarry with the Canaanites (Deut 7:1–5).
- **"Ask me for as great a bride-price and gift as you will" (v12).** The bride-price, *mohar*, is defined in the lexicon as the "purchase-price of wife." It was a payment from the groom's family to the bride's family, and it often served as security for the bride. The ESV footnote gives "engagement present" as an alternative for "gift." Later law dealt with this exact situation. A man who seduced or violated an unmarried woman had to pay the bride-price, and he had to marry her if her father agreed, without the right to divorce her (Exod 22:16–17; Deut 22:28–29). That law protected a woman whose prospects of marriage were otherwise ruined. Shechem is offering, in his own way, what that law would later require. But he makes the offer while holding Dinah in his house, and he speaks as if money can settle it.
- **"The sons of Jacob answered… deceitfully" (v13).** "Deceitfully" is *mirmah*, "deceit, treachery." The lexicon lists only one other use in Genesis: Isaac's words to Esau, "Your brother came deceitfully and took your blessing" (27:35). The sons of the deceiver have learned their father's trade. This time, the narrator explains, it is "because he had defiled their sister Dinah." Their anger is just. Their method is not.
- **"To one who is uncircumcised… would be a disgrace to us" (v14).** The brothers set a religious condition: every male in Shechem must be circumcised. Circumcision was the sign of God's covenant with Abraham (17:9–14). It marked a family set apart to God, and it was never meant as a ticket for joining Jacob's clan by contract. Other peoples in the region practiced circumcision too, which may be why the condition did not seem strange to Hamor. The brothers speak of becoming "one people" (v16). They have no intention of doing so. Even their threat, "we will take our daughter" (v17), quietly admits that Dinah is still in Shechem's hands.
- **"He delighted in Jacob's daughter" (v19).** Shechem agrees at once. The narrator notes that he was "the most honored of all his father's house." Everything he wants, he gets, or so it seems.
- **The speech at the gate (vv20–23).** The city gate was where public business was done, the town council and courthouse of an ancient city. Hamor and Shechem present the deal to the men of the city, and their version is different from the one they gave Jacob. They say nothing about Dinah or the rape. They call Jacob's family "at peace with us" (v21), using *shalem*, the word the narrator used in 33:18 for Jacob coming "safely" to Shechem. And they add a motive they kept from Jacob: "Will not their livestock, their property and all their beasts be ours?" (v23). Each side is deceiving the other. Hamor offered Jacob a share of the land, and tells his own people they will absorb Jacob's wealth.
- **"Every male was circumcised" (v24).** The phrase "all who went out of the gate of his city" probably means all the men of fighting age. The whole city agrees.
- **"On the third day, when they were sore" (v25).** Circumcision of an adult would leave a man in pain and unable to fight for several days. Simeon and Levi, "Dinah's brothers," take their swords and come against the city "while it felt secure." They kill every male, including Hamor and Shechem (v26). The narrator gives no detail beyond that, and none is needed. They take Dinah "out of Shechem's house." She had been there the whole time.
- **The plunder (vv27–29).** Then "the sons of Jacob," apparently the other brothers too, plunder the city: flocks, herds, donkeys, "all their wealth, all their little ones and their wives." The narrator gives the reason again: "because they had defiled their sister." The bitter irony is plain. To avenge the abuse of one woman, they carry off the women and children of a whole town as captives. A wrong done by one man is repaid on an entire city. The punishment is far out of proportion to the crime, and it falls on people who had no part in it.
- **"You have brought trouble on me by making me stink" (v30).** Jacob finally speaks, and what he says is revealing. "Trouble," "stink," "my numbers are few," "I shall be destroyed, both I and my household." The words "me," "I" and "my" fill the sentence. He says nothing about Dinah, nothing about the murdered men, nothing about the misuse of the covenant sign, and nothing about God. His concern is the family's safety among "the Canaanites and the Perizzites." It is a real danger, but it is not the deepest issue. The Tyndale notes observe that once again Jacob responds out of fear. Yet God will answer that fear: as the family leaves for Bethel, "a terror from God fell upon the cities" around them, and no one pursued (35:5).
- **"Should he treat our sister like a prostitute?" (v31).** The brothers get the last word, and the chapter ends on their question with no reply. They are right that Dinah was treated with contempt, used and then bargained for as though she could be paid for. But their answer to that wrong was itself a great wrong.
- **How should the chapter be judged?** Readers have long disagreed about Simeon and Levi. Some later Jewish writings praised them. In the book of Judith, the heroine prays to "the God of my father Simeon," who gave him a sword to avenge a violated virgin (Jdt 9:2), and the book of Jubilees commends Levi's zeal. Genesis itself points the other way. On his deathbed Jacob says of Simeon and Levi, "weapons of violence are their swords… in their anger they killed men… Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce" (49:5–7). He pronounces that they will be scattered in Israel. Simeon's tribe was later absorbed into Judah's territory (Josh 19:1, 9). Levi's tribe was scattered too, but its zeal was turned to God's service at the golden calf (Exod 32:26–29), and the Levites became the priestly tribe living in towns spread across the land. Most Christian interpreters follow Genesis 49: the outrage was justified, the massacre was not.
- **The chapter in the larger story.** Genesis 34 shows the danger of Jacob's family settling down among the Canaanites instead of going on to Bethel. In the next chapter God says, "Arise, go up to Bethel" (35:1), and Jacob's household puts away its foreign gods. The story also shows the cost of Jacob's own patterns. His favoritism and deceit, and now his passivity, are reproduced in his sons. And it foreshadows later stories in Israel's history: the rape of Tamar and Absalom's revenge (2 Sam 13), and the horror at Gibeah that led to civil war (Judg 19–21). Dinah is mentioned only once more in Scripture, in the list of those who went down to Egypt (46:15).
- **Commentators:** All three condemn the massacre and see Jacob's passivity as part of the problem. Guzik calls the chapter one of the most shameful episodes in Israel's history and sees in it the Bible's honesty about its heroes. He judges Dinah's going out unwise but puts the main blame on her parents' lack of care and on Jacob for settling near Shechem instead of going to Bethel. He argues that when Jacob failed to lead, his sons filled the gap sinfully, and that they used the covenant sign as a tool for murder. JFB calls Dinah "a simple, inexperienced, and vain young woman." Henry similarly says "she went to be seen too." Both blame her more than the text does, which places the guilt on Shechem. JFB is fairer to Shechem's family at first ("The prince and his son seem at first sight to have acted honestly"), but faults them for neither expressing regret nor returning Dinah. On v13 JFB says the brothers "made a show of religion—a cloak to cover their diabolical design." Henry is blunt about Simeon and Levi: "This was barbarous indeed," and "Those who unjustly grasp at that which is another's justly lose that which is their own," of the Shechemites' greed. On v30 he writes, "The gross misconduct of wicked children is the grief and shame of their godly parents." On v31 he asks whether nothing less than "the ruin of a whole city" would serve. Guzik is harder on Jacob than Henry is, noting that Jacob shows concern only for himself and not for justice or for the innocent dead. JFB has no notes on vv14–19, 21–29 or 31.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's name does not appear in this chapter, and there is no hero in it. A young woman is wronged, a father stays silent, and brothers answer evil with greater evil. The chapter shows how badly the family of promise needed a deliverer. Scripture's answer is not that wrongs like Dinah's do not matter. They matter so much that God promises to judge them perfectly: "Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord" (Rom 12:19). In Christ, justice and mercy meet. He treated women whom others used and despised with dignity (John 4; Luke 7:36–50; John 8:1–11). And instead of taking up the sword, he bore violence himself, so that guilty people like Simeon and Levi, and like Shechem, could be forgiven and made new. Even Levi's scattered line was turned to God's service, and the final priest of God's people is the one who "offered up himself" (Heb 7:27).
