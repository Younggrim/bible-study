*Setting:* Isaiah 60–62 form the bright center of the book's last section. Chapter 60 pictured Zion flooded with God's light, and chapter 61 introduced the Spirit-anointed one who brings good news to the poor and ended with a wedding: "as a bridegroom decks himself… as a bride adorns herself" (61:10). Chapter 62 picks up the wedding and the promise of a new Zion. Isaiah ministered in Jerusalem around 740–700 BC, but these chapters speak to God's people after the Babylonian exile, about a century and a half later, when Jerusalem lay in ruins and seemed abandoned by God. Many scholars assign chapters 56–66 to a later prophet or disciple of Isaiah writing to the returned exiles; others hold that Isaiah himself saw that future and wrote to it. Either way, the chapter answers the question every ruined city asks: has God forgotten us? The whole chapter is one ESV section.

#### Zion's Coming Salvation (vv1–12)
- **"For Zion's sake I will not keep silent" (v1).** Who speaks? Three answers are given. It may be the prophet, refusing to stop preaching and praying until God acts. It may be the LORD himself, who earlier said, "For a long time I have held my peace… now I will cry out" (42:14) and who swears in v8. Or it may be the Anointed One of 61:1, continuing his speech. The flow from chapter 61 favors the Anointed Servant, and the Tyndale notes read it that way: the Servant commits himself to keep proclaiming until Zion's glory comes. Whoever it is, the point is relentless commitment. Silence would mean giving up on Jerusalem.
- **"Until her righteousness goes forth as brightness, and her salvation as a burning torch" (v1).** "Righteousness" and "salvation" are paired throughout these chapters (51:5–8; 56:1; 59:16–17). Here righteousness means more than moral goodness. It is God putting his people right and vindicating them before the world. The images are light: the "brightness" of dawn (the same word in 60:3) and a blazing torch visible from far off. Compare Jesus: "You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden" (Matt 5:14).
- **"The nations shall see your righteousness, and all the kings your glory" (v2).** Kings came to Jerusalem before to conquer it. Now they come to see it (60:3, 10–11). The Tyndale notes observe that Zion's beauty would not lie in rebuilt walls or treasure but in the righteousness God gives her, and that is what draws others.
- **"You shall be called by a new name that the mouth of the LORD will give" (v2).** In the Bible a new name marks a new identity: Abram to Abraham (Gen 17:5), Jacob to Israel (Gen 32:28), Simon to Peter (Matt 16:18). Earlier Isaiah said Jerusalem would be called "the city of righteousness" (1:26), and Ezekiel's vision ends with the city's name "The LORD Is There" (Ezek 48:35). The risen Christ promises the one who conquers "a white stone, with a new name written on the stone" (Rev 2:17), and "I will write on him… my own new name" (Rev 3:12). Only God can rename a people, because only he can make them new.
- **"A crown of beauty in the hand of the LORD, and a royal diadem in the hand of your God" (v3).** Crowns go on heads, not in hands. The picture is of a king holding up his crown to admire it, or of a crown kept safe in his grasp. Zion, once trampled, is now God's treasure. Malachi uses a similar idea: "they shall be mine… in the day when I make up my treasured possession" (Mal 3:17). Paul calls his converts "my joy and crown" (Phil 4:1).
- **"No more be termed Forsaken… Desolate" (v4).** "Forsaken" (*ʿazubah*) was how exiled Zion described herself: "The LORD has forsaken me; my Lord has forgotten me" (49:14). God's answer then was that a mother may forget her nursing child, "yet I will not forget you" (49:15). "Desolate" (*shemamah*) is the word for land laid waste by war, as Isaiah foresaw in 1:7 and 6:11. The old names described Zion's history accurately. God does not deny they were true. He says they will no longer be true.
- **"My Delight Is in Her… Married" (v4).** In Hebrew the new names are *Hephzibah* ("my delight is in her") and *Beulah* ("married"), the forms the KJV keeps, which is where the hymn "Beulah Land" comes from. Hephzibah was also the name of King Hezekiah's wife, the mother of Manasseh (2 Kings 21:1). JFB sees her as a picture of Jerusalem, though Isaiah does not draw that link. In Israel an unmarried woman with no husband or children had no protector and no future, which is how the exile felt. To be "married" meant belonging, provision and protection. JFB: the marriage of the land to the LORD implies "protection as well as ownership."
- **"For the LORD delights in you" (v4).** This is the reason behind it all. God's delight, not Zion's merit, changes her name. Zephaniah sings the same truth: "he will rejoice over you with gladness… he will exult over you with loud singing" (Zeph 3:17).
- **"As a young man marries a young woman, so shall your sons marry you" (v5).** This line puzzles readers, since sons marrying their mother is an odd picture. The point is probably that the land will again be possessed and cared for by its own people, the way a bridegroom takes his bride. The Hebrew consonants for "your sons" (*banayik*) can also be read "your builder" (*bonayik*), with only the vowels changed, and some scholars and JFB prefer that reading: "so shall your Builder marry you," meaning God (compare 54:5: "your Maker is your husband"). The ESV keeps the traditional reading. Both readings end in the same place.
- **"As the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you" (v5).** Not grudging tolerance but the joy of a groom on his wedding day. The whole Bible moves toward this: Hosea's unfaithful wife won back (Hos 2:16–20), Jesus calling himself the bridegroom (Mark 2:19–20), Paul's "Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Eph 5:25), and the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:7–9).
- **"On your walls, O Jerusalem, I have set watchmen" (v6).** Watchmen stood on city walls day and night to spot danger and to announce messengers (52:8; Ezek 3:17). These watchmen do something different: they pray. The Tyndale notes take them as prophets, and they stand in contrast to Israel's failed watchmen of 56:10, who were "blind… silent dogs; they cannot bark." The broken walls are not even rebuilt yet, but God has already posted his watchmen on them.
- **"You who put the LORD in remembrance" (v6).** One Hebrew word, *mazkirim*, "those who cause to remember." The singular *mazkir* is the title of a royal officer, the "recorder" (2 Sam 8:16; 2 Kings 18:18), whose job was to keep the king's records and remind him of business that needed his attention. God does not forget, but he invites his people to hold his promises up before him in prayer. JFB calls them "the Lord's remembrancers."
- **"Take no rest, and give him no rest until he establishes Jerusalem" (vv6–7).** "Rest" here is *domi*, "quiet" or "silence": literally "no quiet to you… and give him no quiet." This is bold. God tells his people not to let him be quiet. Jesus told a parable along the same lines, of a widow who kept coming to an unjust judge, "to the effect that they ought always to pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1–8). Persistent prayer is not nagging an unwilling God. It is agreeing with a willing one until his promise is done.
- **"And makes it a praise in the earth" (v7).** The goal of the prayer is God's honor. A ruined Jerusalem made God look powerless. A restored Jerusalem would make the whole earth praise him (compare Jer 33:9).
- **"The LORD has sworn by his right hand and by his mighty arm" (v8).** People swear by something greater than themselves. God, having nothing greater, swears by himself (Heb 6:13). His "right hand" and "arm" stand for his strength in action: the "arm of the LORD" bared in 52:10 and revealed in 53:1. The Tyndale notes list the other times God swore an oath, to Noah, to Abraham (Gen 22:16) and to David (Ps 89:3).
- **"I will not again give your grain to be food for your enemies" (v8).** This reverses the covenant curse. Moses warned that if Israel turned from God, "a nation that you have not known shall eat up the fruit of your ground" (Deut 28:33; compare Judg 6:3–6, where the Midianites stripped Israel's harvests). The Tyndale notes point out that God himself had brought Assyria and Babylon on his people to purge them. He promises never to do it again.
- **"Those who garner it shall eat it and praise the LORD… drink it in the courts of my sanctuary" (v9).** The law commanded Israel to bring a tenth of the harvest to God's chosen place and eat it there, rejoicing before the LORD (Deut 14:22–26). The farmer who planted will be the one who eats, and he will eat it as worship. Harvest becomes a feast in God's house.
- **"Go through, go through the gates; prepare the way for the people" (v10).** The doubled commands ("go through, go through… build up, build up") ring with urgency, like the "Comfort, comfort" of 40:1 and "Awake, awake" of 51:9 and 52:1. In 40:3 the road was prepared for the LORD to come. In 57:14 it was cleared for his people. Here both come together, as the Tyndale notes observe. Exiles are coming home, and the way must be clear of every stumbling stone.
- **"Lift up a signal over the peoples" (v10).** A "signal" (*nes*) was a banner raised on a pole on a hilltop to rally people. In 5:26 God raised a signal to summon foreign armies *against* Judah. In 11:10–12 the root of Jesse stands as a signal to gather the nations and the scattered of Israel. Here the banner calls the world to Zion's salvation. Jesus said, "I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself" (John 12:32).
- **"Say to the daughter of Zion, 'Behold, your salvation comes'" (v11).** "Daughter of Zion" is a tender name for Jerusalem and her people. Notice that salvation is spoken of as a person: "your salvation comes; behold, *his* reward is with him." Zechariah echoes this: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion!… your king is coming to you" (Zech 9:9), and Matthew quotes this verse's opening words together with Zechariah's when Jesus rides into Jerusalem (Matt 21:5). The Hebrew word for salvation, *yeshaʿ*, is from the same root as the name *Yeshua*, Jesus.
- **"His reward is with him, and his recompense before him" (v11).** These words repeat 40:10 exactly. "Recompense" (*peʿullah*) is literally "work" or "wages," what is paid out for work done. JFB says the KJV's "work" should be "rather, recompense." The picture is of a king coming home from victory with the spoils, ready to give them out. Jesus closes the Bible with the same words: "Behold, I am coming soon, bringing my recompense with me" (Rev 22:12).
- **"The Holy People, The Redeemed of the LORD… Sought Out, A City Not Forsaken" (v12).** Four new names to finish. "Holy People" was Israel's calling from Sinai (Exod 19:6; Deut 7:6). "Redeemed" (*geʾulim*) comes from *goʾel*, the kinsman-redeemer who bought back a relative's land or freedom (Lev 25:25; Ruth 4). "Sought Out" means sought after, prized, pursued, the opposite of a cast-off wife whom no one wants. And "A City Not Forsaken" answers "Forsaken" in v4 directly. The chapter opened with Zion's shame and closes with her names of honor. Peter applies this vocabulary to the church: "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession" (1 Pet 2:9).
- **Fulfilment.** Interpreters read these promises in layers. They were partly fulfilled when exiles came home under Cyrus and rebuilt Jerusalem. Christian readers see their deeper fulfilment in Christ and his church, the bride drawn from every nation. Many also expect a future restoration of Israel itself, and all look to the final new Jerusalem "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Rev 21:2). These views differ on how much belongs to ethnic Israel, but they agree that the chapter's hope rests on God's own delight and oath.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads v1 as the LORD speaking, while JFB allows either the prophet, as "representative of all the praying people of God," or the Messiah, and the Tyndale notes take it as the Servant. JFB adds that Zion's righteousness is "imputed to it," given rather than earned. Henry sees the prophet resolving "to apply closely and constantly" to preaching and praying, and says God sets watchmen "for the encouragement of his people during the delays of their deliverance." Guzik stresses that the watchmen are prayer warriors, not critics, and that "give him no rest" applies to all God's people. On the oath and harvest, Henry reads the church as promised to "enjoy great plenty." JFB proposes "thy Builder" in v5, which the ESV does not follow, and explains a crown held in the hand as Zion kept "under the Lord's protection." Guzik ties v11's "his reward is with him" to Revelation 22:12 and Christ's rewarding his people's work (1 Cor 3:8–14).

#### Pointing to Christ
The tireless voice of v1 who will not keep silent fits Jesus, who "always lives to make intercession" for his people (Heb 7:25), and the watchmen who give God no rest are his praying church joining him. The bridegroom who rejoices over his bride is the Christ who gave himself for her (Eph 5:25–27). And the salvation that "comes" with its reward in hand is not a thing but a person, the King who rode into Jerusalem on a donkey (Matt 21:5) and who says at the Bible's end, "Behold, I am coming soon." Because of him, people once named Forsaken are called Sought Out.
