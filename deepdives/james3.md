*Setting:* James, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, is writing to scattered Jewish believers, probably in the 40s or 50s. He has already said to be "quick to hear, slow to speak" (1:19) and that religion without a bridled tongue is "worthless" (1:26). Chapter 2 attacked a faith that is all words and no works. Chapter 3 now takes up the words themselves. James begins with those who teach, whose main tool is speech, and then widens to everyone: the tongue is small but steers the whole life, sets fires it cannot put out, and cannot be tamed by any human effort. The second half asks who is truly wise. In the ancient world wisdom was shown in speech, and James insists it is shown in a gentle, peaceable life. The two halves belong together, and they prepare for the quarrels he will confront in 4:1–3.

#### Taming the Tongue (vv1–12)
- **"Not many of you should become teachers, my brothers" (v1).** In the early church teachers were honored, listed near apostles and prophets (Acts 13:1; 1 Cor 12:28), and the synagogue had its respected rabbis. For believers who were poor and looked down on (2:6), teaching may have offered status. James does not forbid teaching but warns against rushing into it. Jesus had warned against seeking the title "Rabbi" (Matt 23:8).
- **"We who teach will be judged with greater strictness" (v1).** James counts himself among them. Teachers shape what others believe and how they live, so they answer for more (Luke 12:48; Heb 13:17). Jesus said that people will give account "for every careless word they speak" (Matt 12:36). Teachers have many words to answer for.
- **"For we all stumble in many ways" (v2).** "Stumble" (*ptaiō*) means to trip, to make a misstep, not to fall away completely. James is honest: he includes himself and everyone. The verb is the same one he used in 2:10, "fails in one point."
- **"If anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man" (v2).** "Perfect" (*teleios*) is the "mature" of 1:4. The tongue is the hardest member to control, so whoever controls it can control everything else, **"able also to bridle his whole body."** "Bridle" picks up 1:26 and leads into the next picture.
- **"Bits into the mouths of horses… we guide their whole bodies" (v3).** A small bit of metal in a horse's mouth turns a large, powerful animal wherever the rider wants. The point is the contrast of size and influence.
- **"Look at the ships also… guided by a very small rudder" (v4).** Ancient ships were steered by one or two large oars at the stern, small compared with the ship. Even a vessel "driven by strong winds" goes where the pilot's small rudder sends it. The grain ship that carried Paul to Malta held 276 people (Acts 27:37). The tongue, likewise, steers the course of a life.
- **"So also the tongue is a small member, yet it boasts of great things" (v5).** "Boasts of great things" is one word, *megalaucheō*: to talk big, to brag. The small tongue has an outsized opinion of itself, and an outsized effect.
- **"How great a forest is set ablaze by such a small fire!" (v5).** The images shift from steering to destruction. In the dry summer of the eastern Mediterranean, one spark could burn a whole hillside of brush and trees. The Greek word for "forest" can also mean brushwood or timber, and either way the image is a small flame and vast damage. Proverbs says the same: "Where there is no wood, the fire goes out, and where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases" (Prov 26:20).
- **"The tongue is a fire, a world of unrighteousness" (v6).** This is one of the hardest verses in the letter to construe, and translations punctuate it differently. "A world of unrighteousness" probably means the tongue holds within itself a whole world of evil. The "world" in James is the order of life set against God (1:27; 4:4). The tongue brings that world right into the body.
- **"Staining the whole body" (v6).** "Staining" (*spiloō*) is the verb behind "unstained" in 1:27, where pure religion keeps oneself "unstained from the world." The tongue is the main way the world's stain gets in.
- **"Setting on fire the entire course of life" (v6).** Literally "the wheel of genesis," the wheel of birth or existence. Greek writers used similar phrases, sometimes of a cycle of rebirth, but James almost certainly means it in the ordinary sense: the whole round of human life from birth onward. The lexicon simply gives "wheel." The tongue can set the whole of a life, from its start to its end, on fire.
- **"And set on fire by hell" (v6).** "Hell" is *Gehenna*, the Valley of Hinnom just south of Jerusalem, where kings of Judah once burned their children to idols (2 Kgs 23:10; Jer 7:31). It became a picture of final judgment. Outside the Gospels, where Jesus uses it eleven times, this is the only place the word appears in the New Testament. The point is the source behind destructive speech: the devil, "a liar and the father of lies" (John 8:44).
- **"Every kind of beast and bird, of reptile and sea creature, can be tamed" (v7).** The four categories recall Genesis 1:26 and 9:2, where God gives humanity dominion over animals. Ancient people trained lions, elephants, birds and snakes, just as people still do. **"But no human being can tame the tongue" (v8).** The creature made to rule the animals cannot rule his own mouth. James does not say God cannot tame it. He says no human can, which is why the help must come from above (1:5, 17; 3:17).
- **"A restless evil, full of deadly poison" (v8).** "Restless" (*akatastatos*) is the word used of the "unstable" double-minded man in 1:8. The tongue is like a wild animal pacing its cage. "Deadly poison" echoes Psalm 140:3, "under their lips is the venom of asps," quoted by Paul in Romans 3:13. Many also hear the serpent in Eden, whose words first poisoned humanity (Gen 3:1–5).
- **"With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse people" (v9).** Devout Jews blessed God many times a day ("Blessed are you, O Lord…"), and believers did the same in worship. James is not imagining wicked people but church members, "we," who sing to God on Sunday and run down their neighbors on Monday.
- **"Who are made in the likeness of God" (v9).** "Likeness" is the word of Genesis 1:26 in the Greek Old Testament. Every human being, however difficult, bears God's image. Genesis 9:6 makes this the reason murder is so serious. James applies it to words: cursing a person made in God's image is an insult to God himself.
- **"From the same mouth come blessing and cursing. My brothers, these things ought not to be so" (v10).** A mild-sounding phrase for something unnatural. The examples that follow show that nature itself does not behave this way.
- **"Does a spring pour forth from the same opening both fresh and salt water?" (v11).** In a dry land, springs were life. A spring that sometimes gave fresh water and sometimes bitter was useless. "Salt" here is literally "bitter" (*pikros*), a word James will use again in v14 for "bitter jealousy." The Tyndale notes add that some springs near the Jordan valley did run salty and could not support a town.
- **"Can a fig tree… bear olives, or a grapevine produce figs?" (v12).** Fig, olive and vine were the three great crops of the land. Jesus used the same reasoning: "Are grapes gathered from thornbushes?" (Matt 7:16), and "out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks" (Luke 6:44–45). What comes out reveals what is inside. James is not saying a Christian never sins in speech (v2). He is saying that a steady mixture of praise and cursing is a contradiction that should alarm us.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns against those who "give yourselves the air of teachers, imposers, and judges," and says, "There is such an abundance of sin in the tongue that it may be called a world of iniquity." He adds, "Hell has more to do in promoting of fire of the tongue than men are generally aware of," and "No man can tame the tongue without supernatural grace and assistance." On v9: "If we bless God as our Father, it should teach us to speak well of, and kindly to, all who bear his image." JFB translates v6 "The tongue, that world of iniquity, is a fire" and takes "course of nature" as "the orb (cycle) of creation." It also notes that man "has lost the likeness of God" yet keeps enough of it to be honored in others. Guzik observes that silence is not the cure, any more than keeping a horse in the barn is horsemanship. The real question is who holds the reins, and he points to the Spirit of God.

#### Wisdom from Above (vv13–18)
- **"Who is wise and understanding among you?" (v13).** The pairing "wise and understanding" echoes Deuteronomy 1:13, where Moses tells Israel to choose "wise, understanding" men as leaders. James is likely still thinking of those who wanted to teach (v1). In Jewish thought wisdom is practical: knowing how to live in God's way (1:5; Prov 9:10).
- **"By his good conduct let him show his works in the meekness of wisdom" (v13).** Wisdom is proved the way faith is proved in chapter 2: "show" it by works (2:18). The mark of real wisdom is "meekness" (*prautēs*), gentle strength that does not push itself forward. Jesus called himself "gentle and lowly in heart" (Matt 11:29), and Moses, Israel's great teacher, was "very meek" (Num 12:3).
- **"If you have bitter jealousy and selfish ambition in your hearts" (v14).** "Jealousy" (*zēlos*) can be a good word, "zeal," but here it is "bitter," the same word as the bitter spring in v11. "Selfish ambition" (*eritheia*) originally meant working for hire, then canvassing for office and building a faction. The lexicon gives "ambition, self-seeking, rivalry." Paul lists it among the works of the flesh (Gal 5:20) and warns against doing anything from it (Phil 2:3). It describes someone who uses teaching to build a following.
- **"Do not boast and be false to the truth" (v14).** Boasting about wisdom while the heart is full of rivalry is a lie against the very truth one claims to teach.
- **"This is not the wisdom that comes down from above, but is earthly, unspiritual, demonic" (v15).** Three steps downward. "Earthly" means limited to this world's values. "Unspiritual" (*psychikos*) means merely natural, without God's Spirit, the word Paul uses for the "natural person" who does not accept the things of the Spirit (1 Cor 2:14; Jude 19). "Demonic" (*daimoniōdēs*), found only here in the New Testament, means having the character of demons, or coming from them. Like the tongue "set on fire by hell" (v6), this so-called wisdom has its source below.
- **"Where jealousy and selfish ambition exist, there will be disorder and every vile practice" (v16).** "Disorder" (*akatastasia*) is the noun from "unstable" (1:8) and "restless" (3:8). The double-minded man, the restless tongue and the disordered church all share one root. Paul uses the same word: "God is not a God of confusion but of peace" (1 Cor 14:33).
- **"But the wisdom from above" (v17).** This is the wisdom James told his readers to ask for (1:5), part of "every good gift… from above" (1:17). It is described not by what it knows but by how it behaves. There are eight qualities, and it reads like a portrait of Jesus.
- **"First pure" (v17).** *Hagnos*, free from defilement and mixed motives. "First" means it comes first in importance and makes the rest possible.
- **"Then peaceable, gentle, open to reason" (v17).** "Peaceable" means peace-loving and peace-making. "Gentle" (*epieikēs*) means reasonable, considerate, not insisting on one's full rights (Phil 4:5). "Open to reason" (*eupeithēs*), only here in the New Testament, means willing to listen and be persuaded, the opposite of the stubborn teacher who must win every argument.
- **"Full of mercy and good fruits" (v17).** Mercy, which "triumphs over judgment" (2:13), and practical deeds, the "works" of chapter 2. Wisdom from above helps the poor brother, not only advises him.
- **"Impartial and sincere" (v17).** "Impartial" is *adiakritos*, from the verb used for the doubter in 1:6 and the favoritism in 2:4. The lexicon lists two possible senses: "without uncertainty," not wavering, or "without making distinctions," not playing favorites. Both fit James, and he may intend the echo of both earlier passages. "Sincere" (*anypokritos*) means literally "without hypocrisy," not playing a part like an actor (*hypokritēs*) on a stage.
- **"A harvest of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace" (v18).** The picture is a farmer sowing seed. Those who make peace plant seed that grows into right living, in themselves and in the community. It echoes "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matt 5:9) and Isaiah 32:17, "the effect of righteousness will be peace." Just as "the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (1:20), only peace produces that harvest. The verse also prepares for the next chapter's question: "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you?" (4:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Wisdom produces meekness, and meekness increases wisdom," and "Holy zeal and bitter envying are as different as the flames of seraphim and the fire of hell." On v17 he writes, "Peace follows purity, and depends upon it," and calls this wisdom "very persuadable, either to what is good or from what is evil." JFB matches the three marks of false wisdom in v15 to "the world, the flesh, and the devil," and explains that purity is put first "because it has respect both to God and to ourselves." It notes that zeal itself is not condemned, only the bitter kind. Guzik calls meekness strength under control, not weakness, and explains *epieikēs* as knowing when to set aside strict justice for the sake of mercy. He also says willingness to yield is not the same as being weak.

#### Pointing to Christ
The "perfect man" who never stumbled in what he said (v2) has lived. Jesus "committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth," and "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return" (1 Pet 2:22–23). The tongue that no human can tame was perfectly governed in him, and his words were "spirit and life" (John 6:63). The list of wisdom's qualities in v17 describes him exactly: pure, peaceable, gentle, open to the outcast, full of mercy, without favoritism or pretense. He is "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24), and the supreme peacemaker, "making peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20). The wisdom from above that James urges believers to ask for is, in the end, the life of Christ formed in them.
