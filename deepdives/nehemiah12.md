*Setting:* Jerusalem in the time of Nehemiah, governor of Judah from 445 BC. Chapter 11 placed the people in their city and towns. Chapter 12 does two things. First it lists the priests and Levites across about a century, from the first return under Zerubbabel and Jeshua in 538 BC down to the high priest Jaddua several generations later. Then it describes the great day the wall was dedicated, which the whole book has been building toward. Two choirs march in opposite directions on top of the wall and meet at the temple, and "the joy of Jerusalem was heard far away." The dedication probably took place soon after the wall was finished (6:15), though some scholars place it later, after the events of chapters 8–11. Nehemiah tells it in the first person again ("Then I brought the leaders of Judah up onto the wall"), so this is from his own memoir. The chapter closes with a short, bright picture of the temple service running as it should, just before chapter 13 shows how quickly it fell apart.

#### Priests and Levites (vv1–26)
- **"The priests and the Levites who came up with Zerubbabel the son of Shealtiel, and Jeshua" (v1).** Zerubbabel was the heir of David's line, and Jeshua the high priest (Ezra 2:2; 3:2). This first list gives the twenty-two heads of priestly houses at the first return. The Tyndale notes stress why such records mattered: only those who could prove priestly descent could serve at the altar (7:63–65).
- **"Seraiah, Jeremiah, Ezra" (v1).** These are names of priestly houses. The Ezra here lived nearly a century before Ezra the scribe, and the Jeremiah is not the prophet. Many of these names reappear in the covenant list of 10:2–8, signed by later heads of the same houses.
- **"Iddo… Abijah" (v4).** Iddo's house produced the prophet Zechariah, "the son of Berechiah, son of Iddo" (Zech 1:1). His name appears in v16 as head of Iddo's house a generation later. Abijah's division was the one to which Zechariah the father of John the Baptist belonged, more than four centuries later (Luke 1:5). JFB makes the same link.
- **"Mattaniah, who with his brothers was in charge of the songs of thanksgiving" (vv8–9).** Two groups of Levites "stood opposite" each other, singing in turns. The Tyndale notes call this antiphonal singing, a call-and-response style. Many psalms seem written for it, with a line and a reply (Ps 136, with its refrain "for his steadfast love endures forever"). Ezra 3:11 says the first returnees "sang responsively" at the temple's foundation.
- **The high priests (vv10–11).** Six generations are listed: Jeshua, Joiakim, Eliashib, Joiada, Jonathan and Jaddua. Eliashib was high priest in Nehemiah's day and helped build the wall (3:1), but he later gave Tobiah a room in the temple (13:4–7). One of Joiada's sons married Sanballat's daughter (13:28). After the kings were gone, the succession of high priests became the backbone of Jewish history, as JFB notes.
- **"Jonathan" and "Johanan" (vv11, 22).** Most scholars think these are the same man. A letter from Jewish soldiers at Elephantine in Egypt, dated about 407 BC, appeals to "Johanan the high priest" in Jerusalem, which fits this list.
- **"The heads of fathers' houses" in the days of Joiakim (vv12–21).** The second list gives the next generation, the heads of the same priestly houses under Jeshua's son Joiakim. Only Hattush is missing. The house of "Ezra" was led by Meshullam (v13), and the house of Iddo by Zechariah (v16). Families carried their callings from generation to generation.
- **"In the reign of Darius the Persian" (v22).** Which Darius is meant is debated. Most think Darius II (423–404 BC), whose reign fits the high priests named. Josephus tells a story of a high priest Jaddua meeting Alexander the Great in 332 BC, during the reign of Darius III, which would make this list run later. Either the list was updated by a later hand, or there was more than one Jaddua. Josephus's story is legend at least in part. Either way, the list shows the record being kept up to date after Nehemiah's time.
- **"Written in the Book of the Chronicles" (v23).** This was probably an official record of temple families, not the biblical books of Chronicles, though those books draw on records like it.
- **"According to the commandment of David the man of God, watch by watch" (v24).** David organized the Levites into divisions for music and praise (1 Chr 23–25). "Man of God" is a title usually given to prophets, and here it honors David's role in shaping worship. The returnees did not invent new forms of worship. They took up the pattern David had set.
- **"In the days of Nehemiah the governor and of Ezra, the priest and scribe" (v26).** The civil and religious leaders are named together, as in 8:9.
- **Commentators:** JFB says only four priestly courses returned from exile, and Zerubbabel or Jeshua restored the twenty-four from them, keeping the old names. On Jaddua he reports that many think he was the high priest whose "dignified appearance, solemn manner, and splendid costume" impressed Alexander. He adds that if not, the Alexander story may concern a later man of the same name. Guzik treats the lists as a record of faithful continuity, generation following generation in God's service. The Tyndale notes date Joiakim's priesthood to around the early years of Ezra's work.

#### Dedication of the Wall (vv27–43)
- **"At the dedication of the wall of Jerusalem" (v27).** "Dedication" is *chanukkah*, the word also used for the dedication of the altar (Num 7:10–11) and the temple (Ezra 6:16–17). Centuries later, the rededication of the temple after the Maccabean revolt (164 BC) gave the Jewish festival of Hanukkah its name (John 10:22). Dedicating the wall meant handing the whole city over to God.
- **"They sought the Levites in all their places" (v27).** Levites lived in villages across the province (11:36). So few lived in the city that they had to be gathered from the countryside. "With gladness, with thanksgivings and with singing, with cymbals, harps, and lyres": the same instruments David used when he brought the ark to Jerusalem (1 Chr 15:16, 28).
- **"The singers had built for themselves villages around Jerusalem" (vv28–29).** Netophah was near Bethlehem. Geba and Azmaveth were in Benjamin, a few miles north. Beth-gilgal may be Gilgal near Jericho. The singers lived close enough to come in for their rotations of service.
- **"The priests and the Levites purified themselves, and they purified the people and the gates and the wall" (v30).** Purification probably included washing, washing clothes, keeping away from anything ritually unclean and offering sacrifices (Exod 19:10, 14–15; Num 8:5–7, 21). The order matters: first the leaders, then the people, then the stones. Even the wall was made holy, because the whole city now belonged to God.
- **"Then I brought the leaders of Judah up onto the wall" (v31).** Nehemiah's own voice returns. The procession probably started on the western side of the city, near the Valley Gate, where Nehemiah had first gone out by night to inspect the ruins (2:13).
- **"Two great choirs that gave thanks" (v31).** The Hebrew for "choirs" is *todot*, literally "thanksgivings." The choirs were named for what they did. They marched along the top of the wall, wide enough to walk on. Tobiah had once sneered that "if a fox goes up on it he will break down their stone wall" (4:3). Now hundreds of people walk it singing.
- **"One went to the south on the wall to the Dung Gate" (vv31–37).** The first choir went south and then up the eastern side of the city, past the Fountain Gate and "the stairs of the city of David," to the Water Gate near the temple. Ezra the scribe led it (v36). Hoshaiah and half the leaders of Judah followed, with priests carrying trumpets and Levites playing "the musical instruments of David the man of God." Zechariah, its trumpeter, was a descendant of Asaph (v35).
- **"The other choir… went to the north, and I followed them" (vv38–39).** The second choir went north and then east around the city with Nehemiah and the other half of the people. They passed the Tower of the Ovens, the Broad Wall, the Gate of Ephraim, the Fish Gate, the Tower of Hananel and the Tower of the Hundred, to the Sheep Gate. These are the same gates and towers named in the building account (3:1–8, 11). Each section repaired by a family in chapter 3 is now walked over in praise.
- **"So both choirs of those who gave thanks stood in the house of God" (v40).** The two processions met at the temple. The circle of the city was closed by praise, and everything led to the house of God. Some readers suggest that Psalm 48 pictures this kind of procession: "Walk about Zion, go around her, number her towers" (Ps 48:12). The Tyndale notes suggest that Psalm 147, which praises the God who "builds up Jerusalem" and "strengthens the bars of your gates" (Ps 147:2, 13), may have been written for this day.
- **"They offered great sacrifices that day and rejoiced" (v43).** The root for "joy" (*samach*) appears five times in this one verse: they rejoiced, God made them rejoice, with great joy, the women and children rejoiced, and the joy of Jerusalem. "God had made them rejoice": the joy was his gift.
- **"The women and children also rejoiced" (v43).** As at the reading of the Law (8:2), everyone was present. This was not only for the officials and choirs.
- **"The joy of Jerusalem was heard far away" (v43).** About ninety years earlier, at the laying of the temple's foundation, "the sound was heard far away," but it was a mixture of weeping and joy that no one could tell apart (Ezra 3:12–13). Now there is no weeping. Nehemiah's enemies had heard the news of the wall with anger (4:1, 7). Now the neighboring villages would hear the singing.
- **Commentators:** Henry compares the fear that marked the building with the joy of the dedication: "Those that sow in tears shall thus reap" (Ps 126:5). He stresses the order of v30: "They purified themselves and then the people," since those who would cleanse others must first be clean. He sees the purifying water as a picture of Christ's blood cleansing the conscience (Heb 9:14). On v43 he writes that "the common people testified theirs by loud shouts, which were heard afar off," praise God accepts even without artistry. He concludes, "Great mercies call for the most solemn returns of praise." JFB says the dedication thanked God for the finished work and honored Jerusalem as "the holy city," and that "the joy of Jerusalem was heard even afar off." Guzik notes that the choirs were named for thanksgiving because true worship always includes gratitude. He adds that the order of purification shows worship must come from clean hearts, not only stirred emotions, and he points to 1 John 1:9 as the way of cleansing for Christians.

#### Service at the Temple (vv44–47)
- **"On that day men were appointed over the storerooms" (v44).** The dedication was not only a party. On the same day, men were put in charge of collecting the tithes and offerings promised in the covenant (10:35–39). The celebration became an ongoing system.
- **"For Judah rejoiced over the priests and the Levites who ministered" (v44).** The people were glad to support those who served. Their giving came from joy, not reluctance.
- **"The service of purification" (v45).** The Levites, singers and gatekeepers kept the temple ritually clean and guarded it from anything unclean (1 Chr 23:28). JFB says they guarded "the ward of the purification."
- **"According to the command of David and his son Solomon" (v45).** David planned the temple's worship, and Solomon set it in place when the temple was built (2 Chr 8:14). The returnees saw themselves as continuing that pattern.
- **"In the days of David and Asaph there were directors of the singers" (v46).** The singers' tradition went back five centuries. Asaph was David's chief musician (1 Chr 16:5, 7).
- **"All Israel in the days of Zerubbabel and in the days of Nehemiah gave the daily portions" (v47).** The writer joins the first return and Nehemiah's own day into one picture. The people supported the singers and gatekeepers, the Levites passed on a tenth to the priests, "the sons of Aaron," and the system worked as it should (Num 18:21–28). It is an ideal snapshot, and the next chapter shows it did not last (13:10).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains "the portions of the law" as portions "prescribed by the law" and says the Levites in turn tithed to the priests. He gives two possible reasons for the joy in v44: the restored temple service or the faithfulness of the priests and Levites. Guzik draws three themes from these verses: generous giving, an ongoing commitment to purity, and setting apart what belongs to God.

#### Pointing to Christ
On the day of dedication, the city was purified, encircled with thanksgiving and handed over to God, and its joy was heard far away. It was the high point of the book, and it lasted only a short time. The Bible's story points to another dedication. Jesus walked in the temple at Hanukkah, the Feast of Dedication (John 10:22–30), and told the crowds that his sheep "will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand." He purified his people not with water or the blood of animals, but with his own blood (Heb 9:13–14). Revelation pictures a city with walls of jasper and gates of pearl, where "nothing unclean will ever enter it" (Rev 21:18–27). Its joy will never fade, because "the Lamb is its lamp" (Rev 21:23).
