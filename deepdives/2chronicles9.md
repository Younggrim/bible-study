*Setting:* The height of Solomon's reign, sometime in the middle of the 900s BC. The temple and palace are finished, worship is in order, and ships sail from the Red Sea for gold (ch 8). Chapter 9 shows the world coming to Jerusalem: a foreign queen, the kings of Arabia and "all the kings of the earth." It follows 1 Kings 10 almost line for line, and then closes Solomon's story with his death. What it leaves out is striking. Between the queen's visit and Solomon's death, Kings tells of his many foreign wives, his idolatry in old age and the enemies God raised against him (1 Kgs 11). Chronicles passes over all of that. Its readers knew the story from Kings. The Chronicler wanted them to remember Solomon as the builder of the temple and as a picture of what the kingdom of God's chosen king could be.

#### The Queen of Sheba (vv1–12)
- **"The queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon" (v1).** Sheba (Hebrew *Sheva*) was a kingdom in southwest Arabia, in modern Yemen, about 1,200 to 1,500 miles from Jerusalem by caravan. It grew rich on frankincense and myrrh, which were carried north on camels. Ethiopian tradition holds that she was an Ethiopian queen, and there were Sabean settlements across the Red Sea in Africa. Most scholars favor Yemen, but the two views can overlap. Her visit was probably partly a trade mission. Solomon's ships on the Red Sea (8:17–18) now competed with her caravans.
- **"To test him with hard questions" (v1).** "Hard questions" (*chidot*) are riddles or puzzling sayings, the word used of Samson's riddle (Judg 14:12). Testing a ruler's wisdom with riddles was a known practice among ancient courts. **"She told him all that was on her mind"**, literally "all that was in her heart." This was more than a game.
- **"There was nothing hidden from Solomon that he could not explain" (v2).** This is God's gift of wisdom from 1:10–12 on display.
- **What she saw (vv3–4).** His wisdom, his house, his table, his officials, his servants' uniforms, his cupbearers, "and his burnt offerings that he offered at the house of the LORD." Kings has the same list (1 Kgs 10:5). The older KJV reads "his ascent by which he went up unto the house of the LORD," because the Hebrew word in Chronicles (*ʿaliyyato*) can mean "his going up." Kings has a very similar word that means "his burnt offering" (*ʿolato*), and the ESV follows that sense in both books. JFB, working from the KJV, thought of a grand stairway or viaduct up to the temple. Either way, the list ends at the house of God, the source of Solomon's glory.
- **"There was no more breath in her" (v4).** She was left breathless, overwhelmed.
- **"Half the greatness of your wisdom was not told me" (v6).** She came as a doubter: "I did not believe the reports until I came and my own eyes had seen it." Seeing for herself changed her mind.
- **"Happy are your men! Happy are these your servants" (v7).** "Happy" is *ʾashre*, the word that opens the Psalms: "Blessed is the man" (Ps 1:1).
- **"Blessed be the LORD your God, who… set you on his throne as king for the LORD your God" (v8).** Kings has "on the throne of Israel" (1 Kgs 10:9). Chronicles makes it *God's* throne. This is one of the Chronicler's great themes: the kingdom of Israel is really the LORD's kingdom, and David's sons sit on it for him (1 Chr 17:14; 28:5; 29:23). The king's job is also stated: "that you may execute justice and righteousness."
- **Did she come to faith?** She blesses "the LORD your God," using his covenant name. Some read this as real faith. Others note that ancient visitors often honored the god of the land they were in, and it may be polite praise of Israel's national God. The text does not tell us. What it does show is a Gentile ruler who came from the ends of the earth and spoke of Israel's God with praise, a picture of the nations coming to Zion (Isa 60:3–6; Ps 72:10–11, 15).
- **"120 talents of gold, and a very great quantity of spices" (v9).** About four and a half tons of gold, the same amount Hiram once sent (1 Kgs 9:14). "There were no spices such as those." Frankincense and myrrh were costly because they came by long caravan routes, taxed at every stop. Centuries later, wise men from the east brought "gold and frankincense and myrrh" to a different king in Judah (Matt 2:11; compare Isa 60:6).
- **"Algum wood" (vv10–11).** Kings spells it *almug* (1 Kgs 10:11–12). No one is sure what tree it was. Suggestions include red sandalwood, from India or Sri Lanka, or a juniper from Lebanon (2:8 lists algum among the woods of Lebanon). From it Solomon made **"supports"** (*mesillot*, an uncertain word, perhaps steps or railings, or in Kings "supports") and **"lyres also and harps for the singers."** The Chronicler, who loves the Levites' music, notes that nothing like these instruments had been seen before.
- **"Solomon gave to the queen of Sheba all that she desired… besides what she had brought" (v12).** Solomon sends her home with more than she gave. The king's generosity outdoes the visitor's gifts.
- **Commentators:** Henry: she "put herself to a great deal of trouble and expense to hear the wisdom of Solomon." He also notes that she traced the happiness of king and kingdom to "the fountain of all bliss, the divine favour." Guzik admires how she sought, travelling far, asking questions and staying long. He presses Jesus' words that "the queen of the South will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it… and behold, something greater than Solomon is here" (Matt 12:42). Guzik is cautious about whether her praise meant saving faith, and he mentions the Ethiopian legend that she bore Solomon a son, Menelik, as a late tradition with no support in Scripture. JFB has no comment on vv5–12.

#### Solomon's Wealth (vv13–28)
- **"666 talents of gold" (v13).** About 25 tons, "in one year." The Tyndale notes point out that the phrase may mean a single record year rather than every year. Kings gives the same figure (1 Kgs 10:14). It is natural to think of Revelation 13:18, where 666 is "the number of a man." Some readers see a deliberate link, a hint that great wealth was becoming Solomon's snare. Most commentators think it is simply the recorded figure, and nothing in Chronicles makes the link. It is best held lightly.
- **"Besides that which the explorers and merchants brought" (v14).** Solomon also collected tolls from the trade routes that crossed his land, and tribute from **"the kings of Arabia"**, desert chiefs who controlled the caravan roads.
- **Gold shields (vv15–16).** Two hundred large shields with 600 shekels (about 15 pounds) of gold each and three hundred smaller ones with 300 shekels each. They hung in **"the House of the Forest of Lebanon,"** Solomon's great hall, named for its rows of cedar pillars (1 Kgs 7:2–5). Gold is too soft for battle, so these were for show. Five years after Solomon died, Pharaoh Shishak carried them off, and Rehoboam replaced them with bronze (12:9–10). That is a picture of the glory fading.
- **"A great ivory throne… overlaid with pure gold" (vv17–19).** This was probably wood inlaid with ivory panels and covered with gold. Lions were royal symbols across the ancient Near East, and for Israel they recalled Judah, "a lion's cub" from whom "the scepter shall not depart" (Gen 49:9–10). Six steps with a lion at each end, twelve in all, perhaps one for each tribe, led up to the seat. **"Nothing like it was ever made for any kingdom."**
- **"Silver was not considered as anything" (v20).** Even his cups were gold. Silver was "as common in Jerusalem as stone" (v27).
- **"The king's ships went to Tarshish" (v21).** Kings says Solomon had "a fleet of ships of Tarshish" (1 Kgs 10:22). Tarshish was a distant port in the far west, probably in Spain, and "ship of Tarshish" became the name for a large ocean-going vessel, the way "East Indiaman" once named a kind of ship. Since Solomon's fleet sailed from the Red Sea (8:17), many think the Chronicler's wording reflects that type of ship rather than a voyage to Spain. JFB reads it the same way. **"Once every three years"** suggests long voyages.
- **"Gold, silver, ivory, apes, and peacocks" (v21).** The last word (*tukkiyyim*) is uncertain. "Peacocks" goes back to old Jewish tradition. Some scholars suggest baboons or another kind of monkey. The point is exotic luxuries from the ends of the earth.
- **"King Solomon excelled all the kings of the earth in riches and in wisdom" (v22).** This fulfills God's promise at Gibeon: wisdom, and also "riches, possessions, and honor, such as none of the kings had who were before you" (1:12).
- **"All the kings of the earth sought the presence of Solomon to hear his wisdom, which God had put into his mind" (v23).** The source of the wisdom is named. Solomon's fame was God's gift.
- **"4,000 stalls for horses and chariots" (v25).** 1 Kings 4:26 has 40,000 in the standard Hebrew text, though some Greek manuscripts of Kings read 4,000, and the ESV footnotes that. Most scholars think 4,000 is original and that the larger number came from a copying error. JFB passes on an older theory that each stall held several horses but says recent critics favor a copyist's slip.
- **"From the Euphrates to the land of the Philistines and to the border of Egypt" (v26).** This was the full extent of land promised to Abraham (Gen 15:18). The promise was fulfilled, at least for a time.
- **"Horses were imported for Solomon from Egypt and from all lands" (v28).** Verses 25–28 repeat almost exactly what was said at the start of Solomon's reign (1:14–17). The Chronicler frames the whole reign with wealth. But careful readers would remember the law of the king in Deuteronomy 17:16–17: he must not "acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt," nor "acquire for himself excessive silver and gold." Chronicles does not comment, but it ends the account of Solomon's riches on that note.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees warning signs in these verses: the gold shields as a warrior's image with no real strength, and the horses from Egypt as plain disobedience to Deuteronomy 17. He also draws on Ecclesiastes, which tradition ascribes to Solomon, to show that wealth did not satisfy him. On 666 he notes the link to Revelation 13:18 without claiming that Solomon was a type of the Antichrist. JFB says the phrase in v21 should be read "the king's ships of Tarshish," ships "in burden and construction" like the big vessels of Tarshish, and on v25 leans toward a copying error in Kings. Henry's comments on these verses could not be fetched.

#### Solomon's Death (vv29–31)
- **"The history of Nathan the prophet… the prophecy of Ahijah the Shilonite… the visions of Iddo the seer" (v29).** The Chronicler names his sources, all now lost. All three were prophets. Nathan confronted David and helped crown Solomon (1 Kgs 1). Ahijah of Shiloh tore his cloak into twelve pieces and told Jeroboam that God would give him ten tribes because Solomon had worshiped other gods (1 Kgs 11:29–39). Iddo wrote "concerning Jeroboam." By naming those books, the Chronicler points his readers to the darker side of Solomon's story without retelling it.
- **"Solomon reigned in Jerusalem over all Israel forty years" (v30).** About 971 to 931 BC. He was the last king to rule "all Israel." Saul, David and Solomon each reigned about forty years, and after Solomon the kingdom split.
- **"Solomon slept with his fathers… and Rehoboam his son reigned in his place" (v31).** "Slept with his fathers" means he died and was buried. It is used of good and bad kings alike. Rehoboam's foolishness will divide the kingdom in the very next scene (10:1–19).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that God had promised Solomon a long life *if* he walked in God's ways (1 Kgs 3:14), and that he seems to have died at about sixty, younger than might be expected. Guzik also observes that "slept with his fathers" says nothing about Solomon's standing with God, since it is used even of Ahab. The Tyndale notes date his reign to 971–931 BC.

#### Pointing to Christ
A foreign queen came from the ends of the earth, heard wisdom, saw the king's glory, praised the LORD and went home with more than she brought. Jesus used her as a witness: "something greater than Solomon is here" (Matt 12:42). Solomon's gold shields were carried off, and his kingdom split within months of his death. Christ's wisdom is God's own wisdom (1 Cor 1:24, 30). His throne is truly "the LORD's throne," and the nations will bring their glory to it forever (Rev 21:24–26). Those who come to him, like the queen, receive far more than they bring.
