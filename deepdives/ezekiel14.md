*Setting:* Babylonia, probably 591 BC, in the same stretch of Ezekiel's ministry as chapters 12–13. Ezekiel lived in his own house at Tel-abib among the exiles (3:15, 24), and the leaders of the exile community came to him to hear what the LORD was saying, as they had done before (8:1) and would do again (20:1). Chapter 13 condemned prophets who told people what they wanted to hear. Chapter 14 turns to the people who went looking for such prophets: men who wanted a word from God while keeping their idols. The second half answers a quiet hope many of them must have held. Surely, they thought, God would spare Jerusalem for the sake of the righteous people still in it, as he once offered to spare Sodom for ten (Gen 18:32). Ezekiel's answer is no.

#### Idolatrous Elders Condemned (vv1–11)
- **"Certain of the elders of Israel came to me and sat before me" (v1).** These were the heads of families who led the exile community. To sit before a prophet was to come as a student or an inquirer, waiting for him to speak (33:31). On the outside it was a model of seeking God. They may have come to ask about Jerusalem's future, or whether they would soon go home.
- **"These men have taken their idols into their hearts" (v3).** Literally, they "made their idols go up upon their hearts." Nothing suggests these elders had carved images on their shelves in Babylon. Their idolatry was inside, in their loyalties and hopes. The word for idols, *gillulim*, is Ezekiel's favorite term for false gods, used nearly forty times in the book. It is probably related to a word for dung pellets, a deliberately crude name for objects people bowed to.
- **"And set the stumbling block of their iniquity before their faces" (v3).** A stumbling block (*mikshol*) is whatever trips someone up and makes them fall. Ezekiel uses the phrase several times (7:19; 18:30; 44:12). These men placed their sin right in front of their own eyes, where they could see it all the time, and still walked toward it. Idols in the heart and an obstacle before the face. The whole man was turned away from God.
- **"Should I indeed let myself be consulted by them?" (v3).** God's question expects the answer no. To "inquire of" or "consult" the LORD (*darash*) was the proper way to seek his will through a prophet. God refuses to be one more oracle consulted alongside other gods. Isaiah heard the same complaint: "this people draw near with their mouth… while their hearts are far from me" (Isa 29:13). Jesus quoted those words to the Pharisees (Matt 15:8–9).
- **"I the LORD will answer him as he comes with the multitude of his idols" (v4).** The answer they get will match what they bring. They come carrying idols, and God will deal with them as idolaters. The prophet is bypassed. God will answer in person.
- **"That I may lay hold of the hearts of the house of Israel" (v5).** This shows the purpose behind the severity. The verb (*tafas*) means to seize or grab hold, as one takes a captive or catches a runaway. God is not trying to be rid of his people. He wants their hearts back, and he will take hold of them by whatever means it takes. **"All estranged from me through their idols."** The word means to drift away and become a stranger. They had become strangers to their own God.
- **"Repent and turn away from your idols, and turn away your faces" (v6).** The Hebrew plays on one verb, *shuv*, three times: turn back, turn away, turn your faces. This is Ezekiel's great call to repentance, repeated later in chapter 18 ("Repent and turn from all your transgressions", 18:30). Before God says anything about judgment, he offers a way out.
- **"Or of the strangers who sojourn in Israel" (v7).** The law applies to the foreigner living among God's people as well as to the native-born (Lev 17:8–10; 20:2). Anyone who joined the community took on its covenant, and no one could treat the LORD as one god among many.
- **"I will set my face against that man; I will make him a sign and a byword" (v8).** "Set my face against" echoes the law: "I will set my face against that person and will cut him off from among his people" (Lev 20:3–6). A byword (*mashal*, the same word as "proverb" in 12:22) is a name people use as a saying, as in "may you end up like him." The man who wanted an oracle becomes an object lesson. **"Cut him off"** may mean death or exclusion from the community. Either way, he loses his place among God's people.
- **"If the prophet is deceived and speaks a word, I, the LORD, have deceived that prophet" (v9).** This is one of the hardest verses in the book. The verb (*patah*) means to entice, persuade or deceive, and it is the word Jeremiah used when he complained, "O LORD, you have deceived me, and I was deceived" (Jer 20:7). The closest parallel is the vision of Micaiah, in which the LORD sends a lying spirit into the mouths of Ahab's prophets because Ahab had already chosen not to listen to the truth (1 Kings 22:19–23). The point in both places is judicial. God does not lie, and he does not trick the innocent (Num 23:19; James 1:13). But when a prophet agrees to tell idolaters what they want to hear, God hands both prophet and inquirer over to the deception they chose. Paul says the same of those who "refused to love the truth": "God sends them a strong delusion" (2 Thess 2:10–11). Readers differ on how directly God acts here. Some stress that God permits and uses the deception, others that he actively sends it as a sentence. Both readings agree that the prophet is guilty and that God remains in control and in the right.
- **"The punishment of the prophet and the punishment of the inquirer shall be alike" (v10).** There are no innocent parties. The prophet who sells comfort and the man who buys it both bear their guilt. Deuteronomy had already made this plain (Deut 13:1–5).
- **"That they may be my people and I may be their God" (v11).** This is the covenant formula, repeated throughout the Bible from Exodus to Revelation (Exod 6:7; Jer 31:33; Rev 21:3). Even here, after a passage about deception and destruction, God states his final aim. Judgment is meant to purify, so that Israel will stop going astray and defiling itself.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Men's corruptions are idols in their hearts, and are of their own setting up." JFB agrees that "the heart is first corrupted, and then the outward manifestation of idol-worship follows," and reads the elders' silence as men unable "to find a beginning of their speech." On v9, JFB says God acts not by mere permission but "through Satan and his ministers," using the deception as "a touchstone to separate the precious from the vile." That is a firmer statement than the Tyndale notes make. They say only that false prophets end up doing God's will without meaning to, confirming rebels in their rebellion, and they point to Deuteronomy 13 and 1 Kings 22. Guzik compares the elders to the Pharisees Jesus rebuked, outwardly devout and inwardly far from God. He follows other scholars in seeing v9 as God answering insincerity with deception that the inquirer has invited.

#### Jerusalem Will Not Be Spared (vv12–23)
- **"When a land sins against me by acting faithlessly" (v13).** The case is put in general terms, like a legal ruling: if any land sins this way, then this will follow. "Acting faithlessly" (*ma'al*) is a term for breaking trust, used of Achan stealing what was devoted to God (Josh 7:1) and of a wife unfaithful to her husband (Num 5:12). It was covenant language. Although the wording is general, the target is Judah.
- **Four judgments, four test cases (vv13–20).** Each case names one disaster: famine (v13), wild beasts (v15), sword (v17), and pestilence (v19). These are the covenant curses of Leviticus 26, which Israel accepted at Sinai (Lev 26:22, 25–26). Ezekiel had already named the same four in 5:17. Each case closes with the same verdict, and the repetition is the point.
- **"Break its supply of bread" (v13).** Literally "break the staff of bread," the same phrase used in Leviticus 26:26 and in Ezekiel 4:16 and 5:16. Bread was the staff people leaned on to stay alive.
- **"Even if these three men, Noah, Daniel, and Job, were in it" (v14).** The three are chosen as men whose righteousness stood out in their own generation, and each had some link to saving others. Noah was righteous in a world God destroyed, and his family was saved with him (Gen 6:9; 7:1). Job was "blameless and upright," and he offered sacrifices for his children (Job 1:1, 5). Daniel was Ezekiel's younger contemporary, taken to Babylon in 605 BC, eight years before Ezekiel. By now he was known for wisdom and faithfulness at Nebuchadnezzar's court (Dan 1–2), and he prayed for his people (Dan 9). Ezekiel mentions his wisdom again in 28:3.
- **Which Daniel?** The Hebrew spelling here is *Dani'el*, slightly different from the usual spelling in the book of Daniel. Texts from ancient Ugarit (in modern Syria), from around 1400 BC, tell of a wise and just king named Danel who judged the cause of widows and orphans. Many scholars think Ezekiel meant this ancient hero, since Noah and Job are also figures from before Israel's history. Others, including the Tyndale notes, argue that small spelling differences in names are common in the Old Testament, that the exiles knew Daniel personally as a model of righteousness, and that Ezekiel, who fought idolatry so fiercely, would not hold up a hero of Canaanite religion as a model of righteousness. The traditional reading is that Ezekiel meant the prophet. The text's point stands either way.
- **"They would deliver but their own lives by their righteousness" (v14).** In the past God had spared people because of a righteous person among them. He promised Abraham to spare Sodom for ten righteous people (Gen 18:32), and he spared Lot's family for Abraham's sake (Gen 19:29). But a time can come when no one's goodness can shelter anyone else. Jeremiah heard the same from God: "Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my heart would not turn toward this people" (Jer 15:1).
- **"They would deliver neither sons nor daughters" (vv16, 18, 20).** The verdict tightens as the cases go on. In v14 the three save "their own lives." From v16 on, they cannot save even their own children. The detail is pointed. Noah's sons were saved with him, and Job's children died while their father lived. Many of Ezekiel's hearers had sons and daughters still in Jerusalem. The message is personal: faith cannot be inherited or passed on to family members. Each person stands before God alone. Ezekiel will say this at length in chapter 18.
- **"As I live, declares the Lord GOD" (vv16, 18, 20).** This is an oath. God swears by his own life, since there is nothing greater to swear by (Heb 6:13). It is repeated three times.
- **"How much more when I send upon Jerusalem my four disastrous acts of judgment" (v21).** Now the general case lands on the real city. If one judgment in a land with three great saints could not be turned aside, what hope is there for Jerusalem, which has no such saints and will face all four judgments together? This happened in the siege of 588–586 BC. The city was starved, struck by plague, put to the sword, and left empty for wild animals (2 Kings 25; Lam 4:9–10; 5:18).
- **"But behold, some survivors will be left in it" (v22).** There is a twist. Some will survive, and they will be brought to Babylon, where Ezekiel's hearers will meet them. But they are not survivors because they were righteous. The text says nothing about their faith. They are spared to be seen.
- **"When you see their ways and their deeds, you will be consoled" (vv22–23).** The comfort is unexpected. The exiles will meet the newcomers and see how they live, and then they will understand. Jerusalem was not destroyed by a harsh or careless God. It was ripe for judgment. The word for "consoled" (*naḥam*) can mean comforted, or settled in one's mind about something painful. The exiles will grieve the loss of their city, but they will be able to accept it as just. **"I have not done without cause all that I have done in it."** The last line of the chapter is a defense of God's justice. He never strikes for no reason.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the line from Noah: "The faith, obedience, and prayers of Noah prevailed to the saving of his house, but not of the old world." On the survivors, he says, "even when God makes the greatest desolations by his judgments, he saves some to be monuments of his mercy." JFB and Guzik read the remnant differently from Henry. Both, with the Tyndale notes, see the survivors as visibly guilty, so that what consoles the exiles is the proof that the judgment was deserved. JFB says the comfort comes "not in words" but from seeing the survivors' "manifest guilt." JFB sees Job as the climax of the three, since he could not save even his own children, and it holds that Daniel's "fame for piety and wisdom was already established," so the mention is no problem for the prophet's historical place. Guzik agrees that the Daniel meant is Ezekiel's contemporary. He also presses the personal point: no one can be saved by someone else's faith or prayers.

#### Pointing to Christ
Noah, Daniel and Job could save only themselves, and not even their sons and daughters. Ezekiel's point is that no human righteousness is great enough to shelter others from God's judgment. That makes Jesus' work stand out. He is the one righteous man whose righteousness is enough for others. "For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (Rom 5:19). He does more than intercede for his people. He bears the judgment for them (1 Pet 3:18). And the God who said "Should I indeed let myself be consulted by them?" still wants to "lay hold of the hearts" of his people (v5). In Christ he has done it, giving a new heart and his Spirit (36:26–27), so that the old covenant promise is fulfilled: "they shall be my people, and I will be their God."
