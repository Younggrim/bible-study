*Setting:* Amos, a sheep-breeder from Judah, is preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760 BC, in the booming reign of Jeroboam II. Chapter 3 told Israel that being chosen by God meant being judged more strictly, and pictured the ivory mansions of Samaria in ruins. Chapter 4 is the second message that opens "Hear this word" (3:1; 5:1). It speaks first to the wealthy women of Samaria, then mocks the busy worship at Israel's shrines, and then lists five disasters God had already sent to wake his people up, each ending with the same sad refrain: "yet you did not return to me." It closes with one of the most famous lines in the prophets, "prepare to meet your God," and a short hymn to the Creator.

#### Israel's Guilt and Punishment (vv1–5)
- **"Hear this word, you cows of Bashan" (v1).** Bashan was the high, fertile plateau east of the Sea of Galilee, today's Golan Heights and the land just east of it. Its cattle were famous for being sleek and fat (Deut 32:14; Ps 22:12; Ezek 39:18). Amos the herdsman uses the image he knows. The Hebrew uses feminine forms, so most readers, with the Tyndale notes and Guzik, take this as the wealthy women of Samaria. JFB thinks it describes the nobles, with the feminine form mocking their softness. Either way, it is not an insult about weight. Guzik notes that in the ancient world being well fed was a sign of wealth. The problem is how the wealth was gained.
- **"Who oppress the poor, who crush the needy" (v1).** These women likely never met the poor they crushed. Their demands drove their husbands to squeeze more from tenants, debtors and the courts (2:6–8). **"Who say to your husbands, 'Bring, that we may drink!'"** The word for "husbands" is literally "lords" or "masters" (*'adoneyhem*), the respectful word a wife used for her husband (Gen 18:12). The irony is that the "lords" are being ordered around. JFB takes the "masters" as the king.
- **"The Lord GOD has sworn by his holiness" (v2).** God can swear by nothing greater than himself (Heb 6:13). His holiness is what makes him utterly different from his creatures and utterly opposed to evil. The Tyndale notes compare 6:8 and 8:7, where God swears again.
- **"They shall take you away with hooks, even the last of you with fishhooks" (v2).** The Hebrew words are rare, and their exact meaning is debated. They may mean hooks, or thorns (JFB), or even baskets for carrying fish. The likeliest picture is of captives led like animals or caught like fish. The Tyndale notes mention an Assyrian monument of King Esarhaddon holding cords that pass through the lips of two captives, and a suggestion that the image is of rings put through the noses of cattle. 2 Chronicles 33:11 says the Assyrians took King Manasseh "with hooks." Women who lived like pampered cattle would be led away like cattle.
- **"You shall go out through the breaches, each one straight ahead" (v3).** The walls of Samaria would be broken. The captives would be marched out single file through the gaps, no gate and no turning aside. The Tyndale notes add that the wall they trusted would fail them. **"Cast out into Harmon."** No one knows where Harmon was. Some think it is a form of Hermon, the mountain to the north on the road toward Assyria. The Greek Old Testament reads "Mount Remman." The point is plain: exile.
- **"Come to Bethel, and transgress; to Gilgal, and multiply transgression" (v4).** This is biting sarcasm. A priest would normally say "Come to Bethel and worship." Amos says "come and sin." Bethel was the royal shrine with Jeroboam I's golden calf (1 Kings 12:28–29). Gilgal, near Jericho, was where Israel first camped in the promised land and renewed the covenant (Josh 4:19–5:9), and by now it was a popular shrine too (Hos 4:15; 9:15). Both places had sacred memories. Neither had God's blessing on the worship offered there.
- **"Bring your sacrifices every morning, your tithes every three days" (v4).** The law called for pilgrimage feasts three times a year and tithes once a year, with a special tithe every third year (Deut 14:22–29). Amos exaggerates their zeal: sacrifices every morning, tithes every three days. The Hebrew may also mean "on the third day" of a festival visit. JFB reads it as "every third year." The Tyndale notes say the point is that Israel was extremely religious but resisted true godliness, and they compare Jesus' rebuke of those who tithe mint while neglecting justice (Luke 11:42).
- **"Offer a sacrifice of thanksgiving of that which is leavened" (v5).** Leaven (yeast) was normally kept off the altar (Lev 2:11), though leavened loaves could be brought with a thank offering to be eaten by the worshiper (Lev 7:13). The Tyndale notes explain the meaning of unleavened bread. Amos may be mocking them for burning leavened bread on the altar, going beyond the law to show off, or he may simply be listing their religious busyness. Guzik mentions both possibilities.
- **"Proclaim freewill offerings, publish them; for so you love to do" (v5).** Freewill offerings were voluntary gifts beyond what was required (Lev 22:18–23). These people made sure everyone knew about them. Jesus warned against the same thing: sounding a trumpet when giving, "that they may be praised by others" (Matt 6:2). **"For so you love to do"** is the sting. Their worship was about what *they* loved, not what God commanded. Guzik makes this point: worship should be measured by whether it pleases God, not whether it pleases us.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines this section as oppressors threatened and idolaters left to the idols they had chosen. JFB stresses that the shrines copied the letter of the law while breaking its spirit through calf worship. Guzik says the shrines were set up to keep people from worshiping at Jerusalem, so their zeal itself was rebellion. The Tyndale notes add the Assyrian background to the hooks.

#### Israel Has Not Returned to the LORD (vv6–13)
- **"I gave you cleanness of teeth in all your cities" (v6).** A grim joke: their teeth were clean because there was nothing to eat. JFB: "want of food." **"Yet you did not return to me."** This line will be said five times (vv6, 8, 9, 10, 11). "Return" (*shuv*) is the great Old Testament word for repentance: to turn around and come back. Henry: "You have been sent for by one messenger after another, but you have not come back, you have not come home."
- **These are the covenant curses.** Every disaster in this list comes from the warnings of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28, where God told Israel what would happen if they turned away from him: famine, drought, blight, locusts, plague, sword, overthrow. The Tyndale notes mention the link. Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the temple also listed them, asking that when such things came, the people would turn and pray and God would forgive (1 Kings 8:35–40). That was what each disaster was for. Israel suffered all of them and never turned.
- **"I also withheld the rain from you when there were yet three months to the harvest" (v7).** In Israel the winter rains fall from about October to April. The "latter rains" of February to April swell the grain before the harvest in May and June. Losing them three months before harvest would ruin the crop just when it mattered most. **"I would send rain on one city, and send no rain on another."** Patchy rain showed that it was not random. JFB: the drought was a deliberate judgment. Just as in Egypt, God made a distinction (Exod 9:26).
- **"Two or three cities would wander to another city to drink water, and would not be satisfied" (v8).** "Wander" is the word for staggering, as if dazed. People went from town to town looking for water, and still did not look to God.
- **"I struck you with blight and mildew" (v9).** "Blight" (*shiddaphon*) is scorching by the hot east wind. "Mildew" (*yeraqon*) is a yellowing disease of the grain. Both appear together in Deuteronomy 28:22 and 1 Kings 8:37. **"Your many gardens and your vineyards, your fig trees and your olive trees the locust devoured."** The word here is *gazam*, a kind of locust, the same word as Joel 1:4. JFB calls it the palmerworm. The rich gardens of the wealthy were stripped too.
- **"I sent among you a pestilence after the manner of Egypt" (v10).** The plagues once fell on Israel's oppressors (Exod 9:3). Now Israel is treated like Egypt, because it acts like Egypt toward its own poor. Deuteronomy 28:60 had warned that "the diseases of Egypt" would come on a disobedient Israel. **"I killed your young men with the sword, and carried away your horses."** This may point to the wars with Damascus a generation earlier, when Israel's army was reduced to "fifty horsemen and ten chariots" (2 Kings 13:7). **"I made the stench of your camp go up into your nostrils."** The smell of unburied dead in a military camp. It is the opposite of the "pleasing aroma" sacrifices were meant to send up to God.
- **"I overthrew some of you, as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah" (v11).** "Overthrew" is *haphak*, the word for Sodom's destruction (Gen 19:25). It is likely a picture of a catastrophe like an earthquake or fire. The Tyndale notes say it would have been hard for Israel to believe God could treat his own people like Sodom. Notice the strange phrase "as when *God* overthrew," God speaking of himself in the third person, perhaps a fixed saying. Isaiah 1:9 uses the same comparison for Judah.
- **"You were as a brand plucked out of the burning" (v11).** A stick snatched from the fire, scorched and smoking but not consumed. Henry: "like Lot out of Sodom." Israel had been spared from total destruction, barely. Zechariah later uses the same image of Joshua the high priest, rescued by grace (Zech 3:2). And still, "you did not return to me."
- **Five warnings, then the meeting.** The list climbs from hunger to thirst to crop failure, plague and war, and finally near-total destruction. Guzik calls the gradual increase an expression of God's love, applying discipline step by step to draw Israel back. The Tyndale notes compare the plagues in Egypt, which also grew worse each time. Here, every warning failed, and something worse than any disaster remains.
- **"Therefore thus I will do to you, O Israel" (v12).** God does not say what "thus" is. The silence is more frightening than a description. JFB thinks it means the end of Israel's existence as a nation. The disasters are over. Now comes God himself.
- **"Prepare to meet your God, O Israel!" (v12).** This line deliberately recalls Sinai. There, God told Moses, "be ready for the third day," using the same verb "prepare" (*kun*), and Moses "brought the people out of the camp to meet God" (Exod 19:11, 15, 17). Then, meeting God meant covenant. Now it means judgment. The Tyndale notes call this one of the two key themes of the book, alongside "let justice roll down" (5:24). Henry turns it toward hope: "Resolve therefore to meet him as a penitent, as a humble suppliant." Guzik offers three ways to hear it: a challenge, like a fighter getting ready for a champion; an invitation, since God calls people to himself; and a summons to the final judgment. The line is a warning, but any warning that comes before judgment is also a mercy.
- **"For behold, he who forms the mountains and creates the wind" (v13).** Amos ends with a hymn to God the Creator. The Tyndale notes suggest these hymn fragments (also 5:8–9 and 9:5–6) may have been sung at Bethel, which would make the irony sharp: they sang about the God they were about to meet. "Forms" (*yatsar*) is the potter's word used for God shaping Adam (Gen 2:7). "Creates" (*bara'*) is the word of Genesis 1:1, used only of God. The God they treated like a local idol made the mountains and the wind.
- **"And declares to man what is his thought" (v13).** The Hebrew is ambiguous. It may mean God reveals *his own* plans to people, through prophets like Amos (3:7). Or it may mean he tells a person what *that person* is thinking, the searcher of hearts (Ps 139:2; Jer 17:10), as JFB takes it. Both are true, and both make the God of v12 one no one can hide from.
- **"Who makes the morning darkness, and treads on the heights of the earth" (v13).** God can turn dawn into night, by storm or eclipse or judgment (5:8; 8:9). Henry: "He often makes the morning darkness, by thick clouds overspreading the sky." Treading on the heights is a picture of God striding over the earth as its master (Mic 1:3). The "high places" were also where Israel's shrines stood. **"The LORD, the God of hosts, is his name!"** The hymn ends by naming him. Henry: "it is folly to contend with him."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this section Israel's stubbornness under God's providence, and urges humility before a God they cannot defeat. JFB lists the judgments as famine, drought, blight, locusts, plague and slaughter, and reads v12 as the threat of the end of the nation. Guzik sees the escalation as loving discipline and the failure to return as the real tragedy. All three treat v13 as a reminder of who Israel's God really is. JFB reads "declares to man what is his thought" as God searching hearts, which fits the context but is one of two possible readings.

#### Pointing to Christ
Five times God says "yet you did not return to me." The message of the whole Bible is that God keeps calling his people home, and in the end he came himself. Jesus told the story of a son who wasted everything and finally "came to himself" in a famine and returned (Luke 15:17–20). The father ran to meet him. "Prepare to meet your God" is a terrifying sentence for people who have refused every warning. But in Christ, God has come out to meet us first (John 1:14), and those who return to him find, like the brand plucked from the fire (Zech 3:2), that their filthy garments are taken away and they are clothed with something new.
