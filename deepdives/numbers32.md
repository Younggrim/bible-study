*Setting:* The plains of Moab, just east of the Jordan across from Jericho, in the last months of Moses' life, around the end of the forty years in the wilderness. Israel has already defeated Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan (21:21–35) and has just returned from the war on Midian with enormous herds of captured livestock (31:32–34). The whole plateau east of the Jordan, from the Arnon gorge in the south up into Bashan in the north, is now in Israel's hands. The next step is to cross the river into Canaan. At exactly that moment, two tribes ask to stay where they are. The chapter is about that request, Moses' fear of a repeat of Kadesh, and the deal that keeps Israel united.

#### Reuben and Gad Settle East of the Jordan (vv1–42)
- **"A very great number of livestock" (v1).** The Hebrew repeats the word for livestock (*miqneh*), stressing just how large their herds were. Israel left Egypt with flocks (Exod 12:38), and the victories over Sihon, Og and Midian had multiplied them. **Jazer** and **Gilead** were rich grazing country: Gilead, the high, well-watered land around the Jabbok River, was famous for its pasture and its balm (Gen 37:25; Jer 8:22). Shepherds looked at it and saw exactly what they needed.
- **The towns named (v3).** Ataroth, Dibon, Jazer, Nimrah, Heshbon, Elealeh, Sebam, Nebo and Beon were Sihon's cities, taken in chapter 21. **Heshbon** had been his capital (21:26). **Nebo** sat by the ridge from which Moses would soon view the land and die (Deut 34:1). **Beon** is the same place called Baal-meon in v38.
- **"Let this land be given to your servants… Do not take us across the Jordan" (v5).** Their request is polite ("if we have found favor in your sight") but the last line is jarring. God had promised the land of Canaan, west of the Jordan, to Abraham. These tribes are asking to stop short of it because the land in front of them suits their business. Reuben was Jacob's firstborn, but he had lost the firstborn's privileges (Gen 49:3–4), and here his tribe again chooses what looks good now.
- **"Shall your brothers go to the war while you sit here?" (v6).** Moses hears the request as desertion. Israel is about to fight for Canaan, and two tribes with tens of thousands of fighting men (26:7, 18) want to sit out the war.
- **"Why will you discourage the heart of the people?" (v7).** The verb means to restrain or frustrate, to take the will out of someone. Moses' deeper fear is that the request will spread. If two tribes think the land east of the river is good enough, why should anyone risk crossing?
- **"Your fathers did this… from Kadesh-barnea" (vv8–13).** Moses tells the story of chapters 13–14 in miniature. Forty years earlier the spies went to the **Valley of Eshcol**, saw the land and its giant grape cluster, and then talked the people out of entering. God's anger was kindled, and he swore that none of that generation would see the land. This is the last generation's story, told to their children on the very edge of the promise.
- **"They have not wholly followed me… Caleb… and Joshua… have wholly followed the LORD" (vv11–12).** The Hebrew idiom is literally "they have not filled after me," meaning they did not follow fully, all the way. Caleb and Joshua did (14:24). That is the dividing line of the whole wilderness story. **"Caleb the son of Jephunneh the Kenizzite"**: the Kenizzites were descended from Kenaz, a grandson of Esau (Gen 36:11). Caleb was apparently from a family that had joined Israel and been absorbed into Judah, which he represented among the spies (13:6). An outsider by descent followed God more fully than most Israelites.
- **"A brood of sinful men" (v14).** Moses' words are sharp: a new generation rising up in their fathers' place to repeat their fathers' sin. **"He will again abandon them in the wilderness, and you will destroy all this people" (v15).** Moses believes another failure of nerve could cost the whole nation its entry into the land.
- **Was the request sinful?** Moses assumed the worst, and the rest of the chapter shows he was at least partly wrong. Reuben and Gad did not refuse to fight. But the request still shows a people choosing by what was good for their flocks rather than by what God had promised. Later history bears this out. The eastern tribes were cut off by the Jordan valley from the rest of Israel and nearly fell into conflict with them (Josh 22). In Deborah's day Reuben stayed "among the sheepfolds" while others fought (Judg 5:16–17), and the eastern tribes were the first carried off into exile by Assyria (1 Chr 5:26).
- **"We will build sheepfolds here… and cities for our little ones" (v16).** The tribes come close to Moses and clarify. They are not asking to skip the war. Their families and herds will stay behind in walled towns, and the men will fight.
- **"Ready to go before the people of Israel" (v17).** They offer more than Moses asked: to go in front, in the most dangerous position. Joshua 4:12–13 records that they did exactly that, crossing the Jordan "armed before the people of Israel."
- **"We will not return to our homes until each of the people of Israel has gained his inheritance" (v18).** They commit to stay until the end of the war, not just the first campaign. Joshua later sends them home with praise for not forsaking their brothers "these many days" (Josh 22:1–4). The conquest took about seven years.
- **"If you will take up arms to go before the LORD" (v20).** Notice Moses' correction. They said they would go "before the people of Israel" (v17). Moses says "before the LORD," and repeats the phrase again and again (vv20, 21, 22, 27, 29, 32). The war is God's war, fought in God's presence, and their promise is made to him, not just to their brothers.
- **"Free of obligation to the LORD and to Israel" (v22).** Once the land is subdued, they will have kept their word and can go home with a clear conscience.
- **"Be sure your sin will find you out" (v23).** This is the chapter's most famous line, and it is often quoted about hidden sins coming to light. In its setting the "sin" is specifically the sin of *not* doing what they promised: sitting at home while their brothers fight. The word "find" (*matsa*) pictures sin almost as a pursuer that tracks its owner down. A broken promise to God will not stay hidden or harmless. It catches up with the one who made it.
- **"Your servants will do as my lord commands" (v25).** No more negotiating. The tribes accept the terms, and they will leave their wives, children and herds in Gilead and trust God to protect them while their men are across the river.
- **Moses gives command to Eleazar and Joshua (vv28–30).** Moses knows he will not live to see the deal kept, so he hands it to the leaders who will. If the eastern tribes fail to fight, they lose Gilead and must take land in Canaan with everyone else (v30). It is a binding public agreement with witnesses, the way an ancient treaty was made.
- **"What the LORD has said to your servants, we will do" (v31).** They now recognize Moses' terms as God's word, and repeat their pledge in front of all the leaders (v32).
- **The half-tribe of Manasseh (v33).** Manasseh appears here suddenly, without having joined the request. Part of the tribe settled east of the Jordan in Gilead and Bashan, and the rest in Canaan (Josh 17). The text does not explain how this came about. Verses 39–42 suggest that some Manasseh clans had already done the fighting in the north and taken the land as their own.
- **Gad's and Reuben's towns (vv34–38).** "Built" here mainly means rebuilt and fortified towns taken in war. **Dibon** is modern Dhiban in Jordan, where the Mesha Stele was found in 1868. That inscription, set up by a Moabite king around 840 BC, says that "the men of Gad had lived in the land of Ataroth from of old" and that the king of Israel had built Ataroth for them. It is striking outside confirmation that Gad lived exactly where this chapter puts it. **Aroer** stood on the north rim of the Arnon gorge, Israel's southern border east of the river.
- **"Their names were changed" (v38).** Nebo was the name of a Babylonian god (Isa 46:1) and Baal-meon carries the name of Baal. Most scholars think the Israelites renamed these places to remove pagan gods' names, as God had commanded: "make no mention of the names of other gods" (Exod 23:13). The new names are not recorded, and the old ones seem to have stuck, which is its own quiet comment on how hard idolatry is to erase.
- **Machir, Jair and Nobah (vv39–42).** **Machir** was Manasseh's son (Gen 50:23), and his clan took Gilead and drove out the Amorites. **Jair** captured a group of villages and called them **Havvoth-jair**, meaning "the tent-villages of Jair" (*chavvah*, a tent-village). Deuteronomy 3:14 and 1 Chronicles 2:21–23 add that he was descended from Judah on his father's side and from Machir on his mother's, which explains how he is counted as Manasseh. **Nobah** took Kenath and gave it his own name. Naming a place after oneself was the normal ancient way of claiming it.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry, following Calvin, thinks the tribes "consulted their own private convenience more than the public good," and warns about people whose "spirits agree too well with this world." He has Moses answer them in effect: you will never be indulged "in this sloth and cowardice." On v23 Henry says, "Sin will, without doubt, find out the sinner sooner or later," and stresses Moses' wording, since "it is God's cause more than your brethren's." JFB is gentler, explaining the request by the region being so well suited to a pastoral people, and seeing "good policy" in leaving enough men behind to guard the conquered land. JFB admits it is "nowhere explained" how half of Manasseh came to join the two tribes, and suggests the renaming was meant to "obliterate all remembrance of those idols." JFB also glosses Havvoth-jair as "tent-villages," which the Hebrew lexicon supports. Guzik takes a middle course. He notes the tribes stopped short of the promised land, but he thinks they answered Moses' concerns well. His main application is that v23 is about the sin of doing nothing: failing to serve and fight alongside fellow believers is a sin that eventually shows itself in spiritual weakness and lost joy. JFB has no separate notes on vv7–15, 18–19 or 21–27, covering them only in its section headings.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two tribes nearly settled for less than what God promised because the land in front of them looked good enough. Moses' answer was that God's people do not sit at ease while their brothers fight, and his test of faithfulness was the same one that divided Joshua and Caleb from their generation: following the LORD wholly. The New Testament presses the same call. Believers are joined to one body, so "if one member suffers, all suffer together" (1 Cor 12:26), and they are urged not to drift short of God's rest (Heb 4:1, 11). Christ himself did not stay where it was comfortable. He crossed over into our world, went "before" his people into the battle with sin and death, and did not return home until the work was finished (John 17:4; Heb 6:20). And where sin would otherwise find us out, he was the one it found, bearing it in his own body on the cross (1 Pet 2:24).
