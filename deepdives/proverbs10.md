*Setting:* Here the book changes shape. Chapters 1–9 were long speeches from a father and from Wisdom herself. Matthew Henry calls them the "porch or preface." With chapter 10 the main collection begins, headed simply "The proverbs of Solomon" and running to 22:16, about 375 short sayings. Solomon reigned about 970–930 BC and is said to have spoken 3,000 proverbs (1 Kgs 4:32). Hezekiah's scribes later copied out more of them (25:1), so the collection was gathered and edited over time. Most sayings are two lines set in contrast: the righteous do this, *but* the wicked do that. The Tyndale notes point out that the order often looks random, like other wisdom collections from Egypt and Mesopotamia, though small clusters share a theme or a key word. Chapter 10 has two such threads: the righteous and the wicked, and especially the tongue. Henry says many sayings here concern "the good government of the tongue, without which men's religion is vain."

#### The Proverbs of Solomon (vv1–32)
- **How to read a proverb.** A proverb is a general truth stated sharply, not a promise for every case. "The LORD does not let the righteous go hungry" (v3) is how God's world usually works, but Job, Jeremiah and Jesus himself knew hunger. The Tyndale notes warn that Job's friends turned this kind of saying into a rule and wrongly accused Job (Job 22:6–11). Proverbs gives the pattern. Job and Ecclesiastes give the exceptions. Read together, they keep the reader honest.
- **"A wise son makes a glad father, but a foolish son is a sorrow to his mother" (v1).** The collection opens where the father's speeches began, in the family. Father and mother are split across the two lines, but in Hebrew poetry this means both parents feel both things. A child's choices are never private. Guzik notes the irony that Solomon, the famously wise son, ended his life in folly (1 Kgs 11:4), and his own son Rehoboam was a foolish one (1 Kgs 12:13–15).
- **"Treasures gained by wickedness do not profit, but righteousness delivers from death" (v2).** Wealth gained wrongly cannot buy off death. JFB takes righteousness here as including generosity, and sees death as "the greatest of all evils." Jesus asked the same question: "What will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul?" (Matt 16:26).
- **"The LORD does not let the righteous go hungry, but he thwarts the craving of the wicked" (v3).** This is the first mention of the LORD in the collection. The point is God's active hand. The wicked's "craving" (*hawwah*) is greedy desire, and God pushes it away. JFB compares Psalm 37:25.
- **"A slack hand causes poverty, but the hand of the diligent makes rich" (vv4–5).** "Slack" (*remiyyah*) means loose or lax, and it can also mean deceitful. JFB takes it as "deceitful, failing of its purpose." "Diligent" (*charuts*) is literally "sharp," keen and decisive. Verse 5 puts it in farm terms: summer harvest in Israel ran from April through September, and missing it meant a hungry winter. Guzik notes that work came before the Fall (Gen 2:15). The Fall made it toil, but it is still a gift.
- **"Blessings are on the head of the righteous, but the mouth of the wicked conceals violence" (v6).** The second line appears again in v11. The wicked man's words cover up violent plans. JFB: his mouth "covereth" violence he means to do later. The repetition marks the start of a run of sayings about speech (vv6–21).
- **"The memory of the righteous is a blessing, but the name of the wicked will rot" (v7).** The Tyndale notes point out how much honor and shame mattered in Israel, as they still do in the Middle East. "Rot" is the word for decay, like wood eaten through. Jewish custom still adds "of blessed memory" after the name of a righteous person who has died. Guzik points to Hebrews 11, a whole chapter of names remembered with blessing.
- **"The wise of heart will receive commandments, but a babbling fool will come to ruin" (v8).** Literally "a fool of lips." The wise listen. The fool talks, and his talking ruins him. The same second line is repeated in v10.
- **"Whoever walks in integrity walks securely… he who makes his ways crooked will be found out" (v9).** Honesty means having nothing to hide and no story to keep straight. Crooked paths always come to light eventually (Luke 12:2).
- **"Whoever winks the eye causes trouble" (v10).** The wink is a secret signal, like the troublemaker of 6:13. The ESV's second line repeats v8 as the Hebrew has it. The Greek translation reads instead "but he who boldly reproves makes peace," which makes a sharper contrast, and some modern versions (NIV, NRSV) follow it. The Tyndale notes mention the alternative and suggest a scribe may have copied the line from v8.
- **"The mouth of the righteous is a fountain of life" (v11).** In a dry land a spring was life itself. The same picture is used for the teaching of the wise (13:14), the fear of the LORD (14:27) and God himself (Ps 36:9; Jer 2:13). Good words refresh and keep people alive.
- **"Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all offenses" (v12).** "Stirs up" is the word for rousing someone from sleep. Hatred wakes up quarrels that could have stayed asleep. "Covers" (*kasah*) means to cover over or conceal, not to deny. JFB: love covers "by forgiveness and forbearance." Peter quotes this line: "love covers a multitude of sins" (1 Pet 4:8), and James echoes it (Jas 5:20). Guzik cautions that this does not mean hiding one's own sin or avoiding necessary correction. Love refuses to keep digging up and spreading what has been dealt with (17:9).
- **"A rod is for the back of him who lacks sense" (v13).** Wisdom is found on the lips of the understanding. The one who lacks sense learns the hard way. The Tyndale notes take it to mean that fools bring pain on themselves by their own words.
- **"The wise lay up knowledge, but the mouth of a fool brings ruin near" (v14).** The wise store knowledge like grain in a barn, to use when needed. The fool spends his words as fast as he gets them.
- **"A rich man's wealth is his strong city; the poverty of the poor is their ruin" (v15).** This is an observation, not a command. Money does protect, and poverty does make people vulnerable. But the Tyndale notes and 18:10–11 add the other side: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower," while the rich man's wall exists only "in his imagination." JFB warns against trusting "uncertain riches."
- **"The wage of the righteous leads to life, the gain of the wicked to sin" (v16).** It is not how much a person earns but where it leads. The wicked man's money funds more sin (JFB: it tempts toward sin).
- **"Whoever heeds instruction is on the path to life, but he who rejects reproof leads others astray" (v17).** Refusing correction never stays private. The person who will not be taught sets an example and takes others with him.
- **"The one who conceals hatred has lying lips, and whoever utters slander is a fool" (v18).** Two wrong ways to handle hatred: hide it behind a smile, or spread it through gossip. Either is folly. The better way is in v12.
- **"When words are many, transgression is not lacking, but whoever restrains his lips is prudent" (v19).** This is one of the best-known sayings in the book. The more someone talks, the more chances there are to sin. Guzik observes that there is more room for sin in talking than in listening. JFB: "Much speech involves risk of sin." James says the same: "be quick to hear, slow to speak" (Jas 1:19), and "if anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man" (Jas 3:2).
- **"The tongue of the righteous is choice silver; the heart of the wicked is of little worth" (v20).** Tongue and heart are paired because words come from the heart (Matt 12:34). JFB: "Right speech is the fruit of a good heart."
- **"The lips of the righteous feed many, but fools die for lack of sense" (v21).** "Feed" is the shepherd's word (*raʿah*). Good words nourish a whole community. Fools cannot even feed themselves.
- **"The blessing of the LORD makes rich, and he adds no sorrow with it" (v22).** "He" is emphatic in Hebrew: it is *the LORD's* blessing that does it. The second line can be read two ways. The ESV has God adding no sorrow, unlike wealth gained wrongly, which brings anxiety (JFB compares Eccl 2:21–23). Others read "and toil adds nothing to it": human effort cannot add to what God gives. The word *ʿetsev* means "pain" or "toil," the same root as the pain and toil of the curse (Gen 3:16–17). Guzik reads v22 with v4: God's blessing and human diligence work together, and neither cancels the other.
- **"Doing wrong is like a joke to a fool, but wisdom is pleasure to a man of understanding" (v23).** "Joke" is literally "laughter" or "sport." A person shows who they are by what they enjoy. The fool plays with sin. The wise delight in wisdom.
- **"What the wicked dreads will come upon him, but the desire of the righteous will be granted" (v24).** Fear and desire are both answered, but differently. The wicked half-know what is coming. The righteous long for God's good, and it comes (Ps 37:4).
- **"When the tempest passes, the wicked is no more, but the righteous is established forever" (v25).** A desert flash storm can sweep away everything in a dry riverbed. The Tyndale notes, JFB and Guzik all connect this with Jesus' two builders, one on rock and one on sand (Matt 7:24–27). "Established forever" is literally "an everlasting foundation."
- **"Like vinegar to the teeth and smoke to the eyes, so is the sluggard to those who send him" (v26).** A rare comparison saying in this chapter. Sour wine sets the teeth on edge, and smoke from an open fire stings the eyes. Sending a lazy man on an errand is just as irritating. JFB: he "causes vexation."
- **"The fear of the LORD prolongs life, but the years of the wicked will be short" (v27).** As in 9:10–11. JFB gives only cross-references here (9:11; Ps 55:23). Again it is a general pattern. Some righteous people die young, and some wicked people live long (Eccl 7:15), but reverence for God tends toward a longer, steadier life.
- **"The hope of the righteous brings joy, but the expectation of the wicked will perish" (v28).** Both groups look forward to something. Only one is right. JFB: the wicked's hopes end "in disappointment."
- **"The way of the LORD is a stronghold to the blameless, but destruction to evildoers" (v29).** The same way of the LORD is a refuge for some and ruin for others, as the same sun softens wax and hardens clay. JFB compares Hosea 14:9: "the ways of the LORD are right… but transgressors stumble in them."
- **"The righteous will never be removed, but the wicked will not dwell in the land" (v30).** The Tyndale notes observe that this is one of the few sayings tied to Israel's covenant, the promise of the land for the obedient and exile for the rebellious (Deut 28). JFB: the "land of promise." Jesus widened it: "the meek… shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5).
- **"The mouth of the righteous brings forth wisdom, but the perverse tongue will be cut off" (vv31–32).** "Brings forth" is the word for a plant bearing fruit. JFB picks up the picture: the good tongue "germinates" like a fruitful plant, while the perverse one is cut down like a barren branch. The chapter ends where the speech theme began: the righteous know "what is acceptable," what fits and pleases, while the wicked know only what is twisted.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the sayings as "short but weighty sentences" whose "scope… is to set before us good and evil, the blessing and the curse," and he declines to sort them under topics, since they "appear best in their own places." Guzik likewise takes each verse on its own while allowing that some are grouped. He presses the personal application: our choices affect those who love us (v1), and we each shape how we will be remembered (v7). JFB notes every verse except v27, which gets only cross-references. All three treat v12 and v25 through the New Testament, Peter on love and Jesus on the two builders.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 10 divides humanity into two groups, the righteous and the wicked, and on that measure none of us belongs on the right side by nature (Rom 3:10). Only one man ever fully lived these sayings. Jesus restrained his lips when he was accused (Isa 53:7; 1 Pet 2:23). His mouth was a "fountain of life" (John 6:68; 7:37–38). His words fed many, literally and spiritually (Mark 6:34–44). He built on rock and is the rock (Matt 7:24; 1 Cor 10:4). And the love that "covers all offenses" is most fully seen at the cross, where he did not merely overlook sin but bore it, so that the unrighteous could be counted righteous in him (2 Cor 5:21).
