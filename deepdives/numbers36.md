*Setting:* The last scene of Numbers, still on the plains of Moab across from Jericho, shortly before Moses' death. In chapter 27 five sisters, the daughters of Zelophehad from the tribe of Manasseh, came to Moses with a problem: their father had died in the wilderness with no sons, so under the usual custom his name and land would vanish. God ruled in their favor, and they were given his inheritance. Now the men of their clan raise a follow-up question. If these women marry into another tribe, what happens to the land? The book that began with a census of fighting men ends with five women and a question about keeping a family's land.

#### Marriage of Female Heirs (vv1–13)
- **"The heads of the fathers' houses of the clan of… Gilead the son of Machir, son of Manasseh" (v1).** These are the leaders of Zelophehad's own clan. Gilead's descendants are the Manasseh clans who had just been given land east of the Jordan (32:39–40), though Zelophehad's family would receive its share west of the river (Josh 17:3–6). They come respectfully, before Moses and the tribal chiefs, the same way the daughters did.
- **"My lord was commanded by the LORD to give the inheritance of Zelophehad… to his daughters" (v2).** They do not challenge God's earlier ruling. They accept it and ask how it should work in practice.
- **"If they are married to any of the sons of the other tribes… it will be taken away from the lot of our inheritance" (v3).** In Israel land passed through the male line. When a woman married, she joined her husband's household, and her children belonged to his tribe. So land she inherited would eventually pass to her sons, and with them, to another tribe. Over time the borders God had drawn by lot (ch 34) would blur and shift.
- **"When the jubilee… comes" (v4).** Every fiftieth year, land that had been sold returned to its original family (Lev 25:10, 13, 23–28). That was God's safeguard against families losing their land for good. But the jubilee only reversed sales. Land passed on by inheritance was not "sold," so in the jubilee it would not come back. In fact the jubilee would seal the transfer, confirming the land as the other tribe's permanent possession. The clan leaders have thought this through carefully.
- **"The tribe of the people of Joseph is right" (v5).** Moses brings the question to God, as he did in chapter 27, and God agrees with the clan leaders. Both rulings stand: the women keep their inheritance, and the tribe keeps its land.
- **"Let them marry whom they think best, only they shall marry within the clan of the tribe of their father" (v6).** The daughters are not assigned husbands. They choose for themselves, within limits. In a world where marriages were usually arranged by fathers, that freedom is notable. The limit applied only to heiresses, women who had inherited land in their own right.
- **"Every one of the people of Israel shall hold on to the inheritance of the tribe of his fathers" (v7).** "Hold on to" is literally "cling to," the same verb used of a man clinging to his wife (Gen 2:24) and of Israel holding fast to God (Deut 10:20). The land was not a commodity to trade. It was a family's share in God's promise. That is why Naboth later refused to sell his vineyard even to the king: "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1 Kgs 21:3).
- **"So no inheritance shall be transferred from one tribe to another" (v9).** This becomes a general rule for every heiress in Israel, not just these five. A single family's case shapes the law for the whole nation, which is how much of Israel's case law grew.
- **"The daughters of Zelophehad did as the LORD commanded Moses" (v10).** They obey without complaint. The same five women who boldly asked for their rights in chapter 27 now willingly accept the limits that come with them.
- **"Mahlah, Tirzah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Noah" (v11).** Their names are recorded again, in a slightly different order from 27:1, and are mentioned once more when they receive their land (Josh 17:3–6). Several of these names also appear as place names in the territory of Manasseh. **Tirzah** later became a city and the first capital of the northern kingdom of Israel (1 Kgs 14:17; 15:33). The Samaria Ostraca, tax receipts from the 700s BC found in the ruins of Samaria, mention districts named **Hoglah** and **Noah**, along with other Manasseh clan names from Numbers 26:30–33. Their family names seem to have stayed attached to the land they inherited for centuries.
- **"Married to sons of their father's brothers" (v11).** They married their first cousins on their father's side. That was common and acceptable in the ancient Near East, and it was not forbidden by the marriage laws of Leviticus 18. It kept the land within Zelophehad's own clan.
- **"These are the commandments and the rules… in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho" (v13).** This is the closing line of the book. It mirrors the end of Leviticus (Lev 27:34), which closed the laws given at Sinai. Numbers began "in the wilderness of Sinai" (1:1) and ends "in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho," on the very edge of the promise. A whole generation has died between those two verses, and a new one stands ready. What remains is Deuteronomy, Moses' final sermons to that new generation before they cross.
- **Why end the book this way?** It can seem an anticlimax to end Numbers with a ruling about inheritance law. But it is fitting. The book has been about whether Israel would trust God enough to enter the land. Now, at the end, a family is so sure they will possess it that they are already arguing about keeping it in the family for generations. The old generation would not go in. These five women, and the clan leaders who care about their tribe's share, show faith that takes God's promise as already settled.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that without this rule some heiresses' land could pass to other tribes, a problem "for which even the Jubilee could not afford a remedy." It stresses that the limit fell "only on those who were heiresses," and calls this an example of "progressive legislation," suggested by circumstances and confined to civil matters, while the laws of worship did not change. Henry notes simply that "The daughters of Zelophehad submitted to this appointment," and draws a lesson for Christian marriage: if Israel's heiresses had to marry within their tribe, Christians should take care to be united "only to such as are united to the Lord" (compare 2 Cor 6:14; 1 Cor 7:39). Guzik agrees with the Tyndale notes that the jubilee covered sold land, not inherited land. He draws a practical lesson: solving one problem often creates another, and wise leaders accept solutions with trade-offs. He adds that the women were still free to choose their husbands, and that keeping tribal lines distinct also preserved the family lines through which the Messiah would come. JFB has no separate notes on vv2–4.

#### Pointing to Christ
Numbers ends with a family holding on to an inheritance they have not yet set foot on, because God promised it. The New Testament speaks of believers in exactly those terms: "heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ" (Rom 8:17), with "an inheritance that is imperishable" (1 Pet 1:4). And in Christ the barriers that limited Zelophehad's daughters fall away. There is no longer a risk of an inheritance passing to "another tribe," because in him "there is neither… male nor female… And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" (Gal 3:28–29). The careful guarding of tribal lines in Numbers served a purpose: it kept Israel's families intact until the promised Son of David came from the tribe of Judah. Through him, sons and daughters alike receive a share that no one can take away.
