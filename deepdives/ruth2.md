*Setting:* Naomi has come home to Bethlehem, a widow who left "full" and returned "empty" (1:21), with her Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth beside her. They arrived "at the beginning of barley harvest" (1:22), around late March or April. The two women have no men to work land for them and apparently no food. Chapter 2 takes place almost entirely in a single harvest field on a single day, followed by a summary of the weeks after. Nothing miraculous happens. A foreign widow goes to work, a landowner notices her, and kindness meets kindness. Yet behind every ordinary step the narrator lets the reader see God's hand guiding things toward the man who can rescue the family.

#### Ruth Meets Boaz (vv1–23)
- **"A relative of her husband's, a worthy man of the clan of Elimelech, whose name was Boaz" (v1).** The narrator tells the reader something Ruth does not know. "Worthy man" is *gibbor chayil*. *Gibbor* means a strong or mighty man and is used of warriors like Gideon (Judg 6:12), and *chayil* means strength, ability or wealth. Here it means a man of standing and substance, and as the chapter unfolds, of character too. The same word *chayil* will describe Ruth in 3:11: "a worthy woman." His name may mean "in him is strength." A pillar in Solomon's temple was later named Boaz (1 Kings 7:21).
- **"Let me go to the field and glean" (v2).** Gleaning was God's provision for the poor. The law told farmers not to reap the edges of their fields or go back for what they dropped, but to leave it "for the poor and for the sojourner" (Lev 19:9–10; 23:22). Deuteronomy adds the widow and the fatherless, and reminds Israel, "You shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt" (Deut 24:19–22). Ruth fits every category: poor, foreign, widowed. It was hard, low-status work, and a foreigner had no guarantee of a welcome, which is why she hopes to find "favor."
- **"Ruth the Moabite" (v2).** She takes the initiative, and she still asks Naomi's blessing. Naomi answers in three words, "Go, my daughter." She is still too numb to do more.
- **"She happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz" (v3).** Literally "her chance chanced upon" Boaz's portion. Fields around Bethlehem were not fenced. They were open land divided into family plots by boundary stones (Deut 19:14), so a gleaner could easily drift from one man's strip to another's. The narrator's "it happened" is a wink at the reader. From Ruth's point of view it was luck. The reader, who has just been told who Boaz is, knows better.
- **"Behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem" (v4).** "Behold" signals another "coincidence": he arrives at just this moment. His greeting, "The LORD be with you," and his workers' reply, "The LORD bless you," show a household where God's name is spoken naturally. It is a sharp contrast with the violence of Judges, set in the same era.
- **"Whose young woman is this?" (v5).** In that culture a woman was identified by the man she belonged to: father, husband, master. Boaz is asking who she belongs to. The answer is, in effect, no one.
- **"The young Moabite woman, who came back with Naomi" (vv6–7).** The foreman's report stresses her foreignness and her diligence. She asked permission before gleaning, "Please let me glean and gather among the sheaves." She has worked "from early morning until now, except for a short rest," probably in the field shelter where workers took shade.
- **"Do not go to glean in another field… keep close to my young women" (v8).** "Keep close" is the same verb, *dabaq*, used of Ruth clinging to Naomi in 1:14. Boaz calls her "my daughter," which may suggest he was a generation older than she was (compare 3:10).
- **"Have I not charged the young men not to touch you?" (v9).** A foreign woman alone in a field was vulnerable to harassment or worse. Boaz gives her protection, and water drawn by the men. In a culture where foreigners and women usually drew water for Israelite men, the roles are reversed.
- **"Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?" (v10).** There is a wordplay in the Hebrew: "take notice of me" is *hakkireni*, and "foreigner" is *nokriyyah*. They come from the same root. Ruth is saying, roughly, "Why do you recognize someone who is unrecognized?" She bows to the ground, overwhelmed.
- **"How you left your father and mother and your native land" (v11).** Boaz has heard the whole story. His words echo God's call to Abraham, "Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house" (Gen 12:1). Ruth has made Abraham's journey of faith in reverse direction, into the land of promise.
- **"Under whose wings you have come to take refuge" (v12).** The picture is a mother bird sheltering her young, a favorite image of God's protection (Deut 32:11; Ps 17:8; 36:7; 91:4). It became a classic Jewish phrase for a convert to Israel's God. Boaz prays that God will reward Ruth. The irony, which becomes clear in 3:9, is that Boaz himself will be the answer. There Ruth asks him to spread his "wings" (the same Hebrew word, meaning the corner of a garment) over her.
- **"You have comforted me and spoken kindly to your servant" (v13).** "Spoken kindly" is literally "spoken to the heart," the tender idiom of Judges 19:3 and Hosea 2:14. "Though I am not one of your servants" shows her humility: she does not even rank with his servant girls.
- **The meal (v14).** Boaz invites her to eat with his workers, "dip your morsel in the wine," probably a sour wine or vinegar used as a refreshing dip in the heat (compare Num 6:3). He himself "passed to her roasted grain," fresh heads of grain parched over a fire, a common field meal. "She ate until she was satisfied, and she had some left over." In a chapter that began in hunger, that line matters. The empty widow will be fed by what Ruth saves.
- **"Let her glean even among the sheaves… pull out some from the bundles for her" (vv15–16).** Gleaners normally worked only after the sheaves had been gathered. Boaz lets her work among them, and goes further, telling his men to drop grain on purpose, and not to "reproach" or "rebuke" her. He gives generously while protecting her dignity. She will still earn it.
- **"About an ephah of barley" (v17).** She beat out the grain with a stick on the spot, separating it from the stalks. An ephah was a dry measure of roughly two-thirds of a bushel, perhaps 25–30 pounds of barley. For one day's gleaning, this is remarkable, enough to feed two women for some weeks.
- **"She also brought out and gave her what food she had left over" (v18).** Ruth gives Naomi her saved lunch too. The leftover in v14 now reaches the one who stayed home.
- **"Blessed be the man who took notice of you" (v19).** Naomi blesses him before she knows who he is. Ruth saves the name for last: "The man's name… is Boaz." The narrator has made the reader wait for this moment.
- **"Whose kindness has not forsaken the living or the dead!" (v20).** "Kindness" is *hesed*, loyal love, again. The Hebrew is ambiguous: "whose" can refer to the LORD or to Boaz. It probably refers to the LORD, who has shown his faithful love through Boaz. Either way, this is a turning point for Naomi. In chapter 1 she said God's hand was against her. Now she sees his kindness reaching both the living (Naomi and Ruth) and the dead (Elimelech and Mahlon, whose family line might still continue).
- **"One of our redeemers" (v20).** The word is *go'el*, a kinsman-redeemer. In Israel the nearest male relative had duties to rescue family members in trouble: buy back family land sold in poverty (Lev 25:25), buy back a relative sold into slavery (Lev 25:47–49), and avenge a murdered relative (Num 35:19). The custom of a brother marrying his dead brother's childless widow (Deut 25:5–10) is related but separate. Ruth will bring them together. "One of" hints that there is more than one, which matters in chapter 4. The same word is used of God himself, Israel's Redeemer (Exod 6:6; Isa 41:14; Job 19:25).
- **"Lest in another field you be assaulted" (vv21–22).** Ruth reports that Boaz told her to stay with his "young men." Naomi gently corrects it: stay with his "young women." In the days of the judges, a foreign woman working alone was in real danger. Boaz's field is safe.
- **"Until the end of the barley and wheat harvests" (v23).** The wheat harvest followed the barley and ended around late May or early June, at the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost). That is seven weeks of daily work, and of daily contact with Boaz, but apparently nothing more. "And she lived with her mother-in-law." The harvest is ending. Ruth still has no husband and no long-term security, which sets up Naomi's plan in chapter 3.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Ruth's willingness to glean a mark of good character: "Humility is one of the brightest ornaments of youth." On her arrival in Boaz's field he writes, "God wisely orders small events," and on v12 he links "under whose wings" to the Jewish idea of a convert gathered under God's protection. JFB explains that gleaning right behind the reapers was "a privilege granted or refused according to the good will or favor of the owner," calls the greeting in v4 a "pious salutation between the master and his laborers," and reads "one of our redeemers" as a hint that another kinsman stood nearer. Guzik stresses that God's hand in v3 is clearer in hindsight than it was at the time, and that Boaz showed kindness to someone who could give him nothing back. He also notes the dignity of the gleaning system, where the owner gives generously and the poor still work. Henry and Guzik both see Naomi's blessing in v20 as her first step out of bitterness.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ruth came as a foreigner with nothing, seeking refuge under the wings of Israel's God, and she found a lord of the harvest who noticed her, protected her, fed her until she was satisfied, and gave her more than she could earn while guarding her dignity. Boaz is a picture, imperfect but striking, of Christ. Jesus is the greater kinsman-redeemer, who became our relative by taking on flesh (Heb 2:14–17) so that he could redeem us. He longed to gather Jerusalem "as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (Matt 23:37), and he welcomes outsiders to his table, where he gives more than enough. What looked like Ruth's chance was God's care. In the same way, God's hand is often clearest when looking back.
