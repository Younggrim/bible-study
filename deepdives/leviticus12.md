*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai. Leviticus 11–15 explains what made a person or thing "clean" or "unclean," that is, fit or unfit to come near God's sanctuary. Chapter 11 dealt with animals. Chapter 12, one of the shortest in the Bible, turns to people, starting with the beginning of life: a mother after childbirth. Its eight verses can seem strange to modern readers, but they reappear at a key moment in the Gospels, when Mary and Joseph bring the infant Jesus to the temple (Luke 2:22–24).

#### Purification After Childbirth (vv1–8)
- **"She shall be unclean seven days. As at the time of her menstruation" (v2).** The uncleanness is tied to the bleeding that follows birth, not to the baby, and not to sex or to sin. The chapter's closing line makes this plain: "she shall be clean from the *flow of her blood*" (v7). The rules for menstruation are in 15:19–24. In Leviticus, blood is the carrier of life (17:11), and losing it, like contact with death, put a person in a state that could not mix with the holy, living God. Uncleanness here is a ritual state, not a moral failure. The Tyndale notes say it became sin only if someone ignored it and brought it into contact with holy things.
- **Childbirth is a blessing, not a curse.** Children are called a gift from the LORD (Ps 127:3), and "be fruitful and multiply" was God's first command (Gen 1:28). Nothing in this chapter blames the mother. But childbirth is also bound up with the fall: "in pain you shall bring forth children" (Gen 3:16). Birth is a moment where life and the effects of the curse meet.
- **"On the eighth day the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised" (v3).** This is the only command about circumcision in Leviticus (26:41 uses the idea only as a picture of the heart). It goes back to God's covenant with Abraham (Gen 17:10–12). The boy is brought into the covenant on the very day his mother's first week of uncleanness ends. Doctors often note that the eighth day is a safer time for minor surgery because a newborn's blood clots better by then. Whatever the medical benefit, the reason the text gives is covenant. Jesus was circumcised "at the end of eight days" (Luke 2:21).
- **"Thirty-three days in the blood of her purifying" (v4).** After the first seven days, a second, milder stage followed. During it she was no longer unclean to the people around her, but she could **"not touch anything holy, nor come into the sanctuary."** That would include eating the holy meat of a peace offering. Seven plus thirty-three makes forty days, a number that often marks a full period of testing or transition in Scripture.
- **"If she bears a female child… two weeks… sixty-six days" (v5).** Both stages double for a daughter, making eighty days in all. The text gives no reason, and readers have long wondered why. Some older interpreters, including JFB, saw it as a mark on women because Eve sinned first (1 Tim 2:14). Many recent scholars suggest instead that a baby girl is herself a future mother, so the birth of one who will bear life is marked more fully. Others point out that newborn girls sometimes have a little vaginal bleeding from their mother's hormones, so two persons were in a sense affected. Whatever the reason, it is not a judgment on a daughter's worth: the very same offering is brought "whether for a son or for a daughter" (v6), and the mother's restoration is identical. It is wise not to press any single explanation beyond what the text says.
- **"A lamb a year old for a burnt offering, and a pigeon or a turtledove for a sin offering" (v6).** The order is surprising: the burnt offering is named first here, though the priest probably offered the sin offering first, as usual (5:8–10). The burnt offering was an act of worship and dedication. The "sin offering" is the *hatta't*, the same word used for sin itself. BDB lists both meanings. Many scholars think it is better called a "purification offering" in passages like this one, because it cleanses a person or place from defilement rather than forgiving a moral wrong. The Tyndale notes take the same view: the point here is restoring her to worship, not forgiving a sinner.
- **"The priest… shall make atonement for her. Then she shall be clean" (v7).** "Make atonement" (*kipper*) here means to cleanse and restore. After about six weeks, or eleven for a daughter, she could come back to the sanctuary and take part fully in Israel's worship. The offering marked her return, and the whole community would know she was restored.
- **"If she cannot afford a lamb… two turtledoves or two pigeons" (v8).** The law made room for the poor, as it did with other offerings (5:7–13). No family was too poor to be restored. This is exactly what Mary brought: "a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons" (Luke 2:24). Luke quotes this verse without comment, but every Jewish reader would know what it meant: Jesus was born into a poor family. Luke even places the scene as Mary completing "their purification according to the Law of Moses" (Luke 2:22).
- **Mary and the sin offering.** Mary, the mother of the sinless Son of God, still brought a *hatta't*. Christians have read that in different ways. Roman Catholic teaching holds that Mary was preserved from sin and submitted to the law out of humility. Most Protestant readers see it as a sign that Mary, like everyone else, needed the Savior she bore (Luke 1:47). Either way, the offering was about the flow of blood after birth, which happened to her as to any mother, and she kept the law faithfully.
- **Commentators:** All three read the chapter as pointing to humanity's fallen condition. Guzik, citing others, suggests each birth brings another sinner into the world, and adds that the long seclusion gave a new mother much-needed rest. JFB says the offerings were "to impress the mind of the parent with recollections of the origin of sin," and calls Mary's two birds "an incontestable proof of the poor and humble condition of the family." Henry reads the burnt offering as gratitude: "This she was to offer in thankfulness to God for his mercy to her" in a safe delivery. He points out that the rich and the poor brought the same sin offering, since atonement costs everyone the same, and that Christ, "made under the law, to redeem those that were under it," is the true offering by which "the corruption of our nature is done away." The Tyndale notes are more cautious than the commentators, tying the uncleanness to the blood rather than to the child's sinful nature.

#### Pointing to Christ
Forty days after Jesus was born, his mother walked into the temple to offer what this chapter required, and she could afford only the poor family's birds. The Son of God entered a world marked by blood and loss, was "born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law" (Gal 4:4–5), and was carried as a baby into the very courts where his own sacrifice would one day make the offerings of Leviticus unnecessary. Even at the beginning of life, Leviticus 12 teaches that we need cleansing and restoration from God. Jesus came, from the beginning of his own life, to bring it.
