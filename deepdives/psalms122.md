*Setting:* Psalm 122 is the third of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the great festivals. Psalm 120 began far away, among liars, and Psalm 121 was on the road through the hills. Now the pilgrims have arrived. Their feet are standing inside the city gates (v2). The title reads "Of David," one of four Songs of Ascents with his name (with 124, 131 and 133). David captured Jerusalem from the Jebusites, made it his capital, brought the ark of the covenant there, and planned the temple his son Solomon would build (2 Sam 5:6–9; 6:12–17; 1 Chron 22). If David wrote it, "the house of the LORD" in v1 would be the tent he pitched for the ark, and the psalm would also look ahead to the city's future. Some scholars think the title means "for David" or "in David's style," and date the psalm later, pointing to the "thrones of the house of David" (v5) as a memory of the royal line. Either way the psalm was sung for centuries by pilgrims who arrived at the gates and looked around the city with joy. It also contains one of the most quoted prayers in the Bible: "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem!" The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Let Us Go to the House of the LORD! (vv1–2)
- **"I was glad when they said to me, 'Let us go to the house of the LORD!'" (v1).** The pilgrim remembers the moment the invitation came, probably neighbors or relatives back home saying, "It's time; let's go up." Pilgrims traveled in groups from the same village or clan (Luke 2:44). His gladness began before the journey did. Worship was not a duty he dragged himself to. He was glad to be asked. David wrote elsewhere, "One thing have I asked of the LORD… that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life, to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD" (27:4).
- **"The house of the LORD" (v1).** This means the place where God chose to put his name and meet his people: first the tabernacle, then Solomon's temple, then the second temple rebuilt after the exile (Deut 12:5; 1 Kings 8:29).
- **"Our feet have been standing within your gates, O Jerusalem!" (v2).** The pronouns shift from "me" to "our." The single pilgrim of vv1 and 120:1 is now part of a crowd. "Have been standing" can also be translated "are standing" or "stood." After days on the road, they are inside. The gates of an ancient city were where people met, traded and held court (Ruth 4:1–2). The pilgrim speaks to the city directly, the way someone greets a place they have longed to see.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the pleasure and profit from means of grace, should make us disregard trouble and fatigue in going to them." JFB notes that "our feet shall stand" (KJV) is "literally, 'are standing.'" Guzik sees both the community's invitation and the individual's joy in accepting it, and argues that God's people should find gathering for worship a glad thing, though he is careful to say that gladness is not the same as entertainment.

#### Jerusalem, Built as a City Bound Firmly Together (vv3–5)
- **"Jerusalem—built as a city that is bound firmly together" (v3).** "Bound firmly together" translates *chubberah*, from *chabar*, "to join." It is the word used for coupling the curtains of the tabernacle so it became "a single whole" (Exod 26:6, 11). Jerusalem was a small city packed on a narrow ridge, its houses close together inside strong walls. To a villager from the countryside it looked solid and tightly knit. The picture is also of a people joined into one.
- **"To which the tribes go up, the tribes of the LORD" (v4).** Jerusalem did not belong to any one tribe. David took it from the Jebusites, so it was neutral ground on the border between Judah and Benjamin, a capital for all twelve. At the festivals all the tribes came together there. "The tribes of the LORD" reminds them who they really belong to. The northern tribes later broke away and built rival shrines at Bethel and Dan so their people would stop going up to Jerusalem (1 Kings 12:26–29). This psalm sings of the unity they abandoned.
- **"As was decreed for Israel, to give thanks to the name of the LORD" (v4).** The Hebrew is literally "a testimony for Israel." The ESV takes it as a law or decree, since the law required the pilgrimages (Exod 23:17; Deut 16:16). Some older commentators read it as a reference to the ark, which held the "testimony," the stone tablets of the covenant (Exod 25:16, 21). The purpose is clear either way: "to give thanks." The pilgrims did not come mainly for trade, politics or sightseeing. They came to thank God.
- **"There thrones for judgment were set, the thrones of the house of David" (v5).** Jerusalem was also the seat of justice. The king heard cases there, and so did the royal officials (2 Sam 15:2–4; 1 Kings 3:16–28; 7:7). The plural "thrones" may mean the king and those who judged under him. Worship and justice were meant to go together in God's city (Isa 1:21, 26). The "house of David" also points forward, since God had promised David a son whose throne would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16; Isa 9:7).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains "compact together" as "all parts united, as in David's time," allows that "testimony" may denote the ark, and says "thrones" stands "for the occupants, David's sons." Henry calls Jerusalem "a type of the gospel church, which is compact together in holy love and Christian communion," and warns that "Satan's maxim always has been, to divide that he may conquer." Guzik, citing Kidner, links the verb in v3 to the coupling of the tabernacle curtains and notes, with Spurgeon and Trapp, that a city belonging to no tribe could belong to all, a picture of unity amid diversity.

#### Pray for the Peace of Jerusalem! (vv6–9)
- **"Pray for the peace of Jerusalem!" (v6).** Literally "ask for the *shalom* of Jerusalem." The line is full of sound play. In Hebrew, "ask," "peace," "Jerusalem" and "secure" all share or echo the sounds *sh* and *l*: *sha'alu shelom yerushalayim yishlayu*. Many think the name Jerusalem itself was heard as "city of peace" (compare Salem in Gen 14:18 and Heb 7:2), though the name's origin is debated. *Shalom* is more than the absence of war. It means wholeness, safety, well-being and right relationships.
- **"May they be secure who love you!" (v6).** "Secure" (*shalah*) means to be at ease, to enjoy quiet prosperity. Those who love God's city share in its peace.
- **"Peace be within your walls and security within your towers!" (v7).** The pilgrims speak a blessing over the city. Walls and towers were its defenses. The prayer is that what protects the city would hold, so that life inside could flourish. "Security" (*shalvah*) again rhymes with "peace."
- **"For my brothers and companions' sake" (v8).** The first reason to pray is love for people. Jerusalem is where his fellow Israelites live and gather, so its welfare is theirs.
- **"For the sake of the house of the LORD our God, I will seek your good" (v9).** The second and deeper reason is God. His house is there. "Seek your good" means more than praying. It means working for the city's well-being. Jeremiah later told the exiles to do the same for Babylon, of all places: "seek the welfare of the city… and pray to the LORD on its behalf" (Jer 29:7).
- **The irony of Jerusalem's history.** Few cities have seen less peace. Jerusalem was besieged and destroyed by Babylon in 586 BC and by Rome in AD 70, and it has been fought over many times since. The prayer is needed precisely because peace is fragile. Christians today apply it in different ways: as a prayer for the literal city and its people, as a prayer for the church, God's people, which the New Testament calls "the Jerusalem above" (Gal 4:26), and as a longing for the "new Jerusalem" where God will dwell with his people forever (Rev 21:2–4). These are not exclusive.
- **Commentators:** JFB says "peace" here includes prosperity, that the welfare of Israel was bound up with the city's civil and religious life, and that "house of… God" may be taken "in wider sense, the Church." Henry: "Those who can do nothing else for the peace of Jerusalem, may pray for it." Guzik notes the wordplay between "peace" and "prosperity," passes on Kidner's point that prosperity is the natural fruit of the justice of v5, and, with Horne, finds two motives in vv8–9: love for neighbors and love for God. He applies it to the church, where inner peace is vital and division does great harm.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 122 rejoices in a city where the tribes are joined into one, where God is worshiped, and where the son of David sits on the throne of justice. Jesus came to that city as the Son of David, and wept over it: "Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace!" (Luke 19:41–42). He is himself the peace the pilgrims prayed for. "He himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his body the dividing wall of hostility" (Eph 2:14). In him Jews and Gentiles are "built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" (Eph 2:22), a temple "bound firmly together." He told a Samaritan woman that the hour was coming when worship would not be tied to one mountain or city (John 4:21–24). And Hebrews says believers have already "come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem… and to Jesus" (Heb 12:22–24). The gladness of "Let us go to the house of the LORD!" now belongs to everyone who comes to God through him.
