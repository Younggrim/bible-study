*Setting:* Chapter 5 left the bride searching the night streets after failing to open the door to her beloved, then describing him to the daughters of Jerusalem as "altogether desirable." Chapter 6 opens with their next question: where has he gone? Her answer shows he was never really lost (vv1–3). Then he speaks, and instead of reproach he pours out praise, repeating parts of his wedding-night description from chapter 4 (vv4–10). The chapter ends with one of the most difficult passages in the Song (vv11–13), in which she goes down to a nut orchard, is swept away by her desire, and is called back by the chorus as "the Shulammite," the only time she is given a name. In Hebrew Bibles the last verse (6:13) is counted as 7:1. For the main ways the Song is read, see the Setting of chapter 1. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song. It labels the speakers, and the headings below follow those exchanges.

#### Where Has Your Beloved Gone? (vv1–3)
- **"Where has your beloved gone, O most beautiful among women? Where has your beloved turned, that we may seek him with you?" (v1).** Her description of him (5:10–16) has won them over. Now they want to help her find him. Guzik notes it's unclear whether they're sincere or teasing, but either way the question moves the scene forward. Henry: "Those who would find Christ, must seek him early and diligently."
- **"My beloved has gone down to his garden to the beds of spices, to graze in the gardens and to gather lilies" (v2).** She knows exactly where he is. Throughout the Song "garden" has pictured the bride herself (4:12–5:1), and the Tyndale notes take it that way: the dream is over, and he is with her again. Guzik takes the garden more literally here and draws a lesson from it: her panicked search through the city failed, but calm thought about who he is and where he'd be led her to him.
- **"I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine; he grazes among the lilies" (v3).** These are the words of 2:16 in reverse. There she began with "my beloved is mine." Now, after the failure and the night of searching, she begins with "I am my beloved's." Her security now rests in belonging to him more than in having him. The next time it appears it goes further still: "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me" (7:10). Guzik draws the same point, and passes on Glickman's emphasis on forgiveness in marriage.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lists three things she knew: where he was, that they still belonged to each other, and that he would want to make things right. He passes on Kinlaw's remark that her answer shows she has not really lost him. Henry: "It is the comfort of the church, that he feeds among the lilies, that he takes delight in his people."

#### Beautiful as Tirzah, Awesome as an Army (vv4–7)
- **"You are beautiful as Tirzah, my love, lovely as Jerusalem" (v4).** He compares her to two cities. Ancient poets often pictured cities as women (Isa 62:1–5; Lam 1:1). Tirzah, whose name means "pleasant" or "delight," was a lovely old Canaanite city in the northern hills (Josh 12:24). After the kingdom split it became the first capital of the northern kingdom, until Omri built Samaria around 880 BC (1 Kgs 16:23–24). Some scholars think pairing Tirzah with Jerusalem points to a time after the split. Others, like JFB, note that Tirzah was famous for its beauty long before then, so it says nothing certain about the date. The Tyndale notes give the history and the meaning of the name.
- **"Awesome as an army with banners" (v4).** "Awesome" (*'ayummah*) means terrifying, awe-inspiring, used of the fearsome Chaldeans (Hab 1:7). "Army with banners" uses the same root as "banner" in 2:4 and "distinguished" in 5:10. Her beauty is so overwhelming that it is almost frightening, like an army in full array. The Tyndale notes: her grandeur makes knees tremble and hearts race.
- **"Turn away your eyes from me, for they overwhelm me" (v5).** One look from her undoes him, as in 4:9. "Overwhelm" may mean "storm against," "arouse," or "make me bold," and the Hebrew verb is rare. It is a confession that he is not in control around her.
- **"Your hair is like a flock of goats… Your teeth are like a flock of ewes… Your cheeks are like halves of a pomegranate behind your veil" (vv5–7).** He repeats, almost word for word, his praise from the wedding night (4:1–3). Nothing has changed. The night of the closed door hasn't lessened his love. The Tyndale notes: the repetition shows his high regard for his wife. Guzik notices that he repeats only the face and leaves out the more intimate features of 4:4–5. He reads it as a reconciliation that is about her as a person and not merely about desire. JFB passes over v7 without comment, since it repeats 4:3.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that the man shows no trace of bitterness over the closed door. He forgives quickly and speaks only praise, a model for married couples after a quarrel. JFB reads the army as the church "not only… on the defensive, but as an army on the offensive," and links the banners to *YHWH Nissi*, "the LORD is my banner" (Exod 17:15). Henry: "True believers alone can possess the beauty of holiness."

#### My Dove, My Perfect One, Is the Only One (vv8–10)
- **"There are sixty queens and eighty concubines, and virgins without number" (v8).** A royal court full of women. Queens were wives of high rank, often foreign princesses married for alliances. Concubines were lesser wives. The numbers may be poetic, the "three… four" pattern of Proverbs 30 raised to "sixty… eighty," meaning "lots… and more." If it refers to Solomon's harem, it must be early, since by the end of his reign he had seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (1 Kgs 11:3). Guzik notes the tension and offers several possible explanations. Carr, cited by Guzik, thinks no particular harem is meant. JFB takes the women as witnesses of the wedding, not Solomon's wives. The Tyndale notes add a caution: Solomon's many marriages are a poor picture of God's faithful love, which is one reason not to press a strict allegory.
- **"My dove, my perfect one, is the only one, the only one of her mother, pure to her who bore her" (v9).** Against all those numbers stands one. "The only one" (*'achat*) is literally "one." To him she is unique, the one and only. "Pure" means "choice," "radiant," the favorite. "Perfect one" is *tammati* again (5:2). Whatever the court holds, his heart holds one woman. This is the Song's ideal, set against the background of royal polygamy: one man, one woman, wholly devoted (Gen 2:24).
- **"The young women saw her and called her blessed; the queens and concubines also, and they praised her" (v9).** Even her rivals admire her. "Called her blessed" is the same phrase as in Proverbs 31:28, where the excellent wife's children "rise up and call her blessed." Guzik notes that praise from others is one of the best ways to build someone up.
- **"Who is this who looks down like the dawn, beautiful as the moon, bright as the sun, awesome as an army with banners?" (v10).** This may be the queens' and concubines' praise, quoted. The ESV sets it in quotation marks. Dawn, moon, sun: she grows brighter with each image. The words for "moon" (*levanah*, "the white one") and "sun" (*chammah*, "the hot one") are poetic names, used together in Isaiah 30:26. The verse ends with the same phrase as v4, "awesome as an army with banners," closing his praise as it opened.
- **Commentators:** JFB on v9: "Hollow professors, like half wives, have no part in the one bride." On v10 it reads the four images as stages of the church's growing light, and notes, "As yet she is not come to the fulness of her light." Henry compares the believer's growth to the morning light growing brighter, and to the moon, which shines only by light borrowed from the sun. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that banners signal distinction, discipline and confidence.

#### The Nut Orchard and the Shulammite (vv11–13)
- **"I went down to the nut orchard to look at the blossoms of the valley, to see whether the vines had budded, whether the pomegranates were in bloom" (v11).** It is not clear who speaks. The ESV gives it to her, but the Tyndale notes say it could be him. The "nut orchard" (*ginnat 'egoz*) is a walnut grove, the only mention of nuts in the Song. Spring is coming again, as in 2:11–13. Budding vines and blooming pomegranates are pictures of love ready to be enjoyed (7:12). Guzik sees renewed love after the quarrel.
- **"Before I was aware, my desire set me among the chariots of my kinsman, a prince" (v12).** This is widely considered the hardest verse in the Song to translate. The Hebrew reads literally something like "I did not know; my soul set me chariots of my-people-noble." The KJV took the last words as a name, "the chariots of Amminadib." The ESV reads "my kinsman, a prince." Others give "the chariots of my noble people" or "my people's prince." The general sense seems to be that she is suddenly carried away, by her own desire, into the royal chariot or the company of her prince. The Tyndale notes take it as desire leading to union, and suggest it may point to Solomon as the husband. Guzik, following the RSV, suggests she may be riding in Solomon's chariot. Every translation is partly a guess here, and it is fair to say so.
- **"Return, return, O Shulammite, return, return, that we may look upon you" (v13).** The chorus calls after her, four times. "Return" may mean "come back" or "turn around" so we can see you, as in a dance. This is the only time she is named. "Shulammite" may mean a woman from Shunem, a town in the Jezreel valley (1 Kgs 1:3; 2 Kgs 4:8), with the letters *l* and *n* switching, as sometimes happened. Or it may be a feminine form of "Solomon," Solomon's counterpart, "Solomon's woman," a play on his name (*shelomoh*) and *shalom*, "peace." The Tyndale notes and Guzik give both views. JFB takes the second.
- **"Why should you look upon the Shulammite, as upon a dance before two armies?" (v13).** The ESV gives this to the man. Others give it to her, modestly asking why anyone would want to watch her. "Two armies" is *machanayim*, which is also the name of a town east of the Jordan, Mahanaim. Jacob named it after he met the angels of God there: "This is God's camp" (Gen 32:1–2). The phrase may mean a well-known dance from Mahanaim, a dance between two lines of dancers, or simply a dance before a large crowd. Guzik passes on Carr's rejection of the idea of a sword dance and Kinlaw's reading of her as modestly reluctant. The question leads straight into the next chapter, where she dances and he describes her once more (7:1–9).
- **Commentators:** JFB sees in Mahanaim the two hosts, Christ's family in heaven and on earth, recalling where "Jacob prevailed in prayer." It reads the walnut, with its bitter husk, hard shell and sweet kernel, as a picture of God's Word, hard until its inner sense is opened. Henry: "In retirement and in meditation the Christian character is formed and perfected." He also says the humble believer may shrink from notice, but the Lord honors them.

#### Pointing to Christ
After her failure at the door, the bride expects nothing but loss. Instead her beloved meets her with praise, repeats his wedding words as if nothing had broken them, and calls her his only one. That is the shape of grace. Peter denied Jesus three times, and the risen Lord met him by the sea, fed him breakfast and gave him his calling back (John 21:15–19). In a court crowded with queens and concubines, the man of the Song says "my dove… is the only one" (v9). Christ's love for his church is like that: one bride, chosen and cherished, "holy and without blemish" (Eph 5:27). She looks down "like the dawn," growing brighter as the sun (v10), as "the path of the righteous is like the light of dawn, which shines brighter and brighter until full day" (Prov 4:18). And she, who began by saying "my beloved is mine," learns to say first "I am my beloved's" (v3), the restful confidence of those who know they are "not your own," because they were "bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:19–20).
