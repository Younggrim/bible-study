*Setting:* Spring, around Passover (4:19 dates the end of the crossing to the tenth day of the first month, the very day Passover lambs were chosen, Exod 12:3). Israel moves the last few miles from Shittim to the east bank of the Jordan. The spies have returned with good news (2:24), and the three days Joshua announced (1:11) are up. In front of them is the river in flood. At most times of year the lower Jordan is a modest stream, perhaps 30 yards wide and a few feet deep, winding through a thicket of brush and tamarisk. In spring, swollen by winter rain and snowmelt from Mount Hermon, it spills over its banks and fills the whole lower channel, fast and muddy. Behind Israel is the wilderness, and across the water is Jericho. There is no bridge, no boat, and no other way in.

#### Israel Crosses the Jordan (vv1–17)
- **"Joshua rose early in the morning" (v1).** The phrase recurs at key moments (6:12; 7:16; 8:10), and it was used of Abraham on the morning he set out to sacrifice Isaac (Gen 22:3). Joshua moves the whole nation, perhaps a couple of million people with flocks, if the census numbers are read at face value, down to the water's edge, and they camp there not knowing how they will cross.
- **"As soon as you see the ark of the covenant" (v3).** The ark was a gold-covered chest holding the stone tablets of the law, with the mercy seat and cherubim on top (Exod 25:10–22). It was the place of God's throne among his people. In the wilderness the ark went ahead to find Israel a resting place (Num 10:33–36). Now it leads them into the land. "The ark" is named about ten times in this chapter. The story is about where God goes, and his people follow.
- **"The Levitical priests" (v3).** Normally the Levites of the clan of Kohath carried the ark (Num 4:15). For special occasions the priests themselves carried it (6:6; 1 Kings 8:3–6). This day is one of those.
- **"About 2,000 cubits" (v4).** A cubit was about 18 inches, so this was roughly 1,000 yards, over half a mile. There were two reasons, both stated or implied. The ark was holy, and people were not to crowd it (Num 4:15; 2 Sam 6:6–7). And with that much open ground, a vast column could still see it: "in order that you may know the way you shall go, for you have not passed this way before." A strange road calls for a clear guide. Later Jewish tradition took 2,000 cubits as the limit of a "Sabbath day's journey" (compare Acts 1:12), drawing partly on this verse.
- **"Consecrate yourselves, for tomorrow the LORD will do wonders" (v5).** To consecrate is to set oneself apart for God. Before the LORD came down at Sinai the people washed their clothes and kept from sexual relations (Exod 19:10–15). Something similar is meant here. "Wonders" (*nifla'ot*) is the word for things only God can do, used of the plagues in Egypt (Exod 3:20). The people prepare their hearts before seeing the miracle, not after.
- **"Today I will begin to exalt you" (v7).** The crossing confirms Joshua as Moses' true successor. Moses' authority was sealed at the Red Sea: "the people feared the LORD, and they believed in the LORD and in his servant Moses" (Exod 14:31). The same happens here (4:14). The aim is not Joshua's fame. It is that Israel "may know that, as I was with Moses, so I will be with you."
- **"You shall stand still in the Jordan" (v8).** The priests' orders were strange. They were to walk into a flooding river carrying a heavy chest and stop there. They had to step in before anything happened.
- **"The living God is among you" (v10).** Canaan's gods were images of wood, stone and metal. Israel's God acts. The crossing would be proof that he would also drive out the land's peoples.
- **The seven nations (v10).** This is the standard list of Canaan's peoples (Deut 7:1). The **Canaanites** lived mainly along the coast and in the lowlands, and their name is sometimes used for everyone. The **Amorites** were in the hill country and east of the Jordan. The **Hittites** here were probably small groups linked to the great Hittite empire in Turkey. The **Hivites** lived around Gibeon (9:7) and in the north. The **Jebusites** held Jerusalem until David (2 Sam 5:6–9). Little is known of the **Perizzites**, perhaps villagers rather than an ethnic group, or of the **Girgashites**. Seven is a number of completeness: every people in the land.
- **"The ark of the covenant of the Lord of all the earth" (v11).** The title is repeated in v13. The Canaanites worshiped local gods tied to their cities, hills and crops. Israel's God is not the god of one people or patch of ground. He owns the whole earth, and so he can give this part of it to whomever he pleases.
- **"Take twelve men… from each tribe a man" (v12).** Their job is not explained until 4:2–8. They will carry the memorial stones.
- **"The waters… shall stand in one heap" (v13).** "Heap" (*ned*) is the word used of the Red Sea in Moses' song: "the floods stood up in a heap" (Exod 15:8; also Ps 78:13). Joshua announces the miracle in advance, so no one could later say it was luck.
- **"The feet of the priests… were dipped in the brink of the water" (v15).** The miracle waited for their first step. The writer adds the reason it mattered: "the Jordan overflows all its banks throughout the time of harvest." This was barley harvest, in March and April. The river was at its highest and most dangerous.
- **"Rose up in a heap very far away, at Adam" (v16).** Adam is usually identified with Tell ed-Damiyeh, where the Jabbok River joins the Jordan, roughly 20 miles upstream from the crossing. **Zarethan** was a town nearby. The water did not part into walls as at the Red Sea. It stopped far upstream, and the riverbed below drained toward "the Sea of the Arabah, the Salt Sea," the Dead Sea. A wide stretch of riverbed was opened so the whole nation could cross at once.
- **Did God use natural means?** The banks near Damiyeh are high, soft marl cliffs. Arab historians record that in AD 1267 a landslide there blocked the Jordan for several hours, and in 1927 an earthquake brought down the banks and stopped the river for about a day. Many believers think God may have used such a collapse here. Others think the text describes a direct, unexplained act. Either way the text's point stands: the water stopped at the exact moment the priests' feet touched it, on the day God said, during the flood. Timing like that is not chance.
- **"The people passed over opposite Jericho" (v16).** They crossed in full view of the city they had come to take.
- **"Stood firmly on dry ground in the midst of the Jordan" (v17).** The priests stood still in the riverbed, holding the ark, while the whole nation went past them. "Dry ground" (*charabah*) means ground no longer under water, the same word used of the Red Sea crossing (Exod 14:21). "On dry ground" is said twice in one verse. A river in flood should leave deep mud. The people walked across on firm footing.
- **"Until all the nation finished passing over" (v17).** The ark stayed in the most dangerous place until the last person was across. It did not go over first and wait on the far bank.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those that would bring great things to pass must rise early," and the ark "was both their honour and their defence." He reads following the ark as following God's word, and following ministers only so far as they follow Christ. JFB writes that "the ark was to accomplish what had been done by the rod of Moses," and notes the firm, pebbly bed of the river near Jericho. Guzik argues the Jordan took more faith than the Red Sea: at the sea Israel was fleeing danger with no choice, while here they walked into danger and cut off their own retreat. He mentions the 1927 landslide but, like the Tyndale notes, insists the stoppage was God's act. On *charabah*, Guzik says it means solid ground rather than bone-dry dust, which fits the lexicon's "dry land." He also mentions later Jewish legends that piled the water impossibly high, and sets them aside.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel crossed into the land behind the ark, the sign of God's presence, and the ark went down into the river first and stayed in the middle until every last person had passed over. Christ, God with us, has gone ahead of his people into the waters of death. He did not cross alone and leave the rest to manage. He stood where the danger was greatest until all whom the Father gave him were brought safely through (John 10:28; Heb 2:14–15). Christians have long sung of "crossing Jordan" as a picture of dying in faith, and the picture holds because someone has already stepped in ahead.
