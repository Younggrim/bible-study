*Setting:* Hosea is preaching in the last years of the northern kingdom, probably under its last king, Hoshea (about 732–722 BC). Hoshea had become a vassal of Assyria and paid tribute, then secretly sent to Egypt for help (2 Kgs 17:3–4). That double game is behind the first verse. Chapter 11 ended with God's heart overturned in compassion and a promise to bring his children home. Chapter 12 returns to the charge sheet, and Hosea does something new: he holds up the nation's ancestor Jacob as a mirror. Jacob the grabber, Jacob the wrestler, Jacob the shepherd who served for a wife. His descendants have his deceit without his desperate faith. The chapter keeps swinging between the patriarch's story and the present day.

#### The LORD's Love for Israel, continued (v1)
- **"Ephraim feeds on the wind and pursues the east wind all day long" (v1).** Feeding on wind is a picture of living on nothing (compare 8:7, "they sow the wind"). The east wind was the hot, dry desert wind, the *sirocco*, that withered crops in a day (13:15; Gen 41:6). Ephraim chases it from morning to night. It is worse than useless; it destroys.
- **"They make a covenant with Assyria, and oil is carried to Egypt" (v1).** This is Hoshea's double dealing. Israel swore loyalty to Assyria and at the same time sent olive oil, one of its main exports, to Egypt as a gift to buy help (2 Kgs 17:4). Treaties were sworn before the gods of both parties, so these alliances were spiritual compromise as well as politics. Hosea earlier called Ephraim "a silly dove," flitting between the two powers (7:11). The result was the siege of Samaria.
- **The chapter division.** In Hebrew Bibles the verse numbers are one ahead here: our 11:12 is their 12:1, and our 12:1 is their 12:2. The ESV starts its new section at v2, so this verse closes off the previous section with a picture of what Ephraim is doing while God is longing to bring them home.

#### The LORD's Indictment of Israel and Judah (vv2–14)
- **"The LORD has an indictment against Judah" (v2).** "Indictment" (*riv*) is a courtroom word for a lawsuit, the same word that opened the book's charges in 4:1. Judah, the southern kingdom, is included. In the days of Ahaz (about 735–715 BC) Judah too went after idols and Assyria (2 Kgs 16). **"And will punish Jacob according to his ways"**: "Jacob" here means the whole people, and the name leads straight into the story of their ancestor.
- **"In the womb he took his brother by the heel" (v3).** The verb is *'aqab*, the root of the name Jacob (*Ya'aqov*). At birth he came out holding Esau's heel (Gen 25:26), and later Esau said bitterly, "Is he not rightly named Jacob? For he has cheated me these two times" (Gen 27:36). "Heel-grabber" came to mean "supplanter," one who trips others up to get ahead. Jacob's descendants were living up to the name.
- **"In his manhood he strove with God" (v3).** "Strove" is *sarah*, the verb behind the name Israel, "he strives with God" or "God strives" (Gen 32:28). Hosea sums up Jacob's life with his two names: from the womb a cheat, as a grown man a wrestler with God.
- **"He strove with the angel and prevailed; he wept and sought his favor" (v4).** This is Jacob at the river Jabbok (Gen 32:22–32), wrestling all night with a man who turns out to be God himself ("I have seen God face to face," Gen 32:30). Hosea calls him "the angel," the messenger of the LORD who is at the same time the LORD. Many Christian interpreters, including JFB, see here the Son of God before his incarnation. Genesis does not say Jacob wept; Hosea adds it. The Hebrew could even mean the angel wept, but most read it of Jacob. The point is that his "prevailing" was not overpowering God. It was clinging to him in tears and begging for a blessing: "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (Gen 32:26). He won by surrender.
- **"He met God at Bethel, and there God spoke with us" (v4).** At Bethel Jacob dreamed of the ladder and heard God's promise (Gen 28:10–22), and later God met him there again and confirmed his new name (Gen 35:9–15). The bitter irony is that Bethel, "house of God," is now the site of the golden calf (10:5, 15). The Hebrew reads "with us": what God said to Jacob, he said to all his descendants. The Greek translation reads "with him."
- **"The LORD, the God of hosts, the LORD is his memorial name" (v5).** "Memorial name" is the name by which God wants to be remembered, the one he gave Moses at the burning bush: "This is my name forever, and thus I am to be remembered" (Exod 3:15). It is YHWH, the covenant name, the God who keeps his promises. "God of hosts" means the commander of heaven's armies. The God who met Jacob has not changed.
- **"So you, by the help of your God, return" (v6).** The verse is God's message to Jacob's children. **"By the help of your God"** is literally "in your God": they cannot return by their own strength, any more than Jacob could. **"Hold fast to love and justice"**: *hesed*, steadfast covenant love, and *mishpat*, right dealing (compare Mic 6:8). **"And wait continually for your God"**: not on Assyria or Egypt. Jacob's story shows the way back: clinging, weeping, waiting.
- **"A merchant, in whose hands are false balances" (v7).** The word for "merchant" is *kena'an*, "Canaan" or "Canaanite." The Canaanites, especially the Phoenician coastal cities, were famous traders, and "Canaanite" became a word for merchant (Prov 31:24; Zeph 1:11). Hosea's point is cutting: Israel, which was meant to drive out the Canaanites, has become one. False balances were rigged scales, condemned by the law (Lev 19:35–36; Prov 11:1) and by Amos in the same years (Amos 8:5).
- **"Ah, but I am rich; I have found wealth for myself" (v8).** Prosperity becomes proof of innocence: "in all my labors they cannot find in me iniquity or sin." There is a pun in Hebrew. "Wealth" is *'on* and "iniquity" is *'awon*. The words sound alike: Ephraim found *'on* and claims no one can find *'awon*. Jesus' words to another rich church sound the same note: "you say, I am rich… not realising that you are wretched" (Rev 3:17).
- **"I am the LORD your God from the land of Egypt" (v9).** God answers the boast by reminding them where their story began: as slaves (compare 13:4; Exod 20:2). **"I will again make you dwell in tents, as in the days of the appointed feast"**: the appointed feast is the Feast of Booths, when Israel lived in shelters for a week to remember the wilderness (Lev 23:42–43). This is judgment: the merchants' fine houses will be exchanged for tents. But it is also hope, because the wilderness is where God first won Israel's heart and will do so again (2:14–15).
- **"I spoke to the prophets… and through the prophets gave parables" (v10).** Israel cannot plead ignorance. God sent Elijah, Elisha, Amos and Hosea himself, with visions and acted parables, including Hosea's own marriage (chs 1–3). "Gave parables" can also be translated "I will bring destruction." The same Hebrew root means "liken" and "destroy," and the ESV takes the first sense.
- **"If there is iniquity in Gilead… in Gilgal they sacrifice bulls" (v11).** Gilead, east of the Jordan, was "a city of evildoers, tracked with blood" (6:8). Gilgal, west of the Jordan, was a centre of false worship (4:15; 9:15). Together they stand for the whole land. **"Their altars also are like stone heaps on the furrows of the field"**: a triple pun. Gilead and Gilgal both sound like *gallim*, "heaps." Farmers piled the stones they cleared from fields into heaps at the edges. The many altars will end up as just that, piles of rubble. Gilead was also where Jacob and Laban made a covenant with a heap of stones (*Gal-ed*, "heap of witness," Gen 31:46–48).
- **"Jacob fled to the land of Aram; there Israel served for a wife" (v12).** Back to Jacob, who ran from Esau to Haran in Aram (Syria) and worked fourteen years for Rachel (Gen 28–29). He went there with nothing and served as a hired herdsman. The verse seems to remind proud Ephraim of its humble origins: a fugitive and a shepherd.
- **"By a prophet the LORD brought Israel up from Egypt, and by a prophet he was guarded" (v13).** The prophet is Moses (Deut 18:15; 34:10). The verb "guarded" (*shamar*) is the same verb as Jacob's "guarded sheep" in v12. Jacob kept sheep for a wife; God kept Israel through a prophet as his flock. And the prophets they despised now (9:7–8) are the same kind of men God used to rescue them then.
- **"Ephraim has given bitter provocation" (v14).** The verdict. **"His Lord will leave his bloodguilt on him"**: their violence (4:2; 6:8) will not be wiped off but left on their heads. **"Will repay him for his disgraceful deeds"**: the verb echoes v2. Yet God is still called "his Lord." JFB notes that "God does not give up His claim to them."
- **Commentators:** All three read Jacob as both a warning and a model. Henry writes that when Jacob's "faith upon the Divine promise prevailed above his fears, then by his strength he had power with God." Guzik stresses that Jacob truly prevailed only when he was broken and wept, and that even returning to God needs God's help. JFB takes the angel of v4 as the Son of God and notes "what a sad contrast, that in this same Beth-el now Israel worships the golden calves!" On v7 all three see the "Canaan" wordplay. Henry says Israel traded "upon Canaanitish principles," and JFB calls it "the greatest reproach to Israel, who despised Canaan." Henry's application on vv7–14: "We had better follow the hardest labour in poverty, than grow rich by sin." Guzik adds that prosperity often blinds a society to its decline. JFB reads v9 first of the return from Babylon and then of a final restoration, while the Tyndale notes see both judgment and hope in the tents.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jacob is the hinge of the chapter: a cheat who met God, wrestled, wept and would not let go until he was blessed. Hosea calls his children to return the same way, "by the help of your God." Jesus is the one in whom that help comes. He is the God who came down and met people face to face, as Jacob met him at Peniel (John 1:14, 18), and he told Nathanael, "an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit," that he himself is Jacob's ladder, the true Bethel where heaven meets earth (John 1:47–51). He is also the greater prophet than Moses, who brings his people out of a slavery deeper than Egypt and keeps them as his flock (John 10:11, 28).
