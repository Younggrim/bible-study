*Setting:* About 1003 BC. David has reigned over Judah from Hebron for seven and a half years. The house of Saul has collapsed from within: Abner, its strongman, was murdered by Joab (ch 3), and Saul's son Ish-bosheth was killed in his bed by two of his own captains (ch 4). Each time, David refused to profit from the killing and punished or condemned the killers. Now the northern tribes have no king, no general and no reason to hold out. This chapter is the high point that the whole story since 1 Samuel 16 has been moving toward. David is made king of all Israel, takes Jerusalem and makes it his capital, is recognized by a foreign king, and twice defeats the Philistines, the enemy Saul never could overcome. Much of it is told again, with additions, in 1 Chronicles 11 and 14.

#### David Anointed King of Israel (vv1–5)
- **"All the tribes of Israel came to David at Hebron" (v1).** The north comes to David, not the other way around. 1 Chronicles 12:23–40 lists the thousands from every tribe who came, and says they feasted for three days.
- **"We are your bone and flesh" (v1).** This is the language of family (Gen 2:23; 29:14). After years of civil war, they are saying, "We belong together. You are one of us." It is the first of three reasons they give.
- **"It was you who led out and brought in Israel" (v2).** The second reason: David had already proved himself. "Led out and brought in" is a phrase for leading an army in war (1 Sam 18:13, 16). Even while Saul was king, David was the one winning Israel's battles.
- **"You shall be shepherd of my people Israel, and you shall be prince over Israel" (v2).** The third and greatest reason: God had said so. David was a shepherd boy when Samuel anointed him (1 Sam 16:11). Now God's word turns his old job into his calling. "Shepherd" was a common title for kings in the ancient Near East, but in Israel it carried a special weight, because the LORD himself is the shepherd of his people (Ps 23:1; 80:1). "Prince" is *nagid*, "leader" or "ruler-designate," the word God used for Saul (1 Sam 9:16) and for David (1 Sam 13:14; 25:30). It stresses that the king is God's appointed leader, under God, not above him. Matthew joins this verse to Micah 5:2 when he tells of the Messiah's birth in Bethlehem: a ruler "who will shepherd my people Israel" (Matt 2:6).
- **"King David made a covenant with them at Hebron before the LORD" (v3).** The king and the people bind themselves to each other, with God as witness. This was not an absolute monarchy. The king had obligations too (compare Deut 17:14–20; 1 Sam 10:25). **"They anointed David king over Israel."** This is his third anointing: by Samuel in private (1 Sam 16:13), by Judah (2:4), and now by all Israel.
- **"David was thirty years old… he reigned forty years" (v4).** Thirty was the age when priests began their service (Num 4:3) and when Joseph stood before Pharaoh (Gen 41:46). Jesus also began his ministry at about thirty (Luke 3:23). David's reign of forty years is split into seven and a half in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem (v5).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "David was anointed king a third time." He sees the slow, step-by-step rise as testing David's faith and as a picture of the Messiah's kingdom, which also grows by degrees. JFB says the covenant was "a formal declaration of the constitution" and seems to have been "accompanied by religious solemnities." Guzik points out that David's years of preparation, roughly fifteen from his anointing as a boy, were not too long for a forty-year reign. God prepares people thoroughly when the work is great.

#### The LORD Gives David Jerusalem (vv6–10)
- **"Jerusalem against the Jebusites" (v6).** Jerusalem was a Canaanite city held by the Jebusites. Judah had once burned part of it (Judg 1:8), but neither Judah nor Benjamin had been able to drive them out (Josh 15:63; Judg 1:21), and a Levite once refused to stay the night there because it was "the city of foreigners" (Judg 19:11–12). It sat on a narrow ridge with steep valleys on three sides and a spring, the Gihon, at its foot. It was small, perhaps ten or twelve acres, but very hard to take.
- **Why Jerusalem?** It belonged to no tribe, lying on the border between Judah and Benjamin. A capital there favored neither the north nor the south. It was also central and easy to defend. Above all, it would become the place God chose "to put his name" (Deut 12:5; 1 Kgs 11:36). This may also be where Melchizedek was king (Gen 14:18, "Salem"; Ps 76:2).
- **"The blind and the lame will ward you off" (v6).** The Jebusites taunt David: their walls are so strong that even disabled men could defend them. The Tyndale notes mention another way to read the Hebrew: "You will not get in here unless you get rid of the blind and the lame." Either way, it is mockery.
- **"Nevertheless, David took the stronghold of Zion, that is, the city of David" (v7).** This is the first time "Zion" appears in the Bible. At first it named only this Jebusite fortress on the southeastern hill. Later the name spread to the temple mount, then to all Jerusalem, and finally to God's people and his heavenly city (Ps 48:1–2; Heb 12:22).
- **"Let him get up the water shaft" (v8).** The word *tsinnor* appears only here and in Psalm 42:7, where the ESV has "waterfalls." The lexicon gives "pipe, spout, conduit," and calls this reading in 2 Samuel "very dubious," because the Hebrew of the verse is hard. For a long time many believed David's men climbed a vertical rock shaft found in 1867, called Warren's Shaft. Later excavations showed that shaft was not usable in David's day, but they also found an older tunnel system and fortified towers protecting the spring, so an attack by way of the water system is still possible. Others think the word means a weapon or a throat, or that it should be read differently. 1 Chronicles 11:6 adds that David promised the command of the army to whoever struck first, and "Joab… went up first," which may be how Joab became commander over all Israel.
- **"'The lame and the blind,' who are hated by David's soul" (v8).** In quotation marks, this is David turning the Jebusites' own taunt back on them. He means the Jebusite defenders, not disabled people in general. The saying "The blind and the lame shall not come into the house" was apparently a proverb that grew out of this event. Its exact meaning is unclear. Some link it to the priestly rules about physical defects (Lev 21:18). Whatever it meant, it makes the very next chapters stand out. David will bring the lame Mephibosheth into his house to eat at his table "like one of the king's sons" (9:11, 13). And in the temple built on this hill, the Son of David will heal "the blind and the lame" who come to him (Matt 21:14).
- **"David lived in the stronghold and called it the city of David" (v9).** A city captured by a king and his private troops, not by any tribe, became his own property. So the capital belongs to the crown. **"The Millo"** means "filling." It was probably a set of stone terraces filling in the steep slope to support buildings above. Archaeologists have uncovered a massive "stepped stone structure" on the eastern slope of the City of David that many identify with it.
- **"David became greater and greater, for the LORD, the God of hosts, was with him" (v10).** The reason for his success is God. "The LORD of hosts" pictures God as commander of heaven's armies. "The LORD was with him" has been said of David again and again since he was a boy (1 Sam 16:18; 18:12, 14, 28).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The enemies of God's people are often very confident of their own strength," and "the pride and insolence of the Jebusites animated David, and the Lord God of hosts was with him." JFB says the blind and lame were posted on the walls "in derisive mockery," and lists three possible meanings of the hard word in v8: "a subterranean passage," a water spout, or throwing the defenders over a cliff. JFB thinks the Millo was "probably a row of stone bastions." Guzik suggests David's men may have used Warren's Shaft, though he admits the tactic is uncertain, and archaeology has since cast doubt on that particular shaft. Guzik also stresses that Jerusalem, with no tribal ties, was a wise choice for a capital that would unite the nation.

#### David's Growing Kingdom (vv11–16)
- **"Hiram king of Tyre sent messengers to David" (v11).** Tyre was the great Phoenician port on the coast north of Israel, famous for trade, shipbuilding and craftsmen. Hiram sends cedar from Lebanon, the finest building timber in the region, along with skilled carpenters and masons. Israel did not yet have craftsmen of that skill. The Tyndale notes say this embassy probably happened somewhat later in David's reign, since Hiram was still alive well into Solomon's reign (1 Kgs 5:1; 9:10–14). The narrator places it here because it shows the theme: foreign kings now recognize David.
- **"David knew that the LORD had established him… for the sake of his people Israel" (v12).** This is one of the most important lines in the chapter. David understands that his kingship is not for himself but for God's people. Saul treated the kingdom as his own. David knows he is a steward.
- **"David took more concubines and wives from Jerusalem" (v13).** In the ancient world this was a mark of a successful king, and the narrator records it next to David's blessings. But it directly breaks Deuteronomy 17:17: the king "shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away." David's growing harem will produce the jealousies, rivalries and sins that tear his family apart, and his son Solomon will take the same path much further (1 Kgs 11:1–4).
- **"Shammua, Shobab, Nathan, Solomon" (v14).** Eleven sons born in Jerusalem are listed (Chronicles has thirteen, 1 Chr 3:5–8; 14:4–7). Two names matter most. Solomon will be David's heir (1 Kgs 1). Nathan, otherwise unknown, is the son through whom Luke traces Jesus' family line (Luke 3:31), while Matthew traces it through Solomon (Matt 1:6). 1 Chronicles 3:5 says Solomon and three of these were sons of Bathsheba.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "David owned it was for his people's sake God had done great things for him," and remarks that a house built with foreign help was no less fit to be used for God. Guzik agrees that David was lifted up not for his own sake but for Israel's. JFB is blunt about v13: "David transgressed an express law, which forbade the king of Israel to multiply wives unto himself." Guzik adds that David likely saw the growing family as blessing, but much of his later trouble came through his relationships with women and through his children. JFB reads Hiram's craftsmen as a sign of how far skilled work in Israel had fallen through the long wars.

#### David Defeats the Philistines (vv17–25)
- **"When the Philistines heard that David had been anointed king over Israel" (v17).** While David ruled only Judah, the Philistines may have seen him as a useful vassal. He had served Achish of Gath (1 Sam 27). A king over a united Israel was a threat, so they strike at once. "Went down to the stronghold" probably means a fortress in the wilderness like the ones he used before (1 Sam 22:4; 2 Sam 23:13–14), not Zion, which is "up." This battle may have come before the capture of Jerusalem.
- **"The Valley of Rephaim" (v18).** A broad valley running southwest from Jerusalem toward Bethlehem. The name recalls the Rephaim, an ancient race of giants (Deut 2:11, 20). The Philistines were trying to cut Judah off from the north, and David off from his new capital.
- **"David inquired of the LORD, 'Shall I go up…? Will you give them into my hand?'" (v19).** This is still David's habit (1 Sam 23:2; 30:8; 2 Sam 2:1). He is now a powerful king, and he still asks first. Saul, by contrast, acted without waiting (1 Sam 13:8–14).
- **"The LORD has broken through my enemies before me like a breaking flood" (v20).** The Hebrew root *parats* means "to break out" or "breach," the way floodwater bursts through a dam. So the place is named **Baal-perazim**, "lord of breakthroughs." Here "Baal" simply means "lord" or "master," and David uses it of the LORD. Isaiah later recalls this battle: "the LORD will rise up as on Mount Perazim" (Isa 28:21). The same root names Perez, the ancestor of David, who "broke through" at his birth (Gen 38:29; Ruth 4:18–22).
- **"The Philistines left their idols there, and David and his men carried them away" (v21).** A neat reversal. Two generations earlier the Philistines carried off the ark of the LORD from the battlefield (1 Sam 4:11). Now Israel carries off the Philistines' gods. 1 Chronicles 14:12 adds that David had them burned, as the law commanded (Deut 7:5, 25).
- **"You shall not go up; go around to their rear" (v23).** The Philistines return, and David asks again rather than assuming God will act the same way twice. This time God gives a different plan.
- **"When you hear the sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees" (v24).** The tree is the Hebrew *baka*. No one is sure what tree it was. "Balsam," "mulberry" and "poplar" are all guesses. It may be related to a verb meaning "to weep," perhaps for a tree that drips sap, and possibly the same word as the "Valley of Baca" in Psalm 84:6. The rustling would be the sound of the LORD's own army moving ahead of David: "then the LORD has gone out before you." David's job is to wait for God's signal, then move.
- **"From Geba to Gezer" (v25).** Gezer was at the western edge of the hill country, toward Philistine land. Chronicles reads "from Gibeon to Gezer" (1 Chr 14:16). The Philistines are driven out of Israel's central hills. Where Saul's reign began and ended under the shadow of the Philistines, David's begins with their defeat.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The Philistines considered not that David had the presence of God with him, which Saul had forfeited and lost." On the trees, he says "David waited till God moved; he stirred then, but not till then," and links this to the apostles waiting for the Spirit before Pentecost (Acts 1:4; 2:2, where the Spirit comes with "a sound… like a mighty rushing wind"). Guzik draws a similar lesson and adds that God does not always direct the same kind of battle the same way, so asking him again is never wasted. JFB thinks the trees were "most probably the poplar," and reads the abandoned idols as the Philistines' household gods. The Tyndale notes observe that every time David fights the Philistines in 2 Samuel, he wins.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everything Israel wanted from a king is found in David here: one of their own flesh and blood, a proven deliverer and God's chosen shepherd. But these are only shadows. Jesus is the King who became our "bone and flesh" in the incarnation (Heb 2:14), who has fought and won the battle his people could not win, and whom the Father appointed to "shepherd my people Israel" (Matt 2:6; John 10:11). David took a city the enemy thought could never fall. Christ breaks into the strongholds of sin and death (2 Cor 10:4; Heb 2:14–15), the true LORD of breakthroughs. And where David's city had a saying that kept out the blind and the lame, the Son of David welcomed them in his Father's house and healed them (Matt 21:14).
