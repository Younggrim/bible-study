*Setting:* The last months of the siege, probably late 587 or early 586 BC. The Egyptian army has gone home, the Babylonians are back around the walls, and the city's bread is running out (37:21). Jeremiah is held in the "court of the guard," a palace courtyard where he can still speak to soldiers and passers-by, and he keeps saying what God has told him: surrender and live, stay and die. This chapter tells of his lowest point, when officials lower him into a muddy cistern to die, and of his rescue by an unlikely hero, a foreign servant at the palace. It ends with the last private conversation between Jeremiah and King Zedekiah, the king's final chance to save the city.

#### Jeremiah Cast into the Cistern (vv1–6)
- **"Shephatiah the son of Mattan, Gedaliah the son of Pashhur, Jucal the son of Shelemiah, and Pashhur the son of Malchiah" (v1).** Four of Zedekiah's officials. Jucal (Jehucal) had carried the king's request for prayer (37:3). Pashhur son of Malchiah had been sent to Jeremiah once before (21:1). Gedaliah son of Pashhur is not the later governor Gedaliah son of Ahikam (40:5). Excavations in the City of David have turned up clay seal impressions bearing the names "Yehukal son of Shelemyahu" (2005) and "Gedalyahu son of Pashhur" (2008), found a few yards apart, likely from the very men named here.
- **"He who stays in this city shall die by the sword, by famine, and by pestilence, but he who goes out to the Chaldeans shall live" (v2).** A repeat of 21:9. **"He shall have his life as a prize of war."** Soldiers came home from battle with plunder. Those who surrendered would come away with only one thing, their lives, but that would be enough (39:18; 45:5).
- **"He is weakening the hands of the soldiers who are left in this city" (v4).** "Weakening the hands" means sapping morale. One of the Lachish Letters, written during this same war, uses the very same phrase. A Judean officer complains there that letters sent from Jerusalem's officials are "weakening the hands" of the people. Morale was a real concern. But the officials' charge turns truth upside down: **"This man is not seeking the welfare of this people, but their harm."** "Welfare" is *shalom*. Jeremiah was the one man who had told them how to have peace (29:7; 38:20).
- **"Behold, he is in your hands, for the king can do nothing against you" (v5).** A king confessing that he is powerless before his own officials. It is one of the most pitiful lines spoken by any king in Scripture. Zedekiah handed over a man he knew to be God's prophet to avoid a confrontation.
- **"They took Jeremiah and cast him into the cistern of Malchiah, the king's son, which was in the court of the guard" (v6).** Jerusalem had few springs, so houses had cisterns cut into the rock, bell-shaped, narrow at the top and wide at the bottom, plastered to hold winter rainwater. A man could not climb out. "The king's son" may mean a prince or a royal official. **"Letting Jeremiah down by ropes."** They did not strike him. They let starvation and exposure do it, so no one would have blood on his hands, as Joseph's brothers once did (Gen 37:20–24). **"There was no water in the cistern, but only mud, and Jeremiah sank in the mud."** Late in the siege the water was gone. Lamentations, traditionally linked to Jeremiah, seems to remember this moment: "they flung me alive into the pit… water closed over my head… I called on your name, O LORD, from the depths of the pit" (Lam 3:53–55). Psalm 69 uses the same picture: "I sink in deep mire, where there is no foothold" (Ps 69:2, 14).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the officials "represent his preaching as having a bad tendency," and JFB grants that without a commission from God Jeremiah's words would indeed have been treason, but that God's word made the outcome certain, so advising surrender was mercy. JFB notes the "dungeon" was a cistern, not a built prison, drained during the siege, and sees Jeremiah sinking in the mire as a picture of the Messiah in Psalm 69. Guzik, quoting Kidner, says Zedekiah's surrender of Jeremiah was the most abject in Scripture until Pilate, and notes that the officials chose a death that would not look like murder.

#### Jeremiah Rescued from the Cistern (vv7–13)
- **"When Ebed-melech the Ethiopian, a eunuch who was in the king's house, heard" (v7).** Ebed-melech means "servant of the king," perhaps a title more than a personal name. "Ethiopian" is literally "Cushite," from Cush, the land on the upper Nile south of Egypt, today's Sudan, not modern Ethiopia. "Eunuch" (*saris*) can mean a castrated man, often in charge of the royal women, or simply a high court official. If he was a eunuch in the literal sense, the Law barred him from the assembly (Deut 23:1). Either way, he was an outsider. **"The king was sitting in the Benjamin Gate."** Probably hearing cases or overseeing the defense at the north wall, the side facing the enemy.
- **"My lord the king, these men have done evil" (v9).** He went to the king in public, at the city gate, and named the officials' act for what it was. That took courage. The same officials had just bent the king to their will. **"He will die there of hunger, for there is no bread left in the city."** The daily loaf from the bakers' street (37:21) was nearly at an end.
- **"Take thirty men with you from here, and lift Jeremiah the prophet out of the cistern before he dies" (v10).** Zedekiah, who had just given way to his officials, now gives way to Ebed-melech. The king bends to whoever pushes last. Thirty men may have been more than needed to lift one starving man. They may have been meant as protection in case the officials tried to stop the rescue. Some ancient manuscripts read "three men," which some scholars prefer.
- **"Old rags and worn-out clothes, which he let down to Jeremiah in the cistern by ropes" (v11).** From a storeroom under the palace treasury. **"Put the rags and clothes between your armpits and the ropes" (v12).** A small, kind detail. Jeremiah was weak and stuck in deep mud. The ropes would have cut into his flesh as he was pulled out. Ebed-melech thought of that. The detail shows a man who cared about the person, not just the task.
- **"They drew Jeremiah up with ropes and lifted him out of the cistern. And Jeremiah remained in the court of the guard" (v13).** Saved from death, but still a prisoner. God's rescue did not mean his troubles were over.
- **Ebed-melech's reward.** After the city fell God sent a personal message to him: "I will deliver you on that day… because you have put your trust in me" (39:15–18). His act of compassion came from faith. In a city where the king and princes rejected God's word, a foreign servant trusted it. The New Testament has a similar figure, the Ethiopian official who believed and was baptized (Acts 8:27–39), and Jesus often found faith in outsiders that he did not find in Israel (Luke 7:9; 17:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Ebed-melech, a foreigner, "had in him more humanity, and more divinity too, than native Israelites had," and on the rags, "See what a good use even old rotten rags may be put to." JFB sees the Gentile rescuing the prophet whom his own people rejected as a foreshadowing of the Gentiles receiving the gospel, and suggests he oversaw the royal women. Guzik notes that "eunuch" can mean a court official and that, as a foreigner and perhaps a eunuch, Ebed-melech would have been shut out of temple worship, yet he showed more compassion than the leaders of Judah. Guzik suggests the thirty men were guards rather than lifters.

#### Jeremiah Warns Zedekiah Again (vv14–28)
- **"King Zedekiah sent for Jeremiah the prophet and received him at the third entrance of the temple of the LORD" (v14).** The location is otherwise unknown, perhaps a private passage between palace and temple (2 Kgs 16:18). **"I will ask you a question; hide nothing from me."** The king wants the truth, again in secret (37:17).
- **"If I tell you, will you not surely put me to death? And if I give you counsel, you will not listen to me" (v15).** Jeremiah has learned. Telling the truth had nearly killed him, and the king never acted on it anyway.
- **"As the LORD lives, who made our souls, I will not put you to death" (v16).** A solemn oath, sworn "secretly." Zedekiah's oaths were not worth much. He had broken his oath of loyalty to Nebuchadnezzar (2 Chr 36:13; Ezek 17:13–19).
- **"If you will surrender to the officials of the king of Babylon, then your life shall be spared, and this city shall not be burned with fire, and you and your house shall live" (v17).** Even at this late hour, God offered a way out. "Surrender" is literally "go out," to go through the gate to the enemy's camp. Jehoiachin had done exactly that in 597 BC and lived (2 Kgs 24:12; Jer 52:31–34). The fate of the city, the temple and the royal family hung on one man's choice.
- **"But if you do not surrender… they shall burn it with fire, and you shall not escape from their hand" (v18).** Both choices are set out plainly, as Moses set life and death before Israel (Deut 30:19).
- **"I am afraid of the Judeans who have deserted to the Chaldeans, lest I be handed over to them and they deal cruelly with me" (v19).** At last the real reason. Zedekiah was not afraid of God, and not mainly of Babylon. He was afraid of mockery and abuse from his own people who had already gone over. "Deal cruelly" can mean to make sport of, to humiliate (Judg 16:25). "The fear of man lays a snare" (Prov 29:25).
- **"You shall not be given to them. Obey now the voice of the LORD… and your life shall be spared" (v20).** Jeremiah answers the fear directly with God's promise.
- **"All the women left in the house of the king of Judah were being led out to the officials of the king of Babylon" (v22).** Jeremiah describes a vision. When a king was conquered, his harem passed to the conqueror. The women of the palace, perhaps including the wives of earlier kings, would be led out singing a mocking lament over Zedekiah. **"Your trusted friends have deceived you and prevailed against you."** "Trusted friends" is literally "men of your peace" (*anshe shelomekha*), the officials and prophets who promised peace (Obad 7). **"Now that your feet are sunk in the mud, they turn away from you."** A bitter twist. Zedekiah had let Jeremiah sink in the mud of the cistern. Now the king himself would be stuck in the mire, and his advisers would leave him there. Zedekiah feared mockery from deserters (v19). If he refused, he would get mockery from his own household.
- **"All your wives and your sons shall be led out to the Chaldeans… and this city shall be burned with fire" (v23).** Literally "you will burn this city." Zedekiah's choice would make him responsible. It came true exactly (39:6–8).
- **"Let no one know of these words, and you shall not die" (v24).** The king's last words to Jeremiah in the book are about protecting himself. He does not say whether he will obey.
- **"Then you shall say to them, 'I made a humble plea to the king that he would not send me back to the house of Jonathan to die there'" (v26).** Jeremiah had made exactly that plea before (37:20), and quite possibly made it again. **"He answered them as the king had instructed him… for the conversation had not been overheard" (v27).** Was this deception? Jeremiah told the truth, but not all of it. The officials, who had just tried to murder him, had no right to the king's private conversation. Interpreters have weighed it differently. Some see it as legitimate discretion, keeping a confidence. Others see it as a fearful half-truth that Scripture records without approving, like Abraham calling Sarah his sister (Gen 20:2, 12). Either way, the text does not hold it up as a model, and it does not condemn it.
- **"And Jeremiah remained in the court of the guard until the day that Jerusalem was taken" (v28).** The prophet who had predicted the fall for forty years would be there to see it.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the "men of thy peace" were the king's ministers and false prophets, and that "sunk in the mire" may echo Jeremiah's own sinking in the cistern. On vv24–27 JFB says Jeremiah's statement "was true, though not the whole truth," that the princes had no right to know, and that Scripture records rather than approves it, comparing Abraham in Genesis 20. Guzik quotes Clarke's similar verdict, telling the truth "but not the whole truth," and sees Jeremiah keeping a word meant for the king between the king and God. On v19 JFB cites Proverbs 29:25 and John 12:43: Zedekiah "feared men more than God." Guzik, citing Cundall, describes Zedekiah as having a wishbone where he needed a backbone, and notes that he always had an excuse for not obeying.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah was charged with not seeking his people's welfare when he was the only one telling them how to live. He was handed over by a ruler who knew he was innocent, lowered into a pit, and left to die, and he was raised up again. Jesus walked the same road further. He was accused of misleading the nation (Luke 23:2), handed over by a governor who said, "I find no guilt in him" (John 18:38), and laid in a tomb cut in the rock. No one lowered rags to him. He went all the way down into death, and God raised him on the third day. Psalm 69, with its mire and its pit, is quoted of him again and again in the New Testament (John 2:17; 15:25; Rom 15:3). And as with Ebed-melech, outsiders who trust his word find themselves saved on the day of judgment.
