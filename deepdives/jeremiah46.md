*Setting:* Chapters 46–51 gather Jeremiah's prophecies against the nations. God had appointed him "a prophet to the nations" at his call (1:5, 10), and these chapters show what that meant. Isaiah (chs 13–23), Ezekiel (chs 25–32) and Amos (chs 1–2) have similar collections. The LORD is not a local god of one small kingdom. He judges every people. Egypt comes first. It was the great power to the south, and Judah's leaders had trusted it again and again against Babylon, most recently the refugees of chapters 42–44. The chapter holds two oracles. The first (vv2–12) is a war poem about the Battle of Carchemish in 605 BC, when Babylon broke Egypt's army on the Euphrates. The second (vv13–26) announces that Nebuchadnezzar will invade Egypt itself. It closes with a word of comfort for Israel (vv27–28). The poems are among the most vivid in the book, full of quick commands, mockery and sudden reversals.

#### Judgment on Egypt (vv1–28)
- **"Concerning the nations" (v1).** This heading covers all of chapters 46–51. In the Greek translation these oracles sit in the middle of the book, after 25:13, and in a different order. The Hebrew places them near the end, with Babylon last.
- **"The army of Pharaoh Neco… which was by the river Euphrates at Carchemish" (v2).** Four years earlier, in 609 BC, Pharaoh Neco II had marched north to help the last of the Assyrians against Babylon. King Josiah tried to stop him at Megiddo and was killed (2 Kgs 23:29; 2 Chr 35:20–24). Josiah's death ended the reform and began Judah's slide. Neco then held Syria and even put Jehoiakim on Judah's throne (2 Kgs 23:34). Carchemish was a fortress city on the upper Euphrates, on today's border between Turkey and Syria, where the main road crossed the river. **"Which Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon defeated in the fourth year of Jehoiakim."** That was 605 BC. The Babylonian Chronicle, a cuneiform record now in the British Museum, says the crown prince Nebuchadnezzar crossed the river, fought the Egyptian army at Carchemish, and beat it so completely that "not a single man escaped to his own country." That same year Nebuchadnezzar became king. It was one of the turning points of ancient history.
- **"Prepare buckler and shield, and advance for battle!" (v3).** The poem opens with officers shouting orders. The "buckler" (*magen*) was a small round shield, and the "shield" (*tsinnah*) was a large body shield for heavy infantry. **"Harness the horses… polish your spears, put on your armor!" (v4).** Every command shows an army splendidly prepared. Some hear mockery in it: Get ready all you like.
- **"Why have I seen it? They are dismayed and have turned backward" (v5).** Without warning, the scene jumps. The same army is running. **"They look not back—terror on every side!"** *Magor missabib*, Jeremiah's signature phrase (6:25; 20:3, 10; 49:29). The terror that surrounded Jeremiah now surrounds Egypt.
- **"The swift cannot flee away, nor the warrior escape" (v6).** Compare Amos 2:14–15. This matches the Chronicle's claim that no one got home. **"In the north by the river Euphrates they have stumbled and fallen."**
- **"Who is this, rising like the Nile, like rivers whose waters surge?" (v7).** Egypt's life depended on the Nile's yearly flood, which rose and spread over the land. Egypt pictured its army the same way, as an unstoppable flood. **"I will rise, I will cover the earth" (v8).** The boast echoes Isaiah's picture of Assyria as a river flood (Isa 8:7–8). Egypt meant to swamp the world. Instead it stumbled at the river's edge.
- **"Men of Cush and Put who handle the shield, men of Lud, skilled in handling the bow" (v9).** Egypt's army included many foreign troops. Cush was Nubia, south of Egypt (modern Sudan). Put is usually taken as Libya, west of Egypt. Lud is less certain. It may be the Lydians of western Turkey, since Pharaoh hired Greek and Lydian soldiers, or an African people (Gen 10:13). The point is a vast, mixed army.
- **"That day is the day of the Lord GOD of hosts, a day of vengeance" (v10).** It looked like a battle between two empires. In fact it was the LORD's day. Josiah's death at Neco's hands is not mentioned, but many readers hear it in the background. **"The Lord GOD of hosts holds a sacrifice in the north country."** The battlefield is pictured as a great sacrificial feast where the victims are the soldiers. Isaiah and Zephaniah use the same grim picture (Isa 34:6; Zeph 1:7).
- **"Go up to Gilead, and take balm, O virgin daughter of Egypt!" (v11).** Gilead, east of the Jordan, was famous for its healing balm. Jeremiah once asked the same about Judah: "Is there no balm in Gilead?" (8:22). Egypt was famous for its doctors and medicine, but **"in vain you have used many medicines; there is no healing for you."** "Virgin daughter" is a poetic title for a nation that had not yet been conquered, which makes the coming humiliation sharper.
- **"Warrior has stumbled against warrior; they have both fallen together" (v12).** The picture is of a panicked army tripping over itself in retreat.
- **"About the coming of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon to strike the land of Egypt" (v13).** The second oracle looks further ahead. The defeat at Carchemish was far away. Now the war will come to Egypt's own soil. Nebuchadnezzar tried to invade in 601 BC and was beaten back with heavy losses on both sides. A later campaign in about 568–567 BC, recorded on a broken Babylonian tablet, did reach Egypt (see 43:8–13).
- **"Proclaim in Migdol; proclaim in Memphis and Tahpanhes" (v14).** These are the very towns where the Judean refugees settled (44:1). The oracle reaches them where they live. Migdol and Tahpanhes guarded the northeast border, the way an invader from Asia would come. Memphis was the old capital.
- **"Why are your mighty ones face down? They do not stand because the LORD thrust them down" (v15).** The ESV footnote says the Hebrew is literally "He does not stand." The Greek translation divides the Hebrew letters differently and reads, "Why has Apis fled? Your chosen calf did not stand." Apis was the sacred bull of Memphis, worshiped as a living god. Many scholars think the Greek has preserved the original sense. Either way, the LORD pushed Egypt's champion down, whether soldier or idol.
- **"Arise, and let us go back to our own people and to the land of our birth" (v16).** The foreign hired soldiers give up and head home. They will not die for Egypt.
- **"Call the name of Pharaoh, king of Egypt, 'Noisy one who lets the hour go by'" (v17).** This is a mocking nickname, something like "Big Noise, Missed His Chance." Pharaoh talks loudly and lets the right moment slip. Some scholars suspect a pun on the pharaoh's name in Egyptian, though no one has proved which one. Isaiah gave Egypt a similar nickname: "Rahab who sits still" (Isa 30:7).
- **"As I live, declares the King, whose name is the LORD of hosts" (v18).** Pharaoh is a king with an empty boast. The LORD is the true King, and he swears by his own life. **"Like Tabor among the mountains and like Carmel by the sea, shall one come."** Mount Tabor rises alone out of the flat Jezreel valley, and Mount Carmel stands high over the Mediterranean coast. Both stand out from everything around them. So Nebuchadnezzar will tower over Egypt.
- **"Prepare yourselves baggage for exile, O inhabitants of Egypt!" (v19).** The land that took in Judah's refugees will produce refugees of its own. **"Memphis shall become a waste."** Memphis was not destroyed by Babylon, though it was later sacked by the Persians in 525 BC. Today almost nothing of the great city stands above ground.
- **"A beautiful heifer is Egypt, but a biting fly from the north has come upon her" (v20).** Egypt worshiped sacred bulls and cows, so the image fits. A sleek young cow is driven wild by a stinging fly. The word for "biting fly" (*qerets*) appears only here, and translators guess at gadfly, horsefly or "destruction." The point is plain: a small sting from the north sends the beautiful heifer running. **"Her hired soldiers… are like fattened calves" (v21).** Well fed and soft, they too turn and flee, ready for slaughter.
- **"She makes a sound like a serpent gliding away" (v22).** The serpent was the symbol of Pharaoh's royal power, the cobra on his crown. Now Egypt can only hiss as it slithers away from the woodcutters. **"Against her with axes like those who fell trees."** **"They shall cut down her forest… because they are more numerous than locusts" (v23).** Egypt was struck by locusts once before, in the plagues of the exodus (Exod 10). Now the invaders swarm like them.
- **"I am bringing punishment upon Amon of Thebes, and Pharaoh and Egypt and her gods and her kings" (v25).** Amon was the chief god of Thebes, Egypt's great southern capital, and for centuries one of the most honored gods in Egypt. The Hebrew name for Thebes is *No*, so this is "Amon of No" (compare Nah 3:8). Pharaoh was thought to be the son of the sun god, so to punish "Pharaoh and… her gods" is to strike Egypt's whole religious order. **"Upon Pharaoh and those who trust in him."** That includes the Judeans in Egypt.
- **"Afterward Egypt shall be inhabited as in the days of old, declares the LORD" (v26).** The judgment is not the end of Egypt. God will restore it. Similar promises are made for Moab (48:47), Ammon (49:6) and Elam (49:39). Isaiah even foresaw a day when the LORD would say, "Blessed be Egypt my people" (Isa 19:25). Ezekiel adds that Egypt would recover but remain "the most lowly of the kingdoms" (Ezek 29:13–15).
- **"But fear not, O Jacob my servant, nor be dismayed, O Israel" (v27).** These two verses are almost the same as 30:10–11. The comfort the Book of Consolation gave the exiles is repeated here, at the end of Egypt's doom. **"I will save you from far away, and your offspring from the land of their captivity."** This is the opposite of the Judeans who fled to Egypt. God's future is with the exiles who went where he sent them.
- **"I will make a full end of all the nations to which I have driven you, but of you I will not make a full end" (v28).** God will discipline Israel, but he will not wipe it out. **"I will discipline you in just measure, and I will by no means leave you unpunished."** Both halves are true together. God's love does not mean his people escape correction, and his correction does not mean his love has ended.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry pictures Egypt "scorning to keep within its own banks and threatening to overflow all the neighbouring lands," and warns, "Valiant men are not always victorious." He calls v10 "a day of sacrifice to divine justice." On v15: "It is not possible that those should fix whom the wrath of God chases." On the nickname of v17 he writes, "Great talkers are little doers." JFB sees in Carchemish a day of vengeance for Josiah, and like the Greek translation it takes the "valiant one" of v15 to be the Apis bull, showing "the palpable impotence of the idol." JFB also reads the heifer of v20 as an Apis image and the mercenaries of v21 as men grown soft on Egypt's luxuries. Guzik passes on the point that God "is not a regional supervisor," and stresses the contrast between Egypt's polished armor and its shameful flight. On vv27–28 Henry writes, "wherever thou art, O Jacob! I am with thee, a very present help," and Guzik notes F. B. Meyer's line that God corrects his people but does not finish them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Egypt trusted its flood, its doctors, its mercenaries and its gods, and none of them could heal it. "There is no healing for you" (v11) is the verdict on every human power that sets itself up in God's place. Yet even here, God promises that Egypt will be inhabited again, and that Jacob will be disciplined but not destroyed. Both promises meet in Christ. The child Jesus found shelter in Egypt (Matt 2:13–15), and on the day of Pentecost visitors from "Egypt and the parts of Libya" heard the mighty works of God in their own languages (Acts 2:10–11). The balm that Gilead could not give, the Great Physician gives freely (Mark 2:17), and the "fear not, I am with you" spoken to Jacob is spoken now to all who belong to him (Matt 28:20).
