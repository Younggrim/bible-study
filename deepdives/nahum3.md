*Setting:* The last chapter of Nahum, written in Judah sometime between 663 BC and 612 BC. Chapter 1 announced that the LORD would bring Nineveh to an end, and chapter 2 painted the city's fall as a battle seen in a vision, ending with God's words "Behold, I am against you" (2:13). Chapter 3 says it again (v5) and gives the reasons in full: bloodshed, lies, plunder and the seduction of nations. It then points to a recent event every reader in the region knew. In 663 BC the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal had sacked Thebes, the ancient royal city of Upper Egypt, which everyone thought could never fall. If Thebes fell, Nahum asks, why should Nineveh think itself safe? The book closes with a funeral song for the king of Assyria, and its final line is a question.

#### Woe to Nineveh (vv1–19)
- **"Woe to the bloody city" (v1).** "Woe" (*hoy*) was the cry of mourners at a funeral (1 Kgs 13:30; Jer 22:18). The prophets use it as a lament spoken over someone still alive, as if their death is already certain. "Bloody city" is literally "city of bloods," the plural used for bloodshed. Assyrian kings recorded their own cruelty with pride. Their inscriptions speak of flaying rebels, impaling them on stakes and building pillars of heads. The Tyndale notes list these practices, and the palace reliefs from Nineveh, now in museums, show them carved in stone.
- **"All full of lies and plunder—no end to the prey!" (v1).** "Lies" means treachery in dealing with other nations: treaties made and broken, promises of protection that became chains (2 Kgs 18:31–32, where the Assyrian envoy promises Jerusalem a land like its own if it surrenders). The "prey" picks up the lion's den of 2:11–12.
- **"The crack of the whip, and rumble of the wheel" (v2).** Nahum writes in sound bites. Each phrase is two or three Hebrew words, like flashes seen and heard in the middle of the battle: whip, wheel, horse, chariot, rider, sword, spear (vv2–3). The Tyndale notes point out that the short, abrupt phrases bring the reader right into the noise.
- **"Hosts of slain, heaps of corpses… they stumble over the bodies!" (v3).** The city that piled up the bodies of others has its own streets piled with dead. Assyria's armies had stacked corpses outside conquered cities. Now its own attackers trip over its fallen. There is a grim fairness here that the whole book insists on: what Nineveh did is done to it.
- **"All for the countless whorings of the prostitute, graceful and of deadly charms" (v4).** The city is pictured as a seductive prostitute. In the prophets, "whoring" usually means idolatry, unfaithfulness to God. Here, since Nineveh never belonged to the LORD in the first place, many think it chiefly means the way Assyria lured nations with offers of alliance and trade and then enslaved them. Henry reads it as idolatry, "spiritual whoredoms." JFB reads it as political deceit, "harlot-like artifices." Both are probably in view. "Deadly charms" (*keshaphim*) means sorceries. Thousands of Assyrian clay tablets record spells, omens and incantations, and Assyrian kings rarely made a decision without consulting diviners.
- **"Who betrays nations with her whorings" (v4).** The verb means literally "sells." Nineveh traded in peoples. Revelation 17–18 uses the same picture for "Babylon the great," the prostitute who rules the kings of the earth and trades in "slaves, that is, human souls" (Rev 18:13). John is drawing on Nahum and the other prophets.
- **"Behold, I am against you, declares the LORD of hosts" (v5).** This is the second time in the book (2:13). The first time the stress fell on military defeat. Now it falls on public shame.
- **"I will lift up your skirts over your face" (v5).** This is a hard image, and it is meant to be. In the ancient world a convicted adulteress or a prisoner of war might be stripped and paraded in public (Isa 47:2–3; Jer 13:22, 26; Ezek 16:37). Assyria had marched captives naked into exile (Isa 20:4). Now the same humiliation falls on it. The point is not crudeness but justice: the proud seducer is exposed for what she is.
- **"I will throw filth at you and treat you with contempt and make you a spectacle" (v6).** "Filth" (*shiqquts*) is the word usually used for idols, "detestable things." Nineveh's own idols become the garbage thrown at her. "Treat you with contempt" (*nabal*) is the verb behind the word for a fool: she is made to look worthless.
- **"Wasted is Nineveh; who will grieve for her?" (v7).** No one mourns. **"Where shall I seek comforters for you?"** This is a quiet play on the prophet's own name. *Nahum* means "comfort," and the verb here is from the same root, *naham*. The book brings comfort to Judah, but for Nineveh there will be no comforter at all.
- **"Are you better than Thebes that sat by the Nile?" (v8).** The Hebrew name is *No'-'Amon*, "city of Amon," after the chief god worshiped there. The KJV has "populous No." Thebes stood on both banks of the Nile, about 400 miles upriver from modern Cairo. Its ruins at Luxor and Karnak are still among the largest temple complexes ever built. For centuries it had been Egypt's royal and religious capital (the Tyndale notes date its height to 1550–1069 BC).
- **"Her rampart a sea, and water her wall" (v8).** The Nile and its canals were Thebes's defense, much as Nineveh trusted the Tigris and its moats. "Sea" here means the broad river, the way the Nile is still called *al-bahr*, "the sea," in Egyptian Arabic.
- **"Cush was her strength; Egypt too… Put and the Libyans were her helpers" (v9).** Cush is the land south of Egypt, modern Sudan. In Nahum's time the kings of Egypt were themselves Cushites, the "Ethiopian" Twenty-fifth Dynasty, so Egypt and Cush fought as one. Put is probably part of Libya, or possibly the land of Punt near Somalia, as the Tyndale notes suggest. Thebes had the whole of northeast Africa behind it.
- **"Yet she became an exile" (v10).** Ashurbanipal's own annals describe the sack of Thebes in 663 BC: its treasures, its people and two great obelisks carried off to Assyria. Every detail of v10 is what Assyria did there: infants killed in the streets, lots cast for the nobles, great men bound in chains. Nineveh is being reminded of its own deeds. The Tyndale notes list the same cruelties elsewhere (2 Kgs 8:12; Ps 137:9).
- **"You also will be drunken; you will go into hiding" (v11).** "Drunken" here means drinking the cup of God's wrath, staggering and helpless (Jer 25:15–28; Hab 2:16). Nineveh, which hid from no one, will look for a place to hide.
- **"Your fortresses are like fig trees with first-ripe figs—if shaken they fall into the mouth of the eater" (v12).** The first figs of early summer were the sweetest, and they fell at a touch (Isa 28:4). No ladder is needed. Assyria's mighty outposts will drop into the enemy's hand as easily as that. History bears it out. Assyria's fortress cities fell one after another in 615–612 BC with little long resistance.
- **"Your troops are women in your midst" (v13).** In the ancient world, where armies were made up of men, this was a common taunt for soldiers who had lost their nerve (Jer 50:37; 51:30). It means they are panic-stricken, not that women are weak in themselves. **"The gates of your land are wide open."** The passes and border forts that guarded Assyria give way, and fire burns the bars of the gates.
- **"Draw water for the siege; strengthen your forts; go into the clay; tread the mortar" (v14).** More mocking advice (compare 2:1). Assyrian walls were built of mud brick, and Nineveh's inner wall was about seven and a half miles around. In a siege, defenders stored water and rushed to make fresh bricks to patch any breach. Nahum tells them to go ahead and try.
- **"There will the fire devour you" (v15).** Right there, at their brick kilns and walls, the fire will catch them. Excavators have found thick ash layers across Nineveh's palaces, the remains of the fire that ended the city in 612 BC.
- **"It will devour you like the locust. Multiply yourselves like the locust" (v15).** Nahum turns the image over and over. First the enemy devours Nineveh like locusts stripping a field (Joel 1:4). Then Nineveh is told to multiply like locusts, as if sheer numbers could save it. They cannot.
- **"You increased your merchants more than the stars of the heavens" (v16).** Nineveh was the center of a vast trading network. **"The locust spreads its wings and flies away."** The merchants who swarmed in for profit strip the land and leave when trouble comes. The Tyndale notes draw this out, and Revelation 18:11–17 shows the same thing: the merchants of Babylon standing far off, mourning their lost trade, not the city.
- **"Your princes are like grasshoppers… settling on the fences in a day of cold" (v17).** Locusts and grasshoppers cannot fly when they are cold. On a cold morning they cling to walls and hedges. When the sun warms them, they take off and vanish. JFB notes the same detail. Assyria's officials look numerous and settled, and in the day of crisis they will simply be gone. The word for "scribes" here is a rare one, probably an Assyrian loanword for a high official.
- **"Your shepherds are asleep, O king of Assyria" (v18).** "Shepherds" was the common Near Eastern title for kings and leaders. Assyrian kings called themselves shepherds of their people. "Asleep" probably means dead (Ps 76:5–6; Jer 51:39). JFB reads it as "death's sleep." The Tyndale notes draw the contrast with Israel's Shepherd, who "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Ps 121:4).
- **"Your people are scattered on the mountains with none to gather them" (v18).** This is the very picture used of Israel without a godly king (1 Kgs 22:17). After 612 BC the last Assyrian king, Ashur-uballit II, held out at Harran in the west until about 609 BC, and then Assyria disappears from history. No one ever gathered it again.
- **"There is no easing your hurt; your wound is grievous" (v19).** "Easing" is a rare Hebrew word that occurs only here, meaning relief or soothing. The wound is fatal. Thebes recovered and was lived in for centuries afterward. Nineveh did not.
- **"All who hear the news about you clap their hands over you" (v19).** Clapping here is the reaction of nations freed from oppression (compare Lam 2:15, where it is mockery, and Ps 47:1, where it is joy). The whole known world cheers. The Tyndale notes say those who suffered under Assyria would hear this as good news.
- **"For upon whom has not come your unceasing evil?" (v19).** The book ends on a question. Very few books of the Bible do, and one of the others is Jonah. Jonah ends with God asking, "Should not I pity Nineveh, that great city…?" (Jonah 4:11). A century later Nahum ends by asking who has not suffered from Nineveh's evil. The two questions stand at the ends of the two books like bookends. God's pity was real, and Nineveh once received it. But mercy refused and cruelty resumed bring the other question in the end.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Nineveh "a city of blood, in which a great deal of innocent blood is shed," and on v4 reads the whoredoms as "idolatries, spiritual whoredoms," while JFB reads them as political seduction, "harlot-like artifices" used to lure nations into subjection. Henry describes v12 vividly: figs that "will fall into the mouth of the eater" if the tree is given "but a little shake." On v18 he says the shepherds "have no life or spirit to appear for the flock." JFB, on v19, sees in the "unceasing evil" a sign of "God's long forbearance, and the consequent enormity of Assyria's guilt." Guzik passes on Boice's survey of the Assyrian kings' own boasts of cruelty and Maier's note on the hundreds of sorcery texts found in Mesopotamia. He also uses the Thebes comparison as a warning to every reader not to assume they will be the exception to God's justice, and he closes with Psalm 73: the righteous need not envy the wicked or take revenge, since God will deal with them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Nahum's last word is that there is "no easing" Nineveh's wound. The gospel answers that question for everyone who turns to God. Isaiah foresaw a Servant who would be "wounded for our transgressions," by whose stripes "we are healed" (Isa 53:5). The cruelty Nahum describes is real, and so is God's anger at it. Yet Jesus prayed for those who crucified him and offers healing even to those whose evil seemed unceasing, as he once offered it to Nineveh through Jonah. Those who refuse it face what Nineveh faced. Those who receive it find a Shepherd who does not sleep and who gathers the scattered (John 10:11–16).
