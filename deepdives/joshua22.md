*Setting:* The land is conquered and divided, and the Levites have their towns (ch 21). Years earlier, before Israel crossed the Jordan, the tribes of Reuben and Gad and half of Manasseh had asked to settle east of the river in the rich grazing land of Gilead and Bashan. Moses agreed on one condition: their fighting men must cross over and fight beside the other tribes until the whole land was won (Num 32). Joshua held them to that promise (1:12–18), and they kept it. Now, perhaps seven years later, Joshua sends them home. The chapter tells what happened next: an altar built by the river, a nation on the edge of civil war, and a crisis settled by honest talk.

#### The Eastern Tribes Return Home (vv1–9)
- **"You have kept all that Moses the servant of the LORD commanded you" (v2).** Joshua's first words are praise. These men had land and families waiting, yet "you have not forsaken your brothers these many days" (v3). The fighting had lasted years: Caleb's age suggests about seven (14:7, 10), and JFB counts "the space of seven years."
- **"The LORD your God has given rest to your brothers" (v4).** The key word of 21:44 again. In 1:15 Joshua had promised that the eastern tribes could go home once their brothers had rest. The condition is met, and the promise is kept on both sides.
- **"Only be very careful to observe the commandment" (v5).** Joshua sends them off with a summary of Deuteronomy in six verbs: love the LORD, walk in his ways, keep his commandments, cling to him, serve him with all your heart and soul. "Cling" (*dabaq*) is the word used of a man holding fast to his wife (Gen 2:24) and of Ruth clinging to Naomi (Ruth 1:14). Deuteronomy uses it for Israel's loyalty to God (Deut 10:20; 13:4). "With all your heart and with all your soul" echoes the Shema (Deut 6:5).
- **"Joshua blessed them and sent them away" (v6).** A leader's blessing was more than good wishes. It placed them under God's favor as they left.
- **"Divide the spoil of your enemies with your brothers" (v8).** The soldiers return rich with silver, gold, bronze, iron, clothing and livestock. Not all the eastern men had crossed: Numbers 32 and Joshua 4:12–13 suggest only some of their fighting men went, about forty thousand, while others stayed to guard the families. Those who guarded the home were to share in the plunder, the same rule Moses set after the war with Midian (Num 31:27) and David later made a statute (1 Sam 30:24–25).
- **"Parting from the people of Israel at Shiloh, which is in the land of Canaan, to go to the land of Gilead" (v9).** The writer carefully names two lands. Canaan, west of the Jordan, was the land God promised to Abraham. Gilead, to the east, was given "by command of the LORD through Moses," but it lay outside Canaan proper. That geography is what the rest of the chapter turns on.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notices the irony that the eastern tribes "were first in the assignment of their lot" but "last in the enjoyment of it." He says praise belongs chiefly to God, "yet instruments must not be altogether overlooked," and he presses v5: "those that have the commandment have it in vain unless they do the commandment." Guzik draws out the order in v5, hearing God's word, loving him, then obeying. Love without listening drifts into error, he says, and obedience without love into legalism. JFB notes that Joshua dismissed them "with high commendations for their fidelity."

#### The Eastern Tribes' Altar of Witness (vv10–34)
- **"An altar of imposing size" (v10).** Literally "an altar great to the sight," built to be seen from far off. It stood at a place called Geliloth (the Hebrew word in vv10–11, which can also mean "the region"), "in the land of Canaan" and "on the side that belongs to the people of Israel" (v11). Most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, place it on the west bank. Its exact site is unknown. JFB guesses it was "an immense pile of stones and earth."
- **Why an altar caused alarm (vv11–12).** The law allowed one altar for sacrifice, at the place God chose, which was now the tabernacle at Shiloh (Lev 17:8–9; Deut 12:5–14). A second altar looked like a rival sanctuary and the first step toward idolatry. Deuteronomy 13:12–15 told Israel to investigate carefully if a town was reported to have turned to other gods, and then to fight it if the report was true. The western tribes gather to make war, but they also do the first thing the law required: they investigate.
- **Phinehas and ten chiefs (vv13–14).** Phinehas, the son of Eleazar the high priest, was the right man to send. At Peor he had stopped a plague by his zeal against open sin, and God gave him "a covenant of a perpetual priesthood" (Num 25:7–13). He also led the priestly side of the war against Midian (Num 31:6). One chief came from each of the ten western tribes (counting western Manasseh, without Levi).
- **"What is this breach of faith…?" (v16).** "Breach of faith" is *ma'al*, unfaithfulness or treachery against God. It is the word used of Achan in 7:1, "the people of Israel broke faith in regard to the devoted things," and the speech will name him in v20. "This day" is repeated again and again in the speech (vv16, 18, 22, 29, 31). The delegation feels that Israel's whole future is being decided now.
- **"Have we not had enough of the sin at Peor?" (v17).** At Peor, on the edge of the land, Israel worshiped Baal and joined in sexual immorality with Moabite women. Twenty-four thousand died in the plague (Num 25:1–9). "From which even yet we have not cleansed ourselves" suggests the after-effects were still felt.
- **"Tomorrow he will be angry with the whole congregation" (v18).** The western tribes are not just policing their neighbors. They believe the sin of one part of Israel brings judgment on all of it, as with Achan: "he did not perish alone for his iniquity" (v20; 7:1, 11).
- **"If the land of your possession is unclean, pass over into the LORD's land" (v19).** A generous offer. If the eastern tribes felt cut off from God's presence, the western tribes would make room for them west of the Jordan, at their own cost. The phrase "the LORD's land where the LORD's tabernacle stands" shows how they saw Canaan: the land where God dwelt.
- **"The Mighty One, God, the LORD! The Mighty One, God, the LORD!" (v22).** The reply begins with an oath. The Hebrew piles up three names of God: *El*, *Elohim*, *YHWH*. Saying it twice is the language of shock. They call God as witness and invite judgment on themselves if they are lying: "He knows; and let Israel itself know!"
- **"You have no portion in the LORD" (vv24–25).** Their reason was fear for the future. The Jordan was a real boundary, and the eastern land was outside Canaan. Some day western children might say, "The river divides us. You are not really God's people." Then eastern children would stop worshiping the LORD.
- **"Not for burnt offering, nor for sacrifice, but to be a witness" (vv26–27).** The altar was a memorial, not a working altar. It was built to the pattern of the real one, "the copy of the altar of the LORD" (v28), so that anyone who saw it would recognize where they belonged. The word for "copy" (*tabnit*) is the word for the "pattern" of the tabernacle shown to Moses (Exod 25:9, 40). It is a model, like the stone memorials of 4:20–24, meant to answer children's questions.
- **"Far be it from us that we should rebel" (v29).** They end where they began, renouncing any rival altar "other than the altar of the LORD our God that stands before his tabernacle."
- **"Today we know that the LORD is in our midst" (v31).** Phinehas, who came prepared to judge, is satisfied. His reasoning: God is among us, because no one has betrayed him. "You have delivered the people of Israel from the hand of the LORD." The eastern tribes had not brought God's anger on the nation.
- **"Spoke no more of making war" (v33).** The congregation hears the report, blesses God, and drops the war. In the book of Judges, Israel will fight bitter civil wars, against Benjamin (Judg 20) and between Ephraim and Gilead (Judg 12:1–6). This time, talking first prevented one.
- **"Called the altar Witness" (v34).** The Hebrew word *'ed*, "witness," is in some manuscripts and missing from others, so some older translations read "called the altar Ed." Without it, the verse reads as if the name of the altar is the sentence that follows. The meaning is the same: "it is a witness between us that the LORD is God."
- **Did the eastern tribes do right?** The text doesn't directly judge them. Their motive was good, and Phinehas accepts it. Many commentators think they still acted unwisely, building something that looked like rebellion without telling anyone first. Later history gave their fear substance: the eastern tribes drifted from the rest of Israel and were the first to fall to Assyria (1 Chr 5:25–26).
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks the eastern tribes meant well but should have consulted God or their brothers first. Of the western tribes he says "God is jealous for his own institutions, and therefore we should be so too," but "their zeal was commendable, but it ought to have been guided with discretion." He praises the inquiry: "many an unhappy strife would be prevented, or soon healed by an impartial and favourable enquiry." At the end he says that, since both sides meant well, "a little want of consideration and good manners should be excused and overlooked." Guzik draws principles for handling conflict: care for holiness, go directly to the people involved, speak with courageous love, try to reconcile before fighting, be willing to pay a price for the other side's good (as the offer of land in v19 did), try to see things from their side, and assume the best of each other. He follows Howard in noting that a west-bank altar would have been impractical for sacrifice by tribes living east of the river, which supports their story. JFB notes that the western tribes chose to send "a deputation consisting of the son of the high priest" and ten princes before taking up arms, and that the eastern tribes answered by "repudiating, in the strongest terms, the alleged crime."

#### Pointing to Christ
The eastern tribes feared that a river would cut their children off from God's people. In Christ the deeper divisions are taken down: he "has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility" (Eph 2:14). The chapter's other worry, about one true altar, is answered in Christ as well. There is one sacrifice, "offered once to bear the sins of many" (Heb 9:28), and "we have an altar" (Heb 13:10) that no copy can replace. And the way Israel handled the crisis, going to the brothers before going to war, is the same way Jesus taught his followers to handle a brother's sin (Matt 18:15).
