*Setting:* God is still speaking to Moses from the tent of meeting at Mount Sinai (1:1). After the burnt offering (ch 1), wholly given to God, and the grain offering (ch 2), a gift of bread, comes the third offering: the peace offering. It is the one sacrifice in the list that ended in a meal. God received the fat, the priests received a share, and the worshiper took the rest home to eat with family and friends. How that meat was divided and how soon it had to be eaten is explained later (7:11–36). Here the focus is on what went to the altar.

#### Laws for Peace Offerings (vv1–17)
- **"A sacrifice of peace offering" (v1).** The Hebrew is *zebah shelamim*. *Zebah* is a sacrifice that is eaten, and *shelamim* comes from the same root as *shalom*. BDB notes that its exact sense is disputed and lists "peace offering," "fellowship offering," "well-being offering" and "thank offering" among the proposals. *Shalom* is far more than the absence of conflict. It means wholeness, well-being and things being right between people. The NIV calls it the "fellowship offering," which captures the shared meal.
- **When was it offered?** Chapter 7 names three kinds: a thank offering for something God had done, a vow offering to keep a promise, and a freewill offering simply out of love (7:12–16). At Sinai, peace offerings sealed the covenant, followed by a meal in God's presence where the elders "beheld God, and ate and drank" (Exod 24:5, 11). Sadly, the same words also describe the golden-calf feast (Exod 32:6).
- **"Male or female… without blemish" (v1).** The burnt offering had to be a male (1:3). Here either sex is allowed, which made it easier for an ordinary family to give. The animal still had to be healthy. There are no birds in this chapter, perhaps because a bird gave too little meat for a meal.
- **"He shall lay his hand on the head of his offering and kill it" (v2).** The same steps as the burnt offering: hand on the head, the worshiper kills the animal, the priests throw the blood on the altar. Even a celebration began with blood. Fellowship with God rested on atonement.
- **The fat, the kidneys and the liver lobe (vv3–4).** Only certain parts were burned: the fat around the entrails, the two kidneys with their fat, and **"the long lobe of the liver,"** probably the fatty lobe next to the kidneys. Hebrew often speaks of the kidneys as the seat of a person's inner thoughts and feelings (Ps 7:9; Jer 17:10, where "mind" is literally "kidneys"). The Tyndale notes suggest this gave those organs special weight. Israel's neighbors read livers to tell the future (Ezek 21:21). In Israel the liver was simply burned for God.
- **"On top of the burnt offering" (v5).** The fat was laid on the burnt offering already on the altar. The Tyndale notes point out that a burnt offering burned there morning and evening (Exod 29:38–42; Num 28:3–4), so the peace offering was literally placed on top of atonement.
- **"The whole fat tail, cut off close to the backbone" (v9).** This is the broad-tailed sheep still raised in the Middle East, whose tail stores fat and can weigh many pounds. It was a prized delicacy, and it went to God. When Samuel honored Saul at a sacrificial meal, he set before him the leg that had been kept for him, a choice portion (1 Sam 9:23–24).
- **"As a food offering to the LORD" (v11).** Literally "food of a fire offering." The Tyndale notes say Scripture can call offerings "the food of God" (21:6), but the Lord does not eat them. Psalm 50:13 asks, "Do I eat the flesh of bulls or drink the blood of goats?" The phrase means the offering belonged to God and was received as a host receives a gift at his table.
- **The goat (vv12–16).** The goat follows the same pattern. Each section repeats the same list of fat and organs, almost word for word, like a set liturgy that every priest learned by heart.
- **"All fat is the LORD's" (v16).** In the ancient world the fat was considered the richest and best part of an animal. "The fat of the land" means the best of it (Gen 45:18). Abel brought "of their fat portions" (Gen 4:4). Giving God the fat meant giving him the best.
- **"A statute forever… that you eat neither fat nor blood" (v17).** Later Leviticus clarifies that the fat banned is the fat of animals used for sacrifice (7:23–25), and blood was never to be eaten because "the life of the flesh is in the blood" and it was given for atonement (17:11). The ban on blood goes back to Noah (Gen 9:4) and is one of the few food rules the Jerusalem council asked Gentile believers to keep, out of regard for Jewish neighbors (Acts 15:20, 29).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the peace offering looked to God "as the giver of all good things," and that it was so called because God and his people "feast together, in token of friendship." He links it to Christ, who "made peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20). JFB calls it "a voluntary tribute of gratitude for health or other benefits," explains that only the fat was burned while the rest became a sacred meal for the offerer and his friends, and says the tail is "of a substance between fat and marrow." JFB has no notes on vv13–16. Guzik makes a helpful distinction: the peace offering did not make peace with God, which was the work of the sin offering; it celebrated a peace already made. He also suggests the ban on fat and blood had health benefits, which may be true but is not the reason the text gives. The text's reason is that the fat and the blood belong to God.

#### Pointing to Christ
The peace offering ended in a meal with God, made possible by blood. Paul says of Christ, "he himself is our peace" (Eph 2:14), and that God reconciled all things to himself, "making peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20). The same order holds: first atonement, then fellowship. "Since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 5:1). And Jesus left his people a meal of their own, bread and cup shared at his table in remembrance of the sacrifice that made peace (1 Cor 10:16; 11:23–26).
