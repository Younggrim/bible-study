*Setting:* Susa, in the spring of about 474 BC, shortly after Haman's decree went out on the thirteenth of Nisan (3:12). Every Jew in the empire, including those back in Judah, has been sentenced to die on one day eleven months away. Esther, safe in the palace and still keeping her identity secret (2:20), does not yet know. This chapter is the center of the book. Most of it is a conversation passed back and forth through a messenger, and in it a young queen who has been hiding moves from fear to courage. It also holds the book's most famous line, and the closest it comes to naming God.

#### Esther Agrees to Help the Jews (vv1–17)
- **"Mordecai tore his clothes and put on sackcloth and ashes" (v1).** These were the standard signs of grief and repentance in the ancient Near East. Sackcloth was coarse goat-hair cloth, and ashes were sprinkled on the head (2 Sam 13:19; Jonah 3:6). Mordecai does not mourn quietly. He goes into the middle of the city and cries out "with a loud and bitter cry." Esau cried out with "an exceedingly great and bitter cry" when he lost his blessing (Gen 27:34), and the same phrase may be meant to echo here. Mordecai's grief is public, and so is his loyalty to his people.
- **"No one was allowed to enter the king's gate clothed in sackcloth" (v2).** The Persian court tolerated nothing sad in the king's presence. Mordecai can come only to the edge. The irony is sharp: the king has just signed a death warrant, and the one thing not allowed near him is mourning.
- **"In every province… great mourning among the Jews, with fasting and weeping and lamenting" (v3).** Fasting in Scripture is almost always joined to prayer (Ezra 8:21–23; Neh 1:4; Dan 9:3; Joel 2:12). The book never says the word "pray," but the reader of the Old Testament can hardly miss it. This is a people crying out to God the way Israel cried out in Egypt (Exod 2:23–25), even though the narrator leaves his name unspoken.
- **"She sent garments to clothe Mordecai… but he would not accept them" (v4).** Esther's first instinct is to fix the problem on the surface. If Mordecai changed clothes he could come into the gate and talk. He refuses. He wants her to understand that this is not a grief to be dressed up.
- **Hathach (vv5–9).** Esther cannot leave the palace and Mordecai cannot enter, so a eunuch appointed to serve her carries messages. The conversation runs through him in four exchanges. JFB describes how messages to the women's quarters always passed through such keepers. The book never says what Hathach thought, but he carries the most dangerous messages in the empire faithfully.
- **"The exact sum of money" (v7).** Mordecai knows the details: the bribe, the plot, how it was done. He sends a copy of the decree itself (v8) so Esther can read it with her own eyes. He also tells her to go "and plead with him on behalf of her people." It is the first time in the book that anyone speaks of Esther's "people" openly.
- **"There is but one law—to be put to death" (v11).** Esther explains why she cannot. The word for law is again *dat*, the unbending Persian law. Anyone who came into the inner court uninvited could be executed, unless the king held out his golden scepter. Greek writers confirm that Persian kings were hard to reach, guarded by layers of protocol. Herodotus says that the Median king Deioces first made a rule that no one could come into the king's presence uninvited, but that messages must pass through attendants. JFB repeats this tradition.
- **"I have not been called to come in to the king these thirty days" (v11).** This is the heart of her fear. She has not seen her husband for a month. She does not know whether she is still in favor. Vashti lost her crown for refusing to come when called; Esther could lose her life for coming when not called.
- **"Do not think to yourself that in the king's palace you will escape" (v13).** Mordecai's reply is blunt. Her crown will not protect her. The decree said "all Jews," and sooner or later someone would find out who she was.
- **"Relief and deliverance will rise for the Jews from another place" (v14).** The word "relief" (*revach*) literally means "space," room to breathe. The lexicon notes it is used only twice in the Old Testament, once for the space between Jacob's herds (Gen 32:16) and once here. Mordecai is sure the Jews will be delivered, with or without her. Why is he so sure? Most readers think he is trusting God's promises to Abraham and to Israel, which could not fail if the whole people were wiped out (Gen 12:3; Jer 31:35–37).
- **"From another place."** Later Jewish writers used "the Place" (*HaMakom*) as a reverent name for God, and some readers think Mordecai is hinting at God here. Most scholars doubt that usage was in place this early, and the plain sense is "from some other source." But even read plainly, the "other place" can only be God's providence. Either way, this is as close as the book comes to naming him.
- **"But you and your father's house will perish" (v14).** Mordecai does not say how. He may mean that if she stays silent, God's judgment will fall on her family, or simply that she will be found out. The point is that silence is not safe.
- **"Who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (v14).** The most famous line in the book. "Who knows" is honest. Mordecai does not claim a revelation. But he looks back over everything that put an orphan on the throne, the deposed queen, the search, the king's favor, and he asks whether it was all for this. Joseph said much the same to his brothers: "God sent me before you to preserve life" (Gen 45:5–7).
- **"Hold a fast on my behalf… for three days, night or day" (v16).** Esther's answer is the turning point of her character. Until now she has obeyed Mordecai; now she gives the orders, and he does "everything as Esther had ordered him" (v17). A fast without food or water for three days was extreme. Most fasts in the law lasted one day (Lev 16:29). The three days probably mean parts of three days, since she goes to the king "on the third day" (5:1). That was the time Jesus also spent in the tomb before he rose "on the third day," and some readers see a pattern here, though the text does not draw it.
- **"I and my young women will also fast as you do" (v16).** Esther's maids, who were Persian palace servants, join the fast. JFB suggests some may have been Jewish or converts, but that is a guess.
- **"Though it is against the law, and if I perish, I perish" (v16).** This is not fatalism. It is surrender. She has weighed the cost and decided that her people's lives matter more than her own. It echoes the attitude of Daniel's friends: "But if not… we will not serve your gods" (Dan 3:18).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees Mordecai's public grief as courage: "he was not ashamed to own himself a friend to the Jews" even when their cause looked "desperate and a sinking cause." He says the law against uninvited visitors was made "in pride, that being seldom seen… he might be adored as a little god." Then he draws the contrast: "It is not thus in the court of the King of kings; to the footstool of his throne of grace we may at any time come boldly." Guzik stresses that Mordecai's confidence was in God's faithfulness, not Esther's, and that God places people in particular positions for his purposes. He quotes Spurgeon urging believers to serve where they already are. JFB softens "command her" (v8), arguing that Mordecai was "imploring rather than commanding" since Esther was now queen.

#### Pointing to Christ
Esther stands between a death sentence and her people, and goes uninvited into the king's presence, knowing it may cost her life. That is a faint outline of what Christ did fully. He did not say "if I perish, I perish"; he knew he would die, and he went anyway, as our intercessor (Heb 7:25). And because he went in, the way is open. Esther feared the inner court; believers are told to "draw near to the throne of grace" with confidence (Heb 4:16), because the King has already held out the scepter, and it was paid for with his Son's blood (Heb 10:19–22).
