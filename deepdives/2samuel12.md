*Setting:* Jerusalem, around 990 BC, some months after chapter 11. David has committed adultery with Bathsheba, arranged the death of her husband Uriah and married her. The child has been born. To everyone watching, the matter is closed. Chapter 11 ended with one line: "the thing that David had done displeased the LORD." Chapter 12 shows what God does about it. He sends a prophet. The chapter moves from rebuke to confession, from judgment to grief, and then, surprisingly, to a new son loved by God and a victory at Rabbah. The heading of Psalm 51 ties that psalm to this moment, "when Nathan the prophet went to him," and Psalm 32 describes the long months of silence before it.

#### Nathan Rebukes David (vv1–15a)
- **"And the LORD sent Nathan to David" (v1).** All through chapter 11 David was the one sending. Now the LORD sends. David did not call for Nathan. God came after him. Nathan was the same prophet who had brought David the great promise of an everlasting house (7:4–17), so he came as a trusted friend, not a stranger with an axe to grind.
- **"There were two men in a certain city, the one rich and the other poor" (v1).** Nathan tells a story that sounds like a legal case brought to the king for judgment, which was part of a king's job (15:2–4). Parables are rare in the Old Testament (compare Jotham's in Judg 9:8–15 and the wise woman's in 14:1–20). A story gets past a man's defenses before he knows what it is about.
- **"One little ewe lamb… it was like a daughter to him" (v3).** Families did sometimes raise a pet lamb in the house. The details pile up tenderness: it ate from his plate, drank from his cup and lay in his arms. "Daughter" in Hebrew is *bat*, the first part of Bathsheba's name (*Bat-sheba*). The Tyndale notes suggest the echo is deliberate.
- **"He took the poor man's lamb" (v4).** "Took" is the same verb as 11:4, "David sent messengers and *took* her," and the verb Samuel used when he warned what kings would do (1 Sam 8:11–17). The rich man had plenty and would not use his own. David had many wives and took the one wife Uriah had.
- **"As the LORD lives, the man who has done this deserves to die" (v5).** Literally "is a son of death." David's anger is real and right, and it is aimed at himself without his knowing it. People are often harshest on the sins of others that mirror their own. Under the law, stealing a sheep did not carry the death penalty.
- **"He shall restore the lamb fourfold" (v6).** David knows his Bible: "four sheep for a sheep" (Exod 22:1). The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) reads "sevenfold" here, but the Hebrew "fourfold" matches the law. Many have noticed that four of David's sons die in the years that follow: this child, Amnon (13:28–29), Absalom (18:14–15) and Adonijah (1 Kings 2:25). The text never says that is a fourfold payment, but the pattern is hard to miss. **"Because he had no pity"** sums up chapter 11.
- **"You are the man!" (v7).** Two words in Hebrew, *attāh hāʾîsh*. Nathan risks his life to say them, since kings did not take kindly to rebuke. He then speaks with the authority of "the LORD, the God of Israel."
- **"I anointed you… I delivered you… I gave you" (vv7–8).** God lists his gifts: the anointing (1 Sam 16), rescue from Saul, Saul's house and household, the whole kingdom. "Your master's wives" probably means that the old king's household passed to the new king as part of the throne. There is no record that David married any of Saul's wives. **"If this were too little, I would add to you as much more."** David did not need to steal. God would have given more. Sin at its root is ingratitude, reaching past what God gives for what he forbids.
- **"Why have you despised the word of the LORD" (v9).** "Despised" (*bāzâ*) means to treat as worthless. It is the verb used of Esau, who "despised his birthright" (Gen 25:34). Nathan names the sins plainly: "You have struck down Uriah… taken his wife… killed him with the sword of the Ammonites." Using an enemy's sword does not make the killing anyone else's.
- **"The sword shall never depart from your house" (v10).** David had shrugged that "the sword devours now one and now another" (11:25). Now the sword turns toward his own family. God had promised that his steadfast love would never depart from David's house (7:15). Both are true together: the covenant stands, and the consequences come. **"Because you have despised me."** To despise God's word is to despise God.
- **"I will raise up evil against you out of your own house… he shall lie with your wives in the sight of this sun" (vv11–12).** This is fulfilled in Absalom's rebellion, when he sleeps with David's concubines in a tent on the palace roof, "in the sight of all Israel" (16:21–22), probably the same roof from which David first saw Bathsheba. **"You did it secretly, but I will do this thing before all Israel."**
- **"I have sinned against the LORD" (v13).** Two words in Hebrew, *ḥāṭāʾtî laYHWH*. No excuse, no blame-shifting, no bargaining. Saul, confronted by Samuel, said "I have sinned" too, but added excuses and asked to be honored before the people (1 Sam 15:24–30). David says only that his sin is against God, as Psalm 51:4 says: "Against you, you only, have I sinned." He had wronged Uriah, Bathsheba and the nation, but all sin is first an offense against God.
- **"The LORD also has put away your sin; you shall not die" (v13).** "Put away" is literally "caused to pass over" or "transferred." The penalty the law named for adultery and murder (Lev 20:10; Num 35:31) is lifted at once. There is no probation and no penance first. Forgiveness follows confession immediately (Ps 32:5; 1 John 1:9).
- **"Because by this deed you have utterly scorned the LORD" (v14).** The Hebrew literally reads "scorned the *enemies* of the LORD." Most scholars think scribes added "enemies" out of reverence, to avoid writing that anyone scorned God himself, a practice seen elsewhere (compare 1 Sam 25:22). The older translations read it as "given occasion to the enemies of the LORD to blaspheme." Either way, David's sin dishonored God publicly.
- **"The child who is born to you shall die" (v14).** Forgiveness removes guilt. It does not always remove consequences. God spares David's life but not his heart, and this is hard to read. The Bible does not hide that the innocent sometimes suffer for the sins of others.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that it was "about nine months" before Nathan came, and imagines David's "harp was out of tune" all that time. He also observes that David later named a son Nathan (5:14; 1 Chr 3:5), the son through whom Luke traces Jesus' line (Luke 3:31). JFB says David's conscience "was still asleep" when he condemned the rich man, so that he was hardest on others when softest on himself. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both point to the four sons who die. Guzik also counts the confession as two Hebrew words, which is right, though his transliteration is loose. Guzik and Henry both draw the line between forgiveness and consequences: David's sin is forgiven, and he still reaps what he sowed. On v8 JFB rightly says David never married Saul's wives. Henry notes the murder and the marriage are each named twice, the marriage perhaps because David thought it put everything right.

#### David's Child Dies (vv15b–23)
- **"Then Nathan went to his house" (v15).** The prophet has done his job and leaves. He will reappear in v25 with good news.
- **"The child that Uriah's wife bore to David" (v15).** Even now the narrator calls her "Uriah's wife." Marriage after the fact did not erase what happened.
- **"David… fasted and went in and lay all night on the ground" (v16).** He pleads for the child in the dust, refusing food and refusing to be lifted up by the elders of his household (v17). This is not the king who shrugged at Uriah's death. Repentance has made him tender again.
- **"On the seventh day the child died" (v18).** A baby boy was circumcised on the eighth day (Gen 17:12), so this child died before he was named or brought into the covenant sign. The servants are afraid to tell David, fearing what grief might drive him to.
- **"David arose from the earth and washed and anointed himself and changed his clothes" (v20).** In the ancient Near East, mourning began at a death, not before. David reverses the expected order. He grieved before and now stops, washing, putting on fresh clothes and going straight to "the house of the LORD," the tent where the ark was kept (6:17), to worship. Then he eats.
- **"Who knows whether the LORD will be gracious to me?" (v22).** David took God's word seriously, but he also knew God sometimes relents when people humble themselves (Jonah 3:9 uses the same "who knows"; compare Hezekiah in 2 Kings 20:1–6). So he prayed. When the answer was no, he accepted it.
- **"I shall go to him, but he will not return to me" (v23).** At the least, David means he will join his son in death. Many Christians read more here, a hope of being reunited, and have found great comfort in it at the death of an infant. Old Testament hope beyond death is often only partly seen. But David's calm, and his going to worship, suggest he believed the child was safe with God. Faithful readers differ on how much the verse itself teaches about infants who die.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the servants' surprise from mourning customs: friends normally came after a few days to raise the mourner, wash him and give him fresh clothes, and David did it all himself without waiting. Henry admires David's composure: "Weeping must never hinder worshipping." He reads v22 as permission to pray boldly for what God has not promised, since "while there is life there is hope." Guzik takes v23 as a sign that infants who die go to be with God, and he extends it to the children of believers from 1 Corinthians 7:14. That second step goes beyond this text, and Christian traditions hold a range of views on it. Henry and the Tyndale notes both see in David's words confidence that life continues after death.

#### Solomon's Birth (vv24–25)
- **"Then David comforted his wife, Bathsheba" (v24).** For the first time she is called by name and "his wife," as Guzik and the Tyndale notes both observe. Bathsheba had now lost a husband and a child. Grief is shared, and comfort is given.
- **"He called his name Solomon" (v24).** In Hebrew *Shelomoh*, from *shalom*, "peace." The Tyndale notes add that the name may also carry the idea of "replacement," from a related root, which fits a son born after a loss. In 1 Chronicles 22:9 God connects the name to the peace he would give Israel in Solomon's days.
- **"And the LORD loved him" (v24).** This short sentence is the gospel in the middle of a dark story. The son of a marriage that began in sin is the one God chooses to love and to make heir to the throne and ancestor of the Messiah (Matt 1:6).
- **"He called his name Jedidiah, because of the LORD" (v25).** *Yedidyah* means "beloved of the LORD." It sounds like, and is close in meaning to, David's own name, which probably means "beloved." The name appears only here. God sends Nathan again, and this time the message is love.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Solomon "a child graciously given," like Seth given after Abel (Gen 4:25), and sees in his name "a token of God's being at peace with them." JFB calls God's love for a son of this marriage "a remarkable instance of divine goodness and grace." Guzik notes that God did not tell David to leave Bathsheba, and that the marriage, though begun wrongly, was to be honored from then on.

#### Rabbah Is Captured (vv26–31)
- **"Now Joab fought against Rabbah" (v26).** This picks up the war from 11:1. It is told here to close the Ammonite story, but most think the city fell earlier, perhaps soon after Uriah's death. The siege may have lasted around two years in all.
- **"I have taken the city of waters" (v27).** Rabbah had a lower town by the water supply, beside the stream that is the source of the Jabbok, and a fortified citadel above. Once the water was cut off, the upper city could not hold out long.
- **"Lest I take the city and it be called by my name" (v28).** Joab, loyal in his own rough way, tells David to come and finish it so the king gets the credit. It is also a jab: the king should be here. David finally goes to war, as he should have done at the start (11:1).
- **"The crown of their king… a talent of gold" (v30).** A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), far too heavy to wear long. The Hebrew *malkām*, "their king," can also be read as *Milcom*, the name of the Ammonite god (1 Kings 11:5), and the ESV footnote gives that option. The crown may have come off the idol's head. Either way, David wore it, perhaps briefly at a ceremony, or the jewel was moved to his own crown.
- **"Set them to labor with saws and iron picks and iron axes" (v31).** The ESV reads this as forced labor, cutting stone and timber and making bricks. That fits Deuteronomy 20:11 and what Solomon later did (1 Kings 9:20–22). The older translations (the KJV "put them under saws") read it as torture. The Hebrew of the parallel in 1 Chronicles 20:3 differs by one letter and can be read "sawed them with saws," which is where that reading comes from. Most modern scholars favor forced labor.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks David was "too haughty and too severe" here, fond of the Ammonite crown and harsh to the captives. JFB defends harsh treatment as justice on a people infamous for cruelty (1 Sam 11:2; Amos 1:13). Both read v31 as torture, following the KJV, where most modern translations see forced labor. Guzik reads the capture as the last step of David's restoration: he returns to leading Israel in battle, and he still has a crown. JFB pictures a crown so heavy it was hung above the throne rather than worn, which is a guess.

#### Pointing to Christ
The God who sent Nathan "sends" first. David did not seek God. God sought David. That is the pattern of the gospel: "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Rom 5:8). David's sin is "put away" the moment he confesses, yet the chapter is honest that someone still dies. The deeper answer is in the Son of David who would come from Bathsheba's line, the truly beloved Son (Mark 1:11), on whom "the LORD has laid… the iniquity of us all" (Isa 53:6). Through him, forgiveness can be full and free, and the God who named Solomon "beloved of the LORD" calls repentant sinners his beloved too.
