*Setting:* The last chapter of Hezekiah's story steps back in time. "In those days" (v1) is a loose link, and the details show the two episodes here happened before the great deliverance of chapter 19: God promises to deliver Jerusalem from Assyria (v6), so the city is not yet saved, and Hezekiah's storehouses are still full (v13), so it is before he stripped them to pay Sennacherib (18:15–16). Hezekiah reigned 29 years (18:2), and he is promised 15 more, so the illness fell in about his fourteenth year, around 703–701 BC. The writer has grouped these stories by theme, not date, as the Tyndale notes explain. After the high point of chapter 19, the chapter shows a sick king saved by prayer and then a healthy king caught by pride, ending with a warning about Babylon that sets up the rest of Kings. Isaiah 38–39 tells the same story and adds the psalm Hezekiah wrote after his recovery (Isa 38:9–20); 2 Chronicles 32:24–33 gives a brief summary.

#### Hezekiah's Illness and Recovery (vv1–11)
- **"Hezekiah became sick and was at the point of death" (v1).** He was about 39 years old. Verse 7 calls the trouble a "boil" (*shechin*), an inflamed sore or abscess, the same word used for the boils on the Egyptians (Exod 9:9) and on Job (Job 2:7). Whatever it was, it was killing him.
- **"Set your house in order, for you shall die; you shall not recover" (v1).** "Set your house in order" means make your final arrangements, for your family and, for a king, for the succession (compare Ahithophel in 2 Sam 17:23). This was urgent. Manasseh was twelve when he became king fifteen years later (21:1), so at this point Hezekiah apparently had no son and no heir to David's throne. JFB makes this point. Guzik sees mercy even in the warning: not everyone is given time to prepare to die.
- **"Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and prayed" (v2).** The Tyndale notes contrast Ahab, who turned his face to the wall and sulked (1 Kings 21:4). Hezekiah turned to the wall to be alone with God, in a crowded sickroom where he could not go to the temple.
- **"Remember how I have walked before you in faithfulness and with a whole heart" (v3).** This is not boasting. Under the covenant God had promised long life to those who obeyed him (Deut 5:33; Ps 91:16), and Hezekiah appeals to that promise. Guzik notes that believers now pray in Jesus' name rather than on their own record (John 16:23–24), while honoring that Hezekiah's appeal was right under the old covenant. **"And Hezekiah wept bitterly."** The hope of resurrection was much dimmer before Christ, who "brought life and immortality to light" (2 Tim 1:10). Hezekiah's own psalm says, "Sheol does not thank you; death does not praise you" (Isa 38:18). JFB adds that he may also have grieved for his unfinished reforms and his kingdom with no heir.
- **"Before Isaiah had gone out of the middle court, the word of the LORD came to him" (v4).** The answer came almost at once. Isaiah had not even left the palace grounds. Isaiah 65:24 promises a day when "while they are yet speaking I will hear."
- **"I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears" (v5).** Notice the title: "the LORD, the God of David your father." God answers as the God of the covenant with David's house. "Leader" (*nagid*) is the title God gave Saul and David when he chose them (1 Sam 9:16; 2 Sam 5:2). David wrote, "You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle" (Ps 56:8).
- **Did God change his mind?** The first message sounded final, and God reversed it. Most interpreters understand such announcements the way Jonah's preaching to Nineveh works: a sentence that states where things are heading, with the door left open to prayer and repentance (Jer 18:7–10; Jonah 3:4–10). Guzik makes this point. God's purposes do not change, but prayer is one of the means he uses to carry them out. Hezekiah did die, just later.
- **"On the third day you shall go up to the house of the LORD, and I will add fifteen years to your life" (vv5–6).** Precise promises: healing in three days, and fifteen more years. It was a rare gift to know exactly how long one had.
- **Were the extra years a blessing?** Some preachers argue Hezekiah should have accepted death, since during the extra years he showed off to Babylon (vv12–19) and fathered Manasseh, Judah's worst king. That goes beyond the text. God himself granted the request and called it healing. Without these years there would have been no son of David to carry the line. What the next section shows is not that the prayer was wrong but that a blessing can be misused.
- **"I will deliver you and this city out of the hand of the king of Assyria" (v6).** This promise only makes sense before the events of chapter 19, confirming that this episode came first. **"For my own sake and for my servant David's sake"** repeats 19:34 exactly.
- **"Bring a cake of figs. And let them take and lay it on the boil" (v7).** Pressed figs were a known remedy for skin sores in the ancient world; a veterinary text from Ugarit prescribes them for horses. God healed, and he used a poultice. JFB says figs were "useful in ripening and soothing inflammatory ulcers." Guzik draws the lesson that using medicine is not a lack of faith. Isaiah's account places this verse after the psalm (Isa 38:21).
- **"What shall be the sign that the LORD will heal me?" (v8).** Asking for a sign is not always unbelief. God offered one to Hezekiah's father Ahaz, who refused it (Isa 7:10–12). The Tyndale notes say Hezekiah's request makes sense, given how great the promise was, and list other signs God gave (Gen 9:12–17; Exod 4:1–9).
- **"Shall the shadow go forward ten steps, or go back ten steps?" (v9).** "Steps" (*maʿalot*) is the ordinary word for a stairway. Most think this was a staircase built by King Ahaz, perhaps leading to an upper room, where the shadow of a pillar or wall crept down the steps as the sun moved, so it worked as a sundial. JFB thought it was a sundial instrument Ahaz brought from Assyria or Babylon. The Great Isaiah Scroll from the Dead Sea calls it "the steps of the upper room of Ahaz" (Isa 38:8), and the KJV's "dial of Ahaz" reflects the sundial idea.
- **"It is an easy thing for the shadow to lengthen ten steps. Rather let the shadow go back" (v10).** The shadow moving forward is what happens every afternoon. Moving backward would be against nature. The Tyndale notes call the retreat unnatural, a clear miracle.
- **"He brought the shadow back ten steps" (v11).** The text does not say how. Christians differ on whether the earth's turning was affected or whether the light itself was bent locally, in Jerusalem. JFB calls it miraculous but "local and temporary." 2 Chronicles 32:31 mentions envoys from Babylon who came "to inquire about the sign that had been done in the land," which suggests it was seen as a wonder. The sign fitted the promise: God turned back the shadow on the dial, and he turned back the shadow of death on Hezekiah's life.
- **Commentators:** Henry summarizes the recovery as coming "in answer to prayer, in performance of a promise, in the use of means," and confirmed by a sign. Guzik and JFB both note the contrast with Ahab's turning to the wall. JFB stresses that Hezekiah had no heir yet, and that his grief came partly from the dim Old Testament light on life after death. Guzik argues that God's first word was not false but conditional, and that healing through figs shows God commonly works through medicine.

#### Hezekiah and the Babylonian Envoys (vv12–21)
- **"Merodach-baladan the son of Baladan, king of Babylon, sent envoys with letters and a present" (v12).** In his own Babylonian language, Marduk-apla-iddina II. He was a Chaldean chief who seized Babylon from Assyria and held it from 721 to 710 BC, until Sargon drove him out, then took it again briefly around 703 BC before Sennacherib expelled him. He spent his life trying to break Assyria's grip. JFB calls him the first Babylonian king in the Bible who broke from Assyria. Babylon at this point was not yet the great empire it would become, but a junior rebel looking for allies.
- **"For he heard that Hezekiah had been sick" (v12).** A get-well visit was the polite reason. The real reason was almost certainly an alliance against Assyria, as JFB, Guzik and the Tyndale notes all say. Chronicles adds that they came to ask about the sign (2 Chr 32:31).
- **"He showed them all his treasure house, the silver, the gold, the spices, the precious oil, his armory" (v13).** Hezekiah was wealthy (2 Chr 32:27–29), and he wanted his guests to see that Judah would be a strong partner. He showed them everything, including the military stores. Henry: "He was too fond of showing them his palace, his treasures, and his magazines." Chronicles names the problem: "Hezekiah did not make return according to the benefit done to him, for his heart was proud" (2 Chr 32:25). It adds, "God left him to himself, in order to test him and to know all that was in his heart" (2 Chr 32:31).
- **What was the sin?** He had just been healed by a miracle, and the Babylonians had come asking about it. It was the perfect moment to tell them about the living God. Instead he talked about himself. Guzik lists pride, ingratitude, trust in a worldly alliance, and a missed chance to honor God. He was also showing a future enemy exactly what there was to take.
- **"What did these men say? And from where did they come to you?" (v14).** Isaiah, like Nathan with David (2 Sam 12:1–7) and God with Adam (Gen 3:9), asks questions he likely knows the answer to, giving the king room to tell the truth. **"They have come from a far country, from Babylon."** Hezekiah seems flattered that envoys came so far. Ironically, "a far country" is exactly what the Gibeonites claimed when they deceived Joshua into a treaty (Josh 9:6, 9).
- **"What have they seen in your house?… They have seen all that is in my house" (v15).** He answers honestly. The repetition "all… nothing… that I did not show them" makes the point heavy.
- **"All that is in your house… shall be carried to Babylon. Nothing shall be left" (v17).** An astonishing prophecy for 700 BC, when Babylon was a struggling city under Assyria's heel. It came true more than a century later, when Nebuchadnezzar carried off the treasures of the temple and palace in 597 and 586 BC (24:13; 25:13–17), as the Tyndale notes record. Isaiah's book turns at exactly this point: right after this prophecy, Isaiah 40 begins, "Comfort, comfort my people," speaking to the exiles in Babylon.
- **"Some of your own sons… shall be eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon" (v18).** "Sons" means descendants. Hezekiah's own son Manasseh was taken to Babylon in chains (2 Chr 33:11). Later, young men "of the royal family and of the nobility" were taken to serve in Nebuchadnezzar's palace, including Daniel and his friends (Dan 1:3–6). "Eunuchs" (*sarisim*) can mean castrated servants, but the word was also used for court officials generally (Potiphar is called one in Gen 39:1 though married), so it is not certain Daniel was literally made a eunuch.
- **"The word of the LORD that you have spoken is good" (v19).** A humble acceptance: God's judgment is right. JFB reads it as "a humble and pious resignation to the divine will," and Henry hears in it, "Yes, certainly it is, and better than I deserve." **"For he thought, 'Why not, if there will be peace and security in my days?'"** This second line is harder to read kindly. It may be simple relief that God has delayed judgment, as he did for Josiah (22:20) and Ahab (1 Kings 21:29). Or it may be the shortsightedness of a man who cares more about his own comfort than about his grandchildren. Guzik reads it as self-centered. Most readers hear both, and the honest text lets them.
- **"How he made the pool and the conduit and brought water into the city" (v20).** This is Hezekiah's Tunnel, one of the great finds of biblical archaeology. Facing an Assyrian siege, Hezekiah blocked the Gihon spring outside the city wall and had a tunnel cut through solid rock, about 1,750 feet (533 m) long, carrying the water to the Pool of Siloam inside the walls (2 Chr 32:2–4, 30). Two teams dug from opposite ends and met in the middle. An inscription found in the tunnel in 1880 describes the moment: the diggers heard each other's voices through the rock, and "the quarrymen struck each man toward his fellow, axe against axe." Visitors can still wade through it. The Pool of Siloam is where Jesus sent a blind man to wash and see (John 9:7).
- **"Are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?" (v20).** The official royal records, now lost, not the biblical book of Chronicles.
- **"Hezekiah slept with his fathers, and Manasseh his son reigned in his place" (v21).** Chronicles says he was buried with honor "in the upper part of the tombs of the sons of David" and "all Judah and the inhabitants of Jerusalem did him honor at his death" (2 Chr 32:33). The last word, though, is the name of his son. One of Judah's best kings is followed by its worst.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this section "Hezekiah's sin, and his recovery from that," reading v19 as genuine humility. JFB reads the Babylonian visit as proposals for "a defensive alliance against their common Assyrian enemy," and calls the display of treasure vanity and a temptation to future plunder. Guzik is sharper than either, seeing pride in the display and self-interest in v19, and concludes that Hezekiah began well but finished poorly, and that extra years did not make him godlier. The Tyndale notes trace the fulfilment of v17–18 through 605, 597 and 586 BC.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hezekiah wept because death seemed the end, and God gave him fifteen years. Jesus also prayed "with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death" (Heb 5:7), and he was not spared death but raised on the third day, as Hezekiah went up to God's house on the third day. Hezekiah's healing was temporary; Christ's resurrection is permanent and shared with all who belong to him. And where Hezekiah showed Babylon his own treasure and kept silent about God, Christ is the treasure himself, "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3).
