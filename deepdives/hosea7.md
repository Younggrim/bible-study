*Setting:* Hosea is preaching to the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim, with its capital at Samaria) in its last chaotic years, roughly 740–725 BC. After Jeroboam II died around 753 BC, the throne became a prize for assassins. Zechariah reigned six months before Shallum killed him; Shallum lasted one month before Menahem killed him; Menahem's son Pekahiah was murdered by Pekah; and Pekah was murdered by Hoshea, the last king (2 Kings 15:8–30; 17:1). In the same years Israel swung between paying tribute to Assyria and seeking help from Egypt. Chapter 6 ended with God wanting to restore his people. Chapter 7 continues the same sentence: every time God moves to heal, their sin is exposed. The chapter is full of vivid pictures: an oven, a half-baked cake, a greying man who has not noticed, a fluttering dove and a bow that will not shoot straight. The ESV continues its heading from 6:1, "Israel and Judah Are Unrepentant," through this chapter.

#### Israel and Judah Are Unrepentant (vv1–16)
- **"When I would heal Israel, the iniquity of Ephraim is revealed" (v1).** This carries on from 6:11 ("When I restore the fortunes of my people"). God is the doctor who comes to heal, but as soon as the bandages come off, the infection shows. **"The thief breaks in, and the bandits raid outside"**: the country has no security, inside the house or on the road. The verb for "breaks in" is the word for digging through a mud-brick wall (compare Matt 6:19).
- **"But they do not consider that I remember all their evil" (v2).** Literally "they do not say to their heart," they never stop to think. The danger is not that God forgets but that they do. **"Their deeds surround them; they are before my face"** pictures their sins standing around them like witnesses in court.
- **"By their evil they make the king glad, and the princes by their treachery" (v3).** At court, wickedness is entertainment, and flattery is rewarded. The princes enjoy plots and lies instead of restraining them. The Tyndale notes say the king is unnamed; many think of Hoshea, but with four kings murdered, it could be any of them.
- **"They are all adulterers; they are like a heated oven" (v4).** "Adulterers" may mean both literal immorality and political betrayal, unfaithfulness to king and to God. The ancient bread oven was a clay cylinder heated with a fire inside. Once the oven was hot, the baker could let it smoulder overnight without stirring the fire, while the dough rose, and then stoke it again in the morning. Their passions are like that: always hot, just waiting.
- **"On the day of our king, the princes became sick with the heat of wine" (v5).** "The day of our king" was probably a festival such as his coronation or birthday, as JFB suggests. The princes get drunk. **"He stretched out his hand with mockers"**: the king joins in with scoffers, unaware that the people around him are plotting. Many think this pictures the night of an assassination: drink, revelry, and then the knife. Elah was murdered while drunk (1 Kings 16:9–10).
- **"For with hearts like an oven they approach their intrigue; all night their anger smolders; in the morning it blazes" (v6).** The plotters wait through the night like the oven, and at dawn the coup flares up. JFB: "Their heart is the oven, their baker the ringleader of the plot." Henry applies it inwardly: "An unclean heart is like an oven heated… it keeps the heat within itself."
- **"They devour their rulers. All their kings have fallen" (v7).** "Rulers" here are "judges," those who govern. The oven consumes everything in it. In about twenty years four kings were murdered. **"None of them calls upon me"**: this is the real tragedy. Not one of the plotters or the kings turned to God in the crisis. Each coup was a human answer to a spiritual problem. (JFB lists Menahem among the murdered kings, but 2 Kings 15:22 says he died and "slept with his fathers.")
- **"Ephraim mixes himself with the peoples" (v8).** "Mixes" is the word used for mixing oil into flour for an offering (Lev 2:5). Israel has blended into the nations through alliances, tribute, foreign gods and foreign ways. God's people were meant to be distinct (Exod 19:5–6; Num 23:9).
- **"Ephraim is a cake not turned" (v8).** Bread was often baked as a flat round cake on hot stones or the oven wall. If it was not turned, one side burned and the other stayed raw dough. Israel is half-baked: burned on one side, raw on the other, good for nothing. Henry applies it to inconsistent religion, zealous at one time and cold at another: "burnt on one side and dough on the other side." The picture also suggests a nation so busy with its schemes that no one is tending what matters.
- **"Strangers devour his strength, and he knows it not" (v9).** Foreigners are draining Israel. Menahem paid Assyria a thousand talents of silver by taxing every wealthy man fifty shekels (2 Kings 15:19–20). Israel thought it was buying security; it was bleeding to death.
- **"Gray hairs are sprinkled upon him, and he knows it not" (v9).** This is one of the most striking images in the book. A man growing old does not notice the grey creeping into his hair until someone points it out. Israel is ageing, declining, near the end, and does not see it. The repeated "he knows it not" echoes Samson, who woke up after his hair was cut and "did not know that the LORD had left him" (Judg 16:20). JFB calls the grey hairs "symptoms of approaching national dissolution."
- **"The pride of Israel testifies to his face; yet they do not return to the LORD" (v10).** This repeats 5:5. Their arrogance is the witness against them, and it is also what keeps them from turning back. **"For all this"**: even after all the warnings, losses and murdered kings, they do not seek God. Amos had the same refrain: "yet you did not return to me" (Amos 4:6–11).
- **"Ephraim is like a dove, silly and without sense" (v11).** The dove was proverbial for being easily fooled. "Without sense" is literally "without heart," that is, without understanding. It flutters from one place to another and flies straight into the trap. **"Calling to Egypt, going to Assyria"**: Israel's foreign policy flapped back and forth. Menahem submitted to Assyria; Pekah rebelled with Syria; Hoshea first paid tribute to Assyria and then secretly sent to "So, king of Egypt," which brought Assyria down on Samaria for good (2 Kings 17:3–6).
- **"As they go, I will spread over them my net" (v12).** Fowlers caught birds by throwing a net over them as they landed. Wherever the dove flies, God's net waits. **"According to the report made to their congregation"** is a difficult phrase. It probably means "as their assembly has been warned," that is, as the covenant curses and the prophets announced (Lev 26; Deut 28). God's discipline will match what they were told.
- **"Woe to them, for they have strayed from me!" (v13).** "Woe" (*oy*) was a cry at funerals. "Strayed" is the word for a bird fluttering away from its nest (Prov 27:8), continuing the dove picture. **"I would redeem them, but they speak lies against me"**: "redeem" (*padah*) is the word used for God buying Israel out of slavery in Egypt (Deut 7:8; 13:5). He is still willing to do it again. Their lies may be false accusations, such as saying God cannot help or does not care, or the lie of giving Baal credit for his gifts.
- **"They do not cry to me from the heart, but they wail upon their beds" (v14).** There is crying, but it is self-pity, not prayer. They lie awake lamenting their losses, but they do not bring them to God. Henry says that prayer without the heart is not really prayer to God at all. Guzik sums it up: they saw their problem, but not their sin.
- **"For grain and wine they gash themselves" (v14).** Cutting oneself was part of Canaanite ritual mourning and pleading with the gods. Baal's prophets on Mount Carmel "cut themselves… with swords and lances" (1 Kings 18:28). The law forbade it (Deut 14:1). There is a textual question here. Most Hebrew manuscripts read "they gather themselves together" (as in the KJV, "they assemble themselves"), but some Hebrew manuscripts and the Greek translation read "they gash themselves." The two Hebrew words differ by a single letter (*r* and *d* look almost alike). The ESV follows the "gash" reading, which fits the Baal context. Either way, they are seeking grain and wine, the gifts, not the Giver (2:8).
- **"Although I trained and strengthened their arms" (v15).** "Trained" is the word for discipline and teaching, used of a father training his son (Deut 8:5). God was the one who gave Israel its military strength under Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14:25–27). JFB notes Calvin's picture of a surgeon binding weak arms to strengthen them. **"Yet they devise evil against me"**: they used God-given strength against him.
- **"They return, but not upward" (v16).** The Hebrew is very short and unclear. The ESV takes it as "not upward," that is, not to God above. The KJV reads "not to the most High," and other versions read "to what is useless" or "to Baal." The sense is clear: they turn, but not to God. Their "repentance" is just changing direction among false hopes. JFB takes it as turning to one "very different from Him, a stock or a stone."
- **"They are like a treacherous bow" (v16).** A bow that is warped or badly strung looks fine but shoots off target, or snaps and hurts the archer. Psalm 78:57 uses the same picture for Israel in the wilderness: "they twisted like a deceitful bow." Henry: "a deceitful bow, which looks as if it were fit for business," but fails when drawn.
- **"Their princes shall fall by the sword because of the insolence of their tongue. This shall be their derision in the land of Egypt" (v16).** Their arrogant speech, perhaps boasts against Assyria or lies against God, will bring them down. Egypt, the ally they trusted, will laugh at them. The irony runs deep: the land God rescued them from will mock them in their ruin.
- **Commentators:** Guzik links vv3–7 to the string of assassinations and admits it is impossible to say which king is meant. He compares v9 to Samson not knowing the LORD had left him, and quotes the Puritan John Trapp that Israel was declining "as a man doth when he grows toward 50." JFB reads the "baker" as "the ringleader of the plot," and the unturned cake as "burnt on one side and unbaked on the other," Israel's half-hearted religion. Henry turns the oven inward, as a picture of lust in the heart: "Thus fiery hot are the lusts of an unclean heart." All three read the dove as a picture of foolish diplomacy, and both Henry and Guzik read v14 as crying out about trouble without repenting of sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel's kings fell one after another, and none of them called on God. The book is waiting for a king who would. Jesus, the son of David, prayed through the night (Luke 6:12), wept from the heart, and in his own crisis said, "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). When the people looked to Egypt and Assyria for rescue, God said, "I would redeem them." In Christ he did what Israel refused: "you were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). Where Israel was a half-baked cake, mixed with the nations, Jesus called himself "the bread of life" (John 6:35), the one offering that was never half done.
