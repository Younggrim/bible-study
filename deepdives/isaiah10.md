*Setting:* The chapter finishes the four-stanza poem against Israel that began in 9:8, and then turns to Assyria itself. The messages probably come from different moments in Isaiah's ministry, roughly 732–701 BC. Verse 9 mentions Samaria's fall as already past, which places at least that section after 722 BC, when the Assyrians destroyed the northern kingdom and deported its people (2 Kgs 17:5–6). Assyria was at the height of its power, and its kings, Tiglath-pileser III, Shalmaneser V, Sargon II and Sennacherib, boasted in their inscriptions of city after city crushed. Judah had invited Assyria in (ch 7), and now that same empire was eyeing Jerusalem. The big question of the chapter is who is really in charge of history. Isaiah's answer is that the most feared empire on earth is only a stick in God's hand.

#### Judgment on Arrogance and Oppression (vv1–4)
- **"Woe to those who decree iniquitous decrees, and the writers who keep writing oppression" (v1).** The last stanza of the poem names the deepest rot: injustice written into law. These are officials and judges who make the system itself serve the powerful, so that oppression is official and on paper.
- **"To rob the poor of my people of their right, that widows may be their spoil" (v2).** Again God calls the poor "*my* people." Widows and orphans had no male protector in that society and were easy to cheat. God's law specially guarded them (Exod 22:22–24; Deut 10:18). These leaders turned them into "spoil" and "prey," the language of war and hunting.
- **"What will you do on the day of punishment… where will you leave your wealth?" (v3).** "Punishment" is literally "visitation," the day God comes to inspect and judge. The ruin comes "from afar," from Assyria. The wealth they stole cannot be hidden or carried away.
- **"Nothing remains but to crouch among the prisoners or fall among the slain" (v4).** Two options only: captivity or death. **"For all this his anger has not turned away, and his hand is stretched out still."** The fourth and final refrain (5:25; 9:12, 17, 21).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the decree-makers as "unrighteous judges" who had unjust decisions recorded, and explains the "glory" of v3 as wealth people tried to deposit somewhere safe. Matthew Henry notes that the woes against oppressors come before the comfort to God's people, as is often the way in the prophets. Guzik ties the refrain to the poem's main point: God's discipline keeps coming because the people keep refusing to turn.

#### Judgment on Arrogant Assyria (vv5–19)
- **"Woe to Assyria, the rod of my anger" (v5).** A startling twist. Until now Assyria has been God's tool against Israel. Now God says "woe" over the tool itself. "Rod" and "staff" were the sticks a shepherd or master used to discipline. Isaiah had promised that the "rod of his oppressor" would be broken (9:4). Here we learn whose rod it really was.
- **"Against a godless nation I send him… to take spoil and seize plunder" (v6).** The "godless nation" is God's own people. "Spoil" and "plunder" are *shalal* and *baz*, the same words in the name Maher-shalal-hash-baz (8:1). His name has come true.
- **"But he does not so intend, and his heart does not so think" (v7).** The heart of the chapter. Assyria's king has no idea he is serving God. He thinks he is serving himself: "it is in his heart to destroy." God uses his ambition for God's purposes, and at the same time holds him fully responsible for it. Scripture holds both together. Joseph's brothers "meant evil," but "God meant it for good" (Gen 50:20). The crucifixion was "the definite plan and foreknowledge of God," and also carried out by "lawless men" who were guilty (Acts 2:23).
- **"Are not my commanders all kings?" (v8).** A real boast of Assyrian rulers, who called themselves "king of kings." Their governors and generals were often former kings of conquered nations. There may be a wordplay too: the Hebrew *sar* means "prince" or "commander," while the Assyrian word *sharru* means "king."
- **"Is not Calno like Carchemish? Is not Hamath like Arpad? Is not Samaria like Damascus?" (v9).** A roll call of conquered cities, moving south toward Jerusalem. Calno and Arpad in northern Syria fell to Tiglath-pileser around 740–738 BC. Carchemish, a great city on the Euphrates, fell to Sargon in 717 BC. Hamath fell in 738 and again in 720 BC. Damascus fell in 732 BC, and Samaria in 722 BC. Each one fell, so why not Jerusalem? Sennacherib's field commander would make this exact speech outside Jerusalem's walls: "Where are the gods of Hamath and Arpad?" (36:18–20).
- **"Shall I not do to Jerusalem and her idols as I have done to Samaria" (v11).** To the Assyrian king, the LORD is just one more local god, like the idols of Samaria. That is his fatal mistake. The irony is that Jerusalem *did* have idols (2:8), but its God was not one of them.
- **"When the Lord has finished all his work on Mount Zion… he will punish… the king of Assyria" (v12).** God's order of business: first he finishes disciplining his own people, then he deals with the proud tool.
- **"By the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom" (v13).** Notice how many times he says "I" and "my." Assyrian royal inscriptions really do read this way. Sennacherib's annals list his conquests in the first person: "I besieged, I conquered, I plundered." He moved whole populations around ("I remove the boundaries of peoples"), and Assyria's policy of mass deportation was exactly that.
- **"As one gathers eggs that have been forsaken, so I have gathered all the earth" (v14).** Conquering nations was as easy as collecting eggs from an abandoned nest: no mother bird flapped a wing or chirped in protest.
- **"Shall the axe boast over him who hews with it?" (v15).** Isaiah's answer to the boast is a picture anyone can understand. A tool does not get the credit. An axe cannot swing itself. A rod does not lift the man holding it. Assyria's power was real, but it was borrowed.
- **"Will send wasting sickness among his stout warriors" (v16).** "Stout warriors" is literally "his fat ones," well-fed elite troops. God will send "leanness" on them. This was fulfilled in 701 BC, when "the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians" in one night (37:36). Some historians think a plague swept the camp. The Greek historian Herodotus records a different story of Sennacherib's army being disabled in Egypt by a swarm of mice, which some connect to a plague. Sennacherib's own annals boast that he shut up Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage," but they never claim he took Jerusalem.
- **"The light of Israel will become a fire, and his Holy One a flame" (v17).** The God who is light to his people is fire to his enemies. Assyria, which treated Judah like "thorns and briers" to be burned (9:18), will itself be burned "in one day." Hebrews says, "our God is a consuming fire" (Heb 12:29).
- **"The remnant of the trees of his forest will be so few that a child can write them down" (v19).** Assyria's vast army, pictured as a great forest, will be cut down until a child learning to count could list the survivors. Within a century Assyria was gone. Its capital, Nineveh, fell to the Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC, and the empire vanished so completely that its great city lay buried under mounds of earth until archaeologists excavated it in the 1840s.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry puts it memorably: "The tyrants of the world are but the tools of Providence." Also: "Great conquerors are often no better than great robbers," and "the tool striving with the workman is no less absurd than the clay striving with the potter." JFB explains that Assyria's satraps often bore the title and crown of kings, and says the king of Assyria, though acting freely, was "unconsciously carrying out God's purposes." JFB, like the Tyndale notes, connects the wasting sickness to the 185,000 who died before Jerusalem. Guzik draws in Joseph, Saul and Judas as other examples of God using human evil without approving it. He also turns v15 on believers: even willing servants of God can grow proud of their usefulness, and they should remember they are only tools (Luke 17:10).

#### The Remnant of Israel Will Return (vv20–34)
- **"The remnant of Israel… will no more lean on him who struck them, but will lean on the LORD" (v20).** Ahaz leaned on Assyria, and Assyria struck them. The remnant learns the lesson the hard way. They will lean on "the Holy One of Israel, in truth," not with lip service (29:13) but in reality.
- **"A remnant will return, the remnant of Jacob, to the mighty God" (v21).** "A remnant will return" is *she'ar yashuv*, the exact name of Isaiah's son (7:3). His name was a prophecy. "Return" (*shuv*) can mean both coming back home from exile and turning back to God in repentance. "The mighty God" is *El Gibbor*, the same title given to the royal child in 9:6. The remnant returns to the God who is also the child's name.
- **"Though your people Israel be as the sand of the sea, only a remnant of them will return" (v22).** God promised Abraham descendants "as the sand that is on the seashore" (Gen 22:17). That promise had been kept. But numbers do not save anyone. Paul quotes vv22–23 in Romans 9:27–28 to explain why, in his day, only some of Israel believed in Jesus. God had always worked through a remnant, and Paul counts himself in it (Rom 11:1–5).
- **"Destruction is decreed, overflowing with righteousness" (v22).** Even God's judgment is righteous. It is not cruelty. The judgment that comes like a flood is right through and through.
- **"O my people, who dwell in Zion, be not afraid of the Assyrians" (v24).** The tone changes again, to comfort. "My people," at last. Assyria may beat them "as the Egyptians did," the way slave drivers once beat Israel in Egypt (Exod 5:14). But God has broken one oppressor before, and he will do it again.
- **"In a very little while my fury will come to an end" (v25).** The discipline has a limit. God's anger against his people is "for a moment, but his favor is for a lifetime" (Ps 30:5; Isa 54:7–8).
- **"As when he struck Midian at the rock of Oreb… his staff will be over the sea" (v26).** Two great rescues from Israel's past. At the rock of Oreb, Gideon's men killed the Midianite prince Oreb (Judg 7:25), and Isaiah has already pointed to "the day of Midian" (9:4). And at the Red Sea, Moses lifted his staff over the waters and Egypt's army drowned (Exod 14:16, 26–28). Assyria had a rod and staff (v5). God has a staff too, and his is stronger.
- **"His yoke from your neck; and the yoke will be broken because of the fat" (v27).** The yoke of 9:4 is broken again. The last line is one of the hardest in Isaiah. The Hebrew reads literally "the yoke will be destroyed from before fat (or oil)." Some take it as an ox grown so fat and strong that it bursts the yoke, a picture of a people grown strong again. Others, following the KJV's "because of the anointing," see the "anointed one," the Messiah, or the anointing of God's Spirit. Many modern scholars think the text has been damaged and join the last words to v28 as a place name. Honesty requires saying the meaning is uncertain.
- **"He has come to Aiath… at Michmash he stores his baggage" (vv28–32).** A breathless poem of an enemy army racing toward Jerusalem from the north, town by town, each name a few miles closer. Aiath (Ai), Migron, Michmash, the steep pass where Jonathan once climbed up to the Philistine garrison (1 Sam 14:4–13), Geba, Ramah, "Gibeah of Saul," the hometown of Israel's first king, Gallim, Laishah, Anathoth, Madmenah, Gebim. Some names are wordplays. "Poor Anathoth" sounds like *'aniyyah 'anatot*, "answer her, Anathoth." Anathoth was the home of the prophet Jeremiah a century later (Jer 1:1).
- **"This very day he will halt at Nob; he will shake his fist at the mount of the daughter of Zion" (v32).** Nob was a priestly town just north of the city, within sight of the temple (1 Sam 21:1). The army stands there and shakes its fist at Jerusalem. The route is odd: it is not the main road, but a rough back way over the Michmash pass, as if for a surprise attack. Scholars debate whether this describes a real march, perhaps the Syro-Ephraimite army of 734 BC or an Assyrian force under Sargon around 713 BC, or is a poetic picture of the threat. When Sennacherib actually came in 701 BC, his main army approached from Lachish in the southwest. Either way, the point of the poem is where it stops: at the gates, and no farther.
- **"The Lord GOD of hosts will lop the boughs with terrifying power" (v33).** Just as the enemy raises his fist, God steps in like a woodsman. The tall trees are hacked down, "the lofty will be brought low," the theme of 2:12–17 again.
- **"He will cut down the thickets of the forest with an axe, and Lebanon will fall by the Majestic One" (v34).** Lebanon's cedars were the tallest, proudest trees known. Assyria's forest will be felled. The axe that boasted (v15) is now cut down by God's axe. And the next verse, the first of chapter 11, sets the contrast: from the stump of a felled tree, a shoot will grow.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the remnant of vv20–22 as only partly fulfilled in Hezekiah's day and fully in a future turning of Israel, following Romans 9. JFB takes "the anointing" of v27 as the Messiah, while noting Maurer's alternative, "because of the fatness." Guzik also reads it as the anointing, which he applies to the Holy Spirit's power, and he notes Bultema's view that it points to Christ, the Anointed One. Guzik ties v26 to the 185,000 and to Sennacherib's later murder by his own sons in the temple of his god (2 Kgs 19:35–37). JFB identifies Lebanon and its forest with "the Assyrian army" and says Nob was "a town of the priests" in sight of Jerusalem. Matthew Henry: "Great armies before him are but as great woods, which he can fell or fire when he pleases."

#### Pointing to Christ
The proud axe boasts and is cut down. The humble remnant leans on the mighty God and is saved. The God who used Assyria's cruelty and then judged it is the same God who, at the cross, took the worst act of human evil and made it the means of salvation, while still holding the guilty responsible (Acts 4:27–28). The remnant returns to *El Gibbor*, the mighty God, and Isaiah has already given that name to a child born to David's house (9:6). Jesus is that mighty God come in the flesh, the one who breaks the yoke from his people's necks and gives them his own easy yoke instead (Matt 11:28–30). And as the chapter ends with a forest felled, Isaiah is about to show the shoot from Jesse's stump: the King whose kingdom no axe can cut down.
