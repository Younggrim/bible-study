*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, as he continues his second sermon (chapters 5–26). Chapter 6 warned that in Canaan Israel would "eat and be full" and might "forget the LORD" (6:11–12). Chapter 7 told them not to fear the nations, because God's love, not their strength, had chosen them. Chapter 8 returns to the danger of forgetting and gives it a whole chapter. Moses sets two landscapes side by side: the harsh wilderness behind them and the rich land in front of them. Both, he says, are God's classroom. The hunger of the desert was meant to humble them, and the plenty of Canaan will test whether they stay humble. The chapter is built around the words "remember" (vv2, 18) and "forget" (vv11, 14, 19), and it contains the verse Jesus quoted when Satan tempted him to turn stones into bread.

#### Remember the LORD Your God (vv1–20)
- **"The whole commandment… that you may live and multiply" (v1).** As in 6:1, "commandment" is singular, the whole way of life. Obedience is not a price paid for life in the land but the shape life takes there.
- **"You shall remember the whole way that the LORD your God has led you these forty years" (v2).** Not only the miracles but "the whole way," including the hard parts. In Deuteronomy, remembering is not nostalgia. It means letting the past shape present trust and obedience. The forty years were a judgment on the first generation's unbelief (1:35), yet God turned even that judgment into training for their children.
- **"That he might humble you, testing you to know what was in your heart" (v2).** "Humble" (*anah*) means to bring low, to afflict. The testing was not because God lacked information. He already knows every heart (Ps 139:1–4). The Bible often speaks of God in human terms like this. The test brought out into the open, for Israel's own sake, what was hidden in them. Hardship shows what kind of faith is really there.
- **"He humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna" (v3).** The order matters. God let them hunger first, so that when the food came they would know it came from him. Manna was "bread from heaven" (Exod 16:4), and its name probably comes from the people's question, *man hu*, "What is it?" (Exod 16:15). "Which you did not know, nor did your fathers know": no one had a category for it. It came one day at a time and spoiled if hoarded (Exod 16:19–20), except on the Sabbath.
- **"Man does not live by bread alone, but man lives by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD" (v3).** This is the chapter's best-known line. The point is not that bread is unimportant. Manna itself came into being because God spoke. Physical life depends on God's word, and so does the deeper life of trusting and obeying him. When Jesus had fasted forty days in the wilderness and Satan said, "command these stones to become loaves of bread," he answered with this verse (Matt 4:3–4). Israel spent forty years in the wilderness and grumbled. Jesus spent forty days and trusted.
- **"Your clothing did not wear out on you and your foot did not swell these forty years" (v4).** Ordinary clothing and sandals would not last forty years of desert walking. Moses says it again at the end of his life (29:5) and Nehemiah 9:21 recalls it. Some Jewish legends added that the children's clothes grew with them, but the text simply credits God's quiet care. The Hebrew word for "swell" (*batseq*) appears only here and in Nehemiah 9:21, describing the blistered, swollen feet of long marches.
- **"As a man disciplines his son, the LORD your God disciplines you" (v5).** The hardships were not a cruel master's punishment but a father's training. The word for discipline (*yasar*) includes correction and teaching. Proverbs 3:11–12 uses the same picture, and Hebrews 12:5–11 quotes it to explain why believers face hardship: "the Lord disciplines the one he loves." Israel was already called God's "firstborn son" (Exod 4:22).
- **"A land of brooks of water, of fountains and springs" (v7).** To people who had seen water come only from a rock, this sounded like paradise. Egypt depended on the Nile and irrigation. Canaan depended on rain from heaven and springs from the hills (11:10–12), which meant it depended directly on God.
- **"Wheat and barley, of vines and fig trees and pomegranates, a land of olive trees and honey" (v8).** These are known in Jewish tradition as the "seven species," the main produce of the land, still celebrated today. "Honey" here may well be date syrup, since the list is of cultivated crops. The rhythm of the verse sounds almost like a song, as if Moses can taste it.
- **"A land whose stones are iron, and out of whose hills you can dig copper" (v9).** Iron and copper were mined in the Arabah, the rift valley south of the Dead Sea, at sites such as Feinan and Timna, and iron ore was found in the north. In Moses' day iron-working was still rare and valuable, and Israel would later be at a disadvantage against the Philistines, who controlled it (1 Sam 13:19–22).
- **"You shall eat and be full, and you shall bless the LORD your God" (v10).** Enjoying God's gifts is not wrong. Eating one's fill is part of the blessing. The danger is enjoying the gift and forgetting the giver. Jewish tradition draws its practice of giving thanks *after* meals (the *Birkat Hamazon*) from this verse.
- **"Take care lest you forget the LORD your God by not keeping his commandments" (v11).** Forgetting God is defined by behavior. It is possible to remember all the right facts about God and still forget him by living as if he had no claim on life.
- **"Then your heart be lifted up" (v14).** Verses 12–14 build up the scene: full stomachs, good houses, growing herds, "silver and gold… multiplied… all that you have is multiplied." The word "multiply" is repeated three times. Then comes the turn: the heart grows proud. A "lifted up" heart is the Bible's picture of pride (Hos 13:6; Ezek 28:2, 5).
- **"Who led you through the great and terrifying wilderness, with its fiery serpents and scorpions" (v15).** The "fiery serpents" were the venomous snakes of Numbers 21:6, "fiery" perhaps from the burning pain of their bite. The **"flinty rock"** was hard stone, the least likely source of water imaginable (Exod 17:6; Num 20:11). Verses 14–16 are one long description of God, piling up what he did, the opposite of forgetting.
- **"To do you good in the end" (v16).** The hard years had a purpose. God's discipline is aimed at his people's good at the end of the road, even when the road itself is painful (compare Rom 8:28; Heb 12:11).
- **"My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth" (v17).** Moses quotes what the heart says in private, not what the mouth says in public. It is the boast of the self-made man. "Wealth" (*chayil*) can mean strength, ability or property.
- **"It is he who gives you power to get wealth" (v18).** Moses doesn't deny that Israel will work hard. He says the strength, skill and opportunity to work all come from God. **"That he may confirm his covenant that he swore to your fathers"** means the prosperity serves God's purposes and keeps his promise to Abraham. Wealth is given for a reason beyond itself.
- **"I solemnly warn you today that you shall surely perish" (v19).** The Hebrew is literally "I testify against you today." It is courtroom language: Moses stands as a witness. Forgetting God leads to following other gods.
- **"Like the nations that the LORD makes to perish before you, so shall you perish" (v20).** This is a startling end to a chapter about blessing. Israel has no special exemption. If they live like the Canaanites, they will share the Canaanites' fate. The northern kingdom fell to Assyria and Judah to Babylon for exactly these reasons (2 Kgs 17:7–18).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry calls the forty years a school and says God's discipline comes from "a loving tender Father to all his children." On v3 he writes that God could sustain his people any way he chose: "We might live upon air if it were sanctified for that use by the word of God." On v16, "God reserves the best till the last for his Israel." On v17 he calls the self-made boast "spiritual idolatry," like sacrificing to our own net (Hab 1:16), and on vv19–20, "If we do as sinners do, we must expect to fare as sinners fare." JFB reads the unworn clothing and unswollen feet as "a special or miraculous interposition of their loving Guardian." It explains that "brass" in older translations means copper ore, "not the alloy brass," and notes that venomous snakes are still common in that desert. JFB adds no separate comment on vv5–6, v10 or vv16–20. Guzik stresses that the test was for Israel's sake, so they would learn what was in their own hearts. He urges believers to feed on Scripture as daily bread rather than only use it in arguments, and notes that Jesus, also hungry in the wilderness, refused to use his power to serve himself. He warns that the worst kind of pride is spiritual pride, the pride of those who think they have earned God's grace. All three agree that prosperity is a sharper test than poverty.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus went into the wilderness for forty days, as Israel spent forty years, and was hungry. Satan tempted him to make bread for himself, and Jesus answered with v3: he would live by his Father's word. Where Israel failed the test of the wilderness, the true Son passed it. Later he fed crowds in a desolate place and then said, "I am the bread of life… This is the bread that comes down from heaven, so that one may eat of it and not die" (John 6:35, 50). The manna lesson reaches its fullest meaning in him. And the warning against the self-made heart finds its answer in the cross, where no one can say, "My power… has gotten me this."
