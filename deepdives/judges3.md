*Setting:* Chapter 2 laid out the pattern of the whole book: Israel turns to other gods, God gives them over to enemies, they cry out, God raises up a judge, and the land has rest until the judge dies. Chapter 3 finishes that introduction by naming the nations God left in the land, and then gives the first three judges. Othniel's story is short and almost a template. Ehud's is long, vivid and darkly funny. Shamgar gets a single verse. All three come from different corners of the land, and each oppressor comes from a different direction: Mesopotamia in the far north-east, Moab across the Jordan, and the Philistines on the coast.

#### Israel's Unfaithfulness, continued (vv1–6)
- **"The nations that the LORD left, to test Israel" (v1).** This continues 2:20–23. God did not fail to drive these nations out. He left them on purpose, for two reasons.
- **"That the generations of the people of Israel might know war" (v2).** The first reason. The new generation "had not experienced all the wars in Canaan." Living among enemies would teach them to fight, and to depend on God in battle as their fathers had. JFB calls this "making them acquainted with war."
- **"The five lords of the Philistines" (v3).** The Philistines were not native Canaanites. They were among the "Sea Peoples" who came from the Aegean region and settled on the southern coast around 1200 BC, though the Bible mentions earlier Philistines in Abraham's day (Gen 21:32). The "lords" (*seren*, a word probably borrowed from their own language) ruled five cities: Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath and Ekron (1 Sam 6:17). They will be Israel's main enemy from Samson to David.
- **The northern nations (v3).** The Sidonians on the coast and the Hivites in the Lebanon mountains, from Mount Hermon to Lebo-hamath, the far northern border God had promised (Num 34:8). The list shows how far short Israel fell of the borders God had set.
- **"To know whether Israel would obey" (v4).** The second reason, and the main one: a test of the heart (2:22; Deut 8:2).
- **"Their daughters they took to themselves for wives… and they served their gods" (vv5–6).** Israel failed the test immediately. The six nations named are the standard list from the promises (Exod 3:8; Deut 7:1). Deuteronomy 7:3–4 forbade exactly these marriages, "for they would turn away your sons from following me." Marriage led to worship. JFB: "reciprocal alliances were formed by marriage."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry, in his concise commentary, says these nations exposed what was in Israel's heart, and adds that "the friendship of the world is more fatal than its enmity." Guzik notes that the Christian life is also a battle rather than a comfortable rest, and that Israel's failure to drive out the nations was both its own choice and part of God's plan.

#### Othniel (vv7–11)
- **"They forgot the LORD their God and served the Baals and the Asheroth" (v7).** "Forgot" does not mean they lost the memory. It means they lived as if he did not matter (Deut 8:11–14). The Asheroth were wooden poles or carved trees honoring the goddess Asherah, Baal's consort, set up beside altars (Deut 16:21).
- **"Cushan-rishathaim king of Mesopotamia" (v8).** The Hebrew says "Aram-naharaim," "Aram of the two rivers," the region of upper Mesopotamia around the Euphrates, where Abraham's relatives had lived (Gen 24:10). The name Cushan-rishathaim means "Cushan of double wickedness." It is almost certainly not what he called himself but a mocking nickname, the kind Israelite storytellers gave their enemies. No outside record names him, and his kingdom lay far away, which has puzzled scholars. Some suggest a different reading of "Aram" as "Edom," a closer enemy, but there is no manuscript support for that change.
- **"Served Cushan-rishathaim eight years" (v8).** Israel chose to "serve" the Baals, so God let them "serve" a foreign king. The same Hebrew verb is used for both.
- **"When the people of Israel cried out to the LORD" (v9).** Not necessarily repentance, but a cry of pain. God answers anyway. Henry: "affliction makes those cry to God who before would scarcely speak to him."
- **"Othniel the son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother" (v9).** The hero who took Debir and married Achsah (1:12–13; Josh 15:16–17). He is from Judah, the tribe that led in chapter 1, and his family had shown faith since the spies. He is the model judge. Nothing bad is said of him.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD was upon him, and he judged Israel" (v10).** The first mention of the Spirit in Judges. The same phrase comes upon Gideon (6:34), Jephthah (11:29) and Samson (14:6). The judges' strength was not their own. "Judged" here comes before "went out to war," and JFB suggests he first set things right at home and then fought: "impelled by a supernatural influence, he undertook the difficult task of government."
- **"The land had rest for forty years" (v11).** Forty is the length of a generation. It may be a round number. Othniel's rest is the pattern the rest of the book falls away from.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Othniel's "military experience qualified him for the work," and at his death remarks, "How powerful the influence of one good man is." Guzik links the Spirit's coming to Zechariah 4:6, "Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit." Henry's concise commentary has Othniel reforming Israel first and then fighting the enemy. The Tyndale notes call Othniel the ideal judge and see the account as the cycle in its plainest form.

#### Ehud (vv12–30)
- **"The LORD strengthened Eglon the king of Moab against Israel" (v12).** God is behind the oppressor. Moab, east of the Dead Sea, was descended from Lot (Gen 19:37). Eglon brings in two allies, Ammon, Moab's brother nation, and Amalek, Israel's oldest enemy (Exod 17:8–16).
- **"They took possession of the city of palms" (v13).** Jericho (1:16; Deut 34:3). The city whose walls God had thrown down is now a foreign king's base on Israel's side of the Jordan. Eglon may have had a palace there. Eighteen years is more than twice as long as Cushan's rule.
- **"Ehud, the son of Gera, the Benjaminite, a left-handed man" (v15).** Benjamin means "son of the right hand," and here is a Benjaminite who is literally "bound in his right hand." The phrase may mean his right hand was impaired, or that he had been trained to use his left. Benjamin was known for left-handed warriors: seven hundred elite slingers in 20:16, and archers and slingers who could use either hand in 1 Chronicles 12:2. The Greek translation renders it "ambidextrous." JFB notes "this peculiarity distinguished many in the Benjamite tribe."
- **"The people of Israel sent tribute by him" (v15).** Tribute was the yearly payment of a conquered people, likely produce and goods carried by a team of bearers (v18).
- **"A sword with two edges, a cubit in length… on his right thigh" (v16).** A cubit here may be a short one, around 13–18 inches, a long dagger. Most men wore a weapon on the left side for a right-hand draw, so a guard searching him would check there. JFB: "the sword was usually worn on the left side."
- **"Eglon was a very fat man" (v17).** A detail that matters later. His name is probably related to *'egel*, "calf." The storyteller is painting Eglon as a fattened calf, ready for slaughter.
- **"He turned back at the idols near Gilgal" (v19).** The Hebrew *pesilim* usually means carved images. The KJV's "quarries" is a less likely reading. Some think these were the twelve stones Joshua set up at Gilgal (Josh 4:20), now treated as idols. JFB takes them as Moabite idols "the sight of which kindled the patriotic zeal of Ehud." In any case they mark the border of Eglon's territory, and Ehud passes them again on his escape (v26).
- **"I have a secret message for you… I have a message from God for you" (vv19–20).** The Hebrew word *dabar* means both "word" and "thing." Ehud's "secret thing" is, in fact, a blade. The king clears the room and rises, perhaps out of respect for a message from God. The message is delivered.
- **"His cool roof chamber" (v20).** An upper room built to catch the breeze, which JFB compares to the garden pavilions of eastern rulers.
- **"The fat closed over the blade… and the dung came out" (v22).** The writer does not spare details. The Hebrew word translated "dung" appears only here and its meaning is uncertain, but the scene is clear. The fattened calf has been slaughtered.
- **"Ehud went out into the porch" (v23).** Another rare word, whose meaning is uncertain. Some think Ehud escaped through a latrine opening. Others think he simply walked out through a vestibule, locking the doors behind him.
- **"Surely he is relieving himself" (v24).** Literally "covering his feet," a polite Hebrew expression. The servants wait "till they were embarrassed," and Ehud's escape depends on their embarrassment. The story is meant to be laughed at: the oppressor's own dignity kept his servants from saving him.
- **"He sounded the trumpet in the hill country of Ephraim" (v27).** The ram's horn (*shofar*) called the tribes to war. "Follow after me, for the LORD has given your enemies… into your hand" (v28). Ehud gives the credit to God.
- **"Seized the fords of the Jordan" (v28).** By holding the river crossings, Israel trapped the Moabite troops on the west side, cutting them off from home. Gideon (7:24) and Jephthah (12:5) use the same tactic. About ten thousand "strong, able-bodied men" (v29) fall. The Hebrew word for "strong" can also mean "fat," which may be one more dig at Eglon's men.
- **"The land had rest for eighty years" (v30).** Twice Othniel's forty. The longest rest in the book.
- **Was Ehud right?** The text calls him a deliverer whom "the LORD raised up" (v15), and the victory is God's (v28). It does not say the Spirit came on him, and it does not comment on his methods. Readers have long noted that this was war against a foreign occupier, not a private murder. Henry is clear that it is not a model for ordinary believers: "No such commissions are now given; to pretend to them is to blaspheme God." Christians are bound instead to be "subject to principalities and powers in all things lawful."
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Ehud turned what others saw as a handicap into the very thing God used, and that the gruesome details read like an eyewitness report. He also points out that the Spirit is not said to come on Ehud. Henry's concise commentary asks, "What message from God but a message of vengeance can a proud rebel expect?" and adds that ministers today bring a message of mercy and salvation first. JFB thinks Ehud's coolness shows "the strength of his confidence that he was doing God service," and says the victory "rescued his country from a state of ignominious vassalage."

#### Shamgar (v31)
- **"Shamgar the son of Anath" (v31).** Anath was a Canaanite goddess of war, and Beth-anath was a Canaanite town (1:33). Shamgar may not have been an Israelite at all, or his family came from such a town, or "son of Anath" was a warrior title. His name is not Hebrew, and the text never calls him a judge, only that "he also saved Israel." He is named again in Deborah's song, in a time when "the highways were abandoned" (5:6).
- **"Killed 600 of the Philistines with an oxgoad" (v31).** An oxgoad was a long wooden pole, perhaps eight feet, with a metal point at one end for driving oxen and a flat blade at the other for scraping a plow. In the hands of a strong man it was a spear. The Philistines later kept Israel from having smiths, so that only Saul and Jonathan had swords (1 Sam 13:19–22). Shamgar used what he had.
- **Where does he fit?** Some Greek manuscripts repeat this verse after Samson's death (16:31), which suggests it was once placed there. In the Hebrew it stands here, as a brief bridge between Ehud and Deborah. JFB notes that "the suffering seems to have been local."
- **Commentators:** Henry's concise commentary says Shamgar had "neither sword nor spear" and took the oxgoad, "the instrument next at hand," because "it is no matter what the weapon is, if God directs and strengthens the arm." Guzik makes the same point, setting Shamgar's goad beside Moses' staff and David's sling, and notes that some scholars see Shamgar as a local warrior rather than a judge in the usual sense. JFB says a strong man with an oxgoad "would do no mean execution."

#### Pointing to Christ
Each of these deliverers was unlikely in some way: a man from an old family, a left-handed Benjaminite with a hidden dagger, an outsider with a farm tool. God's rescues in Judges come through weakness and surprise, which is how he saves in the gospel. Paul says "God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Cor 1:27). The Spirit came on Othniel to save Israel. He came on Jesus "without measure" (John 3:34), and Jesus announced, "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me… to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18). Ehud's victory was won by one man going alone into the enemy's chamber. Christ's victory was won by going alone into death itself, and through it he destroyed "the one who has the power of death" (Heb 2:14).
