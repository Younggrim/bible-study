*Setting:* Soon after Jerusalem's fall in 587 or 586 BC. Gedaliah, the governor Babylon had left over the remnant of Judah, has been murdered at Mizpah by Ishmael, a member of the royal family, along with the Babylonian soldiers stationed there (ch 41). Johanan son of Kareah and the army officers rescued the captives Ishmael was dragging to Ammon. Now the whole group is camped at Geruth Chimham near Bethlehem, on the road south, "intending to go to Egypt" because they fear Babylon's revenge (41:17–18). Jeremiah is with them. For the first time in the book, the leaders and the people come to him of their own accord and ask him to seek the LORD. The chapter shows what happens when people ask for God's guidance after their minds are already made up.

#### Warning Against Going to Egypt (vv1–22)
- **"All the commanders of the forces, and Johanan the son of Kareah and Jezaniah the son of Hoshaiah, and all the people from the least to the greatest" (v1).** The whole remnant comes, high and low. In 43:2 the second leader is called "Azariah the son of Hoshaiah," and the Greek translation reads Azariah here too. He may have gone by both names, or one may be a copyist's slip. A man named Hoshaiah (Hoshayahu) is the writer of one of the Lachish Letters, military notes on pottery from the last days before Jerusalem fell. Some have suggested a link, but the name was common and it cannot be proved.
- **"Let our plea for mercy come before you, and pray to the LORD your God for us" (v2).** The words sound humble. But notice "the LORD *your* God." They speak of him as Jeremiah's God, not theirs. Jeremiah answers with "the LORD *your* God" (v4), reminding them whose God he is, and in v6 they finally say "the LORD our God." **"We are left with but a few, as your eyes see us."** This was exactly the curse of the covenant: "you shall be left few in number" (Deut 28:62).
- **"That the LORD your God may show us the way we should go, and the thing that we should do" (v3).** This is a good prayer, the kind every believer prays. The question is not the words but the heart behind them. Chapter 41 already said they were "intending to go to Egypt" (41:17), and they are already standing on the road there.
- **"I will keep nothing back from you" (v4).** Jeremiah promises to pray and to tell them the whole answer, pleasant or not. Years before, the LORD had told him, "Do not hold back a word" (26:2). He has kept that charge through forty years, and he keeps it now. The prophet Micaiah made a similar vow to King Ahab: "what the LORD says to me, that I will speak" (1 Kgs 22:14).
- **"May the LORD be a true and faithful witness against us if we do not act according to all the word" (v5).** They swear an oath and call God himself to testify against them if they break it. **"Whether it is good or bad, we will obey the voice of the LORD our God" (v6).** "Good or bad" here means welcome or unwelcome, pleasant or hard, not morally good or evil. It is a perfect promise. If they had kept it, the story of chapter 43 would never have happened.
- **"At the end of ten days the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah" (v7).** This is the word the heading in 40:1 has been waiting for. Jeremiah does not answer on the spot or from his own reasoning, though he could easily have guessed what God would say. A prophet could not produce God's word at will. Earlier, when Hananiah broke the yoke, Jeremiah waited until the word came (28:11–12). The ten days also tested the people. They had to wait in fear with Babylon's revenge hanging over them, and every day the pull toward Egypt grew stronger.
- **"Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel, to whom you sent me" (v9).** Jeremiah reminds them that they asked for this. What follows is not his opinion.
- **"If you will remain in this land, then I will build you up and not pull you down; I will plant you, and not pluck you up" (v10).** These are the words of Jeremiah's call. When God first appointed him, he was set "to pluck up and to break down… to build and to plant" (1:10), and the pair returns at key moments (24:6; 31:28). For forty years the plucking up and pulling down came first. Now God offers the building and planting to this tiny remnant, in the ruined land itself.
- **"For I relent of the disaster that I did to you" (v10).** The verb is *nacham*. The lexicon gives "be sorry," "comfort oneself" and "relent," and lists this verse with others where God turns back from harm he has done or threatened (Exod 32:14; Jer 18:8; Jonah 3:10). It does not mean God admits he was wrong. It means that the judgment has done its work and God is ready to turn from it. The ESV's "relent" is better than the KJV's "repent," which sounds to modern ears like confessing a sin.
- **"Do not fear the king of Babylon… for I am with you, to save you and to deliver you from his hand" (v11).** Fear is the real issue in this chapter. "I am with you to deliver you" is the promise God gave Jeremiah himself at his call (1:8, 19). Now God offers it to the whole remnant. Jeremiah has lived it: he was jailed, beaten, dropped in a cistern and finally released by the very Babylonians they fear.
- **"I will grant you mercy, that he may have mercy on you" (v12).** God's mercy will reach them through Nebuchadnezzar's mercy. The king's heart is in the LORD's hand (Prov 21:1). There is no record that Babylon took revenge for Gedaliah's murder on those who stayed. A further deportation in 582 BC (52:30) may or may not have been a reprisal, and scholars differ.
- **"No, we will go to the land of Egypt, where we shall not see war or hear the sound of the trumpet or be hungry for bread" (v14).** God puts their own words in their mouths before they say them. Egypt promised everything Judah had just lost: no war alarms, no siege famine. It was rich, distant and still strong. But to go back to Egypt was to undo the exodus. The law said, "You shall never return that way again" (Deut 17:16), and among the covenant curses was being taken back to Egypt (Deut 28:68). Isaiah had warned, "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help" (Isa 31:1).
- **"If you set your faces to enter Egypt" (v15).** To "set the face" means a fixed resolve, as when Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51). Their minds are made up, and God knows it.
- **"Then the sword that you fear shall overtake you there in the land of Egypt, and the famine of which you are afraid shall follow close after you" (v16).** The very things they run from will run after them. Egypt was not safe. Nebuchadnezzar had beaten Egypt at Carchemish in 605 BC (46:2), and Jeremiah will soon announce that he will strike Egypt itself (43:8–13; 46:13–26). A Babylonian text records a campaign against Egypt in Nebuchadnezzar's 37th year, about 568 BC.
- **"They shall have no remnant or survivor" (v17).** This is about "all the men who set their faces" to go, the willful ones. Later Jeremiah says that a small number will escape and return to Judah (44:14, 28).
- **"As my anger and my wrath were poured out on the inhabitants of Jerusalem, so my wrath will be poured out on you" (v18).** They have just lived through the fall of the city. God says plainly: what happened there will happen to you in Egypt. **"An execration, a horror, a curse, and a taunt."** This is Jeremiah's familiar fourfold phrase for a people whose fate others use as a curse word (24:9; 29:18). **"You shall see this place no more."**
- **"Know for a certainty that I have warned you this day that you have gone astray at the cost of your lives" (vv19–20).** Jeremiah seems to sense their response before they speak. Perhaps he could read it on their faces. The Hebrew in v20 is debated. The verb (*ta'ah*) means "go astray" or "lead astray." Some read "you have led yourselves astray," that is, you deceived yourselves when you sent me. Others read "you have erred at the cost of your lives," as the ESV does. Either way, the point is that their request was not honest. The KJV has "ye dissembled in your hearts."
- **"You have not obeyed the voice of the LORD your God in anything" (v21).** The decision is told before chapter 43 narrates it. Perhaps Jeremiah spoke these last verses after they rejected the word (43:1–4), and the book places them here to finish the message. Or he spoke them as a prophet who knew what was coming. **"You shall die by the sword, by famine, and by pestilence in the place where you desire to go to live" (v22).** The chapter ends on the word "live." They want to go to Egypt to live, and there they will die.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry hears real need in the request, "Pray for us, who know not how to pray for ourselves," but judges their humility largely self-serving. On v7 he says it "teaches us to continue waiting upon God for direction in our way." On v12: "Whatever kindness men do us we must attribute it to God's kindness." On v16: "the evils we think to escape by sin we certainly and inevitably run ourselves upon." He closes: "God's threatenings may be vilified, but cannot be nullified, by the unbelief of man." JFB compares the people to Ahab, who consulted Micaiah only for approval of a plan already made, and sees the ten days as proof that Jeremiah waited on God and did not speak for himself. All three see a decision made before the prayer. Guzik draws out the "your God" wording and passes on F. B. Meyer's warning that people often ask God for guidance after they have decided, hoping he will agree. Guzik's application is that God must be sought with a submitted heart, ready to do whatever he says. The Tyndale notes add that the "I have heard you" of v4 may already hint at Jeremiah's doubts.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Whether it is good or bad, we will obey" (v6) is the right prayer, and the remnant could not keep it. They feared Babylon more than they trusted God's "I am with you." Only one person has ever prayed that prayer and kept it all the way. In Gethsemane, facing a cup far worse than Babylon's sword, Jesus asked for another way and then said, "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). He did not run from the cup but drank it, and so he became the one through whom God now builds and plants a people who cannot be plucked up. Those who are in him hear the same promise Jeremiah gave, now made sure: "I am with you always" (Matt 28:20).
