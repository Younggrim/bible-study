*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. In chapter 27 Moses ordered a ceremony at Mounts Ebal and Gerizim, where Israel would hear the curses of the covenant and answer "Amen." Chapter 28 now sets out the blessings and curses in full. Ancient treaties between a great king and a subject people usually ended this way: the terms, then blessings for keeping them and curses for breaking them. Leviticus 26 has a shorter version of the same thing. This is one of the longest and most sobering chapters in the Law. Fourteen verses of blessing are followed by fifty-four of curses, and the later history of Israel, through the Assyrian and Babylonian exiles and the Roman destruction of Jerusalem, reads like a commentary on it.

#### Blessings for Obedience (vv1–14)
- **"If you faithfully obey the voice of the LORD your God" (v1).** Literally, "if you listen, listening to the voice." The whole chapter hangs on that "if." This is not a way to earn God's choice of Israel, which rested on his love and his oath to the fathers (7:7–8). It is the way a people already redeemed would live in the land and enjoy it. **"High above all the nations"** repeats the promise of 26:19.
- **"All these blessings shall come upon you and overtake you" (v2).** "Overtake" is *nasag*, a word used for pursuers catching up with someone (Gen 31:25; Exod 15:9). Blessings are pictured as chasing God's obedient people down. In v15 and v45 the same verb is used for the curses.
- **Six "blessed" lines (vv3–6).** City and field, children, crops and herds, **"your basket and your kneading bowl"** (the harvest and the daily bread made from it), coming in and going out. Together they cover every place and every part of ordinary life, from the field to the kitchen. These six lines are repeated almost word for word, as curses, in vv16–19.
- **"One way… seven ways" (v7).** An enemy army comes in good order and runs off in a scattered rout. Seven stands for completeness. The same picture is reversed in v25.
- **"Called by the name of the LORD" (v10).** Literally, "the name of the LORD is called over you." To call a name over something was to claim it as one's own (2 Sam 12:28). The nations would look at Israel and see whose people they were.
- **"His good treasury, the heavens, to give the rain to your land in its season" (v12).** Canaan depended on rain, not on a river like Egypt's Nile (11:10–12). The "early" rains of autumn softened the ground for plowing, and the "latter" rains of spring filled out the grain. The Canaanites credited rain to Baal, the storm god. Moses says the LORD holds the storehouse. **"You shall lend to many nations, but you shall not borrow"**: a sign of a prosperous, independent people.
- **"The head and not the tail" (v13).** A leader, not a follower. **"Not turn aside… to the right hand or to the left, to go after other gods"** (v14). The blessings end, as the whole law began, with the first commandment.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, following Harrison, compares the chapter to ancient treaties and says God would make himself known to the nations either way, by blessing or by judgment. He adds that being a holy people was the greatest blessing, since the material gifts meant little without God himself. Henry: "That obedience pleases best which comes from a principle of delight in God's goodness." JFB calls "head, and not the tail" an Eastern way of describing independent power and dignity.

#### Curses for Disobedience (vv15–44)
- **"But if you will not obey" (v15).** Now the other side. The curses are more than three times as long as the blessings. Many think this reflects what Moses expected. He knew this people (31:27–29).
- **The six curses (vv16–19).** These are exact reversals of vv3–6. Each blessing has its mirror image.
- **"Curses, confusion, and frustration" (v20).** Three words, the first two from the same root sounds in Hebrew (*me'erah*, *mehumah*, *mig'ereth*). Confusion (*mehumah*) is the panic God sent on Israel's enemies (7:23). Now it turns on Israel. The cause is stated plainly: **"because you have forsaken me."**
- **Disease and drought (vv21–24).** **"Wasting disease and… fever, inflammation"**: some identify these with tuberculosis and malaria, but the exact illnesses are uncertain. **"The heavens over your head shall be bronze, and the earth under you shall be iron"** (v23): a sky that gives no rain and ground too hard to plow. Remarkably, the same picture appears in an Assyrian vassal treaty of King Esarhaddon (672 BC), which curses a disloyal vassal with ground like iron under a sky like bronze. Moses uses language the ancient world understood. In Elijah's day the sky really did shut for three and a half years (1 Kgs 17:1; Jas 5:17).
- **"Defeated before your enemies… your dead body shall be food for all birds" (vv25–26).** To be left unburied was one of the worst disgraces in the ancient world (Jer 7:33).
- **"The boils of Egypt" (v27).** The sixth plague (Exod 9:8–12). The diseases God put on Israel's oppressors would now fall on Israel. **"Tumors"** is the same word used for the plague on the Philistines when they took the ark (1 Sam 5:6).
- **"Madness and blindness and confusion of mind… you shall grope at noonday" (vv28–29).** Not only bodily disease but a people unable to think straight or see a way out.
- **Futility (vv30–33).** Betrothed but another takes her; a house built but someone else lives in it; a vineyard planted but never enjoyed. These are the exact things that earlier exempted a man from war, so he could enjoy them (20:5–7). Now each will be lost. **"Your sons and your daughters shall be given to another people, while your eyes look on"** (v32).
- **"Driven mad by the sights that your eyes see" (v34).** The grief is too much to bear.
- **"You and your king whom you set over you" (v36).** Israel had no king yet, but Moses had already allowed for one (17:14–20). Both would be carried off to an unknown land. This came true when Assyria took the northern kingdom in 722 BC (2 Kgs 17) and Babylon took King Jehoiachin and later King Zedekiah of Judah (2 Kgs 24–25). **"There you shall serve other gods of wood and stone"**: the gods they wanted would be all they had.
- **"A horror, a proverb, and a byword" (v37).** People would point to Israel as the example of disaster (1 Kgs 9:7; Jer 24:9).
- **Locust, worm, and falling olives (vv38–42).** Hard work with nothing to show for it. Joel's locust plague (Joel 1) and Haggai's warning, "You have sown much, and harvested little" (Hag 1:6), echo this.
- **"The sojourner… shall be the head, and you shall be the tail" (vv43–44).** The exact reversal of v13. The foreigner, once among Israel's most vulnerable, would be above them.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that the curses are far longer than the blessings, as if to impress the warning deeply. He follows Kalland in taking "tumors" (*'ophel*) as a swelling of some kind, possibly hemorrhoids or plague, and the lexicon supports "swelling" without settling the disease. JFB says the curses are the "almost exact counterparts" of the blessings and calls bronze heavens and iron earth strong figures for long drought. He also thinks the knee and leg boils of v35 may be elephantiasis, a guess others share but which cannot be proved.

#### Curses for Disobedience (vv45–68)
- **"A sign and a wonder against you and your offspring forever" (v46).** "Signs and wonders" were the words for the plagues on Egypt (6:22). Now Israel itself would be the sign, showing the nations what it costs to break covenant with God.
- **"Because you did not serve the LORD your God with joyfulness and gladness of heart, because of the abundance of all things" (v47).** This may be the most searching verse in the chapter. The charge is not only disobedience but joyless service in a time of plenty. God's people had everything and still did not delight in him. So **"you shall serve your enemies… in hunger and thirst… lacking everything"** (v48). They would serve someone either way. **"A yoke of iron"**: Jeremiah later acted this out with a wooden yoke, which God replaced with iron (Jer 28:13–14).
- **"A nation… from the end of the earth, swooping down like the eagle, a nation whose language you do not understand" (v49).** In the first place this fits Assyria and Babylon. Their languages, dialects of Akkadian, were foreign to Israel's ears (Isa 28:11; 33:19; Jer 5:15). Habakkuk describes the Babylonians as flying "like an eagle swift to devour" (Hab 1:8). Many older commentators also saw Rome here, whose legions marched under an eagle standard, and the description fits that later disaster just as well.
- **"A hard-faced nation who shall not respect the old or show mercy to the young" (v50).** Literally "strong of face," brazen and pitiless. Assyrian records boast of flaying and impaling defeated enemies.
- **"Until your high and fortified walls, in which you trusted, come down" (v52).** Israel's cities had walls "up to heaven" that once frightened their fathers (1:28). Now Israel's own walls would fail.
- **The siege (vv53–57).** This is the darkest passage in the Law. Hunger in a long siege would be so extreme that parents would eat their own children. **"The most tender and refined"** man and woman (vv54, 56), people who would once not even set a bare foot on the ground, would hide food from their own families. It happened. In the Aramean siege of Samaria, two mothers made a pact to eat their sons (2 Kgs 6:28–29). In the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem, "the hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children" (Lam 4:10; compare 2:20). Josephus records the same horror in the Roman siege of AD 70.
- **"That you may fear this glorious and awesome name, the LORD your God" (v58).** The heart of the matter is not a list of rules but reverence for God himself. **"In this book"** shows Moses already writing these words down (31:9, 24).
- **"Every sickness… that is not recorded in the book" (v61).** The list is not complete. Disobedience would bring more than could be named.
- **"Whereas you were as numerous as the stars of heaven, you shall be left few in number" (v62).** A reversal of the promise to Abraham (Gen 15:5; Deut 10:22).
- **"As the LORD took delight in doing you good… so the LORD will take delight in bringing ruin upon you" (v63).** This is a startling verse. The point is not that God enjoys suffering (Ezek 18:23, 32), but that his justice is as real and as wholehearted as his goodness. He is not reluctant or divided in either. Elsewhere Moses promises that after judgment God will "again take delight in prospering you" (30:9).
- **"The LORD will scatter you among all peoples, from one end of the earth to the other" (v64).** Exile becomes a scattering across the world, the Diaspora. **"No resting place for the sole of your foot… a trembling heart and failing eyes"** (v65). **"In the morning you shall say, 'If only it were evening!'"** (v67). Every hour is dread.
- **"The LORD will bring you back in ships to Egypt… and there you shall offer yourselves for sale… but there will be no buyer" (v68).** The Exodus undone. They would go back to the land of slavery, and be worth so little that no one would buy them. After Jerusalem fell in AD 70, Josephus says Titus sent many captives to forced labor in Egypt and that so many were put on the slave market that buyers were few. After the Bar Kokhba revolt in AD 135, Jewish captives were again sold so cheaply that the market collapsed.
- **Commentators:** Henry on v47: "God is a Master that will be served with gladness, and delights to hear us sing at our work," and "our abundance should be oil to the wheels of our obedience." Henry and JFB both identify the eagle nation with Rome, Henry adding that "the ensign of the Roman armies was an eagle." JFB cites Josephus at length on the Roman war and on captives shipped to Egypt. Guzik also traces the curses to the Roman siege. He adds a sober note: the church's long history of persecuting Jews was a sinful attempt to "help" God curse them, and he points to God's promise that he is not finished with Israel (Ezek 37; Rom 11). Henry on v63: "As the Lord rejoiced over you to do you good, so he will rejoice over you to destroy you."

#### Pointing to Christ
The blessings of this chapter were never fully enjoyed, because Israel, like every people, did not keep "all the words of this law." The curses did come. But the New Testament's answer is that Jesus took the curse into himself. He was "made a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). On the cross the sky went dark at noon (Mark 15:33), as the curse of v29 had warned. He was stripped, scorned, a byword to passersby, and cut off from the land of the living, so that his people could receive "every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places" (Eph 1:3). He wept over Jerusalem because he knew the siege Moses described would come again (Luke 19:41–44; 23:28–29). And v47 shows what God wanted all along: not grudging obedience, but service "with joyfulness and gladness of heart." Christ gives that joy (John 15:11).
