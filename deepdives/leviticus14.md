*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai. Chapter 13 told the priest how to diagnose *tsara'at*, the broad term the ESV translates "leprous disease," covering many skin conditions and even mold. A person declared unclean had to live "outside the camp" (13:46), cut off from worship and community. Chapter 14 answers the question that chapter left hanging: what happens when such a person gets better? It is the law "for the day of his cleansing" (v2), a three-stage ceremony of return. The second half of the chapter looks ahead to life in Canaan, when the same kind of growth might appear in the walls of a house.

#### Laws for Cleansing Lepers (vv1–32)
- **"The priest shall go out of the camp" (v3).** The person cannot come in until declared clean, so the priest goes out to him. The priest does not heal him. He comes to confirm that healing has already happened: **"if the case of leprous disease is healed."** The law assumes that God can and does heal, even though the chapter gives no medicine.
- **"Two live clean birds and cedarwood and scarlet yarn and hyssop" (v4).** The same three items appear in the ceremony of the red heifer, which cleansed people who had touched a dead body (Num 19:6). Hyssop was a small bushy plant used as a sprinkler, the plant dipped in the Passover blood (Exod 12:22). David prays, "Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean" (Ps 51:7), which seems to echo this very rite. Why cedar and scarlet were used is not explained. Many think the red wood and red yarn suggest blood and life, and cedar's resistance to rot suggests lasting health. The Tyndale notes say plainly that their exact meaning is unclear.
- **"Kill one of the birds in an earthenware vessel over fresh water" (v5).** "Fresh water" is literally "living water," meaning water from a spring or stream rather than a cistern. The blood ran into the living water, and the mixture was used for sprinkling.
- **"Sprinkle it seven times… and shall let the living bird go into the open field" (v7).** The person was sprinkled seven times, the number of completeness. Then the living bird, dipped in the blood of the dead one, flew away free. The picture is vivid: one bird dies, and the other, marked with its blood, is released. The Day of Atonement has a similar pair, one goat killed and one sent away into the wilderness carrying the people's sins (16:20–22). In both, uncleanness is carried off, never to come back.
- **"Shave off all his hair and bathe himself" (vv8–9).** The person washes, shaves completely and may now come back into the camp, but must stay **"outside his tent seven days."** On the seventh day he shaves again, every hair, head, beard and eyebrows, and washes again. He comes back like a newborn, with nothing of the old disease left on him.
- **"On the eighth day" (v10).** The third stage, as with the priests' ordination (9:1) and a baby boy's circumcision (12:3), comes on the eighth day, a new beginning. He brings three lambs, flour and **"one log of oil,"** about a third of a quart (roughly 0.3 liters).
- **"Offer it for a guilt offering" (v12).** This is the most surprising part of the ceremony. The guilt offering (*'asham*) was usually brought for a wrong that needed repayment (5:14–6:7). BDB calls it an offering for "offences… that could be estimated and so covered by compensation." Why would a healed person bring one? The Tyndale notes suggest it made up for the tithes and offerings God was not given during the months the person was shut out of worship. Others think it simply marked the person's full return to belonging to God. BDB also points out that Isaiah 53:10 uses this same word for the Servant of the LORD, who makes "an offering for guilt" for others.
- **Blood on ear, thumb and toe (v14).** Blood from the guilt offering went on the right ear, right thumb and right big toe. This exact ritual appears in only one other place in the Bible: the ordination of Aaron and his sons (8:23–24). The highest office in Israel and the most excluded outcast received the same marks. The person being restored is not just tolerated back into the camp. He is consecrated to God, ear, hand and foot.
- **Oil on top of the blood, and on the head (vv15–18).** The priest sprinkled oil seven times "before the LORD" and put more on the same three places, **"on top of the blood."** The rest was poured on the person's head, the way Aaron was anointed (8:12). Oil was used in the ancient world for healing (Isa 1:6; Luke 10:34) and for setting apart. Blood first, then oil: the order is repeated with care.
- **Sin offering, burnt offering, grain offering (vv19–20).** The full set of offerings completes the ceremony. **"Thus the priest shall make atonement for him, and he shall be clean."** The person who was outside the camp now stands again in the worship of Israel.
- **"If he is poor and cannot afford so much" (vv21–32).** The guilt offering lamb cannot be replaced, since its blood is central to the rite, but the other two lambs may be replaced by **"two turtledoves or two pigeons"** and less flour. Note how fully the rite is repeated for the poor man, nearly word for word. He receives exactly the same blood, the same oil and the same restoration. Poverty does not mean a lesser cleansing.
- **Commentators:** All three see the two birds as a picture of Christ. JFB says the slain and released birds "are supposed to typify, the one the death, and the other the resurrection of Christ," and calls the oil "an emblem of healing" and the blood "a token of forgiveness." Henry connects the blood in the water to Christ, who came "not by water only, but by water and blood" (1 John 5:6), and the freed bird to his resurrection. He takes the priest's trip outside the camp as a lesson in welcoming back the repentant warmly rather than with suspicion. Guzik reads the shaving and washing as a picture of a fresh start, like new birth, and notes that the anointing of ear, hand and foot gave the cleansed person something like a priest's calling. He adds that because this ceremony was so rare, a healed leper showing up at the temple would have been a striking testimony, which is why Jesus told healed lepers to go to the priest (Luke 5:14).

#### Laws for Cleansing Houses (vv33–57)
- **"When you come into the land of Canaan" (v34).** Israel is living in tents in the desert, but God speaks as though their homes in the promised land are certain. These rules assume stone houses with plastered walls, which they did not yet have.
- **"And I put a case of leprous disease in a house" (v34).** God speaks of himself as the one who sends it. Some older readers took this as judgment on the owner's sin, as JFB reports. The Tyndale notes read it more simply: Hebrew speech names God as the one behind everything that happens, including things he allows. JFB concludes that "the leprosy was in the house itself," a growth in the walls.
- **"There seems to me to be some case of disease in my house" (v35).** The owner reports it humbly, as something that *seems* to be there. Only the priest can decide.
- **"Empty the house before the priest goes to examine the disease" (v36).** If the house was declared unclean, everything in it would be too. Moving the furniture out first was a small kindness that saved people's belongings.
- **Examining, closing, scraping, rebuilding (vv37–42).** The signs were familiar from chapter 13: **"greenish or reddish spots"** that seemed **"deeper than the surface."** The house was shut up for a week. If the growth had spread, the infected stones were pulled out, the walls scraped, and the debris dumped **"in an unclean place outside the city."** New stones and new plaster replaced them. This is a careful response to spreading mold, rot or mineral growth, damaging to health and to the building.
- **"He shall break down the house" (vv43–47).** If it came back, the whole house was torn down. Anyone who entered while it was shut up became unclean, and anyone who slept or ate there had to wash his clothes.
- **Cleansing a house (vv48–53).** A healed house received the same ceremony as a healed person: two birds, cedar, scarlet yarn, hyssop, living water, sevenfold sprinkling, and a living bird set free. **"So he shall make atonement for the house"** (v53). Here "atonement" clearly means cleansing and purifying, since a house cannot sin.
- **"This is the law for any case of leprous disease" (vv54–57).** A summary closes chapters 13–14, naming all the cases in reverse order: itch, garment, house, swelling, eruption, spot. The purpose is stated one more time: **"to show when it is unclean and when it is clean."**

#### Pointing to Christ
In Leviticus, the priest could go out to the leper, but he could only come after the healing, to confirm it. When Jesus met a man "full of leprosy," he touched him, and "immediately the leprosy left him." Then he sent him to the priest to "make an offering for your cleansing, as Moses commanded, for a proof to them" (Luke 5:12–14). For perhaps the first time in centuries, a priest may have had to carry out this chapter, and the proof pointed to Jesus. The rest of the picture fits too. Like the dead bird and the freed bird, Christ died and rose, and those he cleanses are not merely let back in but consecrated, marked by his blood and anointed by his Spirit to serve God, ear, hand and foot.
