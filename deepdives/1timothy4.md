*Setting:* Paul is writing to Timothy in Ephesus, probably in the early 60s AD. Chapter 3 ended with a hymn to Christ, "the mystery of godliness." Now Paul sets the false teachers' version of godliness beside it: a religion of rules about marriage and food. Then he turns to Timothy himself. Timothy was probably in his thirties, young for a leader in a culture that prized age, and he seems to have been naturally reserved (1 Cor 16:10–11; 2 Tim 1:7). This chapter is Paul's personal charge to him.

#### Some Will Depart from the Faith (vv1–5)
- **"Now the Spirit expressly says" (v1).** Paul may mean a prophecy given through Christian prophets in the churches, or the warnings already in Jesus' teaching (Mark 13:22) and his own (Acts 20:29–30; 2 Thess 2:3). Either way, the trouble in Ephesus was not a surprise. It was foretold.
- **"In later times" (v1).** The New Testament treats the whole period between Christ's first and second comings as "the last days" (Acts 2:17; Heb 1:2; 1 John 2:18). Paul isn't speaking only of the distant future. He is describing Ephesus now.
- **"Depart from the faith" (v1).** The verb is *aphistēmi*, the root of "apostasy." They don't just drift; they turn away from a faith they once professed.
- **"Deceitful spirits and teachings of demons" (v1).** Behind false teaching Paul sees spiritual forces. The teaching didn't come *about* demons; it came *from* them. This doesn't make the teachers less responsible. The next verse puts it on them.
- **"The insincerity of liars whose consciences are seared" (v2).** "Insincerity" is literally "hypocrisy," acting a part. "Seared" is *kaustēriazō*, to brand with a hot iron. It can mean a conscience cauterized until it is numb, or a conscience branded like a slave or criminal, marked as belonging to another master. Either way, it no longer works. Compare the "good conscience" of 1:5, 19.
- **"Who forbid marriage and require abstinence from foods" (v3).** Here is the content of the false teaching. Its roots are probably mixed: Jewish food laws stretched beyond the law, and Greek ideas that the body and the physical world are inferior or evil, ideas that grew into full Gnosticism in the second century. Some may also have thought the resurrection had already come (2 Tim 2:18), so that marriage belonged to the old age (Luke 20:35). Colossians 2:20–23 deals with a similar teaching.
- **"That God created to be received with thanksgiving" (v3).** Paul's answer goes back to Genesis. God made food and marriage and called them good (Gen 1:28–31; 2:18–24; 9:3). Jesus "declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19), and Peter's vision taught the same (Acts 10:15).
- **"Everything created by God is good" (v4).** This echoes the refrain of Genesis 1: "God saw that it was good." Faith doesn't despise God's world. It receives it gratefully.
- **"Made holy by the word of God and prayer" (v5).** Food is set apart for God's use when it is received in line with what God has said about it and with thankful prayer. Jews gave thanks before meals, and Jesus did too (Mark 6:41). The church's old table graces often used words from the Psalms, which may be part of what "the word of God" means here.
- **Commentators:** JFB, Guzik and the Tyndale notes all see the "seared" conscience as a picture of branding. JFB adds that these teachers feel the "brand within" while still putting on a show of holiness. Henry sums up the false teaching neatly: "False teachers forbid as evil what God has allowed, and command as a duty what he has left indifferent." Guzik ties the asceticism to the idea that self-denial puts God in our debt, and stresses that the prayer of v5 is thanks for God's provision, not a ritual. JFB says the creature is "set apart as holy for the use of believing men."

#### A Good Servant of Christ Jesus (vv6–16)
- **"If you put these things before the brothers" (v6).** The verb is gentle: lay it before them, suggest, remind. "Brothers" (*adelphoi*) means the whole church family, men and women. A "good servant" (*diakonos*) is not a celebrity teacher but one who serves.
- **"Being trained in the words of the faith" (v6).** The verb means nourished, brought up on. Timothy feeds others from what has fed him since childhood (2 Tim 3:15).
- **"Irreverent, silly myths" (v7).** Literally "old women's myths," a common put-down in Greek philosophy for stories fit only for gullible people. Paul uses a standard phrase of his day; the point is that these speculations are worthless, not that older women are foolish. The same letter honours older women (5:2).
- **"Train yourself for godliness" (v7).** "Train" is *gymnazō*, from which we get "gymnasium." It originally meant to exercise naked, as Greek athletes did, and then to train body or mind. Ephesus had several large gymnasiums, centres of civic life where young men trained. Paul borrows the picture: the false teachers' rules are a fake workout. The real training is for godliness.
- **"Bodily training is of some value" (v8).** Paul isn't against exercise. He means "of a little value": it helps for a little while, and only in one area of life. Some think he also has in mind the false teachers' bodily disciplines (vv3; Col 2:23). Godliness, by contrast, is useful "in every way," for this life and the next.
- **"The saying is trustworthy" (v9).** The fourth of the five sayings (see 1:15). Scholars disagree whether it points back to v8 or forward to v10. V8 has the balanced, proverb-like sound of such a saying, and most take it that way.
- **"For to this end we toil and strive" (v10).** Two hard words: toil to the point of weariness, and struggle like an athlete in a contest (*agōnizomai*, from which "agony"). Some manuscripts read "suffer reproach" instead of "strive." Godliness is not easy, but the hope is worth it.
- **"Who is the Savior of all people, especially of those who believe" (v10).** A verse read in several ways. (1) God is Savior of all in the sense that he preserves and provides for every person (Ps 36:6; Acts 17:25), and Savior in the full, eternal sense of those who believe. (2) Christ's death is enough for all and is offered to all, but it saves only those who believe. (3) A few scholars argue that "especially" here means "that is," so that God is Savior of all people, "namely" believers. Universalists sometimes cite the verse, but "especially of those who believe" would make no sense if all were saved alike.
- **"Let no one despise you for your youth" (v12).** "Youth" (*neotēs*) could describe a man up to about forty. Timothy had joined Paul about thirteen years earlier (Acts 16:1–3), so he was perhaps in his thirties, younger than many of the elders he had to correct (5:1, 19–20). Paul doesn't tell him to demand respect but to earn it.
- **"In speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity" (v12).** Five areas: what he says, how he lives, how he treats people, how he trusts God, and his sexual and moral integrity. The KJV adds "in spirit," found in later manuscripts. The false teachers failed in every one of these.
- **"The public reading of Scripture, to exhortation, to teaching" (v13).** These three were the pattern of the synagogue service, which Jesus and Paul both took part in (Luke 4:16–21; Acts 13:15): read the Scriptures aloud, urge the people to respond, explain what it means. Most people couldn't afford their own copies, so the public reading was how they heard God's word. The early church kept this pattern, adding the apostles' letters (Col 4:16).
- **"Do not neglect the gift you have" (v14).** "Gift" is *charisma*, a gift of grace. Timothy had received a particular gifting for his ministry. Elsewhere Paul tells him to "fan into flame" this gift (2 Tim 1:6). Gifts can lie unused.
- **"Given you by prophecy when the council of elders laid their hands on you" (v14).** "Council of elders" is *presbyterion*, the word for the Jewish elders' council in Luke 22:66, and the root of "Presbyterian." 2 Timothy 1:6 says it was through "the laying on of my hands," so Paul and the elders together commissioned him, much as the church at Antioch sent out Barnabas and Saul (Acts 13:1–3). Laying on hands showed recognition, blessing and the passing on of a task. The prophecies are the ones Paul mentioned in 1:18.
- **"Practice these things, immerse yourself in them" (v15).** "Practice" can mean to take care of, or to think carefully about (the KJV has "meditate"). "Immerse yourself" is literally "be in them": live inside these duties.
- **"So that all may see your progress" (v15).** "Progress" (*prokopē*) was used for advance on a journey or in learning. Paul doesn't expect Timothy to be finished. He expects him to be growing, visibly. That will answer those who look down on his youth.
- **"Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching" (v16).** Life and doctrine together. A teacher who guards his doctrine but not his life, or his life but not his doctrine, will fail. Paul told the Ephesian elders the same thing years before: "Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock" (Acts 20:28).
- **"You will save both yourself and your hearers" (v16).** Paul doesn't mean that Timothy earns salvation. God saves (v10). But perseverance in the faith is the path along which God brings his people to final salvation (Phil 2:12–13), and faithful teaching is the means by which others come to faith and keep it (Rom 10:14).
- **Commentators:** On v7 JFB calls the myths "anile," the old word for old-womanish, and says Timothy should train toward godliness, not toward the false teachers' ascetic exercises. Henry: "Outward acts of self-denial profit little," and mortifying the body is pointless unless sin is put to death. On v10 JFB says God is potentially the Saviour of all, "of believers alone effectually," much as Guzik does, and Guzik rules out universalism. Henry draws comfort from the verse: "though we lose for Christ, we shall not lose by him." On v12 Guzik estimates Timothy was about thirty, and notes that the word for youth covered men up to about forty. Henry: "Those who teach by their doctrine, must teach by their life." On v14 JFB says the elders joined Paul in the laying on of hands and that a gift can be neglected "by letting it lie unused." On v16 JFB observes the Greek means "in doing this," not "by doing this."

#### Pointing to Christ
The false teachers offered a godliness of things to refuse. Paul offers one of a person to follow. The "mystery of godliness" (3:16) is Christ himself, and godliness means growing into his likeness. He received God's gifts with thanks, blessed bread and broke it, went to weddings and ate with sinners, and set an example in word, conduct, love, faith and purity. He is also "the Savior of all people," the living God in whom the church hopes (v10). Timothy's training, and every believer's, is not a set of exercises to earn God's favour. It is learning to live in the life of the one who has already saved.
