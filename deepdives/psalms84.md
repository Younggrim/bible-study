*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to The Gittith. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah." With Psalm 84 the Asaph collection ends and a second group of psalms by the Sons of Korah begins (84–85; 87–88), following the first group in 42–49. Korah was the Levite who led a rebellion against Moses and was swallowed by the earth, "but the sons of Korah did not die" (Num 16:31–33; 26:11). His descendants became temple gatekeepers and singers (1 Chr 9:19; 26:1; 2 Chr 20:19), a living picture of grace: the family of a rebel now leading God's people in worship. Psalm 84 is a pilgrim song, full of longing for the temple and joy in the journey there. It is close in spirit to Psalms 42–43, also Korahite, where the singer thirsts for God far from Jerusalem. Henry suggests David wrote or inspired it when Absalom's rebellion drove him from the city (2 Sam 15), and the prayer for "your anointed" (v9) fits a king. Others see it simply as the song of a worshiper, perhaps a Levite away from his duty, longing to go up to the feasts. Spurgeon called it "the pearl of psalms." It has three stanzas, each ending in a "blessed" (vv4, 5, 12).

#### How Lovely Is Your Dwelling Place (vv1–4)
- **"How lovely is your dwelling place, O LORD of hosts!" (v1).** "Lovely" (*yedidot*) means beloved, dear. It is related to the name *Jedidiah*, "beloved of the LORD," given to Solomon (2 Sam 12:25). The temple is not admired for its architecture but loved as the place where God chose to dwell. "Dwelling place" is plural in Hebrew, perhaps meaning the whole temple complex with its courts. "LORD of hosts," the commander of heaven's armies, appears four times in this short psalm (vv1, 3, 8, 12).
- **"My soul longs, yes, faints for the courts of the LORD" (v2).** "Longs" is a strong word for yearning, even to the point of exhaustion. "Faints" means to be spent, worn out with desire. The "courts" were the open spaces around the temple building where ordinary worshipers stood. Only priests entered the building itself.
- **"My heart and flesh sing for joy to the living God" (v2).** The whole person, inside and out, is involved. The goal is not a building but "the living God," a phrase that sets Israel's God against lifeless idols (42:2; Josh 3:10). The Tyndale notes stress that the temple stands for God himself: the psalmist really longs for God's presence.
- **"Even the sparrow finds a home, and the swallow a nest for herself… at your altars" (v3).** Small birds nested in the eaves and crevices of the temple buildings. The psalmist, far away, envies them. The sparrow is the most ordinary of birds, and Jesus said two of them were sold for a penny (Matt 10:29). The swallow is restless, always on the wing, yet here it settles. "Altars" means the area around the altar of burnt offering and the altar of incense. JFB: "thine altars—that is, of burnt offering and incense, used for the whole tabernacle." Birds did not literally nest on the altars, where fires burned, but in the courts around them.
- **"My King and my God" (v3).** The language becomes personal. The commander of heaven's armies is "my" King.
- **"Blessed are those who dwell in your house, ever singing your praise!" (v4).** This is the first "blessed" (*'ashrei*, "how happy"). Those who "dwell" there are the priests and Levites who served at the temple and lived in its rooms (1 Chr 9:27, 33). The Korahites were among them. **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** Henry: "He would rather live in a bird's nest nigh God's altars than in a palace at a distance from them." He adds, "It is better to be serving God in solitude than serving sin with a multitude." Guzik, following Boice, sees the sparrow and swallow as pictures of the insignificant and the restless, both finding a home near God. He warns that church gatherings should center on meeting God, not on programs or entertainment.

#### Highways to Zion (vv5–7)
- **"Blessed are those whose strength is in you" (v5).** The second "blessed." This one is not for those who live in the temple but for those on the way to it. Their strength is not their own. The pilgrimage was demanding: days on foot over rough roads, in heat or rain, sometimes facing robbers (Luke 10:30). The Tyndale notes describe these hardships.
- **"In whose heart are the highways to Zion" (v5).** "Highways" (*mesillot*) are raised roads, the pilgrim routes up to Jerusalem. The phrase means their hearts are set on the journey. Long before they set out, their minds are already on the road. Psalms 120–134, "Songs of Ascents," were probably sung along these roads.
- **"As they go through the Valley of Baca they make it a place of springs" (v6).** The meaning of "Baca" is debated. It may come from the word for a balsam or poplar tree, a shrub that grows in dry places (2 Sam 5:23–24), so a dry, barren valley on the way to Jerusalem. Or it may be a play on *bakah*, "to weep," so "the Valley of Weeping" (KJV margin; the Greek Old Testament has "valley of weeping"). No such valley has been identified. Many take it as both: a dry and sorrowful stretch of the road. The Tyndale notes take it mainly as the pilgrim's anguish (23:4). Faith-filled pilgrims turn it into a place of springs. The hard place becomes a place of blessing because they pass through it on the way to God.
- **"The early rain also covers it with pools" (v6).** The "early rain" (*moreh*) is the autumn rain that ended the long dry summer. It came around the Feast of Booths, when pilgrims were on the road. The same Hebrew word can mean "teacher" (Joel 2:23 may play on this). Heaven adds its part to what the pilgrims dig. The prophets use the same picture for the return from exile: "I will make the wilderness a pool of water" (Isa 41:18; 35:6–7).
- **"They go from strength to strength" (v7).** Ordinary travelers grow weaker the farther they go. These pilgrims grow stronger as they draw near Zion. Isaiah says those who wait for the LORD "shall renew their strength" (Isa 40:31). Paul says the inner self "is being renewed day by day" (2 Cor 4:16).
- **"Each one appears before God in Zion" (v7).** The law commanded every Israelite man to appear before the LORD three times a year, at Passover, Weeks and Booths (Exod 23:14–17; Deut 16:16). "Each one" stresses that none is lost on the way.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Their way lay through many a weeping valley, so Baca signifies," and of v7, "Even where they are weak, there they are strong." JFB explains Baca as a valley that worshipers often had to pass through, dry and barren. Guzik notes both meanings of Baca, weeping and drought, and says that longing for God's presence does not take believers out of the world but prepares them to live in it.

#### Look on the Face of Your Anointed (vv8–9)
- **"O LORD God of hosts, hear my prayer; give ear, O God of Jacob!" (v8).** The pilgrim, now in Zion or still on the road, prays. "God of Jacob" recalls the God who met Jacob, a wanderer, and kept his promise to bring him home (Gen 28:15). **Selah.** JFB has no note on this verse.
- **"Behold our shield, O God; look on the face of your anointed!" (v9).** The "anointed" is the king, God's chosen ruler (2:2; 18:50). Kings and priests were anointed with oil when set apart for service (1 Sam 16:13; Exod 28:41). The "shield" is probably also the king, who defends his people (89:18, "our shield belongs to the LORD, our king to the Holy One of Israel"). The worshiper prays for the king as part of worship, because the welfare of God's people was bound up with David's line. JFB: "thine anointed—David (1Sa 16:12)." If Henry is right that the psalm comes from David's exile, the prayer is for himself.
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes the anointed as David or Solomon first, and ultimately the Messiah. The Tyndale notes point out that "anointed" applies to both kings and priests, and link it with Jesus, whom "God anointed… with the Holy Spirit and with power" (Acts 10:38).

#### Better Is One Day (vv10–12)
- **"For a day in your courts is better than a thousand elsewhere" (v10).** One day with God outweighs nearly three years anywhere else. Henry: "A day in thy courts, a sabbath day, a holy day, a feast-day, though but one day, would be very welcome to me." Paul says something similar about knowing Christ: "I count everything as loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord" (Phil 3:8), a link Guzik makes.
- **"I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness" (v10).** The verb is unusual. It may mean to stand at the threshold, or to sit at it, as a beggar or a humble servant would. JFB: "I choose to sit on the threshold," the lowest place. There is a beautiful irony here. The Korahites actually were the temple's doorkeepers (1 Chr 9:19; 26:1). The family of the man who wanted the priesthood for himself (Num 16:10) now counts the doorstep of God's house better than any tent of the wicked. "Tents" may recall the tents of Korah's rebellion (Num 16:26, "depart… from the tents of these wicked men").
- **"For the LORD God is a sun and shield" (v11).** This is the only place in the Old Testament where God is directly called a sun. Many neighboring peoples worshiped the sun as a god, and the Tyndale notes point out that the true God provides all the sun could only pretend to. The sun gives light, warmth, life and guidance. The shield gives protection. JFB: "As a sun God enlightens (Ps 27:1); as a shield He protects." Malachi later promises that "the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings" (Mal 4:2).
- **"The LORD bestows favor and honor" (v11).** The KJV has "grace and glory." "Favor" (*chen*) is grace, undeserved kindness. "Honor" (*kavod*) is glory, weight and splendor. God gives both, now and later. Henry: "grace is glory begun, and is an earnest of it."
- **"No good thing does he withhold from those who walk uprightly" (v11).** "Uprightly" means with integrity, wholeheartedly. This is not a promise that God's people will get whatever they want, but that God will not keep back anything truly good for them (34:10; Rom 8:32, "how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?"). Henry: "no good thing will be withheld from those that walk uprightly."
- **"O LORD of hosts, blessed is the one who trusts in you!" (v12).** The third "blessed" brings the psalm home. Not only those who dwell in God's house (v4), not only pilgrims on the way (v5), but anyone, anywhere, who trusts him, even someone far from the temple, is truly happy. This is the deepest blessing of the three, open to all.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "I would rather be a porter in God's house than a prince in those tents where wickedness reigns," and on v11, "We are here in darkness, but, if God be our God, he will be to us a sun, to enlighten and enliven us." Guzik notes that under the new covenant God's good things come through Jesus' goodness, received by grace, and that walking uprightly follows rather than earns them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 84 longs for the place where God dwells. Jesus is that place. John says the Word "became flesh and dwelt among us," literally "pitched his tent" among us (John 1:14), and Jesus called his own body the temple (John 2:19–21). In him the longing of v2 for "the living God" is met: "whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). He is the anointed one whose face God looks on with favor (v9; Matt 3:17), and through him believers have access not just to the courts but into the holiest place (Heb 10:19–22). He is the sun of righteousness and the shield of his people. And because of him, "blessed is the one who trusts in you" is true for anyone anywhere, since "the hour is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father" (John 4:21–23). The pilgrim road now leads to Christ, and at its end is the city where "the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" are its temple (Rev 21:22).
