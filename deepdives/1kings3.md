*Setting:* Early in Solomon's reign, about 970–967 BC. David has died, and Solomon has removed the men who threatened his throne (ch 2). Now the story turns from how he got the throne to what kind of king he will be. This chapter is the high point of Solomon's spiritual life, and it is written with care. It opens with two quiet warning notes (a foreign marriage and worship at the high places) before the famous dream at Gibeon, where God offers Solomon anything he asks. It closes with the story of two mothers and one baby, which shows his new wisdom at work. Second Chronicles 1 tells the Gibeon story too, adding that the whole assembly went with him. The book will later show Solomon's slide away from God (ch 11), and the seeds of it are already visible here, side by side with real love for the LORD.

#### Solomon's Prayer for Wisdom (vv1–15)
- **"Solomon made a marriage alliance with Pharaoh king of Egypt" (v1).** "Pharaoh" was a title ("great house"), not a name. Most scholars think this was Siamun of Egypt's weak Twenty-first Dynasty. That an Egyptian king gave his daughter to a foreign king was unusual. Egypt normally took brides, not gave them, so it shows how strong Israel had become. Pharaoh later gave her the city of Gezer as a dowry (9:16). Political marriages sealed treaties, and Solomon would make many of them (11:1–3). The law warned kings not to "acquire many wives… lest his heart turn away" and not to lead the people back to Egypt (Deut 17:16–17). This first one is not condemned here. But the verse that opens Solomon's story quietly links him to the place God brought Israel out of.
- **"Brought her into the city of David until he had finished building" (v1).** The verse looks ahead to his three great building projects: his palace, the temple, and the city wall. She later got her own palace (7:8; 9:24). Chronicles adds that Solomon moved her because the places where the ark had been were holy (2 Chr 8:11).
- **"The people were sacrificing at the high places, however, because no house had yet been built" (v2).** "High places" (*bamot*) were open-air worship sites, usually on hilltops, with an altar. Before the temple, Israelites worshiped the LORD at several such places (1 Sam 9:12–14). But the Canaanites used the same kinds of sites for their gods, and Deuteronomy 12 looked forward to one central place God would choose. Kings will use "the high places" as a running measure of every king's faithfulness (15:14; 22:43; 2 Kgs 18:4).
- **"Solomon loved the LORD… only he sacrificed and made offerings at the high places" (v3).** This is the only time the Bible says Solomon "loved the LORD," and it is strong praise. He walked in "the statutes of David his father." But there is an "only." JFB argues the word is not a criticism, since there was no temple yet. Guzik reads it as a small crack in an otherwise good report. The writer may be doing both: excusing it for the time, while noting a habit that would grow.
- **"The king went to Gibeon to sacrifice there, for that was the great high place" (v4).** Gibeon was about six miles northwest of Jerusalem. It was the town whose people tricked Joshua into a treaty (Josh 9). Chronicles explains why it was "great": the tabernacle Moses made and its bronze altar stood there, while the ark was in a separate tent in Jerusalem (1 Chr 16:39–40; 2 Chr 1:3–6). So Solomon went to the most legitimate place of worship available.
- **"Solomon used to offer a thousand burnt offerings on that altar" (v4).** A burnt offering was burned whole, all of it given to God, a sign of total devotion (Lev 1). A thousand shows royal wealth and generosity, offered perhaps over several days of festival, as JFB suggests.
- **"At Gibeon the LORD appeared to Solomon in a dream by night, and God said, 'Ask what I shall give you'" (v5).** God often spoke through dreams in the Old Testament (Gen 28:12; 37:5; Num 12:6). The offer is astonishing, like a blank check. What a person asks for shows what he values most. Guzik compares Jesus' "Ask, and it will be given to you" (Matt 7:7).
- **"You have shown great and steadfast love to your servant David my father" (v6).** Solomon begins with God's past kindness. "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) appears twice: God's covenant love, kept for David and passed to his son (2 Sam 7:15). Solomon sees his own throne as a gift of God's faithfulness, not his own achievement.
- **"I am but a little child. I do not know how to go out or come in" (v7).** Solomon was a young adult, not a child. JFB says probably at least twenty. The word for child (*na'ar*) can mean a young man or a servant, an assistant still learning the job (the Tyndale notes compare Elisha as Elijah's helper, 19:21). "Going out and coming in" was a phrase for leading, especially in war and public life (Num 27:17; 1 Sam 18:16). He means: I don't know how to do this job. That honest humility is the key that unlocks the gift.
- **"Your people whom you have chosen, a great people, too many to be numbered" (v8).** An echo of God's promise to Abraham (Gen 13:16; 15:5). Solomon sees Israel as God's people, not his own possession.
- **"Give your servant therefore an understanding mind to govern your people, that I may discern between good and evil" (v9).** "Understanding mind" is literally "a hearing heart" (*lev shomea*). In Hebrew the "heart" is the center of thinking and choosing, not just feeling. A "hearing" heart is one that listens, to God and to the people who come before him for judgment. "Govern" is literally "judge" (*shaphat*), since the king was the highest court. "Discern between good and evil" means telling right from wrong in hard cases (2 Sam 14:17). He asks for the one thing the job most needs.
- **"It pleased the Lord that Solomon had asked this" (v10).** God names what Solomon did *not* ask: "long life or riches or the life of your enemies" (v11). After ch 2, the last item is pointed. Solomon has just dealt with his enemies, and he does not ask for more.
- **"I give you a wise and discerning mind, so that none like you has been before you" (v12).** God gives what was asked and more. Chapter 4 shows Solomon's wisdom ranging over proverbs, songs, plants and animals (4:29–34), and the queen of Sheba comes to test it (ch 10). The books of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and Song of Songs are associated with his name.
- **"I give you also what you have not asked, both riches and honor" (v13).** This is the pattern Jesus taught: "seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (Matt 6:33). Solomon's riches would become legendary (10:14–29), and in the end a snare.
- **"If you will walk in my ways… then I will lengthen your days" (v14).** Wisdom, riches and honor are promised without condition, but long life has a condition attached. Solomon reigned forty years (11:42), but probably died around sixty, not old by the standards of David's line, and his heart had turned by then (11:4).
- **"Solomon awoke, and behold, it was a dream" (v15).** Not "only a dream." It was a real meeting with God in a dream. JFB adds that the dream reflected the desire already in Solomon's heart, but God's answer was real. Solomon goes straight back to Jerusalem and worships before the ark, the true center of God's presence, with burnt offerings and peace offerings. A peace offering (*shelamim*) was partly eaten by the worshipers in a shared meal, so the feast for his servants was part of the worship. The Tyndale notes show the story is shaped in a mirror pattern: sacrifice and dream (vv4–5), Solomon's request (vv6–9), God's answer (vv10–14), awaking and sacrifice (v15).
- **Commentators:** Henry: Solomon looks very great here, "both at the altar and on the bench, and therefore on the bench because at the altar," his wisdom in judging flowed from his worship. JFB says Pharaoh's daughter is "nowhere censured in Scripture," suggests she may have given up her idols, and reads "only" in v3 as no criticism. Guzik is less sure on both counts. He sees the Egyptian marriage as politically shrewd but spiritually risky, the first step toward 11:1–4, while noting that David had foreign wives without the same ruin. Guzik takes the "hearing heart" as a heart that listens to God, not mere head knowledge. JFB says the wisdom asked for was "not so much of the heart as of the head," the practical skill to govern. Both are true to the Hebrew: the heart here includes the mind.

#### Solomon's Wisdom (vv16–28)
- **"Two prostitutes came to the king and stood before him" (v16).** Prostitution was forbidden by the law (Lev 19:29; Deut 23:17–18), and these women were near the bottom of society. There were no men to speak for them, no family, no witnesses. Yet they could come straight to the king, and he heard them. The Tyndale notes stress that he treated them as mothers. Guzik notes Wiseman's suggestion that the word might mean innkeepers, but the Hebrew *zonah* normally means prostitute, and the ESV is right to translate it that way.
- **"There was no one else with us in the house" (v18).** The case is impossible by normal means. It is one woman's word against another's, with no evidence and no witnesses. That is why it is in the Bible. It is the hardest kind of case, exactly what Solomon asked wisdom to judge.
- **"This woman's son died in the night, because she lay on him" (v19).** Sleeping beside a newborn on a shared mat was ordinary, and smothering by accident was a real danger. The first woman's story is detailed and believable. She noticed in daylight that the dead child "was not the child that I had borne" (v21). A mother knows her child.
- **"No, the living child is mine" (v22).** The two women argue back and forth, and the text repeats their words, so the reader feels the deadlock. Solomon sums up their claims exactly (v23), showing he has listened. That is the "hearing heart" at work.
- **"Bring me a sword… Divide the living child in two" (vv24–25).** The command sounds horrifying. But it is a test. Solomon knows he cannot find the facts, so he finds the heart. JFB: kings in the East often appealed "to the principles of human nature when they are at a loss otherwise."
- **"Because her heart yearned for her son" (v26).** "Yearned" is literally "her compassion grew warm." The word for compassion (*rachamim*) is related to *rechem*, "womb," the love of a mother for the child she carried. The true mother would rather lose her son than see him die. The other woman's words expose her: "He shall be neither mine nor yours; divide him." She was driven by jealousy, not love.
- **"Give the living child to the first woman… she is his mother" (v27).** Solomon's verdict repeats her own words, "by no means put him to death." The mother who would give up her rights to save her child is given the child.
- **"They stood in awe of the king, because they perceived that the wisdom of God was in him to do justice" (v28).** The point is not that Solomon was clever. It is that God's wisdom was in him, as God had promised. "To do justice" (*mishpat*) is the goal of all wisdom in a king (Ps 72:1–4; Prov 29:4). Justice for two despised women was the proof.
- **Commentators:** JFB presents the judgment as a test of "natural feelings." Guzik draws the lesson that God's judgments sometimes look foolish at first, but in time their wisdom is shown, and adds that the people's awe was well placed because they saw God behind the king. The Tyndale notes observe that ancient kings often boasted of their wisdom and justice, but Solomon's was shown, not just claimed, and the rest of chs 4–10 will display it in every area of his rule.

#### Pointing to Christ
Solomon asked for a hearing heart to judge God's people with justice, and God gave it. Isaiah foresaw a greater Son of David on whom "the Spirit of wisdom and understanding" would rest, who would "not judge by what his eyes see… but with righteousness he shall judge the poor" (Isa 11:2–4). Jesus said of himself, "something greater than Solomon is here" (Matt 12:42). Like Solomon he welcomed outcasts, and prostitutes heard him gladly (Matt 21:31). He is the wisdom of God (1 Cor 1:24, 30). And where Solomon saved a child by threatening the sword, Jesus went further: like the true mother who gave up what was hers to save a life, he gave himself up so that others might live. God also makes Solomon's offer to believers in Christ: "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him" (Jas 1:5).
