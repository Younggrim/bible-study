*Setting:* Jerusalem, about 734–732 BC, soon after Isaiah's meeting with Ahaz in chapter 7. Syria and Israel are still threatening Judah, and Ahaz has sent his tribute to Assyria and asked Tiglath-pileser for help (2 Kgs 16:7–9). Many in Jerusalem were in a panic, and some apparently sympathized with the anti-Assyrian alliance. Chapter 8 continues the Immanuel message with a second sign child, Isaiah's own son with a name like a battle cry, and then shows Isaiah and his small band of disciples standing apart from a frightened nation. It ends in darkness, which is exactly where the great light of 9:1–7 will break in.

#### The Coming Assyrian Invasion (vv1–10)
- **"Take a large tablet and write on it in common characters" (v1).** The tablet was probably a large writing surface, wood or stone, set up in public like a placard. "In common characters" is literally "with a man's stylus," ordinary letters anyone could read, not fancy scribal script. The message was meant for everyone.
- **"Belonging to Maher-shalal-hash-baz" (v1).** The name means "the spoil speeds, the plunder hastens," or "quick to the plunder, swift to the spoil." It is the longest personal name in the Bible. *Maher* ("hurry") and *chush* ("hasten") are the same words the mockers used when they dared God to "be quick" and "speed his work" (5:19). God takes them at their word.
- **"I will get reliable witnesses, Uriah the priest and Zechariah" (v2).** The prophecy is written and witnessed *before* the child is even conceived, so that no one could later claim it was made up after the fact. Uriah is likely the same priest who later built a copy of a pagan altar from Damascus for Ahaz (2 Kgs 16:10–16). Isaiah chose a witness who was no friend of his message, which makes the testimony stronger. Zechariah may be the father of Ahaz's wife Abi, mother of Hezekiah (2 Kgs 18:2), though that is only a possibility.
- **"I went to the prophetess" (v3).** Isaiah's wife is called "the prophetess." She may have had a prophetic gift of her own, like Miriam, Deborah and Huldah (Exod 15:20; Judg 4:4; 2 Kgs 22:14), or the title may simply mean "the prophet's wife." The words "conceived and bore a son… call his name" echo 7:14 closely, and some think this child was the near sign of Immanuel. Others note the obvious difference: his name is not Immanuel, and he is not born of a maiden in the sense of 7:14.
- **"Before the boy knows how to cry 'My father' or 'My mother'" (v4).** Within a year or two, before he can say his first words, Damascus and Samaria will be plundered. Tiglath-pileser took Damascus in 732 BC and stripped Israel of Galilee and the land east of the Jordan, carrying its people into exile (2 Kgs 15:29; 16:9). The prophecy came true right on schedule.
- **"This people has refused the waters of Shiloah that flow gently" (v6).** Shiloah (later Siloam) was the channel that carried water from the Gihon spring along the side of the city of David. It was small and quiet but it never failed. It stands for God's quiet care of Jerusalem and his promise to David's line. "This people," again not "my people," preferred the roaring power of Assyria. Many in Judah also "rejoice over Rezin and the son of Remaliah," apparently welcoming the alliance against Ahaz.
- **"The waters of the River, mighty and many, the king of Assyria and all his glory" (v7).** "The River" is the Euphrates, which flooded every spring when the snows of the Armenian mountains melted. Refuse the gentle stream, and you will get the flood.
- **"It will sweep on into Judah… reaching even to the neck" (v8).** Assyria will not stop at Syria and Israel. It will pour on into Judah and nearly drown it, up to the neck, but the head will stay above water. That is exactly what happened in 701 BC: Sennacherib overran Judah's cities, but Jerusalem itself survived (2 Kgs 18:13; 19:32–36).
- **"Its outspread wings will fill the breadth of your land, O Immanuel" (v8).** The image switches from a flood to a giant bird of prey spreading its wings over the land. Then the shocking address: "your land, O Immanuel." The land does not belong to Ahaz or even to Judah. It belongs to the child of 7:14. That is why it cannot finally be destroyed.
- **"Be broken, you peoples, and be shattered" (v9).** Isaiah turns and taunts the nations. Let them arm and scheme, "strap on your armor," repeated twice for scorn. They will be shattered.
- **"Take counsel together, but it will come to nothing… for God is with us" (v10).** The last words are *immanu El*, "God is with us," the name of the child. The name is now a war cry of faith. Psalm 46 sings the same: "The nations rage… The LORD of hosts is with us" (Ps 46:6–7).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the writing was "in ordinary characters which the humblest can read," and that the witnesses were there to show the prophecy was written before the event. JFB reads Shiloah as the "mild, though now weak, sway of the house of David," and says the neck-deep flood spared the head, Jerusalem. On "your land, O Immanuel," JFB says, "in the full sense this is applicable only to Messiah." Guzik quotes Calvin that small, gentle waters with God's blessing are better than the great rivers of the nations, and notes that Judah was only just spared while Israel was overrun. Matthew Henry says making the title of Isaiah's book the name of his child was a way of fixing the message in people's minds.

#### Fear God, Wait for the Lord (vv11–22)
- **"The LORD spoke thus to me with his strong hand upon me" (v11).** "Strong hand" may mean the overwhelming pressure of God's word on the prophet (Ezek 3:14). God warns Isaiah "not to walk in the way of this people." The prophet and his disciples must think differently from the crowd.
- **"Do not call conspiracy all that this people calls conspiracy" (v12).** "Conspiracy" (*qesher*) means a plot or treasonous alliance. In a panicking city, everyone saw conspiracies: the Syria–Israel pact, plots inside Jerusalem, perhaps even Isaiah's refusal to back the Assyrian deal. God tells Isaiah not to get swept up in the fear.
- **"But the LORD of hosts, him you shall honor as holy. Let him be your fear" (v13).** The cure for fear is a greater fear. To "honor as holy" is to treat God as who he is, the Holy One of chapter 6. Whoever fears God rightly does not need to fear anything else. Proverbs: "The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is safe" (Prov 29:25).
- **Peter quotes vv12–13.** Writing to believers facing hostility, Peter says, "Have no fear of them, nor be troubled, but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy" (1 Pet 3:14–15). Where Isaiah wrote "the LORD of hosts," Peter writes "Christ." The early church did not hesitate to put Jesus in the place of Israel's God.
- **"He will become a sanctuary and a stone of offense and a rock of stumbling" (v14).** The same God is two things to two kinds of people. To those who fear him, he is a "sanctuary," a holy place of refuge, like the temple's altar where a fugitive could cling for safety (1 Kgs 1:50). To those who will not trust him, he is a rock in the road that trips them up and a trap that snaps shut. "Both houses of Israel" means north and south, Israel and Judah together.
- **"Many shall stumble on it" (v15).** Five verbs pile up: stumble, fall, be broken, be snared, be taken. The New Testament applies this stone to Jesus. Simeon said the baby Jesus was "appointed for the fall and rising of many in Israel" (Luke 2:34). Paul combines this verse with 28:16 to explain why many in Israel stumbled over Christ (Rom 9:32–33), and Peter does the same (1 Pet 2:6–8). Jesus himself said, "the one who falls on this stone will be broken to pieces" (Matt 21:44).
- **"Bind up the testimony; seal the teaching among my disciples" (v16).** Since the nation will not listen, Isaiah's message is to be tied up and sealed like a legal document and entrusted to his "disciples," the faithful few who did believe. This is probably one way the book of Isaiah came to be preserved. "Teaching" is *torah*, the same word as in 2:3.
- **"I will wait for the LORD, who is hiding his face from the house of Jacob, and I will hope in him" (v17).** God hiding his face means withdrawing his favor in judgment (Deut 31:17–18). Isaiah does not pretend things are fine. But he chooses to wait. To "wait" for the LORD is not passive. It is to keep looking to him in hope while he seems silent, the same verb as "they who wait for the LORD shall renew their strength" (40:31).
- **"Behold, I and the children whom the LORD has given me are signs and portents in Israel" (v18).** Isaiah's family is a walking sermon. Isaiah's name means "the LORD is salvation." Shear-jashub means "a remnant shall return." Maher-shalal-hash-baz means "quick to the plunder." Together the names announce judgment, survival and salvation.
- **Hebrews quotes vv17–18 of Jesus.** "I will put my trust in him," and, "Behold, I and the children God has given me" (Heb 2:13). The writer uses these words to show that Jesus shares our human nature and calls believers his brothers and sisters. Like Isaiah, Jesus stood with a small band of disciples in a nation that would not listen, trusting his Father.
- **"Inquire of the mediums and the necromancers who chirp and mutter" (v19).** When God seemed silent, people turned to the occult. Mediums claimed to contact the dead, as the medium of Endor did for Saul (1 Sam 28). Their voices "chirp" (*tsaphtseph*, the sound of birds twittering) and "mutter" (*hagah*, to moan or murmur), the faint whispering sounds that were supposed to be the spirits speaking. The same verb *hagah* means "meditate" in Psalm 1:2. Some meditate on God's law; others mutter to the dead. All of this was strictly forbidden (Lev 19:31; Deut 18:10–12).
- **"Should not a people inquire of their God? Should they inquire of the dead on behalf of the living?" (v19).** Plain common sense. Why ask the dead about the future of the living, when the living God has spoken?
- **"To the teaching and to the testimony!" (v20).** The battle cry of the faithful: go back to what God has already revealed. Any message that does not agree with God's word "has no dawn," no morning light in it at all. It leads only into deeper night.
- **"They will pass through the land, greatly distressed and hungry" (v21).** The picture is of people wandering through a ruined land after invasion. Hunger turns to rage, and they curse "their king and their God," blaming everyone but themselves.
- **"Turn their faces upward… look to the earth, but behold, distress and darkness" (vv21–22).** They look up and find no help, and they look down and see only darkness. "They will be thrust into thick darkness." This is where the chapter ends, the end of the road for those who refused the gentle waters and the light of God's word. But it sets up the very next words: "But there will be no gloom for her who was in anguish" (9:1).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Those that truly fear God shall not need to fear any evil," and "make him your fear, and you shall find him your hope, your help, your defence." He also warns, "If the things of God be an offence to us, they will be an undoing to us." JFB calls God an "inviolable asylum" for believers and says Hebrews quotes v18 "to prove the manhood of the Messiah," with Isaiah as a type of Christ. JFB explains "chirp" as the faint, bird-like sounds necromancers made, and notes that the Septuagint calls them ventriloquists. Guzik connects the stone of v14 to 1 Peter 2:6–8 and argues that the LORD of hosts in Isaiah is identified with Jesus in the New Testament. On v20 he quotes Spurgeon, who borrows from Robert Murray M'Cheyne: it is God's word, not human commentary, that saves souls.

#### Pointing to Christ
The land belongs to Immanuel, and that is why the flood stops at the neck. The New Testament takes the main lines of this chapter and fixes them on Jesus. He is the LORD of hosts whom Peter tells believers to honor as holy. He is the stone that is a sanctuary to some and a stumbling block to others. He is the one who says with Isaiah, "I will put my trust in him… Behold, I and the children God has given me." In a frightened world that turns to every voice but God's, he is the living Word of the living God. He is also the dawn that the mediums never had, for the darkness of v22 is exactly where Matthew says "the people dwelling in darkness have seen a great light" (Matt 4:16).
