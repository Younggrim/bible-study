*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A Maskil of David." No occasion is named, but the psalm's center is unmistakable: betrayal by a close friend. "It is not an enemy who taunts me… But it is you, a man, my equal, my companion, my familiar friend" (vv12–13). Most readers, including Matthew Henry, connect it with Absalom's rebellion, about 975 BC, when David's son seized Jerusalem and David fled the city on foot (2 Sam 15–18). The friend would then be Ahithophel, David's most trusted counselor, whose advice "was as if one consulted the word of God" (2 Sam 16:23), and who went over to Absalom (2 Sam 15:12). When David heard, he prayed, "O LORD, please turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness" (15:31), close to v9 here. Guzik notes that some details fit awkwardly. It is hard to picture David praying that Absalom would go down to Sheol alive (v15), when he wept, "O my son Absalom" (18:33). JFB says the tone suits David's experience under both Saul and Absalom, "though perhaps neither was exclusively before his mind." The psalm moves through fear (vv1–8), anger at the city and the traitor (vv9–15), and faith (vv16–23). G. Campbell Morgan, quoted by Guzik, sums up the movement well: fear only makes one want to run, and faith alone creates courage.

#### Oh, That I Had Wings Like a Dove (vv1–8)
- **"Give ear to my prayer, O God, and hide not yourself from my plea for mercy!" (v1).** God seems to have turned away. To "hide oneself" is what a person does when they pretend not to see someone in need (Deut 22:1–4; Isa 58:7). JFB: "withhold not help."
- **"I am restless in my complaint and I moan" (v2).** "Restless" means wandering, unable to settle, pacing. "Moan" is the sound of groaning, like the murmur of a crowd or the roar of the sea. The Psalms let God's people bring raw distress to him. Spurgeon, quoted by Guzik, put it well: we may complain *to* God, though not *of* him.
- **"Because of the noise of the enemy, because of the oppression of the wicked" (v3).** "Noise" is literally the enemy's voice: shouting, threats, lies. In Absalom's revolt, the conspiracy began with words: Absalom sat at the city gate telling people that no one in the king's court would hear their cases (2 Sam 15:2–6).
- **"For they drop trouble upon me" (v3).** Literally "they make iniquity totter onto me," or as JFB has it, "make evil doings slide upon me." The picture may be of rocks tipped down on someone, or of guilt dumped on him by slander.
- **"My heart is in anguish within me; the terrors of death have fallen upon me" (v4).** "In anguish" is literally "writhes," as in childbirth. "Terrors of death" are the fears that come when death seems close.
- **"Fear and trembling come upon me, and horror overwhelms me" (v5).** "Horror" is a shuddering, a shaking of the whole body. Clarke, cited by Guzik, notices how the description builds: distress, then fear, then terror, then horror. David is not a calm hero. He is a frightened man.
- **"Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away and be at rest" (v6).** One of the most famous lines in the Psalms, and one of the most human. Doves nest in the high clefts of rocks, out of reach (Song 2:14; Jer 48:28). David wants to escape it all. "Be at rest" is literally "settle," "dwell" (JFB: "permanently"). Henry: "The dove flies low, and takes shelter as soon as she can, and thus would David fly." Mendelssohn's well-known anthem "Hear My Prayer," with its line "O for the wings of a dove," is based on these verses.
- **"I would wander far away; I would lodge in the wilderness. Selah" (v7).** When Absalom took Jerusalem, David did literally flee into the wilderness, east toward the Jordan (2 Sam 15:23, 28; 17:16). Guzik notes that the wilderness was where David had once known God's faithfulness during his years running from Saul. But wanting to fly away is not the same as finding rest. The psalm will find rest somewhere else (v22).
- **"I would hurry to find a shelter from the raging wind and tempest" (v8).** The trouble is pictured as a storm. JFB: "Even a wilderness is a safer place than exposure to such evils."
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that David, like a dove, "cannot endure noise," and links the longing to fly with a gentle spirit overwhelmed by turmoil. Guzik observes that David could not actually escape and had to stand his ground, and that the wish to flee is natural but not the answer. JFB on v2: the words "express full indulgence of grief."

#### Destroy, O Lord, Divide Their Tongues (vv9–15)
- **"Destroy, O Lord, divide their tongues" (v9).** "Destroy" is literally "swallow up" (JFB), "confuse." "Divide their tongues" recalls Babel, where God confused the language of the builders so they could not work together (Gen 11:7–9). The Hebrew word *palag*, "divide," is the root of the name Peleg, "for in his days the earth was divided" (Gen 10:25). David prays that the conspirators will fall into disagreement and their plans fall apart. In Absalom's revolt that is exactly what happened. David's friend Hushai pretended to join Absalom and gave him different advice from Ahithophel's, and Absalom followed Hushai, "for the LORD had ordained to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel" (2 Sam 17:14). Guzik makes the same link.
- **"For I see violence and strife in the city" (v9).** The city is presumably Jerusalem (JFB: "the scene of anarchy"). The holy city, the city of the great King (48:2), has become a place of violence.
- **"Day and night they go around it on its walls" (v10).** The picture is chilling. Watchmen were supposed to walk the walls to protect the city. Here the watchmen are violence and strife themselves, patrolling the walls day and night. Evil is now the city's guard.
- **"Iniquity and trouble are within it; ruin is in its midst" (vv10–11).** Six words for evil pile up in two verses: violence, strife, iniquity, trouble, ruin, oppression and fraud. The whole city is rotten.
- **"Oppression and fraud do not depart from its marketplace" (v11).** The "marketplace" is the open square inside the city gate, where business was done and court cases were heard (Ruth 4:1–2). JFB: "wide places," markets and courts. The very place where justice should be done is where fraud lives.
- **"For it is not an enemy who taunts me—then I could bear it" (v12).** Now the deepest wound. An open enemy you can see coming. You can prepare for him, or "hide from him." But the betrayal of a friend comes from behind, where there was no guard. JFB: the friend's treachery "does not deny, but aggravates, the injury."
- **"But it is you, a man, my equal, my companion, my familiar friend" (v13).** David suddenly speaks to the traitor directly. "My equal" literally means "according to my valuation," someone David treated as on his level. "Companion" (*'alluph*) is a close friend, a confidant (Prov 16:28). "Familiar friend" is someone well known, intimate. Henry pictures David saying, "I have been kind to him, but I find him thus basely ungrateful."
- **"We used to take sweet counsel together" (v14).** "Counsel" (*sod*) means intimate conversation, the sharing of secrets. It fits Ahithophel, who was David's personal counselor. Kidner, cited by Guzik, points out a painful irony: Uriah was one of David's own mighty men (2 Sam 23:39), so David had himself once betrayed a loyal friend. Many readers also note that Ahithophel was probably Bathsheba's grandfather (2 Sam 11:3; 23:34), which may help explain why he turned on David.
- **"Within God's house we walked in the throng" (v14).** They had worshiped together, walking in the crowds of festival processions up to the house of God (42:4). JFB: "with a crowd," in a festal procession. Their friendship had been spiritual, not only political.
- **"Let death steal over them; let them go down to Sheol alive" (v15).** "Go down alive" recalls Korah's rebellion, when the ground opened and the rebels "went down alive into Sheol" (Num 16:30–33). Kidner, cited by Guzik, makes that link. JFB notes the verbs can also be read as statements rather than wishes ("they will go"). Boice, cited by Guzik, notes that David does not curse the friend by name but the whole conspiracy ("them"). As with Psalm 54, David asks God to judge. He does not take vengeance himself.
- **"For evil is in their dwelling place and in their heart" (v15).** Evil is at home in them, inside their houses and inside their hearts.
- **Commentators:** Guzik links v9 with Babel and with Hushai's counsel defeating Ahithophel's, and through Kidner with the Uriah story. Henry reads the city as Jerusalem under Absalom's rule. JFB notes that the switches between "him" and "them" in the psalm (vv13, 15, 20, 23) suggest the chief traitor and his accomplices are in view together.

#### But I Call to God (vv16–23)
- **"But I call to God, and the LORD will save me" (v16).** The turn of the psalm. "I" is emphatic: whatever they do, as for me, I call on God.
- **"Evening and morning and at noon I utter my complaint and moan, and he hears my voice" (v17).** The order is Hebrew: the day begins at sunset (Gen 1:5, "there was evening and there was morning"). Daniel prayed three times a day, facing Jerusalem (Dan 6:10), and devout Jews still pray at these times. David's complaint has not stopped, but now it is joined to confidence: "he hears." Henry: "Let not us be weary of praying often, for God is not weary of hearing."
- **"He redeems my soul in safety from the battle that I wage" (v18).** "Redeems" (*padah*) is the ransom word of 49:7, 15. God buys him back from the battle.
- **"For many are arrayed against me" (v18).** Literally "for with many they were with me," which is hard Hebrew. JFB takes it as "fighting with me." Some read it as: many were against me, but God was with me.
- **"God will give ear and humble them, he who is enthroned from of old" (v19).** God hears David's prayer (v17), and God also "hears" the enemy (JFB: "God hears the wicked in wrath"). "Enthroned from of old" reminds the reader that God has been on his throne long before any rebel prince and will be after.
- **"Because they do not change and do not fear God" (v19).** "Change" (*chaliphot*) can mean they never repent, or that nothing ever changes for them. They have prospered without setbacks, and their easy lives have made them careless of God. JFB cites 73:5. Comfort can harden a heart.
- **"My companion stretched out his hand against his friends; he violated his covenant" (v20).** The ESV footnote shows that the Hebrew just says "He." The ESV supplies "my companion" because the context makes it clear. Friendship in Israel was often sealed by covenant, as between David and Jonathan (1 Sam 18:3). A king's counselor was also bound by oath. The traitor broke both.
- **"His speech was smooth as butter, yet war was in his heart" (v21).** "Smooth as butter" is literally "smoother than cream." "His words were softer than oil, yet they were drawn swords." Pleasant on the surface, deadly underneath. Absalom kissed the people at the gate to win their hearts (2 Sam 15:5–6). JFB: the treachery is aggravated by hypocrisy.
- **"Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you" (v22).** The best-loved line of the psalm, and it comes in the middle of betrayal. David speaks to himself, or to anyone who will listen. "Burden" (*yehav*) is found only here in the Old Testament. The lexicon gives its meaning as "lot," "that which is given," the care and anxiety that are a person's portion. JFB: "literally, 'gift,' what is assigned you." The burden is what God has allowed to be laid on you, and you may throw it back on him. "Sustain" (*kul*) means to provide for, feed, support. It is the word for Joseph providing for his family in famine (Gen 45:11; 50:21). JFB: literally "supply food." God does not promise to take the load away, but to hold up the one carrying it. Kidner makes the same point.
- **"He will never permit the righteous to be moved" (v22).** "Moved" means shaken, toppled, the word used of the city of God in 46:5. The ground may shake, but the one who leans on God will not fall.
- **"But you, O God, will cast them down into the pit of destruction" (v23).** The same verb root as "cast your burden": those who cast their load on God are upheld, and those who reject him are cast down.
- **"Men of blood and treachery shall not live out half their days" (v23).** In the story of Absalom, this came true for both. When Absalom rejected his advice, Ahithophel went home, "set his house in order and hanged himself" (2 Sam 17:23). Absalom was killed hanging by his hair from a tree (2 Sam 18:9–15). Both died young and violently. The Tyndale notes add that in the Old Testament, a long life was a sign of God's blessing (Prov 3:2).
- **"But I will trust in you" (v23).** The last words. "I" is emphatic again. The man who wanted to fly away now stands still and trusts. Guzik notes, with Kidner, that the psalm ends by turning away from the enemies to God alone.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "To cast our burden upon God is to stay ourselves on his providence and promise." Guzik, with Meyer and Spurgeon, warns against casting a burden on God and then picking it up again, and notes that Kidner finds the word "burden" broader than it sounds: one's whole appointed lot. JFB explains "sustain" as "supply food," covering all need, and compares Matthew 6:11.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Tyndale notes point out that Jesus lived this psalm. He too was betrayed by "my companion, my familiar friend," a man who had walked with him and shared his table. Jesus quoted the companion psalm, 41:9, of Judas: "He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me" (John 13:18). Judas' words were "smooth as butter" while war was in his heart. He greeted Jesus, "Rabbi!" and kissed him (Matt 26:49), and Jesus asked, "Judas, would you betray the Son of Man with a kiss?" (Luke 22:48). Like Ahithophel, Judas went out and hanged himself (Matt 27:5). Jesus, like David, crossed the Kidron Valley and went up the Mount of Olives in sorrow (2 Sam 15:23, 30; John 18:1). But Jesus did not fly away. In Gethsemane his soul was "very sorrowful, even to death" (Mark 14:34), and he cast his burden on the Father: "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). Then he took on himself the judgment that belonged to the "men of blood and treachery." Peter, who knew both the cost of betrayal and the mercy that follows, takes up v22 from the Greek Old Testament: "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Pet 5:7).
