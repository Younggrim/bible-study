*Setting:* The apostle John, late in the first century, writing to churches in Asia after a group had broken away from them (2:19). The departing teachers claimed to know God while treating sin as unimportant and withholding love from those they left behind. Chapter 2 ended by introducing a new idea: those who practice righteousness have been "born of him" (2:29). Chapter 3 opens in wonder at being God's children, then draws out what that birth means. God's children do not make a practice of sin, and they love one another in deed, not only in word. John's second great test, love, now takes center stage, and the chapter closes with how an accusing heart can find rest before God.

#### Children of God (vv1–10)
- **"See what kind of love" (v1).** The word for "what kind of" is *potapos*. In older Greek it meant "from what country?" By the New Testament it meant "of what sort?", usually with a note of amazement (Matt 8:27, "What sort of man is this?"). Either way, John is pointing at a love that seems to come from somewhere else entirely. The verb "has given" is perfect tense: a gift given and still held.
- **"That we should be called children of God; and so we are" (v1).** "And so we are" is missing from some late manuscripts (and so from the KJV), but it is well attested and modern translations include it. It is a joyful aside, as if John cannot help adding, "and it's not just a name, it's true." John uses *tekna*, "children" by birth, for believers, and keeps *huios*, "son," for Jesus alone. We are children by new birth (John 1:12–13). He is the Son by nature.
- **"The world does not know us… it did not know him" (v1).** Being misunderstood is a family likeness. The world did not recognize Jesus (John 1:10). It will not recognize his family either.
- **"What we will be has not yet appeared" (v2).** Believers are God's children now, but the full picture is still hidden, like a seed that does not show the shape of the tree. The ESV footnote offers "when it appears" as an alternative to "when he appears." Both fit, but the parallel with 2:28 and the rest of the verse favor Christ's appearing.
- **"We shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is" (v2).** Seeing transforms. Moses' face shone from being near God (Exod 34:29), and Paul says believers are being changed "from one degree of glory to another" as they behold the Lord (2 Cor 3:18). At Christ's return the change will be complete (Phil 3:21; Rom 8:29). "As he is" means not in a mirror dimly but face to face (1 Cor 13:12).
- **"Everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself as he is pure" (v3).** Hope is not idle. A bride who knows the wedding is coming gets ready. "Pure" (*hagnos*) was used of ritual and moral cleanness. The standard is Christ himself.
- **"Sin is lawlessness" (v4).** The word is *anomia*, rebellion against God's law. The false teachers apparently thought sin did not matter to the spiritually enlightened. John defines it as defiance of God. Jesus used the same word for the lawless one at the end (Matt 7:23; 24:12; compare 2 Thess 2:3).
- **"He appeared in order to take away sins, and in him there is no sin" (v5).** This echoes John the Baptist: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Like the spotless Passover lamb (Exod 12:5; 1 Pet 1:19), the one who removes sin had none of his own.
- **"No one who abides in him keeps on sinning" (v6).** This seems to contradict 1:8 and 2:1, where John says believers do sin. The key is the Greek present tense, which often describes an ongoing habit. The ESV brings this out with "keeps on sinning" and "makes a practice of sinning." John is not saying a believer never sins. He is saying a believer cannot settle into sin as the pattern of life. Other explanations have been offered (that John speaks of an ideal, or of certain kinds of sin), but the habitual reading is the most common among modern interpreters and fits the letter best.
- **"Let no one deceive you" (v7).** The false teachers were saying that a person could be righteous without doing righteousness. John says flatly: "Whoever practices righteousness is righteous, as he is righteous."
- **"The devil has been sinning from the beginning" (v8).** Sin's family tree goes back to the serpent in Eden (Gen 3) and beyond. Jesus called the devil "a murderer from the beginning" and "the father of lies" (John 8:44). A life of settled sin shows that family likeness.
- **"To destroy the works of the devil" (v8).** "Destroy" is *lyō*, which means to loose, untie, break up or undo. It is used of loosening sandal straps (Mark 1:7) and of breaking down a dividing wall (Eph 2:14). Christ came to dismantle what the devil built: sin, deception and death. He freed people from the devil's ropes (Luke 13:16; Heb 2:14–15).
- **"God's seed abides in him" (v9).** The ESV footnote notes the Greek says "his seed." "Seed" (*sperma*) is the new life God plants at the new birth (1 Pet 1:23), the divine nature passed on from Father to child. Some think it means the word of God, others the Holy Spirit. Either way, something from God now lives in the believer and will not let them rest in sin.
- **"He cannot keep on sinning, because he has been born of God" (v9).** This is the strongest form of the claim, and the same present tense applies. A child of God may fall, but cannot be at home in sin. It is not about willpower but about nature: the new birth makes settled sin impossible.
- **"By this it is evident" (v10).** There are two families, and their behavior shows which is which. John adds a second mark that leads into the next section: "nor is the one who does not love his brother."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls adoption "wonderful condescending love," and on v3 says, "That we may be saved by hope we must be purified by hope." Guzik observes that being called God's children is more than Adam had, and that believers will be like Christ while keeping their own personalities. JFB confirms that "the oldest manuscripts add, 'And we ARE SO.'" On vv6 and 9, Guzik (following Stott) and Henry both take the habitual reading: the one who abides "continues not in the practice of sin," in Henry's words. JFB puts it a different way: "He sinneth not so long as he abideth in Christ." For JFB the guarantee depends on staying in Christ, more than on what kind of sin is in view. On v8 JFB writes, "From the devil there is not generation, but corruption," meaning the devil can corrupt but cannot give birth to children the way God does. Guzik, quoting Spurgeon, says grace that leaves a life unchanged has not saved it.

#### Love One Another (vv11–24)
- **"This is the message that you have heard from the beginning" (v11).** This matches 1:5 ("This is the message"). The first message was that God is light. This one is that "we should love one another." John holds the two together throughout the letter.
- **"Not be like Cain, who was of the evil one and murdered his brother" (v12).** This is the only direct Old Testament reference in the letter (Gen 4:1–16). "Murdered" (*sphazō*) means to slaughter, as with a sacrificial animal, and is used of the Lamb "slain" in Revelation (5:6). The reason for the murder is chilling: "his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous." Goodness can provoke hatred just because it exposes evil (John 3:19–20).
- **"Do not be surprised… that the world hates you" (v13).** After Cain, this follows naturally. Jesus warned his disciples of the same thing (John 15:18–19). The footnote notes that "brothers" (*adelphoi*) includes brothers and sisters.
- **"We have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers" (v14).** These words echo Jesus: "whoever hears my word and believes him… has passed from death to life" (John 5:24). Love does not earn the passage. It is evidence that it has happened.
- **"Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer" (v15).** John follows Jesus' teaching in the Sermon on the Mount, where anger is the root of murder (Matt 5:21–22). Hatred wants the other person gone. Cain only acted on what was already in his heart.
- **"By this we know love, that he laid down his life for us" (v16).** Love is defined not by a feeling but by the cross. The phrase "laid down his life" is the language of the Good Shepherd (John 10:11, 15, 17–18) and of "greater love has no one than this" (John 15:13). "We ought" follows directly: the same pattern for us.
- **"If anyone has the world's goods and sees his brother in need" (v17).** John moves from the heroic to the everyday. Few will be asked to die for another believer. Many will be asked to share. "World's goods" uses *bios*, "livelihood," the same word as "pride of life" in 2:16. "Closes his heart" is literally "shuts up his bowels," the inner organs Greek speakers saw as the seat of compassion. James makes the same point (James 2:15–16).
- **"Not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth" (v18).** Talk is cheap. Love is measured in actions that are real, not for show.
- **"Reassure our heart before him; for whenever our heart condemns us" (vv19–20).** These verses are hard to translate, and they have been read two ways. Most modern interpreters see comfort here: when a believer's conscience accuses them, they can rest because "God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything." He knows the love he has worked in us, and he knows the sacrifice that covers our sins. Older interpreters, including Calvin, read it as a warning: if even our own heart condemns us, how much more will God, who knows more. The context of reassurance ("reassure our heart," v19) leads most to the first view.
- **"If our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before God" (v21).** "Confidence" is *parrēsia*, the same boldness as 2:28, now in prayer. A clear conscience frees a person to pray openly.
- **"Whatever we ask we receive from him, because we keep his commandments" (v22).** This is not a deal in which obedience buys answers. A child walking with the Father asks for what the Father wants (5:14). Jesus gave the same promise to those abiding in him (John 15:7).
- **"Believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ and love one another" (v23).** All God's commands sum up in one, with two parts: faith in Jesus and love for each other. It is the first time "believe" appears in the letter. "The name" means the whole person and claims of Jesus.
- **"By the Spirit whom he has given us" (v24).** This is the first explicit mention of the Holy Spirit in the letter. The ESV footnotes say the Greek has "him" and "he" here, and it is not always clear whether John means the Father or the Son. The Spirit is the inner witness that the relationship is real, and 4:1–6 will show how to test spirits that claim to speak for God.
- **Commentators:** JFB on v14: "Love, on our part, is the evidence of our justification and regeneration, not the cause of them." Guzik traces Cain's slide from unbelief to pride, hatred and the cover-up. He also notes that hatred can be passive, a matter of simply not caring. On v16 he cites Stott that "lay down" is the same verb Jesus used when he laid aside his outer garments to wash feet (John 13:4). On vv20–21 he says an accusing heart may be hearing the enemy or an oversensitive conscience, and the answer is to trust God's verdict over our feelings. Henry's outline treats vv20–22 as the quieting of the heart before God and the confidence it gives in prayer, which is the comforting reading most modern commentators take.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everything in this chapter flows from what Jesus did. He "appeared in order to take away sins" (v5). He came "to destroy the works of the devil" (v8). And he "laid down his life for us" (v16). His purity is the standard his people hope to share (v3), and seeing him is what will finally make them like him (v2). Cain shows what human nature does with a brother. Christ shows what God's love does: instead of taking his brother's life, he gave his own. That love, poured into children born of God, is what makes them love one another.
