*Setting:* Psalm 10 has no title, which is rare in Book One of the Psalms, where almost every psalm is labeled "of David." The likely reason is that it was first written together with Psalm 9 as one poem. The two share an alphabet pattern (an acrostic), with Psalm 9 covering roughly the first half of the Hebrew alphabet and Psalm 10 the second. Psalm 10 picks up the letter *lamed* in v1 and, after a stretch where the pattern breaks down (vv3–11), resumes with *qoph*, *resh*, *shin* and *tav* in vv12–17. The ancient Greek and Latin translations print them as one psalm. They also share words: "times of trouble" (9:9; 10:1), "the afflicted," "the oppressed," and the prayer "Arise, O LORD" (9:19; 10:12). Yet the mood shifts sharply. Psalm 9 was mostly thanks for victories God had already won over hostile nations. Psalm 10 is mostly lament over the wicked inside society, the powerful man who preys on the poor and gets away with it, while God seems to do nothing. It is one of the Bible's clearest portraits of what is often called "practical atheism": believing in God in theory but living as if he will never call anyone to account. The psalm moves from a cry of "Why?" (v1), through a long description of the wicked (vv2–11), to a plea (vv12–15) and a closing confession of faith (vv16–18).

#### Why Do You Stand Far Away? (v1)
- **"Why, O LORD, do you stand far away?" (v1).** The psalm opens with a complaint, not a hymn. Scripture gives believers permission to ask God "why." The same cry runs through 22:1 ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?") and Habakkuk 1:2–4. It is not unbelief. Only someone who believes God *could* help would ask why he does not.
- **"Why do you hide yourself in times of trouble?" (v1).** "Times of trouble" is the same rare phrase as 9:9, where God was "a stronghold in times of trouble." The psalmist is holding God to what was just said. If God is a stronghold in such times, where is he? The honesty of the question is itself a kind of faith: it brings the problem to God instead of walking away.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God's withdrawings "are very grievous to his people," especially in trouble, but warns that people often judge God's nearness by outward circumstances, and that unbelief is what really creates distance. Guzik passes on Spurgeon: "It is not the trouble, but the hiding of our Father's face, which cuts us to the quick." JFB reads "hidest" as hiding God's eyes or face, a figure for seeming not to notice.

#### In Arrogance the Wicked Pursue the Poor (vv2–6)
- **"In arrogance the wicked hotly pursue the poor" (v2).** "Hotly pursue" pictures a hot, burning chase. The root sin named first is "arrogance," pride. The poor here (*ani*) are the afflicted and powerless, people with no lawyer, no money and no protection.
- **"Let them be caught in the schemes that they have devised" (v2).** The pit-and-net theme of 7:15 and 9:15 returns as a prayer: let their own plots catch them.
- **"For the wicked boasts of the desires of his soul" (v3).** He is not ashamed of his greed. He brags about it.
- **"The one greedy for gain curses and renounces the LORD" (v3).** The Hebrew is difficult. The verb usually means "bless" (*barak*), and the KJV reads it so: the wicked "blesseth the covetous, whom the LORD abhorreth." Many scholars think "bless" is used here, as in Job 1:5 and 2:9, as a polite substitute for "curse," a way scribes avoided writing "curse God" directly. The ESV takes that view. Either way, greed and contempt for God travel together. Paul calls covetousness "idolatry" (Col 3:5).
- **"In the pride of his face the wicked does not seek him" (v4).** Literally "according to the height of his nose." The proud man literally holds his nose in the air. He does not pray, because he does not think he needs to.
- **"All his thoughts are, 'There is no God'" (v4).** The Hebrew is just two words: "No God." This is not a philosopher's argument. It is the way he lives. He may never deny God out loud, but God has no place in his plans or decisions. Psalm 14:1 says the same thing of the "fool." It is a warning that people can be religious in name and still live this way.
- **"His ways prosper at all times; your judgments are on high, out of his sight" (v5).** The hardest part for the psalmist is that the man is doing well. God's judgments seem far away, "on high," too distant to bother him. Psalm 73 wrestles with the same thing at length: "I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked" (73:3).
- **"As for all his foes, he puffs at them" (v5).** He snorts or sneers at his opponents. They are no threat.
- **"He says in his heart, 'I shall not be moved'" (v6).** This is the language a believer uses of trust in God (16:8; 62:2, 6, "I shall not be shaken"). The wicked man steals it and applies it to himself. **"Throughout all generations I shall not meet adversity"** is the boast of someone who thinks he is untouchable. Jesus told a parable about a rich man who said much the same, and God answered, "Fool! This night your soul is required of you" (Luke 12:19–20).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Tyranny, both in state and church, owes its origin to pride." He says the proud do not seek God because they feel no need of him. JFB says of v4, "The face expresses the self-conceit, whose fruit is practical atheism," and reads v3 with the older translation, "he blesseth the covetous." Guzik points out the irony that the man who has no room for God in his thoughts in fact keeps talking about God (vv11, 13). He cannot leave God alone, even while denying him.

#### Like a Lion in His Thicket (vv7–11)
- **"His mouth is filled with cursing and deceit and oppression" (v7).** Paul quotes this verse in Romans 3:14, "Their mouth is full of curses and bitterness," as part of his chain of Old Testament texts proving that "none is righteous, no, not one" (Rom 3:10). That is a sobering move. Paul takes this portrait of the wicked oppressor and says it describes the whole human race apart from God's grace.
- **"Under his tongue are mischief and iniquity" (v7).** The picture is of food held under the tongue to savor it. He enjoys his evil words. Job 20:12 uses the same image: "Though evil is sweet in his mouth, though he hides it under his tongue."
- **"He sits in ambush in the villages" (v8).** "Villages" were small unwalled settlements, the most vulnerable places, with no gates or city guard. This could be a literal bandit, or a picture of the powerful who prey on the weak through legal tricks, land grabs and debt (Mic 2:1–2; Isa 5:8). Either way, it is calculated, not impulsive.
- **"His eyes stealthily watch for the helpless" (v8).** "Helpless" (*chelekah*) is a rare word found only in this psalm (vv8, 10, 14). It seems to mean the unfortunate, the hapless. The repetition of it three times keeps the victim in view.
- **"He lurks in ambush like a lion in his thicket" (v9).** The lion image from 7:2 returns. He is also a hunter: **"he seizes the poor when he draws him into his net"** (v9). Peter later uses the same picture for the devil: "your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour" (1 Pet 5:8).
- **"The helpless are crushed, sink down, and fall by his might" (v10).** The verbs pile up: crushed, sinking, falling. The ESV's "by his might" may also be read "by his strong ones," his gang or his claws.
- **"He says in his heart, 'God has forgotten, he has hidden his face, he will never see it'" (v11).** This is the creed behind the crimes. He does not say there is no God. He says God does not notice. The psalmist asked in v1 why God hides; the wicked man is counting on it. The same words describe two very different hearts: one grieved by God's silence, the other relying on it.
- **Commentators:** JFB pictures the lion watching "with half-closed eyes, appearing not to see," and gathering himself "into as small compass as possible" before springing. He reads v11 as conduct that "implies disbelief or disregard of God's government." Henry suggests the image may describe robbers who "lie in wait for honest travellers," or persecutors plotting in secret. Guzik notes that the victims are called "helpless" three times, and quotes Charnock against the idea that God forgets: "Time cannot raze out that which hath been known from eternity."

#### Arise, O LORD (vv12–15)
- **"Arise, O LORD; O God, lift up your hand" (v12).** The turn comes. "Lift up your hand" means act in power, the way God's hand brought Israel out of Egypt "with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm" (Deut 26:8).
- **"Forget not the afflicted" (v12).** The wicked said "God has forgotten" (v11). The psalmist answers by asking God not to. It echoes 9:12, "he does not forget the cry of the afflicted," and 9:18. Prayer here holds God to what he has promised.
- **"Why does the wicked renounce God and say in his heart, 'You will not call to account'?" (v13).** A second "why," but now directed at the wicked man's foolishness rather than at God's delay. "Call to account" (*darash*) is the same verb as "avenges blood" in 9:12. He thinks no one will ask questions. God is the one who asks.
- **"But you do see" (v14).** This is the heart of the psalm. He said "he will never see it" (v11). The psalmist replies directly, "But you do see." **"You note mischief and vexation, that you may take it into your hands."** God does not only see suffering. He takes it into his hands to deal with it. "Mischief" and "vexation" are trouble and grief, both what is done and how it feels.
- **"To you the helpless commits himself" (v14).** Literally "leaves" or "abandons himself." The helpless man has nowhere to put his burden but on God. Peter's command, "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Pet 5:7), says the same.
- **"You have been the helper of the fatherless" (v14).** In Israel the fatherless, the widow and the foreigner were the classic picture of people with no protector. God named himself their defender (Exod 22:22–24; Deut 10:18; Ps 68:5, "Father of the fatherless"). He takes their side personally.
- **"Break the arm of the wicked and evildoer" (v15).** "Arm" is strength, especially the power to fight and oppress (37:17). The prayer is to remove his power to harm. It is a prayer for justice, not for personal revenge, and it is left in God's hands.
- **"Call his wickedness to account till you find none" (v15).** Search until there is nothing left to find. The wicked man thought God would not "call to account" (v13). The psalmist asks God to do exactly that, thoroughly.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "lift up thine hand" as "exert thy power," says God "carefully examines or beholds all wickedness," and notes that "committeth" can mean "leaves (his burden) on Thee." He reads v15 as God's promise to "utterly destroy the wicked and his deeds." Guzik quotes Horne that God's patience, instead of leading such a man to repentance, "only hardens him in his iniquity," and Trapp: "Where God giveth a praying heart it is sure that he will show a pitying heart." The Tyndale notes set v15 among the psalms' prayers for vengeance, prayers that hand judgment to God rather than take it into human hands.

#### The LORD Is King Forever (vv16–18)
- **"The LORD is king forever and ever" (v16).** The psalm reaches solid ground. The wicked man said "throughout all generations I shall not meet adversity" (v6). Only one king really lasts through all generations. The words recall the song at the Red Sea: "The LORD will reign forever and ever" (Exod 15:18).
- **"The nations perish from his land" (v16).** The land of Canaan belongs to the LORD (Lev 25:23). The nations that once held it, and opposed him, are gone. This ties Psalm 10 back to Psalm 9's foreign nations. The arrogant oppressor at home and the hostile empire abroad are both under the same King.
- **"O LORD, you hear the desire of the afflicted" (v17).** The wicked boasted of "the desires of his soul" (v3). The afflicted also have desires, and God hears them. The difference is not that one has longings and the other does not, but where those longings are taken.
- **"You will strengthen their heart; you will incline your ear" (v17).** "Strengthen" (*kun*) can also mean prepare or establish. God steadies the heart even before the outward rescue comes. He bends down to listen, like a parent leaning to hear a child.
- **"To do justice to the fatherless and the oppressed" (v18).** The psalm ends where God's heart is: with the people who have no one.
- **"So that man who is of the earth may strike terror no more" (v18).** "Man" is *enosh* again, frail humanity, as in 8:4 and 9:20. The tyrant is only a creature of dust. Psalm 9 ended with "let the nations know that they are but men!" Psalm 10 ends the same way. The wicked man's terror is real, but it is temporary, and it has an end set by God.
- **Commentators:** Guzik links the eternal kingship to Exodus 15:18 and quotes Spurgeon that "none but the Lord can prepare a heart for prayer." He closes with G. Campbell Morgan's reading of the last line, that man "of the earth may be terrible no more." JFB: God "executes justice for the feeble" and "represses the pride and violence of conceited, though frail, men."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 10 asks why God seems far away while the helpless are crushed. The gospel's answer is that God did not stay far away. In Jesus he came near and stood with the poor, the fatherless and the oppressed, and on the cross he was himself crushed and seized by men who thought God would not see. Paul quotes v7 to show that the portrait of the wicked describes every human heart (Rom 3:14), and then announces that God's righteousness has been shown "through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe" (Rom 3:22). Jesus did not break the arm of the wicked by force but by bearing their sins, and he rose as the King who reigns forever. One day he will finish the work this psalm prays for, so that no "man of the earth" will strike terror again.
