*Setting:* About 1876 BC on the traditional dating (some scholars place it later). Jacob is 130 years old (47:9). His sons have just come home from Egypt with Pharaoh's wagons and the news that Joseph, mourned as dead for twenty-two years, is alive and governs Egypt (45:25–28). Jacob has said, "I will go and see him before I die." But going to Egypt is no small thing for this family. God promised Canaan to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. When Abraham went to Egypt in a famine it went badly (12:10–20), and God told Isaac plainly, "Do not go down to Egypt; dwell in the land" (26:2). Leaving the promised land for Egypt could look like walking away from the promise. This chapter is the move: God's permission, the roll call of the family, and the reunion of a father and son.

#### Joseph Brings His Family to Egypt (vv1–27)
- **"Came to Beersheba, and offered sacrifices to the God of his father Isaac" (v1).** Beersheba was the last town at the southern edge of Canaan before the desert road to Egypt. Abraham planted a tree and called on the LORD there (21:33), God appeared to Isaac there (26:23–25), and Jacob left from there when he fled from Esau (28:10). Before he leaves the land, Jacob stops at the family altar. Matthew Henry says he chose the place "in remembrance of the communion which his father and grandfather had with God in that place." Jacob does not simply grab the wagons and go. He asks first.
- **"Jacob, Jacob." And he said, "Here I am" (v2).** God calls the name twice, as he did to Abraham on Mount Moriah (22:11) and will to Moses at the bush (Exod 3:4) and to Samuel (1 Sam 3:10). Each time it comes at a turning point. It is also the last time in Genesis God speaks to any of the patriarchs. Interestingly, God calls him "Jacob," the old name, even while the narrator calls him "Israel."
- **"I am God, the God of your father. Do not be afraid to go down to Egypt" (v3).** The words "do not be afraid" show that Jacob was afraid, and with reason. God had told Abraham that his descendants would be "sojourners in a land that is not theirs" and be "afflicted for four hundred years" (15:13). Jacob may have sensed he was leading his family into that very land. God does not deny it. He gives a reason to go anyway: **"there I will make you into a great nation."** Seventy people will go down, and a nation will come out (Exod 1:7; 12:37).
- **"I myself will go down with you… and I will also bring you up again" (v4).** God does not stay behind in Canaan. He goes down with them. This is the promise the whole book of Exodus fulfills. For Jacob personally, it was fulfilled when his sons carried his body back to be buried in Canaan (50:12–13), and for the nation four hundred years later. Henry: "Those that go whither God sends them shall certainly have God with them."
- **"Joseph's hand shall close your eyes" (v4).** A tender promise. Closing the eyes of the dead was a family duty, and God tells Jacob he will die with his lost son beside him. It happens in 50:1. JFB takes it to mean Joseph will stay with him to the end.
- **"In the wagons that Pharaoh had sent" (v5).** The old man rides, carried by his sons, together with the little ones and the wives. They take their livestock and goods too (v6), everything gained in Canaan. This is not a trip to buy food but a move. **"All his offspring he brought with him into Egypt" (v7).** "Daughters" in v7 likely includes daughters-in-law, since Dinah is the only daughter named, as JFB notes.
- **"These are the names of the descendants of Israel, who came into Egypt" (v8).** Exodus opens with these very words, "These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt" (Exod 1:1), deliberately picking up where Genesis leaves off. The list is ordered by Jacob's four wives: Leah's children (vv8–15), Zilpah's (vv16–18), Rachel's (vv19–22), Bilhah's (vv23–25). Each mother's sons come first, then their sons.
- **What the list is for.** It is a family register, the founders of the clans that will make up the twelve tribes. Numbers 26 lists the same clan names centuries later. This is why some names in it are hard to fit into a single journey. Judah's grandsons Hezron and Hamul are listed (v12), though Judah's sons Perez and Zerah were probably still young at this point (38:27–30). Benjamin has ten "sons" (v21), but Numbers 26:40 makes two of them, Naaman and Ard, his grandsons through Bela. Most interpreters think the list counts the founders of the family lines, some of whom were still "in the loins" of their fathers when the move happened (compare Heb 7:9–10). It is a register of who belongs to Israel, not a passenger list.
- **Names worth noticing.** **Shaul, "the son of a Canaanite woman" (v10)** is flagged because the family was not supposed to marry Canaanites (24:3; 28:1), which is one reason a move away from Canaan was good for them (see 38:1–2). **Kohath (v11)** is the grandfather of Moses and Aaron (Exod 6:18–20). **Perez and Hezron (v12)** begin the royal line that leads to David and to Jesus (Ruth 4:18–22; Matt 1:3). **Er and Onan "died in the land of Canaan" (v12)**, a reminder of the dark events of chapter 38. **Serah, "their sister" (v17)**, and **Dinah (v15)** are the only women named in the list besides the wives.
- **The numbers add up (vv15–27).** Leah's line is 33, Zilpah's 16, Rachel's 14 and Bilhah's 7, a total of 70. Leah's 33 counts Dinah and Jacob himself and leaves out Er and Onan, who were dead. The 66 of v26 are those who traveled from Canaan, Jacob's "own descendants," leaving out Jacob, Joseph and Joseph's two sons who were already in Egypt. Add those four and you have **seventy** (v27; Exod 1:5; Deut 10:22). The wives of the sons, and the household servants, are not counted, so the actual company was much larger.
- **Seventy, not seventy-five?** Stephen says Jacob's family numbered "seventy-five persons" (Acts 7:14). He was quoting the Greek translation (the Septuagint), which reads seventy-five here and in Exodus 1:5, and a Dead Sea Scroll of Exodus has seventy-five as well. The Greek counts five more of Joseph's descendants: sons and grandsons of Manasseh and Ephraim (compare 1 Chr 7:14–20). It is a different way of counting the same family, not a contradiction. Stephen used the Bible his hearers read.
- **Why seventy?** It is also the number of the nations listed in the Table of Nations (ch 10). Moses later says God fixed the borders of the peoples "according to the number of the sons of Israel" (Deut 32:8). Many interpreters see the point: a family of seventy, one for each nation, chosen so that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (12:3). The Tyndale notes take this view, though it is a reading of the pattern rather than something the chapter says.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls Egypt "like a mother's womb" for Israel, the place where a family grew safely into a nation. He and JFB both explain the seventy as sixty-six plus Jacob, Joseph and Joseph's sons, and both resolve Stephen's seventy-five through the Greek text's count of Joseph's descendants. JFB estimates the people had grown to some 180,000 men by the Exodus, and adds that the promise to "bring you up again" was fulfilled for Jacob only in his burial, and for Israel in the conquest. Henry sees Jacob's stop at Beersheba as a model: when making a big move, consult God first and go only where he sends.

#### Jacob and Joseph Reunited (vv28–34)
- **"He had sent Judah ahead of him to Joseph to show the way" (v28).** Judah, not Reuben the firstborn, goes ahead to make arrangements. The brother who once led the others into selling Joseph, and who then offered himself for Benjamin, is now the family's leader and guide. Guzik finds it fitting that the ancestor of the Messiah leads the family into Goshen. JFB sees it as a proper courtesy, letting the king and Joseph prepare.
- **"Joseph prepared his chariot and went up to meet Israel his father in Goshen" (v29).** The governor of Egypt rides out in state, as JFB notes, in the chariot fitting his office. But the moment he arrives, rank disappears. **"He presented himself to him"** uses a form of the verb "to see" that is usually used of God "appearing" to someone (12:7; 35:9). Here a son appears to a father who thought he would never see him again.
- **"Fell on his neck and wept on his neck a good while" (v29).** Joseph was seventeen when he last saw his father (37:2). He is now thirty-nine. Twenty-two years of grief end in one long embrace. "A good while" is the narrator's way of saying the weeping went on and on.
- **"Now let me die, since I have seen your face and know that you are still alive" (v30).** Jacob had said that he would go down to Sheol mourning his son (37:35). Now he can die in peace. Compare old Simeon holding the baby Jesus: "Lord, now you are letting your servant depart in peace… for my eyes have seen your salvation" (Luke 2:29–30). In fact Jacob lives seventeen more years in Egypt (47:28), seventeen years with Joseph to match the seventeen years he had him before (37:2).
- **"The men are shepherds… keepers of livestock" (vv31–34).** Joseph plans how to present his family to Pharaoh. He tells them to answer honestly: we are herdsmen, and always have been. **"For every shepherd is an abomination to the Egyptians" (v34).** Egypt's settled farmers looked down on wandering herders, and Egyptian art sometimes pictures herdsmen as rough and unkempt. Joseph is not hiding his family's background to win them a better place. He is using it. Because Egyptians want to keep herders at a distance, the family will be given its own region on the edge of Egypt, good grazing land, close to Joseph, and kept separate.
- **Separation as protection.** The Tyndale notes and Guzik both point out what this meant. In Canaan the family was already intermarrying and drifting toward Canaanite ways (34; 38; 46:10). In Egypt, a proud people who would not eat with them (43:32) or live near them kept them apart. God used Egyptian snobbery to keep Israel distinct while it grew. Later the same separation would make them easy to enslave, but by then they would be a nation.
- **Commentators:** Guzik contrasts Jacob's "now let me die" with his earlier complaint that "all these things are against me" (42:36). The things he thought were against him were God's way of bringing him to this moment. JFB says Joseph's chariot was no sign of pride but what his office required, and that his plan for meeting Pharaoh shows him honoring the king while caring for his brothers. Guzik explains the Egyptians' contempt for shepherds as the attitude of farmers toward herdsmen of the time.

#### Pointing to Christ
God says, "I myself will go down with you… and I will also bring you up again." That is the shape of the gospel. The Son of God came down into our Egypt, our exile and slavery, and went down into death itself, and he will bring his people up again. Hosea later says of Israel, "Out of Egypt I called my son" (Hos 11:1), and Matthew sees that fulfilled when Jesus too goes down to Egypt and comes back (Matt 2:15). The family register runs through Judah, Perez and Hezron toward David and Jesus. And in old Jacob's words, "Now let me die, since I have seen your face," we hear the same peace Simeon found when he held the promised child.
