*Setting:* Psalm 147 is the second of the five "Hallelujah psalms" (146–150) that close the Psalter, each beginning and ending with "Praise the LORD!" Psalm 146 praised God through the voice of one soul. Psalm 147 calls on Jerusalem. It has no heading in Hebrew. Its references to the LORD building up Jerusalem, gathering "the outcasts of Israel" (v2) and strengthening "the bars of your gates" (v13) point to the years after the exile in Babylon, when Jews returned to a ruined city and slowly rebuilt the temple (completed 516 BC) and then the walls under Nehemiah (445 BC). JFB says it was "specially designed to celebrate the rebuilding of Jerusalem," and many have connected it with the joyful dedication of the walls, when the Levites were brought "to celebrate the dedication with gladness, with thanksgivings and with singing, with cymbals, harps, and lyres" (Neh 12:27). That connection is a reasonable guess, not something the psalm states. The Greek Septuagint divides this psalm into two (vv1–11 and vv12–20), which is one reason the psalm numbers in older Catholic Bibles differ from Protestant ones. The psalm has three stanzas, each opening with a fresh call to praise (vv1, 7, 12), and each weaving together two kinds of praise: God's care for his people and his power over creation. The God who counts the stars also binds up broken hearts. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### He Heals the Brokenhearted (vv1–6)
- **"Praise the LORD! For it is good to sing praises to our God" (v1).** The psalm gives three reasons praise is right: it is "good," it is "pleasant," and it is "fitting." Praise is good because God deserves it, pleasant because it brings joy to those who offer it, and fitting because it suits creatures before their Creator (Ps 33:1; 92:1; 135:3). Henry puts it neatly: "Praising God is work that is its own wages."
- **"The LORD builds up Jerusalem" (v2).** The Hebrew verb is a participle, "is building": God is at work, not finished. For returned exiles living among rubble, this was faith speaking. The human builders were Zerubbabel, Ezra and Nehemiah, but the psalm credits the real builder. Psalm 127:1 had said it: "Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain."
- **"He gathers the outcasts of Israel" (v2).** "Outcasts" (*nidche*) means the scattered, the driven away, like sheep scattered from the flock. Isaiah 56:8 uses the same phrase: "The Lord GOD, who gathers the outcasts of Israel." The exile was God's judgment, and the return was God's mercy, a new exodus (Isa 11:12; Jer 31:10).
- **"He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds" (v3).** "Brokenhearted" (*shevure lev*) means those whose hearts are shattered, crushed by grief, guilt or loss. "Binds up" (*chavash*) is the word for wrapping a wound in bandages, the work of a doctor or a nurse. Isaiah 61:1 uses both words together: "he has sent me to bind up the brokenhearted." JFB says the verse applies first to the exiles but states "a general and precious truth." The returning exiles had lost homes, families and their sense of being God's people. God tended those wounds himself.
- **"He determines the number of the stars; he gives to all of them their names" (v4).** The psalm suddenly lifts its eyes from bandages to the night sky. God counted the stars Abraham could not count (Gen 15:5), and he names them, as a king calls the roll of his army (Isa 40:26). In Babylon, the stars were worshiped as gods and studied for omens. Here they are creatures, numbered and named by the LORD, who knows each one. The point is not astronomy but care: the God who knows every star knows every exile. Isaiah 40:26–29 makes exactly this argument: the one who calls the stars by name "gives power to the faint."
- **"Great is our Lord, and abundant in power; his understanding is beyond measure" (v5).** "Beyond measure" is literally "there is no number" (*'en mispar*). The same word, *mispar*, "number," appears in v4. The stars have a number, and God knows it. His understanding has no number at all. Isaiah 40:28 says, "his understanding is unsearchable."
- **"The LORD lifts up the humble; he casts the wicked to the ground" (v6).** "Humble" (*'anavim*) means the lowly, the afflicted, those who do not rely on their own strength. God reverses the world's order. Hannah sang it (1 Sam 2:7–8), Mary sang it (Luke 1:52), and Peter and James quote Proverbs to the same effect: "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (Prov 3:34; Jas 4:6; 1 Pet 5:5). The Tyndale notes link this line to 145:14 and 146:7–9.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v3 as God gathering "outcast sinners by his grace" and healing them with the comfort of pardon. On v5 he writes, "Man's knowledge is soon ended; but God's knowledge is a depth that can never be fathomed." JFB calls God's power over the stars "a pledge of His power to help His people," and gives only cross-references on vv1–2. Guzik passes on Meyer's remark that binding up a single broken heart is more wonderful than numbering the stars, and Kidner's point that the verse overturns the idea that ordinary human troubles are too small for God's notice.

#### He Covers the Heavens with Clouds (vv7–11)
- **"Sing to the LORD with thanksgiving" (v7).** "Sing" here is literally "answer" (*'enu*), as JFB notes. Praise is a response, an answer to what God has done. "Make melody… on the lyre" names the *kinnor*, the small stringed instrument David played (1 Sam 16:23). JFB takes it as grateful praise in reply to God's goodness.
- **"He covers the heavens with clouds; he prepares rain for the earth; he makes grass grow on the hills" (v8).** In Israel's climate, everything depended on the autumn and spring rains. The Canaanites credited rain to Baal, the storm god. Guzik passes on VanGemeren's point that the psalm quietly denies Baal the credit: the LORD covers the sky, the LORD prepares the rain, the LORD makes the grass grow. Jeremiah asked, "Are there any among the false gods of the nations that can bring rain?" (Jer 14:22). The Tyndale notes cite God's power over weather (77:17–18; 104:4).
- **"He gives to the beasts their food, and to the young ravens that cry" (v9).** Why ravens? They were unclean under the law (Lev 11:15) and were thought to neglect their young. Job 38:41 asks, "Who provides for the raven its prey, when its young ones cry to God for help?" Jesus took up this very picture: "Consider the ravens: they neither sow nor reap… and yet God feeds them. Of how much more value are you than the birds!" (Luke 12:24). If God hears the cry of baby ravens, he hears the cry of his people.
- **"His delight is not in the strength of the horse, nor his pleasure in the legs of a man" (v10).** The horse was the ancient world's war machine, pulling chariots and carrying cavalry (Ps 20:7; 33:16–17). "Legs of a man" (*shoqe ha'ish*) means a man's thighs or legs, the strength of a swift runner or a foot soldier. God is not impressed by military power or athletic strength. For a small post-exilic community with no army to speak of, this was good news. The Tyndale notes add that every human strength is God's gift, a reason for praise, not boasting (Jer 9:23–24).
- **"But the LORD takes pleasure in those who fear him, in those who hope in his steadfast love" (v11).** What does please God? Not strength, but fear and hope together. "Fear" is reverent awe, taking God seriously. "Hope" is trusting confidence in his "steadfast love" (*chesed*), his loyal covenant love. On their own, fear could become terror and hope could become presumption. Together they make a healthy faith. Psalm 33:18 has the same pair: "the eye of the LORD is on those who fear him, on those who hope in his steadfast love."
- **Commentators:** Henry urges believers to keep hope and fear in balance, and he sees the clouds of affliction bringing the showers that make righteousness grow. JFB says military advantages do not sway God, while those who fear and trust him win his favor. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's remark that fear and hope are both marks of new birth and balance each other, and Poole's note that ravens were thought careless of their young, which makes God's feeding them all the more striking.

#### Praise the LORD, O Jerusalem (vv12–20)
- **"Praise the LORD, O Jerusalem! Praise your God, O Zion!" (v12).** The third call to praise is addressed to the city itself. "Zion" is the hill of the temple and, by extension, the city and its people. The Septuagint begins a new psalm here.
- **"For he strengthens the bars of your gates" (v13).** Ancient city gates were closed at night and barred with heavy wooden or bronze beams set into the walls. Strong bars meant a secure city. When Nehemiah first heard of Jerusalem, "its gates are destroyed by fire" (Neh 1:3), and his account repeatedly mentions setting up "its doors, its bolts, and its bars" (Neh 3:3, 6, 13–15). JFB reads the bars as "means of defense against invaders." The Tyndale notes say they symbolize defenses against enemies.
- **"He blesses your children within you" (v13).** A city with children has a future. After the deportations and the years in exile, children playing in Jerusalem's streets were a sign of restoration (Zech 8:5).
- **"He makes peace in your borders; he fills you with the finest of the wheat" (v14).** "Peace" (*shalom*) means wholeness and well-being, not only the absence of war. "Finest of the wheat" is literally "the fat of wheat," the best part of the harvest (Deut 32:14; Ps 81:16). Guzik summarizes vv13–14 as four gifts: security, a future, peace and provision.
- **"He sends out his command to the earth; his word runs swiftly" (v15).** God's word is pictured as a runner, a royal messenger racing to carry out the king's command. JFB calls it "a swift messenger." For God, to speak is to act: "he spoke, and it came to be" (Ps 33:9). Paul prayed that "the word of the Lord may speed ahead" (2 Thess 3:1), using the same picture.
- **"He gives snow like wool; he scatters frost like ashes" (v16).** Jerusalem sits about 2,500 feet above sea level and does get snow in some winters. The similes are vivid: snow lying on the hills like fleece, white frost scattered like gray ash.
- **"He hurls down his crystals of ice like crumbs; who can stand before his cold?" (v17).** "Crystals of ice" (*qerach*) means ice or hail. "Like crumbs" (*kefittim*) means like morsels of bread, small pieces. JFB says it "perhaps here denotes hail." The question "who can stand before his cold?" is not exaggeration in a land where people had little protection from winter storms.
- **"He sends out his word, and melts them; he makes his wind blow and the waters flow" (v18).** The same word that brought the freeze brings the thaw. A warm wind blows, and the frozen ground turns into running water. The Tyndale notes say that by speaking his command, the Lord turns harsh winter to refreshing spring. "Wind" (*ruach*) is also the word for breath and Spirit. Henry draws the spiritual parallel: the word and the Spirit soften hearts, and "Converting grace softens the heart that was hard frozen."
- **"He declares his word to Jacob, his statutes and rules to Israel" (v19).** Now the psalm makes a turn. The "word" that commands snow and ice (vv15–18) is the same word given to Israel at Sinai. God speaks to creation, and creation obeys. He speaks to his people, and they receive the far greater gift of knowing his will. "Statutes" (*chuqqim*) and "rules" (*mishpatim*) are two standard names for the laws of the covenant (Deut 4:1).
- **"He has not dealt thus with any other nation; they do not know his rules" (v20).** Moses had said it: "what great nation is there, that has statutes and rules so righteous as all this law that I set before you today?" (Deut 4:8). Paul names it as Israel's chief privilege: "they were entrusted with the oracles of God" (Rom 3:2). The privilege was meant to be shared. Israel was to be a light to the nations (Isa 49:6), and that light would go out through the gospel.
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v20).** The final *Hallelujah* rounds off the psalm.
- **Commentators:** JFB says God revealed his will to Israel "alone," and calls the reader to join "the great hallelujah." It skips v12 entirely. Henry sees Jerusalem as a type of the church and reads the thaw as the gospel and the Spirit at work. Guzik passes on Kidner's observation that a God who speaks wants a relationship, not just obedience, and Maclaren's point that the privilege of receiving God's word brings the responsibility to obey it and make it known. Boice, as Guzik reports, tells how the winds that scattered the Spanish Armada in 1588 led England to strike a medal saying that God blew and they were scattered.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 147 sings of a God who rebuilds a ruined city, gathers the scattered, heals broken hearts and sends his word running into the world. In Jesus all of it comes together. He read Isaiah 61 in the synagogue at Nazareth, the passage that echoes v3, and announced that it was fulfilled in him (Luke 4:18–21). He gathered outcasts: tax collectors, lepers, prostitutes and Gentiles. He pointed to the ravens to teach his disciples not to be anxious (Luke 12:24). He is the Word himself, sent from the Father to the earth (John 1:1, 14), the Word that "upholds the universe" (Heb 1:3) and also melts frozen hearts. And through him the privilege of v20 has been opened wide: the God who once gave his word to Israel alone now makes it known "to all nations" (Rom 16:26), building a new Jerusalem whose gates will never be shut (Rev 21:25).
