*Setting:* About 1003 BC, near the end of David's seven and a half years as king of Judah in Hebron. Saul's son Ish-bosheth has been a king in name only, ruling the northern tribes from Mahanaim, east of the Jordan, while his cousin Abner held the real power (2:8–9). Abner had just quarreled with Ish-bosheth, offered to bring all Israel over to David, feasted with David at Hebron and left "in peace," only to be murdered by Joab at the city gate (3:6–30). David mourned Abner publicly so that all Israel would know he had no part in it (3:31–37). This short chapter removes the last obstacle to David becoming king of all Israel, and once again it does so through a crime David did not want and would not reward.

#### Ish-bosheth Assassinated (vv1–12)
- **"His courage failed, and all Israel was dismayed" (v1).** Literally, "his hands went limp." Ish-bosheth's whole kingdom had rested on Abner. With Abner dead, the king loses heart, and his people with him. A kingdom held up by one man falls when the man falls.
- **"Two men who were captains of raiding bands" (v2).** Baanah and Rechab led small units that made raids, the kind of men a weak king depended on. Notice who they are: "sons of Rimmon a man of Benjamin." They are from Saul's own tribe. Ish-bosheth is betrayed by his own people.
- **"From Beeroth (for Beeroth also is counted part of Benjamin)" (vv2–3).** Beeroth, "wells," was one of four Gibeonite towns that had tricked Joshua into a peace treaty (Josh 9:17). It was later counted in Benjamin's territory (Josh 18:25). The note that "the Beerothites fled to Gittaim" probably refers to Saul's attempt to wipe out the Gibeonites, which is mentioned only later (21:1–2). So these two men may have been Benjaminites living in a town Saul's violence had emptied. The text does not give their motive, but their speech to David (v8) shows they hoped for a reward.
- **"Jonathan, the son of Saul, had a son who was crippled in his feet" (v4).** The story stops for a moment to introduce a child. When news of Gilboa came, he was five years old. His nurse grabbed him and ran, fearing the Philistines would kill the royal family, and in her rush he fell and was lamed for life. The verse is placed here on purpose. With Ish-bosheth about to die, the only grandson of Saul in the direct line is a disabled boy, about twelve years old by now. In that world a lame child was not seen as a possible king. Saul's dynasty is finished.
- **"His name was Mephibosheth" (v4).** Like Ish-bosheth, his name seems to have been changed. 1 Chronicles calls him Merib-baal (1 Chr 8:34; 9:40). The scribes who copied Samuel seem to have replaced "baal" with *bosheth*, "shame," to avoid writing the name of the Canaanite god. This child will return in chapter 9, in one of the most beautiful stories of David's life.
- **"About the heat of the day… as he was taking his noonday rest" (v5).** In the hot climate people rested at midday (compare Gen 18:1). Ish-bosheth lies on his bed in the heat, unguarded.
- **"As if to get wheat" (v6).** The Hebrew is difficult. It seems the two captains came in on the pretense of collecting grain, perhaps rations for their men. The Greek Septuagint has a different text here: the woman who kept the door had been cleaning wheat and dozed off, so the men slipped past her. Some English translations (NRSV, RSV) follow that reading. The ESV follows the Hebrew. Either way, they got in easily.
- **"They stabbed him in the stomach" (v6).** The same blow that killed Asahel (2:23) and Abner (3:27). Three men, struck in the same place, as one violent act leads to the next.
- **"As he lay on his bed in his bedroom… beheaded him" (v7).** Verse 7 retells v6 in more detail, which is a common Hebrew way of telling a story: a summary, then a closer look. The killing is cowardly. Ish-bosheth is in his own home, asleep, unarmed.
- **"Went by the way of the Arabah all night" (v7).** The Arabah is the Jordan Valley. They walk all night from Mahanaim to Hebron, a long journey of perhaps sixty miles or more, carrying the head as proof.
- **"Here is the head of Ish-bosheth, the son of Saul, your enemy, who sought your life" (v8).** They call Saul David's enemy. David never called him that. They also wrap their crime in religious words: "The LORD has avenged my lord the king this day." It is an old temptation, to do something wicked and call it God's will.
- **"As the LORD lives, who has redeemed my life out of every adversity" (v9).** David's answer starts with God. "Redeemed" is *padah*, to ransom or rescue. David is saying that God has been his protector all along. He did not need murderers to deliver him, and he never asked them to.
- **"I seized him and killed him at Ziklag" (v10).** David reminds them of the Amalekite who claimed to have killed Saul (1:14–16). "He thought he was bringing good news." So did these men.
- **"How much more, when wicked men have killed a righteous man in his own house on his bed" (v11).** "Righteous" here means innocent in this case, not that Ish-bosheth was a holy man. He had done nothing to deserve murder. The Amalekite at least claimed Saul asked to die on a battlefield. These men killed a sleeping man at home. "Require his blood at your hand" is the language of Genesis 9:5–6: whoever sheds human blood must answer for it.
- **"Cut off their hands and feet and hanged them beside the pool at Hebron" (v12).** This is harsh to modern readers. The hands that did the murder and the feet that ran to Hebron are cut off, and their bodies are displayed publicly by the town's water supply, where everyone would see. It was a public statement: David would not reward treachery. It also made clear to the northern tribes, who might suspect him, that he had no hand in their king's death. Deuteronomy 21:22–23 required such bodies to be taken down before nightfall.
- **"They took the head of Ish-bosheth and buried it in the tomb of Abner at Hebron" (v12).** David gives his rival an honorable burial, next to the man who had made him king. It is a quiet, dignified ending for the last king of Saul's house.
- **Commentators:** Henry uses Ish-bosheth's midday nap to warn against laziness: "The idle soul is an easy prey to the destroyer." Guzik agrees that Ish-bosheth's unguarded rest showed carelessness. He also points out that Ish-bosheth's courage failed because he had trusted a man, Abner, not God. On the murderers, Henry says they "shed innocent blood, from the basest motives," and that David "would not be beholden to any to help him by unlawful practices." JFB notes that "bloody trophies of rebels and conspirators have always been acceptable to princes in the East," which is exactly why David's refusal stands out. JFB calls the punishment "the attestation of David's abhorrence." On v4 JFB explains that in that culture a lame man was "considered unfit for exercising the duties of sovereignty." Guzik lists David's reasons: he had promised to protect Saul's family (1 Sam 24:21–22), he would not do evil for a good end, and he wanted all Israel to see he took no part in it. JFB does not comment on vv1–3 or vv9–11.

#### Pointing to Christ
Again and again, men try to hand David the kingdom by violence, and again and again he refuses it. He trusts the God "who has redeemed my life out of every adversity" to give him the throne in God's own time. Jesus faced the same temptation in a far greater form. Satan offered him all the kingdoms of the world by a shortcut (Matt 4:8–10), and the crowds tried to make him king by force (John 6:15). He refused, and he received his kingdom by way of the cross. And the lame boy hidden in this chapter is waiting for a king's kindness. In chapter 9 David will seek out Mephibosheth and seat him at his table, a picture of the grace by which the Son of David welcomes those who cannot walk to him on their own (Luke 14:13, 21).
