*Setting:* Some years into Saul's reign, perhaps around 1040–1030 BC. Saul has won real victories (14:47–48), but he has already been told that his dynasty will not last because he would not wait for the LORD's word at Gilgal (13:13–14). Chapter 14 showed him making a reckless oath and nearly executing his own son. Now Samuel brings him one more clear command from God, a test of whether he will be a king under the LORD's authority. The enemy is Amalek, a nomadic people of the Negev desert and the Sinai, descended from Esau's grandson (Gen 36:12). They had attacked Israel right after the Exodus, and the LORD had sworn to blot out their memory (Exod 17:8–16; Deut 25:17–19). This chapter is where Saul himself is rejected as king. It is one of the most searching chapters in the Old Testament on the difference between religious activity and obedience.

#### The LORD Rejects Saul (vv1–35)
- **"The LORD sent me to anoint you… now therefore listen to the words of the LORD" (v1).** Samuel begins by reminding Saul who made him king and what that requires. "Listen" (*shama'*, which also means "obey") is the key word of the whole chapter. It appears again and again: "listen" (v1), "obey the voice" (vv19, 20, 22), "obeyed their voice" (v24).
- **"I have noted what Amalek did to Israel… when they came up out of Egypt" (v2).** Amalek attacked Israel at Rephidim, and Deuteronomy adds the detail that they cut down the stragglers at the rear, "faint and weary," and "did not fear God" (Deut 25:18). It was the first attack on God's newly rescued people. The LORD swore then that he would "have war with Amalek from generation to generation" (Exod 17:16). And Amalek had not changed. They had joined Israel's oppressors in the time of the judges (Judg 3:13; 6:3–5) and were still raiding Israel (14:48). Later they would burn Ziklag and carry off David's family (ch 30).
- **"Devote to destruction all that they have" (v3).** The verb is *charam*, and the noun *cherem* means something "devoted," handed over to God completely and taken out of human use. In war it meant total destruction, with no plunder kept. It was used for Jericho (Josh 6:17–21) and for the Canaanite cities whose sin had reached its full measure (Gen 15:16; Deut 7:1–5; 20:16–18). "Kill both man and woman, child and infant" is one of the hardest commands in Scripture.
- **How Christians read this command.** Faithful readers have handled it in a few ways, and most combine them. First, this was a specific act of judgment commanded directly by God, the Judge of all the earth, after centuries of patience, not a standing permission for any nation or person. Second, it was limited to particular peoples at a particular stage of redemptive history, under the old covenant, and Jesus explicitly sets his kingdom apart from the sword (John 18:36; Matt 26:52). Third, some scholars point out that ancient war reports often used sweeping language ("all," "none left") as stock phrases for decisive victory. Amalekites do appear later (27:8; 30:1), though that may simply be because Saul did not finish the work. None of these explanations makes the passage easy, and it should not be made easy. What the chapter itself stresses is that this was God's judgment, so no one was to profit from it.
- **"Two hundred thousand men on foot, and ten thousand men of Judah" (v4).** Telaim was probably Telem, a town in the far south of Judah (Josh 15:24). As before, the word "thousand" may mean a military unit rather than exactly a thousand men (see 11:8).
- **"Go, depart… lest I destroy you with them" (v6).** The Kenites were a clan of nomadic metalworkers related to Moses' father-in-law, who had guided Israel in the wilderness (Num 10:29–32; Judg 1:16). Saul honors that old kindness and lets them leave before the attack. This is a good act, and it shows that the judgment was aimed at Amalek specifically.
- **"From Havilah as far as Shur, which is east of Egypt" (v7).** The same phrase describes the territory of Ishmael's descendants (Gen 25:18). Shur was the desert on Egypt's northeastern border. Saul's campaign swept across the whole Amalekite region of the northern Sinai and the Negev.
- **"He took Agag the king of the Amalekites alive" (v8).** "Agag" may have been a royal title among the Amalekites, as "Pharaoh" was in Egypt. Balaam had prophesied that Israel's king would be "higher than Agag" (Num 24:7). Kings in the ancient world liked to parade captured enemy kings as trophies, and that may be why Saul kept him.
- **"But Saul and the people spared Agag and the best of the sheep… and all that was good" (v9).** "Spared" here means "had pity on," the same word used in v3: "Do not spare them." Saul did exactly what he was told not to. The text spells out what they did destroy: "All that was despised and worthless." Their obedience went exactly as far as it was free.
- **"I regret that I have made Saul king" (v11).** The verb is *nacham*, "to be sorry, to relent, to grieve." It is used of God before the flood (Gen 6:6) and when he relents from judgment (Exod 32:14; Jonah 3:10). But v29 says, using the same verb, that God "will not… have regret, for he is not a man." Both are true. God does not change his character or his purposes, and he is not caught off guard. But he truly responds to people. When they turn from him, his dealings with them change, and he is genuinely grieved. The Bible uses human language to describe this because there is no other language we could understand. The chapter frames this hard truth on both ends: God regrets making Saul king (v11, v35), and yet God "is not a man, that he should have regret" (v29).
- **"Samuel was angry, and he cried to the LORD all night" (v11).** Angry at Saul, perhaps also at the situation, and maybe even at God's verdict. And he prays all night. Samuel had anointed Saul and loved him. He had promised never to stop praying for Israel (12:23).
- **"He set up a monument for himself" (v12).** This Carmel is a town in the hill country of Judah, south of Hebron, not the famous Mount Carmel in the north (compare 25:2). "Monument" is literally "a hand," a stone marker of victory. Absalom later set up a "monument" with the same word "to keep my name in remembrance" (2 Sam 18:18). The man who was once "little in his own eyes" (v17) is now building memorials to himself.
- **"Blessed be you to the LORD. I have performed the commandment of the LORD" (v13).** Saul greets Samuel warmly and claims full obedience, either fooling himself or trying to fool the prophet.
- **"What then is this bleating of the sheep in my ears?" (v14).** One of the most quietly devastating lines in the Bible. The evidence is making noise all around them.
- **"The people spared the best… to sacrifice to the LORD your God" (v15).** Saul's defense has three parts: the people did it, it was for a good religious purpose, and everything else was destroyed. Note the pronouns: "*the people* spared," but "*we* have devoted to destruction." And note "the LORD *your* God." Saul says it three times in this chapter (vv15, 21, 30). He speaks of the LORD as Samuel's God.
- **"Though you are little in your own eyes, are you not the head of the tribes of Israel?" (v17).** Samuel recalls Saul's early humility (9:21) and reminds him that he is the one responsible. A king cannot blame his army.
- **"Why did you pounce on the spoil?" (v19).** The word "pounce" is the same one used when the hungry army fell on the animals and ate them with the blood (14:32). Whatever Saul said about sacrifice, Samuel sees greed.
- **"I have obeyed the voice of the LORD" (v20).** Saul simply repeats his claim, then admits, almost in passing, "I have brought Agag." Bringing Agag alive *was* the disobedience.
- **"To obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams" (v22).** Samuel answers in poetry, and it becomes one of the central themes of the prophets. The fat was considered the best part of an offering, reserved for God (Lev 3:16). God does not want the best of the plunder burned on an altar if the price is ignoring his word. The prophets press the same point (Ps 40:6–8; 51:16–17; Isa 1:11–17; Hos 6:6; Mic 6:6–8), and Jesus quotes Hosea's version twice: "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Matt 9:13; 12:7).
- **"Rebellion is as the sin of divination, and presumption is as iniquity and idolatry" (v23).** Divination (*qesem*) was seeking hidden knowledge from spirits or omens, forbidden in Israel (Deut 18:10). "Idolatry" here is *teraphim*, household idols (as in Gen 31:19). Samuel's point is that open rebellion against God's word is as serious as consulting spirits or bowing to idols. A self-willed heart has made itself its own god. The irony is bitter. Saul, who disobeyed God here, will one day end his life consulting a medium (ch 28).
- **"Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, he has also rejected you from being king" (v23).** In chapter 13, Saul lost his dynasty. Now he loses his kingship. The sentence will take years to carry out, but in God's sight it is done.
- **"I have sinned… because I feared the people and obeyed their voice" (v24).** At last Saul says "I have sinned," the same words David will say (2 Sam 12:13). But Saul still blames someone else. And he admits the real problem: he "obeyed *their* voice" instead of the LORD's. That was what Israel had done in asking for a king (8:7). Now the king does it too.
- **"Return with me that I may bow before the LORD" (v25).** What Saul wants is for Samuel to be seen at his side, so his position will not be shaken.
- **"Saul seized the skirt of his robe, and it tore" (v27).** Saul grabs the hem of the prophet's robe, perhaps begging, perhaps holding him back. The robe tears, and Samuel turns it into a sign: "The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you this day" (v28). The image returns in later stories. David will cut the hem of Saul's robe in a cave and be struck with guilt (24:4–5). The prophet Ahijah will tear his robe into twelve pieces as a sign that the kingdom is being torn from Solomon's son (1 Kgs 11:29–31).
- **"A neighbor of yours, who is better than you" (v28).** Samuel does not yet know who. Chapter 16 will reveal it is David.
- **"The Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret" (v29).** "Glory" is *netsach*, a rare title for God found only here. The word means "eminence" or "enduring one," "the Everlasting of Israel." Balaam said much the same: "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind" (Num 23:19). The verdict on Saul will not be reversed.
- **"I have sinned; yet honor me now before the elders" (v30).** Saul's second confession immediately turns to his reputation. This is the clearest picture of the difference between sorrow over sin and sorrow over consequences (2 Cor 7:10). Samuel goes back with him, probably so the nation is not thrown into chaos and so the work left undone can be finished.
- **"Agag came to him cheerfully" (v32).** The Hebrew word is uncertain. It may mean "cheerfully" or "confidently," expecting to be spared. Some connect it to a different root and read "in chains" or "trembling." His words, "Surely the bitterness of death is past," fit the first reading: he thought the danger was over.
- **"As your sword has made women childless, so shall your mother be childless among women" (v33).** Samuel's words show that Agag himself was no innocent victim. He had killed many. The judgment matches the crime. Samuel then carries out the command Saul would not complete, "before the LORD in Gilgal," the very place where Saul's kingship had been confirmed (11:15). The story has a long tail. Haman, who tried to wipe out the Jews in Persia centuries later, is called "the Agagite" (Esth 3:1), and his opponent Mordecai is from the family of Kish, Saul's father (Esth 2:5).
- **"Samuel did not see Saul again until the day of his death" (v35).** They lived only a few miles apart, Samuel at Ramah and Saul at Gibeah. The break is final. (Saul will see Samuel once more, in a strange scene at Naioth, 19:24, but the phrase means Samuel never again came to him as his adviser.)
- **"But Samuel grieved over Saul" (v35).** "Grieved" is the word for mourning the dead. Samuel speaks God's hard verdict and still weeps for the man. "And the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king over Israel." The chapter ends where it began, with God's sorrow.
- **Commentators:** All three see partial obedience as disobedience. Henry notes that Saul "destroyed only the refuse, that was good for little," and adds a line that goes to the heart of the chapter: "Those are unfit and unworthy to rule over men, who are not willing that God should rule over them." On God's regret Henry says, "Repentance in God is not a change of mind, as it is in us, but a change of method," much as Guzik, citing older writers, calls it a change in God's work rather than his will. JFB calls the monument "a vainglorious trophy," notes it is literally "a hand," and says Saul was either "blinded by a partial and delusive self-love" or "acting the part of a bold and artful hypocrite." JFB judges Saul's confession as coming from "a sense of danger," not "sincere repentance." Guzik addresses the command against Amalek directly, stressing that it was God's judgment after long patience and that Jesus' kingdom does not advance by the sword. He cites Maclaren's line that partial obedience is complete disobedience, and points out that the leftover Amalekites came back to trouble Israel, through Haman and through the Amalekite who claimed to have killed Saul (2 Sam 1:8–10). On Agag's last words, Henry: "Many think the bitterness of death is past when it is not gone by."

#### Pointing to Christ
Saul offered God sacrifices taken from his own disobedience, and God said, "to obey is better than sacrifice." The letter to the Hebrews takes up Psalm 40 to say the same thing of Jesus: "Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body have you prepared for me… I have come to do your will, O God" (Heb 10:5–7). Jesus is the King who obeyed the Father's voice to the end, and his sacrifice was the obedience. Where Saul "feared the people and obeyed their voice," Jesus would not bend to the crowd, even when it cost him his life. Saul clung to the hem of a robe to keep his honor. Jesus let his own clothes be taken and divided (John 19:23–24) and "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death" (Phil 2:8). And the God who "is not a man, that he should have regret" kept every promise in his Son, in whom "all the promises of God find their Yes" (2 Cor 1:20).
