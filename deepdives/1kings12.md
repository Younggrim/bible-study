*Setting:* About 931 BC, right after Solomon's death. Chapter 11 told how Solomon's foreign wives turned his heart to other gods, and how God announced he would tear the kingdom away and give ten tribes to Solomon's servant. The prophet Ahijah tore his new cloak into twelve pieces and gave ten to Jeroboam son of Nebat, a gifted officer over the forced labor of the house of Joseph (11:26–39). Solomon tried to kill Jeroboam, who fled to Egypt. Now Solomon's son Rehoboam, forty-one years old (14:21), comes to be crowned. In a single meeting the kingdom David built splits in two: Judah in the south, loyal to David's line, and Israel in the north under Jeroboam. The split will last for the rest of the Old Testament. Chronicles tells the same story in 2 Chronicles 10–11.

#### Rehoboam's Folly (vv1–15)
- **"Rehoboam went to Shechem, for all Israel had come to Shechem to make him king" (v1).** Rehoboam had to go to them. Shechem lay in the hill country of Ephraim, the heart of the northern tribes, about 30 miles north of Jerusalem. It was full of history. God first promised the land to Abraham there (Gen 12:6–7). Jacob bought land there (Gen 33:18–20). Joshua renewed the covenant there (Josh 24), and Joseph was buried there (Josh 24:32). It was also where Abimelech had once made himself king and failed (Judg 9). That the tribes met there, not in Jerusalem, hints they came with conditions. David himself had been made king over the northern tribes by a covenant (2 Sam 5:3), and they expected the same.
- **Rehoboam's name.** "Rehoboam" means "the people is enlarged" or "he enlarges the people." The irony is sharp: he will shrink his people to one tribe and a half. His mother was Naamah the Ammonite (14:21), one of the foreign wives. Guzik notes he is the only son of Solomon named in Scripture.
- **"Jeroboam returned from Egypt" (vv2–3).** Pharaoh Shishak had sheltered him (11:40), and that same Shishak will invade Judah five years later (14:25). The Hebrew text of Kings literally says Jeroboam "lived in Egypt." The ESV, with the Greek Septuagint and 2 Chronicles 10:2, reads "returned from Egypt." The Greek version of Kings also has a long extra account of Jeroboam's life that is not in the Hebrew. Most scholars take it as a later retelling. Verse 20 says the people sent for Jeroboam after the break, so vv2–3 may anticipate the outcome, or he may have been present from the start as their spokesman.
- **"Your father made our yoke heavy" (v4).** Solomon's glory had been paid for by the people: food for the court every day (4:22–23), forced labor gangs of 30,000 sent in rotation to Lebanon (5:13–14), heavy taxation, and Jeroboam himself put in charge of the labor of Joseph's tribes (11:28). The north felt it most. Notice what the people do *not* complain about: Solomon's idolatry. JFB says they wanted relief from "civil oppressions, not idolatry," and Guzik makes the same point. Their complaint is real but entirely about money and labor.
- **"Go away for three days, then come again to me" (v5).** Taking three days was a normal step before a serious decision. So far, it is sensible.
- **"The old men, who had stood before Solomon his father" (v6).** These were Solomon's senior advisers. Their counsel is wise and simple: "If you will be a servant to this people today and serve them... then they will be your servants forever" (v7). One day of humility would buy a lifetime of loyalty. It is the Bible's picture of servant leadership. Jesus said, "Whoever would be great among you must be your servant" (Mark 10:43).
- **"The young men who had grown up with him" (v8).** Literally "the children" or "the boys." They were not teenagers. If they grew up with Rehoboam, they were around forty. JFB says they were "inexperienced" rather than young in years. They had grown up privileged in Solomon's palace and knew nothing of the farms and labor gangs. Guzik calls what Rehoboam did "advice shopping," asking around until someone says what you wanted to hear.
- **"My little finger is thicker than my father's thighs" (v10).** The Hebrew is literally "my littleness is thicker than my father's loins." Some scholars think the word for "little finger" is a crude euphemism, a boast about virility. Either way, it is a swaggering claim: the weakest part of me is stronger than the strongest part of my father.
- **"My father disciplined you with whips, but I will discipline you with scorpions" (v11).** "Scorpions" here was probably a whip studded with metal barbs or spikes, whose wounds stung like a scorpion's. The Tyndale notes and JFB, who calls them "thongs thickly set with sharp iron points," both read it that way. The young men want Rehoboam to rule by fear.
- **"The king answered the people harshly" (v13).** He repeats the young men's words almost exactly. Ecclesiastes had warned, "Woe to you, O land, when your king is a child" (Eccl 10:16), and asked who knows "whether he will be wise or a fool" who inherits the work (Eccl 2:18–19).
- **"It was a turn of affairs brought about by the LORD that he might fulfill his word" (v15).** "Turn of affairs" (*sibbah*) means a turning or a cause, something God brought around. The narrator gives two explanations at once. Rehoboam freely chose folly, and God was at work through that folly to fulfill what Ahijah had prophesied (11:29–39). The Bible does this often: Joseph's brothers meant evil, and "God meant it for good" (Gen 50:20). God does not cause the sin, but he governs the outcome. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that God was in the result but had nothing to do with the sin or folly.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Never was man more blinded by pride, and desire of arbitrary power, than which nothing is more fatal." He also has a word for the complainers: "Factious spirits will never want something to complain of," and says "God serves his own wise and righteous purposes by the imprudences and sins of men." Guzik notes that a wise leader recognizes when the people's request is right, and Rehoboam did not. He was not Solomon, and he had to earn loyalty rather than assume it.

#### The Kingdom Divided (vv16–24)
- **"What portion do we have in David? We have no inheritance in the son of Jesse" (v16).** This is almost word for word the cry of Sheba's rebellion against David (2 Sam 20:1). The north–south split was old. It went back to the years when David ruled only Judah from Hebron while Saul's son ruled the north (2 Sam 2:8–11), and it flared again after Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 19:41–43). Calling David "the son of Jesse" is a put-down: he is just a man from a family in Bethlehem.
- **"To your tents, O Israel! Look now to your own house, David" (v16).** "To your tents" was the cry for an assembly to break up and go home. The people walk out. "Your own house" is a jab: let David's family rule only its own tribe.
- **"Rehoboam reigned over the people of Israel who lived in the cities of Judah" (v17).** Many northerners lived in Judah's towns, and they stayed under Rehoboam.
- **"Rehoboam sent Adoram, who was taskmaster over the forced labor" (v18).** Adoram (also called Adoniram) had overseen forced labor under David and Solomon (2 Sam 20:24; 1 Kgs 4:6; 5:14). Of all the men Rehoboam could send to calm the north, he sent the face of the very burden they had complained about. "All Israel stoned him to death." Rehoboam "hurried to mount his chariot to flee to Jerusalem." The king who promised scorpions runs for his life.
- **"So Israel has been in rebellion against the house of David to this day" (v19).** "To this day" shows the writer's sources came from before the northern kingdom fell in 722 BC. From here on, "Israel" in Kings usually means the ten northern tribes, and "Judah" the southern kingdom.
- **"They... made him king over all Israel" (v20).** Jeroboam is now king, exactly as Ahijah foretold. "None that followed the house of David but the tribe of Judah only." Benjamin is named in the next verse. The prophecy spoke of "one tribe" kept for David (11:32), and Benjamin, so small and so close to Jerusalem, was largely absorbed into Judah. Simeon lay inside Judah's territory too. So the "one tribe" is Judah with its neighbors.
- **"180,000 chosen warriors" (v21).** Rehoboam's first instinct is war, to win back by force what he lost by pride.
- **"The word of God came to Shemaiah the man of God" (v22).** "Man of God" is a title for a prophet, a messenger who speaks for God. Shemaiah later wrote a history of Rehoboam's reign (2 Chr 12:15). His message: "You shall not go up or fight against your relatives the people of Israel... for this thing is from me" (v24). "Relatives" is literally "brothers." The tribes are still one family.
- **"So they listened to the word of the LORD and went home again" (v24).** To his credit, Rehoboam obeys. Guzik admires that, even if it came partly from weakness, and passes on Spurgeon's wonder that one man with a simple word from God turned back an army. The peace did not last. "There was war between Rehoboam and Jeroboam continually" (14:30), probably in border skirmishes rather than full invasion.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the people "speak very unbecomingly of David," whatever their grievances, and calls sending Adoram unwise. On Shemaiah: "When we know God's mind, we must submit, how much soever it crosses our own mind." Guzik, with Knapp, says Rehoboam should have been thankful for the two tribes he kept rather than bitter over the ten he lost.

#### Jeroboam's Golden Calves (vv25–33)
- **"Jeroboam built Shechem... and he went out from there and built Penuel" (v25).** "Built" here means fortified. Shechem became his first capital. Penuel (or Peniel) lay east of the Jordan, where Jacob wrestled with God (Gen 32:30). It guarded the trade road and his eastern territory. Later he moved to Tirzah (14:17).
- **"Now the kingdom will turn back to the house of David" (v26).** God had promised Jeroboam, through Ahijah, a "sure house" like David's if he walked in God's ways (11:38). But Jeroboam does not trust the promise. He "said in his heart." Every Israelite was to go up to the place God chose three times a year for the great feasts (Deut 16:16), and that place was now the temple in Jerusalem, in Rehoboam's capital. Jeroboam fears that worship there will draw his people's hearts back to Judah's king, "and they will kill me."
- **"So the king took counsel and made two calves of gold" (v28).** Guzik, following Dilday, notes that Jeroboam took counsel with himself, not with God. Bulls were a common religious symbol in the region. In Canaan the bull was linked to the storm god Baal-Hadad, and in Egypt, where Jeroboam had just lived, to the Apis bull. Many scholars think Jeroboam meant the calves not as new gods but as pedestals or thrones for the invisible LORD, the way the cherubim served above the ark in Jerusalem. That may have been the intent. But the people soon treated the calves as gods, and the prophets condemned them outright (Hos 8:5–6; 10:5; 13:2).
- **"Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" (v28).** These are almost exactly Aaron's words at the golden calf at Sinai: "These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!" (Exod 32:4). The writer wants readers to hear the echo. Israel's oldest sin has been reinstalled as national policy. Even the sales pitch is about convenience: "You have gone up to Jerusalem long enough."
- **"He set one in Bethel, and the other he put in Dan" (v29).** Bethel was on the southern border of Israel, about 11 miles north of Jerusalem, a place full of sacred memory. Abraham built an altar near there (Gen 12:8). Jacob saw the ladder to heaven and named it "house of God" (Gen 28:10–19). The ark rested there in the time of the judges (Judg 20:26–27). Dan was at the far northern edge, where the tribe of Dan had once set up their own shrine with a carved image and priests (Judg 18:30–31). Archaeologists at Tel Dan have uncovered a large raised platform and altar from this period, widely identified as Jeroboam's high place.
- **"Then this thing became a sin" (v30).** The writer's verdict is short. "The people went as far as Dan to be before one" shows how eagerly they took it up. From here on, almost every northern king is judged by the same standard: he "walked in the way of Jeroboam and in his sin which he made Israel to sin" (15:34; 16:26). The phrase or close versions of it run through Kings until the northern kingdom falls (2 Kgs 17:21–23).
- **"He also made temples on high places and appointed priests from among all the people, who were not of the Levites" (v31).** The law limited the priesthood to Aaron's descendants within the tribe of Levi (Num 3:10; 18:7). Jeroboam opened it to anyone. The Chronicler adds that the Levites and other faithful northerners left their homes and moved to Judah (2 Chr 11:13–16). Guzik, via Knapp, calls this a blunder that strengthened Judah. JFB suggests the Levites refused to serve, so Jeroboam had to draw priests from elsewhere. Later Jeroboam ordained anyone who wanted the job (13:33).
- **"A feast on the fifteenth day of the eighth month like the feast that was in Judah" (v32).** The Feast of Booths (Tabernacles), the great harvest festival, fell on the fifteenth day of the *seventh* month (Lev 23:34). Jeroboam keeps the day and moves the month. The Tyndale notes suggest the change may have matched the later harvest in the north or the end of the Canaanite farming year. JFB thinks it was to break the feast's old associations with Jerusalem. Either way, it is close enough to feel familiar and different enough to keep people home.
- **"He offered sacrifices on the altar... in the month that he had devised from his own heart" (vv32–33).** "Devised from his own heart" is the narrator's final judgment. The whole system, calves, shrines, priests, calendar, was man-made. The king himself goes up to the altar to offer. The next chapter begins exactly here, with Jeroboam standing at the altar when a man of God arrives from Judah.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Jeroboam distrusted the providence of God; he would contrive ways and means, and sinful ones too, for his own safety." He adds that "a practical disbelief of God's all-sufficiency is at the bottom of all our departures from him." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's observation that people will accept worship more readily when they design the forms themselves, and he applies "devised from his own heart" to the self-designed faith common today. JFB thinks the calves were modeled on Egyptian bull symbols Jeroboam saw in exile.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two kings fail in one chapter in opposite ways. Rehoboam refuses to serve and loses the kingdom. Jeroboam is given a kingdom by God's promise but will not trust the promise, and builds a religion of convenience to protect his throne. Both stand in contrast with the King to come. Jesus did what the old men advised and Rehoboam refused. He came "not to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45), and his yoke is "easy" and his burden "light" (Matt 11:30), the very thing Israel begged for at Shechem. And where Jeroboam split the worship of God's people, Jesus told a Samaritan woman, from the north's own heartland near Shechem, that the hour was coming when true worshipers would worship the Father "in spirit and truth," neither on that mountain nor only in Jerusalem (John 4:5, 21–24). Ezekiel foresaw the two sticks, Judah and Joseph, joined into one in God's hand under "one king," David's greater Son (Ezek 37:15–24).
