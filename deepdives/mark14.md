*Setting:* Jerusalem and Bethany, Wednesday to early Friday of the last week, around AD 30. Jesus has foretold the temple's destruction and told his disciples to stay awake (ch 13). Now the story he predicted three times (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34) begins to happen. Passover, the great feast remembering Israel's rescue from Egypt, had swelled Jerusalem with pilgrims, perhaps several times its normal population. Chapter 14 moves from a dinner in Bethany to the Last Supper, the garden of Gethsemane, the arrest and a night trial before the high priest. It ends with Peter in tears. Mark tells it simply and lets the contrasts speak: a woman's costly love beside a disciple's betrayal, Jesus' faithfulness beside his friends' failure.

#### The Plot to Kill Jesus (vv1–2)
- **"Two days before the Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread" (v1).**
- **"By stealth… Not during the feast, lest there be an uproar" (vv1–2).** The leaders feared the crowds, full of Galileans who admired Jesus and stirred by a festival of national freedom. Their plan is to wait. God's plan is that the Lamb dies at Passover, and Judas's offer changes their timetable.

#### Jesus Anointed at Bethany (vv3–9)
- **"In the house of Simon the leper" (v3).** John places a similar dinner at Bethany with Martha serving and Lazarus at the table, and names the woman as Mary, Lazarus's sister (John 12:1–8). Luke's story of a sinful woman anointing Jesus in Galilee (Luke 7:36–50) is probably a different event.
- **"An alabaster flask of ointment of pure nard, very costly" (v3).** Nard (spikenard) was a fragrant oil from a plant grown in the Himalayas, imported at great cost. Alabaster flasks had long thin necks that were sealed and had to be broken to open. She broke it, holding nothing back.
- **"Poured it over his head" (v3).** Prophets anointed kings on the head (1 Sam 10:1; 2 Kings 9:6). "Christ" and "Messiah" both mean "anointed one." The only anointing Jesus receives in Mark comes from an unnamed woman, not a priest.
- **"More than three hundred denarii" (v5).** A denarius was a day's wage, so this was nearly a year's income. John says Judas led the complaint, and not because he cared for the poor (John 12:4–6). "Why was the ointment wasted?" The word for "waste" is *apōleia*, which also means "destruction" or "perdition." John uses the same word of Judas himself, "the son of destruction" (John 17:12).
- **"You always have the poor with you" (v7).** Jesus is echoing Deuteronomy 15:11, which goes on: "therefore I command you, 'You shall open wide your hand to your brother, to the needy and to the poor.'" He is not dismissing the poor. He is saying that this moment is unique. Giving to the poor at Passover was expected, and they would have every chance to do good for them later.
- **"She has anointed my body beforehand for burial" (v8).** Whether she understood what she was doing or not, Jesus gives her act its meaning. When the women come with spices on Sunday, there will be no body to anoint (16:1–6). Hers is the only anointing he gets.
- **Commentators:** Guzik follows John in naming the woman as Mary of Bethany and distinguishes her from the woman in Luke 7. He points out the irony of the word *apōleia*: Judas called her gift a waste while wasting his own life, and the lexicon confirms both uses. He also warns against using "she has done what she could" to set the bar so low that doing nothing counts. JFB calls nard "a celebrated aromatic" and her act "a good work on me."

#### Judas to Betray Jesus (vv10–11)
- **"Judas Iscariot, who was one of the twelve" (v10).** Mark keeps repeating "one of the twelve" (vv10, 20, 43). The horror is that it is an insider.
- **"Promised to give him money" (v11).** Matthew gives the sum, thirty pieces of silver (Matt 26:15), the price of a slave in the Law (Exod 21:32) and the wage paid to the rejected shepherd in Zechariah 11:12–13. Why Judas did it is not fully explained. John names greed (John 12:6) and says Satan entered him (John 13:2, 27).
- **Commentators:** JFB connects the silver to Zechariah 11:13. Guzik notes the speculation about forcing Jesus' hand but stays with greed, and stresses that God used Judas's evil without causing it.

#### The Passover with the Disciples (vv12–21)
- **John's timing.** John seems to place the crucifixion on the day the lambs were killed (John 18:28; 19:14), so that Jesus dies as the lambs die. Matthew, Mark and Luke present the Last Supper as a Passover meal. Proposed solutions include different calendars in use by different groups, a broader use of "Passover" in John for the whole week's feasting, or a meal held a day early. Both pictures agree that Jesus died at Passover as its true Lamb (1 Cor 5:7).
- **"A man carrying a jar of water" (v13).** Women usually carried water jars, so a man doing it would stand out.
- **"A large upper room furnished and ready" (v15).** Furnished with couches for reclining. Many think it belonged to the family of John Mark, whose mother's house was a meeting place for the church in Jerusalem (Acts 12:12), but that is tradition, not text.
- **"One of you will betray me, one who is eating with me" (v18).** This echoes Psalm 41:9: "Even my close friend… who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me." To share a meal was to share friendship.
- **"Is it I?" (v19).** The question in Greek expects the answer "no": "Surely not I?" Each one is shaken, and none suspects Judas.
- **"One who is dipping bread into the dish with me" (v20).** All of them were dipping into the shared bowl, so this does not single anyone out. It stresses how close the betrayer is.
- **"As it is written of him, but woe to that man" (v21).** God's plan and human guilt stand side by side without either cancelling the other (Acts 2:23). The Son of Man goes to the death Scripture foretold, and Judas is fully responsible.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the "woe" as a last warning, given in love, that Judas could still have heeded. The Tyndale notes walk through the meal's foods: unleavened bread, bitter herbs, a fruit paste, salt water and four cups of wine linked to the four promises of Exodus 6:6–7.

#### Institution of the Lord's Supper (vv22–25)
- **"He took bread, and after blessing it broke it" (v22).** Jews blessed God, not the food: "Blessed are you, LORD our God, King of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth."
- **"Take; this is my body" (v22).** At Passover the host explained the meaning of the food in terms of the exodus. Jesus explains the bread in terms of himself. Christian traditions understand "this is" differently: Roman Catholics teach that the bread becomes Christ's body (transubstantiation); Lutherans that Christ's body is truly present "in, with and under" the bread; the Reformed tradition (Calvin) that believers truly feed on Christ by the Spirit; and many Baptists and others that the meal is a memorial (Zwingli). All agree that Jesus gives himself to his people at his table.
- **"This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many" (v24).** At Sinai Moses sprinkled sacrificial blood on the people and said, "Behold the blood of the covenant" (Exod 24:8). Jeremiah promised a new covenant in which God would write his law on hearts and "remember their sin no more" (Jer 31:31–34; Luke 22:20 and 1 Cor 11:25 add "new"). "Poured out for many" echoes Isaiah's servant who "poured out his soul to death… and bore the sin of many" (Isa 53:12) and the "ransom for many" of 10:45.
- **"I will not drink again of the fruit of the vine until that day" (v25).** "The fruit of the vine" was the phrase in the Jewish blessing over wine. Jesus looks past the cross to the great banquet of the kingdom (Isa 25:6–8; Rev 19:9).
- **Commentators:** Guzik lays out the same four views of the bread and cup and argues the elements are more than bare symbols. He connects the covenant blood to Exodus 24 and the promises of Jeremiah 31. The Tyndale notes give the same range of views without choosing, and take "many" as meaning all people, as in 10:45.

#### Jesus Foretells Peter's Denial (vv26–31)
- **"When they had sung a hymn" (v26).** The Passover meal closed with the second half of the *Hallel*, Psalms 115–118.
- **"I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (v27).** This is Zechariah 13:7, where God himself strikes his shepherd. "Fall away" is *skandalizō*, to stumble or be tripped.
- **"After I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee" (v28).** Grace before the failure. He promises to regather the scattered sheep, and the angel will repeat it at the tomb (16:7).
- **"Even though they all fall away, I will not" (v29).** Peter compares himself favorably with the others, which is exactly where his trouble starts.
- **"Before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times" (v30).** "This very night": within hours.
- **"He said emphatically" (v31).** The adverb (*ekperissōs*) is found only here in the New Testament, "excessively, vehemently." "And they all said the same." All of them meant it. All of them would run.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the rare adverb and suggests it may preserve Peter's own memory of how loudly he protested. He also says Peter trusted his feelings rather than Jesus' word. The Tyndale notes point out that Jesus' promise of Galilee was kept, and Peter was restored (John 21).

#### Jesus Prays in Gethsemane (vv32–42)
- **"A place called Gethsemane" (v32).** The name is Aramaic for "oil press," an olive grove on the lower slope of the Mount of Olives, just across the Kidron Valley from the temple. John calls it a garden, and Luke says Jesus often went there (John 18:1–2; Luke 22:39).
- **"Greatly distressed and troubled" (v33).** Strong words. The first, *ekthambeomai*, means to be utterly overwhelmed with alarm. The second describes anguish that leaves a person distraught. **"My soul is very sorrowful, even to death" (v34)** echoes the psalmist's "Why are you cast down, O my soul?" (Ps 42:5–6, 11; 43:5). Jesus' horror was not fear of pain alone. He was about to bear the sin of the world and the wrath it deserved (2 Cor 5:21; Gal 3:13).
- **"Abba, Father" (v36).** *Abba* is Aramaic for "father," the word a child used at home, though grown children used it too. It is not baby talk, but it is warm and personal. Jewish prayers seldom addressed God this way. Paul says the Spirit teaches believers to pray the same word (Rom 8:15; Gal 4:6).
- **"Remove this cup from me" (v36).** The cup is the cup of God's wrath (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15). Jesus asked James and John if they could drink it (10:38). Now he asks if it can pass. The request is real. His human will shrinks from it, and he says so.
- **"Yet not what I will, but what you will" (v36).** Here the obedience that Adam refused in a garden is given in another garden. The battle of the cross is won in this prayer.
- **"Simon, are you asleep? Could you not watch one hour?" (v37).** He uses Peter's old name, not "Peter," the rock. The man who promised to die with him could not stay awake.
- **"The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak" (v38).** Good intentions are not enough without prayer.
- **Three times (vv39–41).** Jesus prays three times and finds them asleep three times, a pattern that matches Peter's three denials.
- **"It is enough; the hour has come" (v41).** The Greek word (*apechei*) is hard to pin down. It may mean "enough," or "it is settled," or even "the account is paid." "Betrayed into the hands of sinners" (v41): the same "delivered" word used in all three passion predictions.
- **"Rise, let us be going" (v42).** He does not wait to be dragged out. He goes to meet them.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the anguish as the sorest part of Christ's sufferings and says he "drank up even the dregs of the cup." He notes the irony that Jesus took with him the three who had boasted most of their readiness to suffer. Guzik places Jesus' horror in bearing sin and God's wrath rather than fear of physical pain, and calls Gethsemane the place the real victory was won. He reads *Abba* as intimate and childlike. Many scholars now caution against reading it as "Daddy," since adult children used it too, but the warmth is real.

#### Betrayal and Arrest of Jesus (vv43–50)
- **"A crowd with swords and clubs" (v43).** Sent by the council, the temple police and others. John adds a band of Roman soldiers (John 18:3, 12).
- **"The one I will kiss is the man" (v44).** In the dark, among many men, they needed a sign. A disciple greeting his teacher with a kiss was normal. Judas turns the sign of love into the signal for arrest. **"Rabbi!" And he kissed him (v45).** The verb is a strengthened form, which can mean he kissed him warmly or repeatedly. It makes the scene worse.
- **"Cut off his ear" (v47).** Mark does not name the swordsman or the servant. John says it was Peter, and the servant's name was Malchus (John 18:10). Luke alone says Jesus healed him (Luke 22:51).
- **"As against a robber" (v48).** The word (*lēstēs*) was used of bandits and armed rebels. He will be crucified between two of them (15:27).
- **"They all left him and fled" (v50).** "All" again (v31). The shepherd is struck, and the sheep scatter, just as Zechariah said.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Judas "betrayed him with a kiss," and calls the "Rabbi" greeting a warning against flattery that masks treachery. Guzik notes that a sign was needed because Jesus looked like any other man.

#### A Young Man Flees (vv51–52)
- **"A young man… with nothing but a linen cloth" (v51).** Only Mark has these two strange verses. He had evidently thrown on a sheet and followed in a hurry.
- **"He left the linen cloth and ran away naked" (v52).** The scene shows how complete the abandonment was. Even this last follower escaped by leaving everything, including his clothes.
- **Who was he?** An old suggestion is that he was Mark himself, who signs his Gospel modestly with a memory of his own failure. If the upper room was in his family's house, he might have followed the group out. That cannot be proved, but no better explanation of why the story is told has won agreement.
- **Commentators:** Guzik leans toward the view that it was Mark. Henry thinks the young man was probably not a disciple but someone woken by the noise who followed out of curiosity, and draws the lesson that "there is no hold of those who are led by curiosity only, and not by faith and conscience, to follow Christ." JFB's comments on vv12–52 could not be retrieved.

#### Jesus Before the Council (vv53–65)
- **"They led Jesus to the high priest" (v53).** Caiaphas, high priest from AD 18 to 36 (named in Matt 26:57; John 18:13–14). John adds a first hearing before Annas, his father-in-law and a former high priest (John 18:13).
- **"Peter had followed him at a distance… warming himself at the fire" (v54).** Peter followed, which is more than the others did. But he followed at a distance and sat with the guards.
- **A night trial.** Later Jewish law (in the Mishnah, written about AD 200) forbade capital trials at night or during feasts and required a day's delay before a guilty verdict. Whether those rules applied in Jesus' day is debated, but the proceedings were clearly rushed and irregular. Mark notes a second, morning meeting to make it formal (15:1).
- **"Their testimony did not agree" (vv56, 59).** The Law required two or three witnesses whose testimony matched (Deut 17:6; 19:15). False witnesses were to suffer the penalty they sought for others (Deut 19:16–19).
- **"I will destroy this temple that is made with hands" (v58).** A twisted version of something Jesus did say: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," speaking of his body (John 2:19–21). Mark calls it false witness. The irony is that it is truer than they know. In three days he will rise, and the new temple will be his risen body and his people.
- **"He remained silent and made no answer" (v61).** "Like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7).
- **"Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?" (v61).** "The Blessed" is a reverent way of saying "God" without using the name.
- **"I am" (v62).** For the first time in Mark, Jesus says openly that he is the Christ. The secrecy is over. "I am" (*egō eimi*) also echoes God's name (Exod 3:14), as when Jesus walked on the sea (6:50), though here its first meaning is simply "Yes, I am."
- **"Seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven" (v62).** He joins Psalm 110:1 (the verse he quoted in 12:36) with Daniel 7:13. "Power" is another reverent substitute for God. The prisoner tells his judges that they will see him enthroned beside God and coming as judge. The roles will be reversed.
- **"The high priest tore his garments… You have heard his blasphemy" (vv63–64).** Tearing one's clothes was the required response to hearing blasphemy. Claiming to be the Messiah was not in itself blasphemy. Claiming to sit at God's right hand and come on the clouds, sharing God's throne, was.
- **"They all condemned him as deserving death" (v64).**
- **"Prophesy!" (v65).** They blindfold him and strike him and mock him as a false prophet. At that very moment, down in the courtyard, his prophecy about Peter is coming true.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "I am" as a plain affirmation and connects the abuse to Isaiah 50:6: "I gave my back to those who strike." Guzik notes the irregularities of the trial and that Jesus' silence fulfilled Isaiah 53:7, and says the one on trial was the true judge. He sees Jesus seeming to lose while winning, and the council seeming to win while losing.

#### Peter Denies Jesus (vv66–72)
- **"One of the servant girls of the high priest" (v66).** Not soldiers or officials, but a slave girl. Peter, who drew a sword in the garden, cannot face her question.
- **"I neither know nor understand what you mean" (v68).** The first denial is evasive, a pretense of not understanding. Many manuscripts add "and the rooster crowed" here, which the ESV includes. It fits the "twice" of v30.
- **"This man is one of them… you are a Galilean" (vv69–70).** The second denial comes before a larger group. Matthew adds, "your accent betrays you" (Matt 26:73). Galileans had a distinctive way of speaking Aramaic.
- **"He began to invoke a curse on himself and to swear" (v71).** The third denial is the worst. He calls down a curse, probably on himself if he is lying, and swears an oath. "I do not know this man." He cannot even bring himself to say Jesus' name.
- **"The rooster crowed a second time… he broke down and wept" (v72).** Luke adds that Jesus turned and looked at Peter (Luke 22:61).
- **Why tell this?** Early tradition says Mark's Gospel rests on Peter's preaching. If so, Peter told this story about himself, again and again. The leader of the apostles did not hide his worst night. That is part of the gospel: Jesus is faithful when his best followers fail, and he restores them (16:7; John 21:15–19). Judas also regretted what he did (Matt 27:3–5), but he did not come back. Peter wept and returned.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the third denial "the foulest of all" and ties Peter's tears to the look Luke records: "when he thought thereon, he wept." Guzik traces the slide in stages: following at a distance, sitting with the guards and warming himself at their fire. He notes that the word for the "guards" Peter sat with in v54 is the same as for those who beat Jesus in v65. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both contrast Peter's sorrow, which led to restoration, with Judas's despair.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every person in this chapter fails Jesus or turns on him, except one woman with a jar of perfume. The leaders plot, Judas sells him, the disciples sleep, run and deny him, and the council condemns him. Through it all Jesus does not fail. He gives his body and blood for the many, chooses his Father's will in the garden, walks out to meet his betrayer, keeps silent before false witnesses and then tells the truth about himself though it will cost his life. He is the shepherd who is struck so that the scattered sheep can be gathered again. Peter's tears are not the end of Peter's story, because they are not the end of Jesus' story either: "After I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee."
