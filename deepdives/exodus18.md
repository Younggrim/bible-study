*Setting:* Israel is camped "at the mountain of God" (v5), Mount Sinai, also called Horeb, where God first spoke to Moses from the burning bush (3:1, 12). Moses had lived in this region for forty years as a shepherd for his father-in-law Jethro, a priest of Midian, and had married Jethro's daughter Zipporah (2:16–22). Now Jethro comes to visit, bringing Moses' wife and sons. The chapter has two halves: a family reunion that becomes an act of worship, and a piece of practical advice that reshapes how Israel is governed. Coming right after the attack by Amalek (17:8–16), it gives a deliberate contrast. One group of outsiders attacked God's people; another blesses God and helps them. Exodus may not tell this story in strict time order. See the note on chronology below.

#### Jethro's Advice (vv1–27)
- **"Jethro, the priest of Midian, Moses' father-in-law, heard" (v1).** Jethro is also called Reuel (2:18), which may be a clan name or a second name. The Midianites descended from Abraham through his wife Keturah (Gen 25:1–2), so they were distant relatives of Israel. What Jethro "heard" is what God promised: "my name may be proclaimed in all the earth" (9:16). The news of the exodus is spreading.
- **"Moses' father-in-law" (vv1–27).** The phrase comes thirteen times in this chapter. The story is about family as much as nation.
- **"After he had sent her home" (v2).** We are not told when or why Moses sent Zipporah back. She was with him on the road to Egypt (4:20–26), where the strange incident of their son's circumcision happened. Many think Moses sent his family back to safety during the confrontation with Pharaoh. The word "sent home" can sometimes mean "divorced," but nothing here suggests a broken marriage; Jethro brings her back as "your wife" (v6).
- **"Gershom (for he said, 'I have been a sojourner in a foreign land')" (v3).** The name sounds like *ger sham*, "a sojourner there." Moses named him while he was an exile in Midian (2:22).
- **"Eliezer (for he said, 'The God of my father was my help')" (v4).** *Eliezer* means "my God is help." This is the first time we hear this son's name. **"Delivered me from the sword of Pharaoh."** Moses had fled Egypt with a price on his head (2:15). The names tell Moses' own story: an exile, then a rescued man. Now the whole nation has lived the same story.
- **"Came… to Moses in the wilderness where he was encamped at the mountain of God" (v5).** This mountain is where God promised Moses, "you shall serve God on this mountain" (3:12). The promise is coming true.
- **"Moses went out to meet his father-in-law and bowed down and kissed him" (v7).** Bowing and kissing were normal greetings of respect in the ancient Near East. Moses, now the leader of a nation, still honors the man who took him in as a fugitive. **"They asked each other of their welfare."** Literally "asked about each other's *shalom*," their peace and well-being.
- **"Moses told his father-in-law all that the LORD had done" (v8).** Moses tells both sides: what God did to Pharaoh, **"all the hardship that had come upon them in the way,"** and how God delivered them. "Hardship" is the word used for the "weariness" of the journey (Num 20:14). His testimony is honest.
- **"And Jethro rejoiced" (v9).** The Hebrew verb is rare and suggests delight that runs through the whole person. A foreigner rejoices in what God has done for Israel.
- **"Blessed be the LORD, who has delivered you" (v10).** Jethro uses God's covenant name, *Yahweh*. The word "delivered" comes three times in vv8–10. He sees clearly who did the rescuing.
- **"Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods" (v11).** This is what the plagues were meant to teach (7:5; 9:14; 12:12). Pharaoh never said it. Jethro does. **"Because in this affair they dealt arrogantly with the people."** The Hebrew is compressed. It probably means that God proved greater exactly where the Egyptians acted most proudly, such as drowning Hebrew babies in water and then drowning in water themselves.
- **Was Jethro a convert?** Readers have long debated whether Jethro was already a worshiper of the true God (as Melchizedek was, Gen 14:18–20), whether he came to faith here, or whether he simply added the LORD to his gods as the greatest of them. The words "now I know" suggest new conviction. Many Jewish and Christian interpreters have seen him as a model convert, the first fruit of the nations.
- **"Brought a burnt offering and sacrifices to God" (v12).** A "burnt offering" was wholly burned up as an act of worship. "Sacrifices" here probably means fellowship offerings, which were shared in a meal. Strikingly, the Midianite priest offers the sacrifice before Israel's priesthood has even been set up.
- **"Aaron came with all the elders of Israel to eat bread with Moses' father-in-law before God."** Eating together "before God" was a sign of fellowship and peace, a covenant meal. It foreshadows 24:9–11, when Israel's elders "beheld God, and ate and drank" on the same mountain. Moses is not named at the meal, perhaps because he was host.
- **"The next day Moses sat to judge the people… from morning till evening" (v13).** Judging meant settling disputes and giving God's ruling on them. Moses sits; the people stand in line all day.
- **"Why do you sit alone, and all the people stand around you?" (v14).** Jethro sees the problem at once. The Tyndale notes suggest that Moses may have absorbed something of Pharaoh's way of ruling, with every decision flowing from one man at the top.
- **"Because the people come to me to inquire of God" (v15).** Moses explains that he gives God's answers in their disputes. **"I make them know the statutes of God and his laws" (v16).** This is before the law is given at Sinai, so these "statutes" are what God had revealed so far, such as the instructions about Passover and the Sabbath (12–13; 16:23–29; 15:25–26).
- **"What you are doing is not good" (v17).** A blunt word. "Not good" echoes Genesis 2:18: "It is not good that the man should be alone." Moses is trying to do alone what God never meant one man to carry. **"You will certainly wear yourselves out" (v18).** The verb pictures a leaf withering. Both Moses and the people will be worn down. **"The thing is too heavy for you."** *Kaved* once more, the word for heaviness that runs all through Exodus. Moses later says the same in his own words: "I am not able to carry all this people alone; the burden is too heavy for me" (Num 11:14).
- **"Now obey my voice; I will give you advice, and God be with you!" (v19).** Jethro does not claim to speak for God, but he hopes God will approve. Wise human counsel is one way God guides. **"You shall represent the people before God."** Moses' first job is to stand between the people and God, bringing their hard cases to him.
- **"You shall warn them about the statutes and the laws, and make them know the way" (v20).** His second job is teaching, so people can learn to settle matters themselves. **"The way in which they must walk"** is the Bible's frequent picture of life as a path.
- **"Able men… who fear God, who are trustworthy and hate a bribe" (v21).** Four qualities. "Able" (*chayil*) means capable and strong in character, the word used for the "excellent wife" of Proverbs 31:10. "Fear God" is the root. "Trustworthy" is literally "men of truth" (*emet*). "Hate a bribe" means they hate dishonest gain, not just avoid it. Character comes before skill. Later lists for church leaders follow the same pattern (Acts 6:3; 1 Tim 3:1–13).
- **"Chiefs of thousands, of hundreds, of fifties, and of tens."** The structure mirrors military units, and the same terms appear later for army officers (Num 31:14). It gave every family someone close by to bring a problem to.
- **"Every great matter they shall bring to you, but any small matter they shall decide themselves" (v22).** The simple principle of delegation. **"They will bear the burden with you."** Leadership shared is lighter. Moses keeps the hardest cases and the teaching.
- **"If you do this, God will direct you" (v23).** Literally "and God commands you," that is, if God agrees. Jethro offers his advice under God's authority. **"All this people also will go to their place in peace."** The goal is not only Moses' survival but the people's *shalom*. They will go home with their disputes settled.
- **"So Moses listened to the voice of his father-in-law and did all that he had said" (v24).** The greatest prophet in Israel takes advice from a Midianite priest. Numbers 12:3 calls Moses "very meek," and this is part of what that looks like.
- **"Any hard case they brought to Moses" (v26).** The system works. Deuteronomy 1:9–18 tells the same story from Moses' side, and there the people help choose the leaders.
- **"Then Moses let his father-in-law depart" (v27).** Jethro goes home. A later scene with Moses' in-law Hobab (Num 10:29–32) shows that ties with this family continued, and Judges 1:16 and 4:11 show some of them settling among Israel.
- **When did this happen?** Some details suggest this chapter is placed by theme rather than in strict time order. Israel does not reach Sinai until 19:1–2, but here they are already "at the mountain of God." Jethro offers sacrifices and Moses teaches "statutes," and Deuteronomy 1:6–18 places the appointment of judges when Israel was about to leave Horeb, near the end of their year at Sinai. Many interpreters think the story was placed here to set the friendly Midianite beside the hostile Amalekites of chapter 17. Others, like Henry, see no difficulty in taking it in order. The meaning is the same either way.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "Moses went out to meet Jethro, did homage to him, and kissed him," and that "conversation concerning God's wondrous works is profitable conversation." They "did eat bread before God, soberly, thankfully, in the fear of God." On Moses' long days he writes that "there may be over-doing even in well-doing, and therefore our zeal must always be governed by discretion," and on the judges: "The fear of God is that principle which will best fortify a man against all temptations to injustice." Of Moses' humility: "Those are not so wise as they would be thought to be who think themselves too wise to be counselled." JFB thinks the episode is out of time order, since Israel reached the mount only in the third month (19:1). He describes the greeting as the "kiss" on each side of the head, says Jethro, "as priest of the true God, seems to have presided" at the meal, and calls Moses as judge "a type of Christ in His legislative and judicial characters." JFB has no comment on vv8–11 or vv24–27. Guzik observes that the man trained in Pharaoh's court learned leadership from a desert shepherd-priest, lists four marks of leaders from v21 (ability, fear of God, truthfulness, freedom from greed), and compares the plan to the choosing of the seven in Acts 6 and Paul's charge to pass truth on to faithful men (2 Tim 2:2). He also praises Moses for being teachable.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jethro hears what God has done, rejoices, and confesses, "Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods." He is an early sign of God's promise that through Abraham "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). Gentiles would come to Israel's God, and through Christ they have. Paul says Gentiles who were once "strangers to the covenants of promise" have been "brought near by the blood of Christ" to share the table (Eph 2:12–13), as Jethro ate bread before God with Israel's elders. And Moses, worn out by carrying the people alone, points ahead to the one who can carry them: Jesus, the true judge and mediator (1 Tim 2:5), who says, "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28). He also shares his work, giving gifts and leaders to his church "to equip the saints for the work of ministry" (Eph 4:11–12).
