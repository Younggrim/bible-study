*Setting:* Jerusalem, Friday of Passover week, about AD 30, from early morning to sunset. At dawn the council has heard Jesus say he will sit at God's right hand, and they have the charge of blasphemy they wanted (22:66–71). But only the Roman governor could carry out an execution, and Rome would not put a man to death over a Jewish religious dispute. So chapter 23 begins with the leaders taking Jesus to Pontius Pilate, governor of Judea from AD 26 to 36, with new, political charges. Luke shows Jesus declared innocent again and again, by Pilate, by Herod, by a dying criminal and by a Roman centurion, even as he is condemned and crucified. The chapter ends with his body in a borrowed tomb and the women resting on the Sabbath.

#### Jesus Before Pilate (vv1–5)
- **"The whole company of them arose and brought him before Pilate" (v1).** Pilate normally lived in Caesarea on the coast but came to Jerusalem at the festivals with extra troops to keep order. He probably stayed in Herod's former palace on the western side of the city. Ancient writers describe him as harsh and tactless, and Luke himself mentions Galileans whose blood Pilate mixed with their sacrifices (13:1).
- **Three charges (v2).** "Misleading our nation" makes Jesus a troublemaker. "Forbidding us to give tribute to Caesar" is the opposite of what he said (20:25). "Saying that he himself is Christ, a king" twists a true claim into treason. Blasphemy is not mentioned. Before a Roman judge, they need a rebel.
- **"Are you the King of the Jews?" "You have said so" (v3).** In Greek "you" is emphatic. Jesus accepts the title but not Pilate's meaning of it. John records the longer conversation: "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:33–38).
- **"I find no guilt in this man" (v4).** This is the first of three times Pilate declares Jesus innocent (vv14, 22). Luke writes for readers in the Roman world, and he wants them to see that Rome's own judge found nothing against Jesus, just as Roman officials later find nothing against Paul (Acts 18:14–15; 26:31–32).
- **"From Galilee even to this place" (v5).** The leaders press harder, painting Jesus as an agitator stirring up the whole country. Galilee had a reputation for rebels. The word gives Pilate an idea.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Pilate well understood the difference between armed forces and our Lord's followers," yet Pilate's verdict only made the leaders angrier. Guzik notes that Philo described Pilate as cruel and corrupt, and that the leaders turned a religious charge into three political ones.

#### Jesus Before Herod (vv6–17)
- **"He belonged to Herod's jurisdiction" (v7).** Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, ruled Galilee and Perea. He was in Jerusalem for the feast. Only Luke records this hearing. Pilate hopes to pass the problem to someone else. The verb "sent over" was used for referring a prisoner to another court.
- **"He had long desired to see him" (v8).** This is the Herod who beheaded John the Baptist (9:9) and who was said to want Jesus dead (13:31–32). Jesus called him "that fox." Herod wants a show, "some sign."
- **"He made no answer" (v9).** Jesus says nothing at all to Herod. Isaiah's servant "opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7). Jesus answered Pilate and the council, but Herod had silenced the last prophet who spoke plainly to him.
- **"Arraying him in splendid clothing" (v11).** Herod and his soldiers mock him as a pretend king, dressing him in a bright robe. Herod, like Pilate, finds no crime worth death (v15).
- **"Herod and Pilate became friends with each other that very day" (v12).** Their earlier quarrel may have involved Pilate's killing of Galileans (13:1), or the gold shields Pilate set up in Jerusalem, which Philo says Herod's sons protested to the emperor. Shared contempt for Jesus brings them together. The church later saw Psalm 2 fulfilled here: "the kings of the earth... and the rulers were gathered together, against the Lord and against his Anointed" (Acts 4:25–27).
- **"I did not find this man guilty... Neither did Herod" (vv14–15).** Under the law, two witnesses settled a matter (Deut 19:15). Two rulers have now found him innocent.
- **"I will therefore punish and release him" (v16).** The word means to discipline or teach a lesson, a lighter beating than the brutal scourging before crucifixion. It is still an injustice. If he is innocent, why punish him at all?
- **The missing verse 17.** Some manuscripts add, "Now he was obliged to release one man to them at the festival." The ESV leaves it out because the earliest manuscripts lack it, and it appears to have been copied in from Mark 15:6 and Matthew 27:15. The custom it describes is confirmed by the other Gospels.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The poorest beggar that asked a miracle for the relief of his necessity, was never denied; but this proud prince... is refused." He adds that "the friendships of wicked men are often formed by union in wickedness." JFB quotes Bengel that by sending Jesus back without releasing him, Herod "implicated himself with Pilate in all the guilt of His condemnation" (Acts 4:27). Guzik sees three kinds of rejection in the chapter: the leaders from envy, Pilate from cowardice and Herod from a desire to be entertained.

#### Pilate Delivers Jesus to Be Crucified (vv18–25)
- **"Away with this man, and release to us Barabbas" (v18).** Barabbas means "son of the father" in Aramaic. Some early manuscripts of Matthew call him "Jesus Barabbas" (Matt 27:16–17), so the crowd may have been choosing between two men named Jesus. That reading is uncertain.
- **"Thrown into prison for an insurrection started in the city and for murder" (v19).** Barabbas was exactly what the leaders accused Jesus of being, a rebel against Rome. They ask for the guilty rebel and condemn the innocent one.
- **"Crucify, crucify him!" (v21).** Crucifixion was Rome's punishment for slaves and rebels, meant to be as painful and shameful as possible. To be hanged on a tree also meant, in Jewish eyes, to be under God's curse (Deut 21:23; Gal 3:13).
- **"A third time he said to them" (v22).** Pilate's three declarations of innocence mirror Peter's three denials in the night before. Both men knew the truth and failed to stand by it.
- **"Their voices prevailed" (v23).** This is the moment Luke has been building toward. The governor's power yields to the shouting of a crowd. John adds that they threatened him: "If you release this man, you are not Caesar's friend" (John 19:12).
- **"He released the man... for whom they asked, but he delivered Jesus over to their will" (v25).** Luke repeats Barabbas's crimes so that the exchange is unmistakable. The guilty man walks free, and the innocent one takes his place. Barabbas is the first person in history who could say, quite literally, that Jesus died instead of him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The fear of man brings many into this snare, that they will do an unjust thing, against their consciences, rather than get into trouble," and "If no fault be found in him, why chastise him?" Guzik draws out Barabbas as a picture of substitution. He also suggests the crowd that morning was mostly local Jerusalemites gathered by the leaders, not the Galilean pilgrims who cheered at the triumphal entry, which is possible but not stated in the text.

#### The Crucifixion (vv26–43)
- **"They seized one Simon of Cyrene" (v26).** Roman soldiers could force a civilian to carry a load (Matt 5:41). Cyrene was a city in North Africa, in modern Libya, with a large Jewish community (Acts 2:10; 6:9). Simon was likely a pilgrim, "coming in from the country." The condemned man normally carried the crossbeam to the place of execution, but Jesus, after a night without sleep and a severe scourging, could not. Mark names Simon's sons, Alexander and Rufus, as if Mark's readers knew them (Mark 15:21), and a Rufus is greeted in Rome (Rom 16:13). "To carry it behind Jesus" is a picture of discipleship: "take up his cross daily and follow me" (9:23).
- **"Women who were mourning and lamenting for him" (v27).** These are women of Jerusalem, not the women from Galilee (v49). Mourning for the condemned on the way to execution was a custom, and Jewish women in Jerusalem were known for bringing drugged wine to those being crucified.
- **"Do not weep for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children" (v28).** Even on the way to his death Jesus thinks of others. Forty years later Jerusalem would be besieged and destroyed (19:41–44; 21:20–24).
- **"Blessed are the barren" (v29).** Childlessness was normally a deep sorrow and shame. In the siege it would be a mercy not to watch one's children starve.
- **"To the mountains, 'Fall on us'" (v30).** From Hosea 10:8, a prophecy of judgment on idolatrous Israel. Revelation uses the same words of the last judgment (Rev 6:16).
- **"If they do these things when the wood is green, what will happen when it is dry?" (v31).** Green wood does not burn easily. Dry wood catches at once. If the innocent Jesus suffers like this, how much worse will judgment be on a guilty nation, and on all who reject him? Others take "they" to mean the Romans: if they do this to an innocent man in peacetime, what will they do in the war to come? Both readings point to the same warning.
- **"Two others, who were criminals" (v32).** Isaiah 53:12 is being fulfilled: "numbered with the transgressors," as Jesus said at the supper (22:37).
- **"The place that is called The Skull" (v33).** In Aramaic, *Golgotha*. "Calvary" comes from the Latin *calvaria*, "skull." It was outside the city wall, near a road, where passersby could see. The traditional site, now inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, has the strongest support.
- **"There they crucified him" (v33).** None of the Gospels describes the details. Their first readers had seen crucifixions and did not need them. Victims were nailed or tied to the cross and could hang for hours or days, slowly suffocating as they grew too weak to push up to breathe. The Latin word for the agony gives English the word "excruciating," "out of the cross."
- **"Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (v34).** The first word from the cross in Luke is a prayer for his executioners. He practices what he taught: "pray for those who abuse you" (6:28). Stephen will pray the same (Acts 7:60). Some early manuscripts lack this sentence, while many others include it. The ESV prints it with a footnote. Many scholars accept it as genuine, since it fits Luke's themes so closely and a scribe may have dropped it, unable to believe God would forgive those who killed his Son.
- **"They cast lots to divide his garments" (v34).** The clothing of the condemned belonged to the execution squad. Psalm 22:18: "they divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots."
- **Three groups mock him (vv35–39).** The rulers: "He saved others; let him save himself" (v35), echoing Psalm 22:7–8. The soldiers, offering "sour wine," the cheap drink of soldiers and laborers (Ps 69:21). One of the criminals (v39). The repeated taunt is "save yourself." The irony is that he could have, and by refusing he saves others.
- **"This is the King of the Jews" (v38).** The charge was normally posted on the cross. Pilate meant it as an insult to the Jews and a warning to rebels. Luke wants readers to see that it was true. Some manuscripts add that it was written "in letters of Greek and Latin and Hebrew," which John records (John 19:20).
- **"Do you not fear God?" (v40).** Only Luke records the second criminal. Matthew and Mark say both criminals mocked (Mark 15:32), which may mean that one changed his mind as the hours passed.
- **"We indeed justly... but this man has done nothing wrong" (v41).** He admits his guilt and declares Jesus innocent, the fourth such verdict in the chapter. "Nothing wrong" is literally "nothing out of place."
- **"Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom" (v42).** He calls him simply "Jesus," with no title, a striking closeness. He sees a dying man and believes he is a king who will come into his kingdom. To "remember" in Scripture means to act on someone's behalf (Gen 8:1; 1 Sam 1:19).
- **"Today you will be with me in paradise" (v43).** He asked to be remembered someday. Jesus promises "today." "Paradise" is a Persian word for a walled garden or park. The Greek Old Testament used it for the garden of Eden, and Jewish writings used it for the place of the righteous dead (2 Cor 12:4; Rev 2:7). The man has no time for baptism, good works or a changed life. He is saved by grace through faith, at the last hour.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is certain that true repentance is never too late; but it is as certain that late repentance is seldom true." He calls this "a single instance in Scripture" that should teach us "to despair of none, and that none should despair of themselves." JFB on the green and dry wood: "If such sufferings alight upon the innocent One, the very Lamb of God, what must be in store for those who are provoking the flames?" On the thief's request JFB asks, "Was ever faith like this exhibited upon earth?" and hears Jesus' answer as "Since thou speakest as to the king, with kingly authority speak I to thee." Guzik observes that it was love, not the nails, that held Jesus to the cross, and he gives the old saying about the thief: one such conversion is recorded so that no one despairs, only one so that no one presumes.

#### The Death of Jesus (vv44–49)
- **"About the sixth hour... darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour" (v44).** Jewish hours were counted from sunrise, so this is about noon to 3 p.m. "The whole land" can also mean "the whole earth." Darkness was a sign of God's judgment (Exod 10:21–23; Amos 8:9: "I will make the sun go down at noon").
- **"While the sun's light failed" (v45).** The Greek verb is the one used for an eclipse. But Passover fell at full moon, when a solar eclipse cannot happen. Luke may simply mean the sun stopped shining. Whatever its cause, it was God's doing.
- **"The curtain of the temple was torn in two" (v45).** This was probably the heavy curtain that separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, where only the high priest could enter, once a year (Lev 16). Matthew and Mark say it tore "from top to bottom" (Mark 15:38). Hebrews explains: Jesus opened "a new and living way... through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20). Luke places the tearing before Jesus' death cry, the others after, but all link it to his death.
- **"Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (v46).** Psalm 31:5, a prayer of trust in the middle of suffering. Jesus adds "Father." He dies in trust, not defeat. "Calling out with a loud voice" means he was not slowly fading. He gave up his life (John 10:18). Luke does not record the cry of abandonment, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34), or "It is finished" (John 19:30). Each Gospel writer selects, and together they give the whole.
- **"He breathed his last" (v46).** The verb means "breathed out." Stephen, the first martyr, echoes these last words: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59).
- **"Certainly this man was innocent!" (v47).** The centurion commanded the execution squad. Mark has him say, "Truly this man was the Son of God" (Mark 15:39). Luke's word, *dikaios*, can mean "innocent" or "righteous." He may have said both. For Luke it is the climax of the verdicts: the Roman officer in charge of the execution declares Jesus innocent, and "praised God." The cross is already drawing Gentiles.
- **"Returned home beating their breasts" (v48).** The same gesture as the tax collector in the temple (18:13), a sign of grief and perhaps of repentance. Some of these may have been among the thousands who believed at Pentecost seven weeks later (Acts 2:37–41).
- **"The women who had followed him from Galilee stood at a distance watching" (v49).** These women had supported Jesus' ministry (8:1–3). The male disciples have scattered, except perhaps John (John 19:26). The women watch, and they will be the first witnesses of the empty tomb. "At a distance" may recall Psalm 38:11: "my companions stand aloof from my plague."
- **Commentators:** Henry says the death of Christ is "magnified by the wonders that attended it, and... explained by the words with which he breathed out his soul," and "he was willing to offer himself." Guzik points out that a natural eclipse is impossible at Passover's full moon, and he reads the torn curtain as God himself opening free access to his presence. Guzik includes the cry of John 19:30 at this point, though Luke does not record it. The Tyndale notes link the centurion's verdict to the righteous servant of Isaiah 53:11.

#### Jesus Is Buried (vv50–56)
- **"A man named Joseph, from the Jewish town of Arimathea" (v50).** Arimathea was probably Ramathaim, about twenty miles northwest of Jerusalem, the birthplace of Samuel (1 Sam 1:1). Joseph was "a member of the council," the very body that condemned Jesus. Matthew says he was rich and a disciple (Matt 27:57). John says he had been a secret disciple, "for fear of the Jews," and that Nicodemus helped him (John 19:38–39).
- **"Who had not consented to their decision and action" (v51).** The council's verdict was not unanimous, whatever Mark 14:64's "all" means for those present. Joseph may have been absent or outvoted. Like Simeon and Anna at the beginning of the Gospel (2:25, 38), "he was looking for the kingdom of God."
- **"This man went to Pilate and asked for the body" (v52).** Romans often left crucified bodies on the cross to rot as a warning, or threw them in a common grave. Jewish law required burial before sunset (Deut 21:22–23). Asking a Roman governor for the body of an executed "king" was a public step that would identify Joseph with Jesus, and it took courage (Mark 15:43).
- **"Wrapped it in a linen shroud and laid him in a tomb cut in stone, where no one had ever yet been laid" (v53).** Rock-cut tombs were expensive and belonged to wealthy families. Isaiah said the servant would be "with a rich man in his death" (Isa 53:9). The tomb was new, so there could be no confusion about whose body was there. Like the colt "on which no one has ever yet sat" (19:30), it was set apart for him.
- **"It was the day of Preparation, and the Sabbath was beginning" (v54).** "Preparation" was the name for Friday, the day to prepare for the Sabbath. The Sabbath began at sunset, so the burial was hurried. The verb "was beginning" literally means "was dawning," perhaps a reference to the first evening star or the lamps being lit.
- **"The women... saw the tomb and how his body was laid" (v55).** They are careful witnesses. They know the exact tomb, which matters for the resurrection account that follows. They will not go to the wrong grave.
- **"They returned and prepared spices and ointments" (v56).** Spices and perfumed oils were used to honor the dead and to lessen the odor of decay. The women expect a corpse.
- **"On the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment" (v56).** Even in grief, they keep God's law (Exod 20:8–10). The Son of God rests in a tomb on the seventh day, as God rested after creation (Gen 2:2). The first day of a new creation is coming.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Many, though they do not make any show in outward profession, yet, like Joseph of Arimathea, will be far more ready to do real service, when there is occasion, than others who make a greater noise." On v56 he writes, "Weeping must not hinder sowing." Guzik contrasts Peter's bold promises with Joseph's quiet, costly service.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke makes it plain that Jesus was innocent. Pilate, Herod, a criminal and a centurion all say so. Then why did he die? Barabbas, the guilty man released in his place, gives the answer: the righteous one dies for the unrighteous (1 Pet 3:18). On the cross Jesus prays for his killers, opens paradise to a thief and commits his spirit to his Father. The curtain is torn, the way to God is open, and the body is laid in a new tomb, waiting for the first day of the week.
