*Setting:* Amos, a sheep-breeder from Judah, is preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760 BC, under Jeroboam II. Israel is wealthy, militarily secure and full of religious activity at shrines like Bethel and Gilgal, but its courts are corrupt and its poor are crushed. Chapter 4 ended with God's summons, "prepare to meet your God, O Israel!" Chapter 5 is the third message opening "Hear this word" (3:1; 4:1), and Henry calls it an expansion of that summons. It begins with a funeral song for a nation still alive, offers life three times ("seek me and live"), and contains the verse the book is best known for: "let justice roll down like waters." Many scholars see vv1–17 arranged as a mirror, with a hymn to God at the center (v8): lament, call to seek, injustice, *the LORD is his name*, injustice, call to seek, lament.

#### "Seek the LORD and Live" (vv1–17)
- **"Hear this word that I take up over you in lamentation" (v1).** A lamentation (*qinah*) was a funeral song. The Tyndale notes explain that it had its own limping rhythm in Hebrew, three beats then two, the rhythm of most of the book of Lamentations. Imagine a preacher standing in the crowded courts at Bethel and singing a funeral dirge over the nation, as if Israel were already dead and only waiting to be buried.
- **"Fallen, no more to rise, is the virgin Israel" (v2).** "Virgin" here means a nation never conquered by a foreign power, as the Tyndale notes and JFB say (compare Isa 47:1). Guzik adds that the word also carries the sadness of a young life cut off. **"Forsaken on her land, with none to raise her up."** She lies like a body left in a field. Israel had been strong for decades. Amos says it is already a corpse. Within about forty years, it was gone.
- **"The city that went out a thousand shall have a hundred left" (v3).** Armies were raised by city and clan, and numbered in thousands and hundreds. Nine out of ten will die. The Tyndale notes point out that this reverses God's promise in Leviticus 26:8, that a hundred of Israel would chase ten thousand enemies.
- **"Seek me and live" (v4).** In the middle of the dirge comes an offer. "Seek" (*darash*) means to come to God, ask of him, inquire of him, as one would at a sanctuary. "And live" is an imperative too, literally "seek me *and live*," which JFB says stresses that life is sure for those who truly turn. The funeral song is not yet a death sentence, as the Tyndale notes say. There is still a way out.
- **"Do not seek Bethel… Gilgal… Beersheba" (v5).** Three shrines with rich histories. Bethel was where Jacob saw the ladder to heaven (Gen 28). Gilgal was Israel's first camp in Canaan (Josh 4–5). Beersheba, far in the south, was where Abraham and Isaac called on the LORD (Gen 21:33; 26:23–25). Northern Israelites even "crossed over" the border into Judah to worship there (8:14). Guzik notes the irony: places once tied to God's blessing had become substitutes for him. People went to the shrines instead of seeking the God the shrines were about.
- **Two puns (v5).** "Gilgal shall surely go into exile" is in Hebrew *haggilgal galoh yigleh*, a tongue-twister of g and l sounds, as the Tyndale notes explain. Something like "Gilgal shall go to the gallows" in English. "Bethel shall come to nothing" uses *'aven*, "nothing, wickedness." Hosea calls Bethel "Beth-aven," "house of nothing," instead of "house of God" (Hos 4:15; 10:5). JFB makes the same link.
- **"Seek the LORD and live, lest he break out like fire in the house of Joseph" (v6).** The offer is repeated. "House of Joseph" means the northern kingdom, led by Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh. **"With none to quench it for Bethel."** The idols of Bethel cannot put out God's fire. JFB: no Bethel idol could quench it.
- **"O you who turn justice to wormwood and cast down righteousness to the earth!" (v7).** Wormwood is a bitter plant (Deut 29:18; Prov 5:4). Justice should be sweet and nourishing for the poor. In Israel's courts it had become poison. "Justice" (*mishpat*) and "righteousness" (*tsedaqah*) are a pair that runs all through Amos (5:24; 6:12). *Mishpat* is fair judgment, right decisions in court. *Tsedaqah* is right living and right relationships, especially toward the weak. They had thrown both to the ground.
- **"He who made the Pleiades and Orion" (v8).** Amos breaks in with a hymn, like 4:13 and 9:5–6. The Pleiades (*kimah*, a star cluster) and Orion (*kesil*) appear together in Job 9:9 and 38:31. *Kesil* also means "fool," and some think ancient peoples saw the constellation as a giant bound in the sky for rebellion. JFB says the Pleiades marked spring and Orion the stormy season, so together they stand for the turning of the year. The Tyndale notes say many nations worshiped the stars as gods. Amos says the LORD made them.
- **"Turns deep darkness into the morning and darkens the day into night" (v8).** "Deep darkness" (*tsalmaweth*) is the word of Psalm 23:4, "the valley of the shadow of death." God controls day and night. **"Who calls for the waters of the sea and pours them out on the surface of the earth."** Rain, which Israel credited to Baal, comes from the LORD. It may also hint at the flood (Gen 7). **"The LORD is his name"** sits at the very center of the passage. God who made the stars is the one Israel must meet.
- **"Who makes destruction flash forth against the strong" (v9).** The same God who governs the sky can bring sudden ruin on the strongest fortress. The wealthy trusted in their walls (3:11; 6:13). The Creator can turn their strength into nothing.
- **"They hate him who reproves in the gate" (v10).** The city gate was the courthouse, where elders sat to hear cases (Ruth 4:1–11; Deut 21:19). The one who "reproves" there is the honest judge or witness who calls wrong wrong. JFB takes him as the judge, and the one "who speaks the truth" as the prophet. The powerful hated both. The Tyndale notes say the courts were run by corrupt judges and hired witnesses.
- **"You trample on the poor and you exact taxes of grain from him" (v11).** The rich charged tenant farmers crushing rents in grain, taking much of their harvest. **"You have built houses of hewn stone, but you shall not dwell in them."** The Tyndale notes explain that ordinary people lived in mud-brick houses. Cut stone, dressed by hand, was costly. These mansions were paid for with the grain of the poor. **"You have planted pleasant vineyards, but you shall not drink their wine."** This is one of the covenant curses (Deut 28:30, 39). The reverse is promised in the restoration at the end of the book (9:14).
- **"For I know how many are your transgressions" (v12).** God knows. Nothing done in the court is hidden from him. **"You who afflict the righteous, who take a bribe, and turn aside the needy in the gate."** "Bribe" here (*kopher*) is the word for a ransom, money paid to buy someone out. The law forbade taking it to spare a murderer (Num 35:31). Here they took payoffs to sell justice.
- **"Therefore he who is prudent will keep silent in such a time, for it is an evil time" (v13).** This verse is puzzling. Is Amos approving of silence? Several readings exist. JFB reads it as wise people withdrawing from public affairs they cannot change and waiting for God. The Tyndale notes take it as a description of how bad things had become: witnesses swore they saw nothing, because telling the truth was dangerous (v10). Others take it as irony from Amos, who certainly did not keep silent, or translate "the prudent will wail," since the Hebrew verb can also mean to mourn. The most natural reading is that it describes the times rather than recommends a policy. When truth is punished, even sensible people learn to say nothing.
- **"Seek good, and not evil, that you may live" (v14).** The third "seek… and live." Now it is "seek good." Seeking God and seeking good are not two different things. You cannot seek the LORD at Bethel while seeking bribes in the gate. **"And so the LORD, the God of hosts, will be with you, as you have said."** Israel was saying, "God is with us" (compare Mic 3:11). Amos says that is only true of a people who seek good.
- **"Hate evil, and love good, and establish justice in the gate" (v15).** Repentance means changing what one loves and hates, and then acting on it in public. "Establish" means to set it up and make it stand, like setting up a monument. Paul echoes the line: "Abhor what is evil; hold fast to what is good" (Rom 12:9).
- **"It may be that the LORD… will be gracious to the remnant of Joseph" (v15).** "It may be" is not doubt about God's mercy. JFB says it reflects the people's lack of true repentance, not any unwillingness in God. Like "who knows?" in Joel 2:14 and Jonah 3:9, it refuses to treat grace as automatic. "The remnant of Joseph" is a sobering phrase: Amos already speaks as if only a small part will be left.
- **"In all the squares there shall be wailing" (v16).** The funeral song returns, now sung by everyone. "Alas! Alas!" is the word *ho*, the cry of mourning. **"They shall call the farmers to mourning and to wailing those who are skilled in lamentation."** Professional mourners, often women, were hired for funerals (Jer 9:17–18). There will be so many funerals that farmers will be pulled from the fields to help. Guzik says the professionals will be in short supply.
- **"And in all vineyards there shall be wailing" (v17).** Vineyards were places of joy and harvest songs (Isa 16:10). Now they too will echo with wailing. JFB makes the contrast. **"For I will pass through your midst."** This is Passover language. On the night of the exodus, the LORD "passed through" Egypt, striking the firstborn, and passed over Israel (Exod 12:12, 23). Now he will pass through Israel, and there will be no passing over.
- **Commentators:** Henry builds the chapter around what Israel must do (seek the Lord, seek good) and why: their condition is desperate, sin brought them there, God is ready to be found, and refusing means ruin. JFB takes "virgin" as a state never yet conquered, and reads "it may be" as a reflection of Israel's half-hearted repentance. Guzik notes the wordplays on Gilgal and Bethel and the corrupt courts at the gate. He divides vv10–15 into the cause, the curse and the cure. The Tyndale notes explain the funeral rhythm and the costliness of hewn stone.

#### "Let Justice Roll Down" (vv18–27)
- **"Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD!" (v18).** This is probably the earliest written use of the phrase "the day of the LORD" in the Bible, and Amos uses it to overturn what his hearers believed. They longed for that day as the time God would defeat Israel's enemies and bless his people. The Tyndale notes explain this expectation. Amos says it will be the opposite for them: **"It is darkness, and not light."** God's day is a day of judgment on all evil, and Israel's evil included. Joel, Isaiah, Zephaniah and Malachi all take up the phrase.
- **"As if a man fled from a lion, and a bear met him" (v19).** A shepherd's picture. A man escapes a lion, only to run into a bear. He makes it home, leans against the wall to catch his breath, and a snake hidden in the crack bites him. JFB: escaping one disaster only leads to another. There is no safe place in that day.
- **"Is not the day of the LORD darkness, and not light, and gloom with no brightness in it?" (v20).** Repeated for emphasis. Guzik notes that "darkness" here means disaster, not moral evil. Amos will later speak of the sun going down at noon (8:9).
- **"I hate, I despise your feasts" (v21).** Now God speaks, and the words are as strong as possible. "Hate" and "despise" together. **"I take no delight in your solemn assemblies."** Literally "I will not smell," the word for God accepting the "pleasing aroma" of a sacrifice (Gen 8:21; Lev 26:31). Their worship stinks to him.
- **"Even though you offer me your burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them" (v22).** Three main kinds of offering are named: burnt offerings (wholly given to God), grain offerings, and peace offerings, shared meals of fellowship. **"I will not look upon them."** The Tyndale notes say these offerings had become attempts to control God through ritual rather than expressions of repentance and faith.
- **"Take away from me the noise of your songs; to the melody of your harps I will not listen" (v23).** Even the music, which may have been beautiful, is "noise" to God. Isaiah would soon say the same thing to Judah (Isa 1:10–17).
- **"But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (v24).** This is what God wants instead. The Tyndale notes call it the second key verse of the book, along with 4:12. Most streams in Israel are *wadis*, dry gullies that run with water only after the winter rains and are bone dry in summer. Israel's justice was like that: occasional, seasonal. God wants it to flow all year round, an *'ethan* stream, one that never fails. "Roll down" pictures a flood, powerful and unstoppable, washing away corruption. Martin Luther King Jr. quoted this verse in his "I Have a Dream" speech in 1963, and it is carved on the Civil Rights Memorial in Montgomery, Alabama.
- **Does God reject worship altogether?** No. God commanded these feasts and sacrifices in the law of Moses. His complaint is with worship cut off from obedience, the attempt to please God at the altar while cheating people in the gate. Samuel said it long before: "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22). Jesus taught that if one has wronged a brother, one should leave the gift at the altar and be reconciled first (Matt 5:23–24), a link Guzik makes. Worship and justice belong together.
- **"Did you bring to me sacrifices and offerings during the forty years in the wilderness?" (v25).** This question is debated. Some, like JFB, read it as expecting "yes, but": you offered sacrifices, yet you also carried idols, so your worship was never pure. Others read it as expecting "no": in the wilderness Israel had few sacrifices, yet God was with them, because what he wanted was faithfulness (Jer 7:22–23). Either way, sacrifices were never the heart of the relationship. The Tyndale notes recall the forty years as a punishment for unbelief at Kadesh (Num 14:32–35).
- **"You shall take up Sikkuth your king, and Kiyyun your star-god" (v26).** These names are hard. Most scholars think they are Sakkut and Kaiwan, Babylonian names for the planet Saturn, worshiped as a star god. The Hebrew vowels may have been changed to match the word *shiqquts*, "detestable thing," a way scribes mocked idols. Guzik notes the NIV takes them as "shrine" and "pedestal." The Tyndale notes say the "king" may be Molech (1:15). The verb can be past ("you have carried") or future ("you shall carry"). If future, the irony is cruel: they will carry their idols into exile, while the true God carries his people (Isa 46:1–4).
- **"I will send you into exile beyond Damascus" (v27).** Damascus was the farthest place Israel had fought. "Beyond Damascus" points to Assyria, which deported Israel in 722 BC to its own lands and to "the cities of the Medes" (2 Kings 17:6). The verse closes, **"says the LORD, whose name is the God of hosts,"** echoing v8.
- **Stephen quotes this passage.** In Acts 7:42–43, Stephen quotes vv25–27 from the Greek Old Testament: "You took up the tent of Moloch and the star of your god Rephan… and I will send you into exile beyond Babylon." The Greek had read the Hebrew letters differently, as "Moloch" and "Rephan." Stephen also says "Babylon" instead of "Damascus," applying the passage to the later exile of Judah, as JFB notes. His point is Amos's point: Israel's history was always marked by idolatry mixed with worship.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in vv18–27 Israel's false confidences fail one by one: scorning God's judgments, outward religious observance and long-held privileges will not save them while idolatry continues. He concludes that their only way out is repentance and reformation. JFB says the lion, bear and serpent show one disaster leading to another, and he reads v25 as idols carried alongside sacrifices from the start. Guzik ties the rejected worship to Matthew 5:23–24. The Tyndale notes explain the wadis behind v24.

#### Pointing to Christ
Three times Amos says "seek… and live," and Jesus says the same: "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), and "seek, and you will find" (Matt 7:7). Israel's worship failed because it tried to please God at the altar while trampling people in the gate. Jesus joined the two forever: he gave himself as the one sacrifice God fully accepts (Heb 10:10–14), and he summed up the law as loving God and loving neighbor (Matt 22:37–40). The day of the LORD that Israel wanted was darkness and not light. On the cross, darkness fell at noon (Mark 15:33), as Amos would later say (8:9), and Jesus bore that day for his people, so that for those in him the day of the Lord is now a day of light (1 Thess 5:4–9). From him, justice and righteousness flow like a river that never runs dry (Rev 22:1).
