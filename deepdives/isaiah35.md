*Setting:* Chapter 35 is the bright partner to chapter 34. There, the land of Edom was turned into a wasteland of pitch, thorns and wild creatures. Here, a wasteland is turned into a garden, and a road runs through it carrying God's people home to Zion. Isaiah wrote in the late 700s BC, with Assyria threatening Judah, but the picture looks well beyond that crisis: to the return from exile in Babylon, to the ministry of the Messiah, and finally to the new creation. It closes the first half of Isaiah on a note of pure hope. After four history chapters (36–39), Isaiah 40 picks up its themes again: a highway in the desert, God coming in strength, and comfort for his people.

#### The Ransomed Shall Return (vv1–10)
- **"The wilderness and the dry land shall be glad; the desert shall rejoice and blossom" (v1).** The word for "desert" is *'aravah*, the name also given to the dry rift valley running south from the Dead Sea, a place of almost no rain. The land itself is pictured as a person who sings. Creation shares in the joy of redemption, as Paul says it groans now, waiting to be set free (Rom 8:19–22).
- **"Like the crocus" (v1).** The flower is uncertain. The KJV has "rose," JFB "meadow-saffron," and modern versions "crocus," "lily" or "narcissus." It is probably a bulb flower that comes up in the dry ground after the first rains. The exact flower is less important than the picture: sudden color in a land that looked dead.
- **"The glory of Lebanon shall be given to it, the majesty of Carmel and Sharon" (v2).** These are the same three names that withered in 33:9, now restored. Lebanon was famous for its cedars, Carmel for its wooded beauty, and Sharon for its rich coastal plain. The desert receives the best features of the most fertile regions. The Tyndale notes call this a reversal of earlier judgments (2:13; 10:34; 33:9).
- **"They shall see the glory of the LORD, the majesty of our God" (v2).** The real point of the transformed land is not the flowers. It is God. "Glory" is *kavod*, his weighty, shining presence, which Isaiah saw filling the temple in chapter 6 and which "all flesh shall see" in 40:5. JFB stresses that the Lord, not the land, receives the glory.
- **"Strengthen the weak hands, and make firm the feeble knees" (v3).** Now the prophet turns to the people, and the tone becomes gentle and personal. Hands that hang limp and knees that buckle are pictures of discouragement and exhaustion (Job 4:3–4). The command is to those who are still standing: hold up the others. Hebrews 12:12 quotes this verse to tired Christians under discipline, telling them to straighten up and keep running.
- **"Say to those who have an anxious heart, 'Be strong; fear not!'" (v4).** "Anxious" is literally "hasty," a racing heart, the panic of people who feel the ground giving way. JFB: "a heart fluttered with agitation." The message is not "everything will be fine" but a reason: **"Behold, your God will come."**
- **"Your God will come with vengeance, with the recompense of God. He will come and save you" (v4).** Vengeance and salvation come together, as in 34:8 and 61:2. When God comes to put things right, it is judgment on the oppressor and rescue for the oppressed at the same time. The Tyndale notes call this the core of the good news: God is coming to save his people (40:9; 52:7).
- **"Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped" (v5).** In Isaiah, blindness and deafness are first of all spiritual. God's judgment in 6:9–10 was to make the people unable to see and hear, and Isaiah has promised the reversal (29:18; 32:3). But the promise is also physical, and this is where the chapter becomes remarkable.
- **"Then shall the lame man leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute sing for joy" (v6).** When John the Baptist, in prison, sent to ask whether Jesus was really the one who was to come, Jesus answered by quoting this chapter and 61:1: "the blind receive their sight and the lame walk… and the deaf hear" (Matt 11:4–5; Luke 7:22). In other words, the signs of God's coming in Isaiah 35 were happening in front of them. Luke even uses this picture for the lame man at the temple gate who went "walking and leaping and praising God" (Acts 3:8). Mark records the crowd's reaction when Jesus healed a deaf man with a speech impediment: "He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak" (Mark 7:37), and the rare Greek word for "speech impediment" there (*mogilalos*) is the same one the Greek Old Testament uses in this verse.
- **"For waters break forth in the wilderness, and streams in the desert" (v6).** Water in the desert is Isaiah's repeated sign of God's new work (41:18; 43:19–20; 44:3). It recalls the water from the rock in the wilderness (Exod 17:6). Jesus spoke of "rivers of living water" flowing from those who believe in him, and John explains he meant the Spirit (John 7:38–39).
- **"The burning sand shall become a pool" (v7).** The word is *sharav*. BDB gives "burning heat" and "parched ground," and it occurs only here and in 49:10 ("scorching wind"). JFB and Guzik suggest it means a mirage, as the related Arabic word *sarab* does, which would make a beautiful picture: what used to be a false promise of water becomes real water. That is possible, but the Hebrew lexicon does not give "mirage" as a meaning, so it is best held loosely.
- **"In the haunt of jackals, where they lie down, the grass shall become reeds and rushes" (v7).** In chapter 34 Edom became "the haunt of jackals" (34:13). Here the jackals' dry dens turn into marshland thick with papyrus and reeds, plants that only grow in standing water.
- **"A highway shall be there, and it shall be called the Way of Holiness" (v8).** The word for highway is *maslul*, found only here, from a root meaning to build up or cast up, a raised road. Ancient roads were often little more than tracks, and a properly built highway was something kings made for their armies or processions. This one belongs to God. The Tyndale notes connect it with the path through the Red Sea (51:10). Isaiah keeps returning to this road (11:16; 40:3; 62:10).
- **"The unclean shall not pass over it" (v8).** "Unclean" is the language of the temple (Lev 11–15). This road leads to God's holy city, and only those made clean may walk it. The question of 33:14, "Who among us can dwell with the consuming fire?" is answered with a road for those God has cleansed.
- **"It shall belong to those who walk on the way; even if they are fools, they shall not go astray" (v8).** The Hebrew is difficult, and translations vary. The ESV's reading is the most common one: the road is so clear and so well guarded that even simple people cannot lose it. The word "fools" here (*'ewilim*) means the foolish, those easily led astray, and the point is not that God rewards foolishness but that the way home does not depend on human cleverness. Henry: "The way to heaven is a plain way, and easy to hit." Another reading takes it as "fools will not wander onto it," meaning the wicked will not travel there.
- **"No lion shall be there, nor shall any ravenous beast come up on it" (v9).** Lions really did roam ancient Judah, especially in the Jordan thickets (Jer 49:19), and lonely roads were dangerous (1 Kings 13:24). The road is safe. Peter uses the picture of a lion for the devil, who "prowls around like a roaring lion" (1 Pet 5:8). On this road, he cannot touch the travelers.
- **"But the redeemed shall walk there" (v9).** "Redeemed" is *ge'ulim*, from *ga'al*, the verb of the kinsman-redeemer (*go'el*), the family member who buys back a relative's land or freedom (Lev 25:25; Ruth 4). It is one of Isaiah's great words for God in the second half of the book: "your Redeemer, the Holy One of Israel" (41:14).
- **"The ransomed of the LORD shall return and come to Zion with singing" (v10).** "Ransomed" is a different word, *peduyim*, from *padah*, "to ransom," used for buying back the firstborn (Exod 13:13–15) and for God's rescue of Israel from Egypt (Deut 7:8). BDB lists this verse under God ransoming his people from exile. Guzik connects "ransomed" to *go'el*, but that link belongs to "redeemed" in v9. Both words together say the same thing: God's people are set free at a cost, by one who has the right to claim them.
- **"Everlasting joy shall be upon their heads; they shall obtain gladness and joy, and sorrow and sighing shall flee away" (v10).** Joy is pictured as a crown or garland on their heads. Sorrow and sighing do not just fade. They run away like defeated enemies. This verse is repeated word for word in 51:11. Revelation 21:4 gives the final fulfillment: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore."
- **Which return?** Readers see layers here. The first is the return from Babylon under Cyrus in 538 BC, when exiles crossed the desert back to Jerusalem (Ezra 1). Jesus' healings show the Messiah bringing the promised signs. The full picture, with all sorrow gone and the desert in bloom, still waits for the new creation. The Tyndale notes say the prophecy extends beyond the return from exile to the final establishment of God's kingdom. Guzik also sees a partial fulfillment in Judah's recovery after 701 BC and in the farming of the land in modern Israel.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the chapter as the blessings of the gospel church, with the blossoming wilderness as the Gentile nations coming to faith: "Converting grace makes the soul that was a wilderness to rejoice with joy and singing." He takes the miracles of vv5–6 as both Jesus' bodily healings and "greater wonders… wrought on men's souls." JFB reads vv5–6 as joy at deliverance from Assyria and Babylon, but "literally, true of the antitypical times of Messiah and His miracles." He explains the highway as a raised causeway and says Christ leads the travelers, so even the simple do not wander. Guzik applies "weak hands" and "feeble knees" to believers who have stopped serving or praying, and he stresses that only the cleansed may walk the Way of Holiness, never those trying to pay their own way.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Your God will come and save you… then the eyes of the blind shall be opened." When John asked, "Are you the one who is to come?" Jesus pointed to these very signs. In him, God came. He opened blind eyes, made the lame leap and the mute sing, and he called himself "the way" (John 14:6), the road on which the unclean are made clean and the simple do not go astray. He is the Redeemer who paid the ransom (Mark 10:45). And the joy that ends this chapter is where his road leads: Zion, the city of God, where sorrow and sighing flee away for good.
