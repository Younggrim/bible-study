*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet went to him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba." This is the best known of all the Bible's prayers of repentance, and the title ties it to the darkest chapter of David's life (2 Sam 11–12). At the height of his power, David took Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah, one of his own loyal soldiers. When she became pregnant, he tried to cover it up, and when that failed he arranged for Uriah to be killed in battle and then married her. For perhaps a year he said nothing. Then God sent the prophet Nathan with a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb. David burned with anger at the rich man, and Nathan said, "You are the man!" (2 Sam 12:7). David answered, "I have sinned against the LORD" (12:13). Psalm 51 is that confession opened up. The Tyndale notes point out that the psalm itself never names the event; the title supplies it. The psalm also begins a long run of David psalms (51–65), most of them about evils he suffered. This one is about the evil he did. Church tradition counts it among the seven "penitential psalms" (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143), and in Latin it is known by its first word, the *Miserere* ("Have mercy").

#### Have Mercy on Me, O God (vv1–2)
- **"Have mercy on me, O God" (v1).** The ESV footnote gives "Be gracious to me." The verb (*chanan*) means to show favor to someone who has no claim on it. David does not start with an excuse, an explanation or a promise to do better. He starts by asking for what he does not deserve. JFB: "A plea for mercy is a confession of guilt."
- **"According to your steadfast love; according to your abundant mercy" (v1).** David's plea rests on God's character, not his own. "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) is God's loyal covenant love. "Mercy" (*rachamim*) is related to the word for womb, the tender feeling of a mother for her child (Isa 49:15). These are exactly the words God used to describe himself to Moses after Israel's great sin with the golden calf: "merciful and gracious… abounding in steadfast love… forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exod 34:6–7). David appeals to that self-description.
- **Three words for sin.** In two verses David uses all three main Hebrew words for sin, the same three as Exodus 34:7. "Transgressions" (*pesha'*) means rebellion, deliberately crossing a line (JFB: "literally, 'rebellions'"). "Iniquity" (*'avon*) means twistedness, perversion, and the guilt that comes with it. "Sin" (*chatta't*) means missing the mark, falling short. David does not choose a mild word. He uses them all.
- **Three words for cleansing.** He matches them with three pictures of cleansing. "Blot out" (*machah*) means wipe away, as writing is wiped off a record (JFB: "as from a register"), the word used of God wiping out the world in the flood (Gen 6:7) and of names blotted out of God's book (Exod 32:32). "Wash me thoroughly" (*kabas*) is the word for washing clothes, which was done by treading and beating them in water. It is not a gentle rinse. "Cleanse" (*taher*) is the priest's word for declaring someone ritually clean so they could return to worship (Lev 13:6). David asks God to erase the record, scrub the stain and declare him fit for God's presence.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Have mercy upon me for mercy's sake." Guzik explains the three words for sin as crossing a boundary, twistedness and missing the mark, and cites Maclaren on the sense of hard scrubbing in "wash." JFB on v2: "Purity as well as pardon is desired by true penitents."

#### Against You, You Only (vv3–6)
- **"For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me" (v3).** Here is the difference between this confession and David's year of silence. He no longer hides it. "I" is emphatic in Hebrew. JFB: "Conviction precedes forgiveness." Psalm 32, which many think David wrote after this forgiveness, describes what the silence had been like: "when I kept silent, my bones wasted away… your hand was heavy upon me" (32:3–4).
- **"Against you, you only, have I sinned" (v4).** David plainly sinned against Bathsheba, against Uriah, against his family and his nation. The Tyndale notes call the line hyperbole, an overstatement to make a point. Every sin against a person is first a sin against the God who made that person and gave the command that was broken. Joseph said the same when tempted by Potiphar's wife: "How then can I do this great wickedness and sin against God?" (Gen 39:9). Nathan's charge to David was, "Why have you despised the word of the LORD?" (2 Sam 12:9). JFB: sins against others are violations of God's law, so in that sense they are against him "only."
- **"So that you may be justified in your words and blameless in your judgment" (v4).** David does not argue with the verdict. Whatever God says about him is right. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 3:4 to show that God remains true even when every human proves false: "Let God be true though every one were a liar." Paul quotes the Greek Old Testament, which has "prevail when you are judged," but the point is the same: God is in the right.
- **"Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me" (v5).** David is not saying his mother sinned in conceiving him, or that sex in marriage is sinful. He is saying his problem goes all the way down. His sin with Bathsheba was not a strange slip by an otherwise good man. It came out of a nature bent toward sin from the very start of his life. The Tyndale notes stress that he is not making an excuse but admitting the depth of the problem. Christians have built on this verse in different ways. Augustine and the Western tradition (Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed) read it as a key text for original sin, a sinful nature and guilt inherited from Adam (Rom 5:12–19). Eastern Orthodox Christians speak more of an inherited mortality and tendency to sin than of inherited guilt. All agree that David is confessing a sinful condition, not just sinful acts.
- **"Behold, you delight in truth in the inward being" (v6).** Two "beholds" face each other. Behold what I am (v5); behold what you want (v6). God wants truth, faithfulness and honesty not only in behavior but "in the inward being," a rare word meaning the hidden inner parts (Job 38:36). David had lied, schemed and covered up. God wants the reverse, truth all the way through.
- **"And you teach me wisdom in the secret heart" (v6).** What God desires, he also gives. The same God who wants truth inside will teach wisdom inside.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is good to be particular in the confession of sin, that we may be the more express in praying for pardon." On v5: "This is what we call original sin, because it is as ancient as our original." Guzik makes the same point and notes that David grieves over the sin itself, not just its consequences. JFB says David's confession admits no "palliation of his crime" (no softening of it) and owns God's justice "however severe the sentence."

#### Create in Me a Clean Heart (vv7–12)
- **"Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean" (v7).** "Purge" is a striking word. It is the verb form of the word for sin (*chata'*), and in this form it means literally "un-sin me," take the sin out. Hyssop was a small bushy plant whose stems held liquid well. It was dipped in the blood of the Passover lamb and brushed on the doorposts (Exod 12:22). It was used to sprinkle blood on a healed leper (Lev 14:4–7) and to sprinkle water on someone who had touched a dead body (Num 19:18). David the adulterer and murderer feels like a leper and a man defiled by death. He asks God himself to be the priest and sprinkle him clean. Henry: the expression "alludes to a ceremonial distinction, that of cleansing the leper." JFB: the use of hyssop "suggests the idea of atonement as prominent here."
- **"Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow" (v7).** Notice the confidence: "I *shall* be clean… I *shall* be whiter than snow." Isaiah later gives God's own promise: "though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (Isa 1:18).
- **"Let me hear joy and gladness; let the bones that you have broken rejoice" (v8).** David speaks of his guilt as broken bones, the deepest kind of pain. God's hand had been heavy on him (32:4). The broken bones are what God has done to him, through conviction. But David asks the same God to turn the pain into dancing.
- **"Hide your face from my sins, and blot out all my iniquities" (v9).** He asks God to look away from the sin, though not from him. In v11 he will beg God not to send him away from his face.
- **"Create in me a clean heart, O God" (v10).** "Create" (*bara'*) is the verb of Genesis 1:1, "In the beginning, God created." In the Old Testament it is used only with God as the subject. David is not asking for a repair job. He knows no amount of effort will fix his heart. He needs God to do what only God can do: make something new. God later promised this to the whole nation: "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you" (Ezek 36:26). Henry: "He only that made the heart can new-make it; and to his power nothing is impossible."
- **"And renew a right spirit within me" (v10).** The footnote gives "steadfast." The word (*nakon*) means firm, settled, established. David had been unstable, giving in to his eyes on a rooftop. He asks for a spirit that holds steady. JFB: "constant," "firm," not yielding to temptation.
- **"Cast me not away from your presence, and take not your Holy Spirit from me" (v11).** "Holy Spirit" appears only here and in Isaiah 63:10–11 in the whole Old Testament. David had seen what it looked like when God withdrew his Spirit. When Samuel anointed David, "the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon David from that day forward," and in the very next verse "the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul" (1 Sam 16:13–14). David watched Saul's slow ruin. His great fear is not losing his throne but losing God's presence. In the Old Testament the Spirit came on particular leaders for their task, and David may be thinking especially of his anointing as king. New Testament believers have the promise that the Spirit will be with them forever (John 14:16; Eph 1:13–14), but the prayer still expresses a right fear of drifting from God. Henry: "We are undone if God take his Holy Spirit from us."
- **"Restore to me the joy of your salvation" (v12).** Note what David says he lost: not his salvation but the joy of it. JFB: he had lost "the joy of his salvation." Sin had robbed him of his song.
- **"And uphold me with a willing spirit" (v12).** "Willing" (*nedivah*) means generous, freely given. It is related to the "freewill offering," given gladly and not by obligation (Exod 35:29). David asks for a spirit that obeys gladly, not grudgingly. JFB notes it is David's own spirit made willing, not God's Spirit.
- **Commentators:** Henry, JFB and Guzik all hear more than ritual in "hyssop." Guzik connects it with the Passover blood and the priestly sprinkling, and sees David asking for cleansing through a substitute's blood. Guzik and Kidner stress that "create" describes making something from nothing, a new heart and not a mended one, and Kidner ties v11 to David's memory of Saul. JFB adds a pastoral note on v10: "renew" shows David had once had a right spirit, whose influence had been "interrupted."

#### The Sacrifices of God (vv13–19)
- **"Then I will teach transgressors your ways, and sinners will return to you" (v13).** The forgiven sinner becomes a teacher of sinners. "Return" is the same verb (*shuv*) as "restore" in v12. Restored himself, David will help others be restored. That has happened for three thousand years through this very psalm.
- **"Deliver me from bloodguiltiness, O God" (v14).** Now he names it. "Bloodguiltiness" is literally "bloods," the guilt of shed blood. JFB: "from the guilt of murder." This is Uriah. Under the law there was no sacrifice for deliberate murder or adultery. Both carried the death penalty (Lev 20:10; Num 35:31). David's only hope is God himself, "O God of my salvation."
- **"O Lord, open my lips, and my mouth will declare your praise" (v15).** Guilt had shut his mouth. Only forgiveness can open it. This verse has opened daily prayer for centuries. Jews say it before the central prayer of every service (the Amidah), and Christian morning prayer has begun with it since early times.
- **"For you will not delight in sacrifice, or I would give it" (v16).** This picks up Psalm 50. It is not that sacrifice is wrong. It is that no animal could cover what David had done. The sacrifices of the law were for unintentional sins. For sins done "with a high hand," on purpose, the law offered none (Num 15:27–31). David would give a thousand bulls if that would help. It would not.
- **"The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (v17).** What God wants is a spirit broken of its pride and a heart "contrite," crushed, pulverized. The words are strong. This is not mere regret. It is the end of self-defense. And God "will not despise" it. The proud are opposed, but "the LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (34:18; Isa 57:15; 66:2). Henry: "It is a heart breaking with itself, and breaking from its sin."
- **"Do good to Zion in your good pleasure; build up the walls of Jerusalem" (v18).** The prayer turns from David to the city. There are two main readings. Many scholars think, as the Tyndale notes suggest, that vv18–19 were added after the exile, when Jerusalem's walls really were broken down and the people, like David, needed forgiveness and restoration. Others, including Henry, read it as David's own prayer. His sin had damaged the whole kingdom (2 Sam 12:10–14), and he prays that God will not punish the nation for its king's crime. JFB: "Visit not my sin on Thy Church." Henry: "David was sensible of the wrong he had done to Judah and Jerusalem by his sin." Either way, the forgiven sinner prays for God's people.
- **"Then will you delight in right sacrifices" (v19).** Having said God does not want sacrifice (v16), the psalm ends with sacrifices God does delight in. There is no contradiction. Sacrifice offered instead of a broken heart is worthless. Sacrifice offered from a broken heart is "right." JFB: once God is reconciled, "material sacrifices will be acceptable."
- **Commentators:** Guzik, following VanGemeren, notes that "restore" (v12) and "return" (v13) share a root, so the restored David becomes a means of restoring others. He cites Spurgeon on the broken spirit as a vessel broken of its pride, and points out that David cared more for God's verdict than for the opinion of other kings. JFB on v16: "Praise is better than sacrifice," and "the joys of pardon mingle with sorrow for sin" in true penitents.

#### Pointing to Christ
David had no animal to bring for what he had done, and he knew it. All he could do was throw himself on God's steadfast love and ask God to sprinkle him with hyssop. The New Testament shows how God answered that prayer. As Jesus died, a sponge of sour wine was lifted to him "on a hyssop branch" (John 19:29), and his blood does what the blood of bulls and goats never could: it will "purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God" (Heb 9:13–14). The tax collector in Jesus' parable prayed Psalm 51's first line, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" and "went down to his house justified" (Luke 18:13–14). The new heart David asked God to create is the new covenant Jesus brought: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Cor 5:17). And the Holy Spirit David feared to lose is now given as a seal to every believer (Eph 1:13). Christ is also the son of David whom the psalm cannot describe: the one man "who knew no sin" (2 Cor 5:21), who was not "brought forth in iniquity," and who became sin for us so that sinners like David might become the righteousness of God in him. "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9).
