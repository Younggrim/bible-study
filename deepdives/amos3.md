*Setting:* Amos, a sheep-breeder from Tekoa in Judah, is preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760 BC, during the prosperous reign of Jeroboam II. His opening sermon (chs 1–2) condemned seven nations and then sprang its trap on Israel, which was selling the poor for a pair of sandals and silencing the prophets. Chapters 3–6 now press the case against Israel in a series of messages, three of which begin "Hear this word" (3:1; 4:1; 5:1). Israel believed that being God's chosen people guaranteed his protection. The Tyndale notes say these messages target exactly that mistaken belief. Chapter 3 answers it head on: being chosen makes judgment more certain, not less. The chapter moves from Israel's privilege, through a chain of riddles that explain why Amos must speak, to a summons of foreign witnesses and a picture of Samaria's luxurious ruin.

#### Israel's Guilt and Punishment (vv1–15)
- **"Hear this word… O people of Israel, against the whole family that I brought up out of the land of Egypt" (v1).** "Whole family" reaches beyond the northern kingdom to all twelve tribes, Judah included. JFB says the ten northern tribes are chiefly meant, but the phrase is wide. God begins with the exodus, the act of grace that made Israel his people (2:10).
- **"You only have I known of all the families of the earth" (v2).** "Known" (*yada'*) is a covenant word. It means to choose, to care for, to be in close personal relationship. It is the word used of Adam knowing Eve (Gen 4:1) and of God knowing Abraham, "I have chosen him" (Gen 18:19). The Tyndale notes say it goes far beyond knowing facts. God obviously knows about all nations, as chapter 1 showed. He chose only Israel to be his own (Deut 7:6–8). Henry: "Those that know God are known of him."
- **"Therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (v2).** This is the shock. Israel expected "therefore I will protect you." God says "therefore I will punish you." Privilege raises accountability. Guzik quotes G. Campbell Morgan's line that the measure of our privilege is the measure of our responsibility. JFB adds that ingratitude is added to the other sins. Jesus said the same: "Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required" (Luke 12:48). "Punish" is literally "visit," God coming to inspect and settle accounts.
- **"Do two walk together, unless they have agreed to meet?" (v3).** Now Amos asks seven questions, each expecting the answer "No." All describe cause and effect. Nothing happens without a reason. The first is the most debated. Henry reads it as a statement about God and Israel: "God and man cannot walk together except they be agreed." The Tyndale notes take it of Amos and God: the prophet's message agrees with what God is doing. Many scholars think it is simply the first link in the chain, two travelers who meet because they arranged to, preparing for the point that comes in v6–8.
- **"Does a lion roar in the forest, when he has no prey?" (v4).** A lion roars when it has already caught its prey or is about to spring. JFB: the lion "roars loudly only when he has prey in sight." The young lion growling in its den has made a kill. If God is roaring (1:2), Israel is the prey.
- **"Does a bird fall in a snare on the earth, when there is no trap for it?" (v5).** Bird traps were common: a net or noose, sprung by a trigger. A bird doesn't fall without a trap, and a trap doesn't snap shut without catching something. JFB applies it: God will not withdraw the invaders until they have taken Israel. The Tyndale notes say the trap is the consequence of Israel's sin.
- **"Is a trumpet blown in a city, and the people are not afraid?" (v6).** The trumpet is the *shophar*, blown by a watchman on the wall when an enemy is sighted (Ezek 33:2–6). When it sounds, people run. Amos's preaching is that trumpet, yet Israel is not afraid.
- **"Does disaster come to a city, unless the LORD has done it?" (v6).** This is where the riddles were heading. "Disaster" is *ra'ah*, which in older English was "evil," as in the KJV's "shall there be evil in a city, and the LORD hath not done it?" It means calamity, not moral evil. God is not the author of sin, but he is Lord over war, drought and disaster, and he uses them in judgment (Isa 45:7; Lam 3:38). Henry: "The evil of trouble, personal or public, is from God, and is his doing." JFB makes the same distinction: God sends the calamity, while the evil of sin is the people's own. Guzik stresses that when judgment comes, it will not be bad luck or fate.
- **"For the Lord GOD does nothing without revealing his secret to his servants the prophets" (v7).** "Secret" is *sod*, which means a confidential conversation or a council, the inner circle where plans are made (Jer 23:18, 22). God lets his prophets in on his plans before he acts, as he told Abraham about Sodom (Gen 18:17) and Noah about the flood. The purpose is mercy: a warning gives time to repent. Guzik notes this is not a promise that God reveals every detail of every event in advance, since some things were hidden until their time (Eph 3:5), but that when God brings a judgment like this on his people, he warns them first.
- **"The lion has roared; who will not fear? The Lord GOD has spoken; who can but prophesy?" (v8).** The last two questions bring the point home and reverse the order: effect, then cause. Anyone who hears a lion's roar trembles. Anyone who hears God speak must pass it on. This is Amos's defense against the charge, soon to be made at Bethel, that he had no right to preach in Israel (7:12–15). He is not a professional; he is a man who heard a lion. Henry: "The prophets cannot but make that known to the people which God has made known to them." Jeremiah felt the same fire in his bones (Jer 20:9), and Peter and John said, "we cannot but speak" (Acts 4:20).
- **"Proclaim to the strongholds in Ashdod and to the strongholds in the land of Egypt" (v9).** Now God calls foreign witnesses. Ashdod was one of the main Philistine cities. Egypt was Israel's ancient oppressor. These nations had their own sins (1:6–8), yet even they are invited to look at Samaria and be shocked. Henry: "why should their friends consult their reputation, when they themselves do not consult it?" Guzik notes that Israel's crimes would offend even pagans, judged by plain human justice. The Greek Old Testament reads "Assyria" for "Ashdod," which would pair the two great powers on either side of Israel, but the Hebrew "Ashdod" is likely original.
- **"Assemble yourselves on the mountains of Samaria" (v9).** Samaria, Israel's capital, sat on a hill surrounded by higher hills, a natural grandstand. From there, the witnesses would see **"great tumults within her, and the oppressed in her midst."** The word for "tumults" means panic and disorder, social chaos. The Tyndale notes say that Israel looked strong outside, but was rotten and divided inside.
- **"They do not know how to do right… those who store up violence and robbery in their strongholds" (v10).** "Right" means what is straight, honest. They have done wrong so long they no longer know how to do right. What they store in their fortified mansions is not treasure but "violence and robbery," because the treasure was gained by violence. Henry: "Those know not to do right who think to enrich themselves by doing wrong."
- **"An adversary shall surround the land and bring down your defenses" (v11).** Amos does not name the enemy, but the Tyndale notes say it could only be Assyria, the one great power of the region. The strongholds where they stored their plunder would themselves be plundered. The punishment fits the crime.
- **"As the shepherd rescues from the mouth of the lion two legs, or a piece of an ear" (v12).** Amos speaks as a shepherd. Under the law, a shepherd whose sheep was killed by a wild animal had to bring back the remains as proof, so he would not be charged with stealing it (Exod 22:13; Gen 31:39). So "rescue" here is bitter irony. What is "rescued" is only evidence that the sheep is dead. The Tyndale notes say Israel thought it would be saved because it was chosen, and the only rescue would be a few scraps. Henry: survivors "shall be very few, and those of the meanest and least considerable."
- **"With the corner of a couch and part of a bed" (v12).** The rich of Samaria lounged on luxurious couches (6:4). The second phrase is hard. The Hebrew could be read "the damask of a bed" (fine fabric, a word possibly related to the name Damascus) or "in Damascus on a couch." JFB mentions both and favors a picture of Samaria's luxury. The ESV takes it as "part of a bed." The point is clear in any case: of Samaria's lavish life, only a broken bit of furniture will be left.
- **"Hear, and testify against the house of Jacob" (v13).** The witnesses are called again. "House of Jacob" recalls the nation's ancestor, who first named Bethel and met God there twice (Gen 28:19; 35:6–15). **"The Lord GOD, the God of hosts."** "Hosts" means armies, the angelic and starry armies of heaven. The Tyndale notes say it marks him as the true God of the universe, not a local god.
- **"I will punish the altars of Bethel" (v14).** Bethel, "house of God," was where Jacob saw the ladder to heaven (Gen 28:10–22). Two centuries before Amos, Jeroboam I set up a golden calf there so his people would not go to Jerusalem to worship (1 Kings 12:26–33). The Tyndale notes say it mixed the worship of the LORD with the bull image. It was still the king's sanctuary in Amos's day (7:13). God's judgment begins at the religious center, because false worship lay underneath Israel's injustice.
- **"The horns of the altar shall be cut off and fall to the ground" (v14).** Israelite altars had a horn-shaped projection at each corner (Exod 27:2). Blood of sacrifice was put on them (Lev 4:7), and a person fleeing for his life could grab them for protection (1 Kings 1:50; 2:28). Cutting off the horns meant there would be no refuge, no atonement and no sanctuary. Henry: "If men will not destroy idolatrous altars, God will." About 140 years later King Josiah tore down the altar at Bethel (2 Kings 23:15), as a prophet had foretold in Jeroboam I's own day (1 Kings 13:1–3).
- **"I will strike the winter house along with the summer house" (v15).** The rich had two homes. JFB says winter houses faced south for sun and summer houses stood in cooler hills. Assyrian kings and the kings of Israel did the same (Jer 36:22). Henry: "They indulged a foolish affectation of change and variety."
- **"The houses of ivory shall perish" (v15).** Houses inlaid with ivory panels were the height of luxury. Ahab had built one (1 Kings 22:39). The Tyndale notes say archaeologists digging at Samaria found hundreds of carved ivory fragments from this period, the "Samaria ivories," now in museums. Guzik mentions evidence from other sites of a new divide in the eighth century between large homes and crowded small ones. **"The great houses shall come to an end."** Or "the many houses." Every kind of house falls: winter, summer, ivory and great. Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 BC.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the moral thread: privilege brings responsibility, trouble comes from God's hand, and prophets must speak what they hear. JFB reads the questions of vv3–6 as showing that the prophets speak in agreement with God, and the snare of v5 as the invader who will not be withdrawn until his work is done. Guzik follows the same chain of cause and effect, and notes that God judges Israel by the plain standards of justice that even pagan nations recognized. The Tyndale notes add the archaeology of the ivories and the reminder that Bethel was a royal shrine.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel's privilege was real: God had known them as he knew no other nation. But privilege without faithfulness only made judgment surer. The horns of the altar, the place of refuge and atonement, would be cut off. In Christ, God provides an altar that cannot be cut down (Heb 13:10–12). Jesus is the Good Shepherd who does not arrive too late with two legs and an ear. He lays down his life for the sheep and lets none be snatched out of his hand (John 10:11, 28). And the God who "does nothing without revealing his secret" has now told the whole mystery of his plan in his Son (Eph 1:9–10; Heb 1:1–2), so that the lion's roar can be heard in time.
