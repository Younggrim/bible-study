*Setting:* Directly after the scene at En-gedi, where David spared Saul in the cave and Saul wept, admitted "you are more righteous than I," and went home (24:17–22). David knows better than to trust those tears, and he stays in the wilderness strongholds of southern Judah with his six hundred men. This chapter opens with the death of Samuel, the prophet who anointed both Saul and David. Then comes one of the most vivid stories in the Old Testament. A rich fool insults David, David sets out for revenge, and a wise woman stops him. The story sits between the two times David spares Saul (chs 24 and 26), and it tests him in a new way. Sparing a king out of reverence for God was one thing. Holding back from killing an ordinary man who had insulted him was another, and here David nearly fails.

#### The Death of Samuel (v1)
- **"Now Samuel died" (v1).** Three words in Hebrew end an era. Samuel was the last of the judges, a prophet and priest, the man who prayed Israel through the change from judges to kings. He had been born in answer to Hannah's prayer (ch 1) and had served from boyhood until old age (12:2). His death is mentioned again in 28:3, setting up the scene where Saul seeks him through a medium.
- **"All Israel assembled and mourned for him" (v1).** The whole nation grieved, as it had for Moses (Deut 34:8) and Aaron (Num 20:29). Samuel was honored as one of Israel's greatest intercessors (Jer 15:1; Ps 99:6), and he appears in the list of the faithful in Hebrews 11:32.
- **"They buried him in his house at Ramah" (v1).** "In his house" probably means on his family property or in a family tomb at his hometown, where he had always lived and judged (7:17).
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "All Israel lamented Samuel, and they had reason. He prayed daily for them," and adds, "Those have hard hearts, who can bury faithful ministers without grief." Guzik notes that God's work did not die with Samuel. The prophet's influence kept going in the king he had anointed.

#### David and Abigail (vv1–44)
- **"David rose and went down to the wilderness of Paran" (v1).** The Hebrew reads Paran, a desert far to the south in the Sinai region. The Greek Septuagint reads "Maon," which fits the story that follows. Either way, with Samuel gone David moves further from Saul's reach.
- **"A man in Maon whose business was in Carmel" (v2).** This Carmel is a small town in the hills of Judah, about a mile north of Maon and seven or eight miles south of Hebron, not Mount Carmel on the coast. Saul had set up a monument to himself here after defeating the Amalekites (15:12).
- **"Three thousand sheep and a thousand goats" (v2).** Nabal was very rich. Sheep-shearing was a festive season, like harvest for a farmer, with feasting and celebration and an expected generosity toward neighbors and the poor (2 Sam 13:23–24).
- **"Nabal… and Abigail" (v3).** The two names tell the story. *Nabal* means "fool," not in the sense of someone simple but of someone morally foolish, who lives as if God doesn't matter. "The fool [*nabal*] says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Ps 14:1). *Abigail* means "my father is joy." She is "discerning and beautiful," literally "good of understanding and beautiful of form." He is "harsh and badly behaved."
- **"He was a Calebite" (v3).** Nabal belonged to the clan of Caleb, the faithful spy who settled around Hebron (Josh 14:13–14). There may also be wordplay here, because *keleb* means "dog." The Hebrew written text can be read either as "Calebite" or "as his heart," and some have taken it as "doglike." The noble ancestry only highlights how far this descendant has fallen.
- **"Peace be to you, and peace be to your house, and peace be to all that you have" (v6).** David's greeting is warm and respectful, three times over.
- **"Your shepherds have been with us, and we did them no harm" (v7).** David's men had guarded Nabal's flocks in the open country against raiders and wild animals. Nabal's own shepherd later confirms this: "They were a wall to us both by night and by day" (v16). David is not running a protection racket. He is asking, politely, for a share of the feast in return for real service.
- **"Your son David" (v8).** David calls himself Nabal's "son," a humble and courteous term. He asks only for "whatever you have at hand."
- **"Who is David? Who is the son of Jesse?" (v10).** Nabal knew perfectly well who David was. The whole country sang about him. "The son of Jesse" is the same contempt Saul used (20:27; 22:7). "Many servants these days… are breaking away from their masters" is a jab at David as a runaway from Saul. Nabal's words "my bread and my water and my meat" (v11) are full of "my." He forgets whose land and flocks they really are.
- **"Every man strap on his sword!" (v13).** The line repeats three times in one verse: David, his men, every man. It is the fastest decision David makes in the chapter, and there is no prayer, no inquiring of the LORD as in chapter 23. Four hundred men march, two hundred stay with the gear.
- **"One of the young men told Abigail" (v14).** A servant goes not to Nabal but to his wife. "He is such a worthless man that one cannot speak to him" (v17). "Worthless man" is "son of Belial," the same phrase used for Eli's sons (2:12) and for the men who despised Saul (10:27).
- **"Abigail made haste" (v18).** The word "hurried" comes again and again for her (vv18, 23, 34, 42). Two hundred loaves, two skins of wine, five dressed sheep, roasted grain, raisins and fig cakes. This was a feast's worth of food, loaded on donkeys and sent ahead, just as Jacob sent gifts ahead to calm Esau (Gen 32:13–21). "She did not tell her husband Nabal" (v19). The text does not praise or blame her for this. It simply shows that she had to act and that Nabal could not be reasoned with.
- **"If by morning I leave so much as one male" (v22).** David swears to wipe out every man and boy of Nabal's household. The Hebrew is a crude expression, "one who urinates against the wall." The oath is the kind of rash vow Saul made in 14:24 and 14:44. David, who would not touch Saul, is ready to massacre a household over an insult.
- **Abigail's speech (vv24–31).** It is one of the longest speeches by a woman in the Old Testament, and one of the wisest anywhere in Scripture.
- **"On me alone, my lord, be the guilt" (v24).** She takes the blame, though it was not hers.
- **"As his name is, so is he" (v25).** She does not defend Nabal. She tells David he is not worth the trouble.
- **"The LORD has restrained you from bloodguilt and from saving with your own hand" (v26).** She speaks as though David has already decided not to kill, giving him a way back. "Saving with your own hand" means taking vengeance or securing yourself by your own power, rather than trusting God to do it.
- **"The LORD will certainly make my lord a sure house" (v28).** This is remarkable. She speaks of a lasting dynasty for David before God's promise of it in 2 Samuel 7:16. She calls him the one "fighting the battles of the LORD," the true calling of Israel's king (18:17).
- **"Bound in the bundle of the living in the care of the LORD your God" (v29).** The *tseror* was a pouch or bag tied up to keep valuables safe. David's life is wrapped and kept in God's own care. The phrase became a familiar Jewish prayer for the dead and is still carved, in abbreviated form, on many Jewish gravestones.
- **"The lives of your enemies he shall sling out as from the hollow of a sling" (v29).** The opposite picture. David's enemies will be thrown away like stones from a sling, the weapon David used against Goliath. She is gently reminding him how God won his first great victory, without David needing to take revenge.
- **"No cause of grief or pangs of conscience" (v31).** Literally, no "stumbling" or "staggering of heart." She asks David to think ahead to the day he is king and not to carry a massacre on his conscience. "Remember your servant" when that day comes.
- **"Blessed be the LORD… who sent you this day to meet me!" (v32).** David receives correction gracefully, and from an unexpected source. He blesses God, he blesses her "discretion" (good sense, *ta'am*), and he blesses her. He owns what he was about to do (v34).
- **"He was holding a feast in his house, like the feast of a king" (v36).** Nabal is feasting like a king while David, the true future king, is camping in the wilderness. His heart is "merry," and he is "very drunk." He has no idea how close he came to dying.
- **"His heart died within him, and he became as a stone" (v37).** When he hears in the morning, the shock hits him like a stroke or heart failure. He lies paralysed, and "about ten days later the LORD struck Nabal, and he died" (v38). The text is plain about who acted. David did not need to lift his sword.
- **"Blessed be the LORD who has avenged the insult" (v39).** David sees the lesson Abigail taught him. God kept him from wrongdoing and dealt with Nabal himself. He learns here what he will need when he faces Saul again in chapter 26.
- **"To take her as his wife" (vv39–42).** The proposal is quick, but in the culture a widow without a protector was vulnerable, and Abigail was a wealthy and capable woman. She answers with deep humility: "Your handmaid is a servant to wash the feet of the servants of my lord" (v41). She goes with him, giving up her estate's security to share the life of a fugitive. She will be captured and rescued in chapter 30 and bear David a son, Chileab, also called Daniel (2 Sam 3:3; 1 Chr 3:1).
- **"David also took Ahinoam of Jezreel" (v43).** This Jezreel was a village in the hills of Judah near Maon and Ziph (Josh 15:55–56), not the northern city. Saul's wife was also named Ahinoam (14:50), but the text gives no sign this is the same woman. Ahinoam became the mother of Amnon, David's firstborn (2 Sam 3:2).
- **"Saul had given Michal his daughter… to Palti" (v44).** While David was away, Saul gave his wife to another man, probably to cut David's tie to the royal family. David later demands her back (2 Sam 3:13–16).
- **David's wives.** The Old Testament records polygamy without commanding it, and it repeatedly shows the trouble it brings. Deuteronomy 17:17 warns that a king "shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away." David's large family will later become the source of his deepest heartbreaks (2 Sam 13–18).
- **Commentators:** Henry warns Nabal's kind, "We mistake, if we think we are absolute lords of what we have," and of Abigail's gentle approach says simply, "Yielding pacifies great offences." On the danger of revenge, "The sweetness of revenge is soon turned into bitterness," and of Nabal at his feast, "Sinners are often most secure when most in danger." JFB reads the "bundle of life" as an image of David's life "secure under God's protection," and notes that the name "Nabal" "gave pertinence to his wife's remark." It calls the quick marriage "an unceremonious proceeding" in keeping with the customs of the time. Guzik argues that David was owed a fair share for real service, not running a racket, but says that when David swore revenge he was right about the facts and wrong in his heart. He praises Abigail's speech for lifting David up rather than beating him down, and links Nabal at his feast to Jesus' parable of the rich fool (Luke 12:16–21). He also notes that the text does not explain or justify her acting behind her husband's back, since its focus is on how she turned David from sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Abigail steps between an angry king and a guilty household, takes the blame on herself, brings a gift, and turns away wrath. She is a picture, imperfect but real, of the work of a mediator. Jesus did far more. He did not only plead "on me alone be the guilt." He actually bore the guilt of those who deserved judgment (Isa 53:5–6; 1 Pet 2:24). Nabal had every good gift and treated it as his own, and he died a fool. Jesus tells of a rich man who said, "Soul, you have ample goods… eat, drink, be merry," and God said, "Fool! This night your soul is required of you" (Luke 12:19–20). And David learns that he does not need to save himself with his own hand. That is the heart of the gospel too: it is God who saves, and our lives are "bound in the bundle of the living" in his care.
