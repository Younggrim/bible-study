*Setting:* Capernaum and the countryside of Galilee, early in Jesus' public ministry, around AD 28. Chapter 1 showed Jesus' authority over demons, disease and leprosy, and left him so famous that he could no longer openly enter a town (1:45). Now he comes back to Capernaum, his home base on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee. Chapter 2 begins a group of five conflict stories that runs to 3:6. In each one, religious leaders object to something Jesus or his disciples do: forgiving sins, eating with sinners, not fasting, and (twice) the Sabbath. The objections grow sharper each time, until in 3:6 the Pharisees and Herodians plot to kill him. Each story ends with Jesus revealing more of who he is: the Son of Man with authority to forgive, the physician of sinners, the bridegroom, and the lord of the Sabbath.

#### Jesus Heals a Paralytic (vv1–12)
- **"It was reported that he was at home" (v1).** "At home" probably means Peter's house (1:29). Houses in Capernaum were built of rough black basalt stones around a small courtyard, with flat roofs reached by an outside stair. A main room might hold a few dozen people. **"Not even at the door" (v2)** pictures the crowd packed into the room and spilling out into the street.
- **"He was preaching the word to them" (v2).** Mark wants it clear that teaching came first. The crowds wanted healing, and Jesus gave them the word.
- **"A paralytic carried by four men" (v3).** Only Mark mentions the four, the kind of detail an eyewitness like Peter would remember. The man was probably on a simple mat or pallet that could be carried by its corners.
- **"They removed the roof above him" (v4).** Literally "they unroofed the roof," and "made an opening" is "dug through." A Galilean roof was beams laid across the walls, covered with branches, reeds and packed clay. The friends climbed up, dug a hole while Jesus was speaking below, and let the mat down by ropes. Dust and bits of clay would have been falling on the crowd. Luke, writing for readers who knew tiled roofs, says "through the tiles" (Luke 5:19). It was a bold, inconvenient, even destructive act, and it was all done to get their friend to Jesus.
- **"When Jesus saw their faith" (v5).** Faith is something Jesus can *see*: in this case, a hole in the roof. "Their" likely includes both the four and the paralyzed man. It is a picture of what friends can do for someone who cannot come to Jesus alone.
- **"Son, your sins are forgiven" (v5).** Not what anyone expected. "Son" (literally "child") is tender. Many in that day assumed sickness came from sin (John 9:2), and Jesus does not say this man's paralysis was caused by a particular sin. But he goes to the deeper need first. The verb is passive, "are forgiven," a way of saying God forgives, and Jesus is announcing it on his own authority. He does not say "God will forgive you if you offer a sacrifice." He declares it done.
- **"He is blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v7).** The scribes are right about the theology. Only God can forgive sins against him (Isa 43:25; Ps 103:3). A priest could pronounce forgiveness through the sacrifices God had appointed, but not on his own word. If Jesus is not God, this is blasphemy, which carried the death penalty (Lev 24:16). The charge here is the same one that will condemn him at the end (14:64). They do not say it aloud. They are "questioning in their hearts."
- **"Jesus, perceiving in his spirit" (v8).** He knows what they are thinking, which in Scripture is something God does (1 Sam 16:7; Jer 17:10).
- **"Which is easier…?" (v9).** It is easier to *say* "your sins are forgiven," because no one can check. To say "Rise and walk" is to risk being proved a fraud on the spot. So Jesus does the visible thing to prove the invisible one.
- **"That you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (v10).** This is the first time "Son of Man" appears in Mark, and it is Jesus' favorite name for himself. It could simply mean "a human being" (Ps 8:4; Ezek 2:1). But its deeper source is Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds of heaven and is given "dominion and glory and a kingdom." It let Jesus claim the highest role without the political baggage of "Messiah." "On earth" matters: forgiveness, which belongs in heaven, is now being given on earth, in a crowded house in Capernaum.
- **"Rise, pick up your bed, and go home" (v11).** Three commands to a man who could not move. **"He rose and immediately picked up his bed and went out before them all" (v12).** The crowd that blocked the door now makes way for him. The mat that carried him he now carries.
- **"We never saw anything like this!" (v12).** Even the people who crowded in to see miracles had not seen this. They "glorified God," which is the right response. Whether the scribes joined in, Mark does not say.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that Jesus "saw their faith," and says, "Sin is the procuring cause of all our pains and sicknesses," so forgiveness reaches the root. On the scribes' question, he grants that "None can forgive sins but God only" is true; their error was refusing to see who Jesus was. JFB calls the four bearers "a graphic particular of Mark only" and admits about the roof that "it is impossible to determine precisely how the thing was done." It reads "thy sins be forgiven" as "an authoritative proclamation of the man's pardoned state," and asks, "Is it easier to command away disease than to bid away sin?" Guzik, following Morgan, stresses that the roof was dug through, not just uncovered, and sees the scribes caught by their own logic: they were right that only God forgives, and wrong about who was standing in front of them.

#### Jesus Calls Levi (vv13–17)
- **"He went out again beside the sea" (v13).** Out of the crowded house to the open shore, where more could hear.
- **"Levi the son of Alphaeus sitting at the tax booth" (v14).** Matthew's Gospel calls him Matthew (Matt 9:9), and many people in the New Testament had two names. Capernaum lay on a trade road near the border between Herod Antipas's Galilee and his brother Philip's territory to the east, so goods passing through paid tolls. Levi was likely a toll collector working under a contract for Herod. Toll collectors paid for the right to collect and kept what they could take above the set amount, and many cheated (Luke 3:12–13; 19:8). Pious Jews counted them with thieves and prostitutes, and they were often barred from serving as witnesses in court. A "James the son of Alphaeus" is listed among the twelve (3:18), and some think he was Levi's brother, but that is uncertain.
- **"Follow me.' And he rose and followed him" (v14).** The same call as the fishermen (1:17), but at greater cost. A fisherman could go back to his nets, and Peter later did (John 21:3). A man who walked away from a tax contract could not easily get it back. "Rose" is the same verb used of the paralytic (v12). Both men get up at Jesus' word to a new life.
- **"As he reclined at table in his house" (v15).** At a formal meal people lay on cushions around a low table, leaning on the left elbow. The house is probably Levi's; Luke calls it "a great feast in his house" (Luke 5:29). Levi's first act as a disciple is to throw a party and invite his friends to meet Jesus.
- **"Many tax collectors and sinners" (v15).** "Sinners" was a recognized category: people known for immoral lives, or who worked in trades that made them ritually unclean, or who simply did not keep the Pharisees' strict standards of purity and tithing. They were treated as outsiders to God's people.
- **"The scribes of the Pharisees" (v16).** The Pharisees were a lay movement devoted to keeping the law carefully in every area of life, including purity at meals, and to applying the priests' purity rules to ordinary homes. Sharing a table meant sharing life and acceptance. To eat with sinners was to say they were welcome. That is exactly what Jesus meant to say (Luke 15:1–2).
- **"Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick" (v17).** A common proverb, turned to a sharp point. A doctor who will not go near the sick is useless. **"I came not to call the righteous, but sinners."** Jesus is not saying the Pharisees are righteous and do not need him. He is saying that only people who know they are sick go to the doctor. The irony is that those who think they are well are in the most danger.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "Christ prevented him with this call," meaning Christ came to Levi before Levi came to him, and he quotes the fuller form of the saying, "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance," found in some manuscripts of Mark and in Luke 5:32. Guzik, citing Lane and Wessel, explains why tax collectors were shut out of society and argues that leaving the booth cost Levi more than leaving the nets cost the fishermen. He pictures Jesus as the physician who is always available, diagnoses rightly, heals completely and pays the bill himself. JFB refers these verses to its notes on Matthew 9.

#### A Question About Fasting (vv18–22)
- **"John's disciples and the Pharisees were fasting" (v18).** The law required fasting only on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29–31). Many devout Jews added more. The Pharisees fasted twice a week, on Mondays and Thursdays (Luke 18:12). John's disciples may have been fasting in grief over their teacher's arrest (1:14), or simply as part of his strict way of life. Jesus' disciples were noticeably not fasting, and people wanted to know why.
- **"Can the wedding guests fast while the bridegroom is with them?" (v19).** A Jewish wedding was a week-long celebration, and the guests were excused from certain religious duties to join in. Fasting at a wedding would be absurd and rude. In the Old Testament God himself is the bridegroom of his people (Isa 54:5–6; 62:5; Hos 2:19–20). Jesus quietly takes that role. John the Baptist had used the same image: "The one who has the bride is the bridegroom" (John 3:29).
- **"The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them" (v20).** "Taken away" is a strong word and the first hint in Mark of Jesus' death, long before his clear predictions (8:31). When he is gone, his followers will fast, and the early church did (Acts 13:2–3; 14:23). Fasting is not abolished. It belongs to the right time.
- **"No one sews a piece of unshrunk cloth on an old garment" (v21).** New wool cloth that had not yet been washed would shrink the first time it got wet, pulling against the old fabric around it and tearing a worse hole. The point is not about patching but about trying to fit what is new into old forms that cannot hold it.
- **"New wine is for fresh wineskins" (v22).** Wine was stored in goatskins sewn shut. New wine keeps fermenting and gives off gas. A new skin stretches with it. An old skin, already stretched and brittle, bursts, and both wine and skin are lost. What Jesus brings is not a repair to the religious system of his day, or one more set of rules added to the old ones. It is new, and it needs a new shape. That does not mean the Old Testament was discarded. Jesus fulfills it (Matt 5:17). But the way of life built up around it by human tradition could not contain the kingdom he was bringing.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the disciples did not fast because "the nuptials were yet in the celebrating," and he reads the cloth and wine as Christ's gentleness with young believers, not loading them with strict duties before they could bear them. Guzik agrees that fasting is good in its proper time and reads the wineskins as the new life of the kingdom that old forms cannot contain. The two are different emphases rather than a disagreement: Henry looks at the disciples' weakness, Guzik at the newness of what Jesus brings. JFB refers this section to its notes on Luke 5.

#### Jesus Is Lord of the Sabbath (vv23–28)
- **"His disciples began to pluck heads of grain" (v23).** The law allowed a hungry traveler to pick grain by hand from a neighbor's field, though not to use a sickle (Deut 23:25). Picking was not the problem. The day was. The law forbade work on the Sabbath (Exod 20:8–11; 34:21), and the rabbis had defined "work" in great detail. The Mishnah later lists thirty-nine kinds of forbidden work, including reaping and threshing. Plucking heads of grain counted as reaping, and rubbing them in the hands (Luke 6:1) as threshing.
- **"Why are they doing what is not lawful on the Sabbath?" (v24).** The Pharisees hold Jesus responsible for his disciples, as a teacher was. Notice that God's law did not forbid this. Their own interpretation of it did.
- **"Have you never read what David did?" (v25).** To the experts in Scripture: "Have you never read?" Jesus answers from the Bible itself. In 1 Samuel 21:1–6, David, fleeing from Saul, came to the tabernacle at Nob hungry and was given the holy bread.
- **"The bread of the Presence" (v26).** Twelve loaves were set out on a gold table in the holy place every Sabbath, "before the LORD," and when replaced the old loaves were eaten by the priests alone (Lev 24:5–9). The priest gave them to David and his men because of their need. Scripture does not condemn him. Human need, in that case, came before a ceremonial rule.
- **"In the time of Abiathar the high priest" (v26).** This is a well-known difficulty. In 1 Samuel 21 the priest who gave David the bread was Ahimelech. His son Abiathar escaped the massacre at Nob that followed (1 Sam 22:20) and became David's high priest. Matthew and Luke leave the name out, and some manuscripts of Mark omit the phrase too. Several answers have been suggested. The Greek phrase *epi Abiathar* can mean "in the passage about Abiathar," since Jews referred to sections of Scripture by a key name or event; Mark uses the same construction in 12:26, "in the passage about the bush." Or Abiathar, the more famous priest, was present and later became high priest, so the time is named after him, as one might say "in the days of President Lincoln" about something before he took office. Or the names were used loosely; the two are even reversed in 2 Samuel 8:17 and 1 Chronicles 18:16. None of these settles it beyond doubt. The point of Jesus' argument does not depend on it.
- **"The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (v27).** God gave the Sabbath as a gift: rest for weary bodies, freedom from endless work, time for worship, a weekly reminder of creation and of rescue from slavery (Exod 20:11; Deut 5:15). The Pharisees' rules had turned the gift into a burden. Some Jewish teachers said something similar, that the Sabbath was given to Israel, not Israel to the Sabbath. Jesus is not throwing out the day. He is restoring its purpose.
- **"So the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (v28).** This is the climax. The God who made the Sabbath is its Lord (Isa 58:13, "my holy day"). Jesus claims that authority for himself, just as he claimed the authority to forgive sins in the first story of this chapter. The chapter opens and closes with Jesus doing what belongs to God alone. Hebrews later says that Jesus himself gives the true Sabbath rest that God's people enter (Heb 4:9–10; compare Matt 11:28).
- **Christians differ** on what the Sabbath command means for believers today. Some keep Sunday as the Christian Sabbath. Others see the Sabbath fulfilled in Christ and keep the Lord's day as a day of worship without Sabbath rules (Col 2:16–17; Rom 14:5). This passage does not settle that question, but all agree on its point: Jesus is Lord of the day, and it was meant for human good.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Ritual observances must give way to moral obligations," and he says Christ "is always to be looked upon as Lord of the sabbath." Guzik explains that the Pharisees counted the disciples' plucking and rubbing as reaping, threshing and winnowing, and stresses that Jesus never broke God's Sabbath command, only the human rules added to it. On Abiathar he gives several of the solutions above without insisting on one. He ties v27 to Hosea 6:6, "I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice," which Jesus quotes in Matthew's version of this story (Matt 12:7). JFB refers the section to its notes on Matthew 12 and does not address the Abiathar question here.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every scene in this chapter shows Jesus doing what only God can do, and doing it for people who do not deserve it. He forgives a paralyzed man's sins on his own word, calls a despised tax collector to be his follower, eats with sinners as their physician, calls himself the bridegroom of God's people, and claims to be Lord of the day God made holy. The leaders keep asking "Why?" The answers point to who he is. He is the Son of Man with heaven's authority on earth, who came "not to call the righteous, but sinners." And already there is a shadow: the bridegroom will be "taken away." The one who forgives sins will pay for them.
