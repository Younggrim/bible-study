*Setting:* Babylonia, during the exile, probably in the years after 585 BC. These two chapters carry no date. Chapters 34–37 have promised Israel a good shepherd, a restored land, a new heart, God's Spirit, resurrection from the grave of exile, and a reunited kingdom with God's sanctuary among them forever. A careful reader might ask: what if, after all that, the nations come again? Chapters 38–39 answer that question with one long scene. A vast army from the far ends of the earth, led by a mysterious figure called Gog, attacks God's people as they live in peace, and God himself destroys it without Israel lifting a sword. The style is closer to apocalyptic writing than to Ezekiel's earlier oracles against named nations: the numbers are symbolic, the enemies come from the edges of the known world, and the defeat is cosmic. Revelation picks up the names "Gog and Magog" for the final rebellion against God (Rev 20:7–10). Few chapters in the Bible have drawn more confident predictions, and more failed ones, so this one calls for care.

#### Prophecy Against Gog (vv1–23)
- **"Set your face toward Gog, of the land of Magog" (v2).** Gog appears nowhere else in the Old Testament as a ruler. The only other Gog is an obscure man in a genealogy (1 Chr 5:4). Some scholars connect the name with Gyges, a king of Lydia in western Turkey around 680–644 BC, known to the Assyrians as Gugu. Others think it is a title, like "Pharaoh." Most agree Ezekiel uses it as a name for a distant, shadowy enemy beyond Israel's horizon. **Magog** appears in the Table of Nations as a son of Japheth, alongside Gomer, Meshech and Tubal (Gen 10:2). It seems to mean "the land of Gog," somewhere in the far north.
- **"The chief prince of Meshech and Tubal" (vv2–3).** The Hebrew is *nesi rosh meshech wetuval*. *Rosh* normally means "head" or "chief," and the ESV takes it that way, with a footnote giving the alternative "prince of Rosh." The Greek translation read *Rosh* as a place name, and some older and many popular writers have connected it with Russia, and Meshech and Tubal with Moscow and Tobolsk. That link rests only on sound. "Russia" comes from a medieval Viking people, the Rus, more than 1,500 years after Ezekiel, and no ancient people called Rosh is known in the region. Meshech and Tubal, by contrast, are well known from Assyrian records as Mushki and Tabal, peoples of central and eastern Anatolia (modern Turkey). Ezekiel named them as Tyre's traders (27:13) and placed them in the grave with the fallen nations (32:26).
- **"I will turn you about and put hooks into your jaws, and I will bring you out" (v4).** This is the same picture used for Pharaoh the crocodile (29:4) and for Assyria's king (2 Kgs 19:28). Gog thinks he is a free conqueror. In fact God leads him like a captured animal. The army is described in full: **horses and horsemen… buckler and shield, wielding swords.** It is terrifying, and it is on a leash.
- **"Persia, Cush, and Put are with them… Gomer… Beth-togarmah from the uttermost parts of the north" (vv5–6).** The allies come from the ends of the known world. Persia lay far to the east (modern Iran). Cush was the land south of Egypt (modern Sudan). Put was probably Libya to the west. Gomer is usually identified with the Cimmerians, a horse people who swept into Anatolia from north of the Black Sea in the 700s BC. Beth-togarmah was in eastern Anatolia, near Armenia (27:14). Leaving aside Magog, Gog's own land, the peoples named number seven (Meshech, Tubal, Persia, Cush, Put, Gomer and Beth-togarmah), from north, east, south and west. The Tyndale notes point out that seven and four both suggest completeness: this is the whole world gathered against God's people. None of these peoples were Israel's immediate neighbors. They are the far horizon.
- **"Be ready and keep ready" (v7).** There is irony here. God tells Gog to prepare thoroughly. It will make no difference.
- **"After many days… In the latter years" (v8).** The phrase *be'acharit hashanim*, "at the end of the years," appears only here. With "in the latter days" in v16 (*be'acharit hayamim*), it sets the invasion in the distant future, after Israel's restoration. Elsewhere "the latter days" can mean a future time within history (Deut 4:30) or the end of the age (Dan 10:14). Here it is clearly after the regathering of chapters 34–37: the attack falls on **"the land that is restored from war… whose people were gathered from many peoples… and now dwell securely, all of them."**
- **"Coming on like a storm… like a cloud covering the land" (v9).** The images are those of the day of the LORD (Joel 2:2; Jer 4:13). Earlier the storm of judgment fell on Israel. Now it falls on Israel's enemy.
- **"Thoughts will come into your mind, and you will devise an evil scheme" (v10).** God brings Gog (v4), and Gog comes because of his own greed (v10). Both are true, as in the story of Joseph: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Gen 50:20). God does not put evil into Gog's heart. He uses the evil already there.
- **"The land of unwalled villages… the quiet people who dwell securely… having no bars or gates" (v11).** In the ancient world, a town without walls was either very poor or very safe. Israel is safe because God dwells among them (37:26–28), so they need no walls. Zechariah pictures Jerusalem the same way: "inhabited as villages without walls… I will be to her a wall of fire all around" (Zech 2:4–5). To Gog it looks like easy prey.
- **"To seize spoil and carry off plunder" (v12).** Gog's goal is not ideology. It is loot. Israel has "acquired livestock and goods."
- **"Who dwell at the center of the earth" (v12).** The word is *tabbur*. The lexicon gives "highest part" or "center." The Greek and Latin translations render it "navel," and the word is used that way in later Hebrew. The only other use in the Bible is Judges 9:37, of the high ground of Shechem. Ezekiel 5:5 has already said, "I have set her in the center of the nations." The point is that Israel's land is central to God's purposes for the world, not a claim about geography.
- **"Sheba and Dedan and the merchants of Tarshish… will say to you, 'Have you come to seize spoil?'" (v13).** Sheba and Dedan were Arabian trading peoples (27:20, 22). Tarshish was a distant port in the far west, probably in Spain (27:12; Jonah 1:3). The world's traders watch from the sidelines. Their question can be read as protest or as a merchant's eager interest in buying the plunder. Either way, they do nothing to stop it.
- **"On that day when my people Israel are dwelling securely, will you not know it?" (v14).** God's people are called "my people" (v16) and the land "my land" (v16). Gog is attacking God's own property.
- **"That the nations may know me, when through you, O Gog, I vindicate my holiness before their eyes" (v16).** This is the purpose of the whole scene. In 36:23 God said he would show his holiness "through you," meaning Israel. Now he will show it "through you, O Gog." Even his enemy becomes a means of making him known.
- **"Are you he of whom I spoke in former days by my servants the prophets of Israel?" (v17).** No earlier prophet names Gog. The question may mean that Gog is the full form of the "enemy from the north" earlier prophets spoke of (Jer 1:14–15; 4:6; 6:22), or of the nations' final assault on Zion (Isa 29:5–8; Joel 3:9–16). The Tyndale notes take the question as implying no: those prophecies were fulfilled by Babylon, and Gog is something new. The Hebrew can be read either way.
- **"My wrath will be roused in my anger… in my jealousy and in my blazing wrath" (vv18–19).** The words that once described God's anger against Jerusalem (5:13; 36:5–6) are now turned on Israel's attacker.
- **"There shall be a great earthquake in the land of Israel" (v19).** The shaking reaches every part of creation: **fish, birds, beasts, creeping things, and all the people** (v20), the same categories as the creation account (Gen 1:26). Mountains fall, cliffs collapse, walls tumble. Earthquakes accompany God's coming in judgment throughout Scripture (Exod 19:18; Hag 2:6–7; Zech 14:4–5; Rev 16:18).
- **"Every man's sword will be against his brother" (v21).** The army destroys itself in panic. God did this for Gideon (Judg 7:22), for Jonathan (1 Sam 14:20) and for Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 20:22–23). Israel does not fight at all.
- **"Pestilence and bloodshed… torrential rains and hailstones, fire and sulfur" (v22).** Every weapon of God's past judgments returns: the flood (Gen 7), the fire and sulfur on Sodom (Gen 19:24), the hail on Egypt (Exod 9:23–24) and on the Amorites (Josh 10:11). "Enter into judgment" is a law-court phrase: God brings a case against Gog and carries out the sentence himself.
- **"So I will show my greatness and my holiness and make myself known in the eyes of many nations" (v23).** The chapter ends on the purpose, not the slaughter. Ezekiel's great refrain, **"Then they will know that I am the LORD,"** now reaches "many nations."
- **How should Gog be understood?** Faithful readers have taken three broad approaches. *Historical:* Gog stands for a past enemy. JFB and Henry both thought of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, the Greek king who desecrated the temple in 167 BC, as at least a first fulfilment, and some modern scholars link Gog to Gyges or to the Scythians. *Futurist:* Gog is a real coalition that will invade a restored Israel at the end of the age. Within this view people disagree about when: before or during a final period of tribulation, at Christ's return (linking it with Rev 19:17–21, where birds are summoned to a feast as in 39:17–20), or after the millennium (Rev 20:7–9, which names Gog and Magog). *Symbolic:* Gog is a picture of every final rebellion of evil against God's people, drawn in larger-than-life colors to say that it will never succeed. Revelation itself seems to use the names this way, making Gog and Magog "the nations at the four corners of the earth" rather than one ruler and his land. What all agree on is the message: when God dwells among his people, no assault, however vast, can overthrow them.
- **A word of caution.** Every generation since the Middle Ages has had confident identifications of Gog: the Goths, the Huns, the Mongols, the Turks, the Soviet Union, and more recently various Middle Eastern alliances. Each one failed to fit. The Bible itself does not name a modern nation here, and readers do well not to make it do so.
- **Commentators:** Henry admits the critical expositors "have enough to do" identifying Gog, and he will not settle their disputes. He takes it that "Gog seems to be the king and Magog the kingdom," notes that "Antiochus had an army made up of all the nations here named," and also sees a later fulfilment in the enemies of the church in Revelation 20:8. He observes that vv14–23 retell vv1–13 with God's response added. JFB thinks "Gog" was probably a royal title like "Pharaoh," prefers the reading "prince of Rosh," and identifies Meshech and Tubal with "the Moschi and Tibareni, near the Caucasus." JFB sees Antiochus as "an earnest of the final desolations" under Antichrist, and calls "enter into judgment" a "forensic term." He reads the "navel" of v12 as moral, not physical, centrality. Guzik notes that the only support for Rosh as Russia is the similar sound, and that the Moscow and Tobolsk links are weak, though, with Wiersbe, he allows that a northern power like Russia might be involved. He sets out four possible timings and concludes that each has difficulties and the fulfilment may only be clear when it comes.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel's Gog is the last great enemy of a people God has already redeemed, and he is destroyed by God alone. Jesus told his disciples the same pattern: "In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world" (John 16:33). Revelation shows Gog and Magog gathered "for battle… as many as the sand of the sea," and then simply: "fire came down from heaven and consumed them" (Rev 20:8–9). There is no battle to describe, because the victory was won at the cross, where Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame" (Col 2:15). The people of God may live in unwalled villages because the LORD is their wall.
