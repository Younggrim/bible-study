*Setting:* The chapter opens with another step back in time: "the first month of the second year" (v1), a month before the census of chapter 1 (1:1) and about two weeks after the tabernacle was set up (Exod 40:17). One year has passed since the first Passover night in Egypt. Before Israel leaves Sinai, it keeps the feast that marks its birth as a free people. A practical problem arises with some men who are ritually unclean, and God answers with a new provision. The chapter then describes the cloud over the tabernacle that would lead Israel for the next forty years. Both halves look back to the exodus and forward to the march, and both are about a people living by God's word.

#### The Passover Celebrated (vv1–14)
- **"Let the people of Israel keep the Passover at its appointed time" (v2).** The original command (Exod 12:1–28) was given in Egypt and said to be kept "as a statute forever." Exodus 12:25 had linked it to the land: "when you come to the land." God now commands it in the wilderness as well. This is the only Passover recorded between Egypt and Canaan. The next one mentioned is at Gilgal, forty years later, after the new generation had been circumcised (Josh 5:5–10).
- **"On the fourteenth day of this month, at twilight" (v3).** The Hebrew is literally "between the two evenings," generally understood as between sunset and full dark, or, in later practice, the late afternoon. A Hebrew day began at sundown, so the meal fell at the start of the fifteenth.
- **"According to all its statutes and all its rules" (v3).** The basic rites stayed: the lamb, unleavened bread and bitter herbs (v11). JFB observes that the details that belonged only to the hurried first night, such as eating with sandals on and staff in hand ready to leave (Exod 12:11), were not part of the yearly feast.
- **"So the people of Israel did" (v5).** Full obedience, again. Guzik notes how this makes the complaints that begin in chapter 11 look all the worse.
- **"Certain men who were unclean through touching a dead body" (v6).** Contact with a corpse made a person unclean for seven days (19:11), and the unclean could not eat holy food (Lev 7:20–21). These men had probably buried a relative, a good and necessary act. Who they were is not said. Jewish tradition guessed they were the cousins who carried out Nadab and Abihu (Lev 10:4–5), or the men carrying Joseph's coffin (Exod 13:19). Both are only guesses.
- **"Why are we kept from bringing the LORD's offering… among the people of Israel?" (v7).** Their complaint is a good one. They are not trying to get out of a duty but to be included. They do not want to be left out of the meal that made Israel Israel. The verb means to be held back or made less, as if they were being subtracted from God's people.
- **"Wait, that I may hear what the LORD will command" (v8).** Moses does not invent an answer. He goes into the tent and asks (7:89). It is the first of several cases in Numbers where a real situation leads to new law: the blasphemer (Lev 24:12), the Sabbath breaker (15:34), and the daughters of Zelophehad (27:5). The Tyndale notes observe that the men wanted to keep the law in a case where the law seemed to give them no way to.
- **"If any one of you… is unclean… or is on a long journey" (v10).** God's answer goes further than the question. It covers not only these men but all their descendants, and adds a second case: someone too far away to come. Israel is still in the camp, but God is already thinking of a scattered people living in the land.
- **"In the second month on the fourteenth day" (v11).** A second Passover, one month later, kept in full: unleavened bread, bitter herbs, nothing left till morning. Jewish tradition calls it *Pesach Sheni*, "the second Passover." Centuries later, King Hezekiah used this very provision to hold a national Passover in the second month, because the priests had not consecrated themselves in time (2 Chr 30:2–3). Many came unclean, and Hezekiah prayed, "May the good LORD pardon everyone who sets his heart to seek God… even though not according to the sanctuary's rules of cleanness," and the LORD "heard Hezekiah and healed the people" (2 Chr 30:18–20).
- **"Nor break any of its bones" (v12).** This repeats Exodus 12:46. John saw it fulfilled at the cross when the soldiers did not break Jesus' legs: "Not one of his bones will be broken" (John 19:33–36). The rule also stands behind Psalm 34:20.
- **"But if anyone who is clean… fails to keep the Passover, that person shall be cut off" (v13).** The provision was for those who *could not*, not for those who *would not*. Being "cut off" means removal from the covenant people, whether by death at God's hand or by exclusion. The Tyndale notes say it is not clear which. Skipping the Passover meant refusing the very event that made a person part of Israel. **"That man shall bear his sin"**: no lamb would cover him.
- **"If a stranger sojourns among you and would keep the Passover" (v14).** A "sojourner" (*ger*) was a foreigner living among Israel. He could share the Passover, but by the same rules. Exodus 12:48 makes the key rule explicit: males had to be circumcised. **"You shall have one statute, both for the sojourner and for the native."** The door was open to outsiders, as it had been from the start, when "a mixed multitude" left Egypt with Israel (Exod 12:38).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the unclean men were "painfully perplexed about the course of duty," and that Gentile converts could keep the feast with full privileges, but "circumcision was an indispensable condition." Guzik sees two good principles in tension, that everyone should share the Passover and that the unclean must not profane it, and God's answer honoring both. He applies v13 to the need to truly receive Christ, the Passover lamb, and sees in v14 God's desire to reach all peoples. Henry's outline divides this section into the yearly observance and a provision for those "ceremonially unclean" or unable to keep it at the set time.

#### The Cloud Covering the Tabernacle (vv15–23)
- **"On the day that the tabernacle was set up, the cloud covered the tabernacle" (v15).** This takes us back to Exodus 40:34–38, when the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle so that even Moses could not enter. The pillar of cloud and fire had led Israel since they left Egypt (Exod 13:21–22) and stood between them and Pharaoh's army at the sea (Exod 14:19–20). Now it rests on God's tent, right in the middle of the camp.
- **"Like the appearance of fire until morning… so it was always" (vv15–16).** By day a cloud, by night a glow like fire. It never left. Psalm 105:39 remembers that God "spread a cloud for a covering, and fire to give light by night." In the desert the cloud would have been shade by day and the fire a light and comfort by night (Isa 4:5–6). The Tyndale notes add that even Israel's enemies knew about it (14:14).
- **"Whenever the cloud lifted… the people of Israel set out" (v17).** There was no fixed schedule. When the cloud rose from the tabernacle, the camp packed up; where it settled, they pitched their tents. Chapter 10 adds that the silver trumpets announced each move (10:2), and that Moses prayed at each start and stop (10:35–36).
- **"At the command of the LORD" (vv18–23).** The phrase, literally "at the mouth of the LORD," appears seven times in these six verses. The passage is deliberately repetitive. Its rhythm is the point: they set out when God said, and they stopped when God said, every time.
- **"Many days… a few days… from evening until morning… two days, or a month, or a longer time" (vv19–22).** The stays varied without warning. "A longer time" is literally "days," which in Hebrew can mean a full year, as the KJV renders it. Israel could be told to move after one night or to stay put for a year. Either way, the hardest part may have been the waiting, especially for a people eager to reach their land.
- **"They kept the charge of the LORD" (vv19, 23).** "Charge" (*mishmeret*) is the same word used for the Levites' guard duty (3:7–8). Waiting on God's timing was itself a duty, just as much as carrying the tabernacle.
- **Looking ahead.** This picture of perfect obedience comes just before the march begins, and it describes the whole forty years. But the following chapters show that the people's hearts did not always follow where their feet went. Paul later writes that "our fathers were all under the cloud… and all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea," and yet "with most of them God was not pleased" (1 Cor 10:1–5). Being led by the cloud did not guarantee faith.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the cloud "the visible sign and symbol of God's presence with Israel," says God "kept them night and day," and notes that it kept them ready to "remove at the commandment of the Lord." He warns that "it is uncomfortable staying when God has departed," and treats patient waiting as obedience too, quoting Isaiah: "He that believeth doth not make haste" (Isa 28:16). JFB notes Israel's natural restlessness to reach Canaan and holds up their patience in waiting as an example. Guzik also stresses the cloud's practical mercy, shade by day and warmth by night. He applies the whole passage to being led by God moment by moment, holding plans and possessions loosely, and letting the peace of Christ "rule" in the heart (Col 3:15), a word that means to act as umpire.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter holds two pictures of God's grace in the wilderness. The first is a Passover lamb whose bones were not broken and a God who made a way for the unclean and the far-off to share it. That is what Christ, "our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7), has done: "you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Eph 2:13), and the same table is open to the sojourner and the native alike. The second is a cloud that never left and that set the pace of every step. Jesus promises his people the same: "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20), and he leads them as a shepherd who "goes before them" (John 10:4) by the Spirit he sends (Rom 8:14).
