*Setting:* Chapter 40 comforted the exiles in Babylon with the greatness of God: the Creator who calls every star by name has not forgotten Israel. Chapter 41 turns that truth into a courtroom drama. God summons the nations and their gods to trial, and the question before the court is who really controls history. The evidence is a conqueror from the east, whom chapters 44–45 name as Cyrus of Persia. Cyrus rose to power in the 550s BC and took Babylon in 539 without a battle. A year later he allowed the Jews to go home and rebuild the temple (Ezra 1:1–4). In between the two trial scenes stand some of the most tender promises in the Bible, spoken to Israel as God's servant: "Fear not, for I am with you." This is also where the "servant" theme of Isaiah 40–55 begins.

#### Fear Not, for I Am with You (vv1–20)
- **"Listen to me in silence, O coastlands; let the peoples renew their strength" (v1).** "Coastlands" (*'iyyim*) means the islands and distant shores of the Mediterranean, the ends of the known world. The court is called to order. "Renew their strength" is the very phrase of 40:31. The Tyndale notes see a play on words: those who wait for the LORD renew their strength, so let the nations find new strength, if they can, to argue their case.
- **"Who stirred up one from the east whom victory meets at every step?" (v2).** "Victory" is *tsedeq*, normally "righteousness." The phrase can mean "whom righteousness calls to its service" or "whom victory meets at every step." In Isaiah 40–55, *tsedeq* often means God's righteousness in action, putting things right and so bringing victory.
- **Who is the one from the east?** Most commentators, including JFB and the Tyndale notes, say Cyrus, since v25 speaks of one "from the north… from the rising of the sun," and 44:28–45:1 names him. Persia lay east of Babylon, and Cyrus's armies swept through kingdom after kingdom, Media, Lydia and finally Babylon. Some older Jewish and Christian readers, and Henry in part, took v2 as Abraham, who came from Ur in the east and defeated kings in Genesis 14. Guzik favors Abraham for v2 and Cyrus for v25, so that God shows his control of both past and future. The description of conquering nations "like dust with his sword" fits Cyrus far better.
- **"By paths his feet have not trod" (v3).** He moves so fast he hardly seems to touch the ground, or he marches into lands no army of his people had entered before. Cyrus's campaigns were famous for speed.
- **"Who has performed and done this, calling the generations from the beginning? I, the LORD, the first, and with the last; I am he" (v4).** This is the answer to every question in the chapter. God is not only the cause of Cyrus. He has been calling every generation since the beginning. "I am he" (*'ani hu'*) is a phrase that echoes through Isaiah 40–55 (43:10, 13; 46:4; 48:12) as a near-name of God. Jesus takes up this language: "unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins" (John 8:24), and in Revelation he calls himself "the first and the last" (Rev 1:17; 22:13).
- **"The coastlands have seen and are afraid… Everyone helps his neighbor and says to his brother, 'Be strong!'" (vv5–6).** The nations see the conqueror coming and panic. Their response is not to turn to the living God but to make more idols. It is a sad, almost comic scene of pep talks between workmen.
- **"The craftsman strengthens the goldsmith… saying of the soldering, 'It is good'; and they strengthen it with nails so that it cannot be moved" (v7).** The word "strengthen" (*chazaq*) keeps repeating: they strengthen each other, and they strengthen the idol with nails so it won't fall over. "It is good" is the judgment God spoke over his creation in Genesis 1, here spoken by a metalworker over his solder. The gods who are supposed to protect the nations have to be nailed down to stay upright (40:20).
- **"But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen, the offspring of Abraham, my friend" (v8).** "But you" is the turn. While the nations scramble for gods they make, God speaks to the people he made. This is the first time in Isaiah that Israel is called God's "servant" (*'eved*), a title of honor for those who serve a king, used of Moses and David. In chapters 42–53 the servant theme narrows from the nation to one individual who will do what Israel failed to do.
- **"Abraham, my friend" (v8).** The Hebrew is *'ohavi*, literally "the one who loves me," or "my loved one." Only Abraham is called God's friend in the Old Testament (also 2 Chr 20:7), and James repeats it: "he was called a friend of God" (Jas 2:23). Jesus says to his disciples, "I have called you friends" (John 15:15).
- **"You whom I took from the ends of the earth… I have chosen you and not cast you off" (v9).** God called Abraham from distant Ur. The exiles may have felt that God had rejected them, but the promise is plain. Paul asks the same question about Israel in Romans 11:1 and gives the same answer: "Has God rejected his people? By no means!"
- **"Fear not, for I am with you; be not dismayed, for I am your God" (v10).** One of the most loved verses in Scripture. "Dismayed" (*sha'ah*) means to look around anxiously, the darting glance of someone who is afraid. JFB gives this sense. The reason not to fear is not that the danger is small but that God is present. "I am with you" is the promise given to Isaac (Gen 26:24), Jacob (Gen 28:15), Moses (Exod 3:12), Joshua (Josh 1:5), and in Isaiah's name for the Messiah, Immanuel, "God with us" (7:14).
- **"I will strengthen you, I will help you, I will uphold you with my righteous right hand" (v10).** Three "I will" promises. The craftsmen in v7 strengthened their idols, and here God strengthens his people. Idols must be held up, but God holds his people up. Guzik makes this contrast. The "right hand" is the hand of power (Exod 15:6), and it is "righteous" because it acts faithfully to his promises.
- **"All who are incensed against you shall be put to shame… you shall seek those who contend with you, but you shall not find them" (vv11–12).** Babylon seemed permanent. In a generation it would be so gone that the exiles would look for their oppressors and find no one.
- **"For I, the LORD your God, hold your right hand" (v13).** Picture a parent taking a child's hand to cross a street. God's right hand (v10) takes hold of Israel's right hand. The same words are used of Cyrus in 45:1, "whose right hand I have grasped." Psalm 73:23 says it too: "you hold my right hand."
- **"Fear not, you worm Jacob, you men of Israel!" (v14).** "Worm" (*tola'at*) is an image of weakness and contempt. It is the word in Psalm 22:6, "I am a worm and not a man, scorned by mankind," the psalm Jesus prayed on the cross. JFB makes that link. "Men of Israel" may be "few men of Israel," since the related word means "few." God does not flatter his people. He tells them they are small and weak, and then says "fear not."
- **"Your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel" (v14).** This is the first of thirteen times Isaiah calls God Israel's "Redeemer" (*go'el*). The *go'el* was the nearest relative, who had the right and duty to buy back a family member sold into slavery, recover lost land, or avenge a murder (Lev 25:25, 47–49; Ruth 4:1–10). God takes on himself the duty of the next of kin. Guzik, citing Motyer, makes this point.
- **"Behold, I make of you a threshing sledge, new, sharp, and having teeth" (v15).** A threshing sledge was a heavy wooden board with sharp stones or iron teeth fixed to its underside. Oxen dragged it over harvested grain to break the stalks and free the kernels. The worm becomes a machine. **"You shall thresh the mountains and crush them."** The mountains are the obstacles and powers that seem immovable. Jesus spoke of faith that moves mountains (Matt 17:20), which Guzik connects here.
- **"You shall winnow them, and the wind shall carry them away" (v16).** After threshing, the farmer tossed the grain into the wind so the chaff blew away and the heavy kernels fell. Israel's enemies will be chaff (Ps 1:4). **"And you shall rejoice in the LORD."** The victory is God's, and so is the glory.
- **"When the poor and needy seek water, and there is none… I the LORD will answer them" (v17).** The picture changes to the long journey home through the desert. The exiles are "poor and needy," with tongues dried out from thirst. God hears.
- **"I will open rivers on the bare heights, and fountains in the midst of the valleys" (v18).** Water in impossible places, a new Exodus with even more than the water from the rock (Exod 17:6). The Tyndale notes make this connection.
- **"I will put in the wilderness the cedar, the acacia, the myrtle, and the olive… the cypress, the plane and the pine together" (v19).** Seven kinds of trees, some of which never grow in a desert. Seven is the number of completeness. Trees mean shade for travelers and lasting life, a desert turned into a forest. Several of the tree names are uncertain, and translations vary.
- **"That they may see and know… that the hand of the LORD has done this, the Holy One of Israel has created it" (v20).** Four verbs for understanding (see, know, consider, understand) and two for God's work (done, created). The point of the miracle is that people will recognize God. "Created" is *bara'*, the verb of Genesis 1:1, used in the Bible only with God as subject. The return home will be a new act of creation.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the one from the east in v2 as both Abraham, "the righteous man whom God raised up from the east," and Cyrus. He calls Israel in v14 "the worm Jacob, so little, so weak, and so defenceless," and yet God's chosen threshing instrument. JFB is clear that v2 is Cyrus, and takes "friend" in v8 literally as "loving me," which the Hebrew supports. Guzik stresses that "fear not" is both command and promise, and contrasts idols that must be held up with the God who holds his people up. He also explains "Israel" as "governed by God," but Genesis 32:28 ties the name to Jacob striving with God, and most lexicons explain it as "God strives" or "he strives with God."

#### The Futility of Idols (vv21–29)
- **"Set forth your case, says the LORD; bring your proofs, says the King of Jacob" (v21).** The trial resumes, and now the gods themselves are in the dock. "King of Jacob" reminds the exiles that their king had not lost his throne when Jerusalem's king lost his.
- **"Tell us the former things… or declare to us the things to come" (v22).** The test is simple: can the gods explain the meaning of the past or predict the future? A real god would know what he is doing in history. Babylon was the home of the most elaborate fortune-telling system in the ancient world, from reading the livers of sacrificed sheep to charting the stars. Isaiah mocks it in 47:12–13.
- **"Do good, or do harm, that we may be dismayed and terrified" (v23).** God goes further: do anything at all, good or bad, just to prove you exist. It is the same mocking tone Elijah used on Carmel: "Cry aloud, for he is a god" (1 Kings 18:27).
- **"Behold, you are nothing, and your work is less than nothing; an abomination is he who chooses you" (v24).** The verdict. Idols are nothing, and the person who chooses one becomes something detestable. Psalm 115:8 says those who make idols "become like them."
- **"I stirred up one from the north, and he has come, from the rising of the sun" (v25).** The conqueror again. He comes from the east, where Persia lay, but armies reached Babylon and Israel from the north, along the river routes of the Fertile Crescent. Cyrus also conquered Media and the lands to the north before turning on Babylon. **"He shall call upon my name."** Cyrus was not a worshiper of Israel's God, but his decree did speak of "the LORD, the God of heaven" (Ezra 1:2), and 45:4 says, "I call you by your name… though you do not know me." **"He shall trample on rulers as on mortar, as the potter treads clay."** Workers made mortar and potters softened clay by stamping it barefoot.
- **"Who declared it from the beginning, that we might know?… There was none who declared it" (v26).** No god predicted Cyrus. Only the LORD did, through his prophet. Fulfilled prophecy is God's evidence in this trial, a theme Isaiah returns to repeatedly (42:9; 44:7–8; 46:9–10; 48:3–5).
- **"I was the first to say to Zion, 'Behold, here they are!' and I give to Jerusalem a herald of good news" (v27).** The Hebrew is very compressed, but the sense is that God alone announced the good news of return to Zion beforehand. The "herald of good news" is the *mebasser* of 40:9 and 52:7, the gospel messenger.
- **"But when I look, there is no one; among these there is no counselor" (v28).** God looks around the courtroom for a god who can answer, and there is silence. The same "I looked, but there was no one" returns in 59:16 and 63:5, where God himself acts to save because no one else can.
- **"Behold, they are all a delusion; their works are nothing; their metal images are empty wind" (v29).** "Delusion" is *'awen*, emptiness or wickedness. "Empty wind" is *ruach wa-tohu*, wind and formlessness, the *tohu* of Genesis 1:2. The gods return to the chaos before creation. The verdict leads straight into the next verse: "Behold my servant" (42:1). The idols are empty, and God presents his servant.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the gods to "Produce your cause," and sees that they can show neither knowledge nor power, so their worshipers will be disappointed. JFB says the idols are called an "abomination" in the sense of being the essence of what is detestable, and he notes that the prophecy of Cyrus came about 150 years before he appeared. Guzik contrasts God's many "I will" statements in this chapter, all of them blessing his people, with the proud "I will" boasts of the king of Babylon in 14:13–14, which came to nothing.

#### Pointing to Christ
God tells his weak, worm-like people "fear not, for I am with you," and in Jesus that promise became flesh: Immanuel, God with us. The servant Israel failed to be, Jesus was, and in Psalm 22 he took the very words "I am a worm and not a man" onto his own lips on the cross. He is the Redeemer, the next of kin who paid to buy his people back, and "the first and the last" who holds history in his hands (Rev 1:17–18). The idols are wind and chaos. The one who speaks in this courtroom says, "I am he," and he still holds his people's right hand.
