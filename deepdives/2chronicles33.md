*Setting:* Judah, about 697–640 BC. Hezekiah, one of Judah's best kings, has died and been buried with honor (32:33). His son Manasseh was born in the fifteen extra years God gave Hezekiah after his illness (2 Kings 20:6; 21:1), and he became the worst king in Judah's history. Assyria was at the height of its power under Esarhaddon (681–669 BC) and Ashurbanipal (669–about 627 BC), and Judah was its vassal. Assyrian records list "Manasseh king of Judah" among the western kings who sent tribute and building materials. 2 Kings 21 tells only Manasseh's sins and the judgment they brought. Chronicles tells something Kings leaves out: the worst king of all was humbled, prayed, and was forgiven.

#### Manasseh Reigns in Judah (vv1–9)
- **"Twelve years old… fifty-five years" (v1).** The longest reign of any king of Israel or Judah. The Tyndale notes suggest the first ten years or so were a co-regency with Hezekiah, which would mean he was put on the throne beside his father as a boy. His name means "making to forget," the name Joseph gave his firstborn because God made him forget his hardship (Gen 41:51). This Manasseh made Judah forget the LORD.
- **"According to the abominations of the nations whom the LORD drove out" (v2).** That is the worst possible verdict. The land had been given to Israel because the Canaanites were driven out for these very practices (Deut 18:9–12). Manasseh brought them all back.
- **"He rebuilt the high places that his father Hezekiah had broken down" (v3).** He deliberately undid his father's reform, piece by piece. "Baals" and "Asheroth" are plurals: many local forms of the Canaanite storm god and his female partner.
- **"Worshiped all the host of heaven" (v3).** Star worship was central to Assyrian and Babylonian religion. Adopting it may have been partly political, a way of showing loyalty to Assyria. Guzik also links it to imported Mesopotamian sky worship.
- **"Altars in the house of the LORD… in the two courts" (vv4–5).** Pagan altars inside the LORD's own temple. The Chronicler quotes God's promise right beside it: "In Jerusalem shall my name be forever." The place for God's name is filled with other gods.
- **"He burned his sons as an offering in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom" (v6).** Kings says "son" (2 Kings 21:6). Chronicles says "sons." His grandfather Ahaz did the same (28:3). This valley below Jerusalem later gave its name to *Gehenna*, the word Jesus used for hell.
- **"Fortune-telling and omens and sorcery… mediums and… necromancers" (v6).** Every occult practice Deuteronomy 18:10–11 forbids appears here. A "necromancer" (*yiddeʿoni*) is literally "a knowing one," someone who claimed secret knowledge from the spirits of the dead, as Guzik notes from Payne. Saul's visit to the medium at Endor is the classic case (1 Sam 28).
- **"The carved image of the idol that he had made he set in the house of God" (v7).** 2 Kings 21:7 calls it "the carved image of Asherah," as the Tyndale notes say. The Chronicler again quotes God's promise to David and Solomon alongside it (6:6; 7:16), with its condition: "if only they will be careful to do all that I have commanded" (v8). The promise of the land was never unconditional for any one generation.
- **"To do more evil than the nations whom the LORD destroyed" (v9).** Manasseh did not just sin. He "led Judah… astray." 2 Kings 21:16 adds that he "shed very much innocent blood, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another." Jewish tradition says the prophet Isaiah was among his victims, sawn in two, and many see a reference in Hebrews 11:37. That is tradition, not something the Old Testament records.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes this as Manasseh's "wretched apostasy from God, and revolt to idolatry and all wickedness." Guzik notes that Manasseh was born in Hezekiah's added years, and traces each of his sins to a command in the law. JFB simply refers the reader to its notes on 2 Kings 21.

#### Manasseh's Repentance (vv10–20)
- **"The LORD spoke to Manasseh and to his people, but they paid no attention" (v10).** God sent prophets. 2 Kings 21:10–15 gives their message: God would bring such disaster on Jerusalem that "the ears of everyone who hears of it will tingle," and would "wipe Jerusalem as one wipes a dish." Guzik notes that God owed them no warning at all, and gave one anyway.
- **"Captured Manasseh with hooks and bound him with chains of bronze" (v11).** The word for "hooks" can also mean "thorns," which is why the KJV and JFB read "among the thorns," as if he hid in a thicket. Most modern versions take it as hooks. The Tyndale notes say Assyrian records and carvings show prisoners led by a ring or hook through the nose or lip, which was painful and utterly humiliating. Isaiah used the same picture of Assyria itself: "I will put my hook in your nose" (Isa 37:29; 2 Kings 19:28).
- **"And brought him to Babylon" (v11).** Why Babylon and not Nineveh, Assyria's capital? Babylon was under Assyrian rule, and Esarhaddon rebuilt it and lived there at times, as JFB notes. Between 652 and 648 BC Babylon rebelled against Ashurbanipal under his own brother, and many scholars think Manasseh was suspected of joining that revolt and was brought to Ashurbanipal at Babylon afterward. That is a reconstruction. The text gives no reason. Some doubt the event happened because Kings does not mention it, but Assyrian records do show kings being taken, humbled and sent back as vassals, as Egypt's Necho I was.
- **"When he was in distress, he entreated the favor of the LORD his God and humbled himself greatly" (v12).** "Entreated the favor" is literally "softened the face," a picture of seeking someone's kindness. "Humbled himself" is again the key word of 7:14. The Tyndale notes observe that Manasseh does exactly what Solomon's prayer asked. Note "the LORD *his* God." Even after all he did, God is still called his God.
- **"God was moved by his entreaty… and brought him again to Jerusalem into his kingdom" (v13).** God did not only forgive. He restored. The Tyndale notes point out that Manasseh's story is the nation's story in small: sin, exile to Babylon, repentance, return. The Chronicler's first readers, back from Babylon themselves, would have seen it at once.
- **"Then Manasseh knew that the LORD was God" (v13).** That is the goal of the whole episode. Pharaoh was told the plagues would teach him "that I am the LORD" (Exod 7:5). Manasseh learned it in chains.
- **"He built an outer wall for the city of David west of Gihon… and carried it around Ophel" (v14).** Ophel was the hill between the old city of David and the temple. This was probably more than defense. It shows a restored king working to protect the city he had nearly ruined. The Tyndale notes suggest he may also have been building walls when he was captured. JFB traces the wall's route from the valley to the Fish Gate on the north.
- **"He took away the foreign gods and the idol from the house of the LORD" (v15).** The very idol named in v7 is removed. He threw the altars "outside of the city." The Tyndale notes add that Josiah later had to remove "the altars that Manasseh had made in the two courts" (2 Kings 23:12), so either some were put back under Amon or the clean-up was not complete.
- **"Restored the altar of the LORD… peace offerings and of thanksgiving" (v16).** Thank offerings fit a man who had been brought back from a Babylonian prison.
- **"Nevertheless, the people still sacrificed at the high places, but only to the LORD their God" (v17).** The reform had a limit. The people stopped worshiping other gods but kept worshiping the LORD in places he had not chosen. JFB says this was not idolatry, since it was "unto the Lord their God only." It was still disobedience (Deut 12:13–14), and it left the door open. Guzik quotes Payne: fifty years of paganism could not be undone by a few years of reform.
- **"His prayer to his God" (v18).** Kings does not record it, and neither does Chronicles. A short poem called the *Prayer of Manasseh* was written later, probably in the last centuries BC, to fill that gap. It is included in the Apocrypha, is used in Orthodox and some Anglican worship, and was printed as an appendix in the Latin Vulgate. Protestants do not count it as Scripture, and it is not the prayer the Chronicler read. It is still a moving prayer of repentance, including a line asking God to forgive "for I have sinned more than the number of the sands of the sea."
- **"The Chronicles of the Seers" (v19).** The ESV footnote says most Hebrew manuscripts read "of Hozai," which may be a prophet's name. One Hebrew manuscript and the Greek read "the seers." Either way, it was a prophetic record that no longer survives, as the Tyndale notes say.
- **"They buried him in his house" (v20).** 2 Kings 21:18 says "in the garden of his house, in the garden of Uzza," not in the royal tombs. Henry: "Penitents may recover their comfort sooner than their credit." He was forgiven, but the honor his father received was not given to him.
- **Kings and Chronicles together.** Kings tells Manasseh's sin and its consequences: even after Josiah's reform, judgment came "because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him" (2 Kings 23:26; 24:3). Chronicles tells his personal repentance. Both are true. A man can be fully forgiven by God while the damage he did to others keeps spreading after him. Guzik quotes Morgan to the same effect: forgiveness does not erase a sin's lasting influence.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Sanctified afflictions often prove happy means of conversion," and "God was entreated of him, and heard his supplication." On the limited reform he writes, "It is an easy thing to debauch men's manners, but not so easy to reform them again." Guzik treats the repentance as genuine and quotes Spurgeon, who said he was more amazed by God's mercy than by Manasseh's sin. JFB names Esarhaddon as the Assyrian king and suggests Assyria let Manasseh go home for its own reasons, to keep Judah as a buffer against Egypt, while God was working through it.

#### Amon's Reign and Death (vv21–25)
- **"Amon was twenty-two years old… two years" (v21).** About 642–640 BC. His name may echo Amun, the chief god of Egypt, though it can also be a Hebrew name meaning "faithful." If his father named him after an Egyptian god, it fits the early Manasseh.
- **"Amon sacrificed to all the images that Manasseh his father had made" (v22).** This suggests the images were set aside rather than destroyed, or that Amon remade them. He took up his father's early life, not his later one.
- **"He did not humble himself… as Manasseh his father had humbled himself, but… incurred guilt more and more" (v23).** The contrast is the point of his story. Manasseh is the proof that the worst sinner can repent. Amon is the warning that a man can see repentance in his own father and refuse it. Guzik passes on Trapp: Amon imitated his father's sin without his repentance.
- **"His servants conspired against him and put him to death in his house" (v24).** A palace coup. Scripture gives no motive. The Tyndale notes suggest Egypt may have stirred it up as Assyria weakened, and others think an anti-Assyrian faction was behind it. Both are guesses.
- **"The people of the land struck down all those who had conspired" (v25).** "The people of the land" were the landowners and leading men of Judah, a group that steps in at key moments (23:20–21; 26:1; 36:1). They will not let David's line be cut off. They put Amon's eight-year-old son Josiah on the throne, and he becomes the most faithful king since David (34:1–2).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Amon "reigned very wickedly" and "soon ended his days unhappily." Guzik reads the people's action as a hopeful sign after decades of wicked rule, and passes on Dilday's dry remark that Amon's one good contribution to Judah was his son Josiah. JFB has no notes on vv22–25.

#### Pointing to Christ
If Manasseh could be forgiven, anyone can. He burned his sons, filled the temple with idols and Jerusalem with blood, and when he humbled himself in chains, God heard him. The chapter does not make light of his sin; its effects outlived him and helped bring the exile. But it also shows the width of God's mercy. Paul said the same of himself: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost," and he received mercy so that Christ might display "his perfect patience as an example to those who were to believe" (1 Tim 1:15–16). The mercy Manasseh found rests on what Christ would do. He bore the guilt of sinners, Manasseh's included, and a prisoner's prayer is heard because of him. Amon stands beside his father as the warning: the same mercy can be seen and refused.
