*Setting:* Genesis ended with a coffin in Egypt: Joseph died at 110, telling his brothers that "God will surely visit you" and bring them up to the land he swore to Abraham (Gen 50:24–26). Exodus opens generations later, and the family of seventy has become a people. The book is traditionally credited to Moses, and Jesus speaks of it as "the book of Moses" (Mark 12:26). Its date is debated. Many conservative scholars place the exodus around 1446 BC, counting back 480 years from Solomon's temple (1 Kings 6:1). Many others favor about 1260 BC under Ramesses II, partly because of the city name "Raamses" in v11. Either way, God had told Abraham his offspring would be "sojourners in a land that is not theirs" and "afflicted for four hundred years," and that he would bring them out with great possessions (Gen 15:13–14). Exodus 1 shows the first half of that promise coming true and sets the stage for the second.

#### Israel Increases Greatly in Egypt (vv1–7)
- **"These are the names" (v1).** The Hebrew title of the book is *Shemot*, "Names," from its opening words. The first Hebrew word is actually "And," tying Exodus to Genesis as one continuing story. The list repeats Genesis 46:8, so the reader picks up exactly where the family arrived.
- **The order of the sons (vv2–4).** They are grouped by mother: Leah's six (Reuben to Zebulun), Rachel's Benjamin (Joseph is already in Egypt), then the sons of the two servant women, Bilhah (Dan, Naphtali) and Zilpah (Gad, Asher). These are the twelve tribes in seed form.
- **"Seventy persons" (v5).** The same count as Genesis 46:27. Seventy is also the number of the nations listed in Genesis 10, and some see a deliberate echo: a small family that would one day bless all the nations. The Greek translation (the Septuagint) and a Dead Sea Scroll read 75, likely counting extra grandsons of Joseph, and Stephen follows that figure in Acts 7:14. Both are counting the same family in slightly different ways.
- **"Then Joseph died, and all his brothers and all that generation" (v6).** The last people who remembered how Israel came to Egypt, and the honor Joseph had earned, are gone.
- **"Fruitful… increased greatly… multiplied… grew exceedingly strong… filled" (v7).** Five verbs pile up, and they are the language of creation. God told the first humans to "be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth" (Gen 1:28), and he repeated it to Noah (Gen 9:1, 7) and to Jacob (Gen 35:11). "Increased greatly" is *sharats*, "to swarm" or "teem," the word for fish filling the seas in Genesis 1:20–21. The blessing first given to humanity is now being worked out through Abraham's family, just as God promised (Gen 15:5; 22:17).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the opening "And" binds Exodus to Genesis, and that v7 reuses the vocabulary of Genesis 1, so Israel's growth is God's creation blessing at work. Henry says the census is given so "their increase in Egypt" might look all the more remarkable, from seventy to a nation. The Tyndale notes add that the long stay in Egypt was part of God's design: Canaan could not be given until the sin of its people was "complete" (Gen 15:16).

#### Pharaoh Oppresses Israel (vv8–22)
- **"A new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph" (v8).** "Know" in Hebrew can mean to acknowledge or care about, not just to have heard of. This is very likely a new dynasty, not only a new ruler. Many scholars connect it with the Egyptian rulers who expelled the Hyksos, Semitic foreigners who had governed northern Egypt for about a century (roughly 1650–1550 BC). A native Egyptian king would have had little reason to trust another large Semitic group living on his eastern border. The Bible doesn't name this Pharaoh. "Pharaoh" is a title, from Egyptian *per-aa*, "great house," the palace.
- **"Too many and too mighty for us" (v9).** It is the first time the Bible calls Israel a "people" (*am*). The king's fear is political: in a war they might "join our enemies" and "escape from the land" (v10). He wants their labor but fears their numbers.
- **"Come, let us deal shrewdly with them" (v10).** The "come, let us" echoes the builders of Babel (Gen 11:3–4), another proud human plan against God's purposes. "Lest they multiply" uses the same verb as v7. Pharaoh sets himself against the creation blessing, and v12 answers him with a wordplay: "lest they multiply" (*pen yirbeh*) becomes "the more they multiplied" (*ken yirbeh*).
- **"Taskmasters… heavy burdens" (v11).** "Taskmasters" were labor-gang overseers. Egypt used forced labor from foreigners and prisoners of war, and an Egyptian tomb painting from the 15th century BC (the tomb of Rekhmire at Thebes) shows foreign workers making bricks under overseers carrying sticks.
- **"Store cities, Pithom and Raamses" (v11).** These were supply and garrison towns in the eastern Nile Delta, near Goshen. Most archaeologists place Raamses at Qantir, the site of Pi-Ramesses, Ramesses II's great capital, and Pithom at Tell el-Maskhutah or nearby Tell el-Retabeh. Supporters of the earlier date argue the name "Raamses" was updated later, as Genesis 47:11 already uses it for Jacob's day. Israel did not build the pyramids, which were already more than a thousand years old.
- **"The more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied" (v12).** This is the chapter's central irony and a pattern seen throughout church history: pressure meant to crush God's people often grows them. **"In dread"** is *quts*, a word that blends loathing and fear (used of Moab's dread of Israel in Num 22:3).
- **"Ruthlessly… bitter… hard service" (vv13–14).** "Ruthlessly" is *perek*, "harshness, severity." It appears again only in Leviticus 25:43–53 and Ezekiel 34:4, where God forbids Israel to rule this way over one another. Israel was to remember Egypt and treat people differently. "Made their lives bitter" points ahead to the bitter herbs eaten at Passover (12:8).
- **The word "serve" (vv13–14).** The root *avad*, "to work, serve, be a slave," appears five times in these two verses. It is the key word of the book. Later God tells Pharaoh, "Let my son go that he may serve me" (4:23), using the same word for worship. The question of Exodus is whom Israel will serve: Pharaoh in brick and mortar, or the LORD in worship.
- **"Mortar and brick" (v14).** Stone in Egypt came mostly from the far south and was kept for temples and royal monuments. Most buildings in the Delta were mud brick, sun-dried clay mixed with straw, which is why straw becomes a weapon in chapter 5.

- **"The Hebrew midwives… Shiphrah and… Puah" (v15).** The Hebrew can be read as "the Hebrew midwives" or "the midwives of the Hebrews," so some (including Josephus) thought they were Egyptians. Their names are Semitic, which favors the ESV reading. Shiphrah probably means "beautiful" or "fair," and Puah may mean "girl" or "splendor." A nation this size would need many more than two midwives, so these were probably the leaders of the guild. Notice the irony: the Bible names two slave women and leaves the mighty Pharaoh anonymous.
- **"When you… see them on the birthstool" (v16).** The rare word *obnayim* is literally "two stones." Elsewhere it means a potter's wheel (Jer 18:3). Here it most likely means a birthing stool. Egyptian women gave birth squatting on a pair of bricks, and a decorated "birth brick" has been found at Abydos. Pharaoh's plan is cold and practical: kill the boys quietly at birth, keep the girls as future wives and workers, and Israel would slowly disappear as a people.
- **"But the midwives feared God" (v17).** This is the first act of resistance in Exodus, and it comes from women with no power at all. To "fear God" is reverent awe that takes God more seriously than any human threat (Prov 1:7). It is the same reason Peter and John gave the Jerusalem council: "Whether it is right in the sight of God to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge" (Acts 4:19).
- **"The Hebrew women are not like the Egyptian women, for they are vigorous" (v19).** "Vigorous" (*chayeh*) is related to the word for "life" and also for animals. Some hear a sly joke: Pharaoh, who looked down on the Hebrews, is told they give birth like healthy animals. Whether the answer was a lie is debated. Some say it was deception in a just cause, like Rahab's (Josh 2; Heb 11:31). Others think it was true often enough, especially if the midwives took their time. The text praises their fear of God, not their words, and never comments on the statement itself.
- **"God dealt well with the midwives… he gave them families" (vv20–21).** Literally "he made houses for them." In Hebrew "house" means a family line (2 Sam 7:11). Women who protected others' babies were given households of their own. This is the first time God himself acts in the book, and he acts for two slave women.
- **"Every son… you shall cast into the Nile" (v22).** Quiet murder has failed, so Pharaoh turns to open genocide and makes every Egyptian his agent. The Nile was Egypt's lifeblood and was worshipped as a god. Pharaoh means it as a grave, but the river where Hebrew boys were to die is exactly where the deliverer will be saved (2:3–5). The water Pharaoh used for death will later turn to blood (7:20), and Egypt's army will drown in the sea (14:28). It is the first of many attacks on the line of promise, the "offspring" of the woman (Gen 3:15), which Revelation pictures as a dragon waiting to devour the child (Rev 12:4).
- **Commentators:** Henry on v8: "All that knew him loved him… but when he was dead he was soon forgotten." On v10: "When men deal wickedly, it is common for them to imagine that they deal wisely." JFB dates the change of rule about sixty years after Joseph's death and calls Pithom and Raamses fortified towns on a likely invasion route. Guzik suggests Egypt became a kind of womb where Israel could grow large without being absorbed into the peoples around it, as might have happened in Canaan (compare Gen 34; 38). He also admits that how long the harsh slavery lasted is uncertain. On the midwives, Henry writes: "They feared God, regarded his law, and dreaded his wrath more than Pharaoh's," and v21 teaches that piety builds households. On v22 he says the church's enemies have always been "restless in their endeavours to wear out the saints," but God "that sits in heaven shall laugh at them" (Ps 2:4). JFB explains that to "build up a house" in Hebrew idiom means to have many descendants, and it offers two views of the "stools": low birthing seats, or stone troughs. The lexicon favors the birthing stool. Guzik says many assume the midwives lied, but suggests they may have told a partial truth. Even if they lied, he says, God blessed their refusal to kill, not any deception. The Tyndale notes are blunter: full candor before a murderous ruler mattered less to God than faithfulness to him.

#### Pointing to Christ
Exodus begins with a king who murders baby boys to keep his power, and God quietly preserving the one boy who will save his people. Matthew tells the same story again. Herod orders the boys of Bethlehem killed (Matt 2:16), and the child Jesus escapes to Egypt and is called out of it: "Out of Egypt I called my son" (Matt 2:15; Hos 11:1). Jesus is the greater Moses, leading a greater exodus out of a slavery worse than brick and mortar (Luke 9:31, where his coming death is literally his "exodus"). And Pharaoh's question, whom Israel will serve, is answered in Christ, who frees people from sin so that they may serve God (Rom 6:17–22).
