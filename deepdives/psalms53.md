*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Mahalath. A Maskil of David." Psalm 53 is almost word for word the same as Psalm 14. Matthew Henry: "God, in this psalm, speaks twice, for this is the same almost verbatim with the fourteenth psalm." JFB admits, "Why this repetition of the fourteenth Psalm is given we do not know." The Psalms were gathered from several older collections over centuries, and a song could appear in more than one of them. Psalms 42–83 are often called the "Elohistic Psalter," because they mostly call God *Elohim* ("God") rather than by his covenant name *YHWH* ("the LORD"). Here, every "LORD" in Psalm 14 has become "God." Most scholars think that is the editors' preference for that collection, not a change in meaning. The other real difference is v5, which turns Psalm 14's general statement into a picture of a defeated army. So it seems a song of David was adapted for a time when Israel faced invaders. "Mahalath" is unexplained. It appears again only in the title of Psalm 88. It may be the name of a tune, or come from a word for sickness or sorrow, suggesting a mournful setting. Since Psalm 14's Deep Dive covers the shared verses in full, this one is shorter. It says what the psalm means and focuses on where it differs. Coming right after Psalm 52, it reads as a sequel: Doeg was one example of a "mighty man" who would not make God his refuge. Psalm 53 widens the view to the whole human race.

#### The Fool Says in His Heart (vv1–3)
- **"The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (v1).** The "fool" (*nabal*) is not someone with low intelligence. He is someone morally empty, who lives as if God does not matter, like Nabal, Abigail's husband, whose name meant "fool" and who acted the part (1 Sam 25:25). Guzik cites Kidner's phrase, "aggressive perversity." "In his heart" matters. This is less a philosophical argument than a choice of the will: "No God for me." It is the practical atheism of someone who may never deny God out loud but lives as though he will never answer to anyone (10:4).
- **"They are corrupt, doing abominable iniquity" (v1).** Denying God leads to corruption. "Corrupt" means spoiled, ruined, gone rotten (Gen 6:12, of the world before the flood). Psalm 14:1 says "abominable deeds." Psalm 53 says "abominable iniquity" (*'avel*), a word for injustice, wrong done to others. The change is small but fits the psalm's focus on people who "eat up my people" (v4).
- **"There is none who does good" (v1).** At first this sounds as if it means only the fools of the first line. The next two verses show it is broader.
- **"God looks down from heaven on the children of man" (v2).** The picture recalls God coming down to see the tower of Babel (Gen 11:5) and seeing the wickedness of man before the flood (Gen 6:5). It is a careful search, an inspection.
- **"To see if there are any who understand, who seek after God" (v2).** The test is not cleverness. To "understand" (*maskil*, the same word as in the title) is to have the wisdom to seek God. The fool says there is no God. God looks for anyone who seeks him.
- **"They have all fallen away; together they have become corrupt" (v3).** The result of the search is devastating. In Psalm 14:3 the verb is "turned aside" (*sar*). Here it is "fallen away" (*sag*), backslidden, gone back. The two Hebrew words differ by a single letter, and the meaning is nearly the same. "Become corrupt" is a rare verb meaning to go sour, like milk that has turned.
- **"There is none who does good, not even one" (v3).** Paul quotes these lines in Romans 3:10–12, following the Greek translation, to show that "all, both Jews and Greeks, are under sin" (Rom 3:9). His point is that the psalm's verdict covers everyone, the religious as well as the irreligious. The fool of v1 is not only the other person. Guzik draws the same lesson: the psalm moves from atheists to all humanity, including the reader.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the denial of God is "in the heart," so it is moral as well as intellectual, and that "none who does good" is a statement about fallen human nature, not a denial that anyone ever does a kind act. JFB says vv1–4 repeat 14:1–4 with "few verbal changes." Henry reads the psalm's repetition as a sign of how much the lesson matters: God says it twice.

#### There They Are, in Great Terror (vv4–5)
- **"Have those who work evil no knowledge" (v4).** The question is full of amazement. Do they not understand anything? Their problem is not lack of information but willful blindness.
- **"Who eat up my people as they eat bread" (v4).** "My people" is God speaking. These evildoers devour God's people as casually as they eat a meal, without a second thought. Micah used the same picture of rulers who "eat the flesh of my people" (Mic 3:3).
- **"And do not call upon God?" (v4).** The final charge. They never pray. Compare 50:15, where God says what honors him is to "call upon me in the day of trouble."
- **"There they are, in great terror, where there is no terror!" (v5).** Here Psalm 53 parts from Psalm 14. Psalm 14:5 says, "There they are in great terror, for God is with the generation of the righteous." Psalm 53 adds "where there is no terror": they panic when there is nothing to be afraid of. God warned that disobedient Israel would "flee when none pursues" (Lev 26:17, 36), and Proverbs says it of every wicked person (Prov 28:1). Here the curse falls on Israel's enemies. Their panic comes from God, as when the Midianites turned their swords on one another in Gideon's night attack (Judg 7:22), or when the Syrian army heard the sound of chariots that were not there and ran (2 Kgs 7:6–7). Henry: their fear comes from guilty consciences, because they "had made God their enemy."
- **"For God scatters the bones of him who encamps against you" (v5).** This is the other big change. Psalm 14:6 speaks of evildoers shaming the plans of the poor, "but the LORD is his refuge." Psalm 53 shows an enemy army camped against Jerusalem and its bones left scattered on the field. To lie unburied was the greatest disgrace in the ancient world (Jer 8:1–2; Ezek 6:5), and the Tyndale notes say it means shameful defeat with no decent burial. Some think of the Assyrian army that camped against Jerusalem in 701 BC and was struck down in a single night (2 Kgs 19:35). That is a guess, but it fits.
- **"You put them to shame, for God has rejected them" (v5).** "You" is God's people. They win the victory not by their own strength, but because God has rejected the attackers. In Psalm 14 the wicked shame the poor. In Psalm 53 the tables are turned, and the poor shame the wicked. Henry noticed the change: expressions "concerning the shame which the wicked put upon God's people" are "here left out."
- **Commentators:** Guzik ties "where there is no terror" to the promise in Leviticus 26 and to God's past rescues under Joshua, Gideon, Jonathan and Hezekiah, and draws the moral that attacking God's people is as foolish as denying God. JFB says that where Psalm 14 speaks of God's presence with the righteous, this verse describes "the ruin of the wicked," linking the scattered bones with Psalm 141:7. Henry adds that God's people need not fear enemies whom God himself despises.

#### Oh, That Salvation Would Come Out of Zion (v6)
- **"Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion!" (v6).** The verse matches 14:7, except "the LORD" becomes "God." It is a longing cry: if only. Zion is where God lives among his people (Pss 46–48), so salvation must come from him there.
- **"When God restores the fortunes of his people" (v6).** The older translations had "bringeth back the captivity" (KJV), and the phrase can mean that. But it is used more widely for any turning of misfortune (Job 42:10, of Job's restoration). Guzik, following Maclaren, takes it that way. It does not have to mean the psalm was written after the exile in Babylon.
- **"Let Jacob rejoice, let Israel be glad" (v6).** The psalm that began with the fool's denial ends with God's people rejoicing. The verdict of v3 is true, but it is not the last word. God is the one who rescues.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the verse as a prayer for salvation from Zion and rejoicing at the restoration of the people. Guzik notes that David longs for victory before he can see it, the same faith that thanks God "because you have done it" in 52:9.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul used this psalm to close every escape route: "None is righteous, no, not one; no one understands; no one seeks for God" (Rom 3:10–11). If God looked down from heaven and found no one who seeks him, then salvation could only come from God's side. That is exactly where Paul goes next: "But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law… through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe" (Rom 3:21–22). Jesus said, "the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10). God did not only look down from heaven; he came down. The cry of v6, "Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion!", is answered in Paul's words: "The Deliverer will come from Zion, he will banish ungodliness from Jacob" (Rom 11:26). God's people are not those who were better than the fools of v1. They are fools who were found by the God they were not seeking.
