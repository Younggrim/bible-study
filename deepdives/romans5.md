*Setting:* Paul writes Romans from Corinth around AD 57, near the end of his third journey (Acts 20:2–3), to a church he has never visited. Chapters 1–4 made the case that everyone, Jew and Gentile, stands guilty before God (3:23), and that God puts people right with himself as a gift, through faith in Christ, just as he did with Abraham (ch 4). Chapter 4 ended with Jesus "raised for our justification" (4:25). Chapter 5 is a hinge. It opens a new section (chs 5–8) on what justified people now have and why it is secure, and then steps back to the widest view in the whole letter: two men, Adam and Christ, and the whole human race under one or the other.

#### Peace with God Through Faith (vv1–11)
- **"Therefore, since we have been justified by faith" (v1).** "Therefore" gathers up chapters 1–4. Justification is a verdict in God's court, and the tense looks back on it as settled. Paul now moves from *how* a person is made right with God to *what follows* from it.
- **"We have peace with God" (v1).** This is not first a feeling but a changed relationship. The war between a holy God and rebels is over (v10 calls us "enemies"). Peace (*eirēnē*) carries the Old Testament sense of *shalom*, wholeness and well-being under God's blessing, the peace promised for the Messiah's age (Isa 9:6–7; 52:7). This is "peace *with* God," the ground under the "peace *of* God" (Phil 4:7) that guards the heart.
- **"We have" or "let us have"? (v1).** Greek manuscripts differ by a single letter: *echomen* ("we have") or *echōmen* ("let us have"). The two were pronounced almost alike, so copyists dictating or hearing the text could easily confuse them. Several of the oldest manuscripts read "let us have," but most modern editors and translations, including the ESV, print "we have," because Paul's whole argument here is about what God has already done, not what believers must achieve. No doctrine turns on it: either way, peace with God comes "through our Lord Jesus Christ."
- **"Access by faith into this grace in which we stand" (v2).** "Access" (*prosagōgē*) is a bringing near, approach into someone's presence. It is used three times in the New Testament, each time of approaching God (Eph 2:18; 3:12). Some scholars hear a royal picture, being introduced into a king's audience chamber. Grace here is not only the doorway into the Christian life but the ground believers keep standing on. "Stand" is a perfect tense in Greek: we have taken our stand and remain standing.
- **"We rejoice in hope of the glory of God" (v2).** "Rejoice" is *kauchaomai*, normally "boast." Earlier Paul shut down boasting in the law (2:23) and in works (3:27; 4:2). Now he gives believers something to boast in that is all God's doing. "The glory of God" picks up 3:23, where all "fall short of the glory of God." What sin lost, hope will see restored. The Hebrew background is *kabod*, the weighty, shining presence of God, which the prophets promised would one day dwell among his people (Isa 60:1–2). Paul develops this hope in 8:18–30.
- **"We rejoice in our sufferings" (v3).** The same verb again: believers boast even in afflictions. The word (*thlipsis*) means pressure or crushing hardship, not mild inconvenience. Paul is not praising pain for its own sake. He rejoices in what God does with it.
- **The chain (vv3–4).** Suffering produces **endurance** (*hypomonē*, staying power under a load), endurance produces **character**, and character produces **hope**. "Character" is *dokimē*, the result of being tested: proven, tried-and-true quality, like metal that has come through the fire. James 1:2–4 and 1 Peter 1:6–7 run the same pattern. The overlap suggests this was common early Christian teaching, passed on to new believers who would surely suffer.
- **"Hope does not put us to shame" (v5).** In the Old Testament, being "put to shame" often means being exposed as having trusted in the wrong thing, or being found guilty when God judges (Isa 28:16, which Paul quotes in 9:33). Christian hope will not end that way.
- **"God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit" (v5).** "Poured" (*ekcheō*) is the verb used of the Spirit being poured out at Pentecost (Acts 2:17, 33). The love meant here is God's love for us, not our love for him, as v8 makes clear. After brief mentions (1:4; 2:29), this is the first time Romans speaks of the Spirit living in believers, a preview of chapter 8. The perfect tense again says it was poured and remains. Hope is anchored in two things: what Christ did outside us (vv6–8) and what the Spirit does inside us (v5).
- **"While we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly" (v6).** "Weak" (*asthenēs*) means powerless, unable to help ourselves. "At the right time" can mean God's chosen moment in history (Gal 4:4, "when the fullness of time had come"). It can also mean the very moment of our helplessness was the fitting moment for God to act. Both are true, and the Tyndale notes allow either.
- **"For one will scarcely die for a righteous person" (v7).** Paul distinguishes a "righteous" person, upright and fair, from a "good" person, someone generous and kind. Some scholars think "the good man" meant a benefactor, a patron who had done you real favors. A person might lay down life for someone like that. Few would die for someone merely correct.
- **"But God shows his love for us" (v8).** "Shows" is present tense: the cross keeps on demonstrating it. The point lands by contrast. We were not righteous people or good people, but "sinners." "Died for us" uses *hyper*, "on behalf of." Elsewhere the same word plainly carries the sense "in place of" (2 Cor 5:21; Gal 3:13), and here Christ's death on behalf of the ungodly is the death they deserved.
- **"Much more" (vv9–10).** This is the first of five "much more" arguments in the chapter (vv9, 10, 15, 17, 20). It argues from the harder to the easier. If God already did the hard thing, justifying us by Christ's **blood** (his sacrificial death, 3:25; Lev 17:11) while we were sinners, he will surely do the easier thing and bring us safely through the final judgment, "saved… from the wrath of God."
- **"Enemies… reconciled" (v10).** Reconciliation means a hostile relationship made into friendship. Notice who changes. In the New Testament God reconciles people to himself; he is never said to be reconciled to us (2 Cor 5:18–19). Yet the hostility was not one-sided: God's wrath against sin was real (1:18), and the cross dealt with it. "Saved by his life" points to the risen Christ, who lives and intercedes (8:34). His death opened the way and his life keeps us.
- **"We also rejoice in God" (v11).** The boasting climbs: in hope (v2), in sufferings (v3), and now in God himself. "Reconciliation" is *katallagē*. The King James Version rendered it "atonement," which in 1611 meant "at-one-ment," two estranged parties made one. The word's older sense was "exchange," and enmity exchanged for friendship is exactly the point.
- **Commentators:** On v1, JFB holds that the manuscript evidence makes "let us have peace" the right reading "beyond doubt," while admitting most reject it as odd. Guzik and most modern versions take the plain statement "we have peace," and the Tyndale notes simply lay out both. Henry: "There is more in this peace than barely a cessation of enmity, there is friendship and loving-kindness." On the chain of v3–4, Henry writes that "tribulation in itself worketh impatience; but, as it is sanctified to the saints, it worketh patience," and JFB defines patience as "the quiet endurance of what we cannot but wish removed." On v7 all three draw the line between the merely upright and the generous man, JFB calling the good man "a benefactor to society." Guzik stresses that the reason for God's love lies in God, not in us, since those Christ died for were neither. He also reads *hyper* as "instead of." The lexicon gives that sense for passages like 2 Corinthians 5:21, but lists Romans 5:6–8 under "on behalf of," so the substitution idea here rests on the wider context more than on the word itself. Henry sums up the "much more" logic: "He that hath digged so deep to lay the foundation will no doubt build upon that foundation."

#### Death in Adam, Life in Christ (vv12–21)
- **"Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man" (v12).** Paul now widens the frame to all of human history. "Adam" is both a name and the Hebrew word for "human" (*'adam*). The way Paul sets Adam's one act against Christ's one act shows he treats Genesis 3 as real history: a real man whose real disobedience had real consequences (compare 1 Cor 15:21–22). Sin is pictured as an invader entering the world, with death following behind it (Gen 2:17).
- **The sentence breaks off (v12).** Paul starts "just as…" and never finishes with "so also…." The ESV's dash marks where he stops to explain (vv13–14). The comparison he began is finally completed in v18. Paul thinks faster than he can finish sentences.
- **"Because all sinned" (v12).** This short phrase is one of the most discussed in the Bible. The Greek *eph' hō* most likely means "because." The Latin Vulgate rendered it *in quo*, "in whom," and Augustine built on that to teach that all humanity sinned "in Adam." Most scholars today agree the Greek does not say "in whom," but many still think the context (vv15–19) teaches a real connection between Adam's sin and ours. Three main readings follow.
- **The views on v12.** The *representative* (or federal) view, common in Reformed teaching: Adam acted as head for the whole race, as Christ acts for his people, so his guilt counts for all. The *realist* view, going back to Augustine: all humanity was present in Adam and sinned in him. A third view, strong in Eastern Orthodox teaching and held by many others: from Adam we inherit death and a nature bent toward sin, and each person then sins and is guilty for their own sins. All three agree on what matters most: sin is universal, death comes from it, and no one escapes except through Christ. Jewish writers before Paul were also divided. One text says that "when Adam sinned, a death was decreed for those yet to be born," while another says "each of us has become our own Adam" (2 Baruch 23:4; 54:19).
- **"Sin is not counted where there is no law" (v13).** "Counted" (*ellogeō*) is a bookkeeping word, "charged to one's account." Paul uses it in Philemon 18: "charge that to my account." Between Adam and Moses there was no written law spelling out sins and penalties, so sin was not entered in the ledger the same way. Yet sin was certainly there (Cain, the flood generation, Babel).
- **"Yet death reigned from Adam to Moses" (v14).** If death still ruled over people who did not break a spelled-out command as Adam did, something beyond their own lawbreaking explains it. Paul's answer is their tie to Adam. "Reigned" pictures death as a king, and the reign language returns in vv17 and 21.
- **"Adam, who was a type of the one who was to come" (v14).** A "type" (*typos*) is literally the mark left by a blow, then an impression or pattern, like a seal pressed in wax. Adam is the advance pattern of Christ. Each is a single man whose one act decides the destiny of all who belong to him. Paul calls Christ "the last Adam" in 1 Corinthians 15:45.
- **"But the free gift is not like the trespass" (v15).** Having made the comparison, Paul immediately qualifies it: the two are alike in shape but not in power. "Trespass" (*paraptōma*) is literally a false step, a slip off the right path, and the word is used over and over in these verses (vv15, 16, 17, 18, 20). Against it stands "the free gift" (*charisma*) and "the grace of God."
- **"Many… many" (v15).** "Many" here is Hebrew-style idiom for a great multitude and does not by itself mean "every single person." In the first half of the verse it plainly includes everyone (v12). Whether the second "many" also includes everyone depends on the rest of Paul's teaching, and v17 qualifies it: those "who receive" the gift.
- **"Much more" (vv15, 17).** The point is not that grace is a slightly larger quantity. It is that grace is a stronger *kind* of power. If one man's sin could bring death on the whole race, how much more surely will God's grace through one man bring life.
- **"Following one trespass… following many trespasses" (v16).** The math is reversed. Adam's one sin brought condemnation. Christ's gift came in after the pile-up of countless sins and answered all of them with "justification." Grace does not just undo one mistake. It meets a mountain of them.
- **"Will… reign in life" (v17).** Notice what Paul does *not* say. He does not say "life will reign" to balance "death reigned." He says believers themselves will reign. Under Adam, people were the subjects of death. Under Christ, they become kings. The condition is plain: "those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness."
- **"One trespass… one act of righteousness" (v18).** Here Paul finally finishes the sentence he began in v12. The "one act of righteousness" is Christ's obedient death (Phil 2:8). "Justification and life for all men" has been read in two main ways. Most interpreters say "all" means everyone in Christ, just as "all" in the first half means everyone in Adam, or that life is *offered* to all though only received by faith (v17). A minority take it as a promise that every person will in the end be saved. That reading is hard to square with v17 and with Paul's warnings elsewhere (2:5–8; 2 Thess 1:9). The Tyndale notes take the first view.
- **"Made sinners… made righteous" (v19).** "Made" means "constituted" or "appointed," a legal standing. Disobedience is set against **obedience**, Christ's whole life of doing the Father's will, ending at the cross (Isa 53:11: "by his knowledge shall the righteous one, my servant, make many to be accounted righteous").
- **"The law came in to increase the trespass" (v20).** "Came in" (*pareiserchomai*) means "came in alongside" or "slipped in on the side." Its only other use is the false brothers who "slipped in" at Galatians 2:4. The law was not the main storyline. The main storyline was Adam and Christ, and the law came in alongside. Many Jewish teachers held that the law given to Israel pushed back the effects of Adam's sin. Paul says the reverse. The law made sin more visible and more deliberate, turning wrong into open transgression against a known command (4:15; 7:7–13).
- **"Grace abounded all the more" (v20).** Paul reaches for a stronger word. Sin "increased" (*pleonazō*), and grace "super-abounded" (*hyperperisseuō*), abounding beyond all measure. This verse sets up the question that opens chapter 6: "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?"
- **"As sin reigned in death, grace also might reign" (v21).** Two kings, two kingdoms. Sin ruled with death as its instrument. Grace rules "through righteousness," not by ignoring sin but by dealing with it justly at the cross, and its reign leads "to eternal life." The chapter ends with the full title: "through Jesus Christ our Lord."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Adam a public person whose sin reached all, writing "in him we all sinned." He answers the hard question of v13 with the plain history: "Sin was in the world before the law; witness Cain's murder, the apostasy of the old world." JFB reads "all sinned" as "in that one man's first sin" and treats Adam as federal head, though it does not argue from the Greek grammar. On v15 it agrees that the "much more" is about certainty, not size: "it is not a case of quantity at all." On v19 it takes "made" to mean judicially constituted, not inwardly changed. Guzik takes the same representative view of v12. He points to the death of infants as evidence that all are tied to Adam, while insisting God saves little ones by mercy. He firmly rejects reading v18 as universal salvation, since the gift must be received. On v20 Guzik says grace super-abounds so that no one can out-sin it, though anyone can refuse it, and that grace reigning "through righteousness" means it trains people toward holiness rather than excusing sin. Henry: "The greater the strength of the enemy, the greater the honour of the conqueror."

#### Pointing to Christ
Romans 5 is about two men. Every person begins under the first, Adam, and inherits his story of sin and death. Christ entered that story as the last Adam and did what the first man failed to do. Adam reached for what was not his and brought death. Christ obeyed "to the point of death" and brought life. The chapter's peace, hope and confidence in suffering rest on that exchange: God loved his enemies, Christ died for the ungodly, and the risen Lord now lives to keep those he reconciled. Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.
