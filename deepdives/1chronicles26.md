*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC, for a small community rebuilding around a second temple. David, in the last year of his reign (v31), is setting the temple's service in order before Solomon builds it: the Levites in chapter 23, the priests in chapter 24, the musicians in chapter 25. Now come three more groups of Levites: the gatekeepers who guarded the doors, the treasurers who kept the temple's wealth, and the officials and judges who served God and the king out across the land. It reads like a staff list, and it is one. But the Chronicler's readers were descended from these same gatekeepers (1 Chr 9:17–27; Ezra 2:42; Neh 11:19), and this chapter told them their posts went back to David.

#### The Divisions of the Gatekeepers (vv1–19)
- **"The divisions of the gatekeepers" (v1).** Gatekeepers (*shoʿarim*, from *shaʿar*, "gate") opened and locked the temple gates, guarded the storerooms, and kept out anyone who was ritually unclean or had no right to enter (2 Chr 23:19). It was security work, but holy security. David had set aside 4,000 Levites for it (23:5). Chapter 9 already listed their descendants in the Chronicler's own day and traced their office back past David to Phinehas, who was "the chief officer over them in time past" (9:20).
- **"Meshelemiah the son of Kore, of the sons of Asaph" (v1).** This is not Asaph the famous singer of chapter 25, who was a Gershonite. These gatekeepers were Korahites, and 9:19 names their ancestor as Ebiasaph, so "Asaph" here is most likely a shortened form of that name. Meshelemiah is probably the same man called Shelemiah in v14 and Shallum in 9:17–19. The Korahites are a story of grace: their ancestor Korah led a rebellion and died under God's judgment, "but the sons of Korah did not die" (Num 16:32; 26:11). Their descendants guarded God's house and wrote psalms. One of their psalms says, "I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness" (Ps 84:10).
- **"Obed-edom had sons… for God blessed him" (vv4–5).** Here is the chapter's one comment on a person, and it reaches back to chapter 13. When Uzzah died touching the ark, David left it in Obed-edom's house for three months, "and the LORD blessed the household of Obed-edom and all that he had" (13:14). Eight sons, and in v8 sixty-two able descendants, are that blessing made visible. Scholars debate whether Obed-edom "the Gittite" of 13:13 is the same man as this Levite (also 15:18, 24; 16:38). The Chronicler seems to treat them as one, and the "for God blessed him" points straight back.
- **"Men of great ability" (vv6–9).** The Hebrew phrase (*gibbore hayil*) is the one used for "mighty men of valor" in battle. Guarding gates sometimes took strength. When King Uzziah later forced his way into the temple to burn incense, the priests had to drive him out (2 Chr 26:16–20). Verse 8 adds "qualified for the service," so strength and fitness for holy work go together.
- **Shimri, "not the firstborn" (v10).** Hosah, a Merarite, made his second or later son chief. The text does not say why. Perhaps the firstborn died, or perhaps he was unfit. Either way, the post went by ability rather than birth order, a pattern Scripture often shows (Jacob, Joseph, David himself).
- **The numbers.** Meshelemiah's family gives 18 (v9), Obed-edom's 62 (v8) and Hosah's 13 (v11), 93 leaders in all. They headed the larger body of 4,000. In the Chronicler's own day there were 212 gatekeepers on duty in Jerusalem (9:22), so the post-exile community was a much smaller shadow of David's plan, but the same families held the same posts.
- **"Just as their brothers did, ministering in the house of the LORD" (v12).** Gatekeeping counts as "ministering," the same word used of priests and singers. A man at the gate served God as truly as a man at the altar.
- **"Small and great alike" (v13).** As with the priests (24:31) and the singers (25:8), posts were assigned by lot. No family could claim the best gate by rank, and the lot left the choice with God (Prov 16:33).
- **The four sides (vv14–16).** The east gate faced the temple's front door and was the main entrance, so it went to Shelemiah, the senior family. **Zechariah, "a shrewd counselor" (v14),** got the north. "Shrewd" here is *sekel*, prudence or insight, the same word David prayed Solomon would have (22:12). The gatekeeper's son was valued for his judgment, not only his muscle (compare 9:21). Obed-edom got the south, and his sons **"the gatehouse" (v15)**, literally "the house of the stores" (*asuppim*), a storehouse at the south side (Neh 12:25 uses the word for storerooms at the gates).
- **"For Shuppim and Hosah" (v16).** Shuppim is otherwise unknown among the gatekeepers. Many scholars think the name crept in by a copying slip, since the last word of v15 (*asuppim*) looks very similar. Hosah, already named in v10, is the one family left.
- **"The gate of Shallecheth on the road that goes up" (v16).** The name appears only here. The lexicon tentatively suggests "gate of casting out," which leads some to think it was where refuse left the temple. That is a guess from the name. "The road that goes up" was a raised ascent from the city to the temple mount.
- **"Watch corresponded to watch" (v16).** Guard posts were set to match one another on every side, so no gate was left unwatched.
- **Twenty-four posts (vv17–18).** Six at the east (the busiest gate), four each at the north and south, two and two at the storehouse, four on the western road and two at the **"colonnade"** (*parbar*), 24 posts in all. That matches the twenty-four divisions of priests and singers, so the whole system ran on one rotating pattern. *Parbar* is a rare word, used again only in 2 Kings 23:11 for a structure near the temple's west side. The lexicon calls it an open colonnade or portico, probably a loanword, but nobody knows its exact shape.
- **Commentators:** All three see Obed-edom's large family as God's reward for receiving the ark. Henry: "He that is trusty in little shall be trusted with more," and "The increase and building up of families are owing to the divine blessing." JFB counts "seventy-two descendants," which does not match the text's sixty-two (v8); he may be adding the eight sons and Shemaiah's sons, but the verse itself says sixty-two. Guzik stresses that the gatekeepers' practical work counted as ministry just like the priests', and that the line between sacred and ordinary service fades for a worshipping people. Henry compares the porters to the angels at the gates of the New Jerusalem (Rev 21:12) and to the "keys" Christ gave his church for admitting and excluding (Matt 16:19). JFB thinks Shallecheth was probably the gate where "the accumulated filth and sweepings" were carried out, which the Tyndale notes do not claim and which rests only on the name.

#### The Treasurers, Officers, and Judges (vv20–32)
- **"Ahijah had charge of the treasuries" (v20).** The Hebrew reads strangely here. The Greek Old Testament reads "and the Levites, their brothers," with nearly the same consonants, and several modern versions follow it. JFB also prefers that reading. Either way, Levites kept two treasuries: one for "the house of God" (its regular supplies and money) and one for "the dedicated gifts."
- **Jehieli and his sons (vv21–22).** These were Gershonites from the clan of Ladan (23:7–9). A Jehiel also received the leaders' gifts of precious stones for the temple (29:8), probably the same family.
- **"Shebuel the son of Gershom, son of Moses" (v24).** The great lawgiver's descendants held no special rank. They were simply Levites (23:14–15). "Son" here means descendant, since Moses lived centuries earlier. There is a quiet contrast with Judges 18:30, where another descendant of Gershom served as priest for the Danites' idol. In David's day Moses' family was back guarding the true God's treasures.
- **Shelomoth and the dedicated gifts (vv25–28).** Shelomoth (also spelled Shelomith) was descended from Moses' other son, Eliezer. He kept what had been "dedicated" (*qodashim*, holy things) "from spoil won in battles… for the maintenance of the house of the LORD." David had already set aside silver and gold from his wars (18:11; 22:14).
- **"Samuel the seer and Saul… and Abner… and Joab" (v28).** This list is striking. Samuel, Saul, Abner and Joab were not one happy team. Saul hunted David, Abner fought against him, and Joab murdered Abner. Yet each of them, in his day, gave part of his spoil to God, and the gifts were kept together for the one house. The temple's treasure was gathered across a whole troubled generation.
- **"External duties for Israel, as officers and judges" (v29).** Some Levites worked away from the temple. "Officers" (*shoterim*) were officials or scribes. Deuteronomy 17:8–9 had already sent hard cases to "the Levitical priests" and the judge, and King Jehoshaphat later sent Levites out to teach the law and judge (2 Chr 17:7–9; 19:8–11). Of the 4,000 Levites set aside to be officers and judges (23:4), these families led the way.
- **"For all the work of the LORD and for the service of the king" (vv30, 32).** The phrase is repeated, and it is the point of the section. In Israel the king's affairs and God's affairs were not two separate worlds. These Levites carried both: collecting what was owed to God and the crown, teaching and applying God's law, and keeping the nation loyal.
- **"In the fortieth year of David's reign" (v31).** This dates the whole arrangement to David's final year (29:27). A search was made, and "men of great ability" were found at **Jazer in Gilead**, a Levitical city east of the Jordan (Josh 21:39). The Hebronites were a Kohathite clan named for Hebron, a son of Kohath (23:12), and they supply both groups of officials in vv30–32.
- **East and west (vv30, 32).** 1,700 Hebronites served the large western territory, but 2,700 served the two and a half tribes east of the Jordan. The eastern tribes were cut off from Jerusalem by the river and most exposed to drifting away (compare Josh 22:10–34). More of God's officers were sent where the danger was greater.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the lesson from the war spoils: "The more God bestows upon us the more he expects from us," and "Men of war must honour God with their spoils." He also links the treasuries to Christ, in whom are hidden "all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3). JFB explains "officers" as scribes or secretaries who served the Levite magistrates. Guzik notes the larger force east of the Jordan and passes on another writer's point that Gilead mattered strategically. Henry agrees there were more Levites on that side.

#### Pointing to Christ
Gatekeepers kept the unclean out of God's house, and the line was guarded with real force. Jesus calls himself both the door and the keeper: "I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved" (John 10:9). He does not lower the standard of holiness. He makes the unclean clean and brings them in. The treasuries of dedicated gifts were gathered from spoil won in battle, and Christ, having "disarmed the rulers and authorities" by his cross, now gives gifts from his victory to his people (Col 2:15; Eph 4:8). And the lowly post at the gate, ministering "just as their brothers did," fits his own words that the greatest is the one who serves (Luke 22:26–27). A doorkeeper in his house has the best place there is.
