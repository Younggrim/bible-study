*Setting:* About 946 BC, twenty years into Solomon's reign. The temple took seven years to build and the palace thirteen (1 Kgs 6:38–7:1), and both are now finished. In chapter 7 God appeared to Solomon a second time with a promise and a warning: walk as David walked and the throne will stand, but turn to other gods and this house will become a byword. Chapter 8 is a summary of the rest of Solomon's achievements: building, defense, labor, the ordering of worship and trade by sea. It follows 1 Kings 9:10–28 closely. The Chronicler is not writing a full biography. He gathers the evidence that God kept his word to Solomon, and he puts the ordering of the temple's worship at the center of the list.

#### Solomon's Accomplishments (vv1–18)
- **"At the end of twenty years" (v1).** The house of the LORD and the king's house stand side by side, the work of two decades. The Chronicler mentions the palace only in passing. What matters to him is the temple.
- **"The cities that Hiram had given to him" (v2).** Here Chronicles seems to reverse Kings. In 1 Kings 9:11–13 it is Solomon who gives Hiram, king of Tyre, twenty towns in Galilee, and Hiram is so unimpressed that he calls them "Cabul," which sounds like "good for nothing." The most likely explanation is that Hiram handed the towns back. Some think they had served as security for the gold Hiram lent Solomon (1 Kgs 9:14) and were returned when the debt was settled. Either way, Solomon then rebuilt them and settled Israelites there. JFB suggests the towns were "unsuitable to the commercial habits" of Hiram's people, who lived by the sea.
- **"Solomon went to Hamath-zobah and took it" (v3).** This is the only military campaign of Solomon's that Chronicles records. His name comes from *shalom*, and he was promised to be "a man of rest" (1 Chr 22:9). Hamath lay on the Orontes River in Syria and Zobah was an Aramean kingdom David had defeated (1 Chr 18:3–10). By now the two seem to have been joined under one ruler. Solomon's move secured the northern edge of the kingdom.
- **"He built Tadmor in the wilderness" (v4).** Tadmor is the oasis city later famous as Palmyra, in the Syrian desert about 120 miles northeast of Damascus, on the caravan road to the Euphrates. The parallel in 1 Kings 9:18 is written "Tamar," a town in the south of Judah, though Jewish scribes marked it to be read "Tadmor." Scholars still debate which place Kings meant. The Chronicler clearly means the northern oasis, which fits his picture of Solomon's rule reaching to the Euphrates (9:26).
- **"Upper Beth-horon and Lower Beth-horon, fortified cities with walls, gates, and bars" (v5).** The two Beth-horons guarded the main pass climbing from the coastal plain up to the hill country north of Jerusalem. Joshua chased the Amorites down this slope (Josh 10:10–11). Whoever held it held the western approach to the capital.
- **"Store cities… cities for his chariots… cities for his horsemen" (v6).** These were bases for supplies and for a standing chariot force. Archaeologists have found large buildings with long pillared halls at Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer, cities Kings says Solomon fortified (1 Kgs 9:15). Whether those halls were stables, storehouses or barracks is still debated, and their exact date is argued over too. The text simply shows a king with the resources to build "whatever Solomon desired." But there is a shadow. Deuteronomy warned that Israel's king "must not acquire many horses for himself" (Deut 17:16), and the next chapter shows Solomon importing horses from Egypt (9:28).
- **"The Hittites, the Amorites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites" (v7).** These are the Canaanite peoples Israel did not drive out under Joshua and the judges (Judg 1:21–36). Solomon "drafted" their descendants "as forced labor," permanent state labor gangs, "and so they are to this day" (v8). "To this day" is copied from Kings and reflects the time before the exile.
- **"But of the people of Israel Solomon made no slaves for his work" (v9).** Israelites served as soldiers, officers and charioteers. Kings adds that Israelites did serve in rotating work crews of 30,000 sent to Lebanon (1 Kgs 5:13–14), so they were not entirely spared hard labor. But they were not made permanent slaves. By the end of Solomon's reign, the burden of his projects was heavy enough that the northern tribes revolted over it (10:4).
- **"The chief officers of King Solomon, 250" (v10).** Kings gives 550 (1 Kgs 9:23). The totals reconcile neatly, though. Chronicles has 3,600 foremen (2:18) plus 250 chief officers, and Kings has 3,300 (1 Kgs 5:16) plus 550. Both come to 3,850. The two books appear to divide the same body of supervisors differently. JFB simply judges that one text "is corrupt."
- **"Solomon brought Pharaoh's daughter up from the city of David" (v11).** Kings mentions this Egyptian marriage five times, and it was a sign of Solomon's international standing (1 Kgs 3:1; 9:16, 24). Pharaoh even gave him the city of Gezer as her dowry. Chronicles mentions her only here and never mentions Solomon's seven hundred wives or how they "turned away his heart" in old age (1 Kgs 11:1–8). That is not because the Chronicler hides it. His readers had Kings. He chose to focus on Solomon as temple-builder.
- **"The places to which the ark of the LORD has come are holy" (v11).** Only Chronicles gives Solomon's reason for moving her. The ark had rested in a tent in David's city (1 Chr 15:1; 16:1), and that ground had become holy. A foreign wife who did not worship Israel's God, and who would bring her own customs and gods, should not live there. Solomon's words show he knew the danger, yet he married her anyway. Later, his foreign wives led him to build shrines for their gods (1 Kgs 11:7–8).
- **"Burnt offerings… as the duty of each day required" (vv12–13).** The heart of the chapter. Worship at the new altar followed "the commandment of Moses": daily offerings morning and evening, plus extra offerings on **Sabbaths** and **new moons** (Num 28–29), and the **three annual feasts** (Deut 16:16). These were **Unleavened Bread** in spring, remembering the Exodus; **Weeks** (later called Pentecost), the wheat harvest fifty days later; and **Booths**, in autumn, when the people lived in shelters to remember the wilderness. Solomon had dedicated the temple at Booths (7:8–10).
- **"According to the ruling of David his father" (v14).** Moses gave the law of sacrifice and David organized the people who carried it out: the twenty-four divisions of priests (1 Chr 24), the Levites who led praise (1 Chr 25) and the gatekeepers (1 Chr 26). "David the man of God" is a title usually given to prophets such as Moses, Elijah and Elisha. Chronicles and Nehemiah give it to David (Neh 12:24, 36) because his arrangements for worship came from God (1 Chr 28:19).
- **"They did not turn aside from what the king had commanded" (v15).** The same verb, "turn aside," was in God's warning (7:19, "if you turn aside"). For now, priests and Levites stay on the path. JFB takes "the king" to mean David, whose orders were "divinely authorized."
- **"So the house of the LORD was completed" (v16).** "Completed" is *shalem*, from the same root as *shalom* and the name Solomon. The man of peace finishes the house of peace. The Chronicler means that the temple was not finished until its worship was in order. The building was only part of the work.
- **"Ezion-geber and Eloth on the shore of the sea, in the land of Edom" (v17).** These ports stood at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, the eastern arm of the Red Sea, near today's Eilat and Aqaba. David had conquered Edom (1 Chr 18:12–13), which gave Israel its only outlet to the southern seas.
- **"Hiram sent to him… ships and servants familiar with the sea" (v18).** Israel was not a seafaring people. The Phoenicians of Tyre were the great sailors of the age. Kings says Solomon built the fleet and Hiram supplied crews (1 Kgs 9:26–27). Chronicles says Hiram "sent… ships," possibly meaning shipbuilders and materials sent overland or the expertise to build them. Either way, it was a joint venture.
- **"They went to Ophir… and brought from there 450 talents of gold" (v18).** Ophir was famous for fine gold (Job 22:24; Ps 45:9; Isa 13:12), but where it was is uncertain. Southwest Arabia is the most common view, with East Africa and India as alternatives. Kings gives 420 talents (1 Kgs 9:28). The difference may come from copying, since Hebrew numbers were easily confused, or from counting differently. A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this was something like 15 to 17 tons of gold.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the chariot and horse cities as early signs that Solomon was trusting in military power, setting them against Deuteronomy 17:16 and Psalm 20:7 ("Some trust in chariots and some in horses"). He calls the marriage to Pharaoh's daughter the first of Solomon's unwise marriages and sees v11 as Solomon admitting she was unfit for holy ground while marrying her anyway. On v2 he follows the scholar Martin Selman, who suggests the towns were returned to Solomon. Henry draws a lesson from v11: "Between things sacred and things common the ancient landmarks ought to be kept up." On the daily worship he says "it is good to be in a settled method of devotion," and he praises Solomon because he did not try to "amend, alter, or add to" what David, "in God's name, commanded." His dry note on v18: "Canaan was a rich country, and yet must send to Ophir for gold." JFB places the ports on the Gulf of Aqaba and says Solomon was "determined to cultivate the arts of peace." JFB has no notes on vv12–14 or v16.

#### Pointing to Christ
Solomon's kingdom at its height had strong cities, peace on its borders, gold from Ophir and worship in perfect order. Yet in the middle of the list stands a foreign wife kept away from the holy places, the first crack in a kingdom that would split after his death. The Chronicler's readers had none of this splendor left. But they still had the pattern of daily sacrifice and praise, and the promise of a greater Son of David. Jesus is the true "man of rest" who builds God's house out of living stones (1 Pet 2:5), and the offering he made was once for all (Heb 10:10–12). Under him the worship of God is finally "completed."
