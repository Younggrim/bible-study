*Setting:* Jerusalem, about 835 BC. For six years Athaliah, daughter of Ahab and Jezebel, has ruled Judah after murdering the royal family (22:10). Baal has a temple in the capital with its own priest (v17). Everyone believes David's line is finished. But one baby escaped: Joash, rescued by his aunt Jehoshabeath and hidden in the temple by her husband, the priest Jehoiada (22:11–12). Now the boy is seven. Chapter 23 tells how Jehoiada brought him out and crowned him. The parallel is 2 Kings 11:4–20. Kings stresses the royal bodyguard and the army officers. Chronicles stresses the priests, the Levites, the gathered assembly and the holiness of the temple. Both are true, and each writer highlights what matters for his readers. For the Chronicler, a restored king and restored worship belong together.

#### Joash Made King (vv1–11)
- **"In the seventh year Jehoiada took courage" (v1).** "Took courage" is literally "strengthened himself," the same verb used of kings making themselves strong (1:1; 12:13), and of Jehoram making his throne secure by murder (21:4). Jehoiada's strength goes in the opposite direction. He waited six years, until the boy was old enough to be shown and the people were weary of Athaliah, and then he moved. Patience and courage worked together.
- **"Entered into a covenant with the commanders of hundreds" (v1).** A covenant was a solemn, sworn agreement. Five officers are named, which the Chronicler adds to Kings. In 2 Kings 11:4 these commanders lead "the Carites and the guards," the royal bodyguard. The Carites were probably foreign mercenaries, possibly from Caria in southwest Asia Minor, like David's Cherethites and Pelethites (2 Sam 8:18). Chronicles leaves them out, not because they weren't there but because the writer focuses on the people of God.
- **"Gathered the Levites from all the cities of Judah, and the heads of fathers' houses of Israel" (v2).** "Fathers' houses" were extended family clans, and their heads were the elders of the nation. Calling Judah "Israel" is the Chronicler's habit: Judah was the faithful remnant of all twelve tribes. The coup was not a palace plot by a few. It had the support of the nation's religious and family leaders.
- **"All the assembly made a covenant with the king in the house of God" (v3).** The "assembly" (*qahal*) is the Chronicler's word for Israel gathered before God, as at David's and Solomon's great gatherings (1 Chr 29:1, 10; 2 Chr 6:3). A seven-year-old was being made king, but this was first an act of worship.
- **"Behold, the king's son! Let him reign, as the LORD spoke concerning the sons of David" (v3).** For many in the room this would have been a shock: they had thought there were no sons of David left. Jehoiada's whole case rests on God's promise (2 Sam 7:12–16; 1 Chr 17:11–14). He is not putting his own man on the throne. He is restoring the one God had promised.
- **The Sabbath shift change (vv4–5).** Priests and Levites served in divisions (*mishmarot*) that rotated every week, changing over on the Sabbath (1 Chr 24). At the changeover, both the outgoing and the incoming divisions were at the temple, so twice the usual number of armed men could gather without raising suspicion. Jehoiada split them into thirds: gatekeepers, a group at the palace, and a group at "the Gate of the Foundation." 2 Kings 11:6 calls this "the gate Sur." The two Hebrew names differ by letters easily confused, and no one knows the gate's location for certain.
- **"Let no one enter the house of the LORD except the priests and ministering Levites… for they are holy" (v6).** This is the Chronicler's special concern, not mentioned in Kings. Even in an emergency, even for a coronation, God's house is not to be profaned. The ordinary people stand in the courts, and only those set apart for temple service enter the sacred building. Jehoiada carries out a revolution without breaking God's law of holiness.
- **"Whoever enters the house shall be put to death. Be with the king when he comes in and when he goes out" (v7).** The boy was the only living heir. If an assassin reached him, the line of David would end that day. The Levites formed a living wall around him.
- **"Jehoiada the priest did not dismiss the divisions" (v8).** The off-duty men stayed. That doubled the force and kept the plan secret, since no one went home to talk.
- **"The spears and the large and small shields that had been King David's" (v9).** These were stored in the temple, probably as trophies and dedicated weapons (1 Chr 18:7–11; 2 Sam 8:7). There is fitting symbolism here: David's son is protected with David's weapons. The large shield (*tsinnah*) covered the whole body, the small shield (*magen*) was round and carried on the arm.
- **"From the south side of the house to the north side… around the altar and the house" (v10).** The guard stretched across the temple court in front of the sanctuary, with the great bronze altar of sacrifice in the middle. The king would be crowned within sight of the altar.
- **"Put the crown on him and gave him the testimony" (v11).** The "crown" (*nezer*) was the royal diadem, a word connected with consecration. "The testimony" (*'edut*) is usually God's covenant law, as in the tablets kept in the "ark of the testimony" (Exod 25:16–22). The Tyndale notes read it as a copy of the law, given to the king as Deuteronomy 17:18–20 required: every king was to have his own copy of God's law, read it all his life and not lift himself above his brothers. Some scholars, and the BDB lexicon, suggest a small change of letters to read "bracelets" or "armlets," royal insignia like the armlet Saul wore (2 Sam 1:10). The ESV keeps "testimony," which fits the Chronicler's focus on God's law.
- **"Jehoiada and his sons anointed him" (v11).** Anointing with oil set a king apart as God's chosen one. The Hebrew word "anointed one" is *mashiach*, from which we get "Messiah." Only Chronicles says Jehoiada's sons took part. One of them was probably Zechariah, whom this same Joash would later have stoned to death in the temple court (24:20–22). The reader who knows the end of the story feels the weight of that detail.
- **"Long live the king" (v11).** Literally "May the king live!" The KJV's "God save the king" is a free rendering that became a national anthem. It is the same shout raised for Saul (1 Sam 10:24) and Solomon (1 Kings 1:39).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry praises Jehoiada's prudence, since he "reserved the young prince for so many years till he was fit to appear in public," and his courage: "He ventured his head, but it was in a good cause, and therefore he went on boldly." Henry adds that his "eye to the promise" gave the undertaking its glory. Guzik makes a similar point about timing, choosing the Sabbath when two divisions overlapped, and draws an application: people often live under a false ruler because they don't know the true King is alive. JFB says the commanders "had been probably of the royal guard," which agrees with 2 Kings 11. On "the testimony" JFB offers both views, a copy of the law or royal insignia, which matches the lexicon's suggestion. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both take it as the law of Deuteronomy 17.

#### Athaliah Executed (vv12–15)
- **"When Athaliah heard the noise of the people running and praising the king" (v12).** The palace stood next to the temple, just south of it. Athaliah heard shouting and music, came to see, and walked into the temple courts alone, perhaps still believing she was in control.
- **"The king standing by his pillar at the entrance" (v13).** The king had a set place to stand in the temple court on public occasions. Josiah later stands "by the pillar" to renew the covenant (2 Kings 23:3). It may have been one of the two great bronze pillars, Jachin and Boaz, at the temple porch (3:15–17), or a raised platform like the one Solomon stood on (6:13). Either way, Athaliah sees a boy in the king's place.
- **Trumpeters and singers (v13).** Only Chronicles mentions the singers "leading in the celebration." Throughout the book, true worship comes with music arranged by David (1 Chr 15–16; 25). The coronation is also a festival of praise.
- **"Athaliah tore her clothes and cried, 'Treason! Treason!'" (v13).** Tearing garments was a sign of grief or horror. The word for "treason" (*qesher*) means a conspiracy. The irony is complete. The woman who seized the throne by murdering her grandsons calls the rightful king's coronation a conspiracy. By her standards it was one. By God's, she was the traitor.
- **"Do not put her to death in the house of the LORD" (v14).** Jehoiada again protects the temple's holiness. Blood shed in God's house would defile it (compare 24:21, where Joash breaks exactly this). "Between the ranks" means she was led out under guard, with orders to kill anyone who tried to rescue her.
- **"The entrance of the horse gate of the king's house" (v15).** This was a gate on the palace grounds, probably where horses and chariots entered, near the Kidron Valley on the east side of the city (Jer 31:40; Neh 3:28). The detail echoes the death of Athaliah's mother. Jezebel died trampled by Jehu's horses (2 Kings 9:33). Athaliah dies at the horse gate.
- **Commentators:** Guzik argues that Athaliah's charge of treason was technically correct, but it was a righteous rebellion against a murderous usurper on behalf of the rightful king. He also calls Jehoiada's order to kill her outside the temple both just and wise. JFB places the king "at his pillar" in the court and suggests he may have stood on Solomon's bronze platform. It identifies the horse gate with the entrance to the royal stables near the Kidron.

#### Jehoiada's Reforms (vv16–21)
- **"A covenant between himself and all the people and the king that they should be the LORD's people" (v16).** This is the climax of the chapter. The coronation was the means, and covenant renewal was the goal. 2 Kings 11:17 describes two covenants: between the LORD and king and people, and between the king and people. The Tyndale notes see all three relationships here: people to king, king to God and people to God. Jehoiada, a priest, stands in God's place to make it. The heart of it is the old covenant promise, "you shall be my people" (Exod 6:7; Lev 26:12).
- **"All the people went to the house of Baal and tore it down" (v17).** Baal was the Canaanite storm and fertility god whom Jezebel had promoted in the north (1 Kings 16:31–32). Athaliah brought his worship to Jerusalem, and 24:7 says her sons even used holy things from the temple of the LORD for the Baals. The people act at once. Renewing a covenant with God meant pulling down what competed with him.
- **"They killed Mattan the priest of Baal before the altars" (v17).** Mattan means "gift," a name sometimes shortened from forms meaning "gift of Baal." The law of Moses commanded death for anyone who led Israel to worship other gods (Deut 13:6–10). He dies at the altars he served. Jehu had done the same to Baal's priests in Samaria (2 Kings 10:18–28).
- **"According to the order of David" (v18).** This verse is all Chronicles. Jehoiada restores the temple routine just as David had organized it: Levitical priests offering burnt offerings "as it is written in the Law of Moses," and Levites leading "with rejoicing and with singing." Two authorities stand behind worship: Moses for the sacrifices and David for the music and order (1 Chr 23–26). Jehoiada adds nothing new. He puts back what had been set aside.
- **"So that no one should enter who was in any way unclean" (v19).** Gatekeepers were Levites charged with guarding the purity of the temple (1 Chr 9:17–27; 26:1–19). After six years of Baal's influence, guarding God's house from defilement mattered again.
- **"Brought the king down from the house of the LORD, marching through the upper gate to the king's house" (v20).** The temple stood higher than the palace, so the procession went "down." The order matters: the king goes from the house of God to the house of the king. He receives his throne from God's house, not the other way round. "The upper gate" may be the gate Jotham later rebuilt (27:3).
- **"All the people of the land rejoiced, and the city was quiet" (v21).** "The people of the land" (*'am ha'arets*) usually means the landowners and leading families of Judah, the backbone of the country. "Quiet" is the Chronicler's word for God-given rest, used of Asa's and Jehoshaphat's peaceful years (14:1, 5; 20:30). Six years of fear end with joy and peace.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, following Selman, says the climax of the chapter is the covenant, not the crown. Following Payne, he notes that the political change came with a real religious revival. He also observes that the covenant bound king and people alike, so neither had absolute power. JFB refers v16 to its note on 2 Kings 11:17 and passes over the destruction of Baal's temple without comment. Henry notes in his comment on vv1–11 that one of the sons who anointed Joash was probably Zechariah, later killed by Joash, which made the king's act especially ungrateful. The Tyndale notes stress that Jehoiada restored both the dynasty and the worship David had arranged.

#### Pointing to Christ
A true king, thought to be dead, was hidden in the house of God. At the right time he was brought out, crowned, given God's testimony and anointed, and the usurper's power collapsed. The story has the shape of the gospel. Jesus, the anointed Son of David, was laid in a tomb, and his enemies thought his line was finished. On the third day he came out, and he was declared "the Son of God in power… by his resurrection from the dead" (Rom 1:4). In Joash's story the crowning led to a renewed covenant and pulled-down idols. In ours, the risen King makes a new covenant in his blood (Luke 22:20), and his people turn "to God from idols to serve the living and true God" (1 Thess 1:9). Joash himself would fail. The King Jehoiada's covenant pointed to never does.
