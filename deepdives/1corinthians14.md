*Setting:* Paul writes from Ephesus in about AD 53–55. This chapter ends the section on spiritual gifts (chapters 12–14). Chapter 12 showed that the Spirit gives different gifts to different members of one body, and chapter 13 that love is the way every gift must be used. Now Paul applies both to the Corinthians' actual meetings. Picture a few dozen believers crowded into a house. Several people are speaking in tongues at once, prophets are talking over each other, and visitors are wondering what is going on. Paul does not shut the gifts down. He gives one test for everything said in church: does it build others up? The word "build up" (*oikodomeō*, *oikodomē*, from the word for building a house) runs through the chapter (vv3, 4, 5, 12, 17, 26).

#### Prophecy and Tongues (vv1–25)
- **"Pursue love, and earnestly desire the spiritual gifts, especially that you may prophesy" (v1).** The order matters. Love is to be *pursued*, chased like a runner after a goal. Gifts are to be *desired*. Love comes first and shapes which gifts to want: the ones that serve others best.
- **"One who speaks in a tongue speaks not to men but to God" (v2).** Tongues, as Paul describes them here, are directed to God in prayer and praise (vv14–17). Without interpretation no one else understands. "He utters mysteries in the Spirit" means things hidden from the hearers, not necessarily deep secrets. (The King James adds "unknown" before "tongue" in this chapter, printed in italics because it is not in the Greek.)
- **"Upbuilding and encouragement and consolation" (v3).** This is the clearest description of New Testament prophecy anywhere. It speaks to people, in words they understand, to strengthen them, urge them on and comfort them. "Encouragement" (*paraklēsis*) is related to the word Jesus used for the Holy Spirit, the Helper or Comforter (John 14:16).
- **"The one who speaks in a tongue builds up himself" (v4).** Paul is not scolding here. Being built up in one's own faith is good (Jude 20). But the church meeting is for building up *others*, and that is where prophecy is "greater."
- **"I want you all to speak in tongues, but even more to prophesy" (v5).** Paul is not against tongues. "I want" expresses a wish, like 7:7, where he says he wishes everyone were single as he is. It does not mean everyone will (12:30). **"Unless someone interprets"**: an interpreted tongue does build up the church, and then it works much like prophecy.
- **"If I come to you speaking in tongues, how will I benefit you…?" (v6).** Paul imagines himself visiting Corinth and speaking only in tongues. What use would that be? They need revelation, knowledge, prophecy or teaching: words that make sense.
- **"The flute or the harp… the bugle" (vv7–8).** Three instruments. A flute or a harp playing random notes is not music. A battle trumpet giving an unclear signal leaves the army confused about whether to attack or retreat. Sound is not enough. It has to carry meaning.
- **"You will be speaking into the air" (v9).** Talking to the wind: noise, but nothing reaches anyone.
- **"I will be a foreigner to the speaker" (v11).** The word is *barbaros*. Greeks used it for anyone who did not speak Greek, because foreign languages sounded to them like "bar-bar-bar." Paul says uninterpreted tongues turn brothers and sisters into foreigners to each other in their own church.
- **"Since you are eager for manifestations of the Spirit, strive to excel in building up the church" (v12).** "Manifestations of the Spirit" is literally "spirits." Paul redirects their eagerness rather than crushing it. Want to excel? Excel at building others up.
- **"My spirit prays but my mind is unfruitful" (v14).** When Paul prays in a tongue, his spirit is truly engaged with God, but his mind does not grasp the words, and it bears no fruit for others either. The tongues-speaker should therefore pray for the ability to interpret (v13).
- **"I will pray with my spirit, but I will pray with my mind also" (v15).** Paul does not choose between heart and head. Both belong in worship. "Sing praise" (*psallō*) originally meant playing a stringed instrument, and later singing praise. Early Christian worship was full of song (Col 3:16; Eph 5:19).
- **"How can anyone in the position of an outsider say 'Amen'…?" (v16).** "Amen" is Hebrew for "it is true" or "so be it." Saying "Amen" after a prayer meant joining in it and making it one's own (Neh 8:6; Ps 106:48). The Greek word for "outsider" here is *idiōtēs*, a private person or layman, someone untrained. In vv16, 23 and 24 it probably means an inquirer or new believer who does not have the gift, perhaps sitting in a special place in the meeting. (Our word "idiot" comes from it, but that meaning is much later.)
- **"I thank God that I speak in tongues more than all of you" (v18).** A striking claim from the man correcting the abuse of tongues. Paul is not criticizing from outside. Since we never see him speaking in tongues in public in Acts or the letters, his use was apparently mainly in private prayer.
- **"Five words with my mind… than ten thousand words in a tongue" (v19).** "Ten thousand" (*myrioi*) was the largest Greek number word, the root of "myriad." Five clear words outweigh countless unclear ones.
- **"Do not be children in your thinking. Be infants in evil" (v20).** The Corinthians were impressed by the spectacular, as children are by noise and color (13:11). Paul wants them innocent as infants toward evil, but grown up in their thinking (Matt 10:16).
- **"By people of strange tongues… I will speak to this people" (v21).** Paul quotes Isaiah 28:11–12. He calls it "the Law," meaning the Old Testament Scriptures as a whole (as Jesus does in John 10:34). In Isaiah, Israel's leaders mocked God's prophet, saying his plain teaching was like baby talk. God replied that, since they would not listen to plain words, he would speak to them through the foreign tongue of Assyrian invaders. Hearing a language they could not understand would be a sign of judgment. And "even then they will not listen."
- **"Tongues are a sign not for believers but for unbelievers" (v22).** This is one of the hardest verses in the chapter, because vv23–25 seem to show the opposite: tongues drive unbelievers away and prophecy wins them. Most interpreters resolve it through Isaiah 28. Tongues are a sign *to* unbelievers in the sense of a sign *against* them, a mark of judgment on people who refuse to listen. They confirm the hearer's distance from God and do not draw him in. Prophecy is a "sign for believers," a mark of God's presence with his people, and it can bring an unbeliever to faith. Others read "sign" more broadly. Either way, the practical point is clear: in the gathered church, prophecy helps outsiders and uninterpreted tongues do not.
- **"Will they not say that you are out of your minds?" (v23).** "Out of your minds" (*mainesthe*) is the word for frenzy. To a pagan visitor, a room full of people speaking in tongues at once would sound like the ecstatic cults of Dionysus. The gospel would be mistaken for one more mystery religion.
- **"He is convicted by all… the secrets of his heart are disclosed" (vv24–25).** When God's word is spoken plainly, an unbeliever finds himself searched and seen (Heb 4:12–13; John 4:29). The woman at the well was won by being *known*.
- **"Falling on his face, he will worship God and declare that God is really among you" (v25).** This echoes Isaiah 45:14, where the nations come to Israel and say, "Surely God is in you." It is the goal of Christian worship: not that visitors are impressed with the church, but that they meet God there.
- **Commentators:** All three agree that Paul ranks prophecy above tongues in church because it is understood. Matthew Henry puts the principle plainly: "Public worship should be performed so as to be understood," and "What cannot be understood can never edify." He adds on vv24–25 that truth "plainly and duly taught, has a marvellous aptness to awaken the conscience, and touch the heart." Guzik insists that "builds up himself" (v4) is not a rebuke but simply describes a gift aimed at God, and he stresses that Paul valued tongues highly in private prayer. JFB notes that the speaker is built up because he understands what he is praying in his spirit, and reads the trumpet of v8 as the signal for attack or retreat. On v22, JFB and Guzik both read the Isaiah quote as making tongues a sign of judgment on unbelief. Guzik admits openly that the verse sits in tension with vv23–25, mentions J. B. Phillips's suggestion that a copyist reversed the words, and prefers to read the hard verse in light of the clear ones that follow.

#### Orderly Worship (vv26–40)
- **"Each one has a hymn, a lesson, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation" (v26).** A rare glimpse into an early Christian meeting. There was no single preacher doing everything. Many people contributed what God had given them. "Hymn" is literally "psalm," which may be a psalm of Scripture or a new song. The rule over all of it: "Let all things be done for building up."
- **"Two or at most three, and each in turn, and let someone interpret" (v27).** Three limits on tongues in church: a cap on the number, one at a time, and interpretation required.
- **"Keep silent in church and speak to himself and to God" (v28).** If no interpreter is present, the gift is still real but is to be used quietly, in private prayer. A speaker can choose to stay silent, which shows the gift is not an uncontrollable seizure.
- **"Let two or three prophets speak, and let the others weigh what is said" (v29).** "Weigh" is *diakrinō*, to evaluate or tell apart, the same word as "discerning" in 11:29 and related to "distinguishing between spirits" in 12:10. New Testament prophecy was not beyond question. It was to be tested against Scripture and the apostles' teaching (1 Thess 5:20–21; 1 John 4:1). This is one reason many scholars see church prophecy as different in kind from Old Testament prophecy or Scripture itself.
- **"If a revelation is made to another… let the first be silent" (v30).** Courtesy in the Spirit: no one should hog the floor.
- **"The spirits of prophets are subject to prophets" (v32).** This is a key line. Genuine prophecy does not take a person over. The prophet stays in control and can wait his turn. That was a sharp contrast with pagan oracles, like the priestess at Delphi, who were thought to be seized by the god in a frenzy.
- **"God is not a God of confusion but of peace" (v33).** The reason behind the rules. Disorder in worship misrepresents God. The Spirit who brought order out of chaos at creation (Gen 1:2) does not bring chaos into the church.
- **"As in all the churches of the saints" (v33).** Translations differ on whether this phrase ends v33 or begins v34. The ESV puts it with v34, making the instruction about women a practice shared by all churches. Others attach it to "God is not a God of confusion."
- **"The women should keep silent in the churches" (v34).** This is a difficult passage, and faithful Christians read it in several ways. The plain difficulty is 11:5, where Paul assumes women pray and prophesy in church. So "silent" cannot mean total silence. The main views:
- **Judging prophecy.** The silence applies to the weighing of prophecies in v29. Women were not to take part in publicly judging prophets, especially their own husbands, since that was a work of church authority. Many complementarian scholars hold this view.
- **Disruptive questions.** Verse 35 points to asking questions: "If there is anything they desire to learn, let them ask their husbands at home." Many women of the time had little education, and questions called out during the meeting were disrupting worship. Paul tells them to learn at home instead.
- **A general rule of order.** Some read it as a broader limit on women speaking with authority in the gathered church, alongside 1 Timothy 2:11–12, while still allowing the praying and prophesying of chapter 11.
- **A later addition.** In a handful of Western manuscripts, vv34–35 appear after v40 instead of here. A few scholars, notably Gordon Fee, argue the verses were a marginal note later copied into the text. Every known manuscript includes them, though, and most scholars hold they are original and that the move was a copyist's attempt to tidy the flow.
- **"As the Law also says" (v34).** No Old Testament verse commands women to be silent. Paul probably means the creation order of Genesis 2 (as in 11:8–9), or perhaps Genesis 3:16, as the Tyndale notes and JFB suggest.
- **"Or was it from you that the word of God came?" (v36).** Sharp irony. The Corinthians acted as if the gospel had started with them, or reached only them. They were not free to invent their own practices apart from the other churches (11:16).
- **"A command of the Lord" (v37).** Anyone claiming to be a prophet or "spiritual" (*pneumatikos*, the Corinthians' favorite word) will prove it by recognizing Paul's apostolic authority. True spiritual gifts do not compete with the word of Christ's apostle. They submit to it.
- **"If anyone does not recognize this, he is not recognized" (v38).** A play on words. The one who refuses to acknowledge Paul's teaching is not acknowledged, either by the church as a true prophet or, more seriously, by God (8:3). Some manuscripts read "let him be ignorant," as in the King James.
- **"Earnestly desire to prophesy, and do not forbid speaking in tongues" (v39).** Paul's summary of three chapters. Seek the gift that builds others up most. Do not ban the one that has been misused.
- **"All things should be done decently and in order" (v40).** "Decently" (*euschēmonōs*) means fittingly, in a becoming way, the opposite of "rude" in 13:5. "In order" (*kata taxin*) was used of soldiers in formation. The aim is not stiff formality but worship that fits a God of peace.
- **Commentators:** On order in worship, all three agree. Henry: "God is not the God of confusion, but of peace and good order," and disorders "are to be carefully kept out of all Christian churches." JFB quotes John Newton on v40: "Church government is the best security for Christian liberty." Guzik stresses that v32 rules out wild, uncontrolled behavior as a sign of the Spirit, and that order in worship should be lively, not dead. They differ on vv34–35. Henry reads them as forbidding women to teach in the assembly: they must "not set up for teachers." JFB reads public speech by women as a show of independence from their husbands, contrary to 11:3. Guzik holds that chapter 11 assumes women may pray and prophesy, and takes the silence to concern the judging of prophecy or disruptive talk. He cites a claim that the verb *laleō* here means chatter. The lexicon notes that sense in classical Greek, but in the New Testament *laleō* is the ordinary word for "speak," and it is the same verb Paul uses for speaking in tongues and prophesying throughout this chapter. So the word itself cannot settle the question. None of the three discusses the manuscripts that move vv34–35.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's purpose in giving gifts is that outsiders will fall on their faces and say, "God is really among you." That was always the promise of Immanuel, "God with us" (Matt 1:23). Jesus himself spoke so that people understood, and his words searched hearts: "Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did" (John 4:29). He is the Word made flesh, God speaking plainly and finally (Heb 1:1–2), and the church's words build up only as they point to him. He is also the God of peace, not confusion, who calmed the storm with a word (Mark 4:39).
