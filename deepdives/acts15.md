*Setting:* About AD 49, between Paul's first and second missionary journeys. Paul and Barnabas are back in Syrian Antioch, the mostly Gentile church that sent them out (13:1–3). They have just reported that God "had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles" (14:27) across Cyprus and southern Galatia. That open door raises the question this chapter settles: must Gentiles become Jews, by circumcision and keeping the law of Moses, to belong to God's people? Many scholars think Paul wrote Galatians around this time against the same teaching, and most identify his account in Galatians 2:1–10 with this visit to Jerusalem, though some link it to the earlier famine visit (11:30). Luke places the council at the center of Acts. Everything before it builds toward a church of Jews and Gentiles, and everything after it carries that church to Rome.

#### The Jerusalem Council (vv1–21)
- **"Some men came down from Judea" (v1).** They came from the mother church, which gave their teaching weight. Paul calls such men "false brothers secretly brought in" who wanted to bring believers into slavery (Gal 2:4). Later the council's letter says plainly that they came "although we gave them no instructions" (v24).
- **"Unless you are circumcised… you cannot be saved" (v1).** This was not a question of custom or preference. It was a question of salvation. Circumcision was the sign of the covenant with Abraham (Gen 17:10–14), and Gentiles who joined Israel had always been circumcised (Exod 12:48). So the demand had real Old Testament roots. The question was whether Christ had changed what makes a person part of God's people.
- **"No small dissension and debate" (v2).** Luke's understatement. "Dissension" is *stasis*, a word also used for a riot or uprising (19:40). Paul and Barnabas did not treat this as a minor disagreement to be tolerated.
- **"Appointed to go up to Jerusalem" (v2).** Antioch did not split off on its own. It sent the question to the apostles and elders, so that one church would give one answer. Paul says he went "because of a revelation" and took Titus, an uncircumcised Greek, along as a test case (Gal 2:1–3).
- **Through Phoenicia and Samaria (v3).** The road south ran past churches planted after Stephen's death (8:1–25; 11:19). These were mixed and outsider churches, and they heard of Gentile conversions with "great joy." The joy on the road contrasts with the objection waiting in Jerusalem.
- **"Believers who belonged to the party of the Pharisees" (v5).** Luke calls them believers. They had trusted Jesus as Messiah, and Paul himself had been a Pharisee (23:6; Phil 3:5). Pharisees were known for careful law-keeping, and to them it seemed obvious that Gentile converts must "keep the law of Moses." "It is necessary" is *dei*, the same word Luke uses for God's plan ("the Christ must suffer," 17:3). They believed they were defending God's will.
- **"Much debate" (vv6–7).** The leaders did not rush. They let the argument be heard before anyone ruled.
- **Peter's speech (vv7–11).** This is Peter's last appearance in Acts. He goes back about ten years, to Cornelius (ch 10). God chose him to bring the gospel to Gentiles, and God, "who knows the heart" (v8), gave them the Spirit "just as he did to us" before anyone asked about circumcision (10:44–48; 11:15–17). The Spirit's coming settled who belonged.
- **"Having cleansed their hearts by faith" (v9).** This is the heart of Peter's case. Circumcision was a mark in the flesh, and the food laws kept Jews ritually clean. But God had already done the deeper cleansing inside, by faith. Peter's vision had said, "What God has made clean, do not call common" (10:15).
- **"Why are you putting God to the test" (v10).** The phrase echoes Israel testing God in the wilderness (Exod 17:2; Deut 6:16). To demand more than God demands is to challenge his verdict.
- **"A yoke… that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear" (v10).** Jewish teachers spoke gladly of "the yoke of the Torah," so Peter's words are startling. He does not mean the law was evil (Paul calls it "holy," Rom 7:12). He means no one ever kept it well enough to be saved by it. Jesus offered a different yoke: "my yoke is easy" (Matt 11:29–30).
- **"We will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will" (v11).** Notice the order. Peter does not say Gentiles are saved the way Jews are. He says Jews are saved the way Gentiles are: by grace alone.
- **"All the assembly fell silent" (v12).** Barnabas is named first here, as in Jerusalem he was the better known and trusted man (9:27; 11:22). They do not argue theology. They tell "what signs and wonders God had done." The question is what God has already shown.
- **James (v13).** This is James the brother of Jesus, not James the apostle, who had been killed (12:2). He became a believer after the resurrection (1 Cor 15:7) and now leads the Jerusalem church (12:17; Gal 2:9). He was famous for strict piety and was later called "James the Just." If anyone could reassure the Pharisee believers, it was him.
- **"Simeon has related" (v14).** James uses the old Hebrew form of Peter's name, *Symeōn*, a homey touch from a Jerusalem Jew.
- **"To take from them a people for his name" (v14).** In the Greek Old Testament, *laos* ("a people") is the regular word for Israel, set against *ethnē*, "the nations." James joins the two: God is taking a *laos* out of the *ethnē*. "Visited" (*episkeptomai*) is the word used when God came to rescue Israel from Egypt (Exod 4:31).
- **Amos 9:11–12 (vv16–18).** "The tent of David that has fallen" pictures David's royal house in ruins. Amos promised God would rebuild it, and James says that rebuilding has begun in the risen Son of David. Its purpose is that "all the Gentiles who are called by my name" may seek the Lord. Gentiles are called by God's name *as Gentiles*, without being told to become Jews.
- **The wording of Amos.** James quotes the Greek Septuagint. The Hebrew of Amos reads "that they may possess the remnant of Edom." The Greek translators read the same consonants as "that the remnant of mankind may seek." In Hebrew, "Edom" and "mankind" (*adam*) look almost alike, and so do "possess" and "seek." Both forms lead to the same place: the nations will belong to the restored kingdom of David. James says "the words of the prophets agree" (plural), and passages such as Isaiah 2:2–3 and Zechariah 2:11 say the same.
- **"My judgment is" (v19).** The verb *krinō* can mean "I decide." James gives his ruling, and the whole assembly then agrees (v22). He sums it up: "we should not trouble those of the Gentiles who turn to God." The verb means "to annoy" or "add difficulty." The gospel will not be weighed down with conditions God never set.
- **The four requests (v20).** Abstain from "the things polluted by idols, and from sexual immorality, and from what has been strangled, and from blood." Faithful readers have understood the list in three main ways. (1) Many scholars note that these match the laws in Leviticus 17–18 that applied to foreigners living among Israel: no idol sacrifices, no eating blood, no improper sexual unions. (2) Others see them as courtesies so Jewish and Gentile believers could eat at one table. Idol meat, strangled meat with the blood still in it, and blood itself would revolt a Jewish brother. (3) Some link them to the commands given to Noah, the father of all nations (Gen 9:4). All three agree on the main point: these are not conditions of salvation. Salvation was settled in v11.
- **"Sexual immorality" (v20).** The word is *porneia*, a broad word for sexual sin. It may be on the list because it was so normal in pagan religion and society. Some think it means the forbidden marriages of Leviticus 18 in particular, like marrying a close relative. Either way it is not a mere food custom, and the New Testament forbids it everywhere (1 Cor 6:18; 1 Thess 4:3).
- **"Moses has had in every city those who proclaim him" (v21).** This sentence is hard. The likeliest reading is that Jews in every city hear Moses read each Sabbath, so Gentile believers living near them should respect what Moses taught. Another reading is that the law is in no danger of being forgotten, so the church need not impose it.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the Judaizers' threat as typical of "proud impostors" who "enforce their own inventions under pain of damnation." On v11 he writes, "There is not one way of salvation for the Jews and another for the Gentiles." Guzik also notes that Peter reverses the expected order, saving Jews in the same way as Gentiles. Henry splits the four requests: idolatry and fornication are "always to be abstained from," while blood and things strangled were "not evil in themselves." JFB treats the whole list as "concessions to Jewish feelings," and calls fornication "the characteristic sin of heathendom." Guzik, drawing on Marshall, makes the *laos*/*ethnē* point, and he reads "sexual immorality" as the Leviticus 18 marriage laws. JFB identifies the visit with Galatians 2:1–10 and reads the yoke as "an obligation," not just heavy ritual.

#### The Council's Letter to Gentile Believers (vv22–35)
- **"With the whole church" (v22).** The apostles and elders led, but the whole congregation agreed. The decision was not forced on a divided church.
- **Judas called Barsabbas and Silas (v22).** They are "leading men among the brothers," Jerusalem believers trusted by both sides. A letter could be doubted. Respected Jewish Christians who said the same thing in person could not. Judas is otherwise unknown. Some guess he was related to Joseph Barsabbas (1:23), but nothing confirms it. Silas (Latin *Silvanus*) becomes Paul's partner. He was a Roman citizen (16:37), helped write 1 and 2 Thessalonians (1 Thess 1:1), and later carried 1 Peter (1 Pet 5:12).
- **"To the brothers who are of the Gentiles in Antioch and Syria and Cilicia" (v23).** The letter addresses the churches where the trouble started. Paul later carried the decisions to the Galatian churches as well (16:4). The letter opens and closes in the usual Greek style ("greetings… Farewell"), the only New Testament letter that ends with that plain sign-off.
- **"Unsettling your minds" (v24).** The verb *anaskeuazō* literally meant "to pack up the baggage," and so "to break up camp" or "dismantle." Here it means to unsettle or subvert. The false teachers had torn down what had been built. The council also disowns them: "we gave them no instructions."
- **"Our beloved Barnabas and Paul, men who have risked their lives" (vv25–26).** Paul had just been stoned and left for dead at Lystra (14:19). The Jerusalem church honors the men the Judaizers had been undermining. In this letter Barnabas is again named first.
- **"It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (v28).** The church does not claim the decision as its own idea. It came from Scripture, from what the Spirit had done among the Gentiles, and from the leaders coming "to one accord" (v25). The Spirit is named first.
- **"No greater burden than these requirements" (v28).** "Burden" picks up Peter's "yoke" (v10). The list in v29 gives the same four items as v20, in a different order.
- **"If you keep yourselves from these, you will do well" (v29).** The tone is warm, not legal. It is not "or you will not be saved."
- **A different text.** Some manuscripts, mainly the Western text (Codex Bezae), drop "what has been strangled" and add "and not to do to others what they would not want done to themselves." That turns the list into purely moral rules. Most scholars think it is a later change made once the food issue had faded.
- **"They rejoiced because of its encouragement" (v31).** "Encouragement" is *paraklēsis*, comfort and strengthening. The Gentile believers were not shamed into Judaism. They were assured they belonged.
- **"Judas and Silas, who were themselves prophets" (v32).** In Acts, prophets build up the church with words from God (11:27; 13:1). "Encouraged" is the verb form of the same word in v31.
- **Verse 34 is missing.** The ESV jumps from v33 to v35. Some later manuscripts add "But it seemed good to Silas to remain there," and the King James Version includes it. The oldest manuscripts lack it. It was probably added to explain how Silas was in Antioch for Paul to choose in v40. Silas may have gone home and come back, or the line may be accurate tradition.
- **"Teaching and preaching the word of the Lord, with many others also" (v35).** Antioch had many teachers (13:1). Very probably in this period Peter came to Antioch and withdrew from eating with Gentiles under pressure from "certain men… from James," and Paul rebuked him to his face (Gal 2:11–14). Most scholars place that clash just after the council. It shows how hard it was to live out what had been agreed.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the strong language of v24 shows "indignation" at an unauthorized group trying to bring the church under "legal bondage." On v28 it sees a principle "for the Church in all time": the Spirit guiding, the church confirming. JFB also says the evidence against v34 is "strong" and suggests it was added to explain v40. Henry calls the letter a guide for Gentile converts on "how to govern themselves with respect to Jews," which fits the table-fellowship reading. Guzik stresses that the letter's limited audience did not make the decision narrow: the question of salvation by grace apart from the law was settled for good.

#### Paul and Barnabas Separate (vv36–41)
- **"Let us return and visit the brothers… and see how they are" (v36).** Paul's first concern is pastoral: to strengthen the churches he planted, not first to break new ground.
- **"Barnabas wanted to take with them John called Mark" (v37).** Mark was Barnabas's cousin (Col 4:10). His mother's house was a meeting place for the Jerusalem church (12:12). He had come along on the first journey but left at Perga in Pamphylia and went home (13:13).
- **"One who had withdrawn from them" (v38).** The verb *aphistēmi* means to withdraw or fall away. Paul saw Mark's leaving as a failure of commitment. The work ahead was dangerous, and Paul had been stoned on the last trip. He wanted men who would stay.
- **"A sharp disagreement" (v39).** The word is *paroxysmos*, the root of English "paroxysm." Its basic sense is "stirring up" or "irritation." The only other New Testament use is Hebrews 10:24, where believers "stir up one another to love." The same energy can provoke love or provoke a quarrel. Luke does not hide that two good men lost their tempers.
- **Who was right?** Luke does not say. Paul and Silas are "commended by the brothers to the grace of the Lord" (v40), which may suggest the church sided with Paul, but Luke says nothing like it of Barnabas. Barnabas, whose name means "son of encouragement" (4:36), had once taken a risk on Saul when no one else would (9:27). Now he took the same risk on Mark. Many think both were partly right: Paul about the work, Barnabas about the man.
- **Two teams instead of one (vv39–40).** Barnabas took Mark to Cyprus, his home island (4:36), and Paul took Silas north overland. The quarrel doubled the mission. That does not make the quarrel good, but God worked through it. This is Barnabas's last appearance in Acts. Paul later mentions him warmly (1 Cor 9:6).
- **The end of the story.** Mark later appears with Paul in Rome (Col 4:10; Philem 24), and at the very end Paul writes, "Get Mark… for he is very useful to me for ministry" (2 Tim 4:11). Tradition holds that Mark worked with Peter and wrote the second Gospel. The man Paul would not take turned out to be the one he asked for at the last.
- **"Through Syria and Cilicia, strengthening the churches" (v41).** Cilicia was Paul's home region (Tarsus). He had worked there for years before Barnabas fetched him to Antioch (9:30; 11:25–26; Gal 1:21). The second journey begins with strengthening, not new planting.
- **Commentators:** Henry names the sharpness "the paroxysm (so the word is)" and says the men "were certainly both in fault." He thinks Barnabas let family feeling count too much, and Paul was too severe toward Mark. His lesson: "charity teaches us to be candid in our reflections upon others." JFB says each of these servants of Christ "had something to say for himself" and puts the sharpness down to human weakness. Guzik will not let the split be excused as simply God's plan, and he holds that both men could not have been walking in the Spirit at the moment of the quarrel. Like the others, he points out that God still worked in both of them and later restored Mark.

#### Pointing to Christ
The council's answer comes down to one sentence: "we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will" (v11). The fallen tent of David is being rebuilt in Jesus, the risen Son of David, and its doors open to every nation without a yoke no one could bear. The chapter ends with two good men falling out and a young man who failed being given another chance. That is the same grace at ground level. Christ saves Jews and Gentiles alike by grace, and he does not throw away people like Mark.
