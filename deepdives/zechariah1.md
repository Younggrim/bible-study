*Setting:* Zechariah preached in Jerusalem at the same time as Haggai, in 520–518 BC, to the small community that had returned from exile in Babylon. Like Haggai, he was sent to get the temple rebuilt (Ezra 5:1–2; 6:14). Haggai's first sermon in August 520 had got the people working again (Hag 1). Zechariah's first message came two months later, in October–November 520, between Haggai's second and third messages. Zechariah was a priest as well as a prophet (Neh 12:4, 16), and perhaps young (the "young man" of 2:4 may be Zechariah himself). His name means "the LORD remembers." His book is the longest of the Minor Prophets and one of the most visionary. Chapters 1–6 contain eight night visions given to him in a single night in February 519 BC, each with an angel to explain it. They are full of symbols that can seem strange at first, but their message is simple: God has not forgotten Jerusalem, he will judge the nations that crushed it, and he will rebuild his house and dwell among his people. Chapter 1 holds the opening call to repentance and the first two visions. Many later prophecies of the Messiah come from this book. The New Testament quotes or alludes to Zechariah more than to almost any other prophet, above all in the Gospel passion accounts and in Revelation.

#### A Call to Return to the Lord (vv1–6)
- **"In the eighth month, in the second year of Darius" (v1).** That is October–November 520 BC. The people had been building for about two months (Hag 1:15). Haggai's message of the coming glory of the temple (Hag 2:1–9) had come only a few weeks before.
- **"Zechariah, the son of Berechiah, son of Iddo" (v1).** Ezra calls him "the son of Iddo" (Ezra 5:1; 6:14), using "son" in the sense of descendant. Iddo was his grandfather and head of a priestly family that came home with Zerubbabel (Neh 12:4). Berechiah may have died young, so Zechariah succeeded his grandfather as head of the family (Neh 12:16). The New Testament mentions "Zechariah the son of Barachiah, whom you murdered between the sanctuary and the altar" (Matt 23:35). Some think Jesus meant this prophet, but most think he meant the earlier Zechariah of 2 Chronicles 24:20–22, whose murder in the temple court comes near the end of the Hebrew Bible's order of books.
- **"The LORD was very angry with your fathers" (v2).** The Hebrew doubles the idea: "angry with anger." The exile was not bad luck or simply Babylonian power. It was God's judgment on generations of unfaithfulness. The ruins all around them were the proof.
- **"Return to me… and I will return to you" (v3).** This is the heart of the opening message. The Hebrew verb *shub*, "turn" or "return," was the great word of the earlier prophets for repentance (Jer 3:12–14; Hos 14:1; Joel 2:12–13). Malachi will use the same words a century later (Mal 3:7), and James echoes them: "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you" (Jas 4:8). The people had returned to the land, but that was not the same as returning to God. "LORD of hosts" appears three times in this one verse, stressing that the God who calls them back commands the armies of heaven.
- **"Do not be like your fathers, to whom the former prophets cried out" (v4).** "The former prophets" were the prophets before the exile, such as Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea and Amos. Their message was "Return from your evil ways" (Jer 25:4–5; 35:15; 2 Kgs 17:13), and the people would not listen. Zechariah is the first prophet to look back on the earlier prophets as a group whose words had already been tested by history.
- **"Your fathers, where are they? And the prophets, do they live forever?" (v5).** Both the sinners and the preachers are gone. The generation that ignored the warnings had died, many by sword, famine and exile, and the prophets who warned them had died too.
- **"But my words and my statutes… did they not overtake your fathers?" (v6).** The words outlived the men. The verb "overtake" (*nasag*) is the one used in Deuteronomy's covenant curses: "all these curses shall come upon you and overtake you" (Deut 28:15, 45). God's word pursued and caught them, like a hunter running down his prey.
- **"So they repented and said, 'As the LORD of hosts purposed… so has he dealt with us'" (v6).** This could mean the fathers in exile finally admitted God was just (as in Lam 2:17 and Dan 9:11–14), or that the present generation had responded to Zechariah's preaching and confessed it. Either way, the confession is the model: God did exactly what he said.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up v3: "Let them change their way, and God will change his." On v5 he notes that the prophets were dying men and draws the lesson that only Christ, the prophet who lives forever, never passes away. Of v6 he writes that God's words "took hold of them as the bailiff arrests the debtor." JFB likewise explains "overtake" as a foe catching someone who flees. Guzik observes that the people felt God was far away because of their hardships, but the distance was on their side, not his. He also notes that these returnees were not especially wicked people, and still they needed the warning.

#### A Vision of a Horseman (vv7–17)
- **"On the twenty-fourth day of the eleventh month, which is the month of Shebat" (v7).** That is February 15, 519 BC, exactly five months after building resumed (Hag 1:15) and two months after Haggai's last messages (Hag 2:10, 20). Shebat is a Babylonian month name, one of several the Jews adopted in exile. All eight visions in chapters 1–6 came that one night.
- **"I saw in the night, and behold, a man riding on a red horse!" (v8).** The vision is set at night, a fitting picture of Jerusalem's situation: dark, uncertain, waiting for dawn. The rider is later called "the angel of the LORD" (v11).
- **"Standing among the myrtle trees in the glen" (v8).** Myrtles are low evergreen shrubs with fragrant leaves that grew in the valleys around Jerusalem, especially near water. They were used for the booths at the Feast of Tabernacles (Neh 8:15). Isaiah promised that in the restoration "instead of the brier shall come up the myrtle" (Isa 55:13). "The glen" or "the deep place" suggests a hollow, low and shadowed. Many readers, including Henry and JFB, take the low myrtles in the hollow as a picture of God's people, humble and hidden but still green. The text itself does not explain the myrtles, so this is an interpretation, though a natural one. The point at least is that the Lord's messenger stands among them.
- **"Red, sorrel, and white horses" (v8).** "Sorrel" is reddish-brown. Commentators have tried to assign meanings to the colors, such as war, mixed fortunes and victory. The angel does not explain them, and most modern interpreters think they are not the main point. The horses are like a king's mounted scouts, sent to report on the state of the empire. Persian kings were famous for their network of mounted couriers, and the picture would have been familiar.
- **"What are these, my lord?" (v9).** Two figures guide Zechariah in the visions: "the angel who talked with me," the interpreting angel, and "the angel of the LORD." The interpreting angel explains the visions. The angel of the LORD speaks with God's authority.
- **"These are they whom the LORD has sent to patrol the earth" (v10).** "Patrol" is literally "walk about," the same verb used when Satan says he has been "going to and fro on the earth, and… walking up and down on it" (Job 1:7). God's scouts know the state of the world. Nothing escapes his notice.
- **"All the earth remains at rest" (v11).** This sounds like good news, but it is not. Darius had crushed the rebellions of 522–521 BC, and the Persian Empire was now quiet and secure. Haggai had promised that God would "shake the heavens and the earth" and overthrow kingdoms (Hag 2:6–7, 21–22), and there was no sign of it. The nations were comfortable, and Jerusalem was still a ruin. The "rest" of the nations is the problem.
- **"O LORD of hosts, how long will you have no mercy on Jerusalem?" (v12).** The angel of the LORD intercedes for Jerusalem. This figure, who speaks for God and yet prays to God, appears often in the Old Testament (Gen 16:7–13; Exod 3:2–6; Judg 13:21–22). Many Christian interpreters, including Henry, JFB and Guzik, see him as the Son of God before his incarnation, interceding for his people as he does now (Heb 7:25). Others see him as a high angel bearing God's name. Both views honor the text, but the angel's divine authority is plain throughout these visions.
- **"These seventy years" (v12).** Jeremiah had prophesied seventy years of Babylonian rule and exile (Jer 25:11–12; 29:10). Counting from the destruction of the temple in 586 BC, nearly seventy years had passed by 519. Counting from the first deportation in 605, the years were already more than complete. Either way, the angel's prayer leans on God's own promised time limit, as Daniel's prayer did (Dan 9:2–3).
- **"Gracious and comforting words" (v13).** God's answer begins with tone before content: literally "good words, comforting words." The Hebrew word for comfort (*nichumim*, from *nacham*) echoes Isaiah's great promise, "Comfort, comfort my people" (Isa 40:1).
- **"I am exceedingly jealous for Jerusalem and for Zion" (v14).** The Hebrew doubles the idea again: "jealous with great jealousy." *Qanah* means jealous or zealous, the passion of a husband for his wife (Num 5:14) or of God for his own name and people (Joel 2:18; Ezek 39:25). It is not petty envy but fierce, protective love. Some older writers trace the word to a root meaning "to be red," as a face flushes with strong emotion. The standard Hebrew lexicon does not support that origin, but the picture of intense feeling is right.
- **"Angry with the nations that are at ease; for while I was angry but a little, they furthered the disaster" (v15).** God used Babylon and others to discipline Judah (Jer 25:9; Isa 10:5–6). But they went far beyond what he intended, with cruelty and pride (Isa 47:6; Obad 10–14; Ps 137:7–9). God's "little" anger with his people was discipline. His "great" anger with the nations will be judgment.
- **"I have returned to Jerusalem with mercy; my house shall be built in it" (v16).** This answers v3 directly. God called the people to return to him, and now he says that he has returned to them. Ezekiel had seen God's glory leave the temple and the city (Ezek 10:18–19; 11:23). Now God says he has come back. The temple would be finished, as it was four years later (Ezra 6:15).
- **"The measuring line shall be stretched out over Jerusalem" (v16).** A builder's measuring line marked out a site before construction (Jer 31:38–39). The image prepares for the third vision, in which a man goes out with a measuring line (2:1–2).
- **"My cities shall again overflow with prosperity… comfort Zion… again choose Jerusalem" (v17).** "Again" occurs four times in the Hebrew of this verse. Everything that was lost will be restored. "Choose" recalls God's original choice of Jerusalem as the place for his name (Deut 12:5; 1 Kgs 11:13; Ps 132:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry identifies the rider among the myrtles as "no other than the man Christ Jesus." He reads the myrtles in the hollow as "the low, dark, solitary, melancholy condition of the Jewish church at this time," and says "God's good words will feed the faith" of his people. JFB also identifies the rider as the second person of the Trinity and calls the myrtles a "symbol of the Jewish Church," lowly but fragrant. He takes the red horse as bloodshed, the white as triumph and the sorrel as a mixed state. Guzik identifies the rider with the angel of the LORD and the pre-incarnate Christ. He reads the horses as observers rather than riders of judgment and explains that the world's rest was not God's kind of rest. He says the Hebrew word for "jealous" pictures a face flushed with emotion, which the lexicon does not confirm.

#### A Vision of Horns and Craftsmen (vv18–21)
- **"I lifted my eyes and saw, and behold, four horns!" (v18).** In the Hebrew Bible this is the start of chapter 2, so the chapter numbers differ by four verses between Hebrew and English Bibles. Horns stood for an animal's strength and so, by extension, for kings and nations (Ps 75:4–5, 10; Dan 7:7–8, 24; 8:3–8). Four horns may mean four specific powers or the hostile powers from all four directions of the compass (compare 2:6, "the four winds of the heavens").
- **"The horns that have scattered Judah, Israel, and Jerusalem" (v19).** The past tense points to nations that had already scattered God's people: Assyria, which took the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC, Babylon, which took Judah, and others such as Egypt and Edom. Many older commentators instead read the four as the four world empires of Daniel 2 and 7 (Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome). But Greece and Rome had not yet scattered anyone, so most modern interpreters take the number four as a symbol of all hostile nations everywhere.
- **"Then the LORD showed me four craftsmen" (v20).** The Hebrew word (*charash*) means a skilled worker in metal, wood or stone. If the horns are pictured as iron (compare 1 Kgs 22:11, where a prophet made "horns of iron"), these are smiths with hammers. For every horn there is a craftsman. God has a counter-power ready for every enemy of his people.
- **"These have come to terrify them, to cast down the horns of the nations" (v21).** The nations had raised their horns so that "no one raised his head" in Judah. Now God's workmen frighten them and throw their power down. The word "terrify" means to make them tremble. The same God who used the nations to discipline Judah would break them for their cruelty (v15). The vision does not name the craftsmen. History supplied some: Persia overthrew Babylon, Greece overthrew Persia and so on. The point is that God rules the rise and fall of empires.
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline describes the vision as "the four carpenters that were employed to cut off the four horns" that scattered Judah. JFB reads the horns as the four world empires and the carpenters as God's counter-forces, one for each: "For every enemy of God's people, God has provided a counteracting power." Guzik also leans toward the four empires of Daniel in a broad prophetic sense. He borrows an image from Spurgeon of the craftsmen as rough, strong workmen, not refined gentlemen, fit for breaking hard horns.

#### Pointing to Christ
The first vision shows a figure standing in the hollow among the lowly myrtles, interceding with God for his weak and discouraged people, "how long will you have no mercy?", and receiving "gracious and comforting words." Whether or not the angel of the LORD is the Son of God before the incarnation, the picture fits Jesus exactly. He came down into the low places among his people, "a man of sorrows" (Isa 53:3), and he "always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25). God's answer, "I have returned to Jerusalem with mercy," was most fully kept when God himself came to Jerusalem in the person of his Son, and Zechariah will later describe that arrival: "Behold, your king is coming to you; righteous and having salvation is he, humble and mounted on a donkey" (Zech 9:9; Matt 21:5). The call of v3 still stands for all who hear it. "Return to me, and I will return to you" is the gospel invitation in Old Testament form, and the father running to meet his returning son is its picture (Luke 15:20).
