*Setting:* Jeremiah's oracles against the nations (chs 46–51) end with the longest and fiercest of them all: two chapters against Babylon, the empire that destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC. The note in 51:59–64 dates at least part of this collection to 594/593 BC, the fourth year of King Zedekiah, years before the city fell and while Babylon was at the height of its power under Nebuchadnezzar. That timing matters. For two decades Jeremiah had told Judah to submit to Babylon as God's servant (25:9; 27:6), and had been called a traitor for it. Here the same prophet announces that Babylon's turn is coming. God used the empire as his rod, but the rod is not innocent, and its pride and cruelty will be judged. The chapter is not a tidy narrative but a series of poems and prose oracles, woven together. Scenes of Babylon's fall alternate with scenes of Israel's return, so that every blow against the oppressor becomes good news for the oppressed. The fulfilment began in 539 BC, when Cyrus the Persian, at the head of an army that included the Medes, took Babylon without a long siege, and the next year he allowed the exiles to go home (Ezra 1:1–4).

#### Judgment on Babylon (vv1–46)
- **"Concerning Babylon, concerning the land of the Chaldeans" (v1).** The Chaldeans were a people of southern Mesopotamia whose dynasty, founded by Nabopolassar in 626 BC, made Babylon the capital of a great empire. Nebuchadnezzar, his son, reigned 605–562 BC and is the king who took Jerusalem.
- **"Set up a banner and proclaim, conceal it not" (v2).** The prophet speaks as if the news were already out. A banner on a hilltop was the ancient way to rally troops or spread an alarm. The verbs are in the Hebrew perfect, the "prophetic perfect": what God has decided is announced as already done.
- **"Bel is put to shame, Merodach is dismayed" (v2).** Bel means "lord," the Babylonian equivalent of Baal. By Jeremiah's day it had become a title of **Merodach**, the Hebrew form of Marduk, the patron god of Babylon. So the two names in this line probably point to the same god, heaped together for effect. Isaiah had said the same thing: "Bel bows down; Nebo stoops" (Isa 46:1). A war between empires was also believed to be a war between their gods. When Babylon falls, its god is shown up as nothing.
- **"Her idols are dismayed" (v2).** The word for idols here is *gillulim*, a contemptuous term Ezekiel uses about forty times. Many interpreters hear a play on a word for dung pellets, though the lexicon lists that as one guess among others (it may mean "logs" or "blocks"). Either way, it is a word of scorn, not respect.
- **"Out of the north a nation has come up against her" (v3).** All through Jeremiah, disaster for Judah came "from the north," meaning Babylon (1:14; 6:22). Now the direction is turned back on Babylon itself. The Medes lived north and east of Babylon, and the Persian armies came down on the city from the north. The words "none shall dwell in it" describe the end result, which came slowly, over centuries, rather than in 539 BC (see v39).
- **"Weeping as they come, and they shall seek the LORD their God" (v4).** The first promise for Israel comes right after the first blow against Babylon. **"The people of Israel and the people of Judah shall come together"**: the northern and southern kingdoms, divided since about 930 BC, return as one people. These are tears of repentance and joy together, not the tears of the forced march into exile (compare 31:9).
- **"They shall ask the way to Zion, with faces turned toward it" (v5).** A beautiful picture of pilgrims who don't know the road but know where they are going. **"Come, let us join ourselves to the LORD in an everlasting covenant that will never be forgotten."** This is the new covenant language of 31:31–34 and 32:40. The old covenant was broken and forgotten. This one God himself will keep from being forgotten.
- **"My people have been lost sheep. Their shepherds have led them astray" (v6).** In Jeremiah the "shepherds" are Judah's kings, priests and false prophets (23:1–4). **"Turning them away on the mountains"** probably also hints at the hilltop shrines where Baal was worshiped (2:20; 3:23). **"They have forgotten their fold."** Their true resting place was the LORD himself.
- **"We are not guilty, for they have sinned against the LORD" (v7).** The enemies justify their cruelty by pointing to Israel's sin. They are right that Israel sinned, and even right about the LORD being "the habitation of righteousness" and "the hope of their fathers." But knowing that God disciplined his people did not give them license to devour them. The phrase "habitation of righteousness" was used of Jerusalem in 31:23. Here it is the LORD himself who is the home his people left.
- **"Flee from the midst of Babylon… and be as male goats before the flock" (v8).** The male goats push to the front when a gate is opened. The exiles are to lead the way out, not hang back. Isaiah gives the same call: "Go out from Babylon, flee from Chaldea" (Isa 48:20). When Cyrus's decree came, most exiles actually chose to stay, and only about fifty thousand returned in the first wave (Ezra 2:64–65).
- **"A gathering of great nations, from the north country" (v9).** The Persian army was a coalition. Jeremiah later names "the kings of the Medes" (51:11, 28) along with northern peoples, Ararat, Minni and Ashkenaz (51:27). **"Like a skilled warrior who does not return empty-handed"**: the arrows don't miss.
- **"O plunderers of my heritage" (v11).** The LORD calls Israel "my heritage." The Babylonians had danced over Judah's ruin **"like a heifer in the pasture"** and **"neigh like stallions,"** pictures of well-fed animals frisking without a care.
- **"Your mother shall be utterly shamed… she shall be the last of the nations" (v12).** The "mother" is the city of Babylon or the land of Chaldea that "bore" its people. The once-first of nations becomes the last.
- **"Everyone who passes by Babylon shall be appalled, and hiss" (v13).** The same words were used of Jerusalem (19:8) and of Edom (49:17). Babylon will receive the verdict it once brought on others.
- **"For this is the vengeance of the LORD… do to her as she has done" (v15).** This is the chapter's core principle of justice, repeated in v29. In the Bible, God's "vengeance" is not a fit of temper but the judge's settled answer to wrong, which is why human beings are told to leave it to him (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19). **"She has surrendered"** is literally "she has given her hand," the gesture of yielding. Babylon did in fact open its gates in 539 BC with little fighting.
- **"Cut off from Babylon the sower" (v16).** Babylonia's fields were famous for their yields, watered by canals from the Euphrates and Tigris. Without farmers the land dies. **"Every one shall turn to his own people"**: the foreigners who lived and traded in the capital scatter home.
- **"Israel is a hunted sheep driven away by lions" (v17).** A short history of the two disasters. **"First the king of Assyria devoured him,"** when Assyria swallowed the northern kingdom (734–722 BC). **"Now at last Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon has gnawed his bones,"** when Judah was taken (605–586 BC). The verb pictures a lion crunching what is left of the carcass.
- **"I am bringing punishment on the king of Babylon… as I punished the king of Assyria" (v18).** Nineveh fell in 612 BC, as Nahum had foretold. Judah's generation had seen that empire vanish. Babylon will follow.
- **"He shall feed on Carmel and in Bashan… on the hills of Ephraim and in Gilead" (v19).** These were Israel's richest grazing lands, two west of the Jordan and two east of it, covering both the north and the Transjordan regions that had been lost first. The sheep go back to good pasture.
- **"Iniquity shall be sought in Israel, and there shall be none… for I will pardon" (v20).** This is one of the most striking promises in the book. The sins are not missing because they never happened, but because God has forgiven them, and forgiven sin is not found again. It echoes 31:34: "I will remember their sin no more." **"Those whom I leave as a remnant"** shows that the returning people are survivors kept alive by grace.
- **"The land of Merathaim… the inhabitants of Pekod" (v21).** Both are real names twisted into puns. *Mat marrati*, "land of the bitter sea," was an Akkadian name for southern Babylonia near the Persian Gulf, and the Hebrew *Merathaim* sounds like "double rebellion." The *Puqudu* were an Aramean tribe east of the Tigris, named in Assyrian and Babylonian records, and *Pekod* sounds like "punishment" or "visitation." So the geography becomes a sentence: rebellion meets punishment. **"Devote them to destruction"** uses *ḥaram*, the language of holy war from the conquest of Canaan (Josh 6:21). Babylon is placed under the ban it once applied to others.
- **"How the hammer of the whole earth is cut down and broken!" (v23).** Babylon had been the blacksmith's hammer that smashed nation after nation (compare 51:20–23, where God calls it "my hammer and weapon of war"). A hammer is only a tool. When it starts to think of itself as the craftsman, it is broken.
- **"I set a snare for you and you were taken… and you did not know it" (v24).** The historians Herodotus and Xenophon both say Babylon fell while its people were celebrating a festival, and that Cyrus's men lowered the Euphrates and entered along the riverbed. Daniel 5 tells of Belshazzar's feast on the night of the fall. Babylonian records (the Nabonidus Chronicle) say the city was taken "without battle." The sources differ on details, but they agree that Babylon was caught off guard. **"Because you opposed the LORD"**: literally, Babylon had picked a fight with God.
- **"The LORD has opened his armory and brought out the weapons of his wrath" (v25).** The armies of the north are God's weapons. Isaiah uses the same picture of the Medes as "the weapons of his indignation" (Isa 13:5).
- **"Open her granaries; pile her up like heaps of grain" (v26).** The plunder of a rich city. **"Kill all her bulls" (v27)** probably means her young warriors and leaders, the strong ones, as "bulls" often does in prophetic poetry (Isa 34:7).
- **"To declare in Zion the vengeance of the LORD… vengeance for his temple" (v28).** Refugees and returning exiles run with the news. Nebuchadnezzar had burned the temple (52:13) and carried its vessels to the house of his god (Dan 1:2). Belshazzar later drank from them at his feast (Dan 5:2–4). The LORD takes the desecration personally.
- **"She has proudly defied the LORD, the Holy One of Israel" (v29).** "The Holy One of Israel" is Isaiah's favorite name for God, used only twice in Jeremiah, both times in these Babylon chapters (here and 51:5). Babylon's root sin is pride against the holy God. Verse 31 even addresses her as **"O proud one,"** as if pride were her name (literally "Pride").
- **"The proud one shall stumble and fall, with none to raise him up" (v32).** Proverbs 16:18 in action.
- **"All who took them captive have held them fast; they refuse to let them go" (v33).** The words recall Pharaoh, who refused to let Israel go (Exod 4:23; 9:2). The exile is a second Egypt, and the return will be a second exodus (16:14–15).
- **"Their Redeemer is strong; the LORD of hosts is his name" (v34).** The Redeemer is the *go'el*, the next of kin whose duty was to buy back a relative sold into slavery, redeem lost family land, or avenge a murdered relative (Lev 25:25, 48–49; Ruth 3:12–13). Israel cannot free itself, but it has a Kinsman who is strong. **"He will surely plead their cause"**: he also acts as their lawyer, taking up their case (*rib*) in court. **"That he may give rest to the earth, but unrest to the inhabitants of Babylon."** In Hebrew the two verbs sound almost alike (*hirgia'* and *hirgiz*). The same act that brings the world peace brings Babylon turmoil.
- **"A sword against the Chaldeans" (vv35–37).** Five times "a sword against," and the list sweeps through every source of Babylon's confidence: officials, **"wise men,"** **"diviners"** (Babylon was famous for astrologers and omen readers; compare Isa 47:12–13; Dan 2:2), warriors, horses, chariots, foreign mercenaries and treasures. **"That they may become women"** is an ancient taunt meaning the soldiers will lose all fighting spirit (compare 51:30).
- **"A drought against her waters" (v38).** A sixth line breaks the pattern with a pun: "sword" is *ḥereb* and "drought" is *ḥoreb*, the same consonants with different vowels. Babylon's whole life depended on its rivers and canals. Many readers connect the line with the lowering of the Euphrates in 539 BC, and more broadly with the slow silting and decay of the irrigation system that turned the region to desert. **"It is a land of images, and they are mad over idols."** The word for idols here, *'emim*, means "terrors" or "frightful things." Babylon is crazed by its own fears dressed up as gods.
- **"Wild beasts shall dwell with hyenas… She shall never again have people" (vv39–40).** This repeats Isaiah 13:19–22. Cyrus did not destroy Babylon. He made it a royal city. Its decline was gradual: the Persian king Xerxes damaged it after revolts (around 482 BC), Alexander planned to rebuild it but died there in 323 BC, and the Seleucid kings built Seleucia on the Tigris and moved the population away. By the early centuries AD travelers found it largely empty, and it has lain in ruins ever since. The comparison with **Sodom and Gomorrah** is about finality, not method: once judged, the place does not return.
- **"Behold, a people comes from the north" (vv41–43).** These lines repeat, almost word for word, 6:22–24, where the cruel army from the north came against "daughter of Zion." Now it is **"against you, O daughter of Babylon!"** The king of Babylon hears the report, and **"his hands fell helpless; anguish seized him,"** just as Jerusalem's people had once trembled (6:24). Babylon receives exactly the terror it inflicted. Daniel 5:6 shows Belshazzar's knees knocking when the writing appeared on the wall.
- **"Like a lion coming up from the thicket of the Jordan" (vv44–46).** This repeats 49:19–21, said there of Edom, almost word for word. The dense undergrowth along the Jordan was lion country. The lion is the conqueror God sends. **"For who is like me? Who will summon me? What shepherd can stand before me?"** No ruler can call God to account. **"The little ones of their flock shall be dragged away"** turns the sheep image of vv6 and 17 around: Babylon's own flock is now the prey. **"At the sound of the capture of Babylon the earth shall tremble"**: the fall of the empire shakes the whole known world.
- **How was this fulfilled?** Readers have noticed that the violent destruction described here does not match the peaceful handover of 539 BC very closely. Christians have read this in three ways, often together: the prophecy began to be fulfilled in 539 BC and was completed over the following centuries as Babylon emptied; prophetic poetry speaks in vivid, stock images of total ruin rather than a news report; and Babylon becomes a symbol of every proud power set against God, whose final fall Revelation announces in Jeremiah's own words (Rev 18:2, 4–6). The mismatch is also a mark of authenticity. A writer inventing a prophecy after the fact would have made it fit the events better.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry frames the whole chapter around two threads, the ruin of Babylon and the redemption of God's people. Babylon was "the rod in God's hand for the chastising of all the other nations," and now "that rod shall be thrown into the fire." On v4 he says the returning exiles' tears "flow not from the sorrow of the world… but from godly sorrow," and on v34: "though they are weak, their Redeemer is strong." Guzik ties vv4–5 to the new covenant of Jeremiah 31, Ezekiel 36 and Hebrews 8. He notes that Babylon's walls were not literally thrown down in 539 BC and suggests three ways to account for it: a gradual fulfilment, a softening of judgment after Nebuchadnezzar humbled himself (Dan 4), and a final fulfilment in the fall of Babylon in Revelation 18. He presents the Redeemer of v34 as the kinsman who freely takes up his people's cause. JFB reads the "nation" of v3 as the Medes and sees the desolation as layered: Cyrus's conquest, a later destruction under Persian kings, and the final emptying after Seleucia was built. On v20 JFB pushes the promise forward to a future conversion of Israel, a reading Guzik shares and many other interpreters do not, who see it fulfilled in the forgiveness offered in Christ to the remnant of Israel and the nations. JFB notes the "rest" and "unrest" wordplay in v34, explains Merathaim as "doubly rebellious" and Pekod as "visitation," and reads the word for idols in v38 as "terrors," objects "more fitted to frighten than to attract." JFB gives no separate note on vv10, 22 and 32.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter turns on one line: "Their Redeemer is strong" (v34). Israel could not break Babylon's grip, and no human power could break the deeper grip of sin and death. The Kinsman-Redeemer came in person, took on our flesh so that he could be our next of kin (Heb 2:14–15), and paid the price himself (1 Pet 1:18–19). The promise that iniquity "shall be sought… and there shall be none" (v20) is what his cross accomplishes: sins forgiven are not found again. And the lost sheep of v6, scattered by bad shepherds, are gathered by the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for them (John 10:11). Babylon's fall in Revelation 18 is announced in Jeremiah's words, so the church still reads this chapter as a promise that every proud power set against God will fall, and that his people will walk home to Zion.
