*Setting:* The temple vision continues, dated to the spring of 573 BC, fourteen years after Jerusalem's fall (40:1). Ezekiel's guide has led him in from the east gate through the outer and inner courts (ch 40) and into the temple building as far as the door of the Most Holy Place (ch 41). Now the tour turns back outward. Chapter 42 describes two blocks of rooms where the priests eat the holiest portions of the offerings and change their clothes, and then the guide measures the whole temple area from outside. The chapter ends where the tour began, with the outer wall (40:5), and this time the purpose of that wall is spelled out: "to make a separation between the holy and the common." That one phrase sums up chapters 40–42. The next chapter, the climax of the vision, shows God's glory returning to fill the house (43:1–5).

#### The Temple's Chambers (vv1–20)
- **"He led me out into the outer court, toward the north, and he brought me to the chambers" (v1).** These rooms stood on the north and south sides of the temple building, between the inner court and the outer court, next to the "separate yard" behind the temple (41:12–15). They were boundary rooms: the priests could reach them from the inner court and leave them on the outer side.
- **"A hundred cubits, and the breadth fifty cubits" (v2).** Each block was about 170 by 85 feet. **"Gallery against gallery in three stories" (v3).** Like the side rooms of the temple (41:6), they rose in three floors.
- **"A passage inward, ten cubits wide and a hundred cubits long" (v4).** A walkway ran along the rooms. The Hebrew says "a way of one cubit," which the ESV, following the Greek translation, takes as "a hundred cubits long."
- **"The upper chambers were narrower… they had no pillars like the pillars of the courts" (vv5–6).** Without pillars to carry the upper floors, each story was set back from the one below, like stepped terraces built against a slope. The ground in the outer court was lower than in the inner court (40:18, 31), so the building may have been built into the rise between them.
- **"A wall outside parallel to the chambers… fifty cubits long" (v7).** A screening wall shielded the rooms from the outer court, where ordinary worshipers gathered. What went on inside was not for public view.
- **"Below these chambers was an entrance on the east side" (v9).** The priests entered from the outer court at the lower level.
- **"On the south also… there were chambers… similar to the chambers on the north" (vv10–12).** A matching block stood on the south. As with the gates (40:20–27), the repetition shows a plan in perfect balance.
- **"These are the holy chambers, where the priests who approach the LORD shall eat the most holy offerings" (v13).** The guide speaks again to explain. In the law, certain offerings were "most holy": the grain offering (apart from the handful burned on the altar), the sin offering and the guilt offering (Lev 2:3, 10; 6:16–18, 25–26, 29; 7:6). The priests ate their share of these, and they had to eat them "in a holy place." This was how the priests were fed, since they had no farmland of their own (Num 18:8–10, 20). It was also a meal eaten in God's presence. The God who received the offering shared it with those who served him.
- **"There they shall put the most holy offerings… for the place is holy" (v13).** The rooms also served as storerooms. Offerings were brought in kind, not in money: grain, flour, oil and animals, which needed space.
- **"They shall not go out of it into the outer court without laying there the garments in which they minister, for these are holy" (v14).** The priests' linen garments were made for service before God (Exod 28; Lev 16:23). They were not to be worn among the crowds. Chapter 44 repeats this and gives the reason: "lest they transmit holiness to the people with their garments" (44:19). In Israel's law, holiness, like uncleanness, could be passed on by touch (Lev 6:27). The point is not that the people were bad, but that holy things belong in their proper place, and the line must be kept clear.
- **"They shall put on other garments before they go near to that which is for the people" (v14).** The priest's change of clothes marked the line between serving at the altar and ordinary life.
- **"Now when he had finished measuring the interior of the temple area, he led me out by the gate that faced east" (v15).** The tour ends where it started, at the east gate, where the glory will soon return (43:1–2).
- **"500 cubits by the measuring reed all around" (vv16–19).** The guide measures each side in turn: east, north, south, west. The Hebrew text reads "500 reeds," and the KJV keeps that. A reed was six long cubits, so that would make a square of 3,000 cubits, nearly a mile on each side, far bigger than the whole of ancient Jerusalem. The Greek translation reads "500 cubits," and the ESV and most modern versions follow it. A 500-cubit square, about 860 feet (260 m) each way, matches the total of the inner measurements in chapters 40–41, which strongly supports the cubit reading. The ESV's phrase "by the measuring reed" means the guide used his reed as the measuring tool.
- **"It had a wall around it, 500 cubits long and 500 cubits broad" (v20).** The whole temple area is a perfect square. The Tyndale notes point out that in the tabernacle and Solomon's temple only the Most Holy Place was square. Here the whole complex is. Holiness has spread outward from the center to the edge.
- **"To make a separation between the holy and the common" (v20).** This is the key to chapters 40–42. God told Aaron's sons that their duty was "to distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Lev 10:10). Ezekiel accused the priests of his day of failing at exactly that: "they have made no distinction between the holy and the common" (22:26). Solomon's temple had stood beside the royal palace, and idols and pagan worship had been brought right into it (8:3–16; 43:7–8). The exile was the result. In the new temple, the line is built into the architecture itself.
- **How big is God's holy place?** Readers who take the Hebrew "500 reeds" literally, including Guzik and some dispensational writers, think the size points to a future temple on a transformed landscape, since Zechariah 14:4–10 speaks of the land around Jerusalem being reshaped. Others, like JFB, accept the larger figure but read it as a symbol of the vast growth of God's kingdom. Most modern scholars follow the 500-cubit reading. On any reading, the chapter's emphasis is on separation and holiness, not size.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws lessons from the priests' rooms. Their nearness to the temple teaches "to prefer public worship before private," but "our attendance upon God in solemn ordinances will not excuse us from the duties of the closet." On v14 he says the holy garments "must be laid up, that they may be kept clean and decent for the credit of their service." He follows the "500 reeds" reading, which he takes as "a very large distance between the sanctuary and the profane place." JFB also follows the Hebrew and calculates a square of about one and one-seventh miles, "more than all ancient Jerusalem," which marks the temple's ideal character and the "great enlargement of the kingdom of God." He links the many chambers to the "many mansions" of John 14:2, and on v20 notes that the wall no longer divides Jew from Gentile but separates "the sacred from the profane." Guzik, citing Trapp, sees the priests' meal as a reminder that those who serve God's people should be supported by them. He argues for the reading "rods" (reeds), notes that "cubits" is not in the Hebrew of v20, and, following Feinberg and Alexander, connects the larger area to the changes in the land foretold in Zechariah 14.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel's temple ends with a wall drawn "between the holy and the common," because sinful people could not safely come near a holy God, and holy things could not be carried out among them. In Christ that separation is overcome from the inside. He is the priest who touched lepers and was not defiled, but made them clean (Mark 1:40–42). He did not change out of his holiness before coming near the people: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). And through him, all who belong to God become "a holy priesthood," eating at his table and offering "spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" (1 Pet 2:5). One day there will be no common place left at all: on the bells of the horses and the cooking pots of Jerusalem will be written "Holy to the LORD" (Zech 14:20–21).
