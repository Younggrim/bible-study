*Setting:* Spring of 966 BC, Solomon's fourth year (v2), in Jerusalem. Chapter 2 described the preparations: the treaty with Hiram of Tyre, the Phoenician craftsman Huram-abi and the labor force. Now building begins. First Kings 6–7 describes the temple in great detail, including side rooms, windows, wall carvings and building methods. The Chronicler gives a much shorter account, about a third the length, and he picks his details. He wants readers to see where the temple stood, how it measured against the law, how rich it was, and above all the Most Holy Place where God's throne would rest. His readers, back from exile, worshiped in a second temple so plain that older people who remembered Solomon's temple wept when its foundation was laid (Ezra 3:12; Hag 2:3). The chapter tells them what had been, and it holds out what God had promised.

#### Solomon Builds the Temple (vv1–17)
- **"On Mount Moriah" (v1).** This is the only place outside Genesis 22 that names Moriah. Abraham was told to take Isaac "to the land of Moriah" and offer him "on one of the mountains" there (Gen 22:2), and God provided a ram in his place. Abraham named the spot "The LORD will provide," and Genesis adds, "as it is said to this day, 'On the mount of the LORD it shall be provided'" (Gen 22:14). The Chronicler is the first biblical writer to tie that mountain to the temple site, and Jewish and Christian tradition has followed him. The temple would stand where a father had offered his only son and God had provided a substitute.
- **"Where the LORD had appeared to David his father… on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite" (v1).** After David's sinful census, a plague struck Israel, and the angel of the LORD stopped at this threshing floor. David bought it, built an altar, and God answered with fire from heaven (1 Chr 21:15–22:1). Ornan is called Araunah in 2 Samuel 24. A threshing floor was a flat, open rock on a hilltop where the wind could blow away chaff. So the temple stands on two places of mercy: where Isaac was spared, and where judgment on Jerusalem was stopped. The site has been the Temple Mount ever since, under today's Dome of the Rock.
- **"In the second month of the fourth year of his reign" (v2).** About April–May 966 BC. First Kings 6:1 dates this from the Exodus, "in the four hundred and eightieth year." Chronicles leaves that out. The Tyndale notes observe that the Chronicler often skips the Exodus and focuses instead on David and the land.
- **"In cubits of the old standard… sixty cubits, and the breadth twenty cubits" (v3).** About 90 by 30 feet. The ordinary cubit was about 18 inches (the length from elbow to fingertip). There was also an older, longer "royal" cubit about 20.5 inches, which Ezekiel uses (Ezek 40:5; 43:13). The Chronicler explains that Solomon used the older one, perhaps because his readers in the Persian era used a different measure. The temple's floor plan was twice the size of the tabernacle (Exod 26), keeping the same proportions. Its size was modest by later standards. Its glory was its richness, not its scale.
- **"The vestibule… its height was 120 cubits" (v4).** That would be about 180 feet, a tower six times taller than the building behind it (1 Kgs 6:2 gives the house a height of 30 cubits). Some ancient Greek and Syriac manuscripts read "twenty cubits," and many scholars think a copyist's slip turned 20 into 120. Others take the number literally and picture a towering gateway like the pylons of Egyptian temples. Kings gives no height for the porch.
- **"The nave he lined with cypress and covered it with fine gold and made palms and chains on it" (v5).** The "nave" is the main hall, the Holy Place, 40 cubits long, where the lampstands, table and incense altar stood. The ESV's "nave" translates "the great house." Palm trees, together with cherubim and open flowers (1 Kgs 6:29), made the walls a picture of a garden. Many readers see Eden here: a sanctuary of trees and cherubim where God walks with his people (Gen 2:8–9; 3:24).
- **"Settings of precious stones… gold of Parvaim" (v6).** Parvaim is mentioned only here. It may have been a region of Arabia known for gold, but even the earliest translators did not know where it was. Precious stones recall the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:17–20) and look ahead to the new Jerusalem, built of jewels (Rev 21:18–21).
- **"Its beams, its thresholds, its walls, and its doors… and he carved cherubim on the walls" (v7).** Everything was overlaid with gold. Cherubim were the guardians of God's presence, the same beings who guarded the way to the tree of life (Gen 3:24) and were woven into the tabernacle's curtains (Exod 26:1).
- **"The Most Holy Place… twenty cubits… twenty cubits" (v8).** The innermost room was a perfect cube, 30 feet each way (1 Kgs 6:20 gives the height). The only other perfect cube in Scripture is the new Jerusalem, which is a cube on a vast scale (Rev 21:16). In the end, the whole city becomes the Most Holy Place, where God dwells with his people. **"600 talents of fine gold"**: about 23 tons. Kings gives no figure.
- **"The weight of gold for the nails was fifty shekels" (v9).** About a pound and a quarter. These were probably gold-headed nails, or gold sheeting for nail heads, holding the gold overlay in place. **"The upper chambers"** were probably rooms above the Most Holy Place, which was lower than the main hall (1 Kgs 6:20, 2).
- **"Two cherubim of wood… overlaid… with gold" (v10).** Kings says they were made of olive wood and stood ten cubits (15 feet) tall (1 Kgs 6:23). Ancient thrones in Syria and Phoenicia often had winged creatures carved on their sides or supporting the seat. Here the cherubim's wings spread over the ark, which was God's footstool (1 Chr 28:2). There was no seat. The LORD himself is the one "enthroned above the cherubim" (Ps 99:1), invisible, and the space under their wings was his throne room. The commandment against images (Exod 20:4) forbade images of God or images to worship. These figures represented God's attendants, not God.
- **"The wings… extended twenty cubits" (vv11–13).** Each cherub had two wings of five cubits. Together the four wings stretched wall to wall across the room, touching in the middle over the ark.
- **"The cherubim stood on their feet, facing the nave" (v13).** Literally "their faces toward the house." In the tabernacle the small cherubim on the ark's lid faced each other, looking down at the mercy seat (Exod 25:20). These great figures faced outward, toward the main hall and the doorway, as if guarding the entrance.
- **"The veil of blue and purple and crimson fabrics and fine linen" (v14).** Kings mentions olivewood doors into the inner room (1 Kgs 6:31–32) but no veil. Chronicles mentions a veil like the one in the tabernacle (Exod 26:31–33). There were probably both, with the veil hanging inside the doors. It was this veil, or its successor in Herod's temple, that was torn from top to bottom when Jesus died (Matt 27:51). Hebrews says the way into God's presence is now open "through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20).
- **"Two pillars thirty-five cubits high, with a capital of five cubits" (v15).** First Kings 7:15 and Jeremiah 52:21 say each pillar was 18 cubits high. There are two main explanations. The Chronicler may give the combined length of both shafts (two times 17.5 cubits, rounding 18), perhaps as cast in one length. Or a number was misread in copying, since Hebrew numbers were sometimes written with letters, and the letters for 18 and 35 could be confused. They were bronze (1 Kgs 7:15), about 27 feet tall by Kings' figure, and hollow (Jer 52:21).
- **"Chains like a necklace… a hundred pomegranates" (v16).** The Hebrew reads "chains in the inner sanctuary," which makes little sense on outdoor pillars. A small change gives "like a necklace," which the ESV follows. Pomegranates, with their many seeds, were a sign of fruitfulness and were also embroidered on the hem of the high priest's robe (Exod 28:33–34). Kings gives 200 pomegranates on each capital (1 Kgs 7:20), and Jeremiah 52:23 gives a hundred around each network. The Chronicler's "a hundred" probably counts one row or one chain.
- **"That on the south he called Jachin, and that on the north Boaz" (v17).** *Yakin* means "he establishes" or "he will establish," and *Boʿaz* probably means "in him is strength." Together they may have spelled out a prayer or a confession: God will establish the throne and the house in his strength (compare 1 Chr 17:12; Ps 89:4). Boaz was also the name of David's great-grandfather, the husband of Ruth (Ruth 4:21–22), though whether the pillar was named for him is not known. Free-standing pillars at temple entrances are known from other ancient temples, such as the temple at Ain Dara in Syria and Phoenician temples to Melqart in Tyre.
- **Commentators:** Henry ties Moriah to Abraham's offering of Isaac and says it "was typical of Christ's sacrifice of himself"; "There atonement was made once; and therefore… there atonement must still be made." He reads the rich gold and stones as a foreshadowing of the new Jerusalem. Guzik also links Moriah to Calvary, quoting John Trapp that Calvary was either part of this mount or near it, and he connects the veil to the torn veil and Hebrews 10:19–20. Guzik follows the scholar Martin Selman in reading the porch height as twenty cubits, not 120. JFB takes 120 literally, picturing a porch "in the form of a tower, or two pyramidal towers," and explains the pillar height as two shafts of "seventeen cubits and a half" each. JFB calls the inner room "a perfect cube" and the cherubim "of colossal size, like the Assyrian sculptures." Guzik explains the pillar names as "He shall establish" and "in strength."

#### Pointing to Christ
The temple stood on Mount Moriah, where God spared Isaac by providing a ram, and on Ornan's threshing floor, where God stopped the plague after an altar was built and an offering made. Both places point forward. On a hill outside that same city, God "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32), and the curtain before the Most Holy Place was torn in two. The gold cube that only the high priest could enter once a year becomes, in Revelation, a whole city in the shape of a cube, where "the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3, 16).
