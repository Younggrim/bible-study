*Setting:* Sunday morning, two days after the crucifixion, about AD 30 or 33, and the following Sunday. Jesus was buried in haste on Friday afternoon in a new tomb in a garden near Golgotha, wrapped in linen with about seventy-five pounds of spices by Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus (19:38–42). The Sabbath has passed. The disciples are in hiding, afraid of the same authorities who had Jesus killed. Chapter 20 tells how the risen Jesus met four people or groups in turn: Mary Magdalene, Peter and the beloved disciple at the empty tomb, the frightened disciples behind locked doors, and the doubting Thomas. Each meeting moves someone from grief, fear or unbelief to faith. The chapter ends with John's statement of why he wrote his Gospel.

#### The Resurrection (vv1–10)
- **"On the first day of the week" (v1).** Sunday. The day after the Sabbath became the day Christians gathered to worship (Acts 20:7; 1 Cor 16:2), later called "the Lord's day" (Rev 1:10). The new creation begins on the first day, as the first creation did.
- **"Mary Magdalene came to the tomb early, while it was still dark" (v1).** Mark says other women came with her (Mark 16:1), and Mary's "we do not know" in v2 hints at them. John focuses on her. Her town, Magdala, lay on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Jesus had freed her from seven demons (Luke 8:2). She is often confused with the sinful woman of Luke 7 or with Mary of Bethany, but the Gospels never connect them. It was dark outside, and, as John often uses darkness, dark in her understanding too.
- **"The stone had been taken away from the tomb" (v1).** Wealthy families' tombs were often closed with a large disk-shaped stone rolled in a groove. Matthew 28:2 says an angel rolled it back. The stone was not moved to let Jesus out but to let witnesses in.
- **"They have taken the Lord out of the tomb" (v2).** Mary's first thought is not resurrection but grave robbery, which was common enough that a Roman inscription said to come from Nazareth threatens death for it. No one in the story expected Jesus to rise.
- **"The other disciple outran Peter and reached the tomb first" (v4).** The detail is so unimportant that it reads like an eyewitness memory, probably the beloved disciple's own. JFB: "These particulars have a singular air of artless truth about them." Tradition reasonably suggests John was younger.
- **Three different words for "saw" (vv5–8).** John uses three Greek verbs. The beloved disciple, stooping, "saw" (*blepei*, glimpsed) the linen lying there (v5). Peter, going in, "saw" (*theōrei*, looked carefully, studied) the cloths (v6). Then the beloved disciple went in, "and he saw (*eiden*, perceived) and believed" (v8). The scene moves from a glance, to close inspection, to understanding.
- **"The linen cloths lying there, and the face cloth… folded up in a place by itself" (vv6–7).** The linen strips had been wound around the body with sticky spices between the layers (19:40). The face cloth (*soudarion*) had been wrapped around the head. "Folded up" (*entetuligmenon*) can mean rolled up or wrapped, possibly still in the shape it had around his head. Either way, this is not what grave robbers leave. Thieves would take the body with the costly cloths and spices, or at least not leave the cloths neatly in place. Lazarus came out of his tomb still bound in his grave clothes (11:44) and would need them again. Jesus left his behind. JFB notes they lay "not loosely, as if hastily thrown down."
- **"He saw and believed" (v8).** The beloved disciple is the first person in John's Gospel to believe in the resurrection, and he believes without seeing Jesus, only the evidence he left. That anticipates v29.
- **"As yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead" (v9).** The disciples were not expecting a resurrection and then convinced themselves of one. They came to believe from the evidence, and only later saw how Scripture had foretold it (Ps 16:10; Isa 53:10–12; Luke 24:25–27, 44–46; Acts 2:24–32).
- **"Then the disciples went back to their homes" (v10).** The verse is oddly quiet. They have seen an empty tomb, but they have not yet met the risen Lord.
- **Commentators:** Guzik argues that the orderly grave clothes rule out a robbery, and stresses that the apostles preached not merely an empty tomb but a risen Lord. He mentions the Shroud of Turin and concludes its origin remains uncertain. JFB sees "a singular air of artless truth" in the footrace and notes the disciples believed before they understood the prophecies. Henry admires Mary's early coming: "Love to Christ, if it be cordial, will be constant."

#### Jesus Appears to Mary Magdalene (vv11–18)
- **"Mary stood weeping outside the tomb" (v11).** Peter and John go home. Mary stays. She has come back to the garden and cannot leave.
- **"Two angels in white, sitting where the body of Jesus had lain, one at the head and one at the feet" (v12).** Many have seen a picture of the mercy seat on the ark of the covenant, with a cherub at each end and God's presence between them (Exod 25:18–22). Here the place between the angels is empty, and the atonement is complete.
- **"Woman, why are you weeping?" (v13).** The question is gentle, almost surprised. Tears are the wrong response to an empty tomb. Henry says those who weep at the grave of Christ "must weep, not for him, but for themselves."
- **"She did not know that it was Jesus" (v14).** On the Emmaus road two disciples also failed to recognize him (Luke 24:16). His resurrection body was truly his and still bore his wounds, but it was also changed. Tears, the dim light and the last thing she expected may all have played a part.
- **"Whom are you seeking?" (v15).** This is the question Jesus asked the arresting party (18:4, 7) and, in another form, his first words in this Gospel (1:38). Mary is looking for a corpse. She is about to find the living Lord.
- **"Supposing him to be the gardener" (v15).** The irony runs deep. Jesus was buried in a garden (19:41). The first man was put in a garden "to work it and keep it" (Gen 2:15). The last Adam (1 Cor 15:45) now stands in a garden on the first day of the new creation.
- **"Mary" (v16).** One word, her name, and she knows him. "The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name" (10:3). Grief recognizes what it has lost only when it is personally called.
- **"Rabboni!" (v16).** An Aramaic form of "my teacher," warmer and more personal than "rabbi." She greets him as she always knew him. She has not yet grasped that he is more than the teacher she followed.
- **"Do not cling to me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father" (v17).** The KJV's "Touch me not" misleads. The verb is a present imperative of *haptomai*, which with the negative means "stop holding on to me." Matthew 28:9 says the women took hold of his feet. Jesus is not refusing to be touched, since he invites Thomas to touch him (v27). He is telling Mary that the old relationship is not simply being restored. He is going to the Father, and from there he will be present with them in a new way, through the Spirit (16:7). She cannot keep him by holding on. JFB: "Old familiarities must now give place to new and more awful yet sweeter approaches."
- **"Go to my brothers" (v17).** Jesus has called them servants and friends (15:15). Now, for the first time, he calls them "brothers." Through his death and resurrection, his Father becomes their Father (Heb 2:11–12).
- **"My Father and your Father, to my God and your God" (v17).** Jesus does not say "our Father." His relationship is unique, the eternal Son. Theirs is as adopted children through him. JFB: "His Father essentially, ours not so." But the point is the gift: his Father is now truly theirs (1:12).
- **"I have seen the Lord" (v18).** Mary Magdalene is the first witness of the risen Jesus and the first to announce it. In that culture a woman's testimony carried little weight. Josephus says it was not accepted in court. If the early church had invented the story, it would not have made women its first witnesses. The detail is a mark of truth, and a sign of how Jesus honors those the world overlooks.
- **Commentators:** Guzik explains "do not cling" as a call to stop an action already happening, and also points to the choice of a woman as first witness as evidence that the account is authentic. He sees Mary's devotion as so intense she did not stop to think how she would carry a body. JFB pictures Jesus gently testing her before revealing himself, "He will try her ere he tell her," and notes that no one in the Gospels ever calls Jesus "brother," though he calls his disciples brothers, which JFB treats as pure grace. JFB has no separate note on v14.

#### Jesus Appears to the Disciples (vv19–23)
- **"The doors being locked where the disciples were for fear of the Jews" (v19).** "The Jews" here, as often in John, means the religious leaders who had Jesus killed. The disciples had every reason to think they were next.
- **"Jesus came and stood among them" (v19).** Locked doors do not stop him. His resurrection body is physical, with wounds that can be touched, and he will eat fish (Luke 24:42–43). Yet it is not limited the way ours are. Paul calls it a "spiritual body" (1 Cor 15:44), meaning a body fully shaped by the Spirit, not a ghost.
- **"Peace be with you" (v19).** *Shalom* was the ordinary greeting. On these lips, on this night, it is anything but ordinary. These men had deserted him. His first word is not rebuke but peace, the peace he promised (14:27; 16:33).
- **"He showed them his hands and his side" (v20).** The risen Jesus is the crucified Jesus. His wounds are not erased. They are his identification and his credentials. Revelation 5:6 shows him in heaven as "a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain."
- **"The disciples were glad when they saw the Lord" (v20).** Exactly as he promised: "your sorrow will turn into joy" (16:20–22).
- **"As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you" (v21).** This is John's version of the Great Commission (Matt 28:18–20). Jesus had prayed it to the Father (17:18). Now he says it to them. The church's mission is modeled on his: sent in love, in obedience, into a hostile world, to make the Father known.
- **"He breathed on them and said to them, 'Receive the Holy Spirit'" (v22).** The verb *emphysaō* is the same word the Greek Old Testament uses in Genesis 2:7, where God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" and Adam became a living being. It also appears in Ezekiel 37:9, where the breath comes into the dry bones of Israel. In Hebrew and Greek, "breath" and "spirit" are the same word. The risen Lord breathes new creation life into his people.
- **How does this relate to Pentecost?** Christians have read it in several ways. Some see this as John's version of the Spirit's coming, told without Luke's time frame. Most see two stages: here a real, personal gift of the Spirit to the apostles for their mission, and at Pentecost, fifty days later, the public, powerful outpouring on the whole church (Acts 2). JFB calls this "a symbolical conveyance to them of the Spirit," a foretaste of the fuller gift.
- **"If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them" (v23).** The verbs "are forgiven" and "is withheld" are in the perfect tense, which suggests the disciples are declaring on earth what God has already done. Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches see here the basis of priestly absolution in confession. Most Protestants read it as the authority given to the whole church to proclaim forgiveness to those who repent and believe, and to warn those who refuse (Luke 24:47; Acts 2:38; 10:43), and to exercise church discipline (Matt 18:18). On every reading, the church does not invent forgiveness. It announces what Christ has won. JFB insists the apostles never exercised it "in any literal and authoritative sense," and takes it as a ministerial, declaring power.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lists four gifts Jesus gave that night: assurance, mission, the Holy Spirit, and authority to announce forgiveness and warn of sin unforgiven. He connects the breathing to the breath of life in Genesis, a work of re-creation. JFB calls Pentecost "the more copious" outpouring of what was given here. Henry draws attention to the plain line of joy: "were the disciples glad, when they saw the Lord."

#### Jesus and Thomas (vv24–29)
- **"Thomas, one of the twelve, called the Twin" (v24).** "Thomas" is Aramaic for "twin," and *Didymus* is the Greek equivalent. His twin is never named. In John he is the loyal pessimist, ready to die with Jesus (11:16), honest about his confusion (14:5). He was not there that Sunday night. The text does not say why.
- **"We have seen the Lord" (v25).** The disciples' words repeat Mary's (v18). Now they are the ones bearing witness, and Thomas, like every later believer, is asked to believe on someone else's testimony.
- **"Unless I see… and place my finger into the mark of the nails… I will never believe" (v25).** The Greek is emphatic: "I will certainly not believe." He wants the same proof they had, and more. His demand also tells us the wounds were from nails in the hands and a wound in the side large enough for a hand.
- **"Eight days later" (v26).** Counting inclusively, the next Sunday. The disciples are meeting on the first day of the week again.
- **"Put your finger here… Do not disbelieve, but believe" (v27).** Jesus quotes Thomas's own words back to him. He heard the demand, though he was not visibly present. He does not scold. He meets the doubter exactly where he is. "Do not disbelieve" is literally "Do not become unbelieving (*apistos*), but believing (*pistos*)." John does not say whether Thomas actually touched him. JFB thinks he did not, since Jesus says in v29 "because you have *seen*."
- **"My Lord and my God!" (v28).** This is the highest confession of Jesus in any Gospel, and it comes from the doubter. Some have argued it was only an exclamation of surprise, but a pious Jew would not say "my God" as an oath, and Jesus accepts it as faith (v29). JFB rejects the reading of it "as if it were a mere call upon God in a fit of astonishment." John's Gospel opened with "the Word was God" (1:1). It closes its main story with a disciple saying it to Jesus' face. The emperor Domitian, who reigned when John probably wrote, liked to be addressed as "our Lord and God." John's readers would have noticed whom Thomas said it to.
- **"Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (v29).** This is the last beatitude in the Gospels, and it is for every reader since. Peter writes the same: "Though you have not seen him, you love him" (1 Pet 1:8). Seeing is not better than believing, and those who believe on the apostles' testimony are not second-class disciples.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls Thomas an unbeliever rather than a mere doubter, but admires his honesty and the fullness of his confession once convinced. He notes that Jesus accepts titles of deity he would have rejected if they were false. JFB reads the strength of Thomas's demand as showing the depth of his unbelief and argues that the eight-day pattern marks the first day of the week as the Christian day. Henry is gentle with Thomas: "Thomas is not Judas," and his confession shows that "faith comes off a conqueror, after a struggle with unbelief."

#### The Purpose of This Book (vv30–31)
- **"Jesus did many other signs… which are not written in this book" (v30).** John has selected seven signs from Jesus' ministry, and now the greatest sign, the resurrection. He did not try to tell everything (21:25). He chose.
- **"These are written so that you may believe" (v31).** This is the purpose statement of the whole Gospel. Manuscripts differ by one letter between two tenses of "believe." One means "come to believe," which suggests John wrote to win unbelievers. The other means "keep on believing," which suggests he wrote to strengthen believers. Both are well attested, and the Gospel serves both purposes.
- **"That Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" (v31).** "Christ" is the Greek for "Messiah," the anointed king Israel was waiting for. "Son of God" names his unique relationship with the Father. JFB calls these "the one His official, the other His personal, title." Both have been the heart of the book from its first chapter (1:41, 49).
- **"That by believing you may have life in his name" (v31).** The goal is not information but life, the eternal life of knowing the Father and the Son (17:3). Many scholars think these verses originally ended the Gospel and that ch 21 is an epilogue, written by John or his circle. No manuscript lacks ch 21.
- **Commentators:** Guzik says the selection is deliberately incomplete, because John's aim is that readers would know Jesus personally, not merely know about him. JFB ties the purpose statement back to the blessing on those who believe without seeing in v29.

#### Pointing to Christ
The tomb is empty, the grave clothes are left in order, and the crucified Jesus is alive, still carrying his wounds. He calls a grieving woman by name, gives frightened deserters peace, breathes new creation life into them by the Spirit and sends them out as the Father sent him. He meets a doubter on his own terms and draws out the greatest confession in the Gospels: "My Lord and my God!" John wrote all of it down for those who would never see what he saw, so that they too might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life in his name.
