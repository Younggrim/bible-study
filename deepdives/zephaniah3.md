*Setting:* The last chapter of Zephaniah, from early in the reign of King Josiah (640–609 BC), probably before his reform of 622 BC. Chapter 1 announced the day of the LORD against Judah and the whole earth. Chapter 2 called the humble to seek the LORD and then pronounced judgment on the nations on every side, ending with Nineveh's boast, "I am, and there is no one else." Chapter 3 swings back to Jerusalem, which turns out to be no better than Nineveh. But then the book turns on a single word, "For" (v9), and the darkest prophet in the Old Testament ends with one of its most joyful passages: God himself singing over his people. Few chapters in the Bible move so far, from "Woe" (v1) to "loud singing" (v17).

#### Judgment on Jerusalem and the Nations (vv1–8)
- **"Woe to her who is rebellious and defiled, the oppressing city!" (v1).** At first a reader might think this is still Nineveh, the subject of 2:13–15. By v2 ("her God") and v4 ("her priests") it is clear the city is Jerusalem. Guzik notes this too. The surprise is the point: God's own city is no better than the pagan capital. "Oppressing" (*hayyonah*) is spelled exactly like the Hebrew for "the dove," and some hear a bitter echo of Hosea 7:11, where Israel is a "silly dove." The ESV's reading is the natural one.
- **"She listens to no voice; she accepts no correction. She does not trust in the LORD; she does not draw near to her God" (v2).** Four short "nots." Guzik lays them out as four failures, saving the worst for last: she does not draw near. JFB calls her distrust of the LORD "the parent of all superstitions and wickednesses." The order moves from outward behavior to the heart: refusing to hear, refusing to be corrected, refusing to trust, refusing to come close.
- **"Her officials within her are roaring lions; her judges are evening wolves" (v3).** "Within her" matters. The danger is not outside the walls but inside. Lions roar over their prey. Wolves hunt at dusk, hungry from the day, and "leave nothing till the morning," not even a bone to gnaw on the next day. Habakkuk uses the same picture for the Babylonian cavalry (Hab 1:8). Ezekiel 22:25–28 has a close parallel, as the Tyndale notes point out.
- **"Her prophets are fickle, treacherous men" (v4).** "Fickle" (*pochazim*) means reckless or frothing over like boiling water. A related word describes Reuben, "unstable as water" (Gen 49:4). These prophets say whatever pleases. **"Her priests profane what is holy; they do violence to the law."** The word is *chamas*, violence. The priests were meant to teach and guard the law (Mal 2:7). Instead they twisted it. Every class of leader has failed: officials, judges, prophets and priests.
- **"The LORD within her is righteous; he does no injustice" (v5).** The same words, "within her," now describe the LORD. He lives in the same city as the lions and wolves, and he is the opposite of them. This phrase "in your midst" runs through the whole chapter (vv3, 5, 11, 12, 15, 17), and the chapter's movement can be traced by it.
- **"Every morning he shows forth his justice; each dawn he does not fail" (v5).** The wolves are busy at evening. The LORD is faithful every morning, as sure as sunrise (Lam 3:22–23). **"But the unjust knows no shame."** God's daily faithfulness is on display, and still the guilty do not blush.
- **"I have cut off nations… their cities have been made desolate" (v6).** God speaks of judgments Judah had seen with its own eyes: the northern kingdom of Israel destroyed by Assyria in 722 BC, and the cities of the region overrun. Those were meant to be warnings.
- **"I said, 'Surely you will fear me; you will accept correction'" (v7).** God had hoped, in human terms, that Judah would learn. **"But all the more they were eager to make all their deeds corrupt."** "Were eager" is literally "they rose early." The Tyndale notes draw the contrast with Abraham, who rose early to obey God (Gen 22:3). Compare 2 Chronicles 36:15, where God "rose early" to send his prophets. Here the people rise early to sin (Prov 1:16).
- **"Therefore wait for me" (v8).** The word *chakkah* usually means to wait in hope (Isa 30:18; Hab 2:3, as the Tyndale notes say). After the indictment of vv1–7, the reader expects "wait for my judgment." For the wicked it is a threat. For the faithful it is a promise, because what God has decided turns out, in v9, to be salvation beyond the judgment. JFB reads it as addressed to the faithful remnant.
- **"For the day when I rise up to seize the prey" (v8).** The standard Hebrew text reads *la'ad*, "to the prey." The Greek Septuagint and the Syriac read the same consonants as *le'ed*, "as a witness," and the ESV footnote gives that reading. The Tyndale notes follow it, picturing a courtroom where God first testifies and then judges. Either way, God rises to act.
- **"My decision is to gather nations… for in the fire of my jealousy all the earth shall be consumed" (v8).** This repeats the last line of chapter 1 almost word for word (1:18). The reader is back where the book seemed to end, with the whole world under fire. What comes next is all the more startling.
- **Commentators:** Guzik identifies the city as Jerusalem from its prophets, priests and law, and lists the four failures of v2, calling not drawing near the worst. JFB reads "defiled" as inner moral filth under outward ceremonial purity, explains the wolves as "most ravenous at evening," and shows God's justice morning by morning while the people feel no shame. Henry outlines vv1–7 as God's reproof of Jerusalem with its "aggravations," the things that make the sin worse: God was in her midst, and she had seen what happened to others.

#### The Conversion of the Nations (vv9–13)
- **"For at that time I will change the speech of the peoples to a pure speech" (v9).** The fire of v8 is not the end but a refining. "Speech" is literally "lip" (*saphah*), the same word used of the one language at Babel: "the whole earth had one language (*saphah*)" (Gen 11:1). At Babel, God confused human speech to scatter a proud race. Here he purifies it, so that the peoples can call on him together. Many see the first fulfillment at Pentecost, when people "from every nation under heaven" heard God's works praised in their own languages (Acts 2:5–11). JFB also links the verse to undoing Babel. Guzik passes on a view that Hebrew might become the common language in a future kingdom, but the stress in the text is on purity of worship, not on one particular tongue. Isaiah 6:5 shows why "pure lips" matter: "I am a man of unclean lips."
- **"That all of them may call upon the name of the LORD" (v9).** To "call upon the name of the LORD" is to worship and pray to him (Gen 4:26; 12:8). Joel 2:32 promises that "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved," and Paul applies it to Jew and Gentile alike (Rom 10:12–13). **"And serve him with one accord."** Literally "with one shoulder," like people carrying a load together, as Guzik and JFB both note. Henry: "Purity is the way to unity."
- **"From beyond the rivers of Cush my worshipers… shall bring my offering" (v10).** Cush was the land south of Egypt, and its rivers were the upper Nile, the edge of the known world (Isa 18:1–7). The Tyndale notes say the phrase means the farthest parts of the earth. In chapter 2, the Cushites faced God's sword (2:12). Here worshipers come from beyond them. The first named Gentile convert after Pentecost is an official from Cush, "an Ethiopian, a eunuch… who had come to Jerusalem to worship" (Acts 8:27).
- **"The daughter of my dispersed ones" (v10).** The phrase can mean the scattered Jews of the far south coming home, or it can mean Gentiles who belong to God. Both are possible, and JFB discusses the options.
- **"On that day you shall not be put to shame because of the deeds by which you have rebelled against me" (v11).** Not that the sins never happened, but that they will no longer bring disgrace, because God has dealt with them (v15). **"I will remove from your midst your proudly exultant ones."** "Exultant" is the same word used of Nineveh, "the exultant city" (2:15). The pride that doomed Nineveh will be cut out of Jerusalem too.
- **"I will leave in your midst a people humble and lowly" (v12).** "Humble" (*'ani*) and "lowly" (*dal*) are words for the poor and powerless. They pick up the "humble of the land" of 2:3. Jesus begins his teaching with "Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matt 5:3). Henry writes, "The poor are evangelized, low in their own eyes, afflicted for sin, poor in spirit." The Tyndale notes point out that Jesus himself fulfills these qualities, "gentle and lowly in heart" (Matt 11:29).
- **"They shall seek refuge in the name of the LORD" (v12).** The proud trusted in themselves. The humble take shelter in God's name, his revealed character (Prov 18:10).
- **"They shall do no injustice and speak no lies, nor shall there be found in their mouth a deceitful tongue" (v13).** The remnant become like their God, who "does no injustice" (v5). Revelation uses almost these words of the 144,000 who follow the Lamb: "in their mouth no lie was found, for they are blameless" (Rev 14:5).
- **"For they shall graze and lie down, and none shall make them afraid" (v13).** The people are pictured as a flock at rest, with no lions or wolves among them now (v3). Micah promises the same peace: "every man under his vine… and no one shall make them afraid" (Mic 4:4). Ezekiel 34 tells how God himself will shepherd them.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv8–13 a promised "work of reformation" and says such promises "were to have their full accomplishment in gospel-times and gospel-graces." On v9 he writes, "Converting grace refines the language, not by making the phrases witty, but the substance wise." JFB connects v9 with reversing Babel and v10 with the scattered people bringing offerings. Guzik reads vv9–13 mainly as a picture of a future kingdom and stresses that the remnant will not be proud, knowing they stand by grace alone.

#### Israel's Joy and Restoration (vv14–20)
- **"Sing aloud, O daughter of Zion; shout, O Israel! Rejoice and exult with all your heart" (v14).** Four commands, four different words for joy. The Tyndale notes say the piling up shows the fullness of the joy to come. "Daughter of Zion" is a tender name for Jerusalem and its people. The book that began with "I will utterly sweep away" (1:2) now calls for singing.
- **"The LORD has taken away the judgments against you; he has cleared away your enemies" (v15).** The verbs are in the past tense, so certain that the prophet speaks as if it has already happened. The first problem was God's judgment, and the second the enemy. God removes both.
- **"The King of Israel, the LORD, is in your midst" (v15).** This is the climax of "in your midst" through the chapter. The LORD was in her midst as a righteous judge (v5). Now he is there as King and Savior. The Tyndale notes recall that the LORD was always meant to be Israel's true king (1 Sam 8:7). Nathanael calls Jesus "the King of Israel" (John 1:49), and the crowds shout it as he rides into Jerusalem (John 12:13). Henry: God is "in the midst of thee as a king in the midst of his people."
- **"Fear not, O Zion; let not your hands grow weak" (v16).** Hands that hang limp are a picture of despair and giving up (Isa 35:3; Heb 12:12). When Jesus entered Jerusalem, John records the words "Fear not, daughter of Zion; behold, your king is coming" (John 12:15), quoting Zechariah 9:9 with a "fear not" that may echo this verse.
- **"The LORD your God is in your midst, a mighty one who will save" (v17).** "Mighty one" is *gibbor*, warrior. The Tyndale notes link it to God as the divine warrior and to the Messiah's name, "Mighty God" (Isa 9:6). The verb "save" is *yasha'*, the root of the names Joshua and Jesus.
- **"He will rejoice over you with gladness" (v17).** In v14 the people rejoice. Here God rejoices. Isaiah uses the picture of a bridegroom: "as the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you" (Isa 62:5).
- **"He will quiet you by his love" (v17).** The Hebrew reads literally "he will be silent in his love." It may mean God's love is so deep it needs no words, like a parent watching a sleeping child. JFB reads it as silent, contented delight. Henry notes it can read "silent in his love." Or it may mean his love will quiet his people's fears, as the ESV takes it. Or it may mean he will be silent about their sins, no longer bringing them up. The Greek Septuagint reads "he will renew you," from a very similar Hebrew word. The ESV's rendering is fair, and each reading says something true about God's love.
- **"He will exult over you with loud singing" (v17).** This is one of the most remarkable lines in the Bible. Elsewhere people sing to God. Here God sings over his people with a shout of joy (*rinnah*), the word for a ringing cry of celebration. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's wonder at it. Jesus tells of a shepherd, a woman and a father who rejoice over what was lost and found, and says "there is joy before the angels of God over one sinner who repents" (Luke 15:7, 10).
- **"I will gather those of you who mourn for the festival" (v18).** The Hebrew here is difficult. Most take it as the exiles grieving because they cannot keep the appointed feasts in Jerusalem (Ps 137:1–4; Lam 1:4). JFB reads it this way. God will gather them so they "no longer suffer reproach."
- **"I will save the lame and gather the outcast, and I will change their shame into praise" (v19).** Micah 4:6–7 makes nearly the same promise. In the ancient world the lame and the outcast were the ones left behind. God gathers them first. The Tyndale notes point out that this gathering reverses the threat at the start of the book, when God would sweep the people away (1:2–4). It also answers the gathering of nations for judgment in v8. The Gospels show Jesus doing this very thing, welcoming the lame, the blind and the outcast (Matt 11:5; 21:14).
- **"At that time I will bring you in… when I restore your fortunes before your eyes" (v20).** "Restore your fortunes" (*shuv shevut*) is the exile-and-return phrase again (2:7). "Before your eyes" means they will see it themselves. "I will" comes again and again in vv18–20, as the Tyndale notes say. Salvation is God's work from start to finish. The book ends, as it began, with "says the LORD" (compare 1:2).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees vv14–20 as "a glorious work of salvation," with God "in the midst of thee as a king," who "will rest in his love" and "joy over them with singing." On v19 he notes that "even the lame shall take the prey." JFB reads "rest in his love" as silent, contented delight, set against the outward joy of singing, and takes v18 as exiles grieving the lost feasts. Guzik dwells on God's delight in his people as the reason not to fear or grow weak, and uses Spurgeon to marvel that God himself sings. He applies it to believers: since God rejoices over them, they can serve him without fear and with all their strength.

#### Pointing to Christ
Zephaniah's book moves from the day of wrath to a day of singing, and the hinge is God coming to live "in your midst" as King and Savior. That is what happened in Jesus, the mighty one whose name means "the LORD saves," who rode into Jerusalem to the words "Fear not, daughter of Zion" and who bore the judgments that are "taken away" from his people (v15; 1 Pet 2:24). He purifies lips from every nation, gathers the lame and the outcast, and makes a humble people his own. The same God who sings over them in v17 is the Father who runs to embrace the returning son and says, "let us eat and celebrate" (Luke 15:23).
