*Setting:* This is the heart of the "Book of Consolation" (chapters 30–33), most likely given during the last years before Jerusalem fell in 587 BC, when the city was under siege and its people were dying or being marched away. Chapter 30 promised that God would heal an incurable wound and raise up a King who would draw near to him. Chapter 31 widens the view. It speaks first to the northern tribes, called Ephraim, Samaria and "virgin Israel," who had been scattered by Assyria in 722 BC, and then to Judah. It moves from joyful homecoming, through the tears of Rachel, to Ephraim's repentance, and then to the passage for which Jeremiah is best known: the promise of a new covenant, written on the heart. The New Testament quotes that promise more fully than any other Old Testament passage (Heb 8:8–12), and Jesus spoke of it at the Last Supper.

#### The LORD Will Turn Mourning to Joy (vv1–30)
- **"At that time… I will be the God of all the clans of Israel, and they shall be my people" (v1).** The verse looks back to the end of chapter 30 ("in the latter days," 30:24) and repeats its covenant promise (30:22). "All the clans" means all twelve tribes. The old split between north and south is not the last word.
- **"The people who survived the sword found grace in the wilderness" (v2).** The language recalls the exodus generation, who escaped Pharaoh's sword and met God's favor in the desert. The return from exile is pictured as a second exodus. "Grace" is *chen*, favor freely given, the word used when Noah "found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (Gen 6:8).
- **"The LORD appeared to him from far away" (v3).** "From far away" (*merachoq*) can refer to distance or to time. It may mean God appeared to exiles far off in a foreign land, or it may mean "long ago," at Sinai. Both are true.
- **"I have loved you with an everlasting love" (v3).** One of the most loved lines in the Old Testament. "Everlasting" (*olam*) means without end in either direction. The exile did not cancel God's love. **"Therefore I have continued my faithfulness to you."** Literally "I have drawn you with *chesed*," God's steadfast covenant love. The same verb, *mashak*, appears in Hosea 11:4: "I led them with cords of kindness, with the bands of love." Jesus says, "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (John 6:44).
- **"Again I will build you… O virgin Israel!" (v4).** "Again" is repeated three times in vv4–5. The nation that had played the harlot with idols (3:1–2) is called "virgin" again, not because its past did not happen but because God will make it new. **"Tambourines… the dance of the merrymakers"** recall Miriam's song after the Red Sea (Exod 15:20).
- **"Again you shall plant vineyards on the mountains of Samaria; the planters shall plant and shall enjoy the fruit" (v5).** The Law said a new vineyard's fruit could not be eaten for the first few years (Lev 19:23–25). In exile people planted and others took the harvest (Deut 28:30). Here the same people who plant will enjoy it, a picture of settled peace.
- **"Watchmen will call in the hill country of Ephraim: 'Arise, and let us go up to Zion'" (v6).** After the kingdom split, Jeroboam set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan to keep the northern tribes from going to Jerusalem to worship (1 Kgs 12:26–30). Now the north's own watchmen will call the people to Zion. The division of 930 BC is healed.
- **"Sing aloud with gladness for Jacob… 'O LORD, save your people, the remnant of Israel'" (v7).** A shout of joy that is also a prayer. "Save" is *hosha*, the root behind *hosanna*, "save, we pray" (Ps 118:25; Matt 21:9).
- **"I will bring them from the north country… among them the blind and the lame, the pregnant woman and she who is in labor" (v8).** The north had been the direction of the invader (1:14; 6:22). Now it is the direction of the homecoming. The list is deliberate: the slowest and most vulnerable travelers. No one is left behind. Isaiah pictures the same: "the eyes of the blind shall be opened… the lame man shall leap like a deer" on the highway home (Isa 35:5–6, 8).
- **"With weeping they shall come, and with pleas for mercy I will lead them back" (v9).** They return in tears of repentance and gratitude, not in triumph. **"By brooks of water, in a straight path in which they shall not stumble."** The road from Babylon crossed real desert. God promises water and a level way (Isa 40:3–4). **"For I am a father to Israel, and Ephraim is my firstborn."** Ephraim was Joseph's younger son, yet Jacob crossed his hands and gave him the greater blessing (Gen 48:14–20). "Firstborn" here means the favored, honored son, as God called all Israel "my firstborn son" at the exodus (Exod 4:22).
- **"Hear the word of the LORD, O nations… 'He who scattered Israel will gather him'" (v10).** The same hand that scattered will gather. **"As a shepherd keeps his flock."** God himself will do what Judah's bad shepherds did not (23:1–4; Ezek 34:11–16; John 10:11).
- **"The LORD has ransomed Jacob and has redeemed him from hands too strong for him" (v11).** Two words for rescue. "Ransomed" (*padah*) is the word for buying back a firstborn or a slave by paying a price (Exod 13:13). "Redeemed" (*ga'al*) is the work of the *go'el*, the near kinsman who buys back family land or rescues a relative, as Boaz did for Ruth (Ruth 4:4–6). God acts as Israel's paying rescuer and next of kin.
- **"They shall be radiant over the goodness of the LORD, over the grain, the wine, and the oil" (v12).** "Radiant" means to beam or stream, the face lit up with joy (Ps 34:5). Grain, wine and oil were the very gifts Israel had credited to Baal (Hos 2:8). Now they will be received with thanks from the true giver. **"Their life shall be like a watered garden."** Compare Isaiah 58:11.
- **"I will turn their mourning into joy; I will comfort them, and give them gladness for sorrow" (v13).** This gives the section its ESV heading. Every generation of the community joins in: young women, young men, and the old.
- **"I will feast the soul of the priests with abundance" (v14).** Literally "I will saturate the priests' soul with fatness." Priests lived on portions of the offerings (Lev 7:31–34). Abundant offerings meant abundant provision.
- **"A voice is heard in Ramah, lamentation and bitter weeping. Rachel is weeping for her children" (v15).** Rachel, Jacob's beloved wife, was the mother of Joseph (father of Ephraim and Manasseh) and Benjamin. She died giving birth to Benjamin and was buried on the road to Bethlehem (Gen 35:16–20). 1 Samuel 10:2 places her tomb on the border of Benjamin, near Ramah, about five miles north of Jerusalem. Ramah was also the place where the Babylonians held Judean prisoners before marching them off; Jeremiah himself was there in chains (40:1). So the poem pictures the ancient mother weeping from her grave as her descendants are led past toward exile. **"She refuses to be comforted… because they are no more."**
- **Rachel and Matthew 2.** Matthew quotes v15 after Herod kills the boys of Bethlehem: "Then was fulfilled what was spoken by the prophet Jeremiah" (Matt 2:17–18). Jeremiah was not predicting Herod's massacre in so many words. Matthew sees a pattern. Once again Israel's mothers weep for children torn from them by a cruel king, near the same road. And in both places the weeping is not the end. In Jeremiah, comfort follows in v16. In Matthew, the child Jesus has escaped and will return to bring the true homecoming.
- **"Keep your voice from weeping… for there is a reward for your work… and they shall come back from the land of the enemy" (v16).** God answers the mother who would not be comforted. **"There is hope for your future" (v17).** Literally "there is hope for your end," the same phrase as "a future and a hope" in 29:11.
- **"I have heard Ephraim grieving" (v18).** Now the son speaks. The Hebrew begins with a doubled verb for emphasis: "Surely I have heard." **"You have disciplined me, and I was disciplined, like an untrained calf."** A young ox not yet used to the yoke kicks against it. Hosea said the same of the north: "Like a stubborn heifer, Israel is stubborn" (Hos 4:16). Ephraim admits he was stubborn under correction. **"Bring me back that I may be restored."** Literally "turn me, and I shall turn." The verb *shub*, "turn" or "return," runs all through Jeremiah (3:12, 14, 22). Ephraim knows he cannot turn himself. He asks God to do it.
- **"After I had turned away, I relented… I struck my thigh" (v19).** Striking the thigh was a gesture of grief and shame (Ezek 21:12). **"I bore the disgrace of my youth."** The northern kingdom's sins went back to its beginning, with Jeroboam's calves.
- **"Is Ephraim my dear son? Is he my darling child?" (v20).** The questions expect a surprised "yes." After centuries of rebellion, God still calls him "dear" and "darling," literally "a child of delights." **"As often as I speak against him, I do remember him still."** Even God's words of judgment were spoken by a Father who could not forget his son. **"Therefore my heart yearns for him."** Literally "my inner parts moan" or "churn" for him. Hebrew placed deep feeling in the gut. The same expression appears in Isaiah 63:15 and Song of Songs 5:4. It is the Old Testament's clearest picture of the father in Jesus' parable who sees his son "a long way off" and runs to him (Luke 15:20).
- **"Set up road markers for yourself… consider well the highway, the road by which you went" (v21).** The exiles are told to mark the road as they go, because they will travel it again in the other direction. Hope is built into the march into exile.
- **"How long will you waver, O faithless daughter?" (v22).** "Faithless" (*shobebah*) is a play on the word "return": the turning-away daughter is called to turn back.
- **"The LORD has created a new thing on the earth: a woman encircles a man" (v22).** This is one of the hardest lines in Jeremiah. "Created" is *bara*, the verb of Genesis 1:1, used only for God's acts. "Woman" is *neqebah*, simply "female," and "man" is *geber*, "strong man" or "warrior." "Encircles" (*tesobeb*) means to go around, surround or protect. Readings vary. Many modern scholars take it as a reversal of roles: Israel, the weak "female," once protected by others, will now surround and protect, a sign of a world turned right side up. Others take it to mean that faithless Israel, the woman, will now embrace and cling to her husband, the LORD. Many church fathers, beginning with Jerome and Augustine, read it as a prophecy of the virgin conceiving a son. Most interpreters today think that is more than the words themselves say, though it is not a careless reading. All agree the line announces something God alone does, as new as creation.
- **"The LORD bless you, O habitation of righteousness, O holy hill!" (v23).** Judah will once again bless Jerusalem using the old words of greeting. The city that was a "den of robbers" (7:11) will be a "habitation of righteousness."
- **"I will satisfy the weary soul, and every languishing soul I will replenish" (v25).** Farmers and shepherds, the settled and the wandering, will live together (v24), and the worn-out will be refreshed (Matt 11:28).
- **"At this I awoke and looked, and my sleep was pleasant to me" (v26).** An unusual personal note. Some of these words seem to have come to Jeremiah in a dream or vision. For a man whose messages had so often brought him grief (15:10; 20:14–18), this one was sweet.
- **"I will sow the house of Israel and the house of Judah with the seed of man and the seed of beast" (v27).** A land emptied of people and animals (32:43; 33:10) will be sown like a field and grow full again.
- **"As I have watched over them to pluck up and break down… so I will watch over them to build and to plant" (v28).** This picks up Jeremiah's call, word for word (1:10), and the vision of the almond branch, where God said, "I am watching over my word to perform it" (1:11–12). The God who kept his word in judgment will keep it in restoration. All six verbs of 1:10 appear here.
- **"The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" (v29).** A popular proverb in exile: we are suffering for our parents' sins. Ezekiel quotes and refutes the same saying (Ezek 18:2–4). The Law did warn that the effects of sin would reach later generations of those who hate God (Exod 20:5), and Judah's fall was the climax of long generations of sin (2 Kgs 24:3–4). But the proverb was being used as an excuse.
- **"But everyone shall die for his own iniquity" (v30).** Each person stands before God responsible for his own life (Deut 24:16). In the new era, no one will be able to hide behind a parent's failures.
- **Commentators:** On God's love in v3, Henry writes, "It is the happiness of those who are through grace interested in the love of God that it is an everlasting love." Guzik, following Harrison, stresses *chesed* as covenant faithfulness and argues that God's love for Israel as a people has not been withdrawn. On Ephraim's "turn me" in v18, Henry sees the right prayer of the penitent: "Turn thou me and I shall be turned," since only grace can turn a heart, and JFB likewise says repentance follows God's turning of the sinner. On Rachel, Henry notes the verse "is applied by the evangelists to the great mourning that was at Bethlehem," and Guzik, following Harrison and Cundall, explains Matthew's use as a type, a pattern repeated, not a direct prediction. On v22 the three part ways. JFB argues at length for the church fathers' reading of the virgin birth. Henry reads the woman as "the church of God, that is weak and feeble as a woman," prevailing over the strong. Guzik thinks the virgin-birth reading is not supported by the text and takes it, with Cundall, as a picture of a security so great that the weak protect the strong. On v26 JFB says "'Sleep' here means dream," and Henry draws a pastoral lesson: "Those may sleep sweetly that lie down and rise up in the favour of God."

#### The New Covenant (vv31–40)
- **"Behold, the days are coming… when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah" (v31).** This is the only place in the Old Testament where the phrase "new covenant" (*berit chadashah*) appears, though the idea is present elsewhere (32:40; Ezek 36:26–27; 37:26). A covenant in the Bible is a binding relationship sealed by oath, with promises and obligations. God had made covenants with Noah, Abraham, Israel at Sinai and David. This one is promised to the whole reunited people.
- **"Not like the covenant that I made with their fathers… when I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt" (v32).** The covenant at Sinai (Exod 19–24). "Took them by the hand" is tender, like a father leading a small child (Hos 11:3). **"My covenant that they broke."** The problem was not the law, which was "holy and righteous and good" (Rom 7:12). The problem was the people's hearts. Jeremiah's book is one long record of that broken covenant (11:1–10).
- **"Though I was their husband" (v32).** The verb is *ba'al*, to be master or husband. The covenant was a marriage, and Israel was the unfaithful bride (3:14). The Greek translation, which Hebrews 8:9 quotes, reads "and I showed no concern for them." It probably read a Hebrew word that looks almost the same. The point in both is that the broken covenant brought consequences.
- **"I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (v33).** This is the great difference. The Sinai covenant was written on tablets of stone (Exod 31:18). Moses had urged Israel to have God's words "on your heart" (Deut 6:6), and Jeremiah had said Judah's sin was "engraved on the tablet of their heart" with an iron pen (17:1). The new covenant does not throw away God's law. It puts it inside. "Law" is *torah*, God's instruction. God himself will do the writing, changing the heart so that it wants what God wants. Ezekiel describes the same thing as a new heart and a new spirit, God's own Spirit put within (Ezek 36:26–27). Paul calls believers "a letter from Christ… written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts" (2 Cor 3:3).
- **"And I will be their God, and they shall be my people" (v33).** The covenant formula from 30:22 and Exodus 6:7. The new covenant fulfills what every earlier covenant aimed at.
- **"No longer shall each one teach his neighbor… saying, 'Know the LORD,' for they shall all know me" (v34).** "Know" (*yada*) means personal, experiential knowledge, the word used of intimate relationship. Under the old covenant many Israelites knew about God through priests and prophets while their hearts stayed far from him (Hos 4:1, 6). In the new, every member will know God personally, **"from the least of them to the greatest."** This does not end teaching in the church (Matt 28:20; Eph 4:11). It means no member of the new covenant will be an outsider who needs to be introduced to God.
- **"For I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (v34).** "For" makes forgiveness the foundation of everything else. Knowing God begins with being forgiven by him. "Remember no more" does not mean God forgets in the sense of losing information. It means he will never again bring those sins up against his people (Isa 43:25; Mic 7:19). Hebrews draws the conclusion: "Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin" (Heb 10:17–18).
- **Who the new covenant is for.** Jeremiah addressed it to Israel and Judah. Jesus took it up the night before he died: "This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25). Hebrews 8 quotes vv31–34 in full and says Jesus is its mediator. Christians agree that the new covenant was established by Christ's death and that Gentile believers share in it (Eph 2:12–13). They differ on how it relates to the nation of Israel. Many dispensational interpreters stress that it will still be fulfilled for ethnic Israel as a nation at Christ's return (Rom 11:26–27), while many Reformed and covenant interpreters see it fulfilled in the one church of Jews and Gentiles. Both readings hold that Jews who believe in Jesus share it now.
- **"Thus says the LORD, who gives the sun for light by day and the fixed order of the moon and the stars" (v35).** God guarantees his promise by pointing to creation. "Fixed order" (*chuqqot*) is the word for laws or statutes, used of the decrees that govern the heavens (33:25; Job 38:33). **"Who stirs up the sea so that its waves roar."** This line is borrowed from Isaiah 51:15.
- **"If this fixed order departs… then shall the offspring of Israel cease from being a nation before me forever" (v36).** As long as the sun rises, God's people will not be wiped out. **"If the heavens above can be measured… then I will cast off all the offspring of Israel for all that they have done" (v37).** To an ancient reader the size of the heavens and the depth of the earth's foundations were the very picture of the immeasurable. It is no less true today. God will not cast off his people, "for all that they have done," a phrase that admits how much they had done. Paul makes the same argument: "Has God rejected his people? By no means!" (Rom 11:1–2).
- **"The city shall be rebuilt for the LORD from the Tower of Hananel to the Corner Gate" (v38).** The Tower of Hananel stood at the northeast corner of the city wall (Neh 3:1; 12:39; Zech 14:10). The Corner Gate was at the northwest corner (2 Kgs 14:13). The line runs along the north wall, the side from which every invader came.
- **"The measuring line shall go out farther, straight to the hill Gareb, and shall then turn to Goah" (v39).** Neither place is mentioned anywhere else in Scripture, and their locations are unknown. They were probably on the west and southwest sides. The line goes "farther" than the old wall. The new city is bigger.
- **"The whole valley of the dead bodies and the ashes… shall be sacred to the LORD" (v40).** The Valley of Hinnom, south of the city, where children had been burned to Molech and which Jeremiah said would become "the Valley of Slaughter," with corpses unburied (7:31–33; 19:6). The "ashes" are the fatty ashes of sacrifices or the city's refuse dump. The most defiled ground in Judah will become holy. **"As far as the brook Kidron, to the corner of the Horse Gate toward the east."** The Kidron runs down the east side of the city, and the Horse Gate was near the palace (Neh 3:28). **"It shall not be plucked up or overthrown anymore forever."** The last words echo Jeremiah's call (1:10) once more. Destruction will never again be the last word.
- **Commentators:** All three agree that the new covenant was made through Christ, citing Hebrews 8. JFB holds it is made "with literal Israel and Judah," with believing Gentiles sharing it secondarily, and says one mark of its superiority is "securing by an adequate atonement the forgiveness of sins." Guzik takes a similar view of Israel. He stresses that the new covenant does not abolish the law but writes it on the heart, so that obedience becomes a gift of the covenant rather than the price of entering it. Henry, in his summary, says the blessings are secured "even to the spiritual seed of Israel for ever," which places more weight on the church. On vv35–37 Guzik argues that Israel's future as a people is as fixed as creation. On v39 JFB explains Gareb as the place outside the city where lepers were sent, from a root meaning "to scrape," and Goah as a "toilsome ascent." Those are guesses based on the names. Neither place is known.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every promise in this chapter finds its center in Jesus. He is the Shepherd who gathers the scattered (John 10:16), the Kinsman who redeems at the cost of his own life (Mark 10:45), the Son through whom the Father's yearning love runs to meet the returning child. When Rachel's weeping was heard again in Bethlehem, he was the child who survived, and he would one day give his life for the sons of others. Above all, he is the one who made the new covenant real. At a supper table he took a cup and said, "This is the new covenant in my blood." Through his death, God's law is written on hearts by the Spirit, God is known by the least and the greatest, and sins are remembered no more.
