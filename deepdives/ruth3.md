*Setting:* The barley and wheat harvests are over, about seven weeks after Naomi and Ruth arrived in Bethlehem (2:23). Ruth has gleaned every day in the fields of Boaz, a wealthy relative of Naomi's dead husband, and Naomi has realized he is "one of our redeemers" (2:20), a kinsman with the right and duty to rescue the family. But Boaz has done nothing more than be kind. With the harvest finished, the daily contact ends, and the two widows still have no man to secure their future or carry on the family line. In chapter 1 Naomi could only pray that God would give her daughters-in-law "rest" in a husband's house (1:9). In chapter 3 she makes a plan. The whole chapter happens in one night at the threshing floor, and it is delicate, risky and full of meaning that the original readers would have caught.

#### Ruth and Boaz at the Threshing Floor (vv1–18)
- **"Should I not seek rest for you, that it may be well with you?" (v1).** "Rest" here is *manoach*, close kin to the word Naomi used in 1:9 (*menuchah*): a settled home with a husband. The bitter widow who said she had nothing is now thinking about someone else's future.
- **"Is not Boaz our relative…? He is winnowing barley tonight" (v2).** The threshing floor was a flat area of packed earth or bedrock, often on high ground outside town, where the harvest was brought. Stalks were beaten or trampled to loosen the grain, and then winnowed: tossed into the air with a wooden fork so the evening breeze carried off the chaff while the heavier grain fell back. Winnowing was done in the late afternoon and evening, when the wind off the Mediterranean picked up. The owner often slept by the grain pile to guard it from thieves.
- **"Wash therefore and anoint yourself, and put on your cloak" (v3).** Naomi's instructions sound like preparing a bride. Ezekiel uses the same steps for God's care of Israel as his bride: "I bathed you… and anointed you with oil. I clothed you" (Ezek 16:9–10). Some think Ruth was also setting aside her widow's clothing (compare 2 Sam 14:2). The message is that her mourning is over and she is ready to marry. In the Hebrew text v3 has a small spelling quirk: the written form of "go down" reads "I will go down," while the reading tradition has "you will go down." Translations rightly follow the second.
- **"Do not make yourself known to the man until he has finished eating and drinking" (v3).** The end of harvest was a time of celebration, and a man who has eaten well is relaxed and approachable. Naomi's timing is shrewd.
- **"Uncover his feet and lie down, and he will tell you what to do" (v4).** This is the most debated instruction in the book. The word for "feet" (*margelot*) is not the usual word. It means the place of the feet, the lower part of the body where his legs lay, and it appears elsewhere only in Daniel 10:6. The Old Testament sometimes uses "feet" as a euphemism, and some readers see sexual overtones in the scene. Most interpreters, however, take it as a bold but chaste symbolic act: uncovering his feet would let the cool night air wake him, and lying at someone's feet was a posture of humble petition. The rest of the chapter supports that reading. Boaz treats it as a proposal, praises Ruth's character and sends her home before dawn with her reputation intact. The narrator, who never wastes a word, says only that she lay "at his feet" (vv8, 14).
- **"All that you say I will do" (vv5–6).** Ruth's obedience is complete: she "did just as her mother-in-law had commanded her." Yet at the key moment she will go beyond the script.
- **"His heart was merry" (v7).** It means he was content and in good spirits, not drunk. The same idiom appears in Judges 19:6, where the Levite's father-in-law urges him to "let your heart be merry." "She came softly," quietly, so as not to wake him or anyone nearby.
- **"At midnight the man was startled… behold, a woman lay at his feet!" (v8).** "Startled" means he shuddered or jerked awake. In the dark he cannot see who she is. The word "behold" lets the reader share his surprise.
- **"I am Ruth, your servant" (v9).** In chapter 2 she called herself a *shiphchah* (2:13), the lowest kind of servant. Here she uses *amah*, a word many scholars think marked a servant who could become a wife. It is a small change with a large meaning.
- **"Spread your wings over your servant, for you are a redeemer" (v9).** "Wings" is *kanaph*, which also means the corner or edge of a garment. To spread a garment over a woman was a recognized way of claiming her in marriage. God uses the same phrase of taking Israel as his bride: "I spread the corner of my garment over you… and you became mine" (Ezek 16:8). There is a beautiful echo here. In 2:12 Boaz prayed that Ruth would find refuge under the LORD's "wings." Now she asks Boaz to be the answer to his own prayer. Naomi had said, "he will tell you what to do." Instead Ruth tells him. And she adds "for you are a redeemer," joining two duties that were not automatically linked: buying back family land and marrying a dead relative's widow to give him an heir. She is asking Boaz to do both.
- **"You have made this last kindness greater than the first" (v10).** "Kindness" is again *hesed*, loyal love. Her first kindness was staying with Naomi. This "last kindness" is choosing to marry within Elimelech's family so that Mahlon's name and land would not die out, when she could have married any young man she liked, "whether poor or rich." His words also suggest that Boaz was noticeably older and had not expected her to choose him.
- **"Do not fear… you are a worthy woman" (v11).** "Worthy woman" is *eshet chayil*, the same phrase used of the ideal wife in Proverbs 31:10, "An excellent wife who can find?" Boaz himself was introduced as a man of *chayil* (2:1). In the Hebrew Bible's order, Ruth comes right after Proverbs, so the "worthy woman" of Proverbs 31 is followed by a real one, and she is a Moabite. "All my fellow townsmen" is literally "all the gate of my people": the whole town, where public business was done at the gate, already respects her.
- **"There is a redeemer nearer than I" (v12).** Boaz is honest. Another man is closer kin and has the first right. Boaz will not go around him, even though he plainly wants to marry Ruth. He binds himself with an oath, "as the LORD lives," to act if the other man will not (v13).
- **"Lie down until the morning" (v13).** Sending her back through the dark streets at midnight would have been dangerous, so he lets her rest there.
- **"Arose before one could recognize another… let it not be known" (v14).** She leaves in the gray light before dawn. Boaz protects her reputation, and his own, and avoids any appearance of having struck a private deal before the nearer kinsman has had his chance.
- **Six measures of barley (v15).** The unit is not stated. If it was a *seah* (about a third of an ephah), six would be roughly twice what she gleaned on her first day (2:17), a heavy but possible load for a strong woman, carried in her large shawl and lifted onto her shoulder or head. The gift reassures Naomi and is a pledge of good faith. Most Hebrew manuscripts say "*he* went into the city," meaning Boaz went off to settle things, while some manuscripts and old translations read "*she*," as the ESV does. Both fit the story.
- **"You must not go back empty-handed to your mother-in-law" (v17).** "Empty-handed" is *reqam*, the same word Naomi used in her lament: "the LORD has brought me back empty" (1:21). Boaz did not know Naomi had said this, but the narrator wants the reader to hear it. The God who seemed to empty Naomi is filling her again, measure by measure.
- **"Wait, my daughter… for the man will not rest" (v18).** "Wait" is literally "sit." Ruth has done all she can. Now the matter is in Boaz's hands, and Naomi trusts that he will not rest until it is settled that very day. Ruth's "rest" (v1) depends on Boaz's refusing to rest.
- **Readings differ.** Some modern scholars see more risk, and even sexual tension, in Naomi's plan, and they read it as a daring test of Boaz's honor. Others stress the symbolic, legal side of the scene. Both see the same outcome: in a dark place, at night, with every chance to do wrong, the two act with integrity, and the chapter that could have echoed the dark nights of Judges 19 instead echoes the faithfulness of God.
- **Commentators:** Henry admits Naomi's plan "looks suspicious," but argues that customs differed: "We must therefore think that the thing did not look so ill then as it does now." He praises Boaz's self-control: "He did not offer to violate her chastity, though he had all the opportunity that could be." On v18 he applies Naomi's words to trusting providence: "Sit still, my daughter." Guzik reads lying at the feet as an act of humble submission, not seduction, while admitting the plan was risky and depended on Boaz's character. He ties the spread garment to Ezekiel 16, praises Boaz for doing God's will in the proper order rather than cutting corners, and notes a Jewish tradition reading the six measures as a sign of six descendants, David and the Messiah among them. JFB explains that winnowing was done in the evening "to catch the breezes which blow after the close of a hot day," says that spreading a skirt over someone was "a symbolical action denoting protection" that meant marriage, and reckons the six measures as "six seahs," "rather a heavy load for a woman."

#### Pointing to Christ
Ruth came to her redeemer at night with nothing to offer but herself, and asked him to spread his covering over her. He received her, called her worthy, promised to redeem her, and would not rest until it was done. That is a picture of what Christ does for his people. He is the redeemer who spreads his covering over the needy (Ezek 16:8), and his church is his bride (Eph 5:25–27). Boaz could only act after a nearer kinsman stood aside. Christ has no rival claim to wait on. He finished the work he came to do, and like Boaz, he "will not rest" until all who come to him are brought home.
