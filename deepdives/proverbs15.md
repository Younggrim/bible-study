*Setting:* The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 15 is the last chapter of the first half of the collection. Many scholars notice a change at 16:1: chapters 10–15 are mostly two-line contrasts ("but"), and from chapter 16 on there are more sayings that compare or build on a thought. Chapter 15 also mentions the LORD more often than any chapter so far: he sees everywhere (v3), weighs worship (vv8–9, 29), knows every heart (v11), defends widows (v25). Its sayings on the tongue are some of the best loved in the book, starting with "A soft answer turns away wrath."

#### Gentle Words and the Watching Eyes of God (vv1–7)
- **"A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (v1).** "Soft" (*rak*) means tender or gentle (JFB). "Harsh word" is literally "a word of pain," a word that hurts. "Stirs up" (JFB) is like blowing on smoldering coals. Gideon calmed the angry men of Ephraim with a gentle answer (Judg 8:1–3), while Jephthah's sharp reply to the same tribe led to civil war (Judg 12:1–6). Guzik draws that very contrast. Henry: "If wrath be risen like a threatening cloud… a soft answer will disperse it," and "nothing stirs up anger, and sows discord, like grievous words." Soft does not mean weak. A gentle answer takes more strength than a harsh one (25:15).
- **"The tongue of the wise commends knowledge, but the mouths of fools pour out folly" (v2).** "Commends" means makes it attractive. The Tyndale notes: a wise teacher presents truth in a way people want to hear. Fools "pour out" folly like water from a spring that never shuts off.
- **"The eyes of the LORD are in every place, keeping watch on the evil and the good" (v3).** God sees everything, in every place, all the time (Ps 139:7–12; Heb 4:13). "Keeping watch" is the word for a watchman on the wall. Guzik stresses that God's watching is active, not idle. This is a warning to the wicked, and a comfort to the good: "the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to give strong support to those whose heart is blameless toward him" (2 Chr 16:9).
- **"A gentle tongue is a tree of life, but perverseness in it breaks the spirit" (v4).** "Gentle" is literally "healing" (*marpeʾ*), the same word as in 12:18 and 14:30. This is the fourth "tree of life" in Proverbs (3:18; 11:30; 13:12). Words can heal, and twisted, crooked words crush a person's spirit. JFB: soothing language versus words that grieve.
- **"A fool despises his father's instruction, but whoever heeds reproof is prudent" (v5).** The Tyndale notes: to be wise, learn from mistakes. Prudence is not having no faults but being willing to hear about them.
- **"In the house of the righteous there is much treasure, but trouble befalls the income of the wicked" (v6).** The treasure may be wealth or something deeper. JFB says it "implies utility": the righteous have what is useful. The wicked man's income brings trouble with it. The Tyndale notes: wealth gained wrongly brings problems instead of blessing.
- **"The lips of the wise spread knowledge; not so the hearts of fools" (v7).** "Spread" is the word for scattering seed. The wise sow knowledge wherever they go. Fools have nothing to sow.
- **Commentators:** Henry and Guzik both linger on v1. Guzik uses Gideon and Jephthah, and compares gentle speech to oil on a wound. Henry pictures wrath as a storm cloud dispersed by a soft answer. JFB on v4 reads "a breach of spirit" as grieving instead of calming.

#### What God Delights In (vv8–15)
- **"The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to the LORD, but the prayer of the upright is acceptable to him" (v8).** A sacrifice was costly. A prayer cost nothing. Yet God rejects the expensive offering of the wicked and delights in the simple prayer of the upright. The heart, not the gift, is what he weighs. Guzik links Samuel's rebuke of Saul: "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22). The prophets said the same (Isa 1:11–17; Amos 5:21–24; Mic 6:6–8). Compare 21:27 and 28:9.
- **"The way of the wicked is an abomination to the LORD, but he loves him who pursues righteousness" (v9).** Verse 8 was about worship, verse 9 about everyday life. God cares about both, and worship cannot be separated from conduct. "Pursues" is a strong verb, to chase after.
- **"There is severe discipline for him who forsakes the way; whoever hates reproof will die" (v10).** The person who leaves the right path will meet hard correction. If even that is refused, the end is death. JFB gives only cross-references here.
- **"Sheol and Abaddon lie open before the LORD; how much more the hearts of the children of man!" (v11).** *Sheol* is the realm of the dead. *Abaddon* means "destruction," the place of ruin, paired with Sheol in Job 26:6 and 28:22. In Revelation 9:11 Abaddon becomes the name of the angel of the abyss. The Tyndale notes take it as another name for the realm of the dead, with a note of punishment. If God sees even the depths of the dead, the hidden places of a human heart are wide open to him. JFB: "All the unseen world is open to God." This is a "how much more" argument, common in the rabbis and used often by Jesus (Matt 7:11).
- **"A scoffer does not like to be reproved; he will not go to the wise" (v12).** The scoffer doesn't just reject correction, he avoids the people who might give it. The Tyndale notes: he cannot grow wise because he dodges criticism.
- **"A glad heart makes a cheerful face, but by sorrow of heart the spirit is crushed" (v13).** The inner life shows on the face. "Crushed" (*nekeʾah*) means stricken, broken. Guzik notes that biblical cheerfulness is a calm, godly joy, not loud worldly fun. Compare 17:22.
- **"The heart of him who has understanding seeks knowledge, but the mouths of fools feed on folly" (v14).** "Feed" is the shepherd's verb, grazing. Fools graze on folly as cattle graze on grass. What a person feeds on shows who they are. JFB: the wise grow wiser, and fools more foolish.
- **"All the days of the afflicted are evil, but the cheerful of heart has a continual feast" (v15).** "Afflicted" (*ʿani*) means poor, oppressed. For someone ground down by hardship, every day is hard. But a cheerful heart, literally "good of heart," turns ordinary days into a feast. The Tyndale notes and JFB both say the heart shapes how life is experienced. Paul learned contentment in every circumstance (Phil 4:11–13).
- **Commentators:** Guzik on v8 says God rejects ritual without obedience. The Tyndale notes connect Psalm 40:6–8 and Micah 6. Guzik on v11 uses the verse to show God's complete knowledge. JFB says the unseen world lies open to him.

#### Better a Little with Love (vv16–23)
- **"Better is a little with the fear of the LORD than great treasure and trouble with it" (v16).** "Trouble" (*mehumah*) is turmoil, confusion, panic. JFB: "the anxieties and perplexities attending wealth." The Tyndale notes: fearing God calms inner turmoil. Psalm 37:16 says the same, and Paul calls "godliness with contentment" great gain (1 Tim 6:6).
- **"Better is a dinner of herbs where love is than a fattened ox and hatred with it" (v17).** A "dinner of herbs" was the plain vegetable meal of the poor. JFB: "the plainest food." A "fattened ox" was a luxury, meat fed in a stall for a feast (1 Kgs 4:23; Luke 15:23). The Tyndale notes put it in modern terms: steak is nice, but love matters more. Guzik: love and peace are worth far more than possessions. Compare 17:1.
- **"A hot-tempered man stirs up strife, but he who is slow to anger quiets contention" (v18).** The hot-head lights fires. The patient person puts them out. The Tyndale notes connect James 1:19–20.
- **"The way of a sluggard is like a hedge of thorns, but the path of the upright is a level highway" (v19).** To the lazy, every road is overgrown with thorns, so he never starts (22:13; 26:13). "Level highway" (*selulah*) is a raised, built-up road. Notice the contrast: not the sluggard and the diligent, but the sluggard and the upright. Laziness is a moral failure, not just a practical one. The Tyndale notes: the lazy always find excuses.
- **"A wise son makes a glad father, but a foolish man despises his mother" (v20).** The first line repeats 10:1. The second is sharper: not "grieves" but "despises." The foolish son has grown into a foolish man who looks down on the mother who raised him.
- **"Folly is a joy to him who lacks sense, but a man of understanding walks straight ahead" (v21).** The fool enjoys his folly. The wise person keeps a straight course.
- **"Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed" (v22).** Similar to 11:14 and 20:18. "Counsel" (*sod*) is a confidential conversation among trusted friends. JFB: a wise deference to the views of the wise. Guzik: be humble about one's own judgment. The Tyndale notes: no one sees every side of a problem.
- **"To make an apt answer is a joy to a man, and a word in season, how good it is!" (v23).** "A word in season" is literally "a word in its time." Timing is part of wisdom (25:11; Eccl 3:7; Isa 50:4). JFB: "Good advice blesses the giver and receiver." Guzik: the timing of a helpful word is part of its value.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and the Tyndale notes both make vv16–17 about the priority of love and peace over wealth. JFB reads the "trouble" of v16 as the anxiety that riches bring.

#### The Path of Life and the Fear of the LORD (vv24–33)
- **"The path of life leads upward for the prudent, that he may turn away from Sheol beneath" (v24).** Two directions: upward toward life, downward toward the grave. In context it probably means a long, full life rather than an early death, but the picture of rising "up" away from Sheol opens a window on more. JFB: "Holy purposes prevent sinning," and he points to Colossians 3:2, "Set your minds on things that are above."
- **"The LORD tears down the house of the proud but maintains the widow's boundaries" (v25).** A widow with no husband or grown sons had no one to defend her land. Greedy neighbors could quietly move the boundary stones that marked her fields (Deut 19:14; 27:17; Prov 23:10–11). But God takes her side. The Tyndale notes contrast the proud, who think they can look after themselves, with widows, who were the most vulnerable people in the ancient world. God is "father of the fatherless and protector of widows" (Ps 68:5).
- **"The thoughts of the wicked are an abomination to the LORD, but gracious words are pure" (v26).** "Thoughts" are plans or schemes. "Gracious words" are pleasant, kind words. God hears not only speech but the plans behind it.
- **"Whoever is greedy for unjust gain troubles his own household, but he who hates bribes will live" (v27).** "Troubles his household" repeats 11:29, the word used of Achan, whose greed destroyed his family (Josh 7:24–25). Bribes were forbidden because they "blind the eyes of the wise" (Deut 16:19). Samuel's sons took bribes, and Israel demanded a king (1 Sam 8:3–5).
- **"The heart of the righteous ponders how to answer, but the mouth of the wicked pours out evil things" (v28).** "Ponders" is the word for meditating, the word used of the righteous on God's law (Ps 1:2). Think before speaking. JFB: caution is the fruit of wisdom, rashness of folly.
- **"The LORD is far from the wicked, but he hears the prayer of the righteous" (v29).** God is everywhere (v3), so "far" means distant in favor, not in place. JFB: far "in His love and favor." The Tyndale notes: sincerity matters in prayer. Compare 15:8 and Psalm 34:15–18.
- **"The light of the eyes rejoices the heart, and good news refreshes the bones" (v30).** "Light of the eyes" may be the sparkle in a friend's face, or simply something good to see. "Refreshes" is literally "makes fat," healthy and strong. Good news really does strengthen a person. The word for "good news" (*shemuʿah tobah*) points ahead to the gospel, the best report of all (Isa 52:7).
- **"The ear that listens to life-giving reproof will dwell among the wise" (vv31–32).** Correction is called "reproof of life," correction that leads to life. Refusing it is not just stubborn but self-harming: "whoever ignores instruction despises himself." The person who listens "gains heart," gains understanding. JFB: refusing instruction amounts to despising one's own soul.
- **"The fear of the LORD is instruction in wisdom, and humility comes before honor" (v33).** The first half of the collection ends on the motto of the whole book (1:7; 9:10). Reverence for God is the school wisdom is learned in. And humility comes first, honor afterward, the reverse of 16:18, where pride comes before a fall. The Tyndale notes: fearing God makes a person humble because it shows we are not self-sufficient. Guzik: humility grows from seeing God as he is and ourselves as we are. JFB points to Christ's own path through suffering into glory (Luke 24:26; 1 Pet 1:11).
- **Commentators:** JFB applies v24 to setting the mind on things above and links v33 to Christ's humility before glory. Guzik's comments on v33 echo James 4:10, that God lifts up the humble. The Tyndale notes on v25 stress how exposed widows were.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 15 ends with "humility comes before honor," and that is the shape of Jesus' whole life. He "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death… Therefore God has highly exalted him" (Phil 2:8–9), the very connection JFB sees. He gave the "soft answer" before his accusers (Isa 53:7) and spoke "a word in season" to the weary (Isa 50:4; Matt 11:28). He is the one whose prayer God always hears (John 11:41–42), and through whom the prayers of the upright are accepted (Heb 10:19–22). He defends the widow (Luke 7:11–15; 18:1–8) and condemns those who "devour widows' houses" (Mark 12:40). And he brought the "good news" that refreshes the bones (v30), the gospel itself.
