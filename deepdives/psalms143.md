*Setting:* Psalm 143 is the fourth of the five laments (Psalms 140–144) in the last collection of David's psalms. Its heading is simply "A Psalm of David," with no event named. Some older interpreters, following the Greek Septuagint's added heading, tie it to Absalom's rebellion, when David fled Jerusalem (2 Sam 15–17). Others hear the fugitive years under Saul, as in Psalm 142. Guzik leaves the question open, and the text itself does not settle it. What is clear is the situation: an enemy is pressing hard, David's strength is gone, and he has nowhere to turn but God. Church tradition counts Psalm 143 as the last of the seven "penitential psalms" (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143), the psalms of confession the early church sang in Lent. It earns that place mostly through one verse: "no one living is righteous before you" (v2). Paul echoes that line twice when he explains that no one is justified by works of the law (Rom 3:20; Gal 2:16), and Luther, as Guzik reports, counted this among the "Pauline Psalms." JFB calls it "a prayer for pardon, and for relief from enemies; afflictions, as usual, producing confession and penitence." The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them. A *Selah* after v6 divides the psalm into two halves: lament (vv1–6) and petition (vv7–12).

#### Enter Not into Judgment (vv1–2)
- **"Hear my prayer, O LORD; give ear to my pleas for mercy!" (v1).** The two lines say the same thing twice, the usual pattern of Hebrew poetry, for emphasis. "Pleas for mercy" (*tachanunim*) are requests for grace, for favor that is not owed. David is not making a claim. He is asking.
- **"In your faithfulness answer me, in your righteousness!" (v1).** David grounds the request in God's character, not his own. God's "faithfulness" (*'emunah*) is his reliability, his keeping of his word. God's "righteousness" (*tsedaqah*) here is not the strict justice that condemns. In the Psalms it often means God doing what is right by his covenant people, setting things right and rescuing the wronged (Ps 31:1; 71:2). JFB says God honors "the claims His people may make under the covenant." David appeals to the same righteousness again at the end (v11), so the psalm begins and ends on it.
- **"Enter not into judgment with your servant" (v2).** This is courtroom language. David asks God not to take him to trial. If God judged him strictly on his record, he would lose. The surprising thing is the order: he has just appealed to God's righteousness, and now he begs God not to judge him by it. The two belong together. God's righteousness is the ground of hope for those who stop pleading their own.
- **"For no one living is righteous before you" (v2).** "Is righteous" (*yitsdaq*) means "is in the right," "is justified," the verdict a judge gives. No living person can stand before God and be declared innocent on his own merits (Job 9:2; 25:4; Eccl 7:20). David includes himself. Paul quotes the substance of this line, following the Greek wording "no flesh," in Romans 3:20 and Galatians 2:16. The Tyndale notes point to Romans 3:20–24, where Paul moves from that universal guilt straight to righteousness given as a gift through faith in Christ.
- **"Your servant" (v2).** David calls himself God's servant here and again in the psalm's last line (v12). JFB connects the phrase to God's promise to "my servant David" in 2 Samuel 7, the covenant that made him king. A servant has no merit to offer, but he does belong to a master who has made promises.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We have no righteousness of our own to plead, and therefore must plead God's righteousness." Guzik passes on VanGemeren's point that some psalms protest innocence (Ps 7) while this one confesses guilt, and that both are honest prayers in their place: a believer may be innocent of a particular charge and still unable to stand on his own before God. He also notes Maclaren's observation that danger drove David to deal with his sin before asking for rescue.

#### Crushed to the Ground (vv3–4)
- **"For the enemy has pursued my soul" (v3).** "For" gives the reason for the prayer. "Pursued" (*radaf*) is the word for hunting down, the same verb used of Saul chasing David (1 Sam 23:25; 26:18). "My soul" (*nafshi*) means "my life," or simply "me."
- **"He has crushed my life to the ground" (v3).** "Crushed" (*dikka'*) means beaten down, ground into the dust. The picture is a man knocked flat and pinned.
- **"He has made me sit in darkness like those long dead" (v3).** This line appears almost word for word in Lamentations 3:6: "he has made me dwell in darkness like the dead of long ago." The darkness is the darkness of the grave. David feels as cut off from life as someone buried long ago and forgotten. JFB explains it as being cut off from life's comforts. Clarke, as Guzik notes, wondered whether it also recalls literal caves where David hid.
- **"Therefore my spirit faints within me" (v4).** "Faints" (*tit'attef*) is the same verb as in Psalm 142:3, "when my spirit faints within me." It means to grow weak, to be wrapped in darkness.
- **"My heart within me is appalled" (v4).** "Appalled" (*yishtomem*) is from a root meaning desolate, stunned, devastated. The Hebrew "heart" is the center of thought and will, not just feeling. David's inner life is numb, like a ruined, empty landscape. The Tyndale notes simply call it losing all hope. The psalm does not hide that a man of faith can feel this way.
- **Commentators:** Henry says David "bemoans the weight upon his mind from outward troubles." JFB treats vv3–4 together as the reason for the plea and the distress that leads to confession. Guzik points out that this misery is caused by an enemy, not by a gloomy temperament, and connects the words to Jesus in Gethsemane, "very sorrowful, even to death" (Matt 26:38). He passes on Spurgeon's remark that Christ the head and his people the members share the same experiences.

#### I Remember the Days of Old (vv5–6)
- **"I remember the days of old; I meditate on all that you have done" (v5).** Three verbs climb: remember, meditate, ponder. "Meditate" (*hagah*) is the word in Psalm 1:2, a low, murmuring reflection, turning something over aloud. "Ponder" (*'asocheach*) is related to the "complaint" or musing of Psalm 142:2. The same troubled mind that circled around its trouble now circles around God's works. The "days of old" include the exodus and God's dealings with Israel, and David's own earlier rescues. Psalm 77 makes the same move: in the night of trouble, "I will remember the deeds of the LORD; yes, I will remember your wonders of old" (77:11).
- **Memory as medicine.** The Tyndale notes say remembering God's acts reassures the psalmist of God's faithfulness (42:4; 63:6; 77:3–6). The present gives no grounds for hope, so David looks back to what God has already done. JFB takes it the other way as well: remembering better days sharpens the pain by contrast, and makes him long for their return. Both are true to experience.
- **"I stretch out my hands to you" (v6).** "Stretch out" (*perasti*) means to spread out, the posture of prayer in Israel: standing, arms raised, palms open (Exod 9:29; 1 Kings 8:22; Ps 28:2). Open hands are empty hands, held out to receive.
- **"My soul thirsts for you like a parched land" (v6).** "Parched" (*'ayefah*) means faint, weary, exhausted, often from thirst. It is the "dry and weary land where there is no water" of Psalm 63:1. In Israel's dry summer, cracked ground waits for the autumn rains. David's soul is like that ground. Henry: "as the parched ground for refreshing rain." David does not only want his problem fixed. He wants God himself (Ps 42:1–2).
- **"Selah" (v6).** A musical or liturgical marker, probably a pause. Its exact meaning is unknown. Here it falls at the turn of the psalm, between lament and petition.
- **Commentators:** Henry says David "remembers God's former appearance for his afflicted people." Guzik stresses that this is not nostalgia but deliberate recall of God's works, and passes on Spurgeon's advice to look back to past deliverances when nothing in the present encourages. He also quotes Horne through Spurgeon on Christ, whose hands were stretched out and whose soul thirsted on the cross (John 19:28).

#### Answer Me Quickly (vv7–8)
- **"Answer me quickly, O LORD! My spirit fails!" (v7).** The second half of the psalm is a rush of short requests, about eleven in six verses. "Fails" (*kaletah*) means comes to an end, is used up. JFB: "is exhausted." The Tyndale notes describe it as depression, the anguish of someone who feels his life draining away (31:10; 119:81).
- **"Hide not your face from me" (v7).** God's face turned toward someone means favor, the blessing of Numbers 6:25–26: "the LORD make his face to shine upon you." God's hidden face means the sense of his favor withdrawn (Ps 13:1; 27:9; 30:7). For David that is worse than any enemy.
- **"Lest I be like those who go down to the pit" (v7).** "The pit" (*bor*) is a cistern or dungeon, and in the Psalms a picture of the grave (Ps 28:1; 88:4). This line is also nearly identical to Psalm 28:1. Without God's face, David is as good as dead.
- **"Let me hear in the morning of your steadfast love" (v8).** "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) is God's loyal, covenant love, the love that keeps its promises and does not let go. It returns in v12, bracketing the second half of the psalm. "In the morning" may simply mean "soon" or "first thing," and it also looks past the night of vv3–4. Morning in the Psalms is the time God's help arrives (Ps 30:5; 46:5; 90:14). Kidner, as Guzik reports, hears David looking ahead: the night will not last forever.
- **"For in you I trust" (v8).** Each request in vv7–10 comes with a reason, and the reasons are all about God: in you I trust, to you I lift up my soul, to you I have fled, you are my God.
- **"Make me know the way I should go, for to you I lift up my soul" (v8).** David asks for direction as well as rescue. JFB reads the way as "the way of safety and righteousness." "To you I lift up my soul" is the opening line of Psalm 25 (25:1), another psalm asking to be taught God's paths (25:4–5). Lifting up the soul means directing all one's longing and trust toward God.
- **Commentators:** Henry: David "pleads the wretchedness of his case, if God withdrew from him," and he "prays that he might be enlightened with the knowledge of God's will." Guzik discusses the Hebrew *chesed*. He notes that Nelson Glueck argued in 1927 that it means covenant loyalty, and sides with R. Laird Harris that the traditional sense of love and mercy should not be lost. Most lexicons today hold both together: loyal love. He also passes on Spurgeon's remark that when the soul will not rise by itself, it must be lifted.

#### Teach Me to Do Your Will (vv9–10)
- **"Deliver me from my enemies, O LORD! I have fled to you for refuge" (v9).** The Hebrew of the second line is short and a little unusual, literally "to you I have covered" or "hidden." The ESV, with most translations, takes it as "I have hidden myself in you," fled to God for shelter. Some ancient translations read slightly different consonants and get "I have fled to you." Either way, God is David's hiding place. Henry's comment passes over this verse, and JFB gives only cross-references.
- **"Teach me to do your will, for you are my God!" (v10).** David asks not only to know God's will but to do it. Henry: "Not only show me what thy will is, but teach me how to do it." The reason is personal: "you are my God." A servant (vv2, 12) wants to please his own master. For a man surrounded by enemies, it is striking that the request turns inward. The greatest danger is not only the enemy outside but David's own weakness.
- **"Let your good Spirit lead me on level ground!" (v10).** Nehemiah 9:20 remembers that in the wilderness God "gave your good Spirit to instruct them." Psalm 51:11 pleads, "take not your Holy Spirit from me." The Old Testament speaks of God's Spirit as his presence and power at work, guiding and teaching. Christians read this in the full light of the New Testament, where the Spirit is revealed as a person who leads God's children (Rom 8:14; Gal 5:18; John 16:13). The Tyndale notes say the Spirit brings God's goodness (compare 23:6). The Hebrew could also be read "your Spirit is good; lead me," and some translations take it that way. Either way, the Spirit is the guide.
- **"On level ground" (v10).** The word *mishor* means a plain, a level place free of obstacles. It names the high tableland east of the Jordan (Deut 3:10) and is used for a place of safety and security (Ps 26:12; 27:11). The lexicon also gives it the sense of "uprightness," which is how the KJV renders it here ("the land of uprightness"). JFB notes the literal sense: "an even land." For a man whose path is full of traps (142:3), level ground means both safety and a straight, righteous life. Isaiah promises that God will make "the rough places a plain" (Isa 40:4, the same word).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that David asks to be taught, not to have God do his will for him, and passes on Spurgeon's comment that doing God's will is better than talking about it. Through Trapp he records Cyril of Alexandria's argument that the Spirit must be God, since Jesus said no one is good but God alone (Mark 10:18). That is a later theological use of the verse rather than its plain point, but it shows how early Christians read it. Henry ties v10 to the request in v11 that God would enliven David "to do God's will."

#### For Your Name's Sake (vv11–12)
- **"For your name's sake, O LORD, preserve my life!" (v11).** "Preserve my life" (*techayyeni*) is literally "make me live" or "give me life." The Tyndale notes say it means, in this case, to restore someone from despair. The same verb runs through Psalm 119 ("give me life according to your word," 119:25) and the prayer for national revival in Psalm 85:6: "Will you not revive us again?" "For your name's sake" means for the sake of God's reputation, his revealed character (Ps 23:3; 25:11). David's last pleas, like his first, appeal to who God is.
- **"In your righteousness bring my soul out of trouble!" (v11).** The psalm returns to God's righteousness (v1). "Trouble" (*tsarah*) is the narrow, hemmed-in place of Psalm 142:2. Trapp's line, quoted by Guzik, is that the soul can bring itself into trouble but only God can bring it out.
- **"In your steadfast love you will cut off my enemies" (v12).** This is a hard line for modern readers. God's loyal love (*chesed*) for David means justice against those trying to kill him. JFB: "God's mercy to His people is often wrath to His and their enemies" (compare 31:17). It is a prayer for God to act, not a plan for personal revenge. David leaves the enemies to God. "Cut off" (*tatsmit*) means to put an end to, silence.
- **"For I am your servant" (v12).** The psalm ends where v2 began: "your servant." JFB takes the servant as one chosen by God and so entitled to his care. Kidner, as Guzik reports, says God is pledged to his servant just as the servant is pledged to him. The servant who could not stand in judgment (v2) can still rest his whole case on belonging to God.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws a pointed application: believers "should especially seek the destruction of our sins, our worst enemies." Guzik notes that true revival seeks God's glory, not the glory of the one praying. JFB gives only cross-references on v11.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 143 states the problem the whole Bible must answer: "no one living is righteous before you." Paul takes up that line to show that no one is justified by his own works, and then he announces the answer: "the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe" (Rom 3:22). The righteousness David appeals to in v1, the very thing he could not face in judgment in v2, is the righteousness God gives freely in his Son. Jesus is the one servant who could have stood in judgment on his own merits. Instead he went into the darkness for others, crushed and overwhelmed in Gethsemane, his soul "very sorrowful, even to death." On the cross he thirsted, and his hands were stretched out. He went down to the pit, and on the third day, in the morning, God raised him. Because of him, God does not enter into judgment with those who trust him (Rom 8:1), and his good Spirit leads them on level ground as children of God (Rom 8:14).
