*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Psalm of Asaph. A Song." "Do Not Destroy" (*al-tashcheth*) is also the tune named for Psalms 57–59. Isaiah quotes a vintage saying, "Do not destroy it, for there is a blessing in it" (Isa 65:8), which may have been the opening line of a grape harvesters' song, a fitting tune for a psalm about God's cup of wine. Psalm 75 answers the two psalms before it. Psalm 73 asked why the wicked prosper; Psalm 74 asked how long God would let his enemies scoff. Here God himself speaks: he has set a time, he holds the world together, and he will bring down the proud and lift up the righteous. The Tyndale notes read Psalms 73–75 as a group on God's justice. No event is named. Henry, following older interpreters, connects it with David taking the throne after Saul's death, with Asaph setting David's words to music. JFB thinks of a time of "impending danger." Many modern readers link it, and Psalm 76, with Jerusalem's rescue from the Assyrians in Hezekiah's day (2 Kgs 19), since the Greek Old Testament adds "concerning the Assyrian" to the title of Psalm 76. The key word of the psalm is the Hebrew verb *rum*, "to lift up" or "exalt," which appears in vv4, 5, 6, 7 and 10.

#### Your Name Is Near (v1)
- **"We give thanks to you, O God; we give thanks, for your name is near" (v1).** The psalm begins with thanks, repeated for emphasis. God's "name" is God himself as he has made himself known, his character and presence (Exod 34:5–7). "Your name is near" means God is close, active, and ready to act for his people (Deut 4:7). After Psalm 74's complaint that God seemed far away, this is a striking opening.
- **"We recount your wondrous deeds" (v1).** "Recount" means to tell over in order, to rehearse the story. God's past acts show that he is near now. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, makes this point: nearness is felt through telling what God has done.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Did not we often pray for mercy when we were in pursuit of it; and shall we think it will suffice once or twice to give thanks when we have obtained it?" He adds, "If we share with others in their mercies, we must join with them in their praises." JFB: "God's name or perfections are set forth by His wondrous works."

#### At the Set Time That I Appoint (vv2–5)
- **"At the set time that I appoint I will judge with equity" (v2).** God speaks. The quotation marks in the ESV run through v5. "Set time" (*mo'ed*) is the word for an appointed time or festival, and also for the "meeting place" the enemy destroyed in 74:4, 8. They burned God's meeting places, but they cannot cancel his appointed time. JFB: "literally, 'take a set time.'" Henry and the KJV read "When I shall receive the congregation," as if the king speaks of his coming reign. Most modern translations hear God's voice. "With equity" (*mesharim*) means uprightly, on level ground (67:4). God's timing answers the "how long?" of 74:10. Morgan and Boice, cited by Guzik, both stress that God's judgment is never too early or too late (Hab 2:3; 2 Pet 3:9).
- **"When the earth totters, and all its inhabitants, it is I who keep steady its pillars" (v3).** "Totters" means melts, dissolves, the world coming apart, as it seemed when Jerusalem fell. But God holds up the pillars. The "pillars of the earth" picture the world as a great building resting on foundations God himself set (1 Sam 2:8; Job 38:4–6). Society may shake; God keeps it standing. **Selah** pauses for that truth to sink in.
- **"I say to the boastful, 'Do not boast,' and to the wicked, 'Do not lift up your horn'" (v4).** The "boastful" are the same proud men envied in 73:3. "Horn" in the Bible is a picture of strength and pride, taken from a wild ox or ram tossing its head and goring (Deut 33:17). To "lift up the horn" is to flaunt one's power. Boice, cited by Guzik, compares it to shaking one's fist at God. Poole likens the wicked to dangerous beasts that need to be dehorned.
- **"Do not lift up your horn on high, or speak with haughty neck" (v5).** "On high" may mean "against heaven," as in 73:9, where the wicked "set their mouths against the heavens." "Haughty neck" is literally "with an insolent neck," the stiff neck of an animal that refuses the yoke (Exod 32:9; Acts 7:51). JFB: "insolently."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv2–5 as David's pledge on becoming king, but he applies v3 to Christ: "The world and all the inhabitants of it were dissolved by sin… But Christ bore up the pillars of it." On v4 he writes, "Christ, the son of David, gives us this counsel… Deal not foolishly." JFB allows both readings: "These verses express the purpose of God to administer a just government… Some apply the words to the Psalmist." The Tyndale notes say the wicked, by their boasting, act as though they are wiser than God.

#### God Puts Down One and Lifts Up Another (vv6–8)
- **"For not from the east or from the west and not from the wilderness comes lifting up" (v6).** The psalmist names the points of the compass. "The wilderness" means the desert to the south, the Negev. The north is not named, perhaps because the enemy came from there, or because in Hebrew thought the north was linked with God's mountain (48:2). The Hebrew is a pun. "Lifting up" (*harim*) is spelled the same as "mountains," so the line can also be read "nor from the wilderness of the mountains." The KJV has "promotion cometh neither from the east, nor from the west, nor from the south." The point is the same: no earthly direction, no alliance, no army, gives real power. JFB: "promotion—literally, 'a lifting up.'"
- **"But it is God who executes judgment, putting down one and lifting up another" (v7).** Hannah sang this: "The LORD makes poor and makes rich; he brings low and he exalts" (1 Sam 2:7). Mary echoed it: "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52). Daniel told Nebuchadnezzar that God "removes kings and sets up kings" (Dan 2:21). The Tyndale notes point to Hannah and Mary.
- **"For in the hand of the LORD there is a cup with foaming wine, well mixed" (v8).** The cup of God's wrath is one of the Bible's strongest pictures of judgment (Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15–16; Rev 14:10). "Foaming" (*chamar*) can mean fermenting or red; the KJV has "the wine is red." "Well mixed" means mixed with spices to make it stronger, as Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes. It is the drink of a banquet turned into a sentence. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, notes the grim reversal: the host pours, and the guests must drink.
- **"He pours out from it, and all the wicked of the earth shall drain it down to the dregs" (v8).** "Dregs" (*shemarim*) are the thick sediment at the bottom of the wine, the bitterest part. The wicked must drink it all. JFB: "literally, 'surely the dregs, they shall drain it.'" Caryl, cited by Guzik through Spurgeon, says the drinkers never reach the bottom.
- **Commentators:** Henry: promotion "depends not upon the will of the people, nor the latter on the will of the prince, but both on the will of God." The "fair gale of preferment is not to be expected to blow from any point of the compass, but only from above." On the cup he writes, "There are mixtures of mercy and grace in the cup of affliction when it is put into the hands of God's own people, mixtures of the curse when it is put into the hands of the wicked." Some drops may fall on good people in common calamities, "but the dregs of the cup are reserved for the wicked." Meyer, cited by Guzik, says promotion of every kind is God's gift.

#### The Horns of the Righteous Shall Be Lifted Up (vv9–10)
- **"But I will declare it forever; I will sing praises to the God of Jacob" (v9).** The psalmist responds to God's word. "God of Jacob" recalls the God who stayed faithful to a weak, scheming man and made him Israel (46:7, 11).
- **"All the horns of the wicked I will cut off, but the horns of the righteous shall be lifted up" (v10).** Who speaks? It may be God again, closing the psalm as he spoke in vv2–5 (the Tyndale notes and Guzik take it so), or the king acting under God (Henry, JFB). Either way, the result is the same. The proud lose their power, and the righteous are raised. "Lifted up" is the psalm's key verb one last time. Zechariah saw a vision of four horns that scattered Judah, and four craftsmen sent to cut them down (Zech 1:18–21). Hannah's song ends the same way: God "will exalt the horn of his anointed" (1 Sam 2:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Though not all the heads, yet all the horns, of the wicked will I cut off, with which they push their poor neighbours," linking v10 to Zechariah's vision. He adds that "the exaltation of the Son of David will be the subject of the saints' everlasting praises." JFB: the pious, "acting under His direction, will destroy the power of the wicked, and exalt that of the righteous." Clarke, cited by Guzik, notes that as the power of the wicked is cut off, that of the righteous grows.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 75 says God has a set time, holds up the pillars of a tottering world, and pours a cup the wicked must drain to the dregs. The Gospels show where that cup went. In Gethsemane Jesus prayed, "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me," and then drank it (Matt 26:39; John 18:11). The righteous one took the cup meant for the wicked, so that those who trust him would never have to. At the set time God raised him from the dead and exalted him (Phil 2:9). Zechariah's father sang that God "has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" (Luke 1:69). He is the horn God lifts up, and through him God brings down the proud and raises the humble.
