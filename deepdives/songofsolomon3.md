*Setting:* Chapter 2 ended with the woman calling her beloved to come to her like a gazelle "until the day breathes and the shadows flee." Chapter 3 has two scenes. In the first (vv1–5) she searches the city at night for the one her soul loves, finds him and will not let him go. It ends with the second of the three charges to the daughters of Jerusalem not to awaken love before its time (2:7; 8:4). In the second (vv6–11) a magnificent procession comes up from the wilderness, Solomon's litter surrounded by sixty warriors, and the chorus is called out to see the king crowned "on the day of his wedding." This is the only place the Song clearly mentions a wedding, and the next two chapters, where he first calls her "bride" (4:8–5:1), follow from it. For the main ways the Song is read, see the Setting of chapter 1. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song, and the headings below follow its paragraph breaks.

#### The Night Search (vv1–5)
- **"On my bed by night I sought him whom my soul loves; I sought him, but found him not" (v1).** "By night" is plural in Hebrew, "night after night." She lies awake longing for him. "Him whom my soul loves" comes four times in four verses, and she never calls him by name, only by her love. The repeated "sought him, but found him not" (vv1–2) gives the scene the feel of an anxious dream, and many commentators, Guzik included, read it as a dream or a vivid imagining. Others read it as a real search. The poem works either way.
- **"I will rise now and go about the city, in the streets and in the squares" (v2).** She doesn't wait. She gets up and goes out. The Tyndale notes: the city is probably Jerusalem, and searching all its streets and squares was a bold thing to do. They also point out that a king would hardly be wandering the streets at night. She seems to be looking for an ordinary man in ordinary places.
- **"The watchmen found me as they went about in the city" (v3).** Watchmen patrolled the walls and streets at night, guarding against attack and keeping order (Ps 127:1; Isa 62:6). A woman alone in the streets at night was out of place, and they may have stopped her. She asks, "Have you seen him whom my soul loves?" They don't answer. Here they let her go. In the parallel night scene of 5:7 they beat her. The Tyndale notes: she ignored custom and safety in her desire to find him.
- **"Scarcely had I passed them when I found him whom my soul loves. I held him, and would not let him go" (v4).** Relief and joy. "Would not let him go" recalls Jacob wrestling with God: "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (Gen 32:26). Mary Magdalene clung to the risen Jesus in the garden (John 20:17).
- **"Until I had brought him into my mother's house, and into the chamber of her who conceived me" (v4).** Bringing him home to her mother's house means making things public and proper. In the ancient world marriages were arranged through families, and the "mother's house" may suggest the place where a marriage was planned (Gen 24:28, 67; Ruth 1:8). Guzik, with Kinlaw, takes it to imply marriage, not a secret affair. JFB: the mention of the mother "excludes impropriety."
- **"I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem… that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (v5).** The refrain repeats 2:7 word for word. After the anguish of searching, she warns her friends again: love is powerful, and it hurts as well as delights. Don't rush it. Guzik: love should not be hurried, in feelings or in the body.
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Morgan's point that love "creates a perpetual dread" of losing the beloved, and Glickman's that her sorrow at his absence grows with the joy of his presence. He also passes on Spurgeon's devotional application: Christ sometimes seems to withdraw, and the believer should hold on and not let go. Henry: "We must not rest in any means, but by faith apply directly to Christ," and he takes the night search as the church struggling to find Christ in the shadows of the old ceremonial law. JFB treats the watchmen as ministers who "find" individual souls seeking Christ, and the bed as a picture of spiritual complacency, "the secret of her failure."

#### Solomon's Procession (vv6–11)
- **"What is that coming up from the wilderness like columns of smoke?" (v6).** The scene changes sharply. Someone, perhaps the chorus, sees a cloud rising from the desert road toward Jerusalem. "What" is literally "who" (*mi zot*), and the word is feminine. It may refer to the litter itself (a feminine noun in Hebrew), or to the bride riding in it. Guzik, with Kinlaw and Carr, takes it as the bride arriving in Solomon's litter. The same question returns in 8:5, "Who is that coming up from the wilderness, leaning on her beloved?" The "columns of smoke" may be clouds of dust kicked up by the procession, or incense burned around it.
- **"Perfumed with myrrh and frankincense, with all the fragrant powders of a merchant" (v6).** Myrrh and frankincense were costly resins from southern Arabia and Somalia, carried north by the incense caravans. Both were used in the temple, frankincense in the incense and offerings (Exod 30:34) and myrrh in the holy anointing oil (Exod 30:23). The wise men brought both to the infant Jesus (Matt 2:11). The whole procession smells of wealth and celebration.
- **"Behold, it is the litter of Solomon! Around it are sixty mighty men, some of the mighty men of Israel" (v7).** "Litter" (*mittah*) is a portable couch carried on poles, the way royalty and the rich traveled. David had his "thirty" mighty men (2 Sam 23:8–39). Solomon's escort is twice that, the best soldiers in the land. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that the roads were dangerous, so the guard was real protection. JFB compares the sixty to the armed tribes encamped around the tabernacle.
- **"All of them wearing swords and expert in war, each with his sword at his thigh, against terror by night" (v8).** "Terror by night" means bandits and raiders on the road (Ps 91:5). Some see a wedding-night custom here as well, warding off evil. Either way, the king's bride is safe. Guzik, passing on Nee, notes that what belongs to him now belongs to her, including his protection.
- **"King Solomon made himself a carriage from the wood of Lebanon" (v9).** "Carriage" (*'appiryon*) appears only here in the Old Testament. It is probably a foreign word for a palanquin or sedan chair, possibly related to Greek *phoreion*, or to a Persian or Sanskrit word. Scholars who date the Song late point to this word. Others note that foreign luxury words traveled with foreign luxury goods, and Solomon traded widely (1 Kgs 10:22). The "wood of Lebanon" is cedar, the same costly timber Solomon used for the temple and palace (1 Kgs 5:6; 7:2). The Tyndale notes make the same link.
- **"He made its posts of silver, its back of gold, its seat of purple" (v10).** Silver, gold and purple were royal luxuries. Purple dye came from the murex shellfish of the Phoenician coast and was worth more than its weight in silver. The temple curtains were also woven in purple (2 Chr 3:14).
- **"Its interior was inlaid with love by the daughters of Jerusalem" (v10).** The women of Jerusalem decorated the inside of the carriage with loving care, perhaps with embroidered cushions or inlaid designs. Some scholars, puzzled by "love," suggest a similar-looking Hebrew word for leather or ebony, but the ESV keeps "love." The Tyndale notes: even his servants served him with love.
- **"Go out, O daughters of Zion, and look upon King Solomon, with the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his wedding" (v11).** The "crown" (*'atarah*) here is probably a festive wedding wreath, not the royal crown. Jewish grooms wore crowns at weddings until the rabbis banned them during the Roman wars of the first century (Mishnah Sotah 9:14), and Jewish tradition still calls bride and groom "king" and "queen" for the wedding week. Solomon's mother was Bathsheba, who had worked to secure his throne (1 Kgs 1:11–31). Guzik notes that a priest, not his mother, anointed Solomon king (1 Kgs 1:39), which supports the wedding-wreath reading, and suggests this poem may come from early in his reign, before his many marriages.
- **"On the day of the gladness of his heart" (v11).** The wedding day is the happiest day of his life. Isaiah uses the same picture of God: "as the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you" (Isa 62:5). The Tyndale notes: this is the only clear mention of a wedding in the Song, and the woman is called "bride" from here through 5:1. In the three-person reading, Solomon attends the wedding of a woman who chose her shepherd over him. In the two-person or anthology reading, it is Solomon's own wedding procession.
- **Commentators:** Guzik considers whether the woman could be Abishag the Shunammite, the young woman who cared for David in his old age (1 Kgs 1:3–4), but calls it guesswork and notes that "Shulammite" (6:13) and "Shunammite" may not be the same. Henry: "A wilderness is an emblem of the world," and the believer, like the bride, comes up out of it. On the guard he writes, "The church is well guarded; more are with her than are against her" (2 Kgs 6:16). JFB reads the scene of Christ and his bride and writes of v11: "The crown of thorns was once His nuptial chaplet, His blood the wedding wine cup." It takes the wedding day as chiefly the final marriage of Christ and the church at the end of the age.

#### Pointing to Christ
The bride who searches the night streets for "him whom my soul loves," finds him and holds on, is a picture of every heart that seeks God and will not be satisfied with anything less (Jer 29:13). Mary Magdalene, searching for Jesus' body in the dark, found him alive and clung to him (John 20:1, 11–17). The royal procession coming up from the wilderness, fragrant with myrrh and frankincense, points to a greater King. He received those same gifts as a child (Matt 2:11), and myrrh was used again at his burial (John 19:39). He wore a crown on the day of his wedding too: the crown of thorns, as JFB puts it, the price of a bride. And the day of the gladness of his heart is still coming: "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and his Bride has made herself ready" (Rev 19:7).
