*Setting:* With chapter 20 the long story of David's reign and troubles is finished. Chapters 21–24 are an appendix, a collection of six pieces about David arranged by theme rather than date. They are laid out like a mirror. A national disaster caused by Saul's sin (21:1–14) matches a national disaster caused by David's sin (ch 24). Lists of David's warriors (21:15–22) match the longer list in 23:8–39. And at the center stand two of David's songs (ch 22; 23:1–7), which put the spotlight on David's God rather than on David. The famine story here probably comes from earlier in David's reign, after he had brought Mephibosheth to his table (ch 9) but before Absalom's revolt. The Philistine battles come from various points in his reign, at least one of them late, when David was old enough to tire.

#### David Avenges the Gibeonites (vv1–14)
- **"A famine in the days of David for three years, year after year" (v1).** One bad year could be bad weather. Three in a row made people ask whether God was saying something. The covenant itself warned that drought and failed harvests could be God's discipline on a disobedient nation (Deut 28:23–24).
- **"David sought the face of the LORD" (v1).** He asks God directly, probably through the priest and the ephod or by a prophet. The answer is not what anyone expected: **"There is bloodguilt on Saul and on his house, because he put the Gibeonites to death."** Bloodguilt is the guilt of shedding innocent blood. The law said such blood "pollutes the land," and it could only be cleansed by the blood of the one who shed it (Num 35:33).
- **Who were the Gibeonites? (v2).** Canaanites (here called by the broad name "Amorites") from Gibeon, a few miles north of Jerusalem. When Joshua invaded, they tricked Israel into a peace treaty by pretending to come from far away. Israel's leaders swore an oath to them by the LORD without asking God, and when the trick came out, they still kept the oath: "We have sworn to them by the LORD, the God of Israel, and now we may not touch them" (Josh 9:3–27). That was about four centuries before Saul. The Gibeonites became woodcutters and water carriers for the tabernacle, living among Israel under God's protection.
- **"Saul had sought to strike them down in his zeal" (v2).** This massacre is not recorded anywhere else. The text gives Saul's motive as "zeal for the people of Israel and Judah," which sounds noble. But it was zeal that broke a promise made in God's name. Saul had once been too soft on God's enemies when God told him to act (1 Sam 15). Here he was violent when God had told Israel to keep peace. Some have suggested this happened in the same rampage as Saul's slaughter of the priests at Nob (1 Sam 22:18–19), a priestly town near Gibeon, but that is only a guess.
- **"How shall I make atonement, that you may bless the heritage of the LORD?" (v3).** "Atonement" (*kaphar*) means to cover over a wrong so the relationship can be restored. David wants the injured party to stop cursing Israel and bless it. The wronged have to be heard.
- **"It is not a matter of silver or gold" (v4).** The law refused money as payment for murder: "you shall accept no ransom for the life of a murderer" (Num 35:31). The Gibeonites also say they have no right to execute any Israelite on their own. They are foreigners under protection, and they speak carefully.
- **"Let seven of his sons be given to us… that we may hang them before the LORD at Gibeah of Saul" (v6).** Seven is the number of completeness. The verb for "hang" (*yaqa*) is rare and its exact meaning is uncertain. Ancient translations render it "expose," "impale" or even "crucify," and in Numbers 25:4 it describes a public execution "in the sun before the LORD." Whatever the method, the bodies were left on display. **"Saul, the chosen of the LORD"** may be bitter irony from the Gibeonites. Many scholars think the place was really Gibeon itself, "on the mountain of the LORD" (v9), and the Tyndale notes take it as the high place at Gibeon where Solomon later worshiped (1 Kings 3:4).
- **Is this just? (vv6–9).** It troubles many readers, and rightly. Deuteronomy 24:16 says children are not to be put to death for their fathers' sins. Several things can be said. First, this was not an ordinary court case but the breaking of a national oath sworn in God's name, and God himself named the guilt. Second, "Saul and… his house" (v1) may mean the family shared in the killing. JFB suggests Saul's sons may have carried it out, though the text does not say. Third, the Gibeonites asked for it, and Israel's honor before a wronged people was at stake. Still, the passage does not tell us God commanded this exact remedy. It tells us David agreed, and that God answered prayer "after that" (v14). Faithful readers differ on how much to approve, and the text leaves the tension standing.
- **"But the king spared Mephibosheth… because of the oath of the LORD" (v7).** Saul broke an oath. David keeps one. He had sworn to Jonathan never to cut off his kindness from Jonathan's house (1 Sam 20:14–17, 42). The whole chapter turns on promises made before God.
- **Rizpah and Merab's sons (v8).** Rizpah was Saul's concubine, the woman Abner was accused of taking (3:7). Her two sons, Armoni and a second Mephibosheth (not Jonathan's son), were Saul's own boys. The other five were Saul's grandsons. The Hebrew text here reads "Michal," but Michal had no children (6:23), and it was her sister Merab who married Adriel (1 Sam 18:19). Two Hebrew manuscripts and some Greek copies read "Merab," and the ESV follows them. JFB, working from the "Michal" reading, suggests Michal raised her sister's sons, an old explanation to fit the text.
- **"At the beginning of barley harvest" (v9).** About April. The famine was breaking, or there was at least a crop to begin cutting, and the deaths were timed with the start of the season.
- **"Rizpah… took sackcloth and spread it for herself on the rock" (v10).** Sackcloth was the rough cloth of mourning. She made it her tent and her bed. **"From the beginning of harvest until rain fell upon them"** could mean many weeks, perhaps until the autumn rains in October. Day and night she drove off vultures and jackals. To lie unburied and be eaten by animals was the worst shame in the ancient world (Deut 28:26; Ps 79:2–3). She could not save her sons' lives, but she would not let them be dishonored. She speaks not a word in the chapter. Her silent watch is one of the most moving scenes in the Old Testament.
- **"When David was told what Rizpah… had done" (vv11–12).** Her faithfulness moves the king. He goes and gets the bones of Saul and Jonathan from Jabesh-gilead. The men of Jabesh had made a night march to take Saul's body off the wall of Beth-shan after the Philistines hung it there (1 Sam 31:8–13), repaying Saul for rescuing their town at the start of his reign (1 Sam 11). David had praised them for it (2:4–7).
- **"They buried the bones… in the land of Benjamin in Zela, in the tomb of Kish his father" (v14).** At last Saul's family lies together in the family tomb. David honors the house of the man who hunted him. Zela's location is unknown. Saul's tribe Benjamin, which had just followed Sheba (ch 20), sees David treat Saul's dead with respect.
- **"And after that God responded to the plea for the land" (v14).** The same phrase closes the book (24:25), tying the first and last pieces of the appendix together. Justice was done, the dead were buried, and the rain came.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the famine as a call to look back for the sin behind it and says plainly, "Money is no satisfaction for blood." He adds, "When justice is done on earth, vengeance from heaven ceases," and sees in men hanged before the LORD a dim picture of Christ, who was guiltless and yet hanged on a tree to turn away God's anger. Guzik makes the same link to Deuteronomy 21:23 and Galatians 3:13. He notes that God remembered an oath made four hundred years earlier, and warns against both extremes: assuming every hardship is a judgment, and ignoring God's hand when it may be at work. JFB thinks Saul's real motive was to seize Gibeonite land "for his own people," and defends David: he "cannot be charged with" using this to remove rivals, since these were side branches of Saul's family with no claim to the throne. JFB also takes Rizpah's watch as a common act of grieving relatives.

#### War with the Philistines (vv15–22)
- **"There was war again… And David grew weary" (v15).** The giant-killer of 1 Samuel 17 now runs out of strength in battle. The Tyndale notes observe that this honest detail prepares for the song in chapter 22: David's victories came from God, not from David's own strength.
- **Ishbi-benob, "one of the descendants of the giants" (v16).** "Giants" is *rapha*, used for the Rephaim, a people remembered for their size (Deut 2:10–11; 3:11, Og's huge bed). Here it probably names a clan of big warriors in Gath, the family Goliath came from. His spear point weighed 300 shekels, about 7½ pounds, half the weight of Goliath's (1 Sam 17:7).
- **"Abishai the son of Zeruiah came to his aid" (v17).** Joab's brother, rough and quick to kill (16:9), but loyal to the end. Years before, David had risked his life against a giant for Israel. Now one of his men risks his life for David.
- **"Lest you quench the lamp of Israel" (v17).** A lamp in a house was a sign that the family was still alive. David was the light of the nation, and his men would not let it go out. God later promised David "a lamp" in Jerusalem forever (1 Kings 11:36; 15:4), a promise kept in his line and finally in Christ.
- **Sibbecai the Hushathite and Saph (v18).** Sibbecai is one of David's warriors and later commanded a division (1 Chr 27:11). Gob is unknown. The parallel in 1 Chronicles 20:4 places this battle at Gezer and names the giant Sippai.
- **"Elhanan… struck down Goliath the Gittite" (v19).** This is the best-known puzzle of the chapter, since 1 Samuel 17 says David killed Goliath. The parallel in 1 Chronicles 20:5 reads: "Elhanan the son of Jair struck down Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite." Most conservative scholars think the Samuel text suffered a copying slip. In Hebrew, "Lahmi the brother of" looks a lot like "the Bethlehemite," and the odd name "Jaare-oregim" seems to have picked up *oregim*, "weavers," from "weaver's beam" later in the same line. Others suggest Elhanan was another name for David, or that there were two men called Goliath. The copying explanation is the simplest and is taken by the Tyndale notes. The "spear… like a weaver's beam" is the same phrase used of Goliath's spear (1 Sam 17:7), which fits his brother or kinsman carrying a family weapon.
- **"Six fingers on each hand, and six toes on each foot" (v20).** Extra fingers and toes (polydactyly) is a real and fairly common inherited trait, and it would have run in a family. The detail sets this man apart and shows the writer is recording an actual person.
- **"When he taunted Israel" (v21).** "Taunted" is the same verb as Goliath's "defy" (*charaph*, 1 Sam 17:10, 26). He makes the same mistake Goliath did. **Jonathan the son of Shimei, David's brother**, kills him. Shimei is the same as Shammah, the brother of David who stood by when Goliath fell (1 Sam 16:9; 17:13). A generation later, David's nephew finishes what David started.
- **"They fell by the hand of David and by the hand of his servants" (v22).** David did not have to kill every giant. He started the work, and men he had trained and inspired carried it on.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "David fainted, but he did not flee, and God sent help in the time of need." He turns the giants into the believer's spiritual enemies and calls death "a Christian's last enemy," overcome through Christ. Guzik takes David's weariness as the honest experience of growing older, says a leader's weakness is when others must step up (Eccl 4:9–12), and sees the giant-killers as proof that David left behind a trained generation. JFB notes that the Philistines took heart whenever a "gigantic champion" appeared and counts "four successive contests," the first nearly fatal to David. JFB does not comment on Elhanan and Goliath.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is heavy, and it does not hide its hard edges. Bloodguilt pollutes a land, and the only cure is the blood of the guilty, hanged in the open "before the LORD." Seven men from a guilty house die so that rain can fall. The gospel reverses the direction of that scene. Christ was hanged on a tree, cursed (Gal 3:13), but the guilt was not his. He bore it for others, so that God's blessing could come down on a land under curse. And the faithful mother keeping watch beside the dead foreshadows the women who stood by the cross and came to the tomb.
