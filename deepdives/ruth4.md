*Setting:* The morning after the night at the threshing floor (chapter 3). Ruth, the Moabite widow, has asked Boaz to "spread your wings over your servant, for you are a redeemer" (3:9), and Boaz has sworn to act, but with one obstacle: "there is a redeemer nearer than I" (3:12). Naomi has told Ruth to sit and wait, "for the man will not rest but will settle the matter today" (3:18). Chapter 4 moves from the private world of the threshing floor to the public world of the town gate, where legal business was done before witnesses. Then it moves to a birth, a grandmother's lap and a family tree that ends with the name David. The book that began with three funerals ends with a wedding, a baby and a king.

#### Boaz Redeems Ruth (vv1–12)
- **"Boaz had gone up to the gate and sat down there" (v1).** The gate of an Israelite town was more than a doorway. It was a covered complex of chambers and benches where elders met, contracts were made, cases were judged and news was passed (Deut 21:19; 2 Sam 15:2). Everyone heading out to the fields in the morning would pass through it. Boaz goes up first thing and waits.
- **"And behold, the redeemer… came by" (v1).** "Behold" again marks a well-timed "coincidence," like Boaz's arrival in 2:4.
- **"Turn aside, friend" (v1).** The Hebrew is *peloni almoni*, roughly "Mr. So-and-so." Boaz surely used his name, but the narrator will not record it. The man who refused to preserve another man's name loses his own from the story.
- **"Ten men of the elders of the city" (v2).** Ten made a full official body of witnesses. Later Jewish practice required ten men for a public assembly or a wedding blessing.
- **"Naomi… is selling the parcel of land that belonged to our relative Elimelech" (v3).** This is the first mention of land in the book. Perhaps Elimelech had sold the use of it before leaving for Moab, or perhaps it had lain unworked, and Naomi now has the right to sell or redeem it. Israelite land was not meant to leave the family. It belonged to God, and the people were his tenants (Lev 25:23). If a man had to sell, his nearest kinsman was to "redeem what his brother has sold" (Lev 25:25).
- **"I will redeem it" (v4).** For the nearer kinsman it looks like a good deal. Elimelech's line had no male heir, so buying the field would add it permanently to his own estate, and he would get credit for doing his family duty.
- **"You also acquire Ruth the Moabite, the widow of the dead" (v5).** Here Boaz springs the condition. Whoever redeems the land must also marry Ruth "to perpetuate the name of the dead in his inheritance." Her first son would legally be Mahlon's son and heir, and the land would go back to him. So the redeemer would spend his money on a field he would not keep. Strictly, the law of brother-in-law marriage (Deut 25:5–10) applied to a dead man's brother, which neither man was. Boaz appeals to its spirit and joins it to the redeemer's duty, and the elders accept the case. There is also a small textual question. The written Hebrew reads "I acquire Ruth," while the Jewish scribes' marginal reading, followed by the ancient translations and the ESV, has "you acquire." The second fits the flow of the scene best.
- **"Lest I impair my own inheritance" (v6).** "Impair" means to ruin or damage. Perhaps he already had sons who would lose part of their inheritance, or he could not afford to buy land for another man's heir. He protects his own family's name, and ironically he is the only man in the story whose name is forgotten. He does nothing illegal, and the text does not condemn him. But Boaz is willing to bear a cost he is not.
- **The sandal (vv7–8).** "In former times" shows the custom had died out by the time the book was written, so the writer explains it. A sandal stood for the right to walk on and possess land (compare Josh 1:3; Ps 60:8). Handing it over transferred that right. Deuteronomy 25:9 describes a harsher version: a widow pulls off the sandal of a brother-in-law who refuses her and spits in his face. That shame is absent here, since this man was not a brother and had a real reason. Ruth is not present, and the sandal passes between the two men.
- **"You are witnesses this day" (vv9–10).** Boaz announces both acquisitions publicly: Elimelech's property, and Ruth "to be my wife, to perpetuate the name of the dead in his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off." "Bought" is the ordinary word for acquiring, used here in a legal sense; Ruth is not being purchased like goods, but the redemption of land and the marriage are one transaction. And she is named "Ruth the Moabite" one last time, as she enters Israel.
- **"Like Rachel and Leah, who together built up the house of Israel" (v11).** The crowd blesses the marriage with Israel's founding mothers, the two wives of Jacob who bore the tribes (with their servants). "Built up the house" is a play on words, since the Hebrew for "build" (*banah*) sounds like the word for "son" (*ben*). Rachel is buried near Bethlehem (Gen 35:19), so the blessing has a local touch. "Act worthily" is again *chayil*, the word for Boaz in 2:1 and Ruth in 3:11.
- **"Like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah" (v12).** Perez was Boaz's ancestor (v18) and head of the leading clan of Judah. The parallel is pointed. Tamar was a Canaanite widow who was also denied the brother-in-law marriage she was owed, and she secured an heir by a daring act (Gen 38). Out of that tangled story came Perez, whose name means "breach" or "breaking through." Like Tamar, Ruth is a foreign widow in Judah's line who refuses to let a dead man's name die.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "Boaz, though a judge, would not be judge in his own cause," but did everything openly before witnesses. He likens the nearer kinsman to those who will not take up the gospel because it might cost them, and says of the wedding blessing, "Marriages ought to be blessed, and accompanied with prayer." JFB calls the gate "a roofed building," says ten witnesses were used for marriage and property matters, explains that the shoe here was only "a pledge of the transaction being completed," and calls the people's words "the usual bridal benediction." Guzik reads the unnamed kinsman as quietly disgraced, thinks Boaz framed the matter shrewdly, land first and then Ruth, and stresses the value of a public, witnessed commitment in marriage. The Tyndale notes explain that Boaz drew on the spirit of the redeemer and brother-in-law laws without an exact legal precedent.

#### Ruth and Boaz Marry (vv13–17)
- **"The LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son" (v13).** Ruth was married for about ten years to Mahlon without children (1:4–5). Now, for only the second time in the book, the narrator says directly that the LORD acted. The first was 1:6: "the LORD had visited his people and given them food." Food at the beginning, a son at the end, framing the whole story.
- **"Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a redeemer" (v14).** The women of Bethlehem, who heard Naomi's lament in 1:19–21, now speak again. The "redeemer" here seems to be the baby himself, who will carry on the family line and care for Naomi. "May his name be renowned in Israel" will come true through David.
- **"A restorer of life and a nourisher of your old age" (v15).** "Restorer of life" is literally "one who brings back the soul" (*meshiv nephesh*), using *shuv*, the "return" verb that ran all through chapter 1 (compare Ps 23:3, "he restores my soul"). Naomi who "returned" empty has been "brought back" to life. "Nourisher" means one who sustains and provides, literally "fills" her old age.
- **"Your daughter-in-law who loves you, who is more to you than seven sons" (v15).** Seven was the number of completeness, and seven sons was the ideal family (1 Sam 2:5). The women say Ruth is better than that. It is the only place in the book where anyone says Ruth "loves" Naomi, and it is said by Israelite women about a Moabite.
- **"Naomi took the child and laid him on her lap and became his nurse" (v16).** "Nurse" here means a guardian or caretaker, not a wet nurse (the same word is used of the guardians of Ahab's sons, 2 Kings 10:1). The woman who said "the LORD has brought me back empty" (1:21) now holds a grandson in her lap.
- **"A son has been born to Naomi.' They named him Obed" (v17).** Legally, Obed is Elimelech's heir and so Naomi's. Neighbors naming a child is unique in the Old Testament. Obed means "servant" or "worshiper," probably short for a name like Obadiah, "servant of the LORD." Then comes the surprise: "He was the father of Jesse, the father of David." In one line a small family story becomes the story of Israel's royal house.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Ruth "loved Naomi, and therefore was better to her than seven sons," and reads the name Obed as a sign of humble service. Guzik traces the reversal of Naomi's bitter words in chapter 1 into joy and stresses that children are a gift from God, never something to take for granted. JFB confirms that Obed "means 'servant.'" The Tyndale notes add that "nourisher" literally means to make her old age full, answering her "empty" of 1:21.

#### The Genealogy of David (vv18–22)
- **"These are the generations of Perez" (v18).** "These are the generations" is the formula that structures Genesis (Gen 2:4; 5:1; 11:27; 37:2). The phrase ties this little book to the great story of God's purposes that began there.
- **Ten names, Perez to David (vv18–22).** Ten generations was a common pattern for a complete genealogy (compare Adam to Noah and Shem to Abram, Gen 5; 11). Boaz is seventh, a place of honor, and David tenth. Nahshon was leader of Judah in the wilderness under Moses (Num 1:7; 7:12), and Salmon married Rahab of Jericho (Matt 1:5). Since the period from the wilderness to David is several centuries, most scholars think the list skips generations, as many biblical genealogies do. "Fathered" can mean "was the ancestor of."
- **Why end with a family tree?** Because the point of the whole book, which began "in the days when the judges ruled," is that while Israel had no king, God was quietly preparing one. The famine, the deaths in Moab, a foreign widow's loyalty and a farmer's integrity were all steps toward David.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The genealogy is here drawn from Pharez, through Boaz and Obed, to David, and so leads towards the Messiah." JFB notes that in Scripture "grandfathers are said… to beget their grandchildren," so generations are skipped, and reckons about 380 years between Salmon and David. Guzik ties Bethlehem's importance to David's roots and to Jesus' birth there, and lists the ways Boaz foreshadows Christ: a true kinsman, able and willing to redeem, acting out of love, at his own cost.

#### Pointing to Christ
Matthew's genealogy of Jesus names this family: "Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab, and Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth, and Obed the father of Jesse, and Jesse the father of David the king" (Matt 1:5–6). Tamar, Rahab and Ruth, three outsiders, are written into the line of the Messiah. Boaz is a living picture of what Christ would do. To redeem, a man had to be a near kinsman, able to pay, and willing to bear the cost. Christ became our kinsman by taking on flesh, paid the full price with his own life, and was willing when no one else could be. The nameless redeemer protected his own inheritance. Jesus gave up his to make an outsider his bride and to bring her into his Father's house. And the child laid in Naomi's lap points to another child born in Bethlehem, the Son of David, who is the true "restorer of life" for everyone who comes home empty.
