*Setting:* Psalm 134 is the last of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the yearly festivals. The collection began far from home, with a man living among people who hated peace: "Woe to me, that I sojourn in Meshech" (120:5). It ends here, inside the temple courts at night, with a call to praise and a blessing in return. The psalm has no author's name. It is only three verses long and reads like a short exchange between two groups. In vv1–2 someone calls on "the servants of the LORD" who serve in the temple at night to lift their hands and bless the LORD. In v3 a voice answers with a blessing on the one who called. Many think the pilgrims sang vv1–2 to the priests and Levites as the festival closed or as night fell, and the priests answered with v3, much like the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26. Others think the whole psalm was sung by the temple choirs. Either way, the pilgrims' long climb ends with God's people blessing God and God blessing his people. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Come, Bless the LORD (vv1–2)
- **"Come, bless the LORD" (v1).** The first word is *hinneh*, usually translated "behold." It is the same word that opened Psalm 133. Here the ESV renders it "Come," as a call to attention: look, here and now, do this. Clarke, cited by Guzik, reads it as "take heed."
- **"Bless the LORD" (v1).** To "bless" (*barak*) someone greater than oneself is not to give them something they lack. It means to praise, to speak well of, to honor with thanks. God blesses people by giving them life and good gifts. People bless God by acknowledging that every good gift comes from him (Ps 103:1–2). The word appears three times in three verses: twice for people blessing God and once for God blessing people.
- **"All you servants of the LORD" (v1).** In this setting the "servants" are the priests and Levites who worked in the temple. The study notes identify them this way, and Psalm 135, which follows, uses almost the same words: "you servants of the LORD, who stand in the house of the LORD, in the courts of the house of our God" (135:1–2). The word "servant" (*'eved*) is a title of honor here. Moses (Num 12:7), David (2 Sam 7:5) and the prophets (Amos 3:7) were all called God's servants.
- **"Who stand by night in the house of the LORD" (v1).** "Stand" (*'amad*) is the normal word for priests and Levites on duty before God (Deut 10:8; 18:7). Temple work did not stop at sunset. The lamp in the holy place was to burn "from evening to morning before the LORD" (Exod 27:21), and the fire on the altar was never to go out (Lev 6:12–13). Levites guarded the gates around the clock (1 Chron 9:26–27), and the singers were "on duty day and night" (1 Chron 9:33). Samuel as a boy slept in the house of the LORD "where the ark of God was," with the lamp still burning (1 Sam 3:3). These were the quiet night shifts, when the crowds had gone home and no one was watching.
- **Why mention the night?** Some take "by night" to mean the evening service, at the time of the evening offering, which is JFB's view. Others take it as the whole night watch. The second fits the wording better: these people "stand by night." Night service was lonely, routine and easy to treat as just a job. The call is that even the night watch should be worship. Praise in the dark is a theme across Scripture: "at night his song is with me" (Ps 42:8), "at midnight I rise to praise you" (Ps 119:62), and Paul and Silas "praying and singing hymns to God" at midnight in prison (Acts 16:25).
- **"Lift up your hands to the holy place" (v2).** Raising the hands was the normal posture of prayer and praise in Israel (Ps 28:2; 63:4; 141:2; 1 Kings 8:22). It showed openness, dependence and expectation, like a child reaching up to a parent. "The holy place" (*qodesh*) can mean the sanctuary itself, the inner room of the temple where God's presence was focused. The worshipers lift their hands toward it. The same Hebrew word also means "holiness," so some translate "lift up your hands in holiness" (as in some older versions). That fits Paul's call for people to pray "lifting holy hands" (1 Tim 2:8). Most modern translations, including the ESV, take it as the place.
- **"And bless the LORD!" (v2).** The first half of the psalm begins and ends with the same command. The call is simple and complete: bless the LORD.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the night-watch Levites must "redeem time for holy exercises," and applies the call to all believers: "We are the servants of the Lord; we have a place and a name in his house." He adds that "even by night we are under his eye and have access to him," and that lifting hands is a sign of lifting hearts. JFB pictures the arriving pilgrim bands calling on the priests at the evening sacrifice, and compares the raised hands to Psalm 28:2. Guzik notes that "standing" is the ordinary verb for priestly ministry, and passes on Poole's point that the night watch should not stand silent and idle like statues. He also notes, with Meyer, that it is easier to bless God by day than through the night's hardships.

#### May the LORD Bless You from Zion (v3)
- **"May the LORD bless you from Zion" (v3).** Now the blessing comes back. The "you" is singular in Hebrew (*yevarekheka*), exactly as in the priestly blessing God gave Aaron: "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Num 6:24). Most interpreters read v3 as the priests' reply, blessing the pilgrim or the worshiper who called on them. The study notes describe the exchange this way: the travelers call the temple workers to praise, and in return they receive a long-awaited blessing. The same words appear in another Song of Ascents: "The LORD bless you from Zion!" (128:5).
- **Blessing goes both ways.** The worshipers bless God; God blesses the worshipers. The word is the same, but the direction changes its meaning. People bless God with praise. God blesses people with life, help and his presence. The worshipers give him thanks. He gives them everything else.
- **"From Zion" (v3).** Zion was the hill in Jerusalem where the temple stood, the place God had chosen as his dwelling (132:13–14). The blessing comes from there because God's presence was there. But the pilgrims were about to leave Zion and go home to their villages. The blessing from Zion goes with them.
- **"He who made heaven and earth!" (v3).** The God who blesses from a small hill in Jerusalem is the Creator of everything. The title has run through the Songs of Ascents: "My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (121:2), and "Our help is in the name of the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (124:8). Zion is not the home of a local god with local power. It is the place where the Maker of all things has chosen to meet his people. The pilgrims' journey began with a question, "From where does my help come?" (121:1). It ends with the answer spoken over them as a blessing.
- **The end of the ascents.** The fifteen Songs of Ascents move from distress far away (120) to arrival in Jerusalem (122), through trust, sorrow and hope, to unity among God's people (133) and finally to worship and blessing in God's house (134). The last word of the collection is God's blessing.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the singular "thee" may mean the chief priest or each worshiper personally, and calls the blessings out of Zion "the blessings of the covenant, and of communion with God." He takes comfort that "it is he who made heaven and earth," so every blessing is in his hand, and he draws the lesson that people should pray for those who call them to worship. JFB says the priests answer in the form "directed" in Numbers 6:23, and calls Zion, the Church, the "seat of blessings." Guzik agrees with most commentators that the verse echoes the priestly blessing, and stresses that the singular "you" means God's blessing reaches each believer, not only the crowd. He passes on Spurgeon's point that Zion itself cannot bless anyone, but the LORD who dwells there can.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 134 closes the pilgrim songs inside God's house at night, with servants standing before God and a priest speaking God's blessing over his people. Jesus is the great High Priest who stands before God for his people continually, "since he always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25). He kept the night watch himself: he often prayed through the night (Luke 6:12), and on the night he was betrayed he prayed in the garden while his friends slept (Matt 26:40). The last thing Luke tells about Jesus on earth is that "lifting up his hands he blessed them" and was carried up into heaven (Luke 24:50–51). Through him all his people become "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9), servants who stand in God's house to "offer up a sacrifice of praise to God" (Heb 13:15), by day and by night. The blessing that came from earthly Zion now comes from the heavenly one, where those who serve him stand "before the throne of God, and serve him day and night in his temple" (Rev 7:15).
