*Setting:* This is the last of Isaiah's oracles against the nations (chs 13–23). The series began with Babylon, the great power of the east and of land armies, and it ends with Tyre, the great power of the west and of the sea. Tyre was the leading city of Phoenicia, on the coast of what is now Lebanon, about 35 miles north of Mount Carmel. It had a mainland town and a fortress on a rocky island about half a mile offshore. Its ships sailed the whole Mediterranean, and it founded trading colonies as far away as North Africa (Carthage) and Spain. Tyre and Israel had a long history: King Hiram sent cedar and craftsmen for David's palace and Solomon's temple (1 Kings 5), but Jezebel, the queen who brought Baal worship into Israel, was a Sidonian princess (1 Kings 16:31). Tyre was attacked by Assyria in Isaiah's lifetime, besieged by Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon for thirteen years (about 585–572 BC), and finally captured by Alexander the Great in 332 BC. The oracle is a lament for a world built on trade, and it ends with a surprise: Tyre's wealth will one day belong to the LORD.

#### An Oracle Concerning Tyre and Sidon (vv1–18)
- **"Wail, O ships of Tarshish, for Tyre is laid waste, without house or harbor!" (v1).** Tarshish was a far western port, most likely Tartessus in southern Spain. "Ships of Tarshish" became the name for big ocean-going freighters, the supertankers of the day (2:16; 1 Kings 10:22). The oracle opens and closes with the same cry (v14), framing the poem. The sailors are coming home and find no home port.
- **"From the land of Cyprus it is revealed to them" (v1).** The Hebrew is *Kittim*, from the Phoenician town of Kition on Cyprus. JFB notes that Cyprus's cities "were mostly Phoenician." It was the last stop before the final leg to Tyre, so that is where the crews hear the news, as the Tyndale notes explain.
- **"The merchants of Sidon, who cross the sea, have filled you" (v2).** Sidon, about 25 miles north of Tyre, was the older city. Tyre may have begun as its colony, and "Sidon" often stands for Phoenicia as a whole. The coast was "filled" with wealth by these traders.
- **"Your revenue was the grain of Shihor, the harvest of the Nile" (v3).** Shihor is a name for a branch of the Nile, probably meaning "dark," from its rich silt-laden water. Egypt grew grain, and Tyre shipped it. **"You were the merchant of the nations."** The Tyndale notes compare Tyre to modern trading hubs like London or Hong Kong. Ezekiel 27 gives a long, vivid list of everything Tyre bought and sold.
- **"Be ashamed, O Sidon, for the sea has spoken… 'I have neither labored nor given birth'" (v4).** The sea itself speaks like a mother who now has no children. Tyre's colonies and young men are lost. In the ancient world being childless was a deep shame, and here the "stronghold of the sea" is left barren.
- **"When the report comes to Egypt, they will be in anguish" (v5).** Egypt's economy depended on Tyre's ships. When a great trade center falls, its partners suffer too (compare Rev 18:11, where the merchants weep over Babylon because "no one buys their cargo anymore").
- **"Cross over to Tarshish; wail, O inhabitants of the coast!" (v6).** Refugees flee west to the colonies. **"Whose feet carried her to settle far away" (v7)** recalls Tyre's own colonizing. Tradition dates Carthage's founding by Tyrians to around 814 BC. The city that once sent out settlers now sends out refugees. **"Whose origin is from days of old"**: the historian Herodotus was told Tyre's temple was about 2,300 years old in his day, an exaggeration, but it shows how ancient Tyre felt.
- **"Who has purposed this against Tyre, the bestower of crowns?" (v8).** Tyre "gave crowns" in that it set up kings over its colonies and made kings rich through trade. **"Whose merchants were princes"**: in Tyre, success in business was nobility. The question is meant to be startling. Who could bring down such a city?
- **"The LORD of hosts has purposed it, to defile the pompous pride of all glory" (v9).** This is the key verse. It was not merely Assyria or Babylon; it was God's plan. The target is pride, the same theme that runs through Isaiah from 2:12–17, where God's day comes "against all the ships of Tarshish." Henry: "the sin of pride is fastened upon as that which was the particular ground of God's controversy with Tyre." "Defile" means to treat as common, to profane, the opposite of the glory Tyre claimed.
- **"Cross over your land like the Nile, O daughter of Tarshish; there is no restraint anymore" (v10).** This verse is hard. The Tyndale notes give two readings: the colonists can spread out freely like the flooding Nile, since Tyre's control over them is gone; or they must now work their own land instead of living off trade. "No restraint" may also be "no harbor" or "no shipyard." Either way, Tyre's grip on its colonies is broken.
- **"He has stretched out his hand over the sea; he has shaken the kingdoms" (v11).** God's outstretched hand recalls the Exodus, when Moses stretched out his hand over the sea (Exod 14:21). Tyre ruled the sea, but the sea belongs to God. **"Concerning Canaan"**: the Phoenicians were Canaanites, and the word "Canaanite" came to mean "merchant" (Hos 12:7; Zeph 1:11). The people Israel failed to drive out long ago are still under God's command.
- **"Arise, cross over to Cyprus, even there you will have no rest" (v12).** Sidon is called an "oppressed virgin daughter," a city that had never been taken, now violated. This matches a real event closely. In 701 BC, Sennacherib's annals say that Luli, king of Sidon and Tyre, fled across the sea to Cyprus as the Assyrian army approached, and died there.
- **"Behold the land of the Chaldeans! This is the people that was not" (v13).** The Chaldeans were the people of southern Babylonia. Assyria had flattened Babylon in 689 BC, flooding the city: "Assyria destined it for wild beasts." The point seems to be: look at what happened to Babylon, and know Tyre is not safe. The verse is difficult in Hebrew, and some scholars suggest emending "Chaldeans" to "Canaanites" or "Kittim." The ESV keeps the Hebrew. JFB takes "the people that was not" as the Chaldeans, "a people of yesterday," who rose to become the instrument against Tyre under Nebuchadnezzar.
- **Was Tyre really destroyed?** Assyria attacked it repeatedly, but the island fortress held. Nebuchadnezzar's thirteen-year siege ended in a settlement, and Ezekiel says he got little "wages" from it (Ezek 29:18). In 332 BC Alexander the Great built a causeway out to the island using the rubble of the old mainland city and took it after seven months. Ezekiel had said Tyre's stones and timber would be thrown "into the midst of the waters" (Ezek 26:12). The causeway silted up, and Tyre is now a peninsula. Isaiah's oracle sees all these blows together as one judgment.
- **"Tyre will be forgotten for seventy years, like the days of one king" (v15).** Seventy years is a full lifetime (Ps 90:10), the span of a single royal reign or dynasty. It matches the seventy years of Babylon's dominance that Jeremiah announced, during which Tyre too would drink the cup (Jer 25:11, 22). JFB: "The Babylonian monarchy lasted properly but seventy years." Henry also connects it with Nebuchadnezzar's line. Others take seventy as a round number for a long time.
- **"As in the song of the prostitute" (vv15–16).** Isaiah quotes what sounds like a popular street song, mocking an aging prostitute who wanders the city with a harp so men will remember her. Tyre after its humiliation will try again to attract customers. The image is not mainly about sexual sin. It describes trade done for profit alone, willing to sell anything to anyone (Nah 3:4; Rev 17:2; 18:3).
- **"The LORD will visit Tyre, and she will return to her wages" (v17).** "Visit" (*paqad*) can mean to punish or to attend to in kindness. Here it means restoration. Tyre did recover under the Persians and was again a great port.
- **"Her merchandise and her wages will be holy to the LORD" (v18).** This is the surprise. "Holy to the LORD" was the phrase engraved on the high priest's gold plate (Exod 28:36). Tyre's earnings will be set apart for God. Instead of being "stored or hoarded," they will supply "abundant food and fine clothing for those who dwell before the LORD," that is, his worshipers and priests. Isaiah later pictures the wealth of the nations and the ships of Tarshish coming to Zion (60:5–9). The Law forbade a prostitute's wages from being brought into God's house (Deut 23:18), so this is not God accepting dirty money as it is. It pictures commerce itself converted.
- **Was it fulfilled?** Henry and Guzik both point to the New Testament. Jesus ministered in the region of Tyre and Sidon, healing the daughter of a Syrophoenician woman (Mark 7:24–30), and said Tyre and Sidon would have repented had they seen his miracles (Matt 11:21–22). People from Tyre came to hear him (Mark 3:8). Paul found disciples at Tyre and stayed a week (Acts 21:3–7), and Tyre later became an important Christian center. Many readers also see a further fulfillment when the nations bring their glory into the new Jerusalem (Rev 21:24–26).
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline is simple: Tyre's desolation, likely by Nebuchadnezzar, then its restoration after seventy years. He warns, "Let the ruin of Tyre be a warning to all places and persons to take heed of pride," and on v18 says "the trade of Tyre, and all the gains of her trade, shall be devoted to God and to his honour," noting that some of the rabbis applied it to the days of the Messiah. Guzik likens Tyre to a Babylon of the seas, notes that Hiram and Jezebel both came from this region, and stresses that God humbles those honored for wealth rather than character. JFB is the most historical of the three, tracing sieges by Shalmaneser, Nebuchadnezzar and Alexander, and explains Shihor as "dark-colored" from the Nile's silt. On v18 he says Tyre's gains will be "freely expended in His service."

#### Pointing to Christ
Tyre built a world on buying and selling and thought it could not fall. Its story is a parable of every economy that makes wealth its glory. God brought down its pride, then promised that its gains would become "holy to the LORD." That began when Jesus walked the roads near Tyre and Sidon and a Gentile mother found mercy at his feet, and it continued when a church met in Tyre's streets. Revelation shows the final Babylon mourned by merchants, and then the kings of the earth bringing their glory into the city of the Lamb (Rev 18:11; 21:24). In Christ even the wages of the world can be made holy.
