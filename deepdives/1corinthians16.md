*Setting:* Paul is writing from Ephesus in the spring of about AD 53–55, a few weeks before Pentecost (v8). After the great resurrection chapter he comes down to earth: money, travel plans, coworkers and greetings. The shift is deliberate. Chapter 15 ended with "your labor is not in vain," and chapter 16 shows what that labor looks like: a weekly offering, a hard-pressed church across the sea, a nervous young minister, a household that serves. "Now concerning" (v1, v12) is the same phrase Paul has used to answer the questions in the Corinthians' letter (7:1; 8:1; 12:1), so these are the last items on their list.

#### The Collection for the Saints (vv1–4)
- **"The collection for the saints" (v1).** This was a relief fund for the poor believers in Jerusalem. Paul spent years on it (Rom 15:25–27; 2 Cor 8–9) and finally delivered it at great personal risk (Acts 24:17). The Jerusalem leaders had asked him to "remember the poor," and he was "eager to do" it (Gal 2:10). For Paul it was more than charity. Gentile churches giving to Jewish believers showed that the two were one body. As he puts it in Romans 15:27, the Gentiles had shared in the Jews' spiritual blessings, so they owed help with material ones. "Saints" simply means God's people, set apart for him.
- **"As I directed the churches of Galatia" (v1).** Corinth is not being singled out. This is what all Paul's churches were doing, and a church proud of its gifts should not lag behind.
- **"On the first day of every week" (v2).** Sunday, the day of the resurrection. This is one of the earliest signs that Christians gathered on the first day (Acts 20:7). By the end of the century John calls it "the Lord's day" (Rev 1:10).
- **"Each of you is to put something aside and store it up" (v2).** "Each of you" means everyone gives, not only the wealthy. "Put aside… by himself" suggests setting it apart at home, or bringing it to a common fund when they met. Either way it is regular and planned, not a one-time emotional appeal.
- **"As he may prosper" (v2).** Paul names no fixed amount and no percentage. Giving is in proportion to what God has given. In 2 Corinthians he adds that it should be generous, willing and cheerful (8:12; 9:7).
- **"So that there will be no collecting when I come" (v2).** Paul does not want to arrive and press people for money. He wants the gift ready, given freely, and his visit free for other things.
- **"Those whom you accredit by letter" (v3).** The church chooses its own couriers, and Paul sends them with letters of introduction. He keeps his hands off the money. The same care shows up in 2 Corinthians 8:20–21: "We take this course so that no one should blame us about this generous gift."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The good examples of other Christians and churches should rouse us." He reads "as God hath prospered them" as a reminder that success comes from God, so giving is stewardship, and he notes the Lord's Day as a fitting time to give thanks for the week past. JFB says the first day was "already kept sacred by Christians as the day of the Lord's resurrection." Guzik draws out the principles: regular, personal, proportionate and free from pressure. He also takes the word "collection" (*logeia*) to mean a voluntary gift rather than a tax. The lexicon gives only "collection," so that point rests on the context, which does support it (2 Cor 9:7).

#### Plans for Travel (vv5–11)
- **"After passing through Macedonia" (v5).** Macedonia is northern Greece: Philippi, Thessalonica, Berea. Paul plans to go there first, then come south to Corinth and perhaps spend the winter, when ships stopped sailing (v6). Acts 20:1–3 says he did spend three months in Greece. But the plans changed along the way. He made a quick, painful visit first and then changed his route again, and some in Corinth called him unreliable for it. He answers that charge in 2 Corinthians 1:15–2:4.
- **"If the Lord permits" (v7).** Paul makes plans and holds them loosely (James 4:13–15). His later changes of plan do not contradict this verse. They are this verse at work.
- **"I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost" (v8).** This dates the letter to spring. Pentecost came fifty days after Passover, in May or June.
- **"A wide door for effective work has opened to me, and there are many adversaries" (v9).** A "door" is Paul's picture for an opportunity God opens (2 Cor 2:12; Col 4:3; Acts 14:27). "Effective" is *energēs*, "active, at work," the word Hebrews 4:12 uses for the living and active word of God. Notice the "and." Opportunity and opposition came together. Acts 19 shows both: the whole province hearing the word, and a city in uproar. Paul treats the opposition as a reason to stay, not to leave.
- **"When Timothy comes, see that you put him at ease" (v10).** Paul had already sent Timothy (4:17), by way of Macedonia (Acts 19:22), so he might arrive after the letter. Literally "see that he is without fear among you." Timothy was young (1 Tim 4:12) and perhaps timid by temperament (2 Tim 1:7). He was walking into a church with factions that had little respect even for Paul.
- **"He is doing the work of the Lord, as I am" (v10).** That is Timothy's credential. **"Let no one despise him" (v11).** "Help him on his way in peace" was the custom of sending a traveling minister off with supplies and good will.
- **Commentators:** JFB on v9: "Where great good is, there evil is sure to start up as its antagonist." Henry agrees: "Adversaries and opposition do not break the spirits of faithful and successful ministers." He adds that a hard heart in the hearers discourages a faithful minister more than enemies outside do. JFB also notes that v8 "fixes the date of this Epistle to a few weeks before Pentecost." Guzik observes that Paul's plans did not go as he hoped, and that his "if the Lord permits" left room for God to rearrange them. Henry and Guzik both see Timothy as young and in need of the church's protection.

#### Final Instructions (vv12–18)
- **"Our brother Apollos" (v12).** Some in Corinth said "I follow Apollos" (1:12). Paul still calls him "brother" and has urged him to go. There is no rivalry on Paul's side. **"It was not at all his will to come now."** The Greek just says "it was not at all *the* will." Most take it as Apollos's own choice, perhaps to avoid feeding the party that used his name. A few take it as God's will. Either way, Paul doesn't order Apollos about. Leaders in the early church were coworkers, not a chain of command.
- **"Be watchful, stand firm in the faith, act like men, be strong" (v13).** Four short commands, like orders to a sentry. **Watchful** (*grēgoreō*) means stay awake, the word Jesus used in Gethsemane and in his teaching about his return (Matt 26:41; 24:42). **Stand firm** recalls 15:1 and 15:58. **Act like men** (*andrizomai*, found only here in the New Testament) means "be courageous." The Greek Old Testament uses it for "be strong and courageous" in God's charge to Joshua (Josh 1:6–9) and in Psalm 31:24. It is a call to courage, not a statement about men over women: the whole church is addressed. **Be strong** is passive in Greek, "be strengthened," the strength God gives (Eph 3:16).
- **"Let all that you do be done in love" (v14).** Five words that sum up chapter 13 and the whole letter. Courage without love becomes harshness. Watchfulness without love becomes suspicion.
- **"The household of Stephanas were the first converts in Achaia" (v15).** "First converts" is *aparchē*, "firstfruits," the same word Paul just used of Christ (15:20). Achaia was the Roman province of southern Greece, with Corinth as its capital. Paul baptized this household himself (1:16). Strictly, a few in Athens believed earlier (Acts 17:34), so "firstfruits" may mean first converts in the Corinth area, or the first household.
- **"They have devoted themselves to the service of the saints" (v15).** Literally "they appointed themselves." No one assigned them. They saw needs and took them on.
- **"Be subject to such as these" (v16).** Respect in the church follows service, not status or eloquence. In a church dazzled by gifted speakers, that matters.
- **Stephanas, Fortunatus and Achaicus (v17).** Probably the delegation that brought the Corinthians' letter (7:1) and would carry this one home. Fortunatus ("lucky") and Achaicus ("the man from Achaia") are names often given to slaves or freed slaves, so the church's honored servants may have come from the bottom of society. **"They have made up for your absence"**: they brought Paul the church's presence in person. **"They refreshed my spirit as well as yours" (v18).** Good people make a church better just by being there.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Apollos "did return to Corinth when their divisions were moderated," which is tradition rather than anything in the text. Guzik reads v12 as showing that Paul did not command Apollos. On v13 Henry writes that "A Christian is always in danger, therefore should ever be on the watch," and he warns that "There is a great difference between Christian firmness and feverish warmth and transport." Guzik hears military language in the four commands. JFB has no note on v14. On Stephanas's household JFB stresses that they "Set themselves, (that is, voluntarily) to minister unto the saints."

#### Greetings (vv19–24)
- **"The churches of Asia send you greetings" (v19).** Asia was the Roman province with Ephesus as its capital. The gospel had spread through the region during Paul's stay (Acts 19:10), and these churches now greet a church they have never met.
- **"Aquila and Prisca, together with the church in their house" (v19).** This couple were tentmakers who worked with Paul when he founded the church at Corinth (Acts 18:1–3), so Corinth knew them well. Now they are in Ephesus with a church in their home. Later they do the same in Rome (Rom 16:3–5). There were no church buildings for nearly two centuries. A large house might hold a few dozen people, so a city church was really several house churches. Prisca is often named first, possibly because she was the more prominent of the two.
- **"Greet one another with a holy kiss" (v20).** A kiss on the cheek was an ordinary greeting among family and friends. Among Christians it said "we are family" (Rom 16:16; 1 Pet 5:14). "Holy" sets it apart from flirtation or empty show. To a church split into factions, the command has an edge.
- **"I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand" (v21).** Paul dictated his letters to a secretary (Rom 16:22) and then took the pen for the last lines, a personal signature and a guard against forgeries (2 Thess 3:17; Gal 6:11).
- **"If anyone has no love for the Lord, let him be accursed" (v22).** The verb for love here is *phileō*, warm personal affection, rather than Paul's usual *agapaō*. It is related to the word for the "kiss" (*philēma*) two verses earlier. The test is not knowledge or gifts, which Corinth had in plenty, but love for Jesus himself. **"Accursed"** is *anathema*. In the Greek Old Testament it translates *ḥerem*, something devoted to God for destruction (Lev 27:28–29). Paul is not putting a hex on anyone. He is stating the end of a life without love for Christ: separation from him (Gal 1:8–9).
- **"Our Lord, come!" (v22).** Paul writes this in Aramaic, *Maranatha*, in a Greek letter to a Greek church. It must have been a well-known prayer from the earliest, Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea. It can be divided two ways: *marana tha*, "Our Lord, come!" (the ESV's choice), or *maran atha*, "Our Lord has come" or "is coming." A Christian manual from about AD 100, the *Didache*, uses it in a prayer at the Lord's Supper. Revelation 22:20 has the same prayer in Greek: "Come, Lord Jesus!" It shows that the very first believers prayed to Jesus as Lord, and it ties back to his coming in 15:23.
- **"My love be with you all in Christ Jesus" (v24).** No other letter of Paul's ends quite like this. After sixteen chapters of correction, his last word is love, for all of them, including the ones who opposed him. The letter opened with grace (1:3) and closes with grace and love (vv23–24).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christianity by no means destroys civility." On the house church he writes, "Every Christian family should be as a Christian church." On v22 he warns, "Many who have Christ's name much in their mouths, have no true love to him in their hearts." JFB calls Aquila and Priscilla "A pattern to Christian husbands and wives," and takes *Maranatha* as "the Lord cometh," a motto or watchword "to urge them to preparedness for the Lord's coming." Henry reads it the same way, as the Lord coming to judge. Guzik, with the ESV, reads it as a prayer, "O Lord, come!" The Aramaic can bear both. Guzik also notes that Paul's love for Christ and for this church is what gave his rebukes their weight.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter's last breath is a prayer: "Our Lord, come!" Everything in it, the weekly giving, the travel plans held loosely, the call to courage and love, is the life of people waiting for a King who has risen and will return. The collection itself is a picture of the gospel. The one who "though he was rich, yet for your sake became poor" (2 Cor 8:9) makes rich and poor, Jew and Gentile, into one family that shares what it has.
