*Setting:* The same dark months as chapter 32, around 587 BC. Jeremiah is "still shut up in the court of the guard" in the palace while the Babylonian army presses the siege of Jerusalem. He has just bought a field at Anathoth as a sign that life will return to the land (32:15). Now a second word comes to him in the same place. Chapter 33 closes the "Book of Consolation" (chapters 30–33). Its first half promises healing, forgiveness and joy for a city about to be destroyed. Its second half reaches back to God's promises to David and to the priests, and says they will stand as surely as night follows day. That second half (vv14–26) is not found in the ancient Greek translation of Jeremiah, which is noted below.

#### The LORD Promises Peace (vv1–13)
- **"A second time, while he was still shut up in the court of the guard" (v1).** The word of God was not locked up with the prophet. Paul could write the same from prison: "the word of God is not bound!" (2 Tim 2:9).
- **"Thus says the LORD who made the earth, the LORD who formed it to establish it" (v2).** The Hebrew literally reads "the LORD who does it, the LORD who forms it to establish it." "The earth" comes from the Greek translation, which the ESV follows. In the Hebrew, "it" may be the plan God is about to announce. Either way, the words "formed" (*yatsar*, the potter's verb, 18:1–6) and "establish" stress that what God plans, he carries out. **"The LORD is his name."** God's covenant name, *YHWH*, is itself a pledge (Exod 3:14–15).
- **"Call to me and I will answer you, and will tell you great and hidden things that you have not known" (v3).** One of the best-known verses in the book. In its setting it is an invitation to Jeremiah, confined and surrounded by bad news, to ask God about the future. "Hidden" is *betsurot*, literally "fortified" or "inaccessible," the word used of walled cities "fortified up to heaven" (Deut 1:28). What God is about to reveal could not be reached by human reasoning. A few Hebrew manuscripts read the similar word *netsurot*, "guarded" or "secret," as in Isaiah 48:6. The sense is the same. The verse does not promise private revelations to every believer, but it does show the God who answers those who call on him (Ps 91:15; Matt 7:7).
- **"The houses of this city and the houses of the kings of Judah that were torn down to make a defense against the siege mounds and against the sword" (v4).** Defenders tore down houses near the walls to use the stone to thicken the walls and to clear space. Isaiah describes the same thing during an earlier siege: "you broke down the houses to fortify the wall" (Isa 22:10). Even the royal houses were sacrificed.
- **"They are coming in to fight against the Chaldeans and to fill them with the dead bodies of men" (v5).** The Hebrew is compressed. The likely sense is that the defenders' efforts would only fill those torn-down houses with corpses. **"For I have hidden my face from this city."** God's hidden face means withdrawn favor (Deut 31:17–18). The siege would not succeed because God was not defending the city.
- **"Behold, I will bring to it health and healing" (v6).** The turn comes suddenly. "Health" is *arukah*, the new flesh that grows over a wound, the same word as 30:17. It answers Jeremiah's old cry, "Why then has the health of the daughter of my people not been restored?" (8:22). **"Abundance of prosperity and security."** Literally "abundance of *shalom* and truth." The false prophets had promised "peace and truth" without repentance (14:13). God will give the real thing after judgment.
- **"I will restore the fortunes of Judah and the fortunes of Israel, and rebuild them as they were at first" (v7).** North and south together, as in 30:3. "As at first" looks back to the united kingdom under David.
- **"I will cleanse them from all the guilt of their sin… and I will forgive all the guilt of their sin and rebellion" (v8).** Three words for sin are used: *avon* (guilt or iniquity, a twisting), *chatta'th* (sin, missing the mark) and *pesha'* (rebellion, deliberate revolt). Two words for God's remedy: "cleanse" (*taher*, the word for ritual purification) and "forgive" (*salach*, a word used only of God forgiving). The pile-up of words says no kind of sin is beyond his reach. This is the forgiveness of the new covenant (31:34; 1 John 1:9).
- **"This city shall be to me a name of joy, a praise and a glory before all the nations" (v9).** Jerusalem had become "a horror, a hissing, and a curse" among the nations (25:18). Now its name will bring God praise. The phrase recalls Deuteronomy 26:19. **"They shall fear and tremble because of all the good."** The nations will be shaken, not by Jerusalem's power, but by the goodness God shows her.
- **"In this place of which you say, 'It is a waste without man or beast'" (v10).** God quotes the people's despair (32:43). He does not deny the coming desolation. He promises what will follow it.
- **"The voice of mirth and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" (v11).** Three times Jeremiah announced that these very sounds would fall silent (7:34; 16:9; 25:10). Here the same fourfold phrase returns as a promise. Weddings mean families, a future, children. **"Give thanks to the LORD of hosts, for the LORD is good, for his steadfast love endures forever!"** The refrain of the temple's songs (Ps 106:1; 107:1; 136:1). It was sung when David brought the ark to Jerusalem (1 Chr 16:34) and at the dedication of Solomon's temple (2 Chr 7:3). And it was sung again, exactly as promised, when the returned exiles laid the foundation of the second temple about 536 BC (Ezra 3:11). **"As they bring thank offerings to the house of the LORD."** The *todah*, a sacrifice of thanksgiving (Lev 7:12).
- **"Habitations of shepherds resting their flocks" (v12).** A land "without man or beast" will have pastures again. Shepherds and their flocks are a picture of peaceful country life.
- **"Flocks shall again pass under the hands of the one who counts them" (v13).** In the evening a shepherd counted his sheep as they passed under his hand or his rod into the fold, to make sure none was missing (Lev 27:32; Ezek 20:37). The list of regions is the same as in 32:44: the central hill country, the low foothills of the Shephelah to the west, the dry Negeb in the south, Benjamin, and the area around Jerusalem. The ancient Aramaic paraphrase of the Prophets, the Targum, reads "the one who counts them" as "the Messiah," which shows how early Jewish readers heard the shepherd imagery (23:4–5; Ezek 34:23). Jesus calls himself the good shepherd who knows his own and loses none (John 10:14, 28).
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both note that the word for "hidden" in v3 means "inaccessible," and Guzik, citing Feinberg, links it to the fortified cities of Deuteronomy 1:28, which the lexicon supports. Guzik also uses v3 to urge believers to pray, citing Meyer's saying that little prayer brings little blessing and much prayer much blessing. Henry writes, "We must call upon him, and then he will answer us." On v6 JFB says the word "cure" refers literally to "the long linen bandage employed in dressing wounds," and calls the verse God's answer to 8:22. The lexicon's sense is the healing of a wound, new flesh, rather than the bandage itself, though the two ideas are close. On v8 Henry ties cleansing and pardon together: those who "by sanctifying grace are cleansed from the filth of sin, by pardoning mercy are freed from the guilt of it." Guzik, following Morgan, makes the same distinction between cleansing from defilement and pardon from guilt. JFB notes that the words of v11 were "actually used by the Jews at their restoration" (Ezra 3:11).

#### The LORD's Eternal Covenant with David (vv14–26)
- **A note on the text.** Verses 14–26 are not in the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament made in the centuries before Christ. This is the longest passage missing from the Greek version of Jeremiah, which is about one-eighth shorter overall than the Hebrew and arranges some chapters differently. Fragments of Jeremiah among the Dead Sea Scrolls show that both a shorter and a longer form of the book circulated. Some scholars think the longer Hebrew form added material such as this. Others think the Greek translators worked from a shorter edition, or left out a passage that largely repeats 23:5–6. The verses are in the standard Hebrew text, which the church has always received, and their content matches promises found elsewhere in Jeremiah and the other prophets.
- **"Behold, the days are coming… when I will fulfill the promise I made to the house of Israel and the house of Judah" (v14).** Literally "I will make the good word stand." God's promise is pictured as something he raises up and makes firm.
- **"I will cause a righteous Branch to spring up for David" (v15).** This repeats 23:5. "Branch" (*tsemach*) is a sprout, new growth from a stump. Isaiah pictured David's family tree cut down to a stump, with "a shoot from the stump of Jesse" (Isa 11:1). After the exile the prophet Zechariah used "the Branch" as a title for the coming one (Zech 3:8; 6:12). **"He shall execute justice and righteousness in the land."** What the kings of Judah were supposed to do and did not (22:3, 15–17).
- **"Judah will be saved, and Jerusalem will dwell securely. And this is the name by which it will be called: 'The LORD is our righteousness'" (v16).** In 23:6 this name, *YHWH Tsidqenu*, belongs to the King. Here it is given to the city. The shift is deliberate. The King's righteousness becomes his people's righteousness. It also carries a pointed wordplay. The reigning king's name was Zedekiah, *Tsidqiyyahu*, "the LORD is my righteousness." He had the name without the reality. The coming King would be what Zedekiah's name only claimed, and his people would share his name. The New Testament says the same: Christ Jesus "became to us… righteousness" (1 Cor 1:30), and in him "we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Cor 5:21).
- **"David shall never lack a man to sit on the throne of the house of Israel" (v17).** This echoes God's covenant with David (2 Sam 7:12–16; 1 Kgs 2:4; Ps 89:3–4, 29–37). Yet within months of this word, Zedekiah would be blinded and carried to Babylon, and no descendant of David would sit on a throne in Jerusalem again. Jeremiah had even said of Jehoiachin that none of his offspring would sit on David's throne (22:30). So the promise cannot mean an unbroken line of reigning kings. It means the line of David would never die out, and that its rightful King would one day come. The angel told Mary that her son would receive "the throne of his father David… and of his kingdom there will be no end" (Luke 1:32–33).
- **"The Levitical priests shall never lack a man in my presence to offer burnt offerings" (v18).** God had made a "covenant of a perpetual priesthood" with Phinehas, Aaron's grandson (Num 25:12–13), and Malachi speaks of God's "covenant with Levi" (Mal 2:4–5). Priests did serve again in the second temple (Ezra 6:18). But the temple was destroyed in AD 70 and the sacrifices ended. Christians have read this promise in light of Christ, who is both the King of v17 and the great High Priest who offered himself once for all (Heb 7:23–27; 10:11–14), and who makes his people "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9) offering "a sacrifice of praise" (Heb 13:15). Some interpreters also expect a literal priesthood in a future restored Jerusalem, pointing to Ezekiel 40–48.
- **"If you can break my covenant with the day and my covenant with the night" (v20).** As in 31:35–36, God points to the steady cycle of creation. After the flood he promised that "day and night shall not cease" (Gen 8:22). That promise is called a covenant. If anyone could stop the sun from rising, they could cancel God's covenant with David and with the priests (v21).
- **"As the host of heaven cannot be numbered and the sands of the sea cannot be measured, so I will multiply the offspring of David my servant" (v22).** The language of God's promise to Abraham (Gen 15:5; 22:17). Here it is applied to David's line and the priests, the two families on which the nation's government and worship depended. In Christ the promise to Abraham's offspring reaches "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation" (Rev 7:9).
- **"These people are saying, 'The LORD has rejected the two clans that he chose'" (v24).** "The two clans" are most likely Israel and Judah, the two kingdoms, both now conquered. Some take them to be the royal family and the priestly family of vv17–18. "These people" may be Judah's neighbors gloating over its fall, or Judeans themselves in despair. **"They have despised my people so that they are no longer a nation in their sight."** God calls them "my people" even as others write them off.
- **"If I have not established my covenant with day and night and the fixed order of heaven and earth, then I will reject the offspring of Jacob and David my servant" (vv25–26).** God turns the people's claim upside down. Only if he had not made the world would he reject them. **"To rule over the offspring of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob."** The chapter reaches back past David to the very first promises. **"For I will restore their fortunes and will have mercy on them."** The Book of Consolation ends on its keynote (30:3) and on the word "mercy" (*racham*, the tender love of a mother for her child).
- **Commentators:** All three apply vv15–18 to Christ. Henry says that in taking the name "The LORD is our righteousness," the city "professes to have her whole righteousness, not from herself, but from him." JFB says Jerusalem bears Messiah's name through "the mystical oneness between her" and her Lord. On v18 JFB writes that the covenant with Levi was "broken by the priests, but fulfilled by Messiah," and Henry likewise sees Christ's kingdom and priesthood, and the gospel church, as the fulfillment. Guzik, drawing on Kidner, makes the same point: the fulfillment in Christ is greater than the Jewish form in which the promise was first given. Guzik also cites Feinberg on two points: that this passage is absent from the Greek version, and that vv25–26 show God has not set aside the nation of Israel. JFB likewise reads the "two families" as Judah and Israel and says "my people" shows God has not cast off Israel for ever. Henry places more emphasis on the church.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is spoken into a city about to burn, by a prisoner, and it promises healing, forgiveness and songs at the temple. Every thread runs to Jesus. He is the righteous Branch who sprang from David's cut-down tree, the King whose kingdom has no end, and the Priest who never lacks a successor because "he always lives to make intercession" (Heb 7:25). Zedekiah carried the name "the LORD is my righteousness" and did not live up to it. Jesus lived it fully, and then gave it away, so that his people now bear the name "The LORD is our righteousness." God's covenant with him is as fixed as the sunrise.
