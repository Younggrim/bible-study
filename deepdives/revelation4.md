*Setting:* The seven letters are finished. Until now John has been on earth, on Patmos, with Jesus among the lampstands. Now a door opens and he is called up into heaven's throne room. Chapters 4 and 5 form one scene. Chapter 4 shows God the Creator on his throne, and chapter 5 shows the Lamb who redeems. Everything that follows (the seals, trumpets and bowls) comes from this throne. Before John sees any judgment on earth, he sees who is really in charge. For churches under the pressure of Domitian's court, where the emperor was hailed as "our lord and god," this was the most important thing to see.

#### The Throne in Heaven (vv1–11)
- **"After this I looked, and behold, a door standing open in heaven!" (v1).** "After this" (*meta tauta*) marks a new vision, not necessarily the next event in time. The door is "standing open." Heaven isn't locked to the one God invites. Jacob saw a "gate of heaven" (Gen 28:17), and Ezekiel saw the heavens opened (Ezek 1:1).
- **"Come up here, and I will show you what must take place after this" (v1).** This is the same trumpet voice as 1:10, Christ himself. "What must take place after this" echoes 1:19 and Daniel 2:28–29. Here the four readings part ways:
  - **Futurists** take this as the start of the "things that are to take place after this" (1:19), still future. Many pre-tribulation futurists see John's call upward as a picture of the church's rapture, noting that the word "church" does not appear again until 22:16.
  - **Others**, including many futurists, answer that John is simply being taken up in vision, as Ezekiel was (Ezek 8:3). They add that God's people are still clearly on earth in the chapters that follow (7:3; 13:7).
  - **Historicists** see the start of the sweep of church history.
  - **Preterists** see the coming judgment on Jerusalem or Rome.
  - **Idealists** see the heavenly reality behind every age.

  All agree the throne room itself is a present reality, not just a future one.
- **"At once I was in the Spirit, and behold, a throne stood in heaven, with one seated on the throne" (v2).** The word "throne" appears more than forty times in Revelation, and over a dozen times in this chapter alone. Before any beast or empire appears, the throne is already occupied. John says only "one seated." In the Jewish way, he avoids naming or describing God directly (Exod 20:4; Deut 4:15–19).
- **"The appearance of jasper and carnelian, and around the throne was a rainbow that had the appearance of an emerald" (v3).** John describes light and colour, not a body. Jasper in Revelation is clear and brilliant (21:11), and carnelian (sardius) is blood-red. These two were the last and first stones on the high priest's breastplate (Exod 28:17–20). The rainbow recalls God's covenant with Noah (Gen 9:13–16) and Ezekiel's vision (Ezek 1:28). The God of judgment is surrounded by his own promise of mercy. Ancient readers often explained the gems' meaning, for example purity and justice, but the text doesn't do this, and the main point is overwhelming glory.
- **"Twenty-four thrones, and seated on the thrones were twenty-four elders, clothed in white garments, with golden crowns" (v4).** "Elders" is *presbyteroi*, the same word as church elders. Who they are is one of the book's open questions:
  - **The whole people of God**, both testaments: twelve patriarchs and twelve apostles. This fits 21:12–14, where the new Jerusalem has the tribes' names on its gates and the apostles' names on its foundations.
  - **A priestly picture**, from the twenty-four divisions of priests in the temple (1 Chr 24:1–19). Since the redeemed are made "priests" (1:6), this also points to God's people.
  - **An order of angels**, a heavenly council around God's throne (Ps 89:7; Dan 7:9–10). In favour of this view: in 5:8–10 and 7:13–14 the elders seem to speak *about* the redeemed rather than *as* them, and angels can wear white (Matt 28:3).

  White garments and crowns (*stephanoi*, victors' wreaths) match what Christ promised conquerors in 2:10 and 3:5, 21. That is why many see redeemed people here.
- **"Flashes of lightning, and rumblings and peals of thunder" (v5).** This is Sinai (Exod 19:16). The same storm returns at the end of each series of judgments (8:5; 11:19; 16:18), as if each series rolls out from this throne.
- **"Seven torches of fire, which are the seven spirits of God" (v5).** John explains this himself. The seven spirits are the Holy Spirit in his fullness (1:4; Zech 4:2–6). In chapter 1 the lampstands were churches, and here the torches are the Spirit.
- **"As it were a sea of glass, like crystal" (v6).** "As it were" signals that John is grasping for words. Possible backgrounds:
  - The "expanse… shining like awe-inspiring crystal" above the creatures in Ezekiel 1:22.
  - The pavement of sapphire under God's feet in Exodus 24:10.
  - The great bronze basin in Solomon's temple called "the sea" (1 Kgs 7:23).

  To people who lived beside the dangerous Aegean, a sea perfectly still and clear meant total peace before God. In 15:2 the conquerors stand beside it. In the new creation "the sea was no more" (21:1).
- **"Four living creatures, full of eyes in front and behind" (v6).** "Living creatures" is *zōa*, beings full of life. The KJV's "beasts" is misleading. The lexicon distinguishes *zōon* from *thērion*, the word for the "beast" of chapter 13. They blend Ezekiel's cherubim, which have four faces and are full of eyes (Ezek 1:5–18; 10:12–14), with Isaiah's seraphim, which have six wings and cry "holy" (Isa 6:2–3). Their eyes mean they are fully aware and never sleep. They see everything.
- **"Like a lion… like an ox… with the face of a man… like an eagle in flight" (v7).** In Ezekiel each cherub has all four faces. Here each creature has one. They are often read as representing all creation: the noblest wild animal, the strongest tame animal, humanity, and the greatest bird. Jewish teachers had a saying along these lines. From the second century on, Christians linked the four creatures to the four Gospels. Irenaeus, Augustine and Jerome paired them differently. Jerome's pairing became standard in Christian art: Matthew the man, Mark the lion, Luke the ox, John the eagle. This is a later tradition, not the meaning of the text, but it is a fitting picture: four witnesses to one Lord.
- **"Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord God Almighty, who was and is and is to come!" (v8).** This is the seraphim's song from Isaiah 6:3. Hebrew uses repetition for emphasis, and saying a word three times is the strongest form. "Holy, holy, holy" is the only attribute of God repeated three times in Scripture. "Almighty" (*pantokratōr*) is the Greek Old Testament's word for "LORD of hosts." "Who was and is and is to come" echoes 1:4, 8. "Day and night they never cease": this praise is not an event but the constant atmosphere of heaven. Christians have long heard a hint of the Trinity in the threefold "holy," though the text doesn't spell this out.
- **"Glory and honor and thanks to him who is seated on the throne, who lives forever and ever" (v9).** Thanks is offered to God as the one who lives forever. Emperors died. God doesn't.
- **"The twenty-four elders fall down… They cast their crowns before the throne" (v10).** Client kings in the Roman world laid their crowns before the emperor to show he was their overlord. King Tiridates of Armenia did this before Nero in AD 66. The elders do it before God. Whatever honour they have, they hand back.
- **"Worthy are you, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor and power" (v11).** "Our Lord and God" (*ho kyrios kai ho theos hēmōn*) is almost exactly the title Domitian demanded, *dominus et deus noster*, according to the Roman writer Suetonius. Crowds greeted emperors with "Worthy!" John's readers would have caught it at once. There is one throne, and it isn't in Rome.
- **"For you created all things, and by your will they existed and were created" (v11).** God's right to rule rests on creation. He made everything, so everything is his. The odd order, "existed and were created," probably means all things existed in God's purpose and then came into being, or simply "they exist and were made." Some manuscripts read "they were not, and were created." Creation praise here (ch 4) prepares for redemption praise (ch 5). The God who made the world will not abandon it, and the coming judgments are a Creator reclaiming his creation (11:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry divides the chapter into "the heavenly sight" (vv1–7) and "the heavenly songs" (vv8–11). On v1 he observes that we should wait for God's call before entering, not force our way in. He does not read it as a rapture. Guzik does: John stands for the church called up before the judgments of chapter 6. He presents this as his own futurist view and acknowledges the debate. On the elders, JFB argues: "They are not angels, for they have white robes." He sees the heads of the Old and New Testament churches, patriarchs and apostles. Henry likewise sees "the whole church of God, both in the Old-Testament and in the New-Testament state." Guzik agrees they are redeemed people, adding the priestly courses as support. Henry, unusually, takes the living creatures as gospel ministers, watchful and bold. Most commentators, JFB and Guzik included, see the cherubim. JFB notes that the sea has "depth and transparency, but not the fluidity and instability of the natural sea." He also contrasts *zōon* with *thērion*, which the lexicon confirms. On v8 Henry writes, "They adore three holies in this one God, the Holy Father, the Holy Son, and the Holy Spirit." On v10, Guzik and the Tyndale notes both mention the custom of client kings laying their crowns before an overlord.

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 4 never mentions Jesus by name, and that is deliberate. It shows the throne first: the holy, eternal Creator, surrounded by a rainbow of promise, worshipped without ceasing. That sets up the question the next chapter asks: who is worthy to open God's plan for the world? The answer is the Lamb who was slain, standing at the centre of this same throne (5:6). It is also the throne Jesus promised conquerors they would share (3:21). The God who calls John up is the God the Son came down to make known (John 1:18). Through him, the door that opened for John stands open to everyone who comes to God by him (Heb 10:19–22).
