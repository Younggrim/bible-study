*Setting:* The unnamed preacher of Hebrews is writing in the 60s to weary Jewish Christians tempted to drift back from Christ. So far the sermon has argued that the Son is God's final word, above the angels (ch 1). He became fully human, sharing flesh and blood and death, so that he could be "a merciful and faithful high priest" (2:17). He is greater than Moses (3:1–6). The wilderness generation never entered God's rest because of unbelief, and the readers must not repeat their failure (3:7–4:13). Then the sermon turns to its central theme: "we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God," one who sympathizes with weakness and was tempted in every way, "yet without sin," so believers can draw near to the throne of grace with confidence (4:14–16). Chapter 5 continues that thought without a break. It sets out what a high priest is, shows that Jesus qualifies, and then stops to warn the hearers that they have grown too sluggish to take in what comes next.

#### Jesus the Great High Priest (vv1–10)
- **The chapter break cuts a sentence in half.** The ESV heading began at 4:14, and 5:1 opens with "For," explaining 4:15. If Jesus can "sympathize with our weaknesses," the reason is that a true high priest must be able to do that. Verses 1–10 then give the job description and show, point by point, that Jesus fits it.
- **A mirrored structure.** Many commentators notice that the passage is built in matching halves. Verses 1–4 describe any high priest: he is appointed to act for people (v1), he deals gently because he shares their weakness (vv2–3), and he is called by God (v4). Verses 5–10 then go back over the same points in reverse order for Christ: he is called by God (vv5–6), he shares human weakness through his sufferings (vv7–8), and he is appointed for people as the source of their salvation (vv9–10).
- **"Chosen from among men… on behalf of men in relation to God" (v1).** A high priest had to be one of the people he represented. An angel could not stand in for humans. This is why the Son had to become human (2:14–17). His whole work faced in one direction, "in relation to God": he brought the people's offerings to God and stood before God for them.
- **"To offer gifts and sacrifices for sins" (v1).** "Gifts" probably means offerings given freely and "sacrifices" the animal offerings for sin, though the pair may simply mean every kind of offering. The high priest's greatest task came once a year, on the Day of Atonement, when he alone entered the Most Holy Place with blood (Lev 16). Hebrews will return to that day at length (9:7, 11–14, 25).
- **"He can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward" (v2).** "Deal gently" is *metriopatheō*, found only here in the New Testament. The lexicon gives its root as "moderating one's passions," and so "to bear gently with" someone. Greek writers used it for the middle ground between harshness and indulgence. A good priest did not explode in anger at sinners or shrug their sins off. "The ignorant and wayward" echoes the law's distinction between unintentional sins, for which sacrifices were provided (Lev 4:2; Num 15:27–29), and sins done "with a high hand," in open defiance, for which there was no sacrifice (Num 15:30–31). Hebrews will sound that same sober note later (10:26).
- **"Since he himself is beset with weakness" (v2).** "Beset" is *perikeimai*, "to have something lying around one, to be clothed with." Paul uses it of the chain wrapped around him (Acts 28:20). Every human priest wore his weakness like a garment, and it made him gentle. Jesus also knew weakness, being tired, hungry, grieved and tempted (4:15), but Hebrews is careful to say he had no sin.
- **"Obligated to offer sacrifice for his own sins" (v3).** On the Day of Atonement Aaron first offered a bull for himself and his household, and only then the goat for the people (Lev 16:6, 11, 15). Here Christ and Aaron differ, and Hebrews makes the point directly later: Jesus "has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins" (7:27).
- **"No one takes this honor for himself, but only when called by God, just as Aaron was" (v4).** God chose Aaron (Exod 28:1). When Korah and his followers grasped at the priesthood, the earth swallowed them (Num 16). When King Uzziah burned incense in the temple, he was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21). The readers knew a sad contrast as well. In their own century, Herod and the Roman governors made and unmade high priests at will. Annas and Caiaphas held office by political favor. The ideal is a priest God himself appoints.
- **"So also Christ did not exalt himself" (v5).** Literally, he "did not glorify himself." Jesus said the same: "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who glorifies me" (John 8:54). Hebrews uses "Christ" here, the title meaning "anointed," since priests as well as kings were anointed.
- **"You are my Son, today I have begotten you" (v5).** Psalm 2:7, already quoted in 1:5. Its first setting was the crowning of David's royal heir (compare 2 Sam 7:14), and the New Testament applies it to the resurrection and enthronement of Jesus (Acts 13:33). The writer puts it first because the one who is appointed priest is the Son. Sonship comes before priesthood.
- **"You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek" (v6).** Psalm 110:4. Psalm 110 is the psalm the sermon has already used for the Son sitting at God's right hand (1:3, 13). Now the writer quotes its fourth verse, where the same king is also made a priest by God's oath. That is startling, because in Israel kings came from Judah and priests from Levi, and the two offices were kept apart. Melchizedek is the mysterious king of Salem who was also "priest of God Most High" and blessed Abraham (Gen 14:18–20). He is introduced here and left hanging until chapter 7. Two words matter for the whole argument: "forever," because Aaron's priests all died, and "order," because Jesus' priesthood does not come through Aaron's family at all.
- **"In the days of his flesh" (v7).** This means his earthly life, when he was weak and mortal. The sentence that follows is the most vivid picture of Jesus' inner struggle anywhere outside the Gospels.
- **"Prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears" (v7).** Most interpreters see Gethsemane here, where Jesus was "greatly distressed and troubled," fell to the ground and prayed, "Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me" (Mark 14:33–36; Matt 26:36–46). Luke adds that his sweat became like drops of blood (Luke 22:44). Some early manuscripts lack Luke 22:43–44, and the ESV notes this. Others also include the cry from the cross (Matt 27:46). The Gospels do not mention tears in Gethsemane, so Hebrews may be drawing on a memory the Gospels did not record, or summing up the anguish they describe. "Supplications" is *hiketēria*. The lexicon gives its classical meaning as the olive branch a suppliant carried in his hand, but by this period the word had come to mean simply "supplication." The psalms of the suffering righteous man also lie behind the verse (Ps 22:1–2, 24; Ps 69:3).
- **"To him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard" (v7).** Here is the puzzle: Jesus asked to be spared the cup, and he still died. In what sense was he heard? The Greek says God could save him "out of" death, not only from facing it. The main answers are that God answered by raising him out of death, which is the most common view; that God strengthened him to drink the cup (Luke 22:43); and that his deepest prayer, "not what I will, but what you will," was granted exactly as he prayed it. These views fit together more than they compete. The Tyndale notes take the answer as God's vindication of his righteousness and his appointment as high priest.
- **"Because of his reverence" (v7).** "Reverence" is *eulabeia*. The lexicon gives an older meaning, "caution, discretion," and a later one, "reverence, godly fear." The later meaning fits here, as in 12:28, its only other New Testament use. The KJV's "heard in that he feared" was sometimes taken to mean he was rescued from fear itself, but the noun points to his reverent submission to the Father, not to terror. His prayer was heard because it was the prayer of a Son who yielded.
- **"Although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered" (v8).** There is a sound-play in Greek: *emathen* ("he learned") and *epathen* ("he suffered"). Greek moralists had a proverb built on the same two words, roughly "suffering is schooling." The verse does not mean Jesus moved from disobedience to obedience. He was never disobedient. It means he learned obedience by experience, learning in practice what it costs to obey when obeying means a cross (Phil 2:8). "Although he was a son" admits the surprise: a son might expect to be spared, yet this Son was not, and God's other sons and daughters should not expect to be spared either (12:5–11).
- **"Being made perfect" (v9).** "Made perfect" is *teleioō*, a favorite word in Hebrews (2:10; 7:28; 10:14). It does not mean Jesus was once morally flawed. The lexicon's core sense is "to bring to completion" or "to its goal." There is also a priestly echo. In the Greek Old Testament the same verb translates the Hebrew idiom "to fill the hands," the phrase for ordaining a priest (Exod 29:9; Lev 8:33). The suffering Son has finished the course that qualifies him, and he now stands fully installed as priest.
- **"The source of eternal salvation" (v9).** "Source" is *aitios*, "the cause, the author." Aaron's sacrifices gave a year's cleansing and then had to be repeated. Jesus' salvation is "eternal" (compare 9:12, "eternal redemption"). Isaiah 45:17 had promised Israel "everlasting salvation."
- **"To all who obey him" (v9).** The obedience the Son learned (v8) is matched by the obedience of those he saves. This is not salvation earned by works. In Hebrews, obeying and believing go together: the wilderness generation failed through "disobedience" and "unbelief," the two words used side by side (3:18–19). Trusting Christ means following him.
- **"Being designated by God a high priest" (v10).** "Designated" is *prosagoreuō*, found only here, meaning "to address, to call by name." God publicly named him high priest. The verse returns to Melchizedek and so sets up the next section.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the high priest must be "one of ourselves, bone of our bones, flesh of our flesh," and that he must have compassion on "two sorts of persons," the ignorant and those "out of the way." Of anyone who takes the office uninvited he says, "He is an intruder who is not called of God, as was Aaron." On the tears of v7 he asks, "How many dry prayers, how few wet ones, do we offer up to God!" JFB notes that the high priest's "infirmity" included moral weakness, which "Christ had not," though he had bodily weakness that made him capable of suffering and death. JFB prefers "reverent fear" for *eulabeia* and passes on Trench's view that the word comes from careful handling of a precious, fragile vessel. The lexicon supports "caution" and "reverence" as meanings but does not give that image, which is an argument from the word's roots. JFB also cites the Greek proverb "sufferings, disciplinings" behind v8. Guzik traces Aaron's line through Levi and lists Korah, Saul and Uzziah as men who grabbed priestly roles. He connects *hiketēria* with Gethsemane: the olive branch, the olive garden, and the Lamb who would supply the wool. That is a devotional picture rather than what the word meant in the first century, since the lexicon says the olive-branch sense had faded by then. All three agree that "learned obedience" means learning by experience, not moving from sin to obedience. Henry puts it as "By his passive obedience, he learned active obedience."

#### Warning Against Apostasy (vv11–14)
- **A pause in the sermon.** The ESV's heading runs from 5:11 to 6:12, and the argument about Melchizedek does not resume until 6:20. The preacher deliberately interrupts himself. He has reached his deepest material, and he stops to ask whether his hearers are ready for it. The Tyndale notes see this as a speaker's way of waking up his audience.
- **"About this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain" (v11).** "About this" can also be read "about him," meaning Melchizedek or Christ. Either way, the difficulty is not mainly in the subject. It is in the listeners.
- **"Since you have become dull of hearing" (v11).** "Dull" is *nōthros*, which the lexicon defines as "sluggish, slothful." The Greek Old Testament uses it for someone slack in his work (Sirach 4:29), and classical writers for what is heavy and slow-moving. It is not a word for low intelligence. It describes a will that has gone slack. "Have become" matters: they were not always like this (compare 10:32–34, where they once stood firm under persecution). The same word comes back in 6:12, "so that you may not be sluggish," framing the whole warning. The Tyndale notes allow the word to cover slowness of mind as well as carelessness, but the lexicon's emphasis is on sluggishness.
- **"By this time you ought to be teachers" (v12).** Given how long they had been believers, they should have been able to explain the faith to others. This does not mean every believer should hold a teaching office (compare Jas 3:1). It means believers who have known Christ for years should be able to help newer ones. Instead they needed to go back to the beginning.
- **"The basic principles of the oracles of God" (v12).** "Basic principles" is *stoicheia*, a word whose root sense is things set in a row. It was used for the letters of the alphabet and so for the ABCs of any subject. Paul uses it in a different sense for "the elementary principles of the world" (Gal 4:3; Col 2:8), but here the lexicon gives the plain meaning "rudiments." The writer lists these basics in 6:1–2: repentance, faith, washings, laying on of hands, resurrection and judgment. "Oracles" (*logia*) means God's spoken words. Stephen uses it of the law given at Sinai (Acts 7:38), and Paul uses it of the Scriptures entrusted to Israel (Rom 3:2). For these Jewish believers it probably meant the Old Testament, the very Scriptures that speak of Melchizedek.
- **"You need milk, not solid food" (v12).** Paul uses the same picture with the Corinthians: "I fed you with milk, not solid food, for you were not ready for it" (1 Cor 3:1–2). Greek philosophers also used milk and solid food for beginners and advanced students. Milk is not bad. Peter tells new believers to long for it (1 Pet 2:2). The problem is an adult who still cannot eat anything else.
- **"Unskilled in the word of righteousness, since he is a child" (v13).** "Child" is *nēpios*, an infant, the opposite of "mature" in v14. "The word of righteousness" is read in a few ways. It may mean the gospel message about how God makes people righteous, or teaching about right living. Some take it more simply to mean that an infant cannot follow ordinary speech. In each case the infant has not yet learned to use God's word with skill.
- **"Solid food is for the mature" (v14).** "Mature" is *teleios*, from the same root as "made perfect" in v9. Christ was brought to his goal through suffering. Believers are called to grow toward theirs. The writer will urge them on in 6:1: "let us… go on to maturity."
- **"Their powers of discernment trained by constant practice" (v14).** Three words picture an athlete. "Constant practice" is *hexis*, habit or experience, the result of repeated use. "Trained" is *gymnazō*, from which comes "gymnasium." It originally meant to exercise in an athlete's training, and Hebrews uses it again for God's discipline in 12:11. "Powers of discernment" is *aisthētēria*, literally "the organs of sense." Maturity is not a single experience. It is spiritual senses strengthened by steady use, like muscles.
- **"To distinguish good from evil" (v14).** The phrase recalls the Old Testament description of small children who "have no knowledge of good or evil" (Deut 1:39; Isa 7:15–16). A grown believer can tell nourishment from poison: sound teaching from error (13:9), and faithfulness from the drift back to safety that tempted these readers.
- **Commentators:** Henry admits the subject itself is hard, but says "generally the fault is in the hearers," and "Dull hearers make the preaching of the gospel a difficult thing." He calls it "a sin and shame" for those long in the church "to be children and babes in understanding," and on v14: "There is a spiritual eye, a spiritual appetite, a spiritual taste." JFB notes that the Greek for "dull" "by derivation, means hard to move," and that "ye have become dull" implies they were once earnest. He reads "oracles" as the Old Testament. On v14 he observes that "the mere child puts into its mouth things hurtful and things nutritious, without discrimination," and takes the discernment chiefly as telling sound doctrine from unsound. Guzik makes the same point about babies putting anything in their mouths. He treats dullness as a problem of the heart rather than the ears and sees it as an early warning sign before a believer quits. He says the original means the hearer "has become a babe." The "have become" is genuinely there in vv11–12, but v13 simply says "he is a child." Guzik and JFB both take the discernment as mainly doctrinal, while the wording "good from evil" seems broad enough to include moral judgment too.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews 5 holds two pictures side by side. One is the Son of God on his face in a garden, crying out with tears, learning obedience through suffering, and heard because he yielded to his Father. The other is a church that has grown sluggish and gone back to baby food. The first picture is the answer to the second. The priest who can "deal gently with the ignorant and wayward" is not a distant figure untouched by struggle. He has prayed the hardest prayer anyone has prayed. He is not a priest who took the honor for himself, but one God named and installed forever, and his salvation does not wear off at the end of the year. The way out of dullness is not mainly trying harder. It is looking longer at this high priest. That is the solid food the preacher wants to serve.
