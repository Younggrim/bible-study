*Setting:* Psalm 142 is the third of the five laments (Psalms 140–144) in the last collection of David's psalms. Its heading is unusually full: "A Maskil of David, when he was in the cave. A Prayer." Only one other psalm links David to a cave, Psalm 57, which was written "when he fled from Saul, in the cave." The Bible records two caves in David's years as a fugitive. The first is the cave of Adullam, in the hills southwest of Jerusalem, where David hid after fleeing to the Philistine city of Gath. There his family joined him, and about four hundred men, "everyone who was in distress, and everyone who was in debt, and everyone who was bitter in soul" (1 Sam 22:1–2). The second is a cave near En-gedi, by the Dead Sea, where Saul came in to relieve himself and David cut off a corner of his robe but refused to kill him (1 Sam 24). Guzik thinks Adullam fits best, since the psalm feels like the loneliness before help arrived. JFB notes that the heading does not have to mean the psalm was written inside the cave. It may describe the experience that gave rise to it. "Maskil" (*maskil*) is a word found in the headings of thirteen psalms. It comes from a root meaning "to be wise" or "to give insight," and may mean a teaching psalm or a skillful song. Its exact meaning is uncertain. Henry calls Psalm 142 "a psalm of instruction, because of the good lessons he had himself learnt in the cave." Those lessons are plain enough: a man with no one left to turn to finds that God is enough. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### With My Voice I Cry Out to the LORD (vv1–2)
- **"With my voice I cry out to the LORD; with my voice I plead for mercy to the LORD" (v1).** "With my voice" is repeated. This is prayer spoken aloud, not just silent thought. JFB takes the loud voice as a sign of earnestness. A man hiding in a cave from the king's soldiers would usually stay quiet. David cries out anyway, to the one listener who can help. "Plead for mercy" (*'etchannan*) means to ask for grace, for favor that is not owed. Henry notes that David asks for relief as a favor rather than a debt.
- **"I pour out my complaint before him" (v2).** "Pour out" pictures emptying a jar completely, holding nothing back. Hannah "poured out" her soul before the LORD (1 Sam 1:15), and Psalm 62:8 says "pour out your heart before him." "Complaint" (*siach*) is not grumbling. It means a troubled musing, an anxious meditation, the thoughts that keep circling in the mind. JFB offers "a sad musing," and Kidner, as Guzik reports, points out that the word is less petulant than the English suggests. Psalm 102's heading uses the same words: "when he is faint and pours out his complaint before the LORD."
- **"I tell my trouble before him" (v2).** "Trouble" (*tsarah*) is from a root meaning narrow, tight, hemmed in. It fits a cave. David is not informing God of something God does not know. He is bringing it to him. Maclaren, as Guzik reports, says prayer is not to tell God but to relieve the one who prays.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "There is no cave so deep, so dark, but we may out of it send up our prayers." JFB cross-references Psalm 62:8 and renders "complaint" as "a sad musing." Guzik passes on Kidner's comparison of David to blind Bartimaeus, who would not be silenced (Mark 10:48), and his warning that relapsing into silence is the road to despair. He also notes Spurgeon's remark that caves make good places for prayer, and that more prayer in the palace might have kept David from his later fall.

#### No One Cares for My Soul (vv3–4)
- **"When my spirit faints within me, you know my way!" (v3).** "Faints" (*hit'attef*) means to grow weak, to be wrapped in darkness, to sink. It appears in Jonah's prayer from inside the fish: "When my life was fainting away, I remembered the LORD" (Jonah 2:7; also Ps 77:3; 143:4). At the point where David's strength gives out, he remembers that God knows his path. Henry captures David's comfort: "then it was a pleasure to me to think that thou knewest it." Psalm 139 had marveled that God searches out "my path and my lying down" (139:3). Here that truth carries a man through despair. The "you" is emphatic: *you* know.
- **"In the path where I walk they have hidden a trap for me" (v3).** The hunting picture runs through these laments (140:5; 141:9). David cannot see the traps. God can.
- **"Look to the right and see: there is none who takes notice of me" (v4).** In a law court, a defender or witness stood at a man's right hand (Ps 109:31; 110:5). In battle, a shield-bearer stood there. JFB says the right hand is the protector's place. David looks to his right and finds the place empty. "Takes notice" (*makkir*) means "recognizes," "acknowledges." No one will admit to knowing him. After the priests of Nob were murdered for helping him (1 Sam 22:18–19), and the people of Keilah were ready to hand him over (1 Sam 23:12), it is easy to see why.
- **"No refuge remains to me" (v4).** Literally "refuge has perished from me" (*'avad manos*). *Manos* means a place to flee to. Every hiding place has failed.
- **"No one cares for my soul" (v4).** "Cares for" (*doresh*) is literally "seeks." JFB notes the literal sense, "sought after," for good. Nobody comes looking for David to help him. Only Saul's men come looking for him to kill him. "Soul" (*nefesh*) here means life, or simply "me." The line is one of the loneliest in the Psalms, and it is honest. The psalm does not pretend David felt fine.
- **Commentators:** JFB says "utter desolation is meant." Henry pictures David looking round "to see if any would open their doors to him." Guzik passes on VanGemeren's point that the right hand was the place of a legal advocate, and Maclaren's line that people have companions in joy but must face sorrow by themselves. He also notes Kidner's observation that God soon answered: David's family and a band of followers came to the cave, and that ragged company became the core of his later kingdom (1 Sam 22:1–2).

#### You Are My Refuge (vv5–7)
- **"I cry to you, O LORD; I say, 'You are my refuge'" (v5).** Every other refuge has perished (v4). One is left. "Refuge" here (*machseh*) is a shelter, a place to hide from a storm (Ps 46:1; 91:2). David has a cave to hide in, but he says the LORD is his real shelter.
- **"My portion in the land of the living" (v5).** "Portion" (*cheleq*) is a share of an inheritance, especially land. When Israel's land was divided among the tribes, the priests and Levites received none, because God said, "I am your portion and your inheritance" (Num 18:20). David, a fugitive with no land, no home and no share in anything, says the same: the LORD is my portion (Ps 16:5; 73:26; Lam 3:24). "The land of the living" means this life, not only the life to come (Ps 27:13). God is enough now. Guzik notes Kidner's comment that "portion" takes the language beyond fear and into love.
- **"Attend to my cry, for I am brought very low!" (v6).** "Brought very low" (*dalloti me'od*) means made weak, poor, thin. The same verb begins Psalm 79:8 and 116:6: "when I was brought low, he saved me."
- **"Deliver me from my persecutors, for they are too strong for me!" (v6).** David does not pretend to be strong. Saul had an army. David had a cave. The honest admission of weakness is part of the prayer.
- **"Bring me out of prison, that I may give thanks to your name!" (v7).** "Prison" (*masger*) means a place of confinement, somewhere shut in. The cave was not a jail, but it was a trap with soldiers outside. Most interpreters, including the study notes, take "prison" as a picture of confinement and distress (Ps 107:10; Isa 42:7). David's reason for wanting to be free is not only his own comfort. It is so that he can give thanks.
- **"The righteous will surround me, for you will deal bountifully with me" (v7).** The psalm ends by looking ahead. The man with no one at his right hand (v4) will be surrounded by the righteous. "Surround" (*yakhtiru*) is from the root of *keter*, "crown." The righteous will gather around him like a crown, as Trapp notes through Guzik. "Deal bountifully" (*gamal*) means to treat generously, to reward. Psalm 13 ends the same way: "I will sing to the LORD, because he has dealt bountifully with me" (13:6; also 116:7). In history, this is what happened. The men who gathered at Adullam became David's mighty men (2 Sam 23:8–39), and the fugitive became king.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "Those who in sincerity take the Lord for their God shall find him all-sufficient," and reads v7 as David foreseeing that the righteous will join him in praise. JFB gives only cross-references on vv5–6, and says on v7 that the righteous will surround David "with sympathizing joy." Guzik observes that the psalm moves from complaint to praise, from loneliness to company and from weakness to God's generosity. He passes on Morgan's point that the men gathering to David in the cave would soon carry him to the throne, and notes that the cave was on the road to the crown.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 142 is the prayer of an anointed king, rejected and hunted, with no one at his right hand and no one who cared for his soul. Jesus walked that road to the end. In Gethsemane his closest friends fell asleep (Mark 14:37). At his arrest "all the disciples left him and fled" (Matt 26:56), and Peter denied knowing him, refusing to "take notice" of him (Luke 22:57). On the cross even the sense of his Father's presence was withdrawn: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). He was brought very low, and his persecutors seemed too strong. But God brought him out of the prison of the grave and "dealt bountifully" with him, highly exalting him (Phil 2:9). Now the righteous surround him, a great multitude from every nation (Rev 7:9). Because he was left alone, his people never will be: "I will never leave you nor forsake you" (Heb 13:5). And through him the LORD becomes the portion of all who trust him, an inheritance that is "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading" (1 Pet 1:4).
