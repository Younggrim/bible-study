*Setting:* Chapters 13–23 announced God's judgment on one nation after another. Chapter 24 widens the lens to the whole world. It opens a four-chapter section (chs 24–27) often called "the Isaiah Apocalypse" or "Little Apocalypse," because, like Revelation, it pictures the end of the present world order, God's final victory, and a great feast on his mountain. Unlike the earlier oracles, it names almost no nations or dates. That makes it hard to tie to one event, and the Tyndale notes warn against using it as a timetable. It is better read as a vivid portrait of how history ends. Critical scholars often date these chapters long after Isaiah, while traditional scholars see them as the climax Isaiah himself wrote for the oracles against the nations. Either way, they stand where they do on purpose: after all the particular judgments comes the universal one, and after the universal judgment, the LORD reigns on Mount Zion.

#### Judgment on the Whole Earth (vv1–23)
- **"Behold, the LORD will empty the earth and make it desolate" (v1).** The Hebrew word *'erets* can mean "earth" or "land." JFB takes it here as "the land" of Judah, and some interpreters follow him. Most modern readers, and the Tyndale notes, see the whole earth, because the chapter speaks of "the world" (v4), of the "everlasting covenant" with all humanity (v5), and of heaven and earth together (v21). The two may overlap: judgment starts with God's people and spreads outward (1 Pet 4:17). The verbs sound alike in Hebrew (*boqeq… uboleqah*), as the Tyndale notes point out, the sound of something being poured out to the last drop. **"Twist its surface"** pictures the land turned upside down, like a jar emptied.
- **"As with the people, so with the priest… as with the creditor, so with the debtor" (v2).** Six pairs cover every layer of society: religion, household, market, money. No status will shelter anyone. Hosea had said the same: "like people, like priest" (Hos 4:9).
- **"The earth shall be utterly empty and utterly plundered" (v3).** The Hebrew doubles each verb for emphasis (*hibboq tibboq… hibboz tibboz*), "emptied, emptied… plundered, plundered." **"For the LORD has spoken this word"**: the sentence is sealed. JFB leaves this verse without comment.
- **"The earth mourns and withers… the highest people of the earth languish" (v4).** Drought is the picture. The ground itself grieves, and even the elite wilt. Paul describes creation "groaning" under the curse (Rom 8:20–22).
- **"The earth lies defiled under its inhabitants" (v5).** Human sin pollutes the land itself, as innocent blood was said to pollute it (Num 35:33). Three charges follow: they **"transgressed the laws, violated the statutes, broken the everlasting covenant."** Which covenant? The Tyndale notes think of God's covenant with Noah, made with "every living creature" and called "everlasting" (Gen 9:16). That fits a worldwide judgment, and the flood imagery of v18 supports it. JFB takes it as God's covenant with Israel. Guzik notes that Scripture calls several covenants "everlasting" (with Noah, Abraham, David, and the new covenant). The point is that humanity has broken faith with its Maker, not just broken a rule.
- **"Therefore a curse devours the earth… and few men are left" (v6).** The "curse" (*'alah*) is the penalty attached to a broken covenant oath (Deut 29:20–21). "Scorched" may picture burning heat or fire. **"Few men are left"** introduces a remnant, which returns in v13.
- **"The wine mourns, the vine languishes, all the merry-hearted sigh" (vv7–9).** The poem dwells on the end of parties: tambourines, lyres, songs over wine, all gone. **"Strong drink is bitter to those who drink it"**: the old pleasures no longer work. Revelation's lament over Babylon echoes this: "the sound of harpists and musicians… will be heard in you no more" (Rev 18:22). Henry and the Tyndale notes both observe that those who built their joy on wine have nothing left when it is taken away.
- **"The wasted city is broken down; every house is shut up" (v10).** "Wasted" is *tohu*, the word for the formless earth in Genesis 1:2. The city of man returns to chaos. It is never named. It stands for organized human life without God, like Babel or Babylon. In contrast, chapter 26 opens with "a strong city" whose walls are salvation (26:1).
- **"An outcry in the streets for lack of wine; all joy has grown dark" (vv11–12).** Joy sets like the sun. The gates, the city's defense and its place of business and justice, are "battered into ruins."
- **"As when an olive tree is beaten, as at the gleaning when the grape harvest is done" (v13).** Olives were harvested by beating the branches with sticks, and a few always stayed on the top boughs. Grape-pickers left gleanings for the poor (Deut 24:20–21). Isaiah used the same image of the remnant of Israel (17:6). After judgment, a few remain. JFB: "as in gathering olives, a few remain on the highest boughs."
- **"They lift up their voices, they sing for joy… from the west" (vv14–15).** Suddenly the remnant sings. Praise rises from the west, literally "from the sea," then from the east, "the lights" or sunrise, and from "the coastlands of the sea," the far islands and shores. These are the ends of the earth. Gentile nations join Israel in honoring "the name of the LORD, the God of Israel." Guzik connects this with the great multitude from every nation in Revelation 7:9–14.
- **"Of glory to the Righteous One" (v16).** The song honors God as "the Righteous One" (some translations read "the righteous," meaning God's people). Judgment has shown that God is just.
- **"But I say, 'I waste away, I waste away. Woe is me!'" (v16).** In the middle of the singing, the prophet breaks down. The word is *razi*, found only here (twice), meaning "leanness, wasting." BDB renders it "to me, wasting!" The praise is real, but Isaiah sees that more betrayal and suffering lie ahead before the end. Henry: "The very thought of it frets me, and makes me lean." The Tyndale notes put it this way: the new world has broken in, but the deceit of the old world still has its time.
- **"The traitors have betrayed, with betrayal the traitors have betrayed" (v16).** The Hebrew piles up one root five times (*bogedim bagadu, ubeged bogedim bagadu*). It is the same root Isaiah used of Babylon in 21:2. JFB takes the traitors as foreign nations that conquer Jerusalem by trickery. In the wider sense they are all who break faith, the covenant-breakers of v5.
- **"Terror and the pit and the snare are upon you" (v17).** Another sound-play: *pachad, pachat, pach*. A hunter's tools: a shout to scare the animal, a hidden pit, a trap. **He who flees the noise falls into the pit; he who climbs out is caught in the snare (v18).** There is no escape. Amos used a similar picture: a man runs from a lion and meets a bear (Amos 5:19). Jeremiah quotes these two verses almost word for word against Moab (Jer 48:43–44). Henry: "Fear, and the pit, and the snare… are upon them wherever they are."
- **"The windows of heaven are opened, and the foundations of the earth tremble" (v18).** "Windows of heaven" is the phrase from the flood account (Gen 7:11). The Tyndale notes and JFB both make the connection, and JFB links it to 2 Peter 3, where the world once flooded will be judged again by fire. The earthquake is a sign of God's appearing (6:4; 13:13).
- **"The earth is utterly broken… split apart… violently shaken" (v19).** Three times the Hebrew doubles the verb again, the sound of breaking.
- **"The earth staggers like a drunken man; it sways like a hut" (v20).** The "hut" is a flimsy watchman's shelter in a field (1:8), swaying in the wind. The drunken staggering links back to the wine of vv7–11: the world that lived for its party now reels like a drunk. **"Its transgression lies heavy upon it, and it falls, and will not rise again."** The present order, weighed down with sin, is not repaired. It falls.
- **"The LORD will punish the host of heaven, in heaven, and the kings of the earth, on the earth" (v21).** Judgment reaches above and below. "The host of heaven" can mean the stars, which nations worshiped as gods (Deut 4:19), or spiritual powers behind the nations, as in Daniel 10:13, 20, where unseen "princes" of Persia and Greece oppose God's messenger. The Tyndale notes allow pagan gods, angels or demons. JFB prefers "the invisible rulers of the darkness of this world" (Eph 6:12). Henry takes it as earthly kings and princes. Guzik reads it of fallen angels.
- **"Gathered together as prisoners in a pit… and after many days they will be punished" (v22).** The rebels are rounded up and locked away to wait for their sentence. The New Testament speaks of fallen angels kept in "chains of gloomy darkness" until judgment (2 Pet 2:4; Jude 6), and Revelation pictures Satan bound in the abyss and then released "after" a long period before final judgment (Rev 20:1–3, 7–10). "Punished" translates *paqad*, which can mean "visited" either in judgment or in kindness. JFB mentions both readings. Most take it as final punishment here. Premillennial readers such as Guzik see the "many days" as the thousand years of Revelation 20. Amillennial readers see the whole present age.
- **"Then the moon will be confounded and the sun ashamed, for the LORD of hosts reigns on Mount Zion" (v23).** The sun and moon, worshiped by the nations as gods, blush at the brightness of God's glory. Isaiah later says the sun and moon will no longer be needed, "for the LORD will be your everlasting light" (60:19–20), and Revelation repeats it of the new Jerusalem (Rev 21:23). **"His glory will be before his elders."** At Sinai the seventy elders of Israel "saw the God of Israel" and ate in his presence (Exod 24:9–11). Revelation shows twenty-four elders around God's throne (Rev 4:4). The scene sets up the feast on "this mountain" in the next chapter (25:6).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls chs 24–27 a new sermon to "the whole earth" and reads it at several levels: Sennacherib's wars, Nebuchadnezzar's a century later, and the general misery of a world at war, because "the scripture has many fulfillings, and we ought to give it its full latitude." JFB treats chs 24–27 as "one continuous poetical prophecy" about Judah and the last times, but limits v1 to "the land." Guzik reads the chapter as the future Great Tribulation, with Christ's millennial reign in v23. The Tyndale notes are more cautious, calling the vision impressionistic, not a sequence of events. All agree that the chapter ends with God enthroned.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is bleak: an emptied earth, silenced music, a world that staggers and falls under its own sin. Yet singing breaks out from the ends of the earth, and the last word is the LORD reigning on Zion. Between the ruin and the reign stands Jesus, who bore the curse of a broken covenant (Gal 3:13), "disarmed the rulers and authorities" at the cross (Col 2:15), and will return to make all things new. The prophet's cry, "I waste away," is honest about the cost of evil. The song "Glory to the Righteous One" is honest about how the story ends.
