*Setting:* Moses has entered the cloud on Mount Sinai, where he will stay forty days and nights (24:18). The covenant has just been sealed with blood and a meal in God's presence (24:1–11). Now God tells Moses what Israel is to build: a portable sanctuary, the tabernacle, where the holy God will live in the middle of his people's camp. Chapters 25–31 give the plans, chapters 32–34 tell of the golden calf and its aftermath, and chapters 35–40 tell how the plans were carried out, almost word for word. All told, about a third of Exodus is about the tabernacle. That proportion is itself a message: the goal of the exodus was not only freedom from Egypt, but God dwelling with his people. The plans move from the inside out, starting with the ark in the innermost room. This chapter covers the materials and the three most important pieces of furniture. Such detailed building plans can feel like slow reading, but every detail says something about what it means for a holy God to live among sinners.

#### Contributions for the Sanctuary (vv1–9)
- **"That they take for me a contribution" (v2).** "Contribution" is *terumah*, something lifted up or set apart for God, a gift raised from the rest. **"From every man whose heart moves him."** This is not a tax. The gifts must be freely given. When the people heard the plan, they gave so much that Moses had to tell them to stop (36:3–7). The Tyndale notes contrast this with the golden calf, built from gold Aaron demanded (32:2). Paul echoes the same spirit: "God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Cor 9:7).
- **Where did slaves get all this?** From Egypt. When Israel left, the Egyptians gave them silver, gold and clothing (12:35–36), as God had promised (3:21–22). Wealth plundered from Pharaoh's land now goes to build God's house.
- **"Gold, silver, and bronze" (v3).** The metals step down in value as one moves outward from the ark: pure gold inside the tent, silver and bronze in the frames and court. The closer to God, the more precious the material.
- **"Blue and purple and scarlet yarns and fine twined linen" (v4).** Blue and purple dyes were extracted from sea snails, a costly process, and purple was the color of royalty. Scarlet came from an insect that lives on oak trees. "Fine linen" (*shesh*) is an Egyptian word for the high-quality linen Egypt was famous for. These were royal colors for the house of a king.
- **"Goats' hair, tanned rams' skins, goatskins, acacia wood" (vv4–5).** Goats' hair was woven into tough tent cloth, the material Bedouin tents are still made of. The word translated "goatskins" (*tachash*) is uncertain. The KJV has "badgers' skins," and others suggest dolphin, dugong (a sea cow from the Red Sea) or a fine leather. **Acacia** is one of the few trees that grows in the Sinai desert. Its wood is hard, dense and resists insects.
- **"Oil for the lamps, spices… onyx stones, and stones for setting" (vv6–7).** The oil and spices are explained in 27:20 and 30:22–38, and the stones in the priest's garments (28:9–21).
- **"Let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell in their midst" (v8).** This is the key verse of the last third of Exodus. "Sanctuary" (*miqdash*) means a holy place. "Dwell" is *shakan*, from which comes *mishkan*, "tabernacle," God's dwelling (v9). In Eden God walked with humanity (Gen 3:8). Sin broke that, and the rest of the Bible is the story of God making a way to live with his people again. The tabernacle is a big step. The Tyndale notes point out that it was mainly God's royal residence, not a meeting hall for the people; only priests went inside.
- **"Exactly as I show you concerning the pattern of the tabernacle" (v9).** "Pattern" (*tavnit*) means a model or plan. Moses saw something, not just heard a description. Hebrews says the earthly tabernacle was "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things," quoting v40 (Heb 8:5). God decides how sinners may approach him. People do not get to design their own way into his presence.

#### The Ark of the Covenant (vv10–22)
- **"They shall make an ark of acacia wood" (v10).** "Ark" is *aron*, simply a box or chest. (Noah's ark is a different Hebrew word.) It was the most important object in Israel's worship, and the first thing God describes. **"Two cubits and a half… a cubit and a half… a cubit and a half."** A cubit was the length from elbow to fingertip, about 18 inches (45 cm). So the ark was about 45 inches long and 27 inches wide and high (about 1.1 by 0.7 meters), a chest the size of a large trunk.
- **"Overlay it with pure gold, inside and outside" (v11).** Wood and gold together: an ordinary desert wood covered in the most precious metal. Many Christian readers have seen in this a picture of Christ, truly human and truly divine. That is an old Christian reflection, not something the text states, but it has helped many readers.
- **"Four rings of gold… poles of acacia wood… to carry the ark" (vv12–14).** The ark was carried on poles so no one touched it. **"The poles shall remain in the rings… they shall not be taken from it" (v15).** The ark was always ready to move, a reminder that God was traveling with a pilgrim people. When David moved the ark on a cart instead of carrying it on poles, Uzzah reached out to steady it and died (2 Sam 6:6–7; 1 Chr 15:13–15).
- **"You shall put into the ark the testimony that I shall give you" (v16).** "The testimony" (*edut*) is the two stone tablets of the Ten Commandments (31:18; 40:20). They were Israel's copy of the covenant, kept in the most sacred place, as ancient treaties were kept in a temple. Later Aaron's staff that budded and a jar of manna were placed in or beside it (16:33–34; Num 17:10; Heb 9:4). By Solomon's day only the tablets remained (1 Kgs 8:9).
- **"You shall make a mercy seat of pure gold" (v17).** The Hebrew is *kapporet*, from the root *kaphar*, "to atone" or "to make atonement." It was the solid gold cover on top of the ark. Many have taken *kapporet* to mean simply "lid," but the lexicon says that sense has no support in usage; the word is tied to atonement. Tyndale's 1530 English Bible coined "mercy seat," following Luther's German. The Greek Old Testament calls it *hilastērion*, "place of atonement," and that is the very word Paul uses of Christ: God "put forward" Jesus "as a propitiation by his blood" (Rom 3:25; compare Heb 9:5). On the Day of Atonement the high priest sprinkled blood on it to cover the sins of the people (Lev 16:14–16).
- **"Two cherubim of gold; of hammered work" (v18).** Cherubim are heavenly beings who guard God's holiness. They were placed at the entrance to Eden "to guard the way to the tree of life" (Gen 3:24). Ezekiel describes them with four faces and four wings (Ezek 1:5–11; 10:14), but here we are told only that they had wings and faces. **"Of one piece with the mercy seat" (v19).** They were hammered from the same gold, not attached.
- **"Their faces one to another; toward the mercy seat" (v20).** The cherubim looked down at the mercy seat. They looked at the place where the blood would be sprinkled. Peter writes that the good news of Christ is something "into which angels long to look" (1 Pet 1:12), and many have seen these figures as a picture of that.
- **"There I will meet with you, and from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim… I will speak with you" (v22).** This is the center of everything. Above the law that Israel would break, covered by the place of atonement, God would meet his people and speak to them. Later the ark is called the throne of "the LORD of hosts, who is enthroned on the cherubim" (1 Sam 4:4; 2 Kgs 19:15; Ps 80:1). It was also called God's "footstool" (1 Chr 28:2). In pagan temples an image of the god stood in the inner room. In Israel's there was no image, only an empty throne above a box of covenant words, covered by mercy. Numbers 7:89 shows this promise kept: Moses "heard the voice speaking to him from above the mercy seat."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the ark "a chest, overlaid with gold, in which the two tables of the law were to be kept," and says that in the sanctuary God "showed his presence among them." JFB says the contributions were "not to be imposed as a tax, but to come from their own loyal and liberal feelings," and calls the mercy seat "the propitiatory cover," where "the Shekinah, or symbol of the Divine Presence, rested." JFB takes the unusual view that the cherubim symbolized not angels but "earthly and human beings"; most interpreters, including Guzik and the Tyndale notes, see them as angelic guardians. Guzik makes the striking point that the mercy seat was designed before the law was even delivered: God provided the covering for sin before Israel broke a single command. He links the mercy seat to Romans 3:25, and uses Uzzah's death to show why the poles mattered.

#### The Table for Bread (vv23–30)
- **"You shall make a table of acacia wood" (v23).** About three feet long, eighteen inches wide and twenty-seven inches high (about 90 by 45 by 70 cm). It stood on the north side of the Holy Place, the first room of the tent (26:35). **"A rim around it a handbreadth wide" (v25).** A handbreadth was about three inches (8 cm). The rim kept things from sliding off when it was carried.
- **"Its plates and dishes for incense, and its flagons and bowls with which to pour drink offerings" (v29).** The table held the bread and also the vessels for wine offerings (compare 29:40; Num 28:7). All were pure gold.
- **"You shall set the bread of the Presence on the table before me regularly" (v30).** The Hebrew is literally "bread of the face," bread set before God's face. The KJV calls it "showbread." Leviticus 24:5–9 gives the details: twelve loaves, one for each tribe, set out fresh every Sabbath, with the old loaves eaten by the priests. The table was a picture of fellowship, as if Israel were always at table with God, and also a sign that God provides his people's bread, as he had with the manna. David and his hungry men once ate this holy bread, and Jesus cited the event when he was criticized for letting his disciples pick grain on the Sabbath (1 Sam 21:1–6; Mark 2:25–26).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the table was "to be always furnished with the shew-bread." JFB calls the bread "a symbol of the full and never-failing provision" God makes for his people. Guzik sees in the "bread of faces" a picture of fellowship with God, and he notes that the old loaves were eaten only by the priests.

#### The Golden Lampstand (vv31–40)
- **"You shall make a lampstand of pure gold… of hammered work" (v31).** "Lampstand" (*menorah*) is better than the KJV's "candlestick," since it held oil lamps, not candles. It was made from a single piece of gold, hammered and shaped, not cast in parts. It stood on the south side of the Holy Place, opposite the table (26:35). Since the tent had no windows, it was the only light inside.
- **"Six branches going out of its sides, three… out of one side… and three… out of the other" (v32).** With the central stem, that makes seven lamps (v37). Seven is a number of completeness in Scripture.
- **"Cups made like almond blossoms, each with calyx and flower" (v33).** The lampstand looked like a stylized tree in bloom. The almond is the first tree to blossom in Israel's spring, sometimes as early as January. Its Hebrew name (*shaqed*) sounds like the word for "watching" or "awake" (*shoqed*), and God plays on that in Jeremiah 1:11–12: "I am watching over my word to perform it." Aaron's staff later budded with almond blossoms as a sign that God had chosen him (Num 17:8). Some scholars suggest the lampstand recalls the tree of life in Eden, so that in the tabernacle the way back to God's presence is being shown in gold.
- **"You shall make seven lamps for it… to give light on the space in front of it" (v37).** The lamps were small clay or metal dishes filled with olive oil, with a wick lying in a spout. They were set so their light fell forward, lighting up the table opposite. The Tyndale notes observe that the cups mentioned in vv33–34 add up to twenty-two, while seven lamps are named, so the cups were probably decorations and the lamps sat on top of the seven branches.
- **"Its tongs and their trays shall be of pure gold" (v38).** Tongs for trimming wicks and trays for catching the trimmings. Even the tools for the lampstand's daily care were gold.
- **"It shall be made… out of a talent of pure gold" (v39).** A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg) by most modern estimates. JFB gives a higher older figure, but the point is the same: this was a very costly object.
- **"See that you make them after the pattern for them, which is being shown you on the mountain" (v40).** This is the verse Hebrews quotes (Heb 8:5). The earthly sanctuary was modeled on a heavenly reality. Every detail mattered because it pointed beyond itself.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "The candlestick represents the light of God's word and Spirit, in and through Christ Jesus." JFB describes the branches "shaped like reeds or canes," and puts the talent at 125 pounds troy, an older estimate higher than most modern ones. Guzik notes that the almond is the first tree to bloom in spring, a picture of new life, and links the seven lamps with the "seven torches of fire… which are the seven spirits of God" before the throne in Revelation 4:5.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's first words about the tabernacle tell its purpose: "that I may dwell in their midst." John begins his Gospel by saying it has happened in a new way: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), literally "pitched his tent among us." Each piece of furniture points to Jesus. He is the true mercy seat, "put forward as a propitiation by his blood" (Rom 3:25), where God meets sinners above a broken law and speaks peace. He is "the bread of life" (John 6:35), the bread of God's presence given for the whole world. He is "the light of the world" (John 8:12), shining in the darkness. And in the last vision of the Bible, the tabernacle is no longer a tent in a desert camp: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people" (Rev 21:3).
