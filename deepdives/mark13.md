*Setting:* Tuesday evening of the last week, around AD 30, on the Mount of Olives facing the temple. Jesus has spent two days in the temple courts. He drove out the traders, cursed a barren fig tree as a sign, told the parable of the tenants and silenced every group that tested him (chs 11–12). Now he leaves the temple for the last time. Chapter 13 is the longest speech in Mark, often called the Olivet Discourse, and its parallels are Matthew 24–25 and Luke 21. Jesus foretells the temple's destruction, which happened in AD 70 when Roman armies under Titus burned Jerusalem. He also speaks of his own coming in glory. How those two subjects relate across the chapter is one of the most debated questions in the Gospels, but its main commands are plain: do not be led astray, endure, and stay awake.

#### Jesus Foretells Destruction of the Temple (vv1–2)
- **"What wonderful stones and what wonderful buildings!" (v1).** Herod the Great began rebuilding the temple around 20 BC, and work went on until AD 63, only seven years before it was destroyed. The platform covered about 35 acres. Some of its stones were enormous: one in the western wall is estimated at over 500 tons. Josephus says the gold-plated front blazed in the sun, and from a distance the white stone made it look like a snow-covered mountain. A saying in the Talmud runs, "Whoever has not seen Herod's building has never seen anything beautiful."
- **"There will not be left here one stone upon another" (v2).** For Jews this was unthinkable. The temple was the house of God and the center of national life. Yet Jeremiah had said the same of Solomon's temple (Jer 7:14; 26:6), and it fell in 586 BC. In AD 70 the Romans burned the temple, and Josephus says Titus ordered the whole city and temple razed. Massive stones thrown down from the walls still lie where they fell at the southwest corner of the temple mount. The Western Wall that stands today is part of the platform's retaining wall, not the temple itself.
- **Commentators:** JFB describes the temple's great stones from Josephus. Guzik notes that the fire melted gold into the cracks, and he passes on the tradition that soldiers pried the stones apart to get it. That detail is not in Josephus and is probably a later embellishment. The Tyndale notes answer the objection that some foundation stones still stand: prophecy speaks of complete devastation, and no first-century visitor after AD 70 would have doubted it was fulfilled.

#### Signs of the End of the Age (vv3–13)
- **"Opposite the temple… Peter and James and John and Andrew" (v3).** From the Mount of Olives the whole temple was spread out below. The first four disciples Jesus called (1:16–20) ask privately. Zechariah had foretold that the LORD's feet would stand on this mountain in the day of judgment (Zech 14:4).
- **"When will these things be, and what will be the sign?" (v4).** Their question is about the temple's fall. Matthew's version adds "and of the end of the age" (Matt 24:3), which suggests the disciples assumed the temple's fall and the end of the world would come together. Jesus' answer separates them, at least in part.
- **"See that no one leads you astray" (v5).** His first word is not a timetable but a warning. The verb for "see" or "watch out" (*blepete*) runs through the chapter (vv5, 9, 23, 33) like a refrain.
- **"Many will come in my name, saying, 'I am he!'" (v6).** Josephus describes a string of would-be deliverers in the decades before AD 70: Theudas, who promised to part the Jordan (Acts 5:36), and "the Egyptian," who led thousands to the Mount of Olives promising the city walls would fall (Acts 21:38). Many more have arisen since.
- **"Wars… earthquakes… famines… the beginning of the birth pains" (vv7–8).** These are not signs that the end has come. They are what history between the comings will look like. "The end is not yet." "Birth pains" was a Jewish phrase for the troubles before the messianic age (Isa 13:8; 26:17; Mic 4:9–10). Labor pains are real, painful and purposeful, and they lead to birth. Famine came under Claudius (Acts 11:28), and earthquakes struck the region in the 60s, including one that ruined Laodicea in AD 60.
- **"Councils… synagogues… governors and kings" (v9).** Acts reads like a fulfillment list. Peter and John stood before the council (Acts 4–5). Paul was beaten in synagogues five times (2 Cor 11:24) and stood before governors Felix and Festus and King Agrippa (Acts 24–26). "To bear witness before them": the trials themselves become chances to proclaim Christ.
- **"The gospel must first be proclaimed to all nations" (v10).** "Must" is the same word of divine necessity Jesus used of his own suffering (8:31). Before the end, the good news will go out to the Gentiles. By about AD 60 Paul could write that the gospel was bearing fruit "in the whole world" (Col 1:6), which some take as fulfillment before AD 70. Others see the task still running until Christ returns (Matt 24:14). Either way, the mission is the church's work in the meantime.
- **"It is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (v11).** A promise for believers on trial, not an excuse for neglecting to prepare to teach. Stephen is the clearest example (Acts 6:10; 7).
- **"Brother will deliver brother over to death" (v12).** Faith in Jesus would split families, as Micah 7:6 had described. Roman sources show family members informing on Christians in later persecutions.
- **"The one who endures to the end will be saved" (v13).** "Endures" is *hypomenō*, "to remain under," to stay put under a heavy load instead of slipping out from beneath it. Endurance does not earn salvation. It is the mark of those who truly belong to Christ (Heb 3:14).
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads vv5–8 as general features of the age, not signs of the end itself, and stresses that the Spirit's help at trial is no excuse for lazy preparation in preaching. He defines *hypomenō* as "remain under," which the lexicon supports. JFB thinks the disciples merged the temple's fall and the end of the world in their question. Henry outlines the chapter as predictions of false teachers, wars, persecution, Jerusalem's fall and the end of the world, followed by hints of timing and the call to watch.

#### The Abomination of Desolation (vv14–23)
- **"The abomination of desolation" (v14).** The phrase comes from Daniel (9:27; 11:31; 12:11). The Hebrew word for abomination, *shiqquts*, is used for idols and the detestable things of pagan worship. An "abomination that makes desolate" is a sacrilege so offensive that it drives out true worship and leaves the holy place empty. Jews of Jesus' day saw a first fulfillment in 167 BC, when the Syrian king Antiochus IV Epiphanes set up an altar to Zeus on the temple altar and sacrificed pigs on it (1 Maccabees 1:54). Jesus says it will happen again.
- **"Standing where he ought not to be" (v14).** In Greek "abomination" is neuter but "standing" is masculine. Mark seems to point to a person, not just a thing.
- **"Let the reader understand" (v14).** This is Mark's own aside, or possibly Jesus' words pointing to the reader of Daniel. Either way it signals that a hint is being given and must be grasped.
- **What was it?** Several views are held. (1) Events of AD 66–70: the Zealots who took over the temple, killed priests and installed a high priest by lot; or the Roman armies with their idolatrous standards, which Luke's parallel suggests when it says, "When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then know that its desolation has come near" (Luke 21:20). When the Romans burned the temple, soldiers set up their standards in its court and sacrificed to them (Josephus, War 6.316). (2) A future figure, the "man of lawlessness" Paul says will take his seat "in the temple of God" (2 Thess 2:3–4), at the end of the age. (3) Both: the events of AD 70 as a real fulfillment that also foreshadows a final one. Each view has serious defenders.
- **"Let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains" (vv14–16).** The details are local and practical. Judean houses had flat roofs reached by an outside stair, so a person on the roof could go straight down and run without going inside. A field worker would have left his cloak at the edge of the field. The early church historian Eusebius says the Christians of Jerusalem, warned by a prophecy, fled across the Jordan to Pella before the siege, and so escaped.
- **"Alas for women who are pregnant… Pray that it may not happen in winter" (vv17–18).** Flight is hardest for the most vulnerable. In winter the rains flood the wadis, the dry stream beds, and make travel and survival much harder.
- **"Such tribulation as has not been from the beginning… and never will be" (v19).** Language drawn from Daniel 12:1. Josephus, who saw it, describes the siege of AD 70 as the worst suffering any city had known: famine, civil war inside the walls, crucifixions outside them and, he says, over a million dead. Those who see a future fulfillment note that "never will be" points beyond any single historical event. Those who read it of AD 70 note that this is the language of prophetic intensity, used elsewhere of other disasters (Exod 10:14; Joel 2:2).
- **"For the sake of the elect… he shortened the days" (v20).** God sets limits on even the worst suffering. "The elect" are God's chosen people, here meaning believers.
- **"False christs and false prophets… signs and wonders" (vv21–22).** Miracles alone do not prove a message is from God (Deut 13:1–3; 2 Thess 2:9). "If possible" implies it is not possible for the elect to be finally led astray, though they must stay on guard.
- **"I have told you all things beforehand" (v23).** Forewarned is forearmed. The section ends with the same "be on guard" with which it began.
- **Commentators:** The commentators show the range of views. JFB takes the abomination as the Roman standards, linking Daniel and 1 Maccabees, and reads the great tribulation as the Jewish War. The Tyndale notes take all of vv5–23 as fulfilled around AD 70, pointing to the local details of flight from Judea. Guzik holds that Antiochus was a preview but not the fulfillment, and that the abomination and great tribulation are still future, requiring a working temple. He also holds that the church will be caught up before that tribulation. That is a common position among evangelicals but one many others do not share. Guzik notes that *shiqquts* refers to a detestable idol, which matches the Hebrew lexicon.

#### The Coming of the Son of Man (vv24–27)
- **"After that tribulation, the sun will be darkened" (v24).** These are quotations and echoes of the prophets (Isa 13:10; 34:4; Joel 2:10, 31). In Isaiah 13 such language describes the fall of Babylon. The prophets used cosmic images for the collapse of nations and for the Day of the LORD.
- **Two readings.** Many read vv24–27 as the visible return of Christ at the end of history, after a period ("in those days," left undefined) that follows the fall of Jerusalem. Others read the cosmic language as the prophets used it, for the end of the old temple order in AD 70 and Christ's vindication and reign. Most Christian traditions hold that whatever the near reference, these verses ultimately speak of the real, bodily return of Christ that the rest of the New Testament teaches (Acts 1:11; 1 Thess 4:16–17).
- **"The Son of Man coming in clouds with great power and glory" (v26).** This is Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of Days and receives an everlasting kingdom. In Daniel he comes *to* God to be enthroned. Jesus will quote this verse again before the high priest (14:62), and it is that claim that gets him condemned.
- **"He will send out the angels and gather his elect from the four winds" (v27).** The prophets promised God would gather his scattered people (Deut 30:4; Isa 43:5–6; Zech 2:6). Now the Son of Man does it, and he sends out "*the* angels," God's own angels, as his. The picture is the whole people of God, from every direction, brought to him.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "nearly every expression will be found used of the Lord's coming in terrible national judgments," and so reads the passage first of the Christian era opening after Jerusalem's fall, but it also sees a fuller reference to the final gathering. Guzik reads vv24–27 entirely of Christ's future visible return.

#### The Lesson of the Fig Tree (vv28–31)
- **"As soon as its branch becomes tender and puts out its leaves, you know that summer is near" (v28).** Most trees in Israel are evergreen, so the fig, which loses its leaves in winter, was an obvious marker of the seasons. Its spring leaves mean summer is coming. Two days earlier Jesus had cursed a fig tree in leaf without fruit (11:13–14, 20–21), and the image may recall it.
- **"He is near, at the very gates" (v29).** The Greek can mean "he" or "it" (the end, or the kingdom). Either way, when "these things" happen, something is close.
- **"This generation will not pass away until all these things take place" (v30).** The most debated verse in the chapter. The main views: (1) "this generation" is Jesus' hearers, and "all these things" means the events of vv5–23, the temple's fall, which happened about forty years later, roughly a generation; (2) the word means "this kind of people," whether the Jewish people or unbelieving humanity, which will last to the end; (3) it means the generation alive when the final signs begin. In Mark, "this generation" elsewhere means Jesus' contemporaries (8:12, 38; 9:19), which favors the first view, but the first view has to explain how vv24–27 fit.
- **"Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (v31).** Isaiah said, "The grass withers… but the word of our God will stand forever" (Isa 40:8). Jesus says it of his own words, an astonishing claim for any teacher to make.
- **Commentators:** JFB applies v30 to the disciples' generation and says that, for the destruction of Jerusalem, "the facts entirely correspond." Guzik rejects that and takes "this generation" as the generation that sees the final signs, or perhaps the Jewish people as a race. The two commentators illustrate how far apart careful believers have landed on this verse.

#### No One Knows That Day or Hour (vv32–37)
- **"But concerning that day or that hour" (v32).** "That day" is the Old Testament's "day of the LORD," the day of final judgment and salvation. Many see "but" as a turn. The events of vv5–23 had signs. The final day does not.
- **"Not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (v32).** Matthew 24:36 has the same words, though some manuscripts there leave them out. The phrase was hard for early Christians, and it is a mark of honesty that it survives. The church's classic explanation is that in becoming man, the Son willingly took a true human mind and lived within human limits (Phil 2:6–7; Luke 2:52). As the incarnate Son, he did not use what belonged to him as God, and the Father had not made this known for him to reveal. Notice also the order: angels, then the Son, then the Father. Jesus ranks himself above the angels.
- **"Be on guard, keep awake" (v33).** If even the Son does not give the date, no one else can. Date-setting is forbidden by the passage that is most often used for it. Some manuscripts add "and pray," probably from 14:38.
- **"A man going on a journey… puts his servants in charge, each with his work" (v34).** Each servant has a task. Watching does not mean staring at the sky. It means faithful work while the master is away. The "doorkeeper" had to stay awake to open the moment the master knocked.
- **"In the evening, or at midnight, or when the rooster crows, or in the morning" (v35).** These are the four Roman night watches, from about 6 p.m. to 6 a.m. Mark uses them deliberately. In the next two chapters the same times appear: the Last Supper "when it was evening" (14:17), the arrest in the night, Peter's denial "before the rooster crows" (14:30, 72) and Jesus handed to Pilate "as soon as it was morning" (15:1).
- **"Lest he come suddenly and find you asleep" (v36).** Within hours of this speech, Peter, James and John, three of the four listeners, will be found asleep in Gethsemane three times (14:37–41).
- **"What I say to you I say to all: Stay awake" (v37).** The speech was given privately to four, but it is for every believer in every generation.
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes "nor the Son" as Jesus' voluntary limitation in his incarnation and uses the verse to rule out date-setting. JFB records that this saying is peculiar to Mark and that interpreters have explained it differently. It also reads the doorkeeper as pointing to "the official duty of the ministers of religion," a fair application though the parable addresses every servant. Henry notes that the sermon was private, "preached only to four of his disciples," and yet the closing words widen it to all.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus speaks here as a prophet greater than Jeremiah and Daniel. He foretells the end of the temple and speaks of himself as the Son of Man of Daniel 7, coming in glory to gather his people. His words outlast heaven and earth. Yet he also accepts not knowing the day, as part of the humility in which he came. In a few days the sun will darken at noon over his cross (15:33), and the temple's curtain will tear (15:38). The temple order ends because he has come to replace it. His people wait for him, not anxiously studying signs, but awake, at work, enduring, and sure that he will come.
