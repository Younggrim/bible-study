*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. Of David." A short psalm, just eight verses, and no occasion is named. Unlike the previous few, David now calls himself "the king" (v6), so it most likely comes from his reign, not his years running from Saul. Many readers, JFB and Henry among them, think of Absalom's rebellion, when David fled Jerusalem and took refuge at Mahanaim, east of the Jordan, cut off from the tent where the ark stood (2 Sam 15:24–25; 17:24). Guzik also mentions a campaign far to the north (2 Sam 8:3). Either way, David is far from God's house, worn out, and praying from a distance. The Tyndale notes group Psalms 61–63 together as royal prayers: all three long for God's presence and are concerned with the king's safety. The psalm has three movements: a cry from far away (vv1–3), a longing to dwell with God (vv4–5), and a prayer for the king that reaches beyond David himself (vv6–8).

#### Lead Me to the Rock (vv1–3)
- **"With stringed instruments" (title).** The Hebrew is *neginah*, singular, where other titles have the plural *neginoth* (4, 6, 54, 55). It means music on strings, or possibly a stringed song.
- **"Hear my cry, O God, listen to my prayer" (v1).** "Cry" (*rinnah*) is a loud, ringing cry. It is usually a shout of joy, but here it is a shout of need. Guzik notes the wisdom of asking God to hear, not assuming it.
- **"From the end of the earth I call to you" (v2).** David never traveled to the literal ends of the earth. Guzik and VanGemeren take it as how far away he feels: at the edge, beyond the reach of comfort. The Tyndale notes say it suggests a cry from a place far from God. JFB reads it as "places remote from the sanctuary," and points to Deuteronomy 28:64, where exile scatters Israel "from one end of the earth to the other." Henry: "Heaven is equally accessible from all places." No place is too far for prayer. Jonah prayed from the belly of a fish (Jonah 2:1).
- **"When my heart is faint" (v2).** The verb *'ataph* means to grow weak, to be wrapped up or covered over. JFB: "covered over with darkness." The King James has "overwhelmed." Jonah uses it: "When my life was fainting away, I remembered the LORD" (Jonah 2:7). So does the title of Psalm 102: "A Prayer of one afflicted, when he is faint." Guzik notes that David leaves the cause unnamed, which makes the prayer usable for anyone whose heart is giving out.
- **"Lead me to the rock that is higher than I" (v2).** One of the best-loved lines in the Psalms. The Judean wilderness where David had hidden is full of high crags, places a hunted man could climb to be out of reach (1 Sam 23:25–28; 24:2). Boice, cited by Guzik, makes that connection. But this rock is "higher than I." David cannot climb it in his own strength. He needs to be led to it. JFB: "which otherwise I cannot ascend." God himself is the rock (18:2; 62:2), and the safety David needs is above his own wisdom and ability. When Moses asked to see God's glory, God put him "in a cleft of the rock" (Exod 33:22), a link Henry makes.
- **"For you have been my refuge, a strong tower against the enemy" (v3).** He asks for help now because God has helped before. A "strong tower" (*migdal-'oz*) was a fortified tower in a city wall, the last place of retreat in a siege (Judg 9:51). "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous man runs into it and is safe" (Prov 18:10). Henry: "Past experiences of the benefit of trusting in God… should encourage us to hope that it will not be in vain."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "That which separates us from our other comforts should drive us so much the nearer to God," and "Weeping must quicken praying, and not deaden it." He reads the rock as God's power and promise, "This rock is Christ." Meyer, cited by Guzik, also sees Christ as the rock higher than us. JFB reads "shelter" and "strong tower" as repeating the same thought, as Hebrew poetry often does.

#### Let Me Dwell in Your Tent Forever (vv4–5)
- **"Let me dwell in your tent forever!" (v4).** "Tent" (*'ohel*) is the word for the tabernacle and for the tent David pitched in Jerusalem for the ark (2 Sam 6:17; 1 Chr 15:1), as the Tyndale notes point out. To a man far from home, the thing he misses most is not his palace but God's house. The verb "dwell" (*gur*) usually means to stay as a guest or resident alien. David wants to be God's guest for good. Psalm 15:1 asks, "O LORD, who shall sojourn in your tent?" and Psalm 23:6 answers, "I shall dwell in the house of the LORD forever." Trapp, cited by Guzik, notes that David valued God's tent above his own palace (compare 84:10).
- **"Let me take refuge under the shelter of your wings! Selah" (v4).** The wings again, as in 57:1. Guzik and Henry both mention the two senses: a mother bird sheltering her chicks, and the wings of the cherubim stretched over the ark in the sanctuary (Exod 25:20). In a psalm about God's tent, the second is close at hand. To be under his wings is to be in his presence.
- **"For you, O God, have heard my vows" (v5).** A vow was a solemn promise made to God, often during trouble, to be kept when God answered (Gen 28:20–22; 1 Sam 1:11). The Tyndale notes say these vows might include sacrifices offered in worship (66:13–15; 116:14, 18). JFB: vows here "implies prayers," since the vows went with his requests.
- **"You have given me the heritage of those who fear your name" (v5).** "Heritage" (*yerushah*) is an inheritance, a possession passed down. The Tyndale notes list the possible meanings: the land God promised (as in Ps 60), the joy of God's presence (119:111), or God's blessings generally (16:5–6). JFB: a "part in the spiritual blessings of Israel." Guzik takes it as David's rule over God's people. Henry points to the Levites, whose inheritance was God himself: "those that fear God have enough in him."
- **Commentators:** Henry ties v4 to heaven. The earthly tent was "a type and figure of heaven" (Heb 9:24), so to dwell there "for ever" looks beyond this life. He also says God "keeps an account" of our vows, which "should be a good reason with us… why we should perform our vows." JFB reads v4 as a desire: "So I desire to do" (compare 23:6).

#### Prolong the Life of the King (vv6–8)
- **"Prolong the life of the king; may his years endure to all generations!" (v6).** David suddenly speaks of "the king" in the third person. He may be praying for himself in the formal way kings were spoken of, or the psalm may have been used in worship by the people praying for their king. "Days added to days" is literally what the Hebrew says. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, compares royal prayers like "Long live the king" (1 Sam 10:24). David did live to old age, about seventy (2 Sam 5:4).
- **"May his years endure to all generations!" (v6).** No human king lives through all generations. That points beyond David. God had promised him, "your throne shall be established forever" (2 Sam 7:16). The prayer is for the king, but also for the line of kings God promised, ending in one King who will reign forever. JFB: "himself and his royal line ending in Christ."
- **"May he be enthroned forever before God" (v7).** "Enthroned" is literally "sit," the way a king sits on his throne. JFB: "sit as a king in God's presence." To reign "before God" means to rule in his sight and under his protection. Clarke, cited by Guzik, renders it "sit forever before God's face." The ancient Aramaic paraphrase of the Psalms (the Targum), used in synagogues, applies these verses to "the King Messiah." Henry, in his introduction, says the word "king" in v6 "refers to the King Messiah."
- **"Appoint steadfast love and faithfulness to watch over him!" (v7).** *Chesed* and *'emet*, love and faithfulness, the pair that God "sent out" in 57:3. Here they are posted like royal bodyguards. The verb "appoint" (*manah*) is the word for God appointing the great fish for Jonah (Jonah 1:17). Proverbs 20:28 says the same: "Steadfast love and faithfulness preserve the king, and by steadfast love his throne is upheld." Trapp, cited by Guzik, calls them supporters of the throne. Spurgeon calls them the bodyguard of King Jesus.
- **"So will I ever sing praises to your name, as I perform my vows day after day" (v8).** The psalm ends with two time frames, and Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the balance: "ever," stretching forward without end, and "day after day," the ordinary daily round. Eternal praise is made of daily faithfulness. The vows of v5 are not a one-time payment. They are kept every day. JFB: "for new blessings will new vows of praise ever be paid."
- **Commentators:** Guzik says v7 fits David only partly and Jesus fully, the Son of David promised in 2 Samuel 7. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, says only Christ's everlasting reign answers the prayer. Henry offers both readings of "the king": David, whose life served "his own generation" (Acts 13:36) and blessed many generations after him, and the Messiah. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: since God keeps his promises daily, believers should keep their vows daily.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 61 is a prayer from the edge of the world by a king in exile, longing for God's tent, God's wings and God's rock. It ends by asking for a king who will reign before God forever, guarded by steadfast love and faithfulness. David could not be that king. He died and "was buried, and his tomb is with us to this day," as Peter said at Pentecost (Acts 2:29). But Peter went on: God had sworn to set one of his descendants on his throne, and "this Jesus God raised up" (Acts 2:30–32). Jesus is the King whose years endure to all generations, now "seated at the right hand of the throne of God" (Heb 12:2), enthroned before God forever. He is also the rock higher than us (1 Cor 10:4), the one who "became flesh and dwelt (literally, pitched his tent) among us" (John 1:14), so that people at the end of the earth can call to God and be led home.
