*Setting:* The plains of Moab, in the last days of Moses' life. In chapter 31 God told Moses that Israel would turn to other gods after his death, and he commanded him to write a song and teach it to the people, so that it would "live unforgotten in the mouths of their offspring" as a witness (31:19–21). This chapter is that song, followed by God's command to Moses to climb Mount Nebo and die. The Song of Moses is one of the oldest and most important poems in the Bible. It has the shape of a covenant lawsuit: witnesses are called, God's faithfulness is told, Israel's unfaithfulness is charged, judgment is announced, and then, surprisingly, God turns to rescue his people and judge their enemies. It runs like a summary of Israel's whole later history. The prophets drew on it constantly, and the New Testament quotes it several times.

#### The Song of Moses (vv1–14)
- **"Give ear, O heavens… and let the earth hear" (v1).** Heaven and earth are the witnesses Moses named in 30:19 and 31:28. In ancient treaties the gods were witnesses. Israel's covenant calls creation itself. Isaiah opens his book the same way (Isa 1:2).
- **"May my teaching drop as the rain, my speech distill as the dew" (v2).** In a dry land, rain and dew meant life. Moses prays that his words will soak in gently and make things grow, like light showers on young plants (compare Isa 55:10–11).
- **"For I will proclaim the name of the LORD; ascribe greatness to our God!" (v3).** The song is first about God, and only then about Israel.
- **"The Rock, his work is perfect" (v4).** "Rock" (*tsur*) is the song's main name for God. The word appears eight times in the song (vv4, 13, 15, 18, 30, 31, 37), mostly of God, and with irony of the false gods in vv31 and 37. In rocky Canaan, a great rock was a place of shelter, shade and defense, and something that does not move. Every word of v4 is about God's reliability: **"perfect… justice… faithfulness… without iniquity… just and upright."** Whatever goes wrong in the story, the fault will not be his.
- **"They have dealt corruptly with him; they are no longer his children because they are blemished" (v5).** The Hebrew is very compressed and hard to translate. The point is that Israel's corruption is their own, not God's, and they have acted unlike his children. **"A crooked and twisted generation"**: Paul uses this line of the world in which Christians shine as lights (Phil 2:15), and Jesus uses the same idea (Matt 17:17).
- **"Is not he your father, who created you?" (v6).** God is called Israel's father in the sense that he made them a people and brought them into being (Exod 4:22; Isa 63:16; 64:8). Ingratitude toward such a father is "foolish and senseless."
- **"Remember the days of old… ask your father, and he will show you" (v7).** Faith is handed down. Children are to ask, and parents are to tell (6:20–25).
- **"When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance… he fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God" (v8).** "Most High" (*Elyon*) is an old name for God as ruler over all the earth (Gen 14:19). The verse looks back to the nations scattered at Babel (Gen 10–11). There is a well-known textual question here. The standard Hebrew text reads "the sons of Israel." A Dead Sea Scroll of Deuteronomy reads "the sons of God," and the Greek Old Testament reads "the angels of God." The ESV follows the scroll. On that reading, the nations were placed under spiritual beings (compare Dan 10:13, 20), while the LORD kept Israel for himself. On the standard Hebrew reading, God set the nations' borders with Israel's future place in mind (seventy nations in Gen 10, seventy in Jacob's family, Gen 46:27). Either way, **"the LORD's portion is his people, Jacob his allotted heritage"** (v9). The whole world is his, but Israel is his own family share.
- **"He found him in a desert land, and in the howling waste of the wilderness" (v10).** The wilderness was a place of wind and wild animals. God "found" Israel there, as if he came upon a lost child. **"He kept him as the apple of his eye."** The Hebrew *'ishon* means literally "little man," the tiny reflection you see of yourself in someone else's pupil. The pupil is the part of the body we protect without thinking. David prays, "Keep me as the apple of your eye" (Ps 17:8), and Zechariah says whoever touches God's people "touches the apple of his eye" (Zech 2:8).
- **"Like an eagle that stirs up its nest, that flutters over its young" (v11).** The Hebrew *nesher* can mean an eagle or a large vulture. The picture is of a parent bird pushing the young out to make them fly, hovering over them, and catching them when they fall. "Flutters" is the same rare verb used for the Spirit "hovering over the face of the waters" at creation (Gen 1:2). God said at Sinai, "I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself" (Exod 19:4). Naturalists debate whether eagles actually catch falling young on their wings. The poem's point is God's watchful care while Israel learned to trust him.
- **"The LORD alone guided him, no foreign god was with him" (v12).** This prepares the charge to come. No other god helped Israel in the wilderness, so no other god deserves their worship.
- **"Honey out of the rock, and oil out of the flinty rock" (v13).** Bees nesting in rock crevices, and olive trees that grow in stony ground. God's goodness came even from hard places. **"Rams of Bashan"** (v14): Bashan, east of the Sea of Galilee, was famous for fat livestock. **"Foaming wine made from the blood of the grape"**: a poetic phrase for red wine. The land was abundant.
- **Commentators:** Henry on the dew: "a sweet and comfortable dew to those who are rightly prepared to receive it." On the Rock: "He is a God of truth, whose word we may take and rely upon." On v11: God "did that for them which the eagle does for her nest of young ones," and he applies it to parents training children. JFB calls the "howling" wilderness "a common Oriental expression for a desert infested by wild beasts." Guzik, following Kalland, explains "apple of his eye" as "the little man of the eye," the pupil, and the lexicon agrees.

#### The Song of Moses (vv15–27)
- **"But Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked" (v15).** Jeshurun is a poetic, affectionate name for Israel, probably from *yashar*, "upright" (33:5, 26; Isa 44:2). The name is ironic here. "The upright one" behaved like a well-fed animal that kicks its owner. "Kicked" is a rare verb, used elsewhere only in 1 Samuel 2:29 of Eli's sons scorning God's sacrifices. **"Scoffed at the Rock of his salvation"**: the prosperity God gave became the reason to forget him (8:12–14; 31:20).
- **"They stirred him to jealousy with strange gods" (v16).** God's jealousy is not petty. It is the right anger of a faithful husband whose wife goes after other men.
- **"They sacrificed to demons that were not God" (v17).** "Demons" is *shedim*, a word borrowed from the Akkadian name for protective spirits. The idols were nothing, but behind them stood real spiritual powers. Paul draws on this verse: "what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God" (1 Cor 10:20). **"New gods that had come recently"**: the LORD was the God of the fathers. The idols were fads.
- **"You were unmindful of the Rock that bore you, and you forgot the God who gave you birth" (v18).** "Gave you birth" uses a verb for a woman in labor (*chil*). God is pictured not only as father (v6) but as the one who went through birth pains to bring Israel into being.
- **"I will hide my face from them" (v20).** The threat of 31:17–18 now appears in the song.
- **"They have made me jealous with what is no god… So I will make them jealous with those who are no people" (v21).** This is measure for measure. They provoked God with a "no-god," so he will provoke them with a "no-people," a nation they despise. In the first place this meant foreign invaders. Paul quotes it in Romans 10:19 and builds his argument in Romans 11 on it: God brings Gentiles to faith to make Israel jealous and draw them back (Rom 11:11, 14).
- **"A fire is kindled by my anger, and it burns to the depths of Sheol" (v22).** Sheol is the realm of the dead, pictured as deep below the earth. God's judgment reaches everywhere, from the grave to the roots of the mountains.
- **"I will spend my arrows on them" (v23).** Hunger, plague, wild beasts, snakes, the sword outside and terror inside (vv24–25). This is a short poem of the curses of chapter 28. **"The nursing child with the man of gray hairs"**: no age is spared.
- **"I would have said, 'I will cut them to pieces'… had I not feared provocation by the enemy" (vv26–27).** The surprise turn of the song. God would have ended Israel, except that the nations would boast, **"Our hand is triumphant, it was not the LORD who did all this."** God's concern for his own name is what preserves Israel. Moses had argued the same way when he prayed after the golden calf (9:28; Exod 32:12). The Tyndale notes explain that God's "fear" here is not alarm but concern for his glory.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes, following Kalland, that the verb "kicked" appears only here and in 1 Samuel 2:29, and he quotes Spurgeon's warning that prosperity can be more dangerous than hardship. He stresses that real demonic powers stood behind the idols. JFB calls Jeshurun "a poetical name for Israel" and reads "no people" in v21 as pointing "to the future calling of the Gentiles." Guzik sees the judgment as purifying discipline, not final destruction.

#### The Song of Moses (vv28–43)
- **"They are a nation void of counsel" (v28).** It is not fully clear whether this refers to Israel or to the enemy nations. Many take it of the enemies, who misread Israel's defeat (v27). Others take it of Israel. Either way, **"If they were wise… they would discern their latter end"** (v29): wisdom looks ahead to where a path leads.
- **"How could one have chased a thousand… unless their Rock had sold them?" (v30).** Israel was promised that a few of them would rout many (Lev 26:8; Josh 23:10). If the reverse happens, it can only be because God has handed them over. **"For their rock is not as our Rock"** (v31): even Israel's enemies could see that their gods were no match for the LORD.
- **"Their vine comes from the vine of Sodom" (v32).** The enemy nations are rotten at the root, like Sodom and Gomorrah (29:23). Their wine is snake venom.
- **"Vengeance is mine, and recompense" (v35).** God keeps account and will repay. **"For the time when their foot shall slip"**: Jonathan Edwards preached his famous sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741) on this line. Paul quotes "Vengeance is mine, I will repay" to tell believers not to take revenge themselves (Rom 12:19), and Hebrews quotes it as a warning to those who reject Christ (Heb 10:30).
- **"The LORD will vindicate his people and have compassion on his servants, when he sees that their power is gone" (v36).** This is the hinge of the song. When Israel is helpless, with **"none remaining, bond or free,"** God turns to rescue. Psalm 135:14 quotes the line.
- **"Where are their gods, the rock in which they took refuge?" (v37).** A taunt. The idols "ate the fat" and "drank the wine" of the sacrifices (v38), but cannot lift a finger. Elijah mocked Baal the same way (1 Kgs 18:27).
- **"See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god beside me; I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal" (v39).** The high point of the song, and one of the clearest statements in the Law that there is only one God. Isaiah echoes "I am he" again and again (Isa 41:4; 43:10–13). Hannah sings the same thing: "The LORD kills and brings to life" (1 Sam 2:6). God's hand both wounds and heals (Hos 6:1).
- **"I lift up my hand to heaven and swear, As I live forever" (v40).** Raising the hand was the gesture of an oath. God has no one greater to swear by, so he swears by himself (Heb 6:13).
- **"I will make my arrows drunk with blood" (v42).** The language of the Divine Warrior, using the strong images of ancient battle poetry. **"The long-haired heads of the enemy"** may refer to warriors who let their hair grow during a vow of war (compare Judg 5:2).
- **"Rejoice with him, O heavens; bow down to him, all gods" (v43).** The song ends in worship. This verse is longer in the Dead Sea Scroll and in the Greek Old Testament than in the standard Hebrew text, which reads "Rejoice, O nations, with his people." The ESV follows the scroll. The New Testament quotes both forms: "Rejoice, O Gentiles, with his people" (Rom 15:10), and "Let all God's angels worship him," which Hebrews applies to Christ (Heb 1:6). **"Cleanses his people's land"**: the word is *kipper*, "atone." God himself makes atonement for his land and people.
- **Commentators:** Henry urges readers "seriously to consider the latter end, or the future state." Guzik cites Edwards' sermon on v35 and, from Romans 12:19 and Hebrews 10:30, says that God may use human authorities but no one should take personal revenge. He sees v43 as joy that reaches the Gentiles, fulfilled in Jesus, "the Savior of the world" (John 4:42). JFB has no notes on vv35–43.

#### The Song of Moses (vv44–47)
- **"Moses came and recited all the words of this song… he and Joshua the son of Nun" (v44).** Joshua stands beside Moses, as the new leader who will carry the song forward. His name here is written "Hoshea," his original name (Num 13:16), probably a scribal detail.
- **"Take to heart all the words… that you may command them to your children" (v46).** The song must pass to the next generation, like the law (6:6–7).
- **"For it is no empty word for you, but your very life" (v47).** God's word is not something trivial or optional. It is what life in the land depends on. Jesus says, "the words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life" (John 6:63).
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls the idea that obedience is pointless one of the enemy's lies. Obedience is life.

#### Moses' Death Foretold (vv48–52)
- **"That very day the LORD spoke to Moses" (v48).** There is no delay. The song is finished, and so is Moses' work.
- **"Go up this mountain of the Abarim, Mount Nebo… opposite Jericho, and view the land of Canaan" (v49).** The Abarim are the mountains of Moab east of the Dead Sea. Mount Nebo, about 2,600 feet high, looks across the Jordan Valley to Jericho, and on a clear day as far as the hills around Jerusalem. God had already told Moses he would see the land from here (3:27; Num 27:12–14).
- **"And die on the mountain… as Aaron your brother died in Mount Hor" (v50).** Aaron died on a mountain too, after being stripped of his priestly robes, which were put on his son (Num 20:22–29). **"Be gathered to your people"** suggests more than burial. It points to joining those who had gone before.
- **"Because you broke faith with me… at the waters of Meribah-kadesh… you did not treat me as holy" (v51).** When the people demanded water, God told Moses to speak to the rock, but Moses struck it twice in anger and said, "Shall we bring water for you out of this rock?" (Num 20:10–12). He took credit that belonged to God and misrepresented God's patience as anger. The sin seems small, but Moses was the leader and stood in a place of great privilege.
- **"You shall see the land before you, but you shall not go there" (v52).** Judgment and mercy together. He would not enter, but he would see. Centuries later Moses did stand in the land, on the mount of transfiguration, speaking with Jesus about his coming "departure" at Jerusalem (Luke 9:30–31).
- **Commentators:** JFB says that Moses accepted the word, and "not a murmur of complaint escapes his lips." Guzik stresses the mercy: Moses still sees the land, and he notes from Thompson that Nebo gives a wide view over the Jordan Valley.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul says of the rock that gave Israel water in the wilderness: "the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4). The song calls God "the Rock" again and again, and Israel "scoffed at the Rock of his salvation." Jesus was the stone the builders rejected, who became the cornerstone (Ps 118:22; Matt 21:42). The song's turning point, "the LORD will vindicate his people… when he sees that their power is gone," is the gospel's pattern: God saves the helpless. "I kill and I make alive" is shown most fully in the death and resurrection of Christ. And the song ends with God making atonement for his people and the nations rejoicing with them, which the New Testament sees fulfilled as Gentiles are brought in to glorify God for his mercy (Rom 15:8–10). Moses died outside the land because he struck the rock in anger. Christ, the true Rock, was struck once for all, so that living water would flow to his people (John 7:37–39).
