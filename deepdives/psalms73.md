*Setting:* "A Psalm of Asaph." Book Three of the Psalms (Pss 73–89) opens with eleven psalms of Asaph in a row (73–83, with Ps 50 earlier). Asaph was one of the Levites David appointed to lead worship with cymbals and song before the ark (1 Chr 15:16–19; 16:4–5). He is also called "the seer," a prophet whose songs were still sung in Hezekiah's day (2 Chr 29:30). His descendants, "the sons of Asaph," led temple music for centuries (Ezra 2:41), and the Tyndale notes say many Asaph psalms were probably written by them later. Psalm 73 is a wisdom psalm about the oldest question believers ask: why do the wicked prosper while the faithful suffer? Job, Jeremiah (Jer 12:1) and Habakkuk (Hab 1:13) all ask it, and so does Psalm 37. What makes this psalm unusual is its honesty. Asaph tells the story of his own near-fall into doubt and envy, and of how he was brought back. The Hebrew word *'ak*, "truly" or "surely," marks the turns of the story (vv1, 13, 18). Guzik notes how the pronouns track the movement: first "they" (the wicked), then "I" (his struggle), then "you" (God), and finally "I" and "you" together. Henry outlines it as how he got into the temptation, how he got out of it, and how he was the better for it.

#### Truly God Is Good (vv1–3)
- **"Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart" (v1).** He begins with the conclusion. Before he tells the story of his doubt, he states what he now knows for certain. The KJV and Henry's margin read "Yet God is good," as if the psalm breaks into the middle of an argument he has been having with himself. "Pure in heart" (literally "clean of heart") means a heart undivided toward God (24:4; Matt 5:8). Some scholars suggest that by dividing the Hebrew letters differently, the line could read "God is good to the upright," which would match the second line more closely. The ESV keeps "Israel."
- **"But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped" (v2).** "But as for me" is emphatic. He knew the truth, and yet he nearly fell. The picture is of a man walking on a mountain path whose feet start to slide. "Almost" and "nearly" matter: he did not fall, but it was close (94:18). Boice, cited by Guzik, notes that such a struggle does not disqualify a believer from serving God; Asaph was a worship leader.
- **"For I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked" (v3).** "Envious" (*qana'*) can mean jealous or zealous, a burning feeling. "The arrogant" are boasters, people who brag about themselves. "Prosperity" is *shalom*, the very blessing Psalm 72 just asked God to give the righteous king's people (72:3, 7). It seemed to have gone to the wrong people.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives the "sacred principle" each saint held in this temptation: Job held to God's knowledge (Job 24:1), Jeremiah to his justice (Jer 12:1), Habakkuk to his holiness (Hab 1:13), and the psalmist to his goodness. "Though we may not be able to reconcile all the disposals of Providence with them, we must believe they are reconcilable." On v2 he writes, "There are storms that will try the firmest anchors." Guzik adds that starting with a fixed truth gives a believer an anchor before confusing experience hits.

#### They Are Not in Trouble as Others Are (vv4–12)
- **"For they have no pangs until death; their bodies are fat and sleek" (v4).** They seem to die peacefully, without suffering, and live healthy and well fed. "Fat" in the Bible is a sign of prosperity, though often with a hint of complacency (Deut 32:15). The Hebrew line is a little uncertain; the KJV reads "there are no bands in their death."
- **"They are not stricken like the rest of mankind" (v5).** The ordinary troubles of life seem to miss them.
- **"Therefore pride is their necklace; violence covers them as a garment" (v6).** They wear their pride like jewelry for all to see and their violence like clothes, without shame. Proverbs uses the same image for wisdom worn as "a garland… and pendants for your neck" (Prov 1:9). Clarke, cited by Guzik, notes that a chain around the neck could mark high office (Gen 41:42).
- **"Their eyes swell out through fatness; their hearts overflow with follies" (v7).** Their faces are puffy with rich food, and their imaginations run over with schemes. The Greek Old Testament reads "their iniquity comes out of fatness."
- **"They set their mouths against the heavens, and their tongue struts through the earth" (v9).** A vivid picture: their mouth reaches up to insult God, and their tongue walks around the earth claiming it. They talk as if they own heaven and earth.
- **"Therefore his people turn back to them, and find no fault in them" (v10).** One of the hardest verses in the Psalms to translate. Kidner, cited by Guzik, says so. The ESV takes it to mean that even God's people are drawn to these successful men and admire them. Literally the second line reads "and waters of fullness are drained by them," which could mean the crowd drinks in their words, or (as Henry takes it) that God's people are made to drink a bitter cup to the dregs. The general sense is that the wicked win followers.
- **"And they say, 'How can God know? Is there knowledge in the Most High?'" (v11).** This is practical atheism: not denying God exists, but living as if he does not see or care (10:11; 94:7).
- **"Behold, these are the wicked; always at ease, they increase in riches" (v12).** "At ease" means carefree, secure. This is the summary of his complaint.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the wicked "seem as if by some special privilege they were exempted from the common lot of sorrows," and says of their pride, "It is no harm to wear a chain or necklace; but when pride ties it on… it ceases to be an ornament." The Tyndale notes call vv4–12 a caricature, deliberately exaggerated, of the bliss of the wicked, the opposite of Psalm 1. Guzik agrees that Asaph overstates the case, but says the feeling is real, and many believers share it.

#### All in Vain Have I Kept My Heart Clean (vv13–15)
- **"All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence" (v13).** This is the second *'ak*: "Surely in vain." Here is the bottom of his doubt. If the wicked do so well, what was the point of his purity? Washing the hands was a picture of declaring oneself innocent (26:6; Deut 21:6–7). Malachi's people asked the same: "It is vain to serve God. What is the profit…?" (Mal 3:14).
- **"For all the day long I have been stricken and rebuked every morning" (v14).** While they are not "stricken" (v5), he is stricken all day. "Rebuked" means disciplined. He feels as if God punishes him every morning while rewarding them.
- **"If I had said, 'I will speak thus,' I would have betrayed the generation of your children" (v15).** The first step back. He realizes that if he voiced these thoughts publicly, he would be faithless to God's family, causing others to stumble. "Betrayed" (*bagad*) means to deal treacherously, to break faith. "The generation of your children" means God's people. JFB: the verb is "literally, 'deceive, mislead.'" He did not pretend his doubts away. He brought them to God instead of spreading them. The Tyndale notes say that, instead of discouraging God's people, he took the problem inward and reflected on it.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is bad to think ill, but it is worse to speak it, for that is giving the evil thought an imprimatur—a sanction… If therefore thou hast been so foolish as to think evil, be so wise as to lay thy hand upon thy mouth" (Prov 30:32). He adds that the psalmist's concern for God's people was "the first point he gained." Guzik reads "betrayed" more gently as being "untrue to" God's children.

#### Until I Went into the Sanctuary (vv16–17)
- **"But when I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task" (v16).** "Wearisome" (*'amal*) means toil, trouble. He tried to think his way through and only wore himself out. JFB: "literally, 'studied,' or, 'pondered this riddle'; but in vain."
- **"Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end" (v17).** The turning point of the psalm. "Sanctuary" is plural in Hebrew, "the holy places of God," the temple and its courts. Nothing about the facts changed. He went to worship, and there his perspective changed. He saw "their end" (*'acharit*), literally "their afterward," what comes after. Kidner, cited by Guzik, makes this point: their story is not finished. In the temple he would hear God's word, see sacrifices for sin, and stand in the presence of the eternal God. Guzik lists what worship gave him: God back at the center, truth beyond what he could see, and a reminder of how serious sin is.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If there were not another life after this, we could not fully reconcile the prosperity of the wicked with the justice of God." In the sanctuary, he says, the psalmist "consulted the scriptures, and the lips of the priests," prayed, and "at length, he understood the wretched end of wicked people… even in the height of their prosperity they were rather to be pitied than envied." JFB: he went "to enquire," as Moses met God above the ark (Exod 25:22).

#### You Set Them in Slippery Places (vv18–22)
- **"Truly you set them in slippery places; you make them fall to ruin" (v18).** The third *'ak*. His own feet had almost slipped (v2). Now he sees that the wicked are the ones on slippery ground. Their prosperity sits on ice. Jonathan Edwards used Deuteronomy 32:35, "their foot shall slip in due time," for his sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," and the idea is the same.
- **"How they are destroyed in a moment, swept away utterly by terrors!" (v19).** Ruin comes suddenly (Prov 29:1; Luke 12:20). "Terrors" are sudden disasters, the shock of judgment.
- **"Like a dream when one awakes, O Lord, when you rouse yourself, you despise them as phantoms" (v20).** Their prosperity was like a dream that seems solid while one is asleep and vanishes on waking. "Phantoms" (*tselem*) is the word for "image," as in "the image of God" (Gen 1:27), here meaning a shadowy likeness without substance. When God "rouses himself" to judge, as if from sleep (44:23), the image disappears. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes that God's seeming sleep is never inactivity.
- **"When my soul was embittered, when I was pricked in heart" (v21).** Literally "my heart was soured" and "I was pierced in my kidneys." The kidneys in Hebrew thought were the seat of the deepest feelings. Envy had made him bitter and pained him inside.
- **"I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you" (v22).** An honest confession. "Beast" here is *behemot*, the plural used in Job 40:15 for the great beast "Behemoth," perhaps "a great dumb beast." Poole, cited by Guzik, makes the point. Like an animal, he had judged only by what he could see and taste, with no thought for God or eternity. Nebuchadnezzar became like a beast until "my reason returned to me" and he praised God (Dan 4:34). JFB: "foolish—literally, 'stupid.'"
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the psalmist "speaks of it as a thing done": their ruin is so certain. He argues that since the wicked often die peacefully (v4), v19 "must therefore be meant of eternal destruction on the other side death." On v22 he says, "What a fool am I thus to make myself uneasy without a cause?" and "Fretfulness is a corruption that is its own correction." Guzik adds that animals live for the moment's pleasure without a thought for eternity, which was exactly Asaph's error.

#### Whom Have I in Heaven but You? (vv23–26)
- **"Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand" (v23).** The most moving "nevertheless" in the Psalms. Even when he was brutish and bitter, God had not let go of him. He was not holding on to God so much as God was holding him. That is why his feet only "almost" slipped (v2). Isaiah has the same picture: "I am the LORD your God, who holds your right hand" (Isa 41:13).
- **"You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory" (v24).** God guides him now and will take him to glory later. "Receive" (*laqach*) means "take." It is the word used when God "took" Enoch (Gen 5:24) and when Elijah was "taken" up (2 Kgs 2:3, 5), and in 49:15: "God will ransom my soul from the power of Sheol, for he will receive me." Scholars debate how much the psalmist understood of life after death. Some read it as honor in this life; but many, with the Tyndale notes and Henry, see one of the clearest Old Testament hopes of being taken into God's presence beyond death. "Afterward" echoes "their end" (v17): they have a terrible afterward; he has a glorious one.
- **"Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you" (v25).** Here is the real answer to the problem of the prosperous wicked. They have riches; he has God. And God is better than anything they have, in heaven or on earth.
- **"My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever" (v26).** "Fail" means waste away, come to an end, as in death. "Strength" is literally "rock" (JFB). "Portion" (*cheleq*) is a share of land, an inheritance. When Israel's land was divided, the priests and Levites received no territory, because God told Aaron, "I am your portion and your inheritance" (Num 18:20). Asaph was a Levite. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, makes this link. He had no estate like the rich men he envied, but he had God, "forever" (16:5; Lam 3:24).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "There is scarcely a verse in all the psalms more expressive than this of the pious and devout affections of a soul to God." He writes, "All is well if God be mine," and "If we make God's glory in us the end we aim at, he will make our glory with him the end we shall for ever be happy in." On v23, he notes that the psalmist corrects his earlier complaint: "Though God has chastened me, he has not cast me off." JFB: "receive me to glory—literally, 'take for (me) glory,'" and "God is his only satisfying good." Boice, cited by Guzik, mentions a hymn by Charles Wesley drawn from v25.

#### It Is Good to Be Near God (vv27–28)
- **"For behold, those who are far from you shall perish; you put an end to everyone who is unfaithful to you" (v27).** "Unfaithful" is literally "who goes whoring from you," the Bible's picture of spiritual adultery, turning from God to other loves (Hos 1:2; Jer 3:1). JFB notes this "figure of frequent use." Distance from God is the real poverty.
- **"But for me it is good to be near God" (v28).** The psalm ends where it began, with God's goodness (v1), but now it is personal. "God is good to Israel" has become "for me it is good to be near God." Nearness to God is the good itself, not a means to something else (Jas 4:8). **"I have made the Lord GOD my refuge, that I may tell of all your works."** The man who almost spoke words that would have betrayed God's children (v15) now speaks to tell of God's works.
- **Commentators:** Henry: the psalmist "was hereby quickened to cleave the more closely to God." He calls the temptation Samson's riddle solved: "Out of the eater came forth meat… He that stumbles and does not fall, by recovering himself takes so much the longer steps forward." JFB sets "the lot of apostates" against the one "who finds happiness in nearness to God." Guzik notes that the psalm ends with worship restored and testimony to share.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 73's answer to the prosperity of the wicked is not a theory but a Person: "Whom have I in heaven but you?" Jesus is the one who makes that nearness possible. He was the truly pure in heart who was "stricken" all day long while the arrogant flourished, and he did not say "it is all in vain." Through his death and resurrection God brings near those who were far off (Eph 2:13), holds them by the hand so that no one can snatch them away (John 10:28), and "afterward" receives them into glory (John 14:3). The rich men of this psalm have their portion in this life (17:14). Those who belong to Christ have God himself as their portion forever.
