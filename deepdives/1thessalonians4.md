*Setting:* Paul writes from Corinth around AD 50–51, only months after he was driven out of Thessalonica (Acts 17:1–10). Chapters 1–3 looked back: how the church came to faith, how Paul behaved among them, and his joy at Timothy's report that they were standing firm. Chapter 3 closed with a prayer that the Lord would make them "increase and abound in love" and "establish your hearts blameless in holiness" at Jesus' coming (3:12–13). Chapter 4 opens the second half of the letter by picking up exactly those words: holiness, love, and the coming of the Lord. Timothy's report seems to have raised three needs. New converts from a pagan city needed clear teaching on sexual purity. Some believers needed a nudge to keep working. And some had died since Paul left, leaving the church unsure what would become of them when Jesus returned.

#### A Life Pleasing to God (vv1–12)
- **"Finally, then, brothers" (v1).** "Finally" (*loipon*) does not mean the letter is nearly over. It means "as for the rest" or "so then," a turn from the story of the relationship to practical teaching. Paul does the same in Philippians 3:1, two chapters before the end.
- **"We ask and urge you in the Lord Jesus" (v1).** "Ask" is the softer word, a request between friends. "Urge" is stronger. Paul speaks as a brother, but "in the Lord Jesus" reminds them whose authority stands behind the request.
- **"How you ought to walk and to please God, just as you are doing… more and more" (v1).** "Walk" is the Bible's everyday word for a way of life. Paul is not correcting a failing church. He praises them and then asks for growth. In the Christian life there is no point where a believer has arrived, and the phrase "more and more" comes back in v10.
- **"What instructions we gave you through the Lord Jesus" (v2).** The word for "instructions" (*parangelia*) was used of orders passed down a chain of command. Paul had taught them ethics during his few weeks in the city. This is not new teaching, and it is not merely his opinion.
- **"This is the will of God, your sanctification" (v3).** Believers often ask what God's will is for their lives. Here Paul gives one answer plainly. "Sanctification" (*hagiasmos*) means being made holy, set apart for God. It appears three times in vv3–7 (vv3, 4, 7). Holiness is not something added onto salvation for keen believers. It is what God is doing in every one of them.
- **"Abstain from sexual immorality" (v3).** *Porneia* covers every kind of sexual activity outside marriage. In the Greco-Roman world a married man's visits to prostitutes or affairs with slaves were widely tolerated. Many temples were tied to fertility cults, and in a port city like Thessalonica, sex was cheap and public. Jewish and Christian teaching stood almost alone in saying that sex belongs only within marriage. Converts from that world needed this said clearly and early.
- **"Know how to control his own body" (v4).** This is one of the hardest phrases in the letter. The Greek says literally "to acquire (or possess) his own vessel." The ESV footnote gives the other reading: "how to take a wife for himself." Those who read "vessel" as *wife* point to 1 Peter 3:7, which calls the wife "the weaker vessel," and to the verb *ktaomai*, which normally means "to acquire," not "to keep." Those who read it as *body* point to 2 Corinthians 4:7 ("we have this treasure in jars of clay") and note that a teaching about getting married would not help single believers or women. The lexicon allows both and notes that most older scholars took it as "wife." Either way, the point is the same: a believer's sexual life is to be marked by "holiness and honor," not driven by appetite.
- **"Not in the passion of lust like the Gentiles who do not know God" (v5).** "Passion" (*pathos*) pictures a person carried along, acted upon by desire rather than in charge of it. Paul ties sexual chaos to not knowing God, just as he does in Romans 1:24–27. "Gentiles" here means pagans. The Thessalonian believers were mostly Gentiles by birth (1:9), but in Christ they no longer belong to that way of life.
- **"That no one transgress and wrong his brother in this matter" (v6).** "Transgress" means to overstep a boundary, and "wrong" (*pleonekteō*) means to take more than one's share, to exploit. Adultery steals from someone: a husband, a wife, a future spouse, a family. A few interpreters take "this matter" as a new topic, business dealings, but the definite article ties it to the subject Paul is already on. Sexual sin is never private. It always wrongs a brother or sister.
- **"The Lord is an avenger in all these things" (v6).** Paul had warned them about judgment while he was with them ("as we told you beforehand"). The Lord sees what human courts never catch.
- **"God has not called us for impurity, but in holiness" (v7).** The calling that brought them out of idols (1:9) is a calling into a new kind of life.
- **"Whoever disregards this, disregards not man but God, who gives his Holy Spirit to you" (v8).** The verb "gives" is present tense: God keeps giving his Spirit. The Spirit is literally the *Holy* Spirit, and he lives in them. That is both why sexual sin is so serious (1 Cor 6:19) and why purity is possible. Ezekiel 36:27 promised a day when God would put his Spirit within his people and cause them to walk in his statutes.
- **"Concerning brotherly love" (v9).** "Now concerning" (*peri de*) is how Paul often answers questions a church has sent him (1 Cor 7:1; 8:1; 12:1). It may mean Timothy brought back questions from Thessalonica. "Brotherly love" is *philadelphia*, a word Greeks used for love between blood siblings. Christians applied it to the church family.
- **"Taught by God to love one another" (v9).** The word *theodidaktos*, "God-taught," is found nowhere earlier in Greek literature as far as we know. Paul may have coined it. It echoes Isaiah 54:13, "All your children shall be taught by the LORD," which Jesus quotes in John 6:45. God taught them love through Jesus' command (John 13:34), through the cross, and through the Spirit poured into their hearts (Rom 5:5).
- **"To all the brothers throughout Macedonia" (v10).** Their love already reached beyond their own city to believers in Philippi, Berea and elsewhere, probably including practical help. Within a few years the Macedonian churches would give generously to the poor in Jerusalem, "in a severe test of affliction" and "beyond their means" (2 Cor 8:1–5).
- **"Aspire to live quietly" (v11).** The verb (*philotimeomai*) means "to love honor," and so "to be ambitious." Paul uses it for his ambition to preach where Christ was not known (Rom 15:20) and to please the Lord (2 Cor 5:9). Here it sits beside "quietly" in a deliberate paradox: be ambitious to be quiet. Greek culture admired the man who made a name for himself in public life. Paul points the church toward settled, steady faithfulness.
- **"Mind your own affairs, and… work with your hands" (v11).** Most of the converts were working people, and Paul himself had worked "night and day" so as not to burden them (2:9). Some in the church seem to have stopped working, perhaps because they expected Jesus to return any day, perhaps because they were relying on wealthier believers. When Paul writes 2 Thessalonians, the problem has grown, and he says bluntly, "If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" (2 Thess 3:10). Greeks of the upper classes often looked down on manual labor. The gospel gave it dignity.
- **"Walk properly before outsiders and be dependent on no one" (v12).** "Properly" (*euschēmonōs*) means decently, in a way that makes a good impression. The young church was already under suspicion in the city (Acts 17:5–9). Christians who sponged off others or stirred up trouble would confirm every rumor. Believers who worked hard and kept the peace would quietly commend the gospel.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that "The apostle taught them how to walk, not how to talk," and calls the body "the vessel of the soul," which places him with the *body* reading of v4. JFB takes the other side and reads v4 as getting a wife of one's own, citing 1 Peter 3:7 and Jewish usage, and it reads v6 as a "decorous expression" for the matter of a neighbor's marriage rather than business. Guzik prefers *body* like Henry, and he draws four motives for purity from vv6–8: God avenges, God has called, rejecting this is rejecting God, and God gives his Spirit to make obedience possible. On v11, JFB contrasts quiet ambition with the world's urge "to make a great stir," and Henry warns that Christianity never excuses neglecting one's work.

#### The Coming of the Lord (vv13–18)
- **"We do not want you to be uninformed, brothers" (v13).** Paul uses this phrase when he is about to teach something important (Rom 1:13; 11:25; 1 Cor 10:1; 12:1; 2 Cor 1:8). The Thessalonians were not short of faith but of information. Paul had been with them only a few weeks, and some had died since.
- **"Those who are asleep" (v13).** Sleep was a common picture for death among Jews, Greeks and Christians alike (Dan 12:2; John 11:11–13; Acts 7:60). For pagans it usually meant a sleep with no waking. For Christians it took on a new meaning: sleep is followed by morning. Early Christians called their burial grounds *koimētēria*, "sleeping places," which gives us the word "cemetery." The picture describes how death looks from outside. It does not teach that the soul is unconscious until the resurrection. Paul elsewhere says that to depart is to "be with Christ" (Phil 1:23) and to be "away from the body and at home with the Lord" (2 Cor 5:8).
- **"That you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (v13).** Paul does not forbid grief. Jesus wept at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35). He forbids *hopeless* grief. The ancient world had little to offer at a graveside. A well-known papyrus letter of condolence from second-century Egypt says, in effect, that against such things one can do nothing, so comfort one another. Paul gives the same final instruction (v18), but with something to say first.
- **"Since we believe that Jesus died and rose again" (v14).** Christian hope rests on an event, not a feeling. Notice that Paul says Jesus *died*, not that he fell asleep. He took death at its full weight, so that for those who belong to him it can be called sleep.
- **"Through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep" (v14).** The believers who have died are not lost or left behind. When Jesus comes, they come with him. The Thessalonians' worry may have been that the dead would miss the great day of his coming, or even that dying before he came meant something had gone wrong. Paul answers both.
- **"By a word from the Lord" (v15).** This may be a saying of Jesus not recorded in the Gospels (Paul quotes one in Acts 20:35), or a summary of his teaching such as Matthew 24:30–31, or a word given to Paul by revelation. The point is that Paul is not speculating. He is passing on what the Lord has said.
- **"We who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord" (v15).** Paul puts himself among those who might be alive at Jesus' return. He did not know the date (5:1–2), and he later expects that he may die first (2 Tim 4:6). Some critics say Paul was simply wrong. More likely he speaks as every generation of believers should, living ready for the Lord to come in their lifetime. "Coming" is *parousia*, a word used for the official arrival of a king or emperor at a city, with all the ceremony that went with it.
- **"Will not precede those who have fallen asleep" (v15).** The living will have no head start. The older English "prevent" in the KJV meant "go before."
- **"The Lord himself will descend from heaven" (v16).** "Himself" matters. He does not send an angel. The one who ascended in a cloud will come back the same way (Acts 1:11).
- **"A cry of command… the voice of an archangel… the trumpet of God" (v16).** The "cry of command" (*keleusma*) is a word used only here in the New Testament. It was used of a shouted order, such as an officer giving the word to his troops or a captain to his rowers. Only one archangel is named in Scripture, Michael (Jude 9), who in Daniel 12:1–2 stands up for God's people at the time of the resurrection. Trumpets in Israel gathered the people and announced God's coming (Exod 19:16; Num 10:1–10; Isa 27:13). Jesus spoke of "a loud trumpet call" gathering his elect (Matt 24:31), and Paul speaks of "the last trumpet" at which the dead are raised (1 Cor 15:52). Whether all three sounds are separate or one great summons described three ways, this coming will not be quiet or secret to the church.
- **"The dead in Christ will rise first" (v16).** "First" is only "before the living are caught up," not a separate first resurrection for some believers. The dead are not at a disadvantage. They go first.
- **"Caught up together with them in the clouds" (v17).** "Caught up" is *harpazō*, to seize or snatch away suddenly and with force, the word used when the Spirit "carried away" Philip (Acts 8:39). The Latin translation rendered it *rapiemur*, and from that Latin root comes the English word "rapture." Clouds in Scripture often mark God's presence (Exod 19:9; Dan 7:13; Acts 1:9). "Together with them" is the heart of the comfort: believers who died and believers still alive are reunited at the same moment.
- **"To meet the Lord in the air" (v17).** "Meet" is *apantēsis*, a word used for a welcoming party. When an emperor or general visited a city, leading citizens went out to meet him on the road and escorted him back in. Luke uses the same word when believers walked out from Rome to meet Paul (Acts 28:15), and Jesus uses it in the parable of the ten virgins going out to meet the bridegroom (Matt 25:6).
- **How the church has read v17.** Here faithful Christians differ. Many dispensational and pre-tribulational readers see this as the rapture, a coming of Christ *for* his church before a time of great tribulation, after which he returns *with* his church to reign. Others read it as part of one single return of Christ at the end of history: on the *apantēsis* pattern, the church goes up to meet its King and escorts him back to earth. This is the historic view of most Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran and Reformed Christians, and of many evangelicals. Mid-tribulation and pre-wrath views sit between the two. Every view agrees on what the passage plainly teaches: Jesus himself will come, the dead in Christ will rise, the living will be changed and gathered, and no believer will be left out.
- **"And so we will always be with the Lord" (v17).** Paul gives no timetable and no map. The goal is not a place but a person. After the shouts and trumpet, the passage ends on being with Jesus, forever.
- **"Therefore encourage one another with these words" (v18).** This teaching was given for funerals and grieving families, not for charts and arguments. The same verb (*parakaleō*) closes the next section too (5:11).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "All grief for the death of friends is far from being unlawful," but it must not be the grief of those without hope, and he calls death for the believer "but a sleep." JFB notes the contrast in v14: Christ's death is called death, but believers' death is sleep, because "His death has taken for them the sting from death." It also takes Paul's "we" in v15 as "an affectionate identifying of ourselves with our fellows of all ages," not a mistaken prediction, and reads "and so shall we ever be with the Lord" as "no more parting." Guzik defends a pre-tribulation rapture and argues that only a rescue before judgment would truly comfort. He adds that differences on the timing should never divide Christian fellowship. JFB, by contrast, describes the meeting place "in the air" as not "the place of their lasting abode," which suits the escort reading. All three agree that the comfort is reunion with each other and with the Lord.

#### Pointing to Christ
Holiness, love and hope all run back to Jesus here. Believers are called to holiness because the Lord Jesus stands behind the command and his Spirit lives in them. They love one another because God has taught them in Christ. And they can stand at a graveside with real hope because "Jesus died and rose again." The passage does not end with an event but with a person: "so we will always be with the Lord."
