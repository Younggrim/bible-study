*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus, in the middle of the "Holiness Code" (chs 17–26). Chapter 18 forbade a list of sexual and religious practices. Chapter 19 gave the positive picture of a holy life. Chapter 20 now comes back to many of the same sins and attaches penalties. Chapter 18 said "you shall not"; chapter 20 says what happens if someone does. The order matters: the chapter opens and closes with false religion (Molech, mediums), and the family sins sit between them, as if to say that when worship goes wrong, the home soon follows.

#### Punishment for Child Sacrifice (vv1–9)
- **"Gives any of his children to Molech" (v2).** Molech was a god linked to the Ammonites (1 Kings 11:7), and his worship involved making children "pass through the fire" (18:21), most scholars think as burnt sacrifices. Chapter 18 forbade it, and here it becomes a capital crime, for the native Israelite and for **"the strangers who sojourn in Israel"** alike.
- **"The people of the land shall stone him" (v2).** Stoning was the usual form of execution in Israel. It was done by the community, outside the camp, with the witnesses throwing the first stones (Deut 17:6–7). The whole people took part, because the whole people had been defiled.
- **"I myself will set my face against that man" (v3).** Alongside the human penalty stands God's own judgment. **"Cut him off"** (as in 7:20; 17:10) means God himself removes the person from his people, whether by death, by ending his family line, or by exclusion from the covenant. The reason: it made **"my sanctuary unclean"** and **"profane[d] my holy name."** A child killed for Molech defiled God's dwelling in Israel's midst, even if it happened far from the tabernacle.
- **"If the people of the land do at all close their eyes" (vv4–5).** Looking the other way is itself a sin. If the community tolerates child sacrifice, God will act against the offender "and against his clan." **"Whoring after Molech"** is the Bible's frequent picture of idolatry as unfaithfulness to a husband (Exod 34:15–16; Hos 1–3). Israel did later close its eyes: kings Ahaz and Manasseh sacrificed their own sons in the Valley of Hinnom (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6), and Jeremiah names it as a cause of the exile (Jer 32:35).
- **"Mediums and necromancers, whoring after them" (v6).** Turning to the occult is the same spiritual adultery. Here God himself will cut off the one who consults them, and verse 27 adds the penalty for the practitioner.
- **"Consecrate yourselves… I am the LORD who sanctifies you" (vv7–8).** Both sides of holiness in two verses. Israel must set itself apart, and yet it is God who makes them holy. The same pairing runs through the New Testament: "work out your own salvation… for it is God who works in you" (Phil 2:12–13).
- **"Anyone who curses his father or his mother" (v9).** The Hebrew verb (*qalal*) means to treat as light or contemptible, to curse. This is not a child's tantrum but settled contempt, the opposite of the "revere" of 19:3. Exodus 21:17 gives the same penalty, and Jesus quotes it against Pharisees who used religious loopholes to avoid supporting their parents (Mark 7:10). **"His blood is upon him"** is a legal phrase meaning he is responsible for his own death, and those who carry out the sentence bear no guilt.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Molech's rites "the burning of children in the fire to the honour of a dunghill-god," and on v4 warns, "If magistrates will not do justice upon offenders, God will do justice upon them." On v6: "What greater madness can there be than for a man to go to a liar for information, and to an enemy for advice?" Guzik notes that Deuteronomy 21:18–21 put a rebellious son's case before the elders, not in the parents' hands, and suggests the penalty was rarely carried out. He reads vv7–8 as God's sanctifying work done through the willing cooperation of his people. JFB describes the later Jewish practice of stoning in detail and sees in v4 the horror of parents who would "violate their national covenant."

#### Punishments for Sexual Immorality (vv10–21)
- **Why repeat chapter 18?** Most of these acts were forbidden there (18:6–23). This list ranks them by penalty. Verses 10–16 carry death. Verses 17–19 carry being "cut off." Verses 20–21 end with childlessness. The penalties show how seriously God takes each sin, and they protect the family, which in Israel was the basic unit of the covenant community.
- **Adultery (v10).** **"Both the adulterer and the adulteress shall surely be put to death."** The law punished both, not only the woman. That is why the scene in John 8 rings false: the leaders brought only the woman, though she was "caught in the act" (John 8:4). Capital cases required at least two witnesses (Deut 17:6), so the penalty could only be carried out on clear evidence.
- **A father's wife, a daughter-in-law (vv11–12).** Sleeping with a stepmother is both incest and adultery, since it **"uncovered his father's nakedness."** Relations with a daughter-in-law are called **"perversion"** (*tevel*, confusion), the mixing of roles God keeps apart. Judah's story with Tamar (Gen 38) shows how ugly that confusion could be.
- **"If a man lies with a male as with a woman" (v13).** This repeats 18:22 and adds the penalty: both men are put to death, and the act is called **"an abomination"** (*to'evah*). It sits in the same list as adultery and incest, not above them. The New Testament repeats the moral standard (Rom 1:26–27; 1 Cor 6:9–10), but it does so beside greed, drunkenness and slander, and then says, "such were some of you. But you were washed" (1 Cor 6:11).
- **"A woman and her mother also… burned with fire" (v14).** This is the harshest penalty in the chapter, also used for a priest's daughter who became a prostitute (21:9). Some interpreters think the bodies were burned after stoning, as at Achan's judgment (Josh 7:25). JFB takes that view. The text doesn't say which.
- **Bestiality (vv15–16).** Both man and woman are addressed, and the animal is killed too. The animal is not guilty, but it was the instrument of the sin and would be a standing reminder of it.
- **A sister, a woman in her period, an aunt (vv17–19).** These bring being **"cut off,"** and verse 17 adds that it happens **"in the sight of the children of their people,"** a public disgrace. The word for "disgrace" here (*chesed*) is usually the great word for God's steadfast love, but in this verse it has a rare second meaning, "shame." Verse 18 repeats 18:19 in vivid terms. Verse 19 says only **"they shall bear their iniquity,"** leaving the punishment to God.
- **"They shall die childless" (vv20–21).** For an uncle's wife or a brother's wife, the judgment is childlessness, which in Israel meant the family name would die out (Gen 30:1). This may mean God would withhold children, or that children of such a union would not count as the man's heirs. Since a man was commanded to marry his brother's widow if his brother died childless (Deut 25:5–6), verse 21 likely concerns a brother's wife while the brother was alive or after a divorce. John the Baptist told Herod Antipas, who had married his brother Philip's wife, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife" (Mark 6:18).
- **Were these penalties carried out?** The Old Testament records few such executions, and the strict rules of evidence made them hard to impose. Jewish tradition later added further safeguards. The penalties showed how seriously God viewed these sins, set Israel's courts a standard, and protected the community from sins that spread. Christians generally read these civil penalties as belonging to Israel as a nation under God's direct rule. The moral standards remain, but the church's discipline is exclusion and restoration, not execution (1 Cor 5:1–13; 2 Cor 2:6–8).
- **Commentators:** Henry gives the purpose of the whole chapter: "those who would not be deterred from sin by the fear of God might be deterred from it by the fear of punishment." Guzik makes the same evidence point about witnesses and links v10 to John 8. He says homosexual acts are treated here as one sexual sin among others, not singled out as worse than adultery or incest, and he adds that only God can impose the penalty of childlessness. JFB holds that wherever death is ordered without a method, "stoning is meant." On v20 it offers two readings: "Either by the judgment of God they shall have no children, or their spurious offspring shall be denied" the rights of Israelite children.

#### You Shall Be Holy (vv22–27)
- **"That the land… may not vomit you out" (v22).** The picture from 18:25–28 returns. The land itself rejects people who defile it. Israel would not be exempt because of its privileges. In time, both kingdoms were carried into exile for these very sins (2 Kings 17:7–18; Ezek 22:9–11).
- **"Therefore I detested them" (v23).** The Canaanites were not driven out by chance or because Israel was stronger. God judged them for "all these things," after centuries of patience (Gen 15:16).
- **"A land flowing with milk and honey" (v24).** "Milk" points to rich pasture for flocks, and "honey" probably to date syrup as well as bees' honey. Egypt's farming depended on the Nile and irrigation, but Canaan drank rain from heaven, a land watched by God's own eyes (Deut 11:10–12). The promise sits right beside the warning: God is giving them a good land, and he wants them to keep it.
- **"Who has separated you from the peoples… you shall therefore separate the clean beast from the unclean" (vv24–25).** The Hebrew verb *badal* (to separate, divide) is the same one used in creation, where God separated light from darkness and the waters above from the waters below (Gen 1:4–7). God separated Israel from the nations, and Israel's food laws were to mirror that separation at every meal (ch 11). This is why, when God showed Peter a sheet of unclean animals and said "What God has made clean, do not call common," the point was not lunch. It was that Gentiles were now welcome (Acts 10:9–28).
- **"You shall be holy to me… that you should be mine" (v26).** The goal of all these rules is relationship. God did not set Israel apart to make them proud, but to make them his own. Peter applies this to the church: "a people for his own possession" (1 Pet 2:9).
- **"A medium or a necromancer shall surely be put to death" (v27).** The chapter ends where it began, with false religion. Verse 6 punished the one who consulted mediums. This verse punishes the mediums themselves, who led others astray. Saul had driven the mediums out of the land (1 Sam 28:3), and then went looking for one when God was silent.
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures Israel as an enclosed garden among the nations: "Other nations were the common; they were the enclosure, beautified and enriched with peculiar privileges." On v25, holiness means learning "to discern between things that differ, not to live at large, as if we might say and do any thing." JFB says Israel was set apart "for the all-important end of preserving the knowledge and worship of the true God amid the universal apostasy," and the food distinction was one means to that end. Guzik ties v27's heavier penalty for the medium to the special guilt of leading others into sin (Matt 18:6), and notes that God wants not only obedience but the people themselves.

#### Pointing to Christ
This is one of the hardest chapters in the Bible to read. Again and again: "their blood is upon them." It shows plainly what sin deserves, and it leaves no one feeling safe. The good news is that the blood did not stay on the guilty. Jesus took it on himself. When a woman caught in adultery was brought to him under this very law, he did not deny the law, but he stooped, sent her accusers away, and said, "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (John 8:11). He could say it because he was going to the cross to bear the penalty himself. "I am the LORD who sanctifies you" (v8) finds its answer in him: "you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 6:11). He separated a people to be his own, not by food laws now, but by his blood.
