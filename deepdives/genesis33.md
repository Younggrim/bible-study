*Setting:* The sun has just risen over the Jabbok. Jacob limps away from Peniel with a new name, Israel, and a blessing he did not have to steal (32:28–31). Ahead of him are the gifts he sent in waves, 550 animals and more (32:13–21). Behind them, coming up from the south with four hundred men, is Esau. The two brothers have not seen each other in twenty years. When they parted, Esau was planning to kill Jacob as soon as their father died (27:41). Jacob prayed, "Please deliver me from the hand of my brother" (32:11), and then wrestled all night with God. Dates are approximate, somewhere in the early second millennium BC. Chapter 33 is the answer to that prayer. It is a story of a reunion warmer than anyone expected, followed by a quieter parting and Jacob's first steps back in the land of Canaan, ending at an altar outside the city of Shechem.

#### Jacob Meets Esau (vv1–20)
- **"Esau was coming, and four hundred men with him" (v1).** The phrase repeats the messengers' report word for word (32:6). Nothing about Esau's band has changed, but something in Jacob has. He "lifted up his eyes and looked," a phrase Genesis uses at turning points (compare 22:4, 13; 24:63). Henry reads it as the calm of a man who has prayed.
- **The order of the family (vv1–2).** Jacob arranges his household in rows: the servant wives Bilhah and Zilpah and their sons in front, Leah and her children behind them, "and Rachel and Joseph last of all." If Esau attacks, the ones Jacob loves most will have the best chance to escape. The Tyndale notes observe that Jacob's new name had not yet cured his old favoritism. Every member of the family could see the order, and Leah's sons would remember it. It is a quiet setup for the jealousy that will tear the family apart in chapter 37.
- **"He himself went on before them" (v3).** This is the change. The night before, Jacob sent his gifts and his family ahead while he stayed behind (32:16, 21). Now he walks out in front, alone, toward the brother he wronged. Whatever fear remains, the man from Peniel no longer hides behind others.
- **"Bowing himself to the ground seven times" (v3).** Seven bows was a gesture of complete submission to a superior. Letters from Canaanite rulers to Pharaoh found at Amarna in Egypt (fourteenth century BC) open with almost the same formula: "seven times and seven times I fall at the feet of my lord." Isaac's stolen blessing said, "May your mother's sons bow down to you" (27:29). Now the one who received that blessing bows seven times to his brother. Jacob still believes in the promise, but he no longer grasps at it.
- **"Esau ran to meet him and embraced him and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept" (v4).** Every verb is warm, and the pile-up of them is the point. The kiss that hangs over this family is the one Jacob gave his blind father as part of the deception (27:26–27). This kiss is real. Jewish scribes marked the Hebrew word for "kissed" in this verse with special dots, and later rabbis argued over whether that meant the kiss was unusually sincere or not sincere at all. The plain text gives no hint of a trap. Jesus seems to echo this scene in the parable of the prodigal son, where the father "ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). In both stories, the one who had every right to be angry runs to the one who did wrong.
- **What changed Esau?** Genesis does not say. Twenty years may have cooled his anger, and he has done well for himself in Seir. But the reader knows that Jacob prayed for deliverance and that God has protected him from Laban by speaking in a dream (31:24). The Tyndale notes see Esau's welcome as God's answer to Jacob's prayer, an example of the meaning of the name Israel: God fights for him.
- **"The children whom God has graciously given your servant" (v5).** The verb is *chanan*, "be gracious" or "show favor," the root of the familiar "grace" words in the Old Testament. The lexicon lists this verse and v11 under God's giving of favors. Jacob has learned to describe his life as gift. The families come forward in order and bow (vv6–7). The Tyndale notes point out that only Joseph is named among the children, a hint of the place he will hold in the rest of Genesis.
- **"What do you mean by all this company?" (v8).** The ESV footnote says the word for "company" is literally "camp," the same word as in "two camps" (32:7, 10) and Mahanaim (32:2). Esau has met the droves of animals and wants to know what they mean. Jacob answers honestly: "to find favor in the sight of my lord."
- **"I have enough, my brother" (v9).** Esau calls Jacob "my brother." Jacob keeps calling Esau "my lord" (vv8, 13, 14, 15). The Tyndale notes observe that Jacob is still reversing the old oracle, "the older shall serve the younger" (25:23), out of caution. Esau's refusal is generous: "keep what you have for yourself."
- **"I have seen your face, which is like seeing the face of God" (v10).** Jacob has just named the place Peniel, "the face of God," because he saw God face to face and his life was spared (32:30). Now he sees Esau's face and finds not death but welcome. The word "face" (*panim*) ran all through his anxious plans in 32:20, where he hoped to "appease his face" and that Esau would "lift my face." Now Esau has "accepted" him. The same God who met Jacob at the Jabbok has gone ahead of him to Esau. Jacob's words are not flattery; he is telling Esau what the reader already knows.
- **"Please accept my blessing" (v11).** The ESV keeps the Hebrew word: *berakah*, "blessing," not just "present." Twenty years earlier Jacob took Esau's blessing (27:35–36). Now he presses a blessing back into Esau's hands. Most interpreters see this as a gesture of restitution, even if Jacob does not say so. There is also a small difference in the brothers' words. Esau says "I have enough," literally "I have much" (*rab*, v9). Jacob says "I have enough," literally "I have everything" (*kol*, v11). Esau has plenty. Jacob, because "God has dealt graciously with me," has all he needs. In that culture, accepting a gift was a sign of peace, and refusing one could signal lingering hostility. So Jacob "urged him, and he took it."
- **"Let us journey on our way" (v12).** Esau offers to travel together, or to go ahead as an escort (the ESV footnote gives "along with" as an alternative). Jacob politely declines. His reasons are real: the children are "frail," and the nursing flocks "will die" if driven hard (v13). Anyone who has moved young animals knows it. Jacob then says he will follow slowly "until I come to my lord in Seir" (v14).
- **Did Jacob mean it?** Seir is far to the south, near the Dead Sea's southern end and beyond. Jacob instead turns northwest to Succoth (v17) and then crosses into Canaan to Shechem. Genesis never records a visit to Seir. The Tyndale notes call Jacob's promise a lie that shows his old character. Others think he intended a visit later, or that he simply wanted to avoid an awkward refusal. The brothers did meet again, years later, to bury their father (35:29). Either way, Jacob still keeps his distance. Reconciliation did not mean living side by side.
- **"Let me leave with you some of the people who are with me" (v15).** Esau offers an armed escort. Jacob declines again: "What need is there?" He has God's camp (32:1–2). Esau returns "that day" to Seir (v16).
- **Succoth (v17).** Jacob "built himself a house and made booths for his livestock." The ESV footnote says *Succoth* means "booths," and the lexicon describes a *sukkah* as a rough shelter of woven branches, "for cattle" in this verse. The same word names the later Feast of Booths (Lev 23:42–43). Succoth lay east of the Jordan, in the Jordan Valley near the Jabbok, later part of the territory of Gad (Josh 13:27; Judg 8:5). Many identify it with Tell Deir Alla. Building a "house," not just a tent, suggests Jacob stayed there some time, perhaps several years. That would help explain how Dinah is old enough for the events of chapter 34.
- **"Jacob came safely to the city of Shechem" (v18).** The ESV footnote gives "peacefully" as an alternative. The word is *shalem*, which the lexicon defines here as "safe, unharmed," and it notes this is not a place name, as some old translations took it (the KJV reads "came to Shalem, a city of Shechem"). It echoes Jacob's vow at Bethel: "so that I come again to my father's house in peace" (28:21). God has done what Jacob asked. He is back "in the land of Canaan."
- **Shechem.** Shechem lay in the hill country between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, near modern Nablus. It was Abraham's first stop in Canaan, where God first promised him the land and where he built his first altar (12:6–7). Jacob now camps where his grandfather stood.
- **"For a hundred pieces of money" (v19).** Jacob buys the plot where his tent stands from "the sons of Hamor, Shechem's father." The ESV footnote says the Hebrew is "a hundred *qesitah*," a unit of unknown value, and the lexicon agrees, adding that it may have been a weight. Like Abraham, who bought the cave of Machpelah (ch 23), Jacob owns a small piece of the promised land by purchase. That small plot became important. Joseph's bones were buried there when Israel entered the land (Josh 24:32), and centuries later Jesus sat by "Jacob's well" near "the field that Jacob had given to his son Joseph" (John 4:5–6).
- **"He erected an altar and called it El-Elohe-Israel" (v20).** The ESV footnote explains the name: "God, the God of Israel." This is the first time Jacob uses his new name, and he uses it to say God is his own God. At Bethel he had said, "then the LORD shall be my God" (28:21). Until now he spoke of "the God of my father" (31:5, 42; 32:9). Here he names the altar for "the God of Israel," that is, the God of Jacob himself. Later the same phrase becomes the name of the God of the whole nation.
- **An unfinished vow.** Jacob is home, but not yet where he promised to go. At Bethel he vowed that the pillar would be God's house (28:22), and in 31:13 God called himself "the God of Bethel." Jacob settles near a Canaanite city instead. The next chapter shows what that cost him, and only in chapter 35 does God tell him, "Arise, go up to Bethel" (35:1).
- **Commentators:** On the meeting, Henry writes, "Jacob, at the head of his household, set a better example than Esau at the head of his regiment," and "A humble submissive carriage goes a great way towards the turning away of wrath." JFB says "the cherished enmity of twenty years in a moment disappeared." Guzik notes the favoritism in the order of the family and the fact that Jacob now leads from the front. On the gift, JFB and Guzik make the same cultural point: "In the East the acceptance by a superior is a proof of friendship" (JFB). Henry catches the difference in wording: "Esau's enough was much, but Jacob's enough was all," and adds, "It is a good thing for those that have much to know that they have enough." On Seir, the commentators differ. JFB suggests Jacob may have had in mind a route that would bring him to Isaac at Beersheba. Guzik stops short of calling it a lie, but says Jacob still acts like his old self: it is hard to be Jacob and Israel at the same time. The Tyndale notes are the bluntest, calling it a lie. Henry is gentle on the pace: "It is an unreasonable thing to tie others to our rate." On Succoth, JFB calls it "the first station at which Jacob halted on his arrival in Canaan," but Succoth lay east of the Jordan, outside Canaan proper (Josh 13:27), so Shechem was really his first stop in the land. On the purchase, JFB suggests the *qesitah* was "probably a coin with the figure of a lamb on it." That reading goes back to the old Greek translation, which rendered the word "lambs," but the lexicon calls it a unit of unknown value, and coins did not exist in Jacob's day. Henry says Jacob "is content to pay for his own, to prevent disputes," and both he and JFB draw the same lesson from the altar: "Where we have a tent God must have an altar" (Henry), "Wherever we pitch a tent, God shall have an altar" (JFB). Guzik is more critical: the altar was good, but God had called Jacob to Bethel, and obedience matters more than sacrifice. JFB has no notes on vv2, 6–10, 13 or 15–16.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jacob came to Esau with gifts, bows and careful speeches, hoping to "appease his face." Esau ran to him before any of that could matter. The picture is not perfect, since Esau is no symbol of God, but it is close enough that Jesus seems to borrow it in the parable of the prodigal son, where the father sees his son "while he was still a long way off" and runs to embrace him (Luke 15:20). The deeper reconciliation in Scripture is between sinners and God, and there too the offended party moves first: "in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them" (2 Cor 5:19). Jacob could say of his brother's welcome that it was "like seeing the face of God." In Jesus, the face of God turns toward his people in grace, for God has shone "in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6).
