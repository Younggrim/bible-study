*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapter 53 ended with the Servant who "poured out his soul to death," bore "the sin of many," and was promised that he would "see his offspring." Chapter 54 shows those offspring. It turns from the Servant to the city he died for, pictured as a woman: childless, abandoned, widowed and storm-tossed. God calls her to sing, because her children will be more than she can house, her husband is God himself, and her city will be rebuilt in jewels. Notice the order. The great invitation of chapters 54–55 comes *after* the cross of chapter 53, not before it. The whole chapter is one ESV section.

#### The Eternal Covenant of Peace (vv1–17)
- **"Sing, O barren one, who did not bear" (v1).** In Israel, a wife without children carried deep shame and grief. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah and Elizabeth all knew it (Gen 11:30; 1 Sam 1:6–7; Luke 1:25). Zion in exile is pictured as that woman: emptied of her people, with no future. She is told to sing *before* anything has changed. Hannah's song after her barrenness ended is the model (1 Sam 2:1–10).
- **"The children of the desolate one will be more than the children of her who is married" (v1).** "Desolate" is the word for a ruined, abandoned land (Isa 49:8, 19). The deserted woman will end up with more children than the woman whose husband never left. This follows straight on from 53:10, where the dying Servant "shall see his offspring." His death produces a family.
- **Paul and the barren woman.** In Galatians 4:27 Paul quotes this verse in his allegory of Sarah and Hagar. Sarah, the barren one, stands for "the Jerusalem above," the free mother of all who believe, Jews and Gentiles alike. Her children by promise far outnumber the children of slavery. Paul reads Isaiah 54 as fulfilled in the church gathered by the gospel.
- **"Enlarge the place of your tent" (v2).** Israel's ancestors lived in tents, and a growing family meant adding panels and moving the pegs out. **"Lengthen your cords and strengthen your stakes."** A bigger tent needs longer ropes and deeper pegs, or the first wind will flatten it. The exiles were a small, discouraged remnant. God tells them to plan for growth. William Carey, often called the father of modern missions, preached on vv2–3 in 1792 with the famous summary "Expect great things from God; attempt great things for God," and the Baptist Missionary Society was founded months later.
- **"You will spread abroad to the right and to the left" (v3).** This echoes God's promise to Jacob: "you shall spread abroad to the west and to the east and to the north and to the south" (Gen 28:14), using the same verb. **"Your offspring will possess the nations and will people the desolate cities."** The Tyndale notes connect this to Abraham's offspring possessing the gate of their enemies (Gen 22:17) and to the meek inheriting the earth (Matt 5:5).
- **"Fear not, for you will not be ashamed" (v4).** Three verbs pile up: ashamed, confounded, disgraced. Guzik, citing an Isaiah scholar, notes they are near synonyms for hopes that collapse and leave a person exposed. **"The shame of your youth"** probably means the years of slavery in Egypt, or Israel's early unfaithfulness. **"The reproach of your widowhood"** is the exile, when she seemed to have lost her husband. Both will be forgotten.
- **"For your Maker is your husband" (v5).** The two nouns are plural in Hebrew ("your makers… your husbands"), a plural of majesty that the singular verbs show refers to one God. JFB calls it "plural for singular, to denote excellency." The word for husband (*baʿal*) means owner or lord as well, and it is also the name of the Canaanite storm god Israel kept chasing. Hosea plays on that: one day Israel will call God "My Husband" and no longer "My Baal" (Hos 2:16). The God who made her is the one who married her.
- **Four titles (v5).** Maker, "the LORD of hosts" (commander of heaven's armies), "the Holy One of Israel," Isaiah's favorite name for God, and "your Redeemer," the kinsman who buys back a relative (52:3). Then **"the God of the whole earth he is called."** Israel's husband is not a small local god who lost a war to Babylon's gods. He owns the whole earth.
- **"Like a wife deserted and grieved in spirit, like a wife of youth when she is cast off" (v6).** "A wife of youth" is a man's first love, married young (Prov 5:18; Mal 2:14–15). The picture is of a man who sent away the wife he married young and then calls her back. Jeremiah remembers "the devotion of your youth, your love as a bride" (Jer 2:2).
- **"For a brief moment I deserted you, but with great compassion I will gather you" (v7).** God does not pretend the exile was not real. He says he left her. But the scales are uneven: a brief moment against great compassion. "Compassion" (*rachamim*) comes from the word for womb, a mother's tender love (49:15).
- **"In overflowing anger for a moment I hid my face from you" (v8).** The Hebrew is a rhyming pair, *shetseph qetseph*, "a flood of fury," and *shetseph* appears only here. JFB says the wrath, though brief, "was overflowing while it lasted." God hiding his face is the opposite of the priestly blessing, "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25; Deut 31:17). **"But with everlasting love I will have compassion on you."** "Everlasting love" is *chesed ʿolam*, covenant loyalty without end. A moment of anger against eternal love. Paul says something similar: "this light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory" (2 Cor 4:17).
- **"This is like the days of Noah to me" (v9).** After the flood God swore that the waters would never again cover the earth, and set the rainbow as a sign (Gen 9:8–17). The Hebrew manuscripts differ by a single space here: "the *waters* of Noah" or "the *days* of Noah." The meaning is the same either way. The exile was a flood of judgment. Now God takes an oath about it, as he did after the flood: **"I have sworn that I will not be angry with you, and will not rebuke you."** Every time it rained, Noah's family could look at the rainbow and remember.
- **"The mountains may depart and the hills be removed, but my steadfast love shall not depart from you" (v10).** Mountains are the most permanent thing anyone can see (Ps 90:2). God says his love outlasts them. The verbs match: the mountains may "depart," his love will not "depart." "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) is again the word for loyal covenant love.
- **"My covenant of peace shall not be removed" (v10).** A "covenant of peace" (*berit shalom*) is a binding promise of wholeness, security and right relationship. God gave one to Phinehas the priest (Num 25:12), and Ezekiel promises one for the restored people under the coming David (Ezek 34:25; 37:26). It rests on the "chastisement that brought us peace" (53:5). The peace that the Servant bought is now sealed by covenant. Jesus spoke of "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20).
- **"O afflicted one, storm-tossed and not comforted" (v11).** "Storm-tossed" is the word for being swept along by a gale. The city that was "not comforted" is the city God promised to comfort (40:1; 51:3). Compare "No one comforted her" in Lamentations (Lam 1:2, 9, 17).
- **"I will set your stones in antimony" (v11).** Antimony (*puk*) was the black powder women used as eye-paint. Jezebel "painted her eyes" with it (2 Kings 9:30). Here it is the mortar between the stones, setting them off the way eye-paint sets off the eyes. The bride's city is made up beautifully. **"Lay your foundations with sapphires."** The ancient "sapphire" was probably lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone, as the Tyndale notes suggest.
- **"Your pinnacles of agate, your gates of carbuncles" (v12).** "Pinnacles" is literally "suns," probably battlements or towers that flash in the light. "Carbuncles" are fiery red stones. The exact identity of these gems is uncertain, and translations vary. The point is plain: the ruined city will be rebuilt more beautiful than ever. John's vision of the new Jerusalem builds on this, with foundations "adorned with every kind of jewel" and gates of pearl (Rev 21:18–21). Abraham "was looking forward to the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:10).
- **Literal or picture?** Some read the jewels as literal wealth for a restored Jerusalem. Most Christian interpreters read them, as Revelation does, as a picture of the beauty and permanence of God's redeemed people and their eternal city. Henry says the church "should at length be advanced to greater honour and splendour than ever." Either way the text stresses that God himself is the builder: "I will set… I will make."
- **"All your children shall be taught by the LORD" (v13).** This is better than jewels. The new city's people will know God for themselves, the promise of the new covenant: "they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest" (Jer 31:34). Jesus quotes this verse: "It is written in the Prophets, 'And they will all be taught by God.' Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to me" (John 6:45). **"Great shall be the peace of your children."** *Shalom* again.
- **"In righteousness you shall be established" (v14).** A city founded on righteousness, not on bribes and oppression, which were Jerusalem's sins in chapter 1. Isaiah promised it would one day be called "the city of righteousness, the faithful city" (1:26). Far from oppression and from terror, because the people have no reason to fear.
- **"If anyone stirs up strife, it is not from me" (v15).** In the past God himself sent Assyria and Babylon as his rod (10:5; 47:6). Not anymore. Any attack from now on comes without his sending, and "shall fall because of you." JFB takes this last phrase differently, "shall come over to thy side," which the Hebrew allows: former enemies will join her.
- **"I have created the smith who blows the fire of coals" (v16).** The blacksmith working his bellows to forge a sword, and the "ravager" who uses it, are both God's creatures. JFB: God rules "both him who frames arms and him who destroys them." No weapon is outside his control, which is why the next line can be so bold.
- **"No weapon that is fashioned against you shall succeed" (v17).** This is one of the most quoted promises in the book. It does not promise that believers will never suffer. Isaiah has just described the Servant crushed and pierced. It promises that no attack will finally defeat God's people. Paul says the same: "If God is for us, who can be against us?… we are more than conquerors" (Rom 8:31–37).
- **"You shall refute every tongue that rises against you in judgment" (v17).** The attack moves from the battlefield to the courtroom. Accusers will lose their case. Paul again: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies" (Rom 8:33). Satan is called "the accuser of our brothers" (Rev 12:10).
- **"This is the heritage of the servants of the LORD and their vindication from me" (v17).** The singular "Servant" of chapters 42–53 now becomes plural "servants." From here to the end of the book Isaiah speaks of "servants" (56:6; 63:17; 65:8–15). Those who belong to the one Servant become servants with him. "Vindication" is literally "righteousness" (*tsedaqah*): their being in the right comes "from me." That is the fruit of 53:11, where the Servant makes "many to be accounted righteous."
- **Commentators:** Henry and JFB both read the barren woman's many children as Gentiles brought into the church, with Galatians 4:27 as the key, though JFB adds that "the full accomplishment of it is yet future" for Israel. Guzik also takes up Galatians 4 and suggests the children of the new covenant will outnumber those of the old. JFB draws a practical lesson from v2: lengthening the cords means reaching new people, and strengthening the stakes means building up converts already gathered. On vv7–8 Guzik stresses the contrast between a moment of forsaking and everlasting mercy, and Henry says God "would return in mercy and would not return to contend with them any more." On v17 Guzik quotes Spurgeon on slander and limits the promise to "the servants of the LORD," whose righteousness is from God, not from themselves. Henry: "all attempts against the church should be baffled."

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah 54 is what the cross of chapter 53 produces. The Servant was cut off without children, yet the barren woman ends up with more than she can house. The Servant bore the chastisement that brought peace, and God now swears a covenant of peace that outlasts the mountains. The Servant made many righteous, and their righteousness is "from me." Jesus quoted v13 to say that those taught by the Father come to him, and Paul read v1 as the birth of the church from every nation. The forsaken wife is gathered back by her Maker-husband, and the New Testament names him: Christ, who "loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Eph 5:25).
