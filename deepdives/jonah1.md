*Setting:* Jonah son of Amittai was a real prophet of the northern kingdom of Israel. 2 Kings 14:25 says he foretold that King Jeroboam II (about 793–753 BC) would win back Israel's old borders, and he did. So Jonah was a prophet with good news for his own nation, at a time of prosperity in Israel. He came from Gath-hepher in Galilee, a village about three miles from Nazareth. Assyria, with its great city Nineveh on the Tigris River (across from modern Mosul in Iraq), was the rising superpower of the region, known for its cruelty to conquered peoples. Within a few decades it would destroy the northern kingdom (722 BC). Jonah is different from every other prophetic book. It is almost entirely a story, not a collection of oracles, and the prophet is the one who needs to change. Jesus treated it as history and as a sign of his own death and resurrection (Matt 12:39–41). Most Christians through the centuries have read it the same way, while some modern scholars read it as a parable with a point. Either way, its message is about God's mercy, which is wider than his own prophet wanted it to be.

#### Jonah Flees the Presence of the LORD (vv1–6)
- **"The word of the LORD came to Jonah the son of Amittai" (v1).** This is the standard way a prophetic book begins (Hos 1:1; Joel 1:1). Jonah means "dove," and Amittai is from the Hebrew word for truth or faithfulness. Hosea later calls Israel "like a dove, silly and without sense" (Hos 7:11), and some see a hint of that in the prophet's name, though the text doesn't draw the link.
- **"Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it" (v2).** Nineveh was ancient even then: Genesis 10:11–12 lists it among the first cities built after the flood. "Great" (*gadol*) is a key word in the book. It is used of the city, the wind, the storm, the sailors' fear and the fish. "Call out" (*qara'*) is also a key word, and it comes back in v6 in a surprising mouth. God sending a prophet of Israel to preach to a foreign capital is unusual, and JFB calls it "the only case of a prophet being sent to the heathen" in this way.
- **"For their evil has come up before me" (v2).** The phrase recalls Sodom and Gomorrah, whose outcry rose to God (Gen 18:20–21). God sees the evil of every nation, not just Israel's. The word for evil (*ra'ah*) is another key word in Jonah. It returns as "this evil" (the storm) in vv7–8, and later as Nineveh's "evil way" and the "disaster" God relents from (3:8–10).
- **"But Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD" (v3).** He did rise, as told, but in the opposite direction. Nineveh lay about 500 miles to the northeast. Tarshish was probably Tartessus in southern Spain, the far western edge of the known world, well over 2,000 miles away. JFB, Guzik and the Tyndale notes all favor Spain, though some have suggested Tarsus in Asia Minor. "From the presence of the LORD" is said twice in this verse. Jonah knew God was everywhere (v9; Ps 139:7–10). He was fleeing his service, leaving the land where he stood before God as a prophet. 4:2 gives his reason: he knew God was "gracious and merciful" and might spare Nineveh. He did not want that.
- **"He went down to Joppa… and went down into it" (v3).** Joppa (modern Jaffa, now part of Tel Aviv) was the main port on Judah's coast. Cedar for Solomon's temple was floated there (2 Chr 2:16). "Went down" (*yarad*) is repeated: down to Joppa, down into the ship, then down into the hold (v5), and finally in his prayer, "I went down to the land whose bars closed upon me forever" (2:6). Running from God is a steady descent. Centuries later, in the same town of Joppa, Peter received the vision that sent him to a Gentile household, and he went (Acts 10:5–23).
- **"So he paid the fare" (v3).** A small detail with a point. Disobedience cost Jonah money as well as everything else. Some Jewish interpreters said he hired the whole ship. Guzik notes that a ship being available did not mean God approved. Open doors are not always God's leading.
- **"But the LORD hurled a great wind upon the sea" (v4).** "But" answers Jonah's "but" in v3. "Hurled" (*tul*) is a strong verb, used again when the sailors hurl the cargo (v5) and when they hurl Jonah (vv12, 15). God throws a wind, the sailors throw cargo, and finally they throw the prophet. "The ship threatened to break up" is literally "the ship thought it would break up," as if the ship itself were afraid.
- **"The mariners were afraid, and each cried out to his god" (v5).** The crew of a Phoenician trading ship would come from many nations and worship many gods. They prayed to every god they had, then did everything they could, throwing the cargo overboard. Henry: "Having called upon their gods for help, the sailors did what they could to help themselves."
- **"But Jonah had gone down into the inner part of the ship… and was fast asleep" (v5).** "Fast asleep" is the Hebrew *radam*, deep, heavy sleep, the word used of Adam's sleep (Gen 2:21) and Sisera's (Judg 4:21). Jonah was exhausted, numb or hiding, perhaps all three. The Tyndale notes suggest God may even have kept him asleep so that the sailors' own gods could be shown helpless first.
- **"What do you mean, you sleeper? Arise, call out to your god!" (v6).** The captain, literally "the chief of the rope-pullers," says exactly what God said to Jonah in v2: "Arise… call out." The words God gave the prophet now come back to him from a pagan sailor. **"Perhaps the god will give a thought to us, that we may not perish."** The pagan captain shows more humility before God than the prophet. The Ninevite king later uses the same kind of hopeful "who knows" (3:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry writes simply, "Jonah would not go," and warns that a fair wind can carry a person away from duty. He sees in Jonah's sleep a picture of how sin dulls the conscience: "Jonah was fast asleep." Guzik draws a similar application: a believer asleep in a storm, not praying while others are perishing. He argues Jonah may have had several reasons to run, Nineveh's violence, the danger of the mission, and above all the fear that Assyria would be spared. JFB says Jonah fled where "Jehovah was peculiarly present," the land of Israel, and calls this "the only case of a prophet, charged with a prophetical message, concealing it." JFB explains "the sides of the ship" (KJV) as "the interior recesses."

#### Jonah Is Thrown into the Sea (vv7–16)
- **"Come, let us cast lots, that we may know on whose account this evil has come upon us" (v7).** In the ancient world, a sudden terrible storm meant some god was angry, so someone on board must be to blame. Lots were small marked stones or sticks, drawn or thrown. Israel's law used lots for decisions put before God (Lev 16:8; Josh 18:6), and the apostles used them once (Acts 1:26). Proverbs 16:33 says, "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD." The sailors did not know the LORD, but he guided their lot. JFB compares the lot that exposed Achan (Josh 7:14–18).
- **"What is your occupation? And where do you come from?" (v8).** Four quick questions. The sailors want to know who this man is and which god he has offended. The question "what is your occupation?" is painful, because Jonah's occupation was prophet, and he was running from it.
- **"I am a Hebrew, and I fear the LORD, the God of heaven, who made the sea and the dry land" (v9).** "Hebrew" was the name Israelites used when speaking to foreigners (Gen 40:15; Exod 2:6). JFB notes that he does not say "Israelite." The confession is true and also deeply ironic. He says he "fears" the LORD while running from him, and he names the LORD as maker of "the sea and the dry land," while trying to escape him by sea. Many in the ancient world believed each god ruled a region, a sea god here, a mountain god there. Jonah confesses that his God made both, so there was nowhere to go.
- **"Then the men were exceedingly afraid" (v10).** Literally "they feared a great fear." Their fear grows through the chapter: afraid of the storm (v5), terrified once they know which God is behind it (v10), and at the end fearing the LORD himself (v16). **"What is this that you have done!"** It is the question God asked Eve in the garden (Gen 3:13), now asked by pagans of a prophet.
- **"What shall we do to you, that the sea may quiet down for us?" (v11).** They ask Jonah, since he knows his God. JFB: they ask "as Jonah himself must best know how his God is to be appeased." "The sea grew more and more tempestuous" is literally "the sea was walking and storming," growing worse with every exchange.
- **"Pick me up and hurl me into the sea" (v12).** Jonah's answer is remarkable. "I know it is because of me." He takes the blame. Readers have weighed his motive. It could be honest repentance and a willingness to die so others live. It could be a stubborn preference for death over going to Nineveh (compare 4:3, 8). The Tyndale notes lean toward the second, and Guzik lists several possibilities. Henry reads it more kindly: "Jonah sees this to be the punishment of his iniquity, he accepts it, and justifies God in it." The text leaves it open, but either way he does not repent by turning toward Nineveh. He chooses the sea.
- **"Nevertheless, the men rowed hard to get back to dry land" (v13).** "Rowed hard" is literally "dug," digging their oars into the waves. The pagan sailors try to save the life of the man whose disobedience is drowning them. They show the kind of mercy toward an outsider that Jonah refuses to show Nineveh. "But they could not." JFB quotes Proverbs 21:30: no plan can succeed against the LORD.
- **"O LORD, let us not perish for this man's life, and lay not on us innocent blood" (v14).** Now they pray to the LORD by name, using the covenant name *Yahweh* twice in one short prayer. They have gone from "each… to his god" (v5) to the God of Israel. They fear being guilty of shedding innocent blood (Deut 21:8). **"For you, O LORD, have done as it pleased you."** That is a fine confession of God's sovereignty (Ps 115:3; 135:6).
- **"So they picked up Jonah and hurled him into the sea, and the sea ceased from its raging" (v15).** Immediately the storm stops. The word "ceased" is literally "stood still." The sea's calm was the clearest proof that the LORD had sent the storm. Henry: "Throwing Jonah into the sea put an end to the storm."
- **"Then the men feared the LORD exceedingly, and they offered a sacrifice to the LORD and made vows" (v16).** "Feared the LORD with a great fear," the third stage. Sacrifices and vows were acts of worship and commitment (Ps 116:17–18). The Tyndale notes suggest they may simply have added the LORD to their gods. Guzik notes that many commentators see real faith, especially since they worshiped after the danger was past. JFB thinks they offered a thanksgiving sacrifice at once and vowed more on landing. The irony stands either way. Before Jonah preaches a word to Nineveh, a boatload of Gentiles has come to fear the LORD through him, by his disobedience.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the lot fell upon Jonah" because God uncovers hidden sin, and that the storm ended only when its cause was removed. JFB says "God sometimes sanctioned this mode of deciding in difficult cases," and on v13, "Wind and tide—God's displeasure and God's counsel—were against them." Guzik observes that Jonah's confession in v9 was only partly true, since he was running from the God he said he feared, and suggests his repentance may already have begun. The Tyndale notes highlight the irony of pagan sailors honoring the LORD while his prophet dishonored him.

#### A Great Fish Swallows Jonah (v17)
- **"The LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah" (v17).** In Hebrew Bibles this verse begins chapter 2. "Appointed" (*manah*) is used four times in the book: of the fish, the plant, the worm and the east wind (4:6–8). God commands creation, and creation obeys him, even when his prophet does not. JFB: the fish was "not created specially for this purpose, but appointed in His providence." The Hebrew says only "great fish" (*dag gadol*). It does not say "whale," though the KJV uses "whale" in Matthew 12:40, where the Greek word *kētos* means any huge sea creature. The species is not the point.
- **Could it happen?** Some find the story impossible. The book itself presents it as a miracle of the God "who made the sea and the dry land" (v9), and Henry says Jonah's survival was impossible by nature but not for God. The Tyndale notes add that some sea creatures, like the whale shark and the sperm whale, are large enough to swallow a man whole. Stories have been told of people surviving such a thing, though the best-known modern ones are poorly documented. The issue is not marine biology but whether God can do what the text says. If God raised Jesus from the dead, he can preserve a prophet in a fish.
- **The fish is rescue, not punishment.** Jonah was sinking to his death (2:3–6). The fish swallowed him and kept him alive. Chapter 2 is his prayer from inside it, a prayer of thanks, not despair.
- **"Three days and three nights" (v17).** In Hebrew counting, any part of a day counted as a day, so the phrase need not mean a full seventy-two hours. JFB thinks Jonah was cast onto land "on the third day." Jesus took this as a sign of himself: "For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matt 12:40). On the traditional reckoning, Jesus was buried on Friday and rose on Sunday morning, "the third day," by the same way of counting.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees Jonah in the fish as a type of Christ in the grave. JFB explains the three days by the Hebrew way of counting part of a day as the whole. The Tyndale notes say the book treats the event as history and that Jesus used it to point to his own time in the grave.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus said, "something greater than Jonah is here" (Matt 12:41), and this chapter shows how. Jesus, too, slept in a boat in a terrible storm, and his frightened disciples woke him: "do you not care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38). But Jesus did not need to be thrown overboard. He stood up and calmed the sea with a word, and the disciples "feared a great fear," the same phrase as the sailors in Jonah 1:10 and 16 in the Greek Old Testament (Mark 4:41). Jonah was hurled into the sea for his own sin, and the storm stopped. Jesus gave himself into death for the sins of others, and God's storm of judgment stopped for all who trust him. Jonah ran from God's mercy to the nations. Jesus brought it to them, and from his death and resurrection, the true sign of Jonah, that mercy now goes out to every Nineveh on earth.
