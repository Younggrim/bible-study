*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah." The Sons of Korah were a family of Levite singers and temple gatekeepers (1 Chr 9:19; 2 Chr 20:19), descended from the Korah who rebelled against Moses and died under God's judgment (Num 16), whose sons were spared (Num 26:11). Psalm 47 is the middle of three "songs of Zion" (46–48). Psalm 46 ended with God's word to the raging nations, "I will be exalted among the nations" (46:10). Psalm 47 answers by calling those same nations to clap and shout for joy. No occasion is named. Many, including Matthew Henry, connect it with David bringing the ark up to Mount Zion "with shouting and with the sound of the horn" (2 Sam 6:15). JFB suggests the victory of Jehoshaphat, when the Korahite singers praised God before the battle and the enemy armies destroyed one another (2 Chr 20:19–30). Both are guesses, and the psalm is written so that it can be sung at any celebration of God's kingship. In Jewish worship it is recited seven times on the New Year (Rosh Hashanah) before the ram's horn is blown. The church has long sung it on Ascension Day, because of v5: "God has gone up with a shout." It is a short psalm of nine verses, with three movements: a call to praise (vv1–4), the King going up to his throne (vv5–7), and the King seated over the nations (vv8–9).

#### Clap Your Hands, All Peoples (vv1–4)
- **"Clap your hands, all peoples!" (v1).** "Peoples" is plural: not Israel only, but the nations. The very nations that "rage" in 46:6 are now invited to celebrate. Clapping (*taqa'*) was the noise of a crowd greeting a king at his coronation: when young Joash was crowned, "they clapped their hands and said, 'Long live the king!'" (2 Kgs 11:12). In other places clapping can mean scorn (Lam 2:15; Nah 3:19), but here it is pure joy. Isaiah even pictures the trees clapping their hands at God's salvation (Isa 55:12; Ps 98:8).
- **"Shout to God with loud songs of joy!" (v1).** "Shout" (*rua'*) is the war cry or the cheer of a crowd at victory, the shout raised at Jericho (Josh 6:5) and when the ark came into the camp (1 Sam 4:5). This is not quiet, private devotion. It is the roar of a stadium.
- **"For the LORD, the Most High, is to be feared" (v2).** Every call to praise in this psalm is followed by a "for," a reason. Praise is not empty noise; it answers to who God is. "Most High" (*'Elyon*) is the title Melchizedek used for "God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth" (Gen 14:19). "To be feared" (*nora'*) means awesome, inspiring reverent awe. The nations are called to joy and to awe at the same time.
- **"A great king over all the earth" (v2).** "Great king" was the title the emperors of Assyria claimed for themselves. Sennacherib's spokesman used it as a taunt outside Jerusalem's walls: "Thus says the great king, the king of Assyria" (2 Kgs 18:19, 28). The psalm hands the title to its true owner. The gods of the nations around Israel were local gods, gods of a city or a land. The LORD is King "over all the earth."
- **"He subdued peoples under us, and nations under our feet" (v3).** This looks back to the conquest of Canaan and David's victories (18:47, which uses the same rare verb, "subdued peoples under me"). Putting a foot on a conquered king's neck was an ancient sign of total victory (Josh 10:24). It may seem strange that nations are called to clap about their own defeat (v1). But the psalm sees beyond conquest: by v9 the nations are not crushed but gathered in as God's people. Their subjection becomes their blessing.
- **"He chose our heritage for us" (v4).** The heritage is the promised land, the inheritance God promised to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Deut 32:8–9). Israel did not pick its land; God chose it for them. That is a quiet but deep comfort. Psalm 16:5–6 says the same of the believer: "the lines have fallen for me in pleasant places."
- **"The pride of Jacob whom he loves" (v4).** "Pride" (*ga'on*) here means not arrogance but glory, the thing one rightly boasts in: Jacob's splendid land. The same phrase appears in Amos 8:7 and Nahum 2:2. And the reason for it all is love: "whom he loves." God chose Israel not because they were many or great but "because the LORD loves you" (Deut 7:7–8). Malachi opens the same way: "I have loved you… yet I have loved Jacob" (Mal 1:2). Jacob was the schemer, not the hero, and God loved him anyway.
- **"Selah" (v4).** A pause, probably a musical interlude. A good moment to take in that God's choosing comes from his love.
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures the clapping hands of people "transported with pleasure, that cannot contain themselves," and reads the call to all peoples as "a prophecy of the conversion of the Gentiles and the bringing of them into the church." On v4 he finds "the happiness of the saints, that God himself has chosen their inheritance for them," and gives the believer's response: "He knows what is good for me better than I do for myself." Guzik makes the same application, warning against the urge to choose our own blessings, callings or crosses, and passes on Spurgeon's point that clapping is a language every nation can speak. JFB reads the conquests of v3 as pictures of the church's spiritual conquests, and says God's love "was the sole cause" of Jacob's privileges.

#### God Has Gone Up with a Shout (vv5–7)
- **"God has gone up with a shout, the LORD with the sound of a trumpet" (v5).** "Gone up" (*'alah*) is the verb for going up to Jerusalem and to the temple, and it is the verb used when David "brought up the ark of the LORD with shouting and with the sound of the horn" (2 Sam 6:15). The ark was the throne of the invisible God (1 Sam 4:4), so when the ark went up the hill, God was going up to his throne. "Shout" (*teru'ah*) is the sound of trumpets and voices together, the noise of the Day of Trumpets (Lev 23:24). The "trumpet" is the *shofar*, the ram's horn, blown at coronations ("Absalom is king!" 2 Sam 15:10; 1 Kgs 1:39) and at victories.
- **A king ascending his throne.** The picture is of a victorious king returning from battle and going up to take his seat. The Tyndale notes read it as the LORD returning to heaven after winning his victories, the reverse of his coming down to rescue in 18:9–16. Either way, the King who fought for his people now takes his throne amid cheering.
- **"Sing praises to God, sing praises!" (v6).** "Sing praises" (*zammeru*) comes four times in one verse, and again in v7: five times in two verses. The verb is related to *mizmor*, "psalm," and means to make music, often with instruments. The repetition is like a cheerleader's chant. The psalm will not let praise stay half-hearted.
- **"Sing praises to our King" (v6).** The great King over all the earth (v2) is also "our King." Universal and personal at once.
- **"For God is the King of all the earth" (v7).** Another "for." The reason for the repeated praise is repeated too.
- **"Sing praises with a psalm!" (v7).** The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew word: *maskil*. It is the word found in some psalm titles (Pss 32, 42, 44, 45), and it comes from the verb *sakal*, "to consider, have insight, act wisely" (the lexicon confirms that range). So the line can mean "sing a skillful song," or, as the King James has it, "sing ye praises with understanding." Both senses belong together: skillful music that understands what it sings. Paul seems to echo the thought: "I will sing praise with my spirit, but I will sing with my mind also" (1 Cor 14:15).
- **Commentators:** All three see more than the ark here. Henry writes that the psalm "looks further, to the ascension of Christ into the heavenly Zion, after he had finished his undertaking on earth," and that Christ "went up as a Mediator, typified by the ark and the mercy-seat over it." JFB agrees: "the whole typifies Christ's ascension," comparing 68:18, which Paul applies to Christ (Eph 4:8). On v7 Henry insists that worship must engage the mind: "It is not an acceptable service if it be not a reasonable service." Guzik draws out the same point with Spurgeon's line that we must mind the matter more than the music, and sums up the psalm's call as praise that is joyful, loud, universal, frequent and intelligent.

#### God Reigns over the Nations (vv8–9)
- **"God reigns over the nations" (v8).** Or "God has become king." The same Hebrew phrase (*malak*) begins the "the LORD reigns" psalms (93:1; 96:10; 97:1; 99:1). It was the cry at a coronation: "Jehu is king!" (2 Kgs 9:13). Psalm 47 is often grouped with those enthronement psalms.
- **"God sits on his holy throne" (v8).** The King who went up (v5) has now sat down. Sitting is the posture of a king whose battle is over and who rules. The throne is "holy," set apart, belonging to God alone. Isaiah saw the same throne, "high and lifted up" (Isa 6:1).
- **"The princes of the peoples gather as the people of the God of Abraham" (v9).** This is the climax of the psalm, and it is astonishing. The rulers of the nations, the very peoples "subdued" in v3, now gather *as* God's people. Not as captives at a victory parade but as members of the family. "The God of Abraham" is the key: God promised Abraham, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3), and that he would be "the father of a multitude of nations" (Gen 17:5). Here that promise is seen fulfilled.
- **A small textual question.** The Hebrew reads "the princes of the peoples gather, the people of the God of Abraham," and the ESV supplies "as" to make the sense clear. The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) reads "*with* the God of Abraham," since "people" (*'am*) and "with" (*'im*) are spelled with the same two Hebrew consonants. Either way, the nations' leaders are joining Abraham's God and his people. The ESV reading is the stronger one: they become his people.
- **"For the shields of the earth belong to God" (v9).** "Shields" stands for the rulers and defenders of the nations, the kings who were supposed to protect their peoples (89:18 calls Israel's king "our shield"; Hos 4:18 uses the word for rulers). Every power that defends a nation is God's property. He gives it and he can take it back.
- **"He is highly exalted!" (v9).** The psalm ends where 46:10 pointed: "I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted in the earth!" God's promise has become the congregation's song.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the "princes" as peoples who unite under "covenant with Abraham's God," and notes that "shields" can mean "rulers," as in Hosea 4:18. Henry agrees on the shields: "Magistracy is the shield of a nation," and such shields belong to God and are at his disposal. Guzik cites Kidner and Keil and Delitzsch in seeing Genesis 12:3 fulfilled, with the nobles drawing their own peoples toward God, and looks forward to the welcome of the Gentiles in Romans 4 and Galatians 3.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 47 tells of a King who goes up with a shout after his victory and sits down on his throne, and of nations gathering to him as the people of Abraham's God. The early church could hardly miss what it saw here. After Jesus finished his work on the cross and rose, "he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight" (Acts 1:9). He "sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" (Heb 1:3). And then the gospel went out to "all nations," Gentiles gathered in as the children of Abraham by faith: "if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" (Gal 3:7–9, 29). The psalm's last scene, the princes of the peoples gathered as the people of God, is a glimpse of what John saw at the end: "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Rev 7:9). And Paul says the King who went up will come down again "with a cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God" (1 Thess 4:16).
