*Setting:* About 853 BC, the last year of Ahab's reign. Since Ahab spared Ben-hadad (ch 20), there have been three years of peace between Israel and Syria. Assyrian records explain why: in 853 BC Shalmaneser III of Assyria marched west, and a coalition including Ahab and the king of Damascus fought him to a standstill at Qarqar in Syria. With the common enemy held off, the old quarrel returns. Elijah has pronounced Ahab's doom (21:19), delayed because Ahab humbled himself (21:29). Now the delay runs out. The chapter's main story is about prophecy: four hundred prophets who tell the king what he wants to hear, and one who tells him the truth. It closes 1 Kings with brief summaries of Jehoshaphat of Judah and Ahab's son Ahaziah.

#### Ahab and the False Prophets (vv1–12)
- **"For three years Syria and Israel continued without war" (v1).** Counting part-years as years, as the Bible usually does, this is the time since the treaty of 20:34.
- **"Jehoshaphat the king of Judah came down to the king of Israel" (v2).** This is new. For decades Judah and Israel had been at war (15:16). Now the two royal houses are allied by marriage: Jehoshaphat's son Jehoram married Ahab's daughter Athaliah (2 Kgs 8:18, 26). The Chronicler tells this same story at greater length and says plainly that a prophet rebuked Jehoshaphat: "Should you help the wicked and love those who hate the LORD?" (2 Chr 19:2).
- **"Ramoth-gilead belongs to us" (v3).** Ramoth ("heights") of Gilead was a fortified city east of the Jordan, guarding the King's Highway trade route. It had been a Levitical city of refuge (Josh 20:8) and an administrative center under Solomon (4:13). Ben-hadad had apparently never handed it back as promised (20:34). The city will be fought over for years (2 Kgs 8:28; 9:1).
- **"I am as you are, my people as your people, my horses as your horses" (v4).** Jehoshaphat commits himself fully, before asking God. Henry: "It is strange that Jehoshaphat will go so entirely into Ahab's interests."
- **"Inquire first for the word of the LORD" (v5).** To his credit, Jehoshaphat wants to hear from God, even if a little late. Kings across the ancient world consulted gods before battle, but Jehoshaphat wants the LORD.
- **"About four hundred men" (v6).** These are not the 450 prophets of Baal, who died at Carmel. They seem to speak in the LORD's name, perhaps prophets of the calf shrines at Bethel and Dan, which claimed to worship the LORD in an unlawful way (12:28). They answer, **"Go up, for the Lord will give it into the hand of the king."** Notice they say "the Lord" (*Adonai*, "master"), not the covenant name, and "the king," without saying which king. The answer is vague enough to be right whatever happens.
- **"Is there not here another prophet of the LORD?" (v7).** Jehoshaphat senses something is wrong. Four hundred voices saying exactly what the king wants is not reassuring. Henry: "Unity is not always the mark of a true church and a true ministry."
- **"Micaiah the son of Imlah, but I hate him, for he never prophesies good concerning me" (v8).** Micaiah means "Who is like the LORD?" Ahab's reason for hating him is telling. He does not say Micaiah is wrong. He says Micaiah is unpleasant to listen to. **"Let not the king say so."** Jehoshaphat's mild rebuke is the only check in the room.
- **"Sitting on their thrones, arrayed in their robes, at the threshing floor" (v10).** A threshing floor was a broad, flat, open space, usually near the city gate, used for threshing grain and for public gatherings. The scene is full of pageantry: two kings in royal robes on portable thrones, with hundreds of prophets performing before them.
- **"Zedekiah the son of Chenaanah made for himself horns of iron" (v11).** Zedekiah means "the LORD is righteous." The horn was a symbol of strength (Deut 33:17, where Joseph is pictured as an ox goring nations). His object lesson is vivid: like a bull with iron horns, the king will gore the Syrians. Prophets did use acted signs (20:35–40; Jer 27:2), but a striking performance is not the same as a true word. JFB calls it "little more than a flourish."
- **Commentators:** Henry says the prophets "knew which way his inclination was and designed only to humour the two kings." Guzik suggests these may have been men who claimed to serve the LORD but had become professional flatterers, rather than outright pagans. He notes that Ramoth-gilead's closeness to Judah made the alliance make political sense, but not spiritual sense. JFB calls Ramoth "a Levitical and free town" unjustly held by Syria.

#### Micaiah Prophesies Against Ahab (vv13–28)
- **"Let your word be like the word of one of them" (v13).** The messenger means well, perhaps. Agree, and keep out of trouble. Micaiah's answer is the heart of the chapter: **"As the LORD lives, what the LORD says to me, that I will speak"** (v14). It echoes Balaam's words when a king paid him to curse Israel (Num 22:38).
- **"Go up and triumph; the LORD will give it into the hand of the king" (v15).** Micaiah repeats the false prophets' line word for word, and Ahab knows at once he is being mocked. Something in his tone gave it away, as with Elijah's sarcasm at Carmel (18:27). JFB calls it "ironical mockery." It is a strange irony: Ahab, who hates the truth, can still recognize when he is not hearing it.
- **"How many times shall I make you swear...?" (v16).** Ahab demands the truth he has already said he hates.
- **"I saw all Israel scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no shepherd" (v17).** In the ancient world kings were called shepherds of their people. Sheep without a shepherd means the king is dead. **"These have no master; let each return to his home in peace."** The army will go home, without their king. The phrase "sheep without a shepherd" goes back to Moses, who asked God for a leader so that Israel would not be "as sheep that have no shepherd" (Num 27:17). Jesus saw the crowds the same way and had compassion on them (Matt 9:36; Mark 6:34).
- **"Did I not tell you...?" (v18).** Ahab turns the warning into proof that Micaiah is biased against him. He hears the message as a personal attack, not as a warning he might still heed.
- **"I saw the LORD sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing beside him" (v19).** Micaiah lets them see behind the scene. Two kings sit on thrones at the gate of Samaria, but the real throne is in heaven. The "host of heaven" is God's heavenly court of angels, the same picture as in Isaiah 6, Job 1–2 and Daniel 7. Henry sees the vision as "every man's judgment proceeding from him."
- **"Who will entice Ahab...?" (v20).** The word "entice" (*patah*) means to persuade, lure or deceive. God has decided Ahab's judgment. The question is only how it will come about.
- **"I will be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets" (v22).** This is one of the hardest passages in the Old Testament. How can the God who "never lies" (Titus 1:2; Num 23:19) send a lying spirit? Several things help. First, this is a vision, a picture of the heavenly court, and JFB warns it "must not be interpreted literally" in every detail. Second, God is using the lies Ahab had already chosen. He had surrounded himself with flatterers and hated the truth, and God now gives him over to what he wanted (compare Rom 1:24–28; 2 Thess 2:10–12, "God sends them a strong delusion... because they refused to love the truth"). Third, and most strikingly, God tells Ahab exactly what is happening (v23). A God who really wanted to deceive Ahab would not have announced it. Henry calls it "a lie from the father of lies, but by divine permission." Faithful interpreters differ on whether the "spirit" is an evil spirit permitted by God (as with Saul, 1 Sam 16:14) or simply a vivid way of describing God's judgment. All agree God is not the author of evil, yet rules over it.
- **"The LORD has declared disaster for you" (v23).** This is Ahab's last warning. He could still stay home.
- **"Zedekiah... struck Micaiah on the cheek" (v24).** A slap on the cheek was a public insult (Job 16:10; Lam 3:30). **"How did the Spirit of the LORD go from me to speak to you?"** Zedekiah claims to have the Spirit. Micaiah does not argue: **"You shall see on that day when you go into an inner chamber to hide yourself"** (v25). The truth will show itself. Henry: "God's prophets are not strikers nor persecutors." Jesus too was struck in the face before the high priest for speaking the truth (John 18:22–23).
- **"Put this fellow in prison and feed him meager rations of bread and water, until I come in peace" (v27).** Literally "bread of affliction and water of affliction," the poorest prison fare. Amon the governor and Joash "the king's son" may have been royal officials. "King's son" was sometimes a title for an officer of the court.
- **"If you return in peace, the LORD has not spoken by me" (v28).** Micaiah stakes everything on the test of a true prophet: does the word come true? (Deut 18:22). **"Hear, all you peoples!"** These are exactly the opening words of the prophet Micah (Mic 1:2), the later prophet with almost the same name. Some ancient copies of 1 Kings lack this phrase, and some scholars think a scribe added it to link the two, but the ESV keeps it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that hating the messenger is really hating the message, and treats the heavenly court as a true glimpse behind the curtain, compared with Job 1 and Revelation 12, where God permits evil beings in his presence without any fellowship with them. JFB stresses that the "lying spirit" is "only a permission," not God's own act of lying. Henry and JFB both note Micaiah's restraint when struck. Guzik passes on Wiseman's suggestion that the lying spirit worked through human voices.

#### Ahab Killed in Battle (vv29–40)
- **"So the king of Israel and Jehoshaphat the king of Judah went up" (v29).** After everything Jehoshaphat has heard, he still goes. Loyalty to an ally outweighs a prophet's warning.
- **"I will disguise myself... but you wear your robes" (v30).** Ahab half-believes Micaiah. He tries to cheat the prophecy by going into battle dressed as an ordinary soldier, while his ally makes himself the obvious target. It is selfish and cowardly, and Jehoshaphat agrees to it.
- **"Fight with neither small nor great, but only with the king of Israel" (v31).** Ben-hadad, whom Ahab spared and called "my brother" (20:32), now orders his thirty-two chariot captains to kill Ahab. The mercy that God condemned has come back to kill the one who showed it. The number thirty-two echoes the thirty-two kings of 20:1, now replaced by commanders (20:24).
- **"Jehoshaphat cried out" (v32).** The Syrians chase the only man in royal robes. Jehoshaphat cries out, and they realize he is not Ahab. The Chronicler adds: "Jehoshaphat cried out, and the LORD helped him; God drew them away from him" (2 Chr 18:31).
- **"A certain man drew his bow at random" (v34).** Literally "in his innocence" or "simplicity," shooting without a target in mind. The arrow finds the one gap "between the scale armor and the breastplate," the joint where the overlapping metal scales met the chest plate. Ahab's disguise fooled the Syrian captains. It could not fool God. What looks like chance is the hand of God carrying out his word.
- **"The king was propped up in his chariot facing the Syrians, until at evening he died" (v35).** To his credit, Ahab stays upright so his army will not panic. He dies slowly, bleeding into the floor of the chariot. **"Every man to his city, and every man to his country!"** (v36). Exactly as Micaiah saw: sheep without a shepherd, going home.
- **"They washed the chariot by the pool of Samaria, and the dogs licked up his blood" (v38).** A large pool has been found at the royal quarter of Samaria. Elijah had said the dogs would lick Ahab's blood (21:19). It happens in Samaria, not Jezreel, because God delayed the full judgment to Ahab's son (21:29; 2 Kgs 9:25–26). **"The prostitutes washed themselves in it."** This adds a final disgrace. The Hebrew is clear about the prostitutes, though the KJV read the same words as "they washed his armour." Prostitution was tied to the worship of Baal and Asherah that Ahab had promoted.
- **"The ivory house that he built" (v39).** Archaeologists at Samaria have found hundreds of carved ivory pieces from this era, inlays from furniture and wall panels, in Phoenician style. Amos later condemns those who lie "on beds of ivory" while the poor suffer (Amos 3:15; 6:4). Ahab was a successful king by worldly measures: wealthy, a builder, a strong military power. "The rest of the acts of Ahab" were in the royal records. The writer of Kings cares only about how he stood before God.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees God steering an apparently random arrow to carry out his judgment, and passes on Trapp's line that the wicked may enjoy the journey but the godly have the better end. He also contrasts Ahab's worldly achievements with his spiritual ruin. JFB calls it "a random arrow" and says Ahab disguised himself as a common soldier. The Tyndale notes credit Ahab with courage in staying upright in his chariot.

#### Jehoshaphat Reigns in Judah (vv41–50)
- **"He walked in all the way of Asa his father" (v43).** Jehoshaphat ruled about 872–848 BC, partly alongside his father. Kings rates him a good king, though the high places, local shrines outside Jerusalem, remained. The Chronicler gives much more: he sent teachers of the law through Judah (2 Chr 17:7–9) and appointed judges (2 Chr 19:5–11).
- **"Jehoshaphat also made peace with the king of Israel" (v44).** It ended generations of war, but the alliance had a heavy cost. Through the marriage of his son to Athaliah, Baal worship would come into Judah itself, and Athaliah would later try to wipe out the royal line of David (2 Kgs 11:1).
- **"The remnant of the male cult prostitutes" (v46).** These were men attached to pagan shrines. Asa had begun removing them (15:12). His son finished the job.
- **"There was no king in Edom; a deputy was king" (v47).** Edom, south of the Dead Sea, was under Judah's control at this point. That is why Jehoshaphat could use the port of Ezion-geber on the Gulf of Aqaba.
- **"Ships of Tarshish to go to Ophir for gold" (v48).** "Ships of Tarshish" meant large ocean-going trading ships. Ophir was a source of fine gold, probably in Arabia or East Africa. Jehoshaphat tried to revive Solomon's sea trade (9:26–28), but the ships were wrecked in port. The Chronicler says this was God's judgment for partnering with Ahaziah of Israel (2 Chr 20:35–37). Here Jehoshaphat refuses a second partnership (v49). He has learned, though slowly.
- **Commentators:** Guzik credits Jehoshaphat with a largely godly reign, while calling his son's marriage to Athaliah a serious mistake. He notes that refusing Ahaziah's second offer was to Jehoshaphat's credit. The Tyndale notes list his compromises: the high places, and his alliances with Ahab and later Joram (2 Kgs 3).

#### Ahaziah Reigns in Israel (vv51–53)
- **"He reigned two years over Israel" (v51).** Ahaziah, son of Ahab and Jezebel, ruled about 853–852 BC. Two years, counted in part-years, may mean little more than one.
- **"In the way of his father and in the way of his mother and in the way of Jeroboam" (v52).** A threefold verdict. He followed Ahab's Baal worship, Jezebel's influence and the golden calves set up by Jeroboam. **"He served Baal and worshiped him"** (v53). The story continues without a break into 2 Kings 1, which was originally the same book. The division into 1 and 2 Kings came later, with the Greek translation.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that 1 Kings ends on a low note, with a king who learned nothing from his father's death. He also passes on Poole's suggestion that Ahaziah may have co-reigned briefly with Ahab before his death.

#### Pointing to Christ
Micaiah stands alone against four hundred, is struck in the face for speaking the truth, and is sent to prison on bread and water, trusting that God's word will prove itself. Jesus, the true Prophet, was also struck before the court and condemned for speaking the truth. Micaiah saw Israel "as sheep that have no shepherd." Jesus looked at the crowds of Israel and saw the same thing, and had compassion on them (Mark 6:34). Ahab tried to escape God's word by disguising himself and putting another king in his place. Jesus did the reverse: the true King laid aside his glory, took the place of the target, and let the arrow of judgment strike him, so that his sheep could go home in peace. "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11).
