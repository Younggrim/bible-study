*Setting:* Jacob is still camped near Shechem, in the hill country of central Canaan. The last chapter ended in disaster: Dinah was raped, and Simeon and Levi answered by killing the men of the city, while their brothers plundered it (ch 34). Jacob is afraid the Canaanites will band together and wipe out his family (34:30). Behind all this lies a promise he has not kept. About thirty years earlier, running from Esau, Jacob dreamed of a stairway to heaven at Bethel and vowed that if God brought him home safely, "the LORD shall be my God" and the stone he set up "shall be God's house" (28:20–22). God has done everything he promised. Jacob has stopped short of Bethel. Chapter 34 never mentions God; chapter 35 is full of him. It closes the long account of Isaac's family (25:19–35:29) with three deaths and a renewed covenant, and it hands the story on to Jacob's twelve sons.

#### God Blesses and Renames Jacob (vv1–15)
- **"Arise, go up to Bethel and dwell there" (v1).** God breaks the silence of chapter 34 by speaking first. Bethel lay some fifteen to twenty miles south of Shechem, and it sits higher in the hills, so "go up" is literal as well as spiritual. God reminds Jacob of "the God who appeared to you when you fled from your brother Esau." Jacob does not need a new revelation. He needs to go back to the one he already had and do what he said he would do.
- **"Put away the foreign gods that are among you" (v2).** Jacob's household was larger than his sons. It included servants, the captives taken from Shechem (34:29), and Rachel, who had stolen her father Laban's household gods (31:19, 34). Jacob knew there were idols in his camp and had let them stay. Now he names them. The three commands, put away, purify, change garments, anticipate Israel's preparation to meet God at Sinai, where the people washed their clothes and consecrated themselves (Exod 19:10, 14). Going to God's house means leaving other gods behind.
- **"The God who answers me in the day of my distress" (v3).** Jacob's own testimony is simple and personal. God answered him when he fled Esau, and "has been with me wherever I have gone," exactly what God promised at Bethel: "I am with you and will keep you wherever you go" (28:15). The Tyndale notes put it well: God has kept his side, so Jacob must keep his.
- **The idols and the earrings buried "under the terebinth tree that was near Shechem" (v4).** The earrings were probably amulets, charms worn for luck or protection and tied to the gods they wore them for. Jacob hides them rather than leaving them for someone to dig up and use. The tree near Shechem may be the same landmark where Abram first built an altar in Canaan (12:6–7). Centuries later, at Shechem, Joshua gathered Israel and told them again, "put away the foreign gods that are among you" (Josh 24:23), and set up a stone "under the terebinth" there (Josh 24:26). The same call had to be made in every generation.
- **"A terror from God fell upon the cities" (v5).** Jacob feared being destroyed (34:30). God himself guards the road to Bethel. The word for "terror" is a strong one, a panic sent by God, and the same idea appears when Israel later enters the land (Exod 23:27). The protection comes not because Jacob's sons deserved it but because God had promised to bring Jacob back.
- **"Luz (that is, Bethel)" and "El-bethel" (vv6–7).** Luz was the town's older Canaanite name (28:19). Bethel means "house of God." Jacob now names the altar *El-bethel*, "God of Bethel." At the first visit he was struck by the place. Now he worships the God of the place. "God had revealed himself to him": in Hebrew this verb is plural, which is unusual with *Elohim*, "God." Most read it as simply matching the plural form of the word *Elohim*. Some older interpreters connect it with the angels Jacob saw on the stairway (28:12).
- **"Deborah, Rebekah's nurse, died" (v8).** This is a surprising line. Rebekah's nurse came with her from Haran a hundred years or more earlier (24:59). She must have been very old, and at some point she had joined Jacob's household. She is buried under an oak called **Allon-bacuth**, "oak of weeping." The family mourned a servant like a mother. Rebekah's own death is never recorded in Genesis. Jewish tradition (Rashi, for example) suggested that news of Rebekah's death reached Jacob at this time, which would explain the weeping, but the text does not say so. Rebekah, who sent Jacob away "for a few days" (27:44), apparently never saw him again.
- **"God appeared to Jacob again… and blessed him" (v9).** "Again" looks back to the first appearance at Bethel (28:13–15). This is the third great meeting between God and Jacob, after the stairway at Bethel and the wrestling at the Jabbok (32:24–30).
- **"No longer shall your name be called Jacob, but Israel" (v10).** God gave this name at the Jabbok (32:28). Here, at Bethel, he confirms it. *Jacob* sounds like "heel-grabber," with the sense of one who supplants or cheats (27:36). *Israel* is usually explained as "he strives with God" or "God strives" (32:28). Hosea links the two scenes: Jacob "strove with the angel and prevailed… He met God at Bethel, and there God spoke with us" (Hos 12:4). Unlike Abraham, whose old name disappears once he is renamed (17:5), Jacob is still called "Jacob" alongside "Israel" for the rest of Genesis. The new identity is real, but the old man has not vanished.
- **"I am God Almighty: be fruitful and multiply" (v11).** "God Almighty" is *El Shaddai*. The Hebrew dictionary calls the origin of *Shaddai* uncertain. The Greek Old Testament often rendered it "Almighty," and that is how the English tradition reads it. It is the name God used when he promised Abraham a son in old age (17:1) and the name Isaac used in blessing Jacob as he left home (28:3). It is the God-who-can name, used whenever the promise looks impossible. "Be fruitful and multiply" echoes the first blessing on humanity (1:28) and on Noah (9:1). God's purposes for the whole world are running through this one family.
- **"A nation and a company of nations… kings shall come from your own body" (v11).** "Your own body" is literally "your loins." The promise of kings was first given to Abraham and Sarah (17:6, 16). Only here is it repeated to Jacob, and it comes right before the birth of Benjamin, whose tribe produced Israel's first king, Saul, and in a family whose son Judah will father the line of David (49:10).
- **"The land… I will give to you" (v12).** The land promise passes from Abraham to Isaac to Jacob, and on to "your offspring after you." Jacob owns a single plot near Shechem (33:19). God speaks as though the whole land is already his to hand over.
- **"God went up from him" (v13).** The same words close God's meeting with Abraham (17:22). This was a real visit with a real departure.
- **A pillar, a drink offering and oil (v14).** At the first visit Jacob set up a stone and poured oil on it (28:18). Now he adds a drink offering, the first one mentioned in the Bible. A drink offering was wine poured out before God, and later law made it part of regular worship (Exod 29:40; Num 15:5). **"Jacob called the name of the place… Bethel" (v15).** He names it again, as if to sign the vow paid in full.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums the chapter up as "three communions and three funerals": God's call, Jacob's altar, and God's appearing, then the deaths of Deborah, Rachel and Isaac. Guzik argues that Shechem's troubles came because Jacob settled there instead of going on to Bethel, and he calls this chapter a return to first love (Rev 2:4–5). He reads the earrings as tied to pagan religion and presses home the need to get rid of anything in a home that is tied to the occult, not just stop using it. JFB says the earrings were linked to "incantation and idolatry," and that in v5 "a supernatural panic seized them." JFB also notes that old nurses like Deborah were "not only honored, but loved as mothers." The Tyndale notes add that Jacob's actions at Bethel nearly repeat those of his first visit, a deliberate sign that the vow is being kept.

#### The Deaths of Rachel and Isaac (vv16–29)
- **"Still some distance from Ephrath" (v16).** The family travels south from Bethel toward Hebron, where Isaac lives. Ephrath is the old name for Bethlehem, as v19 explains. Bethlehem means "house of bread." Later it is the home of Ruth and Boaz, the town of David, and the town Micah names for the coming ruler: "But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah… from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel" (Mic 5:2).
- **"Do not fear, for you have another son" (v17).** The midwife's words are bitterly fitting. When Joseph was born, Rachel named him with a prayer: "May the LORD add to me another son!" (30:24). God answers that prayer, and it costs her life. Rachel once cried to Jacob, "Give me children, or I shall die!" (30:1). She received children, and she died giving birth to the second.
- **"As her soul was departing (for she was dying)" (v18).** "Soul" is *nephesh*, the life or breath of a person. The Old Testament speaks of the *nephesh* going out at death. It does not develop a full teaching on the afterlife here, but it does picture death as something leaving, not simply ending.
- **Ben-oni and Benjamin (v18).** Rachel names the boy *Ben-oni*, usually read "son of my sorrow." The Hebrew word *'oni* can also mean "my strength," as when Jacob calls Reuben "the firstfruits of my strength" (49:3), and a few scholars think Rachel meant "son of my vigor." Most follow the ESV's footnote, "son of my sorrow," which suits a dying mother. Jacob will not let the boy carry that name. He calls him *Benjamin*, "son of the right hand." The right hand was the place of honor and strength (Ps 110:1). Since *yamin* can also mean "south" (facing east, the right hand points south), some take it as "son of the south," the only son born in Canaan. Either way, Jacob turns a name of grief into a name of hope. He is the only one of the twelve whose name comes from Jacob.
- **"Rachel died, and she was buried on the way to Ephrath" (v19).** Jacob does not carry her to the family tomb at Machpelah in Hebron (23:19), where Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Rebekah and later Leah were buried (49:31). Rachel, the wife Jacob loved most, lies by the roadside. 1 Samuel 10:2 places her tomb "in the territory of Benjamin." The traditional site just north of Bethlehem has been honored since at least the fourth century AD, though some scholars place it farther north. Many readers also recall Jacob's rash oath to Laban: "Anyone with whom you find your gods shall not live" (31:32). Guzik and others see a link with Rachel's early death. The text does not draw that line itself.
- **"The pillar of Rachel's tomb, which is there to this day" (v20).** Jacob sets up a third pillar in this chapter: a memorial of worship at Bethel, and now a memorial of grief. "To this day" is a phrase from someone writing later, in the land, who could point to the place. Jeremiah later pictures Rachel weeping for her exiled children near Ramah (Jer 31:15), and Matthew hears that weeping again at Bethlehem when Herod kills the baby boys (Matt 2:18).
- **"Israel journeyed on… beyond the tower of Eder" (v21).** This is the first time the narrator calls him Israel. *Migdal-eder* means "tower of the flock," probably a watchtower for shepherds. Micah uses the same phrase for Jerusalem: "you, O tower of the flock… to you shall it come, the former dominion" (Mic 4:8), just before his prophecy about Bethlehem. Later Jewish tradition (in the Targum) said the Messiah would be revealed at the tower of Eder. That is a tradition, not part of Genesis, but it shows how readers have long felt this spot near Bethlehem was charged with promise.
- **"Reuben went and lay with Bilhah his father's concubine" (v22).** Reuben is Jacob's firstborn by Leah. Bilhah was Rachel's servant and the mother of Dan and Naphtali. With Rachel just dead, this may have been more than lust. In the ancient world, taking a king's or chief's concubine was a claim to his place. Absalom did it to David publicly (2 Sam 16:21–22), and Adonijah's request for David's last companion was read as a bid for the throne (1 Kgs 2:22). Some Jewish interpreters suggest Reuben also meant to make sure Bilhah could not replace Rachel as favored wife over his mother Leah. Either way, it was a gross sin against his father.
- **"And Israel heard of it" (v22).** That is all. As with Dinah (34:5), Jacob says nothing at the time. In the Hebrew Bible, the scribes marked an unusual paragraph break in the middle of this verse, as if leaving a silence on the page. The Greek translation adds "and it was evil in his sight," which is probably a scribe filling in the obvious. The response comes on Jacob's deathbed: "Unstable as water, you shall not have preeminence, because you went up to your father's bed" (49:4). 1 Chronicles 5:1 explains that Reuben's birthright passed to Joseph's sons.
- **"Now the sons of Jacob were twelve" (vv22–26).** For the first time all twelve names stand together, grouped by mother: Leah's six, Rachel's two, Bilhah's two, Zilpah's two. The line "born to him in Paddan-aram" is a summary, since Benjamin has just been born in Canaan. These are the heads of the twelve tribes. The list sits right after Reuben's sin and the massacre at Shechem, and it is not a list of heroes. God's people will come from these men anyway.
- **"Jacob came to his father Isaac at Mamre… Hebron" (v27).** Mamre, Kiriath-arba and Hebron are three names for one area, where Abraham and Isaac lived and where the family tomb was (23:2, 19). Jacob left home over twenty years earlier, when Isaac was old, blind and expecting death (27:1–2). Isaac lived on for decades.
- **"The days of Isaac were 180 years" (v28).** Isaac lived longer than Abraham (175, 25:7). By Genesis's own numbers (Isaac was 60 when Jacob was born, 25:26; Jacob was 130 when he went to Egypt, 47:9; Joseph was 30 when he stood before Pharaoh, 41:46), Isaac would have died about a dozen years after Joseph was sold into Egypt. So his death is told here to close Isaac's story, not because it happened next. Genesis often finishes one person's account before moving on.
- **"Gathered to his people, old and full of days" (v29).** The same words are used of Abraham (25:8). To be "gathered to his people" is more than going into the family grave; Abraham was buried far from his ancestors. It points to joining those who went before. **"His sons Esau and Jacob buried him."** Esau is named first, in birth order. Just as Isaac and Ishmael met to bury Abraham (25:9), the brothers who once could not live together stand together at their father's grave.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of Rachel, "Her dying is here called the departing of her soul," and of her roadside grave, "If the soul be at rest after death, it matters little where the body lies." He thinks Jacob renamed the boy so as not to be reminded of her death every time he spoke to him. On Reuben, he writes, "This was Reuben's sin, but it was Jacob's affliction," and notes that "for it, he lost his birthright and blessing." Guzik sees Reuben's sin as fruit of a home full of rivalry, and notes that Reuben, Simeon and Levi all disqualified themselves, so the royal line passes to Judah. He also reminds readers that returning to God did not spare Jacob grief. JFB says Isaac's death is "recorded by anticipation," and puts it fifteen years after Joseph was sold; by the figures above it was nearer twelve. Henry likewise notes Isaac died "many years after" Joseph was sold, and he points to Esau and Jacob's "amicable agreement" at the funeral as proof that God had changed Esau's heart. JFB has no notes on vv21–25 or v27.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter moves from idols buried under a tree to a renewed covenant, from the grief of a roadside grave to the hope of a son "of the right hand." God promises that kings will come from Jacob, and the story stops near Bethlehem, at "the tower of the flock." From that town God later raised up David, and from David's line came the true King, born in Bethlehem and laid in a manger near the shepherds' fields (Luke 2:4–8). Rachel's weeping was echoed at his birth (Matt 2:18), and he is the Son who now sits at the Father's right hand (Heb 1:3). The family list in vv23–26 is full of failure, and the covenant still runs through it, because the promise rests on God Almighty, not on Jacob's sons.
