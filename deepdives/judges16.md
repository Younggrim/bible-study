*Setting:* Samson has judged Israel twenty years "in the days of the Philistines" (15:20), and the Philistines still rule. Chapter 16 is the end of his story and the last of the judges whose deeds are told in Judges. (Chapters 17–21 are an appendix showing how far Israel had fallen.) It opens with Samson walking into the heart of enemy territory for a prostitute, moves to his fatal love for Delilah, and ends in the temple of Dagon, where a blind prisoner prays and pulls the house down. It is the fullest picture in the book of a man richly gifted by God who squandered his gift, and of a God who still answered when he called.

#### Samson and Delilah (vv1–22)
- **"Samson went to Gaza, and there he saw a prostitute" (v1).** Gaza was the southernmost of the five Philistine cities, near the coast on the road to Egypt, deep in enemy land. Again Samson "saw" and wanted (14:1). He sleeps with a prostitute, which the law forbade (Lev 19:29; Deut 23:17–18) and which made a mockery of his calling as a man set apart to God.
- **The gates of Gaza (vv2–3).** The Gazites lock the city gates for the night and wait to kill him at dawn. At midnight Samson tears out "the doors of the gate of the city and the two posts… bar and all." City gates were massive wooden doors, often covered in metal, hung on posts set into stone, with a heavy bar across them. He carries them "to the top of the hill that is in front of Hebron." Hebron was about forty miles away and some 3,000 feet higher, in Judah's hills. JFB thinks "in front of Hebron" means a nearer hill facing toward Hebron, east of Gaza, which is possible. Either way, the message was a taunt: Philistia's main city stood open, with its gates on an Israelite hill. But it was a show of strength, not a victory. He humiliated the Philistines and walked away.
- **"He loved a woman in the Valley of Sorek, whose name was Delilah" (v4).** Sorek is the valley below Zorah, Samson's home country, on the border. It is the first time the text says Samson "loved" a woman. It never says she loved him. Her nationality is not stated, though her dealings with the Philistine lords suggest she was Philistine or worked with them. The meaning of her name is uncertain. Some note that it sounds like the Hebrew word for "night" (*laylah*), a sad contrast with Samson, whose name comes from "sun."
- **"We will each give you 1,100 pieces of silver" (v5).** The "lords of the Philistines" (*seranim*, a special Philistine word for the rulers of the five cities) each offer 1,100 pieces. That is 5,500 pieces of silver, a fortune. Compare Micah's annual wage of ten pieces for a priest (17:10). They do not ask her to kill him but to find out how to "bind him to humble him." The verb means to afflict or humble, and it will be used again of the torment in v19.
- **Three lies (vv6–14).** Each time Delilah asks, and Samson invents an answer. "Seven fresh bowstrings" (v7): bowstrings were made from animal gut or sinew, and fresh ones were moist and strong. "New ropes that have not been used" (v11): the same kind of ropes the men of Judah had tried (15:13). Then, coming closer to the truth, his hair: "If you weave the seven locks of my head with the web" (v13). She weaves his braids into the cloth on her loom and fixes it with the weaving pin, and he walks off with the whole loom. (The ESV fills out vv13–14 from the Greek, since the Hebrew is shorter.) Each time Philistines are waiting in the inner room, and each time she calls, "The Philistines are upon you, Samson!" He knows exactly what she is doing and keeps coming back.
- **"How can you say, 'I love you,' when your heart is not with me?" (v15).** This is the same weapon his Timnite wife used: "You only hate me; you do not love me" (14:16). "She pressed him hard with her words day after day… his soul was vexed to death" (v16). The phrase is literally "his soul was short, to the point of death," the same idiom used of God's compassion for Israel in 10:16. God's patience runs out for his people's misery. Samson's runs out for his own comfort.
- **"He told her all his heart" (v17).** "All his heart" is used three times in two verses (vv17–18). He tells her he is a Nazirite and that a razor has never touched his head. Of the three marks of the Nazirite vow (Num 6:1–8), he had already broken the other two in spirit: he had touched the dead (14:8–9; 15:15) and spent his life at drinking feasts and in the vineyards. His uncut hair was the last outward sign left.
- **"She made him sleep on her knees" (v19).** He sleeps with his head in her lap, she calls a man to shave the seven locks, "then she began to torment him." The verb is the same "humble" or "afflict" the lords used in v5. Samson is subdued.
- **"I will go out as at other times… But he did not know that the LORD had left him" (v20).** This may be the saddest sentence in Judges. Samson's strength was never in his hair. The hair was the sign of his being set apart to God. When he gave up the sign, he showed he had already given up what it stood for, and God withdrew his power. Samson did not even notice. Saul will later suffer the same: "the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul" (1 Sam 16:14). Hosea says of Israel, "gray hairs are sprinkled upon him, and he knows it not" (Hos 7:9).
- **"Gouged out his eyes" (v21).** The eyes that "saw" the woman at Timnah and the prostitute at Gaza are put out. Blinding prisoners was a common way to make a dangerous captive harmless (2 Kgs 25:7). They bring him "down" to Gaza, where he had carried off the gates, bind him with bronze shackles and set him to grind grain at the mill. Grinding was the work of slaves and women. The strongest man in Israel is reduced to an animal's or slave's labor, walking in circles in the dark.
- **"But the hair of his head began to grow again after it had been shaved" (v22).** One small "but." The hair is not magic, but it is a sign. God's purpose for Samson was not over. The Philistines did not notice. Readers are meant to.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Satan ruins men by flattering them into a good opinion of their own safety," and "Samson's eyes were the inlets of his sin." Guzik says Samson kept the outward form of his vow while ignoring its heart, and that he presumed on God's patience, taking past mercy as a guarantee. He reckons the 1,100 pieces at over 140 pounds of silver from each lord. All three agree the power was never in the hair itself. JFB says his strength came from "his peculiar relation to God," and the hair was "a sign of his Nazaritism." On the gates, the Tyndale notes take Hebron at face value, about forty miles uphill, while JFB prefers a hill much nearer Gaza. On v22, JFB passes on Bishop Hall's line that "his hair grew together with his repentance," and Henry says "Samson's afflictions were the means of bringing him to deep repentance." The text does not say he repented, but his prayer in v28 points that way.

#### The Death of Samson (vv23–31)
- **"A great sacrifice to Dagon their god" (v23).** Dagon was an old god of the region, worshiped long before the Philistines arrived. His name is very close to the Hebrew word for grain (*dagan*), and most scholars think he was a grain or storm god. An older idea, held by JFB and others, that he was half man and half fish comes from the similar Hebrew word for "fish" (*dag*) and is now widely doubted. The Philistines took up his worship in their new land. His temples stood in Gaza and Ashdod (1 Sam 5:2).
- **"Our god has given Samson our enemy into our hand" (vv23–24).** In the ancient world, a war between nations was a war between their gods. The Philistines credit Dagon. In their eyes, Samson's fall is the LORD's defeat. The verse is in rhythmic Hebrew, likely a victory chant. The ESV footnote says the last line can also mean "who has multiplied our slain." This is what Samson's sin has brought about: a pagan crowd praising an idol over the God of Israel.
- **"Call Samson, that he may entertain us" (v25).** The word can mean to make sport, to perform, or to be made fun of. They bring him out to be laughed at. "They made him stand between the pillars."
- **"Let me feel the pillars on which the house rests" (v26).** A boy leads him by the hand, so harmless does he now seem. Archaeologists have excavated a Philistine temple at Tell Qasile (in modern Tel Aviv) whose roof rested on two central wooden pillars set on stone bases about two meters apart. It is not this temple, but it shows how a man standing between two such pillars could push on both.
- **"About 3,000 men and women" on the roof (v27).** The flat roof was full of spectators looking down into the courtyard, along with all five lords of the Philistines. The weight on the roof made the building more fragile.
- **"O Lord GOD, please remember me and please strengthen me only this once" (v28).** Samson's second recorded prayer (15:18 was the first). He uses three names for God: *Adonai* (Lord), *Yahweh* (GOD), and *Elohim* (God). "Remember" in the Bible means to act on someone's behalf (Gen 8:1; 30:22). His request is still personal: "that I may be avenged… for my two eyes." Samson never becomes a model saint. But he turns, at last, to the LORD, and the LORD answers.
- **"Let me die with the Philistines" (v30).** He pushes, "and the house fell upon the lords and upon all the people." "So the dead whom he killed at his death were more than those whom he had killed during his life." With all five lords dead, Philistine leadership was crippled for a time.
- **Was it suicide?** Christians have asked this for centuries. Most have said no. Samson did not die to escape his suffering. He died as a soldier dies in battle, striking God's enemies, and God gave the strength in answer to prayer. Augustine argued that he acted by the Spirit's secret command. Hebrews 11:32–34 lists Samson among those who "from weakness were made strong." The text does not praise his motives, but it shows God answering him.
- **"His brothers and all his family came down… and buried him between Zorah and Eshtaol" (v31).** The Philistines, leaderless, do not stop them. His story ends where the Spirit first stirred him, "between Zorah and Eshtaol" (13:25), in his father's tomb. "He had judged Israel twenty years." He began to save Israel (13:5). Samuel and David would finish the work.
- **Commentators:** Henry says flatly that Samson is "not to be accused of self-murder": "He sought not his own death, but Israel's deliverance, and the destruction of their enemies." He adds, "The Lord permits some few to wander wide and sink deep, yet he recovers them at last." JFB agrees it was not "a vindictive suicide" but a public act God approved, and suggests the roof rested on two main beams over two pillars set close together, which fits the excavated Philistine temples. Guzik is more cautious. He sees God answering Samson's prayer but calls his last act a mix of vengeance and self-destruction. He stresses how much potential Samson wasted, that he should have fled temptation as Joseph did (Gen 39), and that he always fought alone. The Tyndale notes say much the same: Samson sought personal revenge, yet God used it to carry out his plan to rescue his people.

#### Pointing to Christ
Samson's end is a dark mirror of the gospel. He was set apart from birth, filled with the Spirit, betrayed for silver by someone close to him, bound, handed over to the enemy, mocked as entertainment at a pagan feast, and he died with his arms stretched out between two pillars, winning a greater victory in his death than in his life. Every one of those points meets Jesus, who was betrayed for silver (Matt 26:15), bound and handed over, mocked by soldiers (Matt 27:27–31), and stretched out his arms on a cross. But the differences matter even more. Samson suffered for his own sins. Jesus suffered for ours. Samson asked to be avenged. Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). Samson's death killed his enemies. Jesus' death reconciled his enemies to God (Rom 5:10). And Samson stayed in the tomb of Manoah, while Jesus walked out of his own.
