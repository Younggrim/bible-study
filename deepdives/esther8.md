*Setting:* Still in Susa, about 474 BC. Haman is dead, impaled on the stake he built for Mordecai (7:10), but his decree is not. It is still the law of the empire that on the thirteenth of Adar, about nine months away, anyone may kill the Jews and take their property (3:13). Persian law, as the book has said from the start, cannot be repealed (1:19). So the problem the story now has to solve is not a villain but a law. Chapter 8 answers it with a second decree, and it is written to mirror chapter 3 almost line for line, so that the reader sees every part of Haman's plan turned around.

#### Esther Saves the Jews (vv1–17)
- **"On that day King Ahasuerus gave to Queen Esther the house of Haman" (v1).** A traitor's property went to the crown, and the king hands it to the queen. "House" means his whole estate. Haman had planned to enrich himself and his killers by plundering the Jews (3:13). Now a Jewish woman owns everything he had. Proverbs: "the sinner's wealth is laid up for the righteous" (Prov 13:22).
- **"Mordecai came before the king, for Esther had told what he was to her" (v1).** Only now does the king learn that the man who saved his life and the queen's guardian are the same man. Esther has kept that secret for five years (2:10, 20).
- **"The king took off his signet ring, which he had taken from Haman, and gave it to Mordecai" (v2).** The same ring that sealed the death of the Jews (3:10) now goes to a Jew. Mordecai becomes chief minister, the post Haman held. **"Esther set Mordecai over the house of Haman."** He manages the estate of the man who had built a stake for him.
- **"She fell at his feet and wept and pleaded" (v3).** Esther goes in to the king again. The text does not say she was summoned, so she may again be risking her life. This time there is no banquet and no careful staging, only tears. Haman is gone, but "the evil plan of Haman the Agagite" is still in force. She calls it what it is.
- **"The king held out the golden scepter" (v4).** As in 5:2, the gesture grants permission to speak. "Esther rose and stood before the king."
- **"If it please the king… and if the thing seems right… and I am pleasing in his eyes" (v5).** Four "if" clauses, the most elaborate courtesy in the book. Esther knows she is asking for something no Persian king could easily give: to "revoke the letters devised by Haman." She is careful to blame Haman alone, not the king who sealed them.
- **"How can I bear to see the calamity that is coming to my people?" (v6).** The queen who once hid her people now cannot stand apart from them. "My people… my kindred" stands in contrast to 2:10, where she "had not made known her people or kindred."
- **"Behold, I have given Esther the house of Haman" (v7).** The king's reply sounds almost defensive: I have already done a lot. Then he admits he cannot do what she asked.
- **"An edict written in the name of the king and sealed with the king's ring cannot be revoked" (v8).** The rule that trapped Darius in the story of Daniel (Dan 6:8, 15) traps Ahasuerus too. He cannot cancel Haman's decree. But he can issue another one. **"Write as you please… in the name of the king."** He hands Mordecai and Esther the same blank authority he gave Haman (3:11).
- **"In the third month, which is the month of Sivan, on the twenty-third day" (v9).** Sivan is May–June, about two months after Haman's decree (3:12). The writing of the new edict copies the wording of chapter 3 almost exactly: scribes summoned, satraps and governors and officials, 127 provinces, every script and language. This time one phrase is added: **"and also to the Jews in their script and their language."** Haman's decree was written about the Jews; Mordecai's is written to them as well.
- **"Mounted couriers riding on swift horses… bred from the royal stud" (v10).** Two rare Persian words appear here. One means horses of the royal service and the other is usually taken to mean the offspring of royal mares. Older translations guessed "mules, camels, and young dromedaries" (KJV), and JFB follows that. Modern scholars have identified the words as Persian terms for the fastest royal post-horses. The point is speed. With the slaughter set for Adar, the news had to cross the empire fast.
- **"The king allowed the Jews… to gather and defend their lives" (v11).** The second decree does not cancel the first. It gives the Jews the legal right to organize and fight back against anyone who attacks them. That changes everything. Under the first decree, resisting would have looked like rebellion against the king. Now it is the king's own command.
- **"To destroy, to kill, and to annihilate… children and women included, and to plunder their goods" (v11).** These are again the exact words of Haman's decree (3:13), turned around. The phrase "children and women" troubles many readers. Some scholars argue that the grammar can be read as part of the description of the attackers' threat: the Jews may destroy any force "that might attack them, [their] children and women," that is, those who attack Jewish families. Others take it at face value as permission to answer like with like. Either way, chapter 9 records what the Jews actually did: they struck their armed enemies, and three times it says "they laid no hand on the plunder" (9:10, 15, 16).
- **"On one day… the thirteenth day of the twelfth month" (v12).** The same day Haman's lot picked (3:7, 13). The second decree does not change the date. It changes the outcome.
- **"The Jews were to be ready on that day to take vengeance on their enemies" (v13).** "Vengeance" (*naqam*) in the Bible often means justice carried out against wrongdoers, not personal spite. The Tyndale notes point out that it carries the sense of putting things right. It is limited here to "their enemies," those who would attack them.
- **"The couriers… rode out hurriedly, urged by the king's command" (v14).** Compare 3:15. The first time, the couriers went out "hurriedly" with a death sentence. Now they go out just as fast with a reprieve.
- **"Royal robes of blue and white, with a great golden crown" (v15).** Blue (or violet) and white were Persian royal colors, the same as the hangings at the king's feast (1:6). Mordecai, who had worn sackcloth in the street (4:1) and been led through the square once in borrowed robes (6:11), now wears his own robes of office. The word for "crown" here is not the queen's crown but a large ornamental headdress, a mark of high rank.
- **"The city of Susa shouted and rejoiced" (v15).** The reversal of 3:15, where "the city of Susa was thrown into confusion." The ordinary people of the capital had never wanted Haman's decree.
- **"The Jews had light and gladness and joy and honor" (v16).** Four words for joy, piled up as the decree had piled up three words for death. "Light" is a picture of prosperity and rescue (Ps 97:11; Isa 9:2). This verse is read aloud at Havdalah, the ceremony that ends the Sabbath in Jewish homes: "The Jews had light and gladness and joy and honor; so be it with us."
- **"A feast and a holiday" (v17).** Yet another *mishteh*. In chapter 4 there was "great mourning… fasting and weeping" in every province (4:3). Now in every province there is feasting. The day of deliverance has not even arrived yet, but the Jews celebrate already, because the king's word is out.
- **"Many from the peoples of the country declared themselves Jews" (v17).** The Hebrew verb *mityahadim* is found only here in the Old Testament. It is formed from the word "Jew," and the lexicon gives it simply as "become a Jew." Some take it as true conversion, Gentiles joining the God of Israel, as Rahab and Ruth did. Others think it means they sided with the Jews or pretended to be Jews out of fear. The text says **"fear of the Jews had fallen on them"**, the same phrase used of the nations' fear of Israel in the conquest (Exod 15:16; Josh 2:9). The Greek translation adds that they were circumcised, which reads it as full conversion.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees the "vanity of laying up treasure upon earth" in Haman's estate passing to the people he hated, and praises Mordecai's humility: he had never used his link to the queen "for any advantage of his own." Henry adds that Mordecai, who would not bow to an Amalekite, surely bowed to the king. JFB explains why the king issued "a contradictory edict" rather than cancelling the first, and says this "strange and even ridiculous" procedure was the only one Persian court rules allowed, citing Daniel 6:14. Guzik draws a gospel picture from the double decree. God's just sentence on sin was not cancelled either; it was satisfied, so that God is "just and the justifier" (Rom 3:26). He also sees in the Jews' joy before the day a picture of believers who rejoice now in a salvation not yet complete (Phil 1:6), and reads v17 as Gentiles drawn to God by what he had done for his people.

#### Pointing to Christ
The first decree could not be revoked, so a second decree had to overrule it. Scripture speaks of a similar problem: God's law against sin is just and does not change, "the wages of sin is death" (Rom 6:23). God did not erase that law. He issued a better word in his Son, in whom the sentence was carried out and the condemned were given life (Rom 8:1–4). And like the couriers rushing out from Susa, the news of that decree is meant to be carried quickly to every people "in its own language" (Rev 7:9), so that those under sentence may hear it, have "light and gladness and joy," and, like the people of the land, come over to the God who saves.
