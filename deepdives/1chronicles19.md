*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Chapter 18 surveyed David's victories on every side. This chapter zooms in on one war, against the Ammonites and their hired Aramean (Syrian) allies. 2 Samuel 10 tells the same story, and the two accounts track each other closely. The Ammonites lived east of the Jordan, on the edge of the desert. Their capital, Rabbah, lies under modern Amman, the capital of Jordan. They were distant relatives of Israel, descended from Lot (Gen 19:38), and the two peoples had a long history of conflict. The war begins with an act of kindness that is thrown back in David's face. What follows is the largest coalition David ever faced, and its defeat finally broke Aramean power south of the Euphrates. In 2 Samuel, this war is the setting for David's sin with Bathsheba, which happened while Joab besieged Rabbah. The Chronicler, focused on David as the founder of the temple and its worship, does not retell that story.

#### The Ammonites Disgrace David's Men (vv1–9)
- **"Nahash the king of the Ammonites died" (v1).** Nahash ("snake") first appears in 1 Samuel 11, besieging Jabesh-gilead and offering to spare it only if every man would have his right eye gouged out. Saul's rescue of the city launched his reign. How Nahash "dealt kindly" with David (v2) is not recorded. It may have been during David's years as a fugitive from Saul, when Saul's enemies would naturally befriend Saul's enemy. Later, during Absalom's revolt, "Shobi the son of Nahash" brought supplies to David (2 Sam 17:27–29), so at least one of Nahash's sons honored the friendship.
- **"I will deal kindly with Hanun… for his father dealt kindly with me" (v2).** "Deal kindly" is *asah hesed*, to show steadfast love, loyal kindness. This is the same *hesed* God has just promised never to take from David's son (17:13). David treats a pagan king with the covenant loyalty that God shows him. Sending condolences at a king's death was also normal diplomacy: it renewed a treaty with the new ruler.
- **"Do you think… that he is honoring your father?" (v3).** Hanun's princes see spies, not mourners. Their suspicion was not crazy. David had just defeated Moab, Ammon's neighbor to the south (18:2), and powerful kings did use embassies to gather information. But they assumed the worst, and Hanun believed them. The Hebrew piles up three verbs: "to search and to overthrow and to spy out."
- **"Shaved them and cut off their garments in the middle, at their hips" (v4).** 2 Samuel 10:4 adds that he shaved off half of each man's beard. In that world a beard was a mark of manhood and dignity. Men cut or shaved it only in deep mourning or disgrace (Isa 15:2; Jer 48:37), and Israelite men were forbidden to mar its edges (Lev 19:27). Cutting the long robes at the hips left them exposed, the way prisoners of war were humiliated (Isa 20:4). Insulting a king's envoys was insulting the king himself, an act close to a declaration of war.
- **"Remain at Jericho until your beards have grown and then return" (v5).** Jericho was the first town on the west side of the Jordan, on the road from Ammon. It had been left in ruins since Joshua and was not fully rebuilt until Ahab's day (1 Kings 16:34), so it was a quiet place to wait. David's first concern is not revenge but the dignity of his men. He spares them from walking into Jerusalem in shame.
- **"They had become a stench to David" (v6).** The verb (*ba'ash*) means "to stink," and in this form "to make oneself odious." The same phrase is used when Jacob's sons massacred Shechem (Gen 34:30) and when Israel attacked the Philistines (1 Sam 13:4). The Ammonites knew war was coming, and they knew they had brought it on themselves.
- **"1,000 talents of silver to hire chariots and horsemen" (v6).** A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this was something like thirty-five tons of silver, a huge sum for a small kingdom. Only Chronicles gives the figure. Hiring foreign troops was common practice. **Mesopotamia** here is *Aram-naharaim*, "Aram of the two rivers," the Aramean lands in the bend of the Euphrates. **Aram-maacah** was a small kingdom near Mount Hermon, and **Zobah** was Hadadezer's kingdom, already beaten once (18:3–8).
- **"They hired 32,000 chariots and the king of Maacah with his army" (v7).** 2 Samuel 10:6 lists 20,000 foot soldiers from Beth-rehob and Zobah, 12,000 men of Tob and 1,000 with the king of Maacah, 33,000 in all. The Chronicler's 32,000 is close to the Samuel total, and JFB argues that the word translated "chariots" here can cover chariotry and its men as a whole. Even so, 32,000 actual chariots would be far more than any ancient army fielded. At the battle of Qarqar in 853 BC, a coalition of a dozen kings, including Ahab, mustered about 4,000. Most scholars therefore read the figure as a total of troops, or think the numbers have been affected by copying.
- **"Encamped before Medeba" (v7).** Only Chronicles names the place. Medeba (modern Madaba in Jordan, famous for its sixth-century mosaic map of the Holy Land) sits on the plateau north of the Arnon, in land Israel claimed (Josh 13:9, 16). The Tyndale notes suggest the alliance was challenging David's hold on this plateau, which he had secured by defeating Moab.
- **"He sent Joab and all the army of the mighty men" (v8).** David stays in Jerusalem and sends his commander with the standing army of professional soldiers, the "mighty men" (11:10–47).
- **"At the entrance of the city… the kings… in the open country" (v9).** The Ammonites stayed close to their city gate, where they could retreat behind walls, while the hired Aramean chariot forces stood in the open plain where chariots could maneuver. Joab marched in between and found himself facing enemies in front and behind.
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that David's kindness was real and active, not just a feeling, and that the Ammonite princes may have suspected David because they were schemers themselves. He reads the order to wait at Jericho as concern for the men's dignity, not a political move to stir anger. Guzik also applies v6: the Ammonites, not David, caused the breach. JFB calls the treatment of the envoys "the grossest indignity," and on v7 argues that, read as riders rather than chariots, the figure "agrees exactly" with 2 Samuel 10:6. That is too neat, since Samuel's total is 33,000, but the closeness of the numbers is real. JFB places Medeba on the frontier "on the Arnon," while it actually stood some miles north of that river.

#### Ammonites and Syrians Defeated (vv10–19)
- **"He chose some of the best men of Israel and arrayed them against the Syrians" (v10).** Joab splits his army. He takes the elite troops against the Arameans, the larger and more dangerous force, and gives the rest to his brother Abishai to hold off the Ammonites. It is a risky plan. If either half broke, the other would be surrounded.
- **"If the Syrians are too strong for me, then you shall help me" (v12).** The two brothers pledge to come to each other's aid. Joab's plan depends on trust, not just numbers.
- **"Be strong, and let us use our strength for our people and for the cities of our God" (v13).** Literally "Be strong and let us show ourselves strong." Joab gives two reasons to fight: "our people" and "the cities of our God." The land is God's, and the war is a defense of his inheritance. The Tyndale notes point out that the aim was to protect Israel's people and towns, not to gain territory.
- **"And may the LORD do what seems good to him" (v13).** This is one of the best lines anyone speaks in David's wars, and it comes from Joab, a hard and often violent man. He does all he can and leaves the result to God. Eli said something similar when he heard God's judgment (1 Sam 3:18), and David said the same when he fled from Absalom (2 Sam 15:26).
- **"They fled before him" (v14).** The text does not describe a long fight. The Arameans broke as soon as Joab advanced, and the Ammonites, seeing their allies run, fled into their city (v15). God had promised that enemies would "come out against you one way and flee before you seven ways" (Deut 28:7). Joab did not besiege Rabbah yet, perhaps because the season was late. He went home, and the city's fall comes in 20:1–3.
- **"The Syrians who were beyond the Euphrates" (v16).** Hadadezer was not finished. He called in Aramean forces from the far side of the Euphrates, his eastern allies or vassals. Their commander was **Shophach** (Shobach in 2 Sam 10:16). Samuel names the battlefield as Helam, probably in northern Transjordan. Chronicles does not give the place.
- **"He gathered all Israel together and crossed the Jordan" (v17).** This time David leads in person. "All Israel" is one of the Chronicler's favorite phrases. The whole nation stands with its king.
- **"The men of 7,000 chariots and 40,000 foot soldiers" (v18).** 2 Samuel 10:18 has "700 chariots, and 40,000 horsemen." The two texts differ by a factor of ten on the chariots and differ on whether the 40,000 were horsemen or foot soldiers. As in 18:4, Hebrew numbers were easy to miscopy, and the original figures cannot now be recovered with certainty. Both accounts agree on the outcome: a crushing defeat and the death of the enemy commander.
- **"They made peace with David and became subject to him" (v19).** Hadadezer's vassals switched sides and became David's vassals. The great Aramean alliance was broken, and the Ammonites now stood alone. The chapter's last line has a quiet irony: "the Syrians were not willing to *save* the Ammonites anymore." Hired help fails. Back in 18:6, 13, it was the LORD who "saved" (gave victory to) David.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws three lessons from Joab's speech: courage is chosen, especially when God supplies strength (Eph 6:10); the people and cities of God were at stake; and Joab prepared fully and left the outcome to the LORD. He warns, following F. B. Meyer, that trusting God to do everything can become an excuse for doing nothing. Guzik also looks ahead to 2 Samuel 11, where the next spring David stayed home and fell into sin with Bathsheba. JFB praises Joab's "faith and piety" and on v18 admits frankly that "either the text in one of the books is corrupt" or the two accounts must be combined. Henry, who treats the chapter only briefly, draws the lesson of v19: "The only safety of sinners consists in submitting to the Lord, seeking peace with him, and becoming his servants." He also applies Joab and Abishai's pact: "Let us assist each other in a good cause."

#### Pointing to Christ
David sent kindness and it was met with suspicion and insult. His messengers were stripped and shamed, and the insult to them was an insult to him. The pattern runs through Scripture: God sent servants and prophets, and they were mistreated, and last of all he sent his Son (Mark 12:1–8). Jesus was stripped, mocked and shamed, the King's own messenger treated like a captive, and still he came with mercy (Phil 2:7–8; Heb 12:2). Joab's words, "may the LORD do what seems good to him," find their deepest form in Gethsemane: "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). And the end of the chapter echoes Henry's lesson. The Aramean kings found safety only by making peace with David and serving him. There is safety for sinners in making peace with God's King, and he has made that peace himself "by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20).
