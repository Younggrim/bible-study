*Setting:* Galilee, around AD 28, continuing the run of miracles that began after the Sermon on the Mount. Chapter 8 ended on the eastern shore, where a Gentile town begged Jesus to leave after he freed two men from demons. He now crosses back to Capernaum. Chapter 9 adds more miracles, but the mood shifts: for the first time, religious leaders object openly. Scribes call him a blasphemer, Pharisees grumble at his dinner guests, even John's disciples question him, and the chapter ends with the charge that he works by the devil's power. Woven through it is the call of Matthew himself, the tax collector who wrote this Gospel. The chapter closes where 4:23 began this section, with Jesus teaching, preaching and healing, and moved with compassion for the crowds.

#### Jesus Heals a Paralytic (vv1–8)
- **"His own city" (v1).** Capernaum, where Jesus had made his home after leaving Nazareth (4:13). Mark tells this story at more length: the crowd was so thick that the man's four friends dug through the roof and lowered him down (Mark 2:1–12). Matthew trims it to the essentials.
- **"When Jesus saw their faith" (v2).** "Their" includes the friends who carried him. Faith here is something visible, the kind that gets up and does something. Faith on someone else's behalf matters to Jesus.
- **"Take heart, my son; your sins are forgiven" (v2).** The man came for his legs and Jesus speaks first to his sins. That is not because every illness is caused by a particular sin (John 9:2–3). It is because the man's deepest need was the one no one could see. "My son" is tender. "Take heart" (*tharseō*) is what Jesus says to the frightened disciples on the water (14:27).
- **"This man is blaspheming" (v3).** The scribes reason correctly in one way: only God can forgive sins against God (Isa 43:25; Ps 103:3). A man could forgive a wrong done to himself, but Jesus forgives as if he were the one sinned against. Either the claim is blasphemy, or Jesus is who he implies. There is no middle ground.
- **"Jesus, knowing their thoughts" (v4).** The scribes "said to themselves" (v3), but Jesus answers aloud. Knowing hearts is also God's prerogative (1 Sam 16:7; Jer 17:10).
- **"Which is easier…?" (v5).** Saying "your sins are forgiven" is easier, since no one can check. Saying "rise and walk" is harder, because everyone will see whether it happens. So Jesus does the visible thing to prove the invisible one.
- **"That you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (v6).** "Authority" (*exousia*) picks up the word the crowds used after the sermon (7:29). The Son of Man of Daniel 7 receives authority in heaven. Jesus says he already exercises it "on earth."
- **"They glorified God, who had given such authority to men" (v8).** The plural "men" may simply be the crowd's way of speaking, or Matthew may hint that this authority is passing to the church Jesus will found (16:19; 18:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry links sin and sickness: "As sin is the cause of sickness, so the remission of sin is the comfort of recovery from sickness." He means sin in general as the root of human suffering, not that this man's illness was a punishment. Guzik notes that the scribes rightly saw that Jesus was claiming what only God can do, and wrongly concluded that he was not God. JFB has no notes on this section in Matthew, referring readers to Mark.

#### Jesus Calls Matthew (vv9–13)
- **"A man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth" (v9).** Capernaum lay on a main road near the border between the territories of Herod Antipas and his brother Philip, and goods crossing the border paid tolls. Matthew probably collected these customs duties for Antipas. Tax rights were sold to the highest bidder, and the collector kept whatever he could get above it, so the job invited extortion. Jews saw tax collectors as traitors and unclean, and lumped them with "sinners," people openly outside the law.
- **Matthew and Levi.** Mark and Luke call him Levi (Mark 2:14; Luke 5:27). Many Jews had two names (Simon Peter, Saul Paul), and "Matthew" may be the name he took or was given. It probably means "gift of the LORD." Only this Gospel calls him "Matthew the tax collector" in the list of apostles (10:3), a humble touch if he is the author, as early church tradition holds.
- **"Follow me." And he rose and followed him (v9).** The fishermen could go back to their boats, and in John 21 some do. A man who walked away from a tax booth could not easily get his post back. Luke adds "leaving everything" (Luke 5:28). A man used to keeping records was an apt person to write a careful Gospel.
- **"Tax collectors and sinners came and were reclining with Jesus" (v10).** Luke says Matthew threw a great feast in his house (Luke 5:29). Eating together meant acceptance and friendship. Matthew's first act as a disciple is to introduce his friends to Jesus.
- **"Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?" (v11).** The Pharisees, a lay movement devoted to strict keeping of the law and its purity rules, ask the disciples, not Jesus. To them, sharing a table with sinners was to share their uncleanness.
- **"Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick" (v12).** The answer is pointed. Jesus is a doctor making house calls. The Pharisees thought they were well, which was their problem.
- **"Go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice'" (v13).** "Go and learn" was a phrase rabbis used to send a student back to study a text, which is sharp irony toward experts in the Law. The quotation is Hosea 6:6. Hosea did not mean God rejected the sacrifices he commanded, but that ritual without love and faithfulness is worthless (1 Sam 15:22; Mic 6:6–8). The Hebrew word behind "mercy" is *hesed*, steadfast covenant love. Jesus quotes the verse again in 12:7. The Pharisees were careful about purity and careless about mercy.
- **"I came not to call the righteous, but sinners" (v13).** "The righteous" here means those who thought themselves righteous. No one is truly well (Rom 3:10). The call is open to anyone who will admit to being sick.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of Matthew's call, "He is found of those that seek him not." Guzik thinks Matthew gave up more than the fishermen did, since he could not go back, and suggests he may once have taxed the very fishermen he now followed, as fish caught in the lake were taxed. JFB argues that the feast happened some time after the call, since Matthew places Jairus's request during it (v18) while Mark and Luke place that later. Either way, Matthew joins them by theme.

#### A Question About Fasting (vv14–17)
- **"The disciples of John" (v14).** John the Baptist was in prison by now (4:12). His followers kept up strict habits of fasting, and many Pharisees fasted twice a week (Luke 18:12), on Mondays and Thursdays. The law required only one fast a year, on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29–31). Fasting was a sign of mourning and repentance, and for some, of longing for the Messiah to come.
- **"Can the wedding guests mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them?" (v15).** Jewish weddings could last a week of feasting, and guests were excused from religious fasts during it. Fasting at a wedding would be absurd. The Old Testament pictures God as Israel's husband (Isa 54:5; 62:5; Hos 2:19–20), and John had called Jesus the bridegroom (John 3:29). The point: the Messiah is here, so this is a time for joy.
- **"The bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast" (v15).** "Taken away" is a quiet, early hint of a violent end, echoing the servant who was "taken away" (Isa 53:8). It is the first hint in Matthew of his death. After the resurrection the church did fast (Acts 13:2–3; 14:23), not to bring the Messiah but while waiting for him to return.
- **"Unshrunk cloth on an old garment" (v16).** A patch of new, unwashed cloth shrinks the first time it gets wet, pulls on the old fabric and makes the tear worse.
- **"New wine… old wineskins" (v17).** Wine was stored in goatskins. New wine is still fermenting and gives off gas, which stretches the skin. A fresh skin stretches with it. An old one, already stretched and brittle, bursts, and both wine and skin are lost.
- **What the pictures mean.** Jesus is not patching up the old ways or pouring himself into the old forms. What he brings is new and needs new forms. That does not mean the Old Testament is thrown away, since he came to fulfill it (5:17), and Matthew alone adds "so both are preserved." But the coming of the kingdom changes how God's people live, as the church learned over questions like food laws and circumcision (Acts 15).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the bridegroom as Christ, with fasting suited to seasons of his absence. He applies the wineskins pastorally: "Young beginners in religion must not be put upon the hardest duties at first, lest they be discouraged." Guzik takes the images more broadly, as Jesus bringing a new covenant rather than repairing Judaism, and likens fulfilment to an acorn growing into an oak. JFB has no notes on this section in Matthew, referring readers to Luke.

#### A Girl Restored to Life and a Woman Healed (vv18–26)
- **"A ruler came in and knelt before him" (v18).** Mark and Luke name him Jairus, a leader of the synagogue, the man who arranged its services (Mark 5:22). He was a respected figure, probably in Capernaum, where Jesus had just been criticized by religious leaders. Kneeling to Jesus took courage.
- **"My daughter has just died" (v18).** Mark and Luke say she was dying when he came and that messengers brought news of her death on the way (Mark 5:23, 35). Matthew, as usual, compresses the story and gives the father's request in its final form. His faith is striking: "come and lay your hand on her, and she will live."
- **"A woman who had suffered from a discharge of blood for twelve years" (v20).** Under the law, a woman with ongoing bleeding was unclean, and so was anything or anyone she touched (Lev 15:25–27). For twelve years she had been cut off from worship and from ordinary contact. Mark adds that she had spent everything on doctors and only got worse (Mark 5:26). The girl was twelve years old (Mark 5:42). The woman had suffered as long as the girl had lived.
- **"The fringe of his garment" (v20).** The law told Israelite men to wear tassels on the four corners of their cloak to remind them of God's commandments (Num 15:37–41; Deut 22:12). Jesus wore them like any observant Jew. The woman came from behind, hoping no one would notice. Later many others touch these same tassels and are healed (14:36).
- **"If I only touch his garment" (v21).** Her faith may have had a touch of superstition in it, as if the cloth had power. Jesus does not reject her for that. He meets her and draws her faith to himself.
- **"Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well" (v22).** He calls her "daughter." In all three accounts of this healing, she is the only person the Gospels show him addressing that way. "Made you well" (*sōzō*) is the ordinary word for "save." The healing reaches past her body. Instead of her uncleanness passing to Jesus, his wholeness passes to her.
- **"The flute players and the crowd making a commotion" (v23).** Mourning was loud and public. Families hired flute players and professional wailing women, and the Mishnah says that even the poorest man should hire at least two flutes and one wailing woman for his wife's funeral.
- **"The girl is not dead but sleeping" (v24).** Jesus is not saying she only fainted. They knew she was dead, which is why they laughed. He means that for him, death is like sleep, something he can wake her from (John 11:11–14). The New Testament speaks of believers who have died as "asleep" for the same reason (1 Thess 4:13–14).
- **"He went in and took her by the hand, and the girl arose" (v25).** Touching a corpse made a person unclean (Num 19:11). Again Jesus touches, and life flows the other way. Elijah and Elisha raised dead children with long effort and prayer (1 Kings 17:17–24; 2 Kings 4:32–37). Jesus takes her hand.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls death a sleep for believers, a short rest before resurrection, and thinks the woman received spiritual as well as bodily healing. Guzik thinks the ruler's faith was smaller than the centurion's, since he believed Jesus had to come and touch her. He also stresses that the woman's faith was imperfect, but what mattered was that it was in the right person. He notes that the mourners' quick laughter shows how hired grief could turn. JFB comments on this event under Mark.

#### Jesus Heals Two Blind Men (vv27–31)
- **"Have mercy on us, Son of David" (v27).** This is the first time anyone in Matthew calls Jesus "Son of David," the title the Gospel opened with (1:1). It meant the promised king from David's line (2 Sam 7:12–16). Isaiah had said that when God came to save, "the eyes of the blind shall be opened" (Isa 35:5). The blind men see more clearly than the leaders who can see.
- **"When he entered the house" (v28).** Jesus does not answer them in the street. He lets them follow him indoors, perhaps to avoid stirring up crowds around a royal title.
- **"Do you believe that I am able to do this?" (v28).** He asks about faith in *him*, not in healing in general. Their answer is short and complete: "Yes, Lord."
- **"According to your faith let it be done to you" (v29).** This does not mean the strength of their faith set the size of the miracle. It means their trust in him would not be disappointed, as with the centurion (8:13).
- **"See that no one knows about it." But they went away and spread his fame (vv30–31).** "Sternly warned" is a strong word, almost "snorted," expressing deep feeling. As with the leper (8:4), Jesus wanted to avoid being seen mainly as a wonder-worker or a political king. Their disobedience was understandable, but it was still disobedience, and it made his work harder.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that this miracle appears only in Matthew and connects "Son of David" with Isaiah 35:5. Henry sees Jesus requiring a confession of faith in his power and reads the call to silence as a lesson in humility. Guzik, following D.A. Carson, thinks Jesus waited until they were indoors to cool messianic excitement after two public miracles.

#### Jesus Heals a Man Unable to Speak (vv32–34)
- **"A demon-oppressed man who was mute" (v32).** The Greek word (*kōphos*) can mean deaf, mute or both. Here the cause was demonic, not physical, though the Bible does not treat all such conditions as demonic. Others brought him, since he could not ask.
- **"The mute man spoke" (v33).** Isaiah's promise continues: "the tongue of the mute sing for joy" (Isa 35:6). Matthew has now shown Jesus doing the very things he later lists for John the Baptist: the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, and the dead are raised (11:5).
- **"Never was anything like this seen in Israel" (v33).** The crowds sense that something unprecedented is happening.
- **"He casts out demons by the prince of demons" (v34).** The Pharisees cannot deny what happened, so they question where his power comes from. This is the first sign of a charge that comes back in full in 12:24, where Jesus answers it with a solemn warning about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. The same works that move the crowds harden the leaders. (A few manuscripts lack v34, but most scholars think it belongs.)
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus' cures remove the cause and not just the symptom, and calls the Pharisees' charge diabolical. JFB calls it "the first muttering" of a slander that later became their standard line. Guzik reports that some rabbis believed an exorcist had to learn a demon's name, so a mute man seemed beyond help, which would explain why the crowd was so amazed.

#### The Harvest Is Plentiful, the Laborers Few (vv35–38)
- **"Teaching… proclaiming… healing" (v35).** This verse repeats 4:23 almost word for word. The two verses bracket chapters 5–9: Jesus' words (chapters 5–7) and his works (chapters 8–9). The next chapter sends the Twelve out to do the same.
- **"He had compassion for them" (v36).** The verb (*splanchnizomai*) comes from the word for the inner organs, the gut, the place where people felt deep emotion. It is a strong word. The lexicon notes that it hardly appears in Greek before the New Testament, and some scholars think it was coined among Greek-speaking Jews. In the Gospels it is used almost only of Jesus, or of figures in his parables who stand for God (18:27; Luke 15:20).
- **"Harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (v36).** "Harassed" (*skyllō*) originally meant flayed or torn, and came to mean troubled or worn out. "Helpless" is literally "thrown down," like sheep lying exhausted. "Sheep without a shepherd" is an Old Testament phrase for a leaderless people (Num 27:17; 1 Kings 22:17). Ezekiel 34 blames Israel's shepherds for scattering the flock and promises that God himself will shepherd them through a coming David. The crowds' religious leaders had loaded them with burdens instead of feeding them (23:4).
- **"The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few" (v37).** The picture changes from sheep to fields. In the prophets, harvest often stands for the final judgment (Joel 3:13), but here it means people ready to be gathered into the kingdom (John 4:35).
- **"Pray earnestly to the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers" (v38).** The harvest belongs to God, and so does the sending. "Send out" (*ekballō*) is the same verb used for casting out demons in vv33–34, a forceful word, almost "thrust out." The disciples are told to pray for workers, and in the very next chapter they become the answer to their own prayer.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws out the strength of the compassion word, passing on Spurgeon's claim that the Gospel writers coined it. The lexicon partly supports this: it notes a scholarly suggestion that the verb was coined among Greek-speaking Jews. Guzik also points to the forceful verb in v38. So does JFB, which renders it "thrust forth" and sees the field as extending to the whole world. Henry urges prayer that God would send workers into his harvest.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter shows the authority of Jesus reaching places no one else could reach: the guilt of a paralyzed man, the table of tax collectors, the twelve-year exclusion of a bleeding woman, the death of a little girl, the darkness of the blind and the silence of the mute. In every case his holiness is not threatened by uncleanness. It overcomes it. He is the physician who came for the sick, the bridegroom whose presence turns fasting into feasting, the Son of David who opens blind eyes, and the shepherd who looks on harassed crowds with compassion. The bridegroom would be "taken away" (v15), and at the cross he would carry the sins he here forgave with a word.
