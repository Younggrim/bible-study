*Setting:* Psalm 141 is the second of the five laments (Psalms 140–144) in the last collection of David's psalms. Its heading says simply "A Psalm of David." JFB notes that its structure and content mark it as by the same author as Psalm 140, and the two share the same pictures of traps and snares (140:5; 141:9). But the danger has shifted. In Psalm 140 the threat came from outside: violent men and their poisoned tongues. In Psalm 141 David is just as worried about what is inside: his own mouth, his own heart and the pull of joining the wicked and enjoying their pleasures. JFB calls it "a prayer for deliverance from sins to which affliction tempted him, and from the enemies who caused it." Henry and many others place it in the years David spent running from Saul, when he was cut off from the tabernacle and its worship. That would explain why he asks for his prayer to be counted as incense and his raised hands as the evening offering. The early church used this psalm in its evening prayers for that reason, and some churches still sing it at evening services. Verses 5–7 are among the hardest lines in the Psalms to translate, and the ESV's reading is one of several possibilities. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### Let My Prayer Be Counted as Incense (vv1–2)
- **"O LORD, I call upon you; hasten to me!" (v1).** The opening is urgent. "Hasten" is a common cry in the Psalms (Ps 22:19; 38:22; 70:1, 5). David does not ask God to come eventually. He asks him to come now.
- **"Let my prayer be counted as incense before you" (v2).** In the tabernacle, the priest burned incense on the golden altar every morning and every evening, in front of the curtain that hid the ark (Exod 30:7–8). The fragrant smoke rose before God. "Be counted" (*tikkon*) means be set in place, established, accepted. The KJV has "set forth." David asks that his words rise to God the way the incense rose.
- **"And the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice!" (v2).** "Evening sacrifice" (*minchat-'arev*) is literally the evening offering. Every evening a lamb was offered with a grain offering (Exod 29:38–41). Over time, "the time of the evening offering" became a set hour of prayer. Elijah prayed at that hour on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18:36), Ezra fell on his knees then (Ezra 9:5), and Gabriel came to Daniel then (Dan 9:21). By New Testament times it was "the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1), about three in the afternoon. Lifting the hands was the posture of prayer (Ps 28:2; 134:2; 1 Tim 2:8).
- **Prayer instead of sacrifice?** The study notes suggest David did not have incense or an offering at hand, so he asks that his prayer be what those things stood for. This does not mean the psalm rejects the temple rituals. It means prayer from the heart was what the rituals were meant to express all along (Ps 51:16–17; Hos 14:2). Revelation shows the heavenly picture: "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (Rev 5:8; 8:3–4).
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "Prayer is a spiritual sacrifice; it is the offering up of the soul." Guzik makes the same link between incense and prayer and adds that incense needs fire to rise, as prayer needs fervor (Jas 5:16). He passes on Trapp's report, from John Chrysostom, that the Greek church sang this psalm in its evening services because of v2.

#### Set a Guard, O LORD, over My Mouth (vv3–4)
- **"Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch over the door of my lips!" (v3).** After the enemies' poisoned tongues in Psalm 140, David now prays about his own. "Door" (*dal*) is the word for a door or the leaf of a door. David asks God to stand like a gatekeeper at his lips, deciding what may go out. Under pressure and provocation, the temptation to answer back in kind is strong. James says, "If anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man" (Jas 3:2). Proverbs says the same: "Whoever guards his mouth preserves his life" (Prov 13:3; 21:23). The study notes observe that wisdom sometimes requires protection from oneself.
- **"Do not let my heart incline to any evil" (v4).** The prayer moves from the mouth to the heart, where speech comes from (Matt 12:34). David knows he is not immune. The pressure of persecution can push a good man toward bitterness, or toward joining the side that is winning.
- **"To busy myself with wicked deeds in company with men who work iniquity" (v4).** "Men" here (*'ishim*) may suggest men of rank and power. VanGemeren, as Guzik reports, takes it this way. The temptation is to throw in with the influential people who do evil. Psalm 1:1 warns of the same path: walking, standing and finally sitting with the wicked.
- **"And let me not eat of their delicacies!" (v4).** "Delicacies" (*man'ammim*) occurs only here. It comes from the same root as "pleasant" (*na'im*) in Psalm 133:1 and 135:3. It means tasty food, the rich dishes of the powerful. Eating at someone's table meant sharing their friendship and their side. Daniel refused the Babylonian king's food for a similar reason (Dan 1:8). The delicacies stand for the comforts and rewards of compromise.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv3–4 as a prayer to be kept from sin in word, in inclination and in the pleasures of sin, "their dainties." Guzik observes that David seems to fear his own corruption more than his enemies. He passes on Kidner's point that if God's house needed doorkeepers, so do the lips of a godly person, and Maclaren's observation that David dreads catching the sin of the wicked more than suffering their hostility.

#### Let a Righteous Man Strike Me (vv5–7)
- **"Let a righteous man strike me—it is a kindness" (v5).** David would rather be corrected by a good man than flattered by bad ones. "Strike" (*halam*) is a strong word, used of a hammer blow (Judg 5:26). But it is "a kindness" (*chesed*), the same word as "steadfast love." Proverbs says, "Faithful are the wounds of a friend; profuse are the kisses of an enemy" (Prov 27:6). David received such rebukes: from Nathan after his sin with Bathsheba (2 Sam 12:7), and earlier from Abigail, who kept him from taking bloody revenge on Nabal (1 Sam 25:32–33). The study notes say wisdom opens itself to correction from godly people (Prov 9:8; 15:31).
- **"Let him rebuke me—it is oil for my head; let my head not refuse it" (v5).** Oil on the head was a sign of honor and refreshment for a guest (Ps 23:5; 133:2). The delicacies of the wicked look like honor but corrupt. The rebuke of the righteous looks like a blow but heals.
- **"Yet my prayer is continually against their evil deeds" (v5).** David accepts correction from the righteous but keeps praying against the deeds of the wicked. The Hebrew of this line is difficult, and Guzik passes on Maclaren's admission that no fully satisfying explanation has been found. The ESV makes good sense of it.
- **"When their judges are thrown over the cliff" (v6).** This verse is very hard. The Hebrew is literally something like "their judges are let go by the hands of the rock." The ESV takes it as the leaders of the wicked being hurled from a cliff, a known form of execution (2 Chron 25:12; Luke 4:29). Others take "the hands of the rock" differently, or read it as the judges being "released into the power of" their judgment. Some older readers, including Henry, see a hopeful sense: once the persecutors are humbled, they will finally listen to David.
- **"Then they shall hear my words, for they are pleasant" (v6).** "Pleasant" (*na'emu*) is again the root of "delicacies" in v4. The play on words seems deliberate: the wicked offer sweet food that corrupts; David offers words that are truly sweet. Henry says, "When the world is bitter the word is sweet." Spurgeon, as Guzik reports, thinks of Saul's death, after which Israel turned to David and listened to him.
- **"As when one plows and breaks up the earth, so shall our bones be scattered at the mouth of Sheol" (v7).** The picture is of soil broken up by a plow, or of wood split and scattered, and of bones lying unburied at the entrance to the grave. Being left unburied was one of the worst disgraces in the ancient world (Jer 8:1–2). The ESV follows the Hebrew "our bones." The Greek translation and some modern translations read "their bones," making the verse about the enemies' fate. In the Hebrew reading, David describes how close he and his companions are to death. Sheol, the realm of the dead, seems to have its mouth open.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls reproof "an excellent oil" that heals rather than breaks the head, and says "we must account it a piece of friendship." He takes v7 as David's people scattered "at the grave's mouth," like chips of wood. Guzik admits these verses are hard to translate. He passes on Trapp's note that "strike" suggests a hammer blow, and Spurgeon's contrast between the cruelty of flattery and the kindness of a faithful rebuke. JFB's commentary on this psalm gives only an introductory note, no verse comments.

#### My Eyes Are Toward You (vv8–10)
- **"But my eyes are toward you, O GOD, my Lord" (v8).** After the dark picture of scattered bones, David turns his eyes away from the danger and toward God. "GOD" in small capitals is *YHWH* and "Lord" is *'Adonai*, the same pairing as in Psalm 140:7. Jehoshaphat prayed in the same way, facing a vast army: "We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you" (2 Chron 20:12; Ps 123:1–2).
- **"In you I seek refuge; leave me not defenseless!" (v8).** "Leave me not defenseless" is literally "do not pour out my life" (*'al te'ar nafshi*). The verb (*'arah*) means to empty, to lay bare. It is the same verb the Edomites used in Psalm 137:7, "Lay it bare, lay it bare." It is also the verb Isaiah uses of the Servant of the LORD, who "poured out his soul to death" (Isa 53:12). David asks that his life not be poured out and left exposed.
- **"Keep me from the trap that they have laid for me and from the snares of evildoers!" (v9).** The hunting images of Psalm 140:5 return. David cannot see every trap, so he asks God to keep him from them.
- **"Let the wicked fall into their own nets, while I pass by safely" (v10).** The study notes point out that this principle of retribution ties the psalm together: evil returns on those who plan it (Ps 7:15–16; 9:15–16; Prov 26:27). The last word is David walking past the traps unharmed. Guzik notes Kidner's description of the line as buoyant, like a man who has slipped through many nets. Haman built a gallows for Mordecai and was hanged on it himself (Esth 7:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv8–10 as David's trust in God for comfort and rescue, and his prayer that the enemies be caught in their own schemes. Guzik links v8 to David's words to Saul in 1 Samuel 26:19, and passes on Maclaren's point that the psalm's last focus is not on the enemies' destruction but on David's own safe passage.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 141 prays for a guarded mouth, a heart kept from evil and a prayer that rises like incense. Jesus is the one in whom that prayer was fully answered. He was tempted in every way, "yet without sin" (Heb 4:15). Under the worst provocation "he committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22). He refused the delicacies of compromise: Satan offered him the kingdoms of the world, and he would not bow (Matt 4:8–10). On the cross he died at about the ninth hour (Matt 27:46–50), the very hour of the evening offering, offering himself as "a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2). And where David prayed "do not pour out my life," Jesus did pour out his soul to death (Isa 53:12), so that his people would not be left defenseless. Now he lives to intercede for them, and their prayers rise before God like incense because they come through him (Heb 7:25; Rev 8:3–4).
