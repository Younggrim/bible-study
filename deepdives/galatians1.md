*Setting:* Galatians may be Paul's earliest surviving letter, and it is certainly his angriest. "Galatia" could mean the old ethnic territory in central Turkey around Ancyra (modern Ankara), or the larger Roman province that reached south to Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra and Derbe, the towns Paul and Barnabas evangelized on the first missionary journey (Acts 13–14). Many scholars favor those southern churches and date the letter around AD 48–49, just before the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15). Others favor the northern region and a date in the mid-50s. Either way the crisis is the same. After Paul left, teachers arrived (usually called Judaizers) who told Gentile believers that faith in Jesus was a good start, but to belong fully to God's people they must also be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. They also cast doubt on Paul himself as a second-hand apostle who had trimmed the real message. Chapter 1 answers both attacks: there is only one gospel, and Paul received it from Christ himself, not from men.

#### Greeting (vv1–5)
- **"Paul, an apostle—not from men nor through man" (v1).** Jesus is set against "man" and beside the Father as the source of Paul's authority, a high claim made almost in passing. An apostle is an authorized envoy, sent with the authority of the one who sends him. Paul denies two things at once: his commission did not *originate* with any human group ("from men"), and it did not come *through* a human go-between ("through man"), not even the Jerusalem leaders. Ananias laid hands on him (Acts 9:17), but the call came from the risen Lord on the Damascus road. Paul defends his office in the first line because his opponents had attacked it.
- **"Who raised him from the dead" (v1).** This is the only greeting in Paul's letters that mentions the resurrection. It matters here because Paul was commissioned by the *risen* Christ. The Twelve knew Jesus before Easter. Paul met him only after it, and that was no less real an appointment.
- **"To the churches of Galatia" (v2).** The address is bare. Elsewhere Paul calls his readers "saints," "beloved," or "the church of God." Here there is no praise at all, which already signals that something is wrong.
- **"Grace to you and peace" (v3).** Paul's usual greeting blends the Greek *charis* (grace) with the Hebrew *shalom* (peace). In this letter it carries an edge, since the Galatians are in danger of trading grace for law (5:4).
- **"Who gave himself for our sins" (v4).** A one-line summary of the gospel. Christ was not merely handed over by others. He *gave himself*. The preposition is *hyper*, "on behalf of, for the sake of."
- **"To deliver us from the present evil age" (v4).** Jews of the time spoke of two ages: "this age," marked by sin and death, and "the age to come," when God would put things right. Paul says the rescue has already begun. The verb *exaireō* means to pluck out or rescue. It is the word Stephen uses for God rescuing Joseph and Israel from Egypt (Acts 7:10, 34). Believers still live *in* the present age, but they no longer belong to it. That is why going back under the law is so serious later in the letter: it is a return to the old age Christ came to rescue them from (4:3, 9).
- **"To whom be the glory forever and ever. Amen" (v5).** A doxology in the opening lines is unusual. If salvation is all God's work, all the glory is his. Any gospel that adds human works to Christ's work takes some of that glory for itself.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that grace comes before peace: "there can be no true peace without grace." He sees the world as "evil" through sin and temptation, and says Christ's rescue usually works by breaking the world's hold on believers, not by removing them from it at once. Guzik makes the same point: deliverance from the *power* of this age now, from its *presence* later. Guzik also says *hyper* in v4 carries the idea of substitution. The lexicon gives its basic sense as "on behalf of" and lists a substitutionary "instead of" sense for other verses (such as 3:13), not for this one. The substitutionary meaning is clearer in 3:13 ("becoming a curse for us") than here. JFB says Paul's commission "needs now to be vindicated against deniers of it," and describes the present age as one that opposes God's glory and lies "under the authority of the Evil One" (compare 1 John 5:19).

#### No Other Gospel (vv6–10)
- **"I am astonished" (v6).** In every other letter to a church, Paul follows the greeting with thanksgiving or praise (Rom 1:8; 1 Cor 1:4; Phil 1:3). Here there is none. He goes straight to rebuke. "I am astonished that…" was also a stock phrase in ancient letters for expressing disappointment, so the original readers would have heard the reproach at once.
- **"So quickly deserting" (v6).** "So quickly" may mean soon after their conversion, or how fast they gave way once the new teachers arrived. "Deserting" translates *metatithēmi*, which in the middle voice means to change sides or go over to another. In 2 Maccabees 7:24 it is used of a tyrant urging a Jewish youth to abandon his ancestral faith. The present tense ("are deserting") shows it is still happening. They have not finished the move yet, and Paul writes to stop it.
- **"Him who called you in the grace of Christ" (v6).** This is the crucial point. They are not just changing doctrines. They are turning away from *God*, who called them. To leave grace is to leave the one who gives it.
- **"A different gospel—not that there is another one" (vv6–7).** Paul uses two Greek words: *heteros* ("different") in v6 and *allos* ("another") in v7. The traditional explanation is that *heteros* means another of a different kind, and *allos* another of the same kind. The lexicon allows that *heteros* can mean different in kind, but also notes that in later Greek the two words often overlap. So the point should rest on what Paul plainly says, not on a fine distinction between the words. The "different gospel" is not a second option. It is not good news at all.
- **"Some who trouble you" (v7).** *Tarassō* means to stir up, agitate or unsettle. The same word describes the effect of the Judaizers in the Jerusalem Council letter (Acts 15:24).
- **"Want to distort the gospel of Christ" (v7).** *Metastrephō* means to turn around or reverse. In Acts 2:20 the sun is "turned" to darkness. Adding the law as a requirement does not improve the gospel. It turns it into its opposite, from God's gift into something earned.
- **"Even if we or an angel from heaven" (v8).** The test of a message is not the messenger. Paul includes himself: if he ever changed his preaching, the Galatians should reject him.
- **"Let him be accursed" (vv8–9).** The word is *anathema*. In the Greek Old Testament it translates the Hebrew *ḥerem*, something devoted to God for destruction, like the spoil of Jericho (Josh 6:17; Lev 27:28–29). This is not just strong language. Paul is handing such a person over to God's judgment. He says it twice, deliberately ("As we have said before, so now I say again").
- **"Contrary to the one you received" (v9).** "Received" is the technical word for a tradition passed on and accepted (1 Cor 15:1–3). The Galatians are not being asked to test a new idea. They already have the gospel, and the measure is what they first received.
- **"Am I now seeking the approval of man, or of God?" (v10).** The opponents seem to have said that Paul dropped circumcision to make the gospel easier for Gentiles to accept. The curse he has just pronounced is his answer, since no one trying to please people talks like that.
- **"If I were still trying to please man" (v10).** "Still" looks back to his life before Christ, when he sought standing among his own people (v14). "Servant" is *doulos*, a slave. A slave has one master, and Paul cannot serve two.
- **Commentators:** All three treat the Galatians as led astray more than as ringleaders. Henry notes that Paul "tempers his reproof with mildness and tenderness towards them," laying the main blame on the teachers. He describes their teaching as "not really another gospel, but the perverting of the gospel of Christ." He also limits the anathema: it is not a license to curse every Christian who differs on smaller matters, only those who overturn salvation by grace. JFB applies v8 to any new revelation: "even though seemingly accredited by miracles, is not to be received." Guzik reads the curse against the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 27. He defends Paul's severity as love, using the picture of a rescue boat secretly loaded with explosives: warning people off it is kindness. On "different" and "another," Guzik makes the kind-versus-kind distinction. The lexicon allows it here but warns that the two words often blur.

#### Paul Called by God (vv11–24)
- **"Not man's gospel" (v11).** Literally "not according to man," meaning neither human in origin nor human in character.
- **"I did not receive it from any man, nor was I taught it" (v12).** Rabbis learned at a teacher's feet, as Paul himself did under Gamaliel (Acts 22:3). His opponents probably said he got his gospel second-hand from Jerusalem and then altered it. Paul says it came "through a revelation of Jesus Christ." The phrase can mean a revelation *from* Christ or a revelation *of* Christ. On the Damascus road it was both.
- **Does this contradict 1 Corinthians 15:3?** There Paul "delivered… what I also received," the core facts of Christ's death, burial and resurrection. Most scholars see no conflict. Paul learned the historical details and early creeds from other believers, but the gospel's meaning for him, that the crucified Jesus is Lord and that Gentiles are welcomed by grace, came straight from the risen Christ.
- **"My former life in Judaism" (v13).** "Judaism" (*Ioudaismos*) appears in the New Testament only here and in v14. In 2 Maccabees it names the Jewish way of life defended against pagan Greek culture. Paul is not describing the Old Testament faith of Abraham. He is describing a way of life marked off by law-keeping, and he once excelled at it.
- **"Persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it" (v13).** *Portheō* means to ravage or lay waste, as an army sacks a city. It is the same word the people of Damascus used of him (Acts 9:21). The imperfect tense pictures an ongoing campaign. Note "the church of *God*": Paul now sees that he was attacking God's own people.
- **"So extremely zealous was I for the traditions of my fathers" (v14).** "Zealous" (*zēlōtēs*) had a heroic pedigree in Judaism. Phinehas (Num 25:11) and Elijah (1 Kings 19:10) were celebrated for violent zeal against those who compromised Israel's faith. Paul's persecution was not ordinary cruelty. It was zeal in that tradition, and he was sure he was serving God. The "traditions of the fathers" were the oral teachings of the Pharisees (Phil 3:5–6; Mark 7:3).
- **"He who had set me apart before I was born" (v15).** This echoes two prophetic calls. Jeremiah: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you… I appointed you a prophet to the nations" (Jer 1:5). And the Servant of the Lord: "The LORD called me from the womb" (Isa 49:1), the one God makes "a light for the nations" (Isa 49:6). Paul sees his calling in that line. The verb *aphorizō* means to mark off or set apart for a purpose. It is used when the Spirit says, "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul" (Acts 13:2). God chose Paul before he had done anything, including during the years he was persecuting the church.
- **"Called me by his grace" (v15).** The sentence turns from "I persecuted… I was advancing… I was zealous" (vv13–14) to "he set me apart… he called… he was pleased." Paul did nothing to bring about his own conversion. He makes the same point about how the Galatians were saved.
- **"To reveal his Son to me" (v16).** The Greek is *en emoi*, "in me," as the ESV footnote notes. It may simply mean "to me." Many readers also hear an inward revelation, Christ made known within Paul's own heart (2:20; 2 Cor 4:6). The purpose clause matters: "in order that I might preach him among the Gentiles." Paul's conversion and his mission to the Gentiles came together (Acts 26:16–18).
- **"I did not immediately consult with anyone" (v16).** *Prosanatithēmi* means to consult or confer with someone for advice or information. Literally Paul did not confer "with flesh and blood," a Hebrew way of saying "any human being" (Matt 16:17).
- **"I went away into Arabia" (v17).** This was probably the Nabataean kingdom, east and south of Damascus, whose king Aretas later tried to arrest Paul (2 Cor 11:32). Luke does not mention the trip. It likely fits within the "many days" of Acts 9:23. Why Arabia? Some think for prayer and study, rethinking Scripture in light of Christ. Others think he went to preach, which would explain the Nabataean king's hostility. Some scholars note that Paul places Mount Sinai "in Arabia" (4:25) and suggest he went, like the zealous Elijah, to the mountain of God. That is an interesting guess but cannot be proven.
- **"Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas" (v18).** Cephas is Peter's Aramaic name (John 1:42). Ancient counting included part-years, so "three years" could be a little over two. *Historeō*, used only here in the New Testament, means to visit or get to know someone, the root of our word "history." Paul went to get to know Peter, not to be approved by him. Fifteen days was long enough to hear Peter's firsthand memories of Jesus, but much too short to be trained by him. Luke tells the same visit from another angle (Acts 9:26–30). Barnabas vouched for Paul, and threats on his life sent him away to Tarsus.
- **"James the Lord's brother" (v19).** He later led the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13; 21:18). He had not believed in Jesus during Jesus' ministry (John 7:5), but the risen Christ appeared to him (1 Cor 15:7). Christians understand "brother" differently. Most Protestants take him as a son of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus. Eastern Orthodox tradition holds he was Joseph's son by an earlier marriage. Roman Catholic tradition, following Jerome, reads "brother" broadly as a cousin. The Greek is ambiguous enough that all three readings exist.
- **"Before God, I do not lie!" (v20).** An oath. Paul only swears like this when his honesty is directly under attack (Rom 9:1; 2 Cor 11:31). His opponents had apparently given their own version of these years, with Paul as Jerusalem's student.
- **"The regions of Syria and Cilicia" (v21).** These were his home region around Tarsus and the area around Antioch (Acts 9:30; 11:25–26), years of preaching far from Jerusalem's oversight.
- **"Unknown in person to the churches of Judea" (v22).** If Paul had been trained by the Jerusalem apostles, the Judean believers would have known his face. They did not.
- **"He who used to persecute us is now preaching the faith he once tried to destroy" (v23).** This was probably a saying that spread through the churches. It uses the same verb as v13, *portheō*. The man who tried to destroy the faith now proclaims it.
- **"They glorified God because of me" (v24).** The churches Paul had once ravaged did not hold him at a distance or put him on probation. They praised God. The contrast with Galatia is pointed: the Judean churches celebrated Paul's gospel, and the Galatians were being told to doubt it. The line may also echo the Servant passage behind v15, where God says, "You are my servant… in whom I will be glorified" (Isa 49:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Paul tells his own story "for the magnifying of that free and rich grace which had wrought so wonderful a change in him." He offers two readings of Arabia: a retreat "for receiving further divine revelations," or a place to preach. JFB takes the second view: "This was a country of the Gentiles; here doubtless he preached." JFB renders v18 "to make the acquaintance of," which matches the lexicon's "visit, become acquainted with." It sums up v24 well: the churches understood "that the former wolf is now acting the shepherd's part." On v19 JFB takes James as a cousin, not a son of Mary, which differs from most Protestant readers today. Guzik passes on Chrysostom's comparison: Paul visited Peter as a visitor goes to see a famous city, not as a pupil reporting to a master. He also connects *aphorizō*, "set apart," with the Pharisees, often understood as "the separated ones." That is wordplay rather than a shared root: the name "Pharisee" probably comes from a Hebrew or Aramaic word, not from the Greek verb.

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole chapter rests on what Christ did and what Christ revealed. He "gave himself for our sins," so nothing can be added to that gift without turning good news into bad. And he revealed himself to a man who was destroying his church, choosing him in grace before he had done anything, good or bad. Paul's own life shows the gospel he defends. The persecutor did not earn his way back. He was called, and the churches he had harmed "glorified God because of me." The grace that turned the wolf into a shepherd is the same grace the Galatians were being tempted to leave behind.
