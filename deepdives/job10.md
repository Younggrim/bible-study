*Setting:* Bildad has just told Job that God never perverts justice, so Job's children must have died for their sins, and Job should repent and be restored (ch 8). In chapter 9 Job answers. He agrees that God is just and all-powerful, but that is exactly his problem: how can a man win a case against a God like that? He ends chapter 9 with a famous lament, "There is no arbiter between us, who might lay his hand on us both" (9:33), and a wish that God would take away his rod so he could speak without terror (9:34–35). Chapter 10 is the rest of that reply. Job stops talking about God and starts talking to him. He doesn't have the arbiter he longed for, so he makes his own case directly: he asks God why he is doing this (vv1–7), reminds God that he made Job with his own hands (vv8–12), accuses God of hiding a hostile plan all along (vv13–17), and comes back to the wish of chapter 3 that he had never been born (vv18–22). It is one of the rawest prayers in the Bible, and it is still a prayer.

#### Job Continues: I Loathe My Life (vv1–22)
- **"I loathe my life; I will give free utterance to my complaint" (v1).** Job had said the same in 7:11 and 9:21. He has decided not to bottle it up. "Bitterness of my soul" is the language of Hannah (1 Sam 1:10) and of Naomi, who asked to be called Mara, "bitter" (Ruth 1:20). Notice where the complaint goes. Job does not walk away from God or curse him, as Satan predicted (1:11). He takes his bitterness straight to God. The book shows that this kind of honest lament is a form of faith, not the opposite of it.
- **"Do not condemn me; let me know why you contend against me" (v2).** These are the only two requests in the chapter, apart from "leave me alone" in v20. "Condemn" means to declare someone guilty, to treat them as wicked. "Contend" is the Hebrew *rib*, a word for quarrelling that is often used of bringing a case in court. The lexicon lists Job 10:2 under that legal sense. Job feels like a defendant who has been sentenced without ever hearing the charge. Strikingly, he never asks to be healed. What torments him most is not the pain itself but not knowing why.
- **"Does it seem good to you to oppress, to despise the work of your hands?" (v3).** Job asks three hard questions here. Is it like God to crush the weak? Would a craftsman throw away what he made with care? And why does God seem to "favor the designs of the wicked," letting evil men prosper while the righteous suffer? The phrase "the work of your hands" prepares for vv8–12. Elsewhere the same thought becomes a prayer of trust: "Do not forsake the work of your hands" (Ps 138:8).
- **"Have you eyes of flesh? Do you see as man sees?" (v4).** Job knows the answer is no. "Man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart" (1 Sam 16:7). That is what makes his situation so confusing. His friends judge by appearances: Job is suffering, so Job must have sinned. But God cannot be fooled that way. So why is God treating him as though he could be?
- **"Are your days as the days of man… that you seek out my iniquity?" (vv5–6).** A human judge, with a short life and limited time, might need to dig hard and fast to catch a guilty man before it is too late. God is eternal. He has no need to hunt through Job's life for some hidden fault. Job feels like a suspect under constant investigation.
- **"Although you know that I am not guilty, and there is none to deliver out of your hand" (v7).** This is the heart of Job's protest. God knows Job is innocent. Job does not claim to be sinless (he has admitted sin in 7:21). He means he is not guilty of the kind of wickedness that would explain this. The reader knows Job is right: God himself called him "blameless and upright" (1:8; 2:3). The second line echoes God's own words, "there is none that can deliver out of my hand" (Deut 32:39). God is both the one who knows Job is innocent and the only one who can rescue him, and that is what makes his silence so painful.
- **"Your hands fashioned and made me, and now you have destroyed me altogether" (v8).** Job turns to a new argument. He reminds God that he is God's own handiwork. The Tyndale notes point out that this begins a series of word pictures, each showing God carefully shaping Job. Why would a maker who took such pains suddenly smash what he made?
- **"Remember that you have made me like clay; and will you return me to the dust?" (v9).** This is the picture of a potter. It goes back to Genesis 2:7, where God formed the man "of dust from the ground," and to the sentence of Genesis 3:19, "to dust you shall return." The Tyndale notes connect the image with the potter passages in Isaiah 45:9 and Jeremiah 18, and add that even though the potter has every right over the clay, Job still dares to ask why. Isaiah turns the same picture into a plea for mercy: "we are the clay, and you are our potter… Be not so terribly angry, O LORD" (Isa 64:8–9). Psalm 103:14 adds comfort: "he remembers that we are dust."
- **"Did you not pour me out like milk and curdle me like cheese?" (v10).** This is a poetic picture of how a human body is formed in the womb. Readers take it in two ways. Many older commentators, including JFB, see conception and early growth in the womb, liquid slowly becoming solid. The Tyndale notes suggest the image is of a newborn, still covered in the creamy coating babies have at birth. Either way, the ancient poet is marvelling at the mystery of how a person comes to be, and he credits the whole process to God.
- **"You clothed me with skin and flesh, and knit me together with bones and sinews" (v11).** The verb translated "knit" is *sakak*. The lexicon lists "weave" among its meanings, and it is the same verb Psalm 139:13 uses: "you knitted me together in my mother's womb." The cross-reference tools pair these two verses. Read side by side, they say the same thing in two moods. David says it in praise: "I am fearfully and wonderfully made" (Ps 139:14). Job says it in anguish: You made me so carefully, so how can you destroy me now? Both men believe God personally shapes every human life before birth.
- **"You have granted me life and steadfast love, and your care has preserved my spirit" (v12).** In the middle of his complaint, Job looks back and confesses God's goodness. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal, faithful love. "Care" is *pequddah*, a word for oversight or "visitation." It often means God visiting in judgment, but the lexicon places this verse under its gracious sense: God's watchful providence. Job knows God has been kind to him for years. That is why the present is so bewildering. This verse is a quiet sign that Job's faith, though shaken, has not let go.
- **"Yet these things you hid in your heart; I know that this was your purpose" (v13).** Here the meaning of "these things" matters. Many interpreters, including JFB, read it as Job's darkest suspicion: all that kindness was a cover, and all along God was secretly planning to watch Job, catch him and destroy him (vv14–17). Others, including Matthew Henry, hear a note of trust: God has reasons hidden in his heart that Job cannot see. Both readings take the words seriously. Read in context, the first is probably what Job means in this moment. But the reader of the book knows the second is true. There really was a hidden purpose, the scene in heaven of chapters 1–2, and it was not hostility. Job is right that God has reasons he hasn't told him. He is wrong about what they are.
- **"If I sin, you watch me and do not acquit me of my iniquity" (v14).** Job feels watched. In 7:20 he called God the "watcher of mankind." In 7:21 he asked why God would not simply pardon his sin. Now he feels that God is keeping a record of every slip and will not let any of it go.
- **"If I am guilty, woe to me! If I am in the right, I cannot lift up my head" (v15).** This is the trap Job feels caught in. If he is guilty, he is doomed. If he is innocent, it makes no difference, because he is still covered in shame and suffering. Lifting the head was a sign of confidence and honor (Ps 3:3). Job cannot do it. In his culture, visible suffering looked like proof of guilt, so even his innocence brings him only "disgrace." He can do nothing but "look on my affliction."
- **"Were my head lifted up, you would hunt me like a lion" (v16).** If Job dared to raise his head, God would hunt him down again like a lion stalking prey. The prophets use the same frightening image of God in judgment (Hos 13:7–8; Lam 3:10). "Work wonders against me" is bitter irony. The verb behind "work wonders" (*pala*) is normally used of God's great saving acts, like the wonders of the exodus (Exod 3:20). In 9:10 Job himself praised God who does "marvelous things beyond number." Now he feels that same wonder-working power turned against him.
- **"You renew your witnesses against me and increase your vexation toward me" (v17).** The courtroom language returns. Each new trouble is like a fresh witness brought into court to prove Job guilty. "Vexation" is *ka'as*, the same word Eliphaz used in 5:2 and Job used of his own anguish in 6:2. The lexicon notes that only here in Job is it used of God's anger. Job threw the word back at Eliphaz in chapter 6. Now he feels God himself has taken it up against him.
- **"You bring fresh troops against me" (v17).** The Hebrew is literally "changes and a host." The lexicon explains "changes" here as relays, like soldiers replaced by fresh ones so the attack never lets up. Picture a city under siege, with new waves of troops arriving while the defenders get no rest. Interestingly, the same word appears in 14:14, where Job wonders whether a man who dies will live again and says he will wait "till my renewal should come."
- **"Why did you bring me out from the womb?" (v18).** Job comes back to the lament of chapter 3, but with a difference. There he cursed the day of his birth and spoke about God (3:3, 23). Here he asks God directly. "Would that I had died before any eye had seen me." He wishes he had gone "from the womb to the grave" (v19) and been spared everything since. Like chapter 3, this is not a plan to end his own life. It is a cry from someone who can see no purpose in his pain.
- **"Are not my days few? Then cease, and leave me alone, that I may find a little cheer" (v20).** The God who seemed to watch him too closely (v14) is now asked to look away. Job made the same request in 7:16, 19. He is not asking for restoration now, only a little relief, a moment of breath before he dies. The word "cheer" is about the smallest comfort imaginable, a brightening of the face.
- **"Before I go—and I shall not return—to the land of darkness and deep shadow" (v21).** "I shall not return" echoes 7:9–10 and David's words about his dead child, "I shall go to him, but he will not return to me" (2 Sam 12:23). "Deep shadow" is *tsalmaveth*, the word behind the KJV's "shadow of death" in Psalm 23:4. The lexicon notes that scholars debate whether it means literally "death-shadow" or simply "deep darkness." Either way it is the blackest darkness the language can describe.
- **"The land of gloom like thick darkness… without any order, where light is as thick darkness" (v22).** Job piles up words for darkness. Across vv21–22 he uses four different Hebrew terms (*choshek*, *tsalmaveth*, *'ephah* and *'ophel*), and the lexicon confirms all four. "Without any order" uses *seder*, a word that appears only here in the whole Old Testament. It means arrangement or order. In death there is no structure, no day and night, nothing in its place. The final line is the bleakest: in that land even the light is darkness. The Tyndale notes observe that in chapter 3 Job pictured Sheol, the place of the dead, as a place of rest (3:13–19). Now he sees only gloom. The Study Bible background notes that in the ancient Near East, and in much of the Old Testament, Sheol was seen as a shadowy place of reduced existence, not yet the full heaven-and-hell picture of the New Testament. Job does not yet see what God will later bring to light. Even so, he will soon reach further: "I know that my Redeemer lives" (19:25).
- **Commentators:** All three agree that Job takes his complaint to God and not away from him, and that his words mix real faith with wrong conclusions. Matthew Henry calls vv3–7 a "peevish expostulation," hears Job saying God seems "to oppress thy subject, to despise thy workmanship, and to countenance thy enemies," and answers that "Eyes of flesh cannot see in the dark, but darkness hides not from God." On vv8–12 he urges humility ("We must not be proud of our bodies") and calls life "a favour that puts us into a capacity of receiving other favours." He reads v13 as trust in hidden purposes, while JFB takes it as "Thy purpose" to afflict. Henry is frank about vv18–19: "This was the language of his passion, and it was a relapse into the same sin he fell into before." On the darkness of death he adds, "That had need be well done which is to be done but once, and done for eternity." JFB says "made" in v8 implies "a work of difficulty and art," explains the clay of v9 as pointing to "wonderful fashioning by the divine potter" rather than weakness, and calls the trials of v17 "a succession of witnesses brought up in proof of his guilt." On v22 JFB writes, "Its only sunshine is thick darkness," and contrasts it with the Christian hope of 2 Timothy 1:10. JFB has no notes on vv18, 19 and 21. Guzik notes that Job never once asks to be healed. He argues that Job is not suicidal, only unable to see any purpose in his pain. He stresses that the reader knows about the scene in heaven and Job does not, and he repeats Mason's point about four words for darkness, which the lexicon supports. He also passes on Spurgeon's list of reasons God may allow believers to suffer, and F. B. Meyer's observation that Job called this life "day" and death "night," while the New Testament turns that around. Guzik suggests that the care Job describes in vv8–12 sits badly with the idea that God makes most people only to condemn them. That is his own theological point, and Reformed readers would answer it differently.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job asked to know why God contended with him, and he asked from a body God had knit together in the womb. The Son of God took that same kind of body, formed in Mary's womb, and in it he bore something Job only feared. Job felt condemned without a charge. Jesus, who truly was innocent, was condemned to settle the charge against others, so that "there is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Rom 8:1). Job felt hunted and surrounded by witnesses. False witnesses lined up against Jesus (Mark 14:56), and he cried his own "why" from the cross (Mark 15:34). And the land of darkness Job dreaded is no longer the last word. Christ "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10). Job pleaded for an arbiter who could lay a hand on both God and man. In Jesus that arbiter has come: "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Tim 2:5).
