*Setting:* About 870 BC, early in Ahab's reign over the northern kingdom of Israel. Chapter 16 ended at Israel's lowest point. Ahab married Jezebel, daughter of the priest-king of Tyre, built a temple for Baal in Samaria, set up an Asherah pole, and "did more to provoke the LORD... than all the kings of Israel who were before him" (16:33). Baal was the Canaanite storm god. His worshipers believed he rode the clouds and sent the rain, dew and harvests. Into that world, with no introduction, steps Elijah. Chapters 17–19 tell his story, and the first round of the contest is about the very thing Baal was supposed to control: rain. Chapter 17 shows Elijah announcing a drought, then being kept alive by God in two unlikely places, a dry ravine and a Gentile widow's kitchen, where God also raises a dead boy.

#### Elijah Predicts a Drought (vv1–7)
- **"Now Elijah the Tishbite, of Tishbe in Gilead" (v1).** His name is a sermon in itself: *Eliyahu*, "my God is Yah" (the LORD). In a land where the court said Baal was god, his very name said otherwise. Guzik makes the point. Gilead was the rugged hill country east of the Jordan, a frontier region far from the royal court. The Hebrew could also be read "of the settlers of Gilead," and JFB takes it to mean Elijah was not a native Israelite. Most follow the ESV and Greek reading, a town called Tishbe, whose exact site is uncertain. We know nothing of his family. The Bible gives him no father's name, no call story and no warning. He simply appears.
- **"As the LORD, the God of Israel, lives, before whom I stand" (v1).** This is an oath. Baal was believed to die each year in the dry season and come back to life with the rains. Elijah swears by the God who "lives," always. "Before whom I stand" is the language of a servant standing ready in his master's presence. JFB says it means "whom I serve." Elijah stands before Ahab, but he stands first before the LORD.
- **"There shall be neither dew nor rain these years, except by my word" (v1).** In Israel, rain comes only in the winter months. In the long dry summer, heavy dew is what keeps crops alive. No rain *and* no dew meant total disaster. This is the covenant curse coming true: if Israel turned to other gods, "he will shut up the heavens, so that there will be no rain, and the land will yield no fruit" (Deut 11:16–17; 28:23–24). It also strikes Baal where he claimed to be strongest. In the Canaanite myths found at Ugarit, the land goes dry when Baal is dead. Now Israel will see the land go dry because the LORD lives. James says this began with prayer: "Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed fervently that it might not rain, and for three years and six months it did not rain" (James 5:17; also Luke 4:25). JFB suggests the drought was severe rather than absolute, though the text gives no hint of exceptions.
- **"Hide yourself by the brook Cherith, which is east of the Jordan" (v3).** Elijah has just made himself the most wanted man in Israel. Ahab will search every nation for him (18:10). God hides him in a ravine east of the Jordan, back toward his home region. A "brook" (*nachal*) is a wadi, a stream bed that flows in the rainy season and dries up in summer. The exact site is unknown. Guzik connects the name Cherith with a Hebrew root meaning "cut," a picture of God cutting Elijah off for a season of training. It is a fair sermon point but not certain.
- **"I have commanded the ravens to feed you there" (v4).** Ravens were unclean birds under the law (Lev 11:15), scavengers known for taking food, not giving it. God uses them to carry bread and meat to his prophet "in the morning... and in the evening" (v6). That echoes Israel's manna and quail in the wilderness (Exod 16:8, 12). The Hebrew for ravens (*'orevim*) could, with different vowels, mean "Arabs" or "people of Arabah," and some have proposed that. JFB weighs those suggestions and keeps the traditional reading, as do almost all translations. The point is that God provided in a way no one would have planned.
- **"So he went and did according to the word of the LORD" (v5).** The phrase "the word of the LORD" runs through this chapter (vv2, 5, 8, 16, 24). Elijah obeys simply, step by step, before he knows how it will turn out.
- **"After a while the brook dried up, because there was no rain in the land" (v7).** The drought Elijah announced reached him too. God's servant is not spared the hardship his own message brought. Guzik passes on Meyer's reflection on "drying brooks," the losses that teach believers to rely on the Giver rather than his gifts.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God wonderfully suits men to the work he designs them for," and on the brook, earthly comforts are such that "we lose them when we most need them, like brooks in summer." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's observation that God can deliver good things by unclean carriers, and sees Cherith as a hidden school after a very public beginning. The Tyndale notes stress that the drought began a contest with Baal that will reach its climax on Mount Carmel in chapter 18.

#### The Widow of Zarephath (vv8–16)
- **"Arise, go to Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon" (v9).** Zarephath was a Phoenician coastal town about seven miles south of Sidon and fourteen north of Tyre. Its ruins at modern Sarafand in Lebanon have been excavated, and a small shrine to Phoenician goddesses was found there. God sends Elijah out of Israel altogether, into Jezebel's home country, the heartland of Baal. The safest place for the prophet of the LORD is in Baal's own backyard. It is also outside Ahab's reach.
- **"I have commanded a widow there to feed you" (v9).** A widow was among the poorest people in the ancient world, with no husband to provide and often no rights. God promised to defend widows (Deut 10:18; Ps 68:5). Now he sends his prophet to depend on one. She does not seem to know she has been "commanded" (v12). God is preparing her before she knows it.
- **"Bring me a little water... Bring me a morsel of bread" (vv10–11).** Water was precious in a drought, but she goes to get it without complaint. Bread is another matter.
- **"As the LORD your God lives, I have nothing baked" (v12).** She recognizes Elijah as an Israelite and swears by his God. The Tyndale notes say this does not mean the LORD was her God yet, only that she is telling the truth. She has "a handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug." She is gathering "a couple of sticks" (literally "two sticks") to cook one last meal: "that we may eat it and die." The drought had reached Phoenicia too. The Jewish historian Josephus, quoting a Phoenician source, mentions a year-long drought in the days of Ethbaal, Jezebel's father.
- **"Do not fear... But first make me a little cake of it" (v13).** It sounds outrageous. But Elijah is not being selfish. He is asking her to trust God's word with the last thing she has, and he gives her the promise first.
- **"The jar of flour shall not be spent, and the jug of oil shall not be empty, until the day that the LORD sends rain" (v14).** "Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel" is spoken in a Baal-worshiping land. The God of Israel is not a local god. He rules in Sidon too. Flour and oil were the staples of daily life, and in Baal's land it is the LORD who provides them (Hos 2:8: "She did not know that it was I who gave her the grain, the wine, and the oil").
- **"She went and did as Elijah said" (v15).** She obeys before she sees anything. "She and he and her household ate for many days," and the jar and jug did not run out (v16). The supply came a little at a time, enough for each day. Guzik, citing Spurgeon, notes that a large stockpile in a famine would have drawn thieves. It is a picture of "give us this day our daily bread" (Matt 6:11) and of the manna, which could not be stored up (Exod 16:19–21).
- **Jesus and this widow.** In his first sermon at Nazareth, Jesus pointed to this story: "There were many widows in Israel in the days of Elijah... and Elijah was sent to none of them but only to Zarephath, in the land of Sidon, to a woman who was a widow" (Luke 4:25–26). The townspeople were so furious they tried to throw him off a cliff. God's mercy reaching a Gentile outsider, while Israel rejected his prophet, offended them deeply.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is God's way, and it is his glory, to make use of, and put honour upon, the weak and foolish things of the world," and "Happy are all who can thus, against hope, believe and obey in hope." The Tyndale notes compare the widow who gave two small coins, all she had (Luke 21:1–4). JFB calls her "the very woman who was appointed by divine providence to support him."

#### Elijah Raises the Widow's Son (vv17–24)
- **"The son of the woman, the mistress of the house, became ill" (v17).** "Mistress of the house" suggests she owned the house, perhaps a home with an upper room, which hints that she had not always been so poor. "There was no breath left in him." The Tyndale notes say this means he had died, and that attempts to explain the miracle away contradict the plain words. The word for breath is the same as in Genesis 2:7.
- **"What have you against me, O man of God? You have come to me to bring my sin to remembrance" (v18).** In grief she lashes out. She assumes her son's death is punishment for some past sin, and that the holy man's presence has made God notice it. This was a common belief (Job 4:7; John 9:2). The Bible does not endorse it. Jesus rejected it (Luke 13:1–5; John 9:3). Guzik suggests she had carried some hidden guilt for a long time.
- **"Give me your son" (v19).** Elijah does not argue. He takes the boy from her arms and carries him to "the upper chamber where he lodged," a roof room with its own outside stairs. The Tyndale notes say this arrangement protected the widow's reputation. He lays the boy on his own bed.
- **"O LORD my God, have you brought calamity even upon the widow with whom I sojourn?" (v20).** Elijah's prayer is honest and bold. He does not understand what God is doing, and he says so. "Sojourn" means he lived there as a guest and foreigner. He takes her grief to God as his own.
- **"He stretched himself upon the child three times" (v21).** He lies over the boy's body, perhaps to share his own warmth and life, perhaps as an acted-out prayer. Elisha later does something similar for the Shunammite's son (2 Kgs 4:34). The power is not in the method. Elijah prays, "Let this child's life come into him again." The word for "life" is *nephesh*, the whole living self.
- **"And the LORD listened to the voice of Elijah" (v22).** This is the first time in the Bible that a dead person is raised to life. God listened to a man. James uses Elijah as proof that "the prayer of a righteous person has great power" (James 5:16–17). Guzik passes on Wiseman's point that this cannot be explained as magic or some ancient version of resuscitation.
- **"See, your son lives" (v23).** "Lives" is the same word as Elijah's oath in v1: "As the LORD... lives." The living God gives life. Elijah "delivered him to his mother." Luke uses almost exactly these words when Jesus raises the widow's son at Nain, "and Jesus gave him to his mother" (Luke 7:15). The crowd there said, "A great prophet has arisen among us!" (Luke 7:16), recognizing the echo of Elijah.
- **"Now I know that you are a man of God, and that the word of the LORD in your mouth is truth" (v24).** The chapter's climax. The flour and oil had kept her alive, but the raising of her son brought her to faith. The Tyndale notes say her words suggest she put her faith in the LORD. A Gentile woman in Baal's homeland confesses what Israel's own king refuses to: the LORD's word is true. JFB: the episode brought her to "a clear knowledge of God, and a firm faith in His word." There is a quiet irony too. In the land of the god who supposedly dies and rises each year, it is the LORD who raises the dead.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Neither faith nor obedience shut out afflictions and death," and "See the power of prayer, and the power of Him who hears prayer." Guzik warns against embellishing the story with the old Jewish tradition, mentioned by Jerome, that this boy grew up to be the prophet Jonah. JFB notes that "a severe domestic calamity" led her to think God was punishing her.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elijah is the great prophet of the Old Testament, the one Malachi promised would come again before the day of the LORD (Mal 4:5). John the Baptist came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17), and Elijah himself stood with Jesus on the Mount of Transfiguration (Matt 17:3). But this chapter points past Elijah to Jesus in plainer ways. Jesus used this very story to announce that his mission reached beyond Israel to outsiders like the widow of Zarephath (Luke 4:25–26). He fed multitudes from a little bread, as God fed this household from a little flour. He gave a widow's dead son back to his mother (Luke 7:11–17). Elijah stretched himself on a dead boy and pleaded for his life. Jesus simply said, "Young man, I say to you, arise" (Luke 7:14), and later rose from death himself, so that he could say to every grieving person, "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25).
