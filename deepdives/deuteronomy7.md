*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Moses is in the middle of his second sermon (chapters 5–26). Chapter 5 repeated the Ten Commandments, and chapter 6 set out the heart of them all: "Hear, O Israel… You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart." Chapter 6 also warned that in Canaan the danger would be forgetting God in plenty and drifting after "the gods of the peoples who are around you" (6:14). Chapter 7 now faces those peoples directly. Israel is about to cross the Jordan into a land already full of nations, cities and shrines. Moses tells them how to deal with the nations, why God chose Israel at all, and why they need not be afraid. It is one of the hardest chapters in the Old Testament for modern readers, and also one of the clearest statements anywhere of God's free, undeserved love.

#### A Chosen People (vv1–26)
- **"Seven nations more numerous and mightier than you" (v1).** The list names the peoples of Canaan. **Hittites** were linked to the great empire in Anatolia (modern Turkey), though the Hittites in Canaan were probably a smaller related group (Uriah the Hittite in 2 Sam 11 is one). **Amorites** lived mainly in the hill country. **Canaanites** were the people of the coast and valleys. **Hivites** lived around Gibeon and Shechem. **Jebusites** held Jerusalem until David took it (2 Sam 5:6–9). Girgashites and Perizzites are hardly known beyond these lists. God promised Abraham the land of ten peoples (Gen 15:19–21). Here there are seven, the number of completeness. Moses admits plainly that they are stronger. Israel's confidence must rest in God, not in its army.
- **"You must devote them to complete destruction" (v2).** The Hebrew verb is *charam*, and the noun is *cherem*, "the ban." It means to hand something over entirely to God, removed from human use, which in war meant destroying it rather than taking it as plunder. Israel had already done this to Sihon and Og (2:34; 3:6). It is the opposite of ordinary conquest: no slaves, no spoil, no profit.
- **How to read the command.** This is a hard text and Christians have read it with care. Several points come from Scripture itself. God had waited four hundred years because "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Gen 15:16), so this is judgment long delayed. Canaanite religion included child sacrifice (12:31; 18:9–12). The command was limited to these nations in this land at this moment, not a standing rule for Israel's wars (20:10–15 gives different rules for distant cities). God warned that he would drive Israel out too if they did the same things (8:19–20; Lev 18:24–28), and he later did. And people could be spared: Rahab and her family by faith (Josh 2; 6:25), the Gibeonites by surrender (Josh 9). Many scholars also note that the language of total destruction was standard ancient war rhetoric. Joshua says the cities were struck down "utterly," yet Judges shows the same peoples still living in the land (Judg 1:21–36). How far that softens the command is debated. What is not in doubt is that the purpose given in the text is religious: to remove a way of worship that would destroy Israel (v4). This was never a license for ethnic hatred, and the New Testament gives the church no such sword. Its weapons "are not of the flesh" (2 Cor 10:4).
- **"You shall make no covenant with them… You shall not intermarry with them" (vv2–3).** No peace treaties and no marriages. The reason is given at once, and it is not about ethnicity: "for they would turn away your sons from following me, to serve other gods" (v4). Rahab the Canaanite and Ruth the Moabite married into Israel and became ancestors of David and of Jesus (Matt 1:5), because they had turned to Israel's God. Solomon is the sad example of the danger: "his wives turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kgs 11:1–4). Paul gives believers the same principle in a new setting (2 Cor 6:14).
- **"Break down their altars… dash in pieces their pillars… chop down their Asherim" (v5).** **Pillars** (*matstsevot*) were standing stones set up at shrines, often for Baal. The **Asherim** were wooden poles or stylized trees for Asherah, a Canaanite mother goddess linked with fertility. Their worship tied crops and children to sex rituals at the shrines. Israel was to leave none of it standing. The command was largely ignored, and Asherah poles turn up again and again in Israel's later history, even in the temple itself (2 Kgs 21:7; 23:6).
- **"For you are a people holy to the LORD your God" (v6).** This is why Israel had to be separate. "Holy" means set apart, belonging to God. Israel was holy first by God's choice, and was then called to live holy lives. Standing came before conduct.
- **"His treasured possession" (v6).** The word is *segullah*, a personal treasure. King David used it of the gold and silver from his private fortune that he gave to the temple (1 Chr 29:3). Of all the peoples of the earth, which all belong to God (Exod 19:5), Israel is his own particular treasure. Peter applies the same title to the church: "a people for his own possession" (1 Pet 2:9; also Titus 2:14).
- **"It was not because you were more in number… for you were the fewest of all peoples" (v7).** God did not choose Israel for its size, and chapter 9 adds that it was not for their goodness either (9:4–6). Israel began as one childless old man and his wife.
- **"But it is because the LORD loves you" (v8).** This is the heart of the chapter, and it is nearly circular on purpose: God loves Israel because he loves Israel. The reason for his love lies in himself, not in them. The verb in v7, "set his love on" (*chashaq*), is a strong word for attachment and desire, used of a man longing for a woman (Gen 34:8). **"And is keeping the oath that he swore to your fathers"** adds faithfulness to affection. God had promised Abraham, and he keeps his word. **"Redeemed you from the house of slavery"** uses *padah*, to buy back or ransom, the word for buying a person out of slavery.
- **"The faithful God who keeps covenant and steadfast love… to a thousand generations" (v9).** This echoes the second commandment (5:9–10), but here it says outright "a thousand generations." From Abraham to Moses is only a handful. God's faithfulness runs far beyond anything Israel can imagine. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love.
- **"And repays to their face those who hate him" (v10).** God's love is not soft on evil. "To their face" means directly and personally, in their own lifetime, not only later on their descendants. He "will not be slack," that is, delay. Love and justice stand side by side in the same breath.
- **"And because you listen to these rules… the LORD your God will keep with you the covenant" (v12).** The Hebrew word for "because" here is *eqev*, which literally means "heel." It gives its name to this section in the Jewish weekly reading cycle. Obedience is the path on which God's covenant blessings are enjoyed, though they were never earned by it (v8).
- **"The fruit of your womb and the fruit of your ground, your grain and your wine and your oil" (v13).** The blessing touches exactly the things Canaanite fertility religion claimed to provide: children, crops and herds. Grain, new wine and olive oil were the three staple crops of the land. Israel does not need Baal or Asherah. The LORD gives what they only pretend to give (Hos 2:8).
- **"None of the evil diseases of Egypt, which you knew" (v15).** This may refer to the plagues, such as boils (Exod 9:9), or to illnesses common in Egypt. Egyptian medical papyri describe diseases of the eyes, the gut and the skin. It recalls God's promise at Marah: "I am the LORD, your healer" (Exod 15:26).
- **"Your eye shall not pity them… for that would be a snare to you" (v16).** A snare was a bird trap. The danger was less the Canaanites' armies than their gods. A misplaced pity that left the shrines standing would trap Israel. Judges 2:1–3 records that this is exactly what happened.
- **"If you say in your heart, 'These nations are greater than I'" (v17).** Moses knows the fear the spies spread forty years earlier (1:28). His cure is memory: **"you shall remember what the LORD your God did to Pharaoh and to all Egypt" (v18).** The same God who broke the greatest empire of the day will deal with the kings of Canaan.
- **"The LORD your God will send hornets among them" (v20).** The Hebrew word *tsir'ah* means "hornet" in BDB, and it occurs only here and in Exodus 23:28 and Joshua 24:12. Some take it literally, swarms of stinging insects driving people from their hiding places. Others take it as a figure for panic and terror that God would send, and some versions translate it "terror." A few have suggested it pictures Egypt, whose pharaohs used a bee as a royal symbol, as if Egyptian raids had weakened Canaan beforehand. Whatever the means, Joshua 24:12 makes the point: "it was not by your sword or by your bow."
- **"The LORD your God is in your midst, a great and awesome God" (v21).** The cure for dread is not a smaller enemy but a bigger view of God, who is present with his people.
- **"Little by little… lest the wild beasts grow too numerous for you" (v22).** God could clear the land at once, but an empty land would go wild, and Israel was too small to fill it yet. Exodus 23:29–30 says the same: "until you have increased and possess the land." Slow victory was also a mercy. Judges 2:20–3:4 adds that the remaining nations would test Israel's faithfulness.
- **"You shall not covet the silver or the gold that is on them" (v25).** Idols were often wood overlaid with precious metal. Melting it down to keep might seem harmless, but it was a trap. Achan learned this at Jericho when he hid devoted silver and gold in his tent (Josh 7:21). It is the same word as the tenth commandment, "covet" (5:21).
- **"You shall not bring an abominable thing into your house and become devoted to destruction like it" (v26).** Whoever keeps what is under the ban falls under it. The chapter ends where it began, with *cherem*. The word "abomination" (*to'evah*) means something utterly repugnant to God.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry stresses that the command was limited to these seven nations and must not be taken as a pattern for other wars. On v1 he writes, "The strength of Israel's enemies magnifies the power of Israel's God." On v8: "Nothing in them, or done by them, did or could make God a debtor to them." On v10: "Those that hate God cannot hurt him, but certainly ruin themselves." JFB admits the command is hard to square with God's character at first sight and grounds it in the Canaanites' "gross idolatry and enormous wickedness." It also offers a second, weaker view that the Canaanites had taken land that belonged to Eber's descendants, which the text doesn't say. On the altars, JFB quotes John Knox: "pull down the nests, and the rooks will disappear." On v22 it says the danger would be avoided "by a progressive conquest and by the use of ordinary means." JFB lists the diseases of Egypt as ophthalmia, dysentery, smallpox and plague. Guzik does not soften the command: he says it is harsh and was meant to be. He points to the Canaanites' long period of warning and their three ways of escape: faith like Rahab, surrender like the Gibeonites, or leaving the land. He also draws out "holy" as a position before it is a practice, and applies "little by little" to slow spiritual growth, contrasting an oak with a squash that grows overnight. All three agree that the root of Israel's election is God's free love and his oath to the fathers, not anything in Israel.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter's center is v8: God loves his people because he loves them, and he redeems them from slavery. The New Testament takes up the same words for the church, a people chosen "not because of our works but because of his own purpose and grace" (2 Tim 1:9) and called God's "own possession" (1 Pet 2:9). Moses tells Israel to destroy what would destroy them. In Christ, God's judgment on sin falls not on his enemies' cities but on his own Son, so that people from every nation, Canaanites included, can be brought in as Rahab was. The war against idols remains, but it is fought now in the heart, "we destroy arguments and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God, and take every thought captive to obey Christ" (2 Cor 10:5).
