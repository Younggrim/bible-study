*Setting:* About 913–886 BC, in the first generation after the kingdom split. Chapter 14 ended with Rehoboam's death and Ahijah's prophecy that Jeroboam's house would be burned away "as a man burns up dung" (14:10). Chapter 15 now moves quickly through four kings, two in each kingdom. From here Kings works like a braided history. It follows one king to his death, then goes back to pick up the kings of the other kingdom who began during his reign, dating each by the other ("in the eighteenth year of King Jeroboam," v1). Each reign gets a standard frame: when he began, how long he ruled, his mother's name (for Judah), a verdict on his heart before God, a few events, a pointer to the court records and his burial. The Tyndale notes lay out this pattern. Chronicles tells the Judah half of this chapter at much greater length (2 Chr 13–16).

#### Abijam Reigns in Judah (vv1–8)
- **"In the eighteenth year of King Jeroboam the son of Nebat, Abijam began to reign" (v1).** About 913 BC. Chronicles calls him Abijah, "my father is the LORD." Abijam may mean "my father is the sea." JFB thinks it is just a variant spelling of the same name. Guzik raises the possibility that the change reflects a drift toward the Canaanite sea god Yam. The text does not say.
- **"He reigned for three years" (v2).** Parts of years were counted as whole years, so it may have been a little over two (JFB makes this point). Short reigns in Kings are often a quiet sign of God's displeasure.
- **"His mother's name was Maacah the daughter of Abishalom" (v2).** Abishalom is another form of Absalom, David's rebel son. So Maacah was probably Absalom's granddaughter, through his daughter Tamar (2 Sam 14:27), with "daughter" meaning descendant. The Tyndale notes take her as his granddaughter. She was Rehoboam's favorite wife (2 Chr 11:20–21). Chronicles once calls Abijah's mother "Micaiah the daughter of Uriel of Gibeah" (2 Chr 13:2), which may reflect another name or another line of descent. JFB suggests the name was changed when she became queen. She will appear again in v10, with Asa.
- **"His heart was not wholly true to the LORD his God, as the heart of David his father" (v3).** "Wholly true" is *shalem*, "whole, complete, at peace," the root of *shalom* and of Solomon's name. Solomon's own heart had not been *shalem* with the LORD in old age (11:4). Abijam's wasn't either.
- **What Chronicles adds.** Chronicles gives a fuller and more positive picture. Abijah stood on a mountain and preached to Jeroboam's army that God's kingdom belonged to David's sons, that the golden calves were no gods, and that Judah still had the true priests and temple (2 Chr 13:4–12). When his outnumbered army was ambushed, Judah cried to the LORD and won a great victory (2 Chr 13:13–20). Kings, writing about the heart, gives the overall verdict. Chronicles shows that even a king with a divided heart could call on God in a crisis and be heard. The Tyndale notes add that he married fourteen wives and had thirty-eight children (2 Chr 13:21).
- **"Nevertheless, for David's sake the LORD his God gave him a lamp in Jerusalem" (v4).** This is the key verse of the section. God had promised David that his house and kingdom would stand forever (2 Sam 7:12–16). A "lamp" burning in a house meant the family line went on. JFB calls it "an Oriental phrase for continuance of family name." The lamp image goes back to 11:36 and on to 2 Kings 8:19. Abijam does not deserve the throne. God gives it to him because of his promise. The Tyndale notes say each king after David was meant to be a light, sharing God's grace.
- **"Except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite" (v5).** David's sin with Bathsheba and his murder of her husband (2 Sam 11) is named plainly. Even the model king is not presented as sinless. But his heart came back to God in repentance, and he never worshiped idols. The writer measures every king of Judah by that heart.
- **"There was war between Rehoboam and Jeroboam all the days of his life" (v6).** The verse repeats 14:30 almost word for word, with Rehoboam's name, though Abijam is now the subject. Many think it is a copyist's repetition. Verse 7 says the same war went on under Abijam. The point is that the border war never stopped.
- **Commentators:** Henry turns v4 toward the gospel: "The Son of David will still continue a light to his church, to establish it in truth and righteousness to the end of time." Guzik stresses that God kept David's line going for David's sake, not because his descendants earned it.

#### Asa Reigns in Judah (vv9–24)
- **"Asa began to reign over Judah, and he reigned forty-one years" (vv9–10).** About 910–869 BC, one of the longest reigns in Judah's history. Chronicles says the land had peace for his first ten years (2 Chr 14:1).
- **"His mother's name was Maacah the daughter of Abishalom" (v10).** The same name as Abijam's mother in v2. Most think she was really Asa's grandmother, since Hebrew "mother" can mean any female ancestor, just as "father" can mean ancestor. Since Abijam reigned so briefly, Maacah kept her powerful role as queen mother into her grandson's reign.
- **"Asa did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, as David his father had done" (v11).** The first good king of Judah since the split. David was actually his great-great-grandfather. Guzik notes the wider sense of "father."
- **"He put away the male cult prostitutes out of the land and removed all the idols that his fathers had made" (v12).** He undid what had spread under Rehoboam (14:24). "Idols" (*gillulim*) is a contemptuous word, likely related to a word for dung, "dung-gods." Ezekiel uses it often.
- **"He also removed Maacah his mother from being queen mother" (v13).** This took real courage. She was the most powerful woman in the kingdom and his own grandmother. "Queen mother" (*gebirah*, "great lady") was an official office. Henry: "Reformation must begin at home." Jesus said one who loves father or mother more than him is not worthy of him (Matt 10:37).
- **"She had made an abominable image for Asherah" (v13).** "Abominable image" is a rare word meaning something horrifying or shuddering. JFB calls it "a very obscene figure," and Guzik passes on Dilday's view that it was probably phallic. Asa "cut down her image and burned it at the brook Kidron." The Kidron valley, just east of Jerusalem between the city and the Mount of Olives, was where reforming kings destroyed idols (2 Kgs 23:4, 6, 12; 2 Chr 29:16). Jesus crossed it on the night of his arrest (John 18:1).
- **"But the high places were not taken away" (v14).** The local hilltop shrines remained. Chronicles says Asa took away the high places (2 Chr 14:3–5), and then says they "were not taken out of Israel" (2 Chr 15:17). The likely answer, as Guzik and the Tyndale notes say: he removed the pagan ones but left the shrines where people worshiped the LORD, though the law said he should be worshiped only at the place God chose (Deut 12:2–14). Popular local worship was hard to uproot, and it lasted until Hezekiah and Josiah.
- **"Nevertheless, the heart of Asa was wholly true to the LORD all his days" (v14).** Again *shalem*, whole. This is high praise, set right against his father's verdict in v3. Yet the rest of the chapter, and Chronicles, show that a whole heart is not a flawless life.
- **"He brought into the house of the LORD the sacred gifts" (v15).** Probably plunder from war, dedicated to God. Chronicles tells of a great victory over Zerah the Cushite (2 Chr 14:9–15), likely an Egyptian commander, and a covenant renewal (2 Chr 15:8–15). Asa refilled the temple treasury that Shishak had emptied (14:26).
- **"Baasha king of Israel went up against Judah and built Ramah" (v17).** Ramah was only about five miles north of Jerusalem, on the main road north. The Tyndale notes say about four. "Built" means fortified. By holding Ramah, Baasha could block anyone traveling to or from Jerusalem, including northerners moving south to follow the true worship (2 Chr 15:9). JFB says Ramah overlooked a narrow ravine between the two kingdoms.
- **"Then Asa took all the silver and the gold... and sent them to Ben-hadad" (v18).** Ben-hadad ("son of Hadad," the storm god) was king of Aram, the Syrian kingdom based in Damascus, which had broken away from Solomon under Rezon (11:23–25). Asa pays him to break his treaty with Israel and attack from the north. It works. Ben-hadad seized a string of towns in Israel's far north, Ijon, Dan, Abel-beth-maacah and the region around the Sea of Galilee (Chinneroth) (v20). Baasha abandons Ramah (v21).
- **Was it right?** Kings does not comment. Chronicles does. The prophet Hanani told Asa, "Because you relied on the king of Syria, and did not rely on the LORD your God, the army of the king of Syria has escaped you." He reminded him of the victory over Zerah and said, "The eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to give strong support to those whose heart is blameless toward him" (2 Chr 16:7–9). Asa was furious and put Hanani in prison (2 Chr 16:10). The man who trusted God against a huge army trusted silver against a smaller one. He also emptied God's house to do it, and he invited a foreign power into the region that would trouble Israel for the next 150 years. Guzik passes on Poole's three charges: misusing what was consecrated, distrusting God, and acting in ill will. JFB: "Asa's religious character is now seen to decline."
- **"They carried away the stones of Ramah and its timber... and with them King Asa built Geba of Benjamin and Mizpah" (v22).** "None was exempt," a full labor draft. Asa used Baasha's own materials to fortify two border towns. Excavations at Tell en-Nasbeh, widely identified with Mizpah, uncovered a massive city wall from about this time. Three centuries later, a cistern Asa made there was used to hide the bodies of men murdered after Jerusalem fell (Jer 41:9).
- **"But in his old age he was diseased in his feet" (v23).** Chronicles adds that the disease became severe, "yet even in his disease he did not seek the LORD, but sought help from physicians" (2 Chr 16:12). The point is not that seeing a doctor is wrong. It is that he sought doctors *instead* of the LORD. The Tyndale notes mention the Talmud's suggestion of gout. Guzik passes on Wiseman's view that it may have been a circulatory disease with gangrene. "Feet" may also be a polite way to speak of the whole lower body.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Even true believers find it hard, in times of urgent danger, to trust in the Lord with all their heart," and "Unbelief makes way for carnal policy, and thus for one sin after another." Guzik calls Asa's story the tragedy of a man who served God well for years, failed a key test, and then refused correction. He passes on Morgan's summary: a life with real faults, rooted in a real desire for God.

#### Nadab Reigns in Israel (vv25–32)
- **"Nadab the son of Jeroboam began to reign over Israel in the second year of Asa... and he reigned over Israel two years" (v25).** About 910–909 BC. His name means "willing" or "generous." He is Jeroboam's only son to reign. "He... walked in the way of his father" (v26). JFB: "No record is given of him, except his close adherence to the bad policy of his father."
- **"Baasha the son of Ahijah, of the house of Issachar, conspired against him" (v27).** Baasha's father shares a name with the prophet but is a different man, as the Tyndale notes point out. Issachar was a northern tribe south of Galilee. Baasha seems to have been an army officer who struck while the army was on campaign.
- **"At Gibbethon, which belonged to the Philistines" (v27).** Gibbethon lay on the edge of the coastal plain, about 20 miles west of Bethel. It had been given to the Levites (Josh 21:23) but was now in Philistine hands. Israel was trying to win it back. It will be under siege again twenty-five years later, when the next coup comes (16:15).
- **"He left to the house of Jeroboam not one that breathed... according to the word of the LORD" (v29).** Ahijah's prophecy came true within about two years of Jeroboam's death (14:10–14). Kings is careful here. God's word was fulfilled, but Baasha was not righteous for doing it. He acted from ambition, and God will judge his house for the same sins, and expressly "also because he destroyed it" (16:7). Guzik passes on Clarke's point that God used one wicked man to punish another. JFB calls the slaughter of rivals' families "a barbarous practice too common in the East."
- **"It was for the sins of Jeroboam" (v30).** The writer makes sure the reader knows why the first northern dynasty ended. Guzik, with Knapp, notes it was the first of nine dynasties in Israel's roughly two hundred years, while Judah kept one, David's.

#### Baasha Reigns in Israel (vv33–34)
- **"Baasha... began to reign over all Israel at Tirzah, and he reigned twenty-four years" (v33).** About 909–886 BC. He keeps Jeroboam's capital at Tirzah.
- **"He did what was evil... and walked in the way of Jeroboam" (v34).** The man who wiped out Jeroboam's family kept Jeroboam's religion. He was not Jeroboam's son, but as Guzik says, he was his spiritual heir. A new king did not mean a new heart. The next chapter shows God's verdict on him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Observe the ruin of the family of Jeroboam; no word of God shall fall to the ground," and "Divine threatenings are not designed merely to terrify." The Tyndale notes observe that Baasha's coup was the first of many in Israel's history.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two lines run through this chapter. In the north, dynasties rise and fall by the sword, and each new king walks in the same old sin. In the south, a king with a divided heart keeps his throne only because God promised David a lamp in Jerusalem (v4). Even good Asa, whose heart was whole, trusted silver in a crisis and doctors instead of God. The lamp kept burning through it all, not because of the kings but because of the promise, until the true Son of David came. He said, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12). Of his kingdom "there will be no end" (Luke 1:33). And his heart was wholly true to the Father in every crisis, even the one that led him across the brook Kidron to his arrest (John 18:1).
