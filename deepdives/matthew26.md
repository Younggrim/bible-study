*Setting:* Jerusalem and the Mount of Olives, from Tuesday evening to early Friday morning of Passover week, spring of AD 30 (some date it AD 33). Jesus has finished his last great teaching, the Olivet Discourse (chs 24–25). Matthew's Gospel now turns to its climax, the story of the cross, which runs through chapter 27. Passover remembered God's rescue of Israel from Egypt, when a lamb's blood on the doorposts turned away judgment (Exod 12). Through this long night Jesus is not swept along by events. He announces each step before it happens.

#### The Plot to Kill Jesus (vv1–5)
- **"When Jesus had finished all these sayings" (v1).** Matthew's formula closing each teaching block (7:28; 19:1), now with "all": the teaching is complete.
- **"After two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified" (v2).** Jesus links his death to the feast. Paul later writes, "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7).
- **"In the palace of the high priest, whose name was Caiaphas" (v3).** Joseph Caiaphas was high priest from about AD 18 to 36, an unusually long term, which suggests he worked well with Rome. His father-in-law Annas still held great influence (John 18:13). In 1990 a bone box inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas" was found south of Jerusalem.
- **"Not during the feast, lest there be an uproar among the people" (v5).** The leaders feared the crowds, many of them Galileans who admired Jesus. Judas's offer changed their plans, and it happened on the feast, on Jesus' timetable.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the irony that the leaders planned to avoid the feast, but Jesus died exactly at Passover, which shows who was really in control. JFB defers this whole chapter to its notes on Mark, Luke and John.

#### Jesus Anointed at Bethany (vv6–13)
- **"In the house of Simon the leper" (v6).** He was likely someone Jesus had healed, since a man with active leprosy could not host a dinner. John says this dinner took place six days before Passover, and that Martha served and Lazarus was at the table (John 12:1–2). Matthew places it here to set her love beside the plot and the betrayal. A different anointing happened earlier in Galilee (Luke 7:36–50).
- **"A woman… with an alabaster flask of very expensive ointment" (v7).** John names her as Mary, the sister of Martha and Lazarus, and says the ointment was pure nard, imported from the Himalayas, worth about three hundred denarii, close to a year's wages (John 12:3–5). She "poured it on his head," as kings and priests were anointed (1 Sam 10:1; Ps 133:2). John adds that she anointed his feet too.
- **"Why this waste?" (v8).** The disciples are indignant. John says the complaint came from Judas, who kept the money bag and stole from it (John 12:4–6). "Waste" is *apōleia*, "destruction," and John calls Judas "the son of destruction" with the same word (John 17:12).
- **"You always have the poor with you, but you will not always have me" (v11).** This echoes Deuteronomy 15:11, which goes on: "therefore I command you, 'You shall open wide your hand… to the needy and to the poor.'" Jesus is not dismissing the poor (25:35–40). This moment is unique.
- **"To prepare me for burial" (v12).** Bodies were anointed with spices before burial. When the women came to anoint his body after the crucifixion, he had risen (Mark 16:1), so this was the only anointing he received.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Where there is true love in the heart to Jesus Christ, nothing will be thought too good to bestow upon him." JFB, on Mark, calls her act "a noble testimony, embodying a principle of immense importance." Guzik identifies the woman as Mary of Bethany from John 12 and draws the lesson that devotion to Christ can look extravagant, even foolish, to onlookers.

#### Judas to Betray Jesus (vv14–16)
- **"Then one of the twelve, whose name was Judas Iscariot" (v14).** "Then" follows straight on from the anointing. "Iscariot" may mean "man of Kerioth," a town in Judea.
- **"What will you give me if I deliver him over to you?" (v15).** Judas makes the offer. Scripture names greed as part of his motive (John 12:6), and John and Luke say Satan entered him (Luke 22:3; John 13:27). Some suggest disappointment with a Messiah who spoke of dying. The Gospels do not explain fully.
- **"They paid him thirty pieces of silver" (v15).** Thirty shekels of silver was the price the law set as compensation when an ox gored a slave to death (Exod 21:31). In Zechariah 11:12–13, the rejected shepherd of Israel is paid off with thirty pieces of silver, "the lordly price at which I was priced by them," and throws it into the house of the LORD. Only Matthew gives the amount (27:3–10).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is not the lack, but the love of money, that is the root of all evil." Guzik weighs several theories about Judas's motives and concludes that Scripture points chiefly to greed, adding that many have sold Jesus for far less than thirty coins.

#### The Passover with the Disciples (vv17–25)
- **"On the first day of Unleavened Bread" (v17).** Passover and the week of Unleavened Bread were treated as one feast (Exod 12:15–20). This is Thursday, when the lambs were sacrificed.
- **A note on the timing.** Matthew, Mark and Luke present the Last Supper as a Passover meal. John seems to place the crucifixion on the day the lambs were killed, "the day of Preparation of the Passover" (John 19:14), so that Jesus dies as the lambs die. Proposed solutions include different calendars or John's "Passover" meaning the whole festival week. No answer is certain. All four Gospels agree that Jesus died at Passover as the true Passover Lamb.
- **"Go into the city to a certain man" (v18).** Jesus keeps the place secret, likely so Judas could not tell the leaders in advance. Mark adds a man carrying a water jar who led them to an upper room (Mark 14:13–15). **"My time is at hand."**
- **"One of you will betray me" (v21).** Each disciple asks, **"Is it I, Lord?"** (v22). The Greek form of the question expects the answer no: "Surely not I, Lord?" None of them suspected Judas.
- **"He who has dipped his hand in the dish with me" (v23).** Diners shared bowls, so this points to someone close. Psalm 41:9 had said, "Even my close friend… who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me" (John 13:18).
- **"The Son of Man goes as it is written of him, but woe to that man" (v24).** God's plan and Judas's full responsibility stand together.
- **"Is it I, Rabbi?" "You have said so" (v25).** The others called him "Lord." Judas calls him "Rabbi," only "teacher." Jesus' answer (*sy eipas*) is a polite way of saying "yes, it is as you say," while leaving the words in the questioner's mouth. He will say the same to Caiaphas (v64).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees Jesus' quiet "You have said so" as one last appeal for Judas to turn back. He notes that the Synoptics and John raise questions of chronology but thinks the meaning matters more than solving them.

#### Institution of the Lord's Supper (vv26–29)
- **The Passover order.** The meal followed a set order: the exodus story, the Hallel Psalms (113–118), bitter herbs, unleavened bread, the lamb, and four cups of wine. Jesus takes the bread and a cup and gives them new meaning.
- **"Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it" (v26).** The bread was unleavened, which Deuteronomy calls "the bread of affliction" (Deut 16:3). **"Take, eat; this is my body."** The host explained the foods; Jesus explains the bread as his own body.
- **"He took a cup, and when he had given thanks" (v27).** "Given thanks" is *eucharisteō*, from which the church took the name "Eucharist" for the Lord's Supper. This was probably the third cup, "the cup of blessing" (1 Cor 10:16).
- **"This is my blood of the covenant" (v28).** At Sinai, Moses sprinkled the people with the blood of sacrifices and said, "Behold the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you" (Exod 24:8). Jeremiah promised a "new covenant," in which God says, "I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (Jer 31:31–34). **"Poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins."** "Many" echoes the Servant of Isaiah 53:12, who "bore the sin of many" (20:28). Matthew alone adds "for the forgiveness of sins."
- **How Christians understand "this is."** Roman Catholics hold that the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood in substance (transubstantiation). Lutherans hold that Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine. Reformed Christians hold that Christ is truly present spiritually and received by faith. Many Baptists and others see the meal mainly as a memorial and proclamation of his death (1 Cor 11:26). All agree that the Lord commanded his people to share it, and that it points to his death for them.
- **"I will not drink again of this fruit of the vine until… my Father's kingdom" (v29).** Jesus looks beyond the cross to the great feast at the end, "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Rev 19:9; Matt 8:11).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Take, eat; accept of Christ as he is offered to you." Guzik sets out the Catholic, Lutheran, Calvinist and Zwinglian views fairly, notes that "Eucharist" comes from the Greek for "giving thanks," which the lexicon confirms, and urges believers not to live as though the new covenant had never been made.

#### Jesus Foretells Peter's Denial (vv30–35)
- **"When they had sung a hymn" (v30).** This was almost certainly the second part of the Hallel, Psalms 115–118, which closed the Passover meal. Jesus sang on his way to the cross, "The LORD is on my side; I will not fear" (Ps 118:6).
- **"You will all fall away because of me this night" (v31).** "Fall away" is *skandalizō*, to stumble. Jesus quotes Zechariah 13:7: "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered." In Zechariah it is God himself who calls for the sword.
- **"After I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee" (v32).** Even before they fail, he promises to restore them. He will meet them in Galilee (28:7, 16).
- **"Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away" (v33).** **"Before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times"** (v34). Mark says "before the rooster crows twice" (Mark 14:30).
- **"Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you!" And all the disciples said the same (v35).** They mean it, and before morning they all flee (v56).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Jesus led his disciples in singing just before his arrest, and that Peter's problem was trusting his own courage.

#### Jesus Prays in Gethsemane (vv36–46)
- **"A place called Gethsemane" (v36).** The name is Aramaic for "oil press." It was an olive grove on the lower slope of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron Valley from the temple. Jesus often went there, so Judas knew where to find him (John 18:2).
- **"Taking with him Peter and the two sons of Zebedee" (v37).** The same three saw his glory on the mountain (17:1). Now they see his anguish. **"He began to be sorrowful and troubled."**
- **"My soul is very sorrowful, even to death" (v38).** He echoes Psalms 42–43, "Why are you cast down, O my soul?" Luke adds that his sweat fell "like great drops of blood" (Luke 22:44).
- **"My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (v39).** "The cup" is the cup of God's wrath against sin, poured out in the prophets on the guilty (Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15; 20:22). Jesus did not fear pain as such. He shrank from bearing the judgment for the sins of the world and being forsaken by his Father (27:46). His prayer shows a real human will that recoils from that, and a perfect submission of that will to the Father. It is the Lord's Prayer lived out: "Your will be done" (6:10; 26:42).
- **"Could you not watch with me one hour?" (v40).** **"Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak" (v41).** "Watch" is the word of the Olivet Discourse (24:42; 25:13).
- **"If this cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done" (v42).** The second prayer moves from "if it be possible" to acceptance. He prays a third time "saying the same words again" (v44).
- **"Sleep and take your rest later on… Rise, let us be going" (vv45–46).** The Greek can be a question ("Are you still sleeping?") or a rueful command. Jesus rises to meet the betrayer. He does not run.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes simply that "he prayed that, if possible, the cup might pass from him," and yet submitted. Guzik says the battle of the cross was won here in prayer, before the arrest, and notes the name means "olive press."

#### Betrayal and Arrest of Jesus (vv47–56)
- **"A great crowd with swords and clubs" (v47).** This was a mixed force: the temple police with clubs, servants of the high priest, and (John says) a detachment of Roman soldiers (John 18:3, 12).
- **"The one I will kiss is the man" (v48).** In the dark the soldiers needed to know whom to seize. A disciple greeted his rabbi with a kiss of respect. **"Greetings, Rabbi!" And he kissed him** (v49).
- **"Friend, do what you came to do" (v50).** "Friend" (*hetairos*) is the same cool word used in 20:13 and 22:12. The Greek phrase that follows is unusual and can be read as a command ("do what you came for") or a question ("why are you here?" as in the KJV). Either way, Jesus is in control.
- **"Drew his sword and struck the servant of the high priest and cut off his ear" (v51).** John names the swordsman as Peter and the servant as Malchus (John 18:10). Luke, the doctor, says Jesus healed it (Luke 22:51).
- **"All who take the sword will perish by the sword" (v52).** Jesus will not be defended by violence. His kingdom does not advance by the sword (John 18:36).
- **"More than twelve legions of angels" (v53).** A Roman legion had about 6,000 soldiers, so this would be over 70,000 angels.
- **"How then should the Scriptures be fulfilled, that it must be so?" (v54).** The arrest is not a defeat but the path the Scriptures foretold (Isa 53:7–12; Zech 13:7).
- **"Have you come out as against a robber…?" (v55).** "Robber" (*lēstēs*) was a word for a violent rebel or bandit.
- **"Then all the disciples left him and fled" (v56).** The scattering of the sheep that Jesus foretold (v31) happens at once.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "No enemies are so much to be abhorred as those professed disciples that betray Christ with a kiss." Guzik observes that Jesus calmly accepted arrest, identifies the swordsman as Peter from John, and draws the lesson that prayer and the word are stronger weapons than the sword.

#### Jesus Before Caiaphas and the Council (vv57–68)
- **"Led him to Caiaphas the high priest" (v57).** John says Jesus was first taken to Annas for questioning (John 18:13, 19–24). Then the council met at Caiaphas's house during the night, and a formal session followed at daybreak (27:1).
- **"Peter was following him at a distance" (v58).** He followed, more than the others did, but at a distance.
- **"The whole council were seeking false testimony" (v59).** The council, or Sanhedrin, was the highest Jewish court, with seventy members plus the high priest. Later Jewish law, recorded in the Mishnah around AD 200, forbids capital trials at night, on feast days, or with a verdict on the same day. Whether those rules were in force in AD 30 is debated, but even by the standards of the law of Moses, a court that searched for false witnesses was perverting justice (Exod 23:1–2). The law required two witnesses to agree (Deut 19:15), and Mark says their testimony did not agree (Mark 14:56).
- **"I am able to destroy the temple of God, and to rebuild it in three days" (v61).** Jesus had said something like this, but about his body: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19–21). The charge twisted his words.
- **"But Jesus remained silent" (v63).** This fulfills Isaiah 53:7: "like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth."
- **"I adjure you by the living God, tell us if you are the Christ, the Son of God" (v63).** Caiaphas places Jesus under oath.
- **"You have said so. But I tell you, from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven" (v64).** "You have said so" is a yes (Mark 14:62 has "I am"). Then Jesus joins two passages: Psalm 110:1, where David's Lord sits at God's right hand (22:44), and Daniel 7:13, the Son of Man coming with the clouds (24:30). "Power" was a reverent way of saying "God."
- **"The high priest tore his robes" (v65).** Tearing clothes showed grief or horror (2 Kgs 18:37). Leviticus 21:10 forbade the high priest to tear his garments in mourning, but later Jewish law required judges to tear their clothes on hearing blasphemy. **"He has uttered blasphemy."** To the council, a bound prisoner claiming to share God's throne was blasphemy, which the law punished with death (Lev 24:16). If his claim was true, it was not blasphemy but the truth.
- **"They spit in his face and struck him… 'Prophesy to us, you Christ! Who is it that struck you?'" (vv67–68).** Mark and Luke say they had blindfolded him (Mark 14:65; Luke 22:64). Isaiah had written, "I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting" (Isa 50:6). They mock him as a false prophet while his prophecy about Peter comes true in the courtyard.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "When Christ was made sin for us, he was silent, and left it to his blood to speak." JFB (on Mark) notes that the witnesses failed to agree, that the oath made an answer legally necessary, and that by answering Jesus witnessed "the good confession" (1 Tim 6:13). Guzik lists ways the trial broke Jewish legal practice and notes that "Power" was a reverent substitute for God's name.

#### Peter Denies Jesus (vv69–75)
- **"Peter was sitting outside in the courtyard" (v69).** While Jesus faced the council inside, Peter warmed himself by a fire with the servants (Mark 14:54; John 18:18).
- **Three denials, each stronger.** First, before a servant girl: **"I do not know what you mean"** (v70), an evasion. Second, at the gateway, before another girl: **"I do not know the man"** (v72), now "with an oath." Third, before the bystanders: **"he began to invoke a curse on himself and to swear, 'I do not know the man'"** (v74). He calls down curses on himself if he is lying. He will not even say Jesus' name. The pressure came from servant girls and bystanders, not soldiers.
- **"Your accent betrays you" (v73).** Galileans spoke Aramaic with a recognizable accent. Peter could not hide where he came from.
- **"And immediately the rooster crowed" (v74).** Luke adds a detail that cuts to the heart: "the Lord turned and looked at Peter" (Luke 22:61).
- **"He went out and wept bitterly" (v75).** Both Peter and Judas failed Jesus that night. Judas was filled with remorse and took his own life (27:3–5). Peter wept and was restored. The risen Jesus asked him three times, "Do you love me?" (John 21:15–17), one question for each denial. The difference was not the size of their sin, but where each one turned afterward.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Sorrow for sin must not be slight, but great and deep." JFB, on Luke, says the immediate cause of Peter's tears was "beyond all doubt, this heart-piercing 'look' which his Lord gave him." Guzik contrasts Judas and Peter: one abandoned the truth altogether, while the other fell back but was brought home, and he sees in Peter's tears the start of repentance that begins with remembering Jesus' words.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is full of people who fail Jesus: leaders who plot, a disciple who sells him, friends who sleep, a crowd with clubs, a court that seeks lies, and a boastful follower who curses. Only a woman with a jar of ointment seems to understand. In the middle of it all Jesus takes bread and a cup and explains what is about to happen: his body given and his blood poured out "for many for the forgiveness of sins." In the garden he chooses the cup of judgment that should have been theirs, and before the council he declares himself the Son of Man who will sit at God's right hand. The night ends with Peter weeping, but even that is not the end. The shepherd struck for the scattered sheep will rise and go before them to Galilee.
