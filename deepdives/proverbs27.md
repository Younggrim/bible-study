*Setting:* The collection copied by "the men of Hezekiah" (25:1–29:27) continues. Chapter 27 is above all a chapter about friendship and the honest words that make it real: the friend's faithful wounds (v6), the sweetness of a friend's counsel (v9), the neighbor near at hand (v10), and "iron sharpens iron" (v17). Around these are sayings on boasting and praise (vv1–2, 21), on what the heart reveals (v19) and on desires that are never satisfied (v20). The chapter closes with a short poem, set off by the ESV as its own paragraph, telling a herdsman to know his flocks well, because riches don't last but faithful care provides for a household year after year (vv23–27). The ESV has no headings inside the collection, so the headings below follow its groupings.

#### Tomorrow, Praise and Heavy Things (vv1–4)
- **"Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring" (v1).** "What a day may bring" is literally "what a day will give birth to." Each day carries events no one can see coming. James builds on this: "you do not know what tomorrow will bring… Instead you ought to say, 'If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that'" (Jas 4:13–15). Jesus' rich fool planned bigger barns and died that night (Luke 12:16–21). The Tyndale notes: plan, but plans succeed only if God wills. Henry adds a pastoral edge: don't presume on tomorrow, and don't delay turning to God (2 Cor 6:2).
- **"Let another praise you, and not your own mouth; a stranger, and not your own lips" (v2).** In Hebrew "boast" (v1) and "praise" (v2) come from the same verb, as the Tyndale notes point out. Boasting is praising yourself. If praise is deserved, let someone else give it. Paul: "it is not the one who commends himself who is approved, but the one whom the Lord commends" (2 Cor 10:18). Henry: "There may be occasion for us to justify ourselves, but not to praise ourselves."
- **"A stone is heavy, and sand is weighty, but a fool's provocation is heavier than both" (v3).** Anyone who has carried rocks or sandbags knows the weight. A fool's irritation, the trouble his anger causes others, weighs more. JFB: a fool's anger is "unreasonable and excessive." Henry: those who can't command their passions "sink under the load."
- **"Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming, but who can stand before jealousy?" (v4).** "Overwhelming" is literally "a flood." Rage is fierce but often passes. Jealousy smolders and is almost impossible to reason with. A husband's jealousy over adultery "will not spare" (6:34). JFB: jealousy is "more unappeasable than the simpler bad passions." Guzik points out that it was "out of envy" that the leaders handed Jesus over (Matt 27:18). Cain's jealousy of Abel led to the first murder (Gen 4:5–8).
- **Commentators:** Guzik is careful that v1 forbids presumption, not planning, and the Tyndale notes say the same with 24:27. JFB and Guzik both read v4 as jealousy being worse than open anger because it hides and won't be reasoned with.

#### Faithful Wounds and a Friend's Counsel (vv5–10)
- **"Better is open rebuke than hidden love" (v5).** Love that never speaks up when a friend is going wrong is not much use. JFB: love not shown in action is useless. Leviticus 19:17 links the two: "You shall reason frankly with your neighbor, lest you incur sin because of him."
- **"Faithful are the wounds of a friend; profuse are the kisses of an enemy" (v6).** A friend's honest correction hurts, but it is "faithful," trustworthy and meant for your good. An enemy can be all kisses and flattery. "Profuse" translates a rare word that the King James rendered "deceitful." Either sense fits: the kisses are many and they lie. Joab kissed Amasa while stabbing him (2 Sam 20:9–10). Judas betrayed Jesus with a kiss (Luke 22:47–48). Nathan's rebuke wounded David and saved him (2 Sam 12). Paul's sharp letter grieved the Corinthians and led them to repentance (2 Cor 7:8–10). The Tyndale notes: a friend's wounds come from caring correction. Henry: "Plain and faithful rebukes are better" than secret hatred or flattery that encourages sin.
- **"One who is full loathes honey, but to one who is hungry everything bitter is sweet" (v7).** Hunger is the best sauce. The well-fed turn up their nose at honey. The starving will eat anything. JFB: luxury brings less happiness than the healthy appetite that comes from work. The Tyndale notes apply it to learning: even the best teaching is wasted on someone who isn't hungry for it. Jesus: "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied" (Matt 5:6).
- **"Like a bird that strays from its nest is a man who strays from his home" (v8).** A bird away from its nest is exposed, and its nest is unguarded. "Strays" suggests running away or wandering off, not a planned move. The prodigal son left home and ended up feeding pigs (Luke 15:13–16), as the Tyndale notes observe. JFB: such a man is "out of duty and in danger." Henry: "Every man has his proper place." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's warning against church-hoppers who never settle anywhere.
- **"Oil and perfume make the heart glad, and the sweetness of a friend comes from his earnest counsel" (v9).** Perfumed oil was used at feasts and to refresh guests (Ps 23:5; Luke 7:46). A friend's sincere advice is just as refreshing. The second line is difficult in Hebrew and is read several ways, but the ESV's sense is widely followed. JFB: "wise counsel is also pleasing."
- **"Do not forsake your friend and your father's friend… Better is a neighbor who is near than a brother who is far away" (v10).** Old family friendships are worth keeping. Solomon kept his father's friendship with Hiram of Tyre (1 Kgs 5:1). His son Rehoboam threw out his father's advisers and lost most of the kingdom (1 Kgs 12:6–19). In a crisis, a nearby neighbor may help faster and more willingly than a relative far away, whether in miles or in heart. This runs against a culture that put family first, and JFB notes blood ties can be less reliable than "genuine friendship." Compare 18:24. Henry: don't depend on relatives "merely for kindred's sake," and above all trust God, the friend closest of all.
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Bridges' observation that Judas' kiss was a bitter part of the Savior's cup, and Trapp's list of deceitful kissers: Joab, Judas, Absalom. On v10 Guzik uses Solomon, Hiram and Rehoboam. JFB and Henry both read v10 as friendship tested by need.

#### A Glad Father, a Loud Blessing and a Dripping Roof (vv11–16)
- **"Be wise, my son, and make my heart glad, that I may answer him who reproaches me" (v11).** A wise child is a parent's best answer to critics. The Tyndale notes: people judge parents by how children behave. Henry turns it to the church: "The good conduct of Christians is the best answer to all who find fault with the gospel" (1 Pet 2:12).
- **"The prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go on and suffer for it" (v12).** Repeats 22:3 almost exactly. Henry: thrusting ourselves into temptation leads to sin.
- **"Take a man's garment when he has put up security for a stranger, and hold it in pledge when he puts up security for an adulteress" (v13).** Repeats 20:16, here with "adulteress" in the second line (the manuscripts of 20:16 vary between the two). The point: don't lend to someone foolish enough to guarantee strangers' debts without taking security yourself.
- **"Whoever blesses his neighbor with a loud voice, rising early in the morning, will be counted as cursing" (v14).** Shouting greetings at dawn outside someone's window is not a blessing. Over-the-top praise, badly timed, comes across as insincere or annoying, even hostile. JFB: excessive zeal in praising "raises suspicions of selfishness." Henry: being fond of praise is "a temptation to pride." The Tyndale notes: speak fittingly for the moment (15:23).
- **"A continual dripping on a rainy day and a quarrelsome wife are alike; to restrain her is to restrain the wind or to grasp oil in one's right hand" (vv15–16).** Building on 19:13. Flat clay roofs leaked in the winter rains, and the drip never stopped. Trying to stop the quarreling is like holding wind or gripping oil: it slips through the fingers. Henry contrasts a neighbor's quarrel, which passes like a shower, with a wife's, which is like constant rain. Guzik, writing to husbands, says the answer is to love as Christ loves the church (Eph 5:25) and leave the changing of hearts to God. The saying is addressed to men, but quarrelsome husbands are no easier to live with.
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on an old story of a courtier rebuked for flattering an emperor, as an illustration of v14, and Morgan's and Bridges' point that true friendship needs no loud protests. JFB gives only cross-references for vv12–13.

#### Iron Sharpens Iron (vv17–22)
- **"Iron sharpens iron, and one man sharpens another" (v17).** One iron tool can hone another's edge. "Sharpens another" is literally "sharpens the face of his friend," and Guzik passes on Kidner's note that "face" here nearly means the whole personality. People grow sharper through honest, sometimes abrasive, conversation with others. The Tyndale notes: wisdom comes from interacting, often critically, with a good friend. JFB: conversation "promotes intelligence," which shows in the face. Paul and Barnabas sharpened each other, sometimes painfully (Acts 15:39; Gal 2:13). Hebrews: "let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works" (Heb 10:24). Henry adds a warning: choose companions carefully, because people shape each other for bad as well as good.
- **"Whoever tends a fig tree will eat its fruit, and he who guards his master will be honored" (v18).** Fig trees take years of care before they bear well, and the one who tends them gets the fruit. The same goes for a faithful servant. The Tyndale notes connect Paul's teaching that workers deserve their wages (1 Cor 9:7–10). JFB: diligence is rewarded "even for the humble servant." Jesus: "If anyone serves me, the Father will honor him" (John 12:26). Henry: "God is a Master who has engaged to honour those who serve him faithfully."
- **"As in water face reflects face, so the heart of man reflects the man" (v19).** The Hebrew is extremely short, literally "as water the face to the face, so the heart of man to man." Readings differ. (1) Your heart shows who you really are, as water shows your face. (2) Looking into another person's heart, you see your own reflected, because human hearts are alike. (3) Looking into your own heart is how you come to know yourself. JFB takes the second: we see our own character in others. Henry: compare your heart with God's word, which James calls a mirror (Jas 1:23–25). Guzik passes on Kidner's note that the Hebrew is deliberately compressed.
- **"Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied, and never satisfied are the eyes of man" (v20).** Sheol is the grave, and Abaddon ("destruction") its depths (15:11). Death always has room for one more. Human eyes are the same, always wanting what they see next. Ecclesiastes: "the eye is not satisfied with seeing" (Eccl 1:8). Eve "saw that the tree… was a delight to the eyes" (Gen 3:6), as Guzik passes on from Waltke. John: "the desires of the eyes" (1 John 2:16). JFB: greed is "as insatiable as the grave." Henry: "Two things are here said to be never satisfied, death and sin." The answer is to find satisfaction in God: "I shall be satisfied with your likeness" (Ps 17:15).
- **"The crucible is for silver, and the furnace is for gold, and a man is tested by his praise" (v21).** The first line repeats 17:3. Where 17:3 says "the LORD tests hearts," this says praise tests a person. How someone handles praise shows what they're made of, whether it puffs them up or they stay humble. Saul was tested and failed when the women sang "David his ten thousands" (1 Sam 18:7–8). Herod accepted praise as a god and was struck down (Acts 12:21–23). JFB: "Praise tests character": the vain seek it, the weak are inflated by it, the wise disregard it. The Tyndale notes: staying humble when praised shows pure character.
- **"Crush a fool in a mortar with a pestle along with crushed grain, yet his folly will not depart from him" (v22).** Grain was pounded in a stone bowl with a heavy pestle to remove the husk. You could pound the fool along with the grain, and the folly still wouldn't come off. Punishment alone doesn't change a heart. JFB: stubborn wickedness is "incurable by the heaviest inflictions." Guzik passes on Waltke's observation that prisons often harden criminals. Henry: "The new-creating power of God's grace alone is able to make a change" (Ezek 36:26).
- **Commentators:** Guzik offers Morgan's three readings of v21: how a person handles praise, what a person praises, and praise as a refiner's fire. JFB and Henry read v19 differently: JFB as seeing ourselves in others, Henry as examining our own hearts by God's word.

#### Know Well Your Flocks (vv23–27)
- **"Know well the condition of your flocks, and give attention to your herds" (v23).** "Know well" doubles the verb: really know. "Give attention" is literally "set your heart on." A good shepherd knows every animal and its health. Delegating everything is risky. Boaz was out in his own fields with his workers (Ruth 2:4). Jesus: "I know my own and my own know me" (John 10:14). Peter told elders, "shepherd the flock of God that is among you" (1 Pet 5:2).
- **"For riches do not last forever; and does a crown endure to all generations?" (v24).** Money disappears (23:5), and even royal dynasties end. These words were copied in Hezekiah's court, by men who had watched the northern kingdom fall to Assyria (2 Kgs 17). What lasts is steady, faithful care of what is in front of you. JFB: lasting possessions require care.
- **"When the grass is gone and the new growth appears and the vegetation of the mountains is gathered" (v25).** The cycle of the seasons: the first hay is cut, new grass sprouts, and hill pasture is gathered as winter fodder. Faithful work moves with God's rhythms of growth. JFB connects Psalm 104:14: God "causes the grass to grow for the livestock."
- **"The lambs will provide your clothing, and the goats the price of a field. There will be enough goats' milk for your food, for the food of your household and maintenance for your girls" (vv26–27).** Wool for clothes, goats to sell for more land, milk for the whole household including servant girls. A well-kept flock provides year after year in a way a pile of money doesn't. The Tyndale notes: farm property can provide for generations, but only with constant care. Henry: "We ought to have some business to do in this world," worked diligently, trusting God to bless it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Kidner's point that the poem shows human labor and God's provision working together, and Trapp's note that "set your heart" means personal attention, not handing it off. JFB: providence supplies enough to those who use the means well.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 27 is full of friendship, and Jesus is the friend it describes. His wounds are faithful (v6): he rebukes and disciplines those he loves (Rev 3:19), and he bore far deeper wounds for us, "wounded for our transgressions" (Isa 53:5), while an enemy's kiss betrayed him (Luke 22:48). He is the friend whose counsel is sweeter than perfume (v9), the near neighbor who comes in the day of calamity (v10; Heb 13:5), and the one who sharpens his people as iron sharpens iron (v17). Where the eyes of man are never satisfied (v20), he says, "whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst" (John 6:35). And where crowns don't endure to all generations (v24), he is the Good Shepherd who knows his flock well (v23; John 10:14) and whose kingdom "shall never be destroyed" (Dan 2:44).
