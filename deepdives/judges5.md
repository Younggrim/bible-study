*Setting:* Chapter 4 told the story of the victory over Sisera in prose. Chapter 5 sings it. The Song of Deborah is one of the oldest poems in the Bible, and most scholars, conservative and critical alike, date it close to the events, perhaps in the 12th century BC. Its Hebrew is old and difficult, with rare words whose meanings are uncertain, and English translations differ more here than almost anywhere in the historical books. Like the Song of Moses at the Red Sea (Exod 15), it was composed to help Israel remember. It praises God first, then the tribes who fought, then Jael. It shames the tribes who stayed home, and ends with a haunting scene in the enemy's house.

#### The Song of Deborah and Barak (vv1–31)
- **"Then sang Deborah and Barak" (v1).** The verb is feminine singular in Hebrew, which suggests Deborah as the main singer, with Barak joining. JFB calls it "evidently the composition of Deborah herself." The song is "on that day," the immediate response to victory.
- **"That the leaders took the lead in Israel" (v2).** The Hebrew is obscure. It may mean "when leaders led," or "when locks of hair were let loose," which some connect to warriors who left their hair uncut as a sign of a vow to God, like the Nazirites (Num 6:5). Either way the theme is the same: leaders led and "the people offered themselves willingly." That pairing is repeated in v9. Both sides answered God's call, and both are cause to "bless the LORD!"
- **"Hear, O kings; give ear, O princes" (v3).** The song calls on foreign rulers to listen. The God of Israel has acted, and kings should take note.
- **"LORD, when you went out from Seir" (vv4–5).** The song looks back to God marching from the south, from Edom and Sinai, with earthquake and storm (Deut 33:2; Ps 68:7–8; Hab 3:3). The God who shook Sinai is the same God who fought for Israel by the Kishon. "The heavens dropped, yes, the clouds dropped water" points ahead to the storm that stopped Sisera's chariots.
- **"The highways were abandoned" (v6).** Life under Jabin. Roads were too dangerous to travel, and people used back paths. "In the days of Shamgar… in the days of Jael" dates the trouble before Jael's act.
- **"The villagers ceased in Israel" (v7).** The Hebrew word (*perazon*) is rare and uncertain. It may mean village life, the unwalled settlements of the countryside, or perhaps warriors. Either way, ordinary Israelite life collapsed until "I, Deborah, arose as a mother in Israel." She did not lead as a general. She cared for the nation as a mother.
- **"When new gods were chosen, then war was in the gates" (v8).** The cause of the trouble, stated in one line. Idolatry brought war to the city gates. JFB: "idolatry was the cause of foreign invasion and internal inability to resist it." "Was shield or spear to be seen among forty thousand in Israel?" Israel was disarmed, much like under the Philistines later (1 Sam 13:19–22). The word translated "thousand" (*'eleph*) can also mean a clan or military unit, so the number may be smaller than it looks.
- **"Tell of it, you who ride on white donkeys" (vv10–11).** Everyone is called to tell the story: the wealthy who ride fine donkeys and sit on rich carpets, and the poor who walk. "At the watering places" where shepherds met, the story would be retold. It celebrates "the righteous triumphs of the LORD." The Hebrew *tsidqot* means "righteous acts," God doing what is right by rescuing his people. This is how much of the Old Testament was first passed on, sung and retold where people gathered.
- **"Awake, awake, Deborah!… Arise, Barak" (v12).** A call to battle, remembered in the song.
- **The roll call of tribes (vv13–18).** The heart of the song. Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir (the western half of Manasseh, Josh 17:1), Zebulun, Issachar and Naphtali came. Issachar is named as "faithful to Barak," and Zebulun and Naphtali "risked their lives to the death" (v18). Chapter 4 mentioned only Zebulun and Naphtali, so the song shows that more tribes joined. Judah and Simeon in the far south are not mentioned at all.
- **"Among the clans of Reuben there were great searchings of heart" (vv15–16).** A sharp line, said twice. Reuben thought long and hard, but stayed among the sheep, listening to shepherds' pipes instead of the war trumpet. Henry: "She observes who kept away, and did not side with Israel, as might have been expected."
- **Gilead, Dan, Asher (v17).** Gilead (Gad and eastern Manasseh) "stayed beyond the Jordan." Dan "stayed with the ships," perhaps working for the coastal peoples. Asher "sat still at the coast of the sea." These tribes had their own business to tend. Henry: "Narrow, selfish spirits care not what becomes of God's church."
- **"The kings came, they fought… at Taanach, by the waters of Megiddo" (v19).** The battle was in the Jezreel Valley, near these Canaanite cities that Manasseh had failed to take (1:27). "They got no spoils of silver." The kings who came for plunder went home empty.
- **"From heaven the stars fought" (v20).** Poetic language for God's power over creation fighting for Israel. Many ancient peoples believed the stars controlled rain. Here they serve the LORD, and the storm comes.
- **"The torrent Kishon swept them away" (v21).** This is how the battle was won. A sudden storm flooded the Kishon, the chariots bogged down, and the river swept men away. The battle fits the Red Sea pattern: God uses water to destroy an army of chariots (Exod 14:23–28). "March on, my soul, with might!"
- **"Then loud beat the horses' hoofs" (v22).** The Hebrew sounds like galloping: *daharot daharot*. Horses run in panic. JFB says it denotes "the hot haste and heavy irregular tramp of the routed foe."
- **"Curse Meroz, says the angel of the LORD" (v23).** Meroz was apparently an Israelite town near the battle that refused to help. Its location is unknown, and it is never mentioned again. The reason is pointed: "they did not come to the help of the LORD." God did not need their help, but they refused to take part in his work. It is the harshest line in the song, harsher than anything said to Reuben.
- **"Most blessed of women be Jael" (v24).** Jael is praised as a woman who took Israel's side. The words "blessed among women" are later spoken of Mary (Luke 1:42). The song praises her loyalty to God's people, not her act of hospitality broken. The question of Jael's methods is discussed in chapter 4.
- **"He asked for water and she gave him milk" (v25).** "Curds in a noble's bowl," a rich drink, offered with honor. JFB explains it as "curdled milk; a favorite beverage in the East."
- **"She crushed his head" (v26).** The song piles up verbs of violence: she struck, crushed, shattered, pierced. Then the slow fall of the general: "he sank, he fell, he lay still… where he sank, there he fell — dead" (v27). The repeated phrases slow the action to a frame-by-frame picture.
- **"Out of the window she peered, the mother of Sisera" (v28).** The song turns from the tent to a palace. Sisera's mother waits at the window, "wailing through the lattice." Two mothers stand in the song: Deborah, "a mother in Israel," and Sisera's mother, waiting for a son who will never come home.
- **"Have they not found and divided the spoil?" (vv29–30).** Her princesses comfort her, and she repeats their answer to herself. Her son is late because he is dividing the plunder: "a womb or two for every man," a crude word for captive women, and fine embroidered cloth. The listener already knows the truth. Sisera lies dead in a woman's tent, and the women he meant to take are safe. The irony is complete.
- **"So may all your enemies perish, O LORD! But your friends be like the sun as he rises in his might" (v31).** The song ends with a prayer. "Your friends" is literally "those who love him." The contrast is between those who oppose God and those who love him. Henry says they "shall shine for ever as the sun in the firmament," and Jesus uses the same picture: "the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (Matt 13:43).
- **"The land had rest for forty years" (v31).** The standard closing of a judge's story (3:11, 30; 8:28).
- **Commentators:** Henry treats the song as a model of praise, quick and wholehearted, giving the credit to God alone. He blames Israel's distress on new gods and lists fear, love of ease and worldly business as the reasons people stayed home, warning, "we cannot keep away from the contest between the Lord and his enemies." Guzik takes v2 as the pattern of blessing: leaders who lead and people who follow willingly. He notes that Meroz was cursed not because God needed it, but because it refused to share in God's work, and he observes that Jael's loyalty to God's cause outweighed the custom of hospitality in the song's eyes. He also points out that the word used for captive women in v30 is crude, reflecting the conquerors' contempt. JFB reads vv20–21 as "a fearful tempest burst upon them and threw them into disorder," and describes Sisera's mother as "agitated between hope and fear." All three agree the song gives God the victory.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Song of Deborah shows that God's victories are meant to be sung, retold "at the watering places" so the next generation hears them. The church still does this. It sings of the greater victory of the cross and resurrection, where Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Col 2:15). The song's call to "come to the help of the LORD" still rings, not because God needs anyone, but because he invites his people to share in his work. And its last prayer, that those who love God would "be like the sun as he rises in his might," points ahead to Christ, whose face on the mountain "shone like the sun" (Matt 17:2), and whose people will shine with him.
