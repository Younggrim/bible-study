*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Verse 1 notes it specifically: "on Mount Sinai." The people are still in the wilderness and own no land at all, yet God gives them laws for a farming life in Canaan. Chapter 23 set the weekly Sabbath and the yearly feasts. Chapter 25 extends the Sabbath pattern to years: every seventh year the land rests, and after seven of those cycles comes the Year of Jubilee, when land returns to its families and Israelites in debt-service go free. Behind it all is one sentence: "the land is mine" (v23). The chapter is God's plan to keep poverty from becoming permanent and to remind Israel that they, and everything they hold, belong to him.

#### The Sabbath Year (vv1–7)
- **"The land shall keep a Sabbath to the LORD" (v2).** The land itself gets a Sabbath. For six years Israel would farm, and in the seventh year **"you shall not sow your field or prune your vineyard"** (v4). Exodus 23:10–11 gave the first form of this law, and Deuteronomy 15:1–2 adds that debts between Israelites were released in the same year.
- **"You shall not reap what grows of itself" (v5).** Grain springs up on its own from seed dropped the year before, and vines still bear fruit. Owners could not harvest it as a crop. But **"the Sabbath of the land shall provide food for you"** (v6): everyone, owner, slave, hired worker, foreigner, livestock and wild animals, could eat what grew, day by day. For one year, rich and poor ate from the same field.
- **Why a fallow year?** Resting the soil helps it recover, and ancient farmers knew it. But the reasons the text gives are about God. The year was **"a Sabbath to the LORD"** (v4). It trained Israel to trust him for food and reminded them the land was his.
- **Did Israel keep it?** Not faithfully. Chapter 26 warns that if Israel disobeyed, God would scatter them, and "then the land shall enjoy its Sabbaths" (26:34). Second Chronicles says the Babylonian exile lasted "until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths… to fulfill seventy years" (2 Chr 36:21). After the exile, Nehemiah's generation promised to keep the seventh year (Neh 10:31), and Jewish sources record it being observed in the centuries that followed (1 Macc 6:49).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the Sabbath year as a lesson that the land belonged to God and that Israel depended on him, and also as sound farming. He ties Israel's neglect of it to the length of the exile (2 Chr 36:20–21). JFB says the land "lay entirely fallow," and calls it "a very peculiar arrangement," with the produce shared among the poor, strangers and animals.

#### The Year of Jubilee (vv8–22)
- **"Seven weeks of years… forty-nine years" (v8).** As Pentecost came after seven weeks of days (23:15–16), Jubilee came after seven weeks of years.
- **"Sound the loud trumpet on the tenth day of the seventh month… on the Day of Atonement" (v9).** The Jubilee began on the day when Israel's sins were covered. Release came after atonement. The trumpet was the *shofar*, the ram's horn, and the word "jubilee" itself comes from Hebrew *yovel*, which can mean "ram" or "ram's horn" (Josh 6:4–5). The year was named after the trumpet that announced it.
- **"Proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants" (v10).** "Liberty" is *deror*, a release, especially of slaves and debts. The same word is used in Isaiah 61:1, "to proclaim liberty to the captives," and in Jeremiah 34:8–17, where Judah freed its slaves and then took them back, and God answered with judgment. The words of verse 10 in the King James Version are cast on the Liberty Bell in Philadelphia.
- **"Each of you shall return to his property and… to his clan" (v10).** Land that had been sold returned to the family that received it when Joshua divided Canaan (Josh 13–21). People who had sold themselves into service went home. Every fifty years the slate was wiped clean.
- **Forty-nine or fifty?** If the Jubilee was the fiftieth year, it came straight after the seventh Sabbath year, so the land would lie fallow two years running. Some scholars think the fiftieth year was counted inclusively and fell in the forty-ninth year. Others take "fiftieth" at face value, and the promise of verses 20–22 fits either. JFB says the "prevailing opinion" favored the fiftieth.
- **"You shall not wrong one another" (vv14–17).** Since land went back at the Jubilee, a "sale" was really a lease of the harvests until then. **"It is the number of the crops that he is selling to you"** (v16). The price had to be fair, more if the Jubilee was far off and less if it was near. **"But you shall fear your God"** (v17). The buyer could not exploit the seller's need.
- **"What shall we eat in the seventh year?" (vv20–22).** God expects the question. His answer: **"I will command my blessing on you in the sixth year, so that it will produce a crop sufficient for three years."** Israel would be eating old grain until the harvest of the ninth year came in. Obedience required faith, the same lesson as the manna, when a double portion fell on the sixth day so no one gathered on the Sabbath (Exod 16:22–30).
- **Was it ever kept?** The Bible never records a Jubilee being celebrated. The prophets condemn those who "join house to house, who add field to field" (Isa 5:8), and Ezekiel speaks of land returning in "the year of liberty" (Ezek 46:17), so the idea was known. Many scholars think it was seldom or never fully practiced. Others think the silence proves little, since Scripture says little about ordinary years.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "This was typical of our redemption by Christ from the slavery of sin and Satan," and he notes that Jesus was to "preach the acceptable year of the Lord." On vv20–22: "The blessing of God upon our provision will make a little go a great way." JFB calls the Jubilee "the most extraordinary of all civil institutions," designed to keep "an equality of condition among the people," and hears an echo of the promise of vv21–22 in 2 Kings 19:29. Guzik notes the Jubilee was not socialism, since only land returned and wealth earned in other ways was not redistributed, and he suggests it kept a permanent underclass from forming. He applies vv20–22 to seeking God's kingdom first (Matt 6:33).

#### Redemption of Property (vv23–34)
- **"The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine" (v23).** This is the key verse of the chapter. Israel would hold the land as tenants, not as absolute owners. **"For you are strangers and sojourners with me."** Israel would be like foreign residents on God's estate. David prayed the same words (1 Chr 29:15), and the New Testament calls believers "sojourners and exiles" whose true homeland is still ahead (1 Pet 2:11; Heb 11:13).
- **"His nearest redeemer shall come and redeem" (v25).** The "redeemer" is the *goel*, the nearest male relative, whose duty was to step in for a family member in trouble. He bought back sold land, freed a relative sold into service (vv48–49), and avenged a murdered kinsman (Num 35:19). The verb *gaal* means to buy back or restore something to its rightful place. In Ruth 4, Boaz acts as *goel* for Naomi's land and marries Ruth. Jeremiah buys his cousin's field at Anathoth even as Babylon besieges Jerusalem, as a sign that God would restore the land (Jer 32:6–15). Above all, God calls himself Israel's *Goel*: "your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel" (Isa 41:14).
- **If no one redeems (vv26–28).** The seller himself could buy his land back if he later prospered, paying for the years still left until the Jubilee. If neither could pay, the land still came back at the Jubilee. No family was meant to lose its inheritance forever.
- **Houses in walled cities (vv29–31).** A house in a walled city could be redeemed only within one year, and after that it stayed with the buyer. City houses were not farmland and were not part of a family's inheritance in the same way. Village houses, tied to the fields, followed the land rules.
- **The Levites' cities (vv32–34).** The Levites received no tribal territory, only towns with pastureland around them (Num 35:1–8). So their houses could be redeemed at any time, and their pastures could never be sold. The one tribe with the least land had the most protection for it.
- **Commentators:** JFB says God's ownership made the land "inalienable," and that the Levites' holdings were "a permanent endowment for the support of the ministry." It thinks walled-city houses were often owned by "trading people or foreigners." Guzik connects the *goel* to Boaz and, through him, to Christ, the kinsman who buys back what his people lost. The Tyndale notes add that the Jubilee also kept the link between each family and the original division of the land under Joshua.

#### Kindness for Poor Brothers (vv35–46)
- **"If your brother becomes poor… you shall support him" (v35).** The chapter now turns from land to people, in three stages of increasing poverty: a man who must sell land (v25), a man who cannot support himself (v35), and a man who must sell himself (v39). At every stage, the law protects him.
- **"Take no interest from him or profit" (vv36–37).** Loans to a poor Israelite were for relief, not business. No interest on money and no markup on food. Deuteronomy 23:19–20 allows interest from a foreigner, likely a merchant trading for profit. The issue was profiting from a brother's need. Christians have long debated how this applies to modern lending, and most see the principle as a ban on exploiting the poor rather than on all interest. Jesus goes further: "lend, expecting nothing in return" (Luke 6:35).
- **"I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (v38).** The reason is Israel's own history. God gave them freely what they could not earn, and they were to treat each other the same way.
- **"You shall not make him serve as a slave… he shall be with you as a hired worker" (vv39–40).** A poor Israelite could sell his labor to pay a debt, but he was to be treated as an employee, not as property. At the Jubilee he went home with his children to his family land (v41). Exodus 21:2 and Deuteronomy 15:12 limit service to six years, and scholars discuss how the two laws fit together. One common view is that the seventh-year release applied to some cases and the Jubilee was a final backstop.
- **"For they are my servants" (v42).** The Hebrew word *eved* means both "servant" and "slave." Israel belonged to God, who bought them out of slavery in Egypt, so no Israelite could own another as a slave. **"You shall not rule over him ruthlessly"** (v43). "Ruthlessly" (*perekh*) is the word for the harsh labor Egypt imposed on Israel (Exod 1:13–14). Israel must never become Egypt to its own people.
- **Slaves from the nations (vv44–46).** Israel was permitted to buy foreign slaves and keep them permanently. This is one of the hard places in the law. The Old Testament did not abolish slavery, but it regulated it more humanely than its neighbors did: a slave who was beaten and injured went free (Exod 21:26–27), a runaway slave could not be returned to his master (Deut 23:15–16), and kidnapping a person to sell was a capital crime (Exod 21:16). The New Testament teaches that in Christ there is "neither slave nor free" (Gal 3:28), and those seeds later bore fruit in the movement to abolish slavery.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls vv35–38 "a most benevolent provision for the poor and unfortunate," and says the poor Israelite was to be treated "not as a slave, but a hired servant." It doubts the ban on usury clearly applies to modern lending at fair rates. Henry: "God does not make his servants slaves, and therefore their brethren must not," and servants "may be used, but must not be abused." Guzik links vv35–38 to Jesus' teaching on lending in Luke 6.

#### Redeeming a Poor Man (vv47–55)
- **"Sells himself to the stranger" (v47).** The hardest case: an Israelite sold into service to a wealthy foreigner living in Israel. Even then, **"he may be redeemed"** (v48). A brother, uncle, cousin or other close relative could pay his price, or he could redeem himself if he prospered.
- **"He shall calculate with his buyer" (vv50–52).** The price was figured as wages for the years left until the Jubilee, so the buyer got fair value and no more.
- **"He shall not rule ruthlessly over him in your sight" (v53).** The whole community was to watch out for him. A foreign master in Israel was bound by Israel's law.
- **"He and his children with him shall be released in the year of jubilee" (v54).** Even if no one could pay, the Jubilee would set him free.
- **"For it is to me that the people of Israel are servants" (v55).** The chapter ends with God's claim on his people. They are his, bought out of Egypt, and no one else can own them for good.
- **Commentators:** Henry links the kinsman-redeemer to the Messiah, the Redeemer "who shall come to Zion" (Isa 59:20). Guzik says the whole system rests on the fact that Israel belonged to God as his servants. JFB's notes do not cover vv47–55.

#### Pointing to Christ
In the synagogue at Nazareth, Jesus opened the scroll of Isaiah and read: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor. He has sent me to proclaim liberty to the captives… to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor." Then he said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:18–21). Isaiah's words echo this chapter's Jubilee. Jesus is the Jubilee in person, proclaiming release after atonement, as the trumpet sounded on the Day of Atonement. He is also the true *goel*, the near kinsman who became one of us so that he could buy us back, "not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). We sold ourselves into slavery to sin. He paid the price, restored the lost inheritance and said, "if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36).
