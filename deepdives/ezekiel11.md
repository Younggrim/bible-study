*Setting:* The last part of the great temple vision of September 592 BC (chs 8–11). Ezekiel's body is in his house in Babylonia, but in the Spirit he is in Jerusalem. He has seen the temple filled with idolatry (ch 8), the city struck by heavenly executioners while the faithful were marked for protection (ch 9), and the glory of the LORD rising from the Most Holy Place and pausing at the east gate (ch 10). Chapter 11 has two halves. First, at that east gate, Ezekiel confronts the city's leaders, who are sure Jerusalem is safe, and one of them drops dead as he speaks. Second, God answers Ezekiel's anguished cry with one of the great promises of the Old Testament: the exiles whom Jerusalem despised are the real future of Israel, God himself will be their sanctuary in a foreign land, and one day he will give them a new heart. Then the glory leaves the city, and the vision ends. Back in Jerusalem, King Zedekiah's court was full of men who had taken over the property of the exiles and who were talking about revolt against Babylon (Jer 27–28).

#### Judgment on Wicked Counselors (vv1–13)
- **"The east gate of the house of the LORD" (v1).** This is where the glory of God had just come to rest (10:19). At the gate where God stands ready to leave, his people's leaders are holding council.
- **"Twenty-five men… princes of the people" (v1).** The same number as the sun worshipers of 8:16, though these seem to be civil leaders, not priests. The gate of a city was where elders and officials met to judge cases and decide policy (Ruth 4:1; Jer 26:10). Two are named. **Jaazaniah the son of Azzur** is a different man from Jaazaniah son of Shaphan in 8:11. The name was common, and an ancient seal reading "belonging to Jaazaniah, servant of the king" has been found at Tell en-Nasbeh, north of Jerusalem, though it cannot be tied to either man. **Pelatiah the son of Benaiah** has a name that means "the LORD rescues," which becomes painfully ironic in v13.
- **"The men who devise iniquity and who give wicked counsel" (v2).** These are policy makers. Their "counsel" was probably to rebel against Babylon and trust Egypt, the course Jeremiah begged Zedekiah not to take (Jer 27:12–15; 37:6–10).
- **"The time is not near to build houses" (v3).** The Hebrew is short and can be read two ways. As a statement, it may mean "there's no need to rush to build houses," either because the city is safe and life can go on, or as a sneer at the prophets. Many read it as a mocking reply to Jeremiah's letter telling the exiles to "build houses and live in them" in Babylon because the exile would be long (Jer 29:5). The Greek Old Testament reads it as a question: "Is it not recently that houses were built?" meaning "the city is rebuilt and thriving." The overall attitude is clear either way: complacent confidence.
- **"This city is the cauldron, and we are the meat" (v3).** Their proverb sounds strange to us, but the point is protection. The best meat goes in the pot, and the iron pot keeps it safe from the fire underneath. The walls of Jerusalem would protect them from the fire of Babylon. There may also be a sneer at the exiles: we are the choice meat, they are the bones and scraps thrown out. Jeremiah had a vision of a boiling pot tipping from the north (Jer 1:13), and Ezekiel will return to the cauldron picture in 24:3–14.
- **"Therefore prophesy against them; prophesy, O son of man" (v4).** The doubled command shows urgency.
- **"For I know the things that come into your mind" (v5).** The elders said "the LORD does not see us" (8:12). God sees not only what they do but what they think (Ps 139:1–4; Heb 4:13).
- **"You have multiplied your slain in this city" (v6).** This may mean innocent people killed by unjust rulers (7:23; 9:9; 22:6, 27), or those who would die because of their reckless policy. Either way, the leaders are responsible for the dead.
- **"Your slain… they are the meat, and this city is the cauldron, but you shall be brought out" (v7).** God turns their proverb inside out. The only "meat" that will stay in the pot is the dead they themselves caused. The leaders will not have the safety of the pot. They will be dragged out of it.
- **"You have feared the sword, and I will bring the sword upon you" (v8).** What they fear, they get. Their whole strategy was designed to avoid the sword, and it will lead them straight to it.
- **"I will judge you at the border of Israel" (vv10–11).** This came true with grim precision. When Jerusalem fell in 586 BC, Zedekiah and his officials fled and were caught. They were taken to Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah in Syria, at the northern border of the land, where Zedekiah's sons and the leaders of Judah were executed (2 Kings 25:6–7, 18–21; Jer 52:9–10, 24–27). They did not die in the "cauldron" of the city, but outside it.
- **"You… have acted according to the rules of the nations that are around you" (v12).** In 5:7 God said they had not even lived up to the rules of the nations. Here he says they copied them. Both were true. They took on the nations' gods and customs, but not the nations' loyalty to their own gods.
- **"While I was prophesying, Pelatiah the son of Benaiah died" (v13).** This takes place within the vision, but most interpreters believe it was real: Pelatiah actually died in Jerusalem at that moment, and when news reached Babylon later it would have confirmed Ezekiel's word. Some think it was purely visionary. Either way, the death of a man named "the LORD rescues" feels like a sign that rescue is gone.
- **"Ah, Lord GOD! Will you make a full end of the remnant of Israel?" (v13).** This is Ezekiel's second desperate cry, nearly word for word the same as 9:8. Even the death of a wicked leader breaks his heart. "Remnant" (*she'erit*) is the key word, and God's answer in the next section is about exactly that: there will be a remnant, but not where Ezekiel expects.
- **Commentators:** JFB links the twenty-five men to the sun worshipers of 8:16 and reads v3 as the scorners mocking Jeremiah's letter of Jeremiah 29:5. Guzik suggests reading v3 as a question that dismisses the threat, and he takes the cauldron as a boast of safety, which is also Henry's reading: they thought themselves "as safe in this city as flesh in a boiling pot." Henry calls Pelatiah's death "an earnest of the complete accomplishment of this prophecy," that is, a down payment guaranteeing the rest. JFB calls him "probably the ringleader of the scorners." Guzik notes the irony of his name. JFB places the judgment "at Riblah, in the land of Hamath," and Guzik ties the border judgment to 2 Kings 25:6. Henry adds that Ezekiel grieved even over a wicked man's death.

#### Israel's New Heart and Spirit (vv14–25)
- **"Your brothers, even your brothers, your kinsmen, the whole house of Israel" (v15).** God's reply begins with a heavy, repeated emphasis on who Ezekiel's real family is. "Kinsmen" is literally "the men of your redemption" (*'anshe ge'ullatekha*). A *go'el* was the close relative with the right and duty to buy back family land if it was sold (Lev 25:25). Jeremiah exercised this right when he bought his cousin's field (Jer 32:7). The point is that Ezekiel's true family, the ones who share his rights and hope, are his fellow exiles.
- **"Go far from the LORD; to us this land is given for a possession" (v15).** This is what the people left in Jerusalem said about the exiles. Their logic was that the LORD's presence was tied to his land and temple. The exiles were far away, so they were far from God, under judgment and out of the covenant. That meant their property was fair game. The people who stayed took over houses and fields left by the deported families of 597 BC and believed God had given them those lands. The same claim appears after the fall: "Abraham was only one man, yet he got possession of the land; but we are many; the land is surely given us to possess" (33:24). Jeremiah's vision of two baskets of figs makes the same point from the other side. The good figs were the exiles. The rotten ones were Zedekiah and those who remained (Jer 24:1–10).
- **"Yet I have been a sanctuary to them for a while in the countries where they have gone" (v16).** This is one of the most comforting verses in the book. The Hebrew phrase *miqdash me'at* can mean "a little sanctuary" or "a sanctuary for a little while." Both meanings fit. The exiles had no temple, no altar and no sacrifices, but they had God himself. Ezekiel had already seen the glory of God by a Babylonian canal (ch 1). Later Jewish tradition applied the phrase to the synagogue, the "little sanctuary" where scattered Israel met for prayer and Scripture (Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 29a). The great irony of the chapter is that the people in Jerusalem had the temple but not God, since he was leaving it. The exiles had God but not the temple.
- **"I will gather you… and I will give you the land of Israel" (v17).** The land the Jerusalemites claimed for themselves will be given back to the very people they despised. This is the promise of a new exodus, gathering God's people from the nations as he once brought them out of Egypt (20:34; 36:24; Jer 23:7–8).
- **"They will remove from it all its detestable things" (v18).** The returning people will finish what Josiah's reform could not. History bears this out in a striking way. After the return from Babylon in 538 BC, Israel had many failings, but the old idolatry of Baal, Asherah and the high places never came back the way it had been before the exile.
- **"I will give them one heart, and a new spirit I will put within them" (v19).** "One heart" means an undivided heart, no longer split between the LORD and idols, as opposed to the "double heart" of Psalm 12:2 and the people who "limp between two opinions" (1 Kings 18:21). It may also mean one heart shared among them, a people united. The Greek Old Testament reads "another heart," and some Hebrew manuscripts read "a new heart," as in 18:31 and 36:26.
- **"I will remove the heart of stone from their flesh and give them a heart of flesh" (v19).** In Hebrew thought the "heart" is the center of thinking, choosing and desiring, not just feeling. A heart of stone is hard, cold and dead, unable to respond to God, the "hard forehead and stubborn heart" of 3:7. A heart of flesh is alive, soft and responsive. God does not tell them to make themselves a new heart here (though 18:31 calls them to). He promises to do the surgery himself. Ezekiel repeats and expands this promise in 36:26–27, where God adds: "I will put my Spirit within you."
- **"That they may walk in my statutes and keep my rules" (v20).** Obedience is the result of the new heart, not the way to get it. This is the same order as Jeremiah's new covenant promise, given in these same years: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jer 31:33). **"They shall be my people, and I will be their God."** This is the covenant formula, the heart of every covenant God made with Israel (Exod 6:7; Lev 26:12; Jer 31:33). The new heart finally makes it true.
- **"But as for those whose heart goes after their detestable things" (v21).** The promise is not for everyone automatically. Those who stay devoted to their idols will receive what their own deeds deserve.
- **"The glory of the LORD went up from the midst of the city and stood on the mountain that is on the east side of the city" (vv22–23).** This is the final stage of the glory's departure: from the Most Holy Place (9:3), to the threshold (10:4), to the east gate (10:19), and now out of the city to the Mount of Olives. There is irony here too. Solomon had built shrines for foreign gods on that very ridge, which was later called "the mount of corruption" (1 Kings 11:7; 2 Kings 23:13). The glory pauses there, overlooking the city, as if waiting. Ezekiel does not say where it went next. In 43:2–4 he will see it return "from the east," back through the same gate. Zechariah later saw the LORD's feet standing on the Mount of Olives on the day of final victory (Zech 14:4).
- **"The Spirit lifted me up and brought me… into Chaldea, to the exiles. Then the vision… went up from me" (v24).** The vision that began in 8:3 ends. Ezekiel is back in his house among the elders who had been sitting before him all along (8:1).
- **"And I told the exiles all the things that the LORD had shown me" (v25).** All of it: the idols, the slaughter, the departing glory, Pelatiah's death, and the promise of a new heart. It was hard news for men hoping to go home soon. But it also told them that they, the despised exiles, were the people God was keeping for his future.
- **Commentators:** Henry puts the chapter's contrast memorably: "Those at Jerusalem have the temple, but without God; those in Babylon have God, though without the temple." JFB says of the exiles, "the despised of men are often the chosen of God," and explains "kinsmen" as literally the men "of thy redemption." Guzik notes that "little sanctuary" means a sanctuary for a short season rather than a small one, and Henry and JFB read it as a quieter, humbler presence. On v19 Henry says "One heart is a sincere and upright heart, its intentions of a piece with its professions," while JFB reads it as the people seeking God together with one mind, and calls the stony heart as hard as "adamant." Guzik takes v19 as the first clear hint of the new covenant in Ezekiel and ties it to Jeremiah 31:31–34. On the glory's last pause, Henry says "God, by going away thus slowly, thus gradually, intimated that he left them with reluctance," and points out that the idols had been set up on that same mountain "to confront God in his temple." Guzik compares the pause to Jesus weeping over Jerusalem from the same hillside. The Tyndale notes say the glory, in effect, went into exile with the exiles.

#### Pointing to Christ
The glory of God left Jerusalem eastward and paused on the Mount of Olives. Six centuries later Jesus came down that same slope, looked at the same city, and wept, because it did not know "the things that make for peace" (Luke 19:37–44). He too pronounced its house "left to you desolate" (Matt 23:38). But he also brought the promise of this chapter to its fulfillment. On the night before he died he took the cup and called it "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20), the covenant in which God writes his law on hearts of flesh. At Pentecost he poured out the Spirit (Acts 2:33), the inner gift Ezekiel promised. And to scattered people who had no temple, he became the true sanctuary: "where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them" (Matt 18:20). From the Mount of Olives he ascended, and the angels said he would come back the same way (Acts 1:11–12).
