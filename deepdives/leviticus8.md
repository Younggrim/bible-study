*Setting:* Israel is still camped at Mount Sinai, in the first month of the second year after leaving Egypt. The tabernacle is standing (Exod 40), and the laws of the offerings have been given (Lev 1–7). But there are no priests yet to carry them out. In Exodus 29 God gave Moses detailed instructions for ordaining Aaron and his sons. Leviticus 8 tells how those instructions were carried out, step by step, before the whole nation. It is the first narrative in Leviticus, and it reads like a report of an obedient servant: "as the LORD commanded Moses" sounds again and again.

#### Consecration of Aaron and His Sons (vv1–36)
- **"Assemble all the congregation at the entrance of the tent of meeting" (v3).** The ordination was public. The people needed to see that Aaron did not take the office for himself. As Hebrews says, "no one takes this honor for himself, but only when called by God, just as Aaron was" (Heb 5:4). When Korah later challenged Aaron's priesthood (Num 16), everyone could remember this day.
- **"This is the thing that the LORD has commanded to be done" (v5).** Moses explains before he acts. The phrase "as the LORD commanded (Moses)" comes in vv4, 9, 13, 17, 21 and 29, and the chapter ends with Aaron and his sons doing "all the things that the LORD commanded by Moses" (v36). The Tyndale notes point out that Moses did not just pass on instructions. He did each step himself so the priests would see exactly how it was done.
- **"Moses brought Aaron and his sons and washed them with water" (v6).** They did not wash themselves. They were washed. Jewish tradition says this was a full immersion, unlike the daily washing of hands and feet at the basin (Exod 30:19). It happened once, at the start. Jesus told Peter, "The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet" (John 13:10).
- **The high priest clothed (vv7–9).** Coat, sash, robe, ephod, woven band, breastpiece and turban go on in order, ending with **"the golden plate, the holy crown"** inscribed "Holy to the LORD" (Exod 28:36). Into the breastpiece go **"the Urim and the Thummim"** (v8). What they were is not known. Most scholars think they were two stones cast like lots to receive yes-or-no answers from God (Num 27:21; 1 Sam 14:41). The names probably mean "lights" and "perfections."
- **"Moses took the anointing oil and anointed the tabernacle" (vv10–11).** This was the special oil of myrrh, cinnamon, cane and cassia that no one else could make (Exod 30:22–33). The altar was sprinkled **"seven times,"** the number of completeness.
- **"He poured some of the anointing oil on Aaron's head" (v12).** The tabernacle and furniture were sprinkled, and Aaron's sons were later sprinkled (v30), but on Aaron the oil was poured. Psalm 133:2 remembers it: "like the precious oil on the head, running down on the beard, on the beard of Aaron." He is the first "anointed one," *mashiah*, the high priest (4:3).
- **The sin offering (vv14–17).** Before they could serve, the new priests needed an offering for their own sins. **"Aaron and his sons laid their hands on the head of the bull"** (v14). The verb *samak* means to lean or rest one's weight on something. BDB uses it of a man leaning his hand on a wall (Amos 5:19), so it was a firm pressing, not a light touch. Moses put the blood on the altar's horns and **"purified the altar"** (v15). "Purified" is literally "de-sinned" (the verb form of *hatta't*). The altar itself, made by human hands, had to be cleansed before it could be used.
- **The burnt offering (vv18–21).** Next came a ram wholly burned, **"a pleasing aroma."** The order is the same as the offerings in chapters 1–7 laid out: first sin is dealt with, then the whole life is given to God.
- **"The ram of ordination" (v22).** "Ordination" is *millu'im*, from the verb "to fill." BDB gives "installation." The Hebrew idiom for ordaining a priest is "to fill his hand" (Exod 28:41; Judg 17:5), and in vv27–28 Moses literally fills their hands with portions of the offering. This ram worked like a peace offering. It ended in a meal (v31).
- **Right ear, right thumb, right big toe (vv23–24).** Blood was put on each extremity. The Tyndale notes read it as covering the whole person: ears to hear God's word, hands to do his work, feet to walk in his ways. The same rite is used only once more, to restore a healed leper to the community (14:14). The priest who served the holy God and the outcast coming back from the edge both needed the same blood.
- **"He put all these in the hands of Aaron… and waved them" (vv25–28).** The fat, the right thigh and the bread went into the priests' hands, were presented before God, and then were burned on the altar. Normally the right thigh went to the officiating priest (7:32). Here it went to God, because the priests were the ones being offered.
- **"It was Moses' portion" (v29).** Moses received the breast, the share of the officiating priest (7:31). For this one week, Moses served as priest. The Tyndale notes explain that priests cannot ordain themselves, so Moses stood in as God's representative.
- **Blood and oil together (v30).** Moses sprinkled a mixture of anointing oil and altar blood on Aaron, his sons and their garments. Both were needed: the blood that atones and the oil that sets apart.
- **"Boil the flesh… and there eat it" (v31).** The new priests ate the ordination meal at the tent's entrance. Anything left was burned (v32). It was holy food for those just made holy.
- **"You shall not go outside the entrance of the tent of meeting for seven days" (v33).** The ordination took a full week, with the offerings repeated each day (Exod 29:35–37). They stayed at the tabernacle **"day and night… so that you do not die"** (v35). Seven days of creation, seven days of consecration: something new was beginning.
- **"And Aaron and his sons did all the things" (v36).** The chapter ends with complete obedience. Chapter 9 tells of the eighth day, when Aaron offers for the first time and God's fire falls. Chapter 10 shows how quickly that obedience could be broken.
- **Commentators:** All three see the priests' ordination as a picture of believers set apart as priests to God (1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 1:6). Guzik notes that the priests were washed and clothed by another, picturing the cleansing and the righteousness believers receive in Christ (Titus 3:5; Rev 3:5). He says *samak* implies pressing hard on the animal's head, which the lexicon supports, and he passes on Spurgeon's point that blood and oil, Christ's sacrifice and the Spirit, belong together. Henry says Aaron's anointing "was to typify the anointing of Christ with the Spirit," and adds that "all believers have received the anointing" (1 John 2:20, 27). He warns that this gives "no cause for boasting." JFB says the public ceremony made the people accept Aaron's office as God's appointment, and the washing taught "the necessity of inward purity." It reads the seven days as "a week's probation," during which "the smallest breach… would lead to the certain forfeiture of their lives."

#### Pointing to Christ
Aaron needed washing, a sin offering for himself and seven days of repeated sacrifices before he could serve. Hebrews draws the contrast: Christ has no need "to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins and then for those of the people, since he did this once for all when he offered up himself" (Heb 7:27). He was anointed not with oil poured by Moses but with the Holy Spirit (Luke 4:18; Acts 10:38). He was made "perfect forever" (Heb 7:28), and the word Hebrews uses for that, *teleioō*, is the same verb the Greek Old Testament uses for "ordain" in Leviticus 8:33. And through him, ordinary believers are washed, clothed and anointed to serve as "a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" (1 Pet 2:5).
