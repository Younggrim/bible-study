*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably early in Jehoiakim's reign (609–598 BC), soon after the visit to the potter's house in chapter 18. There Jeremiah watched soft clay being reshaped, a sign that God could still remake a nation that turned back to him. The people answered, "That is in vain! We will follow our own plans" (18:12). Now Jeremiah buys a finished, fired clay flask, which cannot be reshaped, and smashes it in front of Judah's leaders in the Valley of Hinnom, the very place where children had been sacrificed. Chapter 20 tells what this sermon cost him.

#### The Broken Flask (vv1–15)
- **"Go, buy a potter's earthenware flask" (v1).** The Hebrew word *baqbuq* is a narrow-necked jug, probably for water or wine. The name itself imitates the gurgling sound of liquid poured from it. Jugs of this kind found in excavations are small, a few inches to about ten inches tall. The point of the change from chapter 18 is clear: soft clay can be reworked, but a fired pot that breaks cannot be mended.
- **"Take some of the elders of the people and some of the elders of the priests" (v1).** Jeremiah brings official witnesses, both civil and religious leaders, so no one can later claim they were not warned. Isaiah did the same with "reliable witnesses" (Isa 8:2). Some of them must have respected him enough to come.
- **"The Valley of the Son of Hinnom at the entry of the Potsherd Gate" (v2).** The Valley of Hinnom runs along the west and south sides of old Jerusalem and meets the Kidron Valley to the southeast. The "Potsherd Gate" appears only here. It may be the gate later called the Dung Gate (Neh 2:13), and it was probably named for the broken pottery dumped outside it, perhaps from the potters' workshops nearby. The KJV's "east gate" comes from a different reading of the word. Jeremiah is to smash a pot where broken pots were thrown away.
- **"O kings of Judah" (v3).** The plural reaches beyond the king on the throne to the royal house and the line of kings who allowed and even led the worship in this valley. Ahaz and Manasseh had both burned their own sons as offerings (2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6).
- **"The ears of everyone who hears of it will tingle" (v3).** The same phrase was spoken to the boy Samuel about the fall of Eli's house and the loss of the ark (1 Sam 3:11), and to Manasseh about the coming fall of Jerusalem (2 Kgs 21:12). The news will be so shocking that it rings in people's ears.
- **"Have profaned this place" (v4).** Literally "made this place foreign," treating part of God's own city as though it belonged to another god. **"Other gods whom neither they nor their fathers… have known."** Gods with no history with them, who had done nothing for them, unlike the LORD who brought them out of Egypt.
- **"They have filled this place with the blood of innocents" (v4).** This probably means the children sacrificed here, though it may also include the innocent blood Manasseh shed in the city (2 Kgs 21:16; 24:4).
- **"To burn their sons in the fire as burnt offerings to Baal" (v5).** Elsewhere this worship is linked with Molech (32:35; Lev 18:21; 20:2–5). Baal and Molech may have been worshiped together here, or "Baal" may be a general word for a false god. Some have argued that "passing children through the fire" was only a symbolic dedication, but this verse calls them "burnt offerings," which leaves no doubt that children died. Josiah had defiled Topheth to stop it (2 Kgs 23:10), but the practice seems to have come back after his death.
- **"Which I did not command or decree, nor did it come into my mind" (v5).** Three denials. The LORD had never asked for this. The Law forbade it and called it detestable (Deut 12:31; 18:10). The story of Abraham and Isaac, where God stopped the knife and provided a ram (Gen 22:12–13), had shown from the start that the LORD does not want human sacrifice. "Nor did it come into my mind" is literally "nor did it go up on my heart." It was utterly foreign to his character. The same words appear in 7:31 and 32:35.
- **"This place shall no more be called Topheth, or the Valley of the Son of Hinnom, but the Valley of Slaughter" (v6).** This repeats 7:32. "Topheth" was the shrine or burning-place in the valley. The origin of the name is debated. Older interpreters (JFB among them) linked it to *toph*, a drum, supposing drums were beaten to drown out the children's cries. Most scholars now connect it with a word for "hearth" or "fireplace," perhaps given the vowels of *boshet*, "shame." The place where they slaughtered their children will become the place where they themselves are slaughtered.
- **From Hinnom to Gehenna.** The Hebrew *Ge Hinnom*, "Valley of Hinnom," became the Greek *Geenna*, "Gehenna." By Jesus' day it had become a name for the place of final judgment, and it is the word he uses when he speaks of hell (Matt 5:22, 29; 10:28; Mark 9:43–48). A popular idea that the valley was a burning garbage dump in Jesus' time comes from medieval sources and is uncertain. The link the Bible itself makes is with this chapter: a place of fire and of God's judgment on the worst of sins.
- **"I will make void the plans of Judah and Jerusalem" (v7).** "Make void" is the verb *baqaq*, to empty out or pour out. It makes a wordplay with *baqbuq*, the flask. As the jug is emptied, so their plans and schemes will be poured out on the ground.
- **"I will give their dead bodies for food to the birds… and to the beasts" (v7).** The covenant curse of Deuteronomy 28:26, repeated from 7:33 and 16:4. Unburied bodies were the final disgrace.
- **"A thing to be hissed at" (v8).** As in 18:16, passersby whistle or hiss at the ruins in shock. **"Because of all its wounds."** The city will be covered in wounds like a beaten body.
- **"I will make them eat the flesh of their sons and their daughters" (v9).** This was the most horrifying covenant curse, spelled out in Leviticus 26:29 and Deuteronomy 28:53–57. It came true during the siege of Samaria (2 Kgs 6:28–29), and again in the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem, which lasted about a year and a half from 588 to 586 BC. Lamentations records it with grief: "The hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children" (Lam 4:10; 2:20). There is a terrible logic. The people who burned their children to false gods would be driven by starvation to devour them.
- **"Then you shall break the flask in the sight of the men who go with you" (v10).** The sound of shattering pottery would have been the sermon's exclamation point. In the ancient Near East, smashing a pot could be a curse ritual. Egyptian "execration texts" wrote enemies' names on bowls or figurines and then broke them. The elders would have understood the gesture at once.
- **"As one breaks a potter's vessel, so that it can never be mended" (v11).** Psalm 2:9 and Isaiah 30:14 use the same picture of complete destruction. Revelation 2:27 applies it to Christ's rule over the nations. **"Men shall bury in Topheth because there will be no place else to bury."** The valley of child sacrifice becomes a mass grave.
- **Does "never be mended" mean the end of Israel?** This flask, this generation of rebels, would not be restored. But God had other clay. Jeremiah himself goes on to promise a return from exile and a new covenant (chs 29–33). The old kingdom would not be glued back together. God would form a new people out of the remnant.
- **"Making this city like Topheth" (v12).** All Jerusalem will become as unclean as the valley, filled with corpses (2 Kgs 23:10).
- **"All the houses on whose roofs offerings have been offered to all the host of heaven" (v13).** Houses in Judah had flat roofs, used for sleeping, drying produce and prayer. Many had turned them into small shrines to the sun, moon and stars, burning incense and pouring drink offerings there (32:29; Zeph 1:5; 2 Kgs 23:11–12). Idolatry was not only in the valley. It was on every house. The whole city would share Topheth's uncleanness.
- **"Then Jeremiah came from Topheth… and he stood in the court of the LORD's house" (v14).** Jeremiah climbs back up from the valley to the temple court, the largest public space, and repeats the message to everyone. What the elders saw in the valley, the whole people now hear.
- **"Because they have stiffened their neck, refusing to hear my words" (v15).** "Stiffened their neck" is the picture of an ox refusing the yoke, the same phrase used of Israel at the golden calf (Exod 32:9) and in 17:23. The chapter ends with the reason in a single line. The next verse (20:1) tells how the temple's chief officer responded: he had Jeremiah beaten and locked in the stocks.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that *baqbuq* is named "from the gurgling sound which it makes when being emptied," and that the elders came as witnesses "that the Jews might not afterwards plead ignorance." Guzik, citing Kidner, draws the contrast with chapter 18: nothing is as workable as clay in the making, and nothing as unalterable as the finished pot. He also cites Kidner that 7:31 and 19:5 leave no doubt these were real burnt offerings, not a harmless rite. Henry observes that "the word of the Lord is not bound to any one place; as good a sermon may be preached in the valley of Tophet as in the gate of the temple," and that it was preached there because there they had sinned and there they would suffer. JFB explains "Topheth" from *toph*, "drum," but most modern scholars prefer the meaning "fireplace." On v11 JFB, citing Grotius, says a broken vessel cannot be restored, "but a new one may be made of the same material," and so God raised up a new generation in place of the rebels.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Valley of Hinnom was where Judah burned its children to gods who never asked for them. The LORD said such a thing never entered his mind. Instead, at the cost of his own heart, he gave his own Son, not on a pagan altar at the demand of a cruel god, but as the willing sacrifice who bore the judgment that Hinnom stood for (Rom 8:32; John 3:16). Jesus warned of Gehenna more than anyone, and then went to the cross outside the city walls to rescue people from it (Heb 13:12). Tradition places the field bought with Judas's silver, the potter's field called "Field of Blood" (Matt 27:7–10; Acts 1:19), in this same valley. And though a smashed flask "can never be mended," the risen Christ takes broken people and makes them "vessels for honorable use" (2 Tim 2:21), treasure in jars of clay (2 Cor 4:7).
