*Setting:* About 852 BC, in the northern kingdom of Israel. Ahab has died in battle at Ramoth-gilead, his blood licked up by dogs as Elijah foretold (1 Kgs 22:38). His son Ahaziah has taken the throne and, like his parents, worships Baal (1 Kgs 22:51–53). 1 and 2 Kings were originally one book, and this chapter runs straight on from the last. It is Elijah's final public confrontation with the house of Ahab, and the last story about him before he is taken up to heaven in chapter 2. The question at its center is the one Elijah asked on Carmel: who is God in Israel? Ahaziah, injured and frightened, sends abroad for an answer, and the LORD sends his answer back in person.

#### Elijah Denounces Ahaziah (vv1–18)
- **"After the death of Ahab, Moab rebelled against Israel" (v1).** Moab, east of the Dead Sea, had been under Israel's control since David (2 Sam 8:2) and was paying a huge annual tribute in lambs and wool (2 Kgs 3:4). The story of the revolt is picked up in chapter 3. A famous inscription, the Moabite Stone (Mesha Stele), found in 1868, gives King Mesha's own account of throwing off Israel's rule and names "Omri king of Israel." It is one of the most important outside witnesses to the Bible's history. The one-line note here signals that the house of Ahab is already losing its grip.
- **"Ahaziah fell through the lattice in his upper chamber in Samaria" (v2).** An "upper chamber" was a room on the roof, cooler than the rooms below. The "lattice" was a wooden screen over a window or an opening in the roof or balcony, which let air through and gave privacy, but would not bear a man's weight. JFB thinks it was an opening in the roof itself. The king of Israel is laid low not by a war but by a household accident.
- **"Go, inquire of Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron" (v2).** Ekron was one of the five main cities of the Philistines, on the coastal plain about 40 miles southwest of Samaria. Its god's name in Hebrew means "lord of flies." Scholars widely think this was a deliberate Israelite mockery of the real name, *Baal-zebul*, "Baal the prince" or "Baal of the exalted house," a title known from the texts at Ugarit. Changing one letter turned "prince" into "flies." Others think the god was really worshiped as one who drove away flies and the diseases they carried. JFB calls him "the god of flies, who was considered the patron deity of medicine." Either way, the name came down to the New Testament as a name for Satan: "Beelzebul, the prince of demons" (Matt 12:24; Mark 3:22). The king of Israel asks a god whose very name was a joke, and later a name for the devil, whether he will live.
- **"The angel of the LORD said to Elijah the Tishbite, 'Arise, go up'" (v3).** God sends Elijah to intercept the messengers on the road. The angel of the LORD who fed Elijah in the wilderness (1 Kgs 19:5–7) now sends him back to work.
- **"Is it because there is no God in Israel...?" (v3).** This is the chapter's refrain, repeated three times (vv3, 6, 16). It is a biting question. Ahaziah's father watched fire fall from heaven at Carmel. Ahaziah has seen the LORD's word come true in his father's death. And yet he sends to Philistia. He acts as though Israel had no God. Guzik describes this as living like an atheist in practice: not denying God in theory, but living as though he were not there.
- **"You shall not come down from the bed to which you have gone up, but you shall surely die" (v4).** The king's bed was probably a raised couch, so "come down" is literal. Ahaziah asked whether he would recover. He gets his answer, but from the God he ignored. The phrase "you shall surely die" (*mot tamut*) is the same as God's warning to Adam (Gen 2:17). Henry: "Go, tell him he shall surely die."
- **"Why have you returned?" (v5).** The messengers have turned back without reaching Ekron, on the word of a stranger. The king's own servants find a man in rough clothing more convincing than their king's orders.
- **"He wore a garment of hair, with a belt of leather about his waist" (v8).** Literally "a man, a lord of hair," which could mean "a hairy man" (as the KJV has it) but more likely describes his clothing: a cloak of animal skin or woven goat's or camel's hair, cinched with a plain leather belt. It was the rough dress of a desert prophet (Zech 13:4). **"It is Elijah the Tishbite."** Ahaziah knows instantly. Elijah's appearance was famous in the palace. John the Baptist wore the same clothing, camel's hair and a leather belt (Matt 3:4), and Jesus said John was the Elijah who was to come (Matt 11:14; 17:12–13).
- **"A captain of fifty men with his fifty" (v9).** Fifty was a standard military unit (Exod 18:21; 1 Sam 8:12). Sending fifty soldiers to bring in one prophet shows how much Ahaziah feared him, and how much he wanted to silence him, or perhaps to force him to take the word back.
- **"Elijah, who was sitting on the top of a hill" (v9).** Literally "the mountain." Some think it was Carmel. Elijah is not hiding. He sits where he can be seen, above the king's men.
- **"O man of God, the king says, 'Come down'" (v9).** The captain's words contradict themselves. If Elijah is a "man of God," his master is God, not Ahaziah. The captain treats God's prophet as one of the king's servants. The command "come down" also echoes the judgment on Ahaziah: the king "shall not come down" from his bed, but he orders God's prophet to come down from his hill.
- **"If I am a man of God, let fire come down from heaven" (v10).** Elijah plays on the captain's own words. "Man of God" (*'ish 'elohim*) and "fire of God" (*'esh 'elohim*, v12) sound almost the same in Hebrew. It is wordplay: the "man of God" is answered by the "fire of God." The fire is the same sign that answered Elijah at Carmel (1 Kgs 18:38). This time it falls on those who defy the LORD. Note that Elijah does not cause the fire. He states a condition, and God answers it.
- **"Come down quickly!" (v11).** The second captain has heard what happened to the first, and still speaks more arrogantly, adding "quickly" and "this is the king's order." He knows the danger and presses on. The same judgment falls (v12).
- **Is this cruel?** Many readers find this the hardest part of the chapter. Several points help. The soldiers were not innocent bystanders asked to deliver a message. They came, as agents of a king who worshiped Baal, to seize God's prophet, very likely to kill him. They openly defied God in his prophet's presence. In Israel's covenant, God was the true King, and the king and his soldiers were under his law. And God made the difference clear in the third captain, who was spared at once when he humbled himself. JFB argues that the punishment came "by the direct hand of God," not from Elijah's own spite. Still, the New Testament draws a sharp line. When James and John wanted to "tell fire to come down from heaven" on a Samaritan village, recalling this very story, Jesus turned and rebuked them (Luke 9:54–55). Some manuscripts add, "You do not know what manner of spirit you are of; for the Son of Man came not to destroy people's lives but to save them." Elijah acted in a season of judgment on a royal house at war with God. Jesus came in a season of mercy, and his followers do not call down fire on their enemies.
- **"Fell on his knees before Elijah and entreated him" (v13).** The third captain is wiser. He does not order. He kneels and pleads: **"Please let my life, and the life of these fifty servants of yours, be precious in your sight."** He calls his men Elijah's servants. He admits what happened to the others (v14). He knows who really holds his life.
- **"Go down with him; do not be afraid of him" (v15).** The angel of the LORD tells Elijah to go. Elijah was not refusing out of fear or pride. He was waiting for God's word. Now he goes straight into the palace of the king who wanted him seized, alone. The man who once fled from Jezebel (1 Kgs 19:3) walks into Ahab's son's bedroom without fear.
- **"Thus says the LORD... you shall surely die" (v16).** Elijah says it a third time, to the king's face, word for word. The message has not softened because the king is angry or because Elijah is now in his power. Elijah adds "to inquire of his word," pointing to what Ahaziah was really refusing: not just a prophet, but God's word.
- **"So he died according to the word of the LORD that Elijah had spoken" (v17).** Once again the word of the LORD comes true, a theme that runs through all of Kings. Ahaziah reigned only about two years (1 Kgs 22:51), partly overlapping his father.
- **"Jehoram became king in his place... because Ahaziah had no son" (v17).** Jehoram (also spelled Joram) was Ahaziah's brother, another son of Ahab. Confusingly, Judah's king at this time was also named Jehoram, the son of Jehoshaphat, and the two Jehorams were brothers-in-law. The dating "in the second year of Jehoram the son of Jehoshaphat" seems to clash with 2 Kings 3:1, which dates the same event to Jehoshaphat's eighteenth year. Most scholars resolve this by co-regency: the Judean Jehoram began ruling alongside his father Jehoshaphat years before Jehoshaphat died, and the two dates count from different starting points. Kings often counts reigns this way.
- **"The rest of the acts of Ahaziah" (v18).** The royal records had more, but the writer has told what mattered: in his crisis the king of Israel turned to a foreign god, and the God of Israel answered him.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the chapter: "how great the prophet looks and how little the prince." On Ekron, he calls it "a very wicked thing, upon any occasion or pretence whatsoever, to consult with the devil," and says the idol was "not less a reproach to his reason than to his religion." On the fire, Henry writes, "Those that will sin like others must expect to suffer like them; God is inflexibly just," but he also contrasts Elijah with James and John: Elijah "was concerned for God's glory, you for your own reputation only." Of the third captain he says, "There is nothing to be got by contending with God." Guzik notes that the captain who calls Elijah "man of God" and then commands him condemns himself by his own words, and that submission to rulers ends where it means disobeying God (Acts 5:29). He passes on Wiseman's point that "you shall surely die" sounds like an ancient medical verdict, a diagnosis delivered by God. JFB stresses that Elijah's courage in going down to the king, "unhesitatingly," was "a marvellous instance of faith and obedience." All three agree the third captain's humility made the difference.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ahaziah was dying and wanted to know if he would live, and he turned to "the lord of flies" rather than the living God. The question "Is there no God in Israel?" still stands over every life that looks for help everywhere but God. Elijah's rough clothing and his message of judgment pointed ahead to John the Baptist, who came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" to prepare the way (Luke 1:17). And Jesus is the one who finally answers the captains' story. When his disciples wanted to call down Elijah's fire, he refused, because he came "not to destroy people's lives but to save them." On the cross, the fire of judgment fell on him. The third captain's prayer, "let my life be precious in your sight," is answered for everyone who kneels before Christ: "Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints" (Ps 116:15), and their lives were bought "with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:19).
