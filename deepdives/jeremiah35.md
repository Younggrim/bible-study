*Setting:* This chapter steps back about ten years, from the final siege under Zedekiah to "the days of Jehoiakim," Zedekiah's older brother. Verse 11 places it when Nebuchadnezzar "came up against the land," most likely around 599–598 BC, near the end of Jehoiakim's reign, when Babylon sent raiding bands of Chaldeans, Syrians, Moabites and Ammonites against Judah after Jehoiakim rebelled (2 Kgs 24:1–2). Some date it earlier, to Babylon's first campaign in 605 BC. Country people were crowding into Jerusalem for safety, among them an unusual clan called the Rechabites. The book places this story right after chapter 34 on purpose. There the people of Jerusalem broke their sacred oath to God within weeks. Here a family keeps a merely human rule, given by an ancestor more than two centuries earlier, without fail. God uses the contrast as a living sermon.

#### The Obedience of the Rechabites (vv1–19)
- **"Go to the house of the Rechabites and speak with them and bring them to the house of the LORD" (v2).** "House" here means family or clan. The Rechabites were descendants, or at least followers, of Jonadab (or Jehonadab) son of Rechab. 1 Chronicles 2:55 lists "the Kenites who came from Hammath, the father of the house of Rechab." The Kenites were a desert people related to Moses' father-in-law, who joined Israel in the wilderness and kept a semi-nomadic life (Judg 1:16; 4:11). Jael, who killed Sisera in her tent, was a Kenite (Judg 4:17–21).
- **Who was Jonadab?** About 841 BC, Jehu was anointed king of the northern kingdom and wiped out the house of Ahab and the worship of Baal. On his way to Samaria he met "Jehonadab the son of Rechab," took him up into his chariot, and said, "Come with me, and see my zeal for the LORD" (2 Kgs 10:15–16). Jonadab then stood with Jehu in the temple of Baal when its worshipers were destroyed (2 Kgs 10:23). He was a fierce opponent of the Baal religion that had soaked into Israel's settled farming life. His rules for his descendants seem to have been a protest against that life and a way to keep his family from being swallowed by it.
- **"Into one of the chambers; then offer them wine to drink" (v2).** The temple was surrounded by side rooms used for storage, priests' quarters and meetings (1 Kgs 6:5; 1 Chr 28:12). This would be a public test, done in the house of God.
- **"Jaazaniah the son of Jeremiah, son of Habazziniah" (v3).** The clan's leader. This Jeremiah is not the prophet. The name was common. Jaazaniah means "the LORD hears," fitting for a man whose family listened.
- **"The chamber of the sons of Hanan the son of Igdaliah, the man of God" (v4).** "Man of God" is a title for a prophet (1 Kgs 17:18; 2 Kgs 4:9). Hanan's "sons" may have been his family or his disciples, as the prophets' followers were called "the sons of the prophets" (2 Kgs 2:3). Jeremiah evidently had friends in the temple who were willing to lend him a room. **"Near the chamber of the officials, above the chamber of Maaseiah the son of Shallum, keeper of the threshold."** The "keepers of the threshold" were three senior temple officers, important enough to be executed by the Babylonians along with the chief priest (52:24). The details place the scene right in the middle of the temple's official life, in full view.
- **"I set before the Rechabites pitchers full of wine, and cups, and I said to them, 'Drink wine'" (v5).** Large bowls of wine and cups to pour it, set out ready. Notice what Jeremiah does not say. He does not say "Thus says the LORD, drink." He simply invites them. So they were free to refuse without disobeying God. The test was public and hard. A prophet, in the temple, with wine poured out, was urging them to drink.
- **"We will drink no wine, for Jonadab the son of Rechab, our father, commanded us" (v6).** They answer at once. "Our father" means their forefather, the founder of the clan, and that is how ancient peoples spoke of the founder of a group. **"Neither you nor your sons forever."** The rule had been kept for something like two hundred and forty years.
- **"You shall not build a house; you shall not sow seed; you shall not plant or have a vineyard; but you shall live in tents all your days" (v7).** No wine, no houses, no fields, no vineyards. Every part of the rule kept them from the settled farming life of Canaan. The vineyard was the symbol of that life (1 Kgs 4:25), and Baal was worshiped as the god of its harvests (Hos 2:5, 8). Living in tents kept the Rechabites mobile, simple and separate, like Israel in the wilderness and like Abraham, who lived "in tents" as a stranger in the promised land (Heb 11:9). The abstinence from wine is like the Nazirite vow (Num 6:1–4), though the Nazirite vow was usually for a set time and the Rechabite rule was lifelong and for a whole clan.
- **"That you may live many days in the land where you sojourn" (v7).** This echoes the fifth commandment: "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land" (Exod 20:12). "Sojourn" means live as a resident foreigner. The Rechabites were not Israelites by birth. They lived in the land as guests.
- **"We have obeyed the voice of Jonadab… ourselves, our wives, our sons, or our daughters" (v8).** The whole family kept it, women and children too. **"We have no vineyard or field or seed" (v9).** They had given up the main sources of wealth in an agricultural society.
- **"But when Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came up against the land, we said, 'Come, and let us go to Jerusalem'" (v11).** Their only exception, and they explain it. In wartime, tents in open country were not safe. They took refuge inside the walls, but they did not give up the rest of their way of life. The fact that they mention it at all shows how seriously they took their father's rule. **"The army of the Chaldeans and the army of the Syrians."** "Syrians" are Arameans from Damascus and the region around it, who joined Babylon's raids (2 Kgs 24:2).
- **"Will you not receive instruction and listen to my words?" (v13).** Now God explains the acted parable. The Rechabites were the visual aid. The lesson was for "the people of Judah and the inhabitants of Jerusalem," many of whom had likely seen or heard of the scene in the temple.
- **"The command that Jonadab… gave to his sons… has been kept… I have spoken to you persistently, but you have not listened to me" (v14).** The contrast is drawn sharply, and it can be laid out point by point. Jonadab was a man. God is the LORD. Jonadab spoke once, long ago, and was long dead. God had spoken "persistently," literally "rising early and speaking," again and again. Jonadab's rule demanded hard sacrifices. God's commands were for his people's good. Yet the family kept the man's word, and the nation broke God's.
- **"I have sent to you all my servants the prophets, sending them persistently" (v15).** The same phrase as 7:25 and 25:4. The prophets' message is summed up in four parts: turn from evil ways, amend your deeds, do not go after other gods, and you will stay in the land. It had been repeated for centuries.
- **"The sons of Jonadab… have kept the command that their father gave them, but this people has not obeyed me" (v16).** The heart of the chapter in one sentence.
- **"I have spoken to them and they have not listened, I have called to them and they have not answered" (v17).** God's complaint echoes 7:13 and Isaiah 65:12. So the disaster he had announced would come.
- **"Because you have obeyed the command of Jonadab your father and kept all his precepts" (v18).** God does not say Jonadab's rules are a pattern every believer must follow. What he praises is the faithfulness of the sons. Their loyalty to their father's word was a mirror held up to Judah.
- **"Jonadab the son of Rechab shall never lack a man to stand before me" (v19).** "Stand before" is the language of service. It is used of the Levites (Deut 10:8), of a king's court officials (1 Kgs 10:8), and of prophets (Elijah, "the LORD… before whom I stand," 1 Kgs 17:1). The promise is that Jonadab's line would survive and continue to serve God. The phrase sounds like the promises to David and the Levites in 33:17–18.
- **Did the promise come true?** Scripture mentions "Malchijah the son of Rechab" repairing the Dung Gate in Nehemiah's day (Neh 3:14), which may mean the family returned from exile, though the name could refer to a place. Later Jewish tradition remembered "the children of Jonadab son of Rechab" as a family with a set day to bring wood for the temple altar (Mishnah, Ta'anit 4:5). The early church historian Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius, says that when James the brother of Jesus was being stoned in Jerusalem, "one of the priests of the sons of Rechab" cried out to stop it. These are traditions, not Scripture, but they show the family's name was remembered for centuries.
- **Is the lesson about wine?** Christians have sometimes used this chapter to argue that believers must abstain from alcohol, and some 19th-century temperance societies took the Rechabites' name. The text itself does not make that point. God does not command anyone else to keep Jonadab's rules, and wine is elsewhere treated as one of God's good gifts when used with self-control (Ps 104:15; John 2:1–11), while drunkenness is condemned (Prov 23:29–35; Eph 5:18). The chapter's lesson is about listening and obeying. A believer may choose abstinence for good reasons, but this passage is not where that choice is commanded.
- **Commentators:** All three agree that the point is the faithfulness of the sons, not wine itself. JFB, citing Calvin, says the praise is for "the obedience of the sons," not for the father's rule, and calls the Rechabites "a nomadic tribe belonging to the Kenites." Henry notes that Jeremiah "made the temptation as strong as possible," and observes that "They drank no wine, though they dwelt in a country where was plenty of it." Guzik and JFB both point out that Jeremiah invited them to drink without commanding it in God's name, so the Rechabites were free to refuse. On v11 Henry says such rules may be relaxed "when the necessity of a case calls for it," and Guzik, following Clarke, calls the move into Jerusalem a necessary, temporary exception that they did not let become an excuse for more. Guzik also cites Meyer's caution that loyalty to family custom can sometimes stand in the way of a higher duty to God. On v19 Henry writes that "the greatest blessing that can be entailed upon a family is to have the worship of God kept up in it." JFB mentions travelers' reports of Rechabite descendants in Arabia, which are curiosities rather than evidence.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Rechabites honored the word of a forefather who had died centuries before. Judah would not honor the word of the living God, who kept "rising early" to speak. God's last and greatest word came not through a messenger but through his own Son (Heb 1:1–2), and the story of Israel's deafness reached its climax when the Son himself was rejected (Mark 12:1–9). Yet Jesus is also the faithful Son that Judah never was. He kept every word of his Father, "obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross" (Phil 2:8). He did not live in a fine house either: "the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (Luke 9:58). Through him a people is made who "stand before" God forever as his servants (Rev 7:15), and his command is not a burden: "If you love me, you will keep my commandments" (John 14:15).
