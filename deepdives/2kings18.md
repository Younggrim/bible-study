*Setting:* Judah, about 715–701 BC. Chapter 17 told how the northern kingdom of Israel fell to Assyria in 722 BC and why: two centuries of idolatry. Judah survived, but only as a vassal. Hezekiah's father Ahaz had paid Assyria for protection, copied an Assyrian altar for the temple and shut the temple doors (16:7–18; 2 Chr 28:24). Assyria was the superpower of the day, its empire stretching from Iran to the borders of Egypt, famous for deporting whole peoples and for its cruelty. Into this world comes Hezekiah, one of the best kings Judah ever had. Chapters 18–20 tell his story, and most of 18:13–20:19 appears almost word for word in Isaiah 36–39; 2 Chronicles 29–32 adds a much fuller account of his reforms. The prophet Isaiah was active in Jerusalem throughout his reign.

#### Hezekiah Reigns in Judah (vv1–12)
- **"In the third year of Hoshea… Hezekiah the son of Ahaz… began to reign" (v1).** The dates in this chapter are among the hardest in Kings to fit together. Verse 10 puts Samaria's fall (722 BC) in Hezekiah's sixth year, but v13 puts Sennacherib's invasion, which Assyrian records date firmly to 701 BC, in his fourteenth year. The Tyndale notes solve this with a co-regency: Hezekiah ruled alongside Ahaz from about 728 BC and alone from 715 to 686 BC. Others think a number was miscopied. Guzik calls the chronology a "thorny problem." The events themselves are well attested.
- **"Twenty-five years old… twenty-nine years in Jerusalem. His mother's name was Abi the daughter of Zechariah" (v2).** Judah's kings are always introduced with their mother's name, since the queen mother held real influence. Chronicles spells her name Abijah, "the LORD is my father" (2 Chr 29:1). Some guess her father was the Zechariah who taught Uzziah to fear God (2 Chr 26:5), but that is only a guess. JFB notes that if these numbers are taken simply, Ahaz would have been about eleven when Hezekiah was born, which is part of why co-regencies are proposed.
- **"He did what was right… according to all that David his father had done" (v3).** Only a handful of Judah's kings are compared to David without any "but." Hezekiah's father Ahaz was one of the worst, and his son Manasseh would be the worst of all (ch 21). Godliness is not inherited, in either direction.
- **"He removed the high places and broke the pillars and cut down the Asherah" (v4).** "High places" were local hilltop shrines. Some were used for worship of the LORD, some for Baal, and many mixed the two. Even good kings before Hezekiah had left them standing (12:3; 14:4; 15:4, 35). Hezekiah is the first to remove them, centralizing worship at the temple as Deuteronomy 12 required. "Pillars" were standing stones set up as sacred objects, and "the Asherah" was a wooden pole or tree for the Canaanite mother-goddess. Chronicles adds that he reopened and cleansed the temple and held a great Passover, inviting even the remnant of the north (2 Chr 29–31). Archaeologists have found what may be traces of his reform at Arad and Beersheba, where altars seem to have been dismantled in this period, though the dating is debated.
- **"He broke in pieces the bronze serpent that Moses had made" (v4).** In the wilderness, when snakes bit the grumbling Israelites, God told Moses to make a bronze serpent on a pole, and whoever looked at it lived (Num 21:4–9). Seven centuries later the people were burning incense to it. A gift from God had become an idol. Jesus pointed to that serpent as a picture of himself: "as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up" (John 3:14–15). The sign was never meant to be worshiped; it pointed to the God who healed.
- **"It was called Nehushtan" (v4).** The name is a wordplay. *Nechushtan* sounds like both *nechoshet*, "bronze," and *nachash*, "serpent." Either the people called it that as a title, or, as many read it, Hezekiah gave it a contemptuous name: "a bronze thing," just a lump of metal. Henry: he showed them "it was Nehushtan, nothing else but a piece of brass," and adds, "Good things, when idolized, are better parted with than kept."
- **"He trusted in the LORD… so that there was none like him among all the kings of Judah after him, nor among those who were before him" (v5).** "Trusted" (*batach*) is the key word of the whole story. It appears again and again in the Assyrian speech below (vv19–24, 30). The writer says almost the same about Josiah: "Before him there was no king like him… nor did any like him arise after him" (23:25). The Tyndale notes resolve it this way: Hezekiah was unmatched in *trust*, Josiah in keeping the law of Moses. JFB says David and Solomon are, "of course," excepted, since they ruled all Israel.
- **"He held fast to the LORD" (v6).** "Held fast" (*dabaq*) is the word for a husband clinging to his wife (Gen 2:24) and for Israel's call to "cling" to God (Deut 10:20; 30:20).
- **"The LORD was with him; wherever he went out, he prospered. He rebelled against the king of Assyria" (v7).** Hezekiah stopped paying the yearly tribute his father had promised. Rebellions often broke out when a new Assyrian king took the throne, and Sargon II died in battle in 705 BC, leaving Sennacherib to secure his empire. The Tyndale notes date the revolt to about then. The writer counts it as faithfulness, not recklessness, because Judah's alliance with Assyria had come with Assyria's gods (16:10–18). Guzik notes that rebellion against a foreign master is not always wrong in Kings, though Zedekiah's later broken oath was (24:20; Ezek 17:15–19).
- **"He struck down the Philistines as far as Gaza… from watchtower to fortified city" (v8).** "From watchtower to fortified city" means everything from a lonely shepherd's lookout to a walled town. The Philistines had taken Judah's lowland towns under Ahaz (2 Chr 28:18). Sennacherib's records mention that the people of Ekron handed their pro-Assyrian king Padi over to Hezekiah, so this may be part of the same revolt.

- **Samaria falls (vv9–10).** These verses retell the fall of Samaria from 17:3–6, now dated by Hezekiah's reign: besieged in his fourth year, taken "at the end of three years," in his sixth year (722 BC).
- **"Shalmaneser king of Assyria came up against Samaria" (v9).** Shalmaneser V began the siege and died about the time the city fell; his successor Sargon II claimed the victory and boasted of deporting 27,290 people. The writer simply says "they took it."
- **"Halah… the Habor, the river of Gozan… the cities of the Medes" (v11).** Northern Mesopotamia and western Iran, hundreds of miles away. These are the "lost tribes" of later legend.
- **"Because they did not obey the voice of the LORD… They neither listened nor obeyed" (v12).** One verse sums up the long sermon of 17:7–23. The placement is deliberate. Just as Hezekiah's trust is described, the writer shows what happened to the kingdom next door that did not trust. Guzik notes that this should have warned Judah: the same God, the same covenant, the same consequences. The Tyndale notes say it sets up the test of Hezekiah's faith that follows.
- **Commentators:** Henry introduces the chapter by setting Judah's good king against Israel's ruin: "God has not quite cast off the seed of Abraham." He says Hezekiah "trusted in the Lord God of Israel to bear him out." Guzik stresses the courage it took to remove high places that earlier good kings had tolerated, and draws the lesson that good things can become idols that must be destroyed. JFB credits Hezekiah's piety partly to the counsels of Isaiah and to the disasters his father's reign had brought.

#### Sennacherib Attacks Judah (vv13–37)
- **"Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the fortified cities of Judah and took them" (v13).** This is the campaign of 701 BC, one of the best-documented events in the Old Testament. Sennacherib's own annals, written on clay prisms such as the Taylor Prism, boast that he took 46 of Hezekiah's walled cities and countless villages, deported 200,150 people (the number is probably inflated), and shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." Notice what he does *not* claim: that he took Jerusalem. JFB notes "all" the fortified cities does not mean absolutely all, since Jerusalem and a few strongholds held out. Isaiah's book of prophecy is full of this crisis (Isa 1:7–9; 10:5–34; 22; 29–31).
- **"Hezekiah… sent to the king of Assyria at Lachish" (v14).** Lachish was Judah's second city, a fortress about 30 miles southwest of Jerusalem guarding the road from the coast. Sennacherib was so proud of taking it that he had its siege carved in stone reliefs for his palace at Nineveh, now in the British Museum: battering rams on a siege ramp, archers, captives being impaled or led away. Archaeologists at Lachish have found the Assyrian siege ramp itself and the counter-ramp the defenders built inside.
- **"I have done wrong; withdraw from me. Whatever you impose on me I will bear" (v14).** Literally "I have sinned." The Tyndale notes call it careful diplomatic language, a vassal's formal confession. Still, it is a striking moment: the king who trusted the LORD now tries to buy off Assyria.
- **"Three hundred talents of silver and thirty talents of gold" (v14).** A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), so roughly 11 tons of silver and over a ton of gold. Sennacherib's annals agree on the 30 talents of gold and claim 800 of silver (perhaps counting by a different standard or including other goods), plus Hezekiah's daughters, palace women and musicians, as the Tyndale notes record. The two accounts, one Hebrew and one Assyrian, confirm each other in remarkable detail.
- **"Hezekiah stripped the gold from the doors of the temple… that Hezekiah king of Judah had overlaid" (v16).** A painful detail: the gold Hezekiah himself had put on the temple doors in his reform, he now pulls off to pay the enemy. Guzik calls the payment a lapse into fear and unbelief that only weakened Judah and encouraged Assyria. Some readers are gentler, seeing a king trying everything to spare his people. The story that follows suggests the money bought nothing.
- **"The king of Assyria sent the Tartan, the Rab-saris, and the Rabshakeh with a great army" (v17).** These are titles, not names, borrowed from Assyrian. The *Tartan* was the commander-in-chief, second only to the king. The *Rab-saris* was a high palace official, "chief eunuch." The *Rabshakeh* is traditionally "chief cupbearer," a senior royal adviser; JFB gives all three meanings. Isaiah's account mentions only the Rabshakeh (Isa 36:2). Sending three top officials with an army after taking the tribute shows Sennacherib wanted not money but surrender.
- **"They… stood by the conduit of the upper pool, which is on the highway to the Washer's Field" (v17).** This is the exact spot where, about thirty years earlier, Isaiah met Ahaz and urged him to trust God instead of calling on Assyria (Isa 7:3). Ahaz refused. Now Assyria stands in the same place threatening his son. The water supply was critical in a siege, and Chronicles tells how Hezekiah blocked springs outside the city and dug a tunnel to bring water from the Gihon spring inside the walls (2 Chr 32:2–4, 30; 2 Kings 20:20). That tunnel, over 1,700 feet long through solid rock, can still be walked today, and an inscription found in it describes the moment the two digging crews met.
- **"Eliakim the son of Hilkiah, who was over the household, and Shebnah the secretary, and Joah the son of Asaph, the recorder" (v18).** Three senior officials, matching the Assyrian three. Isaiah had prophesied that Shebna would be removed from his post over the palace and that Eliakim would replace him (Isa 22:15–25), which seems to have happened by now. A tomb inscription in Jerusalem for an official "over the house" may belong to Shebna, though that too is uncertain.
- **"On what do you rest this trust of yours?" (v19).** The Rabshakeh's speech is a masterpiece of propaganda, built around the word "trust" (*batach*), which he hammers again and again. He knocks down every support one by one. **"Do you think that mere words are strategy and power for war?" (v20).** Talk is cheap.
- **"Egypt, that broken reed of a staff, which will pierce the hand of any man who leans on it" (v21).** A reed from the Nile looks like a walking stick, but lean on it and it splinters into your palm. Hezekiah's officials had indeed sought Egypt's help, and Isaiah condemned it: "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help… who trust in chariots" (Isa 30:1–3; 31:1). Ezekiel uses the same picture of Egypt later (Ezek 29:6–7). The enemy's insult happens to be true.
- **"Is it not he whose high places and altars Hezekiah has removed?" (v22).** A clever twist. To an Assyrian, more shrines meant more honor to a god, so Hezekiah's reform must have offended the LORD. The Tyndale notes suggest he was also playing to people in the crowd who resented losing their local shrines. It is a lie wrapped around a fact.
- **"I will give you two thousand horses, if you are able on your part to set riders on them" (v23).** A sneer: you don't even have enough trained cavalry to use a free gift. How then could Judah resist "a single captain among the least" of Assyria's officers (v24)?
- **"The LORD said to me, 'Go up against this land and destroy it'" (v25).** The cruelest argument: your own God sent me. It contains a half-truth. Isaiah had said God would use Assyria as "the rod of my anger" to punish his people (Isa 10:5–6). But Isaiah also said Assyria would go too far and be judged for its arrogance (Isa 10:7–19). The Tyndale notes say the officer's point was that resisting the great king meant resisting God. Guzik notes the enemy often mixes truth with lies to push God's people toward despair.
- **"Please speak to your servants in Aramaic… Do not speak to us in the language of Judah" (v26).** Aramaic was the international language of diplomacy and trade, used across the Assyrian empire, much as English is today. Ordinary people in Jerusalem spoke only Hebrew, "the language of Judah." The officials want to keep the negotiation away from the crowd on the wall. A century later Aramaic would be the everyday language of Jews returning from exile, and by Jesus' day it was what most Jews spoke.
- **"Doomed with you to eat their own dung and to drink their own urine" (v27).** Crude on purpose. He describes the end of a long siege, when food and water run out. The Tyndale notes call it psychological warfare. JFB says the siege would drive them to "dire extremities."
- **"Hear the word of the great king, the king of Assyria!" (v28).** He shouts in Hebrew, mimicking a prophet: "Thus says the king." It is a counterfeit of "Thus says the LORD." "Great king" was the title of the emperors of the ancient world (compare Ps 47:2, where the LORD is "a great king over all the earth").
- **"Make your peace with me… each one of you will eat of his own vine, and each one of his own fig tree" (v31).** "Every man under his vine and fig tree" was Israel's picture of peace under God's blessing (1 Kings 4:25; Mic 4:4). The Assyrian promises God's blessing without God. **"Until I come and take you away to a land like your own land" (v32).** That is, deportation, Assyria's standard policy, dressed up as a better life. It even echoes the description of the promised land (Deut 8:7–9). Guzik calls it forced resettlement in attractive terms.
- **"Where are the gods of Hamath and Arpad? Where are the gods of Sepharvaim, Hena, and Ivvah? Have they delivered Samaria out of my hand?" (v34).** Hamath and Arpad were Aramean city-states in Syria that Assyria had crushed (Isa 10:9). Sepharvaim and Ivvah were among the peoples resettled in Samaria (17:24, 31). Then the sharpest jab: Samaria, whose people also claimed the LORD, fell. The argument is simple. Every god has failed against Assyria; the LORD is just one more. This is where the Rabshakeh crosses the line. He has stopped insulting Hezekiah and started insulting the living God, putting him on a level with idols. Chapter 19 turns on exactly this point (19:4, 16, 22).
- **"But the people were silent and answered him not a word, for the king's command was, 'Do not answer him'" (v36).** A remarkable discipline. There is no point arguing with propaganda shouted from below the wall. Henry: "They held their peace, not for want of something to say," and "There is a time to keep silence, as well as a time to speak." Guzik commends silence as the wise answer, committing the cause to God. Jesus stayed silent before his accusers (Matt 26:63; Isa 53:7).
- **"Came to Hezekiah with their clothes torn" (v37).** Torn clothes signaled grief and horror. The Tyndale notes suggest grief both for the city and for the blasphemy against God. The chapter ends with the question hanging: will the LORD answer when the king of Assyria has mocked him? The next chapter answers.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the Rabshakeh's speech as "a great deal of pride, malice, falsehood, and blasphemy," and notes he had "the impudence to desire audience of the king himself." JFB identifies the three officials by their titles and points to the Lachish reliefs at Nineveh. Guzik reads the stripping of the temple as a failure of faith, observes that parts of the Rabshakeh's speech were true (Egypt really was a broken reed, and God really did use Assyria), and compares his tactics to Satan's preference to talk people into surrender rather than fight. The Tyndale notes add the details from Sennacherib's own annals.

#### Pointing to Christ
The bronze serpent that Hezekiah smashed was a pointer to Christ, who was lifted up so that all who look to him in faith live (John 3:14–15); the pointer had to go because people were worshiping it instead of the God it pointed to. The chapter's question, "On what do you rest this trust of yours?" is the question every believer faces when the voices on the other side of the wall get loud. Jesus too was mocked with the taunt "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (Matt 27:43), and like Hezekiah's men he answered not a word. His trust was vindicated on the third day, and that is where faith rests its trust.
