*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai. This is the last of the five chapters on clean and unclean (Lev 11–15): food, childbirth, skin disease and mold, and now discharges from the body's reproductive organs. It is a frank chapter, and it can feel awkward to read. But it shows that God's concern for holiness reached into the most private parts of ordinary life, and its closing verse explains why it mattered so much: God was living in the middle of the camp (v31). Right after this chapter comes the Day of Atonement (Lev 16), which deals with all the uncleanness that built up during the year.

#### Laws About Bodily Discharges (vv1–33)
- **The shape of the chapter.** The rules are carefully arranged as a mirror. (1) A man's abnormal, ongoing discharge (vv2–15). (2) A man's normal emission (vv16–17). (3) Husband and wife together (v18), at the center. (4) A woman's normal monthly period (vv19–24). (5) A woman's abnormal, ongoing bleeding (vv25–30). The two serious cases bookend it, the two normal cases come next, and marriage sits in the middle. Men and women are treated in parallel, with the same kinds of rules.
- **"When any man has a discharge from his body" (v2).** "Body" is literally "flesh," and here it refers to the male organ. Most scholars think this was an infection with an ongoing discharge, such as gonorrhea. Whether **"his body runs… or… is blocked up"** (v3), the condition makes him unclean.
- **Uncleanness that spreads by contact (vv4–12).** His bed, his seat and his saddle become unclean (vv4, 9). Anyone who touches them, touches him, or is spat on by him must wash clothes, bathe and be unclean **"until the evening."** He was not sent outside the camp like a person with *tsara'at* (13:46). He stayed at home, but those around him had to take care. A clay pot he touched had to be broken, since its porous surface could not be cleaned, while a wooden one was rinsed (v12; compare 11:32–33). One mercy stands out: **"without having rinsed his hands in water"** (v11). If he washed his hands, his touch did not pass on uncleanness. The law gave him a simple way to live among his family.
- **"He shall count for himself seven days… on the eighth day… two turtledoves or two pigeons" (vv13–15).** When healed, he waited a week, bathed in **"fresh water"** (literally "living water," flowing water), and brought the poor person's offering: one bird as a sin offering and one as a burnt offering. No lamb was required. An ongoing illness was a heavy enough burden.
- **"If a man has an emission of semen" (vv16–17).** This is a normal event, not a disease. The man bathes and is unclean until evening. No offering is needed. The same principle applied to soldiers in camp (Deut 23:10–11).
- **"If a man lies with a woman… both of them shall bathe… and be unclean until the evening" (v18).** Marriage and sex are not sinful in the Bible. God created them and blessed them (Gen 1:28; 2:24; Prov 5:18–19; Heb 13:4). The uncleanness here is ritual, brief, and removed by a bath. But it meant a husband and wife could not go straight from the marriage bed to the sanctuary. The nations around Israel often mixed sex and worship through ritual prostitution and fertility cults. Israel's worship was to be completely separate from that (Deut 23:17–18). When Israel prepared to meet God at Sinai, couples were told to abstain for three days (Exod 19:15), and David's men could eat the holy bread only because they had kept themselves from women (1 Sam 21:4–5).
- **"She shall be in her menstrual impurity for seven days" (vv19–23).** The word is *niddah*, which BDB defines as "impurity," used "especially of menstruation." A woman's monthly period is natural, and the text never calls it sin. Like the man in vv4–12, she stays at home, and her bed and seat pass uncleanness to anyone who touches them. No offering was required at the end. Many readers note that these seven days also gave women some rest and privacy each month in a hard-working household.
- **"If any man lies with her… he shall be unclean seven days" (v24).** This probably refers to the period beginning unexpectedly. Deliberately having relations during menstruation is later forbidden outright and carries a heavy penalty (18:19; 20:18). If it happened unknowingly, the man simply shared her seven days of uncleanness.
- **"A discharge of blood for many days, not at the time of her menstrual impurity" (vv25–27).** Ongoing or irregular bleeding made a woman unclean for as long as it lasted, which could mean months or years. Her bed, chair and anything she touched would pass that on, so this condition affected her whole life, her marriage and her place in public worship.
- **"She shall count for herself seven days… on the eighth day… two turtledoves or two pigeons" (vv28–30).** Her cleansing matches the man's in vv13–15 exactly. The law gave the same path back to both.
- **"Lest they die in their uncleanness by defiling my tabernacle that is in their midst" (v31).** This verse is the key to the whole section on purity. The problem was not that bodies are bad. It was that the holy God had chosen to live among his people, and holiness and uncleanness could not mix. Uncleanness brought carelessly near God's dwelling was dangerous, as Nadab and Abihu had already learned (10:1–3). These laws were a kindness. They kept Israel safe while living close to God.
- **"This is the law" (vv32–33).** The summary lists all five cases, **"for anyone, male or female."**
- **Why bodily fluids?** Scripture does not explain every detail. Many scholars note that every case in Leviticus 11–15 involves the loss of life-fluids, blood or semen, or signs of death and decay like skin disease and dead animals. The living God stood against death in every form, and anything that suggested life draining away kept a person from his presence for a time. Guzik adds that the laws also had real health benefits. Henry warns that "we need not be at all curious in explaining these antiquated laws," for "it is enough if we observe the general intention."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the contagion of uncleanness as a picture of sin: "This signified the contagion of sin," and only "the blood of Christ his Son… cleanses us from all sin." He also warns that it is a dangerous state when the soul "is putrefied by that which should purify it," and that sin can take occasion even from God's good commands. JFB thinks the laws were meant "not only to prevent contagion, but to discourage the excesses of licentious indulgence," and on v31 says God would not let his people approach him "when defiled, even by involuntary or secret impurities." Guzik stresses that none of these conditions made a person sinful, only ceremonially unclean. He sees the separation of sex from worship as a guard against the fertility cults of Israel's neighbors, and adds that believers stay clean before God through Christ's word and through confession (John 15:3; 1 John 1:7–9).

#### Pointing to Christ
Mark tells of "a woman who had had a discharge of blood for twelve years" (Mark 5:25), exactly the condition of Leviticus 15:25. For twelve years she had been unclean, and anyone she touched became unclean too. Crowded around Jesus, she reached out and touched his garment, and something happened that Leviticus never imagined: her uncleanness did not pass to him. His cleanness passed to her. "Immediately the flow of blood dried up" (Mark 5:29). Jesus did not scold her. He called her "Daughter" and sent her home in peace (Mark 5:34). Leviticus kept uncleanness away from God's presence. In Jesus, God's presence came close enough to touch, and made the unclean clean.
