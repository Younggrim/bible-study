*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Chapter 23 laid out the yearly calendar of feasts. Chapter 24 turns to two things that happened every day and every week inside the tabernacle: the lamps that burned through the night and the bread set out on the golden table. Then, unusually for Leviticus, the law pauses for a story. A man in the camp blasphemes God's name, and the case leads to a ruling on blasphemy and a restatement of the law of "eye for eye." The two halves seem unrelated, but both are about honoring God's presence among his people: tending it faithfully, and not treating it with contempt.

#### The Lamps (vv1–4)
- **"Pure oil from beaten olives" (v2).** The best oil came from olives crushed gently in a mortar rather than pressed in a mill, giving a clear oil that burned brightly with little smoke. Exodus 27:20–21 gave this same command. Here the people themselves supply it. The light in God's house came from the gifts of ordinary Israelites.
- **"That a light may be kept burning regularly" (v2).** "Regularly" (*tamid*, "continually") is the key word in vv1–9, used four times (vv2, 3, 4, 8). The lamp was to burn **"from evening to morning"** (v3), every night, without fail. Young Samuel slept near the ark "before the lamp of God had gone out" (1 Sam 3:3).
- **"Outside the veil of the testimony" (v3).** The lampstand stood in the Holy Place, just outside the curtain that hid the ark (which held the "testimony," the tablets of the law). It was the only light in a room with no windows.
- **"The lampstand of pure gold" (v4).** This is the seven-branched lampstand, the *menorah*, hammered from a single piece of gold and shaped like an almond tree in bloom (Exod 25:31–40).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes the oil was "cold-drawn, which is always of great purity," and that the priests had to be present daily to clean and trim the lamps. Guzik connects the ever-burning lamp with Jesus, the light of the world (John 8:12), and applies it to believers who need a steady supply of the Spirit to keep shining. That is his application, not the text's own point, though the Bible does link oil and lampstands with the Spirit elsewhere (Zech 4:1–6).

#### Bread for the Tabernacle (vv5–9)
- **"Twelve loaves" (v5).** One for each tribe of Israel. Each loaf used **"two tenths of an ephah"** of fine flour, something like three or four quarts, so these were large loaves. The bread was known as the "bread of the Presence" (Exod 25:30), literally "bread of the face," because it was set out before God's face. The KJV calls it "shewbread."
- **"Two piles, six in a pile" (v6).** The Hebrew can mean "rows" or "piles." Either way, the twelve tribes were always represented before God on the golden table.
- **"Pure frankincense… as a memorial portion" (v7).** The frankincense was burned on the altar as God's share, the way a handful of a grain offering was burned as its "memorial portion" (2:2). The bread itself was not burned.
- **"Every Sabbath day… as a covenant forever" (v8).** Fresh loaves were set out each Sabbath, and the old ones became the priests' food, eaten **"in a holy place"** (v9). The bread was a standing sign of the covenant: the tribes kept before God, and God feeding his people.
- **David and the bread.** When David fled from Saul, the priest Ahimelech gave him and his men this holy bread because nothing else was available (1 Sam 21:1–6). Jesus pointed to that story when the Pharisees criticized his hungry disciples for picking grain on the Sabbath. If David's need came before the ritual rule, how much more did the needs of the Son of David's followers, since "something greater than the temple is here" (Matt 12:1–8).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that only the priests ate the old bread "except in cases of necessity," and points to David (1 Sam 21) and Jesus' use of the story (Luke 6:3–4). Guzik says "showbread" means "bread of the face," and reads it as a picture of fellowship with God that is kept fresh, never stale. The Tyndale notes add that the loaves were unleavened, since part of the offering went to the altar.

#### Punishment for Blasphemy (vv10–16)
- **"An Israelite woman's son, whose father was an Egyptian" (v10).** A "mixed multitude" left Egypt with Israel (Exod 12:38), and this man was one of them, half Israelite and half Egyptian. The text does not say whether he was treated as an outsider, but the fight in the camp may have been over his standing among the tribes. Some Jewish traditions suggest it was a dispute about where he could pitch his tent, since camping was by the father's tribe (Num 2:2). That is a guess, not the text.
- **"Blasphemed the Name, and cursed" (v11).** Two verbs. "Cursed" (*qalal*) means to treat with contempt or call down evil. "Blasphemed" (*naqab*) is unusual. Its common meaning is "to pierce" or "to designate," and BDB lists a separate sense, "to curse," for this passage. Later Jewish interpreters read it as "pronounced," and from this verse came the tradition of never saying God's personal name, YHWH, aloud, replacing it with *Adonai* ("Lord") or *ha-Shem* ("the Name"). That is why most English Bibles print "the LORD" in capitals. The text itself is about cursing with the Name, not merely saying it.
- **"His mother's name was Shelomith, the daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan" (v11).** Leviticus rarely gives names, and this one is recorded. It may be to show this was a real case, remembered by name. Some also read it as a quiet warning about a marriage outside the covenant people, though the text does not blame her.
- **"Till the will of the LORD should be clear to them" (v12).** This is one of four times in the wilderness when Moses had a case he could not settle and asked God directly (also Num 9:6–14; 15:32–36; 27:1–11). Blasphemy was already forbidden (Exod 20:7; 22:28), but the penalty had not been set, and the man was not a full Israelite. Moses waited for God rather than act in anger.
- **"Let all who heard him lay their hands on his head" (v14).** The witnesses laid hands on him, a public statement that they had heard the words and that his guilt was his own. Hands laid on a sacrifice transferred sin to it (1:4; 16:21). Here the witnesses, in effect, put the words he had spoken back on his own head. The execution happened **"out of the camp,"** away from God's dwelling.
- **"Whoever curses his God shall bear his sin… Whoever blasphemes the name of the LORD shall surely be put to death" (vv15–16).** God's ruling makes the case into law. **"The sojourner as well as the native"** (v16). This answered the question the case raised: the man's mixed parentage did not exempt him.
- **Blasphemy and Jesus.** The charge of blasphemy came back at the most important trial in history. Jesus was condemned by the Sanhedrin for claiming to be the Son of Man at God's right hand. "He has uttered blasphemy," said the high priest, and they answered, "He deserves death" (Matt 26:63–66). Stephen was stoned outside the city on the same charge (Acts 6:11; 7:58). What they called blasphemy was the truth.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns, "When quarrels begin we know not what mischief they will make before they end," and praises Moses' patience: "do nothing rashly, for the judgment is God's." He adds that children "will be apt to take after the worse side." JFB suggests the man's habits came from an Egyptian upbringing and notes that foreigners living in the camp were bound by this law. Guzik passes on Harrison's point that a name was "bound up intimately with his character," so to curse God's name was to reject God himself. He describes the later Jewish practice of not pronouncing the divine name.

#### An Eye for an Eye (vv17–23)
- **"Whoever takes a human life shall surely be put to death" (v17).** In the middle of a case about a word against God, the law restates the most basic rule about life: murder is a capital crime. The reason goes back to Noah: "for God made man in his own image" (Gen 9:6). Attacking a person made in God's image is an attack on God's honor, much as blasphemy is.
- **"Whoever takes an animal's life shall make it good, life for life" (v18).** Killing someone's animal required repayment, a live animal for a dead one. A human being cannot be repaid this way, which is why the two cases sit side by side (v21).
- **"Fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (v20).** This principle is often called *lex talionis*, "the law of like for like." It appears three times in the law (Exod 21:23–25; here; Deut 19:21). It sounds harsh to modern ears, but it was a limit, not a license. It set the penalty in proportion to the injury and no more, and it took revenge out of private hands and gave it to the courts. In the ancient world, punishments often depended on class, so that harming a noble cost far more than harming a commoner. Many scholars think Israel's courts usually settled such cases with payment rather than literal maiming (Exod 21:26–27 already shows a slave freed for a lost eye or tooth), and later Jewish law certainly did. The principle was that justice must fit the crime.
- **"The same rule for the sojourner and for the native" (v22).** One law for everyone, rich or poor, Israelite or foreigner. The same equality that held the foreigner accountable in v16 also protected him here.
- **What Jesus did with it.** In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus quoted this law: "You have heard that it was said, 'An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.' But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil" (Matt 5:38–39). He did not abolish justice in the courts. He forbade his followers to use the principle as a personal right to get even. A disciple turns the other cheek and leaves vengeance to God (Rom 12:17–21), while God still gives the governing authorities the job of punishing wrongdoers (Rom 13:1–4).
- **"Thus the people of Israel did as the LORD commanded Moses" (v23).** The story ends with obedience. It was a hard sentence, but the community carried it out together.
- **Commentators:** Henry on the stoning: "Every man should have a stone to throw at him that blasphemes God." On vv19–20 he stresses this was "Not that men might in these cases be their own avengers," but a rule for magistrates. JFB likewise says these penalties were for the judges to apply, not private individuals, and sees Stephen's death as a later case of the same punishment (Acts 7:58–59). Guzik reads "eye for eye" as a ceiling on punishment, since human nature wants to hurt an attacker worse, and says Jesus' teaching in Matthew 5 concerns personal relationships, not the courts.

#### Pointing to Christ
The lamp that never went out and the bread that was always set before God's face are both fulfilled in Jesus. He said, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12), and "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35). In the new Jerusalem, there is no need of lamp or sun, "for the glory of God gives it light, and its lamp is the Lamb" (Rev 21:23). The second half of the chapter points to him too. Jesus was condemned as a blasphemer and led outside the city to die, as this man was led outside the camp (Heb 13:12). But he was innocent, and he bore not his own sin but ours. The law of "eye for eye" demanded that justice be paid in full. At the cross it was, by the one who took the penalty for those who deserved it.
