*Setting:* "A Psalm of David." No occasion is named, and none is needed. David spent his youth as a shepherd near Bethlehem, guarding his father's flock from lions and bears (1 Sam 16:11; 17:34–35). Later God made him the shepherd of a nation: "You shall be shepherd of my people Israel" (2 Sam 5:2). Kings across the ancient Near East called themselves shepherds of their people, and Egypt's pharaohs carried a shepherd's crook as a sign of royalty. David, the shepherd-king, turns the picture around and calls the LORD his shepherd. The psalm comes straight after Psalm 22, the cry of the forsaken sufferer, and before Psalm 24, the King of glory entering his gates. Many readers see the three as a set: in John 10:11 the good shepherd lays down his life; in Hebrews 13:20 God raises "the great shepherd of the sheep"; and in 1 Peter 5:4 "the chief Shepherd" appears in glory. The psalm moves through two pictures: the shepherd and his sheep (vv1–4), and the host and his guest (vv5–6).

#### The LORD Is My Shepherd (vv1–3)
- **"The LORD is my shepherd" (v1).** The first word is *YHWH*, God's covenant name. The almighty God of Psalms 18 and 19, who rides the storm and made the stars, is the one who stoops to tend sheep. Jacob had called God "the God who has been my shepherd all my life long to this day" (Gen 48:15). Isaiah says, "He will tend his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms" (Isa 40:11). And everything turns on the small word "my." David does not say "a shepherd" or "Israel's shepherd," though both are true (80:1). He says "my shepherd."
- **"I shall not want" (v1).** "Want" means lack, go without. The same verb is used of Israel in the wilderness: "These forty years the LORD your God has been with you. You have lacked nothing" (Deut 2:7). It does not mean the sheep gets everything it fancies. It means it never goes without what it needs, because the shepherd decides what it needs.
- **"He makes me lie down in green pastures" (v2).** Sheep lie down when they are full, safe and calm. In the dry hills of Judea, green grass is not everywhere. A shepherd has to know where to find it, and lead the flock there before the heat of the day. "Makes me" does not mean force. It means the shepherd brings about the conditions in which the sheep can rest.
- **"He leads me beside still waters" (v2).** Literally "waters of rest" (*menuchot*). Sheep are nervous of fast-flowing water. The shepherd finds quiet pools or streams where they can drink without fear. The same root names the "resting place" God sought for Israel in the wilderness (Num 10:33).
- **"He restores my soul" (v3).** "Restores" (*shuv*) means to bring back, to turn around. It can mean reviving a tired person, as God's law revives the soul in 19:7, using the same words. It can also mean bringing back a sheep that has wandered off. Both fit. Jesus told of a shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to bring back the one (Luke 15:4–6).
- **"He leads me in paths of righteousness" (v3).** "Paths" (*maggal*) are well-worn tracks, the ruts made by carts or the trails worn by flocks. They are the "right paths," the ones that actually lead somewhere good, as opposed to tracks that wander off a cliff. For a believer, they are the ways of right living too (Prov 4:11).
- **"For his name's sake" (v3).** The shepherd's reputation is at stake. A shepherd who lost his sheep would be shamed. God leads his people rightly not because they deserve it, but because his own name, his character and honor, depend on it (25:11; 31:3; Ezek 36:22).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that while many of David's psalms "are full of complaints… this is full of comforts," and that it "will be" sung by Christians "while the world stands." JFB notes that "the opinion that He [Christ] is the Lord here so described… is not without some good reason" (John 10:14; Heb 13:20; 1 Pet 2:25). JFB says the "green pastures" are "places of cool and refreshing rest," and the still waters are "contrasted with boisterous streams on the one hand, and stagnant, offensive pools on the other." Guzik, drawing on Phillip Keller's book on shepherding, says sheep will not lie down unless they are free of fear, tension with other sheep, pests and hunger, and only the shepherd can deal with all four. He passes on Kidner's description of a shepherd as guide, physician and protector to his flock.

#### Through the Valley (v4)
- **"Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death" (v4).** The word *tsalmavet* is usually read as "shadow of death" (*tsel*, shadow + *mavet*, death), as the ancient Greek translation took it. Some scholars read it as a single word meaning "deep darkness." Elsewhere it describes the darkness of distress, of danger and of the grave (Job 10:21–22; Jer 2:6; Ps 107:10). In Judea, some of the paths between pastures ran down narrow, steep ravines where the sun barely reached, ideal places for wild animals or robbers to wait. The phrase covers any terrifying darkness, and it rightly includes death itself.
- **"I walk through" (v4).** The sheep does not stay in the valley. It is a path to somewhere else. And it walks, it does not run.
- **"I will fear no evil, for you are with me" (v4).** Here the psalm changes from "he" to "you." In vv1–3 David spoke *about* his shepherd. In the dark, he speaks *to* him. The reason he is not afraid is not that the valley is safe. It is that the shepherd is there. "You are with me" is the promise of God's presence that runs through Scripture, from "I will be with you" to Moses (Exod 3:12) to "I am with you always" (Matt 28:20).
- **"Your rod and your staff, they comfort me" (v4).** The rod (*shevet*) was a short, heavy club used to drive off predators and also to count and inspect sheep as they passed under it (Lev 27:32; Ezek 20:37). The staff (*mishenet*) was a longer stick, something to lean on, and to guide sheep. Some think the two are a single shepherd's tool described two ways. Either way, they mean protection and guidance. The sheep is comforted, not threatened, by them.
- **Commentators:** Henry finds "four words which lessen the terror" of death: "It is but the shadow of death… the shadow of a serpent will not sting nor the shadow of a sword kill"; it is a valley, and "valleys are fruitful"; it is "but a walk"; and it is a walk "through it," so God's people "shall not be lost in this valley, but get safely to the mountain of spices on the other side." JFB describes the valley as "a ravine overhung by high precipitous cliffs," which, while "expressive of any great danger," "does not exclude the greatest of all." Guzik makes the same point as Henry about the shadow, and adds that because Jesus went through the full reality of death, his people face only its shadow. He passes on Maclaren's view that rod and staff may be two names for one tool, and Kidner's line that discipline is security.

#### You Prepare a Table Before Me (v5)
- **The second picture.** Now God is a host and David is his guest. In the ancient Near East, hospitality was a sacred duty. Once a guest ate at your table, you were bound to protect him (Gen 19:8; Judg 19:23). Some readers think the shepherd picture continues, with the shepherd preparing grazing ground, but most see a change to a banquet scene.
- **"You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies" (v5).** The enemies have not gone away. They are watching. But they cannot touch the guest, because the host has taken him under his roof. When David fled from Absalom, friends brought food to him and his men in the wilderness, "for they said, 'The people are hungry and weary and thirsty'" (2 Sam 17:27–29). Psalm 22:26 said, "the afflicted shall eat and be satisfied." The Bible's story ends with a banquet too (Isa 25:6; Rev 19:9).
- **"You anoint my head with oil" (v5).** A good host poured olive oil, often perfumed, on an honored guest's head. It refreshed skin dried by sun and wind and made the guest feel welcomed. Jesus once gently rebuked a Pharisee for neglecting this: "You did not anoint my head with oil" (Luke 7:46). For David, anointing also recalled the day Samuel poured oil on his head and set him apart as king (1 Sam 16:13).
- **"My cup overflows" (v5).** Psalm 16:5 called the LORD "my cup," and Psalm 11:6 spoke of the wicked's cup of fire. This cup is filled past the brim. God is not a stingy host.
- **Commentators:** Henry connects the anointing either to "the extraordinary entertainment of special friends, whose heads they anointed with oil" (Luke 7:46), or to David as a sheep like "the poor man's ewe-lamb" of Nathan's parable that "did eat of his own meat, and drank of his own cup" (2 Sam 12:3). JFB treats table, oil and cup as "another figure" of God's care for his child, "who may feast in spite of his enemies." Guzik passes on Maclaren's summary of the believer's life as always conflict, but always a spread table, and Spurgeon's picture of a host calmly setting a meal while the enemy is at the door.

#### Goodness and Mercy (v6)
- **"Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me" (v6).** "Mercy" is *chesed*, God's steadfast covenant love. "Follow" (*radaph*) is a strong word. It usually means "pursue" or "chase," and in the Psalms it is normally used of enemies hunting David down (7:1, 5; 31:15). Here the hunters have been replaced. Instead of Saul's men on his trail, God's goodness and love are chasing him.
- **"All the days of my life" (v6).** Not some days, or the good days, but every day, including the days in the dark valley.
- **"I shall dwell in the house of the LORD forever" (v6).** "House of the LORD" means the place of God's presence. In David's time that was the tabernacle tent (1 Sam 1:7). A guest at a host's table could hope to stay. The standard Hebrew vowels read "I shall *return* to the house of the LORD," a homecoming. The ancient Greek translation and most English versions read "dwell," which differs by one small vowel. Both readings end at home with God.
- **"Forever" (v6).** Literally "for length of days," the same phrase as 21:4. Some take it to mean "for the rest of my life." But a guest of the living God, who "will not abandon my soul to Sheol" (16:10), has every reason to hope that this length of days has no end.
- **Commentators:** Henry says goodness and mercy "shall follow me, as the water out of the rock followed the camp of Israel through the wilderness," and adds, "for whom God loves he loves to the end." He reads the psalm as a resolve "never to leave nor forsake God in a way of duty," since God will never forsake him "in a way of mercy." Guzik passes on Meyer's image of the shepherd in front and goodness and mercy following behind like two angels, and Kidner's point that eating at a host's table created a lasting bond of loyalty.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus took this psalm and applied it to himself: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). That is something no ordinary shepherd does. David's shepherd led him through the valley of the shadow of death. Jesus went into that valley alone, as Psalm 22 shows, and came out the other side, so that his sheep walk only through its shadow. He knows his sheep by name (John 10:3), restores the ones who wander, and spreads a table for them, giving his own body and blood (Luke 22:19–20). He promises that no one will snatch them out of his hand (John 10:28) and that he goes to prepare a place for them in his Father's house (John 14:2). The one who said "The LORD is my shepherd" never knew how close that shepherd would come.
