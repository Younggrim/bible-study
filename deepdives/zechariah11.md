*Setting:* This is the end of the first "oracle" of Zechariah 9–14 (9:1), and it is the darkest chapter in the book. Chapters 9 and 10 promised a humble king, a regathered flock and a God who cares for his sheep. Chapter 11 shows the flock rejecting the good shepherd God gives them. Zechariah is told to act out a parable. He takes the role of shepherd over a flock already marked for slaughter, carries two staffs named Favor and Union, is despised by the flock and paid off with thirty pieces of silver, which he throws into the temple. Then he plays a worthless shepherd who devours the sheep. Like the acted signs of Jeremiah and Ezekiel, the story is meant to be watched as much as heard. Its exact historical reference is one of the hardest puzzles in the prophets, but the New Testament leaves no doubt about where it finally points: Matthew sees it fulfilled when Jesus was betrayed for thirty pieces of silver and the money went to buy a potter's field (Matt 27:3–10). The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading.

#### The Flock Doomed to Slaughter (vv1–17)
- **"Open your doors, O Lebanon, that the fire may devour your cedars!" (v1).** The chapter opens with a short poem of judgment (vv1–3). Lebanon's cedars, Bashan's oaks and the cypresses were the grandest trees the Israelites knew, and the prophets used them as pictures of proud nations and rulers (Isa 2:13; 10:33–34; Ezek 31). Fire sweeps south from Lebanon, through Bashan east of the Sea of Galilee, down to the Jordan valley, the path of an invasion from the north. Some read it as the end of the oracle against the nations in chapter 10. Others read it as judgment coming on Israel's own land and leaders. Jewish tradition took Lebanon as a name for the temple, built of cedar.
- **"Wail, O cypress, for the cedar has fallen" (v2).** If the mightiest tree has fallen, the lesser trees should wail too. The judgment spares no one.
- **"The sound of the wail of the shepherds… the roar of the lions" (v3).** The shepherds wail because their pasture is ruined. The young lions roar because "the thicket of the Jordan," the dense jungle along the river where lions lived (Jer 12:5; 49:19), is burned. "Shepherds" points ahead to the rest of the chapter. Both rulers and the powerful lose their "glory."
- **"Become shepherd of the flock doomed to slaughter" (v4).** Zechariah is to act out what God is doing. The flock is literally "the flock of slaughter," sheep raised for the butcher. The people of God have become livestock to be sold.
- **"Those who buy them slaughter them and go unpunished, and those who sell them say, 'Blessed be the LORD, I have become rich'" (v5).** The buyers may be foreign powers and the sellers Judah's own leaders, or both may be leaders within the nation. The cruelest part is the piety: the sellers thank God for their profit. Ezekiel had condemned shepherds who "eat the fat… but you do not feed the sheep" (Ezek 34:3). **"Their own shepherds have no pity on them."**
- **"I will no longer have pity on the inhabitants of this land" (v6).** This is the hard turn. God hands the people over to what they have chosen: neighbor against neighbor and each "into the hand of his king." The word "king" may point to a foreign ruler. When the people later shouted, "We have no king but Caesar" (John 19:15), that is what they received.
- **"So I became the shepherd of the flock doomed to be slaughtered by the sheep traders" (v7).** The standard Hebrew text reads "the poor of the flock" (*'aniyye ha-tso'n*). The ESV, following the Greek translation, reads the same consonants divided differently as "the traders of the flock" (*kena'aniyye ha-tso'n*). The same choice comes in v11. In the Hebrew reading, it is the poor and humble of the flock who watch and recognize God's word. In the ESV's reading, it is the traders. Both have support.
- **"I took two staffs, one I named Favor, the other I named Union" (v7).** A shepherd usually carried a rod for protection and a staff for guiding (Ps 23:4). "Favor" is *no'am*, pleasantness or grace, the word in "the beauty of the LORD" (Ps 27:4) and "let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us" (Ps 90:17). The KJV has "Beauty." "Union" is *chovlim*, literally "binders" or "cords," what ties things together. The KJV has "Bands." The first stands for God's gracious protection, the second for the unity of the nation. Ezekiel had joined two sticks into one to picture Judah and Israel reunited (Ezek 37:15–22). Zechariah's staffs will be broken.
- **"In one month I destroyed the three shepherds" (v8).** One of the most debated sentences in the Old Testament. Scholars have counted more than forty proposals: three kings, three high priests, three empires (Babylon, Persia, Greece), three Maccabean figures, or three classes of leaders (prophets, priests and kings). None is certain. The point is that the good shepherd removes the bad leaders quickly, in a short span ("one month"), and still the flock does not want him.
- **"But I became impatient with them, and they also detested me" (v8).** The relationship breaks from both sides. The word for "detested" means to loathe or feel disgust. The flock rejects its shepherd.
- **"I will not be your shepherd. What is to die, let it die" (v9).** The shepherd gives the flock up to what it has chosen. **"Let those who are left devour the flesh of one another"** may be literal, describing the horror of famine in a siege, which happened in 586 BC (Lam 4:10) and again in the Roman siege of AD 70, as the historian Josephus records. It is also a picture of a community turning on itself (Gal 5:15).
- **"I took my staff Favor, and I broke it, annulling the covenant that I had made with all the peoples" (v10).** God's "covenant with all the peoples" seems to mean his restraint of the nations, the protection that kept them from destroying his people. When the staff breaks, the nations are free to do what they will.
- **"They knew that it was the word of the LORD" (v11).** Someone was watching and understood. Whether they were the "poor of the flock" or the "traders," the sign was clear.
- **"Give me my wages; but if not, keep them" (v12).** The shepherd's service is over and he asks to be paid off. He leaves it to them to decide what he is worth.
- **"They weighed out as my wages thirty pieces of silver" (v12).** Thirty shekels of silver was the compensation the Law set for a slave gored to death by an ox (Exod 21:32). It was a real sum of money, but as the price of a shepherd who had served them, it was an insult. They valued him at the price of a dead slave.
- **"Throw it to the potter—the lordly price at which I was priced by them" (v13).** "Lordly price" is bitter sarcasm, literally "the magnificent value." The phrase "throw it to the potter" may have been a saying, like "throw it to the dogs," for something worthless. Potters were associated with the temple, which needed endless clay vessels (Lev 6:28), and potters worked in the valley just south of the city (Jer 18:2; 19:1–2). The Syriac translation reads "treasury" (*'otsar*) instead of "potter" (*yotser*), a very similar word. Either way, the money ends up in the temple and with the potter.
- **"So I took the thirty pieces of silver and threw them into the house of the LORD, to the potter" (v13).** The fulfilment in the Gospels is precise. Judas agreed with the chief priests to betray Jesus for thirty pieces of silver (Matt 26:14–15). When Jesus was condemned, Judas "threw down the pieces of silver into the temple" (Matt 27:5). The priests would not put "blood money" into the treasury, so they used it to buy "the potter's field" as a burial place for strangers (Matt 27:6–7).
- **Why does Matthew say "Jeremiah"?** Matthew 27:9–10 quotes this passage as "what had been spoken by the prophet Jeremiah." The most likely answer is that Matthew combines Zechariah 11 with Jeremiah's potter and field passages (Jer 18–19; 32:6–15) and names the more prominent prophet, as Mark 1:2 names Isaiah when quoting Malachi and Isaiah together. Others suggest Jeremiah stood first in the scroll of the Prophets and gave his name to the collection, or that Zechariah drew on a saying of Jeremiah.
- **"Then I broke my second staff Union, annulling the brotherhood between Judah and Israel" (v14).** The hope of reunion promised in 10:6 is now shattered by the flock's rejection of its shepherd. Inner division follows outer exposure. By the time of Jesus, Jews and Samaritans had no dealings with each other (John 4:9), and Jerusalem's own factions tore the city apart during the Roman siege.
- **"Take once more the equipment of a foolish shepherd" (v15).** Zechariah now plays a second part. "Foolish" in the Old Testament is moral, not mental: the fool is the one who rejects God (Ps 14:1). The equipment is the same staff and bag, but in the wrong hands.
- **"A shepherd who does not care for those being destroyed, or seek the young or heal the maimed or nourish the healthy" (v16).** Every duty of a good shepherd is listed and denied. Ezekiel 34:4 has almost the same list: "the weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back." **"Devours the flesh of the fat ones, tearing off even their hoofs"** describes a man who consumes the flock down to the last scrap.
- **"Woe to my worthless shepherd, who deserts the flock!" (v17).** "Worthless" is *'elil*, a word often used for idols, "nothings" (Lev 19:4; Isa 2:8). The KJV has "idol shepherd." He is as empty as an idol and as useless to the sheep. **"May the sword strike his arm and his right eye"**: the arm he should have used to protect the flock withers, and the eye he should have used to watch it goes blind. He is judged in exactly what he neglected.
- **Who are the shepherds?** Readers see the good shepherd in vv4–14 as a picture of God's own care and finally of the Messiah, since the New Testament applies the rejection and the price to Jesus. The worthless shepherd may be the corrupt leaders of the later centuries before Christ, the Roman rulers or false messiahs after him, or a final figure, the "man of lawlessness" Paul describes (2 Thess 2:3–4). Many futurist interpreters see the Antichrist here. The text does not name him, and it is wise to keep the main lesson in view: a people who reject the good shepherd are left with a worthless one.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the prophet as a type of Christ, who took on "the flock of slaughter," and says the staff "Beauty" was "denoting the temple," broken when the covenant ended. He calls the thirty pieces "a goodly price" in irony and links the worthless shepherd to the scribes and Pharisees: "Woe to the idol-shepherd." JFB reads the chapter as the Roman destruction of Judea, calls the thirty pieces "the price of a gored bond-servant," and holds that Matthew names Jeremiah because Jeremiah's potter passages are the source of the image. He sees the foolish shepherd as a final Antichrist. Guzik, drawing on Boice, also reads vv1–3 as the Roman invasion of AD 66–70, takes the three shepherds as prophets, priests and kings, and gives three answers to the Jeremiah question. He applies the chapter to the danger of following attractive false shepherds, noting that a majority can be badly wrong. The Tyndale notes count more than forty proposals for the three shepherds and conclude that none works, which is a fair warning against confidence.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus said, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). He saw a flock "harassed and helpless" and fleeced by its leaders (Matt 9:36; 23:1–36). He healed the maimed, sought the lost and fed the hungry, every duty the worthless shepherd refused. And the flock weighed out his value at thirty pieces of silver, the price of a dead slave. Judas threw the money into the temple, and it bought a potter's field. Even that was in God's hands. The betrayal priced the shepherd low, but it was the means by which he laid down his life for the sheep. "You were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). The good shepherd was struck so that the flock could be gathered, which is exactly where Zechariah goes next (13:7).
