*Setting:* Jerusalem in the fifth century BC, around the time of Ezra and Nehemiah. In chapter 1, God declared his love for Israel ("I have loved you") and then charged the priests with despising his name by offering blind, lame and sick animals at his altar. Chapter 2 continues that second dispute with a direct warning to the priests, measured against the faithful priest God had intended Levi to be. Then Malachi turns to the whole people with a third dispute about faithlessness. Men of Judah were marrying women who worshiped other gods, and some were divorcing the Jewish wives they had married in their youth. The chapter's key word is "faithless" (*bagad*), which appears five times in vv10–16. The chapter ends by opening the fourth dispute, where the people complain that God does not care about justice. That dispute runs on into chapter 3.

#### The Lord Rebukes the Priests (vv1–9)
- **"And now, O priests, this command is for you" (v1).** "Command" here probably means a decree of judgment, the sentence that follows. The priests have been charged in 1:6–14. Now comes the verdict.
- **"If you will not take it to heart to give honor to my name" (v2).** "Take it to heart" means to set one's mind on it, a matter of the will, as the Tyndale notes put it, not of feelings. The issue is the same as in 1:6: "Where is my honor?"
- **"I will curse your blessings. Indeed, I have already cursed them" (v2).** The "blessings" may be the priests' income from the offerings, or the blessings they pronounced on the people (Num 6:22–27). Either way, God will turn their blessings to curses, just as the covenant warned (Deut 28:15–20). "Already cursed" means the process had begun. The poor harvests of the period may be part of it (compare Hag 1:6; Mal 3:9–11).
- **"I will rebuke your offspring" (v3).** "Offspring" is literally "seed," which could mean descendants or the seed grain of the fields. The Greek Old Testament read a word with almost the same letters, "arm," giving "I will cut off your arm," a picture of the priest's arm raised in blessing or sacrifice. The ESV follows the Hebrew.
- **"Spread dung on your faces, the dung of your offerings" (v3).** This is one of the most shocking images in the prophets. The dung (*peresh*) was the contents of the sacrificial animals' intestines, which the Law required to be carried outside the camp and burned with the carcass (Exod 29:14; Lev 4:11–12; 16:27). The priests who treated the offerings with contempt would be treated like the offerings' waste. "You shall be taken away with it": carried out to the refuse heap.
- **"That my covenant with Levi may stand" (v4).** God's purpose in the rebuke is not to destroy the priesthood but to preserve it. The "covenant with Levi" recalls God's promise to Phinehas, Aaron's grandson, "my covenant of peace… the covenant of a perpetual priesthood" (Num 25:12–13), and Moses' blessing of Levi (Deut 33:8–11). Nehemiah used the same idea: "the covenant of the priesthood and the Levites" (Neh 13:29).
- **"My covenant with him was one of life and peace… a covenant of fear, and he feared me" (v5).** God gave life and peace, and Levi responded with reverence. "He stood in awe of my name" is the opposite of the priests who "despise my name" (1:6).
- **"True instruction was in his mouth" (v6).** "Instruction" is *torah*, often translated "law," but its basic meaning is teaching or direction. Priests were Israel's teachers (Lev 10:11; Deut 33:10; Hos 4:6). The ideal priest taught truth, lived with integrity, "walked with me in peace and uprightness, and he turned many from iniquity." That last phrase is reflected in Daniel's picture of those "who turn many to righteousness" shining "like the stars forever" (Dan 12:3).
- **"For he is the messenger of the LORD of hosts" (v7).** "Messenger" is *mal'akh*, the same word behind Malachi's name ("my messenger") and the coming "messenger" of 3:1. This is one of the very few places in the Old Testament where a priest is called God's messenger. The title usually belongs to prophets (Hag 1:13) or angels. The priest's lips should "guard knowledge" so the people can come to him and learn God's will.
- **"But you have turned aside from the way" (v8).** The priests of Malachi's day did the opposite of Levi in every respect. Levi turned many from sin. They "caused many to stumble by your instruction." Their teaching made people fall.
- **"I make you despised and abased before all the people" (v9).** They despised God's name, so God makes them despised. The punishment matches the crime.
- **"But show partiality in your instruction" (v9).** "Show partiality" is literally "lift up faces," the same expression used in 1:8–9 ("will he… show you favor?"). The priests asked God to lift up *their* faces while they lifted up the faces of the powerful in their rulings. They bent the Law for those who could pay or reward them (compare Deut 16:19; Mic 3:11).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the priests are rebuked first because they were meant to lead: "Ministers cannot sin or suffer alone." On the dung he notes that the stomach of the sacrifice was part of the priests' share, so the punishment fits the offense. On v9 he writes, "Because ye do not keep the condition of the covenant, I will not fulfil the promise." He calls the priest "the interpreter of His will." Guzik explains the dung as the waste of the sacrifices burned outside the sanctuary, and traces the priests' sin to empty formalism without a heart for God. He sets Levi before them as the model priest, who revered God, taught truly and turned people from sin. The Tyndale notes observe that the title "messenger" usually belongs to prophets and is applied here to priests because they were to explain God's word to the people.

#### Judah Profaned the Covenant (vv10–16)
- **"Have we not all one Father? Has not one God created us?" (v10).** Malachi now speaks to the whole community and includes himself ("we"). The "one Father" is God, who created Israel as his people (Isa 43:1; 64:8; Deut 32:6). The point is not that all humanity is one spiritual family, though that may be in the background. It is that the people of Judah belong to one family under God, so betraying each other is betraying their Father. "One God" echoes the *Shema* (Deut 6:4).
- **"Why then are we faithless to one another, profaning the covenant of our fathers?" (v10).** "Faithless" (*bagad*) means to act treacherously, to break faith with someone who trusted you. "The covenant of our fathers" is the Sinai covenant, which bound Israel both to God and to one another.
- **"Judah has been faithless, and abomination has been committed" (v11).** "Abomination" is a strong word, often used of idolatry (Deut 7:25–26; 13:14). "Judah has profaned the sanctuary of the LORD, which he loves." "Sanctuary" may mean the temple, or "what is holy," that is, Israel itself as God's holy people (Ezra 9:2, "the holy race"; Jer 2:3).
- **"And has married the daughter of a foreign god" (v11).** This is the heart of the charge. The issue was not ethnicity but worship. A "daughter of a foreign god" is a woman who belongs to and serves another god. The Law forbade such marriages because they would turn Israel's heart to other gods (Deut 7:3–4), as they had turned Solomon's (1 Kgs 11:1–8). Ezra and Nehemiah faced exactly this (Ezra 9–10; Neh 13:23–27). Ruth the Moabite, who said "your God my God" (Ruth 1:16), shows that converts were welcome.
- **"May the LORD cut off from the tents of Jacob any descendant of the man who does this" (v12).** The Hebrew is difficult, literally "the one who wakes and the one who answers." It seems to be an idiom for "everyone," and the ESV renders it "any descendant." The man who does this, and still "brings an offering to the LORD of hosts" as if nothing were wrong, will find his offering no protection.
- **"And this second thing you do. You cover the LORD's altar with tears" (v13).** The weeping may be the men's own, crying out because God no longer accepts their offerings. Or it may be the tears of their abandoned wives, flooding the altar with their grief. JFB and some others take it that way. Either way, God has stopped receiving their worship.
- **"Because the LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth" (v14).** Marriage was not only a private contract. God himself witnessed it. "The wife of your youth" is the woman a man married when both were young (Prov 5:18). The picture suggests older men divorcing the wives who had shared their lives to marry younger women, possibly foreign women from wealthier families. Some scholars suggest an economic motive, marrying into the families who held land and trade, but the text does not say so.
- **"She is your companion and your wife by covenant" (v14).** "Companion" (*chaberet*) means a partner, one joined to another. Proverbs uses "covenant" of marriage in the same way: the adulteress "forsakes the companion of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Prov 2:17). This is one of the clearest Old Testament texts calling marriage a covenant.
- **"Did he not make them one, with a portion of the Spirit in their union?" (v15).** This verse is notoriously hard. The Hebrew is very compressed, literally something like "And not one has made, and a remnant of spirit to him." Translations vary widely. The ESV takes it as a reference to Genesis 2:24, where God made husband and wife "one flesh." Others read "one" as God himself ("Did not the One make…?"). Still others think it answers men who appealed to Abraham's example in taking Hagar. JFB sets out these views, including Calvin's reading that "one" refers to the original unity of marriage.
- **"And what was the one God seeking? Godly offspring" (v15).** God's purpose in marriage includes raising children who know and serve him (compare Deut 6:6–7). Mixed marriages and broken homes threatened exactly that, as Nehemiah found when the children of mixed marriages could no longer speak Hebrew (Neh 13:24).
- **"So guard yourselves in your spirit" (v15).** Unfaithfulness begins inside. Guarding the heart comes before guarding the marriage (Prov 4:23).
- **"For the man who does not love his wife but divorces her… covers his garment with violence" (v16).** The Hebrew here is also difficult. It begins literally, "For he hates, sending away." The older translations (KJV, NASB, NIV 1984) read it as God speaking: "I hate divorce." The ESV, NIV 2011 and others read "the man who hates and divorces," making it a description of the husband. On either reading, divorce of a faithful wife out of dislike is condemned in the strongest terms. "Covers his garment with violence" may draw on the custom of a man spreading his garment over a woman as a sign of marriage (Ruth 3:9; Ezek 16:8). Instead of covering her with protection, he covers himself with violence.
- **What this passage does and does not address.** Malachi is not giving a complete teaching on divorce. The Law permitted divorce in some cases (Deut 24:1–4), and Jesus explained that Moses allowed it "because of your hardness of heart," while pointing back to the creation design (Matt 19:3–9). Paul gives further guidance for believers (1 Cor 7:10–16). Christians differ on the details. But Malachi is clear about God's heart: he is a witness to the marriage covenant, and treachery against a faithful wife is treachery against him.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "God is a common Father to all mankind, and, upon that account, all we are brethren, members one of another." He calls the wife "the wife of thy covenant," a bond the husband may not break while she is faithful. He says God gave one wife to one husband so that they might raise "a godly seed." He also writes that "the God of Israel saith that he hateth putting away," following the older reading. And: "Those that would be kept from sin must take heed to their spirits, for there all sin begins." JFB says sin "against a wife, a daughter of Israel, is a sin against God," and thinks the tears of v13 were those of the "unoffending" wives. Guzik reads the "one Father" as the Creator whose image all people bear, not as a claim that all are saved. He stresses that the issue was faith, not ethnicity, pointing to Ruth. On v16 he gives several reasons divorce is grievous: it breaks a vow, it harms others and it shows a heart turning from God. He also notes the exceptions given elsewhere in the New Testament.

#### The Messenger of the Lord (v17)
- **"You have wearied the LORD with your words" (v17).** The fourth dispute begins. God does not grow tired from effort (Isa 40:28), but he can be wearied by sin (Isa 43:24). Their words are an insult.
- **"Everyone who does evil is good in the sight of the LORD, and he delights in them" (v17).** The people looked around and saw the wicked prospering. They concluded that God must not care, or must even approve. This complaint appears often in the Psalms (Ps 73:2–14) and the prophets (Hab 1:2–4, 13; Jer 12:1).
- **"Or by asking, 'Where is the God of justice?'" (v17).** The question sounds like a sincere longing for justice, but here it is a sneer. God's answer comes in the next verses, which begin 3:1: "Behold, I send my messenger… and the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple." They asked where the God of justice was. He was coming, and his coming would be more than they had bargained for.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "Sinful words are more offensive to the God of heaven than they are commonly thought to be," and "Those who think God a friend to sin affront him and deceive themselves." He answers their question by saying "the God of judgment is at hand." JFB notes that the answer is in 3:1: they wanted a restorer of earthly fortunes, and God promised a judge. Guzik says they misunderstood what God's justice would mean for them, since it would fall on them too.

#### Pointing to Christ
Malachi holds up the faithful priest, the true "messenger of the LORD of hosts," who taught truth, walked with God in peace and "turned many from iniquity" (vv6–7). No priest of Malachi's day lived up to that picture. Jesus did. He is the faithful high priest who taught the truth without partiality (Mark 12:14), lived without sin and turns many from iniquity (Acts 3:26). Malachi also calls marriage a covenant God witnesses and protects. Paul says that covenant points to "Christ and the church" (Eph 5:25–32). Unlike the faithless husbands of Malachi's day, Christ never abandons his bride. "If we are faithless, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself" (2 Tim 2:13).
