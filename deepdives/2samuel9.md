*Setting:* David's reign in Jerusalem, perhaps around 995 BC, some fifteen or more years after Saul and Jonathan died on Mount Gilboa. David is now secure. He has united the tribes, taken Jerusalem, brought up the ark, received God's covenant promise of an everlasting house (ch 7) and defeated enemies on every side (ch 8). Years before, when David was a fugitive, Saul's son Jonathan made a covenant with him. Jonathan knew David would be king, and he asked one thing: "Do not cut off your steadfast love from my house forever, when the LORD cuts off every one of the enemies of David" (1 Sam 20:14–17, 42). Now that the enemies are cut off, David remembers. In the ancient world a new king from a new family usually wiped out the old royal line, so that no one could claim the throne. This short chapter shows David doing the opposite. It is placed between two chapters of war, a quiet scene of grace in the middle of the battles.

#### David's Kindness to Mephibosheth (vv1–13)
- **"Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may show him kindness for Jonathan's sake?" (v1).** "Kindness" is *hesed*, the great Hebrew word for loyal, covenant love that keeps its promises. Jonathan had asked David for *hesed* (1 Sam 20:14–15), and David now goes looking for someone to receive it. He does not wait for anyone to come and ask. He seeks them out.
- **"Ziba… a servant of the house of Saul" (v2).** Ziba had been a steward of Saul's estates. He seems to have done well since Saul's fall: he has fifteen sons and twenty servants (v10). He answers the king with "I am your servant." He will play a mixed and doubtful role later in the story (16:1–4; 19:24–30).
- **"That I may show the kindness of God to him" (v3).** David repeats his question, and this time he calls it "the kindness (*hesed*) of God." Jonathan had used almost the same phrase, "the steadfast love of the LORD" (1 Sam 20:14). David means kindness like God's, kindness that comes from God and copies the way God has treated David himself. He has just received God's promise of unfailing *hesed* (7:15). Now he passes it on.
- **"There is still a son of Jonathan; he is crippled in his feet" (v3).** Ziba mentions the disability first, almost as if to say he is no threat and no use. The reader already knows the story. He was five when the news came from Jezreel, and his nurse dropped him as she fled (4:4).
- **"In the house of Machir the son of Ammiel, at Lo-debar" (v4).** Lo-debar was a town in Gilead, east of the Jordan, not far from Mahanaim, where Ish-bosheth had ruled. Its name sounds like "no thing" in Hebrew, and the prophet Amos later puns on it (Amos 6:13, where the ESV footnote explains it as "nothing"). Mephibosheth was living in hiding, in an obscure place, in someone else's house. Machir, his host, appears again later as one of the men who brought food to David when he fled from Absalom (17:27–29). David's kindness may have won a friend.
- **"Then King David sent and brought him" (v5).** Imagine the message arriving: the king has sent for the grandson of Saul. For Mephibosheth this could only mean one thing, and it was not good.
- **"Fell on his face and paid homage" (v6).** With his lame feet, getting down on the ground before the king would have been hard. He comes expecting the worst.
- **"Mephibosheth!" (v6).** David calls him by name. It is a personal moment. The king is not addressing a political problem but a person. "Behold, I am your servant."
- **"Do not fear" (v7).** These are David's first words after his name. Then three gifts, each larger than the last. First, "I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan." Mephibosheth receives grace not because of anything he has done but because of someone else, his father, and the covenant his father made. Second, "I will restore to you all the land of Saul your father." Saul's family estate at Gibeah in Benjamin had likely passed to the crown or been taken over by others. Now it is returned. Third, **"you shall eat at my table always."** To eat at the king's table was a high honor and a sign of permanent favor and protection. It also meant David would keep him close, in Jerusalem, where he would be safe and could not become the center of a revolt. Kindness and wisdom go together.
- **"What is your servant, that you should show regard for a dead dog such as I?" (v8).** A dead dog was the lowest, most worthless thing a person could name. David once used almost the same words of himself to Saul: "After whom do you pursue? After a dead dog!" (1 Sam 24:14). Mephibosheth's words may show deep humility, or the habit of a man who has spent years thinking of himself as nothing. Grandson of a king, he now sees himself as worthless. David's answer is not argument but action.
- **"All that belonged to Saul and to all his house I have given to your master's grandson" (v9).** David makes the gift legal and public. He calls Ziba in and puts it in his hearing. "Grandson" translates the Hebrew "son," which often means any descendant.
- **"You and your sons and your servants shall till the land for him" (v10).** Ziba and his large household are to work the estate, so that Mephibosheth's household will have income. Mephibosheth himself will not need to farm. He will be at the king's table. Ziba agrees: "According to all that my lord the king commands his servant, so will your servant do" (v11).
- **"Like one of the king's sons" (v11).** This is the heart of the chapter. Mephibosheth is not seated at the end of the table as a guest or a charity case. He is treated as family, like Amnon, Absalom, Solomon and the rest. The grandson of David's old enemy is welcomed as a son.
- **"Mephibosheth had a young son, whose name was Mica" (v12).** This brief note matters. Through Mica, Saul's and Jonathan's line continues. 1 Chronicles 8:34–40 and 9:40–44 trace Mica's descendants for many generations. They were a large clan of mighty warriors and archers in Benjamin. David's kindness kept Jonathan's family alive.
- **"He ate always at the king's table. Now he was lame in both his feet" (v13).** The chapter ends with a pairing. "Always at the king's table," and "lame in both his feet." The king's grace did not heal his feet. But at the table, his feet were hidden. What he could not fix, the king's kindness covered.
- **What came later.** The story of Mephibosheth is not finished. When Absalom rebels, Ziba tells David that Mephibosheth stayed in Jerusalem hoping to get back his grandfather's kingdom, and David gives Ziba all the land (16:1–4). When David returns, Mephibosheth comes out to meet him unwashed and unshaven, saying Ziba deceived him, and David splits the land between them (19:24–30). Who told the truth is still debated. And when the Gibeonites demand seven of Saul's descendants, David spares Mephibosheth "because of the oath of the LORD that was between them, between David and Jonathan" (21:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Jonathan was David's sworn friend, therefore he shows kindness to his son Mephibosheth." He notes how easily gratitude to friends, and to God, is forgotten in the rush of public business, and David's remembering stands out because of it. Guzik makes "the kindness of God" the key to the chapter: David wanted to show someone else the kindness God had shown him. He also observes that Mephibosheth had stayed away from David for years out of fears that were not founded, and that years of hiding in poverty had taught him to see himself as worthless. Guzik reads the chapter both ways: believers are Mephibosheth, receiving undeserved covenant love, and they are also called to act like David. JFB explains the arrangement with Ziba as a share of the produce for working the estate, says the place at the royal table was "as is still the custom with Eastern monarchs," and notes that through Mica "the name and memory of the excellent Jonathan was preserved." JFB has no note on v8. The Tyndale notes add a contrast: Saul once sent for David so that David could serve him (1 Sam 16:19), but David sends for Mephibosheth so that he can serve Mephibosheth.

#### Pointing to Christ
Few Old Testament stories picture the gospel as clearly. A member of the fallen, rebel house, lame and hiding far away, expects judgment when the king sends for him. Instead he hears "Do not fear," receives back what was lost, and is seated at the king's table like one of his sons. All of it comes "for Jonathan's sake," because of a covenant made by someone else. That is how God treats sinners in Christ. He sought us out first (Luke 19:10). As Henry puts it, "The Son of God seeks this lost and ruined race, who sought not after him." He shows kindness for the sake of his Son and the covenant sealed in his blood (Eph 4:32). He adopts us as sons and daughters (Gal 4:4–7). And he seats us at his table, not because our lameness is gone yet, but because his grace covers it, until the day "the lame man shall leap like a deer" (Isa 35:6) at the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:9).
