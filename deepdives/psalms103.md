*Setting:* The title says simply "Of David." Unlike many of David's psalms, this one names no crisis, enemy or event. It is pure praise, looking back over a life in which God has forgiven, healed, rescued and kept. Psalm 103 is paired with Psalm 104: both open and close with "Bless the LORD, O my soul," and they belong together, the first praising God as Redeemer and the second as Creator. It follows Psalm 102, the prayer of a man fading like grass while God's years have no end, and Psalm 103 picks up the same contrast (vv15–17) but sings it from the far side of the trouble. At its centre stands God's own description of himself to Moses after the golden calf (Exod 34:6–7), the most quoted statement of God's character in the Old Testament. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's main movements: praise for what God has done for one person, for what he is to his people, for his everlasting love set against human frailty, and finally a call to all creation.

#### Bless the LORD, O My Soul (vv1–5)
- **"Bless the LORD, O my soul" (v1).** To "bless" (*barak*) God is not to give him something he lacks. It is to speak well of him, to kneel before him in praise and gratitude. When God blesses people he gives them good. When people bless God they acknowledge the good he is and has given. David speaks to his own soul, as in Psalms 42:5 and 62:5. He does not wait to feel like praising. He tells himself to.
- **"And all that is within me" (v1).** Every part of a person is summoned: mind, will, feelings, memory. Jesus' summary of the law asks for the same wholeness, loving God "with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind" (Matt 22:37). Half-hearted praise is not what David has in mind.
- **"Bless his holy name" (v1).** God's "name" is God as he has made himself known. "Holy" means set apart, utterly other and utterly pure. David begins with who God is before he lists what God has done.
- **"Forget not all his benefits" (v2).** "Benefits" (*gemul*) means dealings, what God has done or paid out to someone. Forgetting is Israel's repeated failure in the Old Testament (Deut 8:11–14; Ps 78:11; 106:13). Hezekiah, after God healed him, "did not make return according to the benefit done to him" (2 Chr 32:25). Gratitude runs on memory, so David makes himself remember.
- **Five benefits (vv3–5).** David lists them in a string of participles, each beginning "who…": who forgives, who heals, who redeems, who crowns, who satisfies. The list moves from the deepest need, guilt, to the fullest gift, a life renewed.
- **"Who forgives all your iniquity" (v3).** Forgiveness comes first. "Iniquity" (*avon*) is the crookedness of sin and also the guilt that comes with it. "All" is the key word. Nothing is held back. David, who wrote Psalm 51 after his sin with Bathsheba, knew how much that word meant.
- **"Who heals all your diseases" (v3).** The Old Testament often links sickness and sin (Exod 15:26; Ps 41:3–4), though never as a simple rule (Job; John 9:2–3). Here forgiveness and healing sit side by side, as when Jesus forgave and then healed the paralysed man (Mark 2:5–12). The psalm does not promise that every believer will be healed of every sickness in this life. It praises the God who is the healer, whether by ordinary means or by miracle, and whose full healing comes in the resurrection (Rev 21:4).
- **"Who redeems your life from the pit" (v4).** "Redeems" is *ga'al*, the work of the kinsman-redeemer, the close relative who bought back family land, freed a relative sold into slavery or avenged a death (Lev 25:25, 47–49; Ruth 4). "The pit" (*shachat*) is the grave or the place of the dead. God acts as his people's next of kin and buys them back from death itself.
- **"Who crowns you with steadfast love and mercy" (v4).** The one rescued from the pit is not merely let off. He is crowned. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love, and "mercy" (*rachamim*) is tender compassion, from the word for womb. A beggar pulled from the grave is given a king's crown.
- **"Who satisfies you with good so that your youth is renewed like the eagle's" (v5).** The eagle (*nesher*, which can also mean the great griffon vulture) was a picture of strength and soaring flight. Ancient writers had legends that eagles renewed themselves in old age, and Henry repeats one: the eagle "casts all her feathers" when nearly a hundred and grows new ones. The psalm needs no legend. Its point is that God renews his people's strength, as Isaiah says: "they shall mount up with wings like eagles" (Isa 40:31). The renewal comes from God, not from the bird.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of forgiveness and healing, "This is cured in sanctification; when sin is mortified, the disease is healed," reading the diseases mainly as sickness of the soul, and adds, "Our crimes were capital, but God saves our lives by pardoning them." On the crown he asks, "What is the crown of glory but God's favour?" Guzik takes the diseases more literally, as real bodily sickness, healed by natural means or miracle now and fully in the age to come, and he notes that David speaks to his own soul because it was not praising as it ought. JFB reads the diseases as "penal inflictions," the consequences of sin, so that forgiveness and healing go together, and notes that "redeems" implies a cost. The three differ over how literal the healing is, and the text holds both together.

#### The LORD Is Merciful and Gracious (vv6–14)
- **"The LORD works righteousness and justice for all who are oppressed" (v6).** David widens the view from his own life to God's dealings with all who are wronged. The nearest example follows at once: Israel oppressed in Egypt.
- **"He made known his ways to Moses, his acts to the people of Israel" (v7).** There is a contrast here. The people saw God's *acts*, the plagues, the sea, the manna. Moses was shown God's *ways*, the reasons and character behind the acts. Moses had prayed, "Please show me now your ways, that I may know you" (Exod 33:13), and God answered by passing before him and proclaiming his name.
- **"The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" (v8).** This is a near quotation of Exodus 34:6, God's self-description to Moses on the mountain. Its setting matters: Israel had just made the golden calf, and God revealed his mercy in the aftermath of their worst sin. The same words echo through the Bible (Num 14:18; Neh 9:17; Ps 86:15; 145:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2). "Slow to anger" is literally "long of nostrils," a Hebrew picture of someone whose anger takes a long time to burn hot.
- **"He will not always chide, nor will he keep his anger forever" (v9).** "Chide" is *riv*, which often means to bring a legal case or lawsuit against someone. The prophets use it for God's case against Israel (Mic 6:2; Hos 4:1). God has a just case against sinners, but he does not keep pressing it forever. Isaiah says the same: "I will not contend forever, nor will I always be angry" (Isa 57:16). "Keep" (*natar*) is the word for nursing a grudge.
- **"He does not deal with us according to our sins" (v10).** This is grace stated as plainly as the Old Testament ever states it. God's people do not get what they deserve. The psalm does not explain *how* God can do this and remain just. The New Testament does: in Christ, God is both "just and the justifier" (Rom 3:26).
- **"As high as the heavens are above the earth" (v11).** The first of three comparisons. To an ancient Israelite the height of the sky was as near to infinite as anything could be. So great is God's *chesed* "toward those who fear him." To "fear" God is not to cower but to revere and trust him, taking him seriously as God.
- **"As far as the east is from the west" (v12).** The second comparison. North and south have limits, since travelling north eventually becomes travelling south. East and west never meet. The psalmist probably did not have a globe in mind, but the image works even better for modern readers. Sins God removes are not stored nearby to be brought up later (Mic 7:19; Isa 38:17; Heb 8:12).
- **"As a father shows compassion to his children" (v13).** The third comparison is not about distance but closeness. "Shows compassion" is *racham* again, tender, almost maternal love. The Old Testament calls God Israel's father only rarely (Deut 32:6; Isa 63:16; Jer 31:9), but this verse lays the ground for Jesus' constant teaching about "your Father in heaven" (Matt 6:8–9; Luke 15:20).
- **"For he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust" (v14).** "Frame" is *yetser*, from the verb *yatsar*, "to form" as a potter shapes clay. It is the verb used when God "formed the man of dust from the ground" (Gen 2:7). The lexicon lists this verse under the sense "form of man as made of the dust." God made human beings and knows how fragile they are. He does not expect from dust what only he can do.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws from v7 that God "made known his ways unto Moses" so that later generations could learn of him through the written word, and he treats vv6–18 as God showing himself "just, and kind, and constant" toward his church. Guzik explains the east-west image with the globe: north and south meet at the poles, but east and west never do. He notes the legal sense of the word for "chide," and adds that in the incarnation God's knowledge of human frailty became knowledge by experience (Heb 4:15). JFB stresses that God's kindness is "not earned" and that removed transgressions no longer affect the relationship with him. All three read v10 as pure mercy, and the Tyndale notes connect v13 directly to Jesus' teaching on God as Father.

#### As for Man, His Days Are Like Grass (vv15–19)
- **"As for man, his days are like grass; he flourishes like a flower of the field" (v15).** Verse 14 called people dust. Now they are grass and wildflowers. In Israel grass and flowers sprang up after the winter rains and were gone within weeks. A flower of the field, unlike a garden plant, has no one to shelter it. Isaiah 40:6–8 uses the same picture, and Peter quotes Isaiah (1 Pet 1:24–25).
- **"For the wind passes over it, and it is gone" (v16).** The hot east wind off the desert (the sirocco) could wither a hillside in a day. **"And its place knows it no more"** is a haunting phrase from Job 7:10. A person dies, and soon even the place where they lived forgets them.
- **"But the steadfast love of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting" (v17).** The great "but." Humans are brief. God's *chesed* has no beginning and no end. The comfort is not that people last, but that God's love for them does. This answers Psalm 102's lament directly (102:11–12, 24–27).
- **"And his righteousness to children's children" (v17).** God's faithfulness runs down the generations. The individual is grass, but God's love outlives each generation and reaches the next. Exodus 34:7 spoke of God "keeping steadfast love for thousands," and this verse echoes it.
- **"To those who keep his covenant and remember to do his commandments" (v18).** The promise has a shape. It belongs to those who fear God (vv11, 13, 17), keep his covenant and "remember" his commands. Remembering ties back to v2: just as David must not forget God's benefits, God's people must not forget his word. This is not earning God's love, since v10 already ruled that out. It describes the people who live inside it.
- **"The LORD has established his throne in the heavens, and his kingdom rules over all" (v19).** The reason God's love can be everlasting is that his rule is. His throne is not one among many but over all. This verse links Psalm 103 to the "The LORD reigns" psalms just before it (Pss 93; 96–99) and prepares for the call to all creation that follows.
- **Commentators:** Henry sets v19 apart as praise for God's "government of the world." Guzik passes on a story Spurgeon cites, of Luther telling an anxious Melanchthon to stop trying to govern the world, and he explains *chesed* as loyal covenant love that does not rise and fall with its object's moods. JFB treats God's universal rule as the ground of assurance that he will keep his promises, and reads v16 as saying that a passing wind is enough to end a human life. Guzik and JFB both stress that the promise of v17 is bounded by v18, those who keep his covenant.

#### Bless the LORD, O You His Angels (vv20–22)
- **"Bless the LORD, O you his angels, you mighty ones who do his word" (v20).** David's soul is not big enough for the praise God deserves, so he calls on heaven. Angels are "mighty ones" (literally "mighty in strength") whose greatness lies in obedience: they "do his word, obeying the voice of his word." Jesus taught his followers to pray that God's will be done "on earth as it is in heaven" (Matt 6:10), where angels already do it.
- **"Bless the LORD, all his hosts, his ministers, who do his will!" (v21).** "Hosts" (*tsava*) means armies, the vast ranks of heaven. Some interpreters include the stars and heavenly bodies (Ps 148:2–3). "Ministers" means servants who wait on a king. Hebrews calls angels "ministering spirits sent out to serve" those who will inherit salvation (Heb 1:14).
- **"Bless the LORD, all his works, in all places of his dominion" (v22).** The circle widens one last time, from angels to everything God has made, "in all places." Since his kingdom "rules over all" (v19), every corner of it owes him praise. Psalm 148 develops this call in full, and Revelation 5:13 shows it fulfilled, with every creature in heaven and on earth praising the Lamb.
- **"Bless the LORD, O my soul!" (v22).** After summoning angels and all creation, David comes back to himself. The psalm ends where it began. The great choir does not excuse the single voice. Psalm 104 then begins with the same words.
- **Commentators:** Henry says David "desires the assistance of the holy angels, and all the works of God, in praising him," and urges that in singing this psalm "we must in a special manner get our hearts affected with the goodness of God." Guzik, drawing on Morgan, notes the movement from "my" to "our" to "all" across the psalm, and passes on Kidner's point that the song is not a solo. JFB reads "hosts" as vast multitudes and notes how the psalmist closes by addressing his own soul again.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 103 praises a God who does not deal with sinners according to their sins, but it never says how a holy God can do that. The cross answers. The sins removed "as far as the east is from the west" were laid on Jesus, who bore them "in his body on the tree" (1 Pet 2:24). He is the true kinsman-redeemer of v4, who became our flesh and blood so he could buy his family back from the pit (Heb 2:14–15). He healed the sick and forgave sins together, showing in his own ministry the benefits of v3 (Mark 2:5–12). He knew our frame from the inside, as one who was himself formed in a womb, grew tired, wept and died. And through him those who were dust and grass are given a share in the steadfast love that is "from everlasting to everlasting," with a youth that will be renewed for good in the resurrection.
