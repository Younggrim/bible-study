*Setting:* Psalm 128 is the ninth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. It is a companion to Psalm 127. That psalm said that unless the LORD builds the house, the builders labor in vain, and that children are his gift. Psalm 128 paints the picture of a house the LORD has built: honest work that bears fruit, a wife flourishing at home, children gathered around the table, and grandchildren in old age. It has no author's name and no clear date. The scene is ordinary farm and village life in Israel, the life most pilgrims came from. Families traveled to the festivals together (Luke 2:41–44), so a song like this would have been sung by fathers and mothers walking with their children. The psalm moves outward in three steps: from the individual who fears the LORD (vv1–2), to the household (vv3–4), to Zion and all Israel (vv5–6). It reads like a priestly blessing, and v5 may echo words spoken over the pilgrims when they arrived at the temple. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Blessed Is Everyone Who Fears the LORD (vv1–2)
- **"Blessed is everyone who fears the LORD" (v1).** "Blessed" (*'ashre*) means deeply happy or well off, the same word that opens Psalm 1. It is not limited to priests, kings or the wealthy. It is for "everyone," any man or woman who fears the LORD. The "fear of the LORD" (*yir'ah*) is not terror. It is reverent awe that takes God seriously, trusts him, and wants to please him. It is "the beginning of wisdom" (Ps 111:10; Prov 9:10), and it is the thread that ties this psalm together (vv1, 4).
- **"Who walks in his ways!" (v1).** The fear of the LORD shows itself in how a person lives. "Walks" is the Bible's usual picture for a way of life (Ps 1:1; Deut 10:12). Fear that does not change behavior is not the fear the psalm means.
- **"You shall eat the fruit of the labor of your hands" (v2).** The speaker turns from "everyone" to "you," addressing the pilgrim directly, as a priest would. The blessing is not riches without work. It is work that pays off. Under God's judgment, Israel was warned that others would eat what they planted (Deut 28:30–33; Amos 5:11). Under his blessing, the farmer eats his own harvest. Psalm 127 called anxious, God-less work "in vain." Here work done in the fear of the LORD bears fruit. It is a partial reversal of the curse of Genesis 3, where the ground was cursed and bread came by "the sweat of your face" (Gen 3:17–19).
- **"You shall be blessed, and it shall be well with you" (v2).** "Blessed" here is *'ashre* again. "It shall be well" (*tob*) means good. The two words sum up the life of someone under God's favor.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in vv1–2 the promise of "an honest livelihood" from one's own labor, and says that those who are truly holy are truly happy. JFB, referring back to Psalm 1:1, comments plainly: "It is a blessing to live on the fruits of one's own industry." Guzik stresses that the blessing is open to anyone, whatever their background or ability, and that real fear of God is shown by walking in his ways. He passes on Spurgeon's point that when the heart is joined to God, the feet follow.

#### Your Wife Will Be Like a Fruitful Vine (vv3–4)
- **"Your wife will be like a fruitful vine within your house" (v3).** Grapevines in Israel were often trained up the walls of a house or over a courtyard trellis, giving shade, beauty and fruit. "Within your house" is literally "in the inner parts (or recesses) of your house," the heart of the home. The vine is a picture of fruitfulness, but also of joy and celebration, since wine was for feasts (Judg 9:13; Ps 104:15). The wife is pictured not as a servant but as the life and gladness of the household.
- **"Your children will be like olive shoots around your table" (v3).** Olive trees can live for centuries, and new shoots spring up around the base of an old tree. "Shoots" (*shetilim*) are young plants or saplings. The picture is of children full of life and promise, gathered around the family meal. Olive oil was a basic sign of God's blessing in Israel, used for food, light and anointing (Deut 8:8; Jer 31:12). Vine and olive together were the classic picture of a prosperous, peaceful land.
- **"Around your table" (v3).** The family meal is the center of the scene. The fruit of the father's labor (v2) feeds the children gathered around him. Ordinary daily life, lived in the fear of the LORD, is shown as blessed.
- **"Behold, thus shall the man be blessed who fears the LORD" (v4).** The word "blessed" here is a different Hebrew word, *barak*, which means to receive blessing from God. "Behold" invites the listener to look at the picture just painted. The verse repeats the key phrase "who fears the LORD," bracketing the household scene.
- **Is this a promise for everyone?** Not every faithful believer has a spouse, children, or a fruitful harvest, and Scripture knows that well (Job; Ruth 1; 1 Cor 7:7–8). The psalm is wisdom, not a guarantee in every case. It describes how God's blessing normally looks in the life of a people living in the land under his covenant. Guzik makes this point carefully: it pictures the kind of good God loves to give, it reflects ancient Israel's ordinary life, God may give other blessings instead, and no one fears the LORD perfectly. Jesus himself was unmarried, and Paul honors singleness as a gift (1 Cor 7:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures "the wife... as a vine by the sides of the house," and "the children... as olive plants," and sees in it comfort in family relationships as one of God's gifts to the godly. JFB notes that "by the sides" (KJV) can be rendered "within," which matches the ESV, and that olive plants "are peculiarly luxuriant." Guzik notes that grapes and olives were not survival food so much as the good things of life, signs of joy and plenty, and passes on Kidner's observation that the vine suggests fruitfulness, charm and festivity.

#### The LORD Bless You from Zion (vv5–6)
- **"The LORD bless you from Zion!" (v5).** The psalm now turns into a benediction, perhaps spoken by a priest over the arriving worshipers (compare Num 6:24–26; Ps 134:3). Zion is where God's presence dwelt in the temple, so blessing flows from there. The family's happiness at home is connected to the God who meets his people in his house.
- **"May you see the prosperity of Jerusalem all the days of your life!" (v5).** The blessing widens beyond the household to the city and the nation. A happy home is not meant to be a private world. The family's welfare is bound up with the welfare of God's people as a whole. Psalm 122 prayed, "for the sake of the house of the LORD our God, I will seek your good" (Ps 122:9).
- **"May you see your children's children!" (v6).** Living long enough to see grandchildren was one of the great joys of life in Israel (Gen 50:23; Prov 17:6: "Grandchildren are the crown of the aged"). The psalm moves from the young family at the table to old age surrounded by a third generation.
- **"Peace be upon Israel!" (v6).** The same closing words as Psalm 125:5. The blessing ends with *shalom* for the whole people. Zechariah later pictured restored Jerusalem the same way: "Old men and old women shall again sit in the streets of Jerusalem... And the streets of the city shall be full of boys and girls playing" (Zech 8:4–5).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "blessings out of Zion are the best blessings," and counts seeing the welfare of God's church among the promises to the godly. JFB calls Zechariah 8 a close companion to the psalm, sums up its theme as "the temporal blessings of true piety," and says "long life crowns all other temporal favors." Guzik passes on Kidner's warning that piety can become too individualistic and family life too self-contained, and reads the turn to Jerusalem as a reminder that God's people care for the wider community, not only for their own homes.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 128 is a picture of life under God's blessing: fruitful work, a joyful marriage, children around the table, and peace upon Israel. Jesus is the one man who perfectly feared the LORD and walked in his ways, and so the blessing of this psalm belongs first to him. "The Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him... and his delight shall be in the fear of the LORD" (Isa 11:2–3). Through him the blessing reaches others. The fruit of his labor is a people redeemed, a bride, the church, made fruitful (Eph 5:25–27), and many children brought to glory (Heb 2:10–13). He calls himself the true vine (John 15:1–5), and believers are grafted like shoots into an olive tree (Rom 11:17). The blessing that came "from Zion" becomes the blessing that goes out from Jerusalem to all nations (Luke 24:47; Acts 1:8). And the psalm's final hope, a city full of peace and generations gathered at the table, looks toward the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:9), where every believer, married or single, with children or without, will sit at the table of the one who fears the LORD.
