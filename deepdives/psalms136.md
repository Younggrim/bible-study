*Setting:* Psalm 136 is the only psalm in which every verse ends with the same line: "for his steadfast love endures forever." It was written to be sung responsively. A leader or choir sang the first half of each verse, and the congregation answered with the refrain, twenty-six times. JFB suggests the refrain "was perhaps used as the Amen by the people, in worship." The refrain itself is older than the psalm. It was sung when David brought the ark to Jerusalem (1 Chron 16:34, 41), when the glory of God filled Solomon's temple (2 Chron 5:13; 7:3), when Jehoshaphat's singers marched out ahead of his army (2 Chron 20:21), and when the returned exiles laid the foundation of the second temple (Ezra 3:11). Jeremiah foresaw it being sung again in a restored Jerusalem (Jer 33:11). Psalm 136 follows the same path as Psalm 135, from creation through the Exodus to the gift of the land, often in the same words. Jewish tradition calls it the "Great Hallel" (the great praise), though sources differ on whether that name covers Psalm 136 alone or a larger group. The study notes take it as the last of Psalms 120–136. It is still read at Passover. No author is named. The title "God of heaven" in v26, common after the exile, and the "low estate" of v23 lead many to date it after the return from Babylon. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### Give Thanks to the LORD (vv1–3)
- **"Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good" (v1).** "Give thanks" (*hodu*, from *yadah*) means to acknowledge publicly, to confess, to give credit where it is due. It is thanks spoken out loud, in front of others. The first reason is not something God did but who he is: "he is good." Everything that follows in the psalm is an expression of that goodness.
- **"For his steadfast love endures forever" (v1).** "Steadfast love" translates *chesed*, one of the richest words in the Old Testament. It means loyal, committed love, the kind of love that binds itself by promise and keeps the promise. It is often used for love within a covenant, a bond like a marriage. Older English translations render it "mercy" (KJV) or "lovingkindness." Newer ones say "faithful love" or "steadfast love." No single English word holds all of it: love, kindness, faithfulness and mercy together. "Forever" (*le'olam*) means to the farthest horizon of time. God's love does not wear out or run down.
- **"Give thanks to the God of gods... to the Lord of lords" (vv2–3).** These titles come from Deuteronomy 10:17: "For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great, the mighty, and the awesome God." They do not mean there are many real gods. They mean that whatever the nations call "god" or "lord," the LORD is supreme over all. JFB says simply that the titles "denote supremacy." Three names open the psalm: *YHWH* (the LORD, his personal covenant name), *Elohim* (God) and *'Adonim* (Lord, master).
- **Why repeat the refrain?** The refrain can look monotonous on the page. Sung aloud, it does something else. Every fact about God, every event in Israel's history, is followed by the same answer: this happened because his love never ends. The psalm teaches people to read their whole story that way. Jesus warned against "empty phrases" in prayer (Matt 6:7), but this is not empty. Each repetition is tied to a different act of God.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the refrain is repeated twenty-six times "and yet no vain repetition," and that "the most frequent mention of it, instead of cloying us, should raise us the more." He adds that "the everlasting continuance of the mercy of God is very much his honour." Guzik notes the long scholarly debate about *chesed*. Some, following Nelson Glueck's 1927 study, narrowed it to covenant loyalty. Guzik agrees with others, such as R. Laird Harris, that this drains the word of its warmth. The lexicon supports both sides of the word: kindness and goodness, and faithfulness within a relationship. Guzik also passes on Kidner's point that "give thanks" calls for thoughtful gratitude, grounded in what God has actually done.

#### To Him Who Alone Does Great Wonders (vv4–9)
- **"To him who alone does great wonders" (v4).** "Wonders" (*nifla'ot*) are acts beyond human power or understanding, things that make people marvel (Exod 3:20; Ps 72:18). "Alone" matters. JFB glosses it "excluding all help." No other god shared in creation. In the ESV, vv3–22 run as one long sentence, and line after line begins "to him who..." Each new act of God is added to the thanks.
- **"To him who by understanding made the heavens" (v5).** "Understanding" (*tevunah*) is skill and wisdom. Creation was not random. It was designed. Proverbs says, "The LORD by wisdom founded the earth; by understanding he established the heavens" (Prov 3:19; also Jer 10:12; Ps 104:24).
- **"To him who spread out the earth above the waters" (v6).** This is the language of an observer standing on the land and seeing water on every side, with springs and seas beneath. In Genesis God gathered the waters so that dry land appeared (Gen 1:9–10). Psalm 24:2 says he "founded it upon the seas." The point is not a scientific map of the earth but God's control over the waters, which the ancient world feared as chaos.
- **"To him who made the great lights" (vv7–9).** These verses follow Genesis 1:14–18 closely: "the greater light to rule the day and the lesser light to rule the night—and the stars." Many nations worshiped the sun, moon and stars as gods. Egypt's chief god was the sun god Ra. Genesis and this psalm present them as lamps God made and hung in the sky. They "rule" only in the sense that they mark day and night. God rules them.
- **Creation as love.** The striking thing is the refrain. The psalm does not say God made the heavens because he is powerful, though he is. It says each act of creation is an expression of his steadfast love. Creation is God's first gift.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "by understanding" can be read "in wisdom" and links it to Psalm 104:24. Henry treats vv1–9 as thanks to God as good, as supreme and as Creator, and says "all the streams must be traced up to the fountain." Guzik passes on Maclaren's description of creation as "an act of Divine love," and Meyer's image of the "silver thread of mercy" running through the whole psalm from the beginning.

#### He Brought Israel Out (vv10–16)
- **"To him who struck down the firstborn of Egypt" (v10).** The history begins, as in Psalm 135:8, with the last plague (Exod 12:29). It may seem strange to follow the death of Egypt's firstborn with "for his steadfast love endures forever." But the love in view is God's loyal love for his own people, who were slaves. Their rescue required the defeat of the power that held them.
- **"And brought Israel out from among them" (v11).** The Exodus is the central saving event of the Old Testament. Israel looked back to it the way Christians look back to the cross.
- **"With a strong hand and an outstretched arm" (v12).** This phrase is from Deuteronomy, where it describes the Exodus again and again (Deut 4:34; 5:15; 26:8). God is pictured as a warrior reaching out his arm to rescue. The same picture returns in Isaiah: "the arm of the LORD" (Isa 53:1).
- **"To him who divided the Red Sea in two" (v13).** "Divided in two" is literally "cut into pieces" (*gazar... ligzarim*). The noun is the same rare word Genesis uses for the halves of the animals Abraham cut in two when God made his covenant with him (*gezarim*, Gen 15:17). God passed between the pieces then. Israel passed between the waters now. Maclaren, as Guzik reports, makes this connection, and the Hebrew supports the echo.
- **"And made Israel pass through the midst of it" (v14).** Exodus says the people "walked on dry ground through the sea, the waters being a wall to them on their right hand and on their left" (Exod 14:29).
- **"But overthrew Pharaoh and his host in the Red Sea" (v15).** "Overthrew" (*ni'er*) means "shook off," the verb used in Exodus 14:27: "the LORD threw the Egyptians into the midst of the sea." JFB pictures it as "a contemptuous rejection," like shaking off a snake. Pharaoh had treated the LORD with contempt: "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?" (Exod 5:2). In the end God shook Pharaoh off like dust. (The "Red Sea" is literally *yam suph*, "Sea of Reeds." Its exact location is debated, but it is the body of water the Bible calls the Red Sea.)
- **"To him who led his people through the wilderness" (v16).** Forty years in the desert are summed up in one line. The psalm does not mention Israel's grumbling, the golden calf or the rebellions. It mentions only that God led them, with the pillar of cloud and fire (Exod 13:21), manna (Exod 16) and water from the rock (Exod 17). His love outlasted their unfaithfulness.
- **Commentators:** JFB compares the "strong hand" to Exodus 3:20 and Deuteronomy 4:34, and explains "overthrew" as "shook off." Henry treats vv10–22 as thanks to God as Israel's God and Savior. Guzik passes on Clarke's remark that feeding so great a people for forty years in the desert was itself an astonishing miracle, and Poole's description of a wilderness with no road and no food.

#### He Struck Down Great Kings (vv17–22)
- **"To him who struck down great kings... and killed mighty kings" (vv17–18).** The conquest of the land follows. "Great" (*gedolim*) and "mighty" (*'addirim*) stress how strong Israel's enemies were. Israel was a nation of former slaves who had never fought a war. The victory belonged to God.
- **"Sihon, king of the Amorites... and Og, king of Bashan" (vv19–20).** These are the same two kings named in Psalm 135:11. They ruled east of the Jordan River and were the first kingdoms to fall when Israel approached the Promised Land (Num 21:21–35; Deut 2:24–3:11). Their land became the home of the tribes of Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh (Num 32:33).
- **"And gave their land as a heritage, a heritage to Israel his servant" (vv21–22).** Again almost the same words as Psalm 135:12, with one change. Psalm 135 says "his people Israel." Psalm 136 says "Israel his servant," using the singular, as if the whole nation were one person in God's service (Isa 41:8; 44:1). The land was an inheritance, a gift, not a prize Israel won.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in the gift of the land the faithfulness of God to his promise to Abraham. Guzik passes on Trapp's remark that the kings were "great" both in number and in size, recalling Og's famous bed (Deut 3:11). JFB adds nothing new on these verses.

#### It Is He Who Remembered Us (vv23–26)
- **"It is he who remembered us in our low estate" (v23).** The voice changes to "us." Ancient history becomes the singers' own story. "Low estate" (*shiflenu*) means being brought low, humbled. JFB takes it as "captivity," the exile in Babylon, and many agree. The study notes say only that the circumstances are not specified. Either way, the God who remembered Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Exod 2:24) remembered this generation too. To "remember" in Scripture means to act on behalf of someone, not just to recall them (Gen 8:1; 1 Sam 1:19).
- **"And rescued us from our foes" (v24).** "Rescued" (*paraq*) means to tear away or snatch free, like pulling prey out of a lion's mouth. JFB thinks of the sudden fall of Babylon in 539 BC, which opened the way home for the exiles (Ezra 1:1–4).
- **"He who gives food to all flesh" (v25).** The psalm widens from Israel to every living creature. God feeds not only his people but all people and animals (Ps 104:27–28; 145:15–16; Matt 6:26). JFB notes that after the "special favors to His people" comes "God's goodness to all His creatures." Guzik notes, with VanGemeren, that "all flesh" recalls God's covenant with every living creature after the flood (Gen 9:9–17).
- **"Give thanks to the God of heaven" (v26).** The psalm ends with a new title. "God of heaven" became a common name for the LORD during and after the exile, when Israel lived among other nations (Ezra 1:2; Neh 1:5; Dan 2:18). The study notes point this out. JFB says the title occurs only once before the captivity (Jonah 1:9). That is not quite right, since Abraham uses it in Genesis 24:3 and 7. But the main point holds: it is mainly a late title.
- **Commentators:** Henry applies the "low estate" both to Israel's rescues and to Christ's redemption of his people, and calls God's providence "an inexhaustible fountain." JFB, as above, reads vv23–24 as the captivity and the fall of Babylon. Guzik passes on Kidner's point that the sudden shift to "us" makes every singer part of the story. He also retells Boice's account of Athanasius, the bishop of Alexandria, who in AD 356 led his congregation in singing this psalm while soldiers surrounded the church to arrest him.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 136 sings the same answer to every event: creation, the Exodus, the wilderness, the conquest, the return from exile. "His steadfast love endures forever." The New Testament shows where that love comes to its fullest. God "remembered us in our low estate" by sending his Son, who "humbled himself" and took on our lowly condition (Phil 2:7–8; Luke 1:48). Mary sang that God had "looked on the humble estate of his servant" and "helped his servant Israel, in remembrance of his mercy" (Luke 1:48, 54). Jesus is the one through whom God "made the heavens" (John 1:3; Col 1:16), the true Passover lamb by whose death his people are brought out of slavery (1 Cor 5:7), and the one who "rescued us from the domain of darkness" (Col 1:13). On the night before he died, Jesus and his disciples "sang a hymn" after the Passover meal (Matt 26:30), most likely the Hallel psalms that end with this very refrain (Ps 118:29). Then he went out to show what steadfast love looks like. Nothing in all creation, Paul says, can separate God's people "from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Rom 8:39).
