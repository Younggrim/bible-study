*Setting:* The story opens in Susa, the winter capital of the Persian Empire, in the third year of King Ahasuerus, about 483 BC. Ahasuerus is the Hebrew form of the Persian name the Greeks wrote as Xerxes, son of Darius I. It is roughly fifty years since Cyrus let the Jews go home (Ezra 1), and the temple in Jerusalem has stood rebuilt for about thirty years (Ezra 6:15), but most Jews have stayed scattered across the empire. Esther is their story: the people who did not go back. The author is unknown, and the book was probably written some time after the events, in the Persian period. Two things stand out from the first page. The book is full of feasts, and it never once names God. Yet the whole story is built to show him working behind the curtain, and chapter 1 sets the stage by clearing the throne room of a queen so that another can take her place.

#### The King's Banquets (vv1–9)
- **"From India to Ethiopia over 127 provinces" (v1).** India here means the Indus valley in modern Pakistan, and Ethiopia (Hebrew *Kush*) means the Upper Nile, south of Egypt. Persia was the largest empire the world had yet seen. The 127 "provinces" are smaller districts than the twenty or so great satrapies the Greek historians list. Daniel 6:1 speaks of 120 officials under Darius the Mede, and the number may simply have grown, or the terms count different units.
- **"Susa, the citadel" (v2).** Susa (Hebrew *Shushan*) sat in what is now southwestern Iran. The word "citadel" is *birah*, a fortified palace. The lexicon notes that in Esther it seems to mean the royal fortress as distinct from the city around it, a distinction that matters later (3:15; 8:14–15). Nehemiah served in this same citadel a generation later (Neh 1:1), and Daniel saw a vision there (Dan 8:2). French excavations in the 1800s uncovered Darius's great columned audience hall, which Xerxes also used.
- **"In the third year of his reign he gave a feast" (v3).** The word for feast is *mishteh*, literally a "drinking," and nearly half of its Old Testament uses are in Esther. The book moves from feast to feast: the king's feasts here, Vashti's, Esther's coronation feast (2:18), Esther's two banquets (chs 5 and 7), and finally the feast of Purim (ch 9). The third year, about 483 BC, fits the time Greek sources say Xerxes was gathering his commanders to plan the invasion of Greece. Many scholars think this gathering was part of that planning.
- **"The riches of his royal glory… for many days, 180 days" (v4).** Six months of display. Not every official stayed the whole time; they probably came in turns from their provinces. The point is the king's glory, stated three ways: riches, splendor, pomp. The reader is meant to be impressed, and then to see how hollow it is by the end of the chapter.
- **A seven-day feast for "both great and small" (v5).** This second feast was for everyone in the citadel, held outdoors in the palace garden court because no hall could hold them all.
- **Curtains, couches and a mosaic pavement (vv6–7).** The narrator lingers over the décor: white and violet hangings, silver rods, marble pillars, couches of gold and silver on a floor of porphyry, marble and mother-of-pearl. Guests ate reclining on couches. Every drinking cup was different, which was itself a show of wealth. The long, breathless description slows the story down on purpose, to soak the reader in excess.
- **"There is no compulsion" (v8).** The word "edict" is *dat*, a Persian loanword for "law" or "decree" that shows up again and again in Esther (1:13, 15, 19; 3:8; 4:11). The lexicon notes it is used almost only in Persian-period books. Here it is used with a touch of irony: the great empire of unchangeable laws issues a law about drinking. The usual custom was that guests drank when the king drank, but at this feast each man drank as much or as little as he liked.
- **"Queen Vashti also gave a feast for the women" (v9).** Persian court custom kept the royal women largely apart from the men's banquets. Greek historians name Xerxes' queen Amestris, not Vashti, and the two are not easy to match. Some scholars think Vashti was a title or a favored wife rather than the chief queen. The text does not need the identification to make its point.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry, Guzik and JFB all read the feasts as pride on display. Henry calls it "vain glory, an affection of pomp to no purpose at all," and adds that if Ahasuerus had spent his riches on the temple, as some of his successors did (Ezra 6:8; 7:22), it would "have turned to a much better account." JFB notes that the excavated colonnade at Susa matches the "court of the garden" with its marble pillars. Guzik sets the scene in world history: Athens was at its classical height, and the coming war with Greece would go badly for Xerxes. Henry, unlike the others, finds something to praise: no one was forced to drink, and the men and women feasted apart.

#### Queen Vashti's Refusal (vv10–22)
- **"When the heart of the king was merry with wine" (v10).** This is a polite way of saying he was drunk. The same phrase is used of Nabal (1 Sam 25:36) and of Amnon just before he was killed (2 Sam 13:28). In Scripture it usually comes right before a bad decision.
- **The seven eunuchs (v10).** Eunuchs were castrated servants who guarded the royal women and were trusted with access to the king. The narrator names all seven. The book of Esther loves lists of names and the machinery of the court; one of these men, Harbona, will reappear at the decisive moment (7:9).
- **"With her royal crown, in order to show the peoples and the princes her beauty" (v11).** The king wants to show off his queen the way he has just shown off his gold cups. Some Jewish traditions say he meant her to appear wearing only the crown, but the text does not say that. It is enough that a queen, whom Persian custom kept from public view, was summoned to be stared at by a hall full of drunk men.
- **"But Queen Vashti refused" (v12).** The text gives no reason. Modesty, dignity, fear of what a drunken crowd might do, or simple pride are all possible. The narrator does not praise or condemn her. What the story does show is the irony: the man who rules 127 provinces cannot command his own wife, and he is shamed in front of the very guests he meant to impress.
- **"His anger burned within him" (v12).** Ahasuerus is a king of extremes. He is ruled by wine, anger and the advice of whoever is nearest, a pattern that runs all through the book (2:1; 3:10; 7:7). The Greek historian Herodotus paints the same kind of man, who had the sea whipped when a storm wrecked his bridge.
- **"The wise men who knew the times" (v13).** These were advisers skilled in law and perhaps in astrology, since Persian kings consulted experts about the right moment for decisions. The seven princes "who saw the king's face" (v14) had direct access to him. Ezra 7:14 mentions the king's "seven counselors," the same inner circle.
- **"According to the law, what is to be done?" (v15).** The king wants a legal cover for an act of rage. Memucan gives him none from the law. Instead he turns a private quarrel into a national threat: every wife in the empire will hear of this and "look at their husbands with contempt" (v17).
- **"So that it may not be repealed" (v19).** The idea that a law of the Medes and Persians could not be changed appears here, in Daniel 6:8, 15, and again at the turning point of this book (8:8). It sounds strong, but it becomes a trap. Later, a decree to destroy the Jews cannot be undone, and the only way out will be a second decree that lets them defend themselves. The trap is set in chapter 1.
- **"Give her royal position to another who is better than she" (v19).** The phrase "another who is better" is the hinge of the chapter. Without knowing it, the king's council has opened a door that Esther will walk through. Samuel used almost the same words when Saul lost the kingdom: "to a neighbor of yours, who is better than you" (1 Sam 15:28). That echo may be deliberate, since Saul's failure to destroy Agag sits in the background of Haman, "the Agagite" (3:1).
- **"Every man be master in his own household" (v22).** The decree goes out to every province in its own script and language, using the efficient Persian postal system. The irony is sharp. A law cannot make anyone respected, and broadcasting it to the whole empire only spreads the story of the king's embarrassment. **"Speak according to the language of his people"** probably means that in a mixed marriage the husband's language was to be the household language, a symbol of who was in charge.
- **Where God is in this.** God is not named, but the narrator wants the reader to notice the timing. A drunken order, a refusal, a hasty council and an irreversible decree leave the throne beside the king empty just when it will be needed.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls Vashti's refusal "becoming both the modesty of her sex and her rank as queen," and adds that if the king had been sober he would have seen that "his own honor, as well as hers, was consulted by her dignified conduct." Guzik agrees she was right to refuse, noting that the duty to submit never includes a duty to sin. He also reports the Jewish tradition of the immodest summons without endorsing it, and points out that respect in a marriage cannot be won by decree. Henry is more divided. He blames the king's drinking ("When the wine is in the wit is out") and says he "dishonoured himself as a husband," but he wonders whether Vashti might have obeyed without shame, and whether she refused "in a haughty manner." He sums up: "He that had rule over 127 provinces had no rule over his own spirit." All three see the same purpose behind it: Henry says the chapter's design is "to show how way was made for Esther to the crown."

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 1 shows a king whose glory is all display and whose law is all force. He feasts for half a year and still cannot win the honor he demands. The rest of the Bible sets another King beside him, one who "did not come to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45), who did not put his bride on display but laid down his life for her and makes her beautiful himself (Eph 5:25–27). And though God's name is missing from this chapter, his hand is not. Long before Haman's plot exists, the way is being prepared for the deliverer. That is how the Lord worked again when, "in the fullness of time" (Gal 4:4), the Savior came through history that looked, on the surface, like nothing more than the ambitions of emperors.
