*Setting:* The plains of Moab, at the end of the fortieth year. The new census (ch 26) has just counted the generation that will enter Canaan, and God has said the land will be divided by tribe and clan according to that list. That raises a practical question no one had asked before: what happens to a family with no sons? This chapter answers it, then turns to a bigger question still. Moses will not enter the land (20:12). Who will lead Israel when he is gone? Both halves are about the future: five women claiming a share in land Israel does not yet own, and an old leader asking God for his successor.

#### The Daughters of Zelophehad (vv1–11)
- **"The daughters of Zelophehad the son of Hepher, son of Gilead, son of Machir, son of Manasseh" (v1).** The long family line establishes their standing. They belong to the clan of Hepher in the tribe of Manasseh, the tribe whose numbers had just grown the most (26:34). They were named in the census itself (26:33), unusual for women.
- **Five names: Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah (v1).** Each is named, here and again in 36:11 and Joshua 17:3. Several of their names match place names in Manasseh's later territory in the hill country. Tirzah became a royal city of the northern kingdom (1 Kgs 15:21). Many think these clans and towns took their names from these women's families.
- **"They stood before Moses and before Eleazar the priest and before the chiefs and all the congregation, at the entrance of the tent of meeting" (v2).** The tabernacle entrance was the place of public judgment. Five unmarried women bring their case to the highest court in Israel, in front of everyone. It took courage.
- **"He was not among the company… of Korah, but died for his own sin" (v3).** They anticipate an objection. Korah's rebellion was a sin that might have forfeited a family's share (16:32–33). Their father was not part of it. "Died for his own sin" means he died as the rest of his generation did, under the sentence of chapter 14, not for any special crime. It is a candid line: they do not pretend he was perfect.
- **"Why should the name of our father be taken away from his clan because he had no son?" (v4).** In Israel, land was not just property. It was a family's share in the promise of God, and a man's "name" lived on through the land his descendants held (compare Ruth 4:5, 10). Without a son, Zelophehad's portion would vanish and his name with it. **"Give to us a possession among our father's brothers."** They are asking for land Israel has not yet conquered, on the far side of a river they have not crossed. Their request is an act of faith that God will give the land.
- **"Moses brought their case before the LORD" (v5).** Moses does not decide on his own. This is one of four cases in the wilderness years where a new situation is taken directly to God for a ruling (Lev 24:12; Num 9:8; 15:34).
- **"The daughters of Zelophehad are right" (v7).** God's verdict is short and clear. The verb means they "speak rightly." **"Transfer the inheritance of their father to them."** God does more than make an exception. He makes it law.
- **The order of inheritance (vv8–11).** If a man dies without a son, his land goes to his daughter; if no daughter, to his brothers; then his father's brothers; then the nearest relative in his clan. The aim is to keep land within the family and the clan, so that no family's share in the promise is lost. This was unusual in its world. Some neighboring law codes allowed daughters to inherit, but many did not, and here it is the women who start the reform.
- **"A statute and rule" (v11).** Their case becomes permanent law for all Israel. The story isn't finished: chapter 36 adds that such heiresses should marry within their tribe, so that the land stays with the tribe. Joshua 17:3–6 records that they received their land, as promised.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that "those that seek an inheritance in the land of promise shall have what they seek, and other things shall be added to them," and that through their petition "the point is settled for all future occasions." He adds, "See how God makes heirs, and in his disposal we must acquiesce." JFB reads "died in his own sin" as death "by the common law of mortality to which men, through sin, are subject," not a crime that cost the family its land, and notes that the census had listed only males, which left this gap. Guzik sees the daughters as women of faith, planning for an inheritance in land that Israel did not yet hold.

#### Joshua to Succeed Moses (vv12–23)
- **"Go up into this mountain of Abarim and see the land" (v12).** Abarim means "regions beyond" or "the far side," the range of heights on the east side of the Jordan. Mount Nebo, where Moses will die, is one of its peaks (Deut 32:49; 34:1). The actual climb comes at the end of Deuteronomy. Here God gives Moses notice.
- **"You also shall be gathered to your people, as your brother Aaron was" (v13).** "Gathered to your people" is the Old Testament's gentle phrase for death (Gen 25:8; 49:33), hinting that death is not the end but a joining of those who went before.
- **"Because you rebelled against my word… failing to uphold me as holy at the waters" (v14).** God names the reason again: at Meribah Moses struck the rock in anger instead of speaking to it (20:10–13). The note in brackets locates "the waters of Meribah of Kadesh" for later readers. God forgives his servants, but he does not always remove the consequences of their sin.
- **"Moses spoke to the LORD" (v15).** What he does *not* say is striking. He does not plead for himself here (though Deuteronomy 3:23–26 records that he once asked to enter and was refused). His first thought is for the people.
- **"The God of the spirits of all flesh" (v16).** Moses used the same title when pleading for Israel during Korah's rebellion (16:22). God knows every human spirit from the inside, so he knows who is fit to lead.
- **"Who shall go out before them and come in before them" (v17).** A common Hebrew way of describing a leader in war, who leads the army out to battle and brings it home (1 Sam 18:16; 29:6). Israel will need a commander for the conquest.
- **"That the congregation of the LORD may not be as sheep that have no shepherd" (v17).** In the ancient Near East kings were often called shepherds of their people. The phrase returns in the prophets (1 Kgs 22:17; Ezek 34:5), and Jesus, seeing the crowds, "had compassion for them, because they were… like sheep without a shepherd" (Matt 9:36; Mark 6:34).
- **"Take Joshua the son of Nun, a man in whom is the Spirit" (v18).** Joshua has been Moses' assistant since his youth (11:28; Exod 33:11). He led Israel's army against Amalek (Exod 17:8–13), and he and Caleb were the only spies who trusted God (14:6–9). His name, originally Hoshea, was changed by Moses to Joshua, "the LORD saves" (13:16), the same name as Jesus in its Greek form. The key qualification is not his record but the Spirit of God in him.
- **"Lay your hand on him" (v18).** The verb (*samak*) means to lean or press the hand on, the same act used when an offerer laid a hand on a sacrifice (Lev 1:4) and when Israel set apart the Levites (8:10). Here it marks Joshua as Moses' successor. God says "hand," singular. In v23 Moses lays "his hands" on him, both hands, as if doing more than was asked, gladly. Later Jewish ordination of rabbis took its name, *semikhah*, from this verb, and the early church laid hands on those set apart for ministry (Acts 6:6; 1 Tim 4:14).
- **"Commission him in their sight" (v19).** The handover is public, before the priest and the whole people, so that no one can question it later.
- **"Invest him with some of your authority" (v20).** The word (*hod*) usually means splendor or majesty, the honor of royal office (Ps 45:3). Joshua receives *some* of it, not all. No one else would know God face to face as Moses did (12:6–8; Deut 34:10).
- **"He shall stand before Eleazar the priest, who shall inquire for him by the judgment of the Urim" (v21).** Moses spoke with God directly. Joshua will learn God's will through the high priest, who carried the Urim and Thummim, sacred lots of some kind kept in the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:30), used to ask God yes-or-no questions. Their exact form is unknown. From now on Israel's leadership is shared between a civil and military leader and a priest.
- **"Moses did as the LORD commanded him" (v22).** He hands over his leadership without complaint, to a younger man, publicly, while still alive. Deuteronomy 34:9 says that because Moses laid his hands on him, Joshua "was full of the spirit of wisdom," and the people obeyed him.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the title "God of the spirits of all flesh" as the right one for this prayer, since God is "the Author of all the intellectual gifts and moral graces with which men are endowed," and so can raise up a fit leader. JFB calls laying on of hands "an ancient ceremony," and says Joshua was "honored and qualified in an eminent degree," though no one matched Moses until the Messiah. Guzik notes that Moses was told months before his death so he could prepare, that he did not argue but thought first of the people, and he links "sheep without a shepherd" to Jesus the Good Shepherd and to pastors as under-shepherds. He also sees Joshua's long years as Moses' servant as his training. The Tyndale notes add that Israel would keep military and spiritual leadership separate from here on. Henry's comments on vv12–23 could not be loaded.

#### Pointing to Christ
Five daughters ask for a share in a land they have not yet seen, and God says, "They are right." No one who comes to God for an inheritance he has promised is turned away. Moses, who cannot enter, asks for a shepherd so the people will not be scattered, and God gives them Joshua, whose name is the Hebrew form of "Jesus." The true Joshua does what Moses could not. Moses brought the people to the edge; Jesus brings them in, as the shepherd "who goes out before them" (John 10:4). He does not hold only some of the authority but has "all authority in heaven and on earth" (Matt 28:18). And in him daughters and sons alike are "heirs according to promise" (Gal 3:28–29).
