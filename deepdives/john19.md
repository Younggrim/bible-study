*Setting:* Friday morning to Friday evening of Passover week, about AD 30 or 33. Jesus was arrested overnight, questioned by Annas and then by Caiaphas and the council, and brought at dawn to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor (ch 18). Pilate has declared him innocent once and offered to release him, and the crowd has shouted for Barabbas instead. Chapter 19 tells the rest: the flogging and mockery, Pilate's failed attempts to free him, the sentence, the crucifixion, Jesus' death and his burial before sunset. John was there. He stood at the cross (v26), and he says so plainly: "He who saw it has borne witness" (v35). He tells the story with restraint, never dwelling on the physical horror, and with an eye for Scripture fulfilled, for irony, and for the royal dignity of the one being killed.

#### Jesus Delivered to Be Crucified (vv1–16a)
- **"Pilate took Jesus and flogged him" (v1).** Roman flogging used a whip of leather thongs weighted with bits of bone or metal. It tore the skin and muscle of the back, and men sometimes died from it. In Matthew and Mark the flogging comes right before the crucifixion as part of the sentence. In John, Pilate orders it in the middle of the trial, apparently hoping a beaten, bleeding prisoner would satisfy the crowd (v4; Luke 23:16, 22). He may also have flogged Jesus again after sentencing, or John and Mark may describe one flogging from different angles.
- **"A crown of thorns… a purple robe" (v2).** The soldiers play dress-up with a "king." The thorns may have come from a local thorn bush or from date palm spines, woven to imitate the radiant crowns on emperors' coins. The "purple" robe was probably a faded red soldier's cloak. "Hail, King of the Jews!" mocks the greeting given to Caesar, "Hail, Caesar!" They do not know they are telling the truth.
- **"Behold the man!" (v5).** Pilate presents Jesus, bloodied and dressed as a mock king, perhaps to say, "Look at this pitiful man. Is he a threat?" John hears more. In Zechariah 6:12 the Messiah is called "the man whose name is the Branch." Jesus is the true man, the Son of Man, humanity as God meant it, here at its lowest point. Henry: "It is good for every one of us, with an eye of faith, to behold the man Christ Jesus in his sufferings."
- **"Crucify him, crucify him!" (v6).** Pity has no effect. "Take him yourselves and crucify him" is a sarcastic dare, since they could not legally do it (18:31). Pilate says a second time, "I find no guilt in him."
- **"He has made himself the Son of God" (v7).** Now the real charge comes out. Leviticus 24:16 commanded death for blasphemy. This had been the issue since 5:18, when they sought to kill him for "making himself equal with God."
- **"He was even more afraid" (v8).** Romans believed in sons of gods walking the earth (compare Acts 14:11). Pilate's wife had warned him after a dream (Matt 27:19). He senses he may be dealing with something beyond politics.
- **"Where are you from?" (v9).** It is the question John's Gospel keeps asking: where Jesus comes from tells who he is (7:27–29; 8:14; 9:29). Jesus gives no answer, as Isaiah's Servant "opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7). He had already told Pilate his kingdom was "not from the world" (18:36). Pilate did not listen then.
- **"I have authority to release you and authority to crucify you" (v10).** Pilate boasts of power while showing he knows Jesus is innocent. If he can release him, why doesn't he?
- **"You would have no authority over me at all unless it had been given you from above" (v11).** Jesus speaks as the one really in charge. Pilate's power is real but borrowed (Rom 13:1), and God is using it for his purpose (Acts 2:23). "He who delivered me over to you has the greater sin" most likely means Caiaphas, representing the high priestly leaders, though some think of Judas. Pilate is guilty, but those who knew the Scriptures and still handed over their Messiah are guiltier. Sin has degrees, and knowledge increases responsibility.
- **"You are not Caesar's friend" (v12).** "Friend of Caesar" (*amicus Caesaris*) was an honored title for loyal officials. The leaders threaten to report Pilate to Rome. The emperor Tiberius was famously suspicious, and Pilate's patron Sejanus may already have fallen (AD 31), depending on the date of the crucifixion. Pilate had previously provoked the Jews with Roman standards and temple funds, and a complaint to Rome could end his career. This is the threat that breaks him.
- **"The judgment seat at a place called The Stone Pavement… Gabbatha" (v13).** The judgment seat (*bēma*) was the official platform from which a Roman magistrate pronounced sentence (Acts 18:12; 25:6). "Stone Pavement" (*Lithostrōtos*) was a paved area. The pavement long shown to visitors under the Convent of the Sisters of Zion dates from the second century, and most scholars now place the site at Herod's palace near today's Jaffa Gate.
- **"The day of Preparation of the Passover… about the sixth hour" (v14).** "Preparation" was the usual name for Friday, the day of getting ready for the Sabbath (Mark 15:42). "Of the Passover" may mean Friday of Passover week, or the day the Passover lambs were prepared. The time raises a question, since Mark 15:25 says Jesus was crucified at "the third hour," about 9 a.m. Faithful readers have explained it in three main ways. Some think John counts hours the Roman way, from midnight, making it about 6 a.m. Some think both Gospels approximate, since people without clocks spoke of the day in three-hour blocks, so "about the sixth" and "the third" both fall in the late morning. JFB thinks an early copyist confused two Greek numerals and that John originally wrote "third." If John means noon, it was the hour the priests began slaughtering the Passover lambs.
- **"Behold your King!" (v14).** Pilate says it in mockery and says more than he knows (as Caiaphas did in 11:50).
- **"We have no king but Caesar" (v15).** This is the low point of the trial. The chief priests of Israel, whose daily prayers praised God as their King (Judg 8:23; 1 Sam 8:7; Ps 145:1), now publicly confess the pagan emperor as their only king. To reject the Messiah, they renounce the hope of the Messiah.
- **"He delivered him over to them to be crucified" (v16a).** "Delivered over" (*paradidōmi*) is the word used for Judas betraying Jesus, for the leaders handing him to Pilate, and now for Pilate handing him to the executioners. In 19:30 Jesus himself "gave up" (*paradidōmi*) his spirit. Behind all these handings over stands the Father who "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32).
- **Commentators:** Guzik explains that scourging served to punish, to extract confession and to weaken a prisoner before crucifixion, and notes the irony of "no king but Caesar" from people who had longed for a messianic deliverer. JFB reads "Behold the man!" as an appeal for pity: "There was clearly a struggle in the breast of this wretched man." JFB identifies "he that delivered me" as Caiaphas, representing leaders with "better opportunities and more knowledge," and argues the true reading in v14 is "the third hour."

#### The Crucifixion (vv16b–27)
- **"He went out, bearing his own cross" (v17).** The condemned man usually carried the crossbeam (*patibulum*), not the whole cross. The upright post stood at the site. The other Gospels say Simon of Cyrene was forced to carry it partway (Mark 15:21). John mentions only that Jesus began by carrying it himself. Many have seen Isaac carrying the wood for his own sacrifice up the mountain (Gen 22:6). "He went out" matters too: sin offerings were burned outside the camp, and "Jesus also suffered outside the gate" (Heb 13:11–12).
- **"The Place of a Skull… Golgotha" (v17).** *Golgotha* is Aramaic for "skull." Its Latin equivalent, *calvaria*, gives us "Calvary." It was probably named for the shape of a rocky outcrop, or because it was a place of execution. It lay just outside the city wall then, near a road (v20). Most archaeologists think the site is under the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which has been venerated since at least the fourth century. Gordon's Calvary and the Garden Tomb, north of the Damascus Gate, give a vivid picture of the scene but are unlikely to be the actual place.
- **"There they crucified him, and with him two others… and Jesus between them" (v18).** John uses one word for the crucifixion. His readers knew too well what it meant: nails through wrists and feet (a heel bone with a nail through it was found in a Jerusalem tomb in 1968), and death by exhaustion and suffocation, sometimes over days. Jesus is placed in the middle, as if the worst of the three. "He was numbered with the transgressors" (Isa 53:12).
- **"Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews" (v19).** It was common to post the charge on a placard. Pilate's wording is a final insult to the leaders, but it states the truth. The Latin initials *INRI* (*Iesus Nazarenus Rex Iudaeorum*) appear on many crucifixes.
- **"In Aramaic, in Latin, and in Greek" (v20).** The language of the Jews, of Roman government and of the wider eastern world. Jesus' kingship is announced to everyone, as he said: "I… will draw all people to myself" (12:32).
- **"What I have written I have written" (v22).** Pilate, who caved on the life of an innocent man, now refuses to change a sign. The perfect tense in Greek stresses something permanent. Under God, Pilate has written a lasting truth.
- **"The tunic was seamless, woven in one piece" (v23).** The tunic (*chitōn*) was the long inner garment. Josephus says the high priest's robe was woven without a seam (*Antiquities* 3.161). John does not spell out a meaning, but many readers, Guzik among them, see a hint of Jesus as high priest. Others since the early church have seen the unity of his people. JFB suggests it may have been the loving work of the women who supported him.
- **"They divided my garments among them" (v24).** This is Psalm 22:18 word for word. Psalm 22 is David's cry of an innocent sufferer surrounded by enemies, beginning "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" The soldiers, who had never read it, do exactly what it says.
- **"His mother and his mother's sister, Mary the wife of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene" (v25).** The Greek can be read as three women or four. Most scholars read four, in two pairs, and identify the mother's sister with Salome (Mark 15:40), the mother of Zebedee's sons (Matt 27:56). That would make John Jesus' cousin. JFB reads three, taking "Mary the wife of Clopas" as the sister. Mary Magdalene, from Magdala on the Sea of Galilee, had been freed from seven demons (Luke 8:2). The men had mostly run. These women stayed.
- **"Woman, behold, your son!… Behold, your mother!" (vv26–27).** "Woman" is respectful, as at Cana (2:4), where Jesus' "hour" had not yet come. Now it has. Jesus' brothers did not yet believe (7:5), so he entrusts his mother to the beloved disciple. Even in agony he keeps the command to honor his mother. Henry suggests Jesus did not say "Mother" because that word "would have been a cutting word to her that was already wounded to the heart with grief." Many have also seen in this a picture of the new family of believers formed at the cross (Mark 3:34–35). Some Catholic interpretation sees Mary given as mother to the church here. Most Protestants read the scene more simply as a son's care.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that John describes the crucifixion briefly because his readers knew its horror, and that the deepest suffering was spiritual, not physical. He links the seamless tunic to the high priest's robe. JFB comments that the title was written in three languages so that every onlooker could read it, and observes that the darkness at noon is recorded by "all the other Evangelists, but not here." Henry adds the tender reason for "Woman" quoted above.

#### The Death of Jesus (vv28–30)
- **"Knowing that all was now finished" (v28).** Jesus knew "his hour had come" (13:1) and "all that would happen to him" (18:4). Now he knows the work is done.
- **"I thirst" (v28).** The shortest of Jesus' sayings from the cross. It is real human thirst after hours of blood loss. It also fulfills Scripture, probably Psalm 69:21 ("for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink") and Psalm 22:15 ("my tongue sticks to my jaws"). The one who offered living water (4:14; 7:37) dies thirsty.
- **"Sour wine… on a hyssop branch" (v29).** The "sour wine" (*oxos*) was cheap wine vinegar, the drink of soldiers and laborers. Earlier Jesus had refused wine mixed with myrrh, a painkiller (Mark 15:23). He accepts this to wet his mouth for a final cry. Hyssop is a small, leafy plant, an odd stick for lifting a sponge, and Matthew and Mark say a reed was used. John may mention hyssop for its meaning: Israel used hyssop to brush the Passover lamb's blood on their doorposts (Exod 12:22).
- **"It is finished" (v30).** One word in Greek, *tetelestai*, from *teleō*, to complete or accomplish. It is in the perfect tense: finished, and it stays finished. John has used this root before: Jesus "loved them to the end" (*telos*, 13:1) and "accomplished the work" (17:4). The work the Father gave him, revealing the Father, obeying perfectly and bearing sin as the Lamb of God, is complete. This is a cry of victory, not of defeat. Matthew 27:50 says he cried out with a loud voice. The word was also used in the papyri for a debt paid, which preachers often draw on, but its plain sense in John is "accomplished." Henry: "the work of man's redemption and salvation is now completed."
- **"He bowed his head and gave up his spirit" (v30).** No one takes his life. He lays it down (10:17–18). John says he "handed over" (*paredōken*) his spirit, an act he performs. Luke records his last words as "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46; Ps 31:5).
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls *tetelestai* the cry of a victor and sees in it the completed transaction in which the guilt and wrath owed for sin fell on Christ, the "cup" of 18:11. He notes that Jesus bowed his head as one at rest, and gave up his spirit by choice. JFB lists what was finished, including the Law fulfilled and prophecy accomplished, and says that "Redemption is completed." JFB adds that the loud cry was a triumphant shout at the moment of death.

#### Jesus' Side Is Pierced (vv31–37)
- **"So that the bodies would not remain on the cross on the Sabbath" (v31).** The Law said a hanged body must not stay up overnight (Deut 21:22–23). This Sabbath was "a high day," because it fell during the festival, likely the first full day of Unleavened Bread or the day the first sheaf of the harvest was offered.
- **"That their legs might be broken" (v31).** Breaking the legs (*crurifragium*) with an iron mallet stopped a crucified man from pushing himself up to breathe, so he died quickly. The bones of a crucified man found in Jerusalem in 1968 may show broken leg bones, though this is debated.
- **"They saw that he was already dead" (v33).** Victims often lasted much longer. Pilate was surprised Jesus had died so soon (Mark 15:44). The flogging, loss of blood and the weight he carried had done their work.
- **"One of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once there came out blood and water" (v34).** The soldier made sure. Medical writers have suggested the spear entered the sac around the heart or the chest cavity, releasing blood and a watery fluid, which confirms Jesus truly died. Against later teachers who denied that Christ really had a body or really died, John insists on it (1 John 4:2; 5:6). Many also see meaning: blood that cleanses (1 John 1:7) and water that pictures the Spirit and new life (7:38–39). Henry: "blood for remission, water for regeneration."
- **"He who saw it has borne witness… that you also may believe" (v35).** John steps into his own story to vouch for it. This is not a legend told at a distance. It is sworn testimony.
- **"Not one of his bones will be broken" (v36).** The Passover lamb was to be eaten without breaking any of its bones (Exod 12:46; Num 9:12). Psalm 34:20 says God keeps all the bones of the righteous. Jesus is the Lamb of God (1:29), dying as the Passover lambs were being prepared.
- **"They will look on him whom they have pierced" (v37).** This is Zechariah 12:10, where God says the people of Jerusalem will look on "me, on him whom they have pierced," and mourn as for an only child, and God will pour out a spirit of grace. JFB notes that John follows the Hebrew, not the Greek Old Testament, which softened the verb. John quotes it again in Revelation 1:7 of Christ's return: "every eye will see him, even those who pierced him."
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the soldiers' unplanned choices fulfilled Scripture and reads the blood and water as cleansing from both the guilt and the power of sin. JFB cites a medical explanation for the water and links John's insistence on what he saw to his letters' warnings against those who denied Christ came "in the flesh." JFB has no separate note on v32.

#### Jesus Is Buried (vv38–42)
- **"Joseph of Arimathea, who was a disciple of Jesus, but secretly" (v38).** The other Gospels say he was rich (Matt 27:57), a respected member of the council (Mark 15:43), and had not agreed with its decision (Luke 23:51). Arimathea was probably Ramathaim, northwest of Jerusalem. He had been one of the secret believers of 12:42. Asking Pilate for the body of an executed "king" was dangerous and public. The cross turned a secret disciple into an open one.
- **"Nicodemus also, who earlier had come to Jesus by night" (v39).** John reminds readers of 3:1–2. Nicodemus had cautiously defended Jesus once (7:50–51). Now he comes in daylight.
- **"Myrrh and aloes, about seventy-five pounds in weight" (v39).** The Greek says a hundred Roman pounds (*litrai*), about seventy-five of ours. Myrrh is a fragrant resin, and aloes here is a powdered aromatic wood. The amount is enormous, the kind of honor given to kings (2 Chr 16:14 describes King Asa laid on a bed of spices). Jesus is executed as a criminal and buried as a king. JFB: "an immense quantity, betokening the greatness of their love." Henry: "Joseph served Christ with his interest, Nicodemus with his purse."
- **"Bound it in linen cloths with the spices, as is the burial custom of the Jews" (v40).** Jews did not embalm like Egyptians. They washed the body, wrapped it in strips of linen with spices between the layers to honor the dead and mask decay. The linen cloths will reappear in the empty tomb (20:5–7).
- **"In the garden a new tomb in which no one had yet been laid" (v41).** Only John says the tomb was in a garden. The story of Jesus' suffering began in a garden (18:1) and ends in one, and the resurrection morning will happen there too, when Mary mistakes him for the gardener (20:15). Many see an echo of Eden. A new rock-cut tomb meant no other body could be confused with his. Matthew 27:60 says it was Joseph's own tomb, and Isaiah 53:9 says the Servant was "with a rich man in his death."
- **Commentators:** Guzik describes Jewish burial customs and the cost of a rock-cut tomb, and sees Joseph and Nicodemus as sincere but late disciples, which he turns into a call not to delay following Christ. JFB stresses that the tomb was new and unused, fitting the one who was "separate from sinners." JFB and Henry both admire the size of Nicodemus's gift as a measure of love.

#### Pointing to Christ
John tells the crucifixion as a coronation. Jesus is crowned with thorns, robed in purple, hailed as king by soldiers, presented by Pilate with "Behold your King!" and lifted up under a title written in three languages. But he is a king who suffers. He is the Lamb of God whose bones are not broken, sprinkled with hyssop and pierced so that blood and water flow. The innocent man is declared innocent three times and handed over to die anyway. Even on the cross he cares for his mother. Then, knowing that all is finished, he says "It is finished" and hands over his spirit. Nothing more needs to be added to what he finished. The secret disciples come out of hiding to bury him like a king in a garden tomb, and the story is not over.
