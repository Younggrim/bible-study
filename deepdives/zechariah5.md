*Setting:* These are the sixth and seventh of the eight night visions Zechariah received in February 519 BC (1:7). The returned exiles were five months into rebuilding the temple under the governor Zerubbabel and the high priest Joshua. The first visions were full of comfort: God's anger at the nations, a Jerusalem too big for walls, a priest cleansed and reclothed (ch 3), and a lampstand fed by the Spirit (ch 4). Chapter 5 turns the light inward. A rebuilt temple is not enough if the people who worship in it keep stealing from each other and lying in God's name. So the sixth vision sends God's law through the land like a flying banner, and the seventh shows wickedness itself boxed up and carried away. Together they say that God will not only rebuild his house but purify the people who live around it. Earlier prophets had warned of the same sins (Hos 4:2; Jer 7:9), and Zechariah's own sermon in 7:9–10 and 8:16–17 returns to them.

#### A Vision of a Flying Scroll (vv1–4)
- **"Again I lifted my eyes and saw, and behold, a flying scroll!" (v1).** A scroll was the ordinary form of a book: sheets of leather or papyrus sewn into a long strip and rolled. This one is unrolled and sailing through the air like a banner, readable by everyone. Ezekiel was handed a scroll written "on the front and on the back" with "lamentation and mourning and woe" (Ezek 2:9–10), and this vision shares that picture of a written word of judgment.
- **"Its length is twenty cubits, and its width ten cubits" (v2).** A cubit was about 18 inches, so the scroll is roughly 30 feet by 15, enormous for a scroll. Those are exactly the measurements of the porch of Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 6:3), and of the tabernacle's holy place. Many see the point: the standard of God's house is the standard by which the land is measured. Whether the match is intended or not, the size makes the law impossible to miss.
- **"This is the curse that goes out over the face of the whole land" (v3).** The Hebrew word *'alah* means a curse, but specifically the curse attached to an oath or covenant. When Israel entered the covenant, they swore to it and accepted its sanctions (Deut 29:12–21; 28:15–68). Zechariah's earlier warning (1:4–6) reminded them that those words "overtook" their fathers. "The whole land" could also be read "the whole earth," but the focus here is the land of Judah and its people.
- **"Everyone who steals… everyone who swears falsely" (v3).** Two sins, one written on each side. Theft breaks the eighth commandment and wrongs a neighbor. Swearing falsely in God's name breaks the third and dishonors God. Many interpreters see them as samples from the two "tables" of the law, love for neighbor and love for God, standing for the whole. In a struggling community, theft and false oaths in business and court disputes were exactly the sins that would flourish. Nehemiah later found Jews exploiting their own poor (Neh 5:1–13).
- **"Shall be cleaned out" (v3).** The verb *naqah* means to be emptied or cleared, and in most places "to be held innocent" or "go unpunished." It is the same word in the third commandment: "the LORD will not hold him guiltless who takes his name in vain" (Exod 20:7). The perjurer swears in God's name to clear himself. Instead he will be cleared *out*, purged from the land. The English cannot carry the wordplay, but the Hebrew reader would have heard it.
- **"It shall enter the house of the thief… and consume it, both timber and stones" (v4).** The curse acts like a law officer with a warrant. It goes where it is sent, moves in, and stays until the house is gone. God's word "runs swiftly" (Ps 147:15) and "shall not return to me empty" (Isa 55:11). The punishment fits the crime: men who built their houses on stolen goods and false oaths will see those houses fall. Leviticus 14:45 describes tearing down a house infected with disease, stones and timber, and the picture may echo it.
- **Why this vision now?** The earlier visions promised that God would judge the nations and return to Zion. A careless listener might think that being God's people meant being safe whatever they did. This vision says otherwise. God's presence brings his law with it, and nearness to God's house raises the stakes. The fifth vision promised the temple would be finished. The sixth says the people who gather there must be fit to stand in it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, Henry and JFB all read the scroll as a judgment on sin within God's people, not on the nations. JFB says its flying "shows that its curses were ready swiftly to visit the transgressors," and, like Guzik, links its size to the temple porch. Guzik follows the view that the two sins come from the two halves of the Ten Commandments, sins against neighbor and against God. Henry draws the lesson that even when God is doing kind things for his people, "let the sinners in Zion be afraid," because the blessing of the nation does not cover the unrepentant sinner within it.

#### A Vision of a Woman in a Basket (vv5–11)
- **"Lift your eyes and see what this is that is going out" (v5).** The interpreting angel steps forward again. The word "going out" repeats from v3: the curse went out over the land, and now something else goes out from it. The two visions are a pair. First God judges the sinner. Then he removes sin itself.
- **"This is the basket that is going out" (v6).** The Hebrew is *ephah*, a standard dry measure of a little over half a bushel, and also the name of the container used to measure grain in the market. The ESV's "basket" is a fair rendering, though the prophets' hearers would have pictured a measuring vessel. The ephah was where cheating happened. Amos condemned merchants who "make the ephah small" (Amos 8:5), and Micah asked, "Can I forget any longer the treasures of wickedness in the house of the wicked, and the scant measure that is accursed?" (Mic 6:10). The basket fits the theft of v3.
- **"This is their iniquity in all the land" (v6).** The standard Hebrew text reads "this is their *eye*," which most take as "their appearance" or "what they look like." The ESV, like most modern translations, follows one Hebrew manuscript and the ancient Greek and Syriac in reading "their iniquity," a difference of one letter in Hebrew. Either way, the basket shows the true state of the land.
- **"The leaden cover was lifted" (v7).** The lid is literally a "talent of lead," a round disc weighing about 75 pounds, the heaviest standard weight of the day. No ordinary basket needs a lid like that. Whatever is inside must be kept in.
- **"There was a woman sitting in the basket!… This is Wickedness" (vv7–8).** The word *rish'ah* means wickedness in general, moral evil set against righteousness (Prov 13:6; Ezek 33:12). It is a feminine noun in Hebrew, which is the simplest reason it is pictured as a woman. Some scholars note that it sounds a little like Asherah, the Canaanite fertility goddess whose worship pulled Israel into idolatry for centuries (2 Kgs 17:16; 21:7), and think the woman pictures idolatry in particular. That is a suggestion, not something the text says. The vision is not saying women are especially wicked. It personifies wickedness, as Proverbs personifies both Wisdom and Folly as women (Prov 9).
- **"He thrust her back into the basket, and thrust down the leaden weight" (v8).** The verb "thrust" is used twice. Wickedness tries to get out, and the angel forces her down and seals the lid. Sin is pictured as active and aggressive, something that will not stay contained unless God restrains it.
- **"Two women coming forward!… They had wings like the wings of a stork" (v9).** This is the only place in the Old Testament where winged beings appear as women. The stork had long, broad wings and migrated in great numbers over the land each year, so the picture is of strength and long-distance flight. The Hebrew name for the stork, *chasidah*, is related to *chesed*, "steadfast love," perhaps because storks were known for caring for their young. The stork was an unclean bird (Lev 11:19), and some think these women are agents of evil carrying their own back home. Others see them as God's servants doing his will. The point holds either way: whoever they are, they take wickedness where God wants it.
- **"The wind was in their wings" (v9).** The word for wind, *ruach*, also means "spirit." It may simply describe a strong breeze, but after "by my Spirit" (4:6), some hear a hint that God's Spirit is behind the removal.
- **"To the land of Shinar, to build a house for it" (v11).** Shinar is the old name for Babylonia, the plain where people built the tower of Babel to make a name for themselves (Gen 11:2–4). Nebuchadnezzar carried the temple vessels "to the land of Shinar, to the house of his god" (Dan 1:2). Babylon is where idolatry and pride against God began, and where Judah had just spent seventy years. God sends wickedness back where it came from. The prophets treat Babylon as the very home of idolatry (Isa 46–47; Jer 50–51), and in Revelation "Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes and of earth's abominations" stands for the whole world system opposed to God (Rev 17:5; 18:2).
- **"They will set the basket down there on its base" (v11).** The word *mekunah* appears only here in the Old Testament. It means a stand or pedestal, and words from the same root are used for the stands in Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 7:27). The picture is grimly ironic. In Jerusalem the people are building a house for God. In Shinar a "house" is being built for Wickedness, and she is set up on a pedestal like an idol in its own temple. The thief's house was destroyed (v4), and wickedness gets a house far away.
- **The two visions together.** The flying scroll deals with sinners. The basket deals with sin. Both repeat the word "house" (vv4, 11). Chapter 3 showed Joshua's filthy clothes taken off and God promising to "remove the iniquity of this land in a single day" (3:9). Chapter 5 shows what that removal looks like from the land's point of view. The people cannot clean themselves. God has to carry the evil away.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the basket, the weight and the woman as greed, materialism and dishonest gain, a spirit the returning exiles brought back from Babylon and which God sends back to its source. He takes the stork-winged women as God's servants, not agents of evil, and reads the "base" as a pedestal for an idol, which fits the lexicon's "base" or "stand." Henry reads the vision as a warning about the later Jewish nation "filling up the measure of their iniquity," and says "Guilt is upon the sinner as a talent of lead." He identifies the winged women with the Romans who later scattered the nation, a reading few would follow now. JFB calls Shinar "the capital of the God-opposed world kingdoms," and like Guzik explains the stork as chosen for its long, wide wings. The Tyndale notes take the scene as God's gracious forgiveness, the counterpart of Joshua's filthy clothes taken away in 3:4.

#### Pointing to Christ
The flying scroll is the law doing what the law does: going into every house, exposing theft and false oaths, and bringing the curse with it. No one in Judah could stand under that scroll, and no one since has either. Paul says, "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). The curse that entered the thief's house entered Jesus instead, and he died between two thieves. The basket of wickedness shows the other half of the gospel. Sin is not merely forgiven and left in place. It is carried away, "as far as the east is from the west" (Ps 103:12), like the goat that bore Israel's sins into the wilderness (Lev 16:21–22). Jesus is "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). And the last word on Babylon belongs to him: the house wickedness built in Shinar falls in Revelation 18, and the city that comes down from heaven has nothing unclean in it (Rev 21:27).
