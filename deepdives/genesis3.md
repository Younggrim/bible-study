*Setting:* Chapter 2 ended in peace: a man and a woman in God's garden, with every tree but one open to them, "naked and… not ashamed." Chapter 3 tells how it was lost. Nearly every sorrow in the rest of the Bible traces back here: shame, fear, blame, pain in childbirth, struggle in marriage, hard work, death and exile from God's presence. But so does the hope. Within a few verses of the first sin, God speaks the first promise of a coming deliverer. Christians have long called this chapter "the Fall," though the word itself never appears. For Israel, the story explained why the world they knew was so far from the "very good" of chapter 1, and why access to God's presence in the tabernacle was guarded so carefully.

How the chapter is read varies. Most Christians through history, and all three commentators here, read it as a historical event involving the first couple. Some read it as history told in a symbolic, ancient style. Others read it mainly as a story about every human being. All agree on what it teaches: sin is rebellion against a good God, it brought death and alienation, and God answers it with both judgment and grace.

#### The Fall (vv1–24)
- **"Now the serpent was more crafty than any other beast of the field" (v1).** "Crafty" is *arum*, a near-twin of "naked" (*arummim*) in the previous verse (2:25). The couple is innocent and exposed. The serpent is shrewd. The word can be good, "prudent" in Proverbs (Prov 14:8), but here it means cunning. The serpent is called a creature "that the LORD God had made." It's no rival god, only a creature that turned against its maker. Genesis never names Satan, but the rest of the Bible identifies "that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan" (Rev 12:9; 20:2; 2 Cor 11:3; John 8:44). Readers differ on the details. Most hold that Satan used or possessed a real serpent, while some take "the serpent" as a title for a spiritual being, and Ezekiel 28:13–14's fallen cherub "in Eden" is often read alongside this.
- **"Did God actually say…?" (v1).** The first words of temptation are a question about God's word. The serpent twists it, too. God had said "you may surely eat of every tree" except one (2:16). The serpent makes it sound as though God banned every tree. He paints a generous God as stingy.
- **The woman's reply (vv2–3).** She corrects him, but she also shifts the command. She drops "surely" and "every." She adds "neither shall you touch it," which God hadn't said. She softens "you shall surely die" to "lest you die." It's not clear whether these are her own changes or reflect what Adam passed on to her. Either way, she is already talking about God's word loosely.
- **"You will not surely die" (v4).** Now the serpent flatly contradicts God, using God's own words with a "not" in front. Jesus calls the devil "a liar and the father of lies" and "a murderer from the beginning" (John 8:44), and this verse is where both begin.
- **"You will be like God, knowing good and evil" (v5).** The lie is wrapped in half-truth. Their eyes *would* be opened (v7), and God himself later says they have come to know good and evil (v22). But not in the way they hoped. The bait is to become like God by grabbing, rather than by receiving. Ironically, they were already made in his image (1:27). "God knows" also hints that God is holding out on them.
- **"Good for food… a delight to the eyes… desired to make one wise" (v6).** Three steps of desire. Many compare them to "the desires of the flesh and the desires of the eyes and pride of life" (1 John 2:16) and to Jesus' three temptations in the wilderness (Luke 4:1–13), where the second Adam succeeds where the first failed.
- **"She also gave some to her husband who was with her, and he ate" (v6).** Adam was *with her*. He was there the whole time and said nothing. Paul says the woman "was deceived" (1 Tim 2:14), but he holds Adam responsible: "sin came into the world through one man" (Rom 5:12). Adam knew the command firsthand and ate anyway.
- **"The eyes of both were opened, and they knew that they were naked" (v7).** The serpent's promise comes true in the worst way. What they "know" first is their own exposure. Nakedness, once innocent, now brings shame. **Fig leaves**, which are large and sturdy, make a cover. This is the first human attempt to deal with sin by covering it up, and it doesn't work.
- **"The sound of the LORD God walking in the garden" (v8).** The same verb, "walking" (*hithallek*), is used for God's presence moving among Israel in the tabernacle camp (Lev 26:12; Deut 23:14). Eden was where God was at home with people. "In the cool of the day" is literally "at the wind of the day," probably the evening breeze. Some scholars read it as "in the wind of the storm," God coming in judgment, but the traditional reading is more common. Either way, the couple who once walked freely with God now **hid themselves… among the trees**, using the very trees he gave them.
- **"Where are you?" (v9).** God knows where they are. The question invites confession, and it is the first question God asks in the Bible. It shows that God comes looking for the sinner, as the Son of Man "came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10).
- **"I was afraid, because I was naked" (v10).** Fear of God enters the human heart for the first time. Adam talks about his nakedness, not his disobedience.
- **"Who told you that you were naked?" (v11).** God names the real issue: "Have you eaten of the tree…?"
- **"The woman whom you gave to be with me" (v12).** Adam blames the woman, and behind her, God. **"The serpent deceived me"** (v13). The woman blames the serpent. Both statements are partly true, and neither is a confession. Blame passes down the line, and nobody says "I sinned."
- **The judgments (vv14–19).** God questions the man, then the woman, and never questions the serpent. He then judges in the reverse order: serpent, woman, man. Only the serpent and the ground are "cursed." The man and woman are not cursed directly, though their lives are now hard.
- **"On your belly you shall go, and dust you shall eat" (v14).** Crawling and "licking dust" were pictures of total defeat and humiliation (Ps 72:9; Mic 7:17). Isaiah's picture of the new creation still has "dust… the serpent's food" (Isa 65:25), the enemy defeated for good. Readers debate whether the serpent once moved differently. Some, like Guzik, think so. Others think the curse gave new meaning to how serpents already moved.
- **"I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring" (v15).** This is the turning point of the chapter. God himself sets the woman against the serpent. The friendship the woman struck up with the serpent is broken, and God puts the human race at war with evil. "Offspring" is *zera*, "seed," which can mean many descendants or one. The verse starts with many and then narrows to one: "**he** shall bruise your head."
- **"He shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel" (v15).** The same verb (*shuph*, "bruise, crush") is used for both blows, but a blow to the head is fatal and a blow to the heel is painful but survivable. Christians since at least Irenaeus in the second century have read this verse as the first announcement of the gospel, later called the *protoevangelium*, fulfilled in Christ, "born of woman" (Gal 4:4), who was struck on the cross and through it destroyed "the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil" (Heb 2:14; 1 John 3:8). Paul echoes it: "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Rom 16:20). Some scholars read the verse mainly as the ongoing struggle between humanity and evil, but even they note it promises the woman's line will win.
- **"I will surely multiply your pain in childbearing" (v16).** The woman's judgment touches the two relationships at the heart of her life, children and husband. The word for "pain" (*itstsabon*) is the same as the man's "pain" in v17. Both now share toil and sorrow. Yet childbearing will continue, and through it the promised offspring will come.
- **"Your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you" (v16).** "Desire" is *teshuqah*, which occurs only three times in the Bible. In Song of Songs 7:10 it is loving desire: "his desire is for me." In Genesis 4:7 it is sin's desire to master Cain: "its desire is contrary to you, but you must rule over it," the same pairing of "desire" and "rule" as here. Readers take it in two main ways:
  - **A desire to control:** the wife will want to master her husband, and he will dominate her, so marriage becomes a power struggle. This is the Tyndale notes' and Guzik's reading, based on 4:7.
  - **A longing or dependence:** she will still long for her husband, even when he rules harshly. This is the older and traditional reading.
  - Either way, the verse describes what sin does to marriage, not what God wants it to be. Ephesians 5:21–33 shows marriage being remade in Christ.
- **"Because you have listened to the voice of your wife" (v17).** Adam's sin was not listening to his wife as such. It was listening to her *instead of* God, who had spoken to him directly.
- **"Cursed is the ground because of you" (v17).** The ground, *adamah*, from which *adam* was taken (2:7), now resists him. "Eat" comes five times in vv17–19. They sinned by eating, and now eating comes only with sweat. **"Thorns and thistles"** appear. Paul says creation "was subjected to futility" and "groans" for redemption (Rom 8:20–22). Work itself is not the curse. Work was given in 2:15. The curse is frustration and toil in the work.
- **"For you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (v19).** God's warning in 2:17 comes true. Death enters. Genesis 5 will repeat the refrain "and he died" eight times. Many churches read this verse aloud on Ash Wednesday.
- **"The man called his wife's name Eve, because she was the mother of all living" (v20).** "Eve" is *Chavvah*, which sounds like the Hebrew for "life" or "living" (*chayah*). Adam has just heard the sentence of death, and his response is to name his wife "Life." Many see faith here, trust in the promise of v15 that her offspring would come. Others note this is also the first instance of the man "ruling" (v16) by naming her.
- **"Garments of skins" (v21).** God replaces their flimsy fig leaves with real clothing, and he makes it himself. For skins, animals had to die. Henry, JFB and Guzik see here the first hint of sacrifice: life given to cover the guilty. Many modern scholars hold that the text only shows God's care, providing clothing for a harsh world. Both agree that the covering comes from God, not from them.
- **"The man has become like one of us in knowing good and evil" (v22).** "Us" again, as in 1:26. The Tyndale notes take it as God speaking to his heavenly court; Henry, JFB and Guzik see the Trinity. Some read the line as grim irony: they wanted to be like God and got their wish, but in ruin. JFB argues it's spoken "in deep compassion."
- **"Lest he… take also of the tree of life and eat, and live forever" (v22).** The sentence breaks off mid-thought in Hebrew. Banishment from the tree is both judgment and mercy. To live forever in a fallen state would be its own kind of hell. The way back to the tree of life will be reopened only at the end (Rev 2:7; 22:2, 14).
- **"He drove out the man" (vv23–24).** Being "sent out" and then "driven out" are strong words. The same verb (*garash*, "drive out") is later used for the nations driven out of Canaan (Exod 23:28–31) and for Cain (4:14). **"At the east of the garden"** sets a pattern. In Genesis, moving east often means moving away from God: Cain goes east (4:16), Babel's builders move east (11:2), Lot chooses the east (13:11).
- **"The cherubim and a flaming sword" (v24).** Cherubim are the guardian beings of God's presence. Israel later wove cherubim into the tabernacle curtain that barred the Most Holy Place (Exod 26:31) and set golden cherubim over the ark (Exod 25:18–22). The tabernacle's entrance faced east (Exod 27:13), as Eden's guarded entrance did. Every time Israel saw that curtain, they saw Eden's closed door. At Jesus' death "the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (Matt 27:51).
- **Original sin.** Christians agree that Adam's sin affected the whole human race (Rom 5:12–21; 1 Cor 15:22), but traditions describe it differently. Western churches, following Augustine, Catholic and Protestant alike, generally teach that Adam's descendants inherit both a corrupted nature and the guilt of his sin. Eastern Orthodox churches emphasize inheriting mortality and a tendency to sin rather than guilt. All agree that every person needs the rescue only Christ gives.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "perhaps as sad a story… as any we have in all the Bible" and sums it up: all once "good, very good; but here the scene is altered," "all bad, very bad." On the temptation he says, "Satan teaches men first to doubt and then to deny," and warns that "the garrison that sounds a parley is not far from being surrendered." On v6: "Satan may tempt, but he cannot force." He reads "Where are you?" as "Not, In what place? but, In what condition?" On v15 he says, "No sooner was the wound given than the remedy was provided and revealed," and, "while the heel is bruised on earth, it is well that the head is safe in heaven." He thinks the animals of v21 were "slain, not for food, but for sacrifice, to typify the great sacrifice." JFB takes the serpent as "a real serpent" used by "the spiritual serpent, the seducer," and reads the seed of the woman as "the Messiah, or His Church." It thinks Eve's name may refer to her being "a mother of the promised Saviour," and sees in the skins "the institution of animal sacrifice." Guzik traces Satan's method of questioning God's word, then denying it, then offering to make them like God. He hears "Where are you?" as a grieving father's cry, not an angry demand. He lists how Jesus bore each part of the curse: pain, sweat (Luke 22:44), thorns (John 19:2) and death. He sees a hint of the virgin birth in the *woman's* seed, a point many Christians have made, though the verse itself doesn't spell it out.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everything broken in this chapter is mended in Jesus. Adam failed in a garden of plenty. Jesus resisted the devil in a wilderness, hungry (Matt 4:1–11), and in another garden said, "not my will, but yours" (Luke 22:42). The thorns of the curse became his crown (John 19:2). The sweat of the curse fell from him like drops of blood (Luke 22:44). He returned to the dust in death and rose again, the "last Adam" who gives life (1 Cor 15:22, 45). The serpent bruised his heel at the cross, and there he crushed the serpent's head (Col 2:15). God covered the first sinners with skins. In Christ he clothes sinners with his righteousness (Gal 3:27). And the way to the tree of life, guarded by a flaming sword, is opened again by the Lamb (Rev 22:14).
