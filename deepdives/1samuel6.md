*Setting:* The ark of the covenant has been in Philistine hands for seven months (v1). It was captured at Aphek when Israel tried to use it as a good-luck charm (ch 4). Since then the LORD has humbled the Philistine god Dagon in his own temple and struck Ashdod, Gath and Ekron with tumors and panic (ch 5). Now the Philistines want it gone, but they are afraid to send it back the wrong way. Chapter 6 tells how the ark came home to Israel's border town of Beth-shemesh, and how the joy of its return turned to grief when the men of Beth-shemesh treated God's holiness lightly. It is a chapter about God's holiness, felt by pagans and by Israelites alike.

#### The Ark Returned to Israel (vv1–21)
- **"Seven months" (v1).** That is a long time to endure plague and panic. Pride and the value of a war trophy kept them holding on long after the evidence was in.
- **"The priests and the diviners" (v2).** Diviners read omens to discover the will of the gods, a practice the law strictly forbade in Israel (Deut 18:10–14). The Philistines turn to the religious experts they have. Their question is not whether to send the ark back, but how: "with what shall we send it?" In the ancient world you did not return an offended god's property empty-handed.
- **"Do not send it empty, but by all means return him a guilt offering" (v3).** "Guilt offering" (*'asham*) is the same word used in Leviticus for the offering that paid damages for wronging God or a neighbor (Lev 5:14–6:7). The Philistines do not know the law of Moses, and their offering is closer to a fine paid to calm an angry god. But they rightly sense that they owe something. Notice the words "Then you will be healed, and it will be known to you why his hand does not turn away from you." Even pagans could see that this was not random.
- **"Five golden tumors and five golden mice" (v4).** Making a model of the thing that afflicts you and offering it to a god was a known practice in the ancient world. It was a way of handing over the disease. Five is the number of the Philistine lords and their main cities. Mice (or rats) have not been mentioned before, though the Greek translation mentions them in 5:6. Rats spread bubonic plague, so mice and tumors together fit an outbreak of plague. Some think the mice were a separate plague that ruined the crops ("mice that ravage the land," v5).
- **"Give glory to the God of Israel. Perhaps he will lighten his hand" (v5).** The wordplay of chapters 4–5 continues. "Glory" (*kabod*) is "weight," and God's hand has been "heavy" (*kabed*) on them (5:6, 11). If they give him his due weight, perhaps he will make his hand "light" (*qalal*). To give God glory here means to admit openly that he did this (compare Josh 7:19).
- **"Why should you harden your hearts as the Egyptians and Pharaoh hardened their hearts?" (v6).** "Harden" is again the verb *kabed*, "make heavy," the word Exodus uses for Pharaoh's stubborn heart (Exod 8:15, 32). The Philistines learned the lesson that Pharaoh never did: holding out against Israel's God only makes things worse. "After he had dealt severely with them" uses a verb (*'alal*) that Exodus uses for God "making a mockery of" Egypt (Exod 10:2).
- **"A new cart and two milk cows on which there has never come a yoke" (v7).** A new cart and unworked cows were fit for a sacred use, untouched by ordinary work (compare Num 19:2; Deut 21:3). David will later make the mistake of moving the ark on a new cart too (2 Sam 6:3), with deadly results. The law required Levites to carry it on poles (Num 4:15; 7:9). God bore with Philistine ignorance here in a way he did not with Israel's later carelessness.
- **"Take their calves home, away from them" (v7).** This is the heart of the test. Nursing cows separated from their calves will try to go back to them. Cows that had never been yoked would not naturally pull together in a straight line. Everything in nature says the cows will turn for home. If they go up the road to Israel anyway, the Philistines will know the God of Israel did this.
- **"It happened to us by coincidence" (v9).** The Hebrew is *miqreh*, "chance" or "happening," the same word used in Ruth 2:3, where Ruth "happened" to come to Boaz's field. The Philistines leave room for the idea that it was all bad luck. The test is designed to settle it one way or the other.
- **Beth-shemesh (v9).** The name means "house of the sun," probably from an older Canaanite sun temple. It sat in the Sorek Valley, where the coastal plain meets the hills of Judah, about 6 miles east of Ekron. It was the nearest Israelite town and one of the towns given to the priests (Josh 21:16). Samson grew up in this same valley.
- **"The cows went straight… lowing as they went. They turned neither to the right nor to the left" (v12).** The cows cry for their calves, yet walk away from them, straight up the road. It is a miracle against nature. "Neither to the right nor to the left" is the phrase used for perfect obedience to God's law (Deut 5:32). Two dumb animals obey God more exactly than Israel's elders did. The five lords follow to the border to see for themselves.
- **"Reaping their wheat harvest in the valley… they rejoiced to see it" (v13).** Wheat harvest was in late spring, around May or June. The harvesters look up from the fields and see the ark coming home on its own, with no one leading it. Their joy is right. The God they thought had departed has come back on his own terms.
- **"The field of Joshua of Beth-shemesh… a great stone" (v14).** The cart stops by a large rock. They break up the cart for firewood and offer the cows as a burnt offering, the sacrifice of complete dedication. Strictly, the law called for male animals for burnt offerings (Lev 1:3) and sacrifice at the central sanctuary. But Shiloh was probably gone, and these cows had been chosen by God for this task. Like the Levites' presence (v15), the offering shows joy and gratitude, and the text does not fault it.
- **"The Levites took down the ark" (v15).** Beth-shemesh was a priestly town, so there were Levites on hand to handle the ark as the law required (Num 4:15). The great stone became an altar and a stand for the ark.
- **"One for Ashdod, one for Gaza, one for Ashkelon, one for Gath, one for Ekron" (v17).** This is the only place in this account that names all five Philistine cities. Gaza and Ashkelon were not mentioned in chapter 5, which suggests the plague reached them too (v4: "the same plague was on all of you"). The golden mice were more numerous, one for each Philistine town, "both fortified cities and unwalled villages" (v18).
- **"The great stone… is a witness to this day" (v18).** The rock stood in Joshua's field when the book was written, a landmark for anyone who wanted to check the story. Most Hebrew manuscripts here read "Abel," meaning "meadow," instead of "stone" (*'eben*). The ESV follows the Greek and some Hebrew manuscripts.
- **"He struck some of the men of Beth-shemesh, because they looked upon the ark of the LORD" (v19).** Even the Levites were forbidden to look at the holy things uncovered, on pain of death (Num 4:5, 15, 20). The men of Beth-shemesh apparently opened the ark or stared at it unveiled, treating the throne of God as a curiosity. The Philistines had been ignorant. Israel had God's law, and was held to it. The Greek translation reads differently here: "the sons of Jeconiah did not rejoice with the men of Beth-shemesh when they saw the ark." Some scholars and some translations follow it, but the ESV keeps the Hebrew.
- **"Seventy men" (v19).** The traditional Hebrew text reads "seventy men, fifty thousand men," a phrase that is awkward in Hebrew and impossible for a small village. A few Hebrew manuscripts and the Jewish historian Josephus have only seventy. Most scholars and modern translations conclude that "fifty thousand" was added by a copyist, and the ESV follows the shorter reading with a footnote.
- **"Who is able to stand before the LORD, this holy God?" (v20).** This is the right question, asked in the wrong spirit. "Holy" means set apart, utterly other, pure. Their answer to the question is to send God away: "To whom shall he go up away from us?" They react exactly as Ekron did (5:10–11). God's own people want to be rid of his presence because it is too dangerous. They do not ask how sinful people can stand before a holy God. They only ask how to keep a safe distance.
- **"Come down and take it up to you" (v21).** Kiriath-jearim, "town of forests," lay in the hills about 10 miles northeast of Beth-shemesh, near the border of Judah and Benjamin. It was also called Baalah or Kiriath-baal (Josh 15:9, 60). "Come down… take it up" fits the climb from the valley into the hills. The ark will stay there about twenty years (7:2), until David brings it to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the Egyptians "were forced at last to let Israel go," so the Philistines should yield in time. On the cows, he says they went "contrary to their inclination," and "on lowing for their young ones." He says the reapers' "joy for that was greater than the joy of harvest," and blames the men of Beth-shemesh for an "over-bold curiosity to look into it." JFB says carrying the ark on a cart "was forbidden," but the Philistines' ignorance excused it. He thinks the lords followed the cart to check for fraud and so saw God's glory for themselves. He calls "fifty thousand and threescore and ten" erroneous for a village this size and follows Josephus's seventy. Guzik agrees the original number was seventy. He points out that the Philistines' test was stacked against God, and that God passed it anyway. He also explains that holiness means God's apartness, not only his moral purity, and notes that the men of Beth-shemesh wanted distance from God rather than reconciliation.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Who is able to stand before the LORD, this holy God?" The Philistines could not, and neither could the Israelites at Beth-shemesh. The question hangs over the whole Old Testament (Ps 24:3; 130:3; Mal 3:2). The answer is not to send God away, nor to find some way to make his hand lighter with golden gifts. The answer is a mediator. Under the old covenant, only the high priest could come near the ark, once a year, with blood (Lev 16). In the new, Jesus "entered once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12). The curtain that hid the ark was torn in two at his death (Matt 27:51). Through him, people who could never stand before a holy God are invited to "draw near with confidence" (Heb 4:16; 10:19–22). And the cows that turned neither right nor left, lowing as they went, quietly picture the obedience of the one who set his face to go where his Father sent him, whatever it cost.
