*Setting:* Paul writes from Corinth around AD 51, only months after his first letter to Thessalonica. Chapter 1 encouraged the persecuted church with the promise of Christ's return in justice. Chapter 2 corrected the false claim that "the day of the Lord has come" and told them to stand firm in what the apostles taught. Chapter 3 turns to the last piece of business. In the first letter Paul had gently told the church to "work with your hands" (1 Thess 4:11) and to "admonish the idle" (1 Thess 5:14). The problem had not gone away. Some members had stopped working and were living off others. Many think the confusion about the end fed this, though Paul never says so directly. So the chapter moves from prayer to discipline to blessing, and ends with Paul taking the pen in his own hand.

#### Pray for Us (vv1–5)
- **"Finally, brothers, pray for us" (v1).** As in 1 Thessalonians 4:1, "finally" means "as for the rest," not "in closing." Paul often asks churches to pray for him (Rom 15:30; Eph 6:19; Col 4:3; 1 Thess 5:25). He does not see prayer as a one-way flow from apostle to church.
- **"That the word of the Lord may speed ahead and be honored" (v1).** "Speed ahead" is literally "run" (*trechō*). The lexicon points to Psalm 147:15: "his word runs swiftly." The gospel is pictured like a runner in a race, and "be honored" may carry on the picture of a victor being crowned (1 Cor 9:24). Paul asks for prayer first not for his safety but for the message.
- **"As happened among you" (v1).** The Thessalonians are the proof. The word had run through their city and "sounded forth" across Macedonia and Achaia (1 Thess 1:8).
- **"That we may be delivered from wicked and evil men" (v2).** "Wicked" (*atopos*) means literally "out of place," so improper, perverse. In Corinth at this time Paul faced fierce opposition from the synagogue leaders, who eventually dragged him before the Roman proconsul Gallio (Acts 18:6, 12–17). The prayer was answered. The Lord told Paul in a vision, "no one will attack you to harm you" (Acts 18:9–10), and Gallio threw the case out.
- **"For not all have faith" (v2).** Literally "the faith is not of all." Paul is not surprised by opposition. Not everyone who hears the gospel believes it.
- **"But the Lord is faithful" (v3).** The wordplay is clear in Greek: *pistis* (faith) and *pistos* (faithful). People may lack faith, but the Lord does not lack faithfulness. Paul turns at once from his own trouble to theirs.
- **"He will establish you and guard you against the evil one" (v3).** "Establish" is the word used for Timothy's mission and Paul's prayers (1 Thess 3:2, 13; 2 Thess 2:17). "The evil one" could also be translated "evil," as in the Lord's Prayer, but after chapter 2's description of Satan's work (2:9), most take it as a person. Jesus prayed the same thing for his disciples: "keep them from the evil one" (John 17:15).
- **"We have confidence in the Lord about you" (v4).** The confidence is "in the Lord," not in the Thessalonians themselves. This verse also prepares for what follows: Paul trusts they will obey the commands he is about to give.
- **"May the Lord direct your hearts" (v5).** "Direct" means to make a road straight, clearing obstacles out of the way. Paul used the same verb when praying that God would "direct our way to you" (1 Thess 3:11). The phrase echoes David's prayer in 1 Chronicles 29:18 that God would "direct their hearts toward you."
- **"To the love of God and to the steadfastness of Christ" (v5).** "Love of God" can mean God's love for them or their love for God, and the Greek allows both. "Steadfastness of Christ" can mean the endurance Christ showed in his suffering, or patient waiting for Christ's return, as the KJV takes it ("patient waiting for Christ"). In a letter to a suffering church, the first reading fits well: may their endurance be shaped like his.
- **Commentators:** JFB pictures the word running "without a drag on the wheels," and on v3 writes: "Faith on the part of man answers to faithfulness on the part of God." On v4 it warns, "Have confidence in no man when left to himself." Guzik suggests that the church's prayerlessness may slow the word's progress, and he compares v3 to the Lord keeping the evil one on a leash, as in 1 Corinthians 10:13.

#### Warning Against Idleness (vv6–15)
- **"We command you, brothers, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v6).** The word "command" appears four times in this chapter (vv4, 6, 10, 12). Paul does not usually write this sternly. The gentle approach of the first letter had not worked.
- **"Keep away from any brother who is walking in idleness" (v6).** "Keep away" (*stellō*) originally meant to gather up or furl sails, and so to hold back or keep clear. "Idleness" (*ataktōs*) is the word from 1 Thessalonians 5:14, used of soldiers who break ranks. It means being out of line, but in this chapter Paul spells out what that looked like: not working (vv8, 10–12). "Brother" makes clear these are church members, not outsiders.
- **"Not in accord with the tradition that you received from us" (v6).** "Tradition" again means the apostles' teaching handed on (2:15). Part of that teaching was a way of life, including working for one's own bread.
- **"For you yourselves know how you ought to imitate us" (v7).** In the ancient world teachers taught by example as much as by word. Paul had lived among them as a model (1 Thess 1:6; 2:9).
- **"Nor did we eat anyone's bread without paying for it" (v8).** "Eat bread" was a Hebrew way of saying "make a living" (Gen 3:19). Paul worked as a tentmaker or leatherworker (Acts 18:3), "night and day" (1 Thess 2:9), probably starting before dawn and teaching while he worked.
- **"It was not because we do not have that right" (v9).** Paul taught clearly that gospel workers may be supported by the people they serve (1 Cor 9:3–14; Gal 6:6; 1 Tim 5:17–18). Jesus himself said "the laborer deserves his wages" (Luke 10:7). Paul gave up that right in Thessalonica, as he later did in Corinth, partly to be an example and partly to separate himself from traveling teachers who preached for money (1 Thess 2:5–9). The lesson is not that ministers should never be paid, but that Paul put others' good above his rights.
- **"If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" (v10).** Paul had said this repeatedly while there ("we would give you this command"). The key word is "willing." The rule is about those who *will not* work, not those who *cannot*. The same letters that give this rule urge care for the weak (1 Thess 5:14), and elsewhere Paul organizes help for widows and the poor (1 Tim 5:3–16; Gal 2:10). Jewish teaching prized work. The rabbis said that a father who did not teach his son a trade taught him to steal. Probably within a few decades, the early Christian manual called the Didache told churches that a traveling believer could stay two or three days, but after that, "if he has a trade, let him work and eat." Someone who would not work was trading on Christ's name.
- **"Not busy at work, but busybodies" (v11).** The Greek is a pun: *mēden ergazomenous alla periergazomenous*, "not working but working around." "Busybody" (*periergazomai*) means to waste effort on what is not one's business, to meddle. Idle hands find other people's affairs. Why had they stopped working? Many scholars think some believed the Lord's return was so close that ordinary work no longer mattered, especially after the claim that the day had come (2:2). Others suggest that some poorer members had become dependent on richer believers, as clients did on patrons in Roman society, and saw no need to work. The two may have gone together. Paul does not name the cause. He names the cure.
- **"Do their work quietly and to earn their own living" (v12).** "Command and encourage" again holds firmness and warmth together. "Quietly" picks up 1 Thessalonians 4:11, and "earn their own living" is literally "eat their own bread." The cure for meddling is quiet, ordinary work.
- **"As for you, brothers, do not grow weary in doing good" (v13).** Paul turns to the hardworking majority. Supporting freeloaders could make generous people cynical, and the temptation would be to stop helping anyone. Paul says not to let a few abusers kill generosity toward those truly in need (Gal 6:9).
- **"Take note of that person, and have nothing to do with him, that he may be ashamed" (v14).** "Take note" means to mark him out. "Have nothing to do with" is literally "do not mix with," the same word Paul uses in 1 Corinthians 5:9–11. This may have meant not sharing in the church's common meals, which would hit an idle person directly. The aim is shame that leads to change, not humiliation for its own sake.
- **"Do not regard him as an enemy, but warn him as a brother" (v15).** This verse sets the limit. In ancient culture, a broken relationship often meant open hostility. Paul forbids that. The disciplined person is still a brother. Christians differ in how they practice church discipline today, but this passage shows its spirit: firm, public enough to be clear, and always aimed at bringing the person back (Matt 18:15; Gal 6:1). This is a lighter step than the removal Paul orders for serious unrepentant sin in 1 Corinthians 5.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christianity is not to countenance slothfulness," and "If we are idle, the devil and a corrupt heart will soon find us somewhat to do." JFB catches the pun in v11 as "doing none of their own business, yet overdoing in the business of others," and it also explains "keep away" as "to furl the sails," which the lexicon supports. JFB passes on Bengel's quip on v10: "Let him who will not work show himself an angel," that is, able to live without eating. JFB has no comment of its own on v9, only cross-references. On v15, JFB says the man is "not yet excommunicated." Guzik agrees that the withdrawal aims at restoration, not expulsion. On v10 he notes that the early church helped the needy after checking the need and putting people to work where they could. On v13 Henry urges: "Hold on your way, and hold on to the end."

#### Benediction (vv16–18)
- **"May the Lord of peace himself give you peace at all times in every way" (v16).** Paul usually says "the God of peace" (1 Thess 5:23; Rom 15:33). Here it is "the Lord," which in this letter means Jesus. It recalls his words: "Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you" (John 14:27). For a church facing persecution outside, false teaching about the end, and discipline inside, this is a needed prayer. "At all times in every way" covers every circumstance. Some manuscripts read "in every place."
- **"The Lord be with you all" (v16).** "All" includes the idle members just disciplined. Paul does not write them out of the blessing.
- **"I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand" (v17).** Paul usually dictated letters to a secretary (Rom 16:22). At the end he took the pen himself (1 Cor 16:21; Gal 6:11; Col 4:18). Here he points it out deliberately: "This is the sign of genuineness in every letter of mine." After the confusion caused by "a letter seeming to be from us" (2:2), the church could now check any letter by its handwriting.
- **"The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you all" (v18).** This is the same closing as the first letter (1 Thess 5:28), with "all" added, which may be a quiet reminder that grace is for every member, the faithful and the corrected alike. The "Amen" and the note that the letter was written from Athens are later additions and not in the oldest manuscripts.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the "Lord of peace" as Christ, and the peace as unbroken, "not changing with outward circumstances." On v17 it says the token was to tell "genuine Epistles from spurious ones put forth in my name." Guzik adds that the handwritten greeting was also a sign of personal affection.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is practical, about prayer, work and discipline, but Christ is at the center of it. The word that runs is "the word of the Lord." The Lord is faithful when people are not. The hearts that need straightening are pointed to "the steadfastness of Christ," who endured the cross without growing weary in doing good. And the letter ends with his peace and his grace given to "you all," even to those who have just been warned. The Thessalonians did not need to stop working to wait for him. They needed to work quietly, in the strength of a Lord who is already with them.
