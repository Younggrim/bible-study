*Setting:* The last years of Solomon's forty-year reign, ending about 931 BC. Chapters 3–10 have shown Solomon at his height: wisdom from God, peace on every border, the temple, the palace, the queen of Sheba, and gold beyond counting. Chapter 11 turns sharply. In his old age Solomon's many foreign wives draw him into worshiping their gods, and God announces that the kingdom will be torn apart. The chapter then explains how that came about: two foreign enemies, Hadad of Edom and Rezon of Damascus, and an enemy within, Jeroboam, a gifted officer whom a prophet marks out to rule ten of the tribes. Chapter 12 tells the split itself, which comes right after Solomon's death. Chronicles leaves this chapter out entirely. It ends Solomon's story with his wealth and fame (2 Chr 9).

#### Solomon Turns from the LORD (vv1–8)
- **"Now King Solomon loved many foreign women" (v1).** "Loved" (*ʾahav*) is the same word used at the start: "Solomon loved the LORD" (3:3). The story of his reign begins and ends with what Solomon loved. The list of nations is pointed: Moab, Ammon, Edom, Sidon (Phoenicia) and the Hittites (the city-states of Syria). Most of these marriages were political, since a royal wedding sealed a treaty, and each princess brought her own household and her own gods.
- **"Along with the daughter of Pharaoh" (v1).** She was mentioned favorably earlier (3:1; 9:24), but here she heads the list. The writer has saved the full assessment until now.
- **"You shall not enter into marriage with them… for surely they will turn away your heart after their gods" (v2).** This quotes the substance of Exodus 34:15–16 and Deuteronomy 7:3–4. The command was not about ethnicity. Rahab and Ruth married into Israel, and Ruth was a Moabite. It was about worship: marrying someone devoted to another god would pull an Israelite's heart away. That is exactly what happened.
- **"Solomon clung to these in love" (v2).** "Clung" is *dabaq*, the word of Genesis 2:24, "a man shall… hold fast to his wife." It is also the word Deuteronomy uses for Israel's loyalty to God: "hold fast to him" (Deut 10:20; 13:4). Solomon held fast, but to the wrong things.
- **"700 wives, who were princesses, and 300 concubines" (v3).** Concubines were lower-ranking wives without the status of a queen. The numbers are huge even by ancient royal standards and fit the boast of a great empire. The king's law had said, "he shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away" (Deut 17:17). The writer of Kings uses the same verb twice in vv3–4: **"his wives turned away his heart."** Song of Songs 6:8 mentions sixty queens and eighty concubines, perhaps from an earlier stage of his reign.
- **"When Solomon was old" (v4).** He reigned forty years and came to the throne young (3:7), so he may have been only around sixty. The drift did not happen overnight. It came slowly, over years, from the very marriages that had seemed like good statesmanship. Age did not bring wisdom. It hardened what had been growing.
- **"His heart was not wholly true to the LORD his God, as was the heart of David his father" (v4).** "Wholly true" is *shalem*, whole or complete. This is the very word Solomon used in his own blessing at the temple dedication: "Let your heart therefore be wholly true to the LORD our God" (8:61). David sinned terribly, but he never worshiped another god, and when confronted he repented (2 Sam 12:13; Ps 51). From here on, David's whole heart is the standard by which every king of Judah is measured (15:3, 11; 2 Kgs 18:3).
- **"Ashtoreth the goddess of the Sidonians" (v5).** The Canaanite and Phoenician goddess Astarte, linked with love, fertility and war, known in Mesopotamia as Ishtar. Many scholars think the Hebrew writers deliberately gave her name the vowels of *bosheth*, "shame," a known practice (compare Ish-bosheth for Ish-baal). **"Milcom the abomination of the Ammonites."** Milcom's name is built on *melek*, "king." "Abomination" (*shiqquts*) is a strong word for something detestable, used especially of idols.
- **"So Solomon did what was evil in the sight of the LORD" (v6).** This is the formula the book will use for wicked kings, and here it is first applied to Solomon. **"Did not wholly follow the LORD"** is literally "did not fill up after the LORD," the phrase used of Caleb, who followed the LORD fully (Num 14:24; Josh 14:8). Solomon started well and did not finish.
- **"A high place for Chemosh… and for Molech… on the mountain east of Jerusalem" (v7).** A high place was a hilltop shrine with an altar. The mountain east of Jerusalem is the Mount of Olives, facing the temple across the Kidron Valley. Chemosh is the god named on the Mesha Stele, the Moabite king's own inscription from about 840 BC (compare 2 Kgs 3). Molech was the god associated with child sacrifice (Lev 18:21; 20:2–5). Whether Molech is the same god as Milcom, since both are called "the abomination of the Ammonites," is debated. Many scholars think so. These shrines stood for more than three centuries, until King Josiah tore them down and the southern end of that ridge was called "the mount of corruption" (2 Kgs 23:13).
- **"And so he did for all his foreign wives" (v8).** Solomon did not abandon the LORD's temple. He added other gods beside him. The man who built a house for the LORD's name now built shrines for gods whose names the law said were not even to be spoken (Exod 23:13). Worship of the LORD alongside other gods is, in the Bible's eyes, unfaithfulness, not tolerance.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Solomon's wisdom did not keep him from "grievous and fatal errors," and rejects the view that he only humored his wives: he became "an actual and open idolater." JFB guesses he "could not have been more than fifty" and notes there is "no trace whatever of Egyptian idolatry" in the list. Henry: "Nor will old age cure the heart of any evil propensity," and "Nothing forms in itself a security against the deceitfulness and depravity of the human heart." Guzik thinks Solomon likely imagined he was the exception to God's rule, and contrasts him with David, who had many wives but kept his heart toward God. Guzik also passes on the view that "Ashtoreth" carries the vowels of the word for shame. The Tyndale notes draw the key contrast: David repented when confronted, but Solomon ignored repeated warnings (3:14; 6:12–13; 9:4–9).

#### The LORD Raises Adversaries (vv9–43)
- **"The LORD was angry with Solomon… who had appeared to him twice" (v9).** At Gibeon (3:5) and after the temple was finished (9:2). Few people in Scripture received such direct encouragement from God, which makes the fall worse. Much was given, so much was required (Luke 12:48).
- **"I will surely tear the kingdom from you and will give it to your servant" (v11).** "Tear" (*qara*) is the key word of this chapter. It recalls Samuel's words to Saul when Saul tore Samuel's robe: "The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you this day and has given it to a neighbor of yours" (1 Sam 15:27–28). Solomon is going the way of Saul. "Your servant" is pointed: not a son or a rival king, but one of his own officials.
- **"For the sake of David your father I will not do it in your days" (v12).** Mercy is mixed into the judgment. The delay is for David's sake, not Solomon's, much as God delayed judgment in Josiah's day and Ahab's (2 Kgs 22:20; 1 Kgs 21:29).
- **"I will give one tribe to your son, for the sake of David my servant and for the sake of Jerusalem" (v13).** The promise of 2 Samuel 7 holds. David's house will be disciplined but not cut off. The "one tribe" is Judah, which had absorbed Simeon in the south. Benjamin, which held the area just north of Jerusalem, also stayed with Judah (12:21). Twelve pieces minus ten leaves two, yet the text speaks of "one tribe" (v32). Perhaps Benjamin, so closely bound up with Jerusalem, is counted with Judah, or perhaps Judah is the one tribe given beyond Benjamin. Readers explain the count in different ways, but the meaning is clear: David's line keeps a small remnant.
- **"The LORD raised up an adversary against Solomon, Hadad the Edomite" (v14).** "Adversary" is *satan*, the ordinary Hebrew word for an opponent or accuser (it is used of an angel blocking Balaam's way in Num 22:22). Solomon had boasted that God gave him rest with "neither adversary nor misfortune" (5:4). Now God raises up adversaries. Peace was a gift, and it could be withdrawn.
- **"When David was in Edom… he struck down every male in Edom" (vv15–16).** David's conquest of Edom is told briefly in 2 Samuel 8:13–14 and Psalm 60's title. Joab spent six months hunting down Edom's men. One royal boy escaped.
- **Hadad's flight to Egypt (vv17–22).** He fled through Midian and the wilderness of Paran to Egypt, where Pharaoh welcomed him, gave him land, and married him to **"the sister of Tahpenes the queen."** Egypt liked to keep a rival prince in reserve against a strong neighbor. The irony is that Solomon too had married Pharaoh's daughter: Egypt was hedging its bets. When David and Joab were dead, Hadad went home. Pharaoh's question, **"What have you lacked with me?"** (v22), and Hadad's answer, **"Only let me depart,"** show a man driven by the call of his homeland. Edom would trouble Judah for centuries.
- **"Rezon the son of Eliada… made him king in Damascus" (vv23–24).** Rezon had served Hadadezer of Zobah, an Aramean king David defeated (2 Sam 8:3–8). He gathered a raiding band and seized Damascus. **"He loathed Israel and reigned over Syria" (v25).** This was the start of the Aramean kingdom of Damascus, which would be Israel's most dangerous enemy for the next two centuries (chs 15, 20, 22; 2 Kgs 5–13). Solomon's empire was already shrinking in the north and south.
- **"Jeroboam the son of Nebat, an Ephraimite of Zeredah" (v26).** Ephraim was the leading northern tribe, Joshua's tribe, and it had long resented Judah's dominance (2 Sam 19:41–43; 20:1). **"Whose mother's name was Zeruah, a widow"** reminds readers that Jeroboam rose from nothing. His name likely means "may the people increase" or "the people contend." **"Lifted up his hand against the king"** means he rebelled.
- **"Solomon built the Millo, and closed up the breach of the city of David" (v27).** This is the setting for the rebellion. Jeroboam was working on Solomon's Jerusalem projects (9:15).
- **"He gave him charge over all the forced labor of the house of Joseph" (v28).** "House of Joseph" means the tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh. Solomon put a talented northerner over his own people's work gangs. Jeroboam saw firsthand how heavy the burden was. When the north later complains of Solomon's "heavy yoke" (12:4), Jeroboam is their spokesman.
- **"Ahijah the Shilonite" (v29).** He came from Shiloh, in Ephraim, where the tabernacle had stood in Samuel's day before the ark was lost (1 Sam 4; Jer 7:12). A prophet from the old northern sanctuary now announces that the north will have its own king. Chronicles mentions "the prophecy of Ahijah the Shilonite" as one of its sources for Solomon's reign (2 Chr 9:29).
- **"Tore it into twelve pieces… Take for yourself ten pieces" (vv30–31).** This is a prophetic sign-act, a message acted out, like Jeremiah's linen belt (Jer 13) or Ezekiel's brick (Ezek 4). The garment was **new**, and the kingdom was still new, barely eighty years old. Ten pieces for Jeroboam: the ten northern tribes. The word "tore" ties it again to Saul's torn robe and the torn kingdom (v11).
- **"Because they have forsaken me" (v33).** The Hebrew has "they" in standard texts, but the Greek, Syriac and Latin read "he," that is, Solomon. Either way, the king's sin had spread to the people. Note the gods are listed again by name: Ashtoreth, Chemosh, Milcom.
- **"That David my servant may always have a lamp before me in Jerusalem" (v36).** A lamp burning in a house meant someone still lived there. A dark house was an empty one. God promises that David's family line will not go out. The phrase returns at Judah's worst moments (15:4; 2 Kgs 8:19), and it is one of the threads that leads to Jesus, the light of the world (John 8:12).
- **"I will build you a sure house, as I built for David" (v38).** This is an astonishing offer. Jeroboam is offered the same kind of lasting dynasty David received, on one condition: obedience "as David my servant did." Jeroboam's very first act as king will be to set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan (12:26–33), and his house will be wiped out within two generations (15:29).
- **"I will afflict the offspring of David because of this, but not forever" (v39).** Two of the most hopeful words in Kings. The division is discipline, not the end. The prophets later promised a reunited people under a single Davidic king (Ezek 37:15–28; Hos 3:5).
- **"Solomon sought therefore to kill Jeroboam" (v40).** Solomon, who once asked for an understanding heart, now behaves like Saul hunting David. He fights God's word instead of repenting. Jeroboam escapes **"to Shishak king of Egypt."** Shishak (Shoshenq I), founder of a new Egyptian dynasty around 945 BC, had no ties to Solomon and was happy to shelter his enemy. Five years after Solomon's death he would invade Judah (14:25–26).
- **"The Book of the Acts of Solomon" (v41).** A royal record now lost, which the writer of Kings used as a source. Chronicles also mentions records kept by Nathan, Ahijah and Iddo (2 Chr 9:29).
- **"Solomon reigned in Jerusalem over all Israel forty years" (v42).** The same length as David's reign and Saul's (Acts 13:21). God had promised him long life if he obeyed (3:14), and forty years is a full reign but not an especially long life.
- **"Solomon slept with his fathers" (v43).** The standard phrase for a king's death, used for good and bad kings alike. It says nothing either way about his final state.
- **Did Solomon repent?** The text does not say. Some believe Ecclesiastes, with its weary verdict "vanity of vanities" and its closing "fear God and keep his commandments" (Eccl 12:13), was written by Solomon looking back on his wasted years. Others note that Ecclesiastes never names Solomon and may be written in his voice by a later author. God promised David that his son would be disciplined but that "my steadfast love will not depart from him, as I took it from Saul" (2 Sam 7:14–15), which gives some ground for hope. Scripture leaves the question open.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks the judgment was probably announced "by a prophet," and on v40 says Jeroboam was "content to live in exile and obscurity for awhile, being sure of a kingdom at last." Henry hopes Solomon repented but says the Spirit left it doubtful, as a warning to others. Guzik reads the evidence as leaning toward Solomon dying in apostasy but allows that God may have shown mercy for David's sake, and quotes John Trapp's grim line that it would have been better for Solomon to be buried alive than to fall so in old age. JFB explains the "one tribe" as Judah, Benjamin and Levi with northern settlers, "all reckoned as one tribe," and thinks Ahijah "wrapped up" in his cloak to stay unseen. JFB adds that Shishak "harbored and encouraged the rebellious refugee." Guzik passes on a Jewish tradition that Jeroboam protested the forced labor, which the text does not state.

#### Pointing to Christ
Solomon had every advantage: a godly father's prayers, wisdom from God, two visions, a magnificent temple and peace on every side. He still let his heart be turned. No wise king can save his people if his own heart wanders, and the kingdom of David was torn because of it. Yet God kept a lamp burning in Jerusalem "for the sake of David." That lamp burned on until a greater Son of David came, one whose heart was wholly true to his Father, who said, "I always do the things that are pleasing to him" (John 8:29). Where Solomon gathered a thousand wives and their gods, Christ gathers one bride, the church, and gives himself for her (Eph 5:25). The torn kingdom will be made one under him (Ezek 37:22–24; John 10:16).
