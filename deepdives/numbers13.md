*Setting:* Israel has reached Kadesh in the wilderness of Paran (12:16; 13:26), on the southern edge of Canaan, perhaps a little over a year after leaving Egypt. The journey from Sinai had taken only about eleven days (Deut 1:2). The land promised to Abraham more than four centuries earlier is right in front of them. This chapter and the next are the turning point of the book of Numbers. Twelve men go in to scout the land, and their report decides whether this generation enters it or dies in the desert. Chapter 13 tells the mission and the report. Chapter 14 tells the result.

#### Spies Sent into Canaan (vv1–24)
- **"Send men to spy out the land of Canaan, which I am giving to the people of Israel" (v2).** God's command comes with a promise built in: "which I am giving." The question was never whether the land could be taken, only what it was like. Deuteronomy 1:22–23 adds that the idea first came from the people ("Let us send men before us"), Moses liked it, and here God approves it. The two accounts fit together. What the people asked out of caution, God allowed as a test.
- **"Every one a chief among them" (v2).** These are leaders, one from each of the twelve tribes (Levi, who received no land, is left out, and Joseph is split into Ephraim and Manasseh). They are not the tribal princes listed in chapters 1, 7 and 10, but they are men of standing, so their report will carry weight.
- **The list of names (vv4–15).** Ten of these names are remembered for nothing except this failure. Two are remembered for faith: **Caleb the son of Jephunneh** from Judah (v6) and **Hoshea the son of Nun** from Ephraim (v8). Hoshea was already Moses' assistant and had led the army against Amalek (Exod 17:9–14; 33:11).
- **"Moses called Hoshea the son of Nun, Joshua" (v16).** *Hoshea* means "salvation." *Yehoshua*, Joshua, adds the short form of God's name: "the LORD is salvation" or "the LORD saves." The Exodus passages already call him Joshua, written from a later vantage point, but this is where the change is recorded. The Greek form of the name is *Iēsous*, Jesus. Joshua will one day lead Israel into the land that Moses could not enter.
- **"Go up into the Negeb and go up into the hill country" (v17).** The Negeb is the dry south country between the desert and the hills. The hill country is the central spine of Canaan running north from Hebron, where most of the fortified towns were. Moses sends them up the natural route north.
- **"See… whether the people… are strong or weak… the land… good or bad… camps or strongholds" (vv18–20).** These are standard scouting questions: population, defenses, soil, timber. Gathering information was not wrong in itself. Joshua later sends spies to Jericho (Josh 2:1). The danger lay in what they would do with the information if they forgot who had promised the land.
- **"Be of good courage and bring some of the fruit of the land" (v20).** Moses expects good news, and wants the people to see and taste it. **"The season of the first ripe grapes"** is mid to late summer, around July or August.
- **"From the wilderness of Zin to Rehob, near Lebo-hamath" (v21).** This is the full length of the land, from the far south to the far north in Syria, about 275 miles from end to end, and well over 500 on foot there and back in forty days. Lebo-hamath ("the entrance of Hamath") later marks Israel's northern border at its widest (34:8; 1 Kgs 8:65). The spies walked the whole promise.
- **"They came to Hebron" (v22).** Of all places, Hebron. Abraham had lived there, and he, Sarah, Isaac, Rebekah, Jacob and Leah were buried there in the cave of Machpelah (Gen 23:19; 49:29–31). It was the spot where the promise of the land was most rooted in family memory. And that is exactly where they met the giants.
- **"Ahiman, Sheshai, and Talmai, the descendants of Anak" (v22).** The Anakim were a clan known for their size (Deut 2:10; 9:2). These three were still there when Caleb, forty-five years later, asked for Hebron and drove them out (Josh 14:12; 15:13–14). The men who frightened ten spies were beaten by one of the two who trusted God.
- **"Hebron was built seven years before Zoan in Egypt" (v22).** An unusual aside. Zoan (Greek Tanis) was a great city in the eastern Nile delta, near where Israel had lived (Ps 78:12). The note seems meant to say Hebron was ancient and impressive, as old as the famous cities of Egypt. It also quietly reminds readers that Israel's ancestors lived in Hebron long before they lived in Egypt.
- **"A single cluster of grapes… carried it on a pole between two of them" (v23).** The cluster was large enough to need two men, or at least to be carried that way so it would not be crushed. Travelers in the region have reported very large clusters from vineyards around Hebron. Pomegranates and figs came too. The **Valley of Eshcol** got its name from this: *eshkol* means "cluster" (v24). The image of two men with grapes on a pole is still the logo of Israel's tourism ministry.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Henry both start from Deuteronomy 1:22: the plan came from the people, and Henry reads it as a sign of distrust in the promise. Guzik suggests Moses, perhaps shaken after chapter 12, went along with it, and that God used it as a test. Guzik sees the renaming of Joshua as a picture of the coming Savior who bears the same name. JFB records that grape clusters in that area have been said to weigh ten or twelve pounds. JFB takes "the mountain" of v17 as Mount Seir, but the ESV's "hill country," the highlands of Canaan itself, fits the route better.

#### Report of the Spies (vv25–33)
- **"At the end of forty days" (v25).** Forty is the number of testing in the Bible: forty days of rain (Gen 7:12), Moses' forty days on Sinai (Exod 24:18), Jesus' forty days in the wilderness (Matt 4:2). In the next chapter God will turn these forty days into forty years, one year for each day (14:34).
- **"They came to Moses and Aaron and to all the congregation… at Kadesh" (v26).** The report is given in public, to the whole assembly, not privately to the leaders. That made it far more powerful, and far more dangerous.
- **"It flows with milk and honey, and this is its fruit" (v27).** The spies confirm exactly what God had said (Exod 3:8). "Milk and honey" means rich pasture for flocks and abundant produce, a land of plenty compared to the desert. They hold up the fruit as proof.
- **"However" (v28).** Everything turns on this word (Hebrew *efes ki*, "only, but"). The facts are not in dispute. The people **"are strong,"** the cities **"fortified and very large,"** and the Anakim are there. Canaanite cities of this era did have strong walls, some on raised mounds that made them look even taller. Deuteronomy 1:28 remembers the phrase "cities… fortified up to heaven."
- **The peoples of the land (v29).** The Amalekites, who had already attacked Israel (Exod 17:8), in the south. Hittites, Jebusites and Amorites in the hills (the Jebusites held Jerusalem until David). Canaanites on the coast and in the Jordan valley. This was a crowded land, with no single power to defeat, but no single power to unite against Israel either.
- **"Caleb quieted the people before Moses" (v30).** The crowd is already stirring before the full report is in. Caleb calls for silence and speaks: **"Let us go up at once and occupy it, for we are well able to overcome it."** He does not deny the walls or the giants. He simply puts God's promise ahead of them. Joshua stands with him in 14:6–9. Later God says Caleb had "a different spirit and has followed me fully" (14:24).
- **"We are not able to go up against the people, for they are stronger than we are" (v31).** The ten state the opposite conclusion from the same facts. The difference between them and Caleb was not what they saw. It was whom they counted on.
- **"A bad report" (v32).** The Hebrew *dibbah* means an evil report or slander, a report meant to discredit. It is the same word for Joseph's bad report about his brothers (Gen 37:2). The ten now go beyond the facts.
- **"A land that devours its inhabitants" (v32).** This contradicts what they had just said about its fruitfulness. It may mean the land was so fought over that its people were always being killed, or that they had seen funerals and decided it was unhealthy. Either way, it turns God's good gift into a death trap.
- **"All the people that we saw in it are of great height" (v32).** "All" is plainly an exaggeration. A few clans of very tall men have become a whole nation of giants.
- **"There we saw the Nephilim" (v33).** The Nephilim appear only here and in Genesis 6:4, a mysterious race of mighty men before the flood. The word is usually linked to the Hebrew verb *naphal*, "to fall," but its meaning is uncertain. Since the flood had destroyed everyone outside the ark, the spies' claim that the Anakim "come from the Nephilim" was probably not literal ancestry but a way of saying "these are the monsters of the old stories." Fear reached for legends.
- **"We seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them" (v33).** The order matters: first how they saw themselves, then how they assumed the enemy saw them. They had no way of knowing what the Canaanites thought. Rahab later tells the spies at Jericho what the Canaanites really felt: "the fear of you has fallen upon us… our hearts melted" (Josh 2:9–11). Isaiah turns the image around: it is the people of the earth who are "like grasshoppers" before God (Isa 40:22).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the spies confirmed God's promise about the land even as they discouraged the people, so that even "out of the mouth of adversaries" his truth was upheld. He calls their claim that they were not able the same as saying God was not able to keep his word, and sums up Caleb with Proverbs 28:1, "The righteous are bold as a lion." Guzik makes the "however" the hinge of the chapter: unbelief admits God's promise and then sets it aside, and it often dresses itself up as being realistic. He stresses that Caleb and the ten saw the same things, so faith or fear came from the heart, not the facts. JFB thinks the spies opened with praise of the land so their objections would be more readily believed, reads "devours its inhabitants" as an unhealthy climate, and calls the giant claim "evidently a false and exaggerated report" and the grasshoppers "a strong Orientalism." The Tyndale notes observe that the scouting questions included food, which had been on the people's minds since chapter 11.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joshua's new name, "the LORD saves," is the name the angel gave to Mary's son (Matt 1:21). The first Joshua led Israel into the land Moses could not give them, and Hebrews 4:8–10 says that even his conquest did not bring God's final rest, which is still offered through Christ. The fruit of Eshcol was a foretaste carried back to people who had not yet arrived, and the Spirit is called a similar foretaste of the inheritance to come (Eph 1:14). The chapter's real question is the same one the gospel asks: whether the promises of God count for more than the giants in front of us. "All the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20).
