*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai. The chapter opens by looking back to "the death of the two sons of Aaron, when they drew near before the LORD and died" (v1; see 10:1–2). That disaster raised a hard question: how can sinful people live with a holy God in their midst? Chapters 11–15 showed how much uncleanness ordinary life produces, and 15:31 warned that it could defile God's dwelling. Leviticus 16 gives God's answer, the Day of Atonement, *Yom Kippur*, once a year. It stands near the center of Leviticus and is often called the heart of the book. Hebrews 9–10 reads it as a picture of the work of Christ.

#### The Day of Atonement (vv1–34)
- **"Not to come at any time into the Holy Place inside the veil" (v2).** "Holy Place" here means the inner room, the Most Holy Place, behind the curtain where the ark stood. The tabernacle was like a king's palace, and this was his throne room. In the ancient world, walking into a king's presence uninvited could cost a life (Esth 4:11). Aaron, the high priest, could enter only one day a year, and only in the way God set out.
- **"I will appear in the cloud over the mercy seat" (v2).** The "mercy seat" (*kapporet*) was the solid gold lid on the ark, with two gold cherubim whose wings met above it (Exod 25:17–22). It served as God's throne on earth. The word comes from the same root as *kipper*, "to make atonement," and BDB defines it as "propitiatory," the place where sin is atoned for, rather than simply a "lid." The Greek Old Testament translated it *hilastērion*, and Paul uses that same word for Christ: God "put forward" Jesus "as a propitiation by his blood" (Rom 3:25).
- **"The holy linen coat… these are the holy garments" (v4).** On this day the high priest set aside his splendid robes of blue, purple, scarlet and gold (Exod 28) and wore plain white linen, much like the clothing of the ordinary priests. He came before God as a humble representative of sinners, not in glory. He bathed first, a full washing.
- **The offerings (vv3, 5–6).** A bull for Aaron's own sin offering and a ram for his burnt offering. From the people, **"two male goats for a sin offering, and one ram for a burnt offering."** Note that the two goats together are called one sin offering. Aaron must begin, as always, by making **"atonement for himself and for his house"** (v6; Heb 5:3).
- **"One lot for the LORD and the other lot for Azazel" (vv7–10).** The two goats stood at the tent's entrance and lots were cast. One would die as a sin offering. The other would be **"presented alive before the LORD to make atonement over it"** and then sent away into the wilderness.
- **What is "Azazel"?** The word appears only in this chapter (vv8, 10, 26). There are three main views. (1) Older translations took it as "the goat that goes away," which gave English the word "scapegoat." (2) BDB derives it from a root meaning "remove" and defines it as **"entire removal"** of sin and guilt, "symbol of entire forgiveness." (3) Many modern scholars, and Jewish writings after the Old Testament such as 1 Enoch, take Azazel as the name of a demon in the desert. JFB mentions those who think it "designates Satan." Even on that view, the goat is not an offering to a demon, which the next chapter forbids (17:7). It sends Israel's sins back to the realm of evil where they belong. The Tyndale notes favor the first view as the simplest. What is clear in every view is the result: the sins are carried far away.
- **"A censer full of coals… two handfuls of sweet incense… inside the veil" (vv12–13).** Aaron took coals **"from the altar before the LORD,"** the very thing Nadab and Abihu failed to do (10:1). The incense made a cloud that hid the mercy seat **"so that he does not die."** No one could look directly on God's throne and live (Exod 33:20).
- **Blood on and before the mercy seat (vv14–15).** First the bull's blood, for the priests, then the goat's blood, for the people, sprinkled on the front of the mercy seat and **"seven times"** in front of it. This is the only time in the year blood came into the Most Holy Place, and the only time anyone stood before God's throne.
- **"Atonement for the Holy Place, because of the uncleannesses of the people" (v16).** Atonement here cleanses places as well as people. Israel's sins and uncleanness had built up all year and polluted God's house, **"which dwells with them in the midst of their uncleannesses."** Without this cleansing, God's presence could not stay. Aaron cleansed the inner room, then the tent, then the outer altar with the blood of both animals (vv16–19). He worked from the throne room outward.
- **"No one may be in the tent of meeting" (v17).** Aaron worked completely alone. No one helped him and no one watched.
- **"Lay both his hands on the head of the live goat, and confess over it all the iniquities" (v21).** Usually a worshiper laid one hand on his own offering (1:4). Here Aaron used both hands, and he spoke aloud. Three words for sin are piled up: **"iniquities… transgressions… sins."** All of Israel's sin, of every kind, was put **"on the head of the goat."**
- **"The goat shall bear all their iniquities on itself to a remote area" (v22).** A man **"in readiness"** led it far into the desert and let it go. The picture is distance. Sin is not just covered over. It is carried off where it can never return. David sings, "as far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us" (Ps 103:12), and Micah, "you will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea" (Mic 7:19). Later Jewish tradition says the goat was led to a cliff so that it could not wander back.
- **Two goats, one offering.** The two goats show two sides of one forgiveness. The goat that died shows that sin must be paid for with blood, a life given in place of the sinner. The goat sent away shows that sin, once paid for, is removed and gone. Neither picture alone is enough.
- **Finishing the day (vv23–28).** Aaron took off the linen garments, left them in the tent, bathed again and put on his high-priestly robes. Then he offered the two burnt offerings, for himself and for the people. The man who led the goat away and the man who burned the remains had to wash before coming back into camp. **"The bull… and the goat… whose blood was brought in… shall be carried outside the camp"** (v27) and burned. Hebrews draws on this exact verse: "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (Heb 13:11–12).
- **"In the seventh month, on the tenth day… you shall afflict yourselves" (v29).** The seventh month (September–October) also held the Feast of Trumpets and the Feast of Booths (23:23–43). "Afflict yourselves" (*'anah*, "to humble") came to mean fasting (Ps 35:13; Isa 58:3), and this became the only fast day commanded in the law. The book of Acts simply calls it "the Fast" (Acts 27:9). It was also **"a Sabbath of solemn rest"** (v31). No one could work, Israelite or **"stranger,"** because no one could add anything to the atonement God provided.
- **"You shall be clean before the LORD from all your sins" (v30).** This is the promise at the center of the day. The goal of all the ritual was a people forgiven and clean.
- **"Once in the year because of all their sins" (v34).** It was a statute forever, to be carried out by each high priest after Aaron. **"And Aaron did as the LORD commanded Moses."** After the failure of chapter 10, the chapter ends with obedience.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the restriction "that he come not at all times into the holy place," and says that under the gospel believers may come to God at any time, so the danger now is staying far off, not coming near. He sees Christ in the linen garments, since Christ made atonement wearing "the linen garments of our mortality, clean indeed, but mean." On the scapegoat, he says the confession should be specific and quotes Jeremiah 50:20: the sins of Israel "should be sought for, and not found." And "when we have the comfort of our pardon God must have the glory of it." JFB holds that the two goats "constituted only one offering," and while it reports the view that Azazel means Satan, it sees in the goat a "typical reference to Christ who bore away our sins." Guzik contrasts Aaron's need to offer for himself with Jesus, who needed no sacrifice for himself (Heb 7:26–28). He notes the Talmud's description of the goats as nearly identical, and passes on a rabbinic tradition about a red cloth that was said to turn white when God accepted the sacrifice. He suggests the people's rest pictured salvation as the work of another, not of their own effort.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews 9 walks through this chapter step by step. The high priest went into the inner room "only once a year, and not without taking blood, which he offers for himself and for the unintentional sins of the people" (Heb 9:7). That annual entry showed "that the way into the holy places is not yet opened." But Christ "entered once for all into the holy places, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption" (Heb 9:12). He is both the high priest who needs no offering for himself and the sacrifice whose blood is sprinkled on the true mercy seat. He is also the one who bears sins far away, "who himself bore our sins in his body on the tree" (1 Pet 2:24). When he died, "the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (Matt 27:51). What Aaron could do once a year, trembling behind a cloud of incense, all who belong to Christ may now do every day: "we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus" (Heb 10:19).
