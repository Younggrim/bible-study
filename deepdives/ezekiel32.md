*Setting:* Babylonia, the first day of the twelfth month of the twelfth year of the exile, about March 585 BC. Jerusalem had fallen in the summer of 586, and the news had reached the exiles a couple of months earlier (33:21). Judah's hope in Egypt had come to nothing, and Egypt itself was still standing and proud. Chapter 32 holds the last two of Ezekiel's seven messages against Egypt. The first (vv1–16) is a lament over Pharaoh as a beast hauled out of the water. The second (vv17–32), given two weeks later, follows Pharaoh down into the world of the dead, where the great powers of the past lie in their graves and greet him. Together they close the whole collection of oracles against the nations that began in chapter 25. The second message also gathers up seven nations in all, a fitting end to a section built on sevens.

#### A Lament over Pharaoh and Egypt (vv1–32)
- **"Raise a lamentation over Pharaoh king of Egypt" (v2).** Like the laments over Tyre (27:2; 28:12), this is a funeral song sung before the funeral, a way of saying the death is certain.
- **"You consider yourself a lion of the nations, but you are like a dragon in the seas" (v2).** Pharaoh pictured himself as a lion, the king of beasts. Egyptian art often showed the king as a lion or a sphinx trampling his enemies. God's picture is different: a "dragon" (*tannim*), the great water beast of 29:3, here a crocodile thrashing in the Nile. **"You burst forth in your rivers, trouble the waters with your feet, and foul their rivers."** He does not rule the waters. He just churns them into mud. JFB and Guzik both note the contrast: the lion of his own imagination is really a crocodile stirring up mud.
- **"I will throw my net over you with a host of many peoples, and they will haul you up in my dragnet" (v3).** The net is God's, though many peoples hold it, Babylon and its allies. The great beast is caught like a fish.
- **"I will cast you on the ground… and will cause all the birds of the heavens to settle on you" (vv4–6).** The images from 29:5 are expanded. The carcass is so large that it covers mountains and fills valleys, and its blood drenches the land up to the mountains. The Tyndale notes point out the deliberate exaggeration and the pairing of opposites (mountains and valleys, hills and ravines) that means "everywhere." The blood-soaked land also recalls the first plague, when the Nile turned to blood (Exod 7:17–21).
- **"When I blot you out, I will cover the heavens and make their stars dark" (v7).** "Blot you out" (*kabah*) is the word for snuffing out a lamp. Pharaoh is pictured as a bright light in the sky, and when he is put out, the sun, moon and stars go dark. Egypt worshiped the sun god Ra, and Pharaoh was called his son. The darkness also recalls the ninth plague, three days of darkness over Egypt (Exod 10:21–23). Darkened sun, moon and stars are standard language for the day of the LORD (Isa 13:10; Joel 2:10, 31), and Jesus uses the same words of the end of the age (Matt 24:29).
- **"I will trouble the hearts of many peoples… into the countries that you have not known" (v9).** News of Egypt's fall, or its scattered captives, will reach distant lands. **"The hair of their kings shall bristle with horror… They shall tremble every moment, every one for his own life" (v10).** When the greatest falls, the lesser fear for themselves. Henry: "When the sword of God's justice is drawn against some, to cut them off, it is thereby brandished before others, to give them warning."
- **"The sword of the king of Babylon shall come upon you" (v11).** After the poetry, plain words. The same agent as chapters 29–30. **"All of them most ruthless of nations" (v12)** repeats 28:7 and 30:11. **"They shall bring to ruin the pride of Egypt, and all its multitude shall perish."**
- **"I will destroy all its beasts from beside many waters; and no foot of man shall trouble them anymore" (v13).** The crocodile muddied the water (v2). Now no man or beast will be left to stir it. **"Then I will make their waters clear, and cause their rivers to run like oil" (v14).** Oil flows smoothly and silently. The rivers will be still, the stillness of an empty land. JFB adds that Egypt's power, like the Nile, would no longer flood out over other countries.
- **"This is a lamentation that shall be chanted; the daughters of the nations shall chant it" (v16).** Professional women mourners chanted laments at funerals (Jer 9:17–20). Here the nations' daughters will sing Egypt's dirge.
- **"In the twelfth year, in the twelfth month, on the fifteenth day of the month" (v17).** The Hebrew does not name the month. The ESV supplies "twelfth month," which would put this two weeks after v1. The Greek translation reads "first month." Either way it is the last dated message against the nations.
- **"Wail over the multitude of Egypt, and send them down… to the world below" (v18).** Ezekiel is told not only to mourn but to "send them down." In the Bible a prophet's word does what it says (Jer 1:10). JFB: "The prophet's word was God's, and carried with it its own fulfilment."
- **"Whom do you surpass in beauty? Go down and be laid to rest with the uncircumcised" (v19).** The question of 31:2 and 31:18 returns as a taunt. Egypt was famous for beauty, wealth and elaborate burials, with embalmed bodies, painted coffins and treasure-filled tombs meant to secure eternal life. None of it will help. **"With the uncircumcised"** is the shame of being buried among outsiders. The phrase "uncircumcised, slain by the sword" or something like it appears about a dozen times in vv19–32, tolling like a bell.
- **"The mighty chiefs shall speak of them… out of the midst of Sheol" (v21).** Sheol is the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead. As in Isaiah 14:9–11, where the dead kings rise to greet the fallen king of Babylon, the mighty of the past speak from below: **"They have come down, they lie still."**
- **A tour of the graves (vv22–30).** Ezekiel now walks through Sheol like a cemetery, naming the nations buried there, each with its people's graves around it. The language is deliberately repetitive: "slain, fallen by the sword," "who spread terror in the land of the living," "they bear their shame with those who go down to the pit." JFB notes that "the very monotony of the phraseology gives to the dirge an awe-inspiring effect."
- **"Assyria is there, and all her company" (vv22–23).** The empire of chapter 31, the most feared power of the previous centuries. Its kings boasted in their inscriptions of flaying and impaling their enemies. **"Whose graves are set in the uttermost parts of the pit."** Assyria is in the deepest part. **"Who spread terror in the land of the living."** That phrase becomes the charge against every nation here.
- **"Elam is there, and all her multitude" (vv24–25).** Elam lay east of Babylon in what is now southwest Iran, with its capital at Susa. It was an ancient kingdom (Gen 14:1), famous for archers (Isa 22:6; Jer 49:35), and was crushed by Assyria around 640 BC.
- **"Meshech-Tubal is there" (vv26–27).** Peoples of central and eastern Anatolia (modern Turkey), named among Tyre's traders (27:13) and later as allies of Gog (38:2).
- **"And they do not lie with the mighty… whose swords were laid under their heads" (v27).** Warriors who died with honor were buried with their weapons, the sword placed under the head and the shield over the body. Meshech-Tubal will not get that honor. The Hebrew is debated. The Greek translation reads "they lie with the giants who fell of old," which many scholars think recalls the "mighty men who were of old" of Genesis 6:4. If so, the point may be the reverse: even here they lie with the ancient mighty dead. The ESV takes it as a contrast. **"Whose iniquities are upon their bones"** is also difficult. Many scholars change one word to read "whose shields are upon their bones," which fits the burial picture better.
- **"But as for you, you shall be broken and lie among the uncircumcised" (v28).** The "you" is Pharaoh. In the middle of the list, the prophet turns and points at him.
- **"Edom is there, her kings and all her princes" (v29).** Edom, judged in 25:12–14 and again in chapter 35, lies here too, "for all their might."
- **"The princes of the north are there, all of them, and all the Sidonians" (v30).** The northern rulers are probably the Phoenician city-kings and Syrian states north of Israel. The Sidonians stand for the Phoenicians, including Tyre (chs 26–28).
- **The seven.** With Egypt, the nations in Sheol number seven: Assyria, Elam, Meshech-Tubal, Edom, the princes of the north, the Sidonians and Egypt. Again Babylon is missing. In Ezekiel's day it was God's instrument, and its own reckoning was left for others to announce (Isa 13–14; Jer 50–51).
- **"When Pharaoh sees them, he will be comforted for all his multitude" (v31).** This is grim irony. Pharaoh's only comfort is that he is not alone. JFB calls it "the melancholy satisfaction of not being alone… a very poor one!" Henry: "Who is afraid of a dead lion?"
- **"For I spread terror in the land of the living" (v32).** The Hebrew text reads "his terror," and the margin reads "my terror," which the ESV follows. Each of the dead nations "spread terror in the land of the living." Now God says that he himself sets terror there, through Pharaoh's own end. **"And he shall be laid to rest among the uncircumcised… Pharaoh and all his multitude."** The oracles against Egypt end with the word that has run through them all: *hamon*, "multitude."
- **What does this say about life after death?** The chapter is poetry and should be read as poetry. Nations, not just individuals, lie in graves and speak. Ezekiel is not giving a map of the afterlife. Still, the scene is consistent with what the Old Testament says elsewhere: the dead are not simply gone; Sheol is a real place of the dead, pictured as dark and silent, where the mighty are brought low (Isa 14:9–11; Ps 49:14–15). Guzik notes that the dead here are aware of one another and speak. Others stress that the picture is a poetic dramatization of shame and should not be pressed for details. The Old Testament's view of the afterlife is shadowy compared with the New Testament, where Jesus speaks plainly of judgment and resurrection (John 5:28–29; Luke 16:19–31) and Paul says Christ "brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry draws many lessons: "A great deal of disquiet is often given to the world by the restless ambition and implacable resentments of proud princes"; "The numbers of sinners, though they be a multitude, will neither secure them against God's power nor entitle them to his pity"; and "Death will be a king of terrors to those who, instead of making themselves blessings, make themselves terrors, in their generation." He also notes that a minister who would move others "must make it appear that they are themselves affected with the miseries which sinners bring upon themselves." JFB explains "the sides of the pit" (v23) from Eastern rock-cut tombs with niches along the walls, reads the "princes of the north" as Syria or the Tyrians, and discusses the written and marginal readings of v32. Guzik, citing Wright, sums up the scene: each empire imagines it has found the secret of lasting, but one follows another to death. He notes Clarke's remark that the Pharaoh who called himself a god will be found "among the vulgar dead."

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel 32 ends in a cemetery of empires. Assyria, Elam, Edom, the Phoenicians and finally Egypt lie in their graves, each having "spread terror in the land of the living," each now silent. Every power in the chapter goes down, and none comes up. That is the backdrop for the strangest claim in the New Testament: that one man went down into death and came back. Jesus says, "I died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades" (Rev 1:18). Pharaoh's only comfort in the grave was that others were there too. The comfort Christ offers is that the grave is no longer the end: "because I live, you also will live" (John 14:19). The darkened sun of v7 returned at the cross, when "there was darkness over all the land" (Matt 27:45), but there the one who went down into darkness did so to break death's hold, so that Paul can ask, "O death, where is your victory?" (1 Cor 15:55).
