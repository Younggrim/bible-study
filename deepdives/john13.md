*Setting:* Thursday evening of Passover week, about AD 30 or 33, in an upstairs room in Jerusalem (Luke 22:12). Jesus' public ministry ended with ch 12, where the crowds heard his last appeal and he "hid himself from them." Now John's Gospel turns inward. Chapters 13–17 are spent with the Twelve on the last night, and they take up almost a quarter of the book. Matthew, Mark and Luke describe the bread and cup of the Last Supper. John does not. He tells instead what the others leave out: Jesus washing feet, the exposure of the traitor, the new commandment, and the long farewell teaching that follows. Within hours Jesus will be arrested.

#### Jesus Washes the Disciples' Feet (vv1–20)
- **"When Jesus knew that his hour had come" (v1).** The "hour" that was "not yet" through chs 2–11 and arrived in 12:23 is now here. It is described as departing "out of this world to the Father." John presents the cross not first as defeat but as a homecoming.
- **"He loved them to the end" (v1).** The phrase *eis telos* means both "to the last moment" and "to the uttermost, completely." Both are true. The same root returns in Jesus' last word from the cross, "It is finished" (*tetelestai*, 19:30). Verse 1 is a heading for everything in chs 13–19: what follows is what love to the end looks like.
- **"The devil had already put it into the heart of Judas" (v2).** The Greek is literally that the devil "had already thrown into the heart" that Judas should betray him. Satan prompts, and Judas consents. Luke 22:3 says Satan entered Judas before he went to the priests. John will say it again at the table (v27). Some manuscripts read "supper being over," but the better reading is "during supper," and v26 shows the meal was still going on.
- **"Knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands" (v3).** John sets the scene with Jesus' full awareness of his authority and origin. He does not wash feet because he has forgotten who he is. He washes feet because he knows exactly who he is.
- **"Laid aside his outer garments… taking a towel" (v4).** The verb "laid aside" (*tithēmi*) is the same one Jesus used for laying down his life (10:11, 17–18), and in v12 he "took" (*lambanō*) his garments again, the word for taking up his life again (10:17–18). John seems to want the reader to see the cross in the washing: the Lord lays aside his glory, stoops to serve and cleanses, then takes it up again (Phil 2:6–8).
- **"Began to wash the disciples' feet" (v5).** Roads were dust and animal dung, and people wore sandals. A host provided water, and the washing was done by the lowest household slave. Jewish writings said a Hebrew slave should not even be required to do it. Luke 22:24 records that at this same meal the disciples argued about who was the greatest. No one had volunteered for the basin.
- **"Lord, do you wash my feet?" (v6).** In Greek the pronouns sit side by side for emphasis: "You, my feet?" Peter feels how upside down this is.
- **"What I am doing you do not understand now, but afterward you will understand" (v7).** "Afterward" points past the cross and resurrection and the Spirit's coming (2:22; 12:16; 14:26). The washing is a picture whose meaning the cross will explain.
- **"If I do not wash you, you have no share with me" (v8).** "Share" (*meros*) means a part or portion, like an inheritance share. Jesus moves from water to salvation. To refuse his washing is to refuse him. The pride that will not be served by Jesus is as fatal as the pride that will not serve.
- **"Not my feet only but also my hands and my head!" (v9).** Peter swings from refusal to excess. Typical Peter: wholehearted, and still missing the point.
- **"The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet" (v10).** Two different verbs: *louō*, to bathe the whole body, and *niptō*, to wash part of it. A guest bathed at home and then, walking to the dinner, needed only his feet rinsed. Most readers have seen a spiritual picture here: the one who has been made clean through Christ does not need to be saved again, but does need ongoing cleansing from the dirt of daily sin (1 John 1:7–9). Some manuscripts lack "except for his feet," which would make the saying simply "the bathed person is clean all over," but most scholars and the ESV keep the phrase.
- **"But not every one of you" (vv10–11).** Judas had his feet washed too. Jesus knelt before the man who would sell him, washed him, and still offered him the warning.

- **"Do you understand what I have done to you?" (v12).** Jesus dresses again, returns to his place and becomes Teacher. He has given them a living parable and now explains one layer of it.
- **"You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right" (v13).** Jesus does not lower his status to make the point. He keeps it. The lesson depends on the fact that the highest one has done the lowest job.
- **"You also ought to wash one another's feet" (v14).** Christians have read this two ways. Some churches (some Anabaptist, Brethren and Pentecostal groups, and the Maundy Thursday rite in Catholic and other liturgical churches) practice foot washing as an ordinance. Most Protestants take it as a command to the kind of humble, costly service the act pictured. Either way the substance is service. 1 Timothy 5:10 lists "has washed the feet of the saints" among a widow's good works, which shows it was practiced literally as hospitality.
- **"I have given you an example" (v15).** The word is *hypodeigma*, a pattern or model to copy. Peter later writes the same about Christ's suffering: he left "an example, so that you might follow in his steps" (1 Pet 2:21).
- **"A servant is not greater than his master" (v16).** A common saying that Jesus used in several settings (15:20; Matt 10:24). If the master stooped, the servant has no position too high for stooping.
- **"Blessed are you if you do them" (v17).** Knowing is not the same as doing. This is the only "blessed" saying in John's Gospel apart from 20:29.
- **"He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me" (v18).** The quotation is Psalm 41:9, where David laments a close friend's betrayal, probably Ahithophel's during Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 15:12, 31). Eating together was a bond of loyalty in the ancient Near East. "Lifting the heel" pictures a horse rearing to kick, or someone turning his back in contempt. Ahithophel hanged himself after his betrayal (2 Sam 17:23), as Judas would (Matt 27:5).
- **"That when it does take place you may believe that I am he" (v19).** "I am he" is literally "I am" (*egō eimi*), the phrase that echoes God's self-name (Exod 3:14; Isa 43:10, where God says he speaks beforehand so people "may know and believe me and understand that I am he"). Jesus' foreknowledge of the betrayal is evidence of who he is, not a sign that he lost control.
- **"Whoever receives the one I send receives me" (v20).** The betrayer is one of the sent ones, but his failure does not cancel the mission. The others will go out carrying Christ's own authority.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the cross casting its shadow over almost every phrase here, though it is never named, and stresses that Jesus served from full knowledge of his authority. He also draws out that humility includes letting Jesus serve us, which Peter resisted. JFB sees two cleansings, a once-for-all washing that makes a person clean and the daily washing of the "feet," which it ties to the Lord's Prayer's daily request for forgiveness. JFB says Judas "never experienced that cleansing at all which made the others what they were." Henry: "Christ washed his disciples' feet that he might give a proof of that great love wherewith he loved them." On vv12–20, Guzik argues that foot washing here is a lifestyle of service, not a ceremony, and warns against ritual foot washing that ignores real relationships. JFB likewise reads it as practical humility and criticizes pope and emperor washing beggars' feet as an empty show. JFB also argues that Judas was present for the Lord's Supper, a question the Gospels leave unclear. Henry notes that "there is a mixture of bad with good in the best societies, a Judas among the apostles."

#### One of You Will Betray Me (vv21–30)
- **"Jesus was troubled in his spirit" (v21).** The verb *tarassō* again, as at Lazarus's tomb (11:33) and in 12:27. Betrayal by a friend genuinely grieved him.
- **"The disciples looked at one another" (v22).** No one pointed at Judas. He had hidden his heart so well that the others suspected themselves first (Mark 14:19: "Is it I?").
- **"One of his disciples, whom Jesus loved" (v23).** This is the first appearance of "the disciple whom Jesus loved." He appears at the cross (19:26), the empty tomb (20:2), and by the lake (21:7, 20). John 21:24 says he wrote the Gospel. Church tradition from the second century identifies him as John son of Zebedee, who is never named in this Gospel. Some modern scholars suggest Lazarus or another disciple, but John remains the most likely.
- **"Reclining at table at Jesus' side" (v23).** Literally "in Jesus' bosom." Diners lay on couches around a U-shaped table, leaning on the left elbow. The person on Jesus' right would have his head near Jesus' chest and could lean back to speak privately (v25). The same phrase describes the Son "at the Father's side" in 1:18. As the Son makes the Father known, this disciple makes the Son known.
- **"Simon Peter motioned to him" (v24).** Peter was not close enough to whisper. The detail fits an eyewitness.
- **"It is he to whom I will give this morsel" (v26).** A host would dip a piece of bread in the common dish and hand it to a guest as a mark of honor (compare Ruth 2:14). That means Judas was probably seated close to Jesus, perhaps on his left, the place of honor. Jesus' last gesture to Judas is friendship. It is both an identification and a final appeal.
- **"After he had taken the morsel, Satan entered into him" (v27).** Judas takes the bread and chooses. What Satan had "put into his heart" (v2) now takes over. The text holds both sides: Judas is responsible, and he becomes Satan's instrument.
- **"What you are going to do, do quickly" (v27).** Jesus is not compelling Judas. He is releasing him to the choice already made, and showing that the timing of the hour is in his hands, not Satan's.
- **"Buy what we need for the feast… give something to the poor" (v29).** It was customary to give alms on Passover night, and the festival had more meals to come. The disciples' guesses show how ordinary Judas still looked.
- **"He immediately went out. And it was night" (v30).** Four words in Greek. It is the time of day, since the Passover meal was eaten after sunset. But in John it means far more. Jesus is the light of the world (8:12), and "the light shines in the darkness" (1:5). Judas leaves the light and walks into the dark (11:10; 12:35).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the morsel as a mark of honor and Jesus' last offer of love and repentance, and he reads Judas as a warning that nearness to Jesus and every opportunity do not by themselves produce faith. JFB reads the sop as "a last offer of friendship" and closes the scene with a line worth keeping: there was a "far blacker night in the soul of Judas than in the sky over his head." Henry warns that "many are made worse by the gifts of Christ's bounty," as Judas was by the bread.

#### A New Commandment (vv31–35)
- **"When he had gone out, Jesus said, 'Now'" (v31).** With Judas gone, the events that lead to the cross are moving. Jesus speaks of the cross as glory already present. The word "glorify" appears five times in two verses. The Son is glorified in his obedient death, the Father is glorified in the Son, and the Father will glorify the Son "at once," in the resurrection and exaltation.
- **This begins the Farewell Discourse.** In the Old Testament, great leaders gave farewell speeches before death, gathering their people, comforting them, urging obedience and blessing them (Jacob in Gen 49, Moses in Deuteronomy, Joshua in Josh 23–24, David in 1 Chr 28–29). Jesus' words in chs 13–17 follow that pattern, but with a difference: he speaks of his death as going to the Father, and he promises to come back.
- **"Little children" (v33).** The tender word *teknia* is found only here in the Gospels. John later uses it again and again in his letters (1 John 2:1, 12, 28). Jesus speaks like a father leaving his family.
- **"Where I am going you cannot come" (v33).** He had said this to his opponents (7:34; 8:21) as a warning. To his disciples it is a temporary parting (v36; 14:3).
- **"A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another" (v34).** Love for neighbor was not new (Lev 19:18). Two things are. "New" (*kainos*) means new in kind, fresh, not merely recent. The new thing is the standard: "just as I have loved you," a love just shown with a towel and soon to be shown with a cross. And it is the love of a new community, shaped by what he has done for them. The Latin for "commandment," *mandatum*, gives Maundy Thursday its name.
- **"By this all people will know that you are my disciples" (v35).** The identifying mark of Christ's followers is not a badge, a doctrine or a gift. It is love for one another, visible to outsiders. Around AD 200 Tertullian reported pagans saying of Christians, "See how they love one another."
- **Commentators:** Guzik takes "new" to mean fresh rather than newly invented and says the newness is the self-giving love Jesus displayed. JFB agrees, calling it the old moral law "in a new and peculiar form," with Christ's love as the measure. JFB also notes that Judas's departure seemed to free Jesus to speak openly. Henry: "Brotherly love is the badge of Christ's disciples."

#### Jesus Foretells Peter's Denial (vv36–38)
- **"Lord, where are you going?" (v36).** Peter skips past the love command and fixes on the departure. He wants to go too.
- **"You cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterward" (v36).** Jesus has to go alone to the cross to make the way. But this is also a promise. Peter will follow later, and John 21:18–19 shows that he did, all the way to death. Tradition holds that Peter was crucified in Rome under Nero, about AD 64–67.
- **"I will lay down my life for you" (v37).** Peter uses the same words Jesus used of the Good Shepherd laying down his life for the sheep (10:11). Peter has it reversed. The Shepherd must lay down his life for Peter before Peter can lay his down for the Shepherd.
- **"Will you lay down your life for me?" (v38).** Jesus repeats Peter's words back to him, gently and with a sad irony.
- **"The rooster will not crow till you have denied me three times" (v38).** All four Gospels record this warning (Matt 26:34; Mark 14:30; Luke 22:34). Luke adds that Jesus had prayed that Peter's faith would not fail, and that when he turned again he would strengthen his brothers (Luke 22:32). John shows the denial in 18:15–27, and Jesus' threefold question by the lake, "Do you love me?" (21:15–17), answers it. Judas and Peter both fail Jesus on the same night. One goes out into the night and does not come back. The other weeps and is restored.
- **Commentators:** JFB hears "deep though affectionate irony" in Jesus' reply. Guzik contrasts Peter's emotional confidence with the later faith resting on Christ's finished work and the Spirit's power, and warns that feelings cannot carry the Christian life. Henry draws the lesson that Peter was right in principle, since "Christ should be dearer to us than our own lives," but wrong to trust his own strength to prove it.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter opens with "he loved them to the end" and shows what that love does. The Lord who holds all things in his hands lays down his garments, kneels with a towel and washes feet, including the feet of the man who would betray him and the man who would deny him. The washing is a picture of the cross: the Son lays aside his glory, makes the unclean clean and takes up his life again. No one has a share in him without being washed by him, and no one who has been washed is left without an example. His love becomes the measure of the new commandment, and it holds even when Judas walks out into the night and Peter's boasts collapse.
