*Setting:* The title reads "To the choirmaster: according to Muth-labben. A Psalm of David." *Muth-labben* means something like "death of the son," and was probably the name of a well-known tune, now lost. Older guesses linked it to Goliath, the champion who died before Israel, or to the death of one of David's sons, but none can be proved. No occasion is given, though the psalm celebrates victory over hostile nations, which fits the years when David subdued Israel's neighbors (2 Sam 8). Psalm 9 has a close twin. Psalms 9 and 10 together form a rough acrostic, with pairs of lines beginning with successive letters of the Hebrew alphabet: Psalm 9 runs roughly through the first half of the alphabet and Psalm 10 the second, with some letters missing or out of order. Psalm 10 also has no title, unusual in this part of the Psalter. The ancient Greek and Latin translations print them as one psalm, which is why Catholic and Orthodox Bibles number the psalms one lower from here to Psalm 147. Psalm 9 leans toward thanks for what God has done; Psalm 10 leans toward lament over what he has not yet done. The opening "I will give thanks... I will sing praise to your name, O Most High" (vv1–2) picks up the last line of Psalm 7 almost word for word.

#### With My Whole Heart (vv1–2)
- **"I will give thanks to the LORD with my whole heart" (v1).** "Whole" means undivided. Praise that is half-hearted is not really praise at all. The "heart" in Hebrew is the center of the person, thought, will and affection together.
- **"I will recount all of your wonderful deeds" (v1).** "Wonderful deeds" (*niphla'ot*) are things beyond human ability, works only God could do: the plagues, the Red Sea, the conquest (Exod 3:20; Ps 78:4, 11–12). Praise in the Psalms is not vague. It tells stories. Retelling what God has done is how one generation passes faith to the next (78:4), and forgetting it is how a generation drifts away (78:11).
- **"I will be glad and exult in you" (v2).** Four "I will" statements in two verses: give thanks, recount, be glad, sing praise. The joy is "in you," in God himself, not only in his gifts.
- **"I will sing praise to your name, O Most High" (v2).** "Most High" (*Elyon*) links straight back to 7:17, where David promised to do exactly this. Psalm 7 ended with a vow, and Psalm 9 opens by keeping it.
- **Commentators:** Henry urges praise "with our hearts, and not only with our lips," and "with our whole heart." JFB: "Heartfelt gratitude will find utterance." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line, "Half heart is no heart," and draws out the four parts of praise here: whole-hearted thanks, telling God's works, gladness and singing.

#### You Have Maintained My Just Cause (vv3–6)
- **"When my enemies turn back, they stumble and perish before your presence" (v3).** The enemies retreat not because of David's army but "before your presence," literally "from before your face." The same face that shines on the righteous (4:6) undoes the wicked.
- **"You have maintained my just cause" (v4).** Psalm 7 asked God to judge David's case. Here David thanks him for doing it. The language is courtroom language: "my judgment and my cause," God taking up the side of the one in the right.
- **"You have sat on the throne, giving righteous judgment" (v4).** In Israel the king was the highest judge (1 Kings 3:16–28). David, Israel's king, looks past his own throne to God's.
- **"You have rebuked the nations" (v5).** God's "rebuke" is not a mild scolding. It is the word that dries up the sea (106:9) and scatters armies (76:6). He made the world by speaking, and he can unmake a nation by speaking (2:5).
- **"You have blotted out their name forever and ever" (v5).** In the ancient world a name lived on through children, monuments and records. To have one's name erased was to be utterly gone. The phrase echoes God's sentence on Amalek: "I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven" (Exod 17:14). The contrast with Psalm 8 is deliberate: God's name is majestic in all the earth, while the names of those who oppose him disappear.
- **"The enemy came to an end in everlasting ruins... the very memory of them has perished" (v6).** Ancient cities were often destroyed and rebuilt on the same mound. These will not be. History bears this out: great empires that once threatened Israel, such as Assyria and Babylon, are now ruins dug up by archaeologists.
- **Commentators:** JFB says God rebukes "by acts as well as words" and of the enemy's cities, "their ruins shall never be repaired." Guzik insists God is not neutral between right and wrong, so the real question is not whether God is on our side but whether we are on his. Henry: "God sits in the throne of judgment," and apparent injustices in this life should drive believers to trust the final judgment.

#### The LORD Sits Enthroned Forever (vv7–10)
- **"But the LORD sits enthroned forever" (v7).** The hinge of the psalm. Enemies end; the LORD remains. "Sits" is the posture of a king on his throne. Nothing about him is temporary.
- **"He has established his throne for justice" (v7).** The purpose of God's throne is not just power but justice (89:14; 97:2).
- **"He judges the world with righteousness; he judges the peoples with uprightness" (v8).** "World" (*tevel*) means the whole inhabited earth. Paul quotes the language of this verse at Athens: God "has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed," and proved it by raising him from the dead (Acts 17:31). Psalms 96:13 and 98:9 repeat it. What David saw God doing in his own day is a preview of the last day.
- **"The LORD is a stronghold for the oppressed, a stronghold in times of trouble" (v9).** "Stronghold" (*misgav*) is a high place, a secure height out of reach of attackers (18:2; 46:7, 11, "fortress"). "Oppressed" (*dak*) means crushed, beaten down. The King who rules the world is also the safe place for the crushed.
- **"Those who know your name put their trust in you" (v10).** To "know God's name" is to know what he is like, his character as he has shown it. Trust grows from knowledge. People trust a friend they know and a God they know. That is why recounting his deeds (v1) matters: it feeds trust.
- **"For you, O LORD, have not forsaken those who seek you" (v10).** Believers sometimes *feel* forsaken (22:1), and the psalms do not hide it. But this is the settled record: God has never finally abandoned anyone who seeks him (37:25, 28; Heb 13:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry says God is a "refuge for the oppressed," so no real harm will finally come to them even when he does not act at once, and that the better God is known, the more he is trusted. JFB calls him "a sure refuge" for the oppressed and those who know him. Guzik explains "knowing his name" as knowing his character through what he has done, and quotes F. B. Meyer that knowing God takes "deep, long thoughts" of his nearness, not hurried visits.

#### He Does Not Forget the Cry of the Afflicted (vv11–12)
- **"Sing praises to the LORD, who sits enthroned in Zion!" (v11).** Now David calls others to join. The God enthroned "forever" (v7) has also chosen to dwell in a place, Zion, the hill in Jerusalem where David brought the ark (2 Sam 6). Heaven's King has an address on earth.
- **"Tell among the peoples his deeds!" (v11).** Israel's praise was meant to be heard by the nations. This is missionary language long before the Great Commission (96:3; 105:1).
- **"For he who avenges blood is mindful of them" (v12).** "He who avenges blood" is literally "the one who seeks blood," the one who demands an account for murder. God told Noah, "From his fellow man I will require a reckoning for the life of man" (Gen 9:5), and Abel's blood cried out to God from the ground (Gen 4:10). "Mindful" is *zakar*, the same verb as 8:4: the God who remembers frail humanity also remembers the murdered and oppressed.
- **"He does not forget the cry of the afflicted" (v12).** This is why believers do not take revenge. They leave it to the God who never forgets (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19). There is a textual wrinkle in the word "afflicted": the written Hebrew reads *aniyyim*, the afflicted or poor, and the traditional reading notes give *anavim*, the humble. The two words are close in meaning and in the Psalms often overlap: the poor and the humble are usually the same people.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "blood" as murders and "all oppression" of God's people, and says God "will avenge their cause." Guzik traces the "avenger of blood" idea through Abel (Gen 4), the cities of refuge (Num 35) and Jezebel's end (2 Kings 9), and passes on Spurgeon's comfort that God keeps a record of persecuted saints.

#### From the Gates of Death to the Gates of Zion (vv13–14)
- **"Be gracious to me, O LORD! See my affliction from those who hate me" (v13).** Suddenly the psalm turns to prayer. Victory has been won, but trouble is not over. This is honest. Believers live between God's past acts and his final victory, and both thanks and pleas belong in the same song.
- **"O you who lift me up from the gates of death" (v13).** Ancient cities were walled, and the gate was the place of power and decision. "The gates of death" pictures death as a city that swallows people (107:18; Isa 38:10; Job 38:17). Jesus used the same picture when he said "the gates of hell shall not prevail" against his church (Matt 16:18).
- **"That in the gates of the daughter of Zion I may rejoice in your salvation" (v14).** Two sets of gates face each other: the gates of death and the gates of Zion. "Daughter of Zion" is a tender name for Jerusalem and its people. The city gate was where crowds gathered, so David wants to praise God in public, where everyone can hear. He wants to be rescued not merely to live, but to praise.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the gates of death may mean its "regions," and contrasts them with Zion's gates, while deliverance calls for "signal and public thanks." Guzik says rescue serves God's greater glory, not only the comfort of the rescued.

#### Snared in Their Own Work (vv15–18)
- **"The nations have sunk in the pit that they made" (v15).** The image from 7:15 returns, but now for whole nations. Pits were dug and hidden to trap animals, and nets laid on the ground to catch birds or game. Those who set traps for God's people end up in them.
- **"The LORD has made himself known; he has executed judgment" (v16).** God's judgments are a form of revelation. When evil collapses on itself, God is showing who he is. Pharaoh's army drowned in the sea it chased Israel into; Haman hanged on his own gallows (Esth 7:10).
- **"Higgaion. Selah" (v16).** *Higgaion* appears as a musical note only here. Elsewhere the word means a murmuring sound or meditation (19:14, "meditation"; 92:3, "melody"). Paired with *Selah*, it probably calls for a pause with a musical interlude, a moment to think about what has just been sung.
- **"The wicked shall return to Sheol, all the nations that forget God" (v17).** *Sheol* is the realm of the dead (see 6:5). "Return" may echo Genesis 3:19, "to dust you shall return." The KJV translates it "hell," which goes further than the Hebrew word itself, though the New Testament does teach a final judgment beyond death. The striking phrase is "nations that forget God." Their sin is described not as open rebellion but as forgetting. Moses warned Israel that forgetting the LORD in prosperity was the road to ruin (Deut 8:11–19).
- **"For the needy shall not always be forgotten, and the hope of the poor shall not perish forever" (v18).** Two kinds of forgetting meet: the wicked forget God, but God will not forget the needy. "Not always" and "not forever" are honest. For a time, the poor may *seem* forgotten. But their hope has an end date on its waiting, not on its fulfilment. Mary's song echoes this: God "has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52).
- **Commentators:** Guzik lists Haman, Joseph's brothers and Judas as people caught in their own schemes, but insists that this never excuses the evil they did. On the Higgaion pause he passes on Adam Clarke's comment that nothing a wicked man does is truly in his own interest. On v17 Guzik and Spurgeon both stress that forgetting God may look like a small sin but has eternal results. JFB reads "hell" here as God's vengeance driving the wicked "to the extreme of destruction," and explains the "needy" of v18 as the poor and miserable, humbled by affliction. JFB also says Higgaion means "meditation," which fits the lexicon.

#### Let the Nations Know They Are But Men (vv19–20)
- **"Arise, O LORD! Let not man prevail" (v19).** The battle cry of Numbers 10:35 and Psalms 3:7 and 7:6 returns. "Man" here is *enosh*, the same word for frail, mortal humanity as in 8:4. The psalm that praised God's care for weak man now prays that weak man not be allowed to play God.
- **"Let the nations be judged before you!" (v19).** "Before you," in your presence, echoes v3. The nations are not judged by Israel's standards but by God's.
- **"Put them in fear, O LORD! Let the nations know that they are but men!" (v20).** "Fear" could mean terror, or the reverent fear that leads to repentance. The prayer is that proud nations would learn the most basic fact about themselves: they are human, not divine. Every empire that has claimed godlike power has eventually had to learn it, from Pharaoh to Nebuchadnezzar, who was humbled until he knew "that the Most High rules the kingdom of men" (Dan 4:32).
- **The psalm does not end resolved.** Psalm 9 closes with a prayer, not a victory, and Psalm 10 opens with "Why, O LORD, do you stand far away?" The two psalms together hold thanksgiving and lament side by side.
- **Commentators:** JFB says God should make the nations see their frailty, which will end their pride. Guzik, quoting Trapp, calls prayer the church's weapon, and says that seeing human frailty clearly is what leads to depending on God.

#### Pointing to Christ
The God who "judges the world with righteousness" has appointed a man to do it, and Paul says he proved it by raising Jesus from the dead (Acts 17:31). Jesus is the King enthroned forever and the stronghold for the oppressed. He walked into the gates of death and came back out, holding "the keys of Death and Hades" (Rev 1:18), so that the gates of death cannot hold his people. He did not forget the cry of the afflicted: he took their side, then took their place. The hope of the poor will not perish forever, because the one who said "Blessed are you who are poor" (Luke 6:20) will return to make it true.
