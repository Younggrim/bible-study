*Setting:* Chapter 6 closed with the chorus calling the bride back, "Return, return, O Shulammite," and someone asking why they should watch her "as upon a dance before two armies" (6:13). In Hebrew Bibles that verse is 7:1, so the dance leads straight into this chapter. The man now describes her a third time (4:1–5; 6:4–9). This time he starts at her feet and works up, as one would watching a dancer, and the praise is the boldest in the Song. Then she answers with the fullest statement of belonging in the book, "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me" (v10), and invites him away to the countryside. For the main ways the Song is read, see the Setting of chapter 1. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song. It labels the speakers, and the headings below follow those exchanges.

#### How Beautiful Are Your Feet (vv1–5)
- **Why start with the feet?** In 4:1–5 he began with her eyes and moved down. Here he begins with her feet and moves up to her hair. The most natural explanation is the dance of 6:13: he is watching her move. Whether the dance is public, before the chorus, or private, for him alone, the text does not say, and Guzik weighs both. Either way this is poetry, not a report.
- **"How beautiful are your feet in sandals, O noble daughter!" (v1).** Sandals were a mark of a free person, not a slave, and fine sandals a mark of wealth. "Noble daughter" is literally "daughter of a prince" (*bat-nadiv*). The same word appears in "my kinsman, a prince" (6:12). It need not mean she was born royal. The Tyndale notes and Carr, cited by Guzik, read it as a title of honor: he sees her as noble in character, the equal of royalty. The girl who once said her own brothers made her work the vineyards (1:6) is now addressed as a princess.
- **"Your rounded thighs are like jewels, the work of a master hand" (v1).** "Rounded" means the curve of her hips as she moves. "A master hand" is a skilled craftsman's. The picture is of a piece of fine jewelry, shaped with care. Behind the craftsman stands the Creator who formed her (Ps 139:13–14).
- **"Your navel is a rounded bowl that never lacks mixed wine. Your belly is a heap of wheat, encircled with lilies" (v2).** This is intimate language, fitting between husband and wife. The Tyndale notes take it as her rounded belly or, more intimately, her lower body. Guzik doubts the second reading, since lilies have been used of her breasts (4:5) and his lips (5:13). Wine and wheat were the two staples of an Israelite table (Ps 104:15), so the picture is of a feast: she delights and satisfies him. A heap of wheat ringed with flowers was a familiar harvest sight, threshed grain piled on the threshing floor.
- **"Your two breasts are like two fawns, twins of a gazelle" (v3).** Almost exactly the words of 4:5. The image is soft, young and lovely, not crude.
- **"Your neck is like an ivory tower" (v4).** In 4:4 her neck was "like the tower of David," hung with shields, a picture of strength and dignity. Now it is ivory: smooth, pale and costly. Ivory was a luxury imported from far away (1 Kgs 10:18, 22).
- **"Your eyes are pools in Heshbon, by the gate of Bath-rabbim" (v4).** Heshbon was a city east of the Jordan, north-east of the Dead Sea. It was once the capital of Sihon king of the Amorites (Num 21:26), later held by Israel and then by Moab. Its large reservoirs were famous, and the remains of ancient pools can still be seen there. Bath-rabbim means "daughter of many," likely a city gate where crowds passed. Her eyes are deep, clear and calm, like still water by a busy gate.
- **"Your nose is like a tower of Lebanon, which looks toward Damascus" (v4).** To modern ears this sounds like an insult, but the point is not size. The Tyndale notes think the "tower" may be Mount Hermon, the high peak in the Lebanon range overlooking Damascus. A face that "looks toward" the north suggests poise and dignity. Carr, cited by Guzik, points out that "Lebanon" comes from a root meaning "white," which may point to color instead. JFB notes that the word could mean "face" as well as "nose."
- **"Your head crowns you like Carmel" (v5).** Mount Carmel is the ridge that juts into the Mediterranean above modern Haifa, green and wooded, the scene of Elijah's contest with the prophets of Baal (1 Kgs 18). The name means "garden land" or "orchard." Her head rises over her like Carmel over the coast.
- **"Your flowing locks are like purple; a king is held captive in the tresses" (v5).** Purple dye came from murex shellfish off the Phoenician coast and was so costly that it became the color of kings. Her hair may have been dark with a sheen, as JFB suggests, or simply royal in its beauty. "Tresses" (*rehatim*) is elsewhere the watering troughs where Jacob's flocks drank (Gen 30:38). Carr, cited by Guzik, notes the root means "to run" or "flow," so the picture is of rippling water. And the punch line: the king, who rules everyone, is caught, held prisoner by her hair. She who was praised as "awesome as an army" (6:4) has captured him without a battle.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that the woman is described three times in the Song and the man only once, and passes on Kinlaw's remark that this suggests a wife's need to hear she is beautiful. He reads the three descriptions as fitting three settings: the wedding night (ch 4), reconciliation after conflict (ch 6), and here a more public assurance. Guzik also cites Trapp, who read the navel and the heap of wheat as baptism and the Lord's Supper, as an example of allegory pressed too far. Henry reads the feet spiritually, the believer's walk: they "tread firmly, being well armed against the troubles they meet with in their way" (compare Eph 6:15). JFB likewise renders "feet" as "goings" and links them to Isaiah 52:7, "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news."

#### I Will Climb the Palm Tree (vv6–9a)
- **"How beautiful and pleasant you are, O loved one, with all your delights!" (v6).** He stops describing parts and speaks to her as a whole person. "O loved one" is literally "O love," as if she were love itself. "Delights" is a word for luxury and pleasure (Mic 2:9; Eccl 2:8), and Guzik reads it to include her character as well as her looks.
- **"Your stature is like a palm tree, and your breasts are like its clusters" (v7).** The date palm is tall, straight and graceful. In Hebrew the palm is *tamar*, and Tamar was a woman's name, given to beautiful women in David's family (2 Sam 13:1; 14:27). The palm also flourishes only where there is water, as JFB notes (Exod 15:27; Ps 92:12). JFB takes "clusters" to mean grapes, not dates, from the parallel "clusters of the vine" in v8.
- **"I say I will climb the palm tree and lay hold of its fruit" (v8).** Desire turns to resolve. To harvest dates, a man had to climb the tree. Here a husband speaks frankly of wanting his wife, and the Song treats that desire as good. This is the same delight Proverbs urges: "rejoice in the wife of your youth… let her breasts fill you at all times with delight" (Prov 5:18–19). Guzik stresses that this is right desire within marriage, not lust. Glickman, cited by Guzik, notes that the tone has moved from the careful formality of the wedding night to the easy freedom of a love that has matured.
- **"Oh may your breasts be like clusters of the vine, and the scent of your breath like apples, and your mouth like the best wine" (vv8–9).** "Apples" (*tappuach*) may be apples, apricots or quinces. Scholars are not sure which fruit is meant (see 2:3, 5). He longs for her breath to be sweet and her kisses intoxicating, echoing her own first words in the Song: "your love is better than wine" (1:2).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "The palm-tree is observed to flourish most when it is loaded," a picture of the believer who grows stronger under burdens. JFB takes the palm as a symbol of triumphant joy (John 12:13) and the vine as Christ himself (John 15:1). Guzik keeps to the plain sense of married love, and adds that Song and Proverbs together correct the idea that the Bible is embarrassed by physical love.

#### I Am My Beloved's (vv9b–10)
- **"It goes down smoothly for my beloved, gliding over lips and teeth" (v9).** She breaks in and finishes his sentence. He has just said, "your mouth like the best wine." She answers: that wine flows for him. It is a tender, playful interruption. The ESV's "lips and teeth" follows the ancient Greek and Syriac translations. The Hebrew has "lips of sleepers," which the KJV kept ("causing the lips of those that are asleep to speak"). The ESV's footnote gives this. Glickman, cited by Guzik, pictures the couple falling asleep with the taste of the last kiss lingering.
- **"I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me" (v10).** This is the third and fullest form of her refrain. In 2:16 she said, "My beloved is mine, and I am his." In 6:3, after the night of searching, she turned it around: "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine." Now she does not mention her possession of him at all. She rests entirely in belonging to him and being wanted by him. Glickman, cited by Guzik, puts it this way: she has lost herself in him and found herself.
- **"His desire" (v10).** The Hebrew word, *teshuqah*, occurs only three times in the Bible. In Genesis 3:16, after the fall, God tells the woman, "Your desire shall be contrary to your husband, but he shall rule over you." In Genesis 4:7 sin's "desire is contrary to" Cain, crouching to devour him. Here, the only other use, the desire runs the other way: from the man to the woman, and it is a gift, not a struggle. Many readers see the Song undoing, in a small way, the damage of the fall: love between man and woman as it was meant to be, with no grasping and no ruling over. The Hebrew of Genesis 3:16 is debated, and the ESV's "contrary to" is one reading of it, but the echo is deliberate whichever way that verse is read.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "I am my beloved's, not my own, but entirely devoted to him and owned by him." JFB hears strong assurance in "his desire is toward me," and takes the verse as spoken by the bride and the daughters together, now one company. Guzik passes on Morgan's description of the line as complete surrender met by complete satisfaction.

#### Come, My Beloved (vv11–13)
- **"Come, my beloved, let us go out into the fields and lodge in the villages" (v11).** In 2:10–13 he invited her: "Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away," for spring had come. Now she invites him, in almost the same words. The woman who once felt she had to hide her sunburned face (1:5–6) is secure enough to lead. Guzik notes this shows that the wife, too, may take the initiative in married love. "Villages" may be small farm settlements, or, as some read the word, henna bushes (the same word as in 1:14; 4:13), which would make it "lodge among the henna blossoms."
- **"Let us go out early to the vineyards and see whether the vines have budded" (v12).** This repeats almost exactly the words of 6:11. The vines, grape blossoms and pomegranates are signs of spring and of love ready to be enjoyed (2:13, 15). Glickman, cited by Guzik, sees the relationship come full circle through the seasons and begin again.
- **"There I will give you my love" (v12).** She is frank and unashamed. The word for "love" here (*dodim*) is plural, the word for caresses and love-making, as in 1:2 and 4:10. A married woman offering herself to her husband is honored here, not hushed.
- **"The mandrakes give forth fragrance" (v13).** The mandrake is a low Mediterranean plant with a strong scent, yellow-orange fruit, and a forked root that can look like a human body. In the ancient world it was believed to arouse love and help women conceive. That is why Rachel bargained with Leah for Reuben's mandrakes (Gen 30:14–16). The Hebrew *duda'im* sounds like *dodi*, "my beloved," and *dodim*, "love," a wordplay English cannot keep. The Tyndale notes point it out, and JFB too derives the word from "a root meaning 'to love.'" Estes, cited by Guzik, sees in the mention of mandrakes a hope for children.
- **"Beside our doors are all choice fruits, new as well as old, which I have laid up for you" (v13).** Fruit was stored in the house for the winter. She has saved her best for him. "New as well as old" suggests both the familiar joys of their marriage and fresh ones still to come. The Tyndale notes and Guzik read it that way. Guzik notes the phrase is hard to translate with certainty.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the vineyards as the believer's own soul: "Our own souls are our vineyards," to be watched and tended. On v13 he hears an echo of Jesus' saying about the scribe trained for the kingdom, who brings out of his treasure "what is new and what is old" (Matt 13:52): there is "grace for all occasions, new and old, as the good householder has in his treasury." JFB connects the going out to the villages with the scattering of the disciples after Stephen's death, preaching as they went (Acts 8:4), and adds on v12 that real assurance of love leads to diligence, not laziness. Guzik cites Estes: the Song shows that freedom in love is found within marriage, which protects love as it grows.

#### Pointing to Christ
"A king is held captive" (v5). The Song pictures a king who rules everyone, undone by love for his bride. The gospel goes further. The King of kings bound himself by love, not to a bride who was already lovely but to one he would make lovely, "that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle" (Eph 5:25–27). The bride's last word on belonging is "his desire is for me" (v10). Christians can say that of Christ with full confidence. He prayed, "Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am" (John 17:24). And the word *teshuqah*, last heard in Genesis describing the curse on love between man and woman, here describes love set free. That is the direction of the whole Bible story: what the fall broke, Christ comes to restore.
