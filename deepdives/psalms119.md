*Setting:* Psalm 119 is the longest psalm and the longest chapter in the Bible: 176 verses. It comes straight after the Passover songs (Psalms 113–118) and just before the Songs of Ascents (120–134). It is an acrostic built with great care. There are twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and in Hebrew every one of the eight lines in a stanza begins with that stanza's letter. The ESV prints the letter names, Aleph to Taw, as headings. The effect is a celebration of God's word "from A to Z." Its author is not named. Older commentators often credited David, writing over a lifetime. Many modern scholars place it after the exile, near the time of Ezra, when the people were rebuilding their life around the law (Ezra 7:10). The writer is not young (v100), is under pressure from proud and powerful opponents (vv23, 161), has been afflicted (vv67, 71) and is utterly devoted to God. Nearly every verse names God's word with one of eight Hebrew terms: *torah* ("law," instruction), *'edot* ("testimonies," God's witness to himself), *piqqudim* ("precepts," charges given by an overseer), *chuqqim* ("statutes," things engraved and fixed), *mitsvot* ("commandments"), *mishpatim* ("rules," judgments or rulings), *dabar* ("word") and *'imrah* ("promise," a spoken word). They overlap and are best read as eight facets of one thing. And the psalm is not finally about a book. It is addressed to God almost throughout. Its love for the word is love for the God who speaks. The ESV gives the letter headings only. The phrases added below follow each stanza.

#### Aleph: Blessed Are the Blameless (vv1–8)
- **"Blessed are those whose way is blameless" (v1).** The psalm opens like Psalm 1, with *'ashre*, "how happy." "Blameless" (*tamim*) means whole or complete, a life all of one piece, not sinless perfection. Such people "seek him with their whole heart" (v2). "Whole heart" recurs six times in the psalm (vv2, 10, 34, 58, 69, 145).
- **"Oh that my ways may be steadfast" (v5).** After describing the ideal, the writer sighs: he is not there yet. The whole psalm lives in that gap between longing and attainment, and it closes the stanza with "do not utterly forsake me!" (v8).
- **Commentators:** JFB defines "undefiled" (KJV) as "literally, 'complete,' perfect, or sincere." Henry says "the pardoning mercy of God in Christ, is the only source of a sinner's happiness." Guzik argues that the holy life is the truly happy life, against the idea that holiness is dull.

#### Beth: How Can a Young Man Keep His Way Pure? (vv9–16)
- **"How can a young man keep his way pure?" (v9).** The answer is not willpower but "guarding it according to your word." Youth is the season of strong desires and big decisions (Prov 7:6–23; Eccl 12:1).
- **"I have stored up your word in my heart" (v11).** "Stored up" (*tsaphan*) means to hide away as treasure. A word kept within is there when temptation comes. Jesus answered the devil three times from Deuteronomy (Matt 4:1–11).
- **Lips, delight, meditation (vv13–16).** The word is spoken aloud, valued "as much as in all riches," pondered, and remembered.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God's word is treasure worth laying up." JFB sums up the stanza as treasuring the word, speaking it, meditating on it and delighting in it. Guzik treats "storing up" as memorizing Scripture, a guard against sin.

#### Gimel: Open My Eyes (vv17–24)
- **"Open my eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of your law" (v18).** The problem is not the text but the reader. "Open" (*gal*) means to uncover, as a veil is drawn back. Paul speaks of a veil lifted when one turns to the Lord (2 Cor 3:14–16), and the risen Jesus "opened their minds to understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:45).
- **"I am a sojourner on the earth" (v19).** A sojourner was a resident foreigner without land or rights (39:12; Heb 11:13). A stranger needs directions, so he asks God not to hide his commands.
- **"Princes sit plotting against me" (v23).** Powerful people oppose him. He answers by meditating, and God's testimonies become "my counselors" (v24), literally "the men of my counsel."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Every sanctified soul hungers after the word of God." Guzik, citing Bridges, says the need is for opened eyes, not a new Bible, and passes on Boice's point that the believer does not feel at home in this world.

#### Daleth: My Soul Clings to the Dust (vv25–32)
- **"My soul clings to the dust; give me life" (v25).** Dust is the place of death and mourning (Gen 3:19; 22:15). "Give me life" (*chayyeni*) is the psalm's great prayer, asked nine times (vv25, 37, 40, 88, 107, 149, 154, 156, 159). It means "revive me," "keep me alive."
- **"My soul melts away for sorrow" (v28).** Literally it "drips" with grief, like a leaking roof.
- **"I will run… when you enlarge my heart!" (v32).** He clings to the dust (v25), clings to God's testimonies (v31) and then runs. An enlarged heart is one God has set free from its cramped fears.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the depressed believer submits to God, who revives him through his promises. Guzik, following Spurgeon, notes the move from lying in the dust to running, and with Bridges says a heart enlarged by God makes obedience possible.

#### He: Teach Me the Way (vv33–40)
- **"Teach me… give me understanding… lead me… incline my heart… turn my eyes" (vv33–37).** In Hebrew the letter He begins many causative verbs, so nearly every line is a "cause me to" prayer. Obedience is asked for, not assumed.
- **"Incline my heart to your testimonies, and not to selfish gain!" (v36).** The danger named is greed. "Selfish gain" (*betsa'*) means profit taken dishonestly (1 Sam 8:3; Prov 1:19).
- **"Turn my eyes from looking at worthless things" (v37).** "Worthless" (*shav'*) means empty or vain. What the eyes linger on, the heart follows (Josh 7:21; Matt 6:22–23).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The sin here prayed against is covetousness." JFB says that when we pray for help to keep God's way, we may trust that he will give it. Guzik lists Bible figures ruined by covetousness, from Achan and Gehazi to Judas and Ananias.

#### Waw: I Shall Walk in a Wide Place (vv41–48)
- **"Let your steadfast love come to me… your salvation" (v41).** *Chesed* and salvation come "according to your promise." The writer leans on what God has said, which gives him "an answer for him who taunts me" (v42).
- **"I shall walk in a wide place" (v45).** "Wide place" means freedom. Obeying God feels like open country, not a cell. James calls God's word "the perfect law, the law of liberty" (Jas 1:25).
- **"I will also speak of your testimonies before kings" (v46).** Compare Daniel before Nebuchadnezzar and Paul before Agrippa (Acts 26).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The service of sin is slavery; the service of God is liberty." Guzik, with Spurgeon, calls holiness the King's highway, not a slave's track.

#### Zayin: This Is My Comfort in My Affliction (vv49–56)
- **"Remember your word to your servant, in which you have made me hope" (v49).** He holds God to his own promise. That is not presumption. It is faith.
- **"Your statutes have been my songs in the house of my sojourning" (v54).** Travelers sang on the road. For this pilgrim, God's word is the music of the journey.
- **"I remember your name in the night" (v55).** Night is when fears grow loud. He fills it with God's name.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The word of God speaks comfort in affliction." JFB glosses "horror" (v53) as burning indignation at the wicked. Guzik urges praying God's promises back to him, as Spurgeon did with a book of promises he carried.

#### Heth: The LORD Is My Portion (vv57–64)
- **"The LORD is my portion" (v57).** When Israel divided the land, the priests got no territory. God himself was their share (Num 18:20). The writer claims the same.
- **"When I think on my ways, I turn my feet" (v59).** Reflection leads to turning, and turning to speed: "I hasten and do not delay" (v60). The prodigal "came to himself" and went home (Luke 15:17–20).
- **"At midnight I rise to praise you" (v62).** Paul and Silas sang at midnight in prison (Acts 16:25). The stanza ends looking outward: "The earth, O LORD, is full of your steadfast love" (v64).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "True believers take the Lord for the portion of their inheritance." JFB links v59 to the prodigal son. Guzik calls v59 the turning point of a life.

#### Teth: It Is Good for Me That I Was Afflicted (vv65–72)
- **"You have dealt well" (v65).** Five lines begin with *tov*, "good" (vv65, 66, 68, 71, 72). The stanza is a meditation on God's goodness: "You are good and do good" (v68).
- **"Before I was afflicted I went astray" (v67); "it is good for me that I was afflicted" (v71).** Suffering is called good, not because pain is pleasant but because it brought him back (Heb 12:10–11).
- **"Their heart is unfeeling like fat" (v70).** Literally "gross like fat," dull and insensitive (Isa 6:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Many have knowledge, but little judgment." JFB calls affliction a "wholesome discipline." Guzik notes Boice's point that the stanza's letter begins the word *tov*.

#### Yodh: Your Hands Have Made Me (vv73–80)
- **"Your hands have made and fashioned me" (v73).** The Maker knows how his creature works, so he asks the Maker for understanding (139:13–16).
- **"In faithfulness you have afflicted me" (v75).** The hardship was not a lapse in God's care but an expression of it.
- **"Let those who fear you turn to me" (vv74, 79).** His perseverance encourages others. One believer's endurance strengthens the whole community.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God made us to serve him, and enjoy him." JFB reasons that since God made the psalmist, he can best guide him. Guzik, citing Horne, says the Creator has a stake in what he made.

#### Kaph: Like a Wineskin in the Smoke (vv81–88)
- **"My soul longs… my eyes long… When will you comfort me?" (vv81–82).** This is the low point of the psalm. "Longs" (*kalah*) means to waste away, to be spent with longing.
- **"Like a wineskin in the smoke" (v83).** Skins hung in a smoky tent became blackened, dry and shriveled. He feels dried out and useless, "yet I have not forgotten your statutes."
- **"They have almost made an end of me" (v87).** Even so, "all your commandments are sure" (v86).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The surest token of God's good-will toward us, is his good work in us." JFB says faith must hold on even when the eyes fail. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line that such trials are smoke, but not fire.

#### Lamedh: Your Word Is Firmly Fixed in the Heavens (vv89–96)
- **"Forever, O LORD, your word is firmly fixed in the heavens" (v89).** "Firmly fixed" (*nitsav*) means set up, standing firm. Heaven and earth stand by God's appointment (vv90–91), and his word is steadier still (Isa 40:8; Matt 24:35).
- **"If your law had not been my delight, I would have perished" (v92).** The word is not a hobby. It kept him alive.
- **"I have seen a limit to all perfection, but your commandment is exceedingly broad" (v96).** Every human achievement has an edge. God's word has no edge.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The word of the Lord reaches to all cases, to all times." JFB compares the word's permanence to the settled heavens. Guzik, with Boice, notes how often rulers tried to destroy the Bible and failed.

#### Mem: Oh How I Love Your Law! (vv97–104)
- **"Oh how I love your law! It is my meditation all the day" (v97).** The most famous line of the psalm. Love shows itself in attention.
- **Wiser than enemies, teachers, elders (vv98–100).** This is not boasting about intelligence. Even teachers and elders are second-hand sources. God's word is first-hand.
- **"How sweet are your words to my taste, sweeter than honey" (v103).** Compare 19:10 and Ezekiel eating the scroll that tasted like honey (Ezek 3:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "What we love, we love to think of." Guzik, with Spurgeon, urges love for the whole Bible, not only favorite parts.

#### Nun: A Lamp to My Feet (vv105–112)
- **"Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path" (v105).** An ancient clay oil lamp lit only a few steps ahead. God's word usually shows the next step, not the whole road.
- **"I hold my life in my hand continually" (v109).** An idiom for constant danger (Judg 12:3; 1 Sam 19:5).
- **"Your testimonies are my heritage forever" (v111).** He may own no land, but the word is his inheritance.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "The lamp of the Word is not the sun," a light for now until full day comes. Henry: "The word of God directs us in our work and way." Guzik, with Bridges, says the Spirit must light the lamp for it to shine.

#### Samekh: You Are My Hiding Place (vv113–120)
- **"I hate the double-minded" (v113).** "Double-minded" (*se'aphim*) means divided, wavering people (1 Kings 18:21; Jas 1:8).
- **"You are my hiding place and my shield" (v114).** The refuge is God himself, not just his word (32:7; 91:1–2).
- **"My flesh trembles for fear of you" (v120).** Love for God and awe of God go together.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the double-minded as "divided men." Henry: "Our holy security is grounded on Divine supports." Guzik, with Spurgeon, stresses that the psalmist credits God himself as his defense.

#### Ayin: It Is Time for the LORD to Act (vv121–128)
- **"Give your servant a pledge of good" (v122).** "Pledge" (*'arav*) means to stand surety, to guarantee someone's debt, as Judah did for Benjamin (Gen 43:9). He asks God himself to be his guarantor. Hebrews calls Jesus "the guarantor of a better covenant" (Heb 7:22). This is one of very few verses in the psalm with no term for God's word.
- **"It is time for the LORD to act, for your law has been broken" (v126).** His appeal rests on God's honor, not just his own trouble.
- **"Above gold, above fine gold" (v127).** Compare 19:10.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns that claiming to love God's commandments above worldly gain is hypocrisy unless life bears it out. JFB reads the stanza as a plea of integrity for protection. Guzik, citing Bridges, says asking God to be surety shows the writer knew he was still a sinner needing a guarantor.

#### Pe: The Unfolding of Your Words Gives Light (vv129–136)
- **"The unfolding of your words gives light" (v130).** "Unfolding" (*petach*) means opening, an entrance or doorway. As a door opens, light floods in, and "the simple" understand.
- **"I open my mouth and pant" (v131).** Like a thirsty animal (42:1).
- **"Make your face shine upon your servant" (v135).** This echoes the priestly blessing (Num 6:25).
- **"My eyes shed streams of tears, because people do not keep your law" (v136).** Grief over sin, not only anger at it. Jesus wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains v136 as eyes that "come down (dissolved) like water brooks." Henry: "The wonders of redeeming love will fix the heart in adoration of them." Guzik, citing Boice, notes that the word for "unfolding" can mean a tent door.

#### Tsadhe: Righteous Are You, O LORD (vv137–144)
- **"Righteous are you, O LORD, and right are your rules" (v137).** Stanza Tsadhe opens with *tsaddiq*, "righteous," and the root comes five times. God's word is right because God is.
- **"Your promise is well tried" (v140).** Literally "refined," like metal tested in fire (12:6; Prov 30:5).
- **"I am small and despised, yet I do not forget your precepts" (v141).** Being small in others' eyes does not make the word small in his.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God never did, and never can do wrong to any." JFB says the despised are "distinguished in God's sight by a regard for His law." Guzik, with Spurgeon, notes how the stanza's letter sounds the note of righteousness.

#### Qoph: But You Are Near, O LORD (vv145–152)
- **"With my whole heart I cry" (v145).** This stanza is almost all prayer.
- **"I rise before dawn… my eyes are awake before the watches of the night" (vv147–148).** The night was divided into watches (90:4). He is up before the watchman calls.
- **"They draw near who persecute me… But you are near, O LORD" (vv150–151).** The same word, "near," is used for both. The enemies come close, but God is closer.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Hope in God's word encourages us to continue in prayer." JFB calls this "an intelligent devotion" guided by promises. Guzik, citing Trapp, says the word gave him hope and hope gave him prayer.

#### Resh: Plead My Cause and Redeem Me (vv153–160)
- **"Plead my cause and redeem me" (v154).** Legal and family language: an advocate argues the case, and a kinsman-redeemer (*go'el*) buys back a relative (Lev 25:25; Ruth 4). "Give me life" comes three times in this stanza (vv154, 156, 159).
- **"The sum of your word is truth" (v160).** "Sum" (*rosh*) means the head or total. Every part is true, and so is the whole.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God's mercies are tender; they are a fountain that can never be exhausted." JFB says remembering the law earns nothing but shows a child's spirit toward the Father. Guzik suggests that any claimed revival be tested by whether it is "according to" God's word.

#### Sin and Shin: Great Peace Have Those Who Love Your Law (vv161–168)
- **"Sin and Shin" (ESV heading).** In Hebrew one letter has two sounds, *s* and *sh*, so the ESV names both.
- **"Princes persecute me… but my heart stands in awe of your words" (v161).** He fears God's word more than powerful men.
- **"Like one who finds great spoil" (v162).** The joy of a soldier dividing plunder after victory (Isa 9:3).
- **"Seven times a day I praise you" (v164).** Seven means fullness, "all day long."
- **"Great peace have those who love your law; nothing can make them stumble" (v165).** "Peace" is *shalom*, wholeness and well-being.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the awe of v161 "reverential, not slavish fear." Henry: "Those whose hearts stand in awe of God's word, will rather endure the wrath of man." Guzik, with Spurgeon, says joy in the word grows from reverence for it.

#### Taw: I Have Gone Astray Like a Lost Sheep (vv169–176)
- **"Let my cry come before you… let my plea come before you" (vv169–170).** The psalm ends as it has gone: in prayer, asking for understanding and rescue "according to your word."
- **"My lips will pour forth praise… my tongue will sing" (vv171–172).** "Pour forth" means to bubble up like a spring.
- **"I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek your servant" (v176).** After 175 verses of love for God's word, the last line is a confession. He has not arrived. He is a wandering sheep, and he asks the Shepherd to come and find him (Isa 53:6). Yet he adds, "I do not forget your commandments." The psalm ends with need, not achievement.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "The prayer for understanding of the truth precedes that for deliverance," and it glosses v176 with the lost sheep. Henry: "Let this psalm be a touchstone by which to try our hearts, and our lives." Guzik, citing Morgan, says the psalm closes in deep dependence on God.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 119 describes a person who loves God's word with a whole heart, delights in it day and night, keeps it under persecution and endures affliction without letting go. Every honest reader falls short of that, and the psalm itself ends with "I have gone astray." Only one person ever lived it fully. Jesus said, "My food is to do the will of him who sent me" (John 4:34), answered temptation with Scripture, and was "small and despised" yet never forgot his Father's word. He is the Word made flesh (John 1:14), the light of the world (John 8:12) and the Good Shepherd who "goes after the one that is lost" (Luke 15:4). The psalm's last prayer, "seek your servant," is answered in him: "the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10).
