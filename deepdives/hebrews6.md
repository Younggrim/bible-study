*Setting:* The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to weary Jewish Christians tempted to drift back to the old ways, has just reached its central theme. In 5:1–10 the writer introduced Jesus as a high priest "after the order of Melchizedek," and he plainly wants to explain what that means. Then he stops himself. "About this we have much to say," he says, but it is hard to explain because his hearers "have become dull of hearing" (5:11). They have been believers long enough to teach others, yet they still need milk instead of solid food (5:12–14). Chapter 6 is the rest of that interruption: a call to grow up, one of the sternest warnings in the Bible, a warm word of confidence, and finally God's oath to Abraham, which brings the sermon back to Melchizedek at the last verse.

#### Warning Against Apostasy (vv1–12)
- **Where the section begins.** The ESV section starts at 5:11, so the rebuke about milk and solid food and the first twelve verses of chapter 6 are one unit. The chapter break falls in the middle of the thought, and "therefore" in v1 draws its conclusion from 5:14.
- **"Let us leave the elementary doctrine of Christ" (v1).** Literally "the word of the beginning of Christ," the ABCs of the faith. "Leave" does not mean abandon. It means stop going back over them, the way a builder lays a foundation once and then builds on it. Notice "let us": the writer puts himself alongside his hearers.
- **"And go on to maturity" (v1).** The Greek verb is passive, "let us be carried on." It is the same verb (*pherō*) used in 1:3 of the Son who "upholds" or carries the universe. Growth is something believers press into, but they are being carried by someone else. "Maturity" is *teleiotēs*, completeness, from the same family as "the mature" in 5:14. It means growing into full adulthood in the faith, not sinless perfection.
- **Six foundation stones (vv1–2).** The list falls into three pairs. Repentance and faith are how a person begins. Washings and laying on of hands are the rites that marked a beginning. Resurrection and judgment are the hope at the end. Almost all of these were already taught in the synagogue, which may be the point: a Jewish Christian could fall back to this common ground and quietly leave Jesus out.
- **"Repentance from dead works" (v1).** Works that lead to death, or acts that have no life in them. Later the writer says Christ's blood will "purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God" (9:14).
- **"Instruction about washings" (v2).** The word is *baptismos*, plural, and the ESV footnote gives "baptisms (that is, cleansing rites)." This is not the usual word for Christian baptism (*baptisma*). Elsewhere it is used of Jewish ritual washings of cups and pots (Mark 7:4) and the "various washings" of the old covenant (9:10). New believers from a Jewish background would need to be taught how Christian baptism differs from the many washings they already knew, including John's baptism (Acts 19:3–5).
- **"The laying on of hands" (v2).** In the New Testament hands were laid on people for healing, for blessing, for receiving the Spirit (Acts 8:17; 19:6) and for setting people apart for service (Acts 6:6; 13:3). In the Old Testament a worshiper laid hands on a sacrifice (Lev 1:4).
- **"And this we will do if God permits" (v3).** The writer intends to move on, and he does, but he knows growth is God's gift. Without God even good resolutions go nowhere (Phil 2:12–13).
- **"For it is impossible" (v4).** In Greek "impossible" comes first for emphasis. Hebrews uses the same word three more times, always firmly: it is impossible for God to lie (6:18), for the blood of bulls to take away sins (10:4), and to please God without faith (11:6). The sentence runs from v4 to v6, with the main verb held back until the end: impossible "to restore them again to repentance."
- **Five descriptions (vv4–5).** "Once been enlightened" uses *hapax*, the word for Christ's "once for all" sacrifice (9:28), and *phōtizō*, which the writer uses again for the day his hearers first learned the gospel (10:32). "Tasted the heavenly gift" uses the verb for Jesus tasting death (2:9), a real experience, though elsewhere it can mean only sampling (Matt 27:34). "Shared in the Holy Spirit" uses *metochos*, the word for fishermen who were partners in a boat (Luke 5:7) and for believers who "share in Christ" (3:14). "Tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come" recalls the signs and wonders that confirmed the gospel to them (2:3–4).
- **"And then have fallen away" (v6).** The verb is *parapiptō*, used only here in the New Testament. In the Greek Old Testament it translates a Hebrew word for treachery or breaking faith with God, especially in Ezekiel (Ezek 14:13; 18:24). It is not a picture of stumbling into sin, which every believer does, but of turning one's back on Christ. The writer has the wilderness generation in mind: they saw the plagues, ate the manna and drank from the rock, and still refused to enter the land (3:16–19).
- **"Crucifying once again the Son of God… and holding him up to contempt" (v6).** "Crucifying once again" is *anastauroō*. In ordinary Greek it simply meant "to crucify," but Abbott-Smith and most translations take it here as "crucify again," and the context supports that. To walk away from Jesus back to the old system is to side with those who crucified him and to say he deserved it. "Holding him up to contempt" (*paradeigmatizō*) means making a public example of someone, as a crucified man hung on display. Both verbs are present tense, and they can be read two ways: "since they are crucifying" (the ESV) or "while they keep crucifying." On the second reading, restoration is impossible as long as they keep rejecting him.
- **Land, rain and thorns (vv7–8).** Two fields get the same rain. One bears a crop and is blessed. The other bears "thorns and thistles," the very words of the curse on the ground in Genesis 3:17–18. Farmers burned off such fields. Isaiah's vineyard song makes a similar point (Isa 5:1–7), and Jesus told a parable of seed and soils (Mark 4:3–20). Note the wording: "near to being cursed," not yet cursed.
- **Four main readings of vv4–6.** Few verses have been argued over more, and faithful Christians read them differently.
- **Reading: True believers who can lose salvation.** Wesleyan and Arminian readers, and much of the Orthodox and Catholic traditions, take vv4–5 as a plain description of real Christians, since it is hard to imagine stronger words for Christian experience. They hold that a believer can forsake Christ and be lost. Most in this view do not think such a person can never return, and they point to the present tense in v6.
- **Reading: A real warning that God uses to keep believers.** Some read the passage as describing genuine believers, but say the warning is how God keeps them from falling, much as a sign at a cliff edge keeps people safe. They note v9: the writer is "sure of better things" for these same hearers. Critics ask whether a warning about something that cannot happen really warns.
- **Reading: Those who tasted but were never truly changed.** Many Reformed readers think these people experienced the gospel from close up, like Judas or Simon the magician (Acts 8:13, 18–23), without being born again. "Tasting" can mean sampling, and v9 contrasts them with "things that belong to salvation," which suggests they never had those things. Critics reply that the language of vv4–5 is strong for people who were never believers.
- **Reading: Loss of reward.** Others think the danger is losing usefulness and reward, not salvation, as with the believer who is "saved, but only as through fire" (1 Cor 3:15). Critics point to 10:26–31, which speaks of fiery judgment on God's adversaries.
- **What the views share.** All four agree the passage is about a deliberate turning away from Christ, not about ordinary sin, doubt or a season of coldness. A person troubled about whether they have committed it is plainly not hardened against Christ. In the early church, rigorist groups such as the Montanists and later the Novatianists used these verses to refuse readmission to believers who had lapsed under persecution. The wider church rejected that reading, and some historians think the controversy was one reason Western churches were slow to accept Hebrews.
- **"Yet in your case, beloved, we feel sure of better things" (v9).** "Beloved" appears only here in Hebrews. Having given the warning, the writer immediately reassures them. He is not accusing them of apostasy. He is warning them where the road of drift leads, so they will not take it.
- **"God is not unjust so as to overlook your work and the love… in serving the saints, as you still do" (v10).** Their faith had shown itself in love. Chapter 10 recalls how they stood by fellow believers who were imprisoned and had their property seized (10:32–34). God does not forget, even when people do.
- **"Full assurance of hope until the end" (v11).** Assurance is tied to carrying on, not only to how a person began. Love (v10), hope (v11) and faith (v12) appear together, the same triad as 1 Corinthians 13:13.
- **"So that you may not be sluggish" (v12).** "Sluggish" is *nōthros*, the same word translated "dull" in 5:11. The two uses bracket the whole digression: they have become sluggish in hearing, and they must not become sluggish in living. The remedy is to imitate those who "through faith and patience inherit the promises," the kind of people chapter 11 will list. Abraham comes first.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the six basics were largely shared with Judaism and sees here the real danger: retreating to common ground to avoid persecution while dropping Jesus. He reads *baptismos* as Jewish ceremonial washings, which fits Mark 7:4 and Hebrews 9:10, though the lexicon follows Westcott in thinking the plural also takes in Christian baptism. Henry calls repentance and faith "inseparable twins." On vv4–6 the three differ. Henry says hypocrites can go far: "Persons may taste religion, and seem to like it," without true conversion, and true saints are kept by God's purpose and Christ's prayer. Of real apostates he says "very few instances can be given" of their repenting, yet God can still do it. JFB says "an entire and wilful apostasy is meant; the Hebrews had not yet so fallen away," and rejects the Novatian reading. Guzik lays out the views and declines to fix on one with confidence. He stresses that from a human viewpoint these people looked like Christians, and that a desire to repent shows a person has not fallen away. JFB connects "enlightened" with baptism, and later Christian writers such as Justin Martyr did call baptism "illumination," but the lexicon gives the New Testament sense as spiritual enlightenment, as in 10:32. On v9 Henry says the things that accompany salvation "are better things than ever any dissembler or apostate enjoyed."

#### The Certainty of God's Promise (vv13–20)
- **"When God made a promise to Abraham… he swore by himself" (v13).** The setting is Genesis 22, just after Abraham was ready to offer Isaac. God said, "By myself I have sworn" (Gen 22:16). People swear by someone greater than themselves. God has no one greater, so he staked his oath on his own name.
- **"Surely I will bless you and multiply you" (v14).** From Genesis 22:17, as the ESV footnote notes. The Hebrew is literally "blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you," a doubled form for emphasis.
- **"Having patiently waited, obtained the promise" (v15).** Abraham was 75 when God first promised him offspring (Gen 12:4) and 100 when Isaac was born (Gen 21:5), twenty-five years of waiting. He received Isaac and the oath, though he did not live to see the nation or the land. Hebrews later says he died "not having received the things promised" (11:13). He is the model of "faith and patience" from v12.
- **"An oath is final for confirmation" (v16).** In ancient law, as now, an oath settled a dispute when there was no other proof (Exod 22:10–11). The writer is not discussing whether believers should take oaths. He is reasoning from ordinary life: if an oath ends arguments between people, how much more when God swears.
- **"He guaranteed it with an oath" (v17).** The verb *mesiteuō* comes from the word for a go-between or mediator. Abbott-Smith gives "to interpose, mediate." God stepped in as his own guarantor, adding an oath to a promise that was already true, for the sake of "the heirs of the promise." Those heirs are not only Abraham's family by birth, but all who share his faith (Gal 3:7, 29).
- **"Two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie" (v18).** Most take the two things as God's promise and his oath. Some think the writer is looking ahead to a second oath, Psalm 110:4: "The LORD has sworn and will not change his mind, 'You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek.'" He quotes it in 7:20–21, and it is the very verse he closes this chapter with. Either way the "impossible" of v4 is answered by the "impossible" of v18. It is impossible for an apostate to be restored, and it is impossible for God to lie.
- **"We who have fled for refuge" (v18).** *Katapheugō* means to flee for safety. It recalls the six cities of refuge, where someone who had killed accidentally could flee from the avenger of blood (Num 35:9–34; Josh 20), or the altar a fugitive could grasp (1 Kings 1:50). There is an added echo: the man who fled to a city of refuge could go home safely only after the death of the high priest (Num 35:25, 28), and the passage ends with Jesus as high priest.
- **"A sure and steadfast anchor of the soul" (v19).** *Ankura* is the word for the anchors the sailors dropped in Paul's shipwreck (Acts 27:29). The anchor was a common ancient picture of security, and it became one of the earliest Christian symbols, scratched on tombs in the Roman catacombs. This anchor is strange: it does not go down into the sea but up, "into the inner place behind the curtain." The hope that holds believers steady is fastened in God's own presence.
- **"Behind the curtain" (v19).** The curtain (*katapetasma*) in the New Testament always means the inner curtain of the tabernacle or temple, which closed off the Most Holy Place. Only the high priest went behind it, once a year on the Day of Atonement, with blood (Lev 16:2, 12–15). The Gospels record that this curtain was torn in two when Jesus died (Matt 27:51).
- **"Where Jesus has gone as a forerunner on our behalf" (v20).** *Prodromos* means one who runs ahead: in classical Greek, scouts or advance troops sent before an army. In the Greek Old Testament it is used for first-ripe grapes and figs (Num 13:20; Isa 28:4), the first of a harvest still to come. On the Day of Atonement the high priest went in alone and no one followed him. Jesus goes in as a forerunner, which means others are coming after. Hebrews later invites believers to "enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus" (10:19–20).
- **"A high priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" (v20).** The writer has come back exactly where he broke off in 5:10, this time adding "forever" from Psalm 110:4. The warning and the encouragement are over, and 7:1–28 finally gives the solid food he promised.
- **Commentators:** Henry takes the two unchangeable things as "the counsel and the oath of God," and says, "Here is a refuge for all sinners who flee to the mercy of God." Guzik draws out the cities of refuge at length: within reach, open to all, and safe only inside, with one great difference. The cities sheltered only the accidental killer, while Christ receives the guilty. Henry writes, "Gospel hope is our anchor in the storms of this world," and JFB describes it as "the anchor which prevents the vessel being tossed to and fro." JFB notes that the word for curtain is the one used for the inner veil. The lexicon agrees for the New Testament, though the Greek Old Testament sometimes uses it for the outer screen as well. On v20 JFB: "As John Baptist was Christ's forerunner on earth, so Christ is ours in heaven." Guzik pictures the forerunner as a military scout going ahead, which matches the classical senses in LSJ. He also adds that a forerunner implies followers. JFB also takes v17's verb as God interposing with an oath, as the lexicon allows.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter swings from a warning that has unsettled readers for centuries to an anchor that will not move, and Jesus is at the center of both. To turn from him is to crucify him again, because there is no other sacrifice to turn to. To flee to him is to take hold of a hope that God has bound with his own oath. The high priests of Israel went behind the curtain alone and came out again. Jesus has gone in as a forerunner and stays there as "a high priest forever." The hope of the hearers rests not on how well they have done but on where he is, and the writer means them to keep going because of it.
