*Setting:* Isaac is old, blind and expecting to die soon, though he will in fact live for decades more (35:28–29). Working back from Jacob's age when he later stands before Pharaoh (47:9), older commentators put Isaac at about 137 here, with the twins in their seventies. The family lives in the Negeb near Beersheba (26:33; 28:10). Two earlier scenes frame everything that happens. Before the twins were born, the LORD told Rebekah, "the older shall serve the younger" (25:23). And Esau, coming in hungry from the field, sold his birthright to Jacob for a bowl of stew (25:29–34). The last chapter ended with Esau's two Hittite wives making life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah (26:34–35). Even so, Isaac now plans to give his deathbed blessing to Esau. The chapter is built as a series of two-person scenes, and no one in it comes off well: a father acting on appetite, a mother and son acting on deception, and a brother who wants what he once despised. Yet God's word to Rebekah stands.

#### Isaac Blesses Jacob (vv1–46)
- **One long section.** The ESV puts the whole chapter under one heading, and it reads like a play in scenes: Isaac and Esau (vv1–4), Rebekah and Jacob (vv5–17), Jacob and Isaac (vv18–29), Esau and Isaac (vv30–40), Rebekah and Jacob again (vv41–45), and Rebekah and Isaac (v46). Isaac and Rebekah never speak to each other until the very end, and Jacob and Esau never speak at all. The family is divided before the deception even starts.
- **"His eyes were dim so that he could not see" (v1).** Isaac's failing sight is the opening detail, and the story turns on it. The Tyndale notes suggest it is spiritual as well as physical. Isaac knew what God had said about the twins (25:23), and he is still trying to steer the blessing to his favorite. "Here I am" is Esau's answer, the same reply Abraham gave God, and Isaac gave Abraham, on the way to Moriah (22:1, 7).
- **"Prepare for me delicious food, such as I love" (v4).** "Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game" (25:28). The Tyndale notes observe that, like Esau with the stew, Isaac lets his appetite lead. Food runs through the whole story: Esau lost the birthright over a meal, and now the blessing is lost over one.
- **"That my soul may bless you before I die" (v4).** A father's last words carried great weight in the ancient world. They passed on leadership of the family and its inheritance, and they were treated as binding once spoken. Tablets from Nuzi, a Mesopotamian town of roughly this period, include a lawsuit in which a son's claim was upheld on the strength of his dying father's spoken word. Here, though, more than property is at stake. The blessing of Abraham, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (12:3), has to go to someone. Isaac seems to plan it privately, with no one else present, as if he knows it would be contested.
- **"Before the LORD" (v7).** Rebekah was listening (v5). When she repeats Isaac's words to Jacob, she adds a phrase he did not say: "bless you before the LORD." Whether she is reporting what Isaac meant or raising the stakes to move Jacob, she makes clear this is no ordinary goodbye.
- **"Obey my voice as I command you" (v8).** She says it three times in the chapter (vv8, 13, 43). Rebekah was the one who received God's word about the younger son, and she seems to believe she is protecting it. But she reaches for disguise and goat meat, not prayer. The Tyndale notes put it plainly: the whole family tries to secure God's blessing by their own schemes instead of by faith.
- **"I shall seem to be mocking him" (v12).** Jacob's only objection is practical. He worries about getting caught, not about whether lying to his blind father is wrong. "Mocking" here means acting as a deceiver or trickster. Jacob fears that if he is found out, he will "bring a curse upon myself and not a blessing."
- **"Let your curse be on me, my son" (v13).** Rebekah takes it on herself, and in a sense she does pay. She sends Jacob away for "a while" (v44), and the text never shows them together again. Her death is not even recorded. Genesis notes only the death of her nurse, Deborah (35:8).
- **Goatskins and Esau's best clothes (vv15–17).** Esau was born "all his body like a hairy cloak" (25:25), and the word for "hairy" sounds like Seir, the hill country where his descendants will live. Rebekah covers Jacob's hands and neck with young goats' skins and dresses him in Esau's best garments, which still carry the smell of the fields. Years later, Jacob's own sons will deceive him with a goat and a garment, dipping Joseph's robe in goat's blood (37:31–33). What Jacob does to his father will be done to him.
- **"I am Esau your firstborn" (v19).** Isaac asks, "Who are you, my son?" (v18), and Jacob answers with a flat lie. He claims the very title, "firstborn," that he bought from Esau (25:33). He had the birthright on paper, and now he takes the name too.
- **"Because the LORD your God granted me success" (v20).** Isaac wonders how the hunt went so fast. Jacob brings God into the lie. The Tyndale notes say this comes close to blasphemy. Notice too that Jacob says "your God," not "my God." Only after Bethel does Jacob say, "the LORD shall be my God" (28:21).
- **"The voice is Jacob's voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau" (v22).** Isaac's suspicions keep surfacing: the speed (v20), the voice (v22), and then a direct question, "Are you really my son Esau?" (v24). The Tyndale notes count three times he voices doubt. Each time, his other senses overrule what he hears. The scene plays on all five senses: sight that fails, hearing that is right, touch and taste and smell that are fooled.
- **"Come near and kiss me, my son" (v26).** The kiss is the last test and the most painful part of the lie. Isaac draws in "the smell of his garments" (v27), Esau's clothes, and is convinced.
- **"As the smell of a field that the LORD has blessed" (v27).** The blessing opens with that outdoor smell. Then it asks God for "the dew of heaven and of the fatness of the earth and plenty of grain and wine" (v28). In a dry land with little rain in summer, heavy dew mattered a great deal for crops (compare Deut 33:13, 28). This is a blessing of fertile land and harvest.
- **"Be lord over your brothers" (v29).** The second half gives rule: peoples will serve him, nations bow, and "your mother's sons" will bow. Isaac thinks he is saying it to Esau, and so is directly reversing God's word that "the older shall serve the younger" (25:23). Instead, without knowing it, he confirms that word. The closing line, "cursed be everyone who curses you, and blessed be everyone who blesses you," echoes God's promise to Abraham (12:3). The irony runs on: when the brothers finally meet again, it is Jacob who bows seven times to Esau and calls him "my lord" (33:3, 8).
- **What the blessing leaves out.** Isaac speaks of harvests and rule, but he says nothing here about the land promised to Abraham or the many offspring. Those come only in 28:3–4, when Isaac, now knowing whom he is blessing, sends Jacob off with "the blessing of Abraham." Some interpreters think Isaac held back the core covenant promise from Esau all along. Others think the narrator saves it for the scene where Isaac blesses Jacob openly. Either way, the full blessing comes freely, not by fraud.
- **"As soon as Isaac had finished blessing Jacob" (v30).** The timing is tight. Jacob "had scarcely gone out" when Esau comes in. Esau's words in v31 almost repeat Jacob's in v19, and Isaac asks again, "Who are you?" (v32).
- **"Isaac trembled very violently" (v33).** The Hebrew piles it up: literally "trembled a great trembling, exceedingly." The verb *charad* is the one used of Israel trembling at Mount Sinai (Exod 19:16). The Tyndale notes suggest Isaac realizes that he has been fighting God's plan and that God has overruled him. Then comes the key line: "Yes, and he shall be blessed." Isaac does not try to take it back. Hebrews counts this as faith: "By faith Isaac invoked future blessings on Jacob and Esau" (Heb 11:20).
- **"An exceedingly great and bitter cry" (v34).** It is one of the saddest moments in Genesis. Esau begs, "Bless me, even me also, O my father!" Isaac answers, "Your brother came deceitfully." The word is *mirmah*, which the lexicon gives as "deceit, treachery." It will come back to haunt Jacob. When Laban swaps Leah for Rachel, Jacob protests, "Why then have you deceived me?" (29:25), using a verb from the same root.
- **"Is he not rightly named Jacob?" (v36).** The ESV footnote explains that "Jacob" means "he takes by the heel," or "he cheats." Esau builds a pun on the verb *aqab*, which the lexicon defines as "follow at the heel" and figuratively "overreach" or "circumvent." Jacob came out of the womb holding Esau's heel (25:26), and now he has "overreached" him twice. Esau makes a second wordplay. He lost the *bekorah*, the "birthright," and now the *berakah*, the "blessing." The two words differ by one swapped pair of letters. But Esau's version is lopsided. He says Jacob "took away" his birthright, when Esau himself sold it under oath and "despised" it (25:33–34). The Tyndale notes add that Esau also broke that oath by agreeing to Isaac's plan in the first place.
- **"Have you but one blessing, my father?" (v38).** Hebrews uses Esau as a warning: "afterward, when he desired to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no chance to repent, though he sought it with tears" (Heb 12:17). The "repentance" he could not find is usually read as a change of the outcome (or of his father's mind), not that Esau was sorry for his sin and was refused. His tears were for what he had lost, not for what he had done.
- **"Away from the fatness of the earth shall your dwelling be" (v39).** Isaac's words to Esau use the same phrases as Jacob's blessing, "the fatness of the earth" and "the dew of heaven." The Hebrew preposition can mean either "of" or "away from," and the ESV footnote gives "of" as an alternative. The older translations (KJV) took it as a blessing of rich land. Most modern translations, like the ESV, read it as the reverse of v28, and that fits the geography: Edom, Esau's later homeland, was rugged, dry country southeast of the Dead Sea.
- **"By your sword you shall live, and you shall serve your brother" (v40).** Edom's later history follows these lines. David conquered Edom and put garrisons there (2 Sam 8:14). In the days of Joram king of Judah, "Edom revolted from the rule of Judah" (2 Kgs 8:20–22), breaking the yoke. "Grow restless" is *rud*, which the lexicon gives as "wander restlessly, roam," though it notes the form here is uncertain. The prophets later condemn Edom for violence against Jacob's descendants (Obad 10).
- **"Then I will kill my brother Jacob" (v41).** Esau "hated" Jacob and planned to wait until "the days of mourning for my father," out of respect for Isaac or to avoid grieving him. Rebekah hears of it, though Esau "said to himself," which suggests he said it to others too. Cain's story stands in the background (4:8). This time, brother does not kill brother.
- **"Stay with him a while" (v44).** The Hebrew is literally "a few days." It becomes twenty years (31:38, 41). Rebekah's fear in v45, "Why should I be bereft of you both in one day?", probably means losing Jacob to murder and Esau to the death penalty for murder (9:6). And Isaac's "days of mourning" turn out to be decades away. When Isaac does die, Esau and Jacob bury him together (35:29).
- **"I loathe my life because of the Hittite women" (v46).** Rebekah cannot tell Isaac the real reason Jacob must leave, so she raises a true grievance (26:35) that will move him. Isaac will send Jacob to find a wife among their own relatives, as Abraham sent for Rebekah (ch 24). The Tyndale notes point out that this begins a larger pattern: Jacob runs from Esau, meets God at Bethel, lives in exile, meets God's angels again on the way back, and is finally reconciled with Esau (chs 28–33).
- **Who was right?** Readers have weighed this chapter differently. Some, especially in older Jewish and Christian tradition, defend Rebekah as acting on God's revealed word. Most see everyone at fault: Isaac for resisting God's word, Rebekah and Jacob for lying, Esau for despising the birthright. The text does not grade them, but it shows the costs: a family split, a mother who never sees her son again, and a deceiver who will be deceived. God's purpose comes about in spite of all of them. Paul points to these twins to show that God's choice rests on his own call, not on works, since it was made "though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad" (Rom 9:11–13).
- **Commentators:** All three refuse to excuse the deception. Henry says Rebekah "wronged Isaac by putting a cheat on him; she wronged Jacob by tempting him to wickedness," acting "as if the end would justify, or excuse wrong means," and he adds, "it is too daring for any creature to say, Upon me be thy curse." On Jacob he writes, "Lying is soon learnt," and he wonders "how he could have the assurance to father it upon God." He allows one mitigating point: the fraud was meant "not so much to hasten the fulfilling, as to prevent the thwarting, of the oracle of God." JFB agrees that Jacob's scruples were "founded, not on the evil of the act," and calls the plan "crooked policy" carried out "with unenlightened zeal." JFB also gives Isaac's age as "his hundred thirty-seventh year," and Guzik cites the same figure from Luther. Guzik stresses that Isaac was scheming too, trying to pass the blessing to Esau in secret against God's word, and that the whole family acted in the flesh. His application is that what works is not the test of what is right. On Esau's tears Guzik is blunt, calling them the grief of frustrated selfishness, while Henry is gentler: Esau "made his father's tent to ring with his grief." On Esau's lesser blessing Henry writes, "He shall serve, but he shall not starve," and notes that people outside the covenant may still have "a very good share of outward blessings." JFB connects v40 to Edom being under Israel until it broke free under Joram. JFB has no notes on vv2–3, 5, 12 or 36–38, and comments on vv18–27 and 30–35 only as blocks.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jacob gets his father's blessing by wearing his brother's clothes, coming in under another's name, and smelling like the field of the firstborn. It is a lie, and the chapter never pretends otherwise. But the gospel tells a true story with the same shape. Sinners receive the Father's blessing not in their own name but clothed in Christ, the true Firstborn (Rom 8:29; Col 1:15), since all who are baptized into him "have put on Christ" (Gal 3:27). Unlike Jacob, Christ freely gives what is his, and no one is cheated. Isaac could not take back a blessing once spoken, and the blessing of God in Christ is just as sure: "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20). And where Isaac said, "Cursed be everyone who curses you," Christ became "a curse for us" so that "the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles" (Gal 3:13–14). Rebekah said, "Let your curse be on me," and could not carry it. Jesus could, and did.
