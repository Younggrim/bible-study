*Setting:* Psalm 91 has no title in the Hebrew text, so its author and date are unknown. Jewish tradition, followed by some Christian readers, credits it to Moses along with Psalm 90, on the principle that an untitled psalm belongs to the author of the one before. The two certainly belong together. Psalm 90 calls God "our dwelling place in all generations" and laments human frailty under God's wrath. Psalm 91 picks up the same word: "Because you have made the LORD your dwelling place" (v9). The ancient Greek translation credits it to David, and some link it to the plague after David's census (2 Sam 24:13–15). The psalm has an unusual shape. It moves between voices: a believer's confession ("I will say," v2), words of assurance to someone addressed as "you" (vv3–13), and finally God himself speaking (vv14–16). It is a psalm of trust, and also one of the most misused in the Bible. Satan quoted it to Jesus (Matt 4:6). Psalm 91 has also long been prayed in danger: by Jews in ancient prayers against evil spirits (one Dead Sea Scroll collects it with such prayers), by soldiers in the world wars, and by people in times of plague. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanza breaks.

#### The Shelter of the Most High (vv1–2)
- **"He who dwells in the shelter of the Most High" (v1).** "Shelter" (*seter*) means a hiding place, somewhere concealed and safe (Ps 27:5; 32:7). The KJV's "secret place" catches that. "Dwells" means to live there, not to visit. The promise is for those who make God their home.
- **"Will abide in the shadow of the Almighty" (v1).** "Abide" (*lin*) usually means to stay the night, to lodge. In a hot land, a shadow is relief from the sun (Isa 25:4; 32:2). The picture may also come from the temple, where the cherubim spread their wings over the ark (1 Kgs 8:6–7), or from a bird's wings (v4; Ps 17:8; 36:7).
- **Four names for God (vv1–2).** In two verses the psalmist uses four names: **"Most High"** (*Elyon*), the God over every other power (Gen 14:19); **"Almighty"** (*Shaddai*), the name God used with Abraham (Gen 17:1); **"the LORD"** (*Yahweh*), the covenant name revealed to Moses (Exod 3:14–15); and **"my God"** (*Elohai*). The God who rules all things is also personally "mine."
- **"I will say to the LORD, 'My refuge and my fortress, my God, in whom I trust'" (v2).** The psalm moves from a general truth (v1) to a personal confession. "Refuge" is a place to flee to. "Fortress" (*metsudah*) is a stronghold on high ground, the word David used of the wilderness strongholds where he hid from Saul (1 Sam 22:4–5; Ps 18:2). The promises that follow are for the person who says this and means it.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "it is the character of a true believer that he dwells in the secret place of the Most High," and that believers have "not only… an admittance, but a residence, under God's protection." JFB says the secret place "denotes nearness to God." Guzik points out the four names and draws the same line Henry does: the secret place is somewhere to live, not just to visit now and then.

#### He Will Deliver You (vv3–8)
- **"The snare of the fowler" (v3).** A fowler caught birds with hidden nets, traps and throwing sticks. The picture is of danger that comes without warning, plots and schemes that catch people unaware (Ps 124:7; 141:9; Prov 6:5). Henry describes the snare as "laid unseen and catches the unwary prey on a sudden."
- **"The deadly pestilence" (v3).** Literally "the pestilence of destructions." The Hebrew consonants for "pestilence" (*dever*) can also be read *davar*, "word," and the Greek translation took it as "a troubling word," a slander or curse. Both readings fit the theme of hidden danger.
- **"He will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge" (v4).** "Pinions" are wing feathers. The picture is of a mother bird gathering her chicks, an image Moses used of God bearing Israel through the wilderness "as an eagle… spreading out its wings" (Deut 32:11). Boaz used it of Ruth coming to trust Israel's God: "under whose wings you have come to take refuge" (Ruth 2:12). Jesus used it of himself as he wept over Jerusalem: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (Matt 23:37).
- **"His faithfulness is a shield and buckler" (v4).** The image changes from mother bird to soldier. The "shield" (*tsinnah*) was a large body shield. "Buckler" (*socherah*) appears only here in the Bible, and the BDB lexicon simply gives "buckler," a small shield, perhaps something that surrounds. God's faithfulness, his reliability in keeping his word, is the armour.
- **"The terror of the night… the arrow that flies by day… the pestilence that stalks in darkness… the destruction that wastes at noonday" (vv5–6).** The dangers cover every hour: night and day, darkness and noon. Some are visible (arrows of war), some invisible (disease). "Destruction" (*qetev*) is the word Moses used for "poisonous pestilence" (Deut 32:24). Ancient Jewish interpreters, followed by the early Greek translation, read these as demonic powers, "the noonday demon," and later monks used the phrase for the listlessness that could strike in the middle of the day. The psalm's point is simple: there is no hour at which God stops guarding.
- **"A thousand may fall at your side… but it will not come near you" (v7).** This is the verse that raises the hardest question. Faithful believers do die in plagues and wars. Hebrews 11 lists heroes of faith who "escaped the edge of the sword" and others in the same chapter who "were killed with the sword" (Heb 11:34, 37). How can both be true? The psalm states God's protection in the strongest possible terms, as poetry does, and it often comes true in remarkable ways. But Scripture as a whole shows that the deepest sense of the promise is that nothing can separate God's people from him, and no harm can finally destroy them (Rom 8:35–39). Jesus said in one breath that some of his followers would be put to death and that "not a hair of your head will perish" (Luke 21:16–18).
- **"You will only look with your eyes and see the recompense of the wicked" (v8).** The faithful will see God's justice done. Israel watched from the far shore of the sea as the Egyptians were drowned (Exod 14:30–31), and during the plagues on Egypt, "the plague shall not befall you" was God's word to Israel (Exod 12:13). Many readers hear the Passover behind this stanza.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the promise "is often fulfilled in our preservation from those dangers which are very threatening and very near." He does not say the godly always escape. Guzik also treats the protection as God's general pattern and the psalmist's confident hope, not a guarantee that a believer will never suffer, and tells of Christians who cared for plague victims and were spared. He reads the fowler as a picture of the devil's hidden traps. JFB says the righteous are so safe that "they only see the calamity." The Tyndale notes agree with Guzik that these promises do not guarantee escape from trouble but give a sure expectation of God's goodness.

#### He Will Command His Angels (vv9–13)
- **"Because you have made the LORD your dwelling place" (v9).** The word "dwelling place" (*ma'on*) links this psalm with the first line of Psalm 90. The Hebrew of this verse is abrupt. Literally it reads "For you, LORD, my refuge! You have made the Most High your dwelling." The speaker may be breaking in with his own confession again, as in v2.
- **"No evil shall be allowed to befall you, no plague come near your tent" (v10).** "Tent" fits the wilderness setting and the Passover memory of vv7–8.
- **"He will command his angels concerning you to guard you in all your ways" (v11).** Angels are God's servants for the good of his people (Ps 34:7; Heb 1:14). The verb "guard" (*shamar*) is the same one used in the priestly blessing, "the LORD bless you and keep you" (Num 6:24), and in Psalm 121: "The LORD will keep your going out and your coming in."
- **"On their hands they will bear you up, lest you strike your foot against a stone" (v12).** The picture is of a traveller on rough, stony paths, carried over the dangers.
- **Satan's quotation (Matt 4:5–7; Luke 4:9–12).** The devil took Jesus to the pinnacle of the temple and quoted vv11–12: throw yourself down, and the angels will catch you. He left out "in all your ways." The promise is for someone walking in God's ways, not someone stepping off a ledge to force God's hand. Jesus answered with another Scripture: "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test" (Deut 6:16). He did not doubt the promise. He refused to treat it as a trick. A promise of protection is not a licence for recklessness.
- **"You will tread on the lion and the adder; the young lion and the serpent you will trample underfoot" (v13).** The lion is the danger that attacks openly, the snake the danger hidden in the grass. The trusting person does not merely escape but tramples. Jesus gave his disciples authority "to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy" (Luke 10:19). Many have heard an echo of Genesis 3:15, where the woman's offspring crushes the serpent's head.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the angels bear up the believer "which denotes both their great ability and their great affection." Of the words "in all thy ways," he says "this word the devil left out when he quoted the promise to enforce a temptation, knowing how much it made against him." He also connects the lion and serpent with the devil, "a roaring lion, the old serpent." Guzik makes the same observation about the missing phrase. JFB says the protection "is the result of trust in God, who employs angels as ministering spirits," and that even the fiercest animals "may be trampled on with impunity."

#### Because He Holds Fast to Me in Love (vv14–16)
- **God speaks (v14).** The quotation marks in the ESV signal the change: from here God himself speaks. Up to now the psalmist has told what God will do. Now God says it in the first person, eight times: "I will."
- **"Because he holds fast to me in love" (v14).** The verb is *chashaq*, "to be attached to," "to set one's love on." It is used of a man's longing for a woman (Gen 34:8; Deut 21:11), and especially of God's love for Israel: "it was not because you were more in number… that the LORD set his love on you" (Deut 7:7; 10:15). Here alone, according to the BDB lexicon, it is used of a person's love for God. God set his love on his people first. Here one of them sets his love on God.
- **"I will protect him, because he knows my name" (v14).** "Protect" literally means "set him on high," lift him out of reach, like the high fortress of v2. To know God's name means to know who he is and to trust him (Ps 9:10: "those who know your name put their trust in you").
- **"When he calls to me, I will answer him; I will be with him in trouble" (v15).** This is the most important line for reading the whole psalm. God does not promise that trouble will never come. He promises to be with his servant **in** it (compare Isa 43:2: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you"). Daniel's friends in the furnace are the picture: not kept out of the fire but joined in it (Dan 3:25).
- **"I will rescue him and honor him" (v15).** The faithful person is not only kept safe but given honour (1 Sam 2:30: "those who honor me I will honor").
- **"With long life I will satisfy him and show him my salvation" (v16).** Long life was a common covenant blessing (Exod 20:12; Prov 3:1–2). "Satisfy" means a full life, enough of it. The final words are the climax: **"my salvation."** Simeon, an old man in the temple who had been promised he would not die before seeing the Messiah, took the infant Jesus in his arms and said, "my eyes have seen your salvation" (Luke 2:30).
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up these verses: those who "have set their love upon him… shall be the special favourites of God himself." On long life he asks, "Who would wish to live a day longer than God has some work to do?" JFB calls the love of v14 "that of the most ardent kind" and notes that here "God Himself speaks." Guzik lists the blessings God promises, presence, deliverance, answered prayer, honour, long life and salvation, and stresses that setting one's love on God is a deliberate choice shown in prayer and worship.

#### Pointing to Christ
Satan was right about one thing: Psalm 91 does describe Jesus. He alone dwelt perfectly in the shelter of the Most High and "held fast to God in love." Yet the man to whom the psalm most fully belongs was not spared. Angels did not bear him up at the cross. He refused to call for "twelve legions of angels" (Matt 26:53), and the thousand did not fall at his side. He fell, so that the promise could be kept for others. On the third day God answered him, rescued him and honoured him (Phil 2:9), and showed him salvation. Because of that, his people can pray this psalm honestly. They are not promised that no harm will touch them, but that nothing can bring them out of God's shelter, and he is with them in trouble. He trampled the serpent (Gen 3:15; Rom 16:20). The wings he once offered to gather Jerusalem are still open.
