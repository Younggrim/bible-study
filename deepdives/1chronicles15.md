*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Three months have passed since Uzzah was struck down for touching the ark, and the ark has been resting at the house of Obed-edom, which God blessed (13:14). In the meantime God has blessed David as well, with a palace, a family and two victories over the Philistines (chapter 14). David now tries again, and this time he does it God's way. 2 Samuel 6:12–23 tells this second journey in a dozen verses. The Chronicler takes a full chapter, and much of it is about the Levites: who carried the ark, who sang, who played, who guarded the doors. That detail matters to him for two reasons. First, the difference between the disaster of chapter 13 and the joy of chapter 15 is obedience to God's word about how holy things are handled. Second, the singers and gatekeepers of his own day traced their offices back to this moment (9:22; 16:4–6). The chapter ends with the ark entering Jerusalem amid music and shouting, and one person who looked down from a window and despised it.

#### The Ark Brought to Jerusalem (vv1–29)
- **"David built houses for himself in the city of David. And he prepared a place for the ark of God and pitched a tent for it" (v1).** This was a new tent, not the old tabernacle from Moses' day, which stood at Gibeon with the bronze altar (16:39; 21:29; 2 Chr 1:3–4). For the rest of David's reign Israel had two centers of worship, the ark in Jerusalem and the altar at Gibeon, until Solomon's temple brought them together. Henry: "Wherever we build for ourselves, we must be sure to make room for God's ark."
- **"No one but the Levites may carry the ark of God, for the LORD had chosen them to carry the ark… and to minister to him forever" (v2).** David has gone back to the Law. Moses had written, "The LORD set apart the tribe of Levi to carry the ark of the covenant of the LORD, to stand before the LORD to minister to him" (Deut 10:8). What David missed the first time, he has now found. JFB says David recognized that the earlier failure came from ignoring this rule.
- **"David assembled all Israel at Jerusalem" (v3).** As in 13:5, this is a national event. Guzik, following Martin Selman, notes that bringing up the ark becomes an act of the whole people of God.
- **The Levite clans (vv4–10).** Six family heads with their men, 862 in all. The first three are Levi's three great clans: Kohath, Merari and Gershom. The other three, Elizaphan, Hebron and Uzziel, were subclans of Kohath (Exod 6:18, 22; Num 3:30). So four of the six groups were Kohathites, the branch of Levi that the Law assigned to carry the ark and the holy furniture (Num 4:4–15). David did not simply call "Levites." He called the right Levites.
- **"Zadok and Abiathar" (v11).** Two priests stood at the head of the priesthood under David. **Abiathar** was the sole survivor of Saul's massacre of the priests at Nob, who had fled to David as an outlaw (1 Sam 22:20–23). **Zadok** served at the tabernacle in Gibeon (16:39). Under Solomon, Abiathar was removed and Zadok's family alone held the office (1 Kings 2:26–27, 35).
- **"Consecrate yourselves, you and your brothers" (v12).** "Consecrate" means to make oneself holy, set apart for God's use. It involved washing, clean clothes and abstaining from anything that made a person ritually unclean, including sexual relations for a time, as at Mount Sinai (Exod 19:10–15). JFB notes it was required on grave occasions. Henry: "The breach upon Uzza made the priests more careful to sanctify themselves."
- **"Because you did not carry it the first time, the LORD our God broke out against us, because we did not seek him according to the rule" (v13).** David names the failure plainly. "Broke out" is *parats*, the word of Perez-uzza (13:11). "Seek" is *darash*, the word for what Saul never did (10:14). The first time, Israel sought the ark with enthusiasm but did not seek God "according to the rule." That word is *mishpat*, the prescribed order or ordinance. And note the pronouns: "you did not carry it… the LORD broke out against us… we did not seek him." David does not blame Uzzah alone. He counts himself in. Guzik's point is that David had consulted people but not God (13:1–4).
- **"The Levites carried the ark of God on their shoulders with the poles, as Moses had commanded according to the word of the LORD" (v15).** This is the turning point of the two chapters. When the tribes brought gifts for the tabernacle in the wilderness, Moses gave carts and oxen to the Gershonites and Merarites for the heavy frames and curtains, "but to the sons of Kohath he gave none, because they were charged with the service of the holy things that had to be carried on the shoulder" (Num 7:6–9). The ark was never meant to ride on a cart. Holy things were carried by consecrated people. In fifteen verses the chapter has pointed back to God's law three times (vv2, 13, 15).
- **"David also commanded the chiefs of the Levites to appoint their brothers as the singers" (v16).** David gives the order, but the Levite leaders choose the musicians. Guzik sees a lesson in delegation: David trusted skilled people with work they understood. **"To raise sounds of joy"** is the purpose. Obedience did not mean the end of celebration. It meant celebration done God's way.
- **"Heman… Asaph… Ethan" (v17).** The three chief singers from the three Levite clans, as in 6:33–47. **Ethan son of Kushaiah** is the "Ethan son of Kishi" of 6:44. The Tyndale notes count three lists of musicians across chapters 15–16: one by rank (vv16–18), one by instrument and skill (vv19–22), and one of those appointed to serve before the ark afterward (16:4–6).
- **"Obed-edom and Jeiel" (v18).** The man whose house God had blessed while the ark stayed there (13:14) now appears among the gatekeepers and the musicians (vv18, 21, 24). God's blessing on his household turned into a lifetime of service near the ark. His descendants were still gatekeepers generations later (26:4–8).
- **"Bronze cymbals… harps according to Alamoth… lyres according to the Sheminith" (vv19–21).** The cymbals kept the beat and signaled the start of songs. **Alamoth** is related to *almah*, "young woman," and probably means a high or soprano range. Psalm 46 carries the same term in its title. **Sheminith** means "the eighth," perhaps a lower octave or an eight-stringed instrument (titles of Psalms 6 and 12). The Tyndale notes take both as vocal ranges. These are some of the earliest musical directions recorded anywhere.
- **"Chenaniah, leader of the Levites in music, should direct the music, for he understood it" (v22).** He was chosen for skill, not birth, as JFB notes. The Hebrew word translated "music" here, *massa*, usually means a "burden" or something carried. It can also mean "lifting up," as of the voice. So some translators take Chenaniah to have been in charge of the carrying of the ark rather than the singing. JFB mentions both readings. Either way, the Chronicler honors a man who knew his work. Henry adds that skilled musicians should "not grudge to make others as wise as themselves."
- **"The priests should blow the trumpets before the ark of God" (v24).** Silver trumpets were the priests' instruments, used to call the people and announce God's festivals (Num 10:1–10). **"Berechiah and Elkanah… Obed-edom and Jehiah were to be gatekeepers for the ark"** (vv23–24). JFB suggests the first pair walked in front and the second behind, guarding the ark on the move so that no one would touch it carelessly again. Guzik, following Matthew Poole, makes the same point.
- **"David and the elders of Israel and the commanders of thousands went to bring up the ark… with rejoicing" (v25).** The leaders of the nation walked with it. The word "rejoicing" is the same joy that ended chapter 12.
- **"And because God helped the Levites who were carrying the ark… they sacrificed seven bulls and seven rams" (v26).** Compare 13:10: there, God struck down the man who handled the ark wrongly. Here, God "helped" the men who carried it rightly. The ark was not too heavy for them. Guzik and Henry both suggest that God's help meant steadiness and calm for men who must have been afraid after what happened to Uzzah. 2 Samuel 6:13 adds that after the first six steps David sacrificed an ox and a fattened animal, a sign of thanks that they had been allowed to go on. Seven bulls and seven rams was a full, complete offering (compare Job 42:8).
- **"David was clothed with a robe of fine linen… And David wore a linen ephod" (v27).** Fine linen was the dress of priests and Levites. A linen ephod was a simple sleeveless garment worn by those serving at the sanctuary. The boy Samuel wore one (1 Sam 2:18), and so did the 85 priests of Nob (1 Sam 22:18). The king did not appear in royal robes. He dressed like the Levites around him. JFB notes the ephod was worn by priests but was not reserved to them alone. Guzik notes that David dressed like the other worshipers, putting himself among them.
- **"With shouting, to the sound of the horn, trumpets, and cymbals" (v28).** The "horn" is the *shofar*, the ram's horn. Every kind of sound Israel could make went up together. The scene has long been linked with Psalm 24, a psalm of David: "Lift up your heads, O gates!… that the King of glory may come in" (Ps 24:7), and with Psalm 132, which remembers David's vow to find "a dwelling place for the Mighty One of Jacob" (Ps 132:5). Solomon later quoted Psalm 132 when the ark moved into the temple (2 Chr 6:41).
- **"Michal the daughter of Saul looked out of the window and saw King David dancing and celebrating, and she despised him in her heart" (v29).** The Chronicler gives one verse to what 2 Samuel 6:16–23 tells at length, the angry exchange between Michal and David and her childlessness afterward. He calls her "the daughter of Saul" rather than David's wife. The Tyndale notes suggest the point: Saul did not seek the ark (13:3), and his daughter despised the joy of its arrival. She watched from a window instead of joining the worship. JFB calls her scorn an outburst of "spleen." Guzik, quoting G. Campbell Morgan, says the earthly-minded cannot understand spiritual gladness. Henry answers her well: "her despising him did not make him at all despicable."
- **Commentators:** Henry notes, "It is not any disparagement to the greatest of men to show themselves zealous in the acts of devotion." He sees the sacrifices as thanks for God's help and as atonement for the earlier failure. Guzik draws three principles from vv11–15: work that pleases God is done in the right way, by consecrated people, for the right goal. He insists that the first attempt did not fail because it was big or joyful, but because it came from man's ideas rather than God's word. He also argues that joy in worship should be neither manufactured nor suppressed. JFB adds that the elders and commanders joined the procession to model reverence for the people.

#### Pointing to Christ
The ark was God's throne on earth, and this chapter shows it carried into Zion by consecrated men, with sacrifice, singing and a king dressed as a humble worshiper. The difference between death in chapter 13 and joy in chapter 15 was obedience to what God had said. Christ is the one in whom God came to dwell among his people: "the Word became flesh and dwelt [literally 'tabernacled'] among us" (John 1:14). He did not need to be carried. He carried himself up to Jerusalem, with crowds shouting, and the leaders of the city despised him as Michal despised David (Luke 19:37–40). Christ is also the King who went up after his victory, "with a shout… with the sound of a trumpet" (Ps 47:5), and who entered the true sanctuary, heaven itself, "once for all… by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12, 24). The gates of Psalm 24 lift their heads for him.
