*Setting:* Laban has gone home after the covenant at the heap of witness in Gilead (31:55). Jacob is free of his father-in-law but heading straight toward the man he fears most. Twenty years earlier he took Esau's blessing by deceit, and Esau swore to kill him once their father died (27:41). Rebekah promised to send for Jacob when Esau's anger cooled (27:45), but no message ever came. Now Jacob is east of the Jordan, in the hill country of Gilead, with two wives, two servant wives, eleven sons, a daughter, and large flocks. Esau has settled far to the south in Seir, the mountain country of Edom. Dates are approximate, somewhere in the early second millennium BC. Chapter 32 is framed by two meetings with God's messengers: angels at the start and a mysterious wrestler at the end. Between them Jacob plans, prays, and plans again. By morning he has a new name and a limp.

#### Jacob Fears Esau (vv1–21)
- **"The angels of God met him" (v1).** The last time Jacob saw angels was at Bethel, as he fled the land, when they were going up and down a stairway (28:12). Now, as he comes back, angels meet him again. The two visions bracket his years away. God sent him out with angels and welcomes him back with them.
- **"This is God's camp!" So he called… Mahanaim (v2).** The ESV footnote says *Mahanaim* means "two camps," and the lexicon agrees. Most interpreters take the two camps as God's camp and Jacob's camp, side by side. The name will echo in v7, when Jacob in fear divides his people into two camps of his own. Mahanaim later became a town in Gilead on the border of Gad (Josh 13:26), where Ish-bosheth reigned and David took refuge from Absalom (2 Sam 2:8; 17:24).
- **Angels and messengers (v3).** The Tyndale notes point out a wordplay. The Hebrew word *mal'akim* means both "angels" (v1) and "messengers" (v3). God's *mal'akim* came to Jacob, and now Jacob sends his own *mal'akim* to Esau. The reader is invited to ask which messengers Jacob is really trusting.
- **"My lord Esau… your servant Jacob" (v4).** Jacob turns the old blessing upside down. Isaac had said, "Be lord over your brothers" (27:29), and the oracle before birth said "the older shall serve the younger" (25:23). Now Jacob calls Esau "lord" and himself "servant." He is not giving up the promise, but he is careful not to flaunt it. He lists his wealth (v5) to show he has not come to claim Isaac's estate and has no need of Esau's goods.
- **"Four hundred men with him" (v6).** The messengers bring no word of Esau's mood, only the size of his band. Four hundred men was a small army. Abraham defeated four kings with 318 (14:14), and David's band numbered about four hundred (1 Sam 22:2). Jacob can only assume the worst.
- **"Greatly afraid and distressed" (v7).** Jacob stood up to Laban with boldness, but he knows he wronged Esau. He divides his people and flocks into "two camps" so that one may escape if the other is attacked (v8). It is shrewd, and it is fearful. The man who just saw God's camp now arranges his own.
- **Jacob's prayer (vv9–12).** This is the longest prayer Jacob ever prays, and it is a model of how to pray in fear. Jacob addresses God by his covenant with Abraham and Isaac and by his personal name, the LORD. He reminds God of his own command, "Return to your country" (31:3, 13). He confesses, "I am not worthy of the least of all the deeds of steadfast love and all the faithfulness" (v10). "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love, and "faithfulness" is *emet*, his reliability. Jacob crossed the Jordan with only a staff, and now he has "become two camps." The same phrase that showed his fear in v7 now shows God's generosity. His request is plain: "Please deliver me from the hand of my brother" (v11). He fears for "the mothers with the children," which pictures a massacre (compare Hos 10:14).
- **"Make your offspring as the sand of the sea" (v12).** At Bethel God promised Jacob offspring like "the dust of the earth" (28:14). Jacob quotes it in the form God gave Abraham after Mount Moriah, "as the sand that is on the seashore" (22:17). He holds God to his word. If Esau wipes out the family, the promise cannot stand.
- **The present (vv13–15).** Jacob chooses goats, sheep, camels, cattle and donkeys: 550 animals in the listed totals, plus the camels' calves. The proportion of males to females (one ram to ten ewes) matches sound breeding practice. This gift was meant to be useful, a herd that could keep growing. The word for "present" is *minchah*, which the lexicon gives as "gift, tribute, offering." It is the word later used for the grain offering to God (Lev 2:1), and the word for Cain's and Abel's offerings (4:3–4).
- **Drove after drove (vv16–20).** Jacob sends the animals in separate droves with gaps between them. Each servant repeats the same speech: these belong to "your servant Jacob," a present for "my lord Esau," and "he is behind us." Wave after wave of gifts was meant to soften Esau before the brothers met.
- **Face to face (v20).** Jacob's thought is built on the Hebrew word *panim*, "face." The ESV footnotes give the literal sense: "I may appease his face with the present that goes before my face, and afterward I shall see his face. Perhaps he will lift my face." Verse 21 adds that the present passed on "before his face." "Appease" is *kaphar*, which the lexicon gives here as "cover over, pacify." The lexicon explains it as covering Esau's face so that he does not see the offence. The same verb is used later for making atonement. All this "face" language prepares for v30, when Jacob sees the face of God, and for 33:10, when he says Esau's face is like the face of God.
- **"He himself stayed that night in the camp" (v21).** The gifts go ahead, and Jacob stays behind. The man who prayed for deliverance is still trying to manage the outcome himself.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives the chapter a fine opening: "Never did so many memorable things occur in any march as in this of Jacob's little family." On the prayer, he writes, "Times of fear should be times of prayer," and "Those are best prepared for the greatest mercies that see themselves unworthy of the least." He adds, "The best we can say to God in prayer is what he has said to us." JFB calls the prayer "the first recorded example of prayer in the Bible." That is not strictly so, since Abraham interceded for Sodom (18:23–32) and Abraham's servant prayed at the well (24:12–14), though Jacob's is the first long personal prayer of this kind. Henry counts the gift as "580 in all," which includes thirty calves. Tyndale counts about 550, the totals listed. Guzik reads the gift as Jacob lapsing back into scheming right after he prayed, and argues that a trusting Jacob would have gone first, not last. He also notes, as a reason for Jacob's fear, that Rebekah never sent the message she promised. The Tyndale notes are kinder to the prayer itself, calling it an example of humility and dependence.

#### Jacob Wrestles with God (vv22–32)
- **"Crossed the ford of the Jabbok" (v22).** The Jabbok (today the Zarqa River) flows west into the Jordan through deep gorges, splitting Gilead in two. Jacob sends everyone and everything across in the night (v23). The ESV footnote says "children" may be "sons"; the list counts eleven, and Dinah is not counted. The Tyndale notes point out the sound play: *Ya'aqob* (Jacob) at the *Yabboq* (Jabbok) *ye'abeq* (wrestled). The place, the man and the act all sound alike.
- **"Jacob was left alone" (v24).** For the first time in the story, Jacob has no family, no flocks, no plan and no one to manage. That is where God meets him.
- **"A man wrestled with him until the breaking of the day" (v24).** The verb *abaq* appears only here and in v25. The lexicon links it to the word for "dust," as in getting dusty in a wrestling match. The text calls the wrestler simply "a man." Only as the night goes on does Jacob, and the reader, learn who he is. Hosea calls him "the angel" and says Jacob "strove with God" (Hos 12:3–4). Jacob himself says, "I have seen God face to face" (v30). Many Christians from the early church on have seen in him an appearance of the Son of God before his incarnation, like the angel of the LORD elsewhere in Genesis (16:7–13; 22:11–16). Others prefer to say only that God met Jacob in the form of a man or through his angel. The text leaves the mystery in place.
- **"He did not prevail against Jacob" (v25).** Jacob has been wrestling all his life: with Esau in the womb (25:22–26), with his father's blessing, with Laban. Now he wrestles with God and holds on. Then the man "touched his hip socket," and the hip came out of joint. One touch was enough. The match had only seemed even because the man allowed it.
- **"I will not let you go unless you bless me" (v26).** Something has changed. Jacob can no longer fight, so he clings. Hosea adds a detail Genesis leaves out: "he wept and sought his favor" (Hos 12:4). The man who once stole a blessing by disguise now asks openly for one, from the only one who can give it. The man's request, "Let me go, for the day has broken," may reflect the idea that no one could see God's face and live (Exod 33:20). If Jacob saw him in daylight, he might die.
- **"What is your name?" (v27).** The last time Jacob was asked his name, by his blind father, he lied: "I am Esau your firstborn" (27:18–19). This time he tells the truth: "Jacob." In Genesis 25:26 the name was linked to the heel Jacob grabbed at birth, and Esau later played on it: "Is he not rightly named Jacob? For he has cheated me these two times" (27:36). The lexicon gives the popular sense "supplanter," from "heel," and notes the verb "overreach." The Tyndale notes say that in giving his name Jacob confessed his character.
- **"Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel" (v28).** The ESV footnote says "Israel means He strives with God, or God strives." The text gives the reason for the name: "you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed." The verb is *sarah*, which the lexicon defines as "persist, exert oneself, persevere." It occurs only here and in Hosea 12:3. The lexicon's own reading of the name is "El persisteth" or "Let El persist," and it notes that others take it as "El contends" or "Let El contend." In Hebrew names built with *El*, God is usually the subject (as in Daniel, "God is my judge"). So the name's grammar points to "God strives" or "God persists," while the narrator's explanation plays on Jacob striving with God. Both meanings hold together: Jacob has wrestled with God, and from now on God will fight for him. The older rendering "a prince with God" (Henry, following the KJV) and Guzik's preference for "God rules" come from linking the name to a different Hebrew root meaning "to rule." The lexicon used here does not support that link, though it remains a traditional reading. The name Israel is confirmed at Bethel in 35:10, yet Jacob continues to be called by both names throughout the rest of Genesis. Unlike Abram, whom the narrator never again calls by his old name after 17:5, Jacob's old self lingers.
- **"Why is it that you ask my name?" (v29).** Jacob has given his name, but the wrestler will not give his. In the ancient world, to know a name was in some sense to have a hold on the one named. God is not at Jacob's disposal. The same refusal meets Manoah: "Why do you ask my name, seeing it is wonderful?" (Judg 13:18). Instead of a name, "there he blessed him." Jacob gets what he asked for, not what he tried to grab.
- **"Peniel… I have seen God face to face" (v30).** The ESV footnote says "Peniel means the face of God." The lexicon gives the same meaning, and it notes that the old Greek translation rendered the place name "form of God" here. The next verse spells it *Penuel*, the form used for the town later (Judg 8:8; 1 Kgs 12:25). "Face to face" does not mean Jacob saw God's full glory, which no one can see and live (Exod 33:20). It means the meeting was direct and personal (compare Exod 33:11; Deut 34:10). The phrase "my life has been delivered" uses *natsal*, the same verb as Jacob's prayer, "Please deliver me" (v11). The Tyndale notes draw the conclusion: if Jacob could meet God and survive, he had nothing to fear from Esau. His prayer was answered before Esau even arrived.
- **"The sun rose upon him… limping because of his hip" (v31).** When Jacob fled the land, "the sun had set" (28:11). Now, as he returns, the sun rises. He walks into the morning a changed man, and he walks with a limp. The blessing and the wound came together.
- **"The sinew of the thigh" (v32).** The narrator explains a custom: Israelites did not eat the sinew at the hip socket. This is usually identified with the sciatic nerve. It is not in the law of Moses, but later Jewish law made it binding (Mishnah, Hullin 7), and observant Jews still remove it. Every meal kept the memory of the night at the Jabbok alive.
- **Commentators:** All three see the wrestler as divine. Henry gives several views, including that it was "the eternal Word, the angel of the covenant," and JFB calls him "an angel" who was also God, most likely "the angel of the covenant." Guzik is the most direct, calling him Jesus before the incarnation. On the touch, Henry says the angel put out Jacob's thigh "to show him what he could do, and that it was God he was wrestling with." Guzik and the Tyndale notes agree that the wound broke Jacob's self-reliance, so that clinging was all he had left. On the name, Henry follows the KJV's "a prince with God," and Guzik prefers "God rules." As noted above, the lexicon defines the verb as "persist" or "strive," not "rule." JFB notes that "the old name was not to be abandoned," which fits the way both names keep appearing. On the refused name, JFB says it was withheld so Jacob "might not be too elated with his conquest." On the limp, JFB compares Paul's thorn in the flesh (2 Cor 12:7): when believers are blessed, "there is always something to humble them." Henry adds that Jacob had the blessing "though he went limping to his grave." JFB explains the sinew as "the nerve that fastens the thigh bone in its socket" and says the custom is traditional, not part of the law of Moses. JFB has no notes on vv5, 7–8, 27 or 30.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jacob wanted a blessing all his life and tried to take it by his own strength. At the Jabbok God blessed him by first breaking that strength. The one who wrestled with him came as "a man," and yet Jacob said, "I have seen God face to face." In Jesus the Word became flesh, and "we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). No one has ever seen God, but "the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (John 1:18). The name *Israel* passed to a nation and then, in the New Testament, to all who belong to Christ, "the Israel of God" (Gal 6:16). And the pattern of the night, blessing through weakness, is the pattern of the cross. Christ himself was wounded, and by his wounds his people are healed (Isa 53:5). Paul learned the same lesson Jacob did: "when I am weak, then I am strong" (2 Cor 12:10).
