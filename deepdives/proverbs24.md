*Setting:* Proverbs 24 finishes the "words of the wise" (22:17–24:22), the collection of about thirty sayings that began in chapter 22, and then adds a short appendix headed "These also are sayings of the wise" (vv23–34). Who these wise teachers were isn't stated, probably royal scribes or court teachers whose sayings were gathered alongside Solomon's (see Proverbs 22). The ESV has no headings until v23, so the first headings below follow its paragraph breaks. The chapter has some of the book's most searching sayings: the call to rescue those being led to death and the warning that God knows when we say "we did not know" (vv11–12), the righteous man who falls seven times and rises (v16), the command not to gloat over a fallen enemy (vv17–18), and the closing walk past a sluggard's ruined vineyard (vv30–34).

#### Wisdom Builds a House (vv1–7)
- **"Be not envious of evil men, nor desire to be with them, for their hearts devise violence, and their lips talk of trouble" (vv1–2).** The third warning against envying sinners in this collection (23:17; 24:19). The wicked look like they're having fun and getting ahead, but listen to what they actually plan and talk about: violence and trouble. That's the gang of 1:10–19. Henry: "Envy not sinners." Guzik passes on Bridges' line that envy "curses our blessings, withers our virtues, destroys our peace."
- **"By wisdom a house is built, and by understanding it is established; by knowledge the rooms are filled with all precious and pleasant riches" (vv3–4).** Three words for wisdom, three stages of building: build, establish, fill. JFB notes "house" includes the family. The same three words describe how God made the world: "The LORD by wisdom founded the earth; by understanding he established the heavens; by his knowledge the deeps broke open" (3:19–20). A wise home is built the way God built creation. Wisdom herself "has built her house" (9:1), and "the wisest of women builds her house" (14:1). Guzik passes on Garrett's reading of the "riches" as a loving family and a sense of security, not just possessions. Jesus' parable of the wise and foolish builders makes the same point (Matt 7:24–27).
- **"A wise man is full of strength… for by wise guidance you can wage your war, and in abundance of counselors there is victory" (vv5–6).** Wisdom is stronger than muscle (21:22; Eccl 9:16). Verse 6 repeats 11:14 and 20:18. The best leaders aren't the ones who know everything but the ones who listen to many advisers. Henry: "Piety and prudence in outward affairs, both go together to complete a wise man."
- **"Wisdom is too high for a fool; in the gate he does not open his mouth" (v7).** "Too high" may be a pun. The Hebrew word (*ra'mot*) means "high things" but also "corals," precious items out of reach (Job 28:18). The gate was where the town council and court met. When serious matters come up, the fool has nothing to say. Usually fools talk too much (18:2), but in the place where wisdom is needed, he is silent. The Tyndale notes: leaders need to be wise.
- **Commentators:** JFB gives mostly cross-references here. Guzik and Henry both read vv5–6 as applying to spiritual battles too. Guzik passes on Waltke's point that the fool can't contribute to public decisions.

#### Rescue Those Being Taken to Death (vv8–12)
- **"Whoever plans to do evil will be called a schemer. The devising of folly is sin, and the scoffer is an abomination to mankind" (vv8–9).** "Schemer" is literally "master of plots." People get a reputation from what they plan, even before they act. JFB: he is "so called" even if he doesn't carry it out. Verse 9 says the planning itself is sin. Jesus said the same about lust and hatred in the heart (Matt 5:21–28). Henry: even the first stirrings of sin need repentance. The scoffer is "an abomination to mankind," not only to God. People can't stand him.
- **"If you faint in the day of adversity, your strength is small" (v10).** A wordplay: "adversity" is *tsarah* and "small" is *tsar*, as if to say "in a tight spot, your strength is tight." Trouble doesn't create weakness. It reveals it. JFB: strength "is then truly tested." The Tyndale notes point to Daniel and his friends, who stood firm under pressure (Dan 1; 3). Guzik: we must become weak in ourselves to be strong in God (2 Cor 12:10). Henry: "Under troubles we are apt to despair of relief," but God strengthens. Isaiah: "they who wait for the LORD shall renew their strength" (Isa 40:31).
- **"Rescue those who are being taken away to death; hold back those who are stumbling to the slaughter" (v11).** One of the strongest calls to action in the book. The picture may be innocent people led off to execution, or prisoners and captives, or anyone headed for ruin. "Stumbling" is literally "tottering." JFB takes them as unjustly condemned. The Tyndale notes point to Job, who rescued the poor and the fatherless (Job 29:12–17). Guzik gives Esther as the model, who risked her life to save her people (Esth 4:14–16). Christians have long read this verse as a call to speak for the unborn, the trafficked, the persecuted and the spiritually lost.
- **"If you say, 'Behold, we did not know this,' does not he who weighs the heart perceive it? Does not he who keeps watch over your soul know it, and will he not repay man according to his work?" (v12).** The excuse is ignorance, and God sees through it. He "weighs the heart" (16:2; 21:2) and knows what we knew. The God who "keeps watch over your soul" sees when we refuse to watch over the lives of others. Cain asked, "Am I my brother's keeper?" (Gen 4:9). The priest and Levite passed by on the other side (Luke 10:31–32). "Repay man according to his work" is quoted by Paul (Rom 2:6) and echoed by Jesus (Matt 16:27). Henry: a person who knows a neighbor faces harm "is bound to do all in his power to deliver him."
- **Commentators:** JFB: neglecting a known duty is sin. Guzik passes on Meyer's warning that we can't ignore evil and still claim innocence, and Waltke's point that God will repay the deed of neglect. All three agree vv11–12 condemn deliberate looking away.

#### Honey, the Righteous Who Rise, and the Fallen Enemy (vv13–18)
- **"My son, eat honey, for it is good… Know that wisdom is such to your soul" (vv13–14).** Honey was the sweetest thing an Israelite knew, and good for you too (Jonathan's eyes "became bright" after tasting it, 1 Sam 14:27). Wisdom is the same: sweet and nourishing. "The drippings of the honeycomb" was a picture of pure sweetness (Ps 19:10, where God's word is "sweeter also than honey and drippings of the honeycomb"). The promise of 23:18 is repeated: "there will be a future, and your hope will not be cut off." JFB: wisdom's rewards should stir desire for it, as good food stirs appetite.
- **"Lie not in wait as a wicked man against the dwelling of the righteous; do no violence to his home" (v15).** Addressed directly to the would-be attacker, like a warning sign. Don't try it, because…
- **"For the righteous falls seven times and rises again, but the wicked stumble in times of calamity" (v16).** "Seven times" means again and again, as the Tyndale notes say. The "fall" here is mainly trouble and disaster, not sin, as Henry notes, though it has long been applied to both. The righteous get knocked down many times and keep getting up, because God lifts them (Ps 37:24: "though he fall, he shall not be cast headlong, for the LORD upholds his hand"). The wicked fall once and stay down. Micah: "Rejoice not over me, O my enemy; when I fall, I shall rise" (Mic 7:8). Paul: "struck down, but not destroyed" (2 Cor 4:9). Guzik says it can apply to both trouble and sin, and passes on Clarke's line that the righteous come out of each furnace brighter.
- **"Do not rejoice when your enemy falls, and let not your heart be glad when he stumbles, lest the LORD see it and be displeased, and turn away his anger from him" (vv17–18).** The verb "falls" repeats from v16, but now it's the enemy falling. Gloating is forbidden. The reason given is surprising: God might turn his anger away from the enemy, meaning God may be more displeased with your gloating than with your enemy. Job counted it among his innocence that he had not "rejoiced at the ruin of him who hated me" (Job 31:29). David wept over Saul (2 Sam 1:11–12). Edom was judged for gloating over Judah's fall (Obad 12). The Tyndale notes: this prepares for Jesus' teaching, "Love your enemies" (Matt 5:44). JFB: no one should gloat over evildoers' downfall. Henry: taking pleasure in an enemy's misfortune is forbidden.
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Caesar's reported grief at Pompey's death ("I wanted victory, not revenge") and Ross's dry note that anyone who wants God's anger to continue against the wicked had better not gloat. Henry reads v16 of hardship rather than deliberate sin.

#### Fear the LORD and the King (vv19–22)
- **"Fret not yourself because of evildoers, and be not envious of the wicked, for the evil man has no future; the lamp of the wicked will be put out" (vv19–20).** Nearly word for word from Psalm 37:1, David's psalm on not envying the wicked. "Fret" is literally "get heated." Guzik passes on Garrett's view that "infuriated" is closer. "No future" is the same word as in v14 and 23:18, *acharit*, "end" or "afterward." The wise have a future; the wicked don't. "Lamp put out" means life and family line ended (13:9; 20:20).
- **"My son, fear the LORD and the king, and do not join with those who do otherwise" (v21).** God first, the king second. Peter echoes this: "Fear God. Honor the emperor" (1 Pet 2:17). "Those who do otherwise" is literally "those who change," people who push for revolution. Absalom and Sheba are biblical examples of rebels who ended in disaster (2 Sam 15–20). The apostles show that obeying God sometimes means defying rulers (Acts 5:29), so the order of the two matters.
- **"For disaster will arise suddenly from them, and who knows the ruin that will come from them both?" (v22).** "Them both" is most likely God and the king, who both punish rebellion. JFB takes that view and mentions the alternative, the rebels' own ruin. The old Greek translation adds several lines here about the king's power.
- **Commentators:** Guzik balances v21 with Acts 5:29 and Romans 13:1–7, and Henry says the godly live peaceably and stay away from those who stir up upheaval. The Tyndale notes say the king is linked to the Lord as his representative on earth (1 Sam 12; Ps 2).

#### More Sayings of the Wise (vv23–34)
- **"These also are sayings of the wise" (v23).** A short appendix to the thirty sayings. JFB: "These" introduces more sayings "of the wise."
- **"Partiality in judging is not good. Whoever says to the wicked, 'You are in the right,' will be cursed by peoples, abhorred by nations" (vv23–24).** "Partiality" is literally "to recognize faces," judging by who someone is rather than what they did (Deut 1:17). Calling the guilty innocent isn't just wrong before God (17:15). It makes a judge hated by whole nations. Guzik notes that modern culture often praises evil in the language of righteousness, which this rejects (Isa 5:20).
- **"But those who rebuke the wicked will have delight, and a good blessing will come upon them" (v25).** Honest judges who name wrong as wrong are blessed. Nathan confronted David, and David's response shows a rebuke can bring life (2 Sam 12:1–13).
- **"Whoever gives an honest answer kisses the lips" (v26).** A kiss was a sign of friendship and honor in the ancient Near East. An honest answer is as welcome as a friend's kiss. JFB: "kiss his lips" means love and homage, and "right answer" means plain, honest words. The Tyndale notes: a true friend doesn't hide problems. Compare 27:5–6: "Faithful are the wounds of a friend; profuse are the kisses of an enemy." Judas would later betray with a kiss (Luke 22:48).
- **"Prepare your work outside; get everything ready for yourself in the field, and after that build your house" (v27).** Get the farm working before you build the house, and make sure there's an income before spending it. "Build a house" can also mean "start a family" (Ruth 4:11), so it may also say: be able to provide before marrying. JFB: secure a livelihood first. Guzik passes on Garrett's summary: produce before consuming. Henry: meet basic needs before luxuries, and avoid debt. Jesus warned about the builder who didn't count the cost (Luke 14:28–30).
- **"Be not a witness against your neighbor without cause, and do not deceive with your lips. Do not say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me'" (vv28–29).** Two linked sayings. Don't testify against someone needlessly, and don't let revenge be your reason. "I will do to him as he has done to me" twists the law of "eye for eye" (meant to limit punishment in court, Exod 21:24) into a personal license for payback. Compare 20:22. Jesus: "Do not resist the one who is evil" (Matt 5:38–39). Paul: "See that no one repays anyone evil for evil" (1 Thess 5:15).
- **"I passed by the field of a sluggard, by the vineyard of a man lacking sense… it was all overgrown with thorns; the ground was covered with nettles, and its stone wall was broken down" (vv30–31).** A short story told in the first person, like 7:6–23. Nobody attacked this vineyard. No one had to. Thorns grew, weeds spread, stones fell from the wall, and wild animals could walk in (Isa 5:5). Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line that "neglect is all that is needed to produce evil."
- **"Then I saw and considered it; I looked and received instruction" (v32).** The wise learn from others' mistakes without having to repeat them. Four verbs: saw, considered, looked, received.
- **"A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest, and poverty will come upon you like a robber, and want like an armed man" (vv33–34).** This repeats 6:10–11 almost word for word. The words "a little" are the trap. No single nap ruins the vineyard. A habit of small delays does. Then poverty arrives suddenly, like a robber who breaks in at night. Henry applies it to the soul: an overgrown field pictures a neglected soul. Hebrews warns about neglecting "such a great salvation" (Heb 2:3).
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Henry both apply the sluggard's vineyard to the neglected spiritual life, following Bridges. JFB: learn wisdom from the sluggard's folly. Henry finds three faults of a witness in vv28–29, and the verses themselves name them: testifying without cause, deceiving, and testifying for revenge.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 24 calls on its readers to "rescue those who are being taken away to death" (v11) and warns that no one can plead "we did not know." Jesus did more than rescue. He went to death himself in our place, "led like a lamb to the slaughter" (Isa 53:7), so that those stumbling toward judgment could be brought back. He refused to gloat over his enemies (vv17–18) but wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41) and prayed for those crucifying him (Luke 23:34). He would not pay back what was done to him (v29), for "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return" (1 Pet 2:23). And he is the reason the righteous rise again after every fall (v16): the one who fell into death and rose on the third day promises that his people, though struck down, will rise with him (John 11:25).
