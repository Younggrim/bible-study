*Setting:* Jerusalem, early in Jehoiakim's reign (609–598 BC), on the day after the sermon of chapter 19. Jeremiah had smashed a clay flask in the Valley of Hinnom before Judah's elders and priests, then climbed to the temple court and announced that the city would be broken the same way. This chapter tells what that cost him. It opens with the first recorded physical attack on Jeremiah, and then gives the last and darkest of his "confessions," the private prayers scattered through chapters 11–20 where he pours out his heart to God. These are some of the rawest words in the Bible. They run from a burning sense of call, through a shout of praise, and then straight into a curse on the day he was born.

#### Jeremiah Persecuted by Pashhur (vv1–6)
- **"Pashhur the priest, the son of Immer" (v1).** Immer was one of the twenty-four priestly families David organized (1 Chr 24:14), so this man was a priest from an established line. He is not the Pashhur son of Malchiah of 21:1 and 38:1. The name was common. Most scholars think it is Egyptian in origin. Whether this is the father of the Gedaliah of 38:1 is uncertain.
- **"Chief officer in the house of the LORD" (v1).** Pashhur ran temple security, second in rank to the high priest. He was responsible for keeping order in the courts and dealing with troublemakers. Jeremiah 29:26 describes the same office later held by Zephaniah, with orders to put "every madman who prophesies" in the stocks. To Pashhur, a man shouting in the temple court that the temple would fall was exactly that.
- **"Pashhur beat Jeremiah the prophet" (v2).** The word probably means a formal flogging. The Law allowed up to forty lashes (Deut 25:3). Notice the title: Jeremiah *the prophet*. The narrator reminds us whose messenger is being beaten.
- **"Put him in the stocks" (v2).** The Hebrew *mahpeketh* comes from a root meaning to turn or twist. The lexicon describes an instrument that forced the body into a crooked, cramped position, so the pain grew the longer a prisoner stayed in it. King Asa used the same punishment on the seer Hanani (2 Chr 16:10). Paul and Silas would later sit in the stocks in Philippi and sing (Acts 16:24–25).
- **"The upper Benjamin Gate of the house of the LORD" (v2).** This was a gate on the north side of the temple area, facing the territory of Benjamin, Jeremiah's own tribe. The stocks stood in public view, near the busiest entrance, so the humiliation was part of the punishment. Jeremiah spent the night there.
- **"The LORD does not call your name Pashhur, but Terror on Every Side" (v3).** In Hebrew, *Magor-missabib*. Released the next morning, Jeremiah does not thank his jailer or ask for mercy. He gives him a new name. A name in the Bible often stands for a person's character or destiny. This man who used terror on a prophet will himself become a walking warning. The phrase "terror on every side" is one of Jeremiah's signature sayings (6:25; 46:5; 49:29) and is also found in Psalm 31:13 and Lamentations 2:22.
- **"I will make you a terror to yourself and to all your friends" (v4).** Pashhur's fear will not only be felt. It will spread. He will watch his friends fall by the sword and know that his own lies brought it on them.
- **"I will give all Judah into the hand of the king of Babylon" (v4).** This is the first time in the book that Babylon is named as the enemy. Until now Jeremiah had spoken of a nameless foe from the north (1:14; 4:6; 6:22). After the battle of Carchemish in 605 BC, when Nebuchadnezzar crushed Egypt, there was no doubt who that foe was.
- **"All the wealth of the city… and all the treasures of the kings of Judah" (v5).** Four words pile up: wealth, gains, prized belongings, treasures. Isaiah had warned Hezekiah of the same thing a century earlier (Isa 39:6). It came true in stages: in 597 BC (2 Kgs 24:13) and finally in 586 BC (2 Kgs 25:13–17), when even the bronze pillars of the temple were broken up and hauled away.
- **"To Babylon you shall go, and there you shall die" (v6).** Pashhur would most likely be taken in the first deportation of 597 BC, with King Jehoiachin and the leading men. Burial in a foreign land was a deep disgrace for an Israelite, especially a priest.
- **"To whom you have prophesied falsely" (v6).** So Pashhur was not only a policeman. He was also preaching, and his message was the opposite of Jeremiah's: peace, safety, the temple will stand. Chapters 23, 27 and 28 show how many prophets in Jerusalem were saying the same thing.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns that "when power in the church is abused, it is the most dangerous power that can be employed against it." JFB notes the blow was harder because it came from a fellow priest, a man of Jeremiah's own order, and that temple officers could arrest offenders while judging belonged to the council, which makes the beating look like Pashhur acting on his own. Guzik, drawing on Harrison and Kidner, describes the stocks as a twisting frame whose pain grew over time, which the lexicon supports. He explains the name Pashhur as meaning something like ease or freedom, so that the new name is a reversal. JFB has a similar reading ("largeness on every side"). These folk etymologies make the point, but the name's real origin is uncertain and probably Egyptian.

#### Jeremiah Complains to the LORD (vv7–18)
- **"O LORD, you have deceived me, and I was deceived" (v7).** The verb is *pathah*, to entice or persuade. It is the word for a man seducing a young woman (Exod 22:16) and for Delilah coaxing Samson's secret out of him (Judg 16:5). Jeremiah feels that God talked him into this calling, and that he gave in. The same verb also appears in 1 Kings 22:20–22, where the LORD allows a lying spirit to "entice" Ahab's prophets. Jeremiah is not saying God lied to him. He is saying God overpowered his will, and that the call was harder than he expected when he said yes as a youth (1:6–8).
- **"You are stronger than I, and you have prevailed" (v7).** It sounds like a wrestling match, and the prophet lost. **"I have become a laughingstock all the day."** He has preached destruction for twenty years and it has not come, so he has become a joke.
- **"Violence and destruction!" (v8).** Every sermon comes back to the same cry, and every time it earns him ridicule. The word of the LORD, which should be his honor, has become "a reproach and derision all day long."
- **"If I say, 'I will not mention him…'" (v9).** He has tried quitting. He resolved to go silent, to keep God's name off his lips altogether.
- **"A burning fire shut up in my bones" (v9).** It does not work. God's word is like a fire trapped inside him, and holding it in wears him out more than speaking it. Earlier the LORD had told him, "I am making my words in your mouth a fire" (5:14), and later, "Is not my word like fire?" (23:29). Psalm 39:3 and Job 32:18–20 describe the same pressure. Peter and John said it in their own way: "We cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard" (Acts 4:20). Paul did too: "Woe to me if I do not preach the gospel!" (1 Cor 9:16).
- **"I hear many whispering. Terror is on every side!" (v10).** His enemies have picked up the name he gave Pashhur and turned it into a nickname for Jeremiah himself. Old Mr. Terror-on-Every-Side, there he goes again.
- **"Say all my close friends, watching for my fall" (v10).** Literally "every man of my peace," that is, people who used to greet him with *shalom*. They are waiting for him to slip so they can report him. Compare the plot of his own family in Anathoth (11:21; 12:6), and the friend of Psalm 41:9 who "lifted his heel against me," which Jesus applied to Judas (John 13:18).
- **"Perhaps he will be deceived" (v10).** The same verb *pathah* again. His enemies hope he will be "enticed" into a mistake. Jeremiah feels squeezed: God has persuaded him on one side, and his enemies wait to trap him on the other.
- **"But the LORD is with me as a dread warrior" (v11).** The turn comes with one word: "But." The LORD had promised at his call, "They will fight against you, but they shall not prevail against you, for I am with you" (1:19). Jeremiah remembers it. The enemies who hoped to "overcome" him (v10) "will not overcome" (v11). The same Hebrew verb is used both times.
- **"Who tests the righteous, who sees the heart and the mind" (v12).** Literally "the kidneys and the heart." The kidneys stood for the deepest emotions, and the heart for thought and will. This line repeats 11:20 almost word for word. **"To you have I committed my cause."** He does not take revenge. He hands the case to the Judge.
- **"Let me see your vengeance upon them" (v12).** Like the imprecatory psalms, this is a prayer for justice, not a plan for personal payback. It is offered to God, not carried out by Jeremiah.
- **"Sing to the LORD; praise the LORD!" (v13).** A full hymn of praise, in the middle of his trouble and before anything has changed outwardly. "The needy" is the language of the Psalms for those who have no one but God to defend them (Ps 35:10; 72:12).
- **"Cursed be the day on which I was born!" (v14).** Then, with no transition, the darkest words in the book. Readers have long asked how v13 and v14 can sit side by side. Some think vv14–18 come from a different, earlier moment and were placed here to close the confessions. Others think the order is deliberate and true to life: faith and despair can follow each other within an hour. Either way, the Bible does not tidy it up.
- **Job 3.** The closest parallel is Job cursing his birthday (Job 3:1–26). Jeremiah never curses God, and he never curses his parents. He curses the day and the man who brought the news. Under the Law, cursing father or mother was a capital crime (Exod 21:17), and he stops just short of it.
- **"Cursed be the man who brought the news to my father" (v15).** In that culture the birth of a son was big news, and the messenger expected a reward. Jeremiah's father Hilkiah, a priest (1:1), would have been "very glad."
- **"Like the cities that the LORD overthrew without pity" (v16).** Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 19:24–25). **"A cry in the morning and an alarm at noon."** The sounds of a city under attack, all day long.
- **"Because he did not kill me in the womb" (v17).** This is grief speaking, not a doctrine of life. The irony is sharp. In 1:5 the LORD said, "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you… I consecrated you." Jeremiah wishes the womb had been his grave. The womb was the very place where God set him apart.
- **"Why did I come out from the womb to see toil and sorrow, and spend my days in shame?" (v18).** The chapter, and the confessions, end on a question with no answer. God does not reply here. But the next chapter shows Jeremiah back at work, giving the word of the LORD to King Zedekiah's messengers. He kept going.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls v13 the moment God "turned his sighs into songs and his tremblings into triumphs," but he does not excuse vv14–18. He treats them as a lapse, not a model, and as a warning to guard the heart against sudden despair. JFB says Jeremiah had misunderstood God's promise, which was deliverance in suffering, not exemption from it. He calls the curse on his birth intemperate. Guzik, drawing on Thompson, connects *pathah* to the seduction law of Exodus 22:16, which the lexicon supports, and the Tyndale notes make the same link. Guzik also notes, citing Ryken, that when Jeremiah traced his troubles back to the womb he did not go back far enough, because God's call came before the womb (1:5). He suggests the despair comes after the praise on purpose, to show that faith does not make the struggle disappear, and that dependence on God has to be renewed constantly.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah was beaten by the temple authorities, put on public display, mocked as a joke, betrayed by friends who watched for his fall, and he still committed his cause to the God who judges justly. Every line of that was lived again by Jesus. He was arrested by temple police, flogged, mocked, betrayed by a friend who had shared his bread, and "when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). Jesus also knew the anguish of the dark verses. In Gethsemane he said, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death" (Matt 26:38), and on the cross he cried a "Why?" of his own (Matt 27:46). But where Jeremiah wished he had never been born, Jesus came into the world for this very hour (John 12:27). The fire in Jeremiah's bones could not be put out, and neither could the Word made flesh. The tomb could not hold him.
