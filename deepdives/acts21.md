*Setting:* Spring of about AD 57. In 20:22 Paul said he was "bound by the Spirit" to go to Jerusalem. Here the binding becomes literal. This chapter turns Acts from a story of missionary travel into a story of arrest and trial. From here on Paul is a prisoner, and he remains one to the end of the book.

#### Paul Goes to Jerusalem (vv1–16)
- **"When we had parted from them" (v1).** The verb (*apospaō*) means to tear oneself away. It is the word used of Jesus withdrawing from the disciples in Gethsemane (Luke 22:41). Leaving the Ephesian elders on the beach (20:37–38) was painful.
- **Cos, Rhodes, Patara, Tyre (vv1–3).** Luke writes like a ship's log. At Rhodes the famous Colossus lay toppled, brought down by an earthquake two centuries earlier. At Patara they change to a larger ship and cross open sea, passing south of Cyprus, Barnabas's home and the first stop of Paul's first journey (ch 13).
- **"Through the Spirit they were telling Paul not to go on to Jerusalem" (v4).** This is the hardest verse in the chapter. Paul goes "constrained by the Spirit" (20:22), yet believers speaking "through the Spirit" urge him not to. The most common reading: the Spirit revealed the *danger*, and the believers drew the *conclusion* that he should stay away. That is real revelation plus human interpretation. A minority reading says Paul was stubborn and should have listened. Most commentators reject it because of 23:11, where the Lord himself stands by Paul and says, "Take courage… you must testify also in Rome." That is hardly a rebuke for disobedience.
- **"Kneeling down on the beach, we prayed" (v5).** Whole families, wives and children too, walk them out to the shore. It mirrors the Miletus farewell (20:36). Paul built deep bonds even in a week's stay.
- **Philip the evangelist (vv8–9).** The same Philip as ch 6 (one of the seven chosen to serve tables) and ch 8 (Samaria, the Ethiopian, and settling "at Caesarea," 8:40), about twenty years on. Paul once took part in the persecution that scattered Philip and his friends (8:1–4). Now he is a guest at Philip's table.
- **"Four unmarried daughters, who prophesied" (v9).** This fulfills Joel's promise quoted at Pentecost: "your sons *and your daughters* shall prophesy" (2:17). Luke records nothing they said. Early church writers (Papias, Eusebius) remembered them as valued sources on the first generation.
- **Agabus and the belt (vv10–11).** The same prophet who foretold the famine (11:28). He acts out his message the way the Old Testament prophets did: Isaiah walking barefoot (Isa 20), Jeremiah's buried loincloth (Jer 13), Ezekiel lying on his side (Ezek 4). "**Thus says the Holy Spirit**" echoes the prophets' "Thus says the LORD." The belt (*zōnē*) was a long cloth sash, long enough to bind hands and feet. "Deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles" is almost exactly what Jesus said of himself (Luke 18:32). Paul is walking his Lord's road.
- **"What are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart?" (v13).** "Breaking" (*synthryptō*) is a vivid word, like pounding something to pieces. Their tears are not changing his mind, but they are crushing his heart. Then: "**I am ready not only to be imprisoned but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus.**" He had settled it before the crisis came.
- **"Let the will of the Lord be done" (v14).** An echo of Gethsemane (Luke 22:42). Luke the narrator had been among those pleading ("we… urged him," v12). Then the whole group surrenders the question to God.
- **Mnason of Cyprus, "an early disciple" (v16).** He was perhaps a believer since Pentecost. A Hellenistic Jewish host was a wise choice for a team that included uncircumcised Gentiles, since not every Jerusalem believer would have opened his home to them.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads v4 as the Spirit revealing the danger, with "don't go" being the hearers' own application. He says Paul would otherwise have been disobeying the Spirit by going. He sums up Agabus the same way: the warnings were meant to prepare Paul, not stop him. JFB agrees that they were sent "not to prohibit him from going, but to put his courage to the test." On v13 it adds: "It was well he could add this, for he had that also to do." Henry grants that "it is a thousand pities that such a workman should be laid aside," but insists Paul in prison glorifies God as truly as Paul in the pulpit. On the stop at Tyre he notes that wherever Paul came, "he enquired what disciples were there, found them out, and associated with them."

#### Paul Visits James (vv17–26)
- **"The brothers received us gladly" (v17).** First a warm welcome from ordinary believers, then the official meeting the next day.
- **James and all the elders (v18).** This is James the brother of Jesus, leader of the Jerusalem church (15:13; Gal 1:19), remembered as "James the Just" for his devotion to the law. No apostles are mentioned, so they had probably scattered on mission by now.
- **"He related one by one the things that God had done" (v19).** A detailed report, with the credit going to *God*, "through his ministry." Luke does not say so, but this is almost certainly when the collection was handed over (24:17), the Gentile churches' gift to Jewish believers.
- **"How many thousands… all zealous for the law" (v20).** The word is *myriades*, "tens of thousands." Jewish Christianity in Judea was large and still very Jewish in practice. "Zealous" in the 50s AD also carried a heated nationalist mood that was building toward the war of AD 66.
- **The rumor (v21).** The rumor was that Paul taught Jews to abandon Moses and stop circumcising their sons. It was **false**. Paul taught that *Gentiles* need not become Jews, and that *no one* is justified by the law (Gal 2:16). But he never told Jews to give up their heritage. He circumcised Timothy (16:3) and kept a vow himself (18:18).
- **The plan (vv23–24).** Four Jewish believers were completing a Nazirite vow (Num 6:1–21). It ended with shaved heads and costly sacrifices: lambs, a ram, grain and drink offerings. Paying for them was a recognized act of piety. Paul also needed his own seven-day purification, the normal practice for a Jew returning from years in Gentile lands.
- **"As for the Gentiles… we have sent a letter" (v25).** James restates the Jerusalem Council's decision (15:20, 29). Nothing changes for Gentile believers. This is about Jewish believers' practice, not about salvation.
- **Was Paul right to go along with it?** Christians have long disagreed. One view says it was wise. It was Paul's own principle, "To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews" (1 Cor 9:20). Temple customs were not sin for a Jew. They simply could not save, and love for weaker believers and unity between Jewish and Gentile churches were worth a week and some money. The other view says it was a compromise that backfired, because it put Paul right where the riot broke out. Luke gives no verdict. He reports it, and then shows God carrying out his purpose through what followed (23:11).
- **Commentators:** Guzik does not fault Paul. He reads the episode as 1 Corinthians 9:20 in action, made possible because no one was requiring Gentiles to keep these customs. Henry weighs those who call the plan "fleshly wisdom" but judges Paul's compliance "justifiable enough, as the case stood." He describes the ceremonial law at that point as "dead, but not buried." He even suggests the plan's failure served God's purpose, loosening Jewish believers' hold on the law more than argument could. And he adds a caution against pressing others "to do what is contrary to their own judgment to oblige us." JFB takes the four men to be Jewish Christians and notes the elders "glorified the Lord," constrained to approve Paul's work despite the Jewish character of their own church.

#### Paul Arrested in the Temple (vv27–36)
- **"The Jews from Asia" (v27).** These were pilgrims in town for Pentecost, very likely from Ephesus, since they recognize Trophimus. They were the same opposition Paul met in the synagogue there (19:9). The trouble comes not from Jerusalem locals but from opponents who followed him from Asia.
- **The charges (v28).** Teaching against "the people and the law and this place" is almost word for word what was said against Stephen (6:13). Paul once guarded the coats while Stephen was stoned on those charges. Now they are turned on him.
- **"He even brought Greeks into the temple" (vv28–29).** This was a capital charge, and it was **false**. A stone barrier separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts. Warning notices in Greek and Latin said any foreigner who went past it would have himself to blame for his death. One of the actual stone inscriptions was found in 1871. Luke is honest about how the charge arose: they had *seen* Trophimus with Paul in the city and "**supposed**" the rest. Paul later writes that Christ "has broken down… the dividing wall of hostility" between Jew and Gentile (Eph 2:14). The image is that very barrier, and the letter was written to Ephesus, Trophimus's hometown.
- **"At once the gates were shut" (v30).** The Levite guards seal the inner temple off from the mob. Luke may mean it symbolically as well: the temple shuts its doors as the gospel moves on.
- **The tribune (vv31–32).** A *chiliarchos* commanded a cohort of up to 1,000 men. The Fortress Antonia stood at the northwest corner of the temple courts, with stairs leading down into them, and soldiers watched constantly during festivals. He grabs troops and "**ran down**" at once. The beating stops only when the crowd sees Roman soldiers.
- **"Bound with two chains" (v33).** Agabus's sign is fulfilled. Paul is chained to a soldier on each side, as Peter was in 12:6. The irony: Agabus said the Jews would deliver him to the Gentiles, and in the event the Gentiles *rescue* him from the Jews.
- **"Some… shouting one thing, some another" (v34).** The same confusion as the Ephesus theater (19:32). The riots at Ephesus and Jerusalem mirror each other, one defending an idol's temple, the other God's.
- **"Away with him!" (v36).** The very cry of the crowd at Jesus' trial (Luke 23:18; John 19:15). Luke intends the echo.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls the charges baseless. Paul refused to trust in the law or the temple for righteousness, but he never attacked them. Guzik and JFB both hear the echo of Jesus' trial in "Away with him!" JFB identifies the accusers as Ephesian Jews, "embittered by their discomfiture." It reads the shut doors darkly: shut so "the murder they meant to perpetrate might not pollute that holy place." On Agabus's wording it notes that though the Romans did the binding, it was "at the Jews' instigation." Henry sets the two riots side by side, the same zeal for God's temple as the Ephesians showed for Diana's. He notes the irony that Paul was attacked in the temple, "where he should have been protected, as in a sanctuary."

#### Paul Speaks to the People (vv37–40)
- **"Do you know Greek?" (v37).** The tribune is surprised, because he had assumed his prisoner was a dangerous nobody.
- **"The Egyptian" (v38).** Josephus describes him: a false prophet who, a few years earlier, gathered followers on the Mount of Olives promising Jerusalem's walls would fall at his command. Rome crushed the movement, but he escaped. Josephus gives a much larger number than Luke's 4,000, and Luke's figure is generally thought the more realistic. The "**Assassins**" were the *sicarii*, "dagger-men," who stabbed opponents in festival crowds. The tribune thinks he has caught a terrorist leader.
- **"A Jew, from Tarsus… a citizen of no obscure city" (v39).** Tarsus was a renowned university city, so this is quiet, true pride. He keeps his *Roman* citizenship in reserve until 22:25.
- **"In the Hebrew language" (v40).** Most likely Aramaic, the everyday language of Judea, as the ESV footnote notes. The crowd that wanted him dead falls into "a great hush" at the sound of their own tongue. The speech continues in Acts 22.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that even in mortal danger Paul's concern was to preach, and that he chose the crowd's own language to identify with them. JFB describes the fortress as built by Herod on a high rock at the northwest corner of the temple area and named for Mark Antony. Henry, looking ahead to everything this arrest begins: "When we see the beginning of a trouble, we know not either how long it will last or how it will issue."

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke deliberately shapes this to echo Jesus' last journey. Paul sets his face toward Jerusalem despite warnings, friends try to turn him back, he says "the will of the Lord be done," he is seized in the temple, a mob cries "Away with him!", and he is handed over to the Gentiles. Paul is not the Savior, but he follows him "in the fellowship of his sufferings" (Phil 3:10), down a road Jesus walked first.
