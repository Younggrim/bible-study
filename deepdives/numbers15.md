*Setting:* The story breaks off. Chapter 14 ended with Israel sentenced to wander until the whole adult generation died, and with a failed attack in the hills near Hormah. Chapter 15 suddenly turns to laws about grain, oil and wine to go with sacrifices "when you come into the land." The timing is the message. Right after the worst failure in the book, God speaks as though the land is still certain, because it is: the children will enter it (14:31). The chapter covers the bulk of the thirty-eight years of wandering, about which Numbers says little, and it has four parts: offerings in the land, unintentional and defiant sin, a Sabbath case that shows what defiant sin looks like, and tassels to help Israel remember.

#### Laws About Sacrifices (vv1–21)
- **"When you come into the land you are to inhabit, which I am giving you" (v2).** Not "if." The very first words after the sentence of chapter 14 are a promise. The generation that refused the land will not see it, but God's purpose has not changed. Grace speaks again even after judgment.
- **The occasions (v3).** These offerings go with sacrifices made freely: a burnt offering (wholly burned on the altar), a sacrifice (a peace or fellowship offering, mostly eaten by the worshipper), a vow being fulfilled, a freewill gift, or a festival. They are the joyful, voluntary side of Israel's worship.
- **"A pleasing aroma to the LORD" (v3).** This phrase runs through the chapter (vv7, 10, 13, 14, 24). It does not mean God needs the smell of food. It is a way of saying he accepts the worshipper and is pleased with the offering (Gen 8:21). Paul uses the same picture for Christ's sacrifice (Eph 5:2) and for gifts given to support the gospel (Phil 4:18).
- **Grain, oil and wine with each animal (vv4–10).** With a lamb: a tenth of an ephah of fine flour (roughly two quarts or two liters), a quarter of a hin of oil (about a quart), and a quarter of a hin of wine. With a ram, more. With a bull, more still: three tenths of an ephah of flour and half a hin of oil and of wine. The bigger the animal, the bigger the accompanying gifts.
- **Why bread and wine?** These were the produce of a settled farming life: grain fields, olive groves and vineyards. A nomadic people in the desert could not supply them easily. The law assumes Israel will be planting and harvesting in Canaan. Every time they brought these offerings, the people would be giving back to God the very produce of the land their parents had refused. Grain, oil and wine are the main gifts of the land elsewhere (Deut 7:13; Ps 104:15).
- **"Thus it shall be done for each bull or ram, or for each lamb" (vv11–12).** The rule applies to every animal, no matter how many. Worship was not to be rounded down.
- **"You and the sojourner shall be alike before the LORD" (v15).** A "stranger" or sojourner was a foreigner living among Israel long-term. If he wanted to worship the LORD, he followed the same rules and was welcome on the same terms. **"One law and one rule"** (v16) is a striking statement for the ancient world. The way to God was not by birth but by coming in his appointed way. Israel was always meant to be open to outsiders who joined themselves to the LORD (Exod 12:48–49; Isa 56:6–7).
- **"Of the first of your dough you shall present a loaf" (vv19–21).** This is a firstfruits offering in the kitchen. The grain at harvest already had its firstfruits (Lev 23:10), but here the first portion of everyday bread-making also belongs to God. "Dough" is a rare word, probably meaning coarse meal or the first batch of dough. Every household baking bread would begin by giving God his share. Paul uses this very law: "If the dough offered as firstfruits is holy, so is the whole lump" (Rom 11:16). Jewish practice still keeps a form of it, called *challah*, setting apart a piece of dough.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws attention to "when" in v2: God had not given up on Israel. He sees the dough offering as a way to turn ordinary work into worship and to keep the people's minds on God's promise. Henry reads the restating of offering laws right after Moses' intercession as a sign of reconciliation, and the rule for strangers as an open invitation for outsiders to join the people of God. JFB notes that the extra offerings "increased in proportion to the greater worth and magnitude of its principal," and defines the stranger as "one who had become a proselyte." JFB adds that the dough offering "was to precede the act of eating," just as firstfruits came before the harvest was used.

#### Laws About Unintentional Sins (vv22–31)
- **"But if you sin unintentionally" (v22).** The Hebrew *shegagah* means error, a straying or mistake. It covers sins done through ignorance, carelessness or weakness, when someone did not set out to defy God. Leviticus 4–5 has similar laws. This section seems especially concerned with failing to do something commanded ("do not observe all these commandments," v22), which fits the new offering laws just given.
- **The whole community (vv24–26).** If the whole nation goes wrong without realizing it, the congregation offers a bull as a burnt offering, with its grain and drink offerings, and a male goat as a sin offering. **"The priest shall make atonement… and they shall be forgiven"** (v25). "Atonement" (*kipper*) means to make amends, to cover or wipe away the guilt so that the relationship with God is restored. Sin is serious even when it is not deliberate, but God provides a way for it to be dealt with.
- **One person (vv27–28).** An individual offers a year-old female goat. The same promise follows: **"he shall be forgiven."** Both levels, the nation and the single person, have a remedy.
- **"One law… for the stranger" (v29).** Again, the foreigner living among Israel has the same access to forgiveness as the native-born.
- **"But the person who does anything with a high hand" (v30).** This is the contrast. "With a high hand" is a picture of a fist raised in defiance. The same phrase described Israel marching out of Egypt "defiantly" or boldly in Exodus 14:8. Here it means sinning openly and deliberately, knowing it is against God's word and choosing it anyway.
- **"Reviles the LORD" (v30).** Such a person is not just breaking a rule. They are insulting God to his face. **"He has despised the word of the LORD"** (v31) uses *bazah*, to treat as worthless, the word for Esau despising his birthright (Gen 25:34).
- **"That person shall be utterly cut off; his iniquity shall be on him" (v31).** "Cut off" can mean being put to death, being excluded from the community, or God himself ending a family line. The key point is that no sacrifice is offered for this sin. **"His iniquity shall be on him"**: no one else carries it, and no animal takes his place.
- **Does this mean deliberate sin can never be forgiven?** The Old Testament itself shows forgiveness for deliberate sins. David committed adultery and murder knowingly, and was forgiven when he repented (2 Sam 12:13; Ps 51). The Day of Atonement covered "all their sins" (Lev 16:21), and Leviticus 6:1–7 provides for some knowing wrongs. So "with a high hand" seems to describe not simply a sin committed knowingly, but an attitude of unrepentant defiance, a refusal to submit to God at all. Hebrews 10:26–29 echoes this passage when it warns those who "go on sinning deliberately" after knowing the truth, trampling the Son of God underfoot: "there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins." The way back is always open to the one who turns, but not to the one who will not.
- **Commentators:** Guzik insists that good intentions do not excuse sin: unintentional sin still needed atonement and blood. He contrasts this with defiant sin, for which no sacrifice was provided. Henry describes presumptuous sinners as those who set their will against God's authority, and compares such rebellion to Samuel's words, "as witch-craft, and stubbornness as idolatry" (1 Sam 15:23). JFB explains the "uplifted hand" as meaning "knowingly, wilfully, obstinately," and says the guilt rested on the person alone unless the nation criminally ignored it. JFB also draws a distinction between this law and Leviticus 4, though the Tyndale notes simply treat them as parallel provisions, a minor difference of opinion.

#### A Sabbathbreaker Executed (vv32–36)
- **"They found a man gathering sticks on the Sabbath day" (v32).** This case illustrates the law just given about sinning "with a high hand." The Sabbath command was the clearest of the laws, given at Sinai with the penalty already stated (Exod 31:14–15; 35:2–3), and Exodus 35:3 specifically forbids kindling a fire on the Sabbath. Gathering sticks, most likely for a fire, was not a mistake.
- **Why so severe?** To modern readers, death for gathering sticks seems out of proportion. But the Sabbath was the sign of the covenant between God and Israel, "a sign between me and the people of Israel forever" (Exod 31:16–17). Publicly breaking it, just after a whole generation had been sentenced for refusing God's word, was a deliberate rejection of the covenant itself, in the open. The issue was not firewood. It was defiance of God's plainest command.
- **"They put him in custody, because it had not been made clear what should be done to him" (v34).** The man's guilt was not in doubt. The penalty was death (Exod 31:14), but the method had not been specified. So Moses waits for God's word rather than deciding on his own. The same thing happened with the blasphemer in Leviticus 24:12. This is careful, not hasty, justice.
- **"Outside the camp… stoned him to death" (vv35–36).** The camp was the place of God's presence, so the execution happened outside it. **"All the congregation"** took part, which made it the community's act and showed that the whole people owned the covenant. It also made a public example: Israel would remember what despising God's word cost.
- **Jesus and the Sabbath.** Jesus did not overturn the Sabbath, but he challenged those who turned it into a burden. He said "the Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27) and healed on it as an act of mercy. The difference between his actions and this man's was not about firewood or healing. It was about whether a person was honoring God or defying him.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the act seemed small but was a deliberate violation of a plain command and a quiet contempt for the Creator whose rest the Sabbath celebrates. He notes that the people's zeal to bring the man forward showed they had not wholly abandoned God, and that the penalty was meant to deter others. Guzik takes the man as a clear example of high-handed sin, and the community's response as a sign that God's law outweighed one person's choices. JFB thinks this was probably the first public offense of its kind, which is why the rulers were unsure, and says the Lord, as Israel's King, gave the sentence. The Tyndale notes add that disregard of the Sabbath signaled a rejection of God's gracious covenant.

#### Tassels on Garments (vv37–41)
- **"Make tassels on the corners of their garments" (v38).** The word is *tsitsit*, a tassel or fringe, made of twisted threads hanging from the four corners of the outer cloak (Deut 22:12). Similar decorated hems are seen in ancient Near Eastern art, where they could signal rank.
- **"A cord of blue on the tassel of each corner" (v38).** Blue (*tekhelet*) was an expensive dye, made from the murex sea snail along the coast of Phoenicia. It was the color of royalty and of the tabernacle: the curtains, the high priest's robe and the cord that held his golden plate "Holy to the LORD" (Exod 28:31, 36–37). Every Israelite, not only the priest, would wear a thread of that holy blue.
- **"For you to look at and remember all the commandments of the LORD" (v39).** The tassels were a reminder sewn into everyday clothes. Every time an Israelite dressed, sat or walked, they would see it.
- **"Not to follow after your own heart and your own eyes, which you are inclined to whore after" (v39).** "Follow after" is the same verb (*tur*) used for the spies who "spied out" the land (13:2, 16, 17). The spies had used their eyes, seen giants, and followed their fears. The tassels redirect the eyes to God's word. "Whore after" is Scripture's blunt picture for spiritual unfaithfulness, the same root as "faithlessness" in 14:33.
- **"Be holy to your God… I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (vv40–41).** The commandments rest on redemption. God does not say "obey, and I will save you." He says "I saved you, so be holy." The chapter ends on the same note it began: God is still Israel's God.
- **Tassels in later times.** These verses became the third paragraph of the *Shema*, the prayer recited by observant Jews every morning and evening (with Deut 6:4–9 and 11:13–21). The woman with the bleeding touched "the fringe" of Jesus' garment (Matt 9:20; Mark 6:56), likely this tassel, so Jesus himself wore them. He also criticized those who made "their fringes long" for show (Matt 23:5).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the tassels as a visible reminder of God's covenant and who his people were, but warns that symbols worn to remind us of God can easily become displays of self-righteousness, as with the Pharisees. JFB describes the "riband of blue or rather purple color" and says the fringes were meant to keep the commandments in mind, and on v41 that God would not abandon Israel despite their rebellions. Guzik cites G. Campbell Morgan reading the blue cord as a sign of God's direct rule over his people.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter holds out a promise of the land, offerings that give back its bread and wine, forgiveness for those who stray, and a warning for those who defy God. Each part points forward. The bread and wine that went with Israel's sacrifices became, in Jesus' hands, the signs of his own body and blood (Luke 22:19–20). The sin offering for those who "sin unintentionally" finds its fullness in the one who prayed, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34), and who was led "outside the camp" to suffer (Heb 13:12). And the tassel of royal blue that pointed Israel to God's word was worn by the one who kept that word perfectly. Those who reached out and touched it "were made well" (Matt 14:36).
