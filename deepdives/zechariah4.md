*Setting:* This is the fifth of the eight night visions Zechariah received in February 519 BC (1:7). The returned exiles had been rebuilding the temple for five months, under the governor Zerubbabel and the high priest Joshua. The work was hard and the community was poor. The older people thought the new temple was "as nothing" beside Solomon's (Hag 2:3), and nobody yet knew whether the Persian authorities would allow it to continue (Ezra 5:3–5). The fourth vision (ch 3) dealt with Joshua the priest, cleansed and restored. This fifth vision turns to Zerubbabel the governor, the man responsible for getting the building finished. Its message is one of the best-known lines in the prophets: the work will be done "not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit." The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading. Its structure is a vision (vv1–5), a word of God to Zerubbabel set in the middle (vv6–10a) and then the angel's explanation of the vision (vv10b–14). Some scholars think the middle oracle was inserted from another occasion. More likely it was placed at the vision's center on purpose, because it is the vision's point.

#### A Vision of a Golden Lampstand (vv1–14)
- **"Woke me, like a man who is awakened out of his sleep" (v1).** Zechariah was not literally asleep, but drained or dazed after the previous four visions. The angel rouses him, as Daniel was roused after his visions (Dan 8:18; 10:9–10). Spiritual things need full attention.
- **"A lampstand all of gold" (v2).** The Hebrew word is *menorah*, the name of the seven-branched lampstand in the tabernacle (Exod 25:31–40). Solomon's temple had ten lampstands (1 Kgs 7:49), carried off or destroyed by Babylon (Jer 52:19). The new temple would need a lampstand again, and this vision shows a lampstand that never runs dry. It was the light in the holy place, the sign that God's presence was there and that Israel was to be a light to the nations (Isa 42:6; 60:1–3).
- **"With a bowl on the top of it, and seven lamps on it, with seven lips on each of the lamps" (v2).** This lampstand is not exactly like the one in the tabernacle. A large bowl, a reservoir of oil, sits on top. Seven lamps are around it. Each lamp has seven "lips," spouts or pinched rims where wicks sat. Clay lamps with seven pinched spouts from Israel's earlier centuries have been found by archaeologists, so the picture would have been clear to Zechariah. Forty-nine flames in all means a blaze of light. The KJV's "seven pipes to the seven lamps" reads the word differently, but either way the point is abundant supply and abundant light.
- **"And there are two olive trees by it, one on the right of the bowl and the other on its left" (v3).** Here is the key difference from the tabernacle lampstand. That one had to be filled by hand every day with olive oil brought by the people and tended by priests (Exod 27:20–21; Lev 24:1–4). This one is fed directly from living trees. No human hands are needed to keep it burning.
- **"What are these, my lord?… Do you not know what these are?" (vv4–5).** Zechariah asks, and the angel answers with a question. He does not mock. He makes Zechariah admit that he cannot understand without help. "No, my lord" is the humble answer. The exchange is repeated in v13.
- **"This is the word of the LORD to Zerubbabel" (v6).** Instead of explaining the lampstand item by item, the angel gives its meaning in one sentence addressed to Zerubbabel.
- **"Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit, says the LORD of hosts" (v6).** "Might" (*chayil*) is the word for strength, wealth and especially an army. "Power" (*koach*) is personal strength or ability. Neither military force nor human effort will finish this temple. The Spirit of God will. In the picture, the oil flowing from the trees stands for the Spirit, as oil often does in Scripture: it anointed priests and kings (Exod 29:7; 1 Sam 16:13), and when Samuel anointed David, "the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon David." Zerubbabel had no army and Judah had no money. Haggai had already promised, "My Spirit remains in your midst. Fear not" (Hag 2:5).
- **"Who are you, O great mountain? Before Zerubbabel you shall become a plain" (v7).** The "great mountain" is every obstacle to the work: the opposition of the neighbors (Ezra 4), the Persian bureaucracy (Ezra 5), the poverty and discouragement of the people. It might also include the literal mound of rubble on the temple site. God speaks to the obstacle directly and tells it to flatten. Isaiah promised that "every mountain and hill shall be made low" before God's coming (Isa 40:4). Jesus used the same picture for faith: "If you have faith like a grain of mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, 'Move from here to there,' and it will move" (Matt 17:20).
- **"He shall bring forward the top stone amid shouts of 'Grace, grace to it!'" (v7).** The "top stone" (*ha'even haro'shah*, "the head stone") is the final stone placed to complete the building. Some take it as a ceremonial stone laid at the finish, others as the first stone taken from the old temple's ruins and set in the new. "Grace, grace to it!" is the people's cry as it is set in place. The Hebrew *chen chen* means "favor, favor" or "how beautiful, how beautiful." It may be a prayer for God's blessing on the building, or a shout of admiration. Either way the temple is finished amid praise for God's grace, not human achievement.
- **"The hands of Zerubbabel have laid the foundation of this house; his hands shall also complete it" (v9).** A personal promise to the man who had started the work in 536 BC (Ezra 3:8–10) and restarted it in 520 (Ezra 5:2). It was fulfilled four years later, in 515 BC (Ezra 6:15). "Then you will know that the LORD of hosts has sent me" repeats 2:9, 11. The finished temple would prove that Zechariah's message was from God.
- **"For whoever has despised the day of small things shall rejoice" (v10).** This is the verse's famous phrase. The people who looked at the small foundation and wept (Ezra 3:12; Hag 2:3) are named "whoever has despised." God tells them that small beginnings are not to be scorned. What he begins, he finishes. Jesus' parables of the mustard seed and the leaven teach the same thing about the kingdom (Matt 13:31–33).
- **"And shall see the plumb line in the hand of Zerubbabel" (v10).** A plumb line is a weight on a cord, used to check that a wall is straight (Amos 7:7–8). The Hebrew is literally "the stone, the tin," perhaps a tin weight. Some think it means the final stone of v7 instead, a "separated" or chosen stone. Most translations take it as a plummet. The picture is Zerubbabel at work, checking the walls as they rise. The doubters will see it and rejoice.
- **"These seven are the eyes of the LORD, which range through the whole earth" (v10).** Here the angel returns to the vision. The seven lamps are God's eyes, seeing everything. The phrase recalls Hanani's word to King Asa: "the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to give strong support to those whose heart is blameless toward him" (2 Chr 16:9). The stone with seven eyes in 3:9 points the same way. God is watching over this small project in a small province, and nothing escapes him.
- **"What are these two olive trees… What are these two branches?" (vv11–12).** Zechariah asks about the trees twice, the second time more precisely. He notices that two "branches" of the olive trees pour oil through "two golden pipes." The word for "branches" is literally "ears" or "clusters," as of grain, perhaps the fruit-laden tips of the branches. **"The golden oil"** is literally "the gold," the oil shining like gold as it flows.
- **"These are the two anointed ones who stand by the Lord of the whole earth" (v14).** Literally "the two sons of oil." The word for oil here is *yitshar*, which normally means fresh olive oil as a crop of the land (Deut 7:13). The lexicon notes that this is the only place it is used in connection with anointing. Most interpreters take the two as Zerubbabel the governor, of David's line, and Joshua the high priest, the two leaders God had set apart and was now filling with his Spirit to serve him. They "stand by" the Lord, the posture of servants ready to act. "Lord of the whole earth" is a title of God's universal rule (Josh 3:11; Mic 4:13). Persia ruled the known world, but the LORD rules the whole earth.
- **The two witnesses.** Revelation 11:3–4 takes up this vision for the two witnesses who prophesy in the last days: "These are the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth." Interpreters differ on who they are, but the use shows that Zechariah's vision of Spirit-filled witnesses was seen as reaching beyond Joshua and Zerubbabel.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the church "a candlestick, set up for the enlightening of this dark world," and reads v6 as "not by human might or power, but by his own Spirit." On v7 he says "Faith will remove mountains and make them plains," and on v10: "The day of small things is the day of precious things, and will be the day of great things." He gives two readings of the two anointed ones: the offices of magistrate and minister, held then by Zerubbabel and Joshua, or "Christ and the Spirit, the Redeemer and the Comforter." JFB sees the lampstand as the Jewish nation and finally the church, and says the repeated "grace" means grace "from first to last." He links the shout to the "Hosanna" at Christ's entry into Jerusalem, and identifies the two "sons of oil" primarily as Joshua and Zerubbabel, channels of grace rather than its source. Guzik distinguishes "might" as collective strength and "power" as individual strength, which fits the lexicon's range for the two words. He also identifies the two as Zerubbabel and Joshua, connects them with Revelation 11 and stresses that God's Spirit empowers human work without replacing it.

#### Pointing to Christ
The lampstand fed by living trees is a picture of the Spirit's supply, and the two anointed ones who stand by the Lord point beyond Zerubbabel and Joshua to the one who would hold both their offices. Jesus is the true Anointed One, *Messiah* in Hebrew, *Christ* in Greek, the son of David who is also our high priest (Heb 7:1–3; 8:1). At his baptism the Spirit came on him (Luke 3:22), and he began his ministry by reading, "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me" (Luke 4:18). His kingdom began in the day of small things, with a carpenter's son, twelve disciples and a borrowed tomb. It advanced not by might or power but by the Spirit he poured out at Pentecost. Jesus is also the top stone. The stone the builders rejected has become "the head of the corner" (Ps 118:22; 1 Pet 2:7), and the shout over it is still "Grace, grace!" The church now carries that light. Jesus calls his churches "lampstands" (Rev 1:20) and his people "the light of the world" (Matt 5:14), and they shine only as long as the oil keeps flowing.
