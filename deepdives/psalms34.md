*Setting:* "Of David, when he changed his behavior before Abimelech, so that he drove him out, and he went away." The story is in 1 Samuel 21:10–15. On the run from Saul, David fled to the Philistine city of Gath, Goliath's hometown, carrying Goliath's own sword. The king's servants recognized him: "Is not this David… of whom they sing to one another in dances, 'Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands'?" Terrified, David pretended to be insane, scratching marks on the gate and letting spit run down his beard. The king said, "Do I lack madmen?" and threw him out. From there David went to the cave of Adullam, where his family and a band of 400 desperate men gathered to him (1 Sam 22:1–2). In 1 Samuel the king is called Achish. "Abimelech" ("my father is king") appears to have been a royal title among the Philistines, as with the kings of Gerar in Abraham's and Isaac's day (Gen 20; 26), the way "Pharaoh" was used in Egypt. Like Psalm 25, the psalm is an alphabetic acrostic. Each verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, with one letter (waw) missing and an extra line at the end (v22), exactly as in Psalm 25. The form is careful and composed, so David most likely wrote it some time afterwards, looking back. Henry notes that there is "little in it peculiar to that occasion." It is a thanksgiving (vv1–10) followed by teaching (vv11–22). Peter quotes it twice (1 Pet 2:3; 3:10–12), and John sees it fulfilled at the cross (John 19:36).

#### I Will Bless the LORD at All Times (vv1–3)
- **"I will bless the LORD at all times; his praise shall continually be in my mouth" (v1).** "At all times" includes the humiliating ones. A few days earlier David's mouth had been drooling into his beard in front of a pagan king. Now it is full of praise. Boice, cited by Guzik, notes that he may have acted like a fool, but he was not so foolish as to stop praising God. Paul's "give thanks in all circumstances" (1 Thess 5:18) is the same resolve.
- **A pun in the title.** "Changed his behavior" is literally "changed his *ta'am*," his sense or judgment, the word for good sense or discretion (1 Sam 25:33). The same root is the verb "taste" in v8: "Oh, *taste* (*ta'amu*) and see that the LORD is good." David changed his "taste" to save his life by his own wits. Now he invites everyone to taste the LORD.
- **"My soul makes its boast in the LORD" (v2).** David was saved by a ridiculous trick, and he knows it. He does not boast in his cleverness. He boasts in God. Kidner, cited by Guzik, suggests that Paul may have had this episode in mind when he boasted about being let down in a basket through a window to escape another foreign king (2 Cor 11:30–33). "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1 Cor 1:31; Jer 9:23–24).
- **"Let the humble hear and be glad" (v2).** The "humble" (*'anavim*) are the poor and lowly, people with nothing to rely on but God. Those were exactly the people who gathered to David at Adullam: "everyone who was in distress, and everyone who was in debt, and everyone who was bitter in soul" (1 Sam 22:2).
- **"Oh, magnify the LORD with me, and let us exalt his name together!" (v3).** "Magnify" means to make great, not in the sense of adding to God's greatness, but of making it look as large in our eyes as it really is, the way a magnifying glass makes a small thing look its true size. Henry: "God's praises sound best in concert."
- **Commentators:** Henry suggests that while it was "his fault that he changed his behaviour," it was "God's mercy that he escaped," and the mercy was all the greater because God "did not deal with him according to the desert of his dissimulation." Guzik passes on Poole: whether David sinned in pretending madness is debated, but God's rescue was undeniable. Maclaren, also cited, sees the "seeming idiot scribbling on the gate" turned into "saint, poet, and preacher." JFB: "Even in distress… there is always matter for praising and thanking God."

#### This Poor Man Cried (vv4–7)
- **"I sought the LORD, and he answered me and delivered me from all my fears" (v4).** 1 Samuel says David "was much afraid of Achish king of Gath" (1 Sam 21:12). Here David says God delivered him not only from the danger but from the fear itself. Henry draws out the difference: God delivers from danger "by his providence working for us," and from fear "by his grace working in us," and the second is the greater mercy. Trapp, cited by Guzik, imagines David praying silently even while he was acting the madman.
- **"Those who look to him are radiant" (v5).** "Radiant" (*nahar*) means to shine, to glow with joy. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the same word describes a mother's face lighting up when she sees her lost children come home (Isa 60:5). Moses' face shone after being with God (Exod 34:29). Faces that look toward God reflect his light.
- **"And their faces shall never be ashamed" (v5).** Shame makes the face fall and the head drop. Those who look to God will never have their hope exposed as foolish (25:3).
- **"This poor man cried, and the LORD heard him" (v6).** David points to himself: "this poor man." The anointed future king, the giant-killer, was reduced to a homeless fugitive pretending to be insane. Kidner, cited by Guzik: to feel the force of David's words, one has only to remember his danger and his "abject clowning." Yet the LORD heard him.
- **"The angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him, and delivers them" (v7).** The "angel of the LORD" is a mysterious figure in the Old Testament who sometimes speaks as God himself (Gen 16:7–13; Exod 3:2–6; Judg 13:21–22). He went before Israel out of Egypt and stood between them and Pharaoh's army (Exod 14:19–20). The Tyndale notes make this link. JFB calls him "the angel of the covenant," the commander of the LORD's army (Josh 5:14). Many Christian interpreters have seen him as a preview of the Son of God. Others read "angel" here as a collective term for God's angels, as Henry does (Heb 1:14). "Encamps around" pictures an army camped in a ring. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, thinks of Jacob, who met the angels of God and called the place Mahanaim, "two camps" (Gen 32:1–2). Elisha's servant saw the hills "full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha" (2 Kings 6:17). David alone at Gath was not alone.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The holy angels do us more good offices every day than we are aware of." Guzik notes that v7 is consistent with the idea of guardian angels, but does not prove it. JFB reads "this poor man" as "literally, 'humble,' himself as a specimen of such," and "lightened" (v5) as faces brightened with joy, "opposed to the downcast features of those who are ashamed."

#### Oh, Taste and See That the LORD Is Good (vv8–10)
- **"Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good!" (v8).** This is an invitation to experience, not just to agree. You can describe honey, but the way to know its sweetness is to taste it. JFB: "try and experience." Peter uses this verse for new believers: "if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is good" (1 Pet 2:3). Hebrews speaks of those who "have tasted the goodness of the word of God" (Heb 6:5). Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes both passages use it to describe a first venture into faith, and both urge more than a casual sample.
- **"Blessed is the man who takes refuge in him!" (v8).** "Blessed" again, as in 1:1 and 32:1–2. David had sought refuge in Gath, the Philistine city, and it nearly killed him. The true refuge is God.
- **"Oh, fear the LORD, you his saints, for those who fear him have no lack!" (v9).** The "fear of the LORD" is the reverent awe that leads to obedience and trust (Prov 1:7). It is not terror that drives a person away but reverence that draws a person close. Henry: "not fear him and shun him, but fear him and seek him." "Have no lack" echoes 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."
- **"The young lions suffer want and hunger" (v10).** Young lions (*kephirim*) were the strongest predators in the land, in their prime. If anyone could get what they wanted by force, they could. Yet even they go hungry. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, wonders whether lions prowled near the cave of Adullam. The Greek translation reads "the rich" here, which makes a similar point: even the powerful and wealthy can end up empty.
- **"But those who seek the LORD lack no good thing" (v10).** "Good" is the key word. It does not promise everything wanted, but everything that is truly good. JFB: "they may be afflicted… but this may be a good" (2 Cor 4:17–18; Heb 12:10–11). Paul says God works "all things… for good" for those who love him (Rom 8:28). Poole, cited by Guzik, adds that sometimes needs and hardships are better for believers than bread.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Let God's goodness be rolled under the tongue as a sweet morsel." He reads "taste" and "see" as two kinds of knowing: we taste God's bounty in his gifts and see his beauty in his perfections. Trapp, cited by Guzik: no one needs an argument to believe honey is sweet once he has tasted it. JFB: "good" is emphatic.

#### Come, O Children, Listen to Me (vv11–14)
- **"Come, O children, listen to me; I will teach you the fear of the LORD" (v11).** The psalm turns from thanksgiving to teaching. "Children" (literally "sons") is the address of a wise teacher to his students, as in Proverbs: "Hear, my son, your father's instruction" (Prov 1:8). JFB: "subjects of instruction." David the fugitive becomes David the teacher. Henry thinks of the young men at Adullam, and notes that David, "though a man of war, and anointed to be king, he did not think it below him" to teach the young.
- **"What man is there who desires life and loves many days, that he may see good?" (v12).** The question catches everyone. Who doesn't want a long life and good days? David's answer is surprising. It is not wealth, power or clever escape plans, but the way a person speaks and lives.
- **"Keep your tongue from evil and your lips from speaking deceit" (v13).** The first lesson of the fear of the LORD is about speech. This is pointed, coming from David. At Nob, just before Gath, he had lied to the priest Ahimelech to get bread and a sword, and that lie led to the slaughter of the priests (1 Sam 21:1–2; 22:9–19, 22). At Gath he deceived again. David knew the cost of a lying tongue. James says the same: "If anyone thinks he is religious and does not bridle his tongue… this person's religion is worthless" (Jas 1:26).
- **"Turn away from evil and do good" (v14).** Two halves of one life: turn from what is wrong, and do what is right. Avoiding evil is not enough. Proverbs 3:7: "fear the LORD, and turn away from evil."
- **"Seek peace and pursue it" (v14).** Peace (*shalom*) does not happen by accident. It has to be sought, and then chased like a runner after a prize. Jesus said, "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matt 5:9), and Paul, "So far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all" (Rom 12:18).
- **Peter's use (1 Pet 3:10–12).** Peter quotes vv12–16 almost in full to Christians facing slander and mistreatment. His point: do not repay evil for evil or insult for insult, because the eyes of the Lord are on the righteous. Peter applies David's words to the way persecuted believers should speak and live.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is not being, but well being, that constitutes life," and he reads "life" as reaching beyond this world, since even a long life on earth is "but of few days." JFB: "sins of thought" are included in those of speech, because the mouth speaks from the heart (Luke 6:45). Guzik passes on Boice's point that in this psalm the fear of the LORD is not a feeling but a way of acting: obedience.

#### The Eyes of the LORD Are Toward the Righteous (vv15–18)
- **"The eyes of the LORD are toward the righteous and his ears toward their cry" (v15).** God's eyes watch over his people, and his ears are tuned to them (32:8; 33:18). Henry: as parents who love a child will not let it out of their sight, "none of God's children are ever from under his eye."
- **"The face of the LORD is against those who do evil" (v16).** The same face that shines in blessing (Num 6:25) can be "set against" someone in judgment (Lev 17:10; 20:3). JFB: "opposed to them."
- **"To cut off the memory of them from the earth" (v16).** In Israel, to be forgotten was a terrible fate. A name that died with its owner was a life that left nothing behind (109:13; Prov 10:7).
- **"When the righteous cry for help, the LORD hears and delivers them out of all their troubles" (v17).** The "righteous" here are not sinless. David had just lied and pretended. They are people who belong to God and cry to him. Verse 6 told David's story; v17 makes it a promise for all.
- **"The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (v18).** This may be the tenderest line in the psalm. The "brokenhearted" are people whose hearts have been shattered by grief, guilt or loss. "Crushed in spirit" (*dakke'-ruach*) is literally "crushed to powder." The Tyndale notes connect them with the contrite heart God will not despise (51:17) and with Isaiah: God dwells "with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit" (Isa 57:15). Jesus read from Isaiah 61:1 in Nazareth: he was sent "to bind up the brokenhearted" (Luke 4:18). Meyer, cited by Guzik, notes that broken people drop out of the main current of life while the world rushes on, "but God draws nigh."
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that God sometimes "turns his face from" the wicked because they have left him, but here "he is said to set his face against them, because they have fought against him." He adds that "there is no rhetoric, nothing charming, in a cry, yet God's ears are open to it, as the tender mother's to the cry of her sucking child." JFB: "Humble penitents are objects of God's special tender regard."

#### Many Are the Afflictions of the Righteous (vv19–22)
- **"Many are the afflictions of the righteous" (v19).** This is not a prosperity psalm. The righteous have many troubles. David knew it: hunted by a king, rejected by his own people, humiliated in a foreign court. Jesus promised the same: "In the world you will have tribulation" (John 16:33).
- **"But the LORD delivers him out of them all" (v19).** Not from all afflictions, but out of them all. The troubles are many, but so are the rescues, and the last word is deliverance.
- **"He keeps all his bones; not one of them is broken" (v20).** In context this is a picture of complete protection. God guards the whole person, down to the bones. The Tyndale notes: bones represent a person's entire being. John saw it fulfilled at the cross. The soldiers broke the legs of the two criminals to hasten their deaths, but Jesus was already dead, so they did not break his. "For these things took place that the Scripture might be fulfilled: 'Not one of his bones will be broken'" (John 19:33–36). John is probably also thinking of the Passover lamb, whose bones were not to be broken (Exod 12:46; Num 9:12). Jesus is both the righteous sufferer of Psalm 34 and the true Passover lamb.
- **"Affliction will slay the wicked" (v21).** The same word "affliction" (*ra'ah*, evil, harm) as v19. The righteous are delivered out of their troubles; the wicked are destroyed by theirs. Evil turns back on the one who does it (7:15–16).
- **"Those who hate the righteous will be condemned" (v21).** "Condemned" (*'asham*) means to be held guilty, to bear the guilt. It is related to the "guilt offering" (Lev 5).
- **"The LORD redeems the life of his servants; none of those who take refuge in him will be condemned" (v22).** This is the extra line after the alphabet is finished, as in 25:22. It falls outside the pattern, like a final word added for emphasis. "Redeems" (*padah*) means to ransom. And the same word "condemned" from v21 appears here with "none." Those who hate the righteous will bear guilt. Those who take refuge in God will not. Paul says it outright: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Rom 8:1). JFB makes the same link.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "He that has a broken heart shall not have a broken bone." He adds that "one would not expect to meet with any thing of Christ here, and yet this scripture is said to be fulfilled in him." On v19 he notes that "between the mercy of heaven and the malice of hell, the afflictions of the righteous must needs be many." JFB on vv21–22: the righteous "shall be delivered and never come into condemnation (Joh 5:24; Ro 8:1); the latter are left under condemnation and desolate."

#### Pointing to Christ
David was a poor man who cried and was heard, a fugitive king surrounded by enemies. The greater Son of David became poor for our sake (2 Cor 8:9). He too was mocked and treated as a madman: "He has a demon, and is insane" (John 10:20). Unlike David, he never lied to save himself. "He committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22, quoting Isa 53:9), and Peter quotes Psalm 34 in the very next chapter. On the cross, the eyes of the LORD were on him, and the Father kept all his bones; not one was broken. Yet he was "crushed for our iniquities" (Isa 53:5), the same idea as the crushed in spirit of v18. He took the condemnation that belongs to those who hate the righteous so that "none of those who take refuge in him will be condemned." And now he invites the humble, the brokenhearted and the crushed to do what David did: taste and see that the LORD is good.
