*Setting:* Babylonia, probably 591–589 BC, in the last years before Babylon's armies surrounded Jerusalem. Chapter 20 told Israel's history as a story of rebellion, and chapter 21 announced God's drawn sword. Chapter 22 sets out the charges, in three messages, each beginning "the word of the LORD came to me" (vv1, 17, 23). The first is a list of crimes in the city (vv1–16). The second pictures Jerusalem as a smelting furnace (vv17–22). The third indicts every class of leader and finds no one to stand in the gap (vv23–31). Almost every charge is drawn from the law, especially the Holiness Code of Leviticus 17–26, so this is a covenant lawsuit. God judges Jerusalem by the very terms it agreed to at Sinai.

#### Israel's Shedding of Blood (vv1–31)
- **"Will you judge, will you judge the bloody city?" (v2).** As in 20:4, Ezekiel is told to act as prosecutor. "Bloody city" is literally "city of bloods," the plural meaning many killings. Nahum had used the same title for Nineveh, Assyria's cruel capital: "Woe to the bloody city, all full of lies and plunder" (Nah 3:1). Now Jerusalem, the holy city, wears the name of the city it once feared most. Ezekiel uses the same title again in 24:6, 9.
- **"A city that sheds blood in her midst… and that makes idols to defile herself" (v3).** The two great charges are named at the start: violence against people and idolatry against God. Blood polluted the land (Num 35:33), and idols defiled the people (Lev 19:4). Together they break both tables of the Ten Commandments.
- **"You have brought your days near, the appointed time of your years has come" (v4).** By her own sins Jerusalem has hurried her day of judgment. **"I have made you a reproach to the nations."** The city meant to be "the joy of all the earth" (Ps 48:2) has become a joke to its neighbors (Lam 2:15). **"Full of tumult" (v5)** pictures a city in chaos and disorder.
- **"The princes of Israel in you, every one according to his power, have been bent on shedding blood" (v6).** Those with the most power did the most harm. "According to his power" is literally "according to his arm." Manasseh "shed very much innocent blood, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another" (2 Kings 21:16), and Jehoiakim killed the prophet Uriah (Jer 26:20–23). The princes are the first to be named, and the chapter returns to them in v27.
- **The list of crimes (vv7–12).** These verses read like a reverse of Leviticus 18–20. Ezekiel works through the law and finds every command broken. The repeated phrase "in you" (about a dozen times) drives the point home. These things are happening inside the holy city.
- **"Father and mother are treated with contempt in you" (v7).** The fifth commandment (Exod 20:12) is broken. "Treated with contempt" is the opposite of "honor," which literally means "to treat as heavy." To despise parents was to treat them as light, of no weight. The law made cursing parents a capital crime (Lev 20:9). **"The sojourner suffers extortion… the fatherless and the widow are wronged."** These were the three groups the law singled out for protection, because they had no one to defend them (Exod 22:21–24; Deut 10:18; 24:17). God calls himself "father of the fatherless and protector of widows" (Ps 68:5).
- **"You have despised my holy things and profaned my Sabbaths" (v8).** The holy things were the sacrifices and objects set apart for God. Treating them as ordinary was the sin of Eli's sons (1 Sam 2:12–17). The Sabbath charge repeats chapter 20 (20:13, 16, 21, 24). Jeremiah confronted people carrying loads through Jerusalem's gates on the Sabbath (Jer 17:21–27).
- **"Men in you who slander to shed blood" (v9).** These were informers who spread false charges to get innocent people killed, as Jezebel's hired witnesses did to Naboth (1 Kings 21:10–13). The law forbade going about as a slanderer (Lev 19:16). **"People in you who eat on the mountains"** are those taking part in idol feasts at the high places (18:6).
- **The sexual sins (vv10–11).** Every one is forbidden in Leviticus 18 and 20: relations with a father's wife (Lev 18:8), with a woman during her period (Lev 18:19), with a neighbor's wife (Lev 18:20), with a daughter-in-law (Lev 18:15), and with a half-sister (Lev 18:9). The family, which the law guarded so carefully, had broken down.
- **"In you they take bribes to shed blood; you take interest and profit" (v12).** Corrupt judges could be paid to condemn the innocent (Exod 23:8; Deut 27:25). Interest on loans to the poor was forbidden (18:8, 13). **"But me you have forgotten, declares the Lord GOD."** This is the root of the whole list. Moses had warned, "Take care lest you forget the LORD your God" (Deut 8:11). Every sin in vv6–12 grows from that one.
- **"Behold, I strike my hand at the dishonest gain that you have made" (v13).** God claps his hands in anger, the same gesture as 21:14, 17. "Dishonest gain" (*betsa'*) is profit gained by violence or fraud. It comes up again in v27.
- **"Can your courage endure, or can your hands be strong, in the days that I shall deal with you?" (v14).** Literally, "Can your heart stand?" The city that was so bold in its sins will have no strength when God acts.
- **"I will scatter you among the nations… and I will consume your uncleanness out of you" (v15).** Exile is judgment, but it also has a purpose. It will burn the uncleanness out of the people. History bears this out. After the exile, Israel never again fell into the open idolatry of the years before it.
- **"You shall be profaned by your own doing in the sight of the nations" (v16).** Some translations read it as "I will be dishonored among the nations because of you" (so the NLT, which the Tyndale notes follow). Either way, Israel's scattering brought shame on both the people and the name of the God they represented.
- **"The house of Israel has become dross to me" (v18).** The second message uses the picture of a smelting furnace. Silver ore was heated until the pure metal separated from the impurities, the "dross" or slag, which was skimmed off and thrown away. Isaiah used this image of refining with hope: "I will smelt away your dross as with lye and remove all your alloy" (Isa 1:25). Ezekiel's picture is darker. **"All of them are bronze and tin and iron and lead… they are dross of silver."** Israel was meant to be silver. Now it is all the base metals, with no silver left in it.
- **"I will gather you into the midst of Jerusalem" (v19).** As Babylon's army advanced, people from the countryside fled into the walled city for safety (Jer 35:11). They thought they were running to shelter. In fact they were being gathered into the furnace. The siege that began in 588 BC would be the fire.
- **"I will put you in and melt you… and you shall know that I am the LORD" (vv20–22).** The word "melt" is repeated five times. In normal refining, the goal is to recover silver. Here the Tyndale notes point out that since only slag goes into the furnace, only slag comes out. Guzik and JFB read it more hopefully, as a refining that removes the bad so the good can be separated. Both readings can be held together. For the city, the fire was destruction. For the remnant God preserved through exile, it became purification (Zech 13:9; Mal 3:2–3).
- **"You are a land that is not cleansed or rained upon in the day of indignation" (v24).** In Israel rain was a sign of God's blessing, and drought a sign of his anger (Deut 11:13–17; 1 Kings 8:35). The Hebrew word "not cleansed" differs by one letter from "not rained upon," and the ancient Greek translation reads "rained upon." Either way, the land is dry and dirty, unwashed by God's blessing.
- **"The conspiracy of her prophets in her midst is like a roaring lion tearing the prey" (v25).** Many scholars think this should read "princes" (as in the Greek), since the Hebrew words for "her prophets" and "her princes" differ by one letter, and the prophets appear again in v28. "Princes" fits the picture of a lion devouring lives and taking treasure. If "prophets" is right, it means false prophets who worked together like a pack, getting rich by telling people what they wanted to hear (13:19).
- **"Her priests have done violence to my law… They have made no distinction between the holy and the common" (v26).** This was the priests' basic task: "You are to distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean, and you are to teach the people of Israel all the statutes" (Lev 10:10–11). They had stopped doing it, and stopped teaching it, so the people no longer knew the difference. Malachi says, "the lips of a priest should guard knowledge" (Mal 2:7). In Ezekiel's vision of the restored temple, this duty is restored (44:23).
- **"Her princes in her midst are like wolves tearing the prey" (v27).** Rulers meant to be shepherds have become predators, the theme Ezekiel develops at length in chapter 34. **"Destroying lives to get dishonest gain."**
- **"Her prophets have smeared whitewash for them" (v28).** This echoes 13:10–16. The prophets covered over the princes' crimes with reassuring words. **"Saying, 'Thus says the Lord GOD,' when the LORD has not spoken."**
- **"The people of the land have practiced extortion and committed robbery" (v29).** "The people of the land" were the landowning citizens of Judah, an influential class who had put several kings on the throne (2 Kings 21:24; 23:30). The corruption ran from top to bottom.
- **The model in Zephaniah.** Verses 25–29 closely follow Zephaniah 3:3–4, written about forty years earlier: "Her officials within her are roaring lions; her judges are evening wolves… Her prophets are fickle, treacherous men; her priests profane what is holy; they do violence to the law." Ezekiel takes up Zephaniah's indictment and widens it to include the people of the land. Nothing had changed except that it had gotten worse.
- **"I sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach before me for the land, that I should not destroy it, but I found none" (v30).** This is the heartbreaking climax. The picture is the same as 13:5: a breach in a city wall, and someone needed to rush into the gap and hold it. In the spiritual sense this meant someone who would turn the people to repentance and intercede for them. Moses had done exactly this: "he would have destroyed them, had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath" (Ps 106:23). Phinehas did it too (Ps 106:30). God was looking for someone to stand there. **"But I found none."**
- **What about Jeremiah?** Jeremiah was in Jerusalem at this time, faithful and praying. But God had told him three times not to pray for the people (Jer 7:16; 11:14; 14:11), and he was a lone voice without power. The point is not that no righteous person existed in Jerusalem but that none of its leaders, the princes, priests and prophets named in vv25–28, did their job. Jeremiah 5:1 has a similar challenge: "Run to and fro through the streets of Jerusalem… if you can find a man, one who does justice… that I may pardon her."
- **"Therefore I have poured out my indignation upon them… I have returned their way upon their heads" (v31).** The verbs are in the past tense. The judgment is as good as done. It is also just: their own way comes back on them.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that Jerusalem "is so called, because of her crimes," and that "unmindfulness of God was at the bottom of all this wickedness." On the dross he says that sinners, "especially backsliding professors, are, in God's account, useless and fit for nothing." On the leaders: "All orders and degrees of men had helped to fill the measure of the nation's guilt." He also observes that "it bodes ill to a people when judgments are breaking in upon them, and the spirit of prayer is restrained." JFB reads the "talebearers" as "informers, who by misrepresentations cause innocent blood to be shed," and sees the furnace as one in which "the bad may be consumed, and the good separated." It says that none stood in the gap partly because Jeremiah had been forbidden to pray for the people. Guzik notes the borrowing from Nahum's "bloody city," agrees with Henry that forgetting God is the root of the list, and sees the man God sought fulfilled in Jesus, who always lives to intercede (Heb 7:25).

#### Pointing to Christ
"I sought for a man… who should… stand in the breach before me for the land… but I found none." In Ezekiel's day there was no one. Isaiah saw the same thing: the LORD "saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no one to intercede; then his own arm brought him salvation" (Isa 59:16). God did not find the man he was looking for. He sent him. Jesus is the one who stood in the breach, not only praying for a guilty city but taking its judgment on himself. Where Jerusalem's princes were wolves and its priests failed to teach the difference between holy and common, Jesus is the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11) and the High Priest who is "holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners" (Heb 7:26). And he still stands in the gap: "he always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25).
