*Setting:* Sometime in the middle years of Saul's reign, perhaps around 1020 BC. David has been secretly anointed by Samuel, and the Spirit of the LORD has come on him (16:13), while the Spirit has left Saul (16:14). The Philistines remain Israel's great enemy. Saul has fought them "all the days" of his reign (14:52) without ever breaking their power. This time they push into the Shephelah, the low rolling hills between the Philistine coastal plain and Judah's hill country, the natural corridor for an invasion toward Bethlehem and Hebron. The story of David and Goliath is the best known in the Old Testament. Its point is not that underdogs can win if they believe in themselves. It is that "the battle is the LORD's" (v47), and that the LORD's anointed king fights for his frightened people.

#### David and Goliath (vv1–58)
- **Socoh, Azekah and the Valley of Elah (vv1–3).** Socoh and Azekah were towns in Judah's western foothills, about 15 miles west of Bethlehem. The Valley of Elah ("terebinth tree") is a broad valley running east to west, with a stream bed down the middle. The two armies stood on the ridges on either side, with the valley between. The site can still be visited. Ephes-dammim, "boundary of blood," was apparently a regular battleground.
- **"A champion named Goliath of Gath" (v4).** "Champion" is literally "the man of the between," the man who steps into the space between two armies. In the ancient world, battles were sometimes settled by single combat between champions, as Homer's *Iliad* describes and as happened later between David's and Ish-bosheth's men (2 Sam 2:14–16). Gath was one of the five Philistine cities. When Joshua conquered the land, some of the Anakim, the giant clan that had terrified the spies (Num 13:33), survived in Gath (Josh 11:22). Gath later produced other giants (2 Sam 21:18–22).
- **"Six cubits and a span" (v4).** A cubit was about 18 inches and a span about 9, making him about 9 feet 9 inches. The oldest Hebrew manuscript of Samuel, among the Dead Sea Scrolls, along with the Greek translation and Josephus, reads "four cubits and a span," about 6 feet 9 inches. Either way, he was a giant to men who were usually around five feet tall. Many scholars think "four" is original. Others defend "six." The point of the story is the same.
- **His armor (vv5–7).** A bronze helmet, a coat of scale armor weighing 5,000 shekels (about 125 pounds), bronze greaves on his shins, a bronze javelin slung on his back, and a spear with a shaft "like a weaver's beam" and an iron head of 600 shekels (about 15 pounds). The "weaver's beam" may mean the spear was as thick as the bar of a loom, or that it had a loop and cord like a loom's heddle, which let it be thrown with a spin. The writer lingers over the bronze and iron. The Philistines controlled metal (13:19–22), and their champion is covered in it. He even has a man to carry his shield.
- **"Am I not a Philistine, and are you not servants of Saul?" (v8).** Goliath makes it a contest between peoples and between their gods. "Choose a man for yourselves" is a challenge to Israel's king. Saul, a head taller than anyone in Israel (9:2), was the obvious man to answer. He did not.
- **"I defy the ranks of Israel this day" (v10).** "Defy" (*charaph*) means to taunt, reproach, insult. It runs through the chapter (vv10, 25, 26, 36, 45). Goliath thinks he is insulting Saul's army. David hears him insulting "the armies of the living God" (v26).
- **"Saul and all Israel… were dismayed and greatly afraid" (v11).** The king whose people wanted him to "go out before us and fight our battles" (8:20) is frozen.
- **"David was the son of an Ephrathite of Bethlehem" (v12).** The story pauses to introduce David as if for the first time. Ephrath was an old name for Bethlehem (Gen 35:19; Ruth 1:2; Mic 5:2). David is the youngest of eight sons, and his three eldest brothers are in Saul's army.
- **Two strands of the story.** Readers have long noticed that chapter 17 introduces David as though we have not met him, and that at the end Saul asks whose son he is (vv55–58), even though in chapter 16 David became Saul's musician and armor-bearer. The main Greek manuscript (Codex Vaticanus) has a much shorter version of chapters 17–18 without vv12–31, 41, 48b, 50 and 55–58. Some scholars think two accounts of David's rise were woven together. Others point to v15, which says David "went back and forth from Saul to feed his father's sheep," so his service at court was occasional, and Saul may not have known him well. These are honest questions about how the book was put together, and both sides take the text seriously. There is more on Saul's question at the end of the chapter.
- **"For forty days the Philistine came forward… morning and evening" (v16).** Forty is often a number of testing in Scripture (Israel's forty years in the wilderness, Jesus' forty days in the desert). Twice a day for six weeks, Israel heard the challenge and did nothing.
- **"Take for your brothers an ephah of this parched grain, and these ten loaves" (v17).** Armies of that time often had no supply system, and families fed their own soldiers. An ephah was about half a bushel, roughly 20 to 30 pounds of roasted grain. The ten cheeses for the commander (v18) were a polite gift. "Bring some token" means some proof they were well. David arrives as an errand boy with lunch.
- **"Left the sheep with a keeper" (v20).** A small detail that says something. David does not abandon his duty, even when hurrying to the front. He leaves his supplies with the baggage keeper too (v22), a detail that will matter later in his life (30:24).
- **"The king will enrich the man who kills him… give him his daughter and make his father's house free" (v25).** "Free" probably means free from taxes and forced labor. Saul offers wealth, a royal marriage and privileges, but no one comes forward. Saul's promise of his daughter will come back in chapter 18, and he will not keep it straightforwardly.
- **"Who is this uncircumcised Philistine, that he should defy the armies of the living God?" (v26).** These are David's first recorded words in the Bible. The soldiers talk about the size of the man and the size of the reward. David talks about God. "Uncircumcised" means outside God's covenant (14:6). "The living God" is set against the lifeless gods of the Philistines, like Dagon, who had fallen on his face before the ark (5:3–4). David does ask about the reward, but his concern is "the reproach from Israel."
- **"I know your presumption and the evil of your heart" (v28).** Eliab, the brother who looked like a king (16:6–7), now shows his heart. His contempt—"those few sheep"—and his accusation that David just wants to watch a fight sound like jealousy and perhaps shame. Ironically, he accuses David of the very "evil of heart" the LORD had found in Eliab himself.
- **"What have I done now? Was it not but a word?" (v29).** "Now" suggests this is not the first time Eliab has treated David this way. David does not fight his brother. He turns away and keeps asking.
- **"Let no man's heart fail because of him. Your servant will go and fight" (v32).** The shepherd volunteers. Saul objects: "you are but a youth, and he has been a man of war from his youth" (v33). The word "youth" (*na'ar*) can mean anyone from a child to a young man of military age. David was probably in his late teens.
- **The lion and the bear (vv34–36).** Lions and Syrian brown bears lived in the land in those days (Judg 14:5; 2 Kgs 2:24). A shepherd was expected to defend the flock even at risk to his life (Amos 3:12). David had done just that, chasing down predators and rescuing lambs from their mouths. To him, Goliath is one more wild beast attacking God's flock.
- **"The LORD who delivered me from the paw of the lion… will deliver me from the hand of this Philistine" (v37).** This is the heart of David's courage. It is not confidence in his own skill but faith built on past experience of God's faithfulness. What happened in private, with no audience, prepared him for this public moment. Saul says, "Go, and the LORD be with you!" Readers already know the LORD *is* with him (16:18).
- **Saul's armor (vv38–39).** Saul tries to equip David his way. But David "had not tested them" and cannot even walk. Saul's armor would have made him a smaller, slower copy of the giant. He takes it off. There is irony too: the king who should have been wearing his armor and fighting is dressing someone else to do it.
- **"Five smooth stones from the brook" (v40).** Smooth, rounded stones flew straighter. The sling was a serious weapon, not a toy. It was a leather pouch on two cords, swung around to hurl a stone with great speed and accuracy. Ancient armies had units of slingers, and Benjamin had seven hundred left-handed men who "could sling a stone at a hair and not miss" (Judg 20:16). A skilled slinger could hit a target at a good distance with lethal force. Why five? Perhaps simply as spares. Some note that Goliath had relatives in Gath (2 Sam 21:22), but the text does not say.
- **"He disdained him, for he was but a youth, ruddy and handsome" (v42).** The language repeats David's description from 16:12. Goliath judges by appearance, exactly as the LORD told Samuel not to (16:7).
- **"Am I a dog, that you come to me with sticks?" (v43).** "Dog" was a strong insult in that world (2 Sam 9:8; 16:9). Goliath sees only David's staff. "Cursed David by his gods" makes it plain that this is a contest between gods. Goliath's threat to feed David to the birds and beasts (v44) was the ultimate disgrace, since leaving a body unburied was a curse (Deut 28:26).
- **"I come to you in the name of the LORD of hosts, the God of the armies of Israel, whom you have defied" (v45).** David's answer is the theological center of the chapter. "LORD of hosts" (*YHWH Tsebaoth*) means the LORD who commands armies, both Israel's and heaven's. The title first appears in the Bible in 1 Samuel 1:3. Goliath trusts weapons. David trusts a name, the revealed character and power of God (Ps 20:7: "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God").
- **"That all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel" (v46).** David's aim is not his own glory. He wants the nations to know the true God (compare Josh 4:24; 1 Kgs 18:36–37). And Israel too: "that all this assembly may know that the LORD saves not with sword and spear. For the battle is the LORD's" (v47). Jehoshaphat will hear the same words (2 Chr 20:15).
- **"David ran quickly toward the battle line" (v48).** Faith is not passive. Goliath lumbers forward, and David runs at him.
- **"The stone sank into his forehead, and he fell on his face to the ground" (v49).** The forehead may have been one of the few places not covered by armor, or the helmet may have left a gap. "Fell on his face" echoes Dagon falling on his face before the ark (5:3–4). The Philistine champion bows to the ground before the LORD's anointed.
- **"There was no sword in the hand of David" (v50).** The writer underlines it. The point of v47 is proved. David "cut off his head" with Goliath's own sword (v51), as Benaiah would later kill an Egyptian with his own spear (2 Sam 23:21). The weapon of the enemy becomes the means of his defeat.
- **"When the Philistines saw that their champion was dead, they fled" (v51).** Goliath had promised that if he lost, the Philistines would become Israel's servants (v9). They run instead. Israel, freed from fear, chases them back to their own cities, Gath and Ekron (v52). Shaaraim ("two gates") was a town in the area (Josh 15:36). Some archaeologists identify it with Khirbet Qeiyafa, a fortified town with two gates overlooking the Valley of Elah, dated to around this period, though that identification is debated.
- **"David took the head of the Philistine and brought it to Jerusalem" (v54).** Jerusalem was still a Jebusite fortress and would not be captured until David became king (2 Sam 5:6–9). The writer may be summarizing what David eventually did with the head, or David may have displayed it near Jerusalem as a warning. Goliath's sword turns up later at the sanctuary in Nob, kept behind the ephod (21:9).
- **"Abner, whose son is this youth?" (vv55–58).** After David has served Saul as a musician (16:21–23), the question is surprising. Several explanations have been offered. Saul may have known David but not his family, and he needed to know whose son this was because he had promised to free the hero's "father's house" and give him his daughter (v25). David had perhaps only served occasionally (v15), Saul's mind was troubled (16:14), and young men can change a great deal in a year or two. Or, as noted above, the story may combine two accounts. David answers simply: "I am the son of your servant Jesse the Bethlehemite." That is the last word of the chapter, the name of the house of David.
- **Commentators:** Henry is struck by the contrast: "A shepherd lad, come the same morning from keeping sheep, had more courage than all the mighty men of Israel." On David's answer to Eliab: "with a soft answer he turned away his brother's wrath. This conquest of his own passion was more honourable than that of Goliath." On Saul's armor, "It is not to be inquired how excellent any thing is, but how proper," and David "gloried in his mean appearance and arms, that the victory might be ascribed to the Lord alone." JFB describes the custom of champions trading "torrents of abuse" before combat, and says "David's speech, however, presents a striking contrast to the usual strain of these invectives." JFB places the stone "at the opening for the eyes—that was the only exposed part of his body," and explains Saul's question by noting that young men in the East were often known more by their father's name than their own. Guzik estimates Goliath's height at between 8½ and 9 feet. He builds his application around David's private faithfulness with the sheep becoming public courage, and he warns against using trust in God as an excuse for inaction, since David ran toward the battle. Guzik also suggests the five stones were for Goliath's relatives. The Tyndale notes add that David's victory over a man who cursed him "by his gods" was also a victory over Dagon.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel stood paralyzed for forty days before an enemy too strong for them, and the king who should have fought was afraid. Then the anointed son of Jesse, sent by his father with food for his brothers and scorned by them, went out alone to fight as Israel's representative. His single victory became the whole army's victory. The New Testament tells the same story on a greater scale. Jesus, the Son of David, was sent by his Father to his brothers and rejected by them (John 1:11). He went out alone against the enemy who held God's people "in lifelong slavery" through fear of death (Heb 2:14–15). He defeated him with what looked like weakness, a cross instead of sword and spear, and turned the enemy's own weapon, death, into the means of his defeat. "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Col 2:15). His people do not win the battle. They share in the victory he won, and then rise up and pursue.
