*Setting:* The plains of Moab, on the eve of the conquest. This chapter finishes the calendar of public offerings that began in chapter 28. That chapter covered the daily, weekly and monthly offerings and the spring feasts of Passover and Weeks. This one covers the seventh month, Tishri, in September or October, the busiest and holiest month of Israel's year: the Feast of Trumpets on the first day, the Day of Atonement on the tenth, and the Feast of Booths from the fifteenth for seven days, with a closing assembly on the eighth. It falls at the end of the harvest, when the grapes and olives were gathered in. The lists are repetitive by design. Their pattern, more than any single verse, is what carries the meaning.

#### Offerings for the Feast of Trumpets (vv1–6)
- **"On the first day of the seventh month" (v1).** The seventh month carries the weight of the number seven: as the seventh day was holy, so was the seventh month. Its new moon was marked more solemnly than any other.
- **"It is a day for you to blow the trumpets" (v1).** Literally "a day of shouting" or "of blasts." Leviticus 23:24 calls it "a memorial proclaimed with blast of trumpets." The instrument was probably the ram's horn (*shofar*), though the silver trumpets of 10:1–10 were also blown at the new moons. The blast called Israel to attention, to gather and to remember. Later Jewish tradition made this day *Rosh Hashanah*, "head of the year," the civil New Year, but the Old Testament itself does not call it that.
- **"One bull… one ram, seven male lambs" (v2).** One bull fewer than the other new moons (28:11). **"One male goat for a sin offering, to make atonement for you" (v5).**
- **"Besides the burnt offering of the new moon… and the regular burnt offering" (v6).** The offerings stack. On this day the priests offered the daily lambs, then the new-moon offering, then the trumpet-day offering on top. The layers tell the story: every day belongs to God, every month belongs to God, and some days call for more.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the day was New Year's in the civil calendar, "ushered in by a flourish of trumpets," and that the ordinance gave it "a religious character." Henry's outline frames the chapter as "the three great solemnities of the seventh month."

#### Offerings for the Day of Atonement (vv7–11)
- **"On the tenth day of this seventh month… afflict yourselves" (v7).** The Day of Atonement, *Yom Kippur* (Lev 16; 23:26–32). "Afflict yourselves" means to humble yourselves, and Jewish practice has always understood it as fasting (Ps 35:13; Isa 58:3, 5). It was the one fast day the law required. Acts 27:9 calls it simply "the Fast." It was a day of total rest, more strictly than other feasts: **"You shall do no work."**
- **Why so short?** The day's main rituals are described in Leviticus 16: the high priest entering the Most Holy Place, the blood sprinkled on the mercy seat, and the scapegoat sent into the wilderness carrying the people's sins. Numbers only lists the extra public offerings for the day, one bull, one ram and seven lambs.
- **"Besides the sin offering of atonement" (v11).** This refers to the special sin offerings of Leviticus 16, the bull for the priest and the two goats for the people. Here, one more goat is added. Even on the day of atonement, the offerings for the day themselves needed atonement.
- **Commentators:** JFB distinguishes the "large animal sacrifice for a general expiation" from a further sin offering for sins that "mingled with that day's services." Guzik stresses that this was not a joyful feast but a day of soul-searching about sin, which the national sacrifice put away.

#### Offerings for the Feast of Booths (vv12–40)
- **"On the fifteenth day of the seventh month… you shall keep a feast to the LORD seven days" (v12).** The Feast of Booths or Tabernacles (*Sukkot*). Leviticus 23:39–43 explains it: Israelites lived in shelters made of branches for a week, to remember that God made them live in booths when he brought them out of Egypt. It came after the harvest (Deut 16:13–15), so it was the most joyful festival of the year: "you shall rejoice before the LORD your God" (Lev 23:40). It was later called simply "the feast" (1 Kgs 8:2; John 7:2).
- **"Thirteen bulls… two rams, fourteen male lambs" (v13).** The offerings for this feast are far larger than any other. The rams and lambs are double the usual set. And the bulls start at thirteen.
- **The countdown of bulls (vv17–34).** Thirteen, twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven. Every day one bull fewer. By the seventh day there are seven bulls, and across the week they add up to **seventy**. Every number in the pattern is built on seven: seven days, fourteen lambs a day, seven bulls on the last day, seventy bulls in all. The text does not explain why the number falls. Some think it simply eases the burden as the week goes on. Later rabbis said the seventy bulls were offered for the seventy nations of the world listed in Genesis 10. That is tradition, not the text, but Zechariah does picture the nations coming up to Jerusalem year after year "to keep the Feast of Booths" (Zech 14:16).
- **"In the prescribed quantities" (vv18, 21, 24…).** The phrase repeats day after day. The grain and wine always match the animals by the rule of 15:1–12.
- **"Also one male goat for a sin offering" (vv16, 19, 22…).** It never drops out. Even in the most joyful week of the year, every day includes atonement for sin.
- **"On the eighth day you shall have a solemn assembly" (v35).** The word (*atseret*) means a closing gathering. After the week, a single day with a modest offering: **one bull, one ram, seven male lambs (v36),** the same as Trumpets and the Day of Atonement. The great feast closes quietly. In John's Gospel, "on the last day of the feast, the great day," Jesus stood up and cried out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37). By then the feast included a daily ceremony of pouring water at the altar, a prayer for rain and a reminder of the water from the rock. Most scholars think the "great day" there means the seventh or eighth day.
- **"In addition to your vow offerings and your freewill offerings" (v39).** These two chapters list only the public sacrifices the priests offered for the whole nation. On top of them, ordinary Israelites brought their own offerings: offerings promised in a vow, given freely out of gratitude, and peace offerings shared as meals. The public calendar was the minimum, not the ceiling.
- **The total.** Adding up chapters 28 and 29 gives well over a thousand animals a year in public offerings alone, more than a thousand lambs and over a hundred bulls, along with tons of flour and large quantities of oil and wine. JFB counts 1,241 public animal sacrifices, and Guzik reaches a similar figure. It was a costly, nation-wide confession that everything belonged to God and that sin needed atonement every day.
- **"So Moses told the people of Israel everything just as the LORD had commanded Moses" (v40).** The calendar is handed on, complete.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the sacrifices at the Feast of Booths "varied in a progressive ratio of decrease every day," and gives three reasons: it was the year's end, a chance to "supply any past deficiencies"; it followed the harvest, so called for "a liberal acknowledgment"; and God "consulted the weakness of mankind," making each day "less toilsome and expensive." On v35, JFB links the eighth day to "the great day" of John 7:37. Guzik says the abundance of animals pictured how richly God provided in the wilderness, and he reminds readers that no number of animal sacrifices could take away sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
The seventh month moved from trumpets to atonement to joy: a call to attention, a day of humbling before God, and then a week of celebration under booths of branches, with sin offerings never missing. The New Testament sees each of these fulfilled in Jesus. The trumpet will sound at his return (1 Thess 4:16). He is the high priest who entered "once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption" (Heb 9:12), the true Day of Atonement. And the joy of the Feast of Booths points to the day when "the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them" (Rev 21:3), where the word "dwell" is the word for pitching a tent. At that feast Jesus stood up and offered living water to anyone who was thirsty (John 7:37–38). The "Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), literally "tabernacled," and in him the whole costly calendar finds its end.
