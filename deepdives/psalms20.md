*Setting:* "A Psalm of David," for the choirmaster. This is a royal psalm, a prayer by the people for their king as he goes out to battle. The voices change as it goes: the congregation prays for the king (vv1–5), a single voice, perhaps a priest, a prophet or the king himself, announces that God will answer (vv6–8), and everyone joins in a final cry (v9). Henry suggests it was either written for a particular campaign or used regularly in temple worship whenever the king went to war. It pairs with Psalm 21, which gives thanks for the victory this psalm prays for: "You have given him his heart's desire" (21:2) answers "May he grant you your heart's desire" (20:4). David was a warrior king, and his wars against nations with chariots, like the Arameans (2 Sam 8:3–5; 10:18), give the psalm its edge. Psalm 18 ended with God's "great salvation" for "his king" and "his anointed." Psalm 20 is how Israel prayed for that king.

#### May the LORD Answer You (vv1–5)
- **"May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble!" (v1).** The people speak to the king, but every line is really a prayer to God on his behalf. "Day of trouble" (*tsarah*) is a day of distress, a tight place, like the "distress" of 18:6. Paul asked believers to pray "for kings and all who are in high positions" (1 Tim 2:1–2). This psalm is an early example of exactly that.
- **"May the name of the God of Jacob protect you!" (v1).** God's "name" is God himself as he has made himself known, his character and his promises. "Protect" means set on high, out of reach, like a fortress on a cliff (18:2). Why "the God of Jacob"? Jacob was the patriarch who, running from his brother, met God at Bethel and later said, "let us go up to Bethel, so that I may make there an altar to the God who answers me in the day of my distress" (Gen 35:3). The psalm uses almost the same words. The God who answered Jacob will answer Jacob's king.
- **"Help from the sanctuary… support from Zion!" (v2).** "Sanctuary" is literally "holiness," the holy place. The ark was in Jerusalem on Mount Zion, so the people pray that help will come from the place where God's presence was centered among them (2 Sam 6:17). The army goes out from Zion, and the help comes from there too.
- **"May he remember all your offerings" (v3).** Before battle, a king offered sacrifices (1 Sam 7:9; 13:9–12). "Offerings" (*minchah*) are gifts, often grain offerings. "Burnt sacrifices" (*olah*) were wholly burned on the altar, a picture of complete dedication. "Regard with favor" is literally "make fat" or "accept as fat," the richest part, which belonged to God (Lev 3:16). God does not accept every sacrifice, only those offered with faith and a right heart (1 Sam 15:22; Ps 51:16–17).
- **Selah (v3).** The word appears 71 times in the Psalms and its meaning is uncertain. Most think it marks a pause, musical or reflective. Here it falls between the sacrifice and the request, as if waiting for God to accept the offering.
- **"May he grant you your heart's desire and fulfill all your plans!" (v4).** This assumes the king's heart and plans are aimed in the right direction. A king who wanted what God wanted could pray this boldly (37:4). The next psalm records the answer (21:2).
- **"May we shout for joy over your salvation" (v5).** "Salvation" here means victory, deliverance in battle. The people already picture the celebration.
- **"In the name of our God set up our banners!" (v5).** Each tribe marched under its own banner (Num 2:2–3). Raising banners was a signal of gathering, of advance, or of triumph. Israel would raise its flags in God's name, not the king's. When Moses won the battle against Amalek, he built an altar and called it "The LORD Is My Banner" (Exod 17:15). The Greek translation, reading the Hebrew letters slightly differently, has "we shall be made great," and JFB mentions this as a possible reading.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that David, inspired, could fittingly write a form of prayer for the congregation to pray for him: "it is very proper for those who desire the prayers of their friends to tell them" what to pray. On banners he says Israel would both "wage war in his name" and "celebrate our victories in his name," so that "no instrument shall have any part of the honour that is due to him." JFB reads "accept" in v3 as literally "turn to ashes," a sign of God accepting the sacrifice by fire as at Carmel (1 Kgs 18:38). The lexicon gives "find fat," that is, acceptable, as the sense here, though a related noun means the fatty ashes left on the altar, so JFB's reading has some basis. Guzik notes that the "sanctuary" is simply "holiness," and passes on Spurgeon's comment that v1 reads like a blessing from the pulpit on a Sunday morning.

#### Now I Know (vv6–8)
- **"Now I know that the LORD saves his anointed" (v6).** The voice changes from "we" to "I," and the mood changes from wish to certainty. Something has happened between v5 and v6. Perhaps a priest or prophet received a word from God, or perhaps the sacrifice was accepted. Either way, the battle has not yet been fought, but the outcome is settled. "Saves" (*yasha*) comes from the same root as the name *Yeshua*, Jesus, "the LORD saves" (Matt 1:21). "His anointed" (*mashiach*) is the king, as in 2:2 and 18:50.
- **"He will answer him from his holy heaven" (v6).** Help comes "from Zion" in v2, and now "from his holy heaven." These are not two different sources. The earthly sanctuary was the footstool of God's heavenly throne (11:4; 1 Kgs 8:30).
- **"The saving might of his right hand" (v6).** The right hand is God's strength in action (17:7; 18:35). It is the same right hand that shattered Pharaoh's army (Exod 15:6).
- **"Some trust in chariots and some in horses" (v7).** Chariots were the tanks of the ancient world. Egyptians, Canaanites, Philistines and Arameans all fielded them (Josh 17:16; Judg 4:3; 1 Sam 13:5). Israel, living mostly in hill country, had few. God told Israel's future kings not to "acquire many horses" or lead the people back to Egypt to get them (Deut 17:16). When David captured an Aramean chariot force, he hamstrung most of the horses (2 Sam 8:4). Solomon broke that rule (1 Kgs 10:26–29), and later Isaiah warned, "Woe to those who… rely on horses, who trust in chariots because they are many… but do not look to the Holy One of Israel" (Isa 31:1).
- **"But we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (v7).** The Hebrew has no verb in the first half and a different verb in the second: literally "these in chariots, those in horses, but we in the name of the LORD our God *will make mention*" (or "will remember"). To "make mention" of God's name means to call on it and boast in it. David said the same to Goliath: "You come to me with a sword and with a spear… but I come to you in the name of the LORD of hosts" (1 Sam 17:45). The point is not that Israel had no weapons. It is where their confidence rested.
- **"They collapse and fall, but we rise and stand upright" (v8).** The verbs are in the past tense in Hebrew, describing the victory as if it had already happened, the confidence of faith before the battle. "Stand upright" means to straighten up, to get back on one's feet. The ones who trusted in what they could see fall down. The ones who trusted in what they could not see stand.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the speaker of v6 "speaks as if suddenly assured of a hearing," and reads "his anointed" as "not only David personally, but as the specially appointed head of His Church." On v8 he says "stand upright" is literally "we have straightened ourselves up from our distress and fears." Henry suggests David had the Arameans in mind, "whose forces consisted much of chariots and horsemen" (2 Sam 8:4; 10:18), and has Israel say that even if they had chariots, they would not "build our hopes of success upon that." Guzik passes on Morgan's point that this is the language of faith not after the battle but before it, and Maclaren's comment that the Name is mightier than any weapon.

#### O LORD, Save the King! (v9)
- **"O LORD, save the king!" (v9).** The psalm ends with everyone together, returning to prayer. Even after "now I know," the people keep asking. Assurance does not stop prayer. It fuels it.
- **Two ways to read the verse.** The Hebrew can be divided two ways. The ESV, following the ancient Greek and Latin translations, reads "O LORD, save the king! May he answer us when we call," with "he" meaning God. The traditional Hebrew text, followed by the KJV, reads "Save, O LORD! May the King answer us when we call." In that reading "the King" is God himself, the true King of Israel, or the human king answering his people's call for help. The first reading gave English worship its old prayer, "O Lord, save the king," which Henry notes was "taken into our English Liturgy." Both readings end where the psalm began, with God answering "in the day of trouble" (v1) and "when we call" (v9).
- **Commentators:** JFB, reading the KJV, explains "let the king hear" as the king acting "as God's representative, delivered to deliver," but then says "perhaps a better sense is, 'Lord, save the king; hear us when we call.'" Henry offers both readings. In the first, the people pray that the king will listen to them "for justice and mercy." He also suggests it may point to the Messiah, "that King of kings," the great "Master of requests" who receives his people's prayers. Guzik follows the reading "Save, LORD! May the King answer us," and notes Kidner's point that "when we call" echoes "the day of trouble" in v1.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel prayed for their anointed king going out to battle, and God answered and saved him. The greatest battle was fought by the greatest Anointed One. In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed in his own "day of trouble," and the Father did not spare him the cross. But God did answer "from his holy heaven with the saving might of his right hand": he raised him from the dead and seated him at that right hand (Acts 2:32–33). The name that saves is now the name of Jesus, "for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). Those who trust in chariots, horses, money or power collapse and fall. Those who call on his name rise and stand upright. And the King who was saved is now the King who answers his people when they call.
