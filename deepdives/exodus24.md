*Setting:* Israel is camped at Mount Sinai, about three months out of Egypt. God has spoken the Ten Commandments to the whole nation (20:1–17), and Moses has received the case laws of the "Book of the Covenant" (20:22–23:33). Now the covenant God offered in 19:5–6 is formally sealed. In the ancient world a treaty was made binding by a public reading of its terms, the people's sworn agreement, a sacrifice, a written copy and often a shared meal. All of these happen here. This is one of the high points of the Old Testament: Israel's elders see the God of Israel and eat in his presence. Then Moses climbs into the cloud for forty days to receive the plans for the tabernacle (chs 25–31). The chapter is a hinge between the law Israel must keep and the dwelling place where God will live among them.

#### The Covenant Confirmed (vv1–18)
- **"Come up to the LORD, you and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel" (v1).** Nadab and Abihu were Aaron's two oldest sons (6:23), soon to be ordained as priests (28:1). They saw God on this mountain, and yet not long afterward they died for offering "unauthorized fire" before the LORD (Lev 10:1–2). Seeing God's glory does not guarantee a faithful heart. The **seventy elders** represent the whole people. Seventy was the number of Jacob's family that went down to Egypt (1:5), and later Moses appoints seventy elders to share his burden (Num 11:16–25). Jesus would later send out seventy (or seventy-two) disciples (Luke 10:1). **"Worship from afar."** "Worship" is literally "bow down." Even the leaders keep their distance.
- **"Moses alone shall come near to the LORD" (v2).** Three levels of nearness: the people at the foot of the mountain, the leaders partway up, and Moses alone at the top. The tabernacle will repeat the pattern with its outer court, Holy Place, and Most Holy Place, which only the high priest may enter.
- **Two trips or one?** Verses 1–2 give the command, and vv9–11 tell how it was carried out. Verses 3–8, the covenant ceremony at the foot of the mountain, come in between. The Tyndale notes think the command was given in advance and obeyed at the right time, so the leaders went up only once.
- **"Moses came and told the people all the words of the LORD and all the rules" (v3).** "Words" probably means the Ten Words (20:1), and "rules" the *mishpatim*, the case laws of 21:1. **"All the people answered with one voice."** As at 19:8, they answer as one. **"All the words that the LORD has spoken we will do."** It is a sincere answer. Within forty days they will break the first two commandments with a golden calf (32:1–8).
- **"And Moses wrote down all the words of the LORD" (v4).** This is the first time Exodus says Moses wrote down God's law. (He had already written the account of the battle with Amalek, 17:14.) Treaties had to be written and kept, and God's covenant was not left to human memory. Here Scripture as a written book begins to take shape.
- **"Built an altar at the foot of the mountain, and twelve pillars, according to the twelve tribes" (v4).** The altar stood for God, and the twelve standing stones for the tribes, so both parties to the covenant were represented. The Tyndale notes add that in ancient treaties the gods were called to witness, but Israel was forbidden other gods, so the stones themselves stand as witnesses (compare Josh 24:26–27). These are not the forbidden Canaanite pillars of 23:24, which were images of gods.
- **"He sent young men of the people of Israel, who offered burnt offerings and sacrificed peace offerings" (v5).** The Levitical priesthood did not yet exist. These young men were probably firstborn sons, who served as priests in their families before the Levites took their place (13:2; 19:22; Num 3:12). **Burnt offerings** were wholly burned, a sign of total dedication. **Peace offerings** were mostly eaten in a fellowship meal, which may be the meal the elders eat on the mountain (v11).
- **"Moses took half of the blood and put it in basins, and half of the blood he threw against the altar" (v6).** Blood stands for life given up in death (Lev 17:11). Half goes on the altar, God's side of the covenant. The Tyndale notes suggest this represents God binding himself by oath to keep it (compare Gen 15:9–18, where God alone passed between the pieces). The other half waits for the people's answer.
- **"He took the Book of the Covenant and read it in the hearing of the people" (v7).** This is where the name "Book of the Covenant" comes from, and probably refers to 20:22–23:33. Public reading of the covenant became a pattern in Israel (Deut 31:10–13; Josh 8:34–35; 2 Kgs 23:2; Neh 8:1–8). **"All that the LORD has spoken we will do, and we will be obedient."** Literally "we will do, and we will hear." Jewish tradition makes much of the order: Israel promised to do before they had even heard it all. "Hear" in Hebrew (*shama*) also means to obey.
- **"Moses took the blood and threw it on the people" (v8).** Probably on the leaders or the front rows, as representatives of the whole nation, or on the twelve pillars standing for them. The same blood that went on the altar now goes on the people. Both are bound by one sacrifice. Hebrews 9:19–20 adds that Moses sprinkled the book as well, with water, scarlet wool and hyssop, details drawn from other Old Testament ceremonies.
- **"Behold the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you" (v8).** These words echo through the Bible. Zechariah speaks of "the blood of my covenant with you" (Zech 9:11). At the Last Supper Jesus took the cup and said, "This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (Matt 26:28; Mark 14:24). Luke and Paul record "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25), reaching back to Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant (Jer 31:31). Hebrews quotes this very verse and says, "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins" (Heb 9:20–22). Jesus deliberately took Moses' words and made them about himself.
- **"They saw the God of Israel" (v10).** This is one of the most daring sentences in the Old Testament. Elsewhere God says, "man shall not see me and live" (33:20), and John says, "No one has ever seen God" (John 1:18). The text is careful. It describes nothing of God himself, only what was **"under his feet."** They saw a revelation of God, a vision of his glory, not his full essence. Isaiah describes only the train of his robe (Isa 6:1), and Ezekiel, after a long description, says only "the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD" (Ezek 1:28). Many Christians have also seen in Old Testament appearances of God a revelation of the Son, "the image of the invisible God" (Col 1:15).
- **"As it were a pavement of sapphire stone, like the very heaven for clearness" (v10).** "As it were" signals that words are straining. The Hebrew *sappir* most likely refers to lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone flecked with gold, prized in Egypt and Mesopotamia, rather than the modern clear sapphire. The picture is of a floor like the blue sky itself. Ezekiel saw God's throne above a crystal expanse "like sapphire" (Ezek 1:22, 26), and John saw "a sea of glass, like crystal" before the throne (Rev 4:6). The leaders stand under the floor of heaven and look up.
- **"He did not lay his hand on the chief men of the people of Israel" (v11).** "Chief men" is a rare word (*atsil*), used only here for people. The lexicon gives "side, corner," and by extension "nobles," perhaps as the supports of the people. In other words, God did not strike them down. After the warnings of chapter 19, this is astonishing grace. The blood had been shed and sprinkled first.
- **"They beheld God, and ate and drank" (v11).** "Beheld" is a different verb from "saw" in v10. It is the word often used for prophetic vision (*chazah*). A covenant meal followed the covenant sacrifice. In the ancient world, eating together sealed peace and friendship. Here the leaders of a sinful people sit and eat in the presence of the holy God, and live. It is a foretaste of the Lord's Supper and of the great wedding feast at the end of history (Isa 25:6–8; Rev 19:9).
- **"Come up to me on the mountain and wait there, that I may give you the tablets of stone" (v12).** "Wait there" is literally "be there." **"With the law and the commandment, which I have written."** God himself writes the Ten Commandments on stone (31:18; 32:16). "Law" here is *torah*, which means instruction or teaching, more than just rules. **"For their instruction."** The law was meant to teach.
- **"Moses rose with his assistant Joshua" (v13).** Joshua had led the battle against Amalek (17:9–14) and is now Moses' aide. He goes partway up and seems to wait there, since he is with Moses when they come down (32:17). Moses' successor is being trained by being near him. Later Joshua stays at the tent of meeting when Moses leaves (33:11).
- **"Aaron and Hur are with you. Whoever has a dispute, let him go to them" (v14).** Hur had held up Moses' hands with Aaron during the battle (17:10–12). Jewish tradition says he was Miriam's husband, but the Bible does not say. Moses leaves them in charge. The arrangement fails badly: the golden calf is made under Aaron's watch (32:1–6). Hur is not mentioned again in Exodus.
- **"The cloud covered the mountain. The glory of the LORD dwelt on Mount Sinai" (vv15–16).** "Dwelt" is *shakan*, to settle or live somewhere. From this root comes *mishkan*, "tabernacle," God's dwelling place (25:9), and the later Jewish word *Shekinah* for God's glorious presence. John deliberately uses a Greek echo of it: "the Word became flesh and dwelt (literally 'tabernacled') among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14).
- **"The cloud covered it six days. And on the seventh day he called to Moses" (v16).** Moses waited six days before God spoke. The pattern of six and seven echoes creation (Gen 1:1–2:3), and the instructions for the tabernacle that follow (chs 25–31) are given in seven speeches, the seventh about the Sabbath (31:12–17). Many scholars see the tabernacle presented as a new creation, a small world where God lives with his people. Waiting on God is part of meeting him.
- **"The appearance of the glory of the LORD was like a devouring fire on the top of the mountain in the sight of the people" (v17).** "Glory" is *kavod*, literally "weight" or "heaviness," God's splendor and worth made visible. To the people below, it looked like a fire eating up the mountaintop. Moses later reminds them, "the LORD your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God" (Deut 4:24), and Hebrews repeats it for Christians (Heb 12:29).
- **"Moses entered the cloud and went up on the mountain" (v18).** Into the fire walked one man. **"Forty days and forty nights."** Moses was there without food or water (Deut 9:9). Forty is a number of testing and preparation in Scripture: forty days of rain in the flood (Gen 7:12), Elijah's forty days on the way to this same mountain (1 Kgs 19:8), and Jesus' forty days in the wilderness (Matt 4:2). Those forty days of Moses' absence are also how long Israel's patience lasted before they made the calf (32:1).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the altar as "representing God, and the pillars the tribes of Israel," and calls the split blood "the sign of solemn ratification—half on each party in the transaction." It takes the young men as "probably the oldest sons of particular families," in line with the Tyndale notes, and reads v10 as "a symbol or emblem of His glory" seen at a distance. On v11 JFB says the elders "were not inspired with terror in presence of the calm, benign, radiant symbol of the divine majesty," and that their feast on the peace offering "had a prophetic bearing." JFB thinks Moses was "miraculously supported" during the forty days. Henry notes that "nothing is described but what was under his feet," and says the elders "had some glimpse of his glory." He calls the cloud on the mountain "a token of God's special presence there." Guzik observes that the people's ready promise was overconfident, since they did not yet grasp how much the law demanded, and lays out a pattern for the new covenant from vv3–8: God's words, a sacrifice, a response of faith, and the blood applied. He passes on F. B. Meyer's comment that some eat without seeing God, some see God without eating, and the elders here did both. Guzik also notes that Aaron and Hur, left in charge, would soon fail.

#### Pointing to Christ
The old covenant was sealed with blood thrown on an altar and on a people, followed by a meal in God's presence. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus took a cup and spoke these same words, "my blood of the covenant," and gave it to his disciples to drink (Matt 26:28). What Moses did with animals, Christ did with himself, "not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12). The leaders at Sinai saw only the pavement under God's feet; in Jesus, "we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father" (John 1:14). They ate and drank before God, and lived; so do we at the Lord's Table, as a foretaste of the wedding supper of the Lamb. And like Moses, Jesus went up alone into the presence of God on our behalf, not for forty days but forever, "to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" (Heb 9:24).
