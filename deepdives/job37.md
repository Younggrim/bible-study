*Setting:* This is the end of Elihu's fourth and final speech, which began in chapter 36. The young man who stepped in after Job and his three friends fell silent (32:1–5) has argued that God speaks through suffering to rescue people, and he closed chapter 36 by describing rain, clouds and thunder. Chapter 37 carries the storm straight on. Thunder rolls, lightning flashes, and then come snow, ice and whirlwind. Elihu ends with a string of "Do you know...?" questions and a picture of God coming in golden splendor out of the north. Many readers think a real storm was building as he spoke. Whether or not that is so, the very next verse after this chapter is "Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" (38:1). Elihu has been saying God is beyond reach. God is about to prove him half right.

#### Elihu Proclaims God's Majesty (vv1–13)
- **"At this also my heart trembles and leaps out of its place" (v1).** "This" is the thunder and lightning of 36:29–33. Elihu is not describing a storm from a safe distance. He feels it in his chest. The word for "trembles" is used of fear and awe before God. Good theology about God's power should leave the speaker shaken, not smug.
- **"Keep listening to the thunder of his voice" (v2).** The Old Testament often hears God's voice in thunder: at Sinai (Exod 19:16–19), in the psalm where "the voice of the LORD" breaks cedars and shakes the wilderness (Ps 29:3–9), and when God answers from heaven (1 Sam 7:10; John 12:28–29, where a crowd heard the Father's voice and said "it had thundered"). "Rumbling" is a low, growling roar.
- **"Under the whole heaven he lets it go" (v3).** The lightning reaches "to the corners of the earth," literally "the wings" or "skirts" of the earth, a picture of the world's far edges as the hem of a garment. Nothing is out of the storm's reach, and nothing is out of God's.
- **"After it his voice roars" (v4).** Lightning first, then the thunderclap. Ancient people knew the order even without knowing why. "He does not restrain the lightnings when his voice is heard": once God speaks, nothing holds the storm back.
- **"He does great things that we cannot comprehend" (v5).** This is Elihu's thesis for the whole chapter. Job had said almost the same thing (9:10), and Eliphaz before him (5:9). Everyone in the book agrees God is greater than they can grasp. The question is what that means for a sufferer.
- **"To the snow he says, 'Fall on the earth'" (v6).** God speaks to snow and it obeys, the way he spoke light into being (Gen 1:3). The "downpour, his mighty downpour" is a rain that builds from showers into a deluge. Snow fell in winter on the hills of Edom and northern Arabia, the likely region of Uz.
- **"He seals up the hand of every man, that all men whom he made may know it" (v7).** Heavy snow and winter rain stop farm work and travel. People are shut indoors. Elihu sees a purpose: when human work stops, people have time to notice God's work. Psalm 147:16–18 makes the same move from snow and frost to God's word.
- **"Then the beasts go into their lairs" (v8).** Animals take shelter too, and some sleep through the cold. Creation, human and animal, bends to the season God sends. This is a small preview of chapters 38–39, where God himself will walk Job through the animal world.
- **"From its chamber comes the whirlwind" (v9).** Elihu pictures storehouses for the weather. Later God asks Job, "Have you entered the storehouses of the snow?" (38:22). The "whirlwind" here is *suphah*, a storm wind. It is not the same word as the "whirlwind" (*se'arah*) God speaks out of in 38:1, though the two are near synonyms and appear together in Nahum 1:3. "Scattering winds" are probably the north winds that sweep the sky clear and bring cold.
- **"By the breath of God ice is given" (v10).** The cold wind is God's breath. "The broad waters are frozen fast," literally "made narrow" or "straitened," as ice closes in from the edges of a pool or stream. God's breath gave Adam life (Gen 2:7). The same breath can freeze a lake.
- **"He loads the thick cloud with moisture" (v11).** The heavy cloud is God's cargo ship, filled to the brim. Then "the clouds scatter his lightning."
- **"They turn around and around by his guidance" (v12).** The word for "guidance" is the word for a pilot's steering (Prov 1:5; 11:14). Storms that look aimless are being steered. They "accomplish all that he commands them on the face of the habitable world."
- **"Whether for correction or for his land or for love, he causes it to happen" (v13).** This is the theological heart of the section. "Correction" is *shebet*, literally "rod," the same word for a father's or a king's rod of discipline (2 Sam 7:14; Prov 13:24). "Love" is *chesed*, God's loyal, covenant love. The same rain can be a rod (a flood, a ruined harvest), a gift to the land, or an act of kindness. Scripture shows storms working both ways: hail defeating Israel's enemies (Josh 10:11) and a cloudburst bringing judgment (Gen 7). The weather does not tell a person which it is. Elihu, almost without meaning to, has undermined the friends' assumption that every hardship can be read as punishment.
- **Commentators:** Henry turns v6 into a lovely line: "Saying and doing are not two things with God, though they are with us." On v7 he says that when weather stops work, people should spend the time in devotion rather than idle amusement, and he reads v13 as rain sent "either for the correction of his land or for a mercy to it." Guzik agrees that the pause in work is meant to make people notice God's work, and he draws out the picture of creation obeying God in the storm: what looks like chaos is fully under control, and Elihu implies Job should submit the way the clouds do. JFB says the storm may already have broken before God speaks in 38:1, and it pictures the clouds obeying God "as a ship obeys its helmsman."

#### Elihu Proclaims God's Majesty (vv14–20)
- **"Hear this, O Job; stop and consider the wondrous works of God" (v14).** Elihu names Job directly. "Stop" means "stand still," the posture of someone halted to look and think (compare Exod 14:13; 1 Sam 12:16). Job has been talking and arguing. Elihu asks him to stand silent and look.
- **"Do you know how God lays his command upon them?" (v15).** Now come the questions. They sound like a rehearsal for what God will say in chapter 38: "Do you know...? Have you...? Can you...?" Elihu's questions are fewer and gentler, but the method is the same. Job cannot explain how lightning comes out of a dark cloud.
- **"Do you know the balancings of the clouds?" (v16).** Tons of water float overhead without falling until the right time. How? Even with modern knowledge of air pressure and water vapor, a rain cloud is a wonder. "The wondrous works of him who is perfect in knowledge": here the phrase plainly means God. Elihu had used almost the same phrase in 36:4, where it is debated whether he meant God or himself.
- **"You whose garments are hot when the earth is still because of the south wind" (v17).** The south wind blew off the desert, hot and still, the kind of heavy air that makes clothes stick to the skin (Luke 12:55: "When you see the south wind blowing, you say, 'There will be scorching heat'"). The point is pointed: Job cannot even stop himself sweating. How can he explain the weather?
- **"Can you, like him, spread out the skies, hard as a cast metal mirror?" (v18).** Mirrors in the ancient world were polished bronze, not glass (Exod 38:8). On a cloudless, scorching day the sky looks like a sheet of hammered metal, bright and hard. The image is poetic, not a scientific claim that the sky is solid. "Spread out" (*raqa*) is related to *raqia*, the "expanse" or "firmament" of Genesis 1:6–8. Only God spread out the heavens (Isa 44:24).
- **"Teach us what we shall say to him; we cannot draw up our case because of darkness" (v19).** This is sarcasm with a point. Job had wanted to lay out his case before God like a lawsuit (13:18; 23:4). Elihu says: then teach the rest of us what to say, because we cannot see well enough to argue. "Darkness" is the darkness of human ignorance.
- **"Shall it be told him that I would speak? Did a man ever wish that he would be swallowed up?" (v20).** Elihu will not send God a request for a hearing. To demand to argue with God would be like asking to be swallowed whole. The Tyndale notes connect this with Job's own fear in 13:14, that contesting God could cost him his life. Job wanted an audience. Elihu says no sane person wants that. In a few verses Job will get one.
- **Commentators:** Henry urges the reader to "stand still, and consider," since truth heard without thought does little good. He adds that "we cannot order our speech by reason of darkness," and thinks Elihu felt ashamed of how poorly anyone, himself included, speaks of God. JFB says "perfect in knowledge" in v16 means God, not Elihu, explains the "looking glass" of v18 as a polished metal mirror, and reads v20 as an answer to Job's repeated wish to plead with God (13:3, 18–22). Guzik notices that God will ask Job very similar "Do you know" questions in chapter 38, and passes on Poole and Trapp: if Job cannot understand God's visible works, he cannot expect to understand God's hidden providence. But he also notes that Elihu, for all his true insights, still assumes Job's suffering proves his sin.

#### Elihu Proclaims God's Majesty (vv21–24)
- **"And now no one looks on the light when it is bright in the skies" (v21).** After a storm, the wind clears the clouds and the sun blazes out, too bright to look at. The picture works two ways. God's glory is often hidden behind clouds of circumstance. And when it does break through, human eyes cannot bear it.
- **"Out of the north comes golden splendor" (v22).** "North" is *tsaphon*. The Tyndale notes explain that in texts from ancient Ugarit, Zaphon was the northern mountain where the Canaanite storm god Baal was said to live. The Bible sometimes borrows the name to say that the true God's throne is the high place others imagined for their gods (Ps 48:2; Isa 14:13). "Golden splendor" is literally "gold." It may be the glow of the sky to the north after a storm, or a picture of royal brightness. "God is clothed with awesome majesty": the light is his robe (Ps 104:1–2).
- **"The Almighty—we cannot find him" (v23).** Zophar asked, "Can you find out the deep things of God?" (11:7), and Job himself said we hear only "a whisper" of him (26:14). Paul says the same: "How unsearchable are his judgments" (Rom 11:33), and God "dwells in unapproachable light" (1 Tim 6:16). But Elihu adds what Job needed most: "justice and abundant righteousness he will not violate." The word means God will not oppress or twist justice. Beyond reach does not mean unjust.
- **"Therefore men fear him; he does not regard any who are wise in their own conceit" (v24).** The fitting response to such a God is reverent fear, the beginning of wisdom (Prov 1:7; and Job 28:28, "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom"). Those "wise in heart" in their own eyes get nowhere with him. These are Elihu's last words. He is never mentioned again.
- **What happened to Elihu?** God's speeches begin in the next verse, and the epilogue rebukes the three friends (42:7) but says nothing about Elihu. Readers differ. Some see him as a forerunner who prepared the way for God, since much of what he says is true and God neither praises nor rebukes him. Others think his speeches are full of the same assumptions as the friends' and are simply overtaken: he says God cannot be found, and God immediately shows up. Many modern scholars think Elihu's speeches were added to the book later, because he is not introduced in the prologue or mentioned in the epilogue. Others argue his speeches were part of the design from the start, building a bridge to God's speeches. Either way, he has turned everyone's eyes to the sky at the moment God is about to speak from it.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up Elihu's whole argument in his outline: "That God is great and greatly to be feared." JFB reads v21 as a comfort as well as a warning: God's brightness is still there even when hidden, so the sufferer must wait patiently. It prefers "golden splendour" for v22 and takes the "wise of heart" in v24 as those wise "in their own conceits." Guzik passes on E. W. Bullinger, who reads v21 as the sky cleared after a storm, a picture of God shining when he chooses to reveal himself. Guzik's main point is the irony: Elihu insists God is out of reach and that Job should stop asking for a hearing, and God is about to answer Job directly out of the storm.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elihu ends by saying "we cannot find him." He is right that no one climbs up to God by reasoning or weather-watching. But the Bible's answer is that God comes down. On Sinai he spoke in thunder and the people begged not to hear it (Exod 20:18–19). In Job 38 he will speak out of the whirlwind. And in Jesus the God who commands snow and steers storms stood in a boat on Galilee, "rebuked the wind and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!'" (Mark 4:39). The disciples were more afraid after the calm than during the storm, because they realized who was with them. Elihu's "golden splendor" and "awesome majesty" are real, but John says "we have seen his glory" (John 1:14) in a face. The God who cannot be found has made himself found, and the rod and the loyal love of v13 meet at the cross, where correction fell on Christ so that *chesed* could reach us (Isa 53:5).
