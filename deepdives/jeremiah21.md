*Setting:* Jerusalem, 588 BC, in the last years of Zedekiah (597–586 BC), Judah's last king. The book of Jeremiah is not arranged by date, and this chapter jumps about seventeen years past chapter 20. Nebuchadnezzar has already taken Jerusalem once, in 597 BC, and carried King Jehoiachin, the nobles and the craftsmen to Babylon (2 Kgs 24:10–16). He then put Jehoiachin's uncle Mattaniah on the throne and renamed him Zedekiah. Zedekiah swore loyalty to Babylon, broke his oath and turned to Egypt for help. Now the Babylonian army is back, and the final siege has begun (2 Kgs 25:1). This chapter opens a new section of the book (chs 21–24) about the kings and prophets of Judah's final days. Its first word to the king is the hardest one Jeremiah ever delivered.

#### Jerusalem Will Fall to Nebuchadnezzar (vv1–10)
- **"King Zedekiah sent to him Pashhur the son of Malchiah" (v1).** This is a different Pashhur from the temple officer of 20:1, son of Immer. The chapter placement may be partly because of the shared name. This Pashhur was a royal official, and he later demands that Jeremiah be put to death and throws him into a muddy cistern (38:1–6).
- **"Zephaniah the priest, the son of Maaseiah" (v1).** He was "the second priest," next to the high priest (52:24). He seems more open to Jeremiah. The king sends him again in 37:3, and he reads aloud the letter that demanded Jeremiah be locked up, apparently without acting on it (29:25–29). He was executed by the Babylonians at Riblah after the city fell (52:24–27). He is not the prophet Zephaniah.
- **"Inquire of the LORD for us" (v2).** To inquire meant to ask a prophet to seek God's word on a question. Zedekiah respects Jeremiah enough to send senior men, but he never asks how to repent. He asks for an outcome. Throughout the book he wants Jeremiah's word but never has the courage to obey it (37:17; 38:14–26).
- **"Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon" (v2).** In Jeremiah's Hebrew the name is usually spelled *Nebuchadrezzar*, closer to the Babylonian *Nabû-kudurri-uṣur*, "Nabu, protect my heir." The ESV uses the familiar spelling throughout. He reigned 605–562 BC and is the most important foreign king in the Old Testament.
- **"Perhaps the LORD will deal with us according to all his wonderful deeds" (v2).** "Wonderful deeds" (*niphla'ot*) is the word for God's great saving acts, starting with the exodus (Exod 3:20; Ps 78:4). Zedekiah is almost certainly thinking of 701 BC, when the Assyrian king Sennacherib surrounded Jerusalem and the angel of the LORD struck down his army in a single night (Isa 37:36). That rescue came after King Hezekiah tore his clothes, went to the temple and prayed (Isa 37:1, 14–20). Zedekiah wants Hezekiah's miracle without Hezekiah's repentance.
- **"I will turn back the weapons of war that are in your hands" (v4).** The defenders are still fighting outside the walls. God says he will drive them back inside, and that their weapons will be useless.
- **"I will bring them together into the midst of this city" (v4).** "Them" is probably the Chaldeans. The enemy now outside the walls will stand inside them. "Chaldeans" (*Kasdim*) was the name of the people from southern Mesopotamia whose dynasty now ruled Babylon. In Jeremiah it is simply another name for the Babylonians.
- **"I myself will fight against you with outstretched hand and strong arm" (v5).** This is the shocking center of the chapter. The phrase is the language of the exodus. Moses spoke of the God who rescued Israel "with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm" (Deut 4:34; 5:15; 26:8). Here the words are reversed: "outstretched *hand* and strong *arm*." The same power that once fought *for* Israel against Pharaoh will now fight *against* Israel. Zedekiah asked for the God of the wonderful deeds, and he will get him, on the other side.
- **"In anger and in fury and in great wrath" (v5).** The same three words appear in the covenant warning of Deuteronomy 29:28. Moses had said this is exactly what would happen if Israel abandoned the covenant.
- **"Both man and beast. They shall die of a great pestilence" (v6).** Ancient sieges bred disease. The city was packed with refugees and animals, water ran low and the dead could not be buried outside the walls.
- **"Afterward… I will give Zedekiah king of Judah… into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar" (v7).** Those who survive the city's three killers, pestilence, sword and famine, will face a fourth: the conqueror himself. "Into the hand of" repeats three times. **"He shall not pity them or spare them or have compassion."** Three denials of mercy, the same three verbs God used of himself in 13:14.
- **How it came true.** In July 586 BC the walls were breached. Zedekiah fled by night toward the Jordan, was caught near Jericho and brought to Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah in Syria. His sons were killed in front of him, and then his eyes were put out and he was taken to Babylon in chains (2 Kgs 25:4–7; Jer 39:4–7; 52:7–11). The last thing he ever saw was what Jeremiah had warned him about.
- **"Behold, I set before you the way of life and the way of death" (v8).** This deliberately echoes Moses: "I have set before you today life and good, death and evil… Therefore choose life" (Deut 30:15, 19). In Moses' day the way of life was obedience in the land. Now, painfully, the way of life leads *out* of the city and into the hands of the enemy.
- **"He who goes out and surrenders to the Chaldeans… shall live" (v9).** "Surrenders" is literally "falls to," the ordinary word for deserting to the other side. To those inside the walls this sounded like treason. Jeremiah repeats it in 38:2, and the princes call it demoralizing the troops and demand his death (38:4). He was later arrested on suspicion of deserting himself (37:13–14). Many did go over to the Babylonians (39:9; 52:15), and their lives were spared.
- **"Shall have his life as a prize of war" (v9).** The word is *shalal*, plunder or loot. A soldier came home from battle with booty. The deserter would come away with only one thing, his life, and that would count as a win. The same phrase is used for Ebed-melech (39:18) and Baruch (45:5).
- **Was Jeremiah pro-Babylon?** Not at all. He also prophesied Babylon's own fall (25:12; chs 50–51). His point was that God was using Babylon to judge Judah, and that resisting the judgment was resisting God. Submitting meant admitting God's sentence was just. For those who submitted, the way led through exile to a future (29:10–14).
- **"I have set my face against this city for harm and not for good" (v10).** To "set one's face" is a fixed resolve. The same phrase in Leviticus 17:10 and 20:3–6 describes God turning against a sinner. **"He shall burn it with fire."** This happened a month after the walls fell (52:12–13).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Zedekiah's humility went as far as asking for help but not as far as taking advice, and that "those that put the evil day far from them will be the more terrified when it comes upon them." Guzik, drawing on Meyer, makes the same point: the king wanted relief from consequences, not a change of heart. He also cites Thompson that surrender was the first step toward renewal, and applies Jesus' words about losing one's life to save it (Matt 16:25). JFB says Zedekiah was "a prince having some reverence for sacred things," but weak. He reads v4 as the Jews being driven back from the field into the city and notes that Jeremiah, unlike earlier prophets facing Assyria, calls for unconditional surrender. JFB adds that Zedekiah himself "died of grief," a detail the text does not give. JFB skips vv3 and 6.

#### Message to the House of David (vv11–14)
- **"O house of David!" (v12).** God addresses not just Zedekiah but the whole royal line. David's family had received an everlasting covenant (2 Sam 7:12–16), but that promise never excused the king from God's standards. This short oracle opens a longer section addressed to the royal house that runs through 22:30.
- **"Execute justice in the morning" (v12).** Kings and elders heard cases in the morning at the city gate (2 Sam 15:2; Ps 101:8). The point is promptness. The poor could not afford for justice to be delayed. The king's first duty was to protect the weak, as the royal psalm prays: "May he defend the cause of the poor" (Ps 72:4).
- **"Deliver from the hand of the oppressor him who has been robbed" (v12).** The Hebrew word for "robbed" means someone stripped of property by force or fraud. In chapter 34 Zedekiah and the nobles freed their Hebrew slaves during this very siege and then forced them back into slavery as soon as the army briefly withdrew (34:8–11). This is the injustice this oracle condemns.
- **"Lest my wrath go forth like fire… with none to quench it" (v12).** These words repeat 4:4 almost exactly. Many think this oracle is older than the siege, from a time when a change was still possible, and was placed here to show that the royal house had been warned. Either way, the "lest" means there was a way out.
- **"O inhabitant of the valley, O rock of the plain" (v13).** In Hebrew the address is feminine, speaking to Jerusalem as a woman. Jerusalem sits on rocky ridges surrounded on three sides by valleys (Kidron, Tyropoeon and Hinnom). That made it hard to attack. "Rock of the plain" is unusual. It probably pictures the city rising like a rock above the surrounding terrain, though the exact phrase is debated. Some take it as Mount Zion itself.
- **"Who shall come down against us?" (v13).** This is the pride of a city that feels unbeatable, and its history seemed to prove it. The Jebusites had boasted the same thing before David took it (2 Sam 5:6). The rescue from Sennacherib had only strengthened the sense that God would never let Jerusalem fall (7:4, "the temple of the LORD").
- **"I will punish you according to the fruit of your deeds" (v14).** The judgment is not arbitrary. What they planted, they will harvest (17:10; Prov 1:31; Gal 6:7).
- **"I will kindle a fire in her forest" (v14).** Jerusalem had no real forest. This is probably the "forest" of cedar buildings: Solomon's great hall was called "the House of the Forest of Lebanon" because of its rows of cedar pillars (1 Kgs 7:2), and the royal houses were paneled in cedar (22:7, 14, 23). In the summer of 586 BC the Babylonian commander burned "the house of the LORD and the king's house and all the houses of Jerusalem" (52:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the people's sins grew out of their leaders' neglect, so the call to do justice is aimed first at the royal house. He stresses that the natural defenses of Jerusalem mean nothing when God is against it. JFB reads "in the morning" as "with promptness" and says the call to justice holds out a way to soften the judgment. He takes the "forest" as the dense mass of cedar houses. Guzik suggests, citing Thompson, that the forest may be the royal palace, the House of the Forest of Lebanon, and he also notes the view that the oracle in vv11–12 may come from earlier in the reign, when reform was still possible.

#### Pointing to Christ
Zedekiah wanted the God of the exodus to rescue him without repentance, and found the same strong arm turned against him. The chapter's hardest word is also its door of mercy: the only way to live was to stop resisting, walk out of the city, and give up everything except life itself. Jesus offers the same narrow choice in deeper form. "Whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matt 16:25). The difference is that the One we surrender to is not an enemy. He is the true Son of David, who did what Zedekiah never did, defended the poor and judged with righteousness (Isa 11:4). He also went out of the city himself, bearing the wrath that burned against it, "so that he might sanctify the people through his own blood" (Heb 13:12).
