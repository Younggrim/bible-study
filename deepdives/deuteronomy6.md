*Setting:* The plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Moses is preaching his second and longest sermon (chapters 5–26) to the generation that grew up in the wilderness, just before they cross into Canaan without him. Chapter 5 repeated the Ten Commandments and recalled how the people, terrified at Sinai, asked Moses to hear God for them and promised, "we will hear and do it" (5:27). God answered, "Oh that they had such a heart as this always" (5:29). Chapter 6 now turns to that heart. Before the detailed laws of chapters 12–26, Moses sets down the one command beneath all the others, and then shows how to keep it alive at home, in prosperity and from one generation to the next. This chapter holds the Shema, the most famous words in the Old Testament, which Jesus called the greatest commandment.

#### The Greatest Commandment (vv1–25)
- **"Now this is the commandment" (v1).** "Commandment" is singular. Moses sums up "the statutes and the rules" as one commandment, one whole way of life. He is about to name its center in v5.
- **"That you may fear the LORD your God, you and your son and your son's son" (v2).** Three generations in one sentence. From the start Moses thinks about grandchildren, and the chapter will end with a child's question (v20). To "fear" the LORD in Deuteronomy means reverence that shows up in obedience, not cringing terror. The same people are told to fear him and to love him (10:12).
- **"That your days may be long… that it may go well with you… that you may multiply greatly" (vv2–3).** The commands are not arbitrary hoops. They are the way of life in the land, for Israel's good (v24). **"A land flowing with milk and honey" (v3)** describes rich pasture for goats and sheep (milk) and abundant honey, which could mean wild bees' honey or a syrup boiled down from dates or grapes. After forty years of manna, it sounded like a feast.
- **"Hear, O Israel" (v4).** The first Hebrew word is *shema*, "hear," and the whole confession is named after it: the Shema. In Hebrew, to "hear" includes heeding and obeying. Devout Jews have recited vv4–9, with 11:13–21 and Numbers 15:37–41, every morning and evening for well over two thousand years. When a scribe asked Jesus which commandment is first, he began with these exact words (Mark 12:29–30).
- **"The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (v4).** The Hebrew is just four words: *YHWH eloheinu YHWH echad*, literally "LORD our God LORD one." There is no verb, so it can be read in more than one way, and the ESV footnote gives the options. "The LORD our God, the LORD is one" stresses that God is a single, undivided being, not a family of gods or a god with many local versions, as Baal was worshipped as many local Baals. "The LORD our God is one LORD" says much the same. "The LORD is our God, the LORD alone" stresses that Israel worships him only, which fits the first commandment just repeated (5:7). Both ideas are true and the second may be closer to Moses' point here, but the first lies underneath it: only one God exists to be served.
- **The oneness of God and the Trinity.** The Shema is no problem for Christian faith, and the New Testament repeats it (Mark 12:29; 1 Cor 8:4–6; Jas 2:19). Paul even folds Jesus into it: "one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 8:6). The word *echad* simply means "one." It can describe one day made of evening and morning (Gen 1:5), or two who become "one flesh" (Gen 2:24), so it allows for a unity with parts. But the word alone doesn't prove the Trinity. That teaching rests on the whole Bible's witness to Father, Son and Spirit, not on this one adjective.
- **"You shall love the LORD your God" (v5).** This is remarkable: love is *commanded*. In ancient treaties, a great king called his vassals to "love" him, meaning loyalty that showed up in action. Deuteronomy uses that covenant language but fills it with warmth, because God first "loved your fathers" (4:37; 7:8). Love here is both faithful allegiance and real affection.
- **"With all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might" (v5).** In Hebrew thought the **heart** (*levav*) is the inner person: mind, will and desires together, not only feelings. The **soul** (*nephesh*) is the whole living self, one's very life. **Might** (*me'od*) is a striking word that usually means "very." Here it is a noun: "muchness," everything one has and can do, all one's strength and resources. Jesus' versions add "mind" to make the sense plain in Greek (Mark 12:30). The point is not three compartments but total love, nothing held back.
- **"These words… shall be on your heart" (v6).** Before they are taught to children, they must live in the parent. The Lord's words are to sit on the heart, close and always present. Later the prophets promise that God himself will write his law on hearts (Jer 31:33).
- **"You shall teach them diligently to your children" (v7).** The verb *shanan* normally means "sharpen," as with a sword or arrow (32:41; Ps 45:5). Here, in an intensive form, it means to teach incisively, pressing the words in until they cut a clear edge. Teaching faith at home is not left to priests or to occasional festivals.
- **"When you sit… walk… lie down… rise" (v7).** Two pairs that cover everything: at home and on the road, at night and in the morning. Faith is woven into ordinary conversation, not saved for formal lessons.
- **"Bind them as a sign on your hand… frontlets between your eyes… write them on the doorposts" (vv8–9).** Hand, head and house: what one does, what one thinks and sees, and where one lives. Very likely Moses meant this as a vivid figure, as in Exodus 13:9, 16 and Proverbs 3:3 ("bind them around your neck"). Later Judaism also carried it out literally. *Tefillin* (phylacteries) are small leather boxes holding these Scripture passages, strapped to the arm and forehead during prayer. A *mezuzah* is a small case with a parchment of vv4–9 fixed to the doorframe. Examples of tefillin were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Jesus criticized not the practice but making the boxes large for show (Matt 23:5).
- **"Great and good cities that you did not build… cisterns that you did not dig" (vv10–11).** Four gifts in a row, each with "you did not." Cisterns were bottle-shaped pits cut into rock and plastered to hold rainwater, back-breaking work in a land without many rivers. Israel would inherit it all. Every one of these gifts was grace.
- **"And when you eat and are full, then take care lest you forget the LORD" (vv11–12).** This is the chapter's sharpest warning, and chapter 8 develops it at length. In the wilderness Israel's danger was grumbling in hunger. In Canaan the danger would be forgetting in plenty. To "forget" in Deuteronomy is not a lapse of memory. It means living as if God were not the source of what one has. Israel's later history shows exactly this pattern (Judg 2:10–13; Hos 13:6).
- **"It is the LORD your God you shall fear. Him you shall serve and by his name you shall swear" (v13).** Swearing by his name means making solemn oaths before him alone, acknowledging him as the God who sees and judges. When Satan offered Jesus all the kingdoms of the world in return for worship, Jesus answered with this verse: "You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve" (Matt 4:10). His Greek quotation follows the Greek Old Testament, which says "worship."
- **"You shall not go after other gods… for the LORD your God in your midst is a jealous God" (vv14–15).** The gods of the Canaanites were connected with rain, crops and fertility, so the pull to "go after" them would be strongest just when Israel began farming the land. God's "jealousy" is not petty envy but the rightful zeal of a husband for a wife who belongs to him (5:9). He is "in your midst." The God who dwells among his people will not share them.
- **"You shall not put the LORD your God to the test, as you tested him at Massah" (v16).** At Rephidim the people quarrelled over water and asked, "Is the LORD among us or not?" (Exod 17:1–7). Moses named the place Massah, "testing." To test God is to demand that he prove himself on one's own terms, as if his past faithfulness counted for nothing. Jesus quoted this verse when Satan urged him to jump from the temple and force the Father to rescue him (Matt 4:7).
- **"Do what is right and good in the sight of the LORD" (v18).** The standard is God's sight, not public opinion. Later Judges describes the opposite: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 21:25).
- **"When your son asks you in time to come" (v20).** Moses expects children to ask, and to ask about meaning: "What is the meaning of the testimonies and the statutes and the rules?" Israel's festivals were designed to prompt such questions (Exod 12:26–27; 13:14; Josh 4:6). The Passover meal still includes children asking questions.
- **"We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt" (v21).** The answer to "why these laws?" is not a rule but a story. The laws make sense only in light of the rescue. Note the "we": the parent answers as one who was there, because each generation shares the Exodus as its own story (5:3).
- **"He brought us out from there, that he might bring us in" (v23).** This is the whole movement of the book in one line. God did not rescue Israel from slavery to leave them in the desert. Freedom from Egypt was for the sake of life with him in the land he promised.
- **"For our good always, that he might preserve us alive" (v24).** God's commands are not a burden imposed by a reluctant ruler. They are the instructions of a Father for the flourishing of his children (10:13).
- **"And it will be righteousness for us, if we are careful to do all this commandment" (v25).** This can sound like earning God's favor, but notice the order. The rescue came first (vv21–23), and obedience follows as the right response within the covenant God already made. "Righteousness" here means being in the right before God, living in line with the covenant relationship. Paul quotes a similar line from Leviticus 18:5 to show that law-keeping as a way to be accepted only exposes our failure (Rom 10:5; Gal 3:12). Abraham was counted righteous by faith before any law was given (Gen 15:6). Read in its setting, v25 describes the faithful life of people God had already redeemed. The Tyndale notes read it the same way: obedience shows a righteousness already received by faith.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry calls vv4–5 a brief summary of all religion and says the love required is sincere, strong, supreme and undivided. "Happy they that have this one Lord for their God." On v6 he writes simply, "He that loves God loves his Bible." Of the houses and wells Israel did not build, he warns that easy gifts are the most easily forgotten, "for we are most sensible of the value of that which has cost us dear." On Massah: "No change of condition will cure a disposition of murmur and fret." JFB thinks Moses spoke of hand and forehead "merely in a figurative way, to signify assiduous, earnest, and frequent instruction," though the Jews later took it literally, and it suggests Moses meant God's words to replace the amulets and door inscriptions of Egypt. JFB adds no comment on vv10–19. Guzik argues that *echad* means a compound unity, as against a different word for an absolute single one, and so fits the Trinity. The BDB lexicon gives *echad* as simply "one," used of single things and united groups alike. It allows for that reading but doesn't prove it. Guzik's stronger points are practical: parents, not pastors, carry the main duty of teaching children, and prosperity is a more dangerous test than hardship because it feels safe.

#### Pointing to Christ
When Jesus was asked for the greatest commandment, he quoted v5, and when Satan tempted him in the wilderness, he answered three times from Deuteronomy, twice from this chapter (vv13, 16; Matt 4:1–11). Where Israel tested God in the wilderness and forgot him in plenty, Jesus, the true Son, loved the Father with all his heart, soul and strength, in hunger and when offered the whole world. No one else has kept the Shema perfectly. But those who belong to him are given a new heart and his Spirit, so that the words written on doorposts can be written on hearts (Jer 31:33; Rom 8:3–4). The love the Shema commands becomes possible because "we love because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19).
