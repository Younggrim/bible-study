*Setting:* Jerusalem, about 715 BC (the dates of Hezekiah's reign are debated; some scholars place its start around 729 BC as co-regent with his father). Chronicles was written after the exile, probably in the 400s BC, for Jews rebuilding life around a small second temple. Its writer, often called the Chronicler, retells the history of David's line with a special eye on the temple and its worship. Hezekiah's father Ahaz was one of Judah's worst kings. He sacrificed his sons in the fire, worshiped the gods of Damascus, cut up the temple vessels, "shut up the doors of the house of the LORD," and built altars on every corner of Jerusalem (28:3, 23–25). Judah was beaten by Syria, Israel, Edom and the Philistines, and Assyria squeezed it for tribute (28:5–21). Up north, the kingdom of Israel was falling or had just fallen to Assyria (722 BC). 2 Kings 18–20 tells Hezekiah's story mostly in terms of politics and the Assyrian invasion. Chronicles gives three chapters (29–31) to his religious reform before it reaches Sennacherib.

#### Hezekiah Reigns in Judah (vv1–2)
- **"Twenty-five years old… twenty-nine years" (v1).** His name, *Hizqiyyahu*, means "the LORD strengthens" or "the LORD is my strength."
- **"His mother's name was Abijah the daughter of Zechariah" (v1).** The ESV footnote says Kings spells it *Abi* (2 Kings 18:2). JFB notes that Chronicles gives the full form. Some, Guzik among them, suggest her father was the Zechariah whom Isaiah chose as a "reliable witness" (Isa 8:2), or the Zechariah who counseled Uzziah (26:5). That is possible but cannot be proved. It would mean that while Ahaz ruled badly, a godly grandfather and mother may have been shaping the boy.
- **"According to all that David his father had done" (v2).** Only a few kings get this full comparison with David: Asa (1 Kings 15:11), Jehoshaphat in part, Hezekiah and Josiah (34:2). Kings adds that "there was none like him among all the kings of Judah" in trusting the LORD (2 Kings 18:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "the good and glorious reign of Hezekiah," a king "very zealous, devout, good… none like him." Guzik adds that Hezekiah had watched the northern kingdom collapse for rejecting God and learned from it.

#### Hezekiah Cleanses the Temple (vv3–19)
- **"In the first year of his reign, in the first month, he opened the doors" (v3).** He doesn't wait. JFB explains that the "first month" is Nisan, the start of the religious year, "the season appointed for the celebration of the passover." His father had locked the doors (28:24). The son unlocks them on day one. The Tyndale notes point out a wordplay: "repaired them" is literally "made them strong" (*chazaq*), the same root as Hezekiah's name. The king named "the LORD strengthens" strengthens the LORD's doors.
- **"Assembled them in the square on the east" (v4).** An open square, probably in front of the temple's east gate, since the temple faced east.
- **"Consecrate yourselves, and consecrate the house… carry out the filth from the Holy Place" (v5).** Two steps, as the Tyndale notes say: first the workers are made holy, then the building. "Filth" is *niddah*, footnoted in the ESV as "impurity." The lexicon shows it is the law's word for ritual uncleanness, often of a woman's monthly bleeding (Lev 15:19), and it is used here for the defilement of idolatry. The temple had become ritually unclean through and through.
- **"Turned away their faces… and turned their backs" (v6).** In worship, Israel faced the temple. Their fathers literally turned their backs on it. Ezekiel later sees men in the temple court "with their backs to the temple… worshiping the sun" (Ezek 8:16).
- **"Shut the doors… put out the lamps… not burned incense or offered burnt offerings" (v7).** Each item was a daily duty under the law. The lampstand was to burn "regularly" (Exod 27:20–21), incense was offered morning and evening (Exod 30:7–8), and the burnt offering every morning and evening (Exod 29:38–42). Under Ahaz all of it stopped. The house of God sat dark and silent.
- **"An object of horror, of astonishment, and of hissing" (v8).** This phrasing is the language of the prophets, especially Jeremiah (Jer 25:9, 18; 29:18). The Tyndale notes observe that the whole speech uses words familiar to the Chronicler's post-exile readers. They knew what being a hissing among the nations felt like.
- **"Our sons and our daughters and our wives are in captivity for this" (v9).** Under Ahaz, Israel and Syria carried off huge numbers of Judeans (28:5–8). Some were sent back after the prophet Oded rebuked the northern army (28:9–15), but not all.
- **"It is in my heart to make a covenant with the LORD" (v10).** Hezekiah doesn't blame God or circumstances. He names the sin, then commits to change. JFB takes his acting at the very start of his reign as proof of real piety.
- **"My sons, do not now be negligent" (v11).** "My sons" is a fatherly word from a twenty-five-year-old king to men who were mostly older than he was. "Negligent" means slack or careless. "The LORD has chosen you to stand in his presence" recalls Deuteronomy 10:8. Their calling had not been cancelled by years of disuse.
- **The Levites named (vv12–14).** Fourteen men are listed: two each from the three clans of Levi (Kohath, Merari, Gershon, see Exod 6:16), two from Elizaphan, a leading Kohathite family (Num 3:30), and two each from the families of the three great musicians David appointed, Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun (1 Chr 25:1). The list shows that every branch of the tribe took part.
- **"As the king had commanded, by the words of the LORD" (v15).** The king's order was grounded in God's law, not only royal will.
- **"The priests went into the inner part… the Levites took it and carried it out to the brook Kidron" (v16).** Only priests could enter the sanctuary itself (Num 18:1–7). They carried the rubbish as far as the court, and the Levites took it from there. The Kidron Valley, east of the city, became the dump for idolatrous trash under Asa (15:16), here, and again under Josiah (2 Kings 23:4, 6).
- **Sixteen days (v17).** Eight days for the courts up to the vestibule, eight days for the house itself, finishing on the sixteenth of the first month. That was too late for Passover, which falls on the fourteenth (Exod 12:6). Chapter 30 tells how Hezekiah held it a month late. Guzik reads the time needed as a measure of how badly the temple had been defiled.
- **"All the utensils that King Ahaz discarded… we have made ready and consecrated" (v19).** Ahaz had cut some vessels in pieces (28:24) and pushed others aside for his Syrian-style altar (2 Kings 16:10–18). What could be saved is restored. "Faithless" (*maʿal*) is a key word in Chronicles for breaking faith with God (28:19, 22). It is the opposite of what Hezekiah is doing.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws three lessons from vv12–19. "When God has work to do he will raise up leading men to preside in it." The Levites carried out the rubbish, showing that no one's usefulness should make him "forget their place." And: "Let what is amiss be amended quickly." On v19 he writes that though holy vessels "may be profaned for a while, God will find a time and a way to sanctify them." JFB argues that the impurity was neglect and dirt more than idols, since the doors had been shut, though Ahaz's other altars stood elsewhere (28:24–25). Guzik notes that these Levites had shared in the neglect, and yet the Chronicler names them because they answered when called.

#### Hezekiah Restores Temple Worship (vv20–36)
- **"Hezekiah the king rose early" (v20).** The phrase is used for eager obedience (Gen 22:3). The temple is clean, and the king goes up at once with the city's leaders.
- **"Seven bulls, seven rams, seven lambs, and seven male goats" (v21).** Seven was the number of completeness, and JFB notes the animals were "offered by sevens." The bulls, rams and lambs were burnt offerings, wholly given to God. The goats were the sin offering, for cleansing guilt (Lev 1; 4). The offering was made "for the kingdom and for the sanctuary and for Judah," covering the royal house, the temple and the people.
- **"Their blood was thrown against the altar" (v22).** Repeated three times. The blood of the sacrifice was the means of atonement: "the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood that makes atonement" (Lev 17:11).
- **"They laid their hands on them" (v23).** The king and the assembly put their hands on the goats' heads. Laying on hands identified the worshiper with the animal and pictured guilt being transferred to it (Lev 4:15; 16:21).
- **"To make atonement for all Israel" (v24).** Hezekiah widens it on purpose: not Judah alone, but "all Israel," which included the northern tribes, now crushed by Assyria. "All Israel" is one of the Chronicler's favorite phrases. For him the people of God was always twelve tribes, never just one kingdom. Hezekiah acts on that belief in chapter 30 when he invites the north to Passover.
- **"According to the commandment of David and of Gad the king's seer and of Nathan the prophet" (v25).** David organized the temple music (1 Chr 15:16; 25:1–7). Here the Chronicler adds that his arrangement came with prophetic authority: "the commandment was from the LORD through his prophets." Temple song was not just a king's taste in music. God commanded it.
- **"When the burnt offering began, the song to the LORD began also" (v27).** The music matched the sacrifice exactly, beginning with it and continuing "until the burnt offering was finished" (v28). Guzik cites a scholar who notes that "the song to the LORD" may be a title for a set body of songs, perhaps psalms. While smoke rose, song rose.
- **"Bowed themselves and worshiped" (vv29–30).** The posture comes three times. "The words of David and of Asaph the seer" probably refers to psalms already in use. Twelve psalms carry Asaph's name (Pss 50; 73–83). Calling Asaph a "seer" treats his songs as inspired.
- **"You have now consecrated yourselves to the LORD" (v31).** The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew: "filled your hand for" the LORD. That idiom is the standard phrase for ordaining priests (Exod 28:41; 29:9). After the atonement, the whole assembly is pictured as newly set apart for God and invited to bring their own gifts.
- **"Thank offerings… all who were of a willing heart brought burnt offerings" (v31).** Thank offerings were peace offerings, mostly eaten by the worshipers in a shared meal before God (Lev 7:11–15). Burnt offerings were completely burned. These were freewill gifts, not required.
- **The numbers (vv32–33).** 70 bulls, 100 rams and 200 lambs as burnt offerings, and 600 bulls and 3,000 sheep as other offerings. That was a very large response from a people who had followed Ahaz until weeks before.
- **"The priests were too few… the Levites were more upright in heart than the priests in consecrating themselves" (v34).** Normally the worshiper skinned his own burnt offering (Lev 1:6), but at public offerings the priests did it, and there were not enough of them ready. The Levites stepped in. The Chronicler's comment is pointed: the Levites were more eager to get ready than the priests were. The priests' slowness shows up again in 30:3. Henry says the priests "did not mind their business." Guzik draws the lesson that humble readiness often outruns religious rank.
- **"Thus the service of the house of the LORD was restored" (v35).** Literally "was established." The daily worship that stopped under Ahaz runs again.
- **"Because God had provided for the people, for the thing came about suddenly" (v36).** The joy is not credited to Hezekiah's planning. God prepared the people. The Tyndale notes describe the Chronicler as surprised and delighted by how quickly hearts responded. In about two weeks a nation went from locked doors to crowded courts.
- **Commentators:** Henry ties the offering "for all Israel" to Christ, who "is a propitiation, not for the sins of Israel only" (1 John 2:2), and on the music he writes, "Even sorrow for sin must not put us out of tune for praising God." On v36 he says the reform was done quickly and without opposition, and that God's people will find it "a pleasing surprise… to see how soon it is done." Guzik notes that Hezekiah left the sacrifices to the priests, unlike his great-grandfather Uzziah, who was struck with leprosy for burning incense himself (26:16–21). He also sees in the sin-offering goats a picture of Christ bearing sin. JFB stresses the Levites' greater readiness, "more upright in heart to sanctify themselves than the priests."

#### Pointing to Christ
The temple could not be used until its impurity was carried out and blood was put on the altar. The order matters: cleansing first, then atonement, then song and freewill gifts. Zechariah uses the same word, *niddah*, when he promises that one day "a fountain" will be opened for the house of David "to cleanse them from sin and uncleanness" (Zech 13:1). That fountain opened at the cross. Jesus did not need goats laid under his hands. He bore sin himself, "the propitiation… not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world" (1 John 2:2), wider even than Hezekiah's "all Israel." He also cleansed the temple of his day (John 2:13–17) and then made his people into God's temple (1 Cor 3:16). And as in Hezekiah's day, when that work is done, worship follows, and it can come about suddenly.
