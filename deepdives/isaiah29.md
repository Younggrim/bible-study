*Setting:* This chapter holds the second and third of the six "woes" of chapters 28–33 (29:1, 15). It is set in the years before Sennacherib's invasion of 701 BC, when Jerusalem kept up its feasts and sacrifices while its leaders quietly negotiated with Egypt for protection against Assyria (30:1–2). Isaiah calls Jerusalem by a strange new name, "Ariel," and predicts two things that seemed impossible together: God himself will besiege the city and bring it to the dust, and then, suddenly, the enemy armies will vanish like a dream. Both came true in 701 BC (chs 36–37). The chapter then exposes why the people could not understand God's word: their worship was lip-deep, their wisdom was secret scheming, and they had forgotten that the clay does not instruct the potter. It ends with a burst of hope, the deaf hearing and the blind seeing, words Jesus later pointed to as signs of his own ministry.

#### The Siege of Jerusalem (vv1–24)
- **"Ah, Ariel, Ariel, the city where David encamped!" (v1).** "Ah" is again the funeral cry *hoy*, "woe." "Ariel" is a riddle name, like "the valley of vision" (22:1). It can mean "lion of God," and Guzik and JFB both mention this. But in Ezekiel 43:15–16 the same word (*ari'el*) is the "altar hearth," the top of the great altar where sacrifices burned. BDB gives "hearth, altar-hearth." The Tyndale notes take it that way: Jerusalem is "altar of God," the city defined by its temple. "Where David encamped" recalls David capturing Jerusalem and making it his capital (2 Sam 5:6–9). The irony is that the city David encamped in, God will now encamp against (v3).
- **"Add year to year; let the feasts run their round" (v1).** Go on with your calendar of festivals, year after year. This is sarcasm. Their worship continues on schedule while their hearts are elsewhere (v13; 1:11–14). JFB reads it as formal, heartless sacrifice repeated year after year.
- **"Yet I will distress Ariel… and she shall be to me like an Ariel" (v2).** This is the pun. The city called "altar hearth" will become a real altar hearth, a place soaked with blood and burning, where the sacrifice is the city itself.
- **"I will encamp against you all around, and will besiege you with towers" (v3).** God speaks in the first person. The Assyrians will build the siege towers, but the siege is his. This is a shocking reversal: the God who defended David's city now surrounds it.
- **"Your voice shall come from the ground like the voice of a ghost" (v4).** "Ghost" is *'ob*, the word for a medium's spirit, the dead person a necromancer claimed to call up (8:19; 1 Sam 28:7–8). Mediums were known for whispering and chirping voices that seemed to rise from the ground. Jerusalem, once loud with feasting, will be so humbled that its voice is a faint whisper from the dust, like the dead.
- **"But the multitude of your foreign foes shall be like small dust" (v5).** Now the turn comes, as suddenly as the city fell. The enemies who brought Jerusalem to the dust become dust themselves, "like passing chaff." **"In an instant, suddenly."** In 701 BC, "the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians," and Sennacherib went home (37:36–37).
- **"You will be visited by the LORD of hosts with thunder and with earthquake" (v6).** "Visited" (*paqad*) here means rescued, as the Tyndale notes explain. Thunder, earthquake, whirlwind and fire are the signs of God's appearing, like Sinai (Exod 19:16–18; Ps 18:7–15).
- **"Shall be like a dream, a vision of the night" (vv7–8).** The great army that surrounded Jerusalem will disappear like a dream when you wake up. Isaiah extends the picture: a hungry man dreams he is eating and wakes up still hungry. The nations dream of devouring Zion, and they wake up empty. JFB applies it directly to Sennacherib's army. Henry's outline puts it simply: Jerusalem greatly distressed, but its enemies "baffled and defeated."
- **"Astonish yourselves and be astonished; blind yourselves and be blind!" (v9).** The subject shifts from the city's enemies to its own people. Isaiah uses a Hebrew play on words: "be stunned and stun yourselves; blind yourselves and be blind." If they insist on not seeing, then let them be blind. **"Be drunk, but not with wine"** recalls the drunken priests of 28:7. Now their stupor is spiritual.
- **"The LORD has poured out upon you a spirit of deep sleep" (v10).** "Deep sleep" is *tardemah*, the heavy sleep God put on Adam (Gen 2:21) and Abram (Gen 15:12). Here it is a judgment: those who would not see are allowed to stop seeing, as God said at Isaiah's call (6:9–10). **"Has closed your eyes (the prophets), and covered your heads (the seers)."** The leaders who should have been the city's eyes are blindfolded. Paul quotes this verse, combined with Deuteronomy 29:4, to describe Israel's unbelief in his own day: "God gave them a spirit of stupor" (Rom 11:8).
- **"Like the words of a book that is sealed" (vv11–12).** A "book" was a scroll. Important documents were rolled up and sealed with clay (Jer 32:10–11). The educated man says, "I can't read it, it's sealed." The uneducated man says, "I can't read." Either way, no one reads it. The excuses differ, but the result is the same. Henry: "He sees it is a book, and that is all; he knows nothing of what is in it." Revelation pictures a sealed scroll that only the Lamb is worthy to open (Rev 5:1–9). JFB sees the sealed prophecy opened at last by Christ.
- **"This people draw near with their mouth and honor me with their lips, while their hearts are far from me" (v13).** This is the root problem. Their worship is real enough as performance, but empty at the center. **"Their fear of me is a commandment taught by men."** Their reverence is a set of human customs learned by rote, not a living relationship. Henry: "it is only from the teeth outward." Jesus quotes this verse against the Pharisees, who used their traditions to get around God's commandments (Matt 15:7–9; Mark 7:6–7). He follows the Greek Old Testament, "in vain do they worship me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men," which sharpens the point.
- **"I will again do wonderful things with this people, with wonder upon wonder" (v14).** "Wonderful" is *pele'* again (9:6; 25:1; 28:29). God's answer to hollow religion is to act in ways that leave the experts helpless. **"The wisdom of their wise men shall perish."** Judah's royal advisers, so proud of their Egyptian strategy (v15), will be proven fools. Paul quotes this verse to show that the message of the cross overturns the world's wisdom (1 Cor 1:19), also from the Greek: "I will destroy the wisdom of the wise."
- **"Ah, you who hide deep from the LORD your counsel" (v15).** The third woe. Judah's advisers were plotting the alliance with Egypt in secret, probably hoping to keep it from Isaiah and from Assyria's spies. **"Who sees us? Who knows us?"** They act as though God cannot see in the dark (Ps 94:7–9; Ezek 8:12).
- **"You turn things upside down! Shall the potter be regarded as the clay?" (v16).** This is Isaiah's verdict on their whole approach. They treat God as if he were the one to be managed. JFB: they "invert (turn upside down) the order of things." **"The thing made should say of its maker, 'He did not make me'… 'He has no understanding.'"** The clay denies the potter made it, or says he doesn't know what he's doing. Isaiah returns to the image twice more (45:9; 64:8), and Paul uses it in Romans 9:20–21. Guzik applies it to those who credit the universe to chance rather than a Maker. Henry: God "will turn and manage you, and all your counsels, with as much ease… as the potter forms and fashions his clay."
- **"Lebanon shall be turned into a fruitful field, and the fruitful field shall be regarded as a forest" (v17).** "Fruitful field" is literally *karmel*, "garden land," as JFB notes, the name of the lush Mount Carmel. Lebanon's wild forest becomes farmland, and farmland grows into forest. It is a picture of a total turnaround, and of creation renewed (32:15; 35:1–2). And it comes in "a very little while."
- **"In that day the deaf shall hear the words of a book, and… the eyes of the blind shall see" (v18).** This reverses vv9–12. The sealed book becomes readable, and the blind see. Isaiah repeats the promise in 35:5. When John the Baptist asked from prison whether Jesus was the one, Jesus answered: "the blind receive their sight… and the deaf hear" (Matt 11:4–5).
- **"The meek shall obtain fresh joy in the LORD, and the poor among mankind shall exult in the Holy One of Israel" (v19).** The humble and poor, not the clever advisers, are the ones who rejoice. "The Holy One of Israel" is Isaiah's favorite name for God (1:4).
- **"The ruthless shall come to nothing and the scoffer cease" (v20).** The scoffers of 28:14 are gone. **"Who by a word make a man out to be an offender… and with an empty plea turn aside him who is in the right" (v21).** These are people who twist the courts with false accusations, set traps for anyone who rebukes them "in the gate" (where cases were heard), and use legal nonsense to deny justice to the innocent (Amos 5:10–12). In God's renewed world, that is over.
- **"The LORD, who redeemed Abraham" (v22).** This is the only place in the Old Testament where God is said to have "redeemed" Abraham. Most think of God calling Abraham out of Ur and its idols (Josh 24:2–3), as JFB does. Later Jewish tradition told a story of Abraham rescued from a fiery furnace for refusing to worship idols, but that is legend, not Scripture. The point is that the God who began Israel's story by rescuing Abraham will finish it.
- **"Jacob shall no more be ashamed, no more shall his face grow pale" (v22).** Jacob, the father of the twelve tribes, is pictured looking at his descendants. For centuries he would have blushed or gone pale with shame at what they had become. Now **"when he sees his children, the work of my hands, in his midst, they will sanctify my name" (v23)**. They are God's handiwork, a new creation, and they honor him rightly. "Sanctify my name" is what Jesus taught his disciples to pray: "hallowed be your name" (Matt 6:9).
- **"Those who go astray in spirit will come to understanding, and those who murmur will accept instruction" (v24).** The chapter ends with grumblers becoming teachable. The spiritual blindness of vv9–12 is healed.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as Jerusalem's distress and deliverance, then a rebuke of three kinds of sinners: the dull who ignore warnings, the hypocrites whose worship is formal, and "the politicians" who scheme without God, then promises for the remnant. He applies the siege also to the later fall of Jerusalem to Babylon and to Rome. Guzik thinks the city may have proudly called itself "Ariel, lion of God," and that God uses the name with sarcasm. JFB gives both "Lion of God" and "Hearth of God" and favors reading the dream of vv7–8 as Sennacherib's disappointed army. All three see in v13 the very hypocrisy Jesus confronted.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter describes people who keep the feasts but cannot read the book, honor God with their lips but keep their hearts far off, and plan in secret as if the potter were the clay. Jesus quoted v13 to the religious leaders of his day, and Paul quoted v14 to show how the cross makes the world's wisdom foolish. But the chapter's hope is Christ's work too. The deaf hear, the blind see, the poor rejoice in the Holy One of Israel, and the grumblers learn. Jesus opens the sealed book (Rev 5:9), and he opened blind eyes along the roads of Galilee as a sign that the "very little while" of v17 had begun.
