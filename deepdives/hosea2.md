*Setting:* Hosea is preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel in the mid-700s BC, in the last prosperous years of Jeroboam II. In chapter 1 God told him to marry Gomer, a woman who would be unfaithful, and to give their three children names that were judgments: Jezreel ("God scatters"), No Mercy and Not My People. Then, abruptly, God promised to reverse every name. Chapter 2 is where Hosea's family story turns into God's own voice. It is written like a court case. A wronged husband speaks to the children about their mother, lists her unfaithfulness and announces what he will do. But the case ends not in divorce or execution but in a second courtship and a new wedding. Behind it all is Baal, the Canaanite god of storm and fertility, whom Israel credited with rain, crops, wool and wine. The chapter's question is simple: who really gives Israel her bread?

#### Israel's Unfaithfulness Punished (vv1–13)
- **"Say to your brothers, 'You are my people'" (v1).** In Hebrew Bibles this verse belongs to the promise at the end of chapter 1 (1:10–11 are 2:1–2 there, and this verse is 2:3). It drops the "Lo-" ("not") from the children's names: Lo-ammi becomes *Ammi*, "my people," and Lo-ruhamah becomes *Ruhamah*, "received mercy." The ESV heading starts a new section here, but the verse looks back, and the reversal will come again at the end (v23). JFB reads it as the restored people calling "one another, as brothers and sisters in the family of God."
- **"Plead with your mother, plead" (v2).** "Plead" (*rib*) is a legal word: bring a case, contend. The prophets often frame God's dealings with Israel as a covenant lawsuit (4:1; Mic 6:1–2). The "mother" is the nation as a whole, and the "children" are individual Israelites. God calls on the people to confront their own nation.
- **"For she is not my wife, and I am not her husband" (v2).** This sounds like a divorce formula, and some scholars think it echoes one. But the rest of the chapter shows God does not want a divorce. He is describing the reality she has created: she lives as if she has no husband. The aim is to make her stop. Under the law, adultery carried the death penalty (Deut 22:22), so the surprising thing is not God's anger but his refusal to let her go.
- **"Put away her whoring from her face, and her adultery from between her breasts" (v2).** This may picture jewelry, cosmetics or amulets worn as signs of devotion to Baal or as a prostitute's adornment (compare v13, "her ring and jewelry"). Israel wore her unfaithfulness openly.
- **"Lest I strip her naked and make her as in the day she was born" (v3).** Stripping was a known public shame for an adulteress (Ezek 16:37–39). "As in the day she was born" points back to Israel's beginning: Ezekiel 16:4–8 pictures Israel as a newborn left exposed, whom God rescued, raised and clothed. If she credits Baal for everything, God will take back what he gave and leave her as she was before him. **"Like a wilderness… a parched land… kill her with thirst"**: the bride and the land merge in one picture. For a people who served Baal for rain, drought was the exact opposite of what they hoped for.
- **"Upon her children also I will have no mercy" (v4).** This repeats the daughter's name, No Mercy. The children are not innocent bystanders. They are the people who have taken on their mother's ways.
- **"I will go after my lovers, who give me my bread and my water" (v5).** Israel convicts herself with her own words. "Lovers" means the Baals, the local forms of the Canaanite god (v13; 11:2). The list covers the basics of life: food and water, wool and flax for clothes, oil for cooking, light and skin, and drink. Canaanite religion promised that worshiping the fertility gods, often with ritual sex, would make fields and flocks fertile. Israel came to believe that blessing came from them.
- **"Therefore I will hedge up her way with thorns" (v6).** "Therefore" opens three paragraphs in a row (vv6, 9, 14), each a step in God's plan. The first is to block the road. Thorn hedges and stone walls kept animals out of vineyards; here they keep Israel from reaching her lovers. This is mercy in a hard form: God makes the way of sin painful so she will turn around.
- **"She shall seek them but shall not find them" (v7).** The words echo Amos 8:12, where people seek God's word and cannot find it. Here she seeks her lovers. Their emptiness becomes clear when they cannot be found. **"I will go and return to my first husband, for it was better for me then than now"**: the motive is not yet love. It is the hunger of the prodigal son, who "came to himself" and remembered that his father's servants had bread (Luke 15:17). It is a beginning.
- **"She did not know that it was I who gave her the grain, the wine, and the oil" (v8).** "Know" is one of Hosea's key words (4:1, 6; 6:6). To "know" God in Hebrew means more than knowing facts: it means personal, faithful relationship, like a marriage. Grain, new wine and olive oil were the three main crops of the land and the covenant blessings God promised (Deut 7:13; 11:14). **"Silver and gold, which they used for Baal"**: God's gifts were melted into idols (8:4). JFB says they made "images of Baal, or at least the plate covering of them."
- **"Therefore I will take back my grain in its time" (v9).** The second step: God takes back his gifts. Notice "my grain," "my wine," "my wool." It was always his. He will take it back "in its time," at harvest, just when they expect to gather it. The covenant curses warned of exactly this (Deut 28:38–40).
- **"I will uncover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers" (v10).** The idols cannot save their worshipers. The shame will be public, and "no one shall rescue her out of my hand." The Baals are powerless before the God they replaced.
- **"I will put an end to all her mirth, her feasts, her new moons, her Sabbaths" (v11).** These were festivals God himself commanded (Lev 23; Num 28), but Israel had mixed them with Baal worship until they were more about the harvest party than about God. The Tyndale notes compare Isaiah 1:12–17 and Amos 5:21–24. JFB: "to degenerate Israel worship was a weariness; they cared only for the carnal indulgence on them."
- **"These are my wages, which my lovers have given me" (v12).** "Wages" is a prostitute's fee. Israel thought of her vineyards and fig orchards as payment for her service to Baal. God will turn them into wild thickets for animals. Vines and fig trees were the picture of peace and plenty: "every man under his vine and under his fig tree" (1 Kings 4:25; Mic 4:4).
- **"The feast days of the Baals… and forgot me, declares the LORD" (v13).** The plural "Baals" shows the god was worshiped at many local shrines under different titles. The final charge is the bitterest: "she forgot me." In Hosea, to forget God is the opposite of knowing him (4:6; 13:6). It is not a lapse of memory but a choice to live without him. In the Hebrew, "me" stands first in the phrase for emphasis: "but *me* she forgot."
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both see mercy in the thorn hedge. JFB says "it is the way of sin that is hedged up with thorns," and Guzik argues that God's frustrating of Israel's plans was itself an act of love meant to bring her home. Guzik illustrates v8 with Hosea himself, buying groceries for Gomer that she then credits to her lover, and applies it to every gift people use while ignoring the giver. On "plead," JFB glosses "expostulate," and he says of v2 that "she has deprived herself of her high privilege by spiritual adultery." The Tyndale notes add that the "lawsuit" is aimed at reconciliation, not divorce.

#### The LORD's Mercy on Israel (vv14–23)
- **"Therefore, behold, I will allure her" (v14).** The third "therefore" is the great surprise. After the case against her, the logical next step is final punishment. Instead God says, "I will allure her." The verb (*patah*) means to persuade, entice, even seduce. It is used of a man winning over a young woman (Exod 22:16). God will court his wife again. JFB: God must "so temper judgment with unlooked-for grace as to win her to His ways."
- **"And bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her" (v14).** The wilderness was where God had first brought Israel after the exodus, where he gave her the covenant at Sinai, and where she depended on him alone for manna and water. Jeremiah calls it Israel's honeymoon: "I remember… your love as a bride, how you followed me in the wilderness" (Jer 2:2). Being brought back there means losing everything Baal promised, but finding God again. "Speak tenderly" is literally "speak to her heart," the phrase used of Shechem to Dinah (Gen 34:3) and Boaz to Ruth (Ruth 2:13). Henry: "Those whom God has mercy in store for he first brings into a wilderness."
- **"Make the Valley of Achor a door of hope" (v15).** *Achor* means "trouble." It was the valley near Jericho where Achan was stoned for stealing devoted things, the first sin of Israel in the promised land (Josh 7:24–26). The place of Israel's first failure becomes an entrance to a new beginning. Isaiah also uses Achor as a picture of restoration (Isa 65:10). Henry: "Trouble for sin, if it be sincere, opens a door of hope."
- **"There she shall answer as in the days of her youth" (v15).** "Answer" may mean "respond" to God's wooing, or "sing," as Israel sang at the Red Sea (Exod 15). Either way, the love of the first days returns.
- **"You will call me 'My Husband,' and no longer… 'My Baal'" (v16).** In everyday Hebrew a wife could call her husband *ishi*, "my man," or *baali*, "my master" or "my lord." *Baal* was a common word for "owner" or "husband" as well as the name of the god. Israel had called the LORD "Baal" so often that the two had blurred. God says the very word will be gone. Henry: "Ishi is vir meus—my man; Baali is dominus meus—my lord." JFB: "Affection is the prominent idea in 'Husband'; rule, in 'Lord.'" The point is not that God gives up his lordship but that the relationship he wants is one of love, not mere ownership. Israelites had even given their children names that included "baal": Saul named a son Esh-baal (1 Chr 8:33). Later scribes sometimes replaced the "baal" with *bosheth*, "shame" (Ish-bosheth, 2 Sam 2:8), as if obeying this verse.
- **"I will remove the names of the Baals from her mouth" (v17).** The cure for idolatry is not only to ban the word but to change the heart that loved it. Zechariah 13:2 makes a similar promise.
- **"A covenant on that day with the beasts of the field" (v18).** In v12 wild animals devoured the vineyards. Now creation is at peace with God's people. God will **"abolish the bow, the sword, and war"**, undoing the "broken bow" of 1:5. **"I will make you lie down in safety"** echoes the covenant promise of Leviticus 26:5–6. Isaiah 11:6–9 and Ezekiel 34:25 describe the same peace. JFB places its full realization in the coming kingdom.
- **"I will betroth you to me forever" (v19).** The verb *aras*, "betroth," is repeated three times. In Israel betrothal was a binding legal commitment, with the bride price paid, and broken only by divorce (compare Mary and Joseph, Matt 1:18–19). Remarkably, God does not say "I will take you back." He speaks as to a young virgin being engaged for the first time. The past is not just forgiven; it is made new. JFB: "'Betroth' is thrice repeated, implying the intense love of God to His people."
- **"In righteousness and in justice, in steadfast love and in mercy… in faithfulness" (vv19–20).** The groom pays the bride price, and these five qualities are what God brings to the marriage. *Righteousness* and *justice* mean he will do right by her. *Steadfast love* is *hesed*, the loyal, covenant-keeping love that is one of the great words of the Old Testament. *Mercy* is *rachamim*, the same root as Ruhamah, the tender compassion she had been denied (1:6). *Faithfulness* means he will not change. The bride brings nothing. The husband supplies everything.
- **"And you shall know the LORD" (v20).** This answers v8 ("she did not know") and v13 ("she forgot me"). The verb "know" is often used of marital intimacy (Gen 4:1), and here the whole marriage picture is complete. Jeremiah's promise of the new covenant ends the same way: "they shall all know me" (Jer 31:34).
- **"I will answer the heavens, and they shall answer the earth" (vv21–22).** This is a chain of answers. God answers the sky, the sky gives rain to the earth, the earth gives grain, wine and oil, and they answer Jezreel. The whole created order responds to God's word, and the blessing flows down to his people. Israel had asked Baal for rain; now the true God sends it. Henry calls it "the coherence of second causes with one another, as links in a chain," each depending on God as the first cause.
- **"And they shall answer Jezreel, and I will sow her for myself in the land" (vv22–23).** Here the name Jezreel, "God sows," finally means a planting. The people God scattered he will sow again in their land, like seed that grows.
- **"I will have mercy on No Mercy… 'You are my people'; and he shall say, 'You are my God'" (v23).** All three children's names are reversed. And the chapter ends with the people's own reply, the restored covenant spoken in both directions. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 9:25 to show God calling Gentiles, and Peter alludes to it in 1 Peter 2:10.
- **Commentators:** All three see vv14–15 as grace that comes through trouble. JFB calls the wilderness "moral," not geographic, and says that "the very trouble of Israel's wilderness state will be the 'door of hope.'" Guzik, quoting Spurgeon, says love's allure accomplishes what force cannot. On v16 Guzik sees the Baals offering a master-and-slave religion of fear, while the true God wants freely given love. JFB tentatively links the threefold "betroth" with the three Persons of the Trinity, which is more than the text says. On v19 Henry notes that "the separation begins on our side; we alienate ourselves from God," but God is the one who restores the bond. Guzik ties the chain of answers in vv21–22 to Jesus' promise that those who seek first God's kingdom will have what they need (Matt 6:33).

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter moves from the courtroom to the wilderness to a wedding. It is the gospel in the Old Testament: a guilty bride, a righteous husband who could justly put her away, and a love that pays the bride price itself. In the New Testament, Jesus is the bridegroom (Mark 2:19–20; John 3:29). He brings to his bride the righteousness, justice, steadfast love, mercy and faithfulness she could never bring. Paul says Christ "loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her… so that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle" (Eph 5:25–27). The book of Revelation ends with what Hosea saw from far off: the bride made ready for her husband, and God dwelling with his people, "and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God" (Rev 21:2–3).
