*Setting:* Chapter 56 ended with Israel's watchmen blind, their shepherds greedy and drunk. Chapter 57 shows what happens to a people with leaders like that. Good people die and nobody notices, and the nation sinks into the worst kind of idolatry, including the sacrifice of children. The sins described here match Judah before the exile, especially the long reign of King Manasseh in the 600s BC, who "burned his son as an offering" and "shed very much innocent blood" (2 Kings 21:6, 16). Later Jewish tradition says Isaiah himself was martyred under Manasseh, and Hebrews 11:37 ("they were sawn in two") is often read as a reference to that. Some scholars place the chapter after the return from exile, when the same practices may have resurfaced. The chapter is in two parts: a courtroom charge against spiritual adultery (vv1–13) and one of the tenderest promises in Isaiah, God dwelling with the crushed and lowly (vv14–21).

#### Israel's Futile Idolatry (vv1–13)
- **"The righteous man perishes, and no one lays it to heart" (v1).** Godly people are dying and nobody stops to ask why. "Lays it to heart" means taking something seriously and thinking about what it means. In a healthy community, the death of a good person is a loss everyone feels. In this one, it barely registers.
- **"Devout men are taken away" (v1).** "Devout" translates *chesed*, here meaning people of loyal love, faithful to God. "Taken away" is the verb "gathered," used for being "gathered to one's people" in death (Gen 25:8).
- **"For the righteous man is taken away from calamity" (v1).** Here is what nobody understood: God was removing them before the worst came. God told King Josiah that he would be gathered to his grave in peace and would not see the disaster coming on Jerusalem (2 Kings 22:20). The Tyndale notes say that at times God lets the godly die to spare them harsher days ahead. This is not a promise that every early death has that reason, but it does say that a good person's death is never meaningless to God.
- **"He enters into peace; they rest in their beds who walk in their uprightness" (v2).** "Beds" here means their graves. Death for the righteous is rest, peace, *shalom*. That word frames the chapter. It returns in v19, "Peace, peace," and is denied in v21, "There is no peace… for the wicked." Paul: "to depart and be with Christ… is far better" (Phil 1:23). Revelation: "Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord… that they may rest from their labors" (Rev 14:13).
- **"But you, draw near, sons of the sorceress, offspring of the adulterer and the loose woman" (v3).** The scene shifts to a courtroom. "Draw near" is the summons to stand before the judge (41:1). The insult is pointed: they are not true children of Abraham and Sarah (51:2) but children of witchcraft and unfaithfulness. Jesus says something similar to those who wanted to kill him: if you were Abraham's children, you would do what Abraham did (John 8:39–44).
- **"Whom are you mocking?… and stick out your tongue?" (v4).** They have been mocking the righteous, making faces at them. God asks whom they are really mocking. The answer is him. **"Children of transgression, the offspring of deceit."** Their family resemblance is to rebellion and lies.
- **"You who burn with lust among the oaks, under every green tree" (v5).** Canaanite religion worshiped fertility gods at hilltop shrines and in groves, often with ritual sex to "stir up" the gods to make the land fertile (1:29; Deut 12:2; Jer 2:20; Hos 4:13). "Under every green tree" became the prophets' stock phrase for this.
- **"Who slaughter your children in the valleys, under the clefts of the rocks" (v5).** This is child sacrifice, the worst of Canaan's practices. The Law forbade it as an abomination (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31; 18:10). It happened in the Valley of Hinnom just outside Jerusalem, at a site called Topheth (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31). Kings Ahaz and Manasseh both did it (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6). Psalm 106:37–38 says they "sacrificed their sons and their daughters to the demons." The Valley of Hinnom, *Ge-Hinnom*, later gave its name to Gehenna, the word Jesus used for hell (Matt 5:22).
- **"Among the smooth stones of the valley is your portion; they, they, are your lot" (v6).** The Hebrew is a pun: "smooth stones" (*chalqe*) and "your portion" (*chelqek*) sound alike. A "portion" or "lot" was a family's share of the promised land, and the Levites were told, "I am your portion" (Num 18:20). The psalmist says "The LORD is my chosen portion" (Ps 16:5). These people have swapped God for stones, probably standing stones or smooth stones from a streambed worshiped as idols. **"Shall I relent for these things?"** Should God simply overlook it?
- **"On a high and lofty mountain you have set your bed" (vv7–8).** "Bed" is the picture of adultery throughout these verses. Israel is the wife of the LORD (54:5), and her idolatry is unfaithfulness, carried out in the open on hilltop shrines. Hosea and Ezekiel use the same picture at length (Hos 2; Ezek 16; 23). The language is deliberately shocking. Sin against God is not just rule-breaking. It is betrayal of love.
- **"Behind the door and the doorpost you have set up your memorial" (v8).** God had told Israel to write his words "on the doorposts of your house" (Deut 6:9). Jewish homes still fix a *mezuzah*, a small case with those verses, to the doorpost. These people have put a "memorial" (*zikkaron*), some pagan token or symbol, in exactly the place where God's words belonged. Guzik notes how pointed that would be to an Israelite who knew the command that comes with Deuteronomy 6:4, "the LORD is one."
- **"You have made a covenant for yourself with them" (v8).** They made a covenant, not with God, but with their idols or with foreign powers. "You have looked on nakedness" is literally "you have looked on a hand," likely a euphemism for sexual organs, possibly referring to phallic symbols used in fertility worship.
- **"You journeyed to the king with oil and multiplied your perfumes" (v9).** "The king" is *melek*, which may be a pun on Molech (*molek*), the god to whom children were sacrificed. Or it may mean a foreign king, like Assyria's, whom Judah courted with gifts (2 Kings 16:7–8). JFB allows both. **"You sent your envoys far off, and sent down even to Sheol."** They went to any length, even consulting the dead (8:19). Sheol is the realm of the dead.
- **"You were wearied with the length of your way, but you did not say, 'It is hopeless'" (v10).** Chasing idols is exhausting, but they would not admit it was getting them nowhere. Each time, they found fresh energy to keep going. Jeremiah says the same: "you said, 'It is hopeless, for I have loved foreigners, and after them I will go'" (Jer 2:25).
- **"Whom did you dread and fear, so that you lied, and did not remember me?" (v11).** Fear of the wrong things leads to lies and forgetting God. Isaiah had said long before, "do not fear what they fear… the LORD of hosts, him you shall regard as holy. Let him be your fear" (8:12–13). **"Have I not held my peace, even for a long time, and you do not fear me?"** God's patience was taken as permission. Peter warns about the same mistake: "The Lord is not slow… but is patient toward you" (2 Pet 3:9). Paul: do you "presume on the riches of his kindness"? (Rom 2:4).
- **"I will declare your righteousness and your deeds, but they will not profit you" (v12).** This is sarcasm. Their so-called righteousness will be laid out in public, and it will not help them. Isaiah 64:6 says our righteous deeds are "like a polluted garment."
- **"Let your collection of idols deliver you! The wind will carry them all off, a breath will take them away" (v13).** "Collection" means a crowd of idols, a whole pantheon gathered up. They weigh so little that a puff of wind blows them away. Compare the idols that must be carried and cannot carry anyone (46:1–7).
- **"But he who takes refuge in me shall possess the land and shall inherit my holy mountain" (v13).** In the middle of judgment the promise breaks through. "Land" can also mean "earth." Psalm 37 promises five times that the humble and trusting "shall inherit the land" (Ps 37:9, 11, 22, 29, 34), and Jesus took it up: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5). The "holy mountain" is where foreigners were welcomed in 56:7.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv1–2 as "comforting those that were taken away in their integrity" and "reproving those that did not make a due improvement of such providences," that is, people who did not learn anything from the deaths of good people. JFB ties the righteous dying to Manasseh's reign, and adds that the rest in the grave of v2 is "in contrast to the persecutions which he suffered in this world." Guzik and JFB both describe Molech worship, with drums drowning the children's cries, a detail from later Jewish tradition rather than the Bible itself. Guzik notes that the sins described fit the time before the exile and uses this to support a single author for the book, which is debated. On v8 JFB and Guzik both see the memorial placed where God's law should have been written.

#### Comfort for the Contrite (vv14–21)
- **"Build up, build up, prepare the way, remove every obstruction from my people's way" (v14).** This echoes 40:3, "prepare the way of the LORD," and the raised highway of 35:8 and 62:10. Road-builders raised a roadbed and cleared the stones. The obstacles here are spiritual: whatever trips the people up on the way back to God.
- **"Thus says the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy" (v15).** "High and lifted up" (*ram venissaʾ*) is the phrase from Isaiah's throne-room vision (6:1), and it was given to the Servant in 52:13. "Inhabits eternity" means he lives forever, outside time. "Whose name is Holy" recalls the seraphim crying "Holy, holy, holy" (6:3). This is the most exalted description of God in the book.
- **"I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit" (v15).** This is the surprise. The God who lives in the highest place also lives with the lowest person. "Contrite" is *dakkaʾ*, from the verb "to crush." It is the same root used of the Servant: "he was crushed for our iniquities" (53:5), and "it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (53:10). God lives with the crushed. "Lowly" (*shephal*) means low, humble, brought down. David prayed, "a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (Ps 51:17), and "the LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (Ps 34:18), where "crushed" is the same word.
- **"To revive the spirit of the lowly, and to revive the heart of the contrite" (v15).** "Revive" is literally "make alive." God does not just tolerate the humble. He brings them back to life. Jesus: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 5:3).
- **"For I will not contend forever, nor will I always be angry" (v16).** "Contend" means to bring a case against someone in court. **"For the spirit would grow faint before me, and the breath of life that I made."** If God's anger went on and on, no human could survive it. He remembers that he made us, and that we are frail. Psalm 103:9, 14: "He will not always chide… for he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust."
- **"Because of the iniquity of his unjust gain I was angry, I struck him" (v17).** "Unjust gain" is greed, the sin of the leaders in 56:11. God hid his face in anger, and the people still went their own way. Discipline alone did not change them.
- **"I have seen his ways, but I will heal him" (v18).** This is grace at its plainest. God has watched them keep sinning, *and* he will heal them. Not because they finally got it right, but because he decided to. Isaiah 53:5 says the healing comes "with his wounds." **"Restore comfort to him and his mourners."** "Comfort" is the keynote word of chapter 40 again.
- **"Creating the fruit of the lips" (v19).** God creates praise on the lips of the healed. Hebrews uses this phrase: "let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15). It replaces the drink offerings poured out to idols (v6).
- **"Peace, peace, to the far and to the near" (v19).** The doubled word is emphatic, "perfect peace," as in 26:3. "The near" are Israelites in the land. "The far" are those scattered far away, and, as chapter 56 showed, even Gentiles. Paul quotes this when he describes Christ's work: "he came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near. For through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father" (Eph 2:17–18). Paul reads "the far" as Gentiles and "the near" as Jews.
- **"But the wicked are like the tossing sea; for it cannot be quiet" (v20).** The sea in the Bible often stands for chaos and restlessness (Ps 46:3; 93:3–4). It is never still. **"Its waters toss up mire and dirt."** Every wave drags up mud. JFB notes that the Hebrew pictures a sea that *cannot* rest at all, not one that is only sometimes rough. Jude describes false teachers as "wild waves of the sea, casting up the foam of their own shame" (Jude 13). In the new creation "the sea was no more" (Rev 21:1).
- **"There is no peace," says my God, "for the wicked" (v21).** This line also closed chapter 48 (48:22). It works like a refrain, marking off sections of the book. It is the exact opposite of v19. Peace is offered to everyone, far and near. But those who keep refusing it cannot have it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and the Tyndale notes both connect v19 to Ephesians 2:17, with "far" as Gentiles and "near" as Jews. JFB says the first sense is the end of Israel's afflictions and the larger sense the gospel's peace to Jew and Gentile alike. Guzik describes three steps to peace with God from vv15–19: seeing God's majesty, coming in humility, and receiving his mercy. He notes that *shalom* means far more than the absence of conflict. Henry summarizes vv13–21 as "the gracious returns of God to his people." JFB applies v15 to Israel's future repentance in "the last days." On v20, Guzik ties the restless sea to Revelation 21:1, where the sea is no more.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter places the highest God beside the most crushed person. Isaiah used the same "crushing" word for the Servant (53:5, 10). The One who is "high and lifted up" came down and was crushed himself, so that he could live with the crushed and revive them. Jesus spoke of the "poor in spirit," healed people the leaders ignored, and through the cross "preached peace" to those far off and to those near (Eph 2:17). The restless sea of v20 is a picture of life without him. "Peace, peace" is the offer he makes to everyone who comes.
