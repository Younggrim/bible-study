*Setting:* The second of the five laments over Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC. Like chapter 1, it is an acrostic of twenty-two three-line stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and it opens with the funeral cry *'Ekah*, "How!" But its focus has shifted. Chapter 1 dwelt on the city's loneliness and shame. Chapter 2 looks straight at the hand behind the disaster. The Babylonians are hardly mentioned. Over and over the subject of the verbs is "the Lord," who has acted "like an enemy" against his own city, temple, king and priests. The poem moves in three parts: the narrator describes God's anger (vv1–10), then breaks down in personal grief over starving children and appeals to the city to pray (vv11–19), and finally Zion herself speaks to God (vv20–22). In this chapter and the next two, the letters *pe* and *'ayin* appear in reverse of the usual order (v16 begins with *pe*, v17 with *'ayin*). The same order turns up in a few ancient Hebrew alphabet inscriptions, so it may simply have been an accepted variant of the alphabet.

#### The Lord Has Destroyed Without Pity (vv1–22)
- **"How the Lord in his anger has set the daughter of Zion under a cloud!" (v1).** The cloud had once been the sign of God's glorious presence, filling the tabernacle and temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kgs 8:10–11). Now it is a cloud of anger, darkening the city.
- **"He has cast down from heaven to earth the splendor of Israel" (v1).** Jerusalem's glory, especially the temple, falls from the heights to the dust, like the king of Babylon in Isaiah's taunt (Isa 14:12).
- **"He has not remembered his footstool in the day of his anger" (v1).** The ark of the covenant, and the temple that housed it, were called God's footstool, the place where the heavenly King rested his feet on earth (1 Chr 28:2; Ps 99:5; 132:7). God was enthroned above the cherubim. To ignore his footstool is to abandon his earthly throne room.
- **"The Lord has swallowed up without mercy all the habitations of Jacob" (v2).** "Swallow" (*bala'*) is a key word in the chapter, used five times (vv2, 5 twice, 8, 16). God swallows Israel like a devouring beast, and at the end the enemies boast, "We have swallowed her!" **"Habitations"** can mean pastures or shepherds' dwellings, the countryside towns as well as the capital.
- **"He has cut down in fierce anger all the might of Israel" (v3).** "Might" is literally "horn," the ancient symbol of an animal's strength and so of a king's or nation's power (1 Sam 2:10; Ps 75:10). **"He has withdrawn from them his right hand in the face of the enemy."** The right hand that once shattered Pharaoh's army (Exod 15:6) is pulled back. God did not fight for them.
- **"He has bent his bow like an enemy, with his right hand set like a foe" (v4).** Worse than withdrawing, he turns his weapons against them. Israel had sung of the LORD as "a man of war" who fought its battles (Exod 15:3; Josh 10:14). Now the divine warrior stands on the other side. **"All who were delightful in our eyes"**: the young, the beautiful, the promising.
- **"The Lord has become like an enemy" (v5).** Note the word "like." God has not become their enemy in the deepest sense. He remains their covenant God. But in this hour he acts exactly as an enemy would. **"Mourning and lamentation"** is a pair of Hebrew words that sound alike (*ta'aniyyah wa'aniyyah*), a sound-play something like "moaning and groaning."
- **"He has laid waste his booth like a garden" (v6).** The temple is torn down as easily as a farmer takes apart the temporary shelter he set up in a garden or vineyard for the harvest (Isa 1:8). God had chosen this house as his dwelling. He dismantles it as if it were a hut. **"The LORD has made Zion forget festival and Sabbath."** The calendar of worship has stopped. **"Has spurned king and priest"**: both institutions God had established, monarchy and priesthood, are rejected together.
- **"The Lord has scorned his altar, disowned his sanctuary" (v7).** God had warned that he would cast off a temple turned into "a den of robbers" (Jer 7:11–14). **"They raised a clamor in the house of the LORD as on the day of festival."** The shout of Babylonian soldiers in the temple courts sounded like the crowds who once shouted praise there at the feasts. The bitter echo is deliberate.
- **"He stretched out the measuring line" (v8).** A builder's line was used to mark where to build, but also where to tear down (2 Kgs 21:13; Isa 34:11; Amos 7:7–9). The destruction was not random. It was measured and planned. **"He caused rampart and wall to lament"**: even the stones mourn.
- **"Her gates have sunk into the ground" (v9).** Buried under rubble, as if swallowed by the earth. **"Her king and princes are among the nations."** Zedekiah is blind in a Babylonian prison (Jer 52:11). **"The law is no more."** "Law" is *torah*, "instruction," the teaching priests were meant to give (Mal 2:7). With the temple gone and priests killed or exiled, no one is teaching God's word. Ezekiel had warned: "the law perishes from the priest, and counsel from the elders" (Ezek 7:26). **"Her prophets find no vision from the LORD."** The court prophets who had promised peace (Jer 14:13–15; 23:16–17) have nothing left to say. The true prophets, Jeremiah in Judah, Ezekiel and later Daniel in Babylon, still received God's word, but the official institutions had fallen silent (compare Ps 74:9; Amos 8:11–12).
- **"The elders… sit on the ground in silence; they have thrown dust on their heads and put on sackcloth" (v10).** The standard signs of mourning for the dead (Job 2:12–13). The old men and the young women together, the whole city in grief.
- **"My eyes are spent with weeping; my stomach churns; my bile is poured out to the ground" (v11).** The narrator now breaks down. "Bile" is literally "liver," which Hebrew poetry treated, like the heart and the inner organs, as a seat of the emotions. His whole body is overwhelmed. **"Because infants and babies faint in the streets of the city."** Nothing undoes him like the children.
- **"They cry to their mothers, 'Where is bread and wine?'… as their life is poured out on their mothers' bosom" (v12).** Grain and wine were the basics of an ordinary meal. The children die in their mothers' arms. The poet does not look away.
- **"What can I say for you, to what compare you…? For your ruin is vast as the sea; who can heal you?" (v13).** Comfort often comes from comparing: "Others have suffered this too." But there is no comparison for this. The poet does not have words big enough. "Ruin" is literally "breach," the break in a wall, as vast as the sea.
- **"Your prophets have seen for you false and deceptive visions" (v14).** Literally "empty and whitewash," the same picture Ezekiel used of prophets who daubed whitewash over a cracked wall to hide it (Ezek 13:10–16). **"They have not exposed your iniquity to restore your fortunes."** This is the heart of the charge. If the prophets had named the people's sin, repentance might have turned the disaster aside (Jer 23:22). Instead they said, "Peace, peace," when there was no peace (Jer 6:14; 8:11). **"Oracles that are false and misleading."** "Oracles" is the word for a prophet's "burden" (*massa'*). Jeremiah had mocked those who claimed a "burden of the LORD" they had invented (Jer 23:33–40).
- **"All who pass along the way clap their hands at you; they hiss and wag their heads" (v15).** Gestures of scorn and amazement (Jer 18:16; 19:8). **"Is this the city that was called the perfection of beauty, the joy of all the earth?"** They quote Israel's own songs about Zion back at her (Ps 48:2; 50:2).
- **"They hiss, they gnash their teeth, they cry: 'We have swallowed her!'" (v16).** The enemies claim the victory as their own. **"This is the day we longed for."** Edom, Ammon and Moab, Judah's neighbors, gloated in exactly this way (Ps 137:7; Ezek 25:3–6; Obad 12).
- **"The LORD has done what he purposed; he has carried out his word, which he commanded long ago" (v17).** The enemies say "we did it." The poet answers that God did it. The warnings of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28 had been on the record for centuries. "Long ago" points back to those covenant curses. God's word does not fail, whether it promises blessing or judgment. The terrible fact is also, in a strange way, a mercy: the disaster has meaning, because it came from God, not chance.
- **"Their heart cried to the Lord. O wall of the daughter of Zion, let tears stream down like a torrent day and night!" (v18).** The opening line is hard in the Hebrew. It may mean the people's hearts cried out, or, with a small change, it may be a command: "Cry out from the heart to the Lord." Then the poet speaks to the city wall itself as if it were a woman, calling it to weep without rest.
- **"Arise, cry out in the night… Pour out your heart like water before the presence of the Lord!" (v19).** This is the turning point of the chapter. The same Lord who struck them is the only one to whom they can turn. **"At the beginning of the night watches"**: Israel divided the night into three watches (Judg 7:19). Prayer should begin as soon as night falls and continue through it. **"Lift your hands to him for the lives of your children."** The narrator's grief turns into intercession. Pouring out the heart is the language of honest prayer (1 Sam 1:15; Ps 62:8).
- **"Look, O LORD, and see! With whom have you dealt thus?" (v20).** Now Zion speaks, and she obeys the call to pray. She does not hide the worst. **"Should women eat the fruit of their womb, the children of their tender care?"** During the siege, starving mothers ate their own children, as had happened in Samaria's siege (2 Kgs 6:28–29). Moses had warned of it (Deut 28:53–57) and so had Jeremiah (Jer 19:9). It was the darkest sign of the covenant curse. **"Should priest and prophet be killed in the sanctuary of the Lord?"** The holy place became a killing ground (2 Chr 36:17).
- **"In the dust of the streets lie the young and the old" (v21).** **"You have killed them in the day of your anger, slaughtering without pity."** These are hard words spoken to God. Scripture records them without rebuke. Lament gives the grieving permission to bring their rawest pain to God, as long as they bring it to him.
- **"You summoned as if to a festival day my terrors on every side" (v22).** Bitter irony again. Jerusalem's great feasts drew crowds from every side. Now God has summoned armies, famine and death to a dreadful "festival." **"Terrors on every side"** (*magor missabib*) was Jeremiah's own catchphrase (Jer 6:25; 20:3–4, 10; 46:5; 49:29), a link many have seen as supporting his authorship. **"Those whom I held and raised my enemy destroyed."** The chapter ends, like chapter 1, without an answer, only with the children lost.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry insists that the comparison in v5 is precise: "God is not an enemy to his people, when he is angry with them and corrects them." On v19 he writes, "Prayer is a salve for every sore, even the sorest; a remedy for every malady," and "our business in prayer is to refer our case to the Lord, and leave it with him." Guzik also presses the reversal of vv1–5, that God was fighting against his people rather than for them. Following Poole, he reads the "footstool" as the temple, and he takes the measuring line of v8 as proof the judgment was deliberate, not haphazard. On v14 he notes that without exposing sin, the false prophets could offer no true comfort. JFB reads the footstool as the ark, which Israel "had gloried" in more than in God himself. He explains the "measuring line" as a builder's line used also in demolition, showing "unsparing rigor," and the "liver" of v11 as the seat of strong feeling. On v17, JFB agrees that the enemy should not take credit, since "the Lord did this." On v20 he ties the horrors directly to the curses of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter faces the unbearable: God acting "like an enemy" against his own people because of their sin. The gospel does not soften that truth. It shows where it finally lands. On the cross, the Son of God bore the anger of God against sin, and cried out, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). He was the true temple, torn down and raised again in three days (John 2:19–21). He wept over this same city because it did not know the things that make for peace, and because its false prophets had not exposed its sin (Luke 19:41–44). And the call of v19, "Pour out your heart like water before the presence of the Lord," is now an open invitation, because through Christ the way into God's presence is open, and the one who prays there finds mercy and grace in time of need (Heb 4:16).
