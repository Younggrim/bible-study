*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. Of David, for the memorial offering." Psalm 70 is a short, urgent prayer that repeats, almost word for word, the last five verses of Psalm 40 (40:13–17). In Psalm 40 those lines come after a long song of thanks for past rescue. Here they stand alone, a cry for help with no time for anything else. The title phrase "for the memorial offering" (*lehazkir*, "to bring to remembrance") also heads Psalm 38. It may connect the psalm with the "memorial portion" (*'azkarah*) of the grain offering, the handful burned on the altar as a reminder before God (Lev 2:2), or it may simply mean a prayer that asks God to remember. The Tyndale notes say the exact meaning is unknown. Placed right after Psalm 69 and before Psalm 71, it carries the same urgency as its neighbors: "make haste to answer me" (69:17) and "O my God, make haste to help me!" (71:12). JFB calls it "a suitable appendix to the preceding" psalm.

#### Make Haste to Help Me (vv1–3)
- **"Make haste, O God, to deliver me! O LORD, make haste to help me!" (v1).** In Hebrew the first line has no verb at all, just "O God, to deliver me," so the prayer comes out breathless. The ESV supplies "make haste" from the second line. Psalm 40:13 begins more gently, "Be pleased, O LORD, to deliver me." Here the politeness has been cut away. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes there is no moment to lose.
- **A prayer for every hour.** In the fifth century John Cassian recommended v1 as a prayer to say constantly, in every temptation and need, and the Western church took it into its daily services. Monastic and Anglican prayer offices still open with it: "O God, make speed to save us. O Lord, make haste to help us." Boice, cited by Guzik, passes on Luther's description of this prayer as a shield against fear, presumption and lukewarmness.
- **"Let them be put to shame and confusion who seek my life!" (v2).** "Seek my life" means hunt me down to kill me. Psalm 40:14 has "seek to snatch away my life" and adds "altogether." **"Who delight in my hurt"**: they are not only hostile but pleased by his pain.
- **"Let them turn back because of their shame who say, 'Aha, Aha!'" (v3).** "Aha" (*he'ach*) is a gloating cry, the jeer of someone enjoying another's downfall (35:21; Ezek 25:3). Psalm 40:15 has "Let those be appalled because of their shame." JFB notes the change to "turn back." The prayer asks that their mockery come back on them: let the ones who sneer at his disgrace be disgraced.
- **A prayer for shame, not cruelty.** The request is that their plans fail and that they be "turned back." Boice, cited by Guzik, argues that the kindest thing to pray for those doing evil is that their schemes collapse, so that they may see their folly and turn to God. Psalm 83:16 makes that hope explicit: "Fill their faces with shame, that they may seek your name, O LORD."
- **Commentators:** Henry explains the title: the psalm may "put God in remembrance of his mercy and promises" (Isa 43:26), "not that the Eternal Mind needs a remembrancer," or remind us of past troubles and past prayers, "that when the clouds return after the rain we may have recourse to the same means." He sees in the repetition of Psalm 40 a lesson: "We may in prayer use the words we have often used before: our Saviour in his agony prayed thrice, saying the same words" (Matt 26:44). But the small changes show David "did not design to tie himself or others to them as a form. God looks at the heart, not at the words." On v2 he hopes the enemies may be "so filled with shame as that they may seek thy name." Guzik cites Morgan, who says God never needs hurrying, but welcomes these imperfect, honest prayers and answers the deeper need. Meyer adds that God's delays are not denials.

#### You Are My Help and My Deliverer (vv4–5)
- **"May all who seek you rejoice and be glad in you!" (v4).** In the middle of his own crisis he prays for everyone else who seeks God. Their joy is "in you," in God himself, not only in a happy outcome.
- **"May those who love your salvation say evermore, 'God is great!'" (v4).** Literally "Let God be magnified." To magnify God is not to make him bigger, which no one can do, but to see and declare him as large as he really is. Psalm 40:16 has "Great is the LORD!" Here the covenant name *YHWH* becomes "God" (*Elohim*), and the same switch happens elsewhere in the psalm. Psalms 42–83 mostly prefer *Elohim*, and many scholars think an editor adjusted this copy to match the collection it sits in. **"Those who love your salvation"** describes believers by what they long for: not only being saved but God's saving work itself.
- **"But I am poor and needy; hasten to me, O God!" (v5).** "Poor and needy" (*'ani ve'evyon*) is a stock pair in the Psalms for those who have no resources and no one to defend them except God (40:17; 86:1; 109:22). It describes the psalmist's condition, but it is also a confession of dependence. David the king prays as a beggar. Clarke, cited by Guzik, pictures an afflicted beggar whose need drives him to plead.
- **"Hasten to me" (v5).** Here is the most telling change from Psalm 40, which reads "but the Lord takes thought for me." There the psalmist rests in God's care. Here he can only cry "hurry!" JFB notes this difference too. Both are honest prayers, and both are in Scripture.
- **"You are my help and my deliverer; O LORD, do not delay!" (v5).** The psalm ends as it began, with haste. He has no other help to turn to. Psalm 40:17 ends "do not delay, O my God," and Psalm 70 ends with the covenant name, "O LORD."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Poverty and necessity are very good pleas in prayer to a God of infinite mercy." He carefully distinguishes the right kind of haste: "He that believes does not make haste" (Isa 28:16, KJV), meaning he does not run ahead of God or grab at wrong ways of escape, "but he may make haste by going forth to meet God in humble prayer." On v4 he adds that "there is pleasure and joy even in seeking God." Guzik notes that David holds together great joy in God and a sober sense of his own need. Horne, cited by Guzik, says that with such a Father, poverty becomes riches and weakness strength.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 70 gives words to anyone who has no time for a long prayer, only a cry. Jesus knew that kind of prayer. In Gethsemane he prayed the same words three times (Matt 26:44), and on the cross he was surrounded by those who said "Aha," wagging their heads (Mark 15:29). He became poor and needy for our sake, "so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). And because he was heard (Heb 5:7), those who are poor and needy now can say, "You are my help and my deliverer," knowing that God's help is not late, and that in Christ his salvation has come.
