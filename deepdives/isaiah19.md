*Setting:* After Cush, the land along the upper Nile, Isaiah turns to Egypt, the ancient superpower of the Nile, Israel's old house of slavery and, in Isaiah's day, the ally Judah kept being tempted to lean on against Assyria (30:1–5; 31:1–3). Egypt in the late 700s BC was weak and divided. Several rival dynasties ruled different parts of the country until the Cushite kings from the south conquered it around 728–715 BC (ch 18). Many in Judah still saw Egypt as the great counterweight to Assyria. Henry captures Isaiah's view: Egypt was "a broken reed" (compare 36:6). The chapter falls into two very different halves. Verses 1–15 announce God's judgment: civil war, a cruel master, a failing Nile and foolish advisers. Verses 16–25, five times "in that day," promise something astonishing: Egypt turning to the Lord, worshiping with Assyria and Israel, and being called "my people."

#### An Oracle Concerning Egypt (vv1–15)
- **"The LORD is riding on a swift cloud and comes to Egypt" (v1).** Riding the clouds is a picture of God as the divine warrior-king (Ps 18:9–10; 68:4; 104:3). In Canaanite poetry from Ugarit, Baal was praised as "rider of the clouds," and the Bible pointedly gives that title to the Lord. Jesus spoke of the Son of Man "coming on the clouds of heaven" (Matt 26:64; Dan 7:13). No army is mentioned. The Lord himself comes.
- **"The idols of Egypt will tremble at his presence" (v1).** Egypt had hundreds of gods: Ra the sun, Hapi of the Nile flood, Osiris, Isis, Amun and more. In the exodus the Lord had already executed "judgments on all the gods of Egypt" (Exod 12:12). Guzik connects the plagues with this verse, since many plagues struck at things Egypt worshiped. Henry: the idols "shall be moved, shall be removed at his presence."
- **"I will stir up Egyptians against Egyptians… city against city, kingdom against kingdom" (v2).** This describes Egypt's real condition in Isaiah's time. Historians call it the Third Intermediate Period, when Egypt was split among rival rulers in the Delta, at Memphis and at Thebes. "Kingdom against kingdom" is exactly right: there were several kings at once. The Tyndale notes observe that Egypt had a long history of civil wars and that its unity was fragile. JFB places this around the time of the Cushite takeover.
- **"The spirit of the Egyptians within them will be emptied out" (v3).** Courage and good judgment drain away. In panic they turn to "the idols and the sorcerers, and the mediums and the necromancers," those who claimed to consult the dead. Egypt was famous for magic (Exod 7:11), and Isaiah has already condemned this same turn to the dead in Judah (8:19).
- **"Into the hand of a hard master, and a fierce king will rule over them" (v4).** The king is not named, and candidates span centuries. The Tyndale notes point to the Assyrians: Esarhaddon took Memphis in 671 BC and Ashurbanipal sacked Thebes in 663 BC (Nah 3:8–10). Others suggest the Cushite conqueror Piye, the Egyptian Psammetichus I, or the Persian Cambyses, who conquered Egypt in 525 BC. JFB lists several of these without deciding. The pattern is clear even if the name is not: Egypt would lose its independence to foreign masters.
- **"The waters of the sea will be dried up, and the river will be dry and parched" (vv5–6).** "The sea" here is the Nile, so wide in flood that Egyptians themselves could call it a "sea." Egypt has almost no rain. All its life came from the Nile's yearly flood, which Egyptians honored as the god Hapi. Henry: "the fruitfulness of their country depended wholly upon the overflowing of their river." The "canals" and "branches" are the irrigation channels and the Delta's arms. "Reeds and rushes" include papyrus, used for boats, rope and paper.
- **Fishermen, flax workers and weavers (vv8–9).** Isaiah names Egypt's industries one by one. Nile fishing fed the country. Egyptian linen, made from flax and famously fine and white, was exported across the ancient world (Prov 7:16; Ezek 27:7). "White cotton" may be fine white linen cloth. If the river fails, the whole economy fails.
- **"Those who are the pillars of the land will be crushed, and all who work for pay will be grieved" (v10).** From the leaders at the top to day laborers at the bottom, everyone suffers. The Hebrew is difficult, but the sense of total economic collapse is clear.
- **"The princes of Zoan are utterly foolish" (v11).** Zoan, called Tanis by the Greeks, was a royal city in the northeastern Delta, close to Judah, and a capital in this period. It was very old (Num 13:22). Egypt's royal counselors were famous for wisdom, and Egyptian wisdom literature was admired across the ancient world (1 Kgs 4:30; Acts 7:22). Henry repeats the line bluntly: "the princes of Zoan are fools."
- **"I am a son of the wise, a son of ancient kings" (v11).** The counselors boast of their pedigree: old families, old learning, old royal lines. Isaiah mocks it.
- **"Where then are your wise men? Let them tell you… what the LORD of hosts has purposed" (v12).** True wisdom is knowing what God intends. All their learning cannot tell them that. Paul echoes this question about the world's wise men: "Where is the one who is wise?… Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world?" (1 Cor 1:20).
- **"The princes of Memphis are deluded" (v13).** Memphis (Hebrew *Noph*) was Egypt's ancient capital at the head of the Delta, near the great pyramids. "The cornerstones of her tribes" are the leaders who were supposed to hold the nation up. Instead they "have made Egypt stagger."
- **"The LORD has mingled within her a spirit of confusion… as a drunken man staggers in his vomit" (v14).** The confusion is God's judgment. He mixes it into Egypt like a drink. The image is deliberately ugly: the great empire reeling like a drunk. Compare 29:9–10.
- **"Head or tail, palm branch or reed" (v15).** These are pairs meaning everyone, high and low, as in 9:14–15. Palm branch (the high top of a tree) and reed (the low marsh plant). No one in Egypt can do anything.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the whole first half as God humbling an Egypt that had been a false support for his people. All three see the drying Nile as striking at Egypt's very life and religion. JFB rejects dating the civil war as late as Psammetichus and places it earlier, around the Cushite takeover. Guzik draws out v12: wisdom is not having facts and strategies but knowing what the Lord has purposed. The Tyndale notes, Henry and Guzik all link the downfall of Egypt's wise men to Paul's words in 1 Corinthians 1.

#### Egypt, Assyria, Israel Blessed (vv16–25)
- **Five times "in that day" (vv16, 18, 19, 23, 24).** Each step moves further from judgment toward grace: terror, conversion of some cities, worship, reconciliation of enemies, and finally a shared blessing.
- **"The Egyptians will be like women, and tremble with fear" (v16).** In the ancient world this was a common way to describe soldiers losing their nerve (Jer 51:30; Nah 3:13). The Tyndale notes explain that "the hand that the LORD of hosts shakes over them" is a threatening raised hand, as in 10:32.
- **"The land of Judah will become a terror to the Egyptians" (v17).** A reversal of history. Little Judah, so often tempted to run to Egypt, now frightens Egypt. Not because of Judah's army but "because of the purpose that the LORD of hosts has purposed." The terror is the first step to turning (compare Jonah 3:5).
- **"Five cities… that speak the language of Canaan and swear allegiance to the LORD of hosts" (v18).** "The language of Canaan" is Hebrew, the language of God's people. "Swear allegiance" means bind themselves to the Lord by oath, as covenant people do. "Five" may be a round number for a small but significant group (the Tyndale notes compare 17:6; 30:17). Henry: "Five cities in Egypt shall speak this language."
- **"One of these will be called the City of Destruction" (v18).** Here there is a textual question. Most Hebrew manuscripts read *'ir ha-heres*, "city of destruction." The Dead Sea Isaiah Scroll and some other manuscripts read *'ir ha-cheres*, "city of the sun," a difference of one similar-looking letter. "City of the Sun" would be Heliopolis (biblical On), the great center of sun worship near modern Cairo, where Joseph's father-in-law was a priest (Gen 41:45). The ESV keeps "Destruction" and notes the alternative. Guzik prefers "City of the Sun." On either reading, a city devoted to a false god, or a city marked for destruction, becomes a city of the Lord.
- **"An altar to the LORD in the midst of the land of Egypt, and a pillar to the LORD at its border" (v19).** A pillar (*matsevah*) was a standing stone set up as a memorial or boundary marker, as Jacob and Joshua did (Gen 28:18; Josh 24:26–27). Israel was forbidden pagan pillars (Deut 16:22), but here the stone witnesses to the Lord. Some ancient Jews took this verse literally. Jews in Egypt built a temple on the island of Elephantine around the 500s–400s BC, and around 160 BC the exiled priest Onias IV built a temple at Leontopolis in the Heliopolis district. The historian Josephus says Onias cited this very verse to justify it. The Tyndale notes mention these possibilities. JFB argues the altar was a memorial, not for sacrifice, since Jerusalem was the only lawful place of sacrifice.
- **"It will be a sign and a witness" (v20).** The altar marks Egypt as belonging to the Lord.
- **"When they cry to the LORD because of oppressors, he will send them a savior and defender, and deliver them" (v20).** This is the language of the judges: Israel cried out, and God raised up a savior (Judg 3:9, 15). It is also the language of the exodus: Israel "cried out… and God heard" (Exod 2:23–25). Now Egypt, the old oppressor, is the one crying, and God answers it the same way. Henry sees here "the Saviour and the great one," whom God would send with good news, that is, Christ.
- **"The LORD will make himself known to the Egyptians, and the Egyptians will know the LORD" (v21).** In the exodus, God's goal was that "the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD" (Exod 7:5; 14:18), but through judgment. Now they know him in worship, "with sacrifice and offering," making vows and keeping them.
- **"The LORD will strike Egypt, striking and healing" (v22).** The verb "strike" (*nagaf*) is the word used for the plague on Egypt's firstborn (Exod 12:23), as the Tyndale notes point out. This time the striking leads to healing. The same God who wounds heals (Deut 32:39; Hos 6:1).
- **"A highway from Egypt to Assyria… the Egyptians will worship with the Assyrians" (v23).** Egypt and Assyria were the two great powers that fought over Israel's land for centuries, with Israel crushed between them. The coastal highway was the road armies marched down. Now it becomes a pilgrims' road, and the two great enemies worship together. Isaiah has already used the highway image for God's people coming home (11:16; compare 35:8; 40:3).
- **"Israel will be the third with Egypt and Assyria, a blessing in the midst of the earth" (v24).** God promised Abraham, "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). Israel stands "in the midst," between the two empires, not as a victim but as the channel of blessing.
- **"Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance" (v25).** These are Israel's own titles. "My people" is what God called Israel (Exod 3:7; Hos 1:10). "The work of my hands" is how God speaks of Israel elsewhere (60:21; 64:8). "My inheritance" is Israel's special title (Deut 9:29). The Tyndale notes observe that titles usually reserved for Israel are given to foreign nations. Israel does not lose its place. It keeps "my inheritance." But the nations are brought into the same family. This is one of the most remarkable verses in the Old Testament.
- **How was it fulfilled?** Christians read this in several ways. Some see a historical fulfillment in the large Jewish community of Egypt, especially in Alexandria, where the Hebrew Scriptures were translated into Greek (the Septuagint) in the 200s BC. Most see the main fulfillment in the gospel: Egyptians heard Peter at Pentecost (Acts 2:10), Alexandria became one of the great centers of early Christianity, and Egypt's Coptic church is one of the oldest in the world. Some, especially premillennial readers, also expect a future, literal fulfillment when Christ reigns. Guzik mentions all three. Henry sees fulfillment partly through Jewish refugees in Egypt and more fully through preachers of the gospel planting churches there.
- **Commentators:** Henry says a "spiritual altar, a gospel church, in the midst of the land of Egypt, is as acceptable to God" as the altar at Jerusalem, and that "they shall all be blessed of the Lord." JFB favors the Hebrew "city of destruction" and suggests it reproaches Onias's temple at Leontopolis, while noting the "city of the sun" reading points to Heliopolis. JFB calls the highway "a type of gospel times to come," and says "my people" means Egypt's "entire admission to religious privileges." Guzik links the altar to Onias's temple as a possibility, mentions Athanasius's claim in the 300s that idolatry was dying out in Egypt, and also suggests a future millennial fulfillment. That last view is held by some but not all evangelicals.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter moves from the Lord riding a cloud in judgment to the Lord calling Egypt "my people." The way that happens is the "savior and defender" God sends. Jesus himself was taken to Egypt as a child and called back out of it (Matt 2:13–15), retracing Israel's exodus. In him the wall between Israel and the nations is broken down, "that he might create in himself one new man in place of the two" (Eph 2:14–16). Old enemies on the same highway, worshiping the same God: that is the church, gathered from every nation, with Israel's Messiah at its center.
