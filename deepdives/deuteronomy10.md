*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, still in his second sermon (chapters 5–26). Chapter 9 retold the golden calf at Mount Sinai (Horeb): Moses smashed the tablets of the covenant, ground the calf to dust, and lay on his face forty days pleading for the people's lives. Chapter 10 finishes that story with grace. God gives the tablets again, keeps the priesthood going after Aaron, and sends the people on toward the land. Then Moses steps back from history and asks the great question of the whole sermon: "what does the LORD your God require of you?" His answer, in vv12–22, is one of the finest summaries of Old Testament faith, and it reaches its peak in a command no one could keep by effort alone: "Circumcise… your heart."

#### New Tablets of Stone (vv1–11)
- **"Cut for yourself two tablets of stone like the first" (v1).** The first tablets were made and cut by God himself (Exod 32:16). This time Moses does the cutting, and God does the writing (v2). The covenant Israel broke is not cancelled. It is renewed. Exodus 34:1–4 tells the same scene, and it is there that God proclaims his name: "merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love" (Exod 34:6).
- **"And make an ark of wood… you shall put them in the ark" (vv1–2).** The ark (*aron*, simply "chest" or "box") was the gold-covered chest that would sit in the Most Holy Place, with the atonement cover and cherubim above it (Exod 25:10–22). Exodus records that Bezalel built it later (Exod 37:1). Moses tells the story compressed, as if from his own hands, since he gave the orders. Some scholars think he first made a simple wooden chest to carry the tablets. Either way the point is the same: the law is kept safe at the center of Israel's worship, under the place where atoning blood would be sprinkled (Lev 16:14–15).
- **"Acacia wood" (v3).** Acacia is one of the few trees large enough for timber in the Sinai and Negev deserts. Its wood is hard and resists rot and insects. Older English Bibles call it "shittim wood," from the Hebrew *shittim*.
- **"In the same writing as before, the Ten Commandments" (v4).** Literally "the ten words" (*aseret ha-devarim*). God's word was not changed by Israel's failure. The same law, written again by the same hand, is given back to the people who broke it.
- **"And there they are, as the LORD commanded me" (v5).** The tablets were still there when Deuteronomy was written. By Solomon's day the ark held only the two tablets (1 Kgs 8:9), though Hebrews mentions the pot of manna and Aaron's staff as well (Heb 9:4). The ark disappeared when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC, and no one knows its fate.
- **"The people of Israel journeyed from Beeroth Bene-jaakan to Moserah. There Aaron died" (v6).** The ESV puts vv6–9 in parentheses because Moses' speech breaks off for a short travel note. It may be a comment added by whoever compiled the book, as with the notes in 2:10–12 and 2:20–23. "Beeroth Bene-jaakan" means "the wells of the sons of Jaakan." Numbers 33:30–33 lists the same places in a different order, and says Aaron died on Mount Hor (Num 20:22–29; 33:38). Moserah was probably the area around Mount Hor, though its location is unknown, and the two lists may record different journeys through the same region over the forty years.
- **Why mention Aaron's death here?** It answers a question chapter 9 raised. Aaron made the calf and God was "ready to destroy him" (9:20). Yet Aaron lived on, served as high priest for nearly forty years, and when he died, **"his son Eleazar ministered as priest in his place."** The priesthood was not cancelled by Aaron's sin. Moses' intercession was heard, and God's provision for atonement continued.
- **"At that time the LORD set apart the tribe of Levi" (v8).** "At that time" points back to the golden calf. When Moses called, "Who is on the LORD's side? Come to me," the Levites rallied to him (Exod 32:26–29). Out of the worst day came a tribe set apart for God's service. Their three tasks were to **carry the ark**, to **stand before the LORD to minister to him**, an idiom for serving as a royal attendant serves a king, and to **bless in his name**, which the priests did with the words of Numbers 6:24–26.
- **"Levi has no portion or inheritance with his brothers. The LORD is his inheritance" (v9).** Every other tribe got territory. Levi got God. The Levites would live in forty-eight towns scattered through the land (Num 35:1–8) and be supported by the tithes and offerings (18:1–2). The Psalms take up the phrase for every believer: "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup" (Ps 16:5; also Ps 73:26).
- **"The LORD listened to me that time also. The LORD was unwilling to destroy you" (v10).** The third mention of the forty days (9:18, 25). God heard Moses.
- **"Arise, go on your journey at the head of the people" (v11).** Forgiveness is not the end of the story but a fresh start on the road. The promise to the fathers still stands. The Hebrew for "on your journey" is literally "for the setting out," the word used for breaking camp.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees the ark as a picture of the gospel: "Christ is the ark in which now our salvation is kept safely." On v4 he says, "The written word is as truly the word of God" as the spoken word. On Levi: "A settled ministry is a great blessing to a people." He also notes that God, having accepted Moses as intercessor, kept him on as leader. JFB admits that the sudden travel note in vv6–9 is puzzling and suggests that the station list in Numbers 33 may describe a different journey. It mentions that Josephus thought each tablet held five commandments. JFB has no comment on Levi. Guzik reads the new tablets as God's way of bringing back the banished, a second chance for a failing people. He also sees the priests' three tasks, making atonement, interceding and blessing, fulfilled in Jesus, and he applies v11 to believers today: once restored, keep moving forward rather than staying where the failure happened.

#### Circumcise Your Heart (vv12–22)
- **"And now, Israel, what does the LORD your God require of you" (v12).** "And now" turns from history to response. After all God has done, and all Israel has failed to do, what is left? The question echoes in Micah 6:8: "what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?"
- **"To fear… to walk… to love… to serve… and to keep" (vv12–13).** Five verbs, one life. To **fear** is reverent awe that takes God seriously. To **walk in all his ways** is to live along the path he sets. To **love** him is loyal, whole-hearted devotion (6:5). To **serve** is worship and work offered to him. To **keep** the commands means to guard and obey them. Fear and love are not opposites here. They sit side by side in the same sentence.
- **"For your good" (v13).** The commands are not a tax God collects from his people. They are a gift for their benefit, the way a loving father's instructions are for the child's good.
- **"To the LORD your God belong heaven and the heaven of heavens, the earth with all that is in it" (v14).** "The heaven of heavens" is a Hebrew way of saying the highest heavens. God owns everything. He needs nothing from Israel (Ps 50:10–12).
- **"Yet the LORD set his heart in love on your fathers and chose their offspring" (v15).** "Yet" is the marvel. The owner of the universe set his affection on one family. The verb *chashaq* is the same strong word for attachment as in 7:7. It is the mystery of election: God's choice explained by his love, and his love by nothing outside himself.
- **"Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart, and be no longer stubborn" (v16).** Circumcision was the sign of the covenant given to Abraham (Gen 17:10–14). Every Israelite male bore it. Moses now says the outward sign must match an inward reality. An "uncircumcised" heart is one closed to God, covered over and unresponsive, and "stubborn" is again "stiff-necked" (9:6, 13). Jeremiah repeats the call (Jer 4:4; 9:25–26), and Stephen charged his hearers with being "uncircumcised in heart and ears" (Acts 7:51). Paul takes it up directly: "circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter" (Rom 2:29).
- **Who can do this?** Here Israel is told to circumcise their own hearts. Later in the book Moses promises that God will do it for them: "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your offspring, so that you will love the LORD your God with all your heart" (30:6). The command shows what is needed, and the promise shows who must do it. The New Testament says this happens in Christ, "a circumcision made without hands" (Col 2:11).
- **"God of gods and Lord of lords, the great, the mighty, and the awesome God" (v17).** This is a Hebrew way of saying "the supreme God, the supreme Lord." It doesn't grant that other gods are real. It means no power in heaven or earth is above him. **"Mighty"** (*gibbor*) is the word for a warrior hero. Revelation gives the title "Lord of lords" to the Lamb (Rev 17:14; 19:16).
- **"Who is not partial and takes no bribe" (v17).** "Not partial" is literally "does not lift up faces," an idiom for showing favoritism by looking at who someone is. Ancient gods could be bought off with offerings. Israel's God cannot. Moses had told Israel's judges the same thing (1:17; 16:19). Paul quotes the principle to show that Jew and Gentile stand equally before God's judgment (Rom 2:11), and Peter learned it at Cornelius's house (Acts 10:34).
- **"He executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner" (v18).** Right after the grandest titles in the chapter comes the lowest rank of society. Orphans, widows and resident foreigners had no land and no male protector, and they were the easiest people to cheat in a court. The God of gods takes their side personally. A **sojourner** (*ger*) was a foreigner living long-term in Israel, without the land rights of a citizen. God gives them "food and clothing," the very things he gave Israel in the wilderness (8:3–4).
- **"Love the sojourner, therefore, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (v19).** Israel's ethics grow out of Israel's story. They know what it is to be foreigners and to be oppressed. Leviticus 19:34 says, "you shall love him as yourself." Deuteronomy builds this into its laws: gleanings, tithes and festivals all include "the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow" (14:29; 16:11; 24:19–21). God's people are to be like God.
- **"Serve him and hold fast to him" (v20).** "Hold fast" (*davaq*) is the word used in Genesis 2:24 for a man who "shall hold fast to his wife." It means clinging, loyal attachment.
- **"He is your praise" (v21).** God is the one Israel praises, and he is also Israel's glory, the thing they can boast in (Jer 9:23–24). After chapter 9 stripped away every other ground for boasting, this is the one that remains.
- **"Your fathers went down to Egypt seventy persons, and now… as numerous as the stars of heaven" (v22).** Seventy is the number in Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5. God promised Abraham descendants like the stars (Gen 15:5), and Moses says it has happened. It is proof that God keeps his word, and so a reason to trust him for the land as well.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says fearing God means we must "adore his majesty, acknowledge his authority, stand in awe of his power." On v16: "Cast away from you all corrupt affections and inclinations, which hinder you from fearing and loving God." On the stranger: "We must have a tender concern for all that share with us in the human nature." JFB says Moses here teaches "the true and spiritual meaning of that rite" of circumcision and links it to Romans 2 and to baptism as "the answer of a good conscience toward God" (1 Pet 3:21). It has no separate comments on vv17–22. Guzik treats the five verbs of vv12–13 as the whole of what God asks, adds that poor obedience usually comes from poor appreciation of what God has done, and notes that "your praise" means both the one Israel praises and the one who makes his people worth praising. Guzik and the Tyndale notes agree that "God of gods" doesn't imply the reality of other gods.

#### Pointing to Christ
The tablets were broken by sinners, given again by grace, and laid in the ark under the place where blood was sprinkled for atonement. That is the shape of the gospel: God's law is not lowered, but it is kept safe under the mercy seat. Jesus is the one who kept that law perfectly and whose blood covers those who broke it (Rom 3:25, where "propitiation" is the Greek word for that mercy seat). And the command to circumcise the heart, which no one can keep alone, becomes God's own work in Christ: "In him also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands… by the circumcision of Christ" (Col 2:11). He is also the God who "loves the sojourner," who welcomed outsiders and invites them in. As Levi's inheritance was the LORD, so the believer's portion is Christ himself.
