*Setting:* About 594 BC, during the first years of Zedekiah's reign. Since 597 BC thousands of Judeans had been living in southern Babylonia: King Jeconiah, the queen mother, the officials, the craftsmen and the priests, along with Ezekiel. Many of them had been settled in farming communities along the canals near Nippur, such as Tel-abib by the Chebar canal (Ezek 3:15). Prophets among them, just like Hananiah in Jerusalem (ch 28), were promising that it would all be over soon. So Jeremiah wrote them a letter. It contains one of the most loved verses in the Bible, "I know the plans I have for you" (v11). Read in context, it is not a promise of quick escape. It is a call to settle down for a long stay, pray for the enemy's city, and trust God's timing. The letter provoked an angry reply from Babylon, and the chapter ends with that second exchange.

#### Jeremiah's Letter to the Exiles (vv1–23)
- **"The words of the letter that Jeremiah the prophet sent" (v1).** Prophets usually spoke, but a written word from God carried the same authority. This is one of the first prophetic letters in Scripture. The New Testament letters stand in the same tradition: God's word sent in writing to scattered believers. **"The surviving elders."** "Surviving" may hint that some of the leaders had died, or had been executed after unrest in Babylon (see vv21–22).
- **"King Jeconiah and the queen mother, the eunuchs, the officials… the craftsmen, and the metal workers" (v2).** The same list as 24:1 and 2 Kings 24:12–16. "Eunuchs" may mean court officials in general, since the word came to be used that way.
- **"Elasah the son of Shaphan and Gemariah the son of Hilkiah" (v3).** Elasah was a brother of Ahikam, who had saved Jeremiah's life (26:24). Shaphan's family were Jeremiah's steady friends. Gemariah's father Hilkiah may have been the high priest who found the Book of the Law in Josiah's day (2 Kgs 22:8), but the name was common. **"Whom Zedekiah king of Judah sent to Babylon to Nebuchadnezzar."** An official embassy, perhaps to reassure Babylon of Judah's loyalty after the plotting of chapter 27. Jeremiah's letter went along in the diplomatic pouch.
- **"To all the exiles whom I have sent into exile" (v4).** Nebuchadnezzar deported them, but God says, "*I* have sent" them (also vv7, 14, 20). Their exile was not an accident or a defeat of their God. It was his discipline.
- **"Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce" (v5).** Houses and gardens are not built for a two-year stay. A garden takes years to bear fruit. This echoes the words of Jeremiah's call, "to build and to plant" (1:10). And it fits what archaeologists have found. Clay tablets from a Babylonian village called *Al-Yahudu*, "Judah-town," dating from 572 BC onward, show Judean families farming, leasing land, paying taxes and passing property to their children.
- **"Take wives and have sons and daughters… multiply there, and do not decrease" (v6).** The language of Genesis 1:28 and of Israel in Egypt, where "the people of Israel were fruitful… and multiplied" (Exod 1:7). Exile was not to be a slow death. The people were to grow so they could one day return.
- **"Seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf" (v7).** This is astonishing. Babylon was the city that had burned their towns and carried them off. A psalm from the exile wishes Babylon's babies dashed against rocks (Ps 137:8–9). God tells them instead to pray for it. "Welfare" is *shalom*, peace and wholeness and prosperity, and it appears three times in the verse: seek its *shalom*, for in its *shalom* will be your *shalom*. The same principle runs through the New Testament: pray "for kings and all who are in high positions, that we may lead a peaceful and quiet life" (1 Tim 2:1–2), and "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matt 5:44).
- **"Do not let your prophets and your diviners who are among you deceive you" (v8).** There were Hananiahs in Babylon too. **"Do not listen to the dreams that they dream."** Literally "your dreams that you cause to be dreamed." It may mean the people themselves encouraged the dreamers by wanting good news.
- **"When seventy years are completed for Babylon" (v10).** The number from 25:11–12. Notice "for Babylon." The seventy years are Babylon's appointed time of power, after which God would bring it down. **"I will visit you."** *Paqad* again, God attending to his people in mercy (27:22). **"I will fulfill to you my promise."** Literally "my good word."
- **"For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD" (v11).** "Plans" is *machashabot*, thoughts, intentions or purposes. The exiles' prophets had their plans: home in two years. God had his own, and they were better and slower. **"Plans for welfare and not for evil."** *Shalom* again, the same word as v7. God's purpose for them is good, even in Babylon.
- **"To give you a future and a hope" (v11).** Literally "an end and a hope," that is, a hopeful outcome. The story will not end in exile.
- **Reading v11 rightly.** The "you" throughout this letter is plural, and the promise was given to a community in exile facing seventy years. Most of the adults who first heard it would die in Babylon. So the verse does not promise any individual an easy life or quick success. But the God who spoke it has not changed. His plans for his people are for their good, even through long seasons of hardship, and Christians rightly take comfort from it in that sense (Rom 8:28).
- **"Then you will call upon me and come and pray to me, and I will hear you" (v12).** Exile would drive them to prayer. Daniel is the great example. He read "in the books… according to the word of the LORD to Jeremiah" that seventy years must pass, and he turned to God "by prayer and pleas for mercy with fasting" (Dan 9:2–3).
- **"You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart" (v13).** This echoes Moses' words to Israel about a future exile: "From there you will seek the LORD your God and you will find him, if you search after him with all your heart and with all your soul" (Deut 4:29). The temple was gone, but God could still be found, even in Babylon.
- **"I will restore your fortunes" (v14).** In Hebrew, *shabti et-shebutkem*, a phrase that plays on sounds. It is often rendered "turn your captivity," but it means a full reversal of fortune. It recurs throughout chapters 30–33 (30:3, 18; 31:23; 32:44; 33:7). **"Gather you from all the nations."** The scattering would be undone.
- **"Because you have said, 'The LORD has raised up prophets for us in Babylon'" (v15).** The exiles were proud of their own prophets, who promised that Jerusalem would stand and they would soon return. Verses 16–20 answer that hope.
- **"Concerning the king who sits on the throne of David… your kinsmen who did not go out with you" (v16).** Those still in Jerusalem were not the lucky ones. Zedekiah and the city were headed for destruction. Verses 16–20 do not appear in the Greek version. Some think they were added from Jeremiah's other sermons, while others think the Greek dropped them because v15 and v21 begin the same way.
- **"Like vile figs that are so rotten they cannot be eaten" (v17).** The picture of chapter 24 again. The good figs were the exiles, and the rotten ones were Zedekiah's Jerusalem. "Vile" is a rare word, meaning bursting or disgusting.
- **"Ahab the son of Kolaiah and Zedekiah the son of Maaseiah" (v21).** Two prophets in Babylon, otherwise unknown. Nothing in the text links this Zedekiah to the king. If his father was the same Maaseiah as the father of Zephaniah the priest (21:1; 29:25), he was a brother of the man Shemaiah wrote to in v25, though that is uncertain. **"He shall strike them down before your eyes."** Prophets who stirred up hope of a quick return were a political threat to Babylon, and Nebuchadnezzar would treat them as rebels.
- **"The LORD make you like Zedekiah and Ahab, whom the king of Babylon roasted in the fire" (v22).** Babylon used burning as a punishment. The laws of Hammurabi prescribe it for certain crimes, and Daniel's friends faced the furnace (Dan 3:6). The verse carries a grim wordplay: a "curse" is *qelalah*, "roasted" is *qalam*, and the father's name is Kolaiah. Their names would become a curse.
- **"They have done an outrageous thing in Israel, they have committed adultery with their neighbors' wives" (v23).** "Outrageous thing" is *nebalah*, a word for shocking moral folly, used of the rape of Dinah (Gen 34:7) and of Tamar (2 Sam 13:12). False teaching and immoral living went together (23:14). **"I am the one who knows, and I am witness."** No one else may have known about the adultery, but God did.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "The word of God written is as truly given by inspiration of God as his word spoken was," and that the false hopes "were dreams of their own bespeaking." On v11 he says, "God does nothing by halves." JFB calls the command to pray for Babylon "a foretaste of gospel love towards enemies," and notes the sound-play in "turn your captivity." He also calls the fire "a Chaldean punishment." Guzik, drawing on Feinberg, notes that commanding prayer for one's captors is almost unknown in ancient literature. He cites Feinberg's reading of "a future and a hope" as "a hopeful end." He stresses, following Spurgeon, that God's thoughts are directed *toward* his people, not only *about* them. He also cites Ryken that burning was a punishment for treason in Babylon.

#### Shemaiah's False Prophecy (vv24–32)
- **"To Shemaiah of Nehelam you shall say" (v24).** Shemaiah was an exile in Babylon and a self-appointed prophet. "Nehelam" is unknown as a place. It may be a family name, or possibly a wordplay on the Hebrew for "dream," *chalam*.
- **"You have sent letters in your name to all the people who are in Jerusalem, and to Zephaniah" (v25).** Note the contrast: Jeremiah wrote in the LORD's name (v4), and Shemaiah wrote "in your name," his own. Zephaniah son of Maaseiah was the "second priest," the man Zedekiah sent to Jeremiah in 21:1 and 37:3.
- **"The LORD has made you priest instead of Jehoiada the priest" (v26).** Zephaniah now held Pashhur's old job as temple overseer (20:1). Jehoiada may be the famous priest who guarded the temple and organized its watch in Joash's day (2 Kgs 11:4–18), held up as a model, or an otherwise unknown recent officeholder. **"Over every madman who prophesies."** Prophets were sometimes mocked as madmen (2 Kgs 9:11; Hos 9:7). **"The stocks and neck irons."** The stocks are the *mahpeketh* of 20:2, and "neck irons" is a collar. Shemaiah wanted Jeremiah locked up again.
- **"Why have you not rebuked Jeremiah of Anathoth…?" (v27).** Naming his small hometown may carry a sneer, as "Jesus of Nazareth" could (John 1:46).
- **"Your exile will be long; build houses" (v28).** Shemaiah quotes Jeremiah's letter accurately. His complaint was not that Jeremiah had been misquoted, but that he had told the truth.
- **"Zephaniah the priest read this letter in the hearing of Jeremiah the prophet" (v29).** Zephaniah did not arrest Jeremiah. He showed him the letter. Whether as a warning or out of respect, his act protected Jeremiah. He appears in the book as a man open to Jeremiah, though too weak to act on what he heard.
- **"Because Shemaiah had prophesied to you when I did not send him, and has made you trust in a lie" (v31).** The same charge as against Hananiah (28:15), word for word.
- **"He shall not have anyone living among this people, and he shall not see the good that I will do" (v32).** Shemaiah's punishment fits his crime. He opposed God's long-range plan of good for the exiles, so neither he nor his family line would live to see it. **"For he has spoken rebellion against the LORD."** The phrase from Deuteronomy 13:5 again, as in 28:16.
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that "it is kindness to our friends to let them know their foes," which is what Zephaniah did. He also notes that lying was bad, and lying to God's people to delude them into false hope was worse. JFB sees a possible link between "Nehelamite" and "dreamer," which is possible but uncertain. He compares the sneer in "Jeremiah of Anathoth" to "Jesus of Nazareth." Guzik, citing Kidner, wryly notes that the stocks had never silenced Jeremiah before. He draws the lesson that those who stay faithful through hardship are the ones who live to see God's later goodness.

#### Pointing to Christ
God told the exiles to make a home in a foreign city, pray for its peace, and wait in hope for a homecoming they might not live to see. That is how the New Testament describes the church. Peter writes "to those who are elect exiles of the dispersion" (1 Pet 1:1) and tells them to "keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable" (1 Pet 2:11–12). Believers are told that "here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (Heb 13:14). The plans of welfare and hope that God spoke to the exiles find their deepest yes in Christ (2 Cor 1:20). He is the one through whom the scattered are gathered (John 11:52) and the one in whom God is found by all who seek him with their whole heart. He also taught his followers to do what this letter commands, to pray for those who wrong them, and he did it himself from the cross: "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34).
