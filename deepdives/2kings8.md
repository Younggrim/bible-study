*Setting:* The chapter covers roughly 848–841 BC and moves fast. It opens with a scene from Elisha's ministry that may come from somewhat earlier, then carries the story into Damascus, where God's word to Elijah years before, "anoint Hazael to be king over Syria" (1 Kgs 19:15), is finally carried out. Then it turns south to Judah, where the alliance Jehoshaphat made with Ahab's house through marriage (2 Chr 18:1) is bearing poisoned fruit. Two kings of Judah in a row walk "in the way of the house of Ahab." By the chapter's end, every piece is in place for the bloody overthrow of Ahab's dynasty in chapter 9: Hazael on Syria's throne, Joram of Israel wounded at Jezreel, and Ahaziah of Judah at his side.

#### The Shunammite's Land Restored (vv1–6)
- **"Elisha had said to the woman whose son he had restored to life" (v1).** This is the wealthy woman of Shunem who built a room for Elisha and whose son he raised from the dead (4:8–37). "Had said" points back: the warning came earlier, and the story may have happened before Gehazi's leprosy in chapter 5. The writer of Kings often groups Elisha stories by theme rather than strict order.
- **"The LORD has called for a famine, and it will come upon the land for seven years" (v1).** God "calls" famine like a servant (compare Ps 105:16). Famine was one of the covenant curses on a disobedient Israel (Deut 28:23–24), and Elijah's drought had lasted three and a half years (Jas 5:17). This one is twice as long.
- **"She sojourned in the land of the Philistines seven years" (v2).** The Philistine coastal plain had better rainfall and was not far from Shunem. Like Elimelech and Naomi going to Moab (Ruth 1:1), she leaves the promised land to survive. Unlike them, she goes because a prophet told her to, and she comes back.
- **"She went to appeal to the king for her house and her land" (v3).** "Appeal" is literally "cry out," the word for a plea for justice. While she was gone, her land had been taken over, either by neighbors or by the crown as abandoned property. She has no husband mentioned now (perhaps he had died), and she must go to the king herself.
- **"The king was talking with Gehazi the servant of the man of God" (v4).** This is surprising, since Gehazi was struck with leprosy in 5:27. Either this conversation happened before that, which fits "had said" in v1, or Gehazi is talking with the king from a distance, as lepers could. The text does not explain. The king, probably Joram, wants to hear "all the great things that Elisha has done." He is curious about the prophet even while he will not repent.
- **"Here is the woman, and here is her son whom Elisha restored to life" (v5).** At the very moment Gehazi reaches the story of the raised boy, the woman and the boy walk in. No one arranged it. This is how the Old Testament often shows providence: no miracle, just perfect timing (compare Esth 6:1–3, the sleepless king reading the record of Mordecai).
- **"Restore all that was hers, together with all the produce of the fields" (v6).** The king gives her back her land and the income from seven years of harvests. Compare Ahab, his father, who murdered Naboth to steal a vineyard (1 Kgs 21). The son of Ahab does justice for a widow because of what God had done for her.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the long famine as God's escalating discipline: "If less judgments do not prevail to bring men to repentance, he will send greater and longer." He praises the woman's obedience and the providence that restored her. JFB thinks the Philistine coast was chosen because it was near and less offensive to Israel's king than fleeing to Judah, and explains that a leper could talk to the king from a distance, since ritual uncleanness spread by touch. Guzik raises both explanations for Gehazi's presence and contrasts the king's fairness with Ahab's greed.

#### Hazael Murders Ben-hadad (vv7–15)
- **"Elisha came to Damascus" (v7).** An Israelite prophet walks into the capital of Israel's enemy, and the enemy king treats it as good news. Elisha's fame had spread there since Naaman (ch 5) and the blinded army (6:18–23). He is finishing the second of the three tasks God gave Elijah at Horeb (1 Kgs 19:15–16): anoint Hazael over Syria and Jehu over Israel, and Elisha as prophet.
- **"Inquire of the LORD through him" (v8).** This is a pointed contrast with 1:2, where Ahaziah king of Israel, sick after a fall, sent to ask Baal-zebub, god of Ekron. Israel's own king sent to a foreign god. Syria's king sends to Israel's God.
- **"Forty camels' loads" (v9).** It was customary to bring a gift when consulting a prophet (1 Sam 9:7–8), but this is a royal parade of the goods of Damascus, a major trading city. It was probably displayed in many small loads for show. The text does not say whether Elisha accepted it. **"Your son Ben-hadad"** is a title of respect, as Naaman's servants called him "father" (5:13).
- **"Go, say to him, 'You shall certainly recover,' but the LORD has shown me that he shall certainly die" (v10).** The answer is double: the sickness will not kill him, but he will die. There is a well-known textual wrinkle here. The written Hebrew text has *lo'* spelled "not," which would read "say, 'You shall not recover.'" The traditional reading note (the *qere*) reads the same-sounding *lo* as "to him": "say to him, 'You shall recover.'" Most translations, including the ESV, follow the reading note. Either way Elisha knows the truth, and v14 shows Hazael passing on only the part he liked.
- **"He fixed his gaze and stared at him, until he was embarrassed" (v11).** Literally, Elisha "made his face stand" until Hazael felt shame. The prophet looks into the man and sees what he is about to do. Hazael's discomfort suggests the thought was already there.
- **"And the man of God wept" (v11).** Elisha weeps for his own people. He sees fortresses burned, young men killed, children dashed in pieces and pregnant women ripped open (v12), the worst atrocities of ancient war (compare Hos 13:16; Amos 1:3, 13, where Amos condemns Damascus for exactly this cruelty toward Gilead). Hazael did oppress Israel for decades (10:32–33; 12:17–18; 13:3, 22). Elisha is not a cold announcer of judgment. Like Jeremiah (Jer 9:1) and Jesus over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41–44), he weeps over what he must foretell.
- **"What is your servant, who is but a dog, that he should do this great thing?" (v13).** A "dog" was a scavenger, a term of self-contempt (1 Sam 24:14; 2 Sam 9:8). Hazael's words can be read two ways. Some hear genuine shock: "I could never do such things." Most hear false modesty: "How could a nobody like me rise so high?" He seems more interested in the "great thing" than horrified by it. An inscription of Shalmaneser III of Assyria calls Hazael "son of a nobody" who "seized the throne," confirming he was not of the royal line.
- **"The LORD has shown me that you are to be king over Syria" (v13).** Elisha does not tell him to kill anyone. God's foreknowledge is not God's permission. Hazael will choose his own method.
- **"He took the bed cloth and dipped it in water and spread it over his face, till he died" (v15).** The word for the cloth is rare and probably means a thick coverlet or netting. Soaked, it would smother a sick man and leave no marks. A few older commentators suggested an innocent accident, a wet cloth to cool a fever, but the plain sense, and the way Hazael "became king in his place" the next day, points to murder. Assyrian records confirm that Hazael replaced the king of Damascus around 842 BC.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Affliction brings those to God who in their prosperity had made light of him." On the tears he writes, "The more foresight men have the more grief they are liable to," and he presses Hazael's blindness to himself: "Thou knowest not what thou wilt do when thou comest to be king." JFB says "there was no contradiction" in the double message and that Hazael reported only what suited him. Unusually, JFB doubts the murder and allows that Hazael may have been cooling a fevered king with "an honest intention." Guzik takes the murder as plain and stresses Hazael's full responsibility. Following Spurgeon, he says people are often blind to what they are capable of, and that Hazael should have taken the warning as a call to look at his own heart.

#### Jehoram Reigns in Judah (vv16–24)
- **"In the fifth year of Joram the son of Ahab… Jehoram the son of Jehoshaphat… began to reign" (v16).** Now there are two kings named Jehoram (short form Joram) ruling at the same time, one in Israel and one in Judah, and they are brothers-in-law. The ESV keeps both spellings. The phrase "when Jehoshaphat was king of Judah" suggests that Jehoram reigned alongside his father for a few years before ruling alone. Co-regencies like this were common and explain many of the overlapping dates in Kings.
- **"He reigned eight years in Jerusalem" (v17).** About 848–841 BC as sole king. Chronicles fills in the dark details: he murdered all six of his brothers when he took the throne (2 Chr 21:2–4).
- **"For the daughter of Ahab was his wife" (v18).** This is Athaliah. Jehoshaphat, a good king, arranged the marriage to seal his alliance with Ahab (2 Chr 18:1). It looked like peace between the kingdoms. In fact it brought Baal worship into Jerusalem (11:18) and nearly wiped out David's line (11:1). Paul's warning against being "unequally yoked" (2 Cor 6:14) has few clearer Old Testament examples.
- **"Yet the LORD was not willing to destroy Judah, for the sake of David his servant" (v19).** Here is the hinge of the whole section. Jehoram deserved to have his dynasty ended like Ahab's. God spares it, not for Jehoram's sake but because of his promise to David (2 Sam 7:12–16). **"A lamp to him and to his sons forever"** pictures a lamp kept burning in a house as a sign someone still lives there (1 Kgs 11:36; 15:4; Ps 132:17). David's line flickers here, but it does not go out.
- **"In his days Edom revolted from the rule of Judah" (v20).** Edom, south and east of the Dead Sea, had been under Judah's control since David (2 Sam 8:14) and had no king of its own in Jehoshaphat's day (1 Kgs 22:47). Now it breaks away. This fulfills, in part, Isaac's word to Esau that he would one day "break his yoke" from his brother's neck (Gen 27:40).
- **"Joram passed over to Zair… and his army fled home" (v21).** Zair's location is unknown, probably near Edom's border. The Hebrew is compressed and hard to follow. The likeliest reading is that Edom surrounded Jehoram's forces, he broke out at night with his chariot commanders, and his army scattered home. He escaped, but he lost Edom "to this day" (v22).
- **"Then Libnah revolted at the same time" (v22).** Libnah was a town in the lowlands on Judah's western edge, near Philistine territory. Losing it shows Judah crumbling on both sides. Chronicles adds a reason: "because he had forsaken the LORD" (2 Chr 21:10).
- **"Joram slept with his fathers and was buried with his fathers in the city of David" (v24).** Kings is brief. Chronicles adds that Elijah sent him a letter of judgment, that Philistines and Arabs raided Jerusalem and carried off his sons, and that he died of a terrible bowel disease, "with no one's regret," and was not buried in the royal tombs (2 Chr 21:12–20). The two accounts fit together: buried in David's city, but not in the kings' tombs.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those that are ill-matched are already half-ruined," and of Jehoram's short life, "Bloody and deceitful men shall not live out half their days." JFB names Athaliah as the influence behind his Baal worship and sees the revolt of Edom as a national chastisement. Guzik makes the same point as Henry: the marriage meant to strengthen Judah weakened it. Quoting Wiseman, he explains the lamp as a sign of life and of the covenant kept.

#### Ahaziah Reigns in Judah (vv25–29)
- **"In the twelfth year of Joram the son of Ahab" (v25).** That is 841 BC, one of the pivotal years of the period. By its end both Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah will be dead, Hazael will be firmly on Syria's throne, and Shalmaneser III of Assyria will have marched through and taken tribute from Jehu.
- **"Ahaziah was twenty-two years old" (v26).** The Hebrew text of 2 Chronicles 22:2 says forty-two, which cannot be right, since that would make him older than his father (who died at forty, v17). Some Greek and Syriac manuscripts of Chronicles read twenty-two, and most scholars treat "forty-two" as a copyist's slip with similar-looking numbers.
- **"His mother's name was Athaliah; she was a granddaughter of Omri" (v26).** The Hebrew says "daughter of Omri," which in Hebrew can mean any female descendant. She was Ahab's daughter (v18) and Omri's granddaughter, and the ESV makes that clear. Naming Omri, the founder of the dynasty, underlines which house she belongs to.
- **"He was son-in-law to the house of Ahab" (v27).** The Hebrew word means a relative by marriage. Chronicles says plainly, "his mother was his counselor in doing wickedly" (2 Chr 22:3). He reigns only one year.
- **"To make war against Hazael king of Syria at Ramoth-gilead" (v28).** Ramoth-gilead, east of the Jordan, was the fortress Ahab died fighting for, with Jehoshaphat beside him (1 Kgs 22). Now their sons and grandson repeat the same alliance at the same place against the new Syrian king Elisha had just foretold. **"The Syrians wounded Joram"**, and he goes back to his palace at Jezreel to recover (v29).
- **"Ahaziah… went down to see Joram… in Jezreel, because he was sick" (v29).** A family visit puts the kings of Israel and Judah in the same town at the same moment. Jezreel is where Naboth's vineyard was, the scene of Ahab's great crime (1 Kgs 21). The reader who remembers Elijah's prophecy (1 Kgs 21:19–24) knows what is coming. Chapter 9 begins at once.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns, "Those who partake with sinners in their sins must expect to partake with them in their plagues." JFB is silent on Ahaziah's section. Guzik sees Ahaziah's single year as a sign of shrinking blessing, and follows Clarke and most scholars in treating the "forty-two" of Chronicles as a copyist's error.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is full of kings: a sick king of Syria murdered by his officer, a king of Israel who hears the stories of God's power but never bows to him, and two kings of Judah who throw their lives away by joining Ahab's house. Only one line holds: "Yet the LORD was not willing to destroy Judah, for the sake of David his servant." The lamp promised to David kept burning through kings who deserved to have it snuffed out, until the Son of David came, the one who is himself "the light of the world" (John 8:12) and whose kingdom has no end (Luke 1:32–33). And Elisha's tears over the suffering of his people are a small picture of Jesus, who wept over Jerusalem as he foretold its fall.
