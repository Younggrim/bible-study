*Setting:* The second of Jeremiah's oracles against the nations (chs 46–51) turns from Egypt to the Philistines, Israel's old enemies on the southern coastal plain. They had been there since the days of the judges, when Samson fought them and they captured the ark (Judg 13–16; 1 Sam 4–6). Goliath was a Philistine, and David spent years fighting them (1 Sam 17; 2 Sam 5:17–25). By Jeremiah's day they were no longer a great power. Their cities, chiefly Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod and Ekron, were small kingdoms squeezed between Egypt to the south and whatever empire came from the north. This short poem pictures the Babylonian army sweeping down the coast like a flood. It came true in 604 BC, when Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Ashkelon, and the Philistines never recovered as a people.

#### Judgment on the Philistines (vv1–7)
- **"Concerning the Philistines, before Pharaoh struck down Gaza" (v1).** The heading dates the oracle by an Egyptian attack on Gaza, but it does not say which pharaoh or when. There are three main views. Pharaoh Neco may have taken Gaza in 609 BC on his way north, when he killed Josiah at Megiddo. The Greek historian Herodotus says Neco captured a city called "Kadytis," which many identify with Gaza. Or Neco may have struck Gaza in 601 BC, after Egypt drove Nebuchadnezzar back at the border. Or it may have been Pharaoh Hophra in the 580s. Most scholars favor one of Neco's campaigns. The point of the heading is that Jeremiah spoke before any of the trouble began, while Gaza still stood. The Greek translation leaves this heading out.
- **"Behold, waters are rising out of the north, and shall become an overflowing torrent" (v2).** Egypt pictured itself as the Nile in flood (46:7–8). Now the real flood comes from the north, the direction Jeremiah had warned about since his call (1:13–15). Isaiah had used the same picture for Assyria: the king of Assyria as a river that "will sweep on into Judah, it will overflow and pass on" (Isa 8:7–8). The torrent here is Babylon. **"Men shall cry out, and every inhabitant of the land shall wail."**
- **"At the noise of the stamping of the hoofs of his stallions, at the rushing of his chariots, at the rumbling of their wheels" (v3).** The poem lets the reader hear the army before seeing it: hooves, chariots, wheels. Babylon's chariots were the tanks of the ancient world.
- **"The fathers look not back to their children, so feeble are their hands" (v3).** This is the most painful line in the poem. Terror is so great that fathers run without stopping for their own children. Their hands hang limp. Natural love itself gives way. Jeremiah had seen the same thing in Jerusalem's siege (Lam 4:3–4, 10).
- **"To cut off from Tyre and Sidon every helper that remains" (v4).** Tyre and Sidon were the great Phoenician port cities to the north, on the coast of today's Lebanon. They and the Philistines traded together and joined together against Babylon. Jeremiah had handed the yoke of Babylon to the envoys of Tyre and Sidon too (27:3). With the Philistine cities gone, the Phoenicians would lose their last ally in the south. Nebuchadnezzar later besieged Tyre for thirteen years (Ezek 26–29).
- **"For the LORD is destroying the Philistines, the remnant of the coastland of Caphtor" (v4).** Here the poem says who is really behind the flood. It is not just Nebuchadnezzar. It is the LORD. **Caphtor** was the land the Philistines came from. Amos says God brought "the Philistines from Caphtor" just as he brought Israel from Egypt (Amos 9:7; compare Deut 2:23). Most scholars identify Caphtor with Crete, and "coastland" can also mean island. Egyptian records describe the "Sea Peoples," among them a group called the *Peleset*, who invaded the eastern Mediterranean coast around 1200 BC. Archaeology at Philistine sites has found pottery and customs linked to the Aegean world. "Remnant" may mean that the Philistines had already been reduced by earlier wars, or simply that they were what was left of a people who had come long ago from the sea.
- **"Baldness has come upon Gaza; Ashkelon has perished" (v5).** Shaving the head was a sign of mourning (compare 48:37; Mic 1:16). Gaza, the southernmost Philistine city, sits in mourning. **Ashkelon** was a large port about 12 miles north of Gaza. The Babylonian Chronicle records that in the winter of 604 BC Nebuchadnezzar marched to Ashkelon, captured it, seized its king, plundered it and turned the city into "a mound and heap of ruins." Excavations at Ashkelon have found a thick burn layer from that year, with collapsed buildings and bodies in the streets. Later Babylonian ration lists name "the sons of Aga, king of Ashkelon," held captive in Babylon. Zephaniah had foretold it a generation earlier: "Gaza shall be deserted, and Ashkelon shall become a desolation" (Zeph 2:4).
- **"O remnant of their valley, how long will you gash yourselves?" (v5).** "Their valley" probably means the low coastal plain where the Philistines lived. The Greek translation reads "the remnant of the Anakim," the giant clan whose survivors lived in Gaza, Gath and Ashdod in Joshua's day (Josh 11:22). The two Hebrew words look alike. Most translations keep "valley." Gashing the body was a pagan mourning rite, forbidden to Israel (Lev 19:28; Deut 14:1), and the question "how long" pictures grief that will not end.
- **"Ah, sword of the LORD! How long till you are quiet?" (v6).** The poem now speaks to the sword itself. The voice may be the Philistines begging for relief, or Jeremiah himself, who took no pleasure in judgment (compare 4:19; 9:1). **"Put yourself into your scabbard; rest and be still!"** God's sword appears elsewhere in the prophets (Deut 32:41–42; Isa 34:5–6; Ezek 21:3–17). It is the picture of God's judgment carried out through human armies.
- **"How can it be quiet when the LORD has given it a charge?" (v7).** The answer comes at once. The sword is not swinging on its own. The LORD has given it an assignment, and it cannot rest until the work is done. **"Against Ashkelon and against the seashore he has appointed it."** "Appointed" is the word for setting a meeting or a fixed time (*ya'ad*). The judgment has a place and a set time.
- **What became of the Philistines?** After the Babylonian conquest, they disappear as a distinct people. Their cities were rebuilt and lived on under Persian, Greek and Roman rule, but the people who had troubled Israel since the days of the judges merged into others. Their name survived in a strange way. The Greeks called the coastal region "Philistia," and the Romans later applied the name "Palaestina" to the whole land.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry introduces the chapter as reading the Philistines their doom, "as the former read the Egyptians theirs," and observes that Tyre and Sidon, "though they lay at some distance from them, come in sharers with them in the destruction." His outline sees two movements: the terror of the northern army (vv1–5), and a war that would go on long, with efforts to end it "in vain" (vv6–7). JFB offers both Neco after Megiddo and Hophra returning from his failed attempt to save Jerusalem as the pharaoh of v1. It explains the fathers' flight as each man "thinking only of his own safety," with the feebleness of their hands leaving only their feet to hope in. JFB reads the cry to the sword as Jeremiah speaking in the Philistines' voice. Guzik connects the "waters out of the north" to the same pattern across these oracles, and passes on the Babylonian records listing Philistine and Phoenician kings at Nebuchadnezzar's court. JFB discusses older guesses for Caphtor such as Cappadocia and Cyprus, but most modern scholars and Guzik's sources favor Crete.

#### Pointing to Christ
The poem ends with a sword that cannot rest because the LORD has given it a charge. Judgment on sin is not a mood that passes. It has to be carried through. The good news of the gospel is not that God's sword was simply put away, but that it fell somewhere else. Zechariah heard God say, "Awake, O sword, against my shepherd… Strike the shepherd" (Zech 13:7), and Jesus applied that verse to himself on the night he was arrested (Matt 26:31). In the cross the sword found its rest because it had finished its work. Even the Philistine coast heard of it later. Philip preached Jesus on "the road that goes down from Jerusalem to Gaza" and later in Ashdod and the coastal towns (Acts 8:26, 40).
