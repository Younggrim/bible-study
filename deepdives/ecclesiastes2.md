*Setting:* In chapter 1 the Preacher announced his theme, "All is vanity" (*hebel*, breath or vapor), and began his experiment: to search out by wisdom whether anything under the sun brings lasting gain. Wisdom alone only increased his sorrow (1:18). Chapter 2 continues the test in the first person, as a king with unlimited means. He tries pleasure, then great building projects and wealth (vv1–11), then compares wisdom and folly in the face of death (vv12–17), then thinks about what happens to his life's work when he dies (vv18–23). Each road ends in the same verdict. Then, for the first time, the book shows a glimpse of something else: simple joys received "from the hand of God" (vv24–26). The details of vv4–9 match Solomon's reign closely (1 Kgs 4–10). For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### The Vanity of Self-Indulgence (vv1–11)
- **"Come now, I will test you with pleasure; enjoy yourself" (v1).** He talks to his own heart. The word for "pleasure" is *simchah*, "joy" or "gladness," the same word used for festivals and for joy in God (Deut 16:14–15; Ps 4:7). He means to see whether a life of enjoyment can be the answer. The verdict comes before the details: "this also was vanity." Eaton, cited by Guzik, notes that he is testing himself as much as pleasure.
- **"I said of laughter, 'It is mad,' and of pleasure, 'What use is it?'" (v2).** Laughter cannot answer life's questions. It distracts from them. JFB says laughter is "harmless in its proper place" and "mad" only when made the chief good. Later he will say "sorrow is better than laughter" (7:3).
- **"How to cheer my body with wine — my heart still guiding me with wisdom" (v3).** He was not drunk and lost. He experimented with wine and "folly" on purpose, keeping his head, as a researcher watching himself. His aim was to see "what was good for the children of man to do under heaven during the few days of their life," literally "the number of the days of their life." Life is short, so the question matters.
- **"I made great works. I built houses and planted vineyards for myself" (v4).** The list sounds like Solomon's reign: the temple and his own palace, which took thirteen years (1 Kgs 7:1), the house of the Forest of Lebanon, cities for his chariots and horses (1 Kgs 9:15–19), and vineyards (Song 8:11). Notice how often he says "for myself" in vv4–8. The Tyndale notes observe that these were mainly projects for his own enjoyment.
- **"I made myself gardens and parks" (v5).** "Parks" is *pardes*, a word borrowed from Persian for an enclosed royal garden. Through Greek it became the English word "paradise." The echo of Eden is hard to miss: a man planting a garden with "all kinds of fruit trees" (Gen 2:8–9). He tried to build paradise, and it did not satisfy. JFB: "An earthly paradise can never make up for the want of the heavenly."
- **"I made myself pools from which to water the forest of growing trees" (v6).** Irrigation was serious engineering in a dry land. Three large reservoirs south of Bethlehem have long been called "Solomon's Pools." The present structures are mostly from later centuries, but they show how such gardens were watered.
- **"I bought male and female slaves… great possessions of herds and flocks" (v7).** Solomon's court ate thirty cattle and a hundred sheep a day, besides game (1 Kgs 4:22–23). "Slaves born in my house" were a settled household staff, which JFB thought "more trustworthy" than bought ones.
- **"Silver and gold and the treasure of kings and provinces" (v8).** Gold came from Ophir, tribute from subject kings, and trade from every direction. Silver was "as common in Jerusalem as stone" (1 Kgs 10:27). The Tyndale notes list the sources: trade, gifts and taxes.
- **"Singers, both men and women, and many concubines, the delight of the sons of man" (v8).** "Many concubines" translates two rare Hebrew words, *shiddah weshiddot*, found only here. Their meaning is uncertain. The ESV and NIV take them as a harem, and the Tyndale notes connect it with Solomon's seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (1 Kgs 11:3). Older translations and Jewish tradition read "musical instruments" (KJV). Wright, cited by Guzik, notes the word appears nowhere else. JFB thought it meant "a princess and princesses," that is, wives of high rank. "The delight of the sons of man" points toward the harem reading. This is the very thing that turned Solomon's heart away from God (1 Kgs 11:4).
- **"So I became great and surpassed all who were before me in Jerusalem. Also my wisdom remained with me" (v9).** He became the richest and wisest king in the world (1 Kgs 10:23). He still kept his judgment, so his conclusion is not the complaint of a man who was cheated or who wasted it all.
- **"Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them… this was my reward for all my toil" (v10).** He denied himself nothing. He even admits the work itself gave him pleasure. That joy was real. But it was all the "reward" (*cheleq*, "portion" or "share") he got. JFB calls it "evanescent."
- **"Then I considered all that my hands had done… and there was nothing to be gained under the sun" (v11).** After everything, he steps back to look, the way God looked at creation and saw "it was very good" (Gen 1:31). He sees the opposite: *hebel*, a chasing of wind, no lasting "gain" (*yitron*, 1:3). The pleasure was real while it lasted. It did not last, and it could not answer the question.
- **Commentators:** Guzik describes this as pleasure-seeking of a respectable kind, pursued with taste and self-control, not a slide into vice, and still it failed to give eternal meaning. He passes on Kidner's remark that the Preacher turns from simple pleasure to the joys of creativity. Henry's verdict on the section: "there is no true happiness and satisfaction to be had in mirth and pleasure." JFB, on the gardens, reminds readers what this whole section reaches for and misses: the heavenly paradise.

#### The Vanity of Living Wisely (vv12–17)
- **"What can the man do who comes after the king? Only what has already been done" (v12).** No one will ever run this experiment better than he did. A later king can only repeat it. The Tyndale notes read it that way. Some think he is also already thinking of his heir (vv18–19).
- **"There is more gain in wisdom than in folly, as there is more gain in light than in darkness" (v13).** He does not say wisdom is worthless. It really is better, as much better as seeing is better than stumbling in the dark.
- **"The wise person has his eyes in his head, but the fool walks in darkness" (v14).** A proverb: the wise man sees where he is going. "And yet… the same event happens to all of them." "Event" (*miqreh*) means "what befalls," and here it means death. Both reach the same end.
- **"What happens to the fool will happen to me also. Why then have I been so very wise?" (v15).** If death erases the difference, what was the point of all that wisdom? He calls this, too, *hebel*.
- **"Of the wise as of the fool there is no enduring remembrance… How the wise dies just like the fool!" (v16).** "How" is a cry of grief, the word that opens a lament (2 Sam 1:19; Lam 1:1). Psalm 49:10 says the same: "the wise die; the fool and the stupid alike must perish and leave their wealth to others." The Tyndale notes link this with 1:11: no one remembers.
- **"So I hated life" (v17).** This is a startling line for the Bible, and it is meant to be. Life under the sun, with death at the end of it, became "grievous," literally "evil," to him. Ecclesiastes refuses to hide this feeling. It lets the reader feel the full weight of death before offering any comfort.
- **Commentators:** JFB says worldly wisdom "can never fill the place of the true wisdom," and on v16 that lasting remembrance belongs to the righteous alone (Ps 112:6). On v17 it adds, with a pastor's edge: "The backslider ought to have rather reasoned as the prodigal," and gone home to his Father. Henry: wisdom is "excellent and useful," yet "proves insufficient to make a man happy." Guzik passes on Kidner's thought that if existence has a lie at its center, nothing else can be made to fit.

#### The Vanity of Toil (vv18–26)
- **"I hated all my toil… seeing that I must leave it to the man who will come after me" (v18).** He cannot take it with him (Ps 49:17; Luke 12:20). Death is not only the end of him. It is the moment his life's work passes into someone else's hands.
- **"Who knows whether he will be wise or a fool?" (v19).** For Solomon the answer turned out to be grim. His son Rehoboam rejected his wise counselors, listened to his young friends and lost ten of the twelve tribes in a matter of days (1 Kgs 12:1–20). Within five years the temple and palace treasures, including Solomon's golden shields, were carried off to Egypt (1 Kgs 14:25–26). The Tyndale notes and JFB both draw the line to Rehoboam, a "foreboding too fully realized," JFB says.
- **"I… gave my heart up to despair" (v20).** He turns around and looks back at all his labor, and hope drains away. "Despair" means letting go of any hope that the work will last.
- **"A person who has toiled with wisdom and knowledge and skill must leave everything to be enjoyed by someone who did not toil for it" (v21).** "Skill" means success or competence, the know-how of a craftsman (Exod 31:3–5). He calls this "a great evil," not because inheritance is wrong, but because it shows how little control a person has over what he built.
- **"All his days are full of sorrow… Even in the night his heart does not rest" (v23).** Worry follows the worker home and keeps him awake. Psalm 127:2 gives the contrast: "It is in vain that you rise up early… eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep."
- **"There is nothing better for a person than that he should eat and drink and find enjoyment in his toil. This also, I saw, is from the hand of God" (v24).** The tone changes. This is the first of several such passages (3:12–13, 22; 5:18–20; 8:15; 9:7–10). After so much despair, he points to ordinary, daily good: a meal, a drink, satisfaction in work. And for the first time since 1:13 he names God, and as a giver. These joys are not prizes we seize. They are gifts we receive. JFB notes that the Hebrew reads literally "It is not good in man that he should eat," and some take it as a warning against pretending to be happy. Most translations, ancient and modern, read it as the ESV does.
- **"For apart from him who can eat or who can have enjoyment?" (v25).** The ESV follows the Greek and Syriac translations here, "apart from him." The Hebrew has "apart from me," which would mean "who has had more enjoyment than I?" The ESV gives that in its footnote. Either way, the point stands: Solomon had every pleasure and did not find joy in them until he saw them as from God.
- **"To the one who pleases him God has given wisdom and knowledge and joy, but to the sinner he has given the business of gathering and collecting, only to give to one who pleases God" (v26).** The sinner piles up, and in the end it goes to someone else (Prov 13:22; 28:8; Job 27:16–17). The one who pleases God receives what all the gathering could not buy: wisdom and joy. "This also is vanity" at the end is debated. It may refer to the sinner's gathering, or it may mean that even this pattern is beyond our grasp to understand.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and the Tyndale notes read v24 differently. Guzik takes it as resignation, making the best of a bad situation: under the sun, without eternity, the best one can do is enjoy the moment. He passes on Morgan's line that materialism leads to fatalism. The Tyndale notes take it more warmly: food and work are God's gifts, to be received with thanks and contentment. Henry sits between them, saying that if there is any good in the things of this world, "it is only to those that sit loose to it." Guzik also passes on Eaton's remark that v18–23 is one of the most moving cries of despair in the Old Testament, and he sets it beside 1 Corinthians 15:58.

#### Pointing to Christ
Solomon built a garden, a *pardes*, and found that an earthly paradise could not satisfy. The Bible's story runs from the first garden (Gen 2) to the last, where the river of life flows and the tree of life stands in the city of God (Rev 22:1–2). Between them is a garden called Gethsemane, where Christ said "your will be done," and a garden tomb, where he rose (John 19:41; 20:15). Solomon asked "Why then have I been so very wise?" since the wise man dies just like the fool (vv15–16). Christ, "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24), also died, and on the third day he rose, so that death is no longer the end of the story for those who are his. Solomon hated his toil because he would leave it to a stranger. In Christ the opposite is true: believers are "heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ" (Rom 8:17), inheriting what they did not toil for. And because he lives, the ordinary gifts of v24, food, drink and work, can be received as what they are: good things "from the hand of God."
