*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. This is the last of the tribal genealogies, and it returns to Benjamin, already listed briefly in 7:6–12. The extra space is deliberate. The genealogies began with Judah, David's tribe, and they end with Benjamin, Saul's tribe, which sets up the story that is about to start: Saul's death in chapter 10 and David's rise in chapter 11. Benjamin also mattered to the first readers for its own sake. When the kingdom split, Benjamin stayed with Judah (1 Kings 12:21), went into exile with Judah, and came home with Judah (Ezra 1:5). Its land lay just north of Jerusalem, where many of the returned families now lived. So this chapter is about the returning community's neighbors and kin, and about the royal house that came before David. Much of it is names. The Chronicler's sources were old, the tribe had been nearly wiped out once (Judg 20), and the lists do not always match earlier ones. The chapter ends with a long line of Saul's descendants running down toward the exile.

#### A Genealogy of Saul (vv1–40)
- **"Benjamin fathered Bela his firstborn, Ashbel the second" (v1).** Five sons, numbered in order. This list differs from Genesis 46:21 and Numbers 26:38–40, and from 7:6. JFB suggests some sons had more than one name, "sons" may sometimes mean grandsons, and Benjamin's records were "disordered or destroyed" when the tribe was almost wiped out in the civil war of Judges 20, when only 600 men survived (Judg 20:47).
- **"Bela had sons: Addar, Gera, Abihud" (v3).** Gera appears twice in the list (vv3, 5) and again in v7. The judge Ehud, who killed the king of Moab, was "the son of Gera, the Benjaminite" (Judg 3:15), so the name runs in this family.
- **"These are the sons of Ehud… heads of fathers' houses of the inhabitants of Geba" (v6).** Most interpreters, including JFB, take this to be the judge Ehud's family. Geba was a Benjaminite town a few miles north of Jerusalem, later a Levite town (6:60). **"Carried into exile to Manahath"** refers to some forced move that is not recorded elsewhere, probably a local clash in the time of the judges, not the Babylonian exile. Manahath may be near Jerusalem, in Judah's territory.
- **"Shaharaim fathered sons in the country of Moab after he had sent away Hushim and Baara his wives" (v8).** A Benjaminite living in Moab, east of the Dead Sea, much as Elimelech of Bethlehem did in the days of the judges (Ruth 1:1). "Sent away" means he divorced them. The Chronicler records it without comment, including the sons he later had with Hushim (v11).
- **"Who built Ono and Lod with its towns" (v12).** Lod and Ono lay on the coastal plain to the west. Both were resettled by returning exiles (Ezra 2:33; Neh 11:35), so the first readers knew these towns well. Nehemiah's enemies tried to lure him to "the plain of Ono" to harm him (Neh 6:2). Lod is the later Lydda, where Peter healed a paralyzed man named Aeneas (Acts 9:32–35).
- **"Heads… of the inhabitants of Aijalon, who caused the inhabitants of Gath to flee" (v13).** Aijalon is the valley where Joshua prayed that the moon would stand still (Josh 10:12). Gath was one of the five Philistine cities. Henry suggests this victory over Gath may have avenged the men of Gath who killed Ephraim's sons (7:21), since Beriah's name links both stories. That is a guess, but the echo is there.
- **"These lived in Jerusalem" (v28).** Jerusalem sat on the border between Judah and Benjamin. Joshua's allotment actually placed "Jebus, that is, Jerusalem" in Benjamin's territory (Josh 18:28), though the city was taken and held by David from Judah (11:4–8). Benjaminites, Judahites and Levites lived there side by side, and the next chapter lists many of them after the return (9:3–9). JFB notes that these chief men probably settled there after the exile.
- **Commentators on vv1–28:** Henry admits frankly, "There is little or nothing of history in all these verses; we have not therefore much to observe." He does not try to reconcile every difference with earlier lists, suggesting Ezra "copied them out as they came into his hand." But he finds comfort in the list itself: "the names of multitudes of the Israel of God are here carefully preserved in everlasting remembrance," a picture of the book of life. He also sees God's hand in Benjamin's survival after nearly being destroyed, "for it is the honour of God to help the weakest and raise up those that are most diminished." Guzik, quoting J. Barton Payne, notes that Benjamin ranked second only to Judah among the returned exiles, which explains the length of the record.
- **"Jeiel the father of Gibeon lived in Gibeon" (v29).** "Father" here means founder or leading man. Gibeon, about six miles northwest of Jerusalem, was the town whose people tricked Joshua into a treaty (Josh 9). The Gibeonites were not Israelites, but Benjaminites later settled among them. Saul's family came from this area, and Saul's own hometown, Gibeah, lay nearby. Jeiel's name is not in the Hebrew text of v29 but is supplied from the parallel list in 9:35, which repeats this family almost word for word.
- **"Ner was the father of Kish, Kish of Saul" (v33).** Now Saul's line comes into focus. 1 Samuel 9:1 names Kish's father as Abiel and 1 Samuel 14:51 calls Ner Saul's uncle, so the relationships are hard to fit together. JFB suggests Ner and Abiel may be the same man. Another view is that a generation is skipped, as often happens in these lists.
- **"Saul of Jonathan, Malchi-shua, Abinadab and Eshbaal" (v33).** Jonathan, Malchi-shua and Abinadab died with their father on Mount Gilboa (10:2; 1 Sam 31:2). **Eshbaal** is the son known in 2 Samuel as **Ish-bosheth**, who reigned briefly over the northern tribes after Saul's death (2 Sam 2:8–10). His name in Chronicles means "man of Baal." In Samuel, *baal* has been replaced with *bosheth*, "shame."
- **"The son of Jonathan was Merib-baal" (v34).** Merib-baal is Mephibosheth, Jonathan's son who was lame in both feet, and the same substitution happened to his name. In Saul's day *baal*, which simply means "lord" or "master," may have been used as a title for Israel's God, without reference to the Canaanite god. Once Baal worship became Israel's great temptation, scribes and storytellers replaced the word with "shame" to avoid honoring the false god's name (compare Hos 2:16–17). Chronicles seems to keep the older, original forms.
- **Mephibosheth's story behind the name.** When Saul and Jonathan died, a nurse fled with five-year-old Mephibosheth and dropped him, and he was lame from then on (2 Sam 4:4). Years later David sought him out "for Jonathan's sake," restored Saul's land to him, and had him eat at the king's table "like one of the king's sons" (2 Sam 9:1–13). David had sworn to Jonathan that he would never cut off his kindness from Jonathan's house (1 Sam 20:14–17). This list is that promise kept across centuries.
- **"Merib-baal was the father of Micah" (vv34–38).** From Jonathan the line runs on for about a dozen more generations. The Tyndale notes date Micah to Solomon's time and Ulam (v39) to near the end of Judah's kingdom. Saul lost the throne, but his family was not erased. Jonathan's house survived through the whole history of the monarchy.
- **"Azel had six sons, and these are their names" (v38).** Guzik passes on, with a smile, an old rabbinic boast that commentaries on this one verse would load thousands of camels. He uses it as a warning against forcing hidden meanings onto a simple list.
- **"The sons of Ulam were men who were mighty warriors, bowmen" (v40).** Benjamin was famous for its fighters with bow and sling. Its left-handed slingers "could sling a stone at a hair and not miss" (Judg 20:16), and Benjaminite archers who joined David could shoot with either hand (12:2). JFB explains that heavy bows were strung by bracing them with the foot. Ulam's family numbered **150**, "many sons and grandsons," a family still thriving as the kingdom neared its end.
- **"All these were Benjaminites" (v40).** The last line of the tribal genealogies. The next chapter turns to the people who came back from Babylon, and then the story itself begins with the death of Saul.
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies Eshbaal as Ish-bosheth and Merib-baal as Mephibosheth, and suggests that the Abinadab here is the son called Ishvi in 1 Samuel 14:49. Guzik explains the "baal" to "bosheth" change the same way as above. Henry structures the chapter around its two concerns: the leading men of the tribe, and "a more particular account of the family of Saul." He notes that Benjamin was honored to produce "Saul… the first king of Israel" and to stand with Judah through captivity and return.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter's quiet hero is a promise. Saul fell and his dynasty ended, but Jonathan's son ate at David's table, and Jonathan's descendants were still alive generations later because a king kept his word. That is the gospel in miniature. People who had no claim on the king, weak and lame and from a fallen house, are brought to his table for someone else's sake. The tribe of Benjamin gets one more turn in the story. The greatest Benjaminite was another Saul, "of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews" (Phil 3:5), who persecuted the Son of David until Jesus stopped him on the Damascus road. He later wrote, "Has God rejected his people? By no means! For I myself am an Israelite… a member of the tribe of Benjamin" (Rom 11:1).
