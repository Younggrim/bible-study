*Setting:* Psalm 132 is the thirteenth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. It is by far the longest of them, eighteen verses, and the most royal. The short Songs of Ascents around it are personal and quiet. This one tells a piece of Israel's history: how David brought the ark of the covenant to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6), and how God then promised David a dynasty that would last forever (2 Sam 7). The ark was the gold-covered chest that held the tablets of the law, the sign of God's presence among his people. For decades after the Philistines captured and returned it, it sat neglected in a village called Kiriath-jearim (1 Sam 7:1–2). David made it his first great task as king to bring it home. The psalm has no author's name. Its verses 8–10 appear almost word for word at the end of Solomon's prayer when he dedicated the temple (2 Chron 6:41–42), so many think it was used at that dedication, or at festivals celebrating the ark and the king. Others date it later, after the exile, when there was no king in Jerusalem and God's promise to David was the people's great hope. The psalm has a clear shape. The first half (vv1–10) recalls David's oath to God. The second half (vv11–18) answers with God's oath to David. David swore he would find a dwelling place for the LORD. God swore he would build a house for David. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Remember, O LORD, in David's Favor (vv1–5)
- **"Remember, O LORD, in David's favor" (v1).** The Hebrew is literally "remember for David." To ask God to "remember" is not to suggest he might forget. It is to ask him to act on what he knows (Gen 8:1; Exod 2:24). The people pray on the basis of God's relationship with David, not on their own merit.
- **"All the hardships he endured" (v1).** The word (*'unnoto*, from the root *'anah*) can mean affliction or humility. David's life was full of trouble: years as a fugitive from Saul, wars, his son Absalom's rebellion. Here the focus is probably on the strain and effort he poured into bringing the ark to Jerusalem and preparing for the temple. He told Solomon, "With great pains I have provided for the house of the LORD" (1 Chron 22:14), using a related word.
- **"How he swore to the LORD and vowed to the Mighty One of Jacob" (v2).** The oath itself is not recorded in Samuel or Chronicles, though its spirit is plain in 2 Samuel 7:1–2, where David says, "I dwell in a house of cedar, but the ark of God dwells in a tent." "The Mighty One of Jacob" (*'abir ya'aqob*) is an old title of God, first used in Jacob's blessing on Joseph (Gen 49:24). It recalls the God who protected Jacob through all his wanderings, and it appears twice here (vv2, 5).
- **"I will not enter my house or get into my bed" (v3).** The language is strong: David would not settle down or rest until the matter was done. It is not a literal promise to stay awake for years. It describes a man who could not rest easy while God's ark sat neglected. His own comfort came second.
- **"I will not give sleep to my eyes or slumber to my eyelids" (v4).** Proverbs uses the same words for urgent business that cannot wait (Prov 6:4). There is a contrast with Psalm 127:2, where God "gives to his beloved sleep." David's sleeplessness here is not anxious toil but zeal.
- **"Until I find a place for the LORD, a dwelling place for the Mighty One of Jacob" (v5).** David did find the place. He brought the ark to Jerusalem and pitched a tent for it there (2 Sam 6:17). Later he bought the threshing floor of Araunah, where the temple would be built (2 Sam 24:18–25; 2 Chron 3:1). He was not allowed to build the temple, because he had been a man of war (1 Chron 22:8), but he gathered the materials and drew up the plans for Solomon (1 Chron 28:11–19).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv1–10 as Solomon's prayer at the dedication and notes the humility of pleading David's case rather than his own: "I am not worthy, for whom thou shouldst do this." He says David "would not settle in his bed, till he had brought this matter to some head." JFB gives the literal sense, "remember for David," and calls the oath otherwise unrecorded. Guzik notes, with Maclaren, that David put his own comfort second and God's service first, and observes that although David never built the temple, God honored his heart with a lasting promise.

#### Arise, O LORD, and Go to Your Resting Place (vv6–10)
- **"Behold, we heard of it in Ephrathah" (v6).** "It" is the ark. The voice changes to "we," the people of David's generation. Ephrathah is an old name for Bethlehem, David's hometown (Gen 35:19; Mic 5:2; Ruth 4:11). In Bethlehem, as a young man, David may have heard about the ark, forgotten in a village to the west. Saul's reign had ignored it: "we did not seek it in the days of Saul" (1 Chron 13:3). Matthew Henry takes Ephrathah differently, as a reference to the tribe of Ephraim and Shiloh, where the ark had stood before it was captured. Most modern interpreters and the study notes take it as Bethlehem.
- **"We found it in the fields of Jaar" (v6).** "Jaar" means "forest" or "woods." It is a short form of Kiriath-jearim, "town of forests," the village where the ark had been kept for some twenty years in the house of Abinadab (1 Sam 7:1–2). David and his men went there to bring it up to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6:2; 1 Chron 13:5–6).
- **"Let us go to his dwelling place; let us worship at his footstool!" (v7).** These words, sung by pilgrims climbing toward Jerusalem, made the history their own. The ark was called God's "footstool" (1 Chron 28:2; Ps 99:5). God was pictured as enthroned above the cherubim on the ark's cover, with the ark itself as the place where his feet rested. To worship at his footstool was to bow before the King.
- **"Arise, O LORD, and go to your resting place, you and the ark of your might" (v8).** This echoes the words Moses spoke every time the ark set out in the wilderness: "Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered" (Num 10:35). The ark had been on the move for centuries, from the wilderness to Shiloh, to the Philistines, to Kiriath-jearim, to Jerusalem. Now it would come to rest. Solomon ended his temple prayer with these words (2 Chron 6:41).
- **"Let your priests be clothed with righteousness, and let your saints shout for joy" (v9).** The priests wore special linen garments for their service (Exod 28). The prayer asks for something deeper: that their lives would match their clothes. "Saints" (*chasidim*) are God's faithful people, those who receive and return his steadfast love (*chesed*). When the priests are righteous, God's people rejoice. The prayer is answered more fully in v16.
- **"For the sake of your servant David, do not turn away the face of your anointed one" (v10).** "The anointed one" (*mashiach*, the word behind "Messiah") here means the reigning king of David's line, perhaps Solomon himself. To "turn away the face" of someone means to reject his request. The king asks God to hear him, not because he deserves it, but for David's sake, because of God's promise to David.
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies Ephratah as Bethlehem and "the fields of the wood" as Kiriath-jearim, "City of woods," the place from which the ark was brought. Henry agrees about Kiriath-jearim, "which signifies the city of woods," but places Ephratah "at Shiloh, in the tribe of Ephraim." JFB notes that vv8–9 echo Solomon's prayer (2 Chron 6:41), and on v10 he says "for David's sake" means on account of God's promise. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that everyone God accepts is accepted through the Anointed, and notes that the prayer of v9, for a holy priesthood, is answered by more than the people asked.

#### The LORD Swore to David a Sure Oath (vv11–12)
- **"The LORD swore to David a sure oath from which he will not turn back" (v11).** Now God's oath answers David's. David swore to find a house for God (v2). God swore to build a house for David. The promise is in 2 Samuel 7:11–16, though the word "oath" is not used there. Psalm 89 also calls it an oath: "I have sworn to David my servant" (Ps 89:3, 35). "He will not turn back" uses the same verb as v10, "do not turn away." The people ask God not to turn away; God has already promised he will not turn back.
- **"One of the sons of your body I will set on your throne" (v11).** Literally "of the fruit of your body." God promised a son of David to reign after him (2 Sam 7:12). Peter quoted this verse on the day of Pentecost, saying that David, "knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him that he would set one of his descendants on his throne... foresaw and spoke about the resurrection of the Christ" (Acts 2:30–31).
- **"If your sons keep my covenant and my testimonies that I shall teach them" (v12).** The promise has a condition for each generation. Sons who broke the covenant would be disciplined (2 Sam 7:14; Ps 89:30–32). Many of David's descendants did break it, and in 586 BC Babylon ended the line of kings in Jerusalem. The study notes put it this way: God's promise to David cannot fail, but individual kings could lose its blessings by unfaithfulness. Both are true. God disciplined the line, but he did not cancel the promise (Ps 89:33–37).
- **"Their sons also forever shall sit on your throne" (v12).** "Forever" is the key word. A dynasty that lasted forever would need either an endless line of faithful kings, or one King who would reign forever.
- **Commentators:** JFB stresses that the promise in v12 is conditional for David's sons, while seeing the whole as pointing to "the greater and spiritual kingdom of David's greater Son." Henry says "God will be behind with none of his people in affections or assurances": David's zeal for God was matched and surpassed by God's promise to him. Guzik passes on VanGemeren's observation that "oath" is a poetic way of stressing certainty, and Clarke's note that the condition explains why David's descendants were eventually driven from the throne.

#### The LORD Has Chosen Zion (vv13–18)
- **"For the LORD has chosen Zion; he has desired it for his dwelling place" (v13).** God's choice of David and his choice of Zion go together (Ps 78:68–70). "Desired" (*'awah*) is a word of longing. God did not merely tolerate Jerusalem. He wanted to dwell there.
- **"This is my resting place forever; here I will dwell, for I have desired it" (v14).** God speaks in his own voice from here to the end. The people prayed, "Arise, O LORD, and go to your resting place" (v8). God answers, "This is my resting place forever." He gives more than they asked.
- **"I will abundantly bless her provisions; I will satisfy her poor with bread" (v15).** The blessing of God's presence reaches daily life: food, harvests and care for the poor. A city where God dwells is meant to be a city where the hungry are fed.
- **"Her priests I will clothe with salvation, and her saints will shout for joy" (v16).** The people asked that the priests be clothed "with righteousness" (v9). God promises to clothe them "with salvation," which is even more. The people asked that the saints "shout for joy." God promises it, and the Hebrew strengthens it: they will shout and shout.
- **"There I will make a horn to sprout for David" (v17).** A horn was a symbol of strength, like the horns of a wild ox or ram (Deut 33:17; 1 Sam 2:10). "Sprout" (*tsamach*) is the word for a plant putting out shoots. The prophets used a noun from the same root, *tsemach*, "Branch," as a title for the coming King from David's line: "I will raise up for David a righteous Branch" (Jer 23:5; also Zech 3:8; 6:12). Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, praised God for raising up "a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" (Luke 1:69).
- **"I have prepared a lamp for my anointed" (v17).** A burning lamp in a house was a sign that the family was alive and continuing. God promised to give David "a lamp" in Jerusalem so that his line would not go out, even when his descendants were unfaithful (1 Kings 11:36; 15:4; 2 Kings 8:19).
- **"His enemies I will clothe with shame, but on him his crown will shine" (v18).** The clothing theme reaches its end: priests clothed with salvation, enemies clothed with shame. "Crown" (*nezer*) is the same word used for the holy crown on the high priest's turban (Exod 29:6; 39:30), and "shine" (*yatsits*) sounds like *tsits*, the gold plate engraved "Holy to the LORD" that the high priest wore on his forehead (Exod 28:36). The king's crown is pictured with a priestly glow.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "Christ is the horn of salvation," and explains the lamp beautifully: "a lamp is a successor, for, when a lamp is almost out, another may be lighted by it." JFB reads the horn as enlarged power and the lamp as "the figure of prosperity." Guzik notes that older Jewish interpreters, such as Kimchi, also read the horn of David as the Messiah, and passes on Kidner's observation that the word for crown is the one used for the high priest's diadem, so the king's glory is a holy one.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 132 sets two oaths side by side: David's oath to find a dwelling place for God, and God's oath to set a son of David on the throne forever. Both are fulfilled in Jesus. He is the son of David born in Bethlehem Ephrathah (Mic 5:2; Matt 2:1–6), the horn of salvation raised up in David's house (Luke 1:69), the righteous Branch, and the Anointed One whose face God will never turn away. The angel told Mary, "the Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever" (Luke 1:32–33). He is also the true dwelling place of God. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and he called his body the temple (John 2:19–21). Through him God's people become "a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" (Eph 2:22), and their high priest is clothed with salvation for them (Heb 7:26–27). His enemies will be clothed with shame, and on his head are "many diadems" (Rev 19:12). The lamp God promised David never went out. It shines in the city where "its lamp is the Lamb" (Rev 21:23).
