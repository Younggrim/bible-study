*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to The Gittith. Of Asaph." *Gittith* is uncertain. It may name an instrument or tune from Gath, or a vintage song (the Hebrew *gat* is a winepress), which would suit an autumn harvest feast; it also heads Psalms 8 and 84. Psalm 81 is a festival psalm in two parts. Verses 1–5 call the people to celebrate a feast with music and trumpets. Then, in vv6–16, the singing stops and God himself speaks, as a prophet might deliver God's word in the middle of worship. Which feast is meant? The trumpet "at the new moon" and "at the full moon" points to the seventh month, Tishri (September–October), which began with the Feast of Trumpets at the new moon (Lev 23:24) and reached its climax with the Feast of Booths at the full moon two weeks later (Lev 23:34). Henry takes it of the Feast of Trumpets, Guzik of Booths or Trumpets, and JFB of Passover, which also fell at a full moon and celebrated the exodus the psalm recalls. In later Jewish worship Psalm 81 was sung in the temple every Thursday and is still read at the New Year (Rosh Hashanah). It follows Psalm 80's plea "Restore us" with God's answer: the way back is to listen.

#### Sing Aloud to God Our Strength (vv1–5a)
- **"Sing aloud to God our strength; shout for joy to the God of Jacob!" (v1).** Worship here is loud and glad. "Shout for joy" is the cry of a crowd at a victory or a coronation. "God of Jacob" is a favorite title in the Asaph psalms (75:9; 76:6), reminding the people that their God made his promises to a flawed patriarch and kept them.
- **"Raise a song; sound the tambourine, the sweet lyre with the harp" (v2).** The tambourine (*toph*) was a hand drum, a frame covered with skin, often played by women while dancing, as Miriam did at the sea (Exod 15:20). The lyre (*kinnor*) and the harp (*nevel*) are the two stringed instruments most often named in the Psalms (33:2; 150:3). The Tyndale notes describe these.
- **"Blow the trumpet at the new moon, at the full moon, on our feast day" (v3).** The "trumpet" is the *shofar*, the ram's horn, used to call armies and to announce holy days. Israel's calendar was lunar: each month began at the new moon, which was itself a small festival (Num 10:10; 28:11). "Full moon" (*keseh*) is a rare word, found elsewhere only in Proverbs 7:20. The great pilgrim feasts of Passover and Booths both began at the full moon. JFB glosses "new moon" as "or the month."
- **"For it is a statute for Israel, a rule of the God of Jacob" (v4).** The festival is not a human invention. God commanded it, so keeping it is obedience, not just celebration (Lev 23).
- **"He made it a decree in Joseph when he went out over the land of Egypt" (v5).** "Joseph" stands for the whole nation here, as the Tyndale notes explain. The phrase is unusual (most often "Joseph" means the northern tribes, 80:1), and its use here may tie the psalm to Psalm 80's northern focus. "When he went out over the land of Egypt" means when God went out against Egypt in the plagues (Exod 11:4). The feasts were memorials of that deliverance. JFB: "The feasts, especially the passover, attested God's relation to His people."
- **Commentators:** Guzik urges hearty, full-voiced congregational singing, and quotes Spurgeon's complaint about worship so polite it barely makes a sound. Henry says the psalm was written "not upon occasion of any particular providence, but for the solemnity of a particular ordinance," likely "the feast of trumpets on the new moon of the seventh month." He adds that worship is meant both "to give glory to God and to receive instruction from God," which is exactly the shape of the psalm.

#### I Relieved Your Shoulder (vv5b–7)
- **"I hear a language I had not known" (v5).** A hard line, with two main readings. (1) It continues the memory of Egypt: Israel heard a foreign language there, the speech of their oppressors (114:1, "a people of strange language"). JFB: "language—literally, 'lip,'" and he sees the foreign tongue as adding to their distress. Guzik, following Poole, also takes it this way. (2) It introduces what follows: the psalmist (or the worship leader) hears an unfamiliar voice, God's own, and then reports what God says. Many modern scholars prefer this reading, since the next line begins God's speech. Either way, the psalm now changes from the people's song to God's word.
- **"I relieved your shoulder of the burden; your hands were freed from the basket" (v6).** The scene is Egyptian slave labor. "Burden" is the same word used of Israel's forced labor in Exodus (Exod 1:11; 6:6, "I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians"). The "basket" was used for carrying clay and bricks. Egyptian tomb paintings, such as those in the tomb of the official Rekhmire (about 1450 BC), show foreign laborers carrying baskets of clay and making bricks.
- **"In distress you called, and I delivered you" (v7).** God heard their groaning (Exod 2:23–25; 3:7).
- **"I answered you in the secret place of thunder" (v7).** The "secret place" is literally the hiding place, the cover of the thunder. It may mean the pillar of cloud from which God fought the Egyptians at the sea (Exod 14:24), as JFB says, or the dark cloud and thunder of Sinai, where God spoke from thick darkness (Exod 19:16; 20:21), as Guzik takes it. God was hidden, yet he answered.
- **"I tested you at the waters of Meribah" (v7).** Meribah means "quarreling." At Rephidim the people quarreled with Moses and "tested the LORD" by asking, "Is the LORD among us or not?" (Exod 17:1–7). A second Meribah came near the end of the wilderness years (Num 20:1–13). The irony is that the people tested God, but the psalm says God was testing them, seeing whether they would trust him. **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** Guzik, following Spurgeon, notes that Meribah is a mirror: God's people still doubt him right after he has delivered them. JFB on the "secret place" points to "the cloud from which He troubled the Egyptians (Ex 14:24)." The Tyndale notes stress that God heard Israel's cry in Egypt and rescued them from the slavery that crushed them.

#### Hear, O My People (vv8–10)
- **"Hear, O my people, while I admonish you! O Israel, if you would but listen to me!" (v8).** The language echoes the great command, the *Shema*: "Hear, O Israel" (Deut 6:4). "Admonish" can mean to warn or to testify against. The tone is a father's plea: "if only you would listen."
- **"There shall be no strange god among you; you shall not bow down to a foreign god" (v9).** This is the first commandment in fresh words. A "strange god" is one unknown, outside the covenant. The festival is the right place for this reminder: the feasts were exactly where Israel could slide into mixing in Canaanite harvest gods.
- **"I am the LORD your God, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" (v10).** These are almost the opening words of the Ten Commandments (Exod 20:2; Deut 5:6). God's commands rest on what he has already done. Grace comes first, then the call to loyalty.
- **"Open your mouth wide, and I will fill it" (v10).** A picture of baby birds in the nest, beaks wide open, waiting to be fed. God invites his people to expect much from him. In context the filling is food (v16), but more than food: God's word and every good gift. The Tyndale notes connect it with taking in God's word (Deut 8:3; Ezek 3:1–3). The problem in Israel was never that God gave too little. It was that they would not open their mouths to him.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Spurgeon's picture of nestlings, says believers should come to God with wide-open expectation, aware of their need and his greatness, and should ask largely, on the merits of Jesus, since no one can ask beyond what God is able to give (Eph 3:20). JFB reads v10 as the God who "had delivered them, and would still confer all needed blessings."

#### Oh, That My People Would Listen (vv11–16)
- **"But my people did not listen to my voice; Israel would not submit to me" (v11).** The whole sad history of Psalm 78 in one line. "Would not submit" is literally "was not willing toward me." They did not want him.
- **"So I gave them over to their stubborn hearts, to follow their own counsels" (v12).** "Stubborn hearts" is the phrase Jeremiah uses again and again (Jer 7:24; 9:14). This is one of the most frightening judgments in Scripture: God simply lets people have what they insist on. Paul describes the same judgment three times in Romans 1: "God gave them up" (Rom 1:24, 26, 28). Stephen, retelling Israel's history, says "God turned away and gave them over" (Acts 7:42).
- **"Oh, that my people would listen to me, that Israel would walk in my ways!" (v13).** God's longing is laid bare. This is not a cold judge but a grieved father (Deut 5:29, "Oh that they had such a heart as this"; Isa 48:18, "Oh that you had paid attention to my commandments! Then your peace would have been like a river"). Jesus wept over Jerusalem with the same heart: "How often would I have gathered your children together… and you were not willing!" (Matt 23:37).
- **"I would soon subdue their enemies and turn my hand against their foes" (v14).** "Soon" is the key word. Their long troubles were not God's weakness but the cost of their refusal. The Tyndale notes make this point: Israel's failures were not caused by any lack of power in God.
- **"Those who hate the LORD would cringe toward him" (v15).** "Cringe" means to come fawning, to pretend submission out of fear (18:44; 66:3). **"Their fate would last forever."** The Hebrew is brief and unclear: literally "their time would be forever." It may mean the enemies' punishment would be lasting, or (as some read) that Israel's own time of blessing would last forever. The ESV takes it of the enemies.
- **"He would feed you with the finest of the wheat, and with honey from the rock I would satisfy you" (v16).** "The finest of the wheat" is literally "the fat of the wheat," the best grain (147:14; Deut 32:14). "Honey from the rock" quotes the Song of Moses: "he made him suck honey out of the rock" (Deut 32:13). Wild bees nested in rock crevices in Canaan, so the picture is real, but it also recalls the rock that gave water in the wilderness (78:15–16). The God who drew water from rock could draw honey from it too. The psalm ends not with judgment but with what might have been, a table spread and waiting.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls being left to one's own counsels one of the most serious judgments God sends, and he reads the ending as a tragedy of blessing missed. He applies the warning against "strange gods" to the idols of wealth, pleasure, status and self. JFB: "Obedience would have secured all promised blessings and the subjection of foes." Henry reminds readers that the psalm, "though spoken primarily of Israel of old, is written for our learning, and is therefore to be sung with application."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 81 has God at a festival calling, "Open your mouth wide, and I will fill it," and grieving, "Oh, that my people would listen to me!" Jesus stood up at the Feast of Booths, the very feast this psalm may have been written for, and cried out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37). He is the God who relieves the shoulder of its burden: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28). He is the bread of life, better than the finest wheat (John 6:35), and he is the Rock from which the wilderness drank (1 Cor 10:4). The Father's voice from the cloud said of him, "This is my beloved Son… listen to him" (Mark 9:7). The longing of v13 is still God's word to his people: listen to his Son, open wide, and be filled.
