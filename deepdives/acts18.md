*Setting:* About AD 50–52, the last stage of Paul's second missionary journey. Paul has been driven out of Thessalonica and Berea and has preached on Mars Hill in Athens with a mixed response (ch 17). He arrives in Corinth alone, low on money and, by his own later account, "in weakness and in fear and much trembling" (1 Cor 2:3). He stays a year and a half, longer than anywhere so far, and plants the church that later received 1 and 2 Corinthians. The chapter then moves quickly: a sea voyage east, a short stop at Ephesus, a visit to Jerusalem and Antioch that closes the second journey, the start of the third, and the arrival of a gifted new preacher named Apollos.

#### Paul in Corinth (vv1–17)
- **Corinth (v1).** The city sat on the narrow isthmus joining mainland Greece to the Peloponnese, with a port on each sea: Lechaeum to the west and Cenchreae to the east. Ships and cargo were dragged across the isthmus on a stone trackway to avoid sailing around the stormy south cape. The Romans destroyed old Corinth in 146 BC, and Julius Caesar refounded it in 44 BC as a Roman colony, settled largely with freed slaves. By Paul's day it was the capital of the province of Achaia, rich, new and mixed. It hosted the Isthmian Games every two years.
- **Corinth's reputation.** Old Greek writers coined the verb "to Corinthianize," meaning to live immorally. The geographer Strabo wrote of a thousand temple prostitutes at the temple of Aphrodite, but he was describing the Greek city before 146 BC, and many scholars doubt the number. The Roman city was likely no worse than other ports, but the letters to the Corinthians show how strong the pull of sexual sin, idol feasts and pride was in the church (1 Cor 5–6; 8–10).
- **"A Jew named Aquila, a native of Pontus… with his wife Priscilla" (v2).** Pontus was on the Black Sea coast of northern Turkey. Paul calls her Prisca, and Luke uses the familiar form Priscilla. In four of the six times the pair are named, she comes first (18:18, 26; Rom 16:3; 2 Tim 4:19), which may mean she was the more prominent, or of higher social rank. They later risked their lives for Paul (Rom 16:3–4) and hosted churches in their home in Ephesus and Rome (1 Cor 16:19; Rom 16:5).
- **"Claudius had commanded all the Jews to leave Rome" (v2).** The Roman historian Suetonius says Claudius expelled the Jews because they kept rioting "at the instigation of Chrestus." Most scholars think this is a garbled reference to Christ, meaning disputes in Rome's synagogues over Jesus. The usual date is AD 49. Luke does not say whether Aquila and Priscilla were already believers. They may have come to faith in Rome, or Paul may have led them to Christ in Corinth.
- **"Because he was of the same trade… tentmakers" (v3).** The word *skēnopoios* means "tentmaker," but tents were usually made of leather or goat-hair cloth, so the trade could include leatherwork and awnings. Rabbis were expected to learn a trade alongside the law, so a scholar like Paul could also work with his hands. Paul supported himself so that no one could say he preached for money (1 Cor 9:12–18; 2 Cor 11:7–9; 1 Thess 2:9). A workshop was also a place to talk with customers.
- **"He reasoned in the synagogue every Sabbath, and tried to persuade Jews and Greeks" (v4).** "Greeks" here are Gentiles who attended the synagogue, the God-fearers. A stone inscription reading "synagogue of the Hebrews" has been found at Corinth, though it may be later than Paul.
- **"When Silas and Timothy arrived from Macedonia, Paul was occupied with the word" (v5).** "Occupied" (*synechō*) means held fast or gripped. They brought good news from Thessalonica (1 Thess 3:6) and probably a gift from Philippi (2 Cor 11:9; Phil 4:15), so Paul could put down his tools and preach full-time. Paul wrote 1 and 2 Thessalonians from Corinth around this time, and both open with "Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy."
- **"He shook out his garments" (v6).** A gesture of breaking off responsibility, like shaking dust from the feet (13:51; Luke 9:5). Nehemiah shook out his garment as a sign of God shaking out the faithless (Neh 5:13).
- **"Your blood be on your own heads! I am innocent" (v6).** Paul takes Ezekiel's words about the watchman. If the watchman warns and the people do not listen, their blood is on their own heads (Ezek 33:4–9). Paul has given the warning. **"From now on I will go to the Gentiles"** means in Corinth. In the next city, he goes to the synagogue first again (18:19; 19:8).
- **"Titius Justus, a worshiper of God… next door to the synagogue" (v7).** Paul moves only a few steps, to a God-fearer's house beside the synagogue. Some think he is the "Gaius" who hosted the whole church (Rom 16:23; 1 Cor 1:14), if his full Roman name was Gaius Titius Justus. That is possible, but only a guess.
- **"Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believed" (v8).** The ruler of the synagogue organized its services and looked after the building. His conversion was a major blow to the opposition. Paul names Crispus as one of the few he baptized with his own hands (1 Cor 1:14). Many ordinary Corinthians followed. Paul later reminds them that "not many of you were wise… not many were of noble birth" (1 Cor 1:26).
- **"Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent" (v9).** The Lord's words suggest Paul was afraid. He had been beaten in Philippi, chased from Thessalonica and Berea, and mocked in Athens. The same pattern seemed to be starting again. Three commands in a row: do not fear, keep speaking, do not go quiet.
- **"For I am with you… for I have many in this city who are my people" (v10).** "I am with you" echoes God's promise to Joshua and Jeremiah (Josh 1:5, 9; Jer 1:8). "No one will attack you to harm you" is not a promise of no opposition, which comes at once in v12. It is a promise that no attack will succeed. "My people" is *laos*, the word for Israel (see 15:14). In this famously pagan city, the Lord says he already has many people who have not yet heard. That is why Paul must keep speaking.
- **"He stayed a year and six months" (v11).** This was his longest stay so far, and it laid the foundation of the church. Paul later writes, "I planted, Apollos watered" (1 Cor 3:6).
- **"When Gallio was proconsul of Achaia" (v12).** Lucius Junius Gallio was the elder brother of the Stoic philosopher Seneca, the tutor of Nero. Seneca describes him as gentle and well liked. An inscription found at Delphi, a letter from Emperor Claudius, dates his time as governor to about AD 51–52. This is one of the most important fixed dates for Paul's life. It places Paul in Corinth around AD 50–52.
- **"Brought him before the tribunal" (v12).** The *bēma* was a raised stone platform in the city's forum where the governor sat to judge. Its remains can still be seen in the ruins of Corinth. Paul later writes that "we must all appear before the judgment seat (*bēma*) of Christ" (2 Cor 5:10), using the same word.
- **"Persuading people to worship God contrary to the law" (v13).** The charge is vague on purpose. "The law" could mean Roman law, making Paul a public danger, or Jewish law, making it an internal dispute. Judaism was a recognized religion. The accusers wanted Rome to declare that Paul's preaching was not Judaism and so was not protected.
- **"Questions about words and names and your own law" (vv14–15).** Gallio does not even let Paul speak. He rules there is no crime here, only a religious argument among Jews. "Names" may refer to the dispute over whether Jesus is "the Christ." In effect a senior Roman governor treated Christianity as part of Judaism. That ruling gave the church a kind of legal shelter for years to come, until Nero's persecution in AD 64.
- **"They all seized Sosthenes… and beat him" (v17).** Sosthenes had apparently replaced Crispus as ruler of the synagogue and led the failed case. "They all" most likely means the Gentile crowd, venting their dislike of Jews, though some manuscripts add "the Greeks" and a few readers think the Jews turned on their own failed spokesman. "Gallio paid no attention" shows his indifference. Paul later writes 1 Corinthians with "our brother Sosthenes" (1 Cor 1:1). It may be the same man, converted after his humiliation, but the name was common.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Paul "a tent-maker, an upholsterer," and notes that although "bred a scholar," he was "master of a handicraft trade." He reads the garment-shaking as a sign meant, "if possible, to affect them with it." JFB ties v2 to the edict "mentioned by Suetonius" and admits that "commentators are much divided" on whether Aquila and Priscilla were already Christians. JFB paints Gallio's attitude as "religious indifference, under the influence of an easy and amiable temper." It thinks it "very improbable" that this Sosthenes is the one in 1 Corinthians 1:1. Guzik is more hopeful that he is the same man and later believed. Guzik also reads Paul's fear into the vision's words "do not be afraid," and treats Gallio's ruling as an important legal protection for the church.

#### Paul Returns to Antioch (vv18–23)
- **"Paul stayed many days longer" (v18).** The court victory gave him freedom to keep working. When he left it was by choice, not because he was driven out.
- **"At Cenchreae he had cut his hair, for he was under a vow" (v18).** Cenchreae was Corinth's eastern port, about seven miles away, and later had its own church, served by Phoebe (Rom 16:1). Grammatically "he" could be Aquila, but most take it as Paul, the subject of the sentence. The vow was probably a private Nazirite-type vow of thanks or dedication, kept by not cutting the hair for a time (Num 6:1–21). Some think the hair was cut at the start of the vow, others at the end. A full Nazirite vow ended with an offering at the temple, which may be one reason Paul headed for Jerusalem.
- **Why a vow?** It shows Paul did not reject Jewish practice. He refused to make it a requirement for Gentiles, but he could still keep it himself as a free act of devotion (1 Cor 9:20). He does something similar in 21:23–26.
- **"They came to Ephesus, and he left them there" (v19).** Priscilla and Aquila stay in Ephesus, the capital of Asia, where the Spirit had earlier closed the door to Paul (16:6). They prepare the ground for his return.
- **"When they asked him to stay for a longer period, he declined" (v20).** For once a synagogue asks Paul to stay, and he says no. Some later manuscripts add Paul's words, "I must by all means keep this coming feast in Jerusalem," and the King James includes them. The oldest manuscripts lack them, but they may give the right reason for his haste.
- **"I will return to you if God wills" (v21).** He kept the promise and spent nearly three years there (19:1–20:1; 20:31). Paul holds his plans loosely under God's will (1 Cor 4:19; James 4:15).
- **"He went up and greeted the church, and then went down to Antioch" (v22).** From Caesarea, "went up" is the regular expression for going up to Jerusalem, high in the hills. So this probably means a short visit to the mother church. Luke compresses a journey of many hundreds of miles into one sentence. Antioch was his sending church (13:1–3). This ends the second missionary journey.
- **"Went from one place to the next through the region of Galatia and Phrygia, strengthening all the disciples" (v23).** After "some time" in Antioch, the third journey begins, again first by visiting the churches. Luke covers it in a single verse. Paul's care for young churches is constant (14:22; 15:41; 16:5).
- **Commentators:** JFB holds that it was Paul who cut his hair and suggests the vow was a voluntary one made in a time of trouble, not a full Nazirite vow, which would explain his haste to reach Jerusalem. It takes "gone up" in v22 to mean Jerusalem, Paul's fourth visit there since his conversion, and thinks the feast was probably Pentecost. Guzik also takes it as a Nazirite vow and Paul's own, and he sees God's earlier "no" to Asia (16:6) turning into a "not yet" now that Paul reaches Ephesus. The Tyndale notes put the end of the hair-growing at Cenchreae, at the completion of the vow.

#### Apollos Speaks Boldly in Ephesus (vv24–28)
- **"A Jew named Apollos, a native of Alexandria" (v24).** Alexandria in Egypt was the second city of the empire and the intellectual capital of the Jewish world outside Judea. It was home to a large Jewish community, the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), and the Jewish philosopher Philo, who lived there in this period. Apollos's name comes from the god Apollo, a common name that says nothing about his faith.
- **"An eloquent man, competent in the Scriptures" (v24).** "Eloquent" (*logios*) can mean either well-spoken or learned, and both fit. "Competent" means mighty: he knew his Bible deeply.
- **"Instructed in the way of the Lord… he spoke and taught accurately the things concerning Jesus" (v25).** "Instructed" is *katēcheō*, the root of "catechism," to teach by word of mouth. Apollos had been taught the true story of Jesus. **"Fervent in spirit"** is literally "boiling in spirit" (*zeō*). It may mean his own spirit, burning with zeal, or the Holy Spirit. Paul uses the same phrase in Romans 12:11.
- **"Though he knew only the baptism of John" (v25).** He knew John's call to repent and prepare for the Messiah, and that Jesus was the one. But apparently he did not know the full meaning of Jesus' death and resurrection, Christian baptism into his name, or the Spirit poured out at Pentecost. How he came to know so much and so little is not explained. The twelve disciples in 19:1–7 seem to be in a similar position.
- **"When Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside" (v26).** They did not correct him in public or shame him in front of the synagogue. They took him aside, perhaps into their home. A tentmaking couple taught the brilliant Alexandrian, and he was humble enough to listen. Priscilla is named first again, and she took part in the teaching.
- **"Explained to him the way of God more accurately" (v26).** "Accurately" (*akribōs*) picks up v25. He taught accurately what he knew, and they explained it more accurately. Nothing he knew was wrong. It was incomplete.
- **"The brothers encouraged him and wrote to the disciples to welcome him" (v27).** This is an early letter of recommendation, a common practice among the churches (Rom 16:1–2; 2 Cor 3:1). Achaia means Corinth, where Paul had planted.
- **"He greatly helped those who through grace had believed" (v27).** "Through grace" may go with "believed" (they came to faith by grace) or with "helped" (Apollos helped them by God's grace). Either way grace is the source.
- **"He powerfully refuted the Jews in public, showing by the Scriptures that the Christ was Jesus" (v28).** He did in Corinth what Paul had done (v5), the same message in the same words. Sadly, some in Corinth later turned his gifts into a party: "I follow Apollos" (1 Cor 1:12; 3:4). Paul refused to see him as a rival. "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Cor 3:6), and he urged Apollos to return to Corinth (1 Cor 16:12). Martin Luther suggested Apollos as the author of Hebrews, and some still do, but that is a guess.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Apollos "a lively affectionate preacher; as he had a good head, so he had a good heart." He describes Priscilla and Aquila as setting out "the way of salvation by Jesus Christ, more perfectly." Guzik explains "fervent in spirit" as "boiling" in spirit, which the lexicon confirms (*zeō*, "to boil, be hot"). Guzik praises Apollos's humility in being taught by a tentmaking couple, and mentions the guess that he wrote Hebrews without pressing it. JFB reads "taught diligently" as "accurately," which modern translations follow.

#### Pointing to Christ
A frightened apostle hears the risen Lord say, "I am with you… I have many in this city who are my people." Corinth looked like the last place on earth for a church, but Christ already counted people there as his before any of them believed. The Roman governor dismissed the case as a quarrel about "words and names," but that name was the whole point. The chapter ends with the gospel passing through ordinary hands, tentmakers teaching a scholar and a scholar strengthening a church. Christ builds his church through his promise and his people.
