*Setting:* Early in David's reign over all Israel, probably soon after 1000 BC. David has just been anointed king by all the tribes, captured Jerusalem and made it his capital, and twice defeated the Philistines (ch 5). Now he wants God at the center of the new kingdom. The ark of the covenant was the gold-covered chest holding the tablets of the law, with the mercy seat and two golden cherubim on top (Exod 25:10–22). It was the symbol of God's throne among his people. Decades earlier, perhaps seventy years or more, in Eli's day, the Philistines captured it in battle (1 Sam 4). It brought plagues on their cities, so they sent it back on a new cart (1 Sam 5–6). It then sat for decades in a private house at Kiriath-jearim, almost forgotten through Saul's reign (1 Sam 7:1–2; 1 Chr 13:3). This chapter tells how it came to Jerusalem: a first attempt that ended in death, and a second that ended in joy. 1 Chronicles 13 and 15–16 tell the same story in more detail.

#### The Ark Brought to Jerusalem (vv1–15)
- **"David again gathered all the chosen men of Israel, thirty thousand" (v1).** This is a national event. Chronicles adds that David first consulted the leaders and the whole assembly agreed (1 Chr 13:1–4). The army may also have been needed because Philistine territory was close by.
- **"From Baale-judah" (v2).** This is another name for Kiriath-jearim, about nine miles west of Jerusalem (Josh 15:9; 1 Chr 13:6).
- **"The ark of God, which is called by the name of the LORD of hosts who sits enthroned on the cherubim" (v2).** The long title shows how much the ark meant. God's "name" stands for his presence. "Enthroned on the cherubim" pictures the cherubim on the ark's lid as the sides of God's throne, with the ark as his footstool (1 Chr 28:2; Ps 99:1). David is bringing the true King into the city of the earthly king.
- **"They carried the ark of God on a new cart" (v3).** This is the mistake. A new cart was a sign of honor, and it was how the Philistines had sent the ark back (1 Sam 6:7). But God's law said the ark was to be carried on poles on the shoulders of Levites from the clan of Kohath (Exod 25:14; Num 4:15; 7:9). Even they were not to touch the holy things, "lest they die" (Num 4:15). David followed the Philistines' method, not God's.
- **"Uzzah and Ahio, the sons of Abinadab" (v3).** Abinadab's son Eleazar had been set apart to guard the ark (1 Sam 7:1). "Sons" may mean grandsons here, since the ark had been in the house for many years. Uzzah's name comes from a word for "strength." "Ahio" may simply mean "his brother."
- **"Celebrating before the LORD, with songs and lyres and harps and tambourines and castanets and cymbals" (v5).** The worship is wholehearted and loud. "Castanets" translates a rare word that probably means a rattle or sistrum, a handheld frame with jingling metal pieces. The problem is not the joy. It is that the joy is built on disobedience.
- **"The threshing floor of Nacon" (v6).** A threshing floor was a flat, hard open space where grain was beaten out. Chronicles calls it "the threshing floor of Chidon" (1 Chr 13:9).
- **"Uzzah put out his hand to the ark of God and took hold of it, for the oxen stumbled" (v6).** It seems like a natural reflex. The cart lurches, and he reaches to steady the ark.
- **"God struck him down there because of his error" (v7).** This is one of the hardest verses in the Old Testament. "Error" translates a Hebrew word found only here, and its meaning is uncertain. Chronicles says simply that God struck him "because he put out his hand to the ark" (1 Chr 13:10). Several things help explain it. The law was clear and the penalty was stated (Num 4:15). The Philistines, who did not know God's law, were punished with plagues but not wiped out, while the men of Beth-shemesh who looked into the ark died (1 Sam 6:19). Uzzah's family had kept the ark for years and should have known better than anyone. And God was teaching Israel at the very start of the new kingdom that he is holy and cannot be handled like a trophy or a lucky charm (compare 1 Sam 4:3). This does not make the story easy, but it shows it is not random. Nadab and Abihu (Lev 10:1–3) and Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5:1–11) are similar warnings at the start of new eras.
- **"David was angry because the LORD had broken out against Uzzah" (v8).** David's response is honest and human. He is angry, perhaps at God, perhaps at himself, perhaps at both. "Broken out" is *parats*, the same word as in the previous chapter, when the LORD "broke through" the Philistines at Baal-perazim (5:20). The God who broke out against Israel's enemies now breaks out against Israel. The place is named **Perez-uzzah**, "the breaking out against Uzzah."
- **"How can the ark of the LORD come to me?" (v9).** David is afraid. It is the right question, and the chapter gives the answer. Chronicles shows David finding it in the law: "Because you did not carry it the first time, the LORD our God broke out against us, because we did not seek him according to the rule" (1 Chr 15:13).
- **"The house of Obed-edom the Gittite" (v10).** "Gittite" usually means a man from Gath, a Philistine city, and some think Obed-edom was a Philistine who had joined David. But Chronicles lists an Obed-edom among the Levites who served the ark (1 Chr 15:18, 24; 16:38; 26:4–8), so many think he came from Gath-rimmon, a Levite town (Josh 21:24–25). The name means "servant of Edom."
- **"The LORD blessed Obed-edom and all his household" (v11).** The same ark that brought death to Uzzah brought blessing to Obed-edom. God's presence is both a danger and a gift. It depends on how one comes near. Chronicles later counts Obed-edom's family as large: "for God blessed him" (1 Chr 26:4–5).
- **"So David went and brought up the ark of God… with rejoicing" (v12).** Three months later, David tries again, and this time it is done properly. Chronicles makes it clear that the Levites carried the ark "on their shoulders with the poles" (1 Chr 15:15), and that the priests and Levites purified themselves first (15:12–14).
- **"When those who bore the ark of the LORD had gone six steps, he sacrificed an ox and a fattened animal" (v13).** Note the change: "those who bore," not a cart. After the first six steps, when it was clear God was not angry, David stopped and offered sacrifice. Some think he sacrificed every six steps all the way to Jerusalem, but the text more naturally means once, at the start. Chronicles says they sacrificed "seven bulls and seven rams" because "God helped the Levites" (1 Chr 15:26).
- **"David danced before the LORD with all his might" (v14).** The word means to whirl or spin. Dancing was a normal part of joyful worship in Israel (Exod 15:20; Ps 149:3; 150:4). The king does not stand apart from the people. He leads them in worship.
- **"David was wearing a linen ephod" (v14).** An ephod was a priestly garment, a kind of apron or short tunic (1 Sam 2:18, where the boy Samuel wears one). David has laid aside his royal robes and wears simple linen like the Levites (1 Chr 15:27). It was not indecent, but it was humble, and it was short, which is what Michal will mock.
- **"With shouting and with the sound of the horn" (v15).** The horn is the *shofar*, the ram's horn. Its blast was used to announce a king (15:10; 1 Kgs 1:34). Here it announces the LORD entering his city as King. Many readers connect Psalm 24, "Lift up your heads, O gates… that the King of glory may come in," with this day, and Psalm 132 recalls David's longing to find "a dwelling place for the Mighty One of Jacob."
- **Commentators:** All three see the cart as the root of the disaster. Henry: "The priests should have carried the ark upon their shoulders." The Philistines might use a cart, but "if Israelites do so, it is at their peril, because this was not what God appointed." JFB calls it "a hasty and inconsiderate procedure, in violation of an express statute," and adds that Uzzah, as a Levite, "well instructed, suffered death for his breach of the law," while the Philistines had sinned in ignorance. Henry finds the root of Uzzah's act in his heart: "God saw presumption and irreverence in Uzzah's heart." Guzik lists the wrong assumptions behind Uzzah's reach: that it did not matter who carried the ark or how, that God could not take care of it himself, and that Uzzah's hand was cleaner than the dirt of the threshing floor. Guzik also reads the meaning of the brothers' names as a picture of a showy, self-driven effort, though the name meanings are less certain than his use of them suggests. Henry: "The same hand that punished Uzzah's proud presumption, rewarded Obed-edom's humble boldness." JFB thinks Obed-edom was a Levite from Gath-rimmon, and says David danced "divested of his royal mantle."

#### Michal Despises David (vv16–23)
- **"Michal the daughter of Saul looked out of the window" (v16).** She is called "the daughter of Saul" three times in this scene (vv16, 20, 23), and never "David's wife." The narrator wants us to see her as belonging to the old house. She once loved David (1 Sam 18:20) and once let him down through a window to save his life (1 Sam 19:12). Now she looks down at him through a window and "despised him in her heart."
- **Why did she despise him?** The text does not say, but there are clues. She was a king's daughter and may have found his behavior undignified. She had been taken from Paltiel, the husband who wept for her (3:15–16), and returned to a man with many other wives. And David's triumph was the final end of her father's house. Saul never brought the ark up. David does it with joy.
- **"Inside the tent that David had pitched for it" (v17).** This was a new tent in Jerusalem. The old tabernacle from Moses' day was still standing at Gibeon (1 Chr 16:39; 21:29), where sacrifices continued. **"Burnt offerings and peace offerings."** Burnt offerings were wholly burned as an act of devotion. Peace offerings were shared meals, with part going to God and part eaten by the worshipers. They fit a day of fellowship and joy.
- **"He blessed the people in the name of the LORD of hosts" (v18).** Kings sometimes did this at great national events (1 Kgs 8:14, 55). Then he gives every person, men and women alike, bread, meat (the ESV's "portion of meat" translates an uncertain word) and a cake of raisins (v19). It is a feast for the whole nation.
- **"David returned to bless his household" (v20).** He comes home to share the blessing, and Michal meets him at the door with contempt.
- **"How the king of Israel honored himself today" (v20).** Her words drip with sarcasm. She accuses him of "uncovering himself" in front of the servant girls, like one of "the vulgar fellows," that is, worthless, empty men. She seems to mean that the short linen ephod exposed him as he danced. The Hebrew word for "honored" is from *kavod*, "glory" or "weight." Her point is that he made himself cheap.
- **"It was before the LORD, who chose me above your father and above all his house" (v21).** David's reply is sharp. He says three times that it was all "before the LORD." He did not dance for the women. He danced for God. And he reminds her, painfully, that God chose him over her father.
- **"I will make myself yet more contemptible than this" (v22).** David is not ashamed of humbling himself before God. If worshiping the LORD makes him look foolish to proud people, he will be more foolish still. The servant girls she sneered at would honor him for it.
- **"Michal the daughter of Saul had no child to the day of her death" (v23).** The chapter ends with this flat sentence. It does not say whether God closed her womb as a judgment or whether David simply never slept with her again. Either way, there would be no son of David and Saul together. Saul's line would not share David's throne. (2 Samuel 21:8 mentions five sons of "Michal," but most manuscripts and most scholars think it should read "Merab," her sister, as in 1 Samuel 18:19.)
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Exercises of religion appear mean in the eyes of those who have little or no religion themselves." He notes that "David was contented to justify himself, and he did not further reprove or blame Michal's insolence; but God punished her." JFB says Michal, proud of her royal birth, mocked David for acting "more like a buffoon than a king," and calls David's reply a mark of "warm piety and gratitude." Guzik thinks Michal's childlessness was more likely the result of David no longer living with her as a husband than a direct judgment from God, but he still treats it as a warning: a critical, scornful spirit toward worship makes a life barren. Henry adds a broader lesson for worship: "We ought to serve God with our whole body and soul, and with every endowment and power we possess."

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter holds two truths together. God is so holy that touching his throne carelessly brought death. And God is so good that his presence brought blessing to a household and joy to a whole nation. Both are true of Jesus. In him "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). The holy presence of God came near in a body that could be touched, and people who touched him in faith were not struck down but healed (Mark 5:27–29). He could make that approach safe because he himself is the mercy seat, the place where sin is covered by blood (Rom 3:25, where "propitiation" is the same word used for the mercy seat in Heb 9:5). And like David, the Son of David laid aside his royal glory and took the form of a servant (Phil 2:7), was despised for it (Isa 53:3), and was exalted by God.
