*Setting:* Psalm 107 opens Book 5 of the Psalter (Pss 107–150), and it opens it as an answer. Book 4 ended with a scattered people praying, "Save us, O LORD our God, and gather us from among the nations" (106:47). Book 5 begins, "Let the redeemed of the LORD say so, whom he has… gathered in from the lands" (vv2–3). The prayer has been heard. The psalm has no title or author, and most scholars place it after the return from the Babylonian exile (from 538 BC). It is built like a song with verses and a chorus. Four pictures of people in trouble follow the same pattern: their distress, their cry, God's rescue, and a call to thank him. The two refrains, "Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he delivered them from their distress" and "Let them thank the LORD for his steadfast love, for his wondrous works to the children of man," come four times each. The pictures may describe the exiles' experience in figures, or real kinds of people who joined in thanksgiving at the temple, or both. They are deliberately general, so that anyone rescued from anything can find themselves in them. The psalm then widens to God's rule over land and nations and ends with a call to wisdom. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's own structure.

#### Let the Redeemed of the LORD Say So (vv1–3)
- **"Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever!" (v1).** This is the same opening as Psalm 106 and Psalms 118 and 136. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love, and it is the key word of the whole psalm, appearing in each refrain and in the last line (v43).
- **"Let the redeemed of the LORD say so" (v2).** Thanks should be spoken aloud. Being rescued and keeping quiet about it is not an option. "Redeemed" is *ga'al*, the work of the kinsman-redeemer, who bought back family members from slavery or debt. Isaiah uses the same phrase for the exiles coming home: "the redeemed shall walk there. And the ransomed of the LORD shall return and come to Zion with singing" (Isa 35:9–10).
- **"Whom he has redeemed from trouble" (v2).** "Trouble" here is literally "the hand of the adversary," and the same word for distress (*tsar*) returns in each refrain (vv6, 13, 19, 28).
- **"And gathered in from the lands, from the east and from the west, from the north and from the south" (v3).** The exiles were spread across the Babylonian and Persian empires and beyond. The Hebrew for "south" is literally "from the sea," which is odd, since the sea (the Mediterranean) is west of Israel. It may refer to the Red Sea to the south, or simply mean "from every direction." Jesus used similar language: "many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham" (Matt 8:11).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the psalm shows that God "is not only King of saints, but King of nations, not only the God of Israel, but the God of the whole earth." JFB links "the redeemed" to Isaiah 35:9–10 and reads v3 as "alluding to the dispersion of captives throughout the Babylonian empire." Guzik, citing Romaine, explains the kinsman-redeemer's duty to buy back a relative sold or captured, and notes Poole's point that "south" is literally "the sea."

#### Some Wandered in Desert Wastes (vv4–9)
- **"Some wandered in desert wastes, finding no way to a city to dwell in" (v4).** The first picture is of travellers lost in the desert. Between Babylon and Judah lay hundreds of miles of desert, and caravans could lose the track. "A city to dwell in" means a settled, inhabited place: safety, water, food, people. They cannot find one.
- **"Hungry and thirsty, their soul fainted within them" (v5).** In the desert, losing the way means death. "Soul" (*nephesh*) here is the whole living person, and it is giving out.
- **"Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he delivered them from their distress" (v6).** The first refrain. Nothing in the psalm suggests these travellers were especially godly. They simply cried out. The Tyndale notes observe that the trouble is left general so that readers in many kinds of trouble can identify with it.
- **"He led them by a straight way till they reached a city to dwell in" (v7).** Wandering is replaced by a straight road. God leads like a shepherd (Ps 23:3, "paths of righteousness," literally "straight tracks"). Isaiah promised a highway through the desert for the returning exiles (Isa 40:3–4).
- **"Let them thank the LORD for his steadfast love, for his wondrous works to the children of man!" (v8).** The second refrain. "Children of man" is humanity in general. God's kindness is not limited to Israel.
- **"For he satisfies the longing soul, and the hungry soul he fills with good things" (v9).** Each picture ends with a verse giving the reason for thanks. This one is echoed in Mary's song: "he has filled the hungry with good things" (Luke 1:53). Jesus said, "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied" (Matt 5:6), and "whoever comes to me shall not hunger" (John 6:35).
- **Commentators:** Henry lists this first picture as God's care for his people in their scattering. JFB reads vv4–7 as a vivid picture of the hardships of exiles returning to Jerusalem, and "city of habitation" as possibly the land itself. Guzik notes that the psalm calls people to thank God for his works toward others, not only for their own blessings, and suggests that Mary's song shows how deeply she knew this psalm. The Tyndale notes suggest the wilderness may also picture leaving the path of wisdom (Prov 4:10–15).

#### Some Sat in Darkness (vv10–16)
- **"Some sat in darkness and in the shadow of death, prisoners in affliction and in irons" (v10).** The second picture is of prisoners. Ancient prisons were often pits or dungeons without light, as Joseph's and Jeremiah's were (Gen 40:15; Jer 38:6). "Shadow of death" (*tsalmaveth*) is the word in Psalm 23:4, deep darkness, gloom as thick as death. Isaiah promised that "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light; those who dwelt in a land of deep darkness [*tsalmaveth*], on them has light shone" (Isa 9:2), and Matthew applies it to Jesus (Matt 4:16).
- **"For they had rebelled against the words of God, and spurned the counsel of the Most High" (v11).** This group's trouble was their own fault. Israel's exile itself was the result of rejecting God's word through the prophets (2 Chr 36:15–16). Not every prisoner in the Bible is guilty, since Joseph, Jeremiah and Paul were not, but these were.
- **"So he bowed their hearts down with hard labor; they fell down, with none to help" (v12).** God used their suffering to humble them. Prisoners in the ancient world were often put to forced labour (Judg 16:21; Lam 5:13). "None to help" is the bottom: no friend, no advocate.
- **"Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble" (v13).** The same refrain as v6. God hears the guilty as well as the merely unfortunate. Their sin put them in prison, but their cry brought them out.
- **"He brought them out of darkness and the shadow of death, and burst their bonds apart" (v14).** Every element of v10 is reversed: darkness, the shadow of death, chains.
- **"For he shatters the doors of bronze and cuts in two the bars of iron" (v16).** This line comes almost word for word from God's promise to Cyrus, the Persian king who would free the exiles: "I will break in pieces the doors of bronze and cut through the bars of iron" (Isa 45:2). Babylon's gates really were famous for their bronze. The God who promised it did it.
- **Commentators:** JFB pictures "a dark dungeon with doors and bars of metal," and reads the whole as an image of captivity. Guzik passes on Trapp's line that if sinners bring God a praying heart, they will find in him a pitying heart, and Spurgeon's point that Christ breaks the strongest bonds. Clarke, cited by Guzik, says God handed these rebels over to wicked men because they had forsaken him. Henry places this second picture under "imprisonment and captivity."

#### Some Were Fools Through Their Sinful Ways (vv17–22)
- **"Some were fools through their sinful ways" (v17).** The third picture is of the sick. "Fools" (*ewilim*) in the Bible are not people of low intelligence. They are people who despise wisdom and the fear of God (Prov 1:7). Their sickness is linked to their sin, though the psalm does not say exactly how. Some sins damage the body directly, and some sickness came as God's discipline (1 Cor 11:30). The Bible also warns against assuming every illness is caused by sin (John 9:2–3).
- **"They loathed any kind of food, and they drew near to the gates of death" (v18).** Losing all appetite is a sign of serious illness. "Gates of death" pictures death as a city with gates, and the sick person standing at them (Job 38:17; Ps 9:13).
- **"Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble" (v19).** The refrain again. Fools who have brought trouble on themselves can still cry out, and God still hears.
- **"He sent out his word and healed them, and delivered them from their destruction" (v20).** God heals by speaking. His word created the world (Gen 1:3; Ps 33:6) and his word heals. The centurion said to Jesus, "only say the word, and my servant will be healed" (Matt 8:8), and Jesus did. John calls Jesus himself the Word (John 1:1, 14). "Destruction" may be "pits" or "graves," the place where they were headed.
- **"Let them offer sacrifices of thanksgiving, and tell of his deeds in songs of joy!" (v22).** The thank offering (*todah*) was a sacrifice brought after a rescue, eaten in a shared meal with family and friends (Lev 7:11–15). It was a public celebration, not a private feeling. The New Testament speaks of offering "a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15).
- **Commentators:** JFB says simply, "sin brings its own punishment," often illness, and that God heals by his word. Guzik, citing Kidner, compares the self-inflicted affliction to modern self-destructive habits like addiction. He draws the spiritual lesson that a soul with no appetite for God's word is in danger, and ties v20 to Jesus healing by a word. He also recounts the Scottish reformer George Wishart preaching on v20 to plague-stricken Dundee in 1544. Henry places this picture under sickness.

#### Some Went Down to the Sea in Ships (vv23–32)
- **"Some went down to the sea in ships, doing business on the great waters" (v23).** The fourth picture is of sailors, and it is the longest. Israelites were mostly not a seafaring people and many feared the sea, but traders sailed from Joppa and Ezion-geber (1 Kgs 9:26; Jonah 1:3). "Went down" fits the view from the hills down to the coast.
- **"They saw the deeds of the LORD, his wondrous works in the deep" (v24).** Sailors see things landsmen never see: the vastness of the sea, its creatures, and its storms.
- **"For he commanded and raised the stormy wind" (v25).** The storm is not chance. God commands it, as he "hurled a great wind upon the sea" in Jonah's day (Jonah 1:4). Unlike the other three groups, the sailors are not said to have sinned. Sometimes trouble simply comes, and God is still in it.
- **"They mounted up to heaven; they went down to the depths" (v26).** A small wooden ship climbs a wave and plunges into the trough. **"Their courage melted away"** is literally "their soul melted."
- **"They reeled and staggered like drunken men and were at their wits' end" (v27).** No one can stand on a deck in a storm like that. "At their wits' end" is literally "all their wisdom was swallowed up." Their skill as sailors is useless. That is the point at which they pray.
- **"He made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed" (v29).** God's word calms what his word raised. This is the clearest Old Testament background to Jesus stilling the storm on the Sea of Galilee: "he rebuked the wind and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!' And the wind ceased, and there was a great calm" (Mark 4:39). The disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41). Anyone who knew Psalm 107 knew the answer.
- **"Then they were glad that the waters were quiet, and he brought them to their desired haven" (v30).** "Haven" (*machoz*) occurs only here in the Old Testament and probably means a harbour or trading town. John's Gospel records that after Jesus came to his disciples on the water, "immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going" (John 6:21).
- **"Let them extol him in the congregation of the people, and praise him in the assembly of the elders" (v32).** Thanks go public again, now before the whole community and its leaders. JFB sees the elders' assembly as the gathering for worship.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of all four perils that "those that cry unto God have ever found him a very present help." JFB says these verses show human help failing while God's help succeeds. Guzik notes VanGemeren's reading of "wits' end" as all their wisdom swallowed up, passes on Clarke's first-hand memory of a storm at sea, and connects v29 directly to Jesus stilling the storm. He adds Horne's thought that everyone is on a stormy sea, bound for a desired haven.

#### He Turns Rivers into a Desert (vv33–38)
- **"He turns rivers into a desert, springs of water into thirsty ground" (v33).** The psalm changes style. The four pictures with refrains are finished, and what follows sounds more like wisdom poetry (compare Job 5 and 12). God rules not only individual rescues but the land itself.
- **"A fruitful land into a salty waste, because of the evil of its inhabitants" (v34).** The obvious example is the Jordan plain around Sodom, once "well watered everywhere like the garden of the LORD" (Gen 13:10), turned into a land of salt (Deut 29:23). Israel's own land was warned it could become like this (Lev 26:32–33). Fertility is not a law of nature running on its own. It is under God's moral rule.
- **"He turns a desert into pools of water, a parched land into springs of water" (v35).** The reverse is just as possible. Isaiah promised exactly this for the returning exiles: "I will make the wilderness a pool of water, and the dry land springs of water" (Isa 41:18; 35:6–7).
- **"And there he lets the hungry dwell, and they establish a city to live in" (v36).** This answers the first picture: those who could find no "city to dwell in" (v4) now build one.
- **"They sow fields and plant vineyards and get a fruitful yield" (v37).** This is the ordinary work of farmers, not a miracle, but its success comes from God.
- **"By his blessing they multiply greatly, and he does not let their livestock diminish" (v38).** Growth in families and flocks was the visible sign of God's blessing (Ps 127:3; Deut 28:4).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in these changes "God's hand is to be eyed by his own people, with joyful acknowledgments of his goodness." JFB says God governs both the land's fertility and the rise and fall of rulers. Guzik, citing Horne, gives the Jordan plain after Sodom as the example of v34, and passes on Morgan's point that God's seemingly opposite acts, drying up and watering, are consistent with one character.

#### Whoever Is Wise, Let Him Attend to These Things (vv39–43)
- **"When they are diminished and brought low through oppression, evil, and sorrow" (v39).** Fortunes can turn the other way. The same people blessed in v38 can be brought low, often by unjust rulers.
- **"He pours contempt on princes and makes them wander in trackless wastes" (v40).** This verse quotes two lines from Job 12:21 and 12:24 almost exactly. Proud rulers end up where the lost travellers of v4 began, wandering with no road. God reverses positions.
- **"But he raises up the needy out of affliction and makes their families like flocks" (v41).** Hannah sang this: "He raises up the poor from the dust; he lifts the needy from the ash heap to make them sit with princes" (1 Sam 2:8). Mary sang it too: "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52).
- **"The upright see it and are glad, and all wickedness shuts its mouth" (v42).** The second line echoes Job 5:16: "the poor have hope, and injustice shuts her mouth." When God acts, the scoffers have nothing more to say.
- **"Whoever is wise, let him attend to these things; let them consider the steadfast love of the LORD" (v43).** The psalm ends like a wisdom book. Hosea closes the same way: "Whoever is wise, let him understand these things" (Hos 14:9). The wise person does not see the events of life as random. Wanderings, prisons, sickness, storms, harvests and the fall of princes all become, to the one who considers them, evidence of God's *chesed*. The last word of the psalm is the word that opened it (v1).
- **Commentators:** Henry ends by saying that when believers are in these or similar distresses, "it will be comfortable to sing this psalm, with application," and that because "we are members one of another," we should thank God for others' rescues as well as our own. JFB says "all who take right views will appreciate God's unfailing mercy." Guzik, citing Kidner, says readers should recognize themselves somewhere in the four pictures and praise God with fresh insight, and he passes on VanGemeren's description of the whole as a wisdom psalm.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 107 shows four kinds of helpless people: lost, imprisoned, dying and drowning. In each case they cry out, and God rescues them by his word and power. In Jesus' ministry all four pictures come to life. He is the Good Shepherd who seeks the lost and leads them home (Luke 15:4–7; John 10:11). He came "to proclaim liberty to the captives" and light to those in darkness (Luke 4:18; Matt 4:16). He healed by a word, even at a distance (Matt 8:8, 13). And in a boat on Galilee he did what the psalm says only the LORD does: he stilled the storm and hushed the waves. The disciples' question, "Who then is this?", is answered by this psalm. And the gathering "from the east and from the west, from the north and from the south" (v3) looks ahead to the great gathering of his people from every nation into his kingdom (Luke 13:29; Rev 7:9).
