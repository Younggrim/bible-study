*Setting:* Jerusalem in the fifth century BC, in the days of Ezra and Nehemiah. Malachi has been arguing God's case with a tired, cynical people. Their priests offered sick animals (ch 1), they broke faith with God and with their wives (ch 2), and at the end of chapter 2 they asked, with a sneer, "Where is the God of justice?" (2:17). Chapter 3 answers that question. The Lord is coming, but first he will send a messenger ahead of him, and his coming will refine and judge. The chapter then turns to the fifth dispute, about robbing God in tithes, and the sixth, about whether serving God is worth it. It ends with a quiet scene: a small group who feared the LORD talking together, and God writing their names in a book. In Hebrew Bibles, chapter 4 is part of chapter 3 (3:19–24), so the chapter runs straight on into "the great day of the Lord." All three Synoptic Gospels apply its first verse to John the Baptist (Matt 11:10; Mark 1:2; Luke 7:27).

#### The Messenger of the Lord (vv1–5)
- **"Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me" (v1).** "My messenger" is *mal'akhi*, the very word that is Malachi's name. The picture is of a herald going ahead of a king to clear the road and announce his arrival, as in Isaiah 40:3: "In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD." Jesus identified this messenger as John the Baptist: "This is he of whom it is written, 'Behold, I send my messenger before your face'" (Matt 11:10; Luke 7:27). Malachi 4:5 names him as "Elijah the prophet," whom Jesus also identified with John (Matt 11:14; 17:10–13).
- **"Before me… before your face."** Malachi has the LORD saying "before *me*." When Mark quotes the verse at the opening of his Gospel, it becomes "before *your* face" (Mark 1:2), spoken as if to Jesus. The way John prepared was the way of the LORD, and the one who came after John was Jesus. The Gospel writers saw in Jesus the coming of the LORD himself.
- **"The Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple" (v1).** "The Lord" here is *ha'adon*, "the Lord" with the article, a title used of God himself (Exod 23:17; Isa 1:24). The temple is "his" temple. The people had asked "Where is the God of justice?" (2:17). The answer is: he is coming in person. "Suddenly" means unexpectedly, at a time they would not be ready for. Jesus was presented in the temple as a baby (Luke 2:22–38), and as a man he came to it and cleansed it (Mark 11:15–17).
- **"And the messenger of the covenant in whom you delight" (v1).** This second messenger is different from the first. He comes with the Lord, or is the Lord himself. Many link him with "the angel of the LORD" who led Israel in the wilderness, of whom God said, "my name is in him" (Exod 23:20–23). Christians have long seen the Messiah here, the one who brings the new covenant (Luke 22:20; Heb 8:6). "In whom you delight" is gently ironic. They said they wanted him. Did they know what his coming would mean?
- **"But who can endure the day of his coming?" (v2).** The people expected God to come and judge the nations around them. Malachi warns that his coming will test Israel first. The same warning runs through Amos 5:18–20: "Why would you have the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light."
- **"For he is like a refiner's fire and like fullers' soap" (v2).** A refiner heated silver or gold in a crucible until the impurities rose to the surface and could be skimmed off. A fuller washed and whitened new cloth using a harsh alkaline soap made from plant ash (compare Jer 2:22). Both images are of cleansing, not of destruction. The fire burns away the dross but keeps the metal. The soap removes the stain but saves the garment.
- **"He will sit as a refiner and purifier of silver" (v3).** The refiner sits, watching patiently and closely, until the work is done. JFB notes that the refiner watches the crucible until he sees his own image reflected in the metal. That observation became a well-known illustration, though the text itself does not say it.
- **"He will purify the sons of Levi" (v3).** The cleansing begins with the priests, the very men condemned in 1:6–2:9. The goal is that "they will bring offerings in righteousness to the LORD." The corrupted priesthood will be restored, not abandoned. The New Testament says Christ has made his people "a royal priesthood" who "offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God" (1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rom 12:1).
- **"As in the days of old and as in former years" (v4).** Malachi looks back to an ideal time, perhaps the days of Moses or of David and Solomon, when worship was faithful. The future restoration is described with the language of the best of the past.
- **"Then I will draw near to you for judgment" (v5).** The people had asked for the God of justice. Here he is. "I will be a swift witness against the sorcerers, against the adulterers, against those who swear falsely." The list draws on the Law: sorcery (Exod 22:18; Deut 18:10), adultery (Exod 20:14), false oaths (Lev 19:12).
- **"Against those who oppress the hired worker in his wages, the widow and the fatherless… the sojourner" (v5).** God takes up the cause of the vulnerable. The Law commanded that a hired worker be paid the same day (Lev 19:13; Deut 24:14–15) and that widows, orphans and foreigners be protected (Exod 22:21–24; Deut 24:17). James repeats the warning to the rich: "the wages of the laborers… are crying out against you" (Jas 5:4). The root of all of these sins is in the last phrase: they "do not fear me."
- **Commentators:** Henry says God "observes a method in his work, and, before he comes, takes care to have his way prepared." He writes, "Christ is the angel of this covenant," and adds that "we cannot offer unto the Lord any right performances in religion unless our persons be justified and sanctified." JFB identifies the first messenger as John the Baptist and says Christ was the messenger of both the old and new covenants. He reads the refining as "the fiery test of His heart-searching truth" from Christ's first coming to the final judgment. He says the purified offering "is not expiatory, but prayer, thanksgiving, and self-dedication." Guzik also identifies the two messengers as John and the Messiah, and he passes on Alden's observation that Malachi, like other prophets, blends the first and second comings of Christ into one view. On the refiner he draws on Spurgeon and F.B. Meyer: Christ saves people from their sins, not in them, and he sits close by and watches the fire himself.

#### Robbing God (vv6–15)
- **"For I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed" (v6).** This is one of the great statements of God's faithfulness in Scripture (compare Num 23:19; Jas 1:17; Heb 13:8). Israel's survival did not depend on Israel. If God changed like they did, they would have been destroyed long ago. "Children of Jacob" recalls 1:2: "I have loved Jacob." Jacob the schemer had been loved anyway, and so were his children.
- **"From the days of your fathers you have turned aside from my statutes" (v7).** The problem is not new. It runs back through their whole history (compare Neh 9:16–31).
- **"Return to me, and I will return to you" (v7).** This is the call of the prophets in one line, almost word for word Zechariah 1:3. God's offer is always open. But the people answer, "How shall we return?" They cannot see that they have gone anywhere.
- **"Will man rob God? Yet you are robbing me" (v8).** The verb *qaba'* is rare. The lexicon gives "rob" but marks it as uncertain, and it appears elsewhere only in Proverbs 22:23. The Greek Old Testament read a word with the same letters in a different order, *'aqab*, "to cheat" or "to supplant." That is the root of the name Jacob (Gen 27:36). So there may be a wordplay with v6: "Will man *Jacob* God?" The children of Jacob were acting like their father the cheat.
- **"In your tithes and contributions" (v8).** The tithe was a tenth of the harvest and herds, given to support the Levites (Num 18:21–24), with the Levites passing a tenth to the priests (Num 18:26). Every third year a tithe went to the poor, the widow and the sojourner (Deut 14:28–29). "Contributions" were other required gifts for the priests. Nehemiah found the Levites gone home to their fields because these were not being paid (Neh 13:10).
- **"You are cursed with a curse… the whole nation of you" (v9).** The covenant warned that disobedience would bring failed crops (Deut 28:38–40). The people were short of food because they were holding back from God, and they were holding back from God because they were short of food. It was a downward spiral.
- **"Bring the full tithe into the storehouse, that there may be food in my house" (v10).** The storehouse was the set of rooms in the temple where tithes were kept (Neh 10:38; 13:12). "Full" means the whole tenth, not a part of it.
- **"And thereby put me to the test" (v10).** Elsewhere Scripture forbids putting God to the test (Deut 6:16; Matt 4:7). This is a rare case where God himself invites it, inside his own covenant and on his own terms. The test is whether God will keep his word.
- **"If I will not open the windows of heaven for you and pour down for you a blessing" (v10).** "Windows of heaven" is the phrase used for the floodgates of the flood (Gen 7:11), and for rain from heaven (2 Kgs 7:2). Here they open in blessing, pouring down rain on dry fields "until there is no more need." This is the covenant blessing of Deuteronomy 28:12: "The LORD will open to you his good treasury, the heavens."
- **Is this a promise of wealth for tithers?** Malachi speaks within the Mosaic covenant, to Israel, about a land whose harvest depended on rain and whose blessings and curses were spelled out in the Law. Using this verse as a formula for personal riches misreads it. Christians differ on whether a tenth is still required. The New Testament never commands a tithe for the church, though Jesus affirmed it for the Pharisees (Matt 23:23). What the New Testament does teach is regular, generous, cheerful giving (1 Cor 16:2; 2 Cor 9:6–8). The lasting principle is that God owns everything, that holding back from him is a matter of the heart, and that he can be trusted.
- **"I will rebuke the devourer for you" (v11).** The "devourer" is probably the locust, the great enemy of farmers (Joel 1:4; Amos 4:9). God would stop the pests. "Your vine in the field shall not fail to bear."
- **"All nations will call you blessed, for you will be a land of delight" (v12).** The nations who had looked down on poor, struggling Judah would see God's favor on it (compare Deut 28:10). It recalls the promise to Abraham that all nations would be blessed through his family (Gen 12:3).
- **"Your words have been hard against me" (v13).** The sixth dispute begins. "Hard" means strong, stubborn or harsh. And once again, the people deny it: "How have we spoken against you?"
- **"It is vain to serve God. What is the profit…?" (v14).** They had kept the rules and gone through the motions of repentance, "walking as in mourning," probably in fasting and sackcloth. They expected payment and saw none. Their religion was a bargain, and the bargain had not paid off. Zechariah had already asked the people whether their fasts had really been for God (Zech 7:5).
- **"And now we call the arrogant blessed" (v15).** "Blessed" is the same word as v12. God promised the nations would call Israel blessed. Instead Israel calls the arrogant blessed. "Evildoers… put God to the test and they escape." God invited the faithful to test him by trusting (v10). The wicked test him by defying him, and they seem to get away with it. This is the complaint of Psalm 73, until the psalmist "went into the sanctuary of God" and "discerned their end" (Ps 73:17).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the people "had run away from their Master, and quitted the work he gave them to do." He notes that "robbing God is such a heinous crime that those who are guilty of it are not willing to own themselves guilty," and that "those that deny God his part of their estates may justly expect a curse upon their own part of them." He adds, "Those that will deal with God must deal upon trust." JFB calls God's unchangeableness "the sheet-anchor of the Church," and says the people "mistake utterly the nature of God's service," treating it as a hired bargain instead of love. On v9 he writes, "As ye despoil Me, so I despoil you," and adds, "No man ever yet lost by serving God with a whole heart." Guzik notes that the New Testament does not explicitly command tithing but speaks well of it, that tithing came before the Law (Gen 14:20), and that New Testament giving is to be planned, proportional, generous and cheerful. He suggests a tenth as a good benchmark and warns against asking how little one can give. He passes on James Montgomery Boice's point that the main subject of the passage is God's faithfulness.

#### The Book of Remembrance (vv16–18)
- **"Then those who feared the LORD spoke with one another" (v16).** Not everyone in Judah was a cynic. A smaller group, those who feared God, heard the same message and responded differently. They did not complain. They talked together, encouraging one another. That is a picture of what Hebrews 10:24–25 urges: "let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works… encouraging one another."
- **"The LORD paid attention and heard them" (v16).** The scoffers thought God did not see. He heard every word, both the hard words of v13 and the faithful words of v16.
- **"A book of remembrance was written before him" (v16).** Persian kings kept records of service done to them. Esther 6:1–3 tells how King Ahasuerus, unable to sleep, had "the book of memorable deeds" read to him and found that Mordecai had never been rewarded. The Hebrew phrase is closely related to the one here. God keeps such a record too. Scripture speaks often of God's books: the book Moses asked to be blotted out of (Exod 32:32), the record of tears (Ps 56:8), the books opened at the judgment (Dan 7:10; Rev 20:12) and "the book of life" (Phil 4:3; Rev 21:27).
- **"Those who feared the LORD and esteemed his name" (v16).** "Esteemed" means to think on, to value. This is the reverse of the priests who "despise my name" (1:6).
- **"They shall be mine… in the day when I make up my treasured possession" (v17).** "Treasured possession" is *segullah*, the word God used at Sinai: "you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples" (Exod 19:5; Deut 7:6). The KJV renders it "jewels." The lexicon defines it as valued property, a king's private treasure (1 Chr 29:3). Peter applies the same idea to the church, "a people for his own possession" (1 Pet 2:9; Titus 2:14).
- **"I will spare them as a man spares his son who serves him" (v17).** The chapter returns to the picture of 1:6, "A son honors his father." Those who honored God as Father would be treated as sons. "Spare" means to have compassion on. In the day of judgment they will be shown mercy.
- **"Then once more you shall see the distinction between the righteous and the wicked" (v18).** The scoffers said there was no difference between serving God and not serving him (v14). God says a day is coming when the difference will be plain to everyone. Jesus' parables of the wheat and weeds and of the sheep and goats teach the same (Matt 13:24–30; 25:31–46).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the fear of the Lord "the beginning of wisdom and the root of all religion" and writes, "The saints are God's jewels." He answers the scoffers: "Wait awhile," and the outcome of the wicked will be seen. JFB describes this fear as "reverential and loving fear, not slavish terror." He says the record is kept "against the day when those found faithful among the faithless shall receive their final reward," and gives a practical warning about the need for believers to meet together: "Coals separated soon go out." Guzik links the book of remembrance to God's other books in Scripture and says being God's treasured possession means being valued and kept, whatever one's feelings may say.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Lord whom they sought came suddenly to his temple, just as Malachi said. A messenger prepared his way in the wilderness, and then Jesus came, first as a baby carried in by his parents and later as the one who cleansed the temple. He came as a refiner, and he still is: he purifies a people for himself, making them priests who offer what pleases God (Titus 2:14; 1 Pet 2:5). He is the unchanging one, "the same yesterday and today and forever" (Heb 13:8), and that is why the children of Jacob, and all who belong to him, are not consumed. He knows those who are his and writes their names in the book of life (Rev 21:27). On the last day, the distinction between those who serve God and those who do not will be plain, and his treasured possession will be his forever.
