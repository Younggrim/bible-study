*Setting:* Moses' first meeting with Pharaoh has gone badly. Pharaoh answered "Who is the LORD?" by taking away the straw for Israel's bricks and keeping the quota (5:2, 7–8). The Israelite foremen were beaten and blamed Moses, and Moses brought his complaint straight to God: "Why did you ever send me?… You have not delivered your people at all" (5:22–23). Chapter 6 is God's answer. He doesn't explain the delay. He tells Moses again who he is and what he will do. Then the story pauses for a family record that shows who Moses and Aaron are.

#### God Promises Deliverance (vv1–13)
- **"Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh" (v1).** God doesn't rebuke Moses. He turns his attention to the future. **"With a strong hand he will send them out… drive them out."** Pharaoh refused to "let" Israel go (5:2). Before long he will be desperate to get rid of them (12:31–33).
- **"I am the LORD" (v2).** God introduces himself by name, and the phrase frames the whole speech: it opens v2, comes again in v6, closes v8, and returns in v29. Everything God promises rests on who he is.
- **"As God Almighty, but by my name the LORD I did not make myself known to them" (v3).** "God Almighty" is *El Shaddai*, the name God used with Abraham and Jacob (Gen 17:1; 35:11). Yet the name *YHWH* ("the LORD") is already common in Genesis, and Abraham even called a place "The LORD will provide" (Gen 22:14). How can God say they did not know it? The most common answer is that "know" here means to know by experience. The patriarchs knew the name and received the promises, but they never saw God *as the LORD* keep them, rescuing a nation and giving it the land. Moses' generation would. Another view is that Moses, writing Genesis, used the name the reader knows. JFB reads the line as a question. Moses' own mother's name, Jochebed (v20), seems to contain a short form of the divine name ("Yah is glory"), which supports the first view.
- **"I also established my covenant… I have heard… I have remembered" (vv4–5).** God repeats the language of 2:24. The covenant with Abraham promised the land "in which they lived as sojourners" (Gen 17:8). What God promised the fathers, he will now do for the children.
- **"I will bring you out… deliver you… redeem you… take you" (vv6–7).** These are the heart of the chapter: seven "I will" promises in three verses, set between "I am the LORD" at either end. Jewish tradition took the first four verbs (bring out, deliver, redeem, take) as the reason for the four cups of wine at the Passover meal. Jesus took one of those cups at the Last Supper and called it "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20).
- **"I will redeem you" (v6).** "Redeem" is *ga'al*, the work of the *go'el*, the "kinsman-redeemer." In Israel's law the nearest relative bought back a family member sold into slavery, bought back lost land, or avenged a murdered relative (Lev 25:25, 48–49; Num 35:19). Boaz is the famous example (Ruth 3–4). God calls himself Israel's next of kin, the family member who pays to set them free. Isaiah later names him "your Redeemer, the Holy One of Israel" (Isa 41:14).
- **"With an outstretched arm and with great acts of judgment" (v6).** Egyptian art shows Pharaoh with his arm raised to strike his enemies. Here the arm that strikes belongs to the LORD. This phrase becomes the standard way Israel remembers the exodus (Deut 4:34; Ps 136:12).
- **"I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God" (v7).** This is the covenant formula, the heart of God's relationship with his people. It runs through the whole Bible (Lev 26:12; Jer 31:33; 2 Cor 6:16) and ends at its last page: "They will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God" (Rev 21:3). Redemption is for relationship. **"You shall know that I am the LORD your God"** answers Pharaoh's "I do not know the LORD" (5:2). Israel will come to know him too.
- **"I will bring you into the land… I will give it to you for a possession" (v8).** The promise doesn't end in the wilderness. "Swore" is literally "lifted my hand," the gesture of taking an oath.
- **"They did not listen to Moses, because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery" (v9).** "Broken spirit" is literally "shortness of spirit," the feeling of someone with no breath and no patience left. They aren't rebellious here so much as crushed. God doesn't reject them for it. He goes on with his plan, and they will see it before they believe it.
- **"How then shall Pharaoh listen to me, for I am of uncircumcised lips?" (v12).** An uncircumcised thing in Hebrew is closed, unfit, not set apart for God. Jeremiah speaks of "uncircumcised" ears that cannot hear (Jer 6:10). Moses again says he cannot speak well (4:10). After the beating of the foremen, his confidence is gone.
- **"But the LORD spoke to Moses and Aaron and gave them a charge" (v13).** God doesn't argue. He gives the order again, now to both brothers.
- **Commentators:** Henry explains v3 well: "The patriarchs knew this name, but they did not know him in this matter by that which this name signifies." Guzik takes the same line: the fathers knew the LORD as the one who made the covenant, and this generation would know him as the one who keeps it. Guzik counts seven "I will" promises and contrasts them with the boastful "I will" statements of Isaiah 14:13–14. He also says the verbs are past-tense in form to show certainty. In Hebrew, though, these are the normal narrative forms for future action (a verb with "and" in front), so that point shouldn't be pressed. On v9 Henry writes, "Disconsolate spirits often put from them the comforts they are entitled to, and stand in their own light." JFB says the harsher treatment "entirely crushed their spirits" and calls "uncircumcised lips" "a metaphorical expression" for unfitness.

#### The Genealogy of Moses and Aaron (vv14–30)
- **Why a family list here?** The story stops just as Moses is sent back to Pharaoh. The list shows who Moses and Aaron are: real Israelites, from a real family, with a right to speak for the people. Henry says it shows "they were Israelites, bone of their bone and flesh of their flesh." The Tyndale notes add that God's new acts are tied to his old promises, and the deliverers come from the family he chose. The Gospel genealogies of Jesus do the same (Matt 1; Luke 3).
- **Reuben, Simeon, then Levi (vv14–16).** The list starts like a full tribal list (compare Gen 46:8–11), giving the firstborn Reuben and then Simeon. When it reaches Levi, the third son, it stops and expands. The other tribes are dropped because the point is Moses and Aaron. **"Shaul, the son of a Canaanite woman" (v15)** is a small, honest note that intermarriage had already happened (compare Gen 38:2).
- **Gershon, Kohath and Merari (vv16–19).** These are the three Levite clans who will later carry the tabernacle in the wilderness, each with its own duties (Num 3–4). The ages, 137 for Levi and 133 for Kohath, show the long lives of the generation that went down to Egypt.
- **"Amram took as his wife Jochebed his father's sister" (v20).** Moses' parents are named for the first time (compare 2:1). Marrying an aunt was later forbidden (Lev 18:12), and Moses records it anyway. Some ancient translations (the Greek and Syriac) soften it to "cousin." Henry says Moses "does not conceal it, for he sought not his own praise, but wrote with a sincere regard to truth."
- **How many generations?** The list gives Levi, Kohath, Amram, Moses. God told Abraham Israel would come back "in the fourth generation" (Gen 15:16), and here are four. But Israel was in Egypt 430 years (12:40), and Kohath's descendants number in the thousands by Numbers 3:27–28. Most scholars think some generations are skipped, since "son" in Hebrew can mean "descendant." The Tyndale notes suggest the gap falls between Kohath and Amram.
- **"Aaron took as his wife Elisheba, the daughter of Amminadab and the sister of Nahshon" (v23).** Elisheba ("my God is an oath") is the Hebrew form of Elizabeth, the name of John the Baptist's mother, herself from Aaron's line (Luke 1:5). Nahshon became leader of the tribe of Judah (Num 2:3) and is an ancestor of David and of Jesus (Ruth 4:20–22; Matt 1:4). The priestly family of Levi is joined by marriage to the royal tribe of Judah.
- **Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar and Ithamar (v23).** Aaron's four sons become the first priests. Nadab and Abihu later die for offering "unauthorized fire" (Lev 10:1–2). Eleazar carries the high priesthood forward (Num 20:25–28).
- **Korah (vv21, 24).** Korah, Moses' cousin, is singled out with his sons. He later leads a revolt against Moses and Aaron and dies in judgment (Num 16). His sons survived (Num 26:11), and their descendants, "the sons of Korah," wrote eleven psalms, including Psalm 42 and Psalm 46. Grace runs through even a rebel's family line.
- **"Phinehas" (v25).** Eleazar's son, whose name is Egyptian, probably meaning "the Nubian." He will later stand for God's honor at Peor and receive "a covenant of a perpetual priesthood" (Num 25:7–13). The list is aimed at the future priesthood, Aaron's line through Eleazar to Phinehas.
- **"This Moses and this Aaron" (vv26–27).** The list ends by pointing at its subject: *these* are the men God sent. In v26 Aaron is named first, as the elder. In v27 Moses comes first, as the leader.
- **"I am of uncircumcised lips" (vv28–30).** The story picks up where it left off by repeating vv11–12, a common Hebrew way of returning after a pause. The objection is still hanging, and God answers it in 7:1.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the genealogy proof that Moses and Aaron were truly Israelites, and notes that Moses records his parents' later-forbidden marriage without hiding it. JFB says the table was given "to authenticate the descent of Moses and Aaron" and notes only that some ancient versions read "his cousin" in v20. Guzik reads the list as a record of the priestly and Levitical families whose roles would matter in the tabernacle, and encourages readers not to skip such passages.

#### Pointing to Christ
God answers his discouraged servant with "I am the LORD" and seven promises: bring out, deliver, redeem, take, be your God, bring in, give. Each one is kept in Christ. He is the kinsman-redeemer who became our flesh and blood in order to set us free (Heb 2:14–15). He took the Passover cup of redemption and made it the cup of the new covenant (Luke 22:20). Through him God says to people from every nation, "I will be your God, and you shall be my people" (2 Cor 6:16; Rev 21:3). The genealogy pointing to Aaron's priestly line points beyond it too, to a better high priest "after the order of Melchizedek," who lives forever to intercede for his people (Heb 7:23–25).
