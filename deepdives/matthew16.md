*Setting:* Galilee and the far north, around summer of AD 29, less than a year before the crucifixion. Chapter 15 ended with Jesus feeding four thousand, probably on the Gentile side of the lake, and crossing back by boat. Chapter 16 is the hinge of Matthew's Gospel. It begins with one more demand for a sign and a warning about the leaders' teaching. Then, at Caesarea Philippi, far from the crowds, Jesus asks the question the whole Gospel has been building toward: "Who do you say that I am?" Peter answers, "You are the Christ." And "from that time" (v21) Jesus begins to speak plainly about the cross. The second half of Matthew moves steadily toward Jerusalem.

#### The Pharisees and Sadducees Demand Signs (vv1–4)
- **"The Pharisees and Sadducees came" (v1).** These two groups rarely agreed. The Pharisees were a popular lay movement devoted to the law and its oral traditions, and they believed in angels and the resurrection. The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy who controlled the temple, accepted only the written law and denied the resurrection (22:23; Acts 23:8). They were also more friendly to Rome. That they came together shows how much both now saw Jesus as a threat.
- **"To test him they asked him to show them a sign from heaven" (v1).** "Test" is the word used of Satan tempting Jesus (4:1). They were not asking out of honest doubt. They wanted something spectacular, on their terms, perhaps a sign in the sky like Moses' manna or Elijah's fire from heaven. Jesus had given many signs on earth: the blind seeing, lepers cleansed, the dead raised.
- **"It will be fair weather, for the sky is red" (v2).** The saying is still known: "Red sky at night, sailor's delight; red sky in the morning, sailors take warning." A red sky at sunset in Israel often meant dry air moving in from the east, and in the morning it could mean storm clouds coming from the west.
- **A textual note.** Some important early manuscripts lack the words from "When it is evening" to the end of v3. The ESV keeps them with a footnote. Luke has a similar saying in another setting (Luke 12:54–56). Many scholars think the words are original but were dropped in regions where the weather signs did not fit. Others think they were added from Luke. The meaning of the passage stands either way.
- **"You cannot interpret the signs of the times" (v3).** "Sky" and "heaven" are the same Greek word (*ouranos*), which makes a pun: they can read the sky but not the signs from heaven. The "signs of the times" were Jesus himself and his works (11:4–6).
- **"No sign will be given to it except the sign of Jonah" (v4).** Jesus repeats what he said in 12:39–40: his death and resurrection will be the only sign. "So he left them and departed." The conversation is over.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that "There are signs of the times, by which wise and upright men are enabled to make moral prognostications," meaning that discerning people can read where things are heading. Guzik notes how rare the alliance of Pharisees and Sadducees was, and that signs alone never produce faith in someone who does not want to believe. JFB refers readers to its notes on Mark for vv1–12.

#### The Leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees (vv5–12)
- **"They had forgotten to bring any bread" (v5).** Mark says they had only one loaf with them in the boat (Mark 8:14).
- **"Watch and beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (v6).** Leaven, a small piece of old fermented dough, works through a whole batch of new dough. At Passover all leaven had to be removed from Jewish homes (Exod 12:15), and leaven often pictured corruption that spreads quietly (1 Cor 5:6–8; Gal 5:9). In 13:33 Jesus used leaven for the kingdom's growth. Here he uses it for the opposite, false teaching.
- **"We brought no bread" (v7).** The disciples take Jesus literally and think he is scolding them for forgetting lunch.
- **"O you of little faith" (v8).** Their problem is not forgetfulness about bread but forgetfulness about Jesus. The one who fed thousands twice is in the boat with them.
- **"Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand… Or the seven loaves for the four thousand" (vv9–10).** Jesus refers to both feedings, with their different numbers of loaves and baskets (twelve and seven), using the two different basket words from 14:20 and 15:37. This confirms that Matthew is describing two separate events.
- **"Then they understood that he did not tell them to beware of the leaven of bread, but of the teaching" (v12).** The Pharisees' leaven was a religion of outward rules that missed the heart (15:1–20; 23:1–36). The Sadducees' leaven was a worldly, skeptical religion that denied the resurrection and cozied up to power. Mark adds "the leaven of Herod" (Mark 8:15). All of them led away from Jesus.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "The corrupt principles and practices of the Pharisees and Sadducees are compared to leaven" because they spread silently. Guzik notes that leaven was a familiar Jewish picture for corrupting influence, rooted in Passover, and faults the disciples for both forgetfulness and unbelief so soon after two feeding miracles.

#### Peter Confesses Jesus as the Christ (vv13–20)
- **"The district of Caesarea Philippi" (v13).** This was about twenty-five miles north of the Sea of Galilee, at the foot of Mount Hermon, near one of the main springs of the Jordan River. The area had a long pagan history. A great cave there held a shrine to Pan, the Greek god of nature, and the town was called Paneas (modern Banias). Herod the Great built a white marble temple to Caesar Augustus near the cave. His son Philip the tetrarch rebuilt the town and named it after Caesar and himself, so it was called "Philip's Caesarea" to distinguish it from Caesarea on the coast. It was a place full of rival claims to worship, and it was here that Jesus asked who he was.
- **"Who do people say that the Son of Man is?" (v13).** He first asks about public opinion.
- **"Some say John the Baptist, others say Elijah, and others Jeremiah or one of the prophets" (v14).** All the guesses are complimentary, and all fall short. Herod thought he was John risen (14:2). Many expected Elijah to come before the Messiah (Mal 4:5). Only Matthew mentions Jeremiah. A Jewish writing from before Jesus' time pictures Jeremiah appearing in a vision to pray for the people (2 Macc 15:13–16), and some may have connected Jesus' laments over Jerusalem with the weeping prophet.
- **"But who do you say that I am?" (v15).** "You" is plural and emphatic in Greek. Jesus asks the Twelve. Their answer matters more than the crowd's.
- **"You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (v16).** "Christ" (Hebrew *Messiah*) means "anointed one," the promised king from David's line. "Son of the living God" goes further. The disciples had already said "you are the Son of God" in the boat (14:33), and here Peter puts the two titles together. "The living God," in a town full of shrines to gods of stone, is a pointed phrase (1 Thess 1:9). Peter did not yet understand everything his words meant, as vv22–23 show, but what he said was true.
- **"Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jonah! For flesh and blood has not revealed this to you" (v17).** "Bar-Jonah" is Aramaic for "son of Jonah" (John calls his father John, John 1:42). "Flesh and blood" means human ability. Peter did not reason his way here. The Father revealed it, as Jesus said in 11:25–27: hidden from the wise, revealed to little children.
- **"You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church" (v18).** In Greek, "Peter" is *Petros* and "rock" is *petra*. In Aramaic, which Jesus probably spoke, both would be the same word, *Kepha* (Cephas, John 1:42). It is a deliberate wordplay. Jesus gave Simon this name.
- **What is "this rock"?** This is one of the most debated verses in the Bible. Main views: (1) Peter himself, as the first to confess Christ and the leader of the apostles. Many Protestant scholars today accept this, while noting that it says nothing about successors. Roman Catholic teaching goes further and sees here the foundation of the papacy, passed on to the bishops of Rome. (2) Peter's confession, the truth that Jesus is the Christ. This was common among the Reformers. (3) Christ himself, the rock and cornerstone (1 Cor 3:11; 1 Pet 2:4–8). The wordplay makes it hard to separate Peter from the rock completely. The New Testament elsewhere says the church is built on "the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone" (Eph 2:20), and Acts shows Peter opening the door to Jews (Acts 2), Samaritans (Acts 8) and Gentiles (Acts 10).
- **"My church" (v18).** The word is *ekklēsia*, an assembly of people called together. In the Greek Old Testament it was used for the assembly of Israel. Only Matthew among the Gospels uses the word (here and 18:17). Jesus calls it "*my* church," and says "*I* will build" it. The church belongs to him, and he is its builder.
- **"The gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (v18).** The Greek says "gates of Hades," the realm of the dead (ESV footnote). In the Old Testament "the gates of death" or "the gates of Sheol" are the gates of the grave, which hold their prisoners in (Job 38:17; Isa 38:10). The main point is that death itself will not overpower the church. Jesus will break those gates open by his own resurrection. Many also hear the gates as the powers of evil attacking the church, which cannot win either. The cave at Caesarea Philippi was thought by some locals to be a gateway to the underworld, which would have made the words striking, though that is a background detail rather than the point of the text.
- **"I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven" (v19).** The picture comes from Isaiah 22:22, where God gives Eliakim, the royal steward, "the key of the house of David": what he opens no one shuts. A steward carried the keys and controlled who came in. The risen Jesus later says he holds "the key of David" himself (Rev 3:7). Peter used the keys when he preached and the door opened to thousands (Acts 2:41).
- **"Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven" (v19).** "Bind" and "loose" were rabbinic terms for forbidding and permitting, declaring what God's law requires. They could also mean declaring someone forgiven or not. In 18:18 the same authority is given to the disciples as a group, in the setting of church discipline, and in John 20:23 to the apostles together. The Greek verb forms can be read "shall have been bound," meaning the church on earth declares what heaven has already decided, not that heaven follows the church's lead.
- **"He strictly charged the disciples to tell no one that he was the Christ" (v20).** The crowds expected a political Messiah. Jesus is about to explain that his messiahship goes through a cross. Until they understand that, the title would only mislead.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Peter's answer "short, but it is full, and true, and to the purpose." He lists the three readings of the rock and favors Christ himself, "both its Founder and its Foundation," and says the keys are "not to be understood of any peculiar power that Peter was invested with." JFB sees the church built on Peter as a confessor, says "gates of hell" means it "shall never perish," and calls claims of papal succession "baseless and impudent," which shows the sharp Protestant–Catholic arguments of its time. Guzik leans toward Jesus or Peter's confession as the rock, notes that *ekklēsia* was an everyday word for an assembly, and explains binding and loosing as rabbinic terms for forbidding and permitting.

#### Jesus Foretells His Death and Resurrection (vv21–23)
- **"From that time Jesus began" (v21).** Matthew used these exact words in 4:17, "From that time Jesus began to preach." Now a new stage starts. Jesus has hinted at his death before (9:15; 12:40). Now he says it plainly, and he will say it twice more (17:22–23; 20:17–19).
- **"He must go to Jerusalem and suffer many things" (v21).** "Must" (*dei*) speaks of divine necessity. It is God's plan, foretold in Scripture (Isa 53; Ps 22). "The elders and chief priests and scribes" were the three groups that made up the Sanhedrin, Israel's ruling council. The people who should have welcomed the Messiah will kill him.
- **"And on the third day be raised" (v21).** The resurrection is always part of the prediction. The disciples seem not to have heard it, or not to have understood.
- **"Peter took him aside and began to rebuke him" (v22).** Moments after his great confession, Peter tries to correct his Master. To him, a crucified Messiah was a contradiction. The Messiah was supposed to defeat Rome, not be killed by it. "Far be it from you" is literally "Mercy to you," an idiom meaning "God forbid."
- **"Get behind me, Satan!" (v23).** These are almost the same words Jesus said to Satan in the wilderness: "Be gone, Satan!" (4:10). There Satan offered the kingdoms of the world without the cross. Now Peter offers the same thing. Peter is not possessed, but he is speaking Satan's message. "Get behind me" may also mean "get back in your place, behind me, as a follower," the same words as "come after me" in v24.
- **"You are a hindrance to me" (v23).** "Hindrance" is *skandalon*, a stumbling stone. The irony is sharp: Peter, the rock, has become a rock to trip over.
- **"Not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man" (v23).** Peter was thinking the way people naturally think: success, safety, power. God's way went through suffering. Peter later understood and wrote that Christ "suffered for you, leaving you an example, so that you might follow in his steps" (1 Pet 2:21).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "It did not become Peter to contradict his Master," and that "Whatever appears to be a temptation to sin, must be resisted with abhorrence, and not parleyed with." JFB reads Peter's words as a temptation from Satan that would have made the cross a stumbling block. Guzik stresses that Peter meant well and was sincere, but his thinking was man-centered, and Jesus rebuked the satanic purpose behind his words rather than accusing Peter of deliberate betrayal.

#### Take Up Your Cross and Follow Jesus (vv24–28)
- **"If anyone would come after me" (v24).** What is true of the Master is true of his followers. Jesus spoke of the cross in 10:38. Now, right after predicting his own death, he repeats it.
- **"Let him deny himself" (v24).** This means more than giving up a few comforts. It means saying no to self as the one in charge. It is the same verb used when Peter later denied knowing Jesus (26:34, 75): disciples are to disown self the way Peter disowned his Lord.
- **"And take up his cross" (v24).** A condemned man carried his crossbeam to the place of execution. Everyone in Roman Palestine had seen it. It was a one-way walk. Luke adds "daily" (Luke 9:23): this is an ongoing way of life, not one heroic act.
- **"Whoever would save his life will lose it" (v25).** "Life" and "soul" (v26) are the same Greek word, *psychē*, meaning the self, one's life as a whole. Clinging to self-preservation at all costs ends in loss. Letting go for Jesus' sake ends in finding true life.
- **"What will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul?" (v26).** Jesus had been offered "all the kingdoms of the world" and refused (4:8–10). Nothing in the world is worth trading one's self for, and once it is lost, nothing can buy it back. "What shall a man give in return for his soul?" (compare Ps 49:7–8).
- **"The Son of Man is going to come with his angels in the glory of his Father" (v27).** The suffering Son of Man will also be the glorious judge (Dan 7:13–14). "He will repay each person according to what he has done" quotes Psalm 62:12 and Proverbs 24:12. Salvation is by grace, but deeds are the evidence of what a person has truly trusted in (7:21–27; 25:31–46).
- **"There are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom" (v28).** This verse has puzzled readers for centuries. Main views: (1) the Transfiguration, which follows immediately, where Peter, James and John see Jesus' glory (17:1–8). All three Gospels place this saying just before it, and Peter later describes it as seeing his "majesty" (2 Pet 1:16–18). (2) The resurrection and ascension, when Jesus says "all authority… has been given to me" (28:18). (3) Pentecost and the spread of the church. (4) The destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, seen as the Son of Man's coming in judgment. Some combine these as stages of the kingdom coming in power. Few think Jesus meant his final return would happen within their lifetimes, since he also says no one knows that day (24:36).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "We must deny ourselves absolutely," and "The winning of the world is often the losing of the soul." On v28 he points to the pouring out of the Spirit, the planting of the church and the destruction of Jerusalem, noting that John lived to see much of it. JFB likewise reads v28 as "the firm establishment" of Christ's kingdom within the lifetime of some who were there. Guzik takes it as a promise that some would see glimpses of the kingdom's power and glory, without tying it to one event, and stresses that the cross meant execution, so following Jesus means a real death to self.

#### Pointing to Christ
At a town built to honor Caesar and Pan, a fisherman confesses that a homeless teacher from Nazareth is "the Christ, the Son of the living God." Jesus accepts it, and then shows what kind of Christ he is: one who must suffer, be killed and be raised. He will build his church, and the gates of death will not hold it, because he himself will walk out of death's gates on the third day. Peter wanted a crown without a cross, and Jesus called it Satan's offer. The way to the glory of v27 runs through Jerusalem. The one who would gain the whole world as his inheritance (Ps 2:8) first lost his life for others, and he calls those who follow him to the same path, with the same promise: whoever loses their life for his sake will find it.
