*Setting:* "A Psalm of David." Most commentators, Henry and JFB among them, connect it with the day David brought the ark of the covenant up into Jerusalem, with music, shouting and sacrifices, and set it in the tent he had pitched for it (2 Sam 6:12–19; 1 Chr 15–16). The ark was the gold-covered chest that held the tablets of the law, with the cherubim's wings over its lid. It was the sign of God's throne among his people. Jerusalem had only recently been captured from the Jebusites (2 Sam 5:6–9), and now the LORD himself, symbolized by the ark, was entering his city. The psalm is built like a procession. It opens with the whole earth (vv1–2), climbs to the question of who may come up God's hill (vv3–6), and arrives at the gates, where a call and answer ring out (vv7–10). The Jewish temple service later sang it on the first day of every week. Psalm 23 ended with dwelling in the house of the LORD. Psalm 24 asks who may go up to it, and who the King is who lives there.

#### The Earth Is the LORD's (vv1–2)
- **"The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof" (v1).** Not just Israel, not just Jerusalem, but the whole earth and everything in it. Before the procession reaches God's small hill in Judah, the psalm sets the scale: the God whose ark is entering this city owns the world. "Fullness" means everything that fills it: crops, animals, wealth, people (50:10–12).
- **"The world and those who dwell therein" (v1).** People too belong to God. The nations that worship other gods are still his.
- **"For he has founded it upon the seas and established it upon the rivers" (v2).** God's ownership rests on creation. He made it, so it is his. Genesis 1:9–10 pictures dry land appearing out of the waters. To the surrounding nations, the sea was chaos, even a god to be defeated. Here the seas and rivers are simply what God built on. They pose no threat to his rule.
- **Paul quotes v1.** Writing to Corinthian believers unsure whether they could eat meat sold in the market after it had been offered to idols, Paul answers with this verse: "Eat whatever is sold in the meat market without raising any question on the ground of conscience. For 'the earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof'" (1 Cor 10:25–26). Idols own nothing, so food is not polluted by them. Jewish families used this verse as a blessing before meals, and Paul may be echoing that custom.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the psalm as about the kingdom of Christ, first "his providential kingdom, by which he rules the world." JFB says v2 "poetically represents the facts of Ge 1:9." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's comment that God made the earth full and keeps it full.

#### Who Shall Ascend? (vv3–6)
- **"Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD? And who shall stand in his holy place?" (v3).** The procession is climbing Mount Zion. Now comes the question every worshipper must face. If God owns the whole earth, who has the right to come into his presence? Psalm 15 asked the same question: "who shall dwell on your holy hill?" To "stand" means to stand before God and not be driven out (1:5; 5:5).
- **"He who has clean hands and a pure heart" (v4).** Outside and inside. "Clean hands" (*neqi kappayim*) means innocent hands, hands free of violence, theft or bribery (Isa 1:15–16). "A pure heart" means clean motives and undivided loyalty, what Jesus meant by "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matt 5:8). Ritual washing was required to enter the sanctuary, but this goes deeper: God looks at the life and the heart.
- **"Who does not lift up his soul to what is false" (v4).** "Lift up the soul to" means to long for, to set one's desire and trust on something (25:1, "To you, O LORD, I lift up my soul"). "What is false" (*shav*) means emptiness, vanity, and often idols (31:6; Jer 18:15). This is the same pairing of words as the third commandment: "You shall not *lift up* the name of the LORD your God *in vain* (*lashav*)" (Exod 20:7). The worshipper does not hand his heart to idols or empty things.
- **"And does not swear deceitfully" (v4).** He keeps his word. His oaths are honest (15:4).
- **"He will receive blessing from the LORD and righteousness from the God of his salvation" (v5).** Notice the gift language. He does not earn righteousness by climbing the hill. He "receives" it, from "the God of his salvation." Clean hands and a pure heart describe the kind of person who comes. Righteousness and blessing are what God gives the one who comes.
- **"Such is the generation of those who seek him" (v6).** "Generation" means a group or kind of people, a company. The true worshippers are not identified by birth or status but by the fact that they seek God.
- **"Who seek the face of the God of Jacob" (v6).** The standard Hebrew text reads "who seek your face, O Jacob," which is hard to understand. The ancient Greek and Syriac translations read "the face of the God of Jacob," and the ESV follows them. The KJV keeps the Hebrew, which JFB takes to mean "those who seek Thy face are Thy chosen people," that is, the true Jacob. Either way, the people who seek God's face are the true Israel.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the ceremonially unclean could not enter the temple mount, "which signified that cleanness of conversation which is required in all those that have fellowship with God." He adds, "We make nothing of our religion if we do not make heart-work of it." JFB: "Hands, tongue, and heart are organs of action, speech, and feeling, which compose character," and "lifted up his soul" means "to set the affections… on an object." Guzik argues that under the old covenant a righteous walk was the condition for fellowship with God, while under the new it is the result, and that only Jesus fully met the standard of v4. His righteousness is given to all who believe. He also notes that blessing could in some sense follow obedience, but righteousness always came from God.

#### The King of Glory (vv7–10)
- **"Lift up your heads, O gates!" (v7).** Now the procession reaches the city gates. The gates are spoken to like people, told to raise their heads, as if their lintels were too low for the King who is coming. Jerusalem's gates had just been the gates of a Jebusite fortress. Now they must open for the true King.
- **"Be lifted up, O ancient doors" (v7).** "Ancient" (*olam*) can mean "everlasting" (KJV) or "very old." Jerusalem was already an old city. Abraham met Melchizedek, king of Salem, there centuries before (Gen 14:18). Its old doors are about to receive something older than themselves.
- **"That the King of glory may come in" (v7).** "Glory" (*kavod*) is God's weighty splendor. The ark was the visible sign of God's presence, and its glory had once departed from Israel when the ark was captured (1 Sam 4:21–22, "Ichabod," "no glory"). Now the glory comes home.
- **"Who is this King of glory?" (v8).** A voice from the gates calls out the question, perhaps the gatekeepers in a set dialogue with the procession. It is like the challenge of a sentry: "Who goes there?"
- **"The LORD, strong and mighty, the LORD, mighty in battle!" (v8).** The answer is the covenant name of God and his title as warrior. At the Red Sea Israel sang, "The LORD is a man of war" (Exod 15:3). The ark had gone before Israel through the Jordan and around Jericho (Josh 3–6). The King entering Jerusalem is the one who won all those battles.
- **"Lift up your heads, O gates!" (v9).** The call is repeated, word for word, and so is the question. Repetition builds the drama, the way a crowd shouts the same chant again, louder.
- **"The LORD of hosts, he is the King of glory!" (v10).** The second answer is fuller. "LORD of hosts" (*YHWH tseva'ot*) means the LORD of armies: the armies of Israel, the stars of heaven, and the angels (1 Sam 17:45; Isa 40:26; Ps 103:20–21). It was the title closely tied to the ark itself: "the ark of the covenant of the LORD of hosts, who is enthroned on the cherubim" (1 Sam 4:4; 2 Sam 6:2). That is the name David used when he brought it up to Jerusalem.
- **Selah (v10).** The procession ends with a pause. The King is in.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "The entrance of the ark, with the attending procession, into the holy sanctuary is pictured to us. The repetition of the terms gives emphasis." Henry compares the question in v8 to asking "Who is there?" when someone knocks, and calls the doors "everlasting" because they are "much more durable than the door of the tabernacle." He then applies the scene to "the ascension of Christ into heaven," where the gates "which had been shut against us" are opened to the Redeemer, who "having by his blood made atonement for sin… demanded entrance, not for himself only, but for us." Guzik sees three layers: the ark entering Jerusalem, Jesus' ascension, and an individual heart opening to him as King. He passes on Morgan's sequence of Psalms 22, 23 and 24 as Christ suffering, shepherding and reigning, and Boice's suggestion that this psalm may have been recited as Jesus entered Jerusalem.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?" On honest reflection, the answer is: no one, if the standard is truly clean hands and a pure heart. Only one man has ever fully met it. Jesus climbed the hill and was turned away by men, crucified outside the gate (Heb 13:12). But God raised him and he ascended to the true holy place, "not made with hands… but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" (Heb 9:24). The early church loved to read vv7–10 as heaven's gates opening to the risen King of glory, "strong and mighty," who had won the decisive battle over sin and death (Col 2:15). And because he went in, he brings his people with him. They receive blessing and righteousness "from the God of their salvation," not because their hands were clean, but because his were. Paul calls him "the Lord of glory" (1 Cor 2:8).
