*Setting:* The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 17 circles around relationships: a quiet home versus a quarrelsome one, friends and brothers, gossip that splits friends, parents grieved by a foolish child. It has a run of sayings on justice and bribes (vv8, 15, 23, 26) and closes with the famous observation that even a fool looks wise if he keeps quiet. The LORD appears only twice, as the one who tests hearts (v3) and who hates the reversal of justice (v15), but those two verses hold the chapter together.

#### Peace at Home and the Testing of Hearts (vv1–6)
- **"Better is a dry morsel with quiet than a house full of feasting with strife" (v1).** A "dry morsel" is bread without oil or sauce to dip it in, the plainest meal. "Feasting" is literally "sacrifices," the meat of peace offerings shared at a family celebration (7:14; 1 Sam 20:29). JFB reads it as feasts eaten from the offerings. Picture a festival dinner, the best food of the year, ruined by family fighting. Better bread and peace. Guzik adds that abundance itself can breed envy and conflict. Compare 15:17.
- **"A servant who deals wisely will rule over a son who acts shamefully and will share the inheritance as one of the brothers" (v2).** In ancient households a trusted slave could rise to manage the estate, even inherit. Abraham once expected his servant Eliezer to be his heir (Gen 15:2–3). Joseph, a slave, rose to rule Egypt. JFB connects the daughters of Zelophehad, who received an inheritance against the usual rule (Num 27). The Tyndale notes: wisdom matters more than privilege.
- **"The crucible is for silver, and the furnace is for gold, and the LORD tests hearts" (v3).** Refiners melted ore in a clay crucible over intense heat, and the impurities (dross) rose to the top to be skimmed off. People can test metals. Only God tests hearts. JFB: "God only knows, as He tries" the heart. The Tyndale notes point to Psalm 66:10 and Malachi 3:2–3, where God sits "as a refiner and purifier of silver." Peter applies it to trials: faith "more precious than gold that perishes though it is tested by fire" (1 Pet 1:7). 27:21 repeats the first line and adds that praise is a test too.
- **"An evildoer listens to wicked lips, and a liar gives ear to a mischievous tongue" (v4).** Gossip needs an audience. The person who loves to hear slander shows what kind of person they are. JFB: both "delight in calumny." The Tyndale notes connect the book's many warnings on lies, gossip and slander.
- **"Whoever mocks the poor insults his Maker; he who is glad at calamity will not go unpunished" (v5).** This restates 14:31. To laugh at someone's misfortune is to sneer at the God who made them and who permitted it. Edom was condemned for gloating over Judah's fall (Obad 12). Proverbs 24:17: "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls."
- **"Grandchildren are the crown of the aged, and the glory of children is their fathers" (v6).** Living long enough to see grandchildren was a great blessing (Ps 128:6). And a good parent is a child's glory. The Tyndale notes say "fathers" here includes mothers, and grandchildren show God's blessing on a family line (Gen 12:1–3). JFB: children can take pride in virtuous ancestors.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read v1 as family peace being worth more than abundance. On v3 Guzik says God's truth and love are the standard that refines from within, and the Tyndale notes stress the refining fire removing impurity.

#### Bribes, Rebukes and Starting Quarrels (vv7–14)
- **"Fine speech is not becoming to a fool; still less is false speech to a prince" (v7).** "Fine speech" is literally "a lip of excess," lofty or excellent words. Grand words from a fool don't fit. Lies from a ruler fit even less. "Prince" (*nadib*) can mean a noble or a generous person. The higher the position, the worse the lie.
- **"A bribe is like a magic stone in the eyes of the one who gives it" (v8).** "Magic stone" is literally "a stone of favor" or "charm stone," like a lucky charm. The giver thinks it will open every door. This is an observation of how bribery seems to work, not approval, as v23 makes clear. Guzik notes the word (*shochad*) can mean a gift or a bribe, and the verse may describe bribery from the giver's side. JFB: the one who uses it shows "his high estimate of it." Compare 18:16 and 21:14 (gifts that open doors) with 15:27 and 17:23 (bribes condemned).
- **"Whoever covers an offense seeks love, but he who repeats a matter separates close friends" (v9).** "Covers" is the verb of 10:12, love covering offenses. To cover something is not to pretend it didn't happen but to stop spreading it. "Repeats" means to keep bringing it up, to oneself or to others. JFB: the contrast is "between the peace-maker and tale-bearer." Guzik: some exposure of sin is right, but the full accounting belongs to God, and gossip does real damage. The second line echoes 16:28.
- **"A rebuke goes deeper into a man of understanding than a hundred blows into a fool" (v10).** The law limited beatings to forty lashes (Deut 25:3). A hundred blows is an exaggeration for effect. A wise person is moved by a word. A fool isn't moved by a whipping. David was cut to the heart by Nathan's few words (2 Sam 12:7–13). The Tyndale notes: wise people respond to gentle criticism.
- **"An evil man seeks only rebellion, and a cruel messenger will be sent against him" (v11).** The rebel against authority will meet a messenger who carries out punishment, a king's officer or an angel of judgment (Ps 78:49). JFB: the "cruel messenger" is the one sent to carry it out. Sheba's rebellion ended with his head thrown over the city wall (2 Sam 20:22).
- **"Let a man meet a she-bear robbed of her cubs rather than a fool in his folly" (v12).** The she-bear robbed of her cubs was the proverbial picture of rage in Israel (2 Sam 17:8; Hos 13:8). Syrian brown bears lived in the land then. Yet a fool in full folly is more dangerous. JFB: an angry fool is less reasonable than a wild animal. The Tyndale notes say a fool in his folly may answer correction with violence.
- **"If anyone returns evil for good, evil will not depart from his house" (v13).** David repaid Uriah's loyalty with murder, and Nathan said, "the sword shall never depart from your house" (2 Sam 12:10). Nabal repaid David's protection with insults (1 Sam 25:21). Returning evil for evil is condemned elsewhere (20:22). Returning evil for good is worse still. Jesus calls his people to return good even for evil (Rom 12:17–21).
- **"The beginning of strife is like letting out water, so quit before the quarrel breaks out" (v14).** In a land of cisterns, dams and irrigation channels, a small crack in a dam could become a flood. JFB: "as a breach in a dam." Guzik makes the same picture: once water starts pouring through, it is hard to stop. The Tyndale notes gather other warnings against quarrels (15:18; 20:3; 26:21). The wise walk away while it's still a trickle.
- **Commentators:** JFB and Guzik both draw the dam picture from v14. Guzik on v9 carefully separates covering sin in love from covering it up. JFB on v8 makes the point that the giver overrates his bribe, and the Tyndale notes warn on v12 against trying to correct a fool at the wrong moment.

#### Justice, Friendship and Foolish Sons (vv15–21)
- **"He who justifies the wicked and he who condemns the righteous are both alike an abomination to the LORD" (v15).** "Justify" (*tsadaq*) is a law-court word, to declare someone in the right. Judges were commanded to "acquit the innocent and condemn the guilty" (Deut 25:1). Reversing it is an abomination. JFB: it reverses God's own way of acting. Isaiah: "Woe to those who call evil good and good evil… who acquit the guilty for a bribe" (Isa 5:20, 23). This verse raises the question Paul answers in Romans: how can God "justify the ungodly" (Rom 4:5) without becoming what this proverb condemns? Only because Christ bore their sin, so God is "just and the justifier" (Rom 3:26).
- **"Why should a fool have money in his hand to buy wisdom when he has no sense?" (v16).** A fool with tuition money still lacks "heart," the desire to learn. Wisdom is free to the willing (Isa 55:1) and unaffordable to the unwilling. JFB: money can't buy wisdom for someone who doesn't value it.
- **"A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity" (v17).** One of the great sayings on friendship. A real friend's love doesn't depend on circumstances. And a brother, family, shows his worth when trouble comes. The two lines may be parallel (friend and brother both loyal) or a step up (a true friend becomes a brother in hard times). JFB: a brother's love is "specially seen in adversity." Henry: "Friends must be constant to each other at all times," and he applies the second line to family in the words of Esther 4:14, "who knows but we came into such a family for such a time as this?" Jonathan's love for David is the Bible's great picture of it (1 Sam 18:1–4; 20:17). Compare 18:24: "there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother."
- **"One who lacks sense gives a pledge and puts up security in the presence of his neighbor" (v18).** The cosigning warning again, the fourth time (6:1–5; 11:15; 20:16; 22:26). Repetition shows how many people fall into it. Placed right after v17, it adds balance: loving a friend does not mean risking ruin for him. JFB: the careless man fails to seek advice or ignores it.
- **"Whoever loves transgression loves strife; he who makes his door high seeks destruction" (v19).** "Makes his door high" may mean building a showy, oversized gateway, a display of wealth that invites envy or robbery. JFB reads it that way: "a vain love of costly building." Others take "door" as the mouth (Mic 7:5, "guard the doors of your mouth"), meaning boasting. The Tyndale notes read it as overconfidence that starts fights. Either way, pride invites a fall (16:18).
- **"A man of crooked heart does not discover good" (v20).** Twisted heart, twisted tongue, and the road leads nowhere good.
- **"He who sires a fool gets himself sorrow" (v21).** Two different words for fool are used here (*kesil* and *nabal*). *Nabal* is the coarse, godless fool, like the man named Nabal (1 Sam 25:25: "as his name is, so is he"). JFB: both words suggest "stupidity and impiety." The grief of a parent whose child rejects wisdom is one of the deepest in life (v25; 10:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry and JFB both treat v17 as a portrait of loyal love tested by hardship, and Guzik closes his comment with Jonathan Edwards dying in trust of Jesus as his faithful friend. JFB reads v19 as literal building, while the Tyndale notes read it as pride starting quarrels.

#### A Joyful Heart and a Closed Mouth (vv22–28)
- **"A joyful heart is good medicine, but a crushed spirit dries up the bones" (v22).** "Medicine" (*gehah*) appears only here. It may mean healing or a healthy face. JFB: "The effect of the mind on the body is well known." Modern medicine agrees that hope and joy help the body heal, and despair wears it down. "Dries up the bones" pictures losing strength from the inside. Guzik connects David's description of unconfessed guilt: "my bones wasted away… my strength was dried up" (Ps 32:3–4). Compare 15:13 and 18:14.
- **"The wicked accepts a bribe in secret to pervert the ways of justice" (v23).** Literally "a bribe from the bosom," from the fold of a garment where money was hidden. JFB notes the same. This shows what v8 left unsaid: the bribe works by corrupting justice. "In secret" is the giveaway. Honest gifts don't need to be hidden.
- **"The discerning sets his face toward wisdom, but the eyes of a fool are on the ends of the earth" (v24).** Wisdom is right in front of the discerning. The fool's eyes wander everywhere, always looking for something far away and missing what is near. The Tyndale notes: wisdom must be sought, and fools can't stay focused. Moses said God's word "is not in heaven… nor beyond the sea… but the word is very near you" (Deut 30:11–14).
- **"A foolish son is a grief to his father and bitterness to her who bore him" (v25).** Like v21, but now both parents. The Tyndale notes add that it applies to daughters too.
- **"To impose a fine on a righteous man is not good, nor to strike the noble for their uprightness" (v26).** Punishing the innocent, or flogging an official for doing right, is a perversion of justice like v15. "Not good" is understatement for something terrible. Jeremiah was beaten and jailed for telling the truth (Jer 20:2; 37:15).
- **"Whoever restrains his words has knowledge, and he who has a cool spirit is a man of understanding" (v27).** "Cool spirit" (*qar ruach*) is a calm temperament, not easily heated. The Hebrew manuscripts preserve two spellings here, "cool of spirit" and "precious of spirit" (*yeqar ruach*), a dignified spirit. The ESV and most translations take "cool." JFB: a calm spirit "not excited to vain conversation."
- **"Even a fool who keeps silent is considered wise; when he closes his lips, he is deemed intelligent" (v28).** The chapter ends with a smile. Silence is so wise that even a fool looks wise when he practices it. Guzik makes the same dry observation, with the well-known saying (often credited to Lincoln) that it is better to stay silent and be thought a fool than to speak and remove all doubt. Job told his friends the same: "Oh that you would keep silent, and it would be your wisdom!" (Job 13:5).
- **Commentators:** Guzik on v22 ties the crushed spirit to the physical weight of guilt in Psalm 32. JFB treats vv27–28 together as a call to restraint. All three treat v28 with a light touch, a reminder that Proverbs has a sense of humor.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 17 condemns anyone who "justifies the wicked" (v15), and yet the gospel announces that God "justifies the ungodly" (Rom 4:5). The two meet only at the cross, where the Righteous One was condemned (the very thing v15 calls an abomination) so that the wicked who trust him could be declared righteous (2 Cor 5:21; 1 Pet 3:18). God was not bribed (v23). He paid. Jesus is also the friend who "loves at all times" and the brother "born for adversity" (v17): "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends" (John 15:13), and he "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Heb 2:11). He is the one who covers offenses in love rather than repeating them (v9; Ps 32:1), and the Refiner who tests hearts to purify them, not destroy them (v3; Mal 3:3).
