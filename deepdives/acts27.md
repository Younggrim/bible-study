*Setting:* Autumn of about AD 59. Paul has appealed to Caesar, and Festus sends him to Rome under guard. Luke's "we" returns, so Luke is on board and writing as an eyewitness. The result is one of the most detailed accounts of a sea voyage to survive from the ancient world. In 1848 James Smith, a Scottish yachtsman, sailed and studied the route himself. He found Luke's winds, soundings and drift rates so exact that only someone who had been on that ship could have written them. The chapter is an adventure story, but it is also about how God keeps his word through a storm, and about a prisoner who becomes the real captain.

#### Paul Sails for Rome (vv1–12)
- **"A centurion of the Augustan Cohort named Julius" (v1).** This was an imperial unit, possibly one used for official business such as escorting prisoners. Julius treats Paul well throughout (vv3, 43; 28:16). Some guess he had been in the audience hall when Paul spoke before Agrippa (25:23).
- **"Accompanied by Aristarchus" (v2).** He was a Thessalonian veteran of the Ephesus riot (19:29) and a fellow prisoner in Rome (Col 4:10). Paul also has Luke ("we"). Some suggest the two traveled as Paul's attendants, perhaps even as his slaves, since a citizen of standing was allowed servants. The ship of Adramyttium was a coasting vessel working its way home along Asia Minor.
- **"Julius treated Paul kindly and gave him leave to go to his friends" (v3).** At Sidon there were believers, probably from the scattering after Stephen (11:19). After two years in custody, the church cares for Paul.
- **"Under the lee of Cyprus, because the winds were against us" (v4).** The prevailing summer winds in the eastern Mediterranean blow from the west and northwest. So the ship hugs the coasts and islands for shelter.
- **"A ship of Alexandria sailing for Italy" (v6).** At Myra they transfer to a ship from Egypt's grain fleet. Rome lived on Egyptian wheat, and the government protected these large state-contracted freighters. Grain ships sailed north to Asia Minor and then west because of those same winds.
- **"Fair Havens, near which was the city of Lasea" (v8).** This was a small bay on Crete's south coast, sheltered from the north but open to the winter storms. Ruins of Lasea were found nearby in 1856.
- **"Even the Fast was already over" (v9).** "The Fast" is the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29–31), in late September or early October. Roman sailors considered sea travel dangerous from mid-September and closed it from about mid-November until spring. They were already in the danger season.
- **"I perceive that the voyage will be with injury and much loss… also of our lives" (v10).** Paul had sailed a great deal. By this point he had already been shipwrecked three times, once spending a night and a day adrift (2 Cor 11:25). This sounds like experienced judgment rather than a prophecy. Later God will reveal that no lives will be lost (v22), which shows God's mercy beyond Paul's own forecast.
- **"The centurion paid more attention to the pilot and to the owner of the ship" (v11).** That was reasonable. They were the experts, and the owner had a cargo and a profit at stake. "The majority decided" (v12) to try for Phoenix, a better winter harbor about 40 miles west. Majority opinion and expert advice were both wrong.
- **Commentators:** Henry compares Paul among the prisoners to Christ crucified between thieves. He notes Julius knew a Paul was "not to be used as common prisoners." On Fair Havens he writes: "Every fair haven is not a safe haven; nay, there may be most danger where there is most pleasure." JFB relies on James Smith's study (*Voyage and Shipwreck of St. Paul*) for the geography of Lasea and Fair Havens. Guzik observes that Julius's favor is why Paul could bring companions at all. Henry's concise commentary adds that it was "determined by the counsel of God, before it was determined by the counsel of Festus, that Paul should go to Rome."

#### The Storm at Sea (vv13–38)
- **"When the south wind blew gently, supposing that they had obtained their purpose" (v13).** A gentle south wind looked perfect for a few hours' run along the coast. It is the classic picture of false security.
- **"A tempestuous wind, called the northeaster" (v14).** The Greek is *Eurakylōn*, a sailors' blend of Greek *Euros* (east wind) and Latin *Aquilo* (north wind): an east-northeaster. Older manuscripts behind the KJV read "Euroclydon." "Tempestuous" is *typhōnikos*, the root of "typhoon." It "struck down from the land," rushing off Crete's 8,000-foot mountains.
- **"Could not face the wind… driven along" (v15).** The ship couldn't turn its bow into the gale, so it ran before it.
- **Cauda and the ship's boat (vv16–17).** Behind the small island of Cauda (modern Gavdos) they had a few minutes of shelter. They hauled aboard the dinghy that had been towing behind, now swamped. Then they "**used supports to undergird the ship**," passing cables under the hull to hold the timbers together (called "frapping"). They feared **the Syrtis**, notorious quicksands and shoals off Libya, hundreds of miles south but directly downwind. They "lowered the gear," most likely a sea anchor (the ESV note) or the heavy mainyard, to slow the drift.
- **Cargo, then tackle (vv18–19).** First they throw cargo overboard, like Jonah's sailors (Jon 1:5). Then "with their own hands" they throw out the ship's spare gear. Luke's "we" in v19 in some manuscripts would mean even the passengers helped.
- **"Neither sun nor stars appeared for many days" (v20).** Without a compass, sailors navigated by sun and stars. Blind, leaking and soaked, "all hope of our being saved was at last abandoned." Luke includes himself in that hopelessness.
- **"Men, you should have listened to me" (v21).** It is an "I told you so," but with a purpose. Paul wants them to trust what he says next because his first warning was right.
- **"There will be no loss of life among you, but only of the ship" (v22).** This is the turning point.
- **"An angel of the God to whom I belong and whom I worship" (v23).** That is a confession to a pagan crowd in two verbs, belonging and serving. Paul isn't a man with a god. He belongs to God.
- **"Do not be afraid, Paul; you must stand before Caesar" (v24).** "Must" (*dei*) is God's necessity again (19:21; 23:11). **"God has granted you all those who sail with you."** "Granted" (*charizomai*) means given as a gift of grace. It suggests Paul had been praying for them. 275 lives are spared for the sake of one man and his prayers, the way God would have spared Sodom for ten righteous people (Gen 18:32).
- **"I have faith in God that it will be exactly as I have been told" (v25).** Faith here is taking God at his word before there is any evidence. **"But we must run aground on some island" (v26).** The promise doesn't mean no wreck. It means no deaths.
- **"The fourteenth night… the Adriatic Sea" (v27).** In ancient usage "Adria" meant the whole central Mediterranean between Greece, Italy and Africa, not today's narrower Adriatic. Smith calculated that a ship drifting under these conditions would cover the roughly 475 miles from Cauda to Malta in about thirteen days. That fits Luke's fourteen nights closely.
- **"Twenty fathoms… fifteen fathoms" (v28).** That is about 120 feet, then 90 feet. They are closing on land fast in the dark. **"Four anchors from the stern"** kept the bow pointed toward shore, ready to run aground at daylight. They "prayed for day to come."
- **"Unless these men stay in the ship, you cannot be saved" (vv30–32).** The sailors try to slip away in the boat. Paul sees it and warns the centurion, and now, unlike at Fair Havens, the Roman listens. God's promise does not cancel the means God uses. Paul believes God will save them all, and he still insists the sailors stay at their posts. The soldiers cut the boat loose. It was an act of faith, though a costly one, since the boat might have been useful at the beach.
- **"Take some food… not a hair is to perish from the head of any of you" (vv33–34).** Fear and seasickness had kept them from eating properly for two weeks. "Not a hair" echoes Jesus (Luke 21:18). "It will give you strength" is literally "for your salvation" (*sōtēria*). In Luke's vocabulary, physical rescue points toward the greater salvation.
- **"He took bread, and giving thanks to God in the presence of all he broke it and began to eat" (v35).** The four verbs, took, gave thanks, broke, ate, are the same ones Luke uses for the feeding of the five thousand (Luke 9:16), the Last Supper (22:19) and Emmaus (24:30). Most take this as an ordinary meal, not the Lord's Supper, since nearly everyone aboard was pagan. But Luke's wording is deliberate. A prisoner gives thanks to God in front of 275 terrified men, and they eat and take courage.
- **"276 persons in the ship" (v37).** That is realistic for a large grain carrier. Josephus sailed to Rome around this time on a ship carrying about 600, which also sank. Some manuscripts read "about 76," but 276 is the better-supported reading.
- **"Throwing out the wheat into the sea" (v38).** To float the ship as high as possible for beaching, they throw overboard the very cargo the voyage was for.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the ship as "kicked like a football from wave to wave." He observes that during the storm Paul "hid himself among them, was one of the crowd," but once hope was gone, "though a prisoner, undertook to be their counsellor and comforter." He reckons "one Paul among them worth more than all the rest." He reads the angel's "fear not" through Isaiah 8:12: "fear not their fear." His concise commentary adds that when sinners "give up all hope of saving themselves, they are prepared to understand God's word." JFB, following Smith, identifies the wind as east-northeast, "which answers all the effects here ascribed to it." It takes "lowered the gear" as the heavy mainyard and sail. Guzik calls Paul's "you should have listened" an understandable and justified "I told you so," given only to win a hearing for the hope that followed. The Tyndale notes contrast Paul's leadership with Jonah's: Jonah slept below decks while the sailors prayed, but Paul leads the sailors to God.

#### The Shipwreck (vv39–44)
- **"A bay with a beach" (v39).** Tradition and Smith's survey place this at St. Paul's Bay on Malta's northeast coast. Its soundings still match verse 28.
- **"Loosening the ropes that tied the rudders… hoisting the foresail" (v40).** Ancient ships steered with two large steering oars, one on each side, which had been lashed up during the storm. The foresail (*artemōn*) was a small sail at the bow, used to steer toward the beach.
- **"Striking a reef" (v41).** Literally "a place where two seas meet" (*topos dithalassos*). This was probably a sandbar or channel where cross-currents meet, like the narrow channel between Malta and the islet of Salmonetta. The bow sticks fast in the clay, and the stern breaks apart in the surf.
- **"The soldiers' plan was to kill the prisoners" (v42).** Roman guards paid with their own lives for escaped prisoners (12:19; 16:27). Killing them was the safe move.
- **"But the centurion, wishing to save Paul, kept them from carrying out their plan" (v43).** For the second time, Paul's presence saves everyone. The centurion who once ignored him (v11) now protects him at his own risk.
- **"Some… on planks or on pieces of the ship. And so it was that all were brought safely to land" (v44).** All 276 survive, exactly as God promised (v24). "Brought safely" (*diasōzō*) means saved through. They were not kept out of the storm. They were brought through it.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes they "escaped with their lives and that was all, and that was for Paul's sake." Earlier, Paul had been in danger of being killed along with the other prisoners for their sake. Now they were spared for his. JFB, with Smith, points to St. Paul's Bay as matching every detail. Guzik and the Tyndale notes agree: the survival of every person shows God's word kept to the letter.

#### Pointing to Christ
The storm doesn't stop God's purpose. It carries Paul toward Rome. Jesus once slept in a boat through a storm and woke to calm the sea (Luke 8:22–25). Here the sea isn't calmed, but the promise holds: no one is lost. A prisoner in chains becomes the one through whom 275 others are saved, by his presence, his prayers and the word he passes on from God. That pattern points beyond Paul to the one who said, "of all that he has given me I should lose nothing" (John 6:39). He went down into the deep himself so that everyone who belongs to him would be brought safely through.
