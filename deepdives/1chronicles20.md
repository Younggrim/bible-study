*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Chapter 19 told how Joab and David broke the Ammonite and Aramean alliance, but Rabbah, the Ammonite capital (modern Amman in Jordan), was left standing. This short chapter finishes the Ammonite war and then adds three brief reports of Philistine giants killed by David's men. It is a chapter best read by noticing what it leaves out. Between its first and second sentences, 2 Samuel tells the darkest story of David's life: his adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of her husband Uriah (2 Sam 11:2–12:25). Between verses 3 and 4, 2 Samuel tells of the rape of Tamar, Absalom's murder of Amnon, Absalom's revolt and death, and Sheba's rebellion (2 Sam 13–20). The Chronicler skips all of it. He is not hiding it: his readers had the books of Samuel and knew the story. He is telling a different story, about David as the founder of the temple and its worship, and he moves straight from David's victories to the preparations for God's house.

#### The Capture of Rabbah (vv1–3)
- **"In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle" (v1).** Armies in the ancient Near East usually fought in the dry season. Winter rains turned roads to mud, and spring brought the harvest that could feed an army in the field. A year after the war of chapter 19, Joab goes back to finish the job.
- **"But David remained at Jerusalem" (v1).** These words are copied exactly from 2 Samuel 11:1, where they introduce the Bathsheba story. Readers who knew Samuel would hear them with a jolt. The Chronicler leaves the line in and lets it stand without comment. It is the quietest possible pointer to what he does not retell.
- **"Joab struck down Rabbah and overthrew it" (v1).** 2 Samuel 12:26–29 gives more detail. Joab captured "the city of waters," probably the part of the city that controlled its water supply, and then sent for David so that the king, not the general, would take the city and get the credit. Chronicles compresses this into a single sentence, but v2 and v3 assume David was there ("David… returned to Jerusalem").
- **"David took the crown of their king from his head" (v2).** "Their king" is *malkam* in Hebrew. With different vowels the same letters spell *Milcom*, the name of the Ammonite god (1 Kings 11:5), and the old Greek translation reads it that way. So this may have been the crown from the god's statue rather than from the human king. Either reading makes the same point: Ammon's glory is now on the head of the LORD's anointed.
- **"It weighed a talent of gold, and in it was a precious stone" (v2).** A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg). That is too heavy for anyone to wear for long, which supports the view that it was an idol's crown. Others suggest it was placed on David's head only briefly in a ceremony, or that the precious stone was taken from the crown and set in David's own. The text does not explain.
- **"He brought out the spoil of the city, a very great amount" (v2).** For the Chronicler, this wealth flows into the stores David is gathering for the temple (18:11; 22:14).
- **"Set them to labor with saws and iron picks and axes" (v3).** The Hebrew text here literally reads "he sawed them." 2 Samuel 12:31 has a verb that differs by only one letter and means "he set" or "put them" to saws. Older translations (KJV "cut them with saws") took it as torture or execution. Most modern translations, including the ESV, read it as forced labor: the captives were put to work with saws, picks and axes, and 2 Samuel adds brick-making. Conquered peoples were commonly used as labor gangs in the ancient world, and Israel later did the same (1 Kings 9:20–21). The forced-labor reading fits the tools and the parallel, and the ESV notes the Hebrew in a footnote.
- **"Then David and all the people returned to Jerusalem" (v3).** With Rabbah taken, the wars of expansion are essentially over. Ammon remained a subject people, and one of its later kings sent help to David in his hour of need (2 Sam 17:27).
- **Why leave out Bathsheba?** Some readers find the silence troubling, as if the Chronicler were whitewashing his hero. But the Chronicler does show David's failures where they bear on his theme. He tells of the disaster with the ark (ch 13) and the sinful census that follows in chapter 21, at great length. Both of those failures lead toward the temple. The census leads David to the very site where the temple will stand (21:18–22:1). The Bathsheba story, for all its importance, does not. Its one link to the temple is Solomon, Bathsheba's son, and the Chronicler names him among David's sons (3:5, where Bathsheba appears as "Bath-shua").
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads "David remained at Jerusalem" as the opening of the Bathsheba story, a warning that ease and idleness are often when temptation strikes hardest, and he cites Augustine that David's fall warns those who stand and gives hope to those who have fallen. He sees the crown in v2 as a sign that David's sin, repented of, did not cost him the kingdom. JFB thinks the crown "was not worn on the head, but suspended by chains of gold above the throne," a guess the text neither confirms nor rules out. It gives the talent as 125 pounds, using an older and higher estimate than most scholars use today. On v3, JFB notes the one-letter difference from Samuel and that many take the verse to mean only hard labor, without settling the question. Henry treats chapters 18–20 briefly and draws one lesson for this chapter: "Though the Lord will severely correct the sins of his believing people, he will not leave them in the hands of their enemies."

#### Philistine Giants Killed (vv4–8)
- **"After this there arose war with the Philistines" (v4).** These three reports come from 2 Samuel 21:18–22, part of an appendix to Samuel that collects stories from different points in David's reign. The Chronicler drops the first of Samuel's four stories, in which David himself grew weary in battle and was nearly killed by a giant until Abishai rescued him (2 Sam 21:15–17). The pattern is consistent: the Chronicler keeps David strong and moving toward the temple.
- **"At Gezer" (v4).** 2 Samuel 21:18 has "Gob," an unknown place. Gezer was a major city guarding the road from the coast into the hill country. It stayed in Canaanite hands until Pharaoh captured it and gave it to Solomon as a dowry (1 Kings 9:16).
- **"Sibbecai the Hushathite struck down Sippai" (v4).** Sibbecai was one of David's thirty mighty men (11:29) and later commanded one of the twelve monthly army divisions (27:11). Sippai is Saph in 2 Samuel.
- **"One of the descendants of the giants" (v4).** The Hebrew is literally "the descendants of the Rapha," a term linked with the Rephaim, a people remembered for their great size who once lived in Canaan and east of the Jordan (Deut 2:10–11, 20–21; 3:11). When Joshua conquered the land, a remnant of the related Anakim survived only "in Gaza, in Gath, and in Ashdod" (Josh 11:22). These Philistine champions may have been descendants of that remnant, or "Rapha" may be the name of a clan ancestor.
- **"Elhanan the son of Jair struck down Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite" (v5).** This verse helps with a well-known puzzle. 2 Samuel 21:19 says that "Elhanan the son of Jaare-oregim, the Bethlehemite, struck down Goliath the Gittite." But 1 Samuel 17 says David killed Goliath. Chronicles reads "Lahmi the brother of Goliath." In Hebrew, Samuel's "the Bethlehemite" (*bet hallachmi*) and Chronicles' "Lahmi" (*et lachmi*) look very much alike, and Samuel's "oregim" ("weavers") looks like a scribe's accidental repetition of "weaver's beam" later in the same verse. Many scholars think Chronicles preserves the better reading and Samuel's text was damaged in copying. Others suggest that "Goliath" was a family name or title carried by more than one champion. Either way, the Bible does not contradict itself on who killed the famous Goliath.
- **"The shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam" (v5).** This is the same description used for Goliath's spear (1 Sam 17:7). A weaver's beam may refer to the thick wooden bar of a loom. Some scholars think the comparison is to the loop and cord on a loom's heddle rod, because Aegean warriors used javelins with a thong-and-loop to spin the spear in flight. Either way, it was a heavy weapon matched to a huge man, and the shared feature ties Lahmi to his famous brother.
- **"Six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot" (v6).** Extra digits, called polydactyly, are a known inherited trait, and they sometimes run in families. The detail is the kind of thing an eyewitness remembers.
- **"When he taunted Israel" (v7).** "Taunted" (*charaph*) is the same verb used for Goliath, who "defied the armies of the living God" (1 Sam 17:10, 26, 36). The giant of Gath mocks Israel's God just as his relative did, and meets the same end.
- **"Jonathan the son of Shimea, David's brother" (v7).** It is Shimea who is David's brother. Shimea is also called Shammah, Jesse's third son, who was passed over when Samuel came to anoint a king (1 Sam 16:9). So Jonathan was David's nephew. A generation after David the shepherd boy killed Goliath, his nephew kills another giant from the same city.
- **"They fell by the hand of David and by the hand of his servants" (v8).** David did not kill these giants himself. His men did. The summary gives credit to both, because David's example and leadership had trained a generation of warriors who were no longer afraid of giants. The boy who once stood alone in the Valley of Elah has become the king of an army of giant-killers.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees vv4–8 as proof that Israel could now defeat giants without David personally, and that God raises up new fighters as the older generation passes. He takes the "weaver's beam," with Martin Selman, as a looped javelin of a kind known in the Aegean world, so the detail is not legend. He cites Adam Clarke that extra fingers and toes are a known condition. JFB identifies Gezer with Samuel's Gob but has no notes on vv5–8. Henry's lesson for the giants is that God's "assistance will overcome all advantages of number and strength of those that defy his Israel," and that "all that trust in Christ, shall be made more than conquerors."

#### Pointing to Christ
The crown of Ammon, perhaps taken from the head of a false god, was set on the head of the LORD's anointed. That is a small picture of the triumph of Christ, who receives "many diadems" (Rev 19:12) because the powers that held the nations have been defeated. The chapter's silence about David's sin is not the last word. The Bible elsewhere tells it plainly, and David's own prayer of confession survives (Ps 51). The king God promised would have to be greater than David. Christ is both David's son and the giant-killer who faced the enemy we could not: he came to "destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8). Like David, he does not keep the victory to himself. He trains and strengthens his servants, so that the victory belongs to him and to them: "in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us" (Rom 8:37).
