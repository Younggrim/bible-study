*Setting:* Jonah, a prophet of Israel in the days of Jeroboam II (about 793–753 BC), finally went to Nineveh, the great city of Assyria, and announced, "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!" (3:4). The whole city, from the king down, believed God, fasted, put on sackcloth and turned from its violence. And "God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them" (3:10). Any other prophet would have rejoiced. Chapter 4 shows what Jonah did instead, and it reveals the real reason he ran in chapter 1. Assyria was Israel's great enemy, and within a few decades it would destroy the northern kingdom (722 BC). Jonah knew that, and perhaps God's people reading the book later knew it too. The chapter is a conversation between an angry prophet and a patient God, and it ends with a question nobody answers.

#### Jonah's Anger and the Lord's Compassion (vv1–11)
- **"But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry" (v1).** The Hebrew is stronger and more pointed: "it was evil to Jonah, a great evil." The word *ra'ah* ("evil," "disaster") runs through the whole book. Nineveh's evil came up before God (1:2). Nineveh turned from its evil, and God turned from the evil, the disaster, he had threatened (3:8–10). Now God's mercy is, in Jonah's eyes, a great evil. "He was angry" is literally "it burned to him" (*charah*). Guzik notes that God will later let Jonah feel some real heat (v8).
- **"O LORD, is not this what I said when I was yet in my country?" (v2).** This is a prayer, but an angry one. The prophet who wouldn't speak a word in chapter 1 now tells God, in effect, "I told you so." We finally learn why he ran.
- **"I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" (v2).** Jonah quotes Israel's great confession about God, first spoken by God himself to Moses after the golden calf (Exod 34:6–7). It is the most quoted description of God in the Old Testament (Num 14:18; Neh 9:17; Ps 86:15; 103:8; 145:8; Joel 2:13). "Gracious" (*channun*) is favor that is not earned. "Merciful" (*rachum*) comes from the word for womb, a mother's tender love. "Slow to anger" is literally "long of nostrils," because in Hebrew anger is pictured as hot breath from the nose. "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) is God's loyal covenant love. The Tyndale notes observe that Jonah almost quotes Exodus 34, a passage about Israel's covenant, and applies it to Assyria. Even in the Old Testament, God's heart reached to the nations.
- **"And relenting from disaster" (v2).** Exodus 34 does not have this line, but Joel 2:13 does, in exactly these words, so Jonah and Joel share the same form of the creed. JFB thinks Joel was quoting Jonah. Notice also what Jonah leaves out. Exodus 34:7 goes on: "but who will by no means clear the guilty." Jonah wanted that half for Nineveh. A century later Nahum, prophesying Nineveh's fall, quotes exactly that half: "The LORD is slow to anger and great in power, and the LORD will by no means clear the guilty" (Nah 1:3). The two books belong together.
- **Why did Jonah hate the mercy?** The text gives the reason in his own words: he knew God would forgive them. Readers have filled in more. Assyria was cruel, and it was the coming threat to Israel. Hosea and Amos, his near contemporaries, warned that Assyria would be God's rod on Israel (Hos 11:5; Amos 6:14). Sparing Nineveh might mean doom for his own people. JFB adds that he may also have felt shamed, as if he looked like a false prophet when the threatened judgment didn't come. Guzik stresses plain hatred of Israel's enemies. The irony is heavy: Jonah has just been rescued by the same grace he now resents (2:9), and his complaint about God is the best thing anyone can say about him.
- **"Please take my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live" (v3).** Elijah once said almost the same words under a broom tree (1 Kings 19:4), and JFB draws the comparison. But Elijah was in despair because Israel would not repent. Jonah wants to die because Nineveh did. The Tyndale notes say his wish to die rather than accept God's will shows his hard heart.
- **"Do you do well to be angry?" (v4).** God's reply is a single question, gentle and probing. It can also be translated "Are you very angry?" (JFB mentions that reading), but the ESV's sense fits the rest of the chapter: is your anger *right*? The Tyndale notes call it a rhetorical question whose answer is plainly no. God does not argue, strike or abandon his prophet. He asks. Guzik observes that God's questions are meant to expose the heart.
- **"Jonah went out of the city and sat to the east of the city" (v5).** Jonah does not answer. He walks out, finds a spot east of the city, likely on higher ground across from it, and builds a "booth" (*sukkah*), a rough shelter of branches, like those Israel made at the Feast of Booths. He sits "till he should see what would become of the city." The forty days may not yet have run out, and he seems to hope God will still destroy it. Guzik and the Tyndale notes read it that way. JFB is kinder, thinking he waited out of curiosity rather than spite. Either way, he chose to watch from a distance rather than stay among the people who had repented.
- **The order of events.** Some scholars think v5 looks back to a time before 3:10, when Jonah left the city during the forty days and was waiting to see what would happen. Others read it straight on: even after God relented, Jonah hoped he might change his mind back. The point is the same either way: the prophet sits outside, the city lives inside.
- **"Now the LORD God appointed a plant" (v6).** "Appointed" (*manah*) is the book's word for God's direction of his creatures: the great fish (1:17), the plant, the worm (v7), and the wind (v8). The names of God shift through this scene. It is "the LORD God" (*Yahweh Elohim*) here, then "God" (*Elohim*) for the worm and wind, then "the LORD" for the final speech. The combination *Yahweh Elohim* is rare outside Genesis 2–3, where God plants a garden, which may be a quiet echo.
- **What was the plant?** The Hebrew word, *qiqayon*, appears only in this chapter. The Hebrew lexicon (BDB) says it is usually taken as the castor-oil plant (*Ricinus*), a fast-growing shrub with large leaves, which JFB also favors. The Greek Old Testament made it a gourd, a quick-growing vine. Jerome translated it "ivy" in his Latin Bible, which caused a famous stir: Augustine reported that a congregation in North Africa nearly rioted when they heard "ivy" instead of the familiar "gourd." The ESV wisely says "plant." The miracle is in how fast it grew, not in the species.
- **"To save him from his discomfort" (v6).** "Discomfort" is once more *ra'ah*, "evil." God's gift was meant to rescue Jonah from his *ra'ah*: his sunstroke, and perhaps his bad temper too. **"So Jonah was exceedingly glad because of the plant."** "Glad… a great gladness" is the exact mirror of v1, "evil… a great evil." Jonah is furious over a city of people spared and delighted over some leaves. Henry warns that people quick to be cast down are often quick to be lifted up by small comforts.
- **"God appointed a worm that attacked the plant, so that it withered" (v7).** One small worm at dawn undoes the comfort of a day. JFB: "A small worm at the root destroys a large gourd." Henry draws the lesson of how fragile our creature comforts are.
- **"God appointed a scorching east wind" (v8).** The east wind blows off the desert, hot, dry and dusty. Locals in the Middle East call such winds *sharav* or *khamsin*, and they can raise temperatures sharply and make people ill. A booth of branches without its plant would give little shade. **"The sun beat down on the head of Jonah so that he was faint."** In 2:7, when his life was "fainting away" in the sea, he remembered the LORD. Now, faint in the sun, he asks to die.
- **"Do you do well to be angry for the plant?" (v9).** God asks the same question as v4, now pointed at the plant. This time Jonah answers: **"Yes, I do well to be angry, angry enough to die."** He insists on his right to be angry. JFB compares the phrase "even unto death" with Jesus in Gethsemane, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death" (Matt 26:38). The contrast is striking: Jonah is sorrowful to death over his own comfort, Jesus over the sins of the world.
- **"You pity the plant, for which you did not labor, nor did you make it grow" (v10).** "Pity" (*chus*) means to look on with compassion, to spare. God's argument is from the lesser to the greater. Jonah grieved for a plant he didn't plant, didn't water and had for one day: it "came into being in a night and perished in a night," literally "a son of a night… a son of a night." If he feels that for a plant, what should God feel for a city he made and has tended for centuries?
- **"And should not I pity Nineveh, that great city" (v11).** The book's final word from God, and its final word overall, is a question. Nineveh is "that great city" one last time (1:2; 3:2, 3). God reveals that his mercy toward Nineveh was not reluctant or accidental. It was compassion, the same word he used for Jonah's feeling about the plant.
- **"More than 120,000 persons who do not know their right hand from their left" (v11).** Two readings are common. JFB, Henry and many others take this as young children, too little to know right from left, which would put the whole population near 600,000. Guzik and others take it to mean the whole adult population, morally confused people who didn't know right from wrong, which fits the size of the city better. Either way, God sees people where Jonah saw only enemies. The Tyndale notes add that God wants to save those who genuinely repent.
- **"And also much cattle?" (v11).** The book ends, almost humorously, with cows. Animals fasted and wore sackcloth in chapter 3, and God cares for them too (Ps 36:6; 104:14; Matt 10:29). Henry notes that God "took notice of the abundance of cattle too that were in Nineveh," and that animals are worth more than a plant. If Jonah could pity a vine, should God not pity living creatures?
- **Why does the book end with a question?** We never hear Jonah's answer. Many readers through history have assumed Jonah himself is the source of the story. If so, he learned the lesson in the end, since he told it against himself. But the book leaves the question open on purpose, and it falls on every reader. Israel, who knew the same creed Jonah recited, had to decide whether God's mercy toward their enemies was good news. The Tyndale notes say it plainly: those who have received God's mercy should be glad when the same mercy reaches others, even their enemies.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jonah was "so very much out of temper" that he was "flying in the face of God," and that the chapter sets human sinfulness and God's goodness side by side so that each looks clearer. He closes the book with comfort: "he that repents, and turns from his evil way, shall find mercy with him." JFB explains Jonah's grief partly as wounded pride as a prophet, identifies the plant as the castor-oil plant, and reads the 120,000 as small children. Guzik presses hard on Jonah's hatred of the Assyrians and his self-pity, noting that he quit, separated himself from people and became a spectator, and he reads the 120,000 as morally confused adults. All three agree that God's patience with Jonah is as wonderful as his patience with Nineveh.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jonah sat outside the city hoping to watch it burn. Jesus sat on the Mount of Olives looking at Jerusalem, a city about to reject him, and wept over it (Luke 19:41–44). Jonah was angry "enough to die" over a plant. Jesus was "sorrowful, even to death" for sinners (Matt 26:38), and then did die for them, outside the city wall (Heb 13:12). Jonah's creed was true: God is "gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love." Jesus is what it looks like in a person. And God's last question to Jonah is the question the elder brother hears in Jesus' parable of the prodigal son, standing outside the party while the father pleads with him (Luke 15:28–32). Both stories end without an answer, waiting for ours.
