*Setting:* Babylonia, around 591 BC, among the exiles. This short parable begins a run of picture-messages in chapters 15–24 that come at Jerusalem's coming fall from one angle after another: a useless vine (ch 15), an unfaithful bride (ch 16), two eagles and a vine (ch 17), a lioness and her cubs (ch 19), a sword (ch 21), two sisters (ch 23), and a cooking pot (ch 24). The people of Jerusalem believed that because they were God's chosen people, his vine, they were safe. The prophets had long used the vine as a picture of Israel (Ps 80:8–16; Isa 5:1–7; Jer 2:21; Hos 10:1), and it was a picture of privilege. Ezekiel takes the image and asks one plain question: what good is a vine's wood?

#### Jerusalem, a Useless Vine (vv1–8)
- **"How does the wood of the vine surpass any wood?" (v2).** The question is about the wood, not the grapes. A vine is valued for one thing only, its fruit. As timber it is crooked, thin and soft, worse than almost anything in the forest. Israel had boasted of being the LORD's vine, as if that made it better than the nations around it, the "trees of the forest." Ezekiel's question turns the boast upside down. Without fruit, a vine is worth less than any common tree. It is striking that the chapter never mentions grapes at all. The fruit has already been given up on.
- **"Is wood taken from it to make anything? Do people take a peg from it to hang any vessel on it?" (v3).** A peg was the humblest piece of carpentry in a house, a wooden pin driven into a wall to hang a pot or a lamp. Even for that, vine wood is no good. Isaiah used the peg as a picture of a reliable leader: "I will fasten him like a peg in a secure place" (Isa 22:23), and Zechariah used it of the Messiah, the cornerstone and tent peg out of Judah (Zech 10:4). Jerusalem could not provide even that.
- **"It is given to the fire for fuel" (v4).** The only use left for vine wood is to burn it. Even then it is poor fuel, since it flares and burns out quickly. Vinedressers pruned their vines every year and burned the cuttings, and Ezekiel's hearers would have seen the piles of dead branches smoldering at the edge of every vineyard.
- **"When the fire has consumed both ends of it, and the middle of it is charred" (v4).** Ezekiel pictures a stick that has been in the fire and pulled out, burned at both ends and blackened in the middle. That fit Judah's state in 591 BC. In 605 and 597 BC Babylon had already carried off its best people, its king and its treasures (2 Kings 24:10–16; Dan 1:1–3). What was left in Jerusalem was a scorched remnant. JFB reads the two ends as the two kingdoms, the north already destroyed by Assyria and the south pressed by its enemies, with Jerusalem as the charred middle. That is possible, but the plainer point is about the damage already done.
- **"When it was whole, it was used for nothing. How much less, when the fire has consumed it" (v5).** This is an argument from the greater to the lesser. If Judah produced nothing for God at the height of its strength, under kings like Solomon and Hezekiah, what will it produce now that it is half burned? The people pinned their hopes on what was left of their city. God says it was never their strength that made them valuable, and what remains is worth even less.
- **"So have I given up the inhabitants of Jerusalem" (v6).** Now the parable is explained. The vine is not Israel in general but **"the inhabitants of Jerusalem"**, the people who stayed in the city and thought themselves the true Israel (11:15). God himself puts them into the fire. Isaiah's song of the vineyard ended the same way, with God taking away its hedge and letting it be trampled because it yielded only wild grapes (Isa 5:4–6).
- **"Though they escape from the fire, the fire shall yet consume them" (v7).** Those who survived the first fire, the deportation of 597 BC, thought they had come through the worst. They had escaped once, so surely the danger was over. God says no. The fire is coming back. In 586 BC it came literally: the Babylonians "burned the house of the LORD and the king's house and all the houses of Jerusalem" (2 Kings 25:9).
- **"You will know that I am the LORD, when I set my face against them" (v7).** As so often in Ezekiel, the purpose of judgment is knowledge of God. When the city falls, no one will be able to say it was chance or Babylon's gods. It will be plain that the LORD did it, as he said he would.
- **"Because they have acted faithlessly" (v8).** The parable ends with the reason. The word is *ma'al*, breaking trust, the same word used in 14:13. It is the language of a covenant broken, and it opens the door into chapter 16, where the faithless vine becomes a faithless wife. Leviticus had warned that if Israel acted faithlessly the land would be made desolate (Lev 26:33, 40–43).
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the parable in two lines: "If a vine be fruitful, it is valuable. But if not fruitful, it is worthless and useless, it is cast into the fire." He applies it plainly: "Let us beware of an unfruitful profession." JFB says the vine's "sole excellency is that it should bear fruit," and describes the peg as "the large wooden peg used inside houses in the East to hang household articles on." On v7 JFB reads "fire" broadly as "every kind of calamity," so that survivors of one disaster would meet another. JFB has no separate note on vv5–6 or v8. Guzik makes the same link to the 597 BC deportation as the Tyndale notes, and points out that the parable's silence about fruit says everything. He warns that a privileged position before God means greater accountability, not less.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel was meant to be God's vine, but it bore no fruit and ended in the fire. Jesus stepped into that image and said, "I am the true vine" (John 15:1). He is the faithful Israel who bore the fruit Israel never did. He then speaks to his disciples in words that echo Ezekiel: "If anyone does not abide in me he is thrown away like a branch and withers; and the branches are gathered, thrown into the fire, and burned" (John 15:6). The warning is real, but so is the promise. The worth of a branch was never in its own wood. It was always in the life that flows through it from the vine. "Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing" (John 15:5). Ezekiel shows what a vine is when it is cut off. Jesus shows what a branch can become when it remains in him.
