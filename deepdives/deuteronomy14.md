*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Moses is giving the detailed laws of his second sermon (chapters 12–26), and he is still applying the first part of the Ten Commandments: Israel belongs to the LORD alone. Chapter 12 said where to worship, and chapter 13 said whom never to follow. Chapter 14 turns to ordinary life: how to mourn, what to eat, and what to do with the harvest. Each of these shows that Israel is "a people holy to the LORD" (vv2, 21), set apart even at the funeral and the dinner table. Most of the food list repeats Leviticus 11, more briefly. The chapter ends with the tithe, which turns the harvest into a feast before God and, every third year, into food for the poor. It is one of the most practical chapters in the book, and one where Christians need to read carefully, because Jesus and the apostles changed how these food laws apply.

#### Clean and Unclean Food (vv1–21)
- **"You are the sons of the LORD your God" (v1).** Israel is God's "firstborn son" (Exod 4:22), and here the whole people are his children. This family identity is the reason for everything that follows. A child of the living God should not mourn the way people do who have no hope.
- **"You shall not cut yourselves or make any baldness on your foreheads for the dead" (v1).** In the ancient world, mourners slashed their skin and shaved patches of their heads to show grief, and perhaps to appease or honor the dead. The prophets of Baal cut themselves on Mount Carmel to get their god's attention (1 Kgs 18:28). Leviticus 19:28 and 21:5 forbid the same. Israel could grieve, and the Bible is full of honest grief. But these rites belonged to the worship of other gods and to a hopeless view of death. Paul gives the Christian version: "that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thess 4:13).
- **"A people holy to the LORD… his treasured possession" (v2).** Almost word for word from 7:6. "Holy" means set apart for God. Israel's distinctiveness in grief and in diet is a living sign that they belong to him. *Segullah*, "treasured possession," is a king's personal treasure (1 Chr 29:3).
- **"You shall not eat any abomination" (v3).** The word *to'evah* means something detestable, the same word used of idols (7:25) and child sacrifice (12:31). Here it simply means what God has declared off-limits as food.
- **"The ox, the sheep, the goat, the deer, the gazelle, the roebuck, the wild goat, the ibex, the antelope, and the mountain sheep" (vv4–5).** Three farm animals and seven wild game animals. Deuteronomy, unlike Leviticus 11, lists clean animals by name. Several Hebrew names are rare and the identifications are uncertain, which is why translations differ. The wild animals were hunted in the hill country and desert edges.
- **"Every animal that parts the hoof and has the hoof cloven in two and chews the cud" (v6).** Two tests, and an animal must pass both. These are the grazing animals of the herd and flock.
- **"The camel, the hare, and the rock badger" (v7).** These seem to chew the cud but do not have a split hoof. The **rock badger** is the hyrax, a small, furry animal that lives among rocks (Ps 104:18; Prov 30:26). Modern readers sometimes point out that hares and hyraxes are not true ruminants. The Hebrew phrase is literally "bring up the cud," and it describes what the animals appear to do. Both move their jaws constantly as if chewing, and hares re-eat partly digested food. The law is classifying animals by what an ordinary person could observe, not by modern biology.
- **"And the pig, because it parts the hoof but does not chew the cud" (v8).** The pig was raised and eaten widely by Israel's neighbors and was sometimes used in pagan rites (Isa 65:4; 66:17). Archaeologists have noticed that pig bones are rare at Israelite village sites in the hill country but common at Philistine sites nearby, which suggests the rule was really kept and became a mark of identity. Centuries later, Jews died rather than eat pork when the Greek king Antiochus IV tried to force it on them (2 Macc 6–7).
- **"Whatever has fins and scales you may eat" (v9).** Fish with both fins and scales were clean. That excluded shellfish, eels and catfish, and other creatures of the water without scales.
- **The birds (vv11–18).** No single rule is given for birds. Instead there is a list of forbidden ones. Most are birds of prey or scavengers that eat carrion: eagles, vultures, kites, ravens, owls. The others are water birds such as the cormorant, stork and heron, plus the **hoopoe**, a crested bird that probes dung for insects, and the **bat**, which in Hebrew thinking was a flying creature even though it is not a bird. Many of these Hebrew words are rare, and translations vary.
- **"All winged insects are unclean for you" (v19).** Leviticus 11:21–22 makes an exception for locusts and grasshoppers, which John the Baptist later ate (Matt 3:4). Deuteronomy's summary, "All clean winged things you may eat" (v20), leaves room for that exception.
- **Why these rules?** The Bible doesn't explain them directly, and readers have offered several reasons. **Separation:** the laws marked Israel off from the nations at every meal. This is the reason the text itself gives (vv2, 21; Lev 20:24–26). **Health:** many forbidden animals, such as scavengers, pigs and shellfish, can carry disease, so the laws likely protected Israel, though the Tyndale notes rightly say a food was clean or unclean because God declared it so. **Symbolism:** many scholars note a pattern. Clean animals are those that fit their category normally, while unclean ones cross categories or are tied to death, such as birds that eat carrion. Most likely there was more than one purpose, and above all the laws trained Israel to live as God's people even when eating.
- **"You shall not eat anything that has died naturally… you may give it to the sojourner… or you may sell it to a foreigner" (v21).** An animal that died of itself hadn't been properly bled, and blood was off-limits (12:16, 23). A resident foreigner (*ger*) or a visiting foreigner (*nokri*) was not bound by Israel's holiness laws, so the meat need not be wasted. Leviticus 17:15 treats a resident foreigner who eats such meat as needing to wash, which shows the rules were applied with some differences.
- **"You shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk" (v21).** This appears three times in the law (Exod 23:19; 34:26; here). Its reason is debated. Some think it opposed a Canaanite ritual. A Ugaritic text from Syria was once read as describing just such a rite, though scholars now doubt that reading. Others see a principle of compassion: what was meant to nourish life should not be used to cook it. The law also forbids killing a mother and her young on the same day, and taking a mother bird with her eggs (Lev 22:28; Deut 22:6–7). Later Jewish law expanded this verse into the rule of keeping meat and dairy completely separate, which is the basis of kosher kitchens today.
- **How Christians read the food laws.** Jesus "declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19), teaching that what defiles a person comes from the heart, not the stomach. Peter learned the same lesson in a vision before he went to the Gentile Cornelius (Acts 10:9–16, 28), and Paul writes that "nothing is unclean in itself" (Rom 14:14; also Col 2:16–17; 1 Tim 4:3–5). The food laws were a picture of separation between Israel and the nations. In Christ that wall has come down (Eph 2:14), and Jews and Gentiles now eat at one table. The call to holiness remains. It is now lived out in conduct, not diet (1 Pet 1:15–16).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry's concise commentary says the food laws were given "to keep them from mingling with their idolatrous neighbours," and notes that the New Testament no longer binds believers to them. JFB calls the pagan mourning rites "extravagant and unnatural expressions of hopeless sorrow." It says the kid-in-milk law was "pointed against an annual pagan ceremony" and that carrion could be given to a non-Israelite visitor but not to a convert. JFB passes over the fish law. Guzik gives three reasons for the food laws, health, symbolism and separation, and notes that the last made it hard for Jews to share meals with Gentiles, which is exactly the barrier the New Testament takes down. On v1 he says believers may grieve, but not without hope. Guzik and JFB both read the kid law against a pagan rite, while the Tyndale notes favor compassion. The Ugaritic evidence once used for the pagan-rite view is now doubted, so the question remains open.

#### Tithes (vv22–29)
- **"You shall tithe all the yield of your seed that comes from the field year by year" (v22).** "Tithe" means a tenth. Abraham gave a tenth to Melchizedek (Gen 14:20), and Jacob vowed a tenth to God (Gen 28:22), so the practice is older than the law. The Hebrew is emphatic, literally "tithing you shall tithe."
- **"Before the LORD your God… you shall eat the tithe" (v23).** This surprises many readers. Here the tithe is not handed over and forgotten. The family takes it to the central sanctuary and eats it there, in God's presence. **"That you may learn to fear the LORD your God always."** The feast is a school. Eating God's gifts in his presence teaches that everything comes from him.
- **"If the way is too long… then you shall turn it into money" (vv24–25).** Carrying a tenth of the grain, wine and oil to Jerusalem from the edges of the land was impractical. So it could be sold, the money carried, "bound up in your hand," a purse tied up for the journey, and spent at the sanctuary. By Jesus' day this had become big business at the temple, which is part of the background to the money-changers and animal-sellers Jesus drove out (John 2:13–16).
- **"Spend the money for whatever you desire—oxen or sheep or wine or strong drink, whatever your appetite craves" (v26).** A feast, not a fast. "Strong drink" (*shekar*) means fermented drink, probably beer made from grain. The Bible condemns drunkenness clearly (Prov 20:1; Eph 5:18), but it also counts wine among God's gifts, given "to gladden the heart of man" (Ps 104:15). Christian traditions differ on alcohol. Some practice moderation and others abstain, often out of love for those who struggle (Rom 14:21). Either way, v26 shows that the worship God commanded included real celebration. **"And rejoice, you and your household."** Again Deuteronomy commands joy.
- **"You shall not neglect the Levite" (v27).** The Levites had no farmland. Their food depended on Israel remembering them, so this warning comes again and again (12:12, 19).
- **"At the end of every three years you shall bring out all the tithe of your produce… and lay it up within your towns" (v28).** Every third year the tithe stayed local. It was stored in each town as a fund for those without land or support: **"the Levite… the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow"** (v29). These are the same groups God champions in 10:18. They are not to receive scraps but to "eat and be filled." Chapter 26 gives the prayer the farmer says when he has finished giving this tithe (26:12–15).
- **One tithe or several?** This is debated. Numbers 18:21–24 gives the tithe to the Levites, while here the family eats it at the sanctuary. Jewish tradition reconciled the texts by counting more than one tithe: a first tithe for the Levites, a "second tithe" eaten in Jerusalem, and in the third and sixth years of each seven-year cycle a "poor tithe" instead of the second. The book of Tobit, written a few centuries before Christ, already describes this (Tob 1:6–8). Many modern scholars think instead that there was one tithe, used in different ways. Matthew Henry and Guzik take opposite sides here.
- **"That the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands" (v29).** Generosity to the poor isn't a loss. It is the way of blessing. Proverbs 19:17 puts it this way: "Whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD."
- **How Christians read the tithe.** The New Testament does not command a tithe for believers. Jesus mentions the Pharisees' careful tithing and says they "ought to have done" it without neglecting justice and mercy (Matt 23:23). Paul's teaching on giving stresses that it be regular, proportional, generous and cheerful (1 Cor 16:2; 2 Cor 9:6–7). Many Christians take a tenth as a sensible starting point. Others give by a different measure. All can learn from this chapter's purposes: giving teaches reverence, includes joyful celebration, supports those who serve, and provides for the poor.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry's concise commentary takes the second-tithe view, "A second portion from the produce of their land was required," and says the feasts "promoted friendliness, liberality, and cheerfulness, and raised a fund for the relief of the poor." JFB says that, where distance made payment in kind impractical, the tithe "was by this statute convertible into money." It also notes the Levites' forty-eight towns and other support. JFB doesn't comment on v26's strong drink. Guzik argues for one tithe used in different ways and against the rabbinic count of three. He links the money exchange to the temple money-changers, notes that the tithe was figured on increase rather than total possessions, and says that asking "How little can I give?" shows a wrong heart. He sees a tenth as a reasonable starting point for Christian giving, though not a command.

#### Pointing to Christ
The food laws drew a line around Israel at every meal, saying "you are mine." Jesus kept those laws, and then by his death tore down "the dividing wall of hostility" between Jew and Gentile (Eph 2:14). Now the whole family of God, from every nation, eats together at his table. Holiness is no longer marked by what goes into the mouth but by a heart made clean by him (Mark 7:19–23; Acts 15:9). And the tithe feast, where the family ate and rejoiced before the LORD with the Levite, the foreigner, the orphan and the widow, is a small picture of the kingdom Jesus proclaimed, where "the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind" are invited to the banquet (Luke 14:13, 21).
