*Setting:* About AD 29, late in the Galilean ministry. Chapter 8 showed Jesus' power over storm, demons, disease and death, and kept asking "Who then is this?" Chapter 9 is a hinge in Luke's Gospel. Herod asks the question, the crowds guess, and Peter answers it: "The Christ of God." Then Jesus starts to explain what kind of Christ he is: one who must suffer, die and rise. On the mountain his glory shines through, and Moses and Elijah talk about his "departure" at Jerusalem. From v51 on, Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem," and the long journey that fills Luke 9:51–19:27 begins. The disciples keep missing the point, arguing about greatness and wanting fire from heaven, while Jesus explains what following him will cost.

#### Jesus Sends Out the Twelve Apostles (vv1–6)
- **"Power and authority over all demons and to cure diseases" (v1).** "Power" (*dynamis*) is the ability to do it, and "authority" (*exousia*) is the right to do it. Jesus shares both. The Twelve are sent to do what they have watched him do for eight chapters.
- **"Take nothing for your journey" (v3).** No staff, no traveler's bag for begging, no bread, no money, no spare tunic. They travel light and depend on God and on the welcome of those who receive the message. This was a short, specific mission. Later Jesus tells them to take purse and bag, because conditions will change (22:35–36).
- **"Shake off the dust from your feet as a testimony against them" (v5).** Jews returning from Gentile lands sometimes shook off the dust so as not to bring unclean soil home. Doing it to a Jewish town said, in effect: you have treated God's message like outsiders would. Paul and Barnabas do exactly this in Acts 13:51.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Christ "designed a total rout and ruin to the kingdom of darkness, and therefore gave them power over all devils." JFB notes that he "both qualified and authorized" them, power and authority together. Guzik's principle is that God equips those he sends, and he explains the dust-shaking as treating a rejecting town as if it were Gentile ground.

#### Herod Is Perplexed by Jesus (vv7–9)
- **"Herod the tetrarch" (v7).** Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39. The mission of the Twelve spread Jesus' name so far it reached the palace.
- **"He was perplexed" (v7).** The word means thoroughly at a loss. The popular guesses were John raised from the dead, Elijah returned (Mal 4:5) or one of the old prophets risen.
- **"John I beheaded, but who is this?" (v9).** Luke does not retell John's death (Mark 6:14–29 does). Herod's line carries a guilty conscience. He had silenced one prophet, and now another voice was louder.
- **"He sought to see him" (v9).** He gets his wish at Jesus' trial, when he wants a miracle and Jesus will not say a word to him (23:8–9).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes Herod's wish was not granted until Jesus stood before him as a prisoner. Henry: "Those who oppose God will find themselves more and more embarrassed." Guzik links Herod's unease to his guilt over John.

#### Jesus Feeds the Five Thousand (vv10–17)
- **"He took them and withdrew apart to a town called Bethsaida" (v10).** Bethsaida ("house of fishing") sat near where the Jordan enters the lake on the northeast shore. It was the hometown of Peter, Andrew and Philip (John 1:44). Jesus meant to give the returning apostles rest.
- **"He welcomed them" (v11).** The crowds spoiled the retreat, and Jesus welcomed them anyway. He taught and healed, as before.
- **"You give them something to eat" (v13).** The "you" is emphatic. They have just come back from healing and casting out demons in his name, and now he tests whether they will trust him for bread. Their answer counts what they have, five loaves and two fish, and finds it hopeless.
- **"About five thousand men" (v14).** "Men" means males. With women and children (Matt 14:21) the crowd may have been far larger. "Groups of about fifty" recalls how Moses organized Israel in the wilderness (Exod 18:21), and it turns a mob into an orderly meal.
- **"He looked up to heaven and said a blessing" (v16).** A Jewish host gave thanks to God over bread before breaking it: "Blessed are you, Lord our God, King of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth." The blessing is to God for the food. Then the bread multiplies in Jesus' hands and passes through the disciples' hands to the people. The same four verbs, took, blessed, broke, gave, appear at the Last Supper (22:19) and at Emmaus (24:30).
- **"They all ate and were satisfied… twelve baskets" (v17).** This recalls God feeding Israel with manna in the wilderness (Exod 16) and Elisha feeding a hundred men with twenty loaves, with some left over (2 Kings 4:42–44). Jesus does far more.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those whom Christ feeds he fills; to whom he gives, he gives enough." On the crowds interrupting the retreat he adds, "Pious zeal may excuse a little rudeness; it did with Christ, and should with us." Guzik notes that the miracle happened through the disciples' hands, and from John's account draws the lesson that the Lord uses what is offered to him. JFB only refers to Mark 6.

#### Peter Confesses Jesus as the Christ (vv18–20)
- **"As he was praying alone" (v18).** Again Luke marks a turning point with prayer. Matthew and Mark place this at Caesarea Philippi in the far north. Luke skips the stretch of Mark between the feeding and this scene (Mark 6:45–8:26) and stresses the prayer instead.
- **"Who do the crowds say that I am?" (v18).** The answers repeat Herod's list (vv7–8). People rate Jesus very highly, a prophet sent from God, and still fall short.
- **"But who do you say that I am?" (v20).** The "you" is plural and emphatic.
- **"The Christ of God" (v20).** "Christ" is the Greek for the Hebrew "Messiah," "Anointed One." Israel's kings were "the Lord's anointed" (1 Sam 24:6; Ps 2:2). Peter's phrase means "God's own Anointed," the promised King. Luke's version is the shortest. Matthew 16:16–19 adds "the Son of the living God" and Jesus' blessing on Peter.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives the disciples' confession as their own: "we know that thou art the Christ of God." Guzik stresses that Peter confessed God's Messiah, not the political deliverer the crowds wanted. JFB only gives the parallels in Matthew and Mark.

#### Jesus Foretells His Death (vv21–27)
- **"He strictly charged and commanded them to tell this to no one" (v21).** The word "Messiah" stirred dreams of a warrior king who would drive out Rome. Until they understood the cross, announcing it would cause the wrong kind of excitement.
- **"The Son of Man must suffer many things" (v22).** "Must" (*dei*) is one of Luke's key words: God's plan requires it (17:25; 22:37; 24:7, 26). The "elders and chief priests and scribes" made up the Sanhedrin, Israel's ruling council in Jerusalem. This is the first of Jesus' plain predictions of his death, and it always ends with resurrection "on the third day."
- **"Deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me" (v23).** "To all" widens this beyond the Twelve. In the Roman world, a man carrying a cross was a condemned man walking to his execution. Jesus' hearers had seen it on Galilean roads. Only Luke adds "daily." This is not a single heroic moment but a daily dying to self-will in order to follow.
- **"Whoever would save his life will lose it" (v24).** "Life" is *psychē*, life or self. Holding on to life on one's own terms ends in losing it. Letting go of it for Jesus' sake ends in finding it.
- **"Whoever is ashamed of me and of my words" (v26).** Shame was a powerful force in that culture. A crucified Messiah was shameful by every standard (1 Cor 1:23). The Son of Man coming "in his glory" echoes Daniel 7:13–14.
- **"Some standing here who will not taste death until they see the kingdom of God" (v27).** This is a hard verse. Readings include the Transfiguration, which follows at once; the resurrection and Pentecost; the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70; or the second coming. The Tyndale notes and many interpreters think the Transfiguration is the most likely first fulfillment, since Luke places it immediately after and some of "those standing here" saw the kingdom's glory there.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The body cannot be happy if the soul be miserable in the other world." JFB glosses "would save" as "is minded to save," bent on saving, and takes v27 as the firm establishment of the kingdom within the lifetime of some present. Guzik explains that condemned men carried the crossbeam, and stresses that "daily" makes this a continuing attitude of the heart. He links v27 to the Transfiguration.

#### The Transfiguration (vv28–36)
- **"About eight days after these sayings" (v28).** Mark and Matthew say "after six days." Luke's "about eight" is approximate and probably counts inclusively. Either way, Luke ties the mountain to the sayings about the cross.
- **"Went up on the mountain to pray" (v28).** Tradition since the fourth century names Mount Tabor in lower Galilee. Many scholars prefer Mount Hermon, over 9,000 feet high and near Caesarea Philippi. Only Luke says Jesus went up to pray, and that the change came "as he was praying."
- **"The appearance of his face was altered, and his clothing became dazzling white" (v29).** "Dazzling" means flashing like lightning. Luke avoids Mark's word *metamorphoō*, "transfigured," perhaps because Greek readers associated it with pagan myths of gods changing shape. When Moses came down from Sinai his face shone with reflected glory (Exod 34:29). Jesus' glory is his own.
- **"Moses and Elijah" (v30).** Moses gave the Law, and Elijah stood for the Prophets. Malachi named them together in the Old Testament's final verses (Mal 4:4–5).
- **"Spoke of his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem" (v31).** Only Luke tells the topic. "Departure" is *exodos*. As God rescued Israel from Egypt in the first exodus, Jesus will rescue his people by his death, resurrection and ascension in Jerusalem. He will "accomplish" it, literally "fulfill" it. He is not a victim.
- **"Heavy with sleep" (v32).** Only Luke notes it. The same three will sleep again in Gethsemane while Jesus prays (22:45). They woke up in time to see "his glory," and Peter later wrote of being an eyewitness (2 Pet 1:16–18; compare John 1:14).
- **"Let us make three tents… not knowing what he said" (v33).** "Tents" (*skēnē*) recalls the tabernacle and the booths of the Feast of Tabernacles (Lev 23:42). Peter wants to hold on to the moment, and he puts Jesus on a level with Moses and Elijah.
- **"A cloud came and overshadowed them" (v34).** The cloud is the cloud of God's presence, which led Israel, filled the tabernacle and covered Sinai (Exod 13:21; 24:15–16; 40:34). "Overshadowed" is the word used for the Spirit coming on Mary (1:35).
- **"This is my Son, my Chosen One; listen to him!" (v35).** Three Old Testament echoes. "My Son" is from Psalm 2:7. "My Chosen One" is from Isaiah 42:1, the servant who will suffer. "Listen to him" is from Deuteronomy 18:15, where Moses promised a prophet like himself: "it is to him you shall listen." The answer to Peter's three tents: there is no equal.
- **"Jesus was found alone" (v36).** Moses and Elijah fade. Jesus remains. The Law and Prophets point to him, and when they have done their work, he is the one left standing.
- **Commentators:** JFB sees the Transfiguration as an answer to prayer and identifies the cloud as the Shekinah, the visible glory of God's presence. It notes that "decease" (*exodos*) is a gentle word for death, and that such glimpses of glory are always "departing" ones. Henry says those "know not what they say that talk of making tabernacles on earth for glorified saints in heaven." Guzik reads Peter's tents as an unwitting wish to avoid the cross and to put Jesus level with the prophets, and stresses the command "listen to him" as the point of the whole event.

#### Jesus Heals a Boy with an Unclean Spirit (vv37–43a)
- **"When they had come down from the mountain" (v37).** From glory to chaos.
- **"He is my only child" (v38).** Only Luke says so, as with the widow's son (7:12) and Jairus's daughter (8:42). A father's whole future is at stake.
- **"A spirit seizes him… convulses him so that he foams at the mouth" (v39).** The symptoms resemble epilepsy, and Matthew 17:15 uses a word that means "moonstruck," the ancient term for epileptic. The Gospels attribute this case to a demon. Scripture does not say all seizures are demonic. It says this one was.
- **"I begged your disciples to cast it out, but they could not" (v40).** The same men who had authority "over all demons" (v1) fail. Mark 9:29 gives the reason as prayer. Their power was never their own.
- **"O faithless and twisted generation" (v41).** The words echo Moses' song about Israel, "a crooked and twisted generation" (Deut 32:5). The rebuke takes in the crowd, the scribes (Mark 9:14) and the disciples alike.
- **"Gave him back to his father" (v42).** Luke uses the same phrase as at Nain (7:15). Jesus restores families.
- **"All were astonished at the majesty of God" (v43a).** "Majesty" (*megaleiotēs*) is the word Peter uses for the Transfiguration (2 Pet 1:16). The glory seen on the mountain is now seen in mercy in the valley.
- **Commentators:** Guzik says the disciples' failure taught them dependence, not technique, and suggests that the enemy often strikes hardest when someone is being brought to Christ. JFB reads "the majesty of God" as Christ's divine grandeur. Henry treats the healing as Christ's power where his disciples fail.

#### Jesus Again Foretells His Death (vv43b–45)
- **"Let these words sink into your ears" (v44).** Literally "put these words into your ears." "Delivered into the hands of men" is a play on words: the Son of Man handed over to men.
- **"It was concealed from them, so that they might not perceive it" (v45).** They could not yet grasp a suffering Messiah. Their understanding opens only after the resurrection, when Jesus "opened their minds to understand the Scriptures" (24:45). "Afraid to ask" is painfully human: they half sense they will not like the answer.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v44 as a repeated warning of his sufferings and notes that they "feared" to ask. Henry treats it as Christ's second notice of what lay ahead.

#### Who Is the Greatest? (vv46–48)
- **"An argument arose among them as to which of them was the greatest" (v46).** Right after Jesus speaks of his betrayal, they argue about rank. The argument returns even at the Last Supper (22:24).
- **"Took a child and put him by his side" (v47).** In that world, children had no status or rights, and a child could do nothing for anyone's reputation. Jesus stands the child next to himself, the place of honor.
- **"Whoever receives this child in my name receives me" (v48).** To welcome the lowliest person for Jesus' sake is to welcome Jesus, and in him the Father who sent him. "He who is least among you all is the one who is great" turns the ranking upside down.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the child is a picture of someone with no claim to status, and that Jesus, the humble one, points indirectly to himself. The Tyndale notes stress how shocking it was to give honor to a child.

#### Anyone Not Against Us Is For Us (vv49–50)
- **"We saw someone casting out demons in your name, and we tried to stop him" (v49).** John, who has just failed to cast out a demon (v40), objects to a man who succeeded, because "he does not follow with us." Moses faced the same complaint about Eldad and Medad prophesying outside the camp and answered, "Would that all the LORD's people were prophets" (Num 11:26–29).
- **"The one who is not against you is for you" (v50).** In 11:23 Jesus says the reverse: "Whoever is not with me is against me." Both are true. One warns against narrow suspicion of others doing real work in Jesus' name. The other warns against neutrality toward Jesus himself.
- **Commentators:** JFB treats Christ's answer as a standing principle against exclusiveness. Guzik commends the generous spirit and the Tyndale notes add that it is a proverb, to be applied with wisdom, as 11:23 shows.

#### A Samaritan Village Rejects Jesus (vv51–56)
- **"When the days drew near for him to be taken up" (v51).** "Taken up" (*analēmpsis*) points to the ascension (Acts 1:2, 11), the goal beyond the cross. "Drew near" is literally "were being fulfilled."
- **"He set his face to go to Jerusalem" (v51).** The phrase echoes Isaiah's servant: "I have set my face like a flint" (Isa 50:7). This begins Luke's long travel narrative. The journey is not a straight route, but every step from here leans toward Jerusalem.
- **"A village of the Samaritans" (v52).** The direct road from Galilee to Jerusalem ran through Samaria. Samaritans were descendants of northern Israelites mixed with peoples the Assyrians settled there (2 Kings 17:24–41). They worshiped on Mount Gerizim, not in Jerusalem, and the two peoples despised each other (John 4:9, 20). Pilgrims headed to Jerusalem for a feast were often unwelcome.
- **"Do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them?" (v54).** James and John were nicknamed "Sons of Thunder" (Mark 3:17). They are thinking of Elijah, who called down fire on soldiers sent by the king of Samaria (2 Kings 1:10–12).
- **"He turned and rebuked them" (v55).** Some later manuscripts add "as Elijah did" in v54, and in vv55–56 add, "You do not know what manner of spirit you are of; for the Son of Man came not to destroy people's lives but to save them." The KJV includes these words. The earliest manuscripts lack them, and the ESV leaves them out. The added line fits Jesus' teaching (19:10) even if Luke did not write it here.
- **"They went on to another village" (v56).** Later Samaritans receive the gospel gladly (Acts 8:5–25), and John himself goes there to pray for them (Acts 8:14).
- **Commentators:** JFB names James and John as the "sons of thunder" and says the rebuke targeted the spirit behind the request, since the Son of Man's mission is "for salvation, never destruction." Guzik ties the set face of v51 to Isaiah 50:7 and says that firm resolve was full of mercy, not anger.

#### The Cost of Following Jesus (vv57–62)
- **"I will follow you wherever you go" (vv57–58).** Matthew 8:19 says this man was a scribe. Jesus answers that "the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head." Following him means giving up the security of home.
- **"Lord, let me first go and bury my father" (v59).** Burying a parent was one of the most sacred duties in Jewish life, outranking even some religious obligations. The man may mean his father has just died, or, as many scholars think, that he wants to wait until his father dies and the inheritance is settled, which could be years.
- **"Leave the dead to bury their own dead" (v60).** A deliberately shocking line: let the spiritually dead bury the physically dead. The kingdom is so urgent it outranks even the most honored family duty. "Go and proclaim the kingdom of God" is the call.
- **"Let me first say farewell to those at my home" (v61).** When Elijah called Elisha, Elisha asked to kiss his parents goodbye, and Elijah let him (1 Kings 19:19–21). Jesus asks for more than Elijah did.
- **"No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God" (v62).** A light wooden plow was steered with one hand while the other drove the oxen. A farmer who looked over his shoulder plowed a crooked furrow. The word is "fit," that is, suitable or usable. Elisha burned his plow and followed (1 Kings 19:21).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those who begin with the work of God must resolve to go on with it, or they will make nothing of it." JFB reads "looking back" as reluctance to break with the world. Guzik sees Jesus being honest with all three about the cost, and preferring true followers over a crowd of half-hearted ones.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 9 answers the question the earlier chapters kept asking. Jesus is "the Christ of God," and the Father himself says it on the mountain: "This is my Son, my Chosen One; listen to him!" Yet this Christ must suffer, be rejected and be killed, and on the third day be raised. The glory on the mountain and the cross in Jerusalem belong together. Moses and Elijah speak of his exodus, his death that will set his people free. He feeds the hungry, frees a father's only son, welcomes a child, refuses to call down fire on his enemies and sets his face toward Jerusalem to die for them. Those who follow him take up their cross daily, because he took up his first.
