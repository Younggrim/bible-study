*Setting:* Psalm 135 follows straight on from the last of the Songs of Ascents. Psalm 134 called on "all you servants of the LORD, who stand by night in the house of the LORD" to bless him. Psalm 135 opens with almost the same words and answers that call with a full song of praise. It begins and ends with "Praise the LORD!" (Hebrew *hallelu-yah*, "Hallelujah"), and in later Jewish worship it is paired with Psalm 136; some traditions count the two together as part of the "Great Hallel," the great song of praise. No author is named and no date is given. Most think it was written after the return from exile in Babylon, because it is woven almost entirely out of earlier Scripture. Kidner, as Guzik reports, observes that nearly every verse echoes, quotes or is quoted by another passage. It borrows from the Law of Moses (Exod 3; 18; 19; Deut 32), from Jeremiah (Jer 10), and above all from Psalm 115, whose words about idols it repeats almost exactly. This is not a lack of originality. The psalmist gathers the great truths Israel had already been given and sets them to music. The psalm moves from a call to praise, through God's greatness in creation and in history, to a contrast between the living God and lifeless idols, and back to praise. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### Praise the LORD, for the LORD Is Good (vv1–4)
- **"Praise the LORD! Praise the name of the LORD" (v1).** The opening lines are nearly identical to Psalm 113:1. To praise God's "name" is to praise God as he has made himself known. His name stands for his character and his reputation (Exod 34:5–7). "LORD" in small capitals stands for God's personal covenant name, *YHWH*, which he revealed to Moses at the burning bush (Exod 3:14–15). "Praise the LORD" is *hallelu-yah*, using the short form of that name, *Yah*.
- **"O servants of the LORD, who stand in the house of the LORD" (vv1–2).** These are the words of Psalm 134:1, now widened. Psalm 134 spoke to those serving "by night." Here the call reaches "the courts of the house of our God," the open courtyards where ordinary Israelites gathered. The priests stood in the inner court. The people worshiped in the outer courts. Both are included.
- **"Praise the LORD, for the LORD is good; sing to his name, for it is pleasant!" (v3).** "Good" (*tov*) and "pleasant" (*na'im*) are the same pair of words that opened Psalm 133: "how good and pleasant it is when brothers dwell in unity!" There the words described unity among God's people. Here they describe God himself, and the act of praising him. Psalm 147:1 says the same: "it is good to sing praises to our God; for it is pleasant." It is not entirely clear in v3 whether "it is pleasant" refers to his name or to singing. The Hebrew can be read either way, and both are true.
- **"For the LORD has chosen Jacob for himself" (v4).** The first reason for praise is not creation or the Exodus but God's choice. "Jacob" and "Israel" are two names for the same people, the twelve tribes descended from Jacob, whom God renamed Israel (Gen 32:28). Israel did not choose God first. God chose them, and not because they were great: "It was not because you were more in number than any other people... but it is because the LORD loves you" (Deut 7:7–8).
- **"Israel as his own possession" (v4).** "Possession" translates *segullah*, a word for personal treasure. King David used it for the gold and silver from his own private fortune that he gave for the temple (1 Chron 29:3), and Ecclesiastes for the treasure of kings (Eccl 2:8). At Sinai God told Israel, "you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples, for all the earth is mine" (Exod 19:5). He owns the whole world, yet he calls this people his own treasure.
- **Commentators:** Henry says praise is so delightful that "the work is its own wages," and that "it is a heaven upon earth to be praising God." He adds that those "admitted to the privileges of his house" have the most reason to praise. JFB notes that the priests are specially mentioned and that God's choice of Israel is "the first reason assigned for rendering praise." Guzik stresses that the psalm gives reasons for praise rather than simply stirring up feeling, and he passes on Spurgeon's point that praise should be intelligent, grounded in who God is and what he has done. He also cites Brooks, through Spurgeon, on *segullah* as a king's special jewels kept for his own use. The lexicon supports "treasure" or "personal property" as the meaning.

#### The LORD Is Above All Gods (vv5–7)
- **"For I know that the LORD is great" (v5).** The voice turns personal, "I know." This echoes Jethro, Moses' father-in-law, after he heard how God rescued Israel from Egypt: "Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods" (Exod 18:11). The psalmist's knowledge is not a guess. It rests on what God has done.
- **"Our Lord is above all gods" (v5).** Here "Lord" in ordinary letters is *'adon*, "master" or "sovereign," a different word from *YHWH* in the first line. The nations around Israel believed in many gods, each ruling a piece of the world. The psalm does not stop to argue whether those gods exist. Whatever spiritual beings or imagined powers the nations worshiped, the LORD is far above all of them. The study notes put it this way: the LORD is superior to any real or imagined supernatural being. Verses 15–18 will show what those "gods" actually amount to.
- **"Whatever the LORD pleases, he does, in heaven and on earth, in the seas and all deeps" (v6).** This repeats Psalm 115:3: "Our God is in the heavens; he does all that he pleases." The list covers every realm the ancient world imagined: the sky above, the land, the sea and "the deeps," the waters thought to lie beneath the earth. Pagan religion divided these among different gods. The Greeks later gave the sky to Zeus, the sea to Poseidon and the underworld to Hades. Canaanite religion had Baal, the storm god, and Yam, the sea god. The psalm says one God rules them all, and he does what he pleases. His freedom is not whim. It is the freedom of a good God who is answerable to no one.
- **"He it is who makes the clouds rise at the end of the earth" (v7).** Verse 7 is quoted almost word for word from Jeremiah 10:13 (repeated in Jer 51:16), a chapter that mocks idols. Rain clouds in Israel came in from the Mediterranean, rising on the western horizon (1 Kings 18:44; Luke 12:54). "The end of the earth" means the far horizon.
- **"Who makes lightnings for the rain and brings forth the wind from his storehouses" (v7).** In Canaan, Baal was worshiped as the god of storm, lightning and rain, the one who made the crops grow. Israel was constantly tempted to turn to him (1 Kings 18). This verse claims storm, lightning and wind for the LORD alone. "Storehouses" is a poetic picture of God keeping the weather in his treasuries, releasing it as he chooses (Job 38:22; Ps 33:7).
- **Commentators:** JFB says heaven, earth, seas and deeps together "denote universality." Henry treats vv5–7 as a proof of God's "absolute and universal power" over all creation. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that the pagans divided the world among Jupiter, Neptune and Pluto, while the LORD rules every region. He also notes, with Horne, that the lightning belongs to its real owner, not to Baal, and Poole's point that believers should not be silent about their God when others loudly praise their idols.

#### He Struck Down the Firstborn of Egypt (vv8–12)
- **"He it was who struck down the firstborn of Egypt, both of man and of beast" (v8).** From creation the psalm moves to history. It begins with the tenth and last plague, the death of the firstborn on the night of the first Passover (Exod 12:29). JFB notes that the last plague is named to stand for all of them. The phrase "both of man and of beast" is exact: Exodus says the firstborn of livestock died too (Exod 12:29; 13:15). Egypt worshiped many gods in animal form, and God said, "on all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgments" (Exod 12:12).
- **"Who in your midst, O Egypt, sent signs and wonders" (v9).** The psalmist suddenly speaks to Egypt directly, as if the nation were standing there. "Signs and wonders" is the standard phrase for the plagues (Deut 4:34; 6:22; Neh 9:10). They were "against Pharaoh and all his servants," a deliberate contrast with v1. Pharaoh's servants opposed God and fell. The LORD's servants stand in his house and praise him.
- **"Who struck down many nations and killed mighty kings" (v10).** After the Exodus and the wilderness years, God brought Israel into the land. The same verb, "struck down" (*nakah*), is used for the firstborn of Egypt in v8 and the nations here. The God who judged Egypt also cleared the way into Canaan.
- **"Sihon, king of the Amorites, and Og, king of Bashan" (v11).** These two kings ruled east of the Jordan River. They were the first kings Israel defeated on the way to the Promised Land (Num 21:21–35; Deut 2:24–3:11). Sihon refused Israel passage and attacked them. Og was remembered as a giant whose iron bed was over thirteen feet long (Deut 3:11). Their defeat became a byword in Israel for God's power to give victory (Josh 2:10; Neh 9:22). Psalm 136 names them again (136:19–20).
- **"And gave their land as a heritage, a heritage to his people Israel" (v12).** "Heritage" (*nachalah*) means an inheritance, land handed down within a family. The word is repeated for emphasis. The land was a gift. Israel did not earn it. JFB puts it plainly: "the conquest of Canaan was by God's power, not that of the people" (Deut 8:17–18; Josh 24:12–13).
- **A hard question.** The psalm praises God for striking down nations. Scripture presents the conquest as God's judgment on the long-ripening sin of Canaan (Gen 15:16; Deut 9:4–5), not as Israel's ethnic superiority. The same Law warns that Israel would be expelled in turn if it did the same things (Lev 18:24–28), and that happened in the exile. The psalm celebrates that God keeps his promises and defeats those who oppose his purposes.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the last plague is cited "to illustrate His 'tokens and wonders,'" and stresses that the land came by God's power alone. Henry sees in these victories proof that "no power of hell or earth can prevent the accomplishment of the promise of God." Guzik passes on Maclaren's observation that the psalmists return again and again to the Exodus and the conquest for courage, and Spurgeon's point that God's servants fared far better than Pharaoh's.

#### Your Name, O LORD, Endures Forever (vv13–14)
- **"Your name, O LORD, endures forever, your renown, O LORD, throughout all ages" (v13).** Now the psalmist speaks to God directly. The verse echoes what God said at the burning bush: "This is my name forever, and thus I am to be remembered throughout all generations" (Exod 3:15). "Renown" (*zeker*) is literally "remembrance," the memory of who God is and what he has done. JFB notes that both words "denote that by which God is made known." Idols come and go. Empires rise and fall. The LORD's name lasts.
- **"For the LORD will vindicate his people and have compassion on his servants" (v14).** This line is quoted exactly from the Song of Moses (Deut 32:36). "Vindicate" (*din*) means to judge, to plead someone's cause and give them justice. JFB glosses it "do justice." "Have compassion" (*nacham*) can mean to be moved with pity, or to relent, to change one's dealings. In Deuteronomy 32 the verse comes after God has disciplined his people. When they are helpless, he turns and takes pity on them. For a people returned from exile, that was the hope they lived on.
- **Commentators:** JFB links "have compassion" to God changing his dealings with his people (Ps 90:13). Henry treats vv13–14 as the enduring mercy God shows his people in every age. Guzik passes on Boice's point that God does not change: he is always as good as he has been. He also notes VanGemeren's observation that God's name grows in meaning as he acts in history.

#### The Idols of the Nations (vv15–18)
- **"The idols of the nations are silver and gold, the work of human hands" (v15).** Verses 15–18 repeat Psalm 115:4–8 almost word for word, as the study notes point out. The idols were costly and beautifully made, but they were made. The LORD made heaven and earth (134:3). People made the idols.
- **"They have mouths, but do not speak; they have eyes, but do not see" (v16).** The craftsman carved a mouth, eyes and ears, but no life. The contrast is sharp: the LORD speaks, sees and hears (Ps 94:9). Isaiah mocks the man who cuts down a tree, burns half for firewood and bows down to the other half (Isa 44:14–20).
- **"Nor is there any breath in their mouths" (v17).** Here the psalm departs from Psalm 115. Psalm 115 goes on to mention noses, hands and feet. Psalm 135 ends the list with breath (*ruach*), the word for breath, wind and spirit. Jeremiah said the same of idols: "there is no breath in them" (Jer 10:14). The LORD is the one who gives breath to all living things (Gen 2:7). He brings the wind from his storehouses (v7). The idols cannot breathe at all.
- **"Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them" (v18).** This is the most searching line in the psalm. Worshipers grow like what they worship. Those who give their hearts to lifeless things become spiritually lifeless: unable to see, hear or speak truly about God. Isaiah describes it (Isa 6:9–10; 44:18), and Paul traces it in Romans 1:21–23. JFB notes that the verb can also be read as a wish, "may they become like them," and says idolaters "become spiritually stupid and perish with their idols." The opposite is also true: those who behold the Lord are "being transformed into the same image" (2 Cor 3:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry says idolatry "made the worshippers sensual and senseless." JFB refers the whole section back to Psalm 115:4–8. Guzik passes on Morgan's point that people become like the gods they worship, and Boice's counterpoint that worshiping the true God, by grace, makes people more like him.

#### Bless the LORD, O House of Israel (vv19–21)
- **"O house of Israel... O house of Aaron... O house of Levi... You who fear the LORD" (vv19–20).** The call to praise widens in rings. "House of Israel" is the whole nation. "House of Aaron" is the priests, Aaron's descendants. "House of Levi" is the wider tribe of Levi, who served the priests as singers, gatekeepers and helpers. Psalm 115:9–11 and Psalm 118:2–4 have three groups. Psalm 135 adds the house of Levi, the Levites who were among those "who stand in the house of the LORD" (v2). "You who fear the LORD" may be a summary of all faithful worshipers, or it may include Gentiles who had come to worship Israel's God (1 Kings 8:41–43; Acts 10:2). Guzik, following Spurgeon, takes it to include such converts.
- **"Bless the LORD" (vv19–20).** JFB notes that where Psalm 115 calls each group to "trust in the LORD," Psalm 135 calls them to "bless the LORD." The fourfold "bless the LORD" answers Psalm 134, which closed with the LORD blessing his people "from Zion."
- **"Blessed be the LORD from Zion, he who dwells in Jerusalem!" (v21).** In Psalm 134:3 the blessing came *from* the LORD out of Zion to his people. Here blessing goes back *to* the LORD out of Zion. God who fills heaven and earth chose to dwell among his people in a city on a hill. JFB says that from the Church, "as a center, His praise is diffused throughout the earth."
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v21).** The psalm ends where it began, with *hallelu-yah*.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the closing call together as an invitation to every rank of God's people, from priests to ordinary worshipers, to bless the God whose presence is in Zion. Guzik notes, with Poole, that calling God the one "who dwells in Jerusalem" sets him apart from the local gods of other nations, because the God of Zion is the Maker of all.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 135 praises the God who chose a people as his treasure, who rules creation, who struck down the firstborn of Egypt to set his people free, and who stands alone against lifeless idols. Each theme finds its fullest meaning in Jesus. The firstborn of Egypt died on the night of the first Passover, but the firstborn of Israel were spared under the blood of the lamb. Jesus is "Christ, our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7), and he is himself God's "firstborn" who was not spared (Rom 8:29, 32). Through him people from every nation become God's "own possession" (1 Pet 2:9; Titus 2:14). The name that "endures forever" is the name God has given him, "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9). And where idols have mouths that cannot speak and no breath at all, Jesus is the Word made flesh (John 1:14), who breathed on his disciples and said, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22). Those who look to him do not become lifeless like idols. They are made alive and are changed into his likeness (2 Cor 3:18; Col 3:10).
