*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. David, near the end of his life, is setting the temple's service in order: the Levites in chapter 23, the priests in chapter 24, and now the musicians. Of the 4,000 Levites assigned to praise God (23:5), this chapter names the leading families and divides 288 trained musicians into twenty-four divisions of twelve, matching the twenty-four priestly divisions. The three families are those of Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun, the men David first appointed when the ark came to Jerusalem (15:16–19; 16:4–6, 41–42). Their names stand at the head of many psalms, and their descendants were still leading worship in the Chronicler's day (Ezra 2:41; 3:10; Neh 11:17). This chapter tells those singers where their calling came from. It also says something surprising about what music in God's house is: these men "prophesied" with their instruments.

#### David Organizes the Musicians (vv1–31)
- **"David and the chiefs of the service also set apart" (v1).** The Hebrew is *sare hatsaba*, which the KJV renders "captains of the host," as if army officers helped choose the musicians. But *tsaba* can mean "service" as well as "army," and Numbers 4:3, 23 uses it for the Levites' work at the tabernacle. The ESV's "chiefs of the service" is likely right: these were the leaders already involved in organizing the temple (23:2; 24:6). "Set apart" (*badal*) is the word used for separating something for holy use, as Levi was separated from the other tribes (Num 8:14; Deut 10:8).
- **"Who prophesied with lyres, with harps, and with cymbals" (v1).** This is the most striking phrase in the chapter. To "prophesy" (*naba*) usually means to speak God's word. Here it describes temple music. Music and prophecy were linked in Israel: Saul met "a group of prophets… with harp, tambourine, flute, and lyre… prophesying" (1 Sam 10:5), and Elisha called for a musician before giving God's word (2 Kings 3:15). Interpreters take it in two main ways. Some think the musicians sometimes spoke direct messages from God, as Jahaziel, "a Levite of the sons of Asaph," did before a battle (2 Chr 20:14–17). Others think it means their inspired praise itself declared God's truth to the people. Many of the psalms these men sang were later recognized as Scripture, and the New Testament quotes some of them as words of the Holy Spirit (Acts 4:25). Either way, worship in God's house was not performance. It was a ministry of God's word.
- **Asaph (v2).** He was a Gershonite (6:39–43). Twelve psalms carry his name (Ps 50; 73–83), including Psalm 73, his struggle with the prosperity of the wicked. His four sons served "under the direction of Asaph," who "prophesied under the direction of the king." "Under the direction" is literally "on the hands of." Asaph was under David, and his sons under him.
- **Jeduthun (v3).** He was a Merarite, probably the same man called Ethan elsewhere (15:17, 19). Psalms 39, 62 and 77 mention him in their titles. His family "prophesied with the lyre in thanksgiving and praise." Verse 3 says "six," but most Hebrew manuscripts list only five names. The ESV follows one Hebrew manuscript and the Greek translation in adding **Shimei**, who appears as head of the tenth division in v17.
- **Heman (vv4–5).** He was a Kohathite and the grandson of the prophet Samuel (6:33–34). He had led the singers in the procession of the ark (15:17, 19). Psalm 88, the darkest psalm in the Psalter, is credited to "Heman the Ezrahite," who may be the same man, though the title "Ezrahite" may point to a different Heman, a famous wise man (1 Kings 4:31).
- **Heman's sons and a hidden prayer (v4).** Many scholars have noticed something unusual about the last nine names in v4. With small changes in vowels, they read in Hebrew like lines of a short psalm: *Hananiah, Hanani* ("Be gracious to me, O LORD, be gracious to me"), *Eliathah* ("you are my God"), *Giddalti* ("I have magnified") and *Romamti-ezer* ("I have exalted [your] help"), *Joshbekashah* ("sitting in distress"), *Mallothi* ("I have spoken"), *Hothir* ("abundantly"), *Mahazioth* ("visions"). Some think the sons were named after the words of a psalm, or that a psalm's opening words were later read as names. Others think it is a coincidence of sound. Whatever the explanation, it fits a family whose life was praise.
- **"Heman the king's seer, according to the promise of God to exalt him" (v5).** A "seer" is a prophet (1 Sam 9:9). Gad (21:9) and Jeduthun (2 Chr 35:15) are also called the king's seer. "To exalt him" is literally "to lift up the horn." In the Old Testament a horn stands for strength and honor, from the horns of a wild ox (1 Sam 2:1, 10; Ps 89:17, 24). God honored Heman with a large family to carry on his work. JFB also mentions the possibility that "lift up the horn" refers to Heman's own music, though the ESV's reading fits the next clause better.
- **"Fourteen sons and three daughters" (v5).** A large family was seen as God's blessing (Ps 127:3–5). The daughters are not given duties in the list, but women did sing in Israel's worship (Ps 68:25; Ezra 2:65 lists male and female singers). JFB suggests the daughters sang in the choir, which is possible but not stated.
- **"Under the direction of their father… Asaph, Jeduthun, and Heman were under the order of the king" (v6).** There is a clear chain: sons under fathers, fathers under the king. Worship was not left to individual taste. It had leaders and order. That order was not a cage. Sons learning from fathers is how the music was passed on for centuries.
- **"Trained in singing to the LORD, all who were skillful, was 288" (v7).** "Trained" (*lamad*, "taught") and "skillful" (*mebin*, "understanding") describe musicians who had learned their craft. This number is twenty-four divisions of twelve. The 288 were probably the leaders or masters who directed the larger body of 4,000. Skill matters in worship: "play skillfully on the strings, with loud shouts" (Ps 33:3).
- **"They cast lots for their duties, small and great, teacher and pupil alike" (v8).** As with the priests (24:5, 31), lots decided the order of service. The most famous musician drew from the same lot as the youngest apprentice. "Teacher and pupil" also shows how the music was passed on: each division contained experienced masters and learners.
- **The twenty-four divisions (vv9–31).** Each of Heman's fourteen, Jeduthun's six and Asaph's four sons led a division of twelve, "his sons and his brothers." The names appear with slight differences in spelling from vv2–4: **Izri** (v11) is Zeri, **Jesharelah** (v14) is Asharelah, **Azarel** (v18) is Uzziel, **Shubael** (v20) is Shebuel, and **Jeremoth** (v22) is Jerimoth. The lots mixed the families together. Asaph's four sons all fall among the first seven, while Heman's sons fill most of the later places. Like the priests, each division probably served for a week at a time in rotation.
- **Commentators:** Henry explains: "To prophesy, in this place, means praising God with great earnestness and devout affections, under the influences of the Holy Spirit," and "in raising these affections, poetry and music were employed." He warns that without the Spirit's "life and fervour" in our devotions, even well-ordered worship falls flat. JFB reads "prophesied" as "played with instruments," a usage it thinks "most probably originated in the practice of the prophets," who used music to stir their spirits. JFB also calculates the 288 as twenty-four groups of twelve, the masters of a musical body that "comprised no less than four thousand." Guzik, working from the KJV's "captains of the army," sees a link between the nation's security and its worship, though the ESV's "chiefs of the service" makes that connection less likely. He reads the lots as guarding the great and the teachers from pride and giving the small and the learners their place. He also stresses Heman's role as a father who trained his children in God's service.

#### Pointing to Christ
These Levites sang the psalms, many of them written by David, the "sweet psalmist of Israel" (2 Sam 23:1), and the chapter calls their singing prophecy. The New Testament agrees. Jesus said that everything "written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44). The songs these choirs sang, of a king on Zion (Ps 2), a sufferer who cries "why have you forsaken me?" (Ps 22) and a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek (Ps 110), were prophecies of Christ. On the night before he died, Jesus himself sang a hymn with his disciples (Matt 26:30), most likely the psalms sung at Passover (Ps 113–118). Now the whole church takes up this ministry: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another… singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs" (Col 3:16). Small and great, teacher and pupil alike, all who belong to Christ are set apart to sing.
