*Setting:* Jerusalem, in the first year of Hezekiah's reign, about 715 BC on the most common dating. In two weeks the king reopened the temple his father Ahaz had locked, had it cleaned out, and restored its sacrifices and music (ch 29). The cleansing finished on the sixteenth day of the first month (29:17), two days too late for Passover. To the north, the kingdom of Israel had fallen to Assyria. Samaria was taken in 722 BC, much of its population was deported, and foreigners were settled in their place (2 Kings 17:6, 24). Scholars debate whether this Passover came just before or just after that fall, depending on how Hezekiah's reign is dated; the invitation to a "remnant… who have escaped from the hand of the kings of Assyria" (v6) fits best after it. 2 Kings does not mention this Passover at all. The Chronicler tells it at length because it shows what he cares about most: all twelve tribes worshiping together at God's house. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading, "Passover Celebrated," so the later headings below simply follow its natural breaks.

#### Passover Celebrated (vv1–12)
- **"Hezekiah sent to all Israel and Judah, and wrote letters also to Ephraim and Manasseh" (v1).** Ephraim and Manasseh, the two tribes descended from Joseph, were the heart of the old northern kingdom. JFB notes that the names stand for the whole north. For two hundred years, since Jeroboam split the kingdom and set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:26–33), northerners had been kept away from Jerusalem. Hezekiah writes to invite them back. Henry gives the spirit of it: "Let them take whom they will for their king… so they will but take him for their God."
- **"To keep the Passover in the second month" (v2).** Passover was set for the fourteenth day of the first month (Exod 12:6). The law allowed one exception: anyone unclean from touching a dead body or away on a long journey could keep it a month later (Num 9:9–11). Hezekiah applies that personal rule to the whole nation. The Tyndale notes say this kept to the spirit of the law if not its letter. JFB calls it a permitted exception under Numbers 9.
- **"The priests had not consecrated themselves in sufficient number, nor had the people assembled" (v3).** Two problems: too few priests were ritually ready (as in 29:34), and there was no time to gather the nation. The plan "seemed right to the king and all the assembly" (v4). This was decided together, not imposed.
- **"From Beersheba to Dan" (v5).** The usual phrase is "from Dan to Beersheba," north to south. Here it is reversed, because the invitation starts in Judah and goes north. Either way it means the whole land God gave the twelve tribes, as the Tyndale notes say.
- **"They had not kept it as often as prescribed" (v5).** Passover had been neglected for a long time, perhaps not kept as a national feast for generations. 2 Kings 23:22 says Josiah's later Passover was the first like it "since the days of the judges."
- **"Couriers went throughout all Israel" (v6).** Literally "runners." JFB says they were royal messengers drawn from the king's bodyguard. Their letter is a short sermon built on one Hebrew word, *shuv*, "turn" or "return," used again and again: "return to the LORD… that he may turn again to the remnant of you" (v6); "that his fierce anger may turn away" (v8); "if you return… your brothers… will return to this land… he will not turn away his face" (v9).
- **"The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (v6).** The Chronicler likes to use "Israel" for Jacob here. It reminds the northerners that the name of their nation came from the man to whom God made his promises. They belong to the same family as Judah.
- **"The remnant of you who have escaped from the hand of the kings of Assyria" (v6).** "Kings" is plural. Tiglath-pileser, Shalmaneser and Sargon had each carved away part of the north (2 Kings 15:29; 17:3–6). What was left was a remnant.
- **"Do not now be stiff-necked as your fathers were" (v8).** "Stiff-necked" is a picture from farming: an ox that will not bend its neck to the yoke. God called Israel that at the golden calf (Exod 32:9). The cure is in the next words: "yield yourselves to the LORD," literally "give the hand," the gesture of making a pledge.
- **"The LORD your God is gracious and merciful" (v9).** This echoes what God said of himself to Moses after the golden calf: "a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger" (Exod 34:6). The letter promises that if the north returns, God might even move their captors to send their families home, as Solomon had prayed (1 Kings 8:50).
- **"They laughed them to scorn and mocked them" (v10).** Most northerners treated the runners as a joke. JFB says the messengers "met with open insults and ill usage." The Tyndale notes suggest the newcomers settled by Assyria, and many Israelites too, took Israel's defeat as proof that the LORD was powerless. Henry imagines the mockers saying their own high places "were as good," and observes, "Pride keeps men from yielding themselves to the Lord." Mocking God's messengers is the same sin the Chronicler names at the very end as the reason Judah fell (36:16).
- **"However, some men of Asher, of Manasseh, and of Zebulun humbled themselves" (v11).** Asher and Zebulun were far north, in Galilee. "Humbled themselves" is the first condition God gave Solomon at the temple's dedication: "if my people… humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn… then I will… forgive their sin and heal their land" (7:14). The Tyndale notes trace how this chapter walks through that promise: humbling (v11), turning (vv6–9), praying (v18), seeking (v19), healing (v20).
- **"The hand of God was also on Judah to give them one heart" (v12).** Judah's unity is credited to God, not to Hezekiah's persuasion. As in 29:36, the Chronicler sees God preparing hearts. The obedience was to the king's command, but the king's command was "by the word of the LORD."
- **Commentators:** Henry says Hezekiah's aim was spiritual renewal, not political reunion, and that the northern king did not forbid his people to go. Guzik draws on Numbers 9 for the legal basis of the delay, and from the mockery notes that the northerners offered no argument, only ridicule. He also passes on Trapp's report, from Josephus, that some of the messengers were killed, a later tradition not found in the text. JFB identifies the couriers as royal runners and notes their rough reception.

#### The Feast and the Prayer (vv13–22)
- **"A very great assembly" (v13).** Passover was followed at once by the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread (Lev 23:5–8), and the two are treated as one festival.
- **"They… removed the altars that were in Jerusalem… and threw [them] into the brook Kidron" (v14).** The temple was clean, but the city was not. Ahaz had built "altars in every corner of Jerusalem" (28:24). Before they eat, the worshipers themselves tear them down. JFB identifies these as the altars "which Ahaz had erected in the city."
- **"The priests and the Levites were ashamed" (v15).** The people's zeal put the clergy to shame. The Tyndale notes call it embarrassing for the professionals. Shame did its proper work: they consecrated themselves and went to their posts.
- **"The priests threw the blood that they received from the hand of the Levites" (v16).** Normally the head of each household slaughtered his own Passover lamb (Exod 12:6). Here the Levites did it for everyone who was ritually unclean (v17), so the lambs could still be offered properly. JFB notes this was a departure from normal practice.
- **"Many of them from Ephraim, Manasseh, Issachar, and Zebulun had not cleansed themselves, yet they ate the Passover otherwise than as prescribed" (v18).** The law was clear that an unclean person must not eat holy food (Lev 7:20–21; Num 9:6). These northerners had come long distances, in many cases after years far from true worship, and did not know or could not complete the week-long cleansing.
- **"May the good LORD pardon everyone who sets his heart to seek God" (vv18–19).** Hezekiah does not pretend the rule doesn't matter. He prays for pardon, which admits a real fault. The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew literally: "not according to the cleanness of holiness." His prayer rests on two things: God's goodness, and a heart "set" to seek him. "Sets his heart" means to prepare or fix it firmly.
- **"And the LORD heard Hezekiah and healed the people" (v20).** "Healed" (*rapha*) is the word of 7:14: "I will… heal their land." It may mean that God held back a sickness or plague the law warned of for eating holy things unclean (compare Lev 15:31), which is JFB's reading. More broadly, it means God forgave and accepted them. The Tyndale notes draw the lesson that God cares more about the heart's attitude than strict ritual detail. That is not new: "the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit" (Ps 51:17); "I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice" (Hos 6:6).
- **Readings differ.** Some read the passage as permission to set aside God's commands when the heart is sincere. Most interpreters, Henry among them, see it more carefully: Hezekiah treated the breach as sin needing pardon, and he applied to exceptional newcomers a mercy the law itself already showed in Numbers 9. Sincerity did not make the rule unimportant. It made the worshipers forgivable.
- **"Kept the Feast of Unleavened Bread seven days with great gladness… singing with all their might" (v21).** The ESV footnote says "with all their might" is literally "with instruments of might," as when David brought up the ark (1 Chr 13:8).
- **"Hezekiah spoke encouragingly to all the Levites who showed good skill" (v22).** "Spoke encouragingly" is literally "spoke to the heart." The phrase behind "good skill" can also be read "who taught good knowledge of the LORD." Guzik takes it that way: the Levites taught the newcomers from the north what they had never learned.
- **"Giving thanks to the LORD" (v22).** The Hebrew verb can mean "confess" or "give thanks," since both are a way of acknowledging God. Guzik passes on Poole's view that at this joyful feast, praise is the better sense.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the leaders were loath to send the unclean away, "lest they should discourage new converts." He stresses that Hezekiah's prayer did not excuse an unprepared heart; it only covered missing ceremony. Even sincere worshipers have faults that need pardon, and he says such pardon comes only through Christ's blood. "The Lord hearkened to Hezekiah," and "not only did not lay their sin to their charge," but accepted them. Guzik turns the passage into a pattern for coming to the Lord's Supper: put away idols, prepare the heart, confess, and pray. JFB reads "healed" as God sparing people from threatened disease.

#### Another Seven Days (vv23–27)
- **"The whole assembly agreed together to keep the feast for another seven days" (v23).** Nobody wanted to go home. JFB: "they doubled the period of celebration and kept it fourteen days." The only precedent was Solomon's temple dedication, which ran seven days and then seven more (7:8–9; 1 Kings 8:65).
- **"Hezekiah… gave the assembly 1,000 bulls and 7,000 sheep… the princes gave… 1,000 bulls and 10,000 sheep" (v24).** The king and leaders paid for the extra week, out of a treasury Ahaz had emptied for Assyria (28:21). These were mostly peace offerings, eaten by the worshipers in a shared feast. And by now, at last, "the priests consecrated themselves in great numbers."
- **"The sojourners who came out of the land of Israel, and the sojourners who lived in Judah" (v25).** "Sojourners" are foreigners living among Israel. Moses allowed them to keep Passover if they were circumcised (Exod 12:48–49). The Chronicler lists five groups, so that no one present is left out of the joy.
- **"Since the time of Solomon… there had been nothing like this in Jerusalem" (v26).** For the Chronicler, David and Solomon were the high point: one king, one temple, all Israel. For the first time in more than two hundred years, north and south worshiped together at God's house. Hezekiah is drawn as a second Solomon.
- **"The priests and the Levites arose and blessed the people" (v27).** Probably with the priestly blessing God gave Aaron: "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Num 6:22–27). Guzik passes on Payne's suggestion that the phrase means "the Levitical priests," since blessing was the priests' role.
- **"Their prayer came to his holy habitation in heaven" (v27).** At the dedication Solomon asked God again and again to "hear in heaven your dwelling place" when his people prayed toward this house (6:21, 30, 39). The chapter ends with that prayer answered. The temple on earth is real, but God's true dwelling is in heaven, and he heard.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that by this feast the reform "was greatly advanced and established," and the "nail in God's holy place clenched." Guzik notes how generous the king's and princes' gifts were with an emptied treasury, and links the closing blessing to Numbers 6. JFB notes that the extra days required more priests, who now came forward. JFB has no notes on vv25–27.

#### Pointing to Christ
Passover remembered the lamb whose blood turned away death in Egypt. Here it gathers scattered, unclean, half-taught people from the broken north, and they eat because a king prays for them: "May the good LORD pardon everyone who sets his heart to seek God." Jesus is both the Lamb and the praying King. "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7), and he intercedes for his people (Heb 7:25). Hezekiah's mercy was an exception that needed pardon. Christ's blood makes the unclean truly clean, and his table is open to people from every tribe who come to him humbled, Galilee included. Some laughed at Hezekiah's runners, but some humbled themselves and came. The gospel invitation still gets both answers.
