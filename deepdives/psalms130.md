*Setting:* Psalm 130 is the eleventh of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. Psalm 129 looked back on enemies who had afflicted Israel from outside. Psalm 130 goes deeper, to the trouble inside: sin, guilt, and the need for forgiveness. It has no author's name and no clear date. The church has long counted it among the seven "penitential psalms" (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130 and 143), psalms of sorrow for sin, and it is often called by the first words of its Latin translation, *De Profundis*, "Out of the depths." It has had a remarkable place in Christian history. Martin Luther set it as a hymn, "From depths of woe I cry to thee." The Puritan John Owen wrote a book of several hundred pages on it, much of it on v4. On the afternoon of May 24, 1738, John Wesley heard it sung as an anthem at St Paul's Cathedral in London, and that evening, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street, he felt his "heart strangely warmed." The psalm moves in four short steps: a cry (vv1–2), a confession (vv3–4), a wait (vv5–6), and an invitation to all Israel (vv7–8). The ESV marks those four steps with stanza breaks but gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow them.

#### Out of the Depths I Cry (vv1–2)
- **"Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD!" (v1).** "The depths" (*ma'amaqqim*) are deep waters, the bottom of the sea. In the Psalms deep water is a picture of being overwhelmed, close to death: "I have come into deep waters, and the flood sweeps over me" (Ps 69:2, 14). Jonah prayed from the belly of the fish, "the deep surrounded me" (Jonah 2:2–5). The psalm does not say what put this person in the depths, but vv3–4 make it clear that sin and guilt are at the heart of it.
- **"O LORD... O Lord" (vv1–2).** Two different names. "LORD" in capital letters is *YHWH*, God's covenant name, the name he revealed to Moses (Exod 3:14–15). "Lord" in lower case is *Adonai*, which means Master or Sovereign. The psalm uses both throughout, appealing to the God who made promises to Israel and who rules over all. Even from the depths, the psalmist knows whom to call.
- **"Hear my voice! Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my pleas for mercy!" (v2).** Solomon used the same words in his prayer at the dedication of the temple: "let your eyes be open and your ears attentive to the prayer in this place" (2 Chron 6:40). "Pleas for mercy" (*tachanunim*) comes from the root for grace or favor (*chanan*). The psalmist asks for grace, not for what is owed.
- **Commentators:** JFB takes the depths simply as "great distress." Guzik presses further: the depths here are chiefly the depths of guilt, a kind of spiritual drowning that no amount of self-improvement can fix. He passes on Trapp's remark that the psalmist uses two names for God as if taking hold of him with both hands. Henry heads these verses with "the psalmist's longing for God."

#### If You, O LORD, Should Mark Iniquities (vv3–4)
- **"If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities" (v3).** "Mark" (*shamar*) is a common verb meaning to watch, keep or guard. Here it means to keep a record, to watch for every sin and hold it against the sinner. "Iniquities" (*'awonot*) are crooked, twisted acts, and the word often includes the guilt that sin brings.
- **"O Lord, who could stand?" (v3).** To "stand" before a judge meant to be found innocent, to stand upright and not fall down condemned. The answer to the question is obvious: no one. "None is righteous, no, not one" (Rom 3:10, quoting Ps 14:3). Psalm 143:2 says the same: "no one living is righteous before you." The psalm does not pretend the psalmist is better than others. It begins from honest guilt.
- **"But with you there is forgiveness" (v4).** This is the turning point of the psalm. The word is *selichah*, a rare noun used only three times in the Old Testament (also Neh 9:17; Dan 9:9). The related verb *salach*, "to forgive," always has God as its subject in the Old Testament. People can pardon each other in some ways, but this kind of forgiveness, the removal of guilt before God, belongs to him alone. The disciples' question in the Gospels comes from this background: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7).
- **"That you may be feared" (v4).** This is surprising. One might expect forgiveness to make people relaxed about sin. The psalm says the opposite: forgiveness produces reverent awe. A God who never forgave would be met with terror or despair, and people would turn away. A God who forgives draws people to himself in worship, humility and love. Grace does not lower God in the eyes of the forgiven. It lifts him higher.
- **Commentators:** JFB puts v4 well: forgiveness is given "not that thou mayest be presumed upon, but feared," and "pardon produces filial fear and love," the fear of a child, not of a slave. He explains "stand" as the opposite of "the guilty sinking down in fear and self-condemnation." Henry calls these verses the psalmist's repentance, and simply rests on the words, "But there is forgiveness with thee." Guzik passes on Maclaren's suggestion that the Hebrew word for forgiveness pictures a cutting away. The lexicon gives *selichah* only as "forgiveness" and does not support that picture, so it is better taken as an illustration than a meaning of the word. Guzik also retells how Luther, troubled by guilt as a young monk, was comforted by an older brother who reminded him of the line in the Creed, "I believe in the forgiveness of sins."

#### My Soul Waits for the Lord (vv5–6)
- **"I wait for the LORD, my soul waits" (v5).** "Wait" (*qavah*) means to look eagerly for, to hope in. It can picture a cord stretched tight. Waiting in the Psalms is not idle. It is active, expectant trust that holds on until God acts (Ps 27:14; Isa 40:31).
- **"And in his word I hope" (v5).** The psalmist's hope rests on what God has said. God had revealed himself to Moses as "merciful and gracious... forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exod 34:6–7). That word is the ground for waiting. Hope is not a guess. It leans on a promise.
- **"My soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen for the morning" (v6).** Watchmen kept guard on city walls through the night (Isa 21:11–12). Priests and Levites also kept watch in the temple through the night (Ps 134:1). The last hours before dawn were the longest and coldest. A watchman did not wonder whether morning would come. He only longed for it to come soon. That is the psalmist's waiting: certain that God's answer will come, and longing for it.
- **"More than watchmen for the morning" (v6).** The line is repeated word for word. The repetition slows the reader down and makes the waiting felt. In the Hebrew, v6 has no verb at all: literally "my soul for the Lord, more than watchmen for the morning." The longing is reduced to its bare essentials.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "I wait for him in sincerity, and not in profession only." JFB describes the waiting as eager and anxious, like a watchman's. Guzik stresses that the watchmen never doubted morning would come, only when, and notes that interpreters disagree whether they were soldiers on the wall or priests in the temple, though the point is the same either way.

#### O Israel, Hope in the LORD (vv7–8)
- **"O Israel, hope in the LORD!" (v7).** The psalmist turns from himself to the whole people. What he has found to be true for one sinner, he now offers to all. Psalm 131:3 ends with the same call. A forgiven person naturally wants others to know where forgiveness is found.
- **"For with the LORD there is steadfast love" (v7).** "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) is God's loyal, covenant love, the love that keeps its promises even when his people fail. "With the LORD" echoes v4: "with you there is forgiveness." Forgiveness and steadfast love are found in the same place, with him.
- **"And with him is plentiful redemption" (v7).** "Redemption" (*pedut*) comes from the verb *padah*, to ransom, to buy back at a price. It was used for redeeming the firstborn (Exod 13:13) and for God's rescue of Israel from Egypt (Deut 7:8). "Plentiful" (*harbeh*) means abundant, more than enough. There is no shortage of redemption for anyone who comes.
- **"And he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities" (v8).** The psalm began with one person in the depths of iniquity (v3). It ends with God redeeming all Israel "from all" its iniquities. Most often in the Old Testament, God redeems his people from enemies or slavery. Here he redeems them from sin itself. "He himself" is emphatic in the Hebrew. God will do it.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls mercy God's "darling attribute," saying it is "not only inherent in his nature, but it is his delight." JFB reads the call to Israel as meaning that "all are invited to seek and share divine forgiveness," and notes that "iniquities" can include the punishment that sin brings. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line about hoping in God himself rather than only in his gifts, and Kidner's observation that the psalm moves from the closed-in darkness of the depths to an outward, open hope for the whole people.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 130 asks the question every person must face: "If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?" And it gives the answer: "with you there is forgiveness." The New Testament shows where that forgiveness is found and what it cost. God does not forgive by pretending sin does not matter. He forgives because Jesus went down into the depths for sinners, bearing their iniquities in his own body (Isa 53:5–6; 1 Pet 2:24). "In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace" (Eph 1:7). That is the "plentiful redemption" the psalm promised. The prophecy of v8 is echoed almost word for word when the angel tells Joseph, "you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins" (Matt 1:21), and Paul says Christ "gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness" (Titus 2:14). Those who are forgiven in him do not take grace lightly. They fear the Lord, and they wait with the watchmen for the morning of his return.
