*Setting:* About 850–845 BC, during the reign of Jehoram (Joram) son of Ahab over Israel. Chapter 3 showed Elisha speaking to kings on a battlefield. Chapter 4 turns to ordinary people: a debt-ridden widow, a well-off farmer's wife, a group of hungry prophets in a famine. Elijah's ministry was mostly public confrontation. Elisha's, much of the time, is pastoral care among the communities of faithful prophets and the common people of the land. The chapter is a string of four miracles, and each one shows the LORD providing life where there was none: oil for a widow, a son for a childless woman and then life for that son, safe food from poison, and bread for a hundred from a few loaves. Several of these echo Elijah's miracles at Zarephath (1 Kgs 17), and several point forward to Jesus.

#### Elisha and the Widow's Oil (vv1–7)
- **"The wife of one of the sons of the prophets cried to Elisha" (v1).** The "sons of the prophets" were communities of prophets, and this shows that at least some of them were married men with families. Jewish tradition later identified her husband as Obadiah, who hid the hundred prophets (1 Kgs 18:4) and supposedly went into debt to feed them. That is only a legend, but it is a fitting one.
- **"Your servant feared the LORD, but the creditor has come to take my two children to be his slaves" (v1).** The law allowed a debtor or his children to work off a debt as servants, but limited it: such a servant was to be released in the seventh year or at the Jubilee, and treated as a hired worker, not a slave (Exod 21:2; Lev 25:39–41). Even so, losing both sons would leave a widow with no one to support her. The prophets condemned creditors who sold the poor "for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6). The widow's plea is to God's man, and through him to God, who calls himself "father of the fatherless and protector of widows" (Ps 68:5).
- **"What have you in the house?" (v2).** Elisha starts with what she has. **"Nothing in the house except a jar of oil."** The word for "jar" here is rare and may mean a small flask, the kind used for oil to anoint the body. It was barely anything.
- **"Borrow vessels from all your neighbors, empty vessels and not too few" (v3).** The size of the miracle depends on how many empty jars she collects. Her faith is measured in vessels. She has to go door to door, telling the neighbors, in effect, that she expects God to fill them.
- **"Shut the door behind yourself and your sons" (v4).** This miracle is private. It is not a public display but God's care for one family in their home. Elisha is not even there. The family must trust God's word on their own. Elisha later does the same with the Shunammite's son (v33), and Jesus sent the mourners out before raising Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:40).
- **"There is not another.' Then the oil stopped flowing" (v6).** The oil flows as long as there are empty vessels. It stops when there is no more room, not when God runs out. Henry: "We are never straitened in God, or in the riches of his grace; all our straitness is in ourselves."
- **"Go, sell the oil and pay your debts, and you and your sons can live on the rest" (v7).** Olive oil was a major trade good, so a house full of it was real wealth. She pays the debt, saves her sons, and has enough to live on. The echo of Elijah's widow at Zarephath is clear (1 Kgs 17:14–16): a widow, a small jar of oil, and the LORD's word making it last.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the miracle's size was set by the number of vessels and passes on Spurgeon's point that God fills empty vessels, so emptiness is no barrier. He also notes Elisha made her do the work herself. JFB explains that a creditor "was entitled to claim the person and children of the insolvent debtor," as bondmen until the Jubilee. Henry's line about our "straitness" being in ourselves, not in God, makes the same point as Guzik's about the vessels.

#### Elisha and the Shunammite Woman (vv8–17)
- **"Shunem, where a wealthy woman lived" (v8).** Shunem was a town in the territory of Issachar, on the slope of the hill of Moreh at the eastern end of the Jezreel Valley, a few miles north of Jezreel. JFB identifies it with the modern village of Sulam. It lay on the route between Carmel and the Jordan, so Elisha passed often. Abishag, David's nurse, came from Shunem (1 Kgs 1:3). "Wealthy" is literally "great," meaning prominent and well off.
- **"A holy man of God who is continually passing our way" (v9).** She notices Elisha's character before any miracle. She persuades her husband to build **"a small room on the roof with walls"** (v10), a proper room, not just a tent on the flat roof, furnished with **"a bed, a table, a chair, and a lamp."** It was a simple, generous gift. The room probably had its own outside stair, so Elisha could come and go without disturbing the household.
- **"Would you have a word spoken on your behalf to the king or to the commander of the army?" (v13).** After the Moab campaign (ch 3), Elisha has standing at court. He offers her a favor from the powerful: a tax break, protection, a legal case. Elisha speaks to her through Gehazi, his servant, here first named. Prophets, like kings, often communicated through attendants.
- **"I dwell among my own people" (v13).** Her answer is quiet contentment. She is secure among her own clan, needs no royal favors and wants none. Henry: "It is a happiness to dwell among our own people, that love and respect us, and to whom we are able to do good." She gave without expecting anything back (Luke 14:12–14).
- **"Well, she has no son, and her husband is old" (v14).** Gehazi sees what she would not say. In Israel, a woman without a son had no future security, and the family line and land would pass out of the household. The barrenness was likely also a deep private grief.
- **"At this season, about this time next year, you shall embrace a son" (v16).** These are almost exactly the words the LORD spoke to Abraham about Sarah: "I will surely return to you about this time next year, and Sarah your wife shall have a son" (Gen 18:10, 14). The Shunammite stands in the line of barren women to whom God gave a son: Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, and later Elizabeth (Luke 1:13).
- **"No, my lord, O man of God; do not lie to your servant" (v16).** It is not unbelief so much as a guarded heart. She had given up hope, and to hope again and be disappointed would be worse. Compare Sarah's laughter (Gen 18:12). **"But the woman conceived"** (v17). God keeps the promise she could not bring herself to believe.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the woman took the initiative in hospitality and that her doubt in v16 came from a promise that seemed too good to be true. JFB says the room was "a chamber in the wall," a guest room often attached to houses. The Tyndale notes compare the promise to Abraham and Sarah's and Zechariah and Elizabeth's.

#### Elisha Raises the Shunammite's Son (vv18–37)
- **"He went out one day to his father among the reapers" (v18).** Harvest was the hottest part of the year. **"Oh, my head, my head!"** (v19). It may have been sunstroke, as Adam Clarke suggested, or something like a burst blood vessel in the brain. The father, busy with the harvest, sends him home with a servant.
- **"The child sat on her lap till noon, and then he died" (v20).** The narrator does not soften it. The gift God gave is taken, and the mother holds him as he dies.
- **"She went up and laid him on the bed of the man of God and shut the door" (v21).** She does not prepare him for burial or call mourners. She lays him on Elisha's bed, in the room she built, and shuts the door. She is not giving up.
- **"Why will you go to him today? It is neither new moon nor Sabbath" (v23).** The first day of each month and the Sabbath were holy days, when people might visit a prophet for teaching or worship. So this line shows faithful Israelites in the north still kept these days, gathering around prophets even without the temple. Her husband does not know the boy is dead. **"All is well."** The Hebrew is one word, *shalom*. It may mean "it's all right," or simply "never mind." She will not stop to explain.
- **"Do not slacken the pace for me" (v24).** Carmel was about 15 to 20 miles from Shunem, across the Jezreel Valley. She rides hard.
- **"Is all well with you? Is all well with your husband? Is all well with the child?" And she answered, "All is well" (v26).** *Shalom* four times. Elisha sees her coming at an unusual time and is worried. Her answer to Gehazi is the same single word. Henry: "All well, and yet the child dead in the house!" JFB says her answer "was purposely brief and vague to Gehazi." She will tell her grief only to the man of God. Her "it is well" has become a famous expression of faith, and it inspired the hymn "It Is Well with My Soul." In the story itself, though, it is probably less a declaration of calm trust than a refusal to be stopped.
- **"She caught hold of his feet" (v27).** Gehazi tries to push her off. It was improper for a woman to grasp a man of God. Elisha stops him: **"Leave her alone, for she is in bitter distress."** "Bitter" (*marah*) is the word Naomi used: "Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (Ruth 1:20). **"The LORD has hidden it from me."** Even a great prophet knows only what God reveals.
- **"Did I ask my lord for a son? Did I not say, 'Do not deceive me?'" (v28).** Her words are raw. She did not ask for this. She warned him not to raise her hopes. It is the kind of honest complaint the Psalms are full of.
- **"Take my staff in your hand and go... lay my staff on the face of the child" (v29).** Elisha sends Gehazi ahead with his staff, perhaps the prophet's rod of office (compare Moses' staff, Exod 4:17). "Do not greet him": greetings in the East were long and elaborate, and there is no time. Jesus gave the same instruction to the seventy-two (Luke 10:4).
- **"As the LORD lives and as you yourself live, I will not leave you" (v30).** These are word for word the oath Elisha swore three times to Elijah (2:2, 4, 6). She clings to Elisha as he clung to Elijah. She will not settle for the servant and the staff. So Elisha goes.
- **"There was no sound or sign of life" (v31).** The staff does nothing. There is no magic in a prophet's staff. JFB: the failure taught that help comes through prayer and faith, not the object. Some suggest the problem was Gehazi, whose character later fails (5:20–27), but the text does not say.
- **"He went in and shut the door behind the two of them and prayed to the LORD" (v33).** As with the widow (v4), behind closed doors. Elisha's first act is prayer.
- **"He went up and lay on the child, putting his mouth on his mouth, his eyes on his eyes, and his hands on his hands" (v34).** Elijah did something similar for the widow's son at Zarephath, stretching himself over the boy three times (1 Kgs 17:21). Elisha's action is more detailed, as if he were giving the child his own life, body to body. The warmth returns gradually. He gets up, walks back and forth, perhaps praying, and stretches over him again (v35).
- **"The child sneezed seven times, and the child opened his eyes" (v35).** A homely, striking detail, breath returning to the body. Seven is the number of completeness. The boy is fully alive.
- **"Pick up your son" (v36).** She falls at his feet a second time (v37), now in thanks instead of anguish. **She picked up her son and went out.** She says nothing. The text needs no more. Hebrews 11:35 may have her and the widow of Zarephath in mind: "Women received back their dead by resurrection."
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads laying the boy on the prophet's bed as faith expecting resurrection, not burial, and passes on Maclaren's point that a grieving heart is silenced by careless questions, which explains her "it is well" to Gehazi. Guzik contrasts Elijah and Elisha, who prayed and struggled for the dead, with Jesus, who simply commanded them to rise (John 11:43). JFB thinks Elisha's staff "was probably an official rod." Henry: "Elisha cried unto God in faith; and the beloved son was restored alive to his mother." The Tyndale notes stress that the staff had no magic power: only God gives life.

#### Elisha Purifies the Deadly Stew (vv38–44)
- **"Elisha came again to Gilgal when there was a famine in the land" (v38).** This is probably the Gilgal of 2:1, a center of a prophetic community. The famine may be the seven-year famine of 8:1. **"As the sons of the prophets were sitting before him"**: the posture of disciples sitting at a teacher's feet (compare Ezek 8:1; Luke 10:39). Elisha has become their leader.
- **"Set on the large pot, and boil stew" (v38).** A communal meal, in hard times, with whatever could be found.
- **"Found a wild vine and gathered from it his lap full of wild gourds" (v39).** These were most likely colocynth, a vine of the desert regions with gourds that look like small melons or cucumbers. They are violently bitter and cause severe stomach cramps and diarrhea, and in large amounts can be deadly. The man did not know what they were. In a famine, people eat what they can find.
- **"O man of God, there is death in the pot!" (v40).** The bitter taste warned them. In a famine, they could not afford to throw out the whole pot.
- **"Then bring flour" (v41).** Flour does not neutralize colocynth. As with the salt at Jericho (2:21), the remedy is a sign, and the power is God's. **"There was no harm in the pot."** Henry: "Elisha made hurtful food to become safe and wholesome."
- **"A man came from Baal-shalishah, bringing the man of God bread of the firstfruits" (v42).** Baal-shalishah was a village in the hill country of Ephraim, probably not far from Gilgal. The firstfruits of the harvest belonged to the LORD and were to be given to the priests (Lev 23:20; Num 18:13; Deut 18:4). In the north, the priests at the calf shrines were not true priests of the LORD, so this faithful man brings his offering to the LORD's prophet instead. "Twenty loaves of barley" were small flat cakes. Barley was the grain of the poor, the earliest crop, harvested in spring.
- **"How can I set this before a hundred men?" (v43).** The servant's question is reasonable arithmetic. Elisha repeats the order, adding the word of the LORD: **"They shall eat and have some left."**
- **"They ate and had some left, according to the word of the LORD" (v44).** The pattern is exactly that of Jesus' feeding miracles: a small amount of bread, a doubting helper asking how it could feed so many, the command to give it out, everyone eating, and leftovers. John even notes that the bread Jesus multiplied was barley loaves (John 6:9), and Philip and Andrew ask almost the same question as Elisha's servant (John 6:7–9). But the scale shows the difference: Elisha fed a hundred with twenty loaves. Jesus fed five thousand men with five.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both identify the gourds as likely colocynth. JFB calls it "bitter to the taste, causing colic." Guzik notes that the flour had no power in itself, and passes on Spurgeon's application that poison mixed into teaching needs the gospel added, not just a spoon to fish it out. On the loaves, Guzik passes on Clarke's description of Elisha as a type of Christ. JFB makes a careful distinction: "This was not a miracle of Elisha, but only a prediction of one by the word of the Lord," since Elisha only announced what God would do. Henry: "Elisha also made a little food go a great way." The Tyndale notes stress that the firstfruits, usually given to God and the priests, were here shared with the hungry.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every scene in this chapter has its fuller answer in Jesus. He cared for widows, and raised a widow's only son at Nain, near Shunem, on the other side of the hill of Moreh (Luke 7:11–15). He took a dead girl by the hand, behind closed doors, and said "Little girl, arise" (Mark 5:41). He fed thousands with barley loaves and had baskets left over (John 6:9–13), then said, "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35). Elisha lay on a dead child, his life against the child's death. Jesus went further: he took death into himself on the cross, so that all who are joined to him will rise. And the mother's word, *shalom*, "it is well," which she said with a dead son in the house, can be said truly because of him. In Christ, even death is not the end of the story.
