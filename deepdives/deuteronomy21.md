*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapters 19 and 20 dealt with taking life: murder, manslaughter and war. Chapter 21 begins where they left off, with a killing no one can solve, and then turns to the household: a captive woman taken as a wife, a firstborn son whose mother is unloved, and a son who will not be ruled at all. It closes with the body of an executed criminal hung on a tree. That last law looks small, but the New Testament takes it up as one of the keys to understanding the cross. Running through the whole chapter are two concerns: bloodguilt must not be left on the land, and the weak in a household must not be left at the mercy of the strong.

#### Atonement for Unsolved Murders (vv1–9)
- **"Someone is found slain, lying in the open country, and it is not known who killed him" (v1).** "Slain" (*chalal*) means pierced or struck, clearly killed by violence. The killing left guilt on the land (Num 35:33), and no one could be punished for it. Israel could not simply shrug.
- **"They shall measure the distance to the surrounding cities" (v2).** The nearest town took responsibility. Its people were most likely to know something, and they had the duty to keep their roads safe. The Tyndale notes add a practical point: this procedure kept a grieving family from holding a whole region guilty.
- **"A heifer that has never been worked… a valley with running water, which is neither plowed nor sown" (vv3–4).** Everything is untouched: an animal that has never worn a yoke, ground that has never been farmed, a stream that is *'ethan*, "ever-flowing," not a seasonal wadi. Most interpreters think the untouched place carried the guilt away from the town's fields. **"Break the heifer's neck"** is not the language of sacrifice. No blood is put on an altar, and the place is not a sanctuary. It looks more like an execution acted out in the murderer's place. The same verb is used for breaking the neck of a firstborn donkey that was not redeemed (Exod 13:13).
- **"Then the priests, the sons of Levi, shall come forward" (v5).** The priests witness the rite and give it God's authority, because "by their word every dispute and every assault shall be settled" (compare 17:9).
- **"All the elders… shall wash their hands over the heifer" (v6).** Washing hands was a public sign of innocence (Ps 26:6; 73:13). **"Our hands did not shed this blood, nor did our eyes see it shed"** (v7). The elders swear they did not do it and did not see it. Matthew records Pilate washing his hands before the crowd and saying, "I am innocent of this man's blood" (Matt 27:24). Pilate used the gesture to escape a guilt that was really his.
- **"Accept atonement, O LORD, for your people Israel, whom you have redeemed" (v8).** "Accept atonement" is the verb *kapper*, to cover or wipe away guilt. The elders appeal to God's own act of redemption: he bought this people out of Egypt, so let him not leave blood on them now. Even when human justice failed, Israel could ask God to cover what could not be set right.
- **"So you shall purge the guilt of innocent blood from your midst" (v9).** The same refrain as 19:13. Life was too sacred for a death to go unanswered.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The dread of murder should be deeply impressed upon every heart," and the rite taught the whole nation to care about a stranger's blood. Guzik warns that the ritual would be empty if the elders had not first done all they could to find the killer, and he records two readings from modern scholars: the heifer as a symbolic execution in the murderer's place, or atonement in the sense of satisfying justice rather than a sacrifice. JFB says the rite was meant to stir the magistrates to search for the murderer, and shows "the horror which murder inspired." JFB has no note on vv7–9.

#### Marrying Female Captives (vv10–14)
- **"You see among the captives a beautiful woman, and you desire to take her to be your wife" (vv10–11).** This assumes a war outside Canaan (20:14), since marriage with Canaanites was forbidden (7:3). In the ancient world a captive woman was simply her captor's property. This law does not command or praise what the soldier does. It sets limits around something that would happen anyway, much as Jesus said Moses allowed divorce "because of your hardness of heart" (Matt 19:8).
- **"She shall shave her head and pare her nails… take off the clothes in which she was captured" (vv12–13).** These are signs of mourning and of a clean break with her old life. The Tyndale notes read them as the woman leaving one family and joining another. The clothes "in which she was captured" may have been the finery women of a defeated city sometimes put on, hoping to attract a captor's protection.
- **"Lament her father and her mother a full month" (v13).** The man had to wait a month before taking her as his wife. Her parents were probably dead, killed in the war, or as good as dead to her. The month gave her time to grieve. It also gave his desire time to cool, so that the marriage would not be an act of lust in the heat of battle. **"She shall be your wife."** Not a concubine, not a slave, but a wife with a wife's rights (Exod 21:10).
- **"If you no longer delight in her, you shall let her go where she wants" (v14).** If the marriage failed, she went free. **"You shall not sell her for money, nor shall you treat her as a slave, since you have humiliated her."** "Humiliated" (*'innah*) is the word used for forcing a woman (22:24, 29; Gen 34:2). The law admits that she had been wronged and would not let him wrong her twice by selling her. In a world where captive women had no rights at all, this law gave her some.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the law allows the marriage but does not approve of it, and that marriage must still be honored as a binding bond. Guzik reads the month of waiting as protecting the woman and testing the man's commitment. JFB stresses that by war custom the captor had "sole and unchallengeable control of right to her person," which this law restrained.

#### Inheritance Rights of the Firstborn (vv15–17)
- **"If a man has two wives, the one loved and the other unloved" (v15).** "Unloved" is literally "hated." In Hebrew this often means "loved less," as when Leah was "hated" because Jacob loved Rachel more (Gen 29:30–31). The case almost repeats Jacob's household, where the favored wife's son Joseph received the coat and, in the end, the double portion through his two sons (Gen 48:5; 1 Chr 5:1–2). JFB argues that the Hebrew can describe a man who married twice in succession after the first wife died, though most read it as two wives at once. The law does not approve polygamy. It shows what trouble it brings and limits the damage.
- **"He may not treat the son of the loved as the firstborn" (v16).** A father's feelings for the mother could not decide the son's rights. The firstborn was the firstborn.
- **"By giving him a double portion of all that he has" (v17).** Literally "a mouth of two," two shares. With three sons, the estate was split into four parts and the eldest took two. The firstborn also took charge of the family after his father. Elisha used the same phrase when he asked for "a double portion" of Elijah's spirit (2 Kgs 2:9), asking to be treated as Elijah's firstborn heir. **"He is the firstfruits of his strength"** is what Jacob said of Reuben (Gen 49:3).
- **Did God ever pass over the firstborn?** Often: Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph over Reuben, Ephraim over Manasseh, David over his older brothers, Solomon over Adonijah. But those were God's choices, usually with a reason given, such as Reuben's sin (Gen 49:4). This law stops a *father* from playing favorites.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "This law restrains men from disinheriting their eldest sons without just cause." Guzik notes that Solomon's succession did not follow this rule, and draws on Trapp to connect the double portion with Elisha's request. The Tyndale notes observe that the double share is first spelled out here, though earlier stories assume it (Gen 25:31–34).

#### A Rebellious Son (vv18–21)
- **"A stubborn and rebellious son" (v18).** *Sorer u-moreh* describes settled defiance, not a child's naughtiness. The same pair of words describes Israel itself, "a stubborn and rebellious generation" (Ps 78:8; Jer 5:23). **"Though they discipline him, will not listen."** The parents have tried, and failed.
- **"His father and his mother shall take hold of him and bring him out to the elders" (v19).** Both parents had to agree. This protected the son from one angry parent. And neither parent could act alone. In Rome the father legally held the power of life and death over his children. In Israel, only the town's court could judge the case. The law limited parental power as much as it supported it.
- **"He is a glutton and a drunkard" (v20).** The Tyndale notes take this as a stock phrase for a wasteful, self-indulgent life (Prov 23:20–21). This is a grown son, old enough to drink himself into ruin and drag his family down with him. In a society without police or prisons, where the family was the basic unit of the covenant community, such a son threatened the whole town. The fifth commandment carried the promise of long life in the land (5:16), and this son was trampling it.
- **"All the men of the city shall stone him to death" (v21).** This sounds terrible, and it is meant to. The point is deterrence: **"all Israel shall hear, and fear."** Scripture never records the law being carried out. Later rabbis surrounded it with so many conditions that the Talmud says such a case never happened and never will, and that the law was written only to be studied. Many interpreters agree that it worked mainly as a warning that backed up parents' authority.
- **The son and the Son.** When Jesus' enemies wanted to discredit him, they called him "a glutton and a drunkard" (Matt 11:19; Luke 7:34). Many scholars hear an echo of this verse: they were branding him the rebellious son who deserved death. Jesus' own story of a rebellious son ends differently. The father runs to meet him (Luke 15:20).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "No child was to fare the worse for weakness of capacity, slowness, or dulness, but for wilfulness and obstinacy," and "Nothing draws men into all manner of wickedness… more certainly and fatally, than drunkenness." JFB says the consent of both parents was required to prevent abuse, and that parents are "God's representatives" to their children. Guzik contrasts the law with Greek and Roman fathers' absolute power and records the Jewish tradition that it was never enforced.

#### A Man Hanged on a Tree Is Cursed (vv22–23)
- **"He is put to death, and you hang him on a tree" (v22).** This was not a method of execution. The man was already dead, and his body was hung up as a public warning. Joshua did this with the king of Ai and the five Amorite kings, and took the bodies down at sunset (Josh 8:29; 10:26–27). "Tree" (*'ets*) can mean any wooden post.
- **"His body shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day" (v23).** Even the worst criminal's body was not left for birds and animals. Rizpah guarding her sons' bodies for months shows how terrible that disgrace was felt to be (2 Sam 21:10).
- **"For a hanged man is cursed by God" (v23).** The display did not make the man cursed. It showed that he was already under God's curse for a crime that deserved death. Some take the Hebrew differently, as "an insult to God," because God's image was being dishonored. The ESV and most translations read "cursed by God."
- **"You shall not defile your land" (v23).** A body left hanging defiled the land God was giving.
- **How this applied to Jesus.** By the first century, Jews read this verse about crucifixion. The Temple Scroll from Qumran applies it to men "hanged alive on a tree," and that is why the Jewish leaders asked Pilate to have the bodies taken down before the Sabbath (John 19:31). Peter twice speaks of Jesus as the one they killed "by hanging him on a tree" (Acts 5:30; 10:39). For many Jews this was the great obstacle: how could the Messiah die under God's curse? Paul answers that he did, on purpose, for us: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us—for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'" (Gal 3:13). Paul quotes the verse in words close to the Greek Old Testament, which says "everyone."
- **Commentators:** All three apply v23 to Christ. Henry: "Those who see a man thus hanging between heaven and earth, will conclude him abandoned of both, and unworthy of either," and he sees Christ bearing that very curse. JFB says plainly that "gibbeting is meant," not execution by hanging, and that burial the same day spared the land defilement. Guzik connects the verse to Galatians 3:13–14: Christ took the curse so that the blessing of Abraham might come to us.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is full of people who have no one to speak for them: a murdered stranger whose killer is unknown, a captive woman, a son with an unloved mother. God's law reaches each of them. Then it ends at a tree, with a man under God's curse. Jesus died on that tree, accused of being a glutton and a drunkard, the rebellious son, and was buried before nightfall as this law required. But he was not cursed for his own crime. "He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree" (1 Pet 2:24). The elders over the heifer could only plead, "Accept atonement, O LORD." At the cross that prayer was answered. Guilt that no human court could clear was covered by the blood of one who was truly innocent.
