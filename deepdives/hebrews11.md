*Setting:* The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to weary Jewish Christians tempted to drift back from Jesus to the old ways, has shown that Christ is a better priest with a better covenant and sacrifice. Chapter 10 turned that into a call to hold on, quoting Habakkuk: "my righteous one shall live by faith, and if he shrinks back, my soul has no pleasure in him" (10:38; Hab 2:4). Then he added, "we are not of those who shrink back… but of those who have faith" (10:39). Chapter 11 shows what that faith looks like. It is a roll call, from Genesis to the Maccabean martyrs, of people who trusted God's promises without seeing them fulfilled. "By faith" opens about eighteen sentences, and the chapter builds toward 12:1, where these people become "so great a cloud of witnesses."

#### By Faith (vv1–3)
- **"Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for" (v1).** "Assurance" is *hypostasis*, literally "that which stands under," a foundation or support. The writer used the same word in 1:3, where the Son is "the exact imprint of his *nature*," and in 3:14 for the "confidence" believers must hold firm to the end. In everyday papyri it could mean a title deed, guaranteeing property not yet in hand, and the lexicon allows that sense here. Faith treats what God has promised as solid ground to stand on now.
- **"The conviction of things not seen" (v1).** "Conviction" is *elenchos*, a word used for proof, testing or cross-examination, the kind of evidence that settles a case. Faith is not a leap in the dark. It is being persuaded of realities that can't be seen, because the God who promised them can be trusted.
- **"By faith we understand that the universe was created by the word of God" (v3).** "Universe" is literally "the ages" (*aiōnas*), as in 1:2, all of time and everything in it. Nobody watched creation happen, so even Genesis 1 is received by faith.
- **"What is seen was not made out of things that are visible" (v3).** The writer does not explain *how* God created. He says the visible world does not explain itself. It fits the classic teaching that God created out of nothing, and it sets up the chapter: the seen world rests on an unseen word.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls faith "an old grace" with "the best plea to antiquity," answering anyone who thought the Christians had invented something new. On v3 he adds, "Faith is not a force upon the understanding, but a friend and a help to it." JFB takes *hypostasis* as faith "making them present realities to us," while noting Alford's milder "sure confidence," and renders *elenchos* "demonstration." Guzik compares faith to a sense: as eyes perceive the material world, faith perceives the unseen one, and it goes beyond reason without contradicting it.

#### By Faith (vv4–7)
- **Abel (v4).** Genesis 4:3–5 says only that God "had regard for Abel and his offering" but not for Cain's. Hebrews supplies the reason: Abel offered "by faith." Some point out that Abel brought "the firstborn of his flock," his best; others see a hint of atonement by blood. Either way, the difference lay in the heart first (1 John 3:12).
- **"Though he died, he still speaks" (v4).** Abel is the first human to die in the Bible, murdered by his brother, and his blood "cries" from the ground (Gen 4:10). Later Hebrews says Jesus' blood "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (12:24). Abel's blood called for justice. Jesus' blood speaks forgiveness.
- **Enoch (vv5–6).** Genesis 5:24 says "Enoch walked with God, and he was not, for God took him." The Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, which Hebrews usually quotes) translates "walked with God" as "pleased God," and Hebrews picks up that word. "Taken up" is *metatithēmi*, to move from one place to another.
- **"Without faith it is impossible to please him" (v6).** This is the chapter's principle. Coming to God takes two beliefs: "that he exists" and "that he rewards those who seek him." The second is the harder one. Many people believe in a God; faith believes he is good and generous toward those who seek him.
- **Noah (v7).** He was "warned by God concerning events as yet unseen," a flood no one had ever seen. "Reverent fear" is *eulabeomai*, from the word used of Jesus' "reverence" in 5:7. Noah's fear was respect for God's word, not panic. He spent years building something that made no sense to his neighbors.
- **"By this he condemned the world" (v7).** Noah's obedience showed what everyone else could have done and didn't. He "became an heir of the righteousness that comes by faith," the language Paul uses for Abraham (Rom 4:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Abel's sacrifice was better because it confessed sin and relied on a Redeemer to come, and adds, "It is not seniority of birth, but grace, that makes men truly honourable." JFB agrees that Abel's offering was accepted "because offered in faith." Guzik says the issue was not animal versus vegetable but faith versus unbelief, and stresses that v6 says "impossible," not merely "difficult." On Noah, Henry: "Good examples will either convert sinners or condemn them."

#### By Faith (vv8–16)
- **"Abraham obeyed when he was called" (v8).** Abraham lived in Ur, a wealthy city in southern Mesopotamia, around 2000 BC (Gen 11:31; 12:1–4). God called him to leave country and family "to the land that I will show you." He "went out, not knowing where he was going." Faith here means obedience before the map is filled in.
- **"As in a foreign land, living in tents" (v9).** Abraham owned none of the promised land except a burial cave he had to buy (Gen 23). Three generations lived as resident aliens in tents, holding "the same promise" without possessing it.
- **"The city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (v10).** Tents have no foundations. Abraham was looking past Canaan to a permanent city. "Designer" (*technitēs*) is the craftsman who plans, and "builder" (*dēmiourgos*) the one who makes it. The lexicon notes the first word points to the plan, the second to carrying it out. This city is "the heavenly Jerusalem" (12:22) and "the city that is to come" (13:14; Rev 21:2, 14).
- **"By faith Sarah herself received power to conceive" (v11).** Sarah first laughed at the promise of a son in her old age (Gen 18:12–15), yet in the end she "considered him faithful who had promised." There is a real question about this verse. The phrase "power to conceive" is literally "power for the laying down of seed," normally said of the father, not the mother. So many scholars make Abraham the subject ("by faith he, with Sarah, received power to beget"), as the NIV does, and some manuscripts add that Sarah was "barren." Either way, God gave life where there was none.
- **"Him as good as dead" (v12).** Abraham was about a hundred (Rom 4:19). From that one worn-out man came descendants "as many as the stars of heaven" and "the sand by the seashore," the exact images of God's promise (Gen 15:5; 22:17).
- **"These all died in faith, not having received the things promised" (v13).** The writer pauses the roll call to draw the lesson. "Greeted them from afar" uses the ordinary word for greeting a friend (*aspazomai*).
- **"Strangers and exiles on the earth" (v13).** Abraham called himself "a sojourner and foreigner" (Gen 23:4). Peter uses the same pair of words for Christians (1 Pet 2:11). The patriarchs' lifestyle became a confession: this world is not home.
- **"God is not ashamed to be called their God" (v16).** God names himself "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (Exod 3:6). He attaches his name to men who were often weak and wrong, because they longed for what he promised. It echoes 2:11, where Jesus "is not ashamed to call them brothers."
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Abraham stopped in Haran first (Acts 7:4), yet God records the faith, not the delay. Henry: "Faith has a clear and a strong eye, and can see promised mercies at a great distance." JFB thinks "these all" includes Abel, Enoch and Noah too, so the promise in view is ultimately the Redeemer, not only the land. He says that if they had received it, "it would have been sight, not faith." On Sarah, Guzik and Henry both see her laughter of unbelief forgiven and turned into faith.

#### By Faith (vv17–22)
- **"By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac" (v17).** Genesis 22 opens, "God tested Abraham." God never meant Isaac to die, and he stopped Abraham's hand. The Greek uses a tense for "offered up" that treats the deed as done and settled: in Abraham's heart the offering was complete.
- **"His only son" (v17).** The word is *monogenēs*, "one of a kind," the word John uses of Jesus (John 3:16). Abraham had another son, Ishmael, but Isaac was the unique son of the promise. The test was sharp: God had said, "Through Isaac shall your offspring be named" (v18; Gen 21:12), and now seemed to be asking for Isaac's death.
- **"He considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead" (v19).** "Considered" (*logizomai*) means he reasoned it out. God's promise and God's command seemed to clash, so Abraham concluded that God would raise Isaac. Genesis hints at this: "I and the boy will go over there and worship and come again to you" (Gen 22:5). "Moriah" is where the temple later stood (2 Chr 3:1).
- **"Figuratively speaking, he did receive him back" (v19).** Literally "in a parable." Isaac came off that mountain as if back from the dead, a living picture of the resurrection to come. Christians from the earliest centuries have seen the father, the beloved son, the wood carried up the hill and the lamb God provides (Gen 22:8) as pointing to the cross.
- **Jacob "bowing in worship over the head of his staff" (v21).** The writer combines Genesis 48, where the dying Jacob blesses Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh, with Genesis 47:31. There the Hebrew says Jacob bowed "at the head of his bed," but the Septuagint reads "staff." The Hebrew words for bed (*miṭṭāh*) and staff (*maṭṭeh*) are spelled with the same consonants, because vowels were added later. Hebrews follows the Greek. A staff suits a man who had wandered all his life (Gen 32:10).
- **Joseph "gave directions concerning his bones" (v22).** Joseph rose to rule in Egypt, but he made Israel swear to carry his bones out when God came to them (Gen 50:24–25). Moses carried them out at the exodus (Exod 13:19).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that "considered" is a word for calculating, so Abraham's faith was a reasoned conclusion from God's character. JFB says the writer follows the Septuagint on Jacob's staff and takes the staff as an emblem of pilgrimage. Guzik locates Isaac's faith (v20) in the moment he trembled and yielded to God's choice of Jacob (Gen 27:33).

#### By Faith (vv23–31)
- **Moses' parents (v23).** Pharaoh had ordered every Hebrew baby boy thrown into the Nile (Exod 1:22). Amram and Jochebed (Exod 6:20) hid their son three months. "Beautiful" (*asteios*) is the word used of Moses in the Greek of Exodus 2:2 and in Acts 7:20. They saw something special in the child and feared God more than the king.
- **"Refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter" (v24).** Moses was raised in the royal household of Egypt's New Kingdom, at the height of its wealth and power. He traded a palace for a brickyard, choosing mistreatment over "the fleeting pleasures of sin" (v25), as the hearers of Hebrews once accepted "the plundering of your property" (10:34).
- **"The reproach of Christ" (v26).** How could Moses bear Christ's reproach centuries before Jesus? "Christ" means "anointed." Psalm 89:50–51 speaks of the insults heaped on God's "anointed" and in the Greek Old Testament uses this very wording. Most interpreters think Moses shared the disgrace of God's people, who carried the promise of the coming Christ, and so he shared in Christ's own reproach. Christ counts his people's sufferings as his own (Acts 9:4). Moses "was looking to the reward." The verb *apoblepō* means to look away from everything else and fix the eyes on one thing.
- **"He left Egypt, not being afraid of the anger of the king" (v27).** Exodus 2:14–15 says Moses *was* afraid when he fled to Midian after killing an Egyptian. Some think v27 refers to the exodus itself, when Moses faced Pharaoh's rage without fear (Exod 10:28–29). Others think his flight, though prompted by danger, was in the end an act of faith. "He endured as seeing him who is invisible" is the chapter's theme in a phrase: he lived as if the unseen God were standing in plain sight.
- **"He kept the Passover and sprinkled the blood" (v28).** The lamb's blood on the doorposts was an odd instruction, and obeying it was faith. "The Destroyer" echoes Exodus 12:23. It points to "Christ, our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7).
- **The Red Sea (v29).** The people were terrified and complaining a moment earlier (Exod 14:10–12), yet they walked forward between walls of water. The Egyptians "attempted to do the same" and drowned. The same act without faith becomes presumption.
- **Jericho (v30).** No name is attached here. A whole people marched around a walled city for seven days, armed with trumpets and obedience (Josh 6).
- **"Rahab the prostitute" (v31).** The roll call of Israel's founders ends with a Canaanite woman with a past. She told the spies, "the LORD your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath" (Josh 2:11), and hid them at the risk of her life. Her neighbors are called "disobedient" (*apeitheō*): they had heard the same reports and refused to believe. James uses her as an example of faith shown in deeds (James 2:25), and Matthew lists her in Jesus' family tree (Matt 1:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry points out that Moses made his choice in his prime, when he could enjoy the world most, and says that "Christ accounts himself reproached in their reproaches." JFB agrees and adds, "Christ's people's reproach will ere long be their great glory." Guzik suggests Moses likely did not grasp the full meaning, though his suffering lined him up with Christ's. On Rahab, JFB calls hers "faith which worketh by love," the same faith James describes, so Paul and James do not contradict each other. Guzik calls her faith real but imperfect.

#### By Faith (vv32–40)
- **"Time would fail me" (v32).** A preacher's phrase: now he only names names. Gideon, Barak, Samson and Jephthah are judges, and the list is not in time order: Barak came before Gideon, and Jephthah before Samson. These are not spotless heroes. Gideon asked for sign after sign, Barak wouldn't go without Deborah, Samson was ruled by his appetites, and Jephthah made a rash vow. David committed adultery and murder. Faith, not flawlessness, puts them on the list.
- **Faith that triumphed (vv33–35a).** Each phrase has a story behind it. "Stopped the mouths of lions" recalls Daniel (Dan 6:22). "Quenched the power of fire" recalls Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego (Dan 3). "Women received back their dead by resurrection" points to the widow of Zarephath and the Shunammite woman, whose sons were raised by Elijah and Elisha (1 Kings 17:17–24; 2 Kings 4:18–37).
- **"Some were tortured, refusing to accept release" (v35).** Here the list turns. "Tortured" (*tympanizō*) comes from the word for a drum, and the lexicon gives "torture by beating, beat to death." This almost certainly recalls the Maccabean martyrs of the 160s BC. In 2 Maccabees 6–7 the old scribe Eleazar was beaten to death and seven brothers died refusing to eat pork, confessing that God would raise them up. They wanted "a better life," literally "a better resurrection." Women had received sons back to this life; the martyrs looked for endless life. Protestants do not count Maccabees as Scripture, while Catholic and Orthodox Christians do, but the history is real either way.
- **"Sawn in two" (v37).** Scripture never records this. Jewish tradition (the Talmud and a writing called the *Ascension of Isaiah*) says King Manasseh had Isaiah sawn in two inside a hollow tree. "Stoned" fits Zechariah son of Jehoiada, killed in the temple court (2 Chr 24:20–21; Matt 23:35), and tradition says Jeremiah was stoned in Egypt. Some manuscripts add "they were tempted" to v37. It sits oddly among forms of death, and many scholars think it is an early copying slip for a similar-looking Greek word. The ESV puts it in a footnote.
- **"Of whom the world was not worthy" (v38).** A striking reversal. The world treated them as unfit to live among, but the truth was the opposite. They hid "in deserts and mountains, and in dens and caves." Obadiah hid a hundred prophets in caves (1 Kings 18:4). Elijah's "garment of hair" (2 Kings 1:8) shows the "skins of sheep and goats" (v37).
- **"Did not receive what was promised" (v39).** The chapter closes where v2 began, with God's commendation, but with a twist. Not one of these people received the final promise: the new covenant, the true sacrifice and the heavenly city.
- **"God had provided something better for us" (v40).** "Better" is the key word of Hebrews: a better hope, covenant, sacrifice and promise (7:19, 22; 8:6; 9:23). Christ has now come and "by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (10:14). "Made perfect" means brought to the goal God intended. The saints of old wait for the final completion "apart from us," so the whole people of God will reach it together at the resurrection.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Faith takes hold of that hand of God which has hold of the swords of men." On v39: they had "shadows, but had not seen the substance." JFB explains the order of names as importance, not chronology, and thinks Jephthah's place here makes it unlikely he sacrificed his daughter, a disputed reading. JFB ties v35 to the Maccabean brothers but warns that 2 Maccabees "expressly disclaims inspiration," and on v40 says, "They waited for His coming; we enjoy Him as having come." Guzik stresses that every one of the judges had glaring failures, and weak faith still beats unbelief.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every name in this chapter is waiting for someone. Abel's blood cries out, Abraham reasons that God can raise the dead, Moses bears the reproach of the Anointed, and the martyrs die looking for a better resurrection. None of them receives what was promised, because the promise is Christ. He is the true sacrifice Abel's offering pointed to, the beloved Son who was actually offered where Isaac was spared, the Passover Lamb whose blood turns away judgment, and the firstfruits of the resurrection they hoped for. The chapter ends not with a final hero but with "us." The next words tell believers to look "to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith" (12:2), the one in whom all their faith finally comes home.
