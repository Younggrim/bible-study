*Setting:* The famine Joseph foretold is in its second year (45:6), and it is getting worse. Some months ago the ten brothers came home from Egypt with grain, but without Simeon, whom the governor kept as a hostage until they came back with their youngest brother (42:19–20, 24). They also found every man's silver back in his sack and were terrified (42:35). Jacob refused to let Benjamin go: "My son shall not go down with you" (42:38). Now the grain is gone. Nobody in Canaan knows that the harsh governor is Joseph, the brother they sold for twenty shekels more than twenty years ago. This chapter is the second journey, and it moves from fear to a feast.

#### Joseph's Brothers Return to Egypt (vv1–34)
- **"Go again, buy us a little food" (v2).** Jacob speaks as if nothing has changed, as if the governor's condition can be ignored. "A little food" is how a desperate man talks when the family is down to its last sack. Simeon, still in an Egyptian jail, is not even mentioned.
- **"The man solemnly warned us" (v3).** Judah now steps forward, and from here on he speaks for the brothers. The Hebrew doubles the verb for emphasis ("he warned, warned us"). Judah repeats the governor's words twice (vv3, 5): "You shall not see my face unless your brother is with you." There is no way around it. Without Benjamin there is no audience, and without an audience there is no grain.
- **"Why did you treat me so badly…?" (vv6–7).** Jacob blames his sons for saying they had another brother. They answer honestly: the man asked directly about their father and brother, and they could not have guessed where his questions were going. The irony is that for twenty years these same men hid the truth about a brother from their father. Now they are blamed for telling the truth about one. Notice too that the narrator calls him **"Israel"** in this scene (vv6, 8, 11). The name God gave him (32:28) returns as he moves from clinging to Benjamin toward trusting God with him.
- **"Send the boy with me… that we may live and not die" (v8).** Judah turns Jacob's own words back to him (42:2). Holding on to Benjamin will not keep anyone alive. Everyone will starve, "both we and you and also our little ones." Benjamin is called "the boy," but he is no child. He was born before Joseph was sold, and by the time the family moves to Egypt he has ten sons (46:21). To an old father he is still the baby of the family.
- **"I will be a pledge of his safety. From my hand you shall require him" (v9).** The verb is *ʿarab*, to stand surety, to put yourself up as the guarantee for someone else. It is the language of a man who co-signs a debt and becomes liable for it (Prov 6:1; 11:15). Judah offers himself, not his goods, and not his sons. Compare Reuben's offer: "Kill my two sons if I do not bring him back to you" (42:37), a rash offer that Jacob ignored. Judah's offer is costly and personal: "let me bear the blame forever," literally "I will have sinned against you all the days."
- **This is a changed Judah.** He is the brother who said, "What profit is it if we kill our brother… Come, let us sell him" (37:26–27). He is also the man humbled by Tamar, who finally said, "She is more righteous than I" (38:26). Now he puts his own life on the line for Rachel's other son, the father's new favorite. Chapter 44 will test whether he means it.
- **"If we had not delayed, we would now have returned twice" (v10).** A round trip of about 250 miles each way took weeks. Judah's frustration shows: Jacob's hesitation has cost Simeon months in prison and brought the family to the edge.
- **"Carry a present down to the man" (v11).** Jacob does what he did before meeting Esau: send gifts ahead to soften a powerful man (32:13–21). The word for "present," *minḥah*, is the same word used there, and later for grain offerings. The list is striking: **balm, honey, gum, myrrh, pistachio nuts and almonds.** Balm, gum and myrrh are exactly what the Ishmaelite caravan was carrying down to Egypt on the day the brothers sold Joseph to it (37:25). The brothers are walking the same road with the same goods, and this time they are bringing their brother along instead of selling him. Even in famine, Canaan's trees and bees still produce a little, and Jacob sends the best he has.
- **"Take double the money with you" (v12).** They are to bring back the returned silver plus new silver for this purchase. Hebrew uses one word, *kesep*, for both "silver" and "money." Guzik points out the echo: these men once took silver for Joseph (37:28), and now they carry silver back to him. Jacob hopes it was "an oversight," an honest man's assumption, and the right one to act on.
- **"May God Almighty grant you mercy before the man" (v14).** "God Almighty" is *El Shaddai*, the name God used when he promised Abraham and Jacob a nation and kings (17:1; 35:11). Jacob prays to the God whose promises depend on this family surviving. The word "mercy" is *raḥamim*, compassion, a word related to *reḥem*, "womb," the deep, almost parental feeling of one person for another. Hold on to that word for v30.
- **"If I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved" (v14).** Readers hear this two ways. Some hear resignation close to despair, an old man too tired to fight. Others hear real surrender, like Esther's "if I perish, I perish" (Esth 4:16): he has done what he can, and he leaves the outcome to God. Both may be true at once. Either way, Jacob finally lets go, and letting go is the turning point of the whole story.
- **"Stood before Joseph" (v15).** The narrator moves straight from Canaan to the governor's presence, the long journey covered in one verse.
- **"Bring the men into the house… the men are to dine with me at noon" (v16).** Seeing Benjamin alive is all Joseph needs to change his tone. He orders an animal slaughtered, a real feast, since meat was a luxury most families seldom ate. JFB notes the Hebrew phrase is literally "slaughter a slaughter," a full banquet. Noon was the usual time for the main meal.
- **"The men were afraid because they were brought to Joseph's house" (v18).** Instead of an honor, they see a trap. They imagine the governor will "assault us and fall upon us to make us servants and seize our donkeys." That is the fear of men who once seized a brother and made him a slave. Guilt has trained them to expect what they themselves did to Joseph. The Tyndale notes observe that their guilt kept them from seeing that something good might be happening.
- **They explain at the door (vv19–22).** Before going in, they go to the steward and tell him the whole story of the silver, "our money in full weight," and insist, "We do not know who put our money in our sacks." It is careful and honest. These brothers are learning to tell the truth before they are caught.
- **"Peace to you, do not be afraid. Your God and the God of your father has put treasure in your sacks" (v23).** The steward's words are remarkable from an Egyptian official. "Peace to you" is *shalom lakem*. He speaks of their God, perhaps because Joseph told him what to say, perhaps because he has learned from his master who Joseph's God is. "I received your money" is literally true: the account was settled. Joseph paid it himself. What looked like a trap was a gift, and the steward credits God with it. **Then he brought Simeon out to them.** The lost brother is given back before anyone asks.
- **Water, washed feet, fodder for the donkeys (v24).** This is full hospitality, the same welcome Abraham gave his three visitors (18:4) and Laban's household gave Abraham's servant (24:32). Men who expected chains get a bath and a meal.
- **"They… bowed down to him to the ground" (v26).** In 42:6 the ten bowed. Now all eleven do, Benjamin included, with their present in hand. Joseph's second dream had eleven stars bowing to him (37:9), and now there are eleven. In v28 they bow again, a second time in the same scene.
- **"Is your father well…? Is he still alive?" (v27).** "Well" and "welfare" are both *shalom*. The word has a history in this story. The brothers once "could not speak peacefully" (*shalom*) to Joseph (37:4), and Jacob sent Joseph to see "if it is well" (*shalom*) with them (37:14), the errand that ended with Joseph in the pit. Now Joseph asks about his father's *shalom*, and the brothers answer, **"Your servant our father is well"** (v28). Without knowing it, they call Jacob Joseph's servant. Even the "sun" of the dream bows in their words (37:10).
- **"Benjamin, his mother's son… God be gracious to you, my son!" (v29).** The narrator stresses that Benjamin is Rachel's son, Joseph's only full brother. Rachel died giving birth to him (35:16–19), so these two motherless boys shared a loss the other ten did not. "My son" is how an older man of rank would address a younger one, and it keeps the disguise in place, but every word is full. Joseph's blessing answers Jacob's prayer that God would grant mercy before "the man" (v14).
- **"His compassion grew warm for his brother" (v30).** Here is the word again: *raḥamim*. Jacob prayed for *raḥamim* in the man's heart, and the man's heart is already full of it. The verb "grew warm" (*kamar*) means to be stirred or to burn, the same verb used when the true mother's heart "yearned" for her child before Solomon (1 Kgs 3:26). Joseph cannot hold it in. He finds a room and weeps. He weeps repeatedly in this story (42:24; 45:2, 14–15; 46:29; 50:1, 17), and he is a man with more reason than most to weep.
- **"He washed his face… controlling himself" (v31).** The verb means to hold back or restrain, what a man does when the tears are still pushing behind his eyes. Joseph is not ready to reveal himself. One more test remains.
- **Three tables (v32).** Joseph eats alone because of his rank. His Egyptian officials eat at their own table, and the brothers at a third, "because the Egyptians could not eat with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the Egyptians." "Abomination" (*toʿebah*) means something ritually or socially offensive. The Greek historian Herodotus (5th century BC) reports that Egyptians would not kiss a Greek or use a Greek knife, for reasons of ritual purity. Later the narrator adds that "every shepherd is an abomination to the Egyptians" (46:34). JFB links this to the hated Hyksos "shepherd-kings," but most scholars think Egyptian prejudice against foreigners and herders is enough to explain it. The Egyptians' contempt will turn into protection: it keeps Jacob's family separate in Goshen, where they grow into a nation without being absorbed.
- **"The firstborn according to his birthright and the youngest according to his youth" (v33).** Someone seats the eleven from Reuben down to Benjamin in exact birth order. The chance of getting eleven brothers right by luck is about one in forty million. "The men looked at one another in amazement." Who is this man, and how does he know? The question hangs over the meal.
- **"Benjamin's portion was five times as much as any of theirs" (v34).** Sending dishes from the host's table was a mark of honor. JFB notes that a double portion was a high honor among guests, so five times was extraordinary. This is the test inside the feast. The brothers once hated Joseph because their father loved Rachel's son more than them (37:3–4). Now Joseph openly favors Rachel's other son, in front of all of them. Will the old envy flare up? This time it does not.
- **"They drank and were merry with him" (v34).** The verb (*shakar*) can mean to drink deeply, to the full. JFB says it means they "drank freely" and does not imply drunkenness. For the first time in the chapter the fear lifts. The meal they expected to end in slavery ends in celebration, "with him," the brother they still do not recognize.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees Judah's offer as the fruit of a guilty conscience made tender: Judah's "conscience had lately smitten him for what he had done a great while ago against Joseph," and now "he will not only not wrong him, but will do all he can to protect him." Guzik calls the pledge the first truly good thing recorded of Judah. On Joseph's tears Henry writes, "Tears of tenderness and affection are no disparagement at all, even to great and wise men," and on the meal, "their cares and fears were now over, and they ate their bread with joy." Guzik adds that Joseph's kindness before the brothers have confessed anything is the kind of goodness that leads to repentance (Rom 2:4). JFB explains the gifts as standard Eastern courtesy toward a man of power, and notes that Syria grows the world's best pistachios and Palestine was rich in almonds. JFB says nothing on most of the verses from v19 to v30, the heart of the chapter.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah, the brother who once sold a brother, offers himself as the pledge for another and says, "From my hand you shall require him." From his tribe comes Jesus, whom Hebrews calls "the guarantor of a better covenant" (Heb 7:22), the one who stood surety for people who could not keep themselves. And Joseph shows what grace looks like before it is understood: silver returned, a lost brother set free, a feast prepared, compassion burning behind a hidden face, all given to men who still expect punishment. The brothers eat at the table of the one they wronged long before they know who he is. That is how Christ often meets his people: he feeds them first and makes himself known afterward (Luke 24:30–31).
