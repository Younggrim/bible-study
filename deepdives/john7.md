*Setting:* About six months have passed since the Passover of chapter 6, so it is now autumn, probably AD 29, roughly six months before the cross. Jesus has stayed in Galilee because the Jerusalem leaders want to kill him (5:18). Many disciples have left him after the bread of life teaching (6:66). Now comes the Feast of Booths, also called Tabernacles, the most joyful and crowded festival of the Jewish year. Chapters 7 and 8 take place in and around the temple during this feast, and they are full of argument: about where Jesus comes from, whether he is the Messiah, and whether anyone should listen to him. Through it all, John notes again and again that no one could arrest Jesus, because his "hour had not yet come."

#### Jesus at the Feast of Booths (vv1–24)
- **"He would not go about in Judea, because the Jews were seeking to kill him" (v1).** This is not fear but timing. Jesus will go to Jerusalem to die, but on the Father's schedule, not his enemies'.
- **"The Jews' Feast of Booths" (v2).** Booths (*Sukkot*) came in late September or October, five days after the Day of Atonement, and lasted seven days, with a solemn eighth day after it (Lev 23:33–43; Deut 16:13–15). It celebrated the end of the harvest and remembered Israel's forty years living in tents in the wilderness. Families built shelters of branches and lived in them for the week. Josephus called it the holiest and greatest feast of the Jews. Two ceremonies marked the temple celebrations, a daily water-pouring and the lighting of huge lamps, and Jesus will speak to both (7:37–39; 8:12).
- **"His brothers said to him, 'Leave here and go to Judea'" (v3).** These are Jesus' half-brothers (Mark 6:3 names James, Joses, Judas and Simon). Their advice sounds like public-relations strategy: anyone who wants to be known should go to the big city and perform. "Your disciples" may hint at the followers Jesus had lost after chapter 6.
- **"For not even his brothers believed in him" (v5).** This is John's blunt aside. They knew him better than anyone and still did not believe. Their unbelief did not last: after the resurrection, Jesus appeared to James (1 Cor 15:7), the brothers were praying with the disciples (Acts 1:14), and James became a leader of the Jerusalem church and wrote the letter of James.
- **"My time has not yet come, but your time is always here" (v6).** "Time" here is *kairos*, the right or appointed moment. It parallels Jesus' "hour" (2:4; 7:30). His brothers can go anytime, because their lives are not under a divine timetable or threat. Jesus' every step is.
- **"The world cannot hate you, but it hates me" (v7).** "The world" here is humanity organized against God. The brothers belong to it, so it has no quarrel with them. Jesus exposes its evil, so it hates him. He will tell his disciples the same will be true of them (15:18–19).
- **"I am not going up to this feast" (v8).** Then he goes (v10). Was that inconsistent? Some early manuscripts read "not *yet* going up," which smooths it over, and the KJV follows that reading. The ESV follows other early manuscripts that read "not." Even so, there is no contradiction. Jesus refused to go up *as his brothers proposed*: publicly, with the pilgrim caravan, as a show. "Go up" can also carry a deeper sense in John: Jesus' final "going up" to the Father (20:17) is not yet. He went later, "not publicly but in private" (v10), on his Father's timing.
- **"Where is he?" (v11).** The leaders are watching for him. The crowds argue in low voices: "He is a good man" versus "he is leading the people astray" (v12). The second charge was serious. The Law condemned anyone who led Israel away from God (Deut 13:1–11), and later Jewish writings remember Jesus as one who "led Israel astray." "For fear of the Jews" (v13), meaning the authorities, no one spoke openly. The fear of the leaders silences even people who are inclined to think well of Jesus (9:22; 12:42).
- **"About the middle of the feast Jesus went up into the temple and began teaching" (v14).** He arrives quietly and then teaches openly in the temple courts, probably in Solomon's colonnade or the Court of the Women, where crowds gathered.
- **"How is it that this man has learning, when he has never studied?" (v15).** Literally "knows letters," which meant being trained in the Scriptures under a recognized rabbi. Jesus had no formal rabbinic training (compare Acts 4:13 on Peter and John). The leaders' amazement is a backhanded admission that his teaching was brilliant.
- **"My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me" (v16).** Jesus answers their question about his teacher. His teacher is God. He is not self-taught or self-appointed.
- **"If anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know" (v17).** This is a key principle. Understanding Jesus' teaching is not mainly a matter of intelligence or education. It depends on the will. Someone who truly wants to do God's will will recognize Jesus' words as God's. The problem with the leaders was not their minds but their hearts (5:42–44).
- **"The one who seeks the glory of him who sent him is true" (v18).** A false teacher promotes himself. Jesus seeks only the Father's glory, and that is evidence that he is true.
- **"None of you keeps the law. Why do you seek to kill me?" (v19).** Jesus turns from defense to accusation. They prided themselves on Moses' Law, yet they were plotting murder, which the Law forbids.
- **"You have a demon! Who is seeking to kill you?" (v20).** The crowd, many of them pilgrims, don't know about the leaders' plot. They think Jesus is paranoid or mad. "Having a demon" was a way of calling someone crazy (10:20).
- **"I did one work, and you all marvel at it" (v21).** This is the healing at the pool of Bethesda on the Sabbath, a year or more earlier (5:1–18). It was still the talk of Jerusalem.
- **The circumcision argument (vv22–23).** The Law required a boy to be circumcised on the eighth day after birth (Lev 12:3). If that day fell on the Sabbath, the rabbis agreed the circumcision should still be done, even though it was work. Jesus adds that circumcision came "from the fathers," from Abraham (Gen 17:10–12), before Moses. He then argues from the lesser to the greater, a standard rabbinic method. If it is right to perform a rite affecting one part of a man's body on the Sabbath, how can it be wrong to make "a man's whole body well"? The rabbis themselves reasoned similarly: a later saying held that if circumcision overrides the Sabbath for one member, saving a life should override it for the whole body.
- **"Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment" (v24).** On the surface, Jesus broke Sabbath tradition. Look deeper, and he did what the Sabbath was for: bringing wholeness and rest.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Jesus' brothers became believers only after the resurrection (Acts 1:14), and he rejects the charge that Jesus lied in v8. Jesus refused to go up publicly with the caravan, then went up privately. JFB reads the brothers' words as "strong prejudice and suspicion" more than outright unbelief. On v15, Guzik and JFB both explain "letters" as training in the Scriptures, and JFB says the leaders' remark shows Jesus "had not studied under any human teacher." On v17 JFB writes that "singleness of desire to please God is the grand inlet to light," and Guzik makes the same point: understanding follows a willingness to obey. On vv21–24 JFB notes that circumcision on the Sabbath "was counted no infringement of the Law."

#### Can This Be the Christ? (vv25–31)
- **"Is not this the man whom they seek to kill?" (v25).** Residents of Jerusalem, unlike the pilgrims, know about the plot. They are amazed that Jesus is teaching openly and the leaders do nothing.
- **"Can it be that the authorities really know that this is the Christ?" (v26).** It is a sarcastic or bewildered question. Why else would the leaders let him speak?
- **"When the Christ appears, no one will know where he comes from" (v27).** Some Jews believed the Messiah would be hidden until he suddenly appeared (compare Mal 3:1, "the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple"). In Justin Martyr's second-century *Dialogue with Trypho*, the Jewish speaker says the Messiah would be unknown until Elijah came to anoint him. Jesus, by contrast, was the carpenter from Nazareth, whose family they knew. Yet others in the same chapter expect the Messiah to come from Bethlehem (v42). Popular expectations were mixed.
- **"You know me, and you know where I come from" (v28).** Jesus "proclaimed" (literally "cried out") this. It is partly ironic. They know his earthly hometown, but not his real origin. He comes from the Father, whom they do not know.
- **"I know him, for I come from him" (v29).** Jesus' knowledge of God is firsthand. That claim is what moves them to try to arrest him (v30).
- **"No one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come" (v30).** John says this repeatedly (7:44; 8:20, 59; 10:39). The reason Jesus is not seized is not luck or caution. God is in control of the timing.
- **"When the Christ appears, will he do more signs than this man has done?" (v31).** Many believed, though in John signs-based faith is often shallow (2:23–25). Still, it's a fair question. The Old Testament expected signs in the messianic age (Isa 35:5–6).
- **Commentators:** JFB says v27 "seems to refer to some current opinion that Messiah's origin would be mysterious." Guzik also connects that expectation to the idea of a Messiah who appears suddenly (Mal 3:1), and suggests Jesus' reply in v28 carried a note of irony. All three stress that Jesus' safety came from the Father's timing, not from his enemies' restraint.

#### Officers Sent to Arrest Jesus (vv32–36)
- **"The chief priests and Pharisees sent officers to arrest him" (v32).** The chief priests were mostly Sadducees, the priestly aristocracy who controlled the temple. Pharisees and Sadducees disagreed on much, but they worked together against Jesus. The "officers" were the temple police, Levites who kept order in the temple courts.
- **"I will be with you a little longer, and then I am going to him who sent me" (v33).** Jesus speaks of his death, resurrection and return to the Father. The "little longer" was about six months.
- **"You will seek me and you will not find me" (v34).** This echoes the warnings of Wisdom: "they will seek me diligently but will not find me" (Prov 1:28). There comes a time when opportunity passes. Jesus says something similar to his disciples, but adds the promise that they will follow later (13:33, 36).
- **"Does he intend to go to the Dispersion among the Greeks?" (v35).** The "Dispersion" (*diaspora*) meant Jews living outside the land of Israel, scattered across the Greek-speaking world. Some Jews lived in Alexandria, Antioch, Ephesus and Rome. "Teach the Greeks" may mean Gentiles. The leaders mean it as a sneer: is he going off to teach foreigners because no one here will listen? The irony is that this is exactly what happened through Jesus' followers. John himself may have written this Gospel in Ephesus, among the Greeks.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws out the irony of v35: within a generation the message of Jesus had indeed gone out to the Greeks. JFB says the crowd "cannot comprehend Him, but seem awed by the solemn grandeur of His warning."

#### Rivers of Living Water (vv37–39)
- **"On the last day of the feast, the great day" (v37).** This may be the seventh day, the climax of the water ceremony, or the eighth day, a separate solemn assembly that closed the festival (Lev 23:36). Scholars are divided.
- **The water ceremony.** According to the Mishnah (Sukkah 4:9), each morning of the feast a priest carried a golden pitcher down to the Pool of Siloam, filled it, and brought it back up to the temple in a procession, while the people sang. At the Water Gate a trumpet sounded, and the priest poured the water into a silver bowl beside the altar, from which it drained onto the altar's base. The people sang the Hallel psalms (Ps 113–118), and Isaiah 12:3 was linked with the ceremony: "With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation." The pouring was a prayer for the autumn rains, and it also looked toward the promise of living water flowing from Jerusalem in the last days (Zech 14:8, a chapter linked with the Feast of Booths in Zech 14:16–19). If "the great day" was the seventh day, the pitcher was carried around the altar seven times. If it was the eighth, the ceremony had stopped, and Jesus' cry offered water when the ritual's water had run out.
- **"Jesus stood up and cried out" (v37).** Teachers normally sat to teach (Matt 5:1). Jesus stands and shouts, a public proclamation in the crowded temple courts.
- **"If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (v37).** This echoes Isaiah 55:1: "Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters." The invitation is to "anyone," and the only qualification is thirst.
- **"Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water" (v38).** "Heart" is literally "belly" (*koilia*), the innermost being. No single Old Testament verse says exactly this. Jesus seems to gather several promises together: water from the rock in the wilderness (Exod 17:6; Ps 78:15–16), the river flowing from the temple (Ezek 47:1–12), living waters from Jerusalem (Zech 14:8), and "you shall be like a watered garden, like a spring of water" (Isa 58:11). There is also a grammatical question about whose belly is meant. The ESV and most translations read it as the believer: rivers flow out of the one who believes. Others put the punctuation differently ("If anyone thirsts, let him come to me, and let him drink who believes in me") and read it as Christ himself: rivers flow from him. That fits John 19:34, where water flows from Jesus' side, and Ezekiel's river from the temple, which Jesus called his body (2:21). Both readings are faithful, and both are true: the water comes from Christ and then flows through believers to others.
- **"This he said about the Spirit" (v39).** John explains directly. The living water is the Holy Spirit, promised in the prophets with the same image: "I will pour water on the thirsty land… I will pour my Spirit upon your offspring" (Isa 44:3; compare Joel 2:28).
- **"As yet the Spirit had not been given, because Jesus was not yet glorified" (v39).** The Greek reads literally "for not yet was Spirit," and "given" is added to make the sense. The Spirit was active in the Old Testament and rested on Jesus himself (1:32). John means that the Spirit had not yet been poured out on believers in the new way Jesus promised, which came after his death, resurrection and ascension (16:7; 20:22; Acts 2:33). "Glorified" in John means the cross and everything that follows it (12:23–24).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads "living water" as running water, the Spirit's active grace, and says it flows out of the believer to others, because "a good man is a common good." On v39 he says that "the earnests and first-fruits of the Spirit were given, but the full harvest was not yet come." JFB says the Scripture cited is "not to any particular passage" but passages like Isaiah 58:11, Zechariah 14:8 and Ezekiel 47, and reads "belly" as "his inner man, his soul." JFB also describes the feast's rejoicing with a later Jewish saying: "Whoever had not witnessed it had never seen rejoicing at all." Guzik says the ritual pouring stopped on the eighth day, so Jesus offered real water after the symbol had ended, though, as noted above, not all scholars agree which day was "the great day." Guzik also stresses that the river flows out to others: believers are channels, not reservoirs.

#### Division Among the People (vv40–53)
- **"This really is the Prophet… This is the Christ" (vv40–41).** Some think Jesus is the prophet like Moses (Deut 18:15), others the Messiah. Moses gave water from the rock, and now Jesus offers living water.
- **"Is the Christ to come from Galilee?" (v41).** Galilee was seen as a backwater, mixed with Gentiles (Isa 9:1, "Galilee of the nations"). Judeans looked down on Galileans' accent and their lack of learning (Matt 26:73).
- **"The Christ comes from the offspring of David, and comes from Bethlehem" (v42).** They are right about the Scriptures. Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem, and 2 Samuel 7:12–16 promises David's line. They are wrong about Jesus, who was born in Bethlehem and was of David's line (Matt 1:1; 2:1; Luke 2:4). John doesn't correct them. He trusts his readers to know the story and to see the irony.
- **"So there was a division among the people over him" (v43).** "Division" is *schisma*, the root of "schism." Jesus divides people wherever he goes (9:16; 10:19). Neutral ground keeps shrinking.
- **"No one ever spoke like this man!" (v46).** The temple police return empty-handed. These were not disciples. They were sent to bring Jesus back and couldn't do it. Something in his words stopped them. Their report is one of the most striking testimonies in the Gospels, coming from men with every reason to obey their masters.
- **"Have any of the authorities or the Pharisees believed in him?" (v48).** The leaders appeal to authority: no one important believes. The irony is that one of them, Nicodemus, is about to speak up.
- **"This crowd that does not know the law is accursed" (v49).** The Pharisees looked down on "the people of the land" (*am ha-aretz*), ordinary folk who did not keep the fine points of their traditions. Some rabbinic sayings show the same contempt. A Pharisee would not eat at their table or trust them with his food. The leaders call them "accursed," likely thinking of Deuteronomy 27:26, the curse on those who don't keep the Law.
- **Nicodemus speaks (vv50–51).** "Nicodemus, who had gone to him before, and who was one of them." He doesn't defend Jesus directly. He appeals to legal procedure: "Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing?" (compare Deut 1:16–17; 17:4). The men who boasted that they knew the Law are breaking it. It's a small step for a man who first came at night, but it is public, and it puts him at risk. At the cross he will go further (19:39).
- **"Are you from Galilee too? Search and see that no prophet arises from Galilee" (v52).** They answer with a sneer, not an argument. And their claim is wrong: Jonah came from Gath-hepher in Galilee (2 Kings 14:25), only a few miles from Nazareth. One of the oldest papyrus manuscripts reads "the prophet," which would mean the Prophet like Moses, and that would make their statement less sweeping, but most manuscripts read "a prophet."
- **"They went each to his own house" (v53).** This verse begins the passage the ESV brackets, 7:53–8:11, the woman caught in adultery, which the earliest manuscripts do not include. On this site that passage is covered with chapter 8. The council breaks up without a verdict, and the plot to arrest Jesus has failed again.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the officers could not dismiss words so self-evidently powerful, though they were no friends of Jesus. JFB says they were "strangers to the profound intent of Christ's teaching" but were struck by its "mysterious grandeur." On v49 Henry calls the leaders' contempt unjust, since they themselves were responsible for teaching the people, and JFB says "accursed" was spoken "out of mingled rage and scorn." All three notice that the leaders' claim in v52 is false. Guzik and JFB both name Jonah of Gath-hepher, and JFB adds Elijah the Tishbite, though Elijah's home was probably in Gilead, east of the Jordan, not Galilee. Henry adds Nahum, whose hometown of Elkosh some placed in Galilee. JFB sums up the leaders: "rage is blind, and deep prejudice distorts all facts." Henry observes that a high opinion of Christ, like the crowd's in vv40–41, still falls short of living faith.

#### Pointing to Christ
At the feast that remembered Israel's wilderness years, when God gave water from the rock, Jesus stands in the temple and cries out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink." The water ceremony prayed for rain and looked for the day when living water would flow from Jerusalem. Jesus says that day has come in him, and that the water is the Spirit, poured out once he is glorified on the cross. Around him, people argue about where he comes from, whether he has the right credentials, and whether anyone important believes. Meanwhile his family doubts him, the leaders plot, the crowd is divided, and the officers sent to arrest him come back saying, "No one ever spoke like this man!" The chapter leaves every reader in the same crowd, facing the same question.
