*Setting:* Still in Pharaoh's court, at the end of the ninth plague. Three days of darkness have ended with Pharaoh's last offer refused and his threat: "on the day you see my face you shall die." Moses answered, "I will not see your face again" (10:28–29). Chapter 11 is short, and it reads as Moses' final word in that same meeting. Verses 1–3 step back to tell what God had already told Moses, and verses 4–8 are the announcement of the last and worst plague, the death of the firstborn. It sets up the Passover in chapter 12. Back at the burning bush God had said it would come to this: "Israel is my firstborn son… If you refuse to let him go, behold, I will kill your firstborn son" (4:22–23).

#### A Final Plague Threatened (vv1–10)
- **"Yet one plague more" (v1).** The word for "plague" here is *nega'*, a "stroke" or "blow." Nine signs have come and gone. This one will finish the contest. **"He will drive you away completely."** The Hebrew doubles the verb for emphasis, "driving he will drive you out." Pharaoh, who would not let Israel go, will soon be pushing them out the door.
- **The timing of vv1–3.** Moses has just said he will not see Pharaoh again, yet in v4 he is still speaking to him. Most commentators read vv1–3 as a flashback: God had told Moses this earlier, and the narrator inserts it here before Moses' last speech. Moses then speaks vv4–8 before walking out.
- **"That they ask, every man of his neighbor… for silver and gold jewelry" (v2).** The verb is *sha'al*, "to ask" or "request." The KJV's "borrow" has led some to think Israel tricked the Egyptians, but nothing in the word or the story suggests a loan to be returned. God had promised this to Abraham ("they shall come out with great possessions," Gen 15:14) and to Moses at the bush (3:21–22). The law later commands Israelites not to send a freed slave away empty-handed (Deut 15:13–14). After centuries of unpaid labor, this is closer to back wages.
- **"The LORD gave the people favor in the sight of the Egyptians" (v3).** The Egyptians give gladly. After nine plagues, ordinary Egyptians have seen enough to respect Israel and its God. Some of this gold will later go into the tabernacle (35:22), and some, sadly, into the golden calf (32:2–4).
- **"The man Moses was very great in the land of Egypt."** The stammering shepherd of chapter 4 is now honored by Pharaoh's own servants. Pharaoh alone refuses to see what everyone around him can.
- **"About midnight I will go out in the midst of Egypt" (v4).** For the first time, no staff is lifted and no dust is thrown. God himself will act. "About midnight" is precise in the hour but leaves the night unnamed, so all Egypt is left waiting.
- **"Every firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die" (v5).** The firstborn son carried the family's name, inheritance and future. Egypt began its war on Israel by killing Hebrew baby boys (1:16, 22). Now the judgment falls where the sin began.
- **"From the firstborn of Pharaoh who sits on his throne, even to the firstborn of the slave girl who is behind the handmill."** The phrase runs from the top of Egyptian society to the bottom. Pharaoh's son was the heir to a king Egyptians thought of as divine. A slave girl turning a stone hand mill to grind grain did the lowest work in the land (compare Isa 47:2). No one is too high to be reached or too low to be noticed. 12:29 adds "the captive who was in the dungeon." **"All the firstborn of the cattle"** shows the judgment reaches Egypt's sacred animals as well.
- **Why the firstborn?** This troubles many readers. The text gives the answer in 4:22–23: Israel is God's firstborn, and Pharaoh has enslaved him and refused ten times to let him go. Pharaoh's father had drowned Israel's sons. The judgment is severe, but it comes after long warning, and chapter 12 will show God providing a way of escape through the blood of a lamb for anyone who will take it.
- **"There shall be a great cry throughout all the land of Egypt" (v6).** "Cry" is *tse'aqah*, the word used for Israel's cry under slavery, which God heard (3:7, 9). The cry of the oppressed now becomes the cry of the oppressor. 12:30 tells how it came true: "there was not a house where someone was not dead."
- **"But not a dog shall growl against any of the people of Israel" (v7).** Literally "not a dog shall sharpen its tongue." In the middle of the night, with wailing in every Egyptian house, Israel's homes are so quiet that not even a dog barks at them. **"The LORD makes a distinction between Egypt and Israel."** This distinction has run through the plagues (8:22–23; 9:4, 26; 10:23). Here it is life and death.
- **"All these your servants shall come down to me and bow down to me" (v8).** Moses tells Pharaoh that his own officials will bow to Moses and beg Israel to leave. It happened, and Pharaoh himself joined them (12:31–33). **"And after that I will go out."**
- **"He went out from Pharaoh in hot anger."** Literally "in burning of the nose," the common Hebrew idiom for anger. The meek Moses (Num 12:3) is angry. It is not a temper tantrum but grief and indignation at a king who has lied again and again and will now bring death on his own people.
- **"Pharaoh will not listen to you, that my wonders may be multiplied" (v9).** God had said this from the beginning (7:3–4). The refusals have not defeated God's plan; they have become the stage on which he shows who he is.
- **"Moses and Aaron did all these wonders before Pharaoh" (v10).** This verse closes the whole plague cycle that began in 7:8. It sums up both sides: God's servants did all he asked, and **"the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart."** The next chapter turns from Egypt's judgment to Israel's rescue.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the death of the firstborn "had been first threatened" (4:23) but came last, after gentler warnings, and that "the slaves at the mill" were not "too low to be taken notice of." He says "there should not so much as a dog bark" at Israel, that Moses was "provoked to a holy indignation," and that Pharaoh's refusal was "a certain sign that God himself had, in a way of righteous judgment, hardened his heart." JFB calls the announcement "made in the most solemn manner to the king" and explains "I will go out" as "language used after the manner of men." He calls grinding at the mill "the lowest employment" and Moses' anger "holy and righteous indignation at the duplicity, repeated falsehood, and hardened impenitence of the king." JFB leaves vv2–3 for 12:35 and does not comment on vv9–10. Guzik reads the silver and gold as back wages for years of forced labor, and asks readers how many of God's own warnings they have ignored. The Tyndale notes add that Egypt, a culture obsessed with life after death, now learns that life and death are in the LORD's hand alone.

#### Pointing to Christ
The tenth plague points straight ahead to the Passover lamb. The firstborn of Egypt die, and Israel's firstborn are spared, not because they are better, but because blood is on the door (12:13). The same God who said "every firstborn shall die" later gave his own Son, "the firstborn of all creation" (Col 1:15), to die in his people's place. At the cross there was darkness, and then a great cry (Matt 27:45–46, 50). Christ is "our Passover lamb" who "has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7), and in him God makes a distinction no judgment can cross: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Rom 8:1).
