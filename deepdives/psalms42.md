*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Maskil of the Sons of Korah." This psalm opens Book Two of the Psalter (Psalms 42–72). Book Two has a distinctive habit: it mostly calls God *Elohim*, "God," where Book One mostly used the covenant name *Yahweh*, "the LORD." Psalm 42 uses "God" again and again and "LORD" only once (v8). The Sons of Korah were a family of Levites who served as gatekeepers and temple singers (1 Chr 9:19; 26:1; 2 Chr 20:19). Their ancestor Korah led a rebellion against Moses and was swallowed by the earth, but "the sons of Korah did not die" (Num 16:31–33; 26:11). Eleven psalms carry their name (42, 44–49, 84–85, 87–88). "Maskil" probably means a skillful or instructive psalm, from *sakal*, "to act wisely" (see Ps 32). Psalms 42 and 43 almost certainly belong together. Psalm 43 has no title, which is rare in Book Two, and the same refrain runs through both (42:5, 11; 43:5). Many Hebrew manuscripts join them as one psalm. The singer is far from Jerusalem, in the far north near the sources of the Jordan, taunted by people who do not share his faith. JFB suggests a Levite with David in exile during Absalom's revolt. The Tyndale notes suggest the voice of Israel in exile. The text does not say.

#### As a Deer Pants for Flowing Streams (vv1–5)
- **"As a deer pants for flowing streams" (v1).** "Pants" (*'arag*) is a rare verb. It is used only here and in Joel 1:20, where "even the beasts of the field pant for you because the water brooks are dried up." The picture is not a deer pausing for a pleasant drink but an animal in a drought, desperate for water that is not there. JFB: "desires in a state of exhaustion." The word for deer is masculine but the verb is feminine, which has led some to picture a doe.
- **"So pants my soul for you, O God" (v1).** What he longs for is not a better situation but God himself. David says the same in the wilderness: "my soul thirsts for you; my flesh faints for you, as in a dry and weary land where there is no water" (63:1).
- **"My soul thirsts for God, for the living God" (v2).** "The living God" stands against idols, which are dead, and against dry streambeds. God is "the fountain of living waters" (Jer 2:13). Jesus stood up at the Feast of Booths and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37).
- **"When shall I come and appear before God?" (v2).** "Appear before God" is the technical language of the Law for the three pilgrim feasts, when every Israelite man was to "appear before the LORD" at the sanctuary (Exod 23:17; Deut 16:16). JFB: "the stated personal appearance of the Jews at the sanctuary." He is cut off from temple worship, and to him that feels like being cut off from God's face.
- **"My tears have been my food day and night" (v3).** He cries so much that he is not eating. Grief has replaced food.
- **"While they say to me all the day long, 'Where is your God?'" (v3).** This is the cruelest taunt. It suggests God has abandoned him, or does not exist, or cannot help. Shimei said much the same to David (2 Sam 16:7–8), and the crowd at the cross mocked, "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (Matt 27:43). The taunt comes back in v10, framing the psalm's middle section.
- **"These things I remember, as I pour out my soul" (v4).** "Pour out" is the picture of emptying a jar. He holds nothing back (62:8; 1 Sam 1:15, where Hannah "poured out my soul before the LORD").
- **"How I would go with the throng and lead them in procession to the house of God" (v4).** As a Levite he had led the pilgrim crowds up to the temple, singing. The Songs of Ascents (Pss 120–134) were probably sung on such journeys. "A multitude keeping festival": the word for "keeping festival" is *chagag*, related to *chag*, "feast," and can even mean dancing in a festal ring. The memory of those days is sweet and painful at once. JFB thinks the verbs could be future ("I will remember") but most read them as recalling the past.
- **"Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me?" (v5).** The refrain. The psalmist speaks to himself. He questions his own feelings instead of simply following them. "Cast down" means bowed low, sunk. "Turmoil" is the word for the roaring of the sea or the growl of a bear (Isa 59:11). Martyn Lloyd-Jones, whom Guzik cites, made this verse the heart of his book *Spiritual Depression*: most of our unhappiness comes from listening to ourselves instead of talking to ourselves.
- **"Hope in God; for I shall again praise him" (v5).** "Hope" here is the verb for waiting with expectation. He does not deny his feelings, but he tells them where to look. "I shall again praise him" is confident: the present darkness is not the end.
- **"My salvation and my God" (vv5–6).** The standard Hebrew text reads "for the salvation of his face" at the end of v5, with "my God" at the start of v6 (KJV: "for the help of his countenance. O my God"). A few Hebrew manuscripts and ancient versions read "the salvation of my face and my God," and the ESV follows them, making v5 match v11 and 43:5 exactly. JFB notes "countenance" means "face" and links it to the priestly blessing, "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). Either way, salvation is pictured as God's face turned toward him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Living souls never can take up their rest any where short of a living God." He says what grieved the psalmist most was losing free access to God's house, not losing comforts, and on v5 he warns, "When the soul rests on itself, it sinks." Henry goes further than the text when he says being cast down "springs from unbelief and a rebellious will." Guzik is gentler. He lists honest causes for the psalmist's sorrow (distance from worship, mockery, painful memories, trials and God's apparent delay) and concludes that none of them outweighs God's greatness. Guzik notes that the Sons of Korah were descendants of a rebel spared by grace, which might explain their love of praise. JFB links the thirst to Psalm 63:1.

#### Deep Calls to Deep (vv6–11)
- **"My soul is cast down within me; therefore I remember you" (v6).** He takes his downcast soul straight to God. His memory has been a source of pain (v4). Now he chooses to remember not just the past but God himself.
- **"From the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar" (v6).** Mount Hermon, over 9,000 feet high, stands at the far north of Israel. Its snow and springs feed the headwaters of the Jordan near Dan and Caesarea Philippi. That was about as far from Jerusalem as one could get and still be in the land. "Hermon" is plural in Hebrew, "the Hermons," the peaks of the range. "Mizar" means "small" or "little." No one knows where it was. The Tyndale notes suggest a peak in the Hermon range. JFB thinks the name may express contempt for the place of exile, "the little hill," compared with Zion. Some see a contrast: the little hill where he stands, and the great hill of God he longs for.
- **"Deep calls to deep at the roar of your waterfalls" (v7).** In spring the melting snows of Hermon send torrents crashing down the gorges at the Jordan's source. Standing there, he hears one roaring cataract seem to call to the next. Then the landscape becomes a picture of his soul. "Deep" is *tehom*, the primeval deep of creation (Gen 1:2). Trouble after trouble, one wave answering another. JFB: "the heavy waves of sorrow which overwhelmed him."
- **"All your breakers and your waves have gone over me" (v7).** Notice "*your* waterfalls," "*your* breakers." Even the waves that drown him are God's. That is frightening, but it is also comfort: they are not outside his control. Jonah prays almost the same words from the belly of the fish: "all your waves and your billows passed over me" (Jonah 2:3).
- **"By day the LORD commands his steadfast love, and at night his song is with me" (v8).** The only use of *Yahweh*, the covenant name, in the psalm. Here, in the middle of the floods, is the turning point. God "commands" his steadfast love (*chesed*), sending it out like a king giving orders to a servant. By night, God gives him a song (Job 35:10: "God my Maker, who gives songs in the night"). Paul and Silas sang hymns at midnight in prison (Acts 16:25).
- **"A prayer to the God of my life" (v8).** The taunters ask "Where is your God?" He answers by praying to "the God of my life," the living God of v2, the one who holds his life.
- **"I say to God, my rock: 'Why have you forgotten me?'" (v9).** Faith and complaint sit side by side. He calls God "my rock," steady and dependable, and in the same breath asks why God has forgotten him. The Psalms allow both. Honest questions spoken to God are not unbelief. They are a form of faith.
- **"As with a deadly wound in my bones, my adversaries taunt me" (v10).** Literally "with a crushing in my bones." The KJV: "as with a sword in my bones." Words can wound like a weapon. The taunt "Where is your God?" (v3) returns, and it hurts all the more now.
- **"Why are you cast down, O my soul…?" (v11).** The refrain returns word for word. The struggle is not settled once and for all. Hope has to be chosen again. But each time the refrain comes, it ends in the same place: "my salvation and my God." The final word of the psalm is "my God," the answer to "Where is your God?"
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The way to forget our miseries, is to remember the God of our mercies," and "Let us never think that the God of our life, and the Rock of our salvation, has forgotten us." He adds that Christ's word "will calm every storm, and turn midnight darkness into the light of noon." Guzik reads "deep calls to deep" both as trouble upon trouble and as the depth of God's care answering the depth of human need. He notes that the repeated refrain shows hope is an ongoing conversation with oneself, not a single decision. JFB calls the taunts "infidel suggestions" and renders the end of v11 as the God who "cheers me, driving away clouds of sorrow from my face."

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus knew this psalm from the inside. In Gethsemane he said, "My soul is very sorrowful" (Matt 26:38). The Greek of the Septuagint uses the same word for "cast down" in this refrain. On the cross the crowd threw the taunt of v3 at him, and he cried, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46), the question of v9 in its sharpest form. The waves and breakers of God went over him, so that they would not finally go over his people. And he is the answer to the thirst of v1. To the Samaritan woman he promised water that becomes "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14). The one who longs to "come and appear before God" can now draw near through him, "since we have a great high priest over the house of God" (Heb 10:19–22).
