*Setting:* Around the Sea of Galilee and then far to the north at Caesarea Philippi, probably the summer or autumn of AD 29. Jesus has been moving through Gentile regions, where he helped a Syrophoenician mother and healed a deaf man in the Decapolis (7:24–37). Chapter 8 is the hinge of Mark's Gospel. The first half of the book has kept asking, "Who is this?" (4:41). Here, after one more feeding miracle and one more round of the disciples' confusion, Peter finally answers: "You are the Christ." Then, at once, Jesus begins to teach what kind of Christ he is: one who must suffer, die and rise. From here on, the road leads to Jerusalem and the cross.

#### Jesus Feeds the Four Thousand (vv1–10)
- **"In those days, when again a great crowd had gathered" (v1).** This is a second feeding, not a retelling of the first. Mark and Matthew both record it, and Jesus himself later refers to the two separately (vv19–20; Matt 16:9–10). The setting seems to be the Decapolis on the eastern side of the lake (7:31), so the crowd was probably largely Gentile. The first feeding took place among Jews in Galilee.
- **"I have compassion on the crowd, because they have been with me now three days" (v2).** In 6:34 Jesus had compassion because the people were like sheep without a shepherd. Here it is because they are hungry. He cares about bodies as well as souls. **"Some of them have come from far away" (v3).** "From far away" is a phrase the Old Testament and the early church used of Gentiles (Josh 9:6; Isa 60:4; Acts 2:39; Eph 2:13, 17).
- **"How can one feed these people with bread here in this desolate place?" (v4).** It is hard to believe the disciples ask this after the five thousand. But people often forget what God has done when a new need arises. Israel did the same in the wilderness: God gave water from the rock, and soon after they asked, "Can God spread a table in the wilderness?" (Ps 78:19–20).
- **"Seven" loaves (v5).** Jesus again starts with what they have.
- **"Having given thanks, he broke them and gave them to his disciples" (v6).** "Given thanks" is *eucharisteō*, the root of the word "Eucharist." In 6:41 Mark used "blessed." Both are the same Jewish act of thanking God at a meal, and both point forward to the Last Supper (14:22–23).
- **"Seven baskets full" (v8).** The word for basket here is different from the first feeding. There it was *kophinos* (6:43), a smaller wicker basket a traveling Jew might carry. Here it is *spyris*, a larger basket or hamper, the kind Paul was lowered in over the wall of Damascus (Acts 9:25). Some interpreters see meaning in the numbers: twelve baskets for the tribes of Israel among Jews, and seven, the number of fullness, among Gentiles, perhaps recalling the seven nations of Canaan (Deut 7:1). Others, like the Tyndale notes, warn against reading symbols into the numbers, since none of the Gospel writers explains them. What is clear is that Jesus feeds Jews and Gentiles alike, and there is more than enough for both.
- **"About four thousand people" (v9).** Matthew again adds "besides women and children" (Matt 15:38).
- **"The district of Dalmanutha" (v10).** No one knows for certain where this was. Matthew calls it Magadan (Matt 15:39). Both were probably near Magdala on the western shore, back in Jewish territory.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "The bounty of Christ is inexhaustible." JFB takes the separate details, the number of loaves and the two kinds of basket, as proof of "the distinctness of the two miracles," and says of Jesus' compassion that it was "an expression of that deep emotion in the Redeemer's heart which always preceded some remarkable interposition for relief." Guzik notes that some scholars treat this as a retelling of the five thousand, but argues that the disciples' forgetfulness is exactly how believers behave.

#### The Pharisees Demand a Sign (vv11–13)
- **"Seeking from him a sign from heaven to test him" (v11).** Jesus had done many miracles. They wanted something more: a spectacular sign "from heaven," directly from God, on their terms, that would force them to believe or that he would fail to produce. "Test" is the same word used for Satan's testing of Jesus in the wilderness (1:13).
- **"He sighed deeply in his spirit" (v12).** A stronger word than the sigh in 7:34, used only here in the New Testament. It is grief and frustration at hearts set against him.
- **"Why does this generation seek a sign?" (v12).** "This generation" echoes the wilderness generation that tested God despite all they had seen (Deut 32:5, 20; Ps 95:10). **"Truly, I say to you, no sign will be given to this generation."** The Greek is a Hebrew oath form, literally "if a sign shall be given," with the curse left unspoken: "may I be cursed if…" It is a solemn refusal. Matthew adds, "except the sign of Jonah" (Matt 12:39; 16:4), meaning his resurrection. Miracles were never meant to compel faith in people determined not to believe.
- **"He left them, got into the boat again, and went to the other side" (v13).** A quiet but serious picture. Jesus walks away from those who demand proof on their own terms.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the refusal as grief over their stubborn unbelief. JFB says the Pharisees were "not in the least desiring evidence for their conviction, but hoping to entrap Him," and explains "no sign" as "a Jewish way of expressing a solemn and peremptory determination." Guzik, citing Lane, says the demand came from minds already made up, and that Jesus' miracles were meant to show mercy, not to win over hardened opponents. The Tyndale notes add that asking God for a sign is sometimes acceptable in Scripture (Judg 6:36–40), but not as obstinate testing.

#### The Leaven of the Pharisees and Herod (vv14–21)
- **"They had only one loaf with them in the boat" (v14).** A detail only Mark gives. Some see a hint: they have one loaf in the boat, and he is the Bread of Life (John 6:35). Whether Mark intends that or not, the irony is that the one who fed thousands is sitting with them.
- **"Beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and the leaven of Herod" (v15).** Leaven was a bit of old fermented dough kept back to raise the next batch. A little spread through the whole lump (1 Cor 5:6). In Scripture it usually stands for a corrupting influence. Matthew says the leaven of the Pharisees is their teaching (Matt 16:12). Here in Mark the context suggests their unbelief, the demand for a sign they had just made. The leaven of Herod may be his worldly, political idea of power, or his habit of hearing the truth and refusing to obey it (6:20). Both can spread quietly into disciples.
- **"They began discussing with one another the fact that they had no bread" (v16).** They take Jesus' words literally and think he is scolding them for forgetting to buy bread. They are thinking about lunch.
- **"Do you not yet perceive or understand? Are your hearts hardened?" (v17).** Jesus asks a rapid series of questions. "Hardened" is the same word used of the Pharisees in 3:5 and of the disciples in 6:52. **"Having eyes do you not see, and having ears do you not hear?" (v18).** This echoes Jeremiah 5:21 and Ezekiel 12:2, and Jesus' own words about "those outside" in 4:12. The insiders are behaving like outsiders.
- **"How many baskets… Twelve… And the seven for the four thousand… Seven" (vv19–20).** They remember the facts perfectly. They have not grasped what the facts mean: that Jesus can provide anything they need, and that he is the one who feeds God's people in the wilderness.
- **"Do you not yet understand?" (v21).** The chapter's question. "Yet" holds out hope: they will understand, but not yet. The question is left hanging, and the next story answers it in a picture.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "But a little while ago He was tried with the obduracy of the Pharisees; now He is tried with the obtuseness of His own disciples," and on v17, "How strong an expression to use of true-hearted disciples!" It reads Herod's leaven as that of the Herodians, "rather a political party," yet just as hostile to Jesus' spiritual teaching. Guzik, citing Barclay, explains leaven as a pinch of old dough spreading through a batch, and reads both leavens as false ideas about the Messiah's kingdom, political for Herod and religious for the Pharisees. Henry says their remembered experiences of Christ's goodness should have strengthened their trust.

#### Jesus Heals a Blind Man at Bethsaida (vv22–26)
- **"They came to Bethsaida" (v22).** A fishing town at the north end of the lake, just east of where the Jordan flows in. It was the hometown of Peter, Andrew and Philip (John 1:44). Philip the tetrarch had rebuilt it as a city and named it Julias. Jesus later grieved over its unbelief (Matt 11:21).
- **"He took the blind man by the hand and led him out of the village" (v23).** As with the deaf man (7:33), he takes him aside, away from the crowd. A blind man led by the hand of Jesus is a tender picture.
- **"When he had spit on his eyes and laid his hands on him, he asked him, 'Do you see anything?'" (v23).** Again Jesus uses spittle and touch, things a blind man can feel.
- **"I see people, but they look like trees, walking" (v24).** The man must have once had sight, since he knows what trees look like. He sees blurred shapes, tall and upright, and can tell they are people only because they move.
- **"Then Jesus laid his hands on his eyes again… and he saw everything clearly" (v25).** This is the only miracle in the Gospels that happens in two stages. Why? It is certainly not because Jesus' power was lacking. The best explanation is its place in the story. It comes right after the disciples' failure to see (v18) and right before Peter's confession (v29). The disciples are like this man after the first touch. They see something, that Jesus is the Messiah, but not clearly. They still need a second touch, which comes slowly through the cross and the resurrection, to see what kind of Messiah he is. The story is a parable in action. It also reminds readers that people often come to see Jesus clearly little by little.
- **"Do not even enter the village" (v26).** Jesus again guards against crowds who would come only for miracles.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "It is the only recorded example of a progressive cure," and on v24 reads the man as saying, in effect, "I see men; for I see them as trees walking," able to tell them apart only "by their motion." It quotes Bengel that leading the man out by hand showed "great humility." Henry links the stages to spiritual sight that "often begins dimly and grows clearer." Guzik makes the same application, quoting Calvin through Wessel that grace sometimes flows "by drops." The Tyndale notes suggest the two-stage healing points ahead to the opening of the disciples' eyes in the next scene.

#### Peter Confesses Jesus as the Christ (vv27–30)
- **"The villages of Caesarea Philippi" (v27).** About twenty-five miles north of the Sea of Galilee, at the foot of Mount Hermon, where one of the main springs of the Jordan bursts from a cave. The place had long been a shrine to the Greek god Pan, and the town was called Paneas (today Banias). Herod the Great built a white marble temple there to honor Caesar Augustus, and his son Philip rebuilt the town and named it after Caesar and himself. It was a place full of claims to divinity, pagan gods and the emperor. Here, far from Jerusalem and the crowds, Jesus asks who he is.
- **"Who do people say that I am?" (v27).** **"John the Baptist; and others say, Elijah; and others, one of the prophets" (v28).** The same guesses Herod heard (6:14–15). All are high opinions. None is high enough.
- **"But who do you say that I am?" (v29).** "You" is emphatic in Greek. Popular opinion is not enough. Every disciple must answer for himself.
- **"You are the Christ" (v29).** "Christ" (*Christos*) is the Greek for the Hebrew *Mashiach*, "Messiah," the "Anointed One," the king promised from David's line (2 Sam 7:12–16; Ps 2:2). This is the first time in Mark that a human being calls Jesus the Christ. Mark told his readers in the first verse (1:1). Now, halfway through the book, the disciples see it. Matthew records the fuller confession and Jesus' blessing on Peter, "flesh and blood has not revealed this to you" (Matt 16:16–19). Mark, writing from Peter's own preaching, gives the plain confession and moves straight to Peter's failure, perhaps because Peter told it that way.
- **"He strictly charged them to tell no one about him" (v30).** The title was true, but in that day it carried heavy political hopes: a warrior-king who would drive out Rome (compare John 6:15). Jesus would not let it be spread until he had shown what it truly meant.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees Peter's confession as the foundation of Christian faith. Guzik explains the people's guesses as seeing Jesus as a reformer, a miracle-worker or a prophet, but not as the Messiah, and notes with Wessel that "Christ" had come to mean an ideal, empowered king, often misunderstood politically. JFB refers this section to its notes on Matthew 16.

#### Jesus Foretells His Death and Resurrection (vv31–38)
- **"He began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things" (v31).** "Began" marks a new stage. From now on Jesus will speak of his death again and again (9:31; 10:33–34). "Must" (*dei*) is a word of divine necessity. It is God's plan, foretold in Scripture: the suffering servant of Isaiah 53, the rejected stone of Psalm 118:22. The suffering is not a defeat of the plan; it is the plan.
- **"Rejected by the elders and the chief priests and the scribes" (v31).** The three groups that made up the Sanhedrin, the ruling council in Jerusalem. They are the ones who will condemn him (14:53).
- **"And after three days rise again" (v31).** Every time Jesus foretells his death, he foretells his resurrection too. The disciples seem not to have heard that part. "After three days" was a common way of saying "on the third day" (Matt 16:21).
- **"He said this plainly" (v32).** Not in parables now, but openly.
- **"Peter took him aside and began to rebuke him" (v32).** The word "rebuke" is the same strong word used of Jesus rebuking demons and the storm. Peter, who has just confessed him as the Christ, now tries to correct him. In Peter's mind a crucified Messiah was a contradiction. The Messiah was supposed to win, not die. Matthew gives his words: "Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you" (Matt 16:22).
- **"Turning and seeing his disciples, he rebuked Peter" (v33).** Jesus looks at the others first. Peter is speaking for all of them, and the correction is for all of them.
- **"Get behind me, Satan!" (v33).** Strong words. Peter is not possessed. But in urging Jesus to avoid the cross, he is repeating Satan's temptation in the wilderness: take the kingdom without the suffering (Matt 4:8–10). "Get behind me" may also mean, "Get back in your place, behind me, as a follower," the same word as "come after me" in v34. **"You are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man."** Peter's love for Jesus was real, but his way of thinking was merely human.
- **"Calling the crowd to him with his disciples" (v34).** This teaching is for everyone, not just the twelve.
- **"If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me" (v34).** Three commands. "Deny himself" means to say no to self as the master of one's life, not just to give up certain pleasures. "Take up his cross" had only one meaning in that world. Romans forced condemned men to carry the crossbeam to the place of execution, and Jesus' hearers had seen it. After the revolts that broke out when Herod the Great died, about the time of Jesus' birth, the Roman governor Varus crucified two thousand rebels, Josephus says. To take up a cross was to walk toward death. "Follow me" is the same call given to the fishermen (1:17), now with its full cost spelled out. Luke adds "daily" (Luke 9:23).
- **"Whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake and the gospel's will save it" (v35).** The word for "life" is *psychē*, which also means "soul," the whole self. The same word is used in vv36–37, where the ESV translates it "soul." Jesus is playing on its range: holding on to this life at all costs means losing true life; giving it up for Jesus means finding it. "And the gospel's" is found only in Mark, a word for his readers in Rome who faced death for their faith.
- **"What does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his soul?" (v36).** The language of business: profit and loss. Imagine owning everything, and losing one's own self. Satan once offered Jesus "all the kingdoms of the world" (Matt 4:8–9). He refused.
- **"What can a man give in return for his soul?" (v37).** There is no price that can buy it back. Psalm 49:7–8: "the ransom of their life is costly." Only one ransom is enough, and Jesus will give it (10:45).
- **"Whoever is ashamed of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation" (v38).** "Adulterous" is the prophets' word for a people unfaithful to God (Hos 2:2–5). In the Roman world, following a crucified Jew was shameful. Paul wrote, "I am not ashamed of the gospel" (Rom 1:16).
- **"The Son of Man also be ashamed when he comes in the glory of his Father with the holy angels" (v38).** The suffering Son of Man of v31 is also the glorious Son of Man of Daniel 7:13–14 who will come to judge. The one who is about to be shamed on a cross will return in glory. Mark's chapter ends here, though the thought runs on into 9:1.
- **Commentators:** Henry presents discipleship as renouncing self, bearing the cross and following Christ, even to death, and insists no worldly gain can make up for the loss of the soul. Guzik stresses that the cross was an instrument of execution, not a picture of mild hardship, and follows Wiersbe in separating self-denial, giving up things, from denying self, surrendering to Christ. He reads "must" in v31 against Isaiah 53, and on "Get behind me, Satan" says Peter's love was sincere but his thinking was man-centered, the leaven of v15 showing in his own heart. The Tyndale notes say much the same: Peter unknowingly spoke for Satan and repeated his temptation. JFB refers vv27–38 to its notes on Matthew 16.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everything in Mark has been building to Peter's words, "You are the Christ," and everything after builds to the cross. Jesus is the one who feeds Jews and Gentiles in the wilderness, who sighs over hard hearts, and who touches a blind man twice until he sees clearly. His disciples need that second touch too. They see that he is the Messiah, but not yet that the Messiah must suffer. Jesus says plainly that he will be rejected, killed and raised, and he calls everyone who would follow him to walk the same road. It sounds like loss. But the one who gives his life for the whole world is the only one who can give a soul back, and whoever loses his life for his sake will find it.
