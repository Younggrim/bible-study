*Setting:* This is the last of Zechariah's eight night visions, all given on one night in February 519 BC (1:7), while the returned exiles were rebuilding the temple under the governor Zerubbabel and the high priest Joshua. The series began with horsemen patrolling a world at ease while Jerusalem lay in ruins (1:8–11). It ends with chariots going out to the four winds, and God's anger toward the nation that destroyed Jerusalem finally at rest. Then the visions give way to an acted sign in broad daylight. A delegation of Jews had just arrived from Babylon with gifts of silver and gold. Zechariah is told to make a crown from it and set it, surprisingly, on the head of the high priest. With that sign the night visions reach their goal: a coming Branch who will build God's temple and be both king and priest.

#### A Vision of Four Chariots (vv1–8)
- **"Four chariots came out from between two mountains" (v1).** Chariots were the fastest and most powerful weapons of the ancient world, used by kings in war. Here they stand for God's swift action in history. "Two mountains" is literally "*the* two mountains," as if the reader should know which. Many take them as Mount Zion and the Mount of Olives, with the Kidron Valley between them, the place from which God goes out to act in 14:4. Others see them as a heavenly gateway where God's servants come and go.
- **"The mountains were mountains of bronze" (v1).** Bronze suggests strength and permanence. Solomon's temple had two great bronze pillars at its entrance, named Jachin and Boaz (1 Kgs 7:15–22), and the Tyndale notes see the mountains as a larger picture of them, the gateway to God's house. Others take the bronze as a picture of God's firm, unshakable decrees.
- **Red, black, white and dappled horses (vv2–3).** The first vision had red, sorrel and white horses (1:8). Revelation 6:1–8 uses similar colors for horses of conquest, war, famine and death, and some carry those meanings back here. Zechariah himself gives no meaning to the colors, and it is wise not to press them. The phrase "all of them strong" (v3) may belong only to the dappled horses. The Hebrew word *'amutstsim* is uncertain. The ESV takes it as "strong." Some ancient translations read it as another color.
- **"Going out to the four winds of heaven" (v5).** The Hebrew *ruchot* can mean "winds" or "spirits." The ESV has "winds," and some translations have "spirits." Either way the picture is of God's agents sent in every direction, "after presenting themselves before the Lord of all the earth." Like servants in a royal court, they report to the throne and are sent out again (Job 1:6; Ps 104:4).
- **"The black horses go toward the north country… the dappled ones go toward the south country" (v6).** The "north country" is Babylon. It lay east of Judah, but invaders from there always came down along the Fertile Crescent and entered from the north (Jer 1:14; 6:22; Zech 2:6–7). The "south country" is Egypt, Israel's other old oppressor. Nothing is said of where the red horses go, and the Hebrew of "the white ones go after them" is difficult (some read "toward the west").
- **"They were impatient to go and patrol the earth" (v7).** Literally they "sought to go." God's servants are eager to do his will, but they wait until he says "Go." In the first vision the riders found the earth "at rest" while Jerusalem suffered (1:11). Now the chariots go out to set that right.
- **"Then he cried to me" (v8).** The speaker is no longer only the interpreting angel. The voice speaks of "my Spirit," so it is the LORD himself, or the angel of the LORD speaking for him. The series of visions ends with God's own word.
- **"Have set my Spirit at rest in the north country" (v8).** The word *ruach* can mean God's Spirit, but also his anger or temper, as when Gideon's answer caused the men of Ephraim's "anger" to subside (Judg 8:3, same word). The verb *nuach* means to rest or settle. God's wrath against Babylon has been carried out and is now satisfied. In 1:15 he was "exceedingly angry with the nations that are at ease." Now that anger is quieted. Babylon had already fallen to Persia in 539 BC, and for the exiles still there, this was also a signal: judgment on Babylon is done, so come home (2:6–7).
- **How the visions fit together.** The first and eighth visions both have horses patrolling the earth, framing the series. In the middle are the visions of Joshua (ch 3) and the lampstand (ch 4), about the two leaders. The whole set moves from the nations being at ease while God's people suffer, to God's people being cleansed and God's enemies judged. The world's history is not running loose. God sends out his chariots.
- **Commentators:** Henry prefers a broad reading, taking the chariots as God's providence over the world carried out by angels, who "are often called the chariots of God." He reads the bronze mountains as God's fixed decrees, from which every event comes out. JFB takes the mountains as Moriah and Olivet and the chariots as the four world empires of Daniel, a reading Guzik mentions but thinks less likely than the link with Revelation 6. All three read v8 as God's anger at Babylon satisfied, which fits the use of *ruach* for anger in Judges 8:3. The Tyndale notes caution, rightly, that Zechariah assigns no meaning to the colors.

#### The Crown and the Temple (vv9–15)
- **"Take from the exiles Heldai, Tobijah, and Jedaiah, who have arrived from Babylon" (v10).** Many Jews stayed in Babylon after 538 BC, and they sent gifts to support the work in Jerusalem (Ezra 1:4–6; 8:25–30). These three men had just come with such gifts. Their names fit the moment: Heldai probably means "robust" or "enduring," Tobijah "the LORD is good," and Jedaiah "the LORD knows." "Go the same day": the sign was to be done while the visitors were still in town, so they could carry the report back.
- **"The house of Josiah, the son of Zephaniah" (v10).** Josiah was evidently the host of the visitors. In v14 he is called **Hen**, which means "grace" or "favor." It is probably a nickname or title ("the gracious one") rather than a different man. Likewise **Helem** in v14 is probably another form of Heldai.
- **"Make a crown, and set it on the head of Joshua… the high priest" (v11).** The Hebrew word *'atarot* is plural, "crowns" (ESV footnote). It may be a single elaborate crown of several bands or circlets, perhaps one silver and one gold, or the plural may stress its grandeur. This is a royal crown, not the high priest's turban with its gold plate (Exod 28:36–38). That is what makes the act startling. In Israel kings and priests were kept apart. King Uzziah was struck with leprosy for trying to burn incense in the temple (2 Chr 26:16–21). No high priest had ever worn a king's crown.
- **Why Joshua and not Zerubbabel?** Zerubbabel was the descendant of David, the obvious candidate for a crown. Some scholars think the text once had Zerubbabel's name and was later changed, but no manuscript supports that. The text crowns Joshua on purpose. Crowning Zerubbabel might have looked like rebellion against Persia, but the deeper reason is in v12: the crown on a priest's head points to someone greater than either man. Joshua does not become king. He acts as a living sign.
- **"Behold, the man whose name is the Branch" (v12).** In 3:8 Joshua and his fellow priests were called "a sign" pointing to "my servant the Branch." Now the title appears again. The Hebrew *tsemach* means a sprout or shoot, new growth from the ground. Jeremiah used it for the coming son of David: "I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he shall reign as king" (Jer 23:5; 33:15; compare Isa 4:2; 11:1). David's royal line had been cut down like a tree in the exile. God promises a shoot from the stump.
- **"He shall branch out from his place" (v12).** A play on words: the Branch (*tsemach*) will branch out (*yitsmach*). He will grow from where he is, out of humble ground, not imported from outside. Isaiah described the Servant as growing up "like a young plant, and like a root out of dry ground" (Isa 53:2).
- **"He shall build the temple of the LORD" (vv12–13).** It is said twice for emphasis. In one sense Zerubbabel was building it right then (4:9). But the repetition and the emphatic "It is he who" point beyond the stone temple of 515 BC to a greater temple the Branch himself would build. David's son was always meant to build God's house (2 Sam 7:13).
- **"Shall bear royal honor, and shall sit and rule on his throne" (v13).** The word for honor, *hod*, means majesty or splendor, often used of God himself (Ps 104:1). This is full kingship.
- **"And there shall be a priest on his throne" (v13).** The Hebrew can also be read "by his throne," and the Greek translation has "a priest at his right hand." Readers divide here. One view sees two people, a king and a priest side by side, ruling in harmony. The other, held by most Christian interpreters and suggested by the crowning of a single man, sees one person who is both. The crown is placed on one head, and the "man whose name is the Branch" is the subject throughout. Psalm 110 had already joined the two: the king at God's right hand is "a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" (Ps 110:1, 4), the king-priest of Salem (Gen 14:18).
- **"The counsel of peace shall be between them both" (v13).** "Them both" could be the two persons (king and priest), or the two offices. Either way there is no rivalry, only *shalom*, wholeness and harmony. In Israel's history kings and priests often clashed. In the Branch, ruling and interceding work as one.
- **"The crown shall be in the temple of the LORD as a reminder" (v14).** After the ceremony the crown was not worn. It was stored in the temple as a memorial, honoring the donors and keeping the promise visible. Every time a priest saw it, it said: the King-Priest is still coming.
- **"Those who are far off shall come and help to build the temple of the LORD" (v15).** Immediately the three men from Babylon were proof: Jews from far away were giving to the work. But the phrase reaches further. Isaiah used "far off" for the nations (Isa 57:19; 60:9–10), and 2:11 already promised that "many nations shall join themselves to the LORD."
- **"If you will diligently obey the voice of the LORD your God" (v15).** The words echo Deuteronomy 28:1 almost exactly. The sentence breaks off, as if the prophet stops mid-thought, and the condition hangs in the air. God's promise of the Branch is sure. Whether *this* generation would share in its blessings depended on their obedience. Chapter 7 begins the call to that obedience.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Joshua "a type of Christ the high priest of our profession" and says the honors once divided between the houses of Aaron and David meet in Christ alone. He adds a pastoral note: because the king on the throne is also a priest, his people need not dread his rule. JFB sums up the two offices in one line: "As Priest He expiates sin; as King, extirpates it," and reads the counsel of peace as the harmonizing of mercy and justice in one person. Guzik also takes the crowned priest as a picture of Jesus and points out that the Hebrew of v12 opens "Behold, the man," the same words Pilate used of Jesus in his crown of thorns (John 19:5). He reads the temple the Branch builds as God's people (Eph 2:19–22; 1 Pet 2:5) and identifies Hen with Josiah. The Tyndale notes suggest the crown was two bands, one silver and one gold, standing for the two offices. That is an attractive guess the text does not confirm.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is one of the clearest Messianic pictures in the prophets. A priest named Joshua, *Yeshua*, the same name as Jesus, is crowned as a sign of the Branch who will build God's temple and sit as priest on his throne. Jesus grew up as a shoot from dry ground in Nazareth. He said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," speaking of his body (John 2:19–21), and now he is building a living temple out of Jews and Gentiles, the "far off" brought near by his blood (Eph 2:13–22). Pilate presented him in a crown of thorns with the words "Behold the man!" (John 19:5). After he offered himself for sins, "he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" (Heb 1:3). He is the king who rules and the priest who intercedes, "a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" (Heb 7:17), and in him justice and mercy are at peace.
