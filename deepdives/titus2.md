*Setting:* Paul writes to Titus, a Gentile co-worker he had once taken to Jerusalem as a test case for whether Gentile believers must be circumcised (Gal 2:1–3). The letter most likely comes from the years between Paul's first and second Roman imprisonments, around AD 62–64, when he and Titus had worked on Crete and Paul left Titus behind to finish organizing the young churches (1:5). Chapter 1 set out what kind of men should lead them and warned against teachers who "profess to know God, but… deny him by their works" (1:16), people who were "upsetting whole families" (1:11). Chapter 2 answers that by turning to the households themselves. It tells each group in the church how to live, then explains why in one of the richest summaries of the gospel in Paul's letters.

#### Teach Sound Doctrine (vv1–15)
- **One heading, three movements.** The ESV puts the whole chapter under one heading, and it hangs together tightly. Verses 1–10 tell older men, older women, younger women, younger men, Titus himself and slaves how to live. Verses 11–14 begin with "For" and give the reason: the grace of God that has appeared in Christ. Verse 15 sends Titus out to teach it. The chapter opens and closes with the same Greek verb, *laleō*, "speak" ("teach" in v1, "declare" in v15), so "these things" in v15 means everything in between.
- **"But as for you, teach what accords with sound doctrine" (v1).** "But as for you" sets Titus against the false teachers of 1:10–16. "Sound" is *hygiainō*, "to be healthy," the word Luke uses for the servant restored to health (Luke 7:10). English gets "hygiene" from the same root. In the letters to Timothy and Titus, healthy teaching is set against teaching that "will spread like gangrene" (2 Tim 2:17). Notice what Paul counts as sound doctrine here: not a list of beliefs but a way of life that grows from them.
- **A word that runs through the chapter.** The *sōphrōn* family of words appears five times (vv2, 4, 5, 6, 12): "self-controlled," "train," "sensibly." The root idea is a sound, sensible mind, one that keeps desires and impulses in check. Greek moralists prized it as one of the great virtues. Paul asks it of every group, and in v12 he says where it really comes from.
- **Household codes.** Greek and Roman writers from Aristotle onward wrote about "household management," usually pairing husbands and wives, fathers and children, masters and slaves. The New Testament uses the same form (Eph 5:21–6:9; Col 3:18–4:1; 1 Pet 2:18–3:7), but changes it. Here people are grouped first by age and sex, the less powerful are spoken to directly as people who make their own moral choices, and the motive throughout is the reputation of the gospel. Three purpose clauses carry that motive: "that the word of God may not be reviled" (v5), "so that an opponent may be put to shame" (v8), "so that in everything they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior" (v10).
- **Older men (v2).** "Sober-minded" first meant not drunk and came to mean clear-headed. "Dignified" means worthy of respect. Then comes the familiar triad of faith and love, but with "steadfastness" (*hypomonē*) where Paul usually puts hope (1 Cor 13:13; 1 Thess 1:3). Endurance is the form hope takes in someone who has walked a long road. Age does not produce these things by itself. Older men are to be "sound" in them, healthy, not just long-standing.
- **Older women (v3).** "Reverent in behavior" uses *hieroprepēs*, found only here in the New Testament, meaning fitting for something sacred. Their everyday conduct should suit people who belong to God. "Slanderers" is *diabolos*, the word used of the devil, the accuser. "Slaves to much wine" uses the language of slavery a few verses before slaves are addressed; drinking was a known problem on Crete. "Teach what is good" is a single word, *kalodidaskalos*, not found in Greek before the New Testament. The older women are teachers.
- **"And so train the young women" (vv4–5).** "Train" is *sōphronizō*, found only here: to bring someone to a sound mind, to teach good sense. Paul does not ask young Titus to do this himself. Wise older women are better placed, and it protects everyone. The list begins with love for husbands and children, which is something that can be learned. "Working at home" follows the oldest manuscripts (*oikourgos*). Later manuscripts, followed by the KJV, have a near-twin word meaning "keepers at home." The point in either form is diligence in the household, in a world where the household was also the main place of work. Readers differ on how far the verse applies to paid work outside the home today; the text itself is about faithfulness, not a rule about employment. "Submissive to their own husbands" echoes Ephesians 5:22, where it sits alongside the husband's call to self-giving love (Eph 5:25).
- **"That the word of God may not be reviled" (v5).** Christian teaching on freedom and equality in Christ (Gal 3:28) could look, to outsiders, like a threat to the household and so to society. A church whose homes fell apart would bring the message itself into disgrace. Paul echoes Isaiah 52:5, quoted in Romans 2:24: "The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you."
- **Younger men (v6).** Only one instruction: "be self-controlled," the same *sōphrōn* root again. For young men, Paul seems to think this one virtue covers a great deal.
- **"Show yourself… a model of good works" (vv7–8).** Titus was probably a young man himself. "Model" is *typos*, the mark left by a blow, then a pattern to copy (1 Tim 4:12; 1 Pet 5:3). His teaching must show "integrity," which the oldest manuscripts read as incorruptness, uncorrupted by motives such as money (1:11), and "sound speech that cannot be condemned." Later manuscripts add "sincerity" in v7, which the ESV leaves out. The aim is that an opponent will be "put to shame, having nothing evil to say about us." Peter gives the same strategy: silence critics by doing good (1 Pet 2:12, 15).
- **Bondservants (vv9–10).** Slaves may have made up something like a sixth or more of the Roman empire's population. Many worked in households as stewards, accountants and tutors and handled their masters' money, which is why "pilfering" was the standard accusation. The verb *nosphizomai* is the one Luke uses for Ananias keeping back part of the price (Acts 5:2). Roman slavery was not based on race and many slaves were later freed, but it was still slavery, often brutal. Paul does not tell slaves to rebel, and he does not approve of the system either. Elsewhere he tells slaves to gain freedom if they can (1 Cor 7:21) and asks Philemon to receive a slave back "as a beloved brother" (Philem 16). Here he speaks to the slave's own dignity: even a person with no social standing can make the gospel attractive.
- **"Adorn the doctrine of God our Savior" (v10).** "Adorn" is *kosmeō*, to put in order, arrange, decorate. It is used of setting a house in order and of a bride dressed for her husband (Rev 21:2). The doctrine is already true. A faithful life makes it beautiful to people who watch. The people the world valued least are given the highest calling in the paragraph.
- **"God our Savior" and then Christ our Savior (vv10, 13).** Titus calls God "Savior" three times (1:3; 2:10; 3:4) and Christ "Savior" three times (1:4; 2:13; 3:6), alternating deliberately. "Savior" (*sōtēr*) was also a common title for kings and emperors in this period. Paul gives it to God and to Christ.
- **"For the grace of God has appeared" (v11).** This "for" is the hinge of the chapter. Paul's ethics always grow out of the gospel. "Appeared" is *epiphainō*, used in the Greek Old Testament for giving light and in Luke 1:79 of the dawn that will "give light to those who sit in darkness." Grace has dawned, in the coming of Jesus. Grace here is almost a person, God's favor made visible in Christ.
- **"Bringing salvation for all people" (v11).** "All people" is the point after a list of every kind of person: old and young, men and women, slave and free, Jew and Cretan. It does not mean every individual will be saved. It means the gospel is for every kind of person, as Paul says when he urges prayer for all (1 Tim 2:1–6).
- **"Training us" (v12).** The verb *paideuō* comes from *pais*, "child," and means to bring up and educate a child. In some places it includes correction (Heb 12:6–10), but the lexicon files this verse under training and instruction. Grace does not only pardon. It teaches. Saved people go to school under grace for the rest of their lives.
- **"To renounce… and to live" (v12).** First a "no": to ungodliness, which ignores God, and to "worldly passions," desires shaped by a world that has turned from him. Then a "yes": to live "self-controlled, upright, and godly," that is, rightly toward self, toward others and toward God. Greek moral writers often listed these virtues together. In Crete, a place known for "liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" (1:12), Paul says the virtues the philosophers admired are what grace actually produces. And it happens "in the present age," not later.
- **"Waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory" (v13).** The Christian life sits between two appearings: grace appeared (v11) in Christ's first coming, and glory will appear (v13) at his return. "Appearing" is *epiphaneia*, the word Greeks used when a god showed himself or a ruler made a state visit. "Our blessed hope" and "the appearing" are probably one thing: the hope *is* his appearing (2 Tim 4:8; 1 John 3:2–3).
- **"Our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (v13).** This is one of the clearest places in the New Testament where Jesus is called God (with John 1:1; 20:28; Rom 9:5; Heb 1:8; 2 Pet 1:1). In Greek a single article governs both "great God" and "Savior," which normally means one person. Peter uses the identical construction in "our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ" (2 Pet 1:11), where no one doubts it is one person. The KJV's "the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ" allowed a two-person reading. Most modern translations read it as the ESV does. In a world where emperors were hailed as god and savior, the claim was pointed: those titles belong to Jesus.
- **"Who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness" (v14).** "Gave himself" is Jesus' own language: he came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45; 1 Tim 2:6). "Redeem" (*lytroō*) means to set free by paying a ransom. The phrase echoes Psalm 130:8: "he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities." Christ frees his people not only from the penalty of lawlessness but from lawlessness itself.
- **"A people for his own possession" (v14).** "Purify for himself a people" echoes God's promise in Ezekiel 37:23: "I will… cleanse them; and they shall be my people." "His own possession" is *periousios*, the Greek Old Testament's word for Israel as God's treasured possession at Sinai (Exod 19:5; Deut 7:6; 14:2). The Hebrew behind it, *segullah*, is used for a king's private treasure (1 Chr 29:3). The KJV's "a peculiar people" meant "his own," not "odd." Peter applies the same Sinai language to the church (1 Pet 2:9). This mixed church of Cretans and Jews is now God's treasured people.
- **"Zealous for good works" (v14).** "Zealous" is *zēlōtēs*, the word that also gave the Zealots their name. The chapter's whole point lands here. Good works do not earn salvation (3:5), but salvation produces people eager to do good (Eph 2:8–10). That answers the false teachers, who were "unfit for any good work" (1:16).
- **"Exhort and rebuke with all authority. Let no one disregard you" (v15).** "Authority" (*epitagē*) is a word for command, used in 1:3 of the "command of God." Titus's authority is the authority of the message, not of his age or personality. "Disregard" means to look past or think little of someone. Paul tells Timothy something similar (1 Tim 4:12), and the warning reaches the Cretans too, who hear the letter read aloud.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives "to every one his portion," and on older men writes that "those who are full of years should be full of grace and goodness." On Titus as a model: "good doctrine and good life must go together." On v13 Henry, writing about seventy years before Granville Sharp published his rule on the Greek article, argues that "they are not two subjects, but one only," and JFB makes the same point from "but one Greek article," adding that Paul never uses "appearing" of the Father. Guzik does not discuss the grammar but argues that grace, rightly understood, produces obedience rather than carelessness. On v14 all of them agree that the cross aims at a changed life: Henry's "Christ died to purify as well as to pardon," JFB's redemption from "the being of all iniquity," not only its penalty, and Guzik's point that redemption buys people for God's service. JFB and Guzik both stress correction and discipline in "training" (v12), which is a fair overtone, though the lexicon puts the weight on teaching. Guzik also repeats a claim that *periousios* meant the king's share of battle spoils, which the lexicon does not support; it means "one's own special possession." JFB notes the oldest manuscripts read "workers at home" in v5.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter's ethics are ordinary: sober old men, kind young wives, honest slaves, a young pastor who keeps his speech clean. What makes them extraordinary is v11–14. The grace that trains these people is not an idea but the appearing of Jesus, "our great God and Savior," who "gave himself for us." He is both the reason they live this way and the one they are waiting for. Every faithful life in an ordinary household becomes a small picture of him, an adornment on the doctrine of God our Savior.
