*Setting:* Still at Mount Sinai, apparently on the same eighth day as chapter 9, or very soon after. Aaron has offered his first sacrifices, the glory of the LORD has appeared, and fire from God has consumed the offerings while the people shouted for joy (9:23–24). Leviticus 10 is only the second narrative in the book, and it turns that joy into horror. Aaron's two oldest sons, Nadab and Abihu, had been among the privileged few who went up Sinai with Moses and the seventy elders and "beheld God, and ate and drank" (Exod 24:1, 9–11). Now they die at the altar on the day their priesthood begins.

#### The Death of Nadab and Abihu (vv1–7)
- **"Each took his censer and put fire in it and laid incense on it" (v1).** A censer was a fire pan for carrying live coals, with incense sprinkled on top (16:12–13). Burning incense was one of the most sacred priestly duties, done in the Holy Place at the golden altar (Exod 30:7–8).
- **"Unauthorized fire… which he had not commanded them" (v1).** The Hebrew is *'esh zarah*, literally "strange fire." *Zar* means foreign or outside, used of a stranger to a family, a foreigner to the land, or a "foreign god" (Ps 44:20). BDB puts this verse with Exodus 30:9's "unauthorized incense" under the sense "strange to the law." The text names the fault only as doing what God "had not commanded." What exactly was wrong is not spelled out, and readers have suggested several possibilities: coals taken from somewhere other than the altar God had just lit (9:24; 16:12), incense not mixed by the prescribed recipe (Exod 30:34–38), the wrong time, or entering a place only the high priest could go. The common thread is that they came to God in their own way, not his.
- **"Fire came out from before the LORD and consumed them" (v2).** The wording deliberately repeats 9:24. The same fire that a moment ago showed God's acceptance now shows his judgment. "Consumed" does not mean their bodies were burned to ash, since they were later carried out "in their coats" (v5). They were struck dead.
- **Why so severe?** First moments set patterns. When the church began, Ananias and Sapphira died for lying to the Holy Spirit (Acts 5:1–11). When the ark came to Jerusalem, Uzzah died for touching it (2 Sam 6:6–7). In each case God was showing at the start that his presence is real and holy, and not to be handled casually.
- **"Among those who are near me I will be sanctified, and before all the people I will be glorified" (v3).** Moses gives the reason in God's own words. "Sanctified" means treated as holy, set apart from everything ordinary. Being "near" God is a privilege, but it raises the stakes rather than lowering them (Luke 12:48).
- **"And Aaron held his peace" (v3).** The verb is *damam*, "to be silent, still." BDB also lists it for the sun standing still (Josh 10:12–13) and for being struck dumb in amazement (Exod 15:16). Aaron had argued and excused himself at the golden calf (Exod 32:22–24). Here he does not argue. Whether his silence was submission, shock or both, he does not accuse God.
- **Mishael and Elzaphan (vv4–5).** These are Aaron's cousins, sons of his father's brother Uzziel (Exod 6:18, 22). They were Levites but not priests. Aaron and his surviving sons could not touch the dead without becoming unclean (21:1–12), so the cousins carry the bodies out **"in their coats,"** the priestly tunics the young men had just been dressed in (8:13).
- **"Do not let the hair of your heads hang loose, and do not tear your clothes" (v6).** Loose hair and torn clothes were the normal signs of grief (Gen 37:34; 2 Sam 1:11). The priests on duty, still anointed, could not show public mourning, or they would seem to protest God's judgment. The high priest was never allowed these signs (21:10). **"Let your brothers, the whole house of Israel, bewail"** — the nation was free to grieve. The sorrow was real; it simply belonged to others to express it.
- **"Do not go outside the entrance of the tent of meeting… for the anointing oil of the LORD is upon you" (v7).** They are still within their week of ordination, or have just ended it, and their consecration takes priority even over a funeral. **"They did according to the word of Moses."** After the disobedience of two sons comes the obedience of the rest of the family.
- **Commentators:** All three see the sin as worship done on human terms. Henry calls it presumption, not ignorance, offered "in a light careless way, without due reverence and seriousness," and says "presumptuous sinners bring upon themselves a swift destruction." JFB thinks the sons intruded on the incense service and perhaps into the Most Holy Place, "a presumptuous and unwarranted intrusion into a sacred office which did not belong to them," and reads "consumed" as "killed," since their bodies and robes remained. Guzik notes that their spectacular experience on Sinai did not keep them faithful, and draws the lesson that we may come to God just as we are but only on his terms. On Aaron's silence, JFB says he bowed to "the righteous judgment of God." The Tyndale notes add that the priests' mourning might have looked like disagreement with God's justice.

#### Instructions for Priests (vv8–20)
- **"And the LORD spoke to Aaron" (v8).** This is the only time in Leviticus that God speaks to Aaron alone. Until now God's words have come through Moses. On the darkest day of his life, Aaron is addressed directly as the high priest.
- **"Drink no wine or strong drink… when you go into the tent of meeting" (v9).** "Strong drink" (*shekar*) was beer or other fermented drink. The rule comes right after the deaths, and many readers, the Tyndale notes among them, think Nadab and Abihu may have been drinking. The text never says so, and JFB calls it "nothing more than conjecture." Either way, the rule stands: a priest on duty must be sober. Ezekiel repeats it for the priests of the future temple (Ezek 44:21), and Paul applies the principle to church leaders (1 Tim 3:3; Titus 1:7).
- **"Distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (v10).** This verse is a key to the rest of Leviticus. "Common" means ordinary, not yet set apart. "Clean" and "unclean" describe whether a person or thing could come near the sanctuary. Chapters 11–15 deal with clean and unclean; chapters 17–27 deal more with holiness. A priest had to see the lines clearly, and clouded judgment was dangerous.
- **"You are to teach the people of Israel all the statutes" (v11).** Priests were not only sacrifice-handlers. They were Israel's teachers (Deut 33:10; Mal 2:7). Malachi later rebukes priests who failed in this: "the lips of a priest should guard knowledge."
- **Eating the holy portions (vv12–15).** Moses reminds the grieving priests to eat their share of the grain offering **"unleavened beside the altar, for it is most holy"** (v12; see 6:14–18). The breast and thigh of the peace offerings were less restricted: they could be eaten **"in a clean place"** by the priest's whole family, **"your sons and your daughters"** (v14; 7:31–34). The priesthood's support came from these God-given shares, "a due forever."
- **"Moses diligently inquired about the goat of the sin offering, and behold, it was burned up!" (v16).** The Hebrew repeats the verb, "seeking, he sought," to show how carefully Moses searched. The goat was the people's sin offering from 9:15. By the rule of 6:26–30, when the blood was not brought into the Holy Place, the priests were to eat the meat in a holy place. Burning it was the rule only for offerings whose blood went inside. After what had happened that morning, Moses fears another breach.
- **"That you may bear the iniquity of the congregation, to make atonement for them" (v17).** This is a striking line. Eating the sin offering was not only the priests' payment; it was part of the atoning work itself. In some way the priests carried the people's guilt by taking the offering into themselves.
- **"Such things as these have happened to me! If I had eaten the sin offering today, would the LORD have approved?" (v19).** Aaron answers for his sons. His meaning is debated. He may mean that a family under such judgment and grief was in no fit state to eat a most holy meal, or that eating would seem like celebrating on the day his sons died. The Tyndale notes also suggest he may have felt that eating would not have mended anything after God's judgment had fallen on his house.
- **"And when Moses heard that, he approved" (v20).** Literally, "it was good in his eyes." Moses, who has insisted on exact obedience all through the chapter, accepts a departure from the letter when it comes from reverence rather than carelessness. That is the key difference between Aaron here and his sons in v1: Nadab and Abihu did something God had not commanded because they took him lightly, and Aaron left something undone because he took him seriously. Jesus' point about David eating the holy bread runs in the same direction (Matt 12:3–7).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees v10 as the reason for the alcohol rule: priests needed clear minds to tell holy from common. He also reflects that it is easier to burn a sin offering than to eat it, that is, to condemn sin from a distance than to bear it with sinners, and sees Christ as the one who fully bore it. JFB thinks Moses repeated the rules about the priests' portions because grief might make them forget. JFB is blunter than most on vv16–20: "Aaron was decidedly in the wrong," but Moses "sympathized with his deeply afflicted brother" and "said no more." Most readers, including the Tyndale notes, judge Aaron's answer more kindly, since the text says Moses was satisfied.

#### Pointing to Christ
On one day Israel saw both sides of God's fire: the fire that accepts a sacrifice and the fire that consumes those who come near on their own terms. Hebrews says, "let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire" (Heb 12:28–29). The difference between the two fires is the sacrifice God provides. Nadab and Abihu died because they came without it. Jesus is the priest who never offered strange fire, who drew near on God's terms perfectly, and who bore the iniquity of the congregation not by eating an offering but by becoming one. Because of him, believers may "draw near with confidence to the throne of grace" (Heb 4:16), with boldness, but never with carelessness.
