*Setting:* "In the days when the judges ruled" (v1). The book of Ruth is set in the same troubled era as the book of Judges, probably around 1100 BC, three generations before David. Its tone could hardly be more different. Judges ended with Israel's tribes at war and a cry for a king (Judg 21:25). Ruth is a quiet family story about a widow, a foreign daughter-in-law and a farmer in Bethlehem, and it ends with the birth of David's grandfather. In English Bibles it follows Judges, as in the Greek Old Testament. In the Hebrew Bible it sits among the Writings, one of five short scrolls read at Jewish festivals, and it is read at the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost), the wheat-harvest festival. The author is not named. Jewish tradition credits Samuel, but the closing genealogy reaching David (4:17–22) suggests it was written at least as late as David's reign. The book is a masterpiece of storytelling, and nearly every name and repeated word in it matters.

#### Naomi Widowed (vv1–5)
- **"There was a famine in the land" (v1).** Famine in Canaan was usually caused by drought, and the hill country around Bethlehem depended on rain. In Deuteronomy, drought and famine are among the covenant curses for unfaithfulness (Deut 28:23–24), and Judges shows a nation deep in unfaithfulness. The text does not say this famine was a judgment, but readers who have just finished Judges will wonder.
- **"A man of Bethlehem in Judah" (v1).** Bethlehem means "house of bread." The irony is plain: the man leaves the "house of bread" because there is no bread. Bethlehem was a small town about six miles south of Jerusalem. It has already appeared in the dark stories at the end of Judges (Judg 17:7; 19:1). Ruth turns Bethlehem from a place of sad stories into the hometown of David.
- **"To sojourn in the country of Moab" (v1).** To "sojourn" means to live somewhere as a resident foreigner, meaning to stay only for a while. Moab lay east of the Dead Sea, on a high plateau that often received rain when Judah did not, about fifty miles from Bethlehem by road around the north end of the sea. Moab's history with Israel was bitter. The Moabites descended from Lot's incestuous union with his daughter (Gen 19:30–37). They had hired Balaam to curse Israel, their women had led Israel into idolatry at Peor (Num 22–25), and only a few generations earlier King Eglon of Moab had ruled Israel for eighteen years (Judg 3:12–14).
- **The names (v2).** Elimelech means "My God is king." In a book set in the days when "there was no king in Israel," his name says who the real King is, though his move to Moab may suggest he did not trust that King to provide. Naomi means "pleasant" or "my delight," which she will soon reject (v20). Mahlon and Chilion are harder. They sound like words for "sickness" and "failing" or "destruction," and many think they were chosen or remembered for that sound, though the meanings are uncertain. "Ephrathites" means they belonged to Ephrathah, the old clan name of Bethlehem's area (Gen 35:19; Mic 5:2).
- **"They… remained there" (v2).** Literally "they were there." A temporary stay becomes settled life.
- **"These took Moabite wives" (v4).** Mahlon married Ruth and Chilion married Orpah (4:10). The law did not explicitly forbid marriage to Moabites, as it did with the Canaanite nations (Deut 7:1–3), but it excluded Moabites from "the assembly of the LORD" to the tenth generation (Deut 23:3), and later Israel read these laws together (Neh 13:1, 23–27). Interpreters disagree how far to fault these marriages. Many older writers, JFB and Henry among them, see them as a breach of the law. Others note that the book itself never condemns them, and that God brings great good out of one of them.
- **"About ten years… the woman was left" (vv4–5).** Ten years of marriage and no children. Then both sons die. The phrase "the woman was left" strips Naomi down to nothing: no husband, no sons, no grandsons. In that world a widow without sons had no one to work the land or protect her and no one to carry on the family name. The ESV calls her simply "the woman," as if even her name has been lost.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks Elimelech meant well but that "his removal into the country of Moab, upon this occasion," could not be justified, and draws from the deaths a sober lesson: "wherever we go we cannot out-run death." JFB calls the Moabite marriages "a violation of the Mosaic law" and notes that Jewish writers took the sons' early deaths as judgments. Guzik likewise reads the move as a step away from God and stresses the irony that Elimelech, whose name means "God is king," did not live as though God were king. The Tyndale notes are more restrained, describing Naomi's emptiness more than assigning blame.

#### Ruth's Loyalty to Naomi (vv6–18)
- **"The LORD had visited his people and given them food" (v6).** "Visited" means God came near in care and action (compare Gen 50:24; Exod 4:31). This is one of only two places where the narrator says directly that the LORD did something. The other is 4:13: "the LORD gave her conception." Food at the start, a child at the end, both described with the same verb "gave." The whole book happens between these two acts of God, and everything in between is ordinary people making choices while God works quietly through them.
- **"Return" (vv6–7).** The Hebrew verb *shuv*, "return" or "turn back," appears twelve times in this chapter. It is also the Old Testament's main word for repentance. Naomi "returns" to Judah, and she keeps urging her daughters-in-law to "return" to Moab. The great irony is that Ruth, who has never been to Bethlehem, is said to "return" there with her (v22).
- **"Go, return each of you to her mother's house" (v8).** Naomi sends them back to their mothers' homes, where marriages might be arranged for them.
- **"May the LORD deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me" (v8).** "Kindly" is *hesed*, one of the great words of the Old Testament. It means loyal, faithful love, love that goes beyond what duty requires. God shows *hesed* to his covenant people (Exod 34:6–7). Here a Moabite is praised for showing it. The word will return at key moments (2:20; 3:10).
- **"That you may find rest, each of you in the house of her husband" (v9).** "Rest" is security: a home, a husband, a settled life. Naomi wants that for them and believes they cannot have it with her. In 3:1 she will set out to find exactly this rest for Ruth.
- **"Have I yet sons in my womb…?" (vv11–13).** Naomi refers to the custom that later appears in law as levirate marriage (Deut 25:5–10): when a man died childless, his brother married the widow, and their first son carried on the dead man's name. Naomi's point is that she has no more sons and will never have any, so staying with her means no husband for them. Her reasoning is painfully practical, almost sarcastic.
- **"The hand of the LORD has gone out against me" (v13).** Naomi never doubts that God is in control. That is exactly what makes her grief so sharp. She believes God has acted, and acted against her.
- **"Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her" (v14).** Orpah's kiss is a goodbye, and she is not condemned for it. She does what Naomi asked. But "clung" is *dabaq*, the word for a man holding fast to his wife (Gen 2:24) and for Israel holding fast to the LORD (Deut 10:20; Josh 23:8). Ruth's choice is a covenant commitment.
- **"Gone back to her people and to her gods" (v15).** Moab's chief god was Chemosh (Num 21:29; 1 Kings 11:7). Naomi names what Ruth would be going back to. Returning to Moab meant returning to its gods.
- **"Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (v16).** This is the heart of the chapter. Ruth commits to Naomi personally, to Naomi's people, and to Naomi's God. Her four pledges climb: where you go, where you lodge, your people, your God. Then she goes further: "Where you die I will die, and there will I be buried" (v17). Even in death she will not go home to Moab.
- **"May the LORD do so to me and more also" (v17).** This is a standard Israelite oath formula, used by Eli, Saul and David (1 Sam 3:17; 14:44; 2 Sam 3:9). What is striking is that Ruth swears by "the LORD," Israel's covenant name for God, *Yahweh*, not by Chemosh. A Moabite woman calls on Israel's God by name.
- **"She said no more" (v18).** Naomi stops arguing, but she does not say anything warm either. Literally she "ceased to speak to her." Nor will she mention Ruth when they arrive in Bethlehem.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads Orpah's kiss as real affection without commitment, a picture of those who love Christ but will not leave other things for him: "Orpah's kiss showed she had an affection for Naomi and was loth to part from her." JFB thinks Naomi's urging was "the wisest and most prudent course," a fair test of their resolve, and notes that the brother-in-law custom was "afterwards expressly sanctioned by the law of Moses." Guzik reads Ruth's words as a Gentile's real conversion to the God of Israel, drawn through Naomi even at Naomi's lowest point, and stresses that Ruth acted while Orpah only felt. The Tyndale notes point out that "clung" is the marriage and covenant word.

#### Naomi and Ruth Return (vv19–22)
- **"The whole town was stirred" (v19).** The word means a buzz or commotion. The women ask, "Is this Naomi?" She has been gone more than ten years, and grief has changed her.
- **"Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (v20).** Mara means "bitter." It recalls the bitter waters at Marah, which the LORD made sweet (Exod 15:23–25). "The Almighty" is *Shaddai*, an old name for God from the time of the patriarchs (Gen 17:1), often linked with his power and his gift of blessing and children (Gen 49:25). Naomi uses it to say the all-powerful God has turned his power against her.
- **"I went away full, and the LORD has brought me back empty" (v21).** She left with a husband and two sons. She comes back without them. Her complaint is honest and, like the lament psalms, it is spoken to God and about God, not away from him. But it is not the whole truth. She did not leave "full," since she left in a famine, and she has not come back "empty," since Ruth is standing beside her. "The LORD has testified against me" is courtroom language: she feels God has been a witness for the prosecution.
- **"Ruth the Moabite her daughter-in-law… who returned from the country of Moab" (v22).** The narrator keeps calling her "Ruth the Moabite" (2:2, 21; 4:5, 10). Her foreignness is never forgotten. Yet the narrator says she "returned," as if Bethlehem were already her home.
- **"At the beginning of barley harvest" (v22).** Barley ripened first, around late March or April, followed by wheat several weeks later. The famine is over, the fields are full, and the last line of a bitter chapter quietly opens a door. Bread has come back to the house of bread, and chapter 2 will take place in those fields.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Naomi's request a humble acceptance of God's hand rather than a complaint against him, and he notes the timing: barley harvest, "the first of their harvests," was providential, giving them food for the year. Guzik likewise thinks Naomi is honest about her pain without turning against God, and he sees the barley harvest as the start of blessings she cannot yet see. JFB places the arrival around "the end of our March." The Tyndale notes observe that Naomi's complaint was real, but God was about to give her exactly what she needed.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ruth's story begins in a famine in Bethlehem and ends with a son born in Bethlehem, the grandfather of David. Centuries later another child is born in the "house of bread," and he says, "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35). Ruth, a foreigner from a cursed people, takes refuge under the God of Israel and is written into the family line of the Messiah (Matt 1:5). Her words, "Your people shall be my people, and your God my God," are the words of every outsider who has come home to God through Christ. And Naomi's bitter "empty" is not the last word. The God who seemed to be against her was already at work, as he is in every story told by people who think they have come home with nothing.
