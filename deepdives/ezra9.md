*Setting:* Ezra reached Jerusalem in the summer of 458 BC with about five thousand returning exiles, a fortune in temple gifts and a royal commission to teach God's law (chs 7–8). Some months later (compare 10:9, the ninth month), the community's leaders bring him a report he did not expect. Many of the people, including priests, Levites and leading men, have married women from the surrounding peoples who worship other gods. Eighty years after the first return, the small community that had once refused any religious partnership with its neighbors (4:1–3) was quietly dissolving into them. The prophet Malachi, who probably preached in this same period, raises the same complaint: "Judah… has married the daughter of a foreign god" (Mal 2:11). The chapter is almost entirely Ezra's own prayer, written in the first person.

#### Ezra Prays About Intermarriage (vv1–15)
- **"After these things had been done" (v1).** The treasure was delivered, the sacrifices offered and the king's orders handed to the officials (8:33–36). Only then does the deeper problem surface. Ezra's teaching may have prompted it: as people heard the Law, some leaders realized how far the community had drifted.
- **"Have not separated themselves from the peoples of the lands with their abominations" (v1).** The key word is *abominations*, the idolatry and detestable practices of these peoples (Deut 18:9–12). The issue is religion, not race. The Law welcomed outsiders who turned to the LORD. Rahab the Canaanite and Ruth the Moabite are both in the line of David and of Jesus (Matt 1:5). What the Law forbade was marriage that would pull Israel to other gods: "for they would turn away your sons from following me, to serve other gods" (Deut 7:4).
- **The list of peoples (v1).** It is drawn from the Law. Canaanites, Hittites, Perizzites, Jebusites and Amorites come from the list of nations in Deuteronomy 7:1. Ammonites and Moabites come from Deuteronomy 23:3, and Egyptians from Deuteronomy 23:7–8. Some of these peoples no longer existed as nations in Ezra's day. The point is that the people of the land were repeating the old sins those names stood for. Solomon's foreign wives "turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kgs 11:1–8), and the result was idolatry at the heart of the kingdom.
- **"The holy race has mixed itself" (v2).** Literally "the holy seed." Israel was set apart as "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6), not because they were better than others, but because God chose them to carry his promise to the world. Isaiah used the same phrase for the remnant that would survive judgment: "the holy seed is its stump" (Isa 6:13). The fear was that this seed, through which the promised Messiah would come, would be lost.
- **"In this faithlessness the hand of the officials and chief men has been foremost" (v2).** "Faithlessness" is *ma'al*, a breach of trust, especially against God, like a spouse being unfaithful. The leaders were not dragged along. They led the way. When the people meant to guard the community's faith set the example of compromise, the whole community followed.
- **"I tore my garment and my cloak and pulled hair from my head and beard" (v3).** Tearing clothes was a sign of grief and horror (Gen 37:34; 2 Kgs 22:11). Pulling out hair was an even stronger sign of anguish. Years later Nehemiah faced the same sin and pulled out the hair of the offenders (Neh 13:25). Ezra pulled out his own.
- **"And sat appalled" (v3).** The Hebrew word (*meshomem*) means stunned, devastated, desolate. It comes from the same root used of a land laid waste. Ezra sat speechless for hours.
- **"All who trembled at the words of the God of Israel" (v4).** Others gathered around him. These are people who took God's word seriously. Isaiah describes them: "this is the one to whom I will look: he who is humble and contrite in spirit and trembles at my word" (Isa 66:2). The same group appears again in 10:3. A movement of repentance began with a small circle of people who feared God.
- **"Until the evening sacrifice" (vv4–5).** The evening offering was made about 3 p.m. (compare Acts 3:1). It was a set hour for prayer (Dan 9:21). Ezra had sat in silence since the morning. He waited for the hour when sacrifice was offered for sin, and then he prayed.
- **"Fell upon my knees and spread out my hands" (v5).** Kneeling showed humility. Spreading out the hands, palms up, was the common Old Testament posture of prayer (1 Kgs 8:22, 54). It is the posture of someone with nothing to offer, asking to receive.
- **"I am ashamed and blush to lift my face to you" (v6).** Two words for shame: one for the inner sense of disgrace, the other for its outward sign, the humiliation that shows on the face. His hands are raised, but his face is down. Compare the tax collector who "would not even lift up his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast" (Luke 18:13).
- **"Our iniquities" (v6).** Ezra had not married a foreign wife. He had just arrived. Yet he says "our," never "their." He stands inside the covenant community and owns its sin as his own. Nehemiah (Neh 1:6–7) and Daniel (Dan 9:5–11) prayed the same way. "Higher than our heads… mounted up to the heavens" is the picture of a flood rising over a drowning man.
- **"From the days of our fathers to this day" (v7).** Ezra surveys Israel's history in one sentence: sin, and then kings and priests handed over "to the sword, to captivity, to plundering, and to utter shame." The exile was not bad luck. It was God's just judgment, as the prophets had warned. And "as it is today": the community still lives under foreign rule.
- **"For a brief moment favor has been shown" (v8).** The return from exile is a short season of grace, perhaps eighty years, a brief moment compared with Israel's long history of sin. Ezra fears that it is being wasted.
- **"A secure hold within his holy place" (v8).** The Hebrew is *yated*, a tent peg or a nail driven into a wall. Isaiah used it for Eliakim: "I will fasten him like a peg in a secure place" (Isa 22:23). The image is of something small but firmly fixed. The remnant had a tiny foothold, a single peg, in God's holy place, the rebuilt temple.
- **"That our God may brighten our eyes and grant us a little reviving in our slavery" (v8).** Bright eyes are a sign of renewed life and strength (1 Sam 14:27, 29; Ps 13:3). "Reviving" means a restoring of life. Note the modest words: *a little* reviving. "For we are slaves" (v9). They were home, but still subjects of Persia (compare Neh 9:36).
- **"Has extended to us his steadfast love before the kings of Persia" (v9).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's covenant faithfulness. He worked through Cyrus, Darius and Artaxerxes to rebuild the temple.
- **"To give us protection in Judea and Jerusalem" (v9).** The word is *gader*, a wall or fence, like the stone wall around a vineyard (Isa 5:5). Jerusalem's city walls were still broken down, and Nehemiah would rebuild them thirteen years later. Most scholars therefore take this as a picture of God's protection through the Persian kings, not the literal city wall. A few argue it shows Nehemiah came before Ezra, but the image does not require that.
- **"What shall we say after this?" (v10).** Ezra has no defense to offer. God had been kind, and the people had answered his kindness with disobedience. Compare Paul's question about those who would keep sinning that grace may abound: "What shall we say then?… By no means!" (Rom 6:1–2).
- **"Which you commanded by your servants the prophets" (v11).** Moses was the first and greatest of the prophets (Deut 18:15; 34:10). What follows is not one quotation but a summary of several passages. "A land impure with the impurity of the peoples" recalls Leviticus 18:24–28. "Do not give your daughters to their sons" is Deuteronomy 7:3. "Never seek their peace or prosperity" is Deuteronomy 23:6. "That you may be strong and eat the good of the land" echoes Deuteronomy 11:8 and Isaiah 1:19. Ezra the scribe prays in the words of Scripture.
- **"That you may… leave it for an inheritance to your children forever" (v12).** The Law tied faithfulness to the future of the next generation. Mixed marriages to idolaters endangered their children's faith. Nehemiah later found children of such marriages who could not even speak the language of Judah (Neh 13:24).
- **"You… have punished us less than our iniquities deserved" (v13).** Even the exile, with all its horror, was mercy. God could have destroyed the nation completely. Instead he preserved a remnant and brought it home. Ezra sees judgment and grace together, which is how the Psalms see it too: "He does not deal with us according to our sins" (Ps 103:10).
- **"Shall we break your commandments again?" (v14).** The word "again" carries the weight. Israel had done this before, and it led to exile. To do it again after being restored would be to despise God's mercy. Ezra fears a judgment that would leave "no remnant, nor any to escape."
- **"You are just" (v15).** Ezra does not accuse God of being harsh. He says God is right, *righteous*, in everything he has done, including leaving them a remnant. The just God is also the God who has kept a remnant alive.
- **"Behold, we are before you in our guilt, for none can stand before you because of this" (v15).** The prayer ends without a request. Ezra does not ask for forgiveness or plead God's reputation, as Moses did (Exod 32:11–13). He simply lays the guilt before God and waits. It is a confession so honest that it leaves everything to God's mercy. Psalm 130:3–4 answers it: "If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand? But with you there is forgiveness."
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Henry both stress that the problem was idolatry, not ethnicity. Guzik notes that the peoples are named for their "abominations." Henry writes that "disbelief of God's all-sufficiency" lies behind such compromises, and that "those who are best acquainted with the word of God" will take them seriously. JFB points out how hard the problem was to fix "when matrimonial alliances had been formed, families had been reared, affections engaged." Guzik draws attention to Ezra's "our iniquities" and to the contrast with Nehemiah, who pulled out the offenders' hair rather than his own. Henry sees in the evening sacrifice "a type of the blessed Lamb of God, who in the evening of the world, was to take away sin by the sacrifice of himself," and writes that "holy shame is as necessary in true repentance as holy sorrow." Guzik, following Kidner, notes the striking absence of any request at the end. The Tyndale notes read v9's "protection" as God's protection, not Nehemiah's later wall, as Guzik does.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezra stands in the gap for a guilty people. He has not committed their sin, yet he calls it "our iniquities" and carries it before God at the hour of sacrifice. In that he points to Jesus, who "had done no violence" yet "was numbered with the transgressors" and "bore the sin of many" (Isa 53:9, 12). Ezra could only confess and wait, because "none can stand before you." Jesus answers that line. He is the one who stands before God for his people, "always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25), and gives those who were once unclean a secure place, not just a tent peg, in God's house. The "holy seed" that Ezra feared would be lost was kept, and from it came the promised offspring of Abraham, "who is Christ" (Gal 3:16).
