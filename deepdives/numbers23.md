*Setting:* On the hills overlooking the plains of Moab, near the end of the forty years in the wilderness. Balak, king of Moab, has brought Balaam, the famous diviner from the Euphrates, some 400 miles to curse Israel (ch 22). On the road, God stopped Balaam with an angel and a talking donkey and told him to speak only what God gave him. Now the rituals begin. Balaam will speak seven oracles in all, four of them major (23:7–10; 23:18–24; 24:3–9; 24:15–19). This chapter holds the first two. Israel, camped below, has no idea any of this is going on. The chapter's pattern repeats like a comic routine: build seven altars, offer the sacrifices, go and wait for a word, come back, and bless the very people Balak paid to have cursed.

#### Balaam's First Oracle (vv1–12)
- **"Build for me here seven altars… seven bulls and seven rams" (v1).** Seven was a number of completeness across the ancient Near East. Fourteen large animals was a lavish offering. Balaam is going by the book, his book: a pagan ritual meant to win a god's favor before asking for a curse. **Balak and Balaam offered on each altar (v2).** A king of Moab offering burnt offerings, apparently to Israel's God, hoping to buy him.
- **Reading omens.** The Tyndale notes suggest these sacrifices may have been tied to *extispicy*, reading the future from the livers and organs of sacrificed animals, a highly developed art in Mesopotamia. Balaam later gives up "omens" (24:1), which fits. Israel's law banned all of it (Deut 18:9–14).
- **"Perhaps the LORD will come to meet me" (v3).** Balaam goes off alone **"to a bare height,"** a windswept hilltop, the kind of place diviners went to watch for signs.
- **"God met Balaam" (v4).** Balaam reports his seven altars to God, as if the ritual put God in his debt. God ignores the boast. **"The LORD put a word in Balaam's mouth" (v5).** The same phrase is used of true prophets (Deut 18:18; Jer 1:9). Balaam is a mouthpiece, not a willing one.
- **"From Aram Balak has brought me… from the eastern mountains" (v7).** Aram is upper Mesopotamia, Balaam's homeland, "Aram of the two rivers" (Gen 24:10; Deut 23:4). The word "discourse" (*mashal*) usually means a proverb or parable, a weighty, carefully shaped saying. The oracles are poetry, built on Hebrew parallelism, where the second line echoes and sharpens the first.
- **"How can I curse whom God has not cursed?" (v8).** The theme of the whole story in one line. Balak thought a curse was a tool that a skilled professional could aim at anyone. Balaam admits that his words have no power of their own. God decides who is blessed.
- **"From the top of the crags I see him" (v9).** Balak brought Balaam up high to see the people he was supposed to curse. What Balaam sees instead is what God sees.
- **"A people dwelling alone, and not counting itself among the nations" (v9).** Israel is set apart. Not isolated by choice or by geography, but by belonging to God (Exod 19:5–6; Deut 33:28). Their laws, their worship and their calling made them different from every nation around them. That difference was their protection, as long as they kept it. Chapter 25 will show what happened when they gave it up.
- **"Who can count the dust of Jacob?" (v10).** An echo of God's promise to Jacob: "your offspring shall be like the dust of the earth" (Gen 28:14; also 13:16). Balak called them a horde that covered the earth (22:5, 11), and in his fear he was right. What frightened him was God's promise come true. **"The fourth part of Israel"** may refer to the camp's four divisions, three tribes on each side of the tabernacle (ch 2): even one quarter of the camp was beyond counting. Some translators instead read a word meaning "dust cloud," but the ESV keeps the traditional reading.
- **"Let me die the death of the upright, and let my end be like his!" (v10).** "Upright" (*yashar*) is related to *Jeshurun*, a poetic name for Israel (Deut 32:15; 33:5). Balaam wants to die like a member of God's people. The story ends with bitter irony: he died by the sword among the Midianites he advised against Israel (31:8, 16). He wanted the death of the righteous without the life of the righteous.
- **"What have you done to me?… you have done nothing but bless them" (v11).** Balak paid for a curse and got a blessing. **"Must I not take care to speak what the LORD puts in my mouth?" (v12).**
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "How shall I curse those whom God has not cursed?", and says Balaam saw Israel as "a people dwelling alone, and foresaw they would continue so." On v10 Guzik observes that many want the reward of a righteous death while refusing a righteous life. JFB says the heathen "attached a mysterious virtue to the number *seven*," and thinks Balaam used the altars to "mystify and delude the king." It reads "the fourth part" as one of the camp's four divisions, and calls Israel "a distinct, peculiar people, separated from the rest of the nations."

#### Balaam's Second Oracle (vv13–30)
- **"Come with me to another place… You shall see only a fraction of them" (v13).** Balak thinks the problem is the view. If Balaam sees less of Israel, maybe he will be less impressed, and maybe the god will be more willing. Pagan religion often worked this way: a god tied to a place, a curse that worked better from the right angle.
- **"The field of Zophim, to the top of Pisgah" (v14).** *Zophim* means "watchers" or "lookouts," perhaps a post where sentinels or omen-watchers kept watch. Pisgah is the ridge from which Moses will later see the whole land before he dies (Deut 34:1). The same seven altars, the same sacrifices, the same result.
- **"What has the LORD spoken?" (v17).** Balak, a worshiper of Chemosh, has to ask what Israel's God said. Without meaning to, he is acknowledging who is really in charge.
- **"Rise, Balak, and hear" (v18).** Balaam speaks to the king directly now, almost as a rebuke.
- **"God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind" (v19).** This is the heart of the chapter. "Change his mind" is *nacham*, to relent or be sorry. People break their word because they change their minds, or because they cannot do what they promised. God does neither. Balak hoped a second try would get a different answer from God. Balaam tells him that God's blessing is not a mood. Scripture elsewhere says God "relented" (Exod 32:14; Jonah 3:10). Those passages describe God responding as he always said he would to repentance or intercession. His purposes and promises do not shift (1 Sam 15:29; Mal 3:6; Heb 6:17–18).
- **"Has he said, and will he not do it?" (v19).** Two rhetorical questions, both expecting "Of course he will." **"He has blessed, and I cannot revoke it" (v20).** The verb means to turn back or reverse. Balaam is honest about his limits: he is outranked.
- **"He has not beheld misfortune in Jacob, nor has he seen trouble in Israel" (v21).** The two Hebrew words (*aven* and *amal*) can mean either "trouble, misfortune" or "wickedness, mischief," and translations divide. The KJV reads "iniquity… perverseness," and that reading has been treasured as a picture of God seeing his people as forgiven. The lexicon gives "trouble" as the sense here, and in context Balaam is saying God sees no disaster ahead for Israel. Either way it is not a claim that Israel was sinless; the wilderness record says otherwise. It is a statement that God has not turned against them.
- **"The LORD their God is with them, and the shout of a king is among them" (v21).** The shout is the cheer a people raise for their king when he goes out with them to war or returns in victory (1 Sam 10:24). Israel's King is God himself, and he is in their camp. This is one of the first places in Scripture to call God Israel's king (compare Exod 15:18; Deut 33:5).
- **"Like the horns of the wild ox" (v22).** The *re'em* was the aurochs, the huge wild ox of the ancient Near East, now extinct, famous for its strength and untamable temper (Job 39:9–10). The Greek translators guessed "unicorn," which the KJV followed, and others later guessed "rhinoceros." The lexicon is clear: wild ox. Its horns are a picture of irresistible strength. Whether the strength is God's or Israel's, it is God who gives it.
- **"There is no enchantment against Jacob, no divination against Israel" (v23).** The diviner declares his own trade useless. The Hebrew can also be read "there is no enchantment *in* Jacob," meaning Israel does not use such arts, because God speaks to them directly. Both are true, and the ESV's "against" fits the story: Balak's whole plan was magic aimed at Israel, and it cannot work.
- **"What has God wrought!" (v23).** People will look at Israel and see God's work, not theirs. Samuel Morse chose these words for the first official telegraph message in 1844, a striking example of the line's afterlife.
- **"As a lioness it rises up and as a lion it lifts itself" (v24).** The oracle ends with Israel as a lion that will not lie down until it has eaten. It echoes Jacob's blessing on Judah, "a lion's cub… who dares rouse him?" (Gen 49:9), and Balaam will repeat it (24:9). For Balak, who had hoped to drive Israel off, it is the worst possible ending.
- **"Do not curse them at all, and do not bless them at all" (v25).** Balak gives up on the curse and just wants Balaam to stop. Balaam: **"All that the LORD says, that I must do" (v26).**
- **"Perhaps it will please God that you may curse them for me from there" (v27).** Balak refuses to learn. He tries a third place, **"the top of Peor, which overlooks the desert" (v28),** near the shrine of the Baal of Peor (25:3). The chapter ends with seven more altars and fourteen more animals (vv29–30), setting up the third oracle.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Men change their minds, and therefore break their words; they lie, because they repent." On v21 he writes, "Not but that there was iniquity in Jacob, and God saw it," taking the verse to mean God did not see it so as to destroy them. JFB agrees: many sins were punished, but no "universal and hopeless apostasy had as yet appeared." JFB thought the *re'em* was a rhinoceros, as many in its day did. The lexicon supports "wild ox," as the ESV has it. JFB adds that no magic could defeat a people "under the shield of Omnipotence," and that Zophim may mean "the field of sentinels." Guzik stresses that God's word does not change and that what God has blessed cannot be undone, and reads the "shout of a king" as the cry of victory around a present king. JFB has no notes on vv24–30.

#### Pointing to Christ
"God is not man, that he should lie." Balak tried location after location, ritual after ritual, hoping to change God's mind about his people, and could not. Every promise God made to Abraham, that his offspring would be blessed and be a blessing, finds its "Yes" in Christ (2 Cor 1:20). The King whose shout was in Israel's camp later came to live among his people in person, and the "lion of the tribe of Judah" (Rev 5:5) conquered not by devouring his enemies but by being slain for them. No enchantment can prevail against those whom God has blessed in him, and no accusation can reverse the verdict God has given: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Rom 8:1).
