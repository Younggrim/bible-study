*Setting:* Moses has been on Mount Sinai for forty days and nights (24:18). Only weeks earlier the people heard God speak the Ten Commandments from the mountain, including "You shall have no other gods before me" and "You shall not make for yourself a carved image" (20:3–4). They answered with one voice, "All the words that the LORD has spoken we will do" (24:3). For seven chapters God has been describing a tabernacle so that he could live among them. Now the camera swings from the mountaintop to the camp below, and what is happening there is the worst possible answer to everything God has just said. Deuteronomy 9:7–21, Psalm 106:19–23 and Stephen's speech in Acts 7:39–41 all look back on this day as the classic picture of Israel's unfaithfulness.

#### The Golden Calf (vv1–35)
- **"When the people saw that Moses delayed to come down" (v1).** The word "delayed" carries a sense of embarrassing slowness. Waiting is the test. The Tyndale notes point out that impatience with God's timing is behind other great failures too, such as Saul's sacrifice when Samuel was late (1 Sam 13:8–13).
- **"Up, make us gods who shall go before us" (v1).** God had already been going before them in the pillar of cloud and fire (13:21). The cloud was still on the mountain (24:15–18). They wanted a god they could see and handle, one that would go where they wanted. The word "gods" is *elohim*, which can mean God or gods. Here the verb with it is plural, "gods who shall go," so they are speaking like pagans.
- **"As for this Moses, the man who brought us up out of the land of Egypt" (v1).** They give the credit for the exodus to Moses, not to God, and speak of him with contempt: "this Moses." Once their mediator is out of sight, their faith collapses.
- **"Take off the rings of gold" (v2).** The gold came from Egypt. The Egyptians had given it to them as they left (12:35–36), just as God promised (3:21–22). God meant some of that same gold for the tabernacle (25:3). Instead, it went into an idol. Aaron may have hoped that asking for their jewelry would make them think twice. If so, it did not work. **"All the people took off the rings of gold"** (v3), eagerly.
- **"Fashioned it with a graving tool and made a golden calf" (v4).** Aaron shaped the gold with a tool, probably a wooden figure overlaid with gold, or a cast figure finished by hand. Hebrew *'egel* means "calf," a young bull. The bull was a symbol of strength and fertility across the ancient world. In Egypt the Apis bull was worshiped at Memphis. In Canaan, the god El was called "bull," and Baal was often pictured standing on a bull. The Tyndale notes suggest the writer may call it a "calf" with some scorn: they wanted a mighty bull and got a little calf.
- **"These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!" (v4).** This is almost a mirror of the opening words of the Ten Commandments: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (20:2). The very rescue God did is now credited to a statue made from Egypt's gold. Psalm 106:20 says, "They exchanged the glory of God for the image of an ox that eats grass," and Paul echoes this in Romans 1:23. Centuries later, King Jeroboam set up two golden calves and used the same words (1 Kgs 12:28), and that sin haunted the northern kingdom until its fall.
- **"Tomorrow shall be a feast to the LORD" (v5).** Aaron uses God's personal name, *YHWH*. He seems to have presented the calf as a picture of the LORD, or as a pedestal for him, rather than as a different god. Some scholars compare it to the cherubim on the ark, over which God was enthroned unseen. But God had forbidden any image of himself (20:4, 23). Whatever Aaron intended, the result was idolatry. Calling it "the LORD" did not change what it was. This is a breach of the second commandment as much as the first.
- **"They rose up early… offered burnt offerings… sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play" (v6).** They copied the covenant meal of 24:5, 11, but with a calf in God's place. "Play" is *tsachaq*, "laugh, sport." The same verb describes Isaac "laughing with" (caressing) Rebekah in Genesis 26:8, and many think it means sexual revelry here, as often went with fertility worship. Paul quotes this verse in warning the Corinthians: "The people sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play" (1 Cor 10:7), and goes straight on to sexual immorality (10:8).
- **"Go down, for your people, whom you brought up out of the land of Egypt" (v7).** God calls them "your people," Moses' people, not "my people." It is the language of a broken relationship, as if a father said "your son" to his wife. He uses their own words: they said Moses brought them up (v1). **"Corrupted themselves"** is the same verb used of the earth before the flood (Gen 6:11–12).
- **"They have turned aside quickly" (v8).** Within six weeks of hearing God's voice from the fire, they have broken the first two commandments.
- **"It is a stiff-necked people" (v9).** The picture is of an ox that stiffens its neck and refuses to turn when the farmer pulls the rope. The phrase appears here for the first time and becomes a standard description of Israel (33:3, 5; 34:9; Deut 9:6; Acts 7:51).
- **"Now therefore let me alone… that I may consume them, in order that I may make a great nation of you" (v10).** This is a real offer and a real test. God could start over with Moses, as he started with Abraham. But notice: no one has asked God to spare them, yet God says "let me alone." Many interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, hear in this an opening for prayer. God is inviting Moses to stand in the gap.
- **"But Moses implored the LORD his God" (v11).** Literally he "softened the face" of the LORD, that is, he sought his favor. Moses gives three reasons. First, they are **"your people, whom you have brought out of the land of Egypt"** (v11). He hands the people right back to God. Second, God's name: **"Why should the Egyptians say, 'With evil intent did he bring them out'?"** (v12). Destroying them would let the nations slander God. Third, God's promise: **"Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel… to whom you swore by your own self"** (v13; Gen 22:16–17). Moses does not argue that the people deserve mercy. He appeals entirely to God's own character, honor and oath.
- **"And the LORD relented from the disaster" (v14).** "Relented" is *nacham*, which can mean "be sorry, have compassion, change one's course." The KJV says "repented." Scripture also says God "is not a man, that he should… change his mind" (Num 23:19; 1 Sam 15:29). These fit together. God's character and promises never change, and one thing that never changes is that he responds to repentance and intercession (Jer 18:7–10; Jonah 3:10). His threats of judgment are real, but they are often warnings, and God had placed Moses in exactly the place to pray. This is the language of a real relationship, in which prayer truly matters, not a picture of a God who was talked out of a tantrum.
- **"Tablets that were written on both sides… the work of God, and the writing was the writing of God" (vv15–16).** The text slows down to stress what Moses is carrying: God's own handwriting.
- **Joshua and the noise (vv17–18).** Joshua had gone part of the way up the mountain with Moses (24:13) and had not been in the camp. He was a soldier, and he heard war. Moses, who already knew from God what was happening, answers with a little poem in Hebrew: not the sound of victory, not the sound of defeat, but **"the sound of singing."**
- **"Saw the calf and the dancing… and broke them at the foot of the mountain" (v19).** On the mountain, Moses pleaded for the people. In the camp, seeing it with his own eyes, his anger burned. God's wrath "burned hot" (v10), and now Moses' does too, the same phrase. Breaking the tablets was probably more than a fit of temper. In the ancient world breaking a treaty document meant the treaty was void. Israel had broken the covenant, and Moses showed it. God does not rebuke Moses for this, though later he reminds him that he was the one who broke them (34:1).
- **"Burned it with fire and ground it to powder and scattered it on the water and made the people of Israel drink it" (v20).** Deuteronomy adds that the water was "the brook that ran down from the mountain" (Deut 9:21). The idol is utterly destroyed, the god they trusted reduced to dust they had to swallow. Some see a link with the test of the "bitter water" for a suspected unfaithful wife (Num 5:11–31). Israel, God's bride, had been unfaithful, and drinking the dust brought the sin home to every person.
- **"What did this people do to you that you have brought such a great sin upon them?" (v21).** Moses holds Aaron responsible. A leader cannot hide behind the crowd.
- **Aaron's excuses (vv22–24).** First he blames the people: **"You know the people, that they are set on evil"** (v22). Then he tells the story as if he had no part in it: **"I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf"** (v24). Compare v4, where he "fashioned it with a graving tool." It is the oldest excuse in the book, the same blame-shifting as Adam and Eve (Gen 3:12–13). Deuteronomy tells us that God was so angry with Aaron that he would have destroyed him, but Moses prayed for him too (Deut 9:20).
- **"The people had broken loose (for Aaron had let them break loose, to the derision of their enemies)" (v25).** "Broken loose" means out of control, unrestrained. The same verb is used in Proverbs 29:18: "Where there is no prophetic vision the people cast off restraint." The KJV's "naked" is one possible meaning of the word, and some think it was literally true here. The ESV takes it as moral chaos. Either way, Israel had made the LORD look like just another pagan god in front of the nations.
- **"Who is on the LORD's side? Come to me" (v26).** Moses calls for a decision. **"All the sons of Levi gathered around him."** This was Moses and Aaron's own tribe. Not every Israelite had joined the revelry, but the Levites were the ones willing to act.
- **"Each of you kill his brother and his companion and his neighbor" (v27).** This is one of the hardest scenes in Exodus. Three things help us read it. First, it is presented as God's command, a judicial act under the covenant the people had sworn to keep only weeks before (24:3–8), and idolatry was a capital crime under it (22:20). Second, "about three thousand" out of some 600,000 men (v28) shows this was not indiscriminate slaughter. Most think it fell on the ringleaders and those still carrying on with the idol after Moses returned. Third, the point is that no family tie could outrank loyalty to God. Moses blesses Levi for this in Deuteronomy 33:8–9: "He said of his father and mother, 'I regard them not.'" Jesus says something similar in milder form: whoever loves father or mother more than him is not worthy of him (Matt 10:37). None of this gives Christians license to use violence for God. The church's sword is the word of God (Eph 6:17), and church discipline is by exclusion, not execution (1 Cor 5:13).
- **Three thousand.** It is often noted that at Sinai, when the law was given, about three thousand died. At Pentecost, the feast that later Jewish tradition linked with the giving of the law, when the Spirit was given, about three thousand were saved (Acts 2:41). Paul's words fit: "the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (2 Cor 3:6).
- **"Today you have been ordained for the service of the LORD" (v29).** Literally "fill your hands today," the same idiom used of the priests' ordination (28:41; 29:9). Levi's zeal for God here leads to its role as the tribe set apart for the tabernacle (Num 3:5–13; 8:5–19). Compare Phinehas, a Levite whose zeal against idolatry also won his line a lasting covenant of priesthood (Num 25:10–13).
- **"You have sinned a great sin… perhaps I can make atonement for your sin" (v30).** Moses names the sin plainly and offers to go back up the mountain. **"Perhaps."** He does not know whether God will forgive.
- **"Please blot me out of your book that you have written" (v32).** The sentence in Hebrew breaks off in the middle, as if he cannot finish it: "if you will forgive their sin—." Then: if not, blot me out. God's "book" is his record of the living, those who belong to him (Ps 69:28; Dan 12:1). The New Testament speaks of "the book of life" (Phil 4:3; Rev 3:5). Moses is willing to be cut off from God and from life itself if it would save his people. Paul expresses the same heart for Israel in Romans 9:3: "I could wish that I myself were accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of my brothers."
- **"Whoever has sinned against me, I will blot out of my book" (v33).** God declines. Each person answers for his own sin (Ezek 18:4, 20). Moses cannot be the substitute. He is a sinner himself. The offer was a noble one, but it took a sinless one to make it work.
- **"Go, lead the people… my angel shall go before you" (v34).** The journey to Canaan will go on, and God's promise stands. But there is a chilling difference. God speaks of "my angel," not "I." Chapter 33 takes up this question: will God himself go with them? **"In the day when I visit, I will visit their sin upon them."** "Visit" means to come to inspect and deal with. Judgment was postponed, not cancelled. The generation that came out of Egypt would die in the wilderness (Num 14:29–35).
- **"Then the LORD sent a plague on the people, because they made the calf, the one that Aaron made" (v35).** Whether this is a separate plague or sums up the judgment already described is not clear. The last line is pointed: the people made the calf, and so did Aaron. Both are guilty.
- **Commentators:** All three see impatience at the root of the sin. Henry says, "they were weary of waiting for the promised land," and of Aaron, "we do not find that he said one word to discountenance their proposal." He notes that Aaron "calls it a feast to Jehovah," but says that representing God by an image turns his truth into a lie. JFB calls the calf a copy of "the god Apis" and argues, like Henry, that they meant to worship Jehovah through it, so the sin was against the second commandment. JFB calls Aaron's answer "a shuffling excuse," and on the "blot me out" offer contrasts Moses' willingness to die with Christ, who actually did die for the ungodly (Rom 5:8). On v10, Henry writes that Moses "wisely took the hint which God gave him when he said, Let me alone," and that he "is standing in the gap to turn away the wrath of God" (Ps 106:23). He also notes that God "will not blot any out of his book but those that by their wilful disobedience have forfeited" their place. Guzik takes God's offer to Moses as a real temptation that Moses refused, and explains "relented" as language from the human point of view: Moses' prayer did not change God's purpose but moved the people from the place of judgment to the place of mercy. Guzik claims *'egel* means a young bull rather than a calf, but the lexicon simply gives "calf," which can be a young bull. He sees Moses' offer as a man sensing that animal blood could not cover this sin, and he looks ahead to Christ, who was made sin for us (2 Cor 5:21). On the Levites, Guzik and Henry both take the lesson that being on the LORD's side means deciding and acting, even against one's own friends.

#### Pointing to Christ
While God was giving the plans for his dwelling, his people were making a god of their own. The chapter shows how quickly the human heart turns to idols, even in the shadow of Sinai, even among leaders. But it also shows a mediator standing between a holy God and a guilty people. Moses pleaded God's name and God's promise, and he offered his own life, but he could not take their place. Jesus could. He is the mediator who did not say "perhaps." He was "made to be sin who knew no sin" (2 Cor 5:21), cut off so that his people's names would never be blotted out of the book (Rev 3:5). "He always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25), and the law that was broken at the foot of the mountain he writes on his people's hearts (Jer 31:33).
