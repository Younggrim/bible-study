*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. This chapter finishes the covenant renewal begun in chapter 29, and it is Moses' last direct appeal before the narrative turns to his death. Chapter 28 set out the blessings and curses, and chapter 29 ended with a picture of the land ruined and Israel thrown into "another land." Chapter 30 looks past that disaster. Moses speaks as though exile is certain, and then promises that it will not be the end: if Israel turns back to the LORD, he will gather them and change their hearts. The chapter closes with one of the Bible's great calls to decision: "Therefore choose life."

#### Repentance and Forgiveness (vv1–10)
- **"When all these things come upon you, the blessing and the curse" (v1).** Note "when," not "if." Moses has already told them how the story will go (31:16–21, 29). Israel would enjoy blessing and then fall under the curse. The question is what happens next.
- **"And you call them to mind among all the nations where the LORD your God has driven you" (v1).** Literally, "bring them back to your heart." Turning starts with remembering, in exile, far from the land. The prodigal son "came to himself" in a far country before he went home (Luke 15:17).
- **The key word: "return" (vv1–10).** The Hebrew verb *shuv*, "turn, return," runs through this section seven times: they "call to mind" (literally "bring back," v1), **"return to the LORD"** (v2), God will **"restore your fortunes"** and gather them **"again"** (v3), they will **"again obey"** (v8), God will **"again take delight"** (v9), and they will **"turn to the LORD"** (v10). Israel turns to God, and God turns to Israel. *Shuv* is the Old Testament's main word for repentance.
- **"You and your children… with all your heart and with all your soul" (v2).** The words of the Shema again (6:5). Real repentance is not a change of habit only, but a return of the whole person to God.
- **"The LORD your God will restore your fortunes and have mercy on you" (v3).** "Restore your fortunes" is *shav… shevut*, a wordplay: literally "turn your turning" or "return your captivity." Older translations read "turn thy captivity" (KJV). The phrase came to mean a complete reversal of ruin, as when "the LORD restored the fortunes of Job" (Job 42:10) or brought back the exiles (Ps 126:1, 4).
- **"If your outcasts are in the uttermost parts of heaven" (v4).** "Heaven" here means the far horizon, the ends of the earth. No one would be scattered too far for God to bring back. Nehemiah prayed this verse back to God (Neh 1:8–9).
- **The return from exile.** Part of this came true when the Persian king Cyrus allowed Jews to return from Babylon in 538 BC (Ezra 1). But many stayed scattered, and the prophets still looked for a greater gathering (Isa 11:11–12; Jer 31; Ezek 36–37). Readers differ on how the rest is fulfilled. Many dispensational and some other readers see a future national restoration of Israel, and some see the modern return of Jews to the land as a step toward it. Many Reformed and other readers see it fulfilled in Christ, who gathers the true Israel, Jews and Gentiles, into one people. Most agree that the heart change of v6 is the part that matters most.
- **"The LORD your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your offspring" (v6).** This is the center of the chapter. Circumcision was the sign of the covenant, cutting away the foreskin. In 10:16 Moses commanded, "Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart." Here God promises to do it himself. Israel's deepest problem, a heart that did not understand (29:4), will be solved by God. The goal: **"so that you will love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul, that you may live."** God gives what he commands. This is the seed of the new covenant (Jer 31:33; 32:39–40; Ezek 36:26–27), and Paul says "circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter" (Rom 2:29; compare Col 2:11).
- **"The LORD your God will put all these curses on your foes" (v7).** The curses that fell on Israel will turn to their oppressors, as God promised Abraham: "him who dishonors you I will curse" (Gen 12:3).
- **"The LORD will again take delight in prospering you, as he took delight in your fathers" (v9).** This answers 28:63, where the LORD would "take delight" in bringing ruin. The same God who judged with his whole heart will restore with his whole heart.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Consideration is the first step towards conversion," and turning back to God is "the life and soul of repentance." He reads the chapter as showing that "mercy at length rejoices against judgment, and has the last word." JFB argues the return from Babylon cannot be the full fulfillment, since Israel was not then scattered "among all the nations" nor multiplied above their fathers, and he looks for its "full and complete accomplishment" in Israel's turning to the gospel. Guzik agrees the spiritual restoration of v6 is still future for Israel as a nation, and he sees the modern regathering of Jews as a large but partial fulfillment of vv3–5. He also stresses, from Genesis 12:3, that Christians who persecuted Jews were wrong.

#### The Choice of Life and Death (vv11–20)
- **"This commandment that I command you today is not too hard for you, neither is it far off" (v11).** "Too hard" is *nifla'ah*, "too wonderful, beyond reach," the word used of things beyond human ability (Gen 18:14; Ps 131:1). God's law is not a mystery known only to experts. It has been spoken plainly.
- **"It is not in heaven… neither is it beyond the sea" (vv12–13).** Heaven above and the far side of the sea were the two most distant places imaginable. No one needs a hero to climb to heaven or cross the ocean to fetch God's word. Ancient stories told of heroes who did such things to find hidden wisdom, like Gilgamesh's search for the secret of life. Israel does not need that. God has come down and spoken.
- **"But the word is very near you. It is in your mouth and in your heart, so that you can do it" (v14).** "In your mouth" because they recited it (6:7) and "in your heart" because it was to be loved and remembered (6:6). This does not mean anyone could keep the law perfectly. Moses has just said that Israel would fail and need God to change their hearts (v6). It means God's will was known and within reach, not hidden.
- **Paul's use of vv11–14.** In Romans 10:6–8 Paul quotes these verses and applies them to Christ: "Do not say in your heart, 'Who will ascend into heaven?' (that is, to bring Christ down) or 'Who will descend into the abyss?' (that is, to bring Christ up from the dead)… 'The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart' (that is, the word of faith that we proclaim)." Paul's point is that no one needs to do the impossible to be saved. Christ has already come down and risen. The gospel is near, to be confessed with the mouth and believed with the heart (Rom 10:9). Paul reads Moses' words in light of v6, where God himself puts the word in the heart. Paul uses "the abyss" rather than "the sea." Some Jewish writings of his day also linked the sea in this passage with the deep, and the Aramaic paraphrases expanded it with Moses and Jonah.
- **"See, I have set before you today life and good, death and evil" (v15).** The whole of Deuteronomy comes down to this. "Good" and "evil" here mean prosperity and disaster, the outcomes of each path.
- **"By loving the LORD your God, by walking in his ways" (v16).** Love comes first, then obedience. The commands are the shape love takes.
- **"But if your heart turns away… you shall surely perish" (vv17–18).** The danger begins in the heart, as in 29:18.
- **"I call heaven and earth to witness against you today" (v19).** In ancient treaties the gods of both nations were called as witnesses. Israel had only one God, so Moses calls on creation itself, which would outlast the generation listening (4:26; 31:28; 32:1). The prophets later called the same witnesses (Isa 1:2; Mic 6:1–2).
- **"Therefore choose life, that you and your offspring may live" (v19).** One choice, with consequences for children and grandchildren. God sets out both ways and urges the right one. The Tyndale notes observe that blessing and life go together, and curse and death go together.
- **"Loving the LORD your God, obeying his voice and holding fast to him, for he is your life and length of days" (v20).** "Holding fast" is *davaq*, the word used for a man clinging to his wife (Gen 2:24) and Ruth clinging to Naomi (Ruth 1:14). Life is not only a reward God gives for obedience. God himself is their life. Moses ends where the promise began, with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv11–14 "the righteousness of faith set before them in the plainness and easiness of the commandment," a phrase that follows Paul. On v19: "Those shall have life that choose it," and those who die "die because they will die." JFB says Paul "has applied this passage to the Gospel," and compares Moses' earnestness here to Paul's farewell to the Ephesian elders (Acts 20:26–27). Guzik is careful to say vv11–14 do not mean the law could be kept perfectly to earn righteousness. The old covenant always included sacrifice, which pointed to the Messiah, and an Israelite could know he was a sinner saved through a substitute, "seeing by moonlight" what Christ shows in full sun. He follows Maclaren in saying that refusing to choose is itself a choice.

#### Pointing to Christ
Deuteronomy 30 holds two things side by side: a command ("choose life") and a promise ("the LORD your God will circumcise your heart"). The gospel shows how they meet. Christ is "the word made flesh" who came down from heaven and rose from the abyss, so that no one has to climb up or go down to find God (Rom 10:6–8). And by his Spirit he does the heart surgery Moses could only promise: "In him also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands" (Col 2:11). Moses said, "He is your life." Jesus said, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), and "I came that they may have life and have it abundantly" (John 10:10). Choosing life, in the end, means choosing him.
