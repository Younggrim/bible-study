*Setting:* The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to Jewish Christians worn down by pressure and tempted to drift back from Jesus, has just finished its great roll call of faith (ch 11). Abel, Abraham, Moses, Rahab and the nameless martyrs all trusted God's promise without seeing it fulfilled, "that apart from us they should not be made perfect" (11:40). Chapter 12 turns from them to the readers. It moves from a picture (a race in a crowded stadium), to an explanation of suffering (a father training his sons), to a warning (Esau), and ends with the sermon's last and greatest contrast: two mountains, Sinai and Zion.

#### Jesus, Founder and Perfecter of Our Faith (vv1–2)
- **"Therefore" (v1).** Everything in chapter 11 was building to this. The heroes are not a museum to admire but a reason to run.
- **"So great a cloud of witnesses" (v1).** "Cloud" (*nephos*) was a common Greek way to describe a vast crowd, like a mass of spectators in a stadium. "Witnesses" (*martyres*) picks up chapter 11, where these people were repeatedly "commended," literally "witnessed to" (11:2, 4, 5, 39). Two readings sit together here. They are witnesses in the sense of testifying: their lives say that faith in God is worth it. Many also picture them as spectators filling the stands. The text leans more on the first, and it does not teach that the departed watch our daily lives, though some Christians draw that hope from it.
- **"Lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely" (v1).** Runners stripped down to run. A "weight" is not necessarily a sin. It is anything that slows a runner down, good things included. Sin is named separately. "Clings so closely" is *euperistatos*, a word found nowhere earlier in Greek literature. The lexicon gives "readily besetting," something that easily surrounds and trips a runner, like a long robe tangling the legs.
- **"Run with endurance the race that is set before us" (v1).** The race (*agōn*) is a contest, the same word behind "agony." "Endurance" (*hypomonē*) is the theme of the chapter (vv1, 2, 3, 7). This is not a sprint but a long-distance race, and the course is "set before us," marked out by God, not chosen by the runner.
- **"Looking to Jesus" (v2).** The verb means to look away from other things and fix the eyes on one. Runners who watch the crowd or their rivals stumble. The faithful of chapter 11 are an encouragement, but Jesus is the one to watch.
- **"The founder and perfecter of our faith" (v2).** "Founder" is *archēgos*, used of Jesus also in 2:10 ("the founder of their salvation") and Acts 3:15 and 5:31. It means both originator and leader, the pioneer who goes first and opens the way. "Perfecter" (*teleiōtēs*) appears only here in the New Testament. Jesus started the life of faith and also brought it to its finish line. "Our" is added in English; the Greek says simply "of the faith," so Jesus may be pictured as the one who ran faith's whole course perfectly himself, not only the one who produces faith in us. Both are true.
- **"For the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame" (v2).** The same phrase, "set before," is used of our race (v1) and his joy. Crucifixion was designed to humiliate: naked, public, reserved for slaves and rebels. Jesus did not pretend the shame wasn't real. He counted it as nothing compared with the joy ahead: the Father's presence, a redeemed people, the throne. A minority of scholars read "for" as "instead of" ("instead of the joy before him, he endured the cross"), pointing to the joy he could have kept. Most take it as "for the sake of."
- **"Is seated at the right hand of the throne of God" (v2).** This is Psalm 110:1 for the fifth time in the sermon (1:3, 13; 8:1; 10:12). The runner has finished. The shame of the cross ended in the seat of honor.
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that a weight need not be a sin, so the choice is often between good and best. He reads *euperistatos* several ways at once (easily avoided, admired, ensnaring, dangerous) to describe kinds of sin, but the lexicon supports only "readily besetting," so those extra meanings are preaching, not the word itself. JFB takes the witnesses as "the spectators pressing round to see the competitors" while insisting their main role is to testify to God's faithfulness. It names the Hebrews' besetting sin as "UNBELIEF," and says of Jesus, "The coming joy disarmed of its sting the present pain." Henry calls Jesus "a greater example than he had yet mentioned."

#### Do Not Grow Weary (vv3–17)
- **"Consider him who endured from sinners such hostility" (v3).** "Consider" is a word for careful calculation, weighing it up. "Weary" and "fainthearted" were used of runners who collapse near the end. The cure for spiritual exhaustion is not looking inward but looking at what Jesus endured.
- **"You have not yet resisted to the point of shedding your blood" (v4).** The picture shifts from racing to boxing, with sin as the opponent. These believers had suffered insult, prison and the loss of property (10:32–34), but none had yet been killed. Many scholars see this as a clue that the letter came before Nero's bloody persecution in Rome (AD 64).
- **"Have you forgotten the exhortation that addresses you as sons?" (v5).** The writer quotes Proverbs 3:11–12 and treats it as God speaking to them now. Scripture is not old advice. It "addresses you."
- **"The discipline of the Lord" (vv5–11).** "Discipline" is *paideia*, from *pais*, "child." It means the whole work of raising a child: training, teaching and correction. The word appears eight times in these seven verses as noun or verb. In the Greek translation of the Old Testament it renders the Hebrew *musar*, the key word of Proverbs. Hardship here is not mainly punishment for a specific sin but training. The same suffering that was persecution from the side of men was, from God's side, a father's schooling.
- **"The Lord disciplines the one he loves" (v6).** Love is the source, not anger. "Chastises" is a strong word for whipping, and the writer does not soften it. Love can hurt for the child's good.
- **"God is treating you as sons" (v7).** In the ancient world fathers were responsible for training their heirs, and a father who never corrected his son had given up on him.
- **"Illegitimate children and not sons" (v8).** *Nothos* means a child born outside marriage, often of a slave or a concubine. Such a child had no inheritance, so a father might not bother to train him. To have no discipline at all would be the alarming thing.
- **"The Father of spirits… and live" (v9).** An argument from lesser to greater. Earthly fathers gave physical life. God is the Father of our spiritual life. If we respected our human fathers, how much more should we yield to him, and the result is "life."
- **"For a short time as it seemed best to them" (v10).** Human fathers train for a few years and with limited wisdom, sometimes getting it wrong. God's discipline is never mistaken and has one aim: "that we may share his holiness."
- **"Later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it" (v11).** The writer is honest: it "seems painful," and it is. "Trained" is *gymnazō*, from the word for training naked in the gymnasium, the root of "gymnastics." The fruit comes "later" and only to those who let the training do its work.
- **"Lift your drooping hands and strengthen your weak knees" (v12).** Taken from Isaiah 35:3, a chapter about God making a highway for his ransomed people to return home. The picture is an exhausted runner, arms hanging and legs buckling, being told to get up.
- **"Make straight paths for your feet, so that what is lame may not be put out of joint" (v13).** This echoes Proverbs 4:26 in the Greek Bible. The concern is for others: the "lame" are the weak members of the church. When the strong walk a straight path, the limping can follow it and heal instead of being injured further.
- **"Strive for peace with everyone, and for the holiness without which no one will see the Lord" (v14).** "Strive" means pursue, chase down. Peace and holiness are held together: peace that gives up holiness is not real peace, and holiness that ignores peace is not the holiness God gives. Seeing the Lord is the final hope (Matt 5:8; 1 John 3:2). This is not salvation earned by holiness. Holiness is the shape of the life that grace produces (v10).
- **"See to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God" (v15).** "See to it" is *episkopeō*, related to *episkopos*, "overseer." Here the whole church does the overseeing, watching out for one another. "Fails to obtain" means falling short or lagging behind, like a straggler dropping out of the race.
- **"Root of bitterness" (v15).** A quotation of Deuteronomy 29:18, where Moses warns of "a root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit," meaning a person in Israel who turns to other gods. The root is out of sight at first, then springs up and poisons "many." In context it is not only a bitter mood but a person drifting from God whose influence spreads.
- **"Sexually immoral or unholy like Esau" (v16).** Genesis never calls Esau sexually immoral. Jewish tradition (Jubilees, Philo) did, perhaps because of his marriages to Hittite women (Gen 26:34–35). Most read the word as describing the people the church must watch for, with "unholy" (godless, treating sacred things as common) applying to Esau. **"Sold his birthright for a single meal"** (Gen 25:29–34): the firstborn's double inheritance and the covenant promise traded for stew. Notice the word "birthright," *prōtotokia*, "firstborn rights." It returns in v23.
- **"He found no chance to repent, though he sought it with tears" (v17).** Literally "he found no place of repentance." This is the hard verse. Genesis 27:34–38 records his "exceedingly great and bitter cry." The "it" he sought with tears could be the blessing (Gen 27:38) or repentance itself; both nouns are feminine in Greek, so grammar cannot settle it. Most take the point to be that his tears came too late to change what he had done: Isaac would not reverse the blessing. Esau wept over losing the blessing, not over despising God. The warning fits the sermon's earlier warnings (6:4–6; 10:26–31): there is a point where turning away hardens into a settled refusal. It is not a picture of a sincerely repentant person turned away by God.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God's correction is no condemnation," and "It is always for our profit." On v14 he warns against trading holiness for quiet: "there can be no true peace without holiness." Guzik adds that discipline is one reason for trials but not the only one, and that it is never God punishing sins already paid for at the cross. JFB quotes Chrysostom: "not to be chastised is a mark of bastardy." On v17, JFB and Henry both say the repentance Esau sought was really a change in his father's decision, which God would not allow. JFB adds that Esau's tears were "not for his sin." Guzik agrees that Esau could still have turned to God but could not undo his choice.

#### A Kingdom That Cannot Be Shaken (vv18–29)
- **Two mountains.** The sermon's final contrast sets Sinai (vv18–21) against Zion (vv22–24). The writer does not say "you have not come to Sinai *yet*" but "you have not come" there at all. Christians have arrived, by faith, at the other mountain already.
- **"What may be touched, a blazing fire and darkness and gloom and a tempest" (v18).** This is Exodus 19–20 and Deuteronomy 4–5: smoke, fire, thick cloud, earthquake. "What may be touched" means a physical, earthly mountain, in contrast with the heavenly one. Later manuscripts add "mountain" here (KJV "the mount that might be touched"). The earliest leave it out, as the ESV does, but the meaning is the same.
- **"The sound of a trumpet and a voice" (v19).** The people begged that God stop speaking and Moses speak instead (Exod 20:19; Deut 5:25–27). Keep that in mind for v25.
- **"If even a beast touches the mountain, it shall be stoned" (v20).** From Exodus 19:12–13. The mountain was fenced off. God was present, but no one could come near.
- **"Moses said, 'I tremble with fear'" (v21).** These exact words are not in Exodus. The nearest is Deuteronomy 9:19, where Moses says, "I was afraid," after the golden calf. Jewish tradition remembered Moses trembling at Sinai. The point is that even the mediator was terrified.
- **"But you have come to Mount Zion" (v22).** Zion was the hill of David's city and the temple, the place God chose to dwell. "The heavenly Jerusalem" is the city Abraham looked for (11:10, 16). "Have come" is the same verb used for drawing near to God in worship (4:16; 10:22). The list that follows names seven or eight realities, piling up in a long rush of "and… and… and."
- **"Innumerable angels in festal gathering" (v22).** "Festal gathering" (*panēgyris*) was the word for a national festival crowd, a joyful holiday assembly. At Sinai angels were present at the giving of the law (Deut 33:2; Acts 7:53; Gal 3:19), but here they are celebrating. Translations divide the phrases differently (KJV "the general assembly and church of the firstborn"); most modern versions attach the festal gathering to the angels.
- **"The assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven" (v23).** "Assembly" is *ekklēsia*, "church." "Firstborn" is plural: all God's people share the firstborn's rights through Christ, the true Firstborn (1:6). This is the reverse of Esau, who sold his firstborn rights (v16). "Enrolled" means registered as citizens (Luke 10:20; Phil 4:3; Rev 21:27).
- **"God, the judge of all" (v23).** Even in the welcoming scene, God is still the judge. The difference is that his people now come to him through the mediator.
- **"The spirits of the righteous made perfect" (v23).** Believers who have died, the faithful of chapter 11 among them. In 11:40 they were waiting not to "be made perfect" apart from us. Christ's single offering has now "perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (10:14).
- **"Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant" (v24).** Moses was the trembling mediator at Sinai. Jesus is the mediator of the covenant promised in Jeremiah 31 (Heb 8:6–13; 9:15).
- **"The sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (v24).** Abel's blood "cries to me from the ground" (Gen 4:10), crying for justice. Abel "still speaks" by faith (11:4). Jesus' blood, sprinkled like the covenant blood at Sinai (Exod 24:8; Heb 9:19–22), speaks forgiveness. Most take Abel's "blood" as his murder; some, including Guzik, think of his sacrifice. Either way, Jesus' blood says something Abel's never could.
- **"See that you do not refuse him who is speaking" (v25).** The sermon's last warning, and the same lesser-to-greater argument as 2:1–4. Israel at Sinai begged God to stop speaking. Now God speaks "from heaven" in his Son (1:2). Refusing that voice is more serious, not less.
- **"Yet once more I will shake not only the earth but also the heavens" (v26).** Sinai shook (Exod 19:18; Judg 5:4–5). Haggai 2:6 promised a final shaking, in a prophecy given to discouraged builders of the second temple. Haggai's point was that God would fill his house with glory. The writer reads "once more" as pointing to a last shaking that removes everything created and temporary.
- **"That the things that cannot be shaken may remain" (v27).** For readers tempted to cling to the temple and its system, this is pointed. The earthly shadows will pass (8:13). What remains is the kingdom.
- **"Let us be grateful for receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken" (v28).** "Be grateful" is literally "let us have grace," which can mean thankfulness (ESV) or holding on to grace. The kingdom is *received*, not built. The response is "acceptable worship, with reverence and awe," and the word for worship (*latreuō*) is the priestly service word used throughout the sermon. Chapter 13 will show what that worship looks like in daily life.
- **"For our God is a consuming fire" (v29).** From Deuteronomy 4:24. The fire of Sinai has not gone out. The God of Zion is the same holy God, and grace does not make him tame. That is why awe, not casualness, is the right response to his welcome.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the whole contrast together: "The gospel state is mild, and kind, and condescending, suited to our weak frame," and Christ's blood "pleads not for vengeance, as the blood of Abel did… but for mercy." He also calls God "a God of strict justice," a fire still. JFB prefers "the full festal assembly of angels, and the Church of the first-born," as the ESV has it, and sees the shaking as beginning at Christ's first coming and completed at his return. Guzik points out that Sinai's terror did not produce lasting holiness (the golden calf followed within weeks), and argues that grace rightly received produces reverence, never license.

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole chapter fixes the eyes on Jesus. He is the runner who finished the race the faithful of chapter 11 were still running, the Son who learned obedience through suffering and so makes sense of the Father's discipline (5:8), and the true Firstborn whose birthright he shares with those who had forfeited theirs. At Sinai the mediator trembled and the people asked God to stop speaking. At Zion the mediator's own blood speaks a better word, and God's voice from heaven becomes an invitation. The kingdom cannot be shaken because its King is already seated at the right hand of the throne of God.
