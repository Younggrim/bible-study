*Setting:* Chapters 12 and 13 have shown the church's enemies at full strength: the dragon thrown down and furious, the beast from the sea demanding worship, and the beast from the earth forcing everyone to take its mark or be shut out of buying and selling. Chapter 13 ended with the number 666 and a world that seems to belong to the beast. Chapter 14 is heaven's answer, given before the last series of judgments, the seven bowls (chs 15–16). It has three scenes: the Lamb standing with his people, three angels with three messages, and two harvests of the earth. It previews the end, and much of it is told again at greater length in chapters 17–20.

The four ways of reading Revelation differ mainly over who the 144,000 are, what Babylon is, and when the harvests happen:
- **Futurists** (Guzik) see the 144,000 as the Jewish believers sealed in chapter 7, now shown safe at the end of a future great tribulation, with the harvests describing Christ's return and the final battle.
- **Historicists** like Henry read the 144,000 as the faithful church through long years of corruption, the three angels as stages of the Reformation, and Babylon as papal Rome.
- **Preterists** generally see Babylon as first-century Rome (or, for some, apostate Jerusalem), and the judgment as God's verdict on the powers that persecuted the early church.
- **Idealists** see the chapter as a picture of how every age ends for two kinds of people, those who belong to the Lamb and those who belong to the beast.

All agree on the point: the beast does not get the last word, and those who refuse his mark are not losers but the Lamb's own.

#### The Lamb and the 144,000 (vv1–5)
- **"On Mount Zion stood the Lamb" (v1).** Chapter 12 ended with the dragon standing on the sand of the sea (12:17), waiting for a beast to rise from the water. Now the Lamb stands on solid ground. "Stood" recalls the Lamb "standing, as though it had been slain" (5:6): he was killed, and he is on his feet. Mount Zion was the hill of Jerusalem where David brought the ark and the temple later stood. Psalm 2:6 is in the background: "I have set my King on Zion, my holy hill." Readers differ on which Zion. Guzik takes it as the earthly Jerusalem, where Christ will reign. Many others see the heavenly Zion, "the city of the living God" (Heb 12:22), since the next verses happen "before the throne." Henry reads it as the church: "Mount Sion is the gospel church."
- **"144,000 who had his name and his Father's name written on their foreheads" (v1).** These are the 144,000 sealed "on their foreheads" in 7:3–4. The contrast with 13:16–17 is the point. The beast marks his people on the forehead or hand. The Lamb marks his with his own name and his Father's (3:12; 22:4). Everyone in Revelation carries someone's name. The Tyndale notes add that the two names together show the unity of the Father and the Son. As in chapter 7, readers differ on whether the 144,000 are believing Israel (Guzik, JFB) or the whole people of God pictured as a complete army (12 × 12 × 1,000).
- **"A voice… like the roar of many waters… like the sound of harpists" (v2).** The sound is overwhelming and beautiful at once. "Many waters" is the sound of Christ's voice in 1:15, and harps belong to the worship around the throne (5:8).
- **"A new song… No one could learn that song except the 144,000 who had been redeemed from the earth" (v3).** A "new song" in the Psalms celebrates a fresh rescue (Ps 40:3; 98:1). Only the redeemed can learn this one, because only they have lived it. John doesn't give the words. The Tyndale notes compare the things Paul heard in paradise and could not tell (2 Cor 12:4). "Redeemed" is literally "bought," the word of 5:9, where the Lamb "ransomed people for God" by his blood. Only the beast's people could buy and sell (13:17), but these have been bought themselves, at a far higher price.
- **"These who have not defiled themselves with women, for they are virgins" (v4).** "Virgins" is *parthenos*, normally used of young women. The lexicon notes that here it describes chaste men. Most readers take it as a picture, not a rule about marriage, for two reasons:
  - In the Old Testament, unfaithfulness to God is called adultery and prostitution (Jer 3:6–9; Hos 1–3). The great enemy of the next chapters is a prostitute, Babylon (17:1–5). Those who refuse her are "virgins." Paul uses the same image for the church as "a pure virgin" betrothed to Christ (2 Cor 11:2).
  - Israel's soldiers kept from women while on campaign (Deut 23:9–10; 1 Sam 21:5). The 144,000 are counted like an army in chapter 7, kept pure for holy war.

  Guzik allows that literal celibacy in a time of great distress may also be meant (compare 1 Cor 7:26). Scripture never treats marriage itself as defiling (Heb 13:4), so the defilement is best taken as the idolatry the next verses condemn.
- **"These follow the Lamb wherever he goes" (v4).** This is discipleship in a phrase: "My sheep hear my voice… and they follow me" (John 10:27). "Wherever" includes the road to the cross.
- **"Redeemed from mankind as firstfruits for God and the Lamb" (v4).** "Firstfruits" (*aparchē*) were the first sheaves of harvest, given to God as a sign that the whole harvest was his (Lev 23:9–14). The word can mean a first portion with more to follow (1 Cor 15:20), or simply an offering set apart for God (Jas 1:18 calls all believers "a kind of firstfruits"). Futurists tend to see the 144,000 as the first of a greater harvest. Others see the whole church as God's firstfruits from humanity. Either way the word prepares for the harvests at the end of the chapter.
- **"In their mouth no lie was found, for they are blameless" (v5).** This echoes the Servant who had "no deceit in his mouth" (Isa 53:9; 1 Pet 2:22) and the remnant of Zephaniah 3:13. It matters because the beast's whole system runs on deception (13:14), and liars end outside the city (21:8; 22:15). "Blameless" is the word for a sacrifice "without blemish" (1 Pet 1:19). It doesn't mean they never sinned. They were washed by the Lamb (7:14) and stayed true.
- **Commentators:** JFB takes the virgins as faithful "in contrast to the apostate Church, Babylon." It calls the song "as it were" a new song, "for it is, in truth, as old as God's eternal purpose," and notes that the oldest manuscripts read "His name and His Father's name," as the ESV does. Henry says the Lamb's followers "kept themselves clean from the wicked abominations of the followers of antichrist." Guzik reads the scene as a direct reply to chapter 13: just when the beast seems to have won, God's people stand with the Lamb, not one missing. He suggests the new song is learned through what they suffered on earth.

#### The Messages of the Three Angels (vv6–13)
- **"Another angel flying directly overhead, with an eternal gospel to proclaim" (v6).** "Directly overhead" is mid-heaven, where all can see and hear, like the eagle of 8:13. This is the only time Revelation uses the noun "gospel." It is **eternal** because God's good news does not change. Jesus said the gospel would be preached "throughout the whole world as a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come" (Matt 24:14). The audience, "every nation and tribe and language and people," is the same list the beast claimed in 13:7. The beast claims every nation, and God's offer goes to every nation.
- **"Fear God and give him glory, because the hour of his judgment has come" (v7).** Good news that sounds like a warning is both. Judgment is coming, so there is still time to turn. "Give him glory" is Revelation's phrase for real repentance (11:13; 16:9).
- **"Worship him who made heaven and earth, the sea and the springs of water" (v7).** The call rests on creation, as in Paul's preaching at Lystra (Acts 14:15). The beast rose from the sea, but God made the sea. The same four realms are struck by the trumpets (8:7–12) and soon by the bowls (16:2–4). The Creator is the Judge of what he made.
- **"Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great" (v8).** This is Babylon's first mention, announced as fallen before she is even described (chs 17–18). The words are from Isaiah 21:9. The past tense treats the future as done, because God has decided it. Historic Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and its temple in 586 BC. After Rome did the same in AD 70, Jews and Christians used "Babylon" as a code name for Rome (1 Pet 5:13 likely does). The Tyndale notes take Babylon as Rome, standing for every proud, corrupt world power. Henry, as a Protestant writing in 1710, says "By Babylon is generally understood Rome," and applies it to "the papal Babylon." JFB identifies her with "the harlot, the apostate Church." Guzik sees humanity's organized rebellion against God, religious and political.
- **"She who made all nations drink the wine of the passion of her sexual immorality" (v8).** This is from Jeremiah 51:7: "Babylon was a golden cup in the LORD's hand, making all the earth drunken." "Passion" is *thymos*, a word for strong feeling and hot anger, the same word translated "wrath" in v10. The wordplay is deliberate. Babylon pours the nations her intoxicating *thymos*, and those who drink it will be given God's *thymos*. The "immorality" is mainly spiritual: idolatry and the seductive pull of wealth and power (18:3), though in pagan feasts the two often went together (2:14, 20).
- **"If anyone worships the beast and its image and receives a mark" (v9).** The third angel speaks to the exact choice of 13:15–17. The beast threatened death to those who refused his mark. God warns of something worse for those who take it. Every reader stands between two threats and must decide which to fear (Matt 10:28). Early Christians faced a version of this when ordered to burn incense to Caesar and call him Lord.
- **"The wine of God's wrath, poured full strength into the cup of his anger" (v10).** "Full strength" is *akratos*, unmixed. Wine was normally mixed with water, so unmixed wine was drink at its strongest. This is the word's only New Testament use. The cup of wrath runs through the prophets (Ps 75:8; Jer 25:15), and Jesus prayed in Gethsemane, "let this cup pass from me" (Matt 26:39). Guzik and JFB both contrast "wrath" (*thymos*) as boiling anger with "anger" (*orgē*) as settled anger. The lexicon treats the two as near-synonyms, so that traditional contrast shouldn't be pressed. Revelation pairs them for emphasis (16:19; 19:15).
- **"Tormented with fire and sulfur in the presence of the holy angels and… the Lamb" (v10).** Fire and sulfur fell on Sodom (Gen 19:24) and became the standard picture of final judgment (Isa 34:9–10). Those who refused the Lamb's mercy stand before the Lamb as Judge (6:16).
- **"The smoke of their torment goes up forever and ever, and they have no rest, day or night" (v11).** Isaiah said of Edom, "its smoke shall go up forever" (Isa 34:10), and the same smoke rises over Babylon (19:3). "No rest, day or night" mirrors the living creatures, who "day and night… never cease" to worship (4:8). Christians read these verses in two main ways. Most of the church through history, including Henry ("forever miserable in soul and body"), JFB, Guzik and the Tyndale notes, holds to eternal conscious punishment. A minority of evangelicals hold that the wicked are finally destroyed, and read the rising smoke as a lasting memorial of a finished judgment, as with Sodom (Jude 7). Both agree the warning is real and meant to be heeded.
- **"Here is a call for the endurance of the saints, those who keep the commandments of God and their faith in Jesus" (v12).** The same call follows the beast's threats in 13:10. The warning is not for believers to gloat over. It is there so they will hold on when it costs them their living or their life.
- **"Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on" (v13).** This is the second of Revelation's seven blessings (1:3). "From now on" most likely means from this point in the conflict: those who die faithful under the beast are blessed from the moment they die. For a church facing execution, this was the word it most needed.
- **"'Blessed indeed,' says the Spirit, 'that they may rest from their labors, for their deeds follow them!'" (v13).** The Holy Spirit adds his own "yes," one of the few times he speaks directly in the book. The beast's worshipers have no rest (v11), but those who die in the Lord rest. Their deeds follow them like companions, not like a ticket sent ahead. Works don't earn heaven, but faithful work is not forgotten (1 Cor 15:58).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the angels historically: "The progress of the Reformation appears to be here set forth." He says "The gospel is an everlasting gospel; it is so in its nature, and it will be so in its consequences," and that the works of the blessed dead "do not go before as their title, or purchase." JFB calls the message "the Gospel which announces the glad tidings of the everlasting kingdom of Christ," preached as a witness to all nations rather than a promise that all will believe. On v10 it writes, "there is no drop of water to cool its heat." Guzik sees the eternal gospel as the same gospel, preached as a last call of grace before judgment. He notes angels rarely preach in Scripture, so this is no excuse for the church to neglect its mission. He contrasts the wicked, forced to drink the cup, with Jesus, who drank it willingly for his people.

#### The Harvest of the Earth (vv14–20)
- **"Seated on the cloud one like a son of man, with a golden crown on his head, and a sharp sickle in his hand" (v14).** This is Daniel 7:13 and the title of Jesus in 1:13. The crown is a *stephanos*, a victor's wreath, as JFB and Guzik both note, not the royal diadem of 19:12. Nearly all readers take the figure as Christ. A few think he is an angel, since he takes word from "another angel" (v15). Most answer that the angel only brings the Father's word about the timing, which Jesus said belongs to the Father (Mark 13:32). Jesus pictured the end of the age as a harvest (Matt 13:30, 39).
- **"Put in your sickle, and reap… for the harvest of the earth is fully ripe" (v15).** This is Joel 3:13: "Put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe. Go in, tread, for the winepress is full." "Fully ripe" is literally "dried up." God waits until the time is fully come. He is not hasty.
- **"The earth was reaped" (v16).** Readers divide over what this first harvest gathers:
  - **God's people.** In the Gospels, grain harvest usually means gathering believers into God's barn (Matt 3:12; John 4:35–38). The firstfruits of v4 lead naturally to a full harvest, and nothing is said of wrath until the second scene. Henry and JFB take this view. Henry: "when the believers are ripe for heaven, then the wheat of the earth shall be gathered into Christ's garner."
  - **Judgment.** Joel 3:13 is about judging the nations, and "dried up" sounds like over-ripeness. Guzik reads both scenes as judgment.
  - **Both.** Some see a general gathering of all people (Matt 13:30), with the grape harvest focusing on the wicked.
- **"Another angel came out from the altar, the angel who has authority over the fire" (vv17–18).** The altar is where the martyrs cried, "How long… before you will judge?" (6:9–10), and where the prayers of the saints rose and fire was thrown to the earth (8:3–5). This judgment is God's answer to his suffering people's prayers. JFB makes the link, pointing to the altar "upon which were offered the incense-accompanied prayers of all saints."
- **"Gather the clusters from the vine of the earth" (v18).** Israel was meant to be God's vine but produced wild grapes (Isa 5:1–7). Jesus is "the true vine" (John 15:1). The "vine of the earth" is its opposite: life grown from the earth, not from God.
- **"The great winepress of the wrath of God" (v19).** A winepress was a stone basin where workers trampled grapes barefoot, and the splashing juice was a natural picture of blood. Isaiah 63:3 is the source: "I have trodden the winepress alone… their lifeblood spattered on my garments." Christ returns to this at his coming, when he "will tread the winepress of the fury of the wrath of God the Almighty" (19:15).
- **"The winepress was trodden outside the city" (v20).** Winepresses stood outside town, near the vineyards. "Outside the city" was also the place of execution (Lev 24:14). The Tyndale notes take the city as Jerusalem, and Joel places the judgment of the nations in the "Valley of Jehoshaphat" (Joel 3:2, 12), which tradition later located just east of Jerusalem. Many see an irony: Jesus also "suffered outside the gate" (Heb 13:12). The judgment he bore outside the city for his people now falls outside the city on those who refused him.
- **"Blood flowed… as high as a horse's bridle, for 1,600 stadia" (v20).** A stadion was about 607 feet, so 1,600 stadia is about 184 miles, roughly the length of the land of Israel. The number is also 40 × 40, or 4 × 4 × 100. Four is Revelation's number for the earth's corners (7:1), so it may stand for judgment filling the whole land or world. Jewish apocalyptic writing used similar pictures of blood reaching a horse's chest to show total defeat (1 Enoch 100:3). Futurists usually see a real battle around Jerusalem, the same as Armageddon (16:16; 19:17–21), and Guzik suggests even then it means the spray of a vast battle, not a river five feet deep. Others read it as a picture of complete, inescapable judgment.
- **Commentators:** Henry and JFB both read the grain harvest as the gathering of the righteous and the vintage as judgment. Henry calls the winepress "some terrible calamity, probably the sword, shedding the blood of the wicked." JFB insists that "the slaughter of the apostates is what is here spoken of, not their eternal punishment," so this is the final battle, not hell. On the distance it notes, "Sixteen hundred is a square number; four by four by one hundred," and that the figure does not quite match Jerome's length of Palestine. Guzik differs on the first harvest, reading both reapings as judgment and stressing God's patience until the harvest is fully ripe. The Tyndale notes link the winepress to the battle of 19:17–21 and stress that God's judgment is just (16:5–7).

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 13 made the beast look unstoppable. Chapter 14 shows the Lamb standing on Zion with every one of his people, singing a song only the redeemed can learn. What separates the two crowds is whose name they carry. The cup of God's wrath is real and full strength, and Jesus asked in Gethsemane that it might pass from him, then drank it so that those who trust him never will. He suffered "outside the gate," and now he comes on the cloud with a sickle to gather his harvest home. That is why heaven can say, in a time of martyrdom, "Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord."
