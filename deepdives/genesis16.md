*Setting:* About ten years have passed since Abram left Haran at seventy-five (12:4; 16:3). In chapter 15 God promised him an heir "from your own body" and descendants as many as the stars, and "he believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness" (15:4–6). But the promise named Abram as father and never named Sarai as mother, and Sarai is still barren, the fact Genesis set down before Abram's story even began (11:30). Chapter 16 is what happens when faith that has just been praised grows tired of waiting. It is a family story about a couple, an Egyptian slave woman and the child she carries, and it is the first time in Scripture that the angel of the LORD appears. He comes not to Abram but to a runaway servant in the desert.

#### Sarai and Hagar (vv1–16)
- **"Now Sarai, Abram's wife, had borne him no children" (v1).** The chapter opens by repeating the problem of 11:30. Sarai is about seventy-five now (Abram is ten years older, 17:17), well past childbearing. **Hagar** is "a female Egyptian servant," most likely one of the servants Pharaoh gave Abram during the trip to Egypt (12:16). That earlier episode was itself a lapse of faith, and its consequences have followed the family home.
- **"The LORD has prevented me from bearing children" (v2).** Sarai is right that God holds the womb (30:2; 1 Sam 1:5), but she draws the wrong conclusion from it. If the LORD has closed her womb, she reasons, she must find another way. Nothing in the text suggests she or Abram prayed, which is what Isaac later does for barren Rebekah (25:21).
- **"It may be that I shall obtain children by her" (v2).** Literally, "perhaps I shall be built up by her." The verb is *banah*, "to build," and it sounds like *ben*, "son." In the ancient world a family was a house, and children built it. Rachel uses the same words when she gives her servant Bilhah to Jacob (30:3).
- **A legal custom, not a secret affair.** Marriage contracts from Nuzi in Mesopotamia required a childless wife to provide a slave woman for her husband, and the Laws of Hammurabi (§§144–147) regulate the same arrangement. The child counted as the wife's, and could become the heir. So Sarai's plan was respectable by the standards of her world. The Tyndale notes make the point that a custom being acceptable didn't make it the way God meant to keep his promise.
- **"And Abram listened to the voice of Sarai" (v2).** Many interpreters hear an echo of the fall. God told Adam, "Because you have listened to the voice of your wife" (3:17), and in v3 Sarai "took" Hagar and "gave her to Abram her husband," as Eve "took" the fruit and "gave some to her husband" (3:6). The writer doesn't spell out the parallel, but the wording invites it: a husband passively going along with a plan that puts human management in place of trust.
- **"After Abram had lived ten years in the land of Canaan" (v3).** The note of time explains the pressure. Ten years of waiting have passed. Abram will be eighty-six when Ishmael is born (v16) and ninety-nine before God speaks again of Sarai's son (17:1, 19). Waiting would get longer, not shorter.
- **"Gave her to Abram her husband as a wife" (v3).** The word is "wife," not "concubine," though Hagar's standing was clearly lower. Throughout the chapter Sarai keeps calling her "my servant," and the angel will call her "servant of Sarai" (v8), never Abram's wife.
- **"She looked with contempt on her mistress" (v4).** Literally, "her mistress became light in her eyes." The verb is *qalal*, to be slight or of little account. It is the same root God used in his promise to Abram: "him who dishonors you I will curse" (12:3). Hagar's pregnancy proves the problem was never Abram, and in a culture that measured a woman's worth by her children, the slave now looks down on the barren mistress. Proverbs names this exact situation among the things the earth cannot bear: "a maidservant when she displaces her mistress" (Prov 30:21–23).
- **"May the wrong done to me be on you!" (v5).** The word for "wrong" is *ḥamas*, usually "violence," the word for the corruption that filled the earth before the flood (6:11). The lexicon gives it a wider sense here: injury, harsh treatment, abusive words. Sarai blames Abram for a plan that was her own idea, and then calls on the LORD to "judge between you and me." The marriage that was meant to receive the promise is now arguing in court terms.
- **"Your servant is in your power; do to her as you please" (v6).** Abram keeps the peace by stepping back. Hagar is carrying his child, yet he hands her over without a word of protection. The Laws of Hammurabi (§146) allowed a mistress to demote a slave-wife who claimed equality after bearing children, but not to sell her. Abram stays within the custom and does nothing more.
- **"Then Sarai dealt harshly with her, and she fled" (v6).** The verb is *'anah*, to humble, mistreat, afflict. It is the same verb God used one chapter earlier when he told Abram that his descendants would be "afflicted for four hundred years" in a foreign land (15:13), and the verb used for Egypt's treatment of Israel (Exod 1:11–12). The irony is sharp: a Hebrew mistress afflicts an Egyptian slave, and the slave runs away toward Egypt. Later the roles will be reversed.
- **"The angel of the LORD found her by a spring of water in the wilderness" (v7).** This is the first appearance of "the angel of the LORD" in the Bible, and he comes to an Egyptian slave woman, not to the patriarch. "Found" suggests he was looking for her. **Shur** lay on the route from southern Canaan toward the Egyptian border (25:18; Exod 15:22), so Hagar was heading home. The name is often taken to mean "wall," after a line of Egyptian border forts, but the lexicon calls that explanation doubtful.
- **Who is the angel of the LORD?** In v13 Hagar names "the LORD who spoke to her," and the angel speaks as God himself: "I will surely multiply your offspring" (v10). Elsewhere he is also distinguished from the LORD (Zech 1:12). The Tyndale notes take him as an appearance of God, likely a Christophany, the Son appearing before his incarnation. Many Christian interpreters agree. Others, including many Jewish readers, understand an angelic messenger who carries God's name and speaks with his full authority. The text holds both sides together: the messenger is sent, and the messenger is God.
- **"Hagar, servant of Sarai, where have you come from and where are you going?" (v8).** He calls her by name, the first time anyone in the story has. Sarai and Abram only ever said "my servant" and "your servant." The two questions are not for his information, as with "Where are you?" to Adam (3:9). They invite her to tell the truth about her life, and she answers honestly about the first. She has no answer to the second.
- **"Return to your mistress and submit to her" (v9).** "Submit" here is the same verb *'anah* that described Sarai's harsh treatment in v6, now in a form meaning "humble yourself." The command is hard, and modern readers rightly feel its weight. It is not approval of what Sarai did. In context, the desert meant likely death for a pregnant woman alone, and her child's future was tied to Abram's household. The command comes wrapped in promises, and God does not send her back unseen.
- **"I will surely multiply your offspring so that they cannot be numbered for multitude" (v10).** These are nearly the words God spoke to Abram (13:16; 15:5), and later to Abraham about Ishmael (17:20; 21:13, 18). Hagar is one of very few women in Scripture to receive a promise of countless descendants spoken directly to her. Ishmael's twelve sons become twelve princes (25:12–16).
- **"You shall call his name Ishmael, because the LORD has listened to your affliction" (v11).** *Yishma'el* means "God hears." Her "affliction" is *'oni*, from the same root as "dealt harshly" in v6. God names exactly what was done to her. The same phrase is used when God sees Leah's affliction (29:32) and Israel's misery in Egypt (Exod 3:7). This angelic birth announcement ("you are pregnant and shall bear a son… you shall call his name") follows a pattern repeated for Samson's mother (Judg 13:3–5) and for Mary (Luke 1:31).
- **"He shall be a wild donkey of a man" (v12).** The *pere'* is the wild donkey of the steppe, which Job describes as free, untamed and roaming the wilderness (Job 39:5–8). To a nomad that was not an insult. It means a free, hardy man of the desert whom no one would ever own, which is a striking promise to give a slave woman. "His hand against everyone and everyone's hand against him" adds that his life would be full of conflict.
- **"He shall dwell over against all his kinsmen" (v12).** The phrase is literally "in the face of all his brothers." It can mean living in defiance of them or simply living alongside them, to their east. Genesis 25:18 repeats it when Ishmael's descendants settle "from Havilah to Shur." The tension between Ishmael's line and Isaac's appears later in Scripture (21:9; Ps 83:6), but the text also shows Isaac and Ishmael burying their father together (25:9).
- **"You are a God of seeing" (v13).** In Hebrew, *El Roi*. The lexicon gives *ro'i* as "seeing," so "a God who sees." Hagar is the first person in Scripture to give God a name. Her second line is difficult in the Hebrew, and translations differ; the ESV gives "Truly here I have seen him who looks after me." Either way, the slave whom nobody in Abram's tent really saw has been seen by God, and has seen him and lived (compare Jacob at Peniel, 32:30).
- **"Therefore the well was called Beer-lahai-roi" (v14).** The name means "well of the Living One who sees me." **Kadesh** is Kadesh-barnea in the northern Sinai, where Israel later camped for years. **Bered** is unknown. The well becomes part of the family's story: Isaac is living there when Rebekah arrives (24:62), and he settles there after Abraham's death (25:11). The place where God saw an outcast becomes the home of the son of promise.
- **"Abram called the name of his son, whom Hagar bore, Ishmael" (v15).** Hagar must have told Abram what happened in the desert, because he gives the boy the name the angel chose. So Hagar returned as she was told, and the family accepted God's word through her. Three times in two verses the text says "Hagar bore Abram" a son. The son is truly Abram's, but he isn't the son of the promise, and for thirteen years Abram seems to think he is (17:18).
- **"Abram was eighty-six years old" (v16).** The chapter ends with a number. The next time Genesis speaks, Abram is ninety-nine (17:1). Thirteen silent years lie between.
- **Hagar in Galatians.** Paul reads this story as an allegory of two covenants (Gal 4:21–31). Ishmael was "born according to the flesh," by human planning; Isaac was "born through promise." Hagar stands for Sinai and slavery, Sarah for the free Jerusalem above. Paul isn't judging Hagar as a person. He uses the two births to show the difference between trying to secure God's blessing by our own means and receiving it as a gift.
- **Commentators:** All three agree with the Tyndale notes that the plan was a failure of trust, not of custom. Henry writes, "He that believes does not make haste," and "Fleshly wisdom, as it anticipates God's time of mercy, so it puts us out of God's way." JFB calls it "a wrong step—indicating a want of simple reliance on God." Guzik adds that Abram, who should have led his household by affirming God's power, failed to lead, and that he went on to abdicate responsibility for Hagar. On v4 Henry observes that "mean and servile spirits, when favoured and advanced… are apt to grow haughty," but he also says God "is displeased with" the hardships "harsh masters unreasonably put upon their servants," and that "those are not always in the right who are most loud and forward in appealing to God." JFB says Sarai's harsh treatment may have meant "bursts of temper, or blows, as the original may bear." The lexicon supports "mistreat" or "afflict" but doesn't specify what form it took. Guzik identifies the angel as the eternal Son, reasoning from John 1:18 that no one has seen the Father, which is close to the Tyndale view. Henry, on v12, writes, "His hand against every man—this is his sin; and every man's hand against him—this is his punishment," and on v13, "The God with whom we have to do is a seeing God." JFB reads v12 as describing the Arab peoples' "rude, turbulent, and plundering character," a 19th-century generalization that goes beyond the text, which speaks of one man's free and contested life. Henry links the chapter to Galatians 4 and remarks that "the carnal seed in the church are sooner brought forth than the spiritual." JFB has no comment on vv2, 4, 6, 8–10 or 14–16.

#### Pointing to Christ
Genesis 16 is a story of human failure: a promise grasped at, a slave used and then thrown aside, a husband who stays silent. Yet the LORD goes looking for the one person no one else in the story bothered to call by name. The angel who finds Hagar at the spring, whom many Christians have seen as the Son before his birth, shows a heart Jesus would show openly: he sat by a well and spoke to a Samaritan woman whom others ignored (John 4), and he came "to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10). Hagar's child, born of human planning, couldn't carry the promise. That would take a birth only God could bring about, first Isaac's and finally Christ's, the true seed of Abraham (Gal 3:16). The God who sees and hears the afflicted is the one who sent his Son to be "afflicted" himself (Isa 53:7), so that all who are in Christ are children "not of the slave but of the free woman" (Gal 4:31).
