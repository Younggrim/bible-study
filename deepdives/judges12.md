*Setting:* Jephthah has beaten the Ammonites "with a great blow" (11:33), but he came home to his only daughter and a vow he would not break. Now the victory brings more trouble, this time from inside Israel. The proud tribe of Ephraim, which once complained to Gideon for leaving them out (8:1–3), marches across the Jordan to complain again. Gideon calmed them with a soft answer. Jephthah answers with a sword, and Israelites kill Israelites at the river crossings. The chapter then closes with three more short notices of "minor judges," Ibzan, Elon and Abdon, before the long Samson story begins.

#### Jephthah's Conflict with Ephraim (vv1–7)
- **"The men of Ephraim were called to arms, and they crossed to Zaphon" (v1).** Ephraim lived west of the Jordan in the central hills. Zaphon was a town in the Jordan Valley on the east side, in Gad's territory near where the Jabbok meets the Jordan. The Hebrew word *tsaphon* also means "north," which is how older translations took it.
- **"Why did you… not call us…? We will burn your house over you with fire" (v1).** This is the same complaint they made to Gideon, but now with a death threat. A man who had just lost his only child is told his house will be burned with him in it. Ephraim was the leading tribe of the north, home to Joshua and the tabernacle at Shiloh, and it did not like victories it could not share.
- **"When I called you, you did not save me" (v2).** Jephthah says he did call them. The earlier chapter does not record that call, so it may simply be unmentioned. JFB thinks Ephraim deliberately held back until the danger had passed. Either way, Jephthah's answer is the opposite of Gideon's (8:2–3). Gideon flattered Ephraim. Jephthah blames them.
- **"I took my life in my hand" (v3).** A Hebrew idiom for risking death (1 Sam 19:5; 28:21). Jephthah again gives the credit to God: "the LORD gave them into my hand." His question, "Why then have you come up to me this day to fight against me?", is a fair one.
- **"You are fugitives of Ephraim, you Gileadites" (v4).** Ephraim's insult explains why the Gileadites were so ready to fight. The Ephraimites sneered that the people of Gilead were runaways from the western tribes, refugees living on the fringe "in the midst of Ephraim and Manasseh." It was a slur on their standing as true Israelites. Gilead's half of Manasseh was in fact a full part of Israel, settled there by Moses (Num 32:39–40).
- **"Captured the fords of the Jordan" (v5).** The Jordan could only be crossed at shallow points. Ehud (3:28) and Gideon (7:24) held the fords against foreign enemies. Now Israelites hold them against Israelites. The tactic of Israel's wars of deliverance is turned against a brother tribe.
- **"Then say Shibboleth" (v6).** The word means "ear of grain" or "flowing stream," a fitting word at a river ford. The Ephraimites' dialect had no "sh" sound at the start of a word, so they said "Sibboleth." One syllable gave them away. The word has passed into English: a "shibboleth" is a test word, custom or slogan that shows which group someone belongs to. Armies have used tests like this throughout history.
- **"42,000 of the Ephraimites fell" (v6).** That is a staggering loss for one tribe, more than the whole of Ephraim's fighting men in the second census (32,500, Num 26:37). The Hebrew word for "thousand" (*'eleph*) can also mean a clan or military unit, so some scholars read "forty-two units." Either way, it was a slaughter, and it was Israel killing itself.
- **"Jephthah judged Israel six years" (v7).** His time is short. He dies without heirs, since his only child is gone, and is "buried in his city in Gilead." The Hebrew literally says "in the cities of Gilead," which the ESV and most translations smooth to "his city" following the Greek. Despite everything, Jephthah is named among the people of faith in Hebrews 11:32. God used a flawed man, and the record does not hide his flaws.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Pride was at the bottom of the quarrel; only by that comes contention," and "No contentions are so bitter as those between brethren or rivals for honour." JFB describes Ephraim's "jealous, rash, and irritable temper" and says they "had purposely delayed appearing till the crisis was past." JFB defines Shibboleth as "a stream" and Sibboleth as "a burden." The lexicon supports "stream" (and "ear of grain"), but the mispronounced form is simply the same word said with an "s." It is not a different word meaning "burden," so the contrast should not be pressed. Guzik sees Ephraim as people who would not step out when asked but complained once God was glorified, and he reminds Christians that a true servant does not need the credit. He also draws the lesson that speech shows who people really are. The Tyndale notes contrast Jephthah's blaming answer with Gideon's soft one (Prov 15:1).

#### Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon (vv8–15)
- **Three short notices.** As with Tola and Jair (10:1–5), these entries give a name, a hometown, years of service and a burial place. They cover twenty-five years in eight verses, and none mentions an enemy. Matthew Henry: "The happiest life of individuals, and the happiest state of society, is that which affords the fewest remarkable events."
- **"Ibzan of Bethlehem" (v8).** Most readers think of Bethlehem in Judah, David's town. But there was also a Bethlehem in Zebulun in the north (Josh 19:15), and since the next two judges are northerners, some scholars think this is the one meant. A Jewish tradition in the Talmud identifies Ibzan with Boaz of Bethlehem, Ruth's husband, but nothing in the text supports that.
- **"Thirty daughters he gave in marriage outside his clan" (v9).** Ibzan had thirty sons and thirty daughters and arranged marriages for all of them outside his clan. Marriage was the way ancient families made alliances, and this was a man building a network of influence. Large families, many wives and wide alliances are marks of a ruler acting like a small king.
- **"Elon the Zebulunite" (vv11–12).** Ten years, buried at Aijalon in Zebulun. In Hebrew the names Elon and Aijalon are spelled with nearly the same letters, a small echo between the man and his burial place. Elon was also the name of a clan in Zebulun (Gen 46:14). This Aijalon is in the north, not the better-known Aijalon in the south where the sun stood still (Josh 10:12).
- **"Abdon the son of Hillel the Pirathonite" (vv13–15).** Forty sons and thirty grandsons "who rode on seventy donkeys." As with Jair (10:4), riding donkeys was a sign of wealth and rank. Seventy is the number of Gideon's sons (8:30), and the echo is a quiet warning: a large ruling family did not end well for Gideon. Pirathon was in Ephraim's hill country, the hometown of one of David's mighty men (2 Sam 23:30).
- **"In the hill country of the Amalekites" (v15).** The Amalekites were desert raiders, Israel's oldest enemy (Exod 17:8–16). This note suggests some of them had once lived in, or given their name to, a part of Ephraim's hills (compare 5:14, "their root in Amalek").
- **What these lists are for.** The minor judges show Israel being governed in quieter years, without great battles. But there is no sign of spiritual renewal under them either. The sons and donkeys and arranged marriages show leaders growing rich, while the book's refrain is about to sound again: "the people of Israel again did what was evil" (13:1). After Abdon there is only Samson.
- **Commentators:** Henry ends with a heavenward thought: what matters most is "to enjoy communion with God our Saviour while we live." Guzik records the facts of these judges without drawing much from them. JFB passes over vv8–15 without notes, saying only that the records of these judges concern their families and estate. The Tyndale notes observe that if Ibzan's Bethlehem was the northern one, each of the twelve judges in the book would come from a different tribe, a pattern some scholars think the author intended.

#### Pointing to Christ
At the Jordan fords a single word decided who lived and who died, and Israel turned its weapons on itself over pride and an insult. The story shows what happens when God's people care more about honor and tribe than about the God who saved them. Jesus came to break that pattern. He makes one people out of rivals, "and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility" (Eph 2:14). His people are not marked by an accent or a tribe but by their love for one another (John 13:35). And when the test is finally given, it is not how a person pronounces a word but whether they call on his name: "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Rom 10:13).
