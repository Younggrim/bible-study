*Setting:* The last chapter of 2 Samuel closes the appendix that began in chapter 21. Its first story told of a national disaster caused by Saul's sin, a famine ended when justice was done and God "responded to the plea for the land" (21:14). This last story tells of a national disaster caused by David's own sin, ended when David offers sacrifice and God "responded to the plea for the land" (v25). The date is uncertain, probably late in David's reign, since the census takes nearly ten months and assumes a settled kingdom stretching from Dan to Beersheba. The book that began with the death of Saul ends not with David's death but at an altar on a threshing floor north of the city of David, the hill that will become the site of Solomon's temple. A parallel account appears in 1 Chronicles 21, with some differences noted below.

#### David's Census (vv1–17)
- **"Again the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel" (v1).** "Again" links back to the famine in 21:1. The reason for God's anger is not given. Some think of the revolts of Absalom and Sheba, when Israel turned against the king God had chosen. The text does not say, and it is wise not to fill the gap with certainty.
- **"He incited David against them" (v1).** "Incited" is *suth*, to stir up or entice, often toward something harmful. David used it to Saul: "If it is the LORD who has stirred you up against me" (1 Sam 26:19). The parallel in 1 Chronicles 21:1 says, "Then Satan stood against Israel and incited David to number Israel." Both are true, from different angles. Satan tempted, intending harm. God, in his sovereign purpose, allowed it as a judgment on Israel, as he allowed Satan to test Job (Job 1–2) and sent a lying spirit to Ahab's prophets (1 Kings 22:19–23). Scripture holds both truths together: God is never the author of sin (Jas 1:13), and nothing happens outside his rule.
- **"Go, number Israel and Judah" (v1).** Why was a census wrong? Moses counted Israel twice at God's command (Num 1; 26). The law required a ransom payment at every census "that there be no plague among them" (Exod 30:12), which shows the people belonged to God, not the king. The text never says exactly what David did wrong, but the clues point to pride and trust in numbers. He wants **"to know the number"** (v2), and the count is of **"valiant men who drew the sword"** (v9): a military muster. A king with God's promises in hand wanted to know how strong he was. Psalm 20:7 had said, "Some trust in chariots… but we trust in the name of the LORD our God."
- **"Why does my lord the king delight in this thing?" (v3).** Joab, of all people, is the voice of conscience. He has murdered two rivals without a second thought, but he sees that this is wrong. Chronicles adds his words, "Why should it be a cause of guilt for Israel?" (1 Chr 21:3). He wishes the nation a hundredfold growth, which is God's business to give, not David's to count.
- **"The king's word prevailed" (v4).** Commanders and general together could not change his mind.
- **The route of the census (vv5–8).** It starts east of the Jordan at Aroer on the Arnon gorge (the border with Moab), moves north through Gad and Gilead to Dan, swings west to the coast near Sidon and Tyre, and comes down through the old Canaanite cities to Beersheba in the far south. It is a counterclockwise sweep around the whole land, taking **"nine months and twenty days."** That was plenty of time for David to stop it, and he did not.
- **"800,000… and the men of Judah were 500,000" (v9).** Chronicles gives 1,100,000 for Israel and 470,000 for Judah, and adds that Joab refused to count Levi and Benjamin, "for the king's command was abhorrent to Joab" (1 Chr 21:5–6). Several explanations have been offered. The Tyndale notes suggest Chronicles includes a standing army of 300,000 not counted here, and that 500,000 is a round number. JFB works out a detailed reconciliation along similar lines. Large numbers in ancient texts were also easily miscopied. Readers who hold Scripture in high regard admit the figures are hard to square exactly, and the main point is untouched.
- **"David's heart struck him" (v10).** The same phrase as when he cut off the corner of Saul's robe and his conscience troubled him (1 Sam 24:5). Here the conscience comes too late to stop the census, but it comes before any prophet does. **"I have sinned greatly… I have done very foolishly."** The word "foolishly" is the one Samuel used to Saul when he would not wait (1 Sam 13:13). The difference between David and Saul is not that David never sinned but that he confessed when he did.
- **"The prophet Gad, David's seer" (v11).** Gad had been with David since his outlaw days, when he told him to leave the cave and go into Judah (1 Sam 22:5). "Seer" was an older word for prophet (1 Sam 9:9). Chronicles says Gad and Nathan helped write the history of David's reign (1 Chr 29:29).
- **"Three things I offer you" (v12).** Forgiveness does not always remove consequences. David's guilt can be taken away, but there will still be discipline, and God lets David choose its form.
- **"Three years of famine" (v13).** The Hebrew text of Samuel reads "seven years." The ESV, with the Greek translation, follows 1 Chronicles 21:12, which reads "three," matching the pattern of three years, three months, three days. JFB tries to keep "seven" by adding the famine just past, but most think "seven" is a slip in copying. **"Flee three months before your foes"** was something David knew well from Absalom's revolt. **"Three days' pestilence"** would come straight from God.
- **"Let us fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is great; but let me not fall into the hand of man" (v14).** David chooses the punishment that leaves the most room for mercy. He had seen how cruel men could be, and how kind God was even when he disciplined. A plague also struck the palace as easily as anywhere else. David did not choose the option that would hurt others while sparing himself.
- **"70,000 men" (v15).** The count of fighting men that David wanted to boast in is cut down. What he trusted in is what God reduced.
- **"The LORD relented from the calamity" (v16).** "Relented" is *nacham*, to be sorry or change course. It does not mean God made a mistake, but that he turned from carrying out a judgment, as he did when Moses prayed (Exod 32:14). **"It is enough; now stay your hand."** The destroying angel, like the one at the Passover (Exod 12:23), stops at the edge of Jerusalem.
- **"By the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite" (v16).** A threshing floor was a flat, open place, usually on high ground to catch the wind, where harvested grain was beaten and tossed so the breeze blew away the chaff. Araunah (called Ornan in Chronicles) was one of the original Jebusite inhabitants of Jerusalem, living on in the city David took from his people (5:6–9). His floor was on the hill just north of the city of David.
- **"These sheep, what have they done?" (v17).** David the shepherd boy is now the shepherd-king, and he prays like one. In v10 he asked forgiveness for himself. Now he stands between the angel and the people: **"Please let your hand be against me and against my father's house."** Chronicles says David and the elders were dressed in sackcloth, lying face down, when they saw the angel "with a drawn sword in his hand stretched out over Jerusalem" (1 Chr 21:16). The Tyndale notes add a caution: Israel was not simply innocent, since God's anger was against Israel from the start (v1). But David's plea is still the right instinct for a leader.
- **Commentators:** Henry puts the sin plainly: "The pride of David's heart, was his sin in numbering of the people." He adds, "God judges not of sin as we do," and "God seldom allows those whom he loves the pleasures they sinfully covet." JFB says "The act of numbering the people was not in itself sinful," since Moses did it by God's command. The fault was David's motive, "pride and vainglory," distrust of God, and perhaps "ambitious designs of conquest." On v1, JFB says God "permitted Satan to tempt David," Satan being "the active mover." Guzik reads the "he" of v1 in light of 1 Chronicles 21:1 the same way, and ties the sin to Exodus 30:12: Israel was God's to count, and a ransom was owed. JFB and Guzik both think David chose the plague partly because it would put him at the same risk as his people.

#### David Builds an Altar (vv18–25)
- **"Go up, raise an altar to the LORD on the threshing floor of Araunah" (v18).** The place where the angel stopped becomes the place of sacrifice. God himself chooses the spot.
- **"When Araunah looked down, he saw the king" (v20).** The threshing floor was above the city, so the king and his servants had to climb to it. Araunah bows with his face to the ground.
- **"Here are the oxen for the burnt offering and the threshing sledges… for the wood" (v22).** Threshing sledges were heavy wooden boards with sharp stones set underneath, dragged over the grain by oxen. Araunah offers everything: the place, the animals and the fuel. **"All this, O king, Araunah gives to the king" (v23).** The Hebrew can be read "All this, O king, Araunah gives," or "all this the king Araunah gives," which led some to think Araunah had been the last Jebusite king of Jerusalem. JFB suggests he may have been a former chieftain. That is a possibility, not something the text makes clear.
- **"I will not offer burnt offerings to the LORD my God that cost me nothing" (v24).** One of the most quoted lines in the book. A gift that costs nothing is not really a gift. David could have taken the floor by royal right, as Samuel warned kings would (1 Sam 8:14). Instead he pays for it, so that the offering is truly his.
- **"Fifty shekels of silver" (v24).** About 1¼ pounds of silver. Chronicles says David paid "600 shekels of gold" for "the site" (1 Chr 21:25). The usual explanation is that the 50 shekels bought the threshing floor and oxen for this day's sacrifice, and the 600 shekels of gold bought the whole surrounding area for the future temple. JFB takes it that way.
- **"Burnt offerings and peace offerings" (v25).** Burnt offerings were wholly burned, a sign of complete surrender and of atonement for sin (Lev 1:4). Peace offerings were shared in a meal, a sign of restored fellowship with God (Lev 3; 7:11–15). Atonement first, then fellowship. Chronicles adds that God answered "with fire from heaven upon the altar" (1 Chr 21:26).
- **"So the LORD responded to the plea for the land" (v25).** The same words that closed the Gibeonite story (21:14). The appendix and the whole of 2 Samuel end with a plague stopped by sacrifice and a prayer answered.
- **Why this place matters.** Chronicles adds, "Here shall be the house of the LORD God" (1 Chr 22:1). Solomon built the temple "on Mount Moriah… on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite" (2 Chr 3:1). Moriah is where Abraham was told to offer Isaac and where God provided a ram instead (Gen 22:2, 13–14). So the place where Abraham's son was spared, where the destroying angel stopped, and where Israel's sacrifices would be offered for a thousand years is the same hill. Most identify it with today's Temple Mount.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the end of the plague at Jerusalem as mercy for the sake of a greater sacrifice, connecting the place to Isaac on Moriah and to Christ. He insists that worship must cost something, "God hates robbery for burnt-offering," and calls Christ the true altar and sacrifice by which God's wrath is turned away. Guzik makes the same Moriah connection and draws the plain lesson that worship which costs nothing is worth nothing. He notes that burnt offerings dealt with sin and peace offerings celebrated fellowship. JFB suggests Araunah gave "with royal munificence," explains the 50 shekels as the price of the floor and oxen only, and reads the two kinds of sacrifice as expiation followed by thanksgiving.

#### Pointing to Christ
2 Samuel ends with a shepherd-king asking that the judgment fall on him rather than on his sheep, a destroying angel stopped, and a costly sacrifice on Moriah. David could only ask to bear the punishment. Jesus, the good shepherd, actually laid down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). On the hill where Abraham's son was spared, God would one day not spare his own Son (Rom 8:32), and the sacrifice that turned away wrath cost the giver everything.
