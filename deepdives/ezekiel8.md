*Setting:* September 592 BC, in Ezekiel's house by the Chebar canal in Babylonia. It is fourteen months since his first vision, and he has spent much of that time acting out the siege of Jerusalem. Now the elders of the exiled community are sitting in front of him, probably hoping for good news from home. Instead God lifts the prophet up "in visions of God" and takes him six hundred miles to Jerusalem, to the temple where he had been raised to serve as a priest. Chapters 8–11 are one long vision. In chapter 8 Ezekiel is led on a tour of the temple courts, from the outer gates toward the center, and at each step he sees worse idolatry. Chapter 9 shows the city judged, chapter 10 the glory of God rising to leave, and chapter 11 the glory departing from the city altogether. The background is the reign of Zedekiah, Judah's last king, about six years before Jerusalem fell. Josiah's great reform (622 BC) had cleared idols out of the temple, but within a generation they were back.

#### Abominations in the Temple (vv1–18)
- **One heading, four scenes.** The ESV gives the chapter one heading. Ezekiel is shown four scenes, each one introduced by "you will see still greater abominations" (vv6, 13, 15): an idol at the north gate (vv3–6), elders burning incense in a hidden room (vv7–13), women weeping for Tammuz (vv14–15), and men worshiping the sun at the temple door (vv16–18). Each scene is closer to the heart of the temple, and each involves a different group: the public, the leaders, the women, and those nearest the altar.
- **"In the sixth year, in the sixth month, on the fifth day" (v1).** That is, the sixth year of King Jehoiachin's exile, about September 18, 592 BC. Ezekiel dates his visions with care (1:1–2; 20:1; 24:1), like a man keeping a legal record.
- **"As I sat in my house, with the elders of Judah sitting before me" (v1).** Since 3:24 Ezekiel has stayed at home, and people come to him. The elders were the heads of families and leaders of the community in exile. They come to the prophet again in 14:1 and 20:1. Whatever they hoped to hear, what follows is the reason there is no good news. Ezekiel reports the vision to them at its end (11:25).
- **"A form that had the appearance of a man" (v2).** The Hebrew text reads "fire" (*'esh*), but the Greek Old Testament reads "man" (*'ish*), a one-letter difference, and the ESV follows it. The figure, fire below the waist and gleaming above, matches the one on the throne in 1:26–27. This is the LORD himself, or one who bears his glory, not an ordinary angel. **"Like gleaming metal"** renders *ḥashmal*, the same mysterious glowing substance of 1:4, 27.
- **"Took me by a lock of my head" (v3).** The hand takes Ezekiel by the hair and the Spirit carries him **"in visions of God."** His body stayed in Babylon (11:24 says the vision lifted from him there). What he saw was real, but he saw it by revelation, not by physical travel.
- **"The image of jealousy, which provokes to jealousy" (v3).** The word for "image" is *semel*, rare in the Bible. It is used of the idol King Manasseh set up in the temple, "the image of the idol that he had made" (2 Chr 33:7, 15), and in the law's ban on "the form (*semel*) of any figure" (Deut 4:16). Many think this was a statue of the Canaanite goddess Asherah, since Manasseh had put an Asherah image in the temple too (2 Kings 21:7), and Josiah had burned it (2 Kings 23:6). Now one stands there again. It "provokes to jealousy" because the LORD is a husband who will not share his bride (Exod 20:5; Deut 32:16, 21).
- **"The glory of the God of Israel was there" (v4).** The irony is sharp. The glory of God and the idol stand side by side in the same courts. The glory that once filled Solomon's temple (1 Kings 8:10–11) is still present, but not for long.
- **"North of the altar gate" (v5).** The north gate of the inner court was where sacrificial animals were brought (Lev 1:11 places the slaughter on the north side of the altar), and the royal palace lay to the south and the north approach was the main way in. An idol stood at the doorway every worshiper passed.
- **"To drive me far from my sanctuary" (v6).** This is the key to chapters 8–11. Their sin is not just breaking a rule. It is pushing God out of his own house. By chapter 11 he will have gone.
- **"There was a hole in the wall… dig in the wall" (vv7–8).** This part of the vision has a dreamlike quality. Ezekiel digs through and finds a hidden door. The point is that this sin was secret. Some of the worst idolatry was not on display at the gate but behind walls.
- **"Every form of creeping things and loathsome beasts" (v10).** Carved or painted on the walls all around were reptiles, insects and unclean animals, exactly what the law forbade Israel to make images of (Deut 4:17–18) and what the law called unclean (Lev 11). Many think the influence was Egyptian, where gods were often pictured as animals. Judah's leaders were looking to Egypt for help against Babylon at this time (17:15; Jer 37:5–7). Paul describes the same downward slide: they "exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling… birds and animals and creeping things" (Rom 1:23).
- **"Seventy men of the elders of the house of Israel" (v11).** The number recalls the seventy elders who went up the mountain with Moses and "saw the God of Israel" (Exod 24:9–11), and the seventy who received a share of Moses' Spirit (Num 11:16–25). Their successors now stand in a dark room offering incense to animal pictures. Burning incense was the priests' job (Num 16:40; 2 Chr 26:18), so these laymen are both idolaters and usurpers.
- **"Jaazaniah the son of Shaphan" (v11).** This is one of the saddest names in the chapter. Shaphan was Josiah's secretary who read the newly found Book of the Law to the king and helped launch the reform (2 Kings 22:8–14). His other sons were faithful: Ahikam protected Jeremiah from a mob (Jer 26:24), and Gemariah's room was where Baruch read Jeremiah's scroll (Jer 36:10). Shaphan's grandson Gedaliah later governed Judah after the fall. Jaazaniah went the other way. His name means "the LORD hears," and he lives as though the LORD does not. He is a different man from the Jaazaniah son of Azzur in 11:1.
- **"In the dark, each in his room of pictures" (v12).** "Room of pictures" (*maskit*) means carved images or idol-figures. The phrase may mean each man had his own private shrine as well as the shared one. **"The LORD does not see us, the LORD has forsaken the land."** This is the root of it all. They had decided God was either blind or gone. Perhaps the deportation of 597 BC convinced them that the LORD had failed, and other gods deserved a try. The psalms describe the wicked saying the same (Ps 10:11; 94:7). God answers by showing Ezekiel exactly what they did in the dark (Luke 12:2–3).
- **"There sat women weeping for Tammuz" (v14).** This is the only mention of Tammuz in the Bible. Tammuz (Sumerian *Dumuzi*) was a Mesopotamian god of shepherds and vegetation, lover of the goddess Ishtar. In the myth he dies and goes down to the underworld, and women mourned him every year in the dry summer when plants withered. Later the Greeks knew a similar figure as Adonis. The fourth month of the Jewish calendar still bears his name, Tammuz (June–July), a leftover from Babylonian month names adopted during the exile. Women weeping for a dead nature god sat at the very gate of the house of the living God.
- **"At the entrance of the temple of the LORD, between the porch and the altar" (v16).** Now Ezekiel stands in the inner court, the space only priests should enter. "Between the porch and the altar" is where priests stood to pray for the people: "Between the vestibule and the altar let the priests, the ministers of the LORD, weep and say, 'Spare your people'" (Joel 2:17). Here about twenty-five men stand **"with their backs to the temple of the LORD, and their faces toward the east, worshiping the sun."** The temple faced east, so to face the rising sun they had to turn their backs on the Most Holy Place. It is a perfect picture of apostasy. Sun worship had come in under Manasseh (2 Kings 21:5) and Josiah had removed the horses and chariots dedicated to the sun (2 Kings 23:11). Twenty-five may be the heads of the twenty-four priestly divisions plus the high priest (1 Chr 24), though the text does not say so. Centuries later, the Mishnah records that at the Feast of Booths the priests would go to the east gate, turn around to face the temple, and say: "Our fathers when they were in this place turned with their backs toward the temple and their faces toward the east… but as for us, our eyes are turned toward the LORD" (Sukkah 5:4).
- **"Is it too light a thing… that they should fill the land with violence" (v17).** False worship and social injustice come as a pair. A people who turn their backs on God soon turn on each other. Chapter 7 already said "the land is full of bloody crimes" (7:23).
- **"Behold, they put the branch to their nose" (v17).** No one is sure what this means. Suggestions include a ritual of holding a sacred twig or bundle of plants to the face during sun worship (later Persian priests held a bundle of twigs called the *barsom*), an obscene or insulting gesture, or a figure of speech for contempt like "thumbing the nose." There is also an old Jewish scribal tradition that the original text read "*my* nose," and that scribes changed it to "their nose" out of reverence, since the sense would be that the people's sin was a stench in God's nostrils. Whatever the exact act, it shows contempt for God.
- **"Therefore I will act in wrath… though they cry in my ears with a loud voice, I will not hear them" (v18).** They said "the LORD does not see us" (v12). God answers that he will not hear them. The same sentence was spoken in Proverbs 1:28 and Jeremiah 11:11. It is not that God has lost his compassion, but that the time for warning is over. In chapter 9 the judgment begins.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both trace the image of jealousy back to Manasseh's idol, and JFB identifies it as Astarte or Asherah, "set up by Manasseh as a rival to Jehovah in His temple." Guzik sees four kinds of people in the four scenes: royal or public worship, the elders, the women, and the priests. He calls Jaazaniah the black sheep of a godly family. JFB notes the irony that Jaazaniah means "Jehovah hears," while the elders acted as if he did not. JFB takes the seventy as the Sanhedrin and the twenty-five as the representatives of the priesthood. Henry: "There is a great deal of secret wickedness in the world," and those "are ripe indeed for ruin, who lay the blame of their sins upon the Lord." On Tammuz, JFB says, "Instead of weeping for the national sins, they wept for the idol," and Henry mentions that the yearly lamenting "was attended with infamous practices." Guzik lists the main readings of the branch to the nose and leaves it open. JFB thinks the phrase is proverbial for turning up the nose in scorn, but also mentions a branch held up while singing to the sun. JFB reads the figure of v2 as the Messiah, "the Angel of the covenant," which is one traditional Christian reading of the Lord's appearances in the Old Testament.

#### Pointing to Christ
The elders in Ezekiel's day said, "The LORD does not see us." The tour of the temple proved them wrong, room by room. Jesus came to the same temple and found it turned into a market. He drove the traders out, saying, "You have made it a den of robbers" (Mark 11:17). Then he spoke of a temple that would replace it: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," meaning his own body (John 2:19–21). Ezekiel's vision ends with priests whose backs are turned on God's house. In Jesus, God's dwelling is a person who never turned his back on the Father. Through him, people who had turned away can turn around again and come near. Paul says believers are now God's temple (1 Cor 3:16; 2 Cor 6:16), and he calls them out of every idol, hidden and open, because "what agreement has the temple of God with idols?"
