*Setting:* The night attack at the spring of Harod has broken Midian's army (ch 7). The Ephraimites have caught and killed the princes Oreb and Zeeb at the Jordan fords, and Gideon has crossed the river after the two kings who escaped. Chapter 8 finishes Gideon's story, and it is a hard ending. The man who once hid in a winepress wins every battle, but along the way he takes revenge on fellow Israelites, refuses a crown in words while living like a king, and makes a golden object that leads all Israel into idolatry. As soon as he dies, Israel goes back to Baal.

#### Gideon Defeats Zebah and Zalmunna (vv1–21)
- **"What is this that you have done to us?" (v1).** Ephraim was the strongest tribe in the central hills, home of the tabernacle at Shiloh, and proud of its leading place (Joshua was an Ephraimite). They are angry that a man from the smaller clan of Abiezer in Manasseh started the war without them. In fact Gideon did call them, at the end (7:24). Their complaint is about honor and probably plunder.
- **"Is not the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the grape harvest of Abiezer?" (v2).** Gleanings are the leftovers picked after the main harvest. Gideon's answer: your leftovers outweigh my whole harvest. Ephraim captured two princes, while Gideon's clan only began the battle. It is generous, even flattering, and it works: "their anger… subsided" (v3; Hebrew "their spirit" eased). It is a fine example of "a soft answer turns away wrath" (Prov 15:1). Jephthah, faced with the same complaint from the same tribe, will answer harshly, and civil war follows (12:1–6).
- **"Exhausted yet pursuing" (v4).** The 300 have fought all night and chased the enemy for miles, and still they cross the Jordan. This short phrase has encouraged weary believers for centuries.
- **Succoth and Penuel (vv5–9).** Both were Israelite towns east of the Jordan, in the territory of Gad, along the Jabbok River. Their names carry history. Succoth ("booths") is where Jacob built shelters after he met Esau (Gen 33:17). Penuel ("face of God") is where Jacob wrestled with God and was renamed Israel (Gen 32:30–31). Now the towns that bear those memories will not give bread to Israel's own army.
- **"Are the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna already in your hand?" (v6).** The officials mock Gideon. Their logic is fear: if they feed his men and Midian survives, the raiders will come back and punish them. The Tyndale notes say the tribes east of the Jordan lived under constant Midianite pressure. Gideon only asked for bread, not soldiers.
- **"I will flail your flesh with the thorns of the wilderness" (v7).** The image is threshing: beating grain with thorny branches, or dragging a threshing sledge across it. Gideon threatens to do that to the officials' bodies. The Tyndale notes say such tearing of a defeated enemy's flesh was a known practice in the ancient Near East (compare Amos 1:3). The Tyndale notes also observe that from here Gideon's earlier conversations with the LORD stop. He no longer asks God anything.
- **"I will break down this tower" (v9).** Towns had a fortified tower as their last refuge. Penuel trusted its tower more than God's deliverer.
- **Karkor (vv10–12).** The remnant of Midian, about 15,000 men, has camped far out on the eastern plateau, on the caravan route to the desert. "There had fallen 120,000 men who drew the sword." That is the first time the total size of Midian's army is given, and it shows what 300 men and God's panic had done. Gideon takes the long way round "by the way of the tent dwellers" and hits the camp from the east, where they did not expect it, "for the army felt secure." He captures both kings.
- **A young man who could write (v14).** Gideon picks up a young man of Succoth, who writes down the names of seventy-seven officials and elders. The Tyndale notes point out that an ordinary young man could write. The simple alphabet had spread in Israel by this time, a quiet sign that literacy was not only for scribes.
- **"Taught the men of Succoth a lesson" (v16).** The Hebrew verb normally means "made known" or "taught." The Greek translation and many scholars read a very similar Hebrew word, "threshed," matching the threat in v7. Either way, Gideon carries out what he promised. At Penuel he goes further: he breaks down the tower "and killed the men of the city" (v17), which he had not threatened. The man who won by God's power is now settling scores with fellow Israelites.
- **"Where are the men whom you killed at Tabor?" (v18).** Here the reason for Gideon's relentless pursuit comes out. During one of the earlier raids, the kings killed Gideon's brothers near Mount Tabor. The kings answer with a compliment: "Every one of them resembled the son of a king." It is either flattery or plain truth, and either way it does not save them.
- **"The sons of my mother" (v19).** In a household with several wives, full brothers from the same mother were especially close. "As the LORD lives, if you had saved them alive, I would not kill you." This is blood vengeance, the duty of a family member to avenge a killing (Num 35:19). Gideon acts now as an avenger of his family as much as the LORD's deliverer.
- **"Rise and kill them!" (v20).** Gideon tells his young son Jether to do it, which would humiliate the kings and give the boy honor. But Jether is afraid, "because he was still a young man." The fearful father has a fearful son. The kings answer, "Rise yourself and fall upon us, for as the man is, so is his strength" (v21). Death at a boy's hand was shameful. They want to die by a warrior.
- **"The crescent ornaments… on the necks of their camels" (v21).** These were moon-shaped pendants, possibly tied to moon worship among desert peoples (compare Isa 3:18). Gideon keeps them. The plunder is about to become important.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry on Ephraim: "envy is best removed by humility." JFB calls Gideon's answer "mild and truly modest." Guzik notes that Gideon did not feed their pride but pointed them to what God had done through them. On v4, Henry's phrase is "Fainting, and yet pursuing." On Succoth, Henry draws a hard lesson: "Many are taught with the briers and thorns of affliction, who would not learn otherwise." Guzik observes that opposition often comes from one's own side, and that Succoth only wanted to help once the outcome was sure. He suggests Penuel may have been worse because it actively sided with Midian, but he grants the text does not say so. The Tyndale notes are more critical than Henry, saying Gideon's diplomacy with Ephraim showed him the Lord's servant far more than his harsh punishments did. JFB explains "the sons of my mother" as expressing a closeness beyond the word "brother."

#### Gideon's Ephod (vv22–28)
- **"Rule over us, you and your son and your grandson also" (v22).** Israel offers Gideon a hereditary throne, a dynasty. Notice what they say: "for *you* have saved us." Seven chapters of God's insistence that *he* saves, and that he shrank the army so no one would boast (7:2), and the people still credit the man.
- **"I will not rule over you… the LORD will rule over you" (v23).** This is a fine answer and good theology. God is Israel's King. The question of a human king will hang over the rest of the book (17:6; 21:25) and come to a head in 1 Samuel 8, where God tells Samuel, "they have rejected me from being king over them."
- **"Every one of you give me the earrings from his spoil" (v24).** Gideon declines the crown but asks for the gold. The narrator explains that the enemy wore gold earrings "because they were Ishmaelites." Ishmaelites and Midianites were both descended from Abraham, and the names seem to overlap for desert traders (compare Gen 37:25–28, 36, where the two names are used for the same caravan).
- **1,700 shekels of gold (v26).** A shekel was about 11 grams, so this is roughly 40 pounds of gold, plus the kings' crescents, pendants, purple robes (purple dye was costly and royal) and the camels' collars. It is a king's treasure.
- **"Gideon made an ephod of it" (v27).** An ephod was properly the high priest's vest-like garment, worked with gold, which held the breastpiece with the Urim and Thummim used to ask God's will (Exod 28:6–30). Gideon may have meant it as a way to ask God for guidance, as he had liked to do with the fleece. But 40 pounds of gold is more than a garment. It was probably a gold image or covered object, like the ephod that becomes part of Micah's idol shrine (17:5; 18:14–20). He set it up in his hometown of Ophrah, which competed with the true worship at the tabernacle in Shiloh.
- **"All Israel whored after it there, and it became a snare to Gideon and to his family" (v27).** "Whored" is the Bible's strong word for spiritual unfaithfulness, Israel as an unfaithful wife. "Snare" is the very word Moses and Joshua used to warn that the Canaanites' gods would trap Israel (Exod 23:33; Judg 2:3). The man who tore down his father's Baal altar (6:25–27) ends up building something Israel worships in its place.
- **Why did he do it?** The text does not say. Some think he meant well, wanting to honor God and keep the victory remembered, and that the misuse came later. Others see pride and a quiet claim to religious leadership. The text simply reports the result: it became a snare.
- **"The land had rest for forty years in the days of Gideon" (v28).** This is the last time Judges says "the land had rest." From here the cycle runs downhill. The Tyndale notes point out that the peaceful intervals grow shorter and shorter after Gideon.
- **Commentators:** Henry praises the refusal ("No good man can be pleased with any honour done to himself, which belongs only to God") and then grieves the ephod: "Many are led into false ways by one false step of a good man." JFB thinks the ephod began as a civil or ceremonial garment, like the one David wore (1 Chr 15:27), which "became an object to which religious ideas were attached." Guzik suggests it may have set up a rival to the tabernacle at Shiloh, and adds that sincerity does not make something right. He also notes Gideon declined the throne in words but went on to live like a king. Guzik reckons the gold at over 50 pounds. Using the ESV's own weight for a shekel, about 40 pounds is closer, though either way it was a fortune.

#### The Death of Gideon (vv29–35)
- **"Jerubbaal the son of Joash went and lived in his own house" (v29).** The narrator switches back to the name "Jerubbaal," "Let Baal contend," just as Israel is about to go back to Baal.
- **"Seventy sons… for he had many wives" (v30).** A large harem and many sons were marks of a king in the ancient world (Deut 17:17 warns Israel's future kings against exactly this). Gideon refused the title but gathered the trappings.
- **"He called his name Abimelech" (v31).** The name means "my father is king." It is hard to read that as an accident. The son of Gideon's concubine in Shechem will take the name seriously and seize a throne in chapter 9. Shechem was a mixed Canaanite and Israelite city, and a concubine there tied Gideon to its people.
- **"In a good old age" (v32).** This is said elsewhere of Abraham (Gen 25:8) and David (1 Chr 29:28). Gideon dies honored and is buried in his father's tomb. The outward blessing is real. The spiritual legacy is not.
- **"As soon as Gideon died… made Baal-berith their god" (v33).** "Baal-berith" means "Baal of the covenant." Its temple was in Shechem (9:4). The irony is bitter: Israel was bound by covenant to the LORD, and now they make a covenant god of Baal. The ephod may have smoothed the path.
- **"Did not remember the LORD… and they did not show steadfast love to the family of Jerubbaal" (vv34–35).** "Remember" in the Bible is not just recall but loyalty (compare Deut 8:18). "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, covenant loyalty and kindness. Israel forgets both their God and the man God used to save them, which sets up the slaughter of Gideon's sons in chapter 9.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "No wonder if those who forget their God, forget their friends." Guzik calls Gideon a political success but a spiritual failure, a man who handled adversity better than prosperity, and he reads Abimelech's name as a sign that Gideon wanted a family throne. He notes the Old Testament does not directly condemn polygamy but shows its painful fruit, which chapter 9 proves. The Tyndale notes say old age usually signals God's blessing, but Gideon's spiritual blessing and leadership had declined. JFB has no notes on vv29–35.

#### Pointing to Christ
Gideon is a true hero of faith (Heb 11:32), and he is also a warning. He said the right thing ("the LORD will rule over you") and then built something that drew Israel away from the LORD. He took vengeance on his own people and kept the gold. The best judge so far could save Israel from Midian but not from itself, and the book keeps showing the need for a better king. Jesus is the deliverer who refused the crown the crowds pressed on him (John 6:15) because his kingdom came through a cross, who emptied himself instead of gathering gold (Phil 2:7), and who, unlike Gideon's ephod, never draws his people away from true worship but brings them to the Father (John 14:6). He finishes well, and those who follow him are kept from the snares that caught Gideon.
