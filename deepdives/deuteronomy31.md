*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last days of Moses' life. The great speeches of Deuteronomy are over, ending with "choose life" in chapter 30. Chapters 31–34 tell how Moses prepared Israel to go on without him. In this chapter he does three things that any careful leader does before he dies: he hands leadership to Joshua, he puts the written law into safe keeping with a plan to read it regularly, and, at God's command, he writes a song for the people to sing. The mood is sober. God tells Moses plainly that Israel will turn away after his death. The song of chapter 32 is meant to meet them when that happens.

#### Joshua to Succeed Moses (vv1–8)
- **"I am 120 years old today" (v2).** Moses' life falls into three periods of forty years: in Pharaoh's court, as a shepherd in Midian, and leading Israel in the wilderness (Acts 7:23, 30, 36). **"I am no longer able to go out and come in"** is a phrase for leading people in battle and daily life (Num 27:17; 1 Kgs 3:7). It may mean his strength was failing, but 34:7 says "his eye was undimmed, and his vigor unabated." Most think the deeper reason follows: **"The LORD has said to me, 'You shall not go over this Jordan.'"** God had barred him from the land for his sin at Meribah (Num 20:12; Deut 32:51).
- **"The LORD your God himself will go over before you… and Joshua will go over at your head" (v3).** The order matters. God goes first, and Joshua leads under him. The real leader of Israel is not changing. Moses is great but not irreplaceable.
- **"As he did to Sihon and Og" (v4).** Past victories east of the Jordan (2:24–3:11) are the pledge of victories to come west of it.
- **"Be strong and courageous" (v6).** This phrase becomes the motto of the next book. God says it to Joshua again in Joshua 1:6, 7 and 9, and David says it to Solomon about building the temple (1 Chr 22:13; 28:20). The courage is not self-confidence. Its ground is given: **"for it is the LORD your God who goes with you."**
- **"He will not leave you or forsake you" (v6).** "Leave" is *raphah*, "let go, let drop, fail," and "forsake" is *'azab*, "abandon." God will neither let them fall nor walk away. Hebrews 13:5 takes this promise, first given to Israel at the Jordan, and applies it to every believer: "I will never leave you nor forsake you."
- **"Moses summoned Joshua and said to him in the sight of all Israel" (v7).** The handover is public, so no one can question it. Joshua had been Moses' assistant since the battle against Amalek (Exod 17:9), went up the mountain with him (Exod 24:13), stayed at the tent of meeting (Exod 33:11), and was one of the two faithful spies (Num 14:6–9). He had already been publicly appointed with the laying on of hands (Num 27:18–23). Now Moses repeats to Joshua, almost word for word, what he told the people (v8). Leader and people stand on the same promise.
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures Moses as "loth to leave them, fearing that when he had left them they would leave God." Guzik notes that Moses was kept out by God's decree, not frailty, and that leaders face a stricter standard (Jas 3:1). He points to Hebrews 13:5 as extending the promise of v6 to all believers. JFB says the people were to take "encouragement from the continued presence of their covenanted God."

#### The Reading of the Law (vv9–13)
- **"Then Moses wrote this law and gave it to the priests… and to all the elders" (v9).** The Tyndale notes see this as strong evidence for the traditional view that Moses wrote Deuteronomy, though "this law" may mean the core of the book rather than every word, since the book also records his death (ch 34). Giving it to both priests and elders put God's word in the care of both religious and civil leaders. Neither group alone controlled it.
- **"At the end of every seven years, at the set time in the year of release, at the Feast of Booths" (v10).** The "year of release" was the seventh year, when debts were cancelled (15:1–11). The Feast of Booths (Tabernacles) came in the autumn, when Israel lived in shelters for a week to remember the wilderness (16:13–15). It was a time of joy, and the whole nation was gathered. The law was read at the high point of the calendar, not in a dull meeting.
- **"You shall read this law before all Israel in their hearing" (v11).** In an age when few people owned written texts, hearing was how most people learned. Recorded public readings are few: Joshua at Ebal (Josh 8:34–35), King Jehoshaphat's teachers (2 Chr 17:7–9), Josiah after the book of the law was found (2 Kgs 23:1–3), and Ezra at the Feast of Booths after the exile, reading from early morning until midday while the people wept (Neh 8:1–18). That last reading closely follows this command.
- **"Men, women, and little ones, and the sojourner" (v12).** Everyone heard, not just the leaders. **"That they may hear and learn to fear the LORD."** Hearing leads to learning, learning to fearing, and fearing to doing.
- **"Their children, who have not known it" (v13).** Every generation has to learn the faith for itself. The children who did not see the Exodus or Sinai must hear the word. One generation's knowledge does not pass automatically to the next (compare Judg 2:10).
- **Commentators:** JFB thinks two copies were made, one for the priests and elders, and that the seven-year reading also guarded the text's accuracy. Henry says the reading kept each generation acquainted with the law. Guzik suggests that the rarity of recorded public readings helps explain how far Israel drifted, and he applies the command to regular public teaching of Scripture for all ages.

#### Joshua Commissioned to Lead Israel (vv14–29)
- **"Behold, the days approach when you must die" (v14).** God speaks plainly to Moses about his death, as a friend would.
- **"Present yourselves in the tent of meeting" (v14).** The tent of meeting was the tabernacle, where God met with Moses (Exod 33:7–11). **"The LORD appeared in the tent in a pillar of cloud… over the entrance"** (v15). The same cloud that led Israel out of Egypt (Exod 13:21) now stands over the commissioning. God confirms Joshua as he once confirmed Moses.
- **"You are about to lie down with your fathers" (v16).** A gentle Old Testament phrase for death, used of the kings (1 Kgs 2:10). Then comes a hard prophecy: **"this people will rise and whore after the foreign gods."** Unfaithfulness to God is often pictured as adultery (Exod 34:15–16; Hos 1–3). God does not hide from Moses what is coming. Judges shows it began within a generation (Judg 2:11–13).
- **"I will forsake them and hide my face from them" (v17).** A painful echo of v6. God promised not to forsake his people, and now he says he will forsake them, but only after **"they will forsake me"** (v16). To "hide the face" is the reverse of the priestly blessing, "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). It means God withdrawing his favor and protection. **"Have not these evils come upon us because our God is not among us?"** The people will notice his absence, but they will draw only half the lesson.
- **"I will surely hide my face" (v18).** Interestingly, the book of Esther never mentions God's name, and Jewish interpreters long connected this verse with that story, finding the name Esther in the Hebrew phrase "I will surely hide" (*haster astir*). It is wordplay, not proof, but it reflects a real point: God's providence can work even while his face seems hidden.
- **"Now therefore write this song and teach it to the people of Israel. Put it in their mouths" (v19).** Laws are easy to forget. Songs stay in the memory for a lifetime. God gives Israel a song that they will sing even after they have forgotten the law. **"That this song may be a witness for me against the people."** In days of disaster, the song would remind them that God had warned them, and that the trouble was the result of their own unfaithfulness, not God's weakness.
- **"They have eaten and are full and grown fat" (v20).** The danger is not hunger but plenty, exactly as Moses warned in 6:10–12 and 8:10–14. Prosperity breeds forgetfulness. Chapter 32 uses the same picture: "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked" (32:15).
- **"For I know what they are inclined to do even today" (v21).** "Inclined" is *yetser*, "intention, inclination," the same word used of the human heart before the flood: "every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually" (Gen 6:5). Later Jewish teaching spoke of the *yetser hara*, the evil inclination. God knows the heart before it acts.
- **"And the LORD commissioned Joshua… 'Be strong and courageous… I will be with you'" (v23).** The words "I will be with you" are what God said to Moses at the burning bush (Exod 3:12). The same promise passes to the next leader. Joshua's name, *Yehoshua*, means "the LORD saves" (Num 13:16). In Greek it becomes *Iēsous*, the name of Jesus.
- **"When Moses had finished writing the words of this law in a book to the very end" (v24).** The work is complete.
- **"Put it by the side of the ark… that it may be there for a witness against you" (v26).** The stone tablets of the Ten Commandments were inside the ark (10:1–5; Heb 9:4). This book was placed beside it. Ancient treaties were often kept in temples, before the gods. Israel's covenant was kept before the LORD's throne. Many think this was the "Book of the Law" found in the temple centuries later, in King Josiah's day (2 Kgs 22:8), which led to a great reform.
- **"For I know how rebellious and stubborn you are… How much more after my death!" (v27).** Moses is not bitter. He is honest. Forty years of grumbling, the golden calf and the refusal at Kadesh (ch 9) have taught him. **"Provoking him to anger through the work of your hands"** (v29) usually means idols.
- **"Call heaven and earth to witness against them" (v28).** This prepares for the opening words of the song: "Give ear, O heavens… and let the earth hear" (32:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the song "a witness for God against all those that break covenant with him," and suggests the book placed by the ark may be the one found in Josiah's time. Guzik follows Morgan in saying songs outlast commandments, and Kalland in reading the hidden face as the reverse of the Aaronic blessing. He notes that Joshua's name in Greek is the name of Jesus. JFB says the hidden face means "the withdrawal of the divine favor and protection," and that the book was kept beside the ark "for greater security and reverence." He suggests the cloud appeared at the tent's door because only priests could enter the sanctuary. JFB has no notes on vv23–25.

#### The Song of Moses (v30)
- **"Then Moses spoke the words of this song until they were finished, in the ears of all the assembly" (v30).** This verse introduces chapter 32. The ESV places the heading here because the song begins in the next verse. Moses had sung once before, at the Red Sea (Exod 15). That song celebrated what God had done. This one warns about what Israel will do, and what God will still do for them in the end (32:36, 43). Revelation pictures the redeemed singing "the song of Moses… and the song of the Lamb" (Rev 15:3).

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses could not take Israel into the land. That was left to Joshua, *Yehoshua*, "the LORD saves," whose name in Greek is Jesus. The law, for all its goodness, could bring people to the edge of the promise but not into it. The New Testament draws the same contrast: "the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (John 1:17), and "if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on" (Heb 4:8). The promise "I will never leave you nor forsake you" is secure for believers (Heb 13:5) because Jesus himself was forsaken. On the cross God hid his face from his Son, and Jesus cried, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). He bore that abandonment so his people would never have to.
