*Setting:* Chapter 19 ended with a Levite sending the twelve pieces of his murdered concubine throughout Israel and the whole nation saying, "consider it, take counsel, and speak" (19:30). Chapter 20 is the answer. For once in Judges, all Israel acts "as one man," but not against a foreign oppressor. They go to war against one of their own tribes. Phinehas, Aaron's grandson, is still serving before the ark (v28), which places these events early in the period, perhaps a generation after Joshua. The chapter is long, repetitive and full of numbers, and that is part of its message: the narrator makes the reader feel the size of the disaster. Justice is done, but at a staggering cost, and by the end a whole tribe of Israel has nearly disappeared.

#### Israel's War with the Tribe of Benjamin (vv1–48)
- **"From Dan to Beersheba, including the land of Gilead" (v1).** Dan, in the far north, and Beersheba, in the far south, mark the length of the land, with Gilead east of the Jordan included. The phrase uses the city just founded in chapter 18, another sign the narrator writes from a later time. "The congregation assembled as one man to the LORD at Mizpah." Mizpah ("watchtower") is in Benjamin, a few miles north of Jerusalem, not the Mizpah of Jephthah in Gilead (11:11). Samuel will later gather Israel at this same place (1 Sam 7:5–6; 10:17).
- **"400,000 men on foot that drew the sword" (v2).** The numbers in this chapter are huge. The census in Numbers 26 counted about 600,000 fighting men in all of Israel. Some scholars think the Hebrew word *eleph*, usually "thousand," can also mean a military unit or clan, which would make the armies much smaller. Others take the numbers at face value. Either way, the point is clear: this was the whole nation under arms.
- **"(Now the people of Benjamin heard…)" (v3).** Benjamin had received a piece of the body too (19:29), so they knew what was coming. They stay away.
- **The Levite's account (vv4–7).** He is called "the husband of the woman who was murdered." His story is true as far as it goes, but he shapes it. He says "the leaders of Gibeah" (literally "the lords," the town's landowners) "meant to kill me," though in 19:22 they had demanded to abuse him, not kill him. Above all he leaves out that he himself pushed his concubine out the door (19:25). He calls the crime "abomination and outrage," and it was. But the man asking Israel for justice was not innocent in it.
- **"None of us will go to his tent" (vv8–11).** The tribes swear to stay until it is settled. One man in ten will gather food for the rest, chosen by lot. Israel is "united as one man" three times in this chapter (vv1, 8, 11). It is the only time in Judges the whole nation acts together, and the irony is bitter: their great unity is for a war against their brothers.
- **"Give up the men… that we may put them to death and purge evil from Israel" (vv12–13).** This is the right first step. The law set out exactly this process for a town that went over to wickedness: inquire, search out, ask diligently (Deut 13:12–14), and "purge the evil from your midst" (Deut 17:7; 22:22). Israel does not demand Benjamin's destruction, only the handing over of the guilty men.
- **"But the Benjaminites would not listen" (v13).** Here the tragedy turns. Benjamin chooses tribe over justice. By shielding the guilty they make their crime their own.
- **Benjamin's army (vv14–16).** 26,000 men, plus 700 from Gibeah. Among them, "700 chosen men who were left-handed; every one could sling a stone at a hair and not miss." Left-handed fighters were a Benjaminite specialty, as Ehud showed (3:15), and David's Benjaminite recruits could sling with either hand (1 Chr 12:2). There is an irony here too, since Benjamin means "son of the right hand." A sling was a leather pouch on two cords, whirled and released. Skilled slingers could hurl a stone accurately at great speed, and armies used them as artillery. The word "miss" is *chata'*, the most common Old Testament word for sin. Its basic sense is missing a target. These men never missed a target, and their tribe missed the most important one.
- **"Who shall go up first for us…?' 'Judah shall go up first'" (v18).** This is almost word for word the question at the start of the book: "Who shall go up first for us against the Canaanites?… Judah shall go up" (1:1–2). The book has come full circle, but now the enemy is Benjamin. "Bethel" can also be translated "the house of God" (as the KJV does). The ark was there at this time (v27), probably moved from Shiloh for the war. They ask God only *who* should go first, not *whether* to go. They have already decided.
- **22,000 dead (vv19–21).** Benjamin wins the first day. The larger army loses badly.
- **"They… wept before the LORD until the evening" (vv22–23).** Now they ask the question they skipped: "Shall we again draw near to fight against our brothers?" The word "brothers" appears for the first time. God says, "Go up against them."
- **18,000 more dead (vv24–25).** They obeyed and lost again. God's "go up" was not a promise of victory, and the text does not explain why he let them fall twice. The commentators suggest reasons, but the narrator leaves it open.
- **Weeping, fasting and sacrifices (vv26–28).** This time "all the people of Israel, the whole army" come. They weep, fast until evening, and offer burnt offerings (a sign of total dedication and atonement) and peace offerings (restoring fellowship). It is the fullest act of repentance in the chapter. Their question is humbler too: "Shall we go out once more… or shall we cease?" And only now God promises the outcome: "tomorrow I will give them into your hand."
- **"Phinehas the son of Eleazar, son of Aaron" (v28).** This is the zealous priest who stopped the plague at Baal-peor (Num 25:7–13). His presence anchors the story in the first generation or two after Joshua, which tells us chapters 19–21 are not in time order with the rest of the book.
- **The ambush (vv29–36a).** The tactic is the one Joshua used at Ai (Josh 8): a main force pretends to flee, the defenders chase it away from the city, and a hidden force rushes in behind them. Benjamin, confident after two victories, says, "They are routed before us, as at the first" (v32). They "did not know that disaster was close upon them" (v34). Baal-tamar and Maareh-geba are places near Gibeah whose exact sites are unknown.
- **"The LORD defeated Benjamin before Israel" (v35).** The narrator is clear about who won the battle. Benjamin loses 25,100 men.
- **The battle retold (vv36b–46).** The narrator goes back and tells it again from closer up: the signal of smoke, the moment the Benjaminites "looked behind them, and behold, the whole of the city went up in smoke to heaven" (v40). "Went up in smoke to heaven" is the language of a whole burnt offering, and of Ai and Sodom (Josh 8:20; Gen 19:28). The totals differ slightly (25,100 in v35, a rounded 25,000 in v46), the sort of variation that comes from summarizing the same day twice. The figures add up roughly: 18,000 + 5,000 + 2,000 = 25,000, with 600 survivors, and JFB reckons about a thousand more had fallen in the first two days.
- **"600 men… to the rock of Rimmon… four months" (v47).** Rimmon was a rocky crag in the wilderness east of Bethel, full of caves where a small group could hold out. Six hundred men is all that is left of a tribe of 26,700. There is a quiet echo of chapter 18, where 600 Danites set out to destroy a peaceful town.
- **"The city, men and beasts and all that they found" (v48).** Israel then sweeps through Benjamin's towns and burns them, killing everyone, not just soldiers. This is the treatment the law reserved for Canaanite cities under the ban, or an Israelite town that had gone over to idols (Deut 13:15–16). Israel's oath and anger carry it past the guilty men to the women, children and livestock of a whole tribe. Chapter 21 will show them waking up to what they have done.
- **Commentators:** All three see Benjamin's refusal to give up the guilty as the root of the war. Henry calls it "the obstinacy of the Benjamites in protecting their criminals." On the two defeats, Henry says Israel "took it for granted that God would bless them," and warns, "We must never lay the weight on an arm of flesh." He asks whether there were "not with them, even with them, sins against the Lord their God," a question many have pressed, since the same Israel had let Dan's idol shrine stand. JFB likewise blames their "too proudly relying on their superior numbers," and says the first inquiry came too late. Guzik reads the defeats as humbling a nation that thought the sin problem belonged only to Benjamin, and stresses that their weeping became real repentance only when it came with sacrifice. He also notes that the word for "miss" in v16 is the root of the word for sin, which the lexicon supports. JFB and Guzik both compare the ambush to Ai. On the end of the chapter, Guzik and Henry agree Israel went too far: Henry calls the slaughter of the whole tribe "difficult to justify" as Israel's own act, though he sees God's hand of judgment in it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel was right to want justice for a terrible crime, and Benjamin was wrong to protect the guilty. Yet the chapter shows that a sinful people cannot carry out justice without its own sin mixing in: wrath spills over, the innocent die with the guilty, and the nation is left grieving its own victory. Only when Israel came with weeping, fasting and sacrifice did God give the outcome. That points beyond itself. The one perfectly just judgment in history fell not on a guilty tribe but on the innocent Son of God, who bore it in the place of the guilty. At the cross justice and mercy met without excess and without compromise, "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:26).
