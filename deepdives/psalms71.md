*Setting:* Psalm 71 has no title, which is unusual this deep into Book Two of the Psalms. Many readers, Henry among them, think it continues Psalm 70 and that David wrote it in his old age, perhaps during Absalom's rebellion, "the great trouble of his later days," or Sheba's revolt that followed (2 Sam 15–20). Some Hebrew manuscripts even join Psalms 70 and 71 into one. Guzik prefers to treat the author as unnamed, since the psalm itself does not say. What the psalm does make plain is that the writer is old (vv9, 18), has trusted God since childhood (vv5–6, 17), and is under attack from people who think God has given up on him (v11). It is woven from lines of other psalms, especially 22, 31, 35 and 40. Guzik, following Maclaren, takes that as the mark of a man soaked in Scripture whose prayers come out in the words he has prayed all his life. Henry calls it the psalm "above any other… fitted for the use of the old disciples of Jesus Christ." The Tyndale notes describe it as less a cry of abandonment than a confession of confidence and hope. It moves from prayer (vv1–13) to rising praise (vv14–24), and the word "continually" or "all the day" runs through it (vv3, 6, 8, 14, 15, 24).

#### In You, O LORD, Do I Take Refuge (vv1–3)
- **"In you, O LORD, do I take refuge; let me never be put to shame!" (v1).** These first three verses are almost the same as 31:1–3. "Put to shame" means disappointed in one's hope, left looking foolish for having trusted. He asks that his trust in God never be shown to be misplaced (Rom 5:5).
- **"In your righteousness deliver me" (v2).** He appeals to God's righteousness, his faithfulness to do what is right and to keep his promises, not to his own record. "Righteousness" becomes the psalm's key word, appearing five times (vv2, 15, 16, 19, 24).
- **"Be to me a rock of refuge, to which I may continually come" (v3).** A small but lovely difference from 31:2. Psalm 31 has "a rock of refuge, a strong fortress," but here the Hebrew reads "a rock of dwelling" (*me'on*), a place to live and keep coming back to, not just a fortress to run to in a crisis. The two words differ by one Hebrew letter. Henry: those who live in communion with God "shall be welcome to resort to him continually upon all occasions, and not be upbraided as coming too often."
- **"You have given the command to save me" (v3).** God has already issued the order for his rescue. JFB: "literally, 'ordained,'" as in 44:4, "ordain salvation for Jacob."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God will never disappoint the hope that is of his own raising," and "Those that will deal with God must deal upon trust." He compares God's promise to a command, like "Let there be light," spoken and so done. Guzik cites Spurgeon's picture of a weak man made safe inside a strong fortress.

#### My Trust from My Youth (vv4–8)
- **"Rescue me, O my God, from the hand of the wicked, from the grasp of the unjust and cruel man" (v4).** "Cruel" (*chomets*) comes from the root for something soured or leavened, the same root as the "sour wine" of 69:21. JFB: "literally, 'sour,'" a man embittered and harsh.
- **"For you, O Lord, are my hope, my trust, O LORD, from my youth" (v5).** "Lord" here is *Adonai*, the Master, and "LORD" is *YHWH*, the covenant name. God is not only the giver of hope, he is the hope. Guzik makes this point.
- **"Upon you I have leaned from before my birth; you are he who took me from my mother's womb" (v6).** Compare 22:9–10: "you are he who took me from the womb; you made me trust you at my mother's breasts." Before he could know or choose God, God was carrying him. A life of faith did not begin with his decision but with God's care. "Took me" may picture God as the one who delivered him at birth, a midwife's work. Trapp, cited by Guzik, puts it this way: as in the womb I lived on you, so from the womb.
- **"My praise is continually of you" (v6).** A lifetime of being carried calls for a lifetime of praise.
- **"I have been as a portent to many" (v7).** "Portent" (*mopheth*) is a sign or wonder, the word used for the plagues of Egypt (Exod 7:3). It is also used of prophets whose lives were living signs to the people (Isa 8:18; Ezek 12:6, 11). The lexicon lists this verse under "one protected by the LORD." The meaning may be positive (others marvel at how God has kept him) or negative (people see his troubles as a warning, a sign of God's judgment). Henry gives both. Horne, cited by Guzik, links it to Christ, "a sign that is opposed" (Luke 2:34). **"But you are my strong refuge."** Whatever others make of him, God is his shelter. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes how "but you" turns his eyes from the watchers to God.
- **"My mouth is filled with your praise, and with your glory all the day" (v8).** He asks for, or declares, a mouth so full of praise that there is no room for complaint.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "He that was our help from our birth ought to be our hope from our youth. If we received so much mercy from God before we were capable of doing him any service, we should lose no time when we are capable." On v8, he says those who love God want to praise him "not only in their morning and evening devotions… but all the day." JFB reads vv6–9 as "his history from early infancy" showing God's care, so that past rescues are "at once occasions of praise and ground of confidence for the future."

#### Do Not Cast Me Off in Old Age (vv9–13)
- **"Do not cast me off in the time of old age; forsake me not when my strength is spent" (v9).** This is the heart of the psalm's fear. In the ancient world, as in ours, old people could be pushed aside when they could no longer work or fight. "Cast me off" is the word for throwing away something no longer useful. The psalmist fears that God might treat him the same way. The Tyndale notes observe that in his weakness he needs God's strength more, not less (compare Isa 46:4: "even to your old age I am he, and to gray hairs I will carry you").
- **Faith does not always grow stronger with age.** Guzik notes that some believers finish worse than they started, and gives David with Bathsheba, Solomon's idolatry in old age (1 Kgs 11:4) and King Asa's last years (2 Chr 16:7–12). It is right, he says, citing Barnes through Spurgeon, to pray for grace to finish well.
- **"For my enemies speak concerning me… 'God has forsaken him; pursue and seize him, for there is none to deliver him'" (vv10–11).** The cruelest attack is theological: his suffering is taken as proof God has dropped him. JFB points to the plotting against David in Absalom's revolt, when Ahithophel urged an attack while David was weary (2 Sam 17:1–12). Rabshakeh used the same tactic on Hezekiah (2 Kgs 18:25, 30). At the cross the rulers said, "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now, if he desires him" (Matt 27:43). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, calls the enemies' claim a barefaced lie.
- **"O God, be not far from me; O my God, make haste to help me!" (v12).** These are lines from 22:11, 19 and 38:21–22, and the last line repeats 70:1. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notices the move from "O God" to "O my God," a tighter grip of faith.
- **"May my accusers be put to shame and consumed" (v13).** "Accusers" is the noun from *satan*, the adversary or accuser. The prayer echoes 35:4, 26 and 40:14. It asks that the shame they intended for him should cover them.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the enemies' reasoning false: "All are not forsaken of God who think themselves so or whom others think to be so." And he adds, "He is a Master that is not wont to cast off old servants." He reads v13 as said "from a spirit of prophecy, not a spirit of passion." Clarke, cited by Guzik, likewise reads these lines as declarations of what will happen.

#### I Will Hope Continually (vv14–16)
- **"But I will hope continually and will praise you yet more and more" (v14).** The turning point. Literally "I will add to all your praise." He has praised God all his life, and he intends to keep adding to it. Henry: "The longer we live the more expert we should grow in praising God."
- **"My mouth will tell of your righteous acts, of your deeds of salvation all the day, for their number is past my knowledge" (v15).** He cannot count God's saving deeds, so he will never run out of things to say (40:5). JFB: "innumerable, as he had not time to count them." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: where he cannot count, he will believe.
- **"With the mighty deeds of the Lord GOD I will come" (v16).** He will come into God's presence, or before the people, bringing God's deeds as his theme, the way a worshiper comes bringing an offering. The KJV reads "I will go in the strength of the Lord GOD." The Hebrew word (*gevurot*) can mean strength or mighty deeds. "Lord GOD" is *Adonai YHWH*, the Master who is also the covenant God.
- **"I will remind them of your righteousness, yours alone" (v16).** "Yours alone": not his own righteousness. Paul would later say the same thing about the righteousness God gives by faith in Christ (Phil 3:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We must always go about God's work in his strength," and he applies "thy righteousness, even thine only" to "the righteousness of Christ… We must depend upon God's strength for assistance and upon Christ's righteousness for acceptance" (Isa 45:24). JFB reads "righteousness" here as God's "faithful performance of promises to the pious." Horne, cited by Guzik, applies v16 to facing spiritual enemies in God's strength, not our own.

#### Even to Old Age and Gray Hairs (vv17–21)
- **"O God, from my youth you have taught me, and I still proclaim your wondrous deeds" (v17).** A lifetime pupil of God, still talking about what he learned. "Wondrous deeds" (*nifla'ot*) are the great saving acts of God like the exodus (Exod 3:20).
- **"So even to old age and gray hairs, O God, do not forsake me, until I proclaim your might to another generation" (v18).** He does not ask to live longer for his own sake. He asks to live long enough to pass the faith on. "Your might" is literally "your arm," the picture of God's power used at the exodus ("with an outstretched arm," Deut 26:8). Psalm 78:4 states the same duty: "We will… tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD."
- **"Your righteousness, O God, reaches the high heavens… O God, who is like you?" (v19).** The question echoes the Song of the Sea: "Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?" (Exod 15:11). Guzik notes the exodus echoes in the psalm.
- **"You who have made me see many troubles and calamities will revive me again" (v20).** God does not only allow the troubles. The psalmist says God "made me see" them, acknowledging God's hand even in hardship. "Revive" (*chayah*) means to give life, to make alive again. The written Hebrew text has "us" in v20, while the scribes' marginal reading has "me." The ESV and KJV follow "me," but "us" would show the psalmist speaking for God's people as well as himself.
- **"From the depths of the earth you will bring me up again" (v20).** "The depths of the earth" is the realm of the dead, the lowest place imaginable (63:9; 86:13). The psalmist feels as good as buried, and expects God to raise him. The Tyndale notes say he hoped for renewed earthly life, but that such language later became grounds for believing in the resurrection of the dead. JFB reads it simply as "debased, low condition."
- **"You will increase my greatness and comfort me again" (v21).** VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, notes that "greatness" picks up the "great things" of v19: God's saving greatness becomes his. The verb "comfort me" carries the sense of God turning back to him to console him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those that have been taught of God from their youth… may be sure that he will not leave them when they are old and gray-headed." He reads v20 with tenderness: "He does not say, 'Thou hast burdened me with those troubles,' but 'shown them to me,' as the tender father shows the child the rod." He ends by pointing to Christ: "his Father showed him great and sore troubles, but quickened him and brought him up from the grave." Morgan, cited by Guzik, says old age is either a pitiful or a beautiful sight, depending on whether it chills or inspires the young. Poole sees this psalm itself as a monument left for future generations.

#### O Holy One of Israel (vv22–24)
- **"I will also praise you with the harp for your faithfulness, O my God" (v22).** The harp (*nevel*) and lyre (*kinnor*) were stringed instruments. David was famous as a player (1 Sam 16:23). "Faithfulness" (*'emet*) is God's reliability, his truth. JFB notes "will… praise" is literally "will thank."
- **"O Holy One of Israel" (v22).** This title appears only three times in the Psalms (71:22; 78:41; 89:18) but about twenty-five times in Isaiah, where it is a favorite. It joins God's otherness, his holiness, with his closeness, his belonging to Israel. Kidner, cited by Guzik, makes that point.
- **"My lips will shout for joy… my soul also, which you have redeemed" (v23).** Lips, tongue and soul all praise. "Redeemed" (*padah*) is the ransom word again (69:18). He speaks of his redemption as already done.
- **"My tongue will talk of your righteous help all the day long, for they have been put to shame" (v24).** The psalm ends with the enemies' shame described as already done, the confidence of faith. "Talk" (*hagah*) is the word for murmuring or meditating, the word in 1:2 for meditating on God's law. He will turn God's righteousness over and over, all day.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "lip-labour, though ever so well laboured, if that be all, is but lost labour in serving God; the soul must be at work." And, "Redeemed souls ought to be joyful thankful souls." He explains the title: "It is God's honour that he is a Holy One; it is his people's honour that he is the Holy One of Israel." Horne, cited by Guzik, sees the church's worship on earth as an echo of the worship of the redeemed in heaven.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 71 is the prayer of a believer at the end of a long road, still carried by the God who held him at birth, still mocked by those who say God has forsaken him, still hoping to tell the next generation. Jesus heard the same taunt at the cross (Matt 27:43), and the Father did not forsake him in the end. From "the depths of the earth" God brought him up again (Eph 4:9–10). Because he was raised, old believers whose strength is spent can pray this psalm with confidence. God does not throw away his old servants. In Christ they will be revived again, and their redeemed souls will sing to the Holy One of Israel.
