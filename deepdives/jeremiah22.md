*Setting:* This chapter collects Jeremiah's messages to the last kings of David's line, arranged by king rather than by date, and covering about a dozen years (609–597 BC). Good King Josiah had been killed fighting Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo in 609 BC (2 Kgs 23:29). After him came three of his sons and one grandson, and each was worse than the last. Jehoahaz (also called Shallum) reigned three months and was carried off to Egypt. Jehoiakim reigned eleven years as a puppet of Egypt and then of Babylon. Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin (also called Coniah) reigned three months and was carried off to Babylon. Zedekiah, the last, was addressed in chapter 21. Chapter 22 continues the message "to the house of David" begun at 21:11. It ends with a sentence on Coniah that seems to cut David's line off from the throne, and chapter 23 answers with the promise of a righteous Branch.

#### Message to the House of David (vv1–10)
- **"Go down to the house of the king of Judah" (v1).** The temple stood on the highest ground in Jerusalem, and the palace lay just south of it and lower down, so Jeremiah literally goes down from the temple courts to the palace. He takes God's word into the seat of government.
- **"O king of Judah, who sits on the throne of David" (v2).** The king is reminded whose throne it is. It is David's, held on God's terms (2 Sam 7:12–16; Ps 132:11–12). **"Your people who enter these gates."** The palace gates were where people came for justice, so the whole system of royal justice is being addressed.
- **"Do justice and righteousness" (v3).** In Hebrew this is the pair *mishpat* and *tsedaqah*, the classic summary of what God requires of a king (2 Sam 8:15; Ps 72:1–2). *Mishpat* is fair judgment, and *tsedaqah* is right dealing that flows from a right relationship with God.
- **"The resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow" (v3).** These three groups had no family land or male protector. The Law named them again and again as God's special concern (Exod 22:21–24; Deut 10:18; 24:17). A society's treatment of them was the test of its faithfulness. **"Nor shed innocent blood in this place."** Jehoiakim had the prophet Uriah hunted down in Egypt and killed (26:20–23).
- **"Kings who sit on the throne of David, riding in chariots and on horses" (v4).** This repeats the offer of 17:25. Obedience would keep David's line on the throne in Jerusalem.
- **"I swear by myself, declares the LORD" (v5).** God has no one greater to swear by (Gen 22:16; Heb 6:13). The oath makes the warning certain. **"This house shall become a desolation."** "House" means both the palace and the royal dynasty. Both would be ruined.
- **"You are like Gilead to me, like the summit of Lebanon" (v6).** Gilead, the hill country east of the Jordan, was famous for its forests and fertile land. Lebanon was famous for its great cedars. The palace was built with so much cedar that one of its halls was called "the House of the Forest of Lebanon" (1 Kgs 7:2). God says the royal house is precious to him, and yet he will turn it into a wasteland.
- **"I will prepare destroyers against you" (v7).** "Prepare" is literally "consecrate." God sets apart the invaders for a holy task, just as he called Babylon his servant (25:9). **"They shall cut down your choicest cedars."** The palace's cedar beams would end up as firewood (52:13).
- **"Why has the LORD dealt thus with this great city?" (v8).** Moses had predicted this very question from the nations, and its answer (Deut 29:24–26; 1 Kgs 9:8–9). Jerusalem's ruin would be a lesson the world could read.
- **"Weep not for him who is dead" (v10).** That is Josiah. The whole nation had mourned him, and Jeremiah himself wrote a lament for him (2 Chr 35:24–25). But Josiah died in faith and was buried in his own land. **"Weep bitterly for him who goes away."** That is his son Shallum, who would die in exile without ever seeing home again. Exile was worse than death.
- **Commentators:** Henry puts it memorably: "dying saints may be justly envied, while living sinners are justly pitied." He also notes that "God never casts any off until they first cast him off." JFB says God swears in v5 "because it seemed to them incredible that the family of David should be cast off." Guzik, drawing on Kidner, suggests vv1–5 may be an earlier message, perhaps to Jehoiakim, from a time when repentance was still possible. JFB also thinks Jehoiakim is the main target. He raised Pharaoh's tribute by oppression (2 Kgs 23:35), and he killed Uriah.

#### Message to the Sons of Josiah (vv11–30)
- **"Shallum the son of Josiah" (v11).** Shallum was probably his personal name and Jehoahaz his throne name (1 Chr 3:15; 2 Kgs 23:30–34). The people of the land made him king, passing over an older brother, perhaps because they expected him to continue Josiah's anti-Egyptian policy. After three months Pharaoh Neco summoned him to Riblah, put him in chains and took him to Egypt, where he died (2 Kgs 23:33–34). He was the first king of Judah to die in exile.
- **"Woe to him who builds his house by unrighteousness" (v13).** Now Jehoiakim, though he is not named until v18. Neco had made him king and charged the land a heavy tribute, which Jehoiakim squeezed out of the people (2 Kgs 23:35). At the same time he built himself a grand new palace.
- **"Who makes his neighbor serve him for nothing and does not give him his wages" (v13).** Forced labor was a normal practice for ancient kings, and Solomon used it too (1 Kgs 5:13–14). But the Law commanded that a worker be paid the same day (Lev 19:13; Deut 24:14–15). Note the word "neighbor." The king and his laborers were fellow Israelites, bound together by the same covenant. James later condemns the same sin: "the wages of the laborers… which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you" (Jas 5:4).
- **"Paneling it with cedar and painting it with vermilion" (v14).** Vermilion (*shashar*) was an expensive red pigment, probably red ochre. Cedar had to be imported from Lebanon. At Ramat Rahel, about three miles south of the old city, archaeologists found a large royal palace from this period with fine stone windows and carved capitals. Many connect it with Jehoiakim, though that identification is debated.
- **"Do you think you are a king because you compete in cedar?" (v15).** Jehoiakim tried to prove he was a real king with a building program. **"Did not your father eat and drink and do justice and righteousness?"** Josiah enjoyed the ordinary good things of life. He was no ascetic, and he also ruled justly. "Then it was well with him."
- **"He judged the cause of the poor and needy… Is not this to know me?" (v16).** This is one of the most important lines in Jeremiah. To "know" God in Hebrew is not just to have information about him. It is to be in a relationship that shows up in how one treats people. Justice for the poor is not something added to knowing God. It is what knowing God looks like. Compare 9:23–24, Hosea 6:6 and 1 John 3:17–18.
- **"Eyes and heart only for your dishonest gain" (v17).** Four charges: greed, bloodshed, oppression and violence. Josiah's son was the opposite of Josiah.
- **"They shall not lament for him, saying, 'Ah, my brother!'" (v18).** These were the stock cries of an Israelite funeral (1 Kgs 13:30). "Ah, lord! Ah, his majesty!" were the cries for a king. No one would weep for Jehoiakim.
- **"With the burial of a donkey he shall be buried" (v19).** A donkey was not buried at all. Its carcass was dragged out and left for scavengers. Jeremiah 36:30 adds, "his dead body shall be cast out to the heat by day and the frost by night." Second Kings 24:6 says only that he "slept with his fathers," the usual formula for a king's death, and does not mention burial. He died in late 598 BC, just as the Babylonian army arrived. How his body came to be dishonored, the text does not say. Some suggest he was killed in a palace coup, others that his body was dug up or left outside during the siege.
- **"Go up to Lebanon… Bashan… Abarim" (v20).** Jerusalem, pictured as a woman, is told to climb three mountain regions, in the north, northeast and southeast, and scream for help. Abarim is the range in Moab that includes Mount Nebo, where Moses looked over the promised land (Num 27:12; Deut 32:49). **"All your lovers are destroyed."** The "lovers" are Judah's foreign allies, especially Egypt (2:36–37). They will not come.
- **"I spoke to you in your prosperity, but you said, 'I will not listen'" (v21).** In good times Judah felt no need for God. "From your youth" points back to the wilderness (2:2; 3:25).
- **"The wind shall shepherd all your shepherds" (v22).** A wordplay: the Hebrew verb for "shepherd" also means "graze." The rulers who should have fed the flock will be "grazed" on by the wind, the Babylonian invasion (4:11–12). "Shepherds" is Jeremiah's usual word for kings and leaders, which prepares for 23:1–4.
- **"O inhabitant of Lebanon, nested among the cedars" (v23).** This is Jerusalem's royal set, living in cedar palaces like birds high in the trees, thinking they are safe. **"Pain as of a woman in labor."** Sudden, unavoidable, overwhelming pain (6:24; 13:21).
- **"Though Coniah the son of Jehoiakim… were the signet ring on my right hand" (v24).** Coniah is a shortened form of Jeconiah, also called Jehoiachin. He became king at eighteen, reigned three months, and surrendered to Nebuchadnezzar in March 597 BC (2 Kgs 24:8–15). A signet ring was the most personal thing a man owned. It bore his seal and carried his authority. Even if this king were that precious, God would pull him off and throw him away.
- **"The mother who bore you" (v26).** Nehushta, the queen mother (2 Kgs 24:8). Queen mothers held real power in Judah, and Jeremiah had warned her and her son together (13:18). Both went into exile (29:2).
- **"Is this man Coniah a despised, broken pot?" (v28).** The picture recalls the smashed flask of chapter 19. The questions expect the people's puzzled answer. Many in Judah still idolized Jehoiachin and expected him home soon (28:4). He was the king of the exiles, and Ezekiel dates his visions by "the exile of King Jehoiachin" (Ezek 1:2).
- **Jehoiachin in Babylon.** Babylonian ration tablets from around 592 BC, found in Nebuchadnezzar's palace, list oil rations for "Ya'ukinu, king of the land of Yahud" and his five sons. In 562 BC the next Babylonian king released him from prison and gave him a seat at the royal table for the rest of his life (2 Kgs 25:27–30; Jer 52:31–34). He did live to see some kindness, but never his homeland (v27).
- **"O land, land, land, hear the word of the LORD!" (v29).** The triple call is a solemn summons, like calling the whole land to witness (6:19; Deut 32:1). The Hebrew word can mean "land" or "earth."
- **"Write this man down as childless" (v30).** Jehoiachin did have sons (1 Chr 3:17–18). The picture is of a family register: in the record that counts, the royal succession, he is entered as having no heir. **"None of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David."** None did. His grandson Zerubbabel led the return from exile, but as governor under Persia, never as king (Hag 1:1).
- **What about the promise to David?** Matthew's genealogy of Jesus runs through Jechoniah (Matt 1:11–12), so the curse raises a real question. Christians have given several answers. Many note that Jesus was Joseph's legal heir but not his physical son, and that Luke traces a line back to David through Nathan, not Solomon (Luke 3:31). Others point to Haggai 2:23, where God tells Zerubbabel, Jehoiachin's grandson, "I will make you like a signet ring, for I have chosen you." That deliberately reverses this verse, and some read it as God lifting the curse. Either way, the earthly throne was empty for centuries, until the promise found its fulfilment in a King of a different kind.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "those that are lifted up with great pride are commonly reserved for some great disgrace in life or death," and notes from v21 that "many will never be ashamed of their sins till they are brought by them to the last extremity." JFB says that God "will repay in justice those who will not in justice pay those whom they employ." He catches the wordplay of v22: "the pastors, whose office it is to feed the sheep, shall themselves be fed on." He also says "Coniah" is a contemptuous shortening of the king's name. That is a traditional view. The shorter form may simply be a variant. Guzik, drawing on Thompson, makes the point that the word "neighbor" in v13 shows the king ignoring a shared covenant bond. He links v16 to 1 John 3, which says love for others shows whether a person knows God. On v30 he follows the view that Luke traces Mary's line through Nathan, which is one of the views above, not the only one.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is a procession of failed kings: one carried off to Egypt, one buried like a donkey, one thrown away like a broken pot. Each was measured by one standard. Did he do justice and righteousness, and defend the poor? Each fell short, and the throne of David was left empty. The next chapter answers: "I will raise up for David a righteous Branch" (23:5). Jesus is that King. He did not build his house by unpaid labor. He gave his own life for his people. He came "to proclaim good news to the poor" (Luke 4:18), and he knows the Father perfectly (Matt 11:27). The signet ring torn from Coniah's hand was given back in a promise to Zerubbabel, and from Zerubbabel's line came Jesus (Matt 1:12–16). He is the Son of David whose kingdom will have no end (Luke 1:32–33).
