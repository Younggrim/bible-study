*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Lilies. Of David." "Lilies" (*Shoshannim*) probably names a tune, the same one used for Psalm 45, though the two psalms could hardly be more different: Psalm 45 is a royal wedding song, and Psalm 69 is the cry of a man going under. The Tyndale notes point out that Psalms 69–71 form a group of laments from a king in anguish, answered by Psalm 72, a prayer for the king's blessing. No event in David's life is named. Guzik takes the title at face value and suggests the closing mention of "the cities of Judah" (v35) could fit the years David reigned over Judah alone (2 Sam 2:1–11). Others think a later king or prophet in Judah's dark days wrote it, perhaps someone like Jeremiah, who was mocked, rejected by his family, and literally sank in the mire of a cistern (Jer 38:6). What is certain is how the New Testament reads it. It is one of the psalms the New Testament quotes most often. JFB counts seven references to Christ and the gospel: John 2:17; 15:25; 19:28–29; Acts 1:20; Romans 11:9–10; 15:3; and the gall and vinegar of the passion accounts. Henry sums it up: "like the Ps. 22… it begins with the humiliation and ends with the exaltation of Christ."

#### The Waters Have Come Up to My Neck (vv1–3)
- **"Save me, O God! For the waters have come up to my neck" (v1).** Literally "to my *nephesh*," a word that can mean soul, life or throat. The KJV has "unto my soul." The ESV's "neck" catches the physical picture: the water is at his throat. JFB: "endanger my life by drowning," and he points to Jonah: "The waters closed in over me to take my life" (Jonah 2:5).
- **"I sink in deep mire, where there is no foothold" (v2).** Not just deep water but mud, with nothing solid underfoot. In the Psalms, the deep waters stand for chaos, death and overwhelming trouble (18:16; 42:7; 124:4–5). The opposite picture is in 40:2: "He drew me up from the pit of destruction, out of the miry bog, and set my feet upon a rock." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, finds four kinds of mire here.
- **"I am weary with my crying out; my throat is parched. My eyes grow dim with waiting for my God" (v3).** He has prayed until his voice gave out, and watched for God's help until his eyes failed. This is the honest weariness of long, unanswered prayer (6:6; 119:82). He still calls him "my God."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "His throat is dried, but his heart is not; his eyes fail, but his faith does not." He links this to Christ on the cross, who cried "Why have you forsaken me?" but still said "My God, my God." He also sees in vv1–2 the inward agony of Jesus: "Now is my soul troubled" (John 12:27).

#### Those Who Hate Me Without Cause (vv4–8)
- **"More in number than the hairs of my head are those who hate me without cause" (v4).** "Without cause" (*chinnam*) means for nothing, gratuitously. Jesus quotes this: "they hated me without a cause" (John 15:25; compare 35:19). Barnhouse, cited by Guzik through Boice, says the cause was not in Jesus but in their own hearts.
- **"What I did not steal must I now restore?" (v4).** He is being made to pay back what he never took, accused and punished as a thief while innocent. The Law said a thief must restore what he stole (Lev 6:4–5). Here the innocent man pays instead. JFB: he "suffered wrongfully under the imputation of robbery."
- **"O God, you know my folly; the wrongs I have done are not hidden from you" (v5).** This is the line that keeps the psalm from being a simple prophecy of Christ. The psalmist is innocent of the charges, but he is not sinless, and he says so. "Folly" (*'ivvelet*) in the Bible means moral foolishness, not low intelligence. "Wrongs" (*'ashmot*) are guilt, offenses. Poole, cited by Guzik, takes the first as sins of ignorance and the second as grosser sins. JFB reads it differently, as a challenge: "If sinful, thou knowest."
- **David is a type, not a copy.** A "type" is a person or event that foreshadows Christ without matching him in every point. JFB: "David's condition as a sufferer may typify Christ's, without requiring that a parallel be found in character." Jesus suffered what David suffered, but without David's sin. Henry and Guzik both apply v5 to Christ in another way: he had no sin of his own, but he bore ours, and God saw it all on him (2 Cor 5:21).
- **"Let not those who hope in you be put to shame through me" (v6).** He fears that his collapse would discourage everyone watching him. If God abandons him, what will others conclude about trusting God? Guzik, following VanGemeren, notes the three titles here: Lord (*Adonai*), GOD (*YHWH*) of hosts, God of Israel, each a reason God should act.
- **"It is for your sake that I have borne reproach" (v7).** His suffering is not punishment for his own sin but the price of serving God. "Reproach" (*cherpah*), scorn and disgrace, is a key word in the psalm: it appears in vv7, 9 (twice), 10, 19 and 20.
- **"I have become a stranger to my brothers, an alien to my mother's sons" (v8).** "My mother's sons" stresses the closest family. Jesus' own brothers did not believe in him (John 7:5), and once his family came to take him home, saying, "He is out of his mind" (Mark 3:21). JFB points to both passages.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives a careful warning on v4. We tend to use "they hate me without a cause" to justify anger at our enemies, "But it is rather an argument why we should bear it patiently, because then we suffer as Christ did." Applying "I restored that which I took not away" to Christ, Henry calls it a description of the satisfaction Christ made for sin: he paid a debt he did not owe. Guzik draws the contrast with Adam, who grasped at what was not his, and Christ, who "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" (Phil 2:6).

#### Zeal for Your House Has Consumed Me (vv9–12)
- **"For zeal for your house has consumed me" (v9).** "Consumed" is literally "eaten me up" (KJV). His passion for God's house, God's worship and God's honor burned in him like a fire that devoured him. After Jesus drove the money changers out of the temple, "his disciples remembered that it was written, 'Zeal for your house will consume me'" (John 2:17). The verb in John is future: this zeal would cost him his life. Guzik notes David's own zeal in planning the temple (2 Sam 7; 1 Chr 22).
- **"The reproaches of those who reproach you have fallen on me" (v9).** Those who hated God took it out on God's servant. Paul quotes this line to explain how Christ lived: "For Christ did not please himself, but as it is written, 'The reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me'" (Rom 15:3). Paul uses it to call strong believers to bear with the weak.
- **"When I wept and humbled my soul with fasting, it became my reproach" (v10).** JFB: literally "wept away my soul." Even his repentance and prayer were mocked. **Sackcloth** (v11), rough cloth made of goat hair, was worn in mourning and repentance. **"A byword"** means a proverb, a joke everyone repeats.
- **"I am the talk of those who sit in the gate, and the drunkards make songs about me" (v12).** The city gate was where the elders sat as judges and the town's business was done (Ruth 4:1; Prov 31:23). So he is mocked from the top of society to the bottom, from the town council to the tavern. At Jesus' trial the rulers mocked him, and at the cross so did the soldiers and passers-by (Mark 15:29–32).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We must not think it strange if we be ill spoken of for that which is well done." He notes that both halves of v9 are applied to Christ in the New Testament: his zeal for the Father's house and his self-denial in bearing others' reproach. Guzik quotes Spurgeon that the ruling passion of Jesus was God's glory, and Poole that "eaten up" means worn out by an intense passion. Henry adds, on the drunkards' songs, a word on "the bad consequences of the sin of drunkenness."

#### At an Acceptable Time, Answer Me (vv13–18)
- **"But as for me, my prayer is to you, O LORD" (v13).** The turn of the psalm. They mock; he prays. Henry: "Though we may be jeered for well-doing, we must never be jeered out of it."
- **"At an acceptable time, O God" (v13).** Literally "a time of favor." Isaiah uses the same phrase: "In a time of favor I have answered you" (Isa 49:8), and Paul applies it to the gospel age: "Behold, now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Cor 6:2). The psalmist is saying God's timing is his to choose, and he leaves it there.
- **"In the abundance of your steadfast love… in your saving faithfulness" (v13).** His appeal rests on God's *chesed*, his covenant love, and on God's *'emet*, faithfulness. Guzik translates *chesed* "loyal love." He does not appeal to his own goodness.
- **"Deliver me from sinking in the mire… let not the flood sweep over me, or the deep swallow me up, or the pit close its mouth over me" (vv14–15).** The drowning pictures from vv1–2 return, now as prayer. "The pit" is a well or cistern, a picture of the grave. A well's mouth closing over someone means no way out. Jeremiah was lowered into exactly such a pit (Jer 38:6).
- **"Answer me, O LORD, for your steadfast love is good" (v16).** He keeps returning to God's character. "Abundant mercy" (*rachamim*) is the tender compassion of a mother for her child (the word is related to "womb").
- **"Hide not your face from your servant… make haste to answer me" (v17).** God's hidden face means his felt absence (13:1; 27:9).
- **"Draw near to my soul, redeem me; ransom me because of my enemies!" (v18).** Two words for rescue at a price. "Redeem" (*ga'al*) is the work of a kinsman-redeemer, the family member who buys a relative out of debt or slavery (Lev 25:25; Ruth 4). "Ransom" (*padah*) is paying the price to set someone free (Exod 13:13). He asks God to act as his next of kin. **"Because of my enemies"**: so they cannot say God failed him.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that need is no bar to God's hearing: "God will not drive us from him, though it is need that drives us to him." He also points out the progress of the psalm: "He gave himself up for lost in the beginning of the psalm; yet now he has his head above water." JFB: "With increasing reliance on God, he prays for help."

#### They Gave Me Sour Wine to Drink (vv19–21)
- **"You know my reproach, and my shame and my dishonor; my foes are all known to you" (v19).** Three words for disgrace. God has seen it all.
- **"Reproaches have broken my heart, so that I am in despair" (v20).** Insults can break a heart as surely as wounds can break a body. **"I looked for pity, but there was none, and for comforters, but I found none."** At Jesus' arrest "they all left him and fled" (Mark 14:50), a parallel JFB notes. Isaiah 63:5 has the same loneliness: "I looked, but there was no one to help."
- **"They gave me poison for food, and for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink" (v21).** "Poison" (*rosh*) is a bitter, poisonous plant, often paired with wormwood (Deut 29:18; Lam 3:19). The KJV calls it "gall." "Sour wine" (*chomets*) is wine gone to vinegar, the cheap drink of soldiers and laborers. Instead of comfort, his enemies gave him bitterness. The Gospels show this happening to Jesus. Before the crucifixion, soldiers offered him wine mixed with gall, which he refused (Matt 27:34). On the cross, he said "I thirst," "to fulfill the Scripture," and they gave him sour wine on a sponge (John 19:28–30; Matt 27:48). The Greek words the Gospels use for gall and sour wine (*cholē*, *oxos*) are the very words of the Greek Old Testament in this verse, a point Kidner makes (cited by Guzik).
- **Commentators:** JFB: "The Psalmist may have thus described by figure what Christ found in reality." Guzik makes the same distinction: for David it was a metaphor, for Jesus it was literal. Guzik also passes on Horne's comment about the bitter "comforts" the world gives the afflicted, and urges believers not to add to others' grief. Henry says nothing was more painful to Christ in his sufferings than the reproach and shame, which he bore for our dishonor of God.

#### Let Their Own Table Become a Snare (vv22–29)
- **The hardest part of the psalm.** These verses are an imprecation, a prayer calling down God's judgment on enemies (compare 35; 109; 137). They are hard to read after Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). Christians have read them in several ways. Some, like Henry, take them less as personal prayers than as prophecies of what will happen to those who reject God's anointed. Others see an honest prayer that hands vengeance over to God instead of taking it (Rom 12:19), and a cry for justice against people who torment the helpless (v26). Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the gulf between the type, David, and the antitype, Christ, who blessed those who cursed him. Morgan, cited by Guzik, adds that Jesus forgave freely but never made peace with deliberate evil. All three ideas can be true together.
- **"Let their own table before them become a snare… a trap" (v22).** Their feasts, their comfort and security, should become the thing that catches them, as a bird is caught by bait. JFB: "blessings shall become curses." "When they are at peace" is literally "for those at peace" (*shelomim*), their security. Paul quotes vv22–23 in Romans 11:9–10 to describe the hardening of those in Israel who rejected Christ, but he goes straight on to ask: "did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means!" (Rom 11:11). He looks ahead to their restoration (Rom 11:23–26).
- **"Let their eyes be darkened… and make their loins tremble continually" (v23).** They gave him bitterness when his eyes failed (v3). Now let their eyes fail. JFB: "continually to shake—literally, 'to swerve' or bend in weakness."
- **"May their camp be a desolation; let no one dwell in their tents" (v25).** "Camp" (*tirah*) is an encampment or walled settlement. JFB calls it their "palaces." Peter applied this verse to Judas after his death: "May his camp become desolate, and let there be no one to dwell in it" (Acts 1:20). And Jesus' words over Jerusalem echo it: "your house is left to you desolate" (Matt 23:38).
- **"For they persecute him whom you have struck down" (v26).** This is the reason for the curses. They kick a man who is already down. They see him suffering under God's hand and add to it with taunts. Isaiah 53:4 describes the Servant "stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted," and JFB notes that God's purpose in Christ's suffering did not make his persecutors less guilty (Acts 2:23). **"Recount the pain"**: they make the wounded man's suffering into gossip.
- **"Add to them punishment upon punishment" (v27).** The Hebrew word *'avon* means both iniquity and its punishment, as Trapp (cited by Guzik) and Henry both note. **"May they have no acquittal from you"** is literally "let them not come into your righteousness."
- **"Let them be blotted out of the book of the living; let them not be enrolled among the righteous" (v28).** The picture is of a city register of citizens. To be blotted out is to lose your place among God's people, and in the end, life itself. Moses offered to be blotted out of God's book for Israel's sake (Exod 32:32). The New Testament speaks of the "book of life" (Phil 4:3; Rev 3:5; 20:15). Maclaren, cited by Guzik, says it means being shut out of the covenant community, more than physical death.
- **"But I am afflicted and in pain; let your salvation, O God, set me on high!" (v29).** A one-verse stanza. After the storm of curses, he returns to his own need. "Set me on high" means put me out of reach, safe on a high place (20:1; 91:14).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads these verses as "not David's prayers against his enemies, but prophecies" of the judgment that fell on those who rejected Christ. He applies v22 to the altar and its sacrifices, "which should have been for their welfare," becoming a snare. But he also stresses that those who had a hand in Christ's death and repented "found mercy" (Acts 2:23; 3:14–15), and that "the gospel excludes none that do not by their unbelief exclude themselves." On v26 he gives a pastoral warning: "nothing is more provoking to God than to insult over those whom he has smitten," and the troubled in spirit "ought to be very tenderly dealt with." Guzik leaves open whether the verses are wish or prediction, and cites Dickson, via Spurgeon, who counts ten distinct judgments in them.

#### I Will Praise the Name of God with a Song (vv30–36)
- **"I will praise the name of God with a song; I will magnify him with thanksgiving" (v30).** The psalm that began underwater ends in song. "Magnify" means to make great, to speak of God's greatness so that others see it.
- **"This will please the LORD more than an ox or a bull with horns and hoofs" (v31).** A grateful song pleases God more than a costly sacrifice (40:6; 50:13–14; 51:16–17). "Horns and hoofs" means a full-grown animal. Horne, cited by Guzik, notes that a young bull was considered fit for sacrifice once it had grown both. There may be wordplay: "song" (*shir*) and "ox" (*shor*) sound almost the same in Hebrew. JFB notes that "rather contemptuous terms are used" for the animal, which sets it beneath the praise.
- **"When the humble see it they will be glad; you who seek God, let your hearts revive" (v32).** This answers his fear in v6. Instead of being shamed by his fall, God's people will be encouraged by his rescue. "Let your hearts revive" (literally "let your heart live") is the same phrase as 22:26.
- **"For the LORD hears the needy and does not despise his own people who are prisoners" (v33).** "His prisoners": those bound for his sake. JFB: "prisoners—peculiarly liable to be despised." Hebrews urges believers to "remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them" (Heb 13:3).
- **"Let heaven and earth praise him, the seas and everything that moves in them" (v34).** The man who nearly drowned now calls the sea itself to praise God.
- **"For God will save Zion and build up the cities of Judah" (v35).** The personal rescue widens to the whole people. God will rebuild what was ruined, and his people will live there again. The Tyndale notes suggest these verses apply the whole prayer to Judah's desperate condition, which many scholars take as a sign the closing verses were added or adapted for a later time of national ruin.
- **"The offspring of his servants shall inherit it, and those who love his name shall dwell in it" (v36).** The land will not be lost for lack of heirs. Those who love God's name will live there, and so will their children (37:29).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "humble and thankful praises are more pleasing to God than the most costly pompous sacrifices," "the calves of our lips, not the calves of the stall" (Heb 13:15). He sees in v32 the exaltation of the Savior: when Christ is raised, "the humble shall look upon it and be glad." JFB reads vv34–36 as pointing to "the spiritual privileges and blessings of the Church," though the imagery is drawn from the land of Israel.

#### Pointing to Christ
The New Testament writers turned to Psalm 69 again and again because they saw Jesus in it. He was hated without cause (v4), estranged from his brothers (v8), consumed by zeal for his Father's house (v9), mocked by rulers and drunkards (v12), left without comforters (v20), and given sour wine for his thirst (v21). He even restored what he did not steal, paying the debt of sinners who had robbed God of his glory. Yet he is greater than David: he had no folly to confess (v5), and where David called down judgment, Jesus prayed for his enemies. The judgment the psalm describes is real, and it falls on those who refuse him, but Jesus bore its weight first, for those who would turn. The psalm ends with the humble glad, the prisoners heard, and Zion rebuilt, which is where the cross leads.
