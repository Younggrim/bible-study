*Setting:* The story returns to Joseph where 37:36 left him: sold by Midianite and Ishmaelite traders to Potiphar, a senior official of Pharaoh, in Egypt. Joseph is seventeen or a little older, a slave in a foreign land, cut off from his father, his language and his God's people. Egypt was already an ancient civilization, rich from the Nile and governed by a large class of royal officials. Chapter 38 has just shown Judah, a free man in Canaan, going looking for a prostitute. Chapter 39 sets beside it Joseph, a slave in Egypt, refusing a woman who pursues him every day. The chapter is framed by one phrase repeated four times: "the LORD was with Joseph" (vv2, 3, 21, 23). His circumstances fall from steward to prisoner, but that line never changes.

#### Joseph and Potiphar's Wife (vv1–23)
- **"Joseph had been brought down to Egypt" (v1).** The narrator picks up the thread from 37:36, with the same details: Potiphar, "an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard." "Captain of the guard" is literally "chief of the slaughterers," probably the head of Pharaoh's bodyguard and the officer in charge of the royal prison. "Officer" (*saris*) can mean "eunuch," but the Hebrew dictionary also lists it as a general title for a court official, and Potiphar has a wife. His name is Egyptian, probably "he whom Ra gave," Ra being the sun god (compare Potiphera in 41:45). JFB connects it with being "devoted to the sun." The narrator also calls him **"an Egyptian"**, which seems obvious. It underlines how foreign Joseph's new world is.
- **"The LORD was with Joseph, and he became a successful man" (v2).** Here the narrator uses God's covenant name, *the LORD* (Yahweh), the name he made known to Israel's fathers. The Tyndale notes observe that apart from the judgments in chapter 38, this name has not appeared since Jacob left Laban (31:49). God has been silent through the selling of Joseph. Now we learn he was there all along. "Successful" does not mean comfortable. Joseph is still a slave. It means his work prospered, and God was the reason.
- **"His master saw that the LORD was with him" (v3).** An Egyptian official, with his own gods, could see that Joseph's God was at work. Joseph's faith showed in how he worked. JFB notes that he "continued a worshipper of the true God" in an idolater's house.
- **"He made him overseer of his house" (v4).** Egyptian records show that large households had a steward who ran the estate, managed the other servants and kept the accounts. Joseph rises from household slave to that position. It is a pattern that repeats through the story: wherever Joseph is put, he ends up in charge (39:22; 41:40).
- **"The LORD blessed the Egyptian's house for Joseph's sake" (v5).** God promised Abraham that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (12:3). Laban said much the same of Jacob: "the LORD has blessed me because of you" (30:27). Now an Egyptian household is blessed because of one of Abraham's descendants, "in house and field," and soon all Egypt and the surrounding nations will be fed through him.
- **"He had no concern about anything but the food he ate" (v6).** Potiphar trusted Joseph so completely that he did not even check the accounts. The mention of food may reflect Egyptian rules about eating apart from foreigners (43:32). Some interpreters take "food" as a polite way of referring to his private life, including his wife, which v9 might support ("nor has he kept back anything from me except you"). The simpler reading is that Potiphar kept only his own table under his control.
- **"Joseph was handsome in form and appearance" (v6).** The Hebrew phrase is the same one used of his mother Rachel (29:17). This is the hinge of the chapter. The same gift that helped him win favor also brought him into danger.
- **"His master's wife cast her eyes on Joseph and said, 'Lie with me'" (v7).** "After a time" suggests Joseph has been in the house for some years. Her words are only two words in Hebrew, blunt and commanding, as if speaking to a servant. She never has a name. She is defined by what she wants.
- **Joseph's answer (vv8–9).** He gives reasons, in order. First, trust: his master has put everything in his hands. Second, the boundary: "nor has he kept back anything from me except you, because you are his wife." Third and highest: **"How then can I do this great wickedness and sin against God?"** Joseph sees adultery as a betrayal of his master, but above all as a sin against God. David later prayed in the same way after his adultery: "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (Ps 51:4). Joseph speaks of "God" (*Elohim*), the general word, perhaps because he is speaking to an Egyptian. JFB calls his reply "the true principle of moral purity."
- **"Day after day… he would not listen to her, to lie beside her or to be with her" (v10).** The temptation was not a single moment. It went on day after day, from a woman who had power over him as a slave. Joseph did not just refuse the act. He refused even to be with her. Proverbs gives the same advice about the adulteress: "Keep your way far from her, and do not go near the door of her house" (Prov 5:8).
- **"None of the men of the house was there" (v11).** She chooses her moment when no witnesses are present. Some later Jewish writers imagined Joseph wavering that day, but the text gives no hint of it. He "went into the house to do his work."
- **"He left his garment in her hand and fled" (v12).** She grabbed his garment, probably his outer cloak. He let go of it and ran. Paul's counsel is the same: "Flee from sexual immorality" (1 Cor 6:18) and "flee youthful passions" (2 Tim 2:22). Some temptations should be argued with. This one was to be run from. For the second time a garment is taken from Joseph and used to tell a lie about him. His brothers took his robe and used it to deceive their father (37:31–33). Now his master's wife takes his cloak and uses it to deceive her husband. The word "garment" comes six times in vv12–18.
- **"He has brought among us a Hebrew to laugh at us" (vv13–15).** She calls the household servants and turns them against Joseph. She calls him "a Hebrew," a word outsiders used for Abraham's people (14:13), and in Egypt a word of contempt (43:32). She speaks of "us," uniting Egyptians against the foreigner, and she blames her husband: "he has brought" him. "To laugh at us" uses the verb *tsachaq*, the root of Isaac's name. It can mean to mock, but the dictionary also lists "toy with," and it is used of Isaac caressing Rebekah (26:8). She implies an insult and a sexual assault in one word. Her story has one true detail: he did leave his garment. She changes "in her hand" to "beside me."
- **"The Hebrew servant, whom you have brought among us" (vv16–18).** She keeps the cloak until her husband comes home and tells him the same story, adjusted for him. To the servants she said "a Hebrew." To her husband she says "the Hebrew servant," a reminder that he is a slave, and that Potiphar himself brought him in. She makes her husband partly responsible.
- **"His anger was kindled" (v19).** The text does not say at whom. A slave accused of assaulting his master's wife could expect to be executed. Instead Potiphar puts Joseph in the royal prison. Many readers, Guzik among them, think Potiphar may not have fully believed her. His anger may have been at the situation, or at his wife, as much as at Joseph. Others think Potiphar valued Joseph too much to kill him. The text leaves it open, but the lighter sentence is itself a quiet mercy.
- **"The prison, the place where the king's prisoners were confined" (v20).** The word for prison, *sohar*, occurs only in this part of Genesis. The Hebrew dictionary suggests "round house," perhaps a fortress or tower, or an Egyptian word in Hebrew dress. JFB describes it as a partly underground "roundhouse" attached to an officer's house, used for state prisoners. This was not a common jail but where the king's own officials were held, which is why Pharaoh's cupbearer and baker are sent there (40:3). Psalm 105 remembers the suffering the narrator leaves out: "His feet were hurt with fetters; his neck was put in a collar of iron; until what he had said came to pass, the word of the LORD tested him" (Ps 105:18–19).
- **"The LORD was with Joseph and showed him steadfast love" (v21).** "Steadfast love" is *hesed*, God's loyal, covenant love, the love that keeps its promises. This is the first time the Joseph story uses it. The man who was faithful to his master and his God is met by the faithfulness of God. And again, he finds "favor," this time with the keeper of the prison.
- **"Whatever was done there, he was the one who did it" (vv22–23).** In Potiphar's house and now in prison, Joseph is put in charge. The chapter ends almost as it began: "the LORD was with him. And whatever he did, the LORD made it succeed." The circumstances have collapsed. The verdict on Joseph's life has not changed.
- **Joseph and Judah.** Read with chapter 38, the contrast is sharp. Judah, free, at home, a widower, went to a woman he took for a prostitute. Joseph, a slave, far from home, alone, refused a powerful woman day after day. In both stories a personal item of the man's is held as evidence (38:18; 39:12). Judah's was proof of guilt; Joseph's was used for a lie. Yet God is at work through both men: Judah will be humbled into a leader, and Joseph will be raised up through suffering.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter in two parts: Joseph honored by God's providence, which made him "in effect, a master," and by God's grace, which made him "more than a conqueror over a strong temptation to uncleanness"; then Joseph suffering, falsely accused, imprisoned, and comforted with "the tokens of God's special presence." On the garment, Henry says he "left his garment, as one escaping for his life," and adds, "It is better to lose a good coat than a good conscience." Guzik draws practical lessons from Joseph's refusal: he kept busy, avoided being alone with her, called sin by its name, and simply said no. He also notes that Joseph did not defend himself before Potiphar, like Jesus silent before his accusers (Isa 53:7; Matt 27:12–14), and he passes on Derek Kidner's suggestion that Potiphar may have had a "faint misgiving" about the charge. JFB stresses that she called Joseph "a Hebrew" because Hebrews were despised by Egyptians, so her words also insulted her husband. JFB thinks the prison keeper may have known Joseph was innocent. Its description of Egyptian women as "very loose in their morals" is a sweeping judgment of its time that the text does not make. JFB has no notes on vv4, 6, 8, or vv10–13.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joseph is innocent, tempted, falsely accused, stripped of his garment, silent before his accusers, and counted among criminals. Through it all the LORD is with him, and his suffering becomes the road to his exaltation. Jesus was tempted in every way as we are, "yet without sin" (Heb 4:15). He was falsely accused, his garments were taken (John 19:23–24), and he "was numbered with the transgressors" (Isa 53:12). Joseph fled temptation and was punished for doing right. Jesus never sinned and bore the punishment for the sins of others. Joseph's faithfulness brought blessing to an Egyptian household. Christ's faithfulness brings blessing to all the families of the earth, as God promised Abraham.
