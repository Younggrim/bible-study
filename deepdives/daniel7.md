*Setting:* Daniel 7 is the hinge of the book. The stories of chapters 1–6 are over, and the visions of chapters 7–12 begin. In time it steps back to "the first year of Belshazzar" (v1), about 553 BC, so it comes before the feast of chapter 5 and the lions' den of chapter 6. Daniel is around sixty-five and still in Babylon. This is also the last chapter of the Aramaic section of the book (2:4–7:28), the part written in the international language of the day and addressed to the nations. Chapter 7 deliberately echoes chapter 2. There Nebuchadnezzar saw four kingdoms as a splendid statue of metals, crushed by a stone. Here Daniel sees the same four kingdoms as they look from heaven: wild beasts rising out of a stormy sea, judged by God and replaced by the kingdom of "one like a son of man."

#### Daniel's Vision of the Four Beasts (vv1–8)
- **"He wrote down the dream and told the sum of the matter" (v1).** Daniel records "the sum," the main points, not every detail. Up to now he interpreted other people's dreams. From here on, the dreams are his, and he needs an interpreter himself (v16).
- **"The four winds of heaven were stirring up the great sea" (v2).** In the Old Testament the sea often stands for chaos and the restless nations (Ps 65:7; Isa 17:12–13; 57:20). The four winds point to every direction, the whole world in turmoil. Revelation picks up the image: John sees a beast "rising out of the sea" that combines features of all four of Daniel's (Rev 13:1–2).
- **The interpretive key (v17).** The angel later says plainly that the beasts are "four kings," meaning kings and their kingdoms (compare v23). Most readers line them up with the four metals of chapter 2, though, as below, they disagree about the last two.
- **"Like a lion and had eagles' wings" (v4).** Winged lions guarded the gates and palaces of Babylon and appear on its glazed-brick walls. Jeremiah pictured Nebuchadnezzar as both lion and eagle (Jer 4:7; 49:19, 22). Almost everyone agrees this is Babylon, the head of gold. **"Its wings were plucked off… made to stand on two feet like a man, and the mind of a man was given to it."** This recalls chapter 4 in reverse: Nebuchadnezzar was given the mind of a beast, then restored to his right mind once he was humbled.
- **"Like a bear… raised up on one side" (v5).** A bear is heavy and powerful rather than swift. "Raised up on one side" probably pictures one half stronger than the other, which fits the Medo-Persian alliance, where Persia came to dominate Media (compare the ram with one horn "higher than the other," 8:3, 20). The **"three ribs"** in its mouth are usually taken as three main conquests, most often Lydia, Babylon and Egypt. **"Arise, devour much flesh"** fits Persia's huge expansion under Cyrus and Cambyses.
- **"Like a leopard, with four wings… four heads" (v6).** A leopard is fast, and with four wings even more so. Alexander the Great conquered from Greece to India in about ten years (334–323 BC). After his death his empire split into four kingdoms under his generals, which the "four heads" seem to picture (compare the four horns of 8:8, 22, where Greece is named).
- **"A fourth beast, terrifying and dreadful and exceedingly strong" (v7).** No animal is named. It is simply beyond comparison. **"Great iron teeth"** links it to the iron legs of chapter 2 (2:33, 40). It crushes and tramples whatever it does not devour. Most Christian interpreters since the early church have taken it as Rome, which absorbed the remains of Alexander's world.
- **"Ten horns" (v7).** A horn is the Bible's common picture for strength and especially for a king (Ps 75:10; Zech 1:18–21). The angel interprets them as ten kings (v24).
- **"Another horn, a little one" (v8).** It begins small, uproots three other horns, and has **"eyes like the eyes of a man, and a mouth speaking great things."** The eyes suggest intelligence, perhaps cunning. "Great things" means boasting and arrogance (v25; Rev 13:5). Note that this horn is still a "man." For all its terror, it is only human.
- **Which kingdoms? The main views.** The traditional view, held by Jerome, the Reformers and most conservative interpreters, is: Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome. Many in this camp, especially dispensational interpreters, see the ten horns and little horn as a future, final form of Rome and its last ruler, the Antichrist (2 Thess 2:3–4; Rev 13). Historicist Protestants once identified the little horn with the papacy. Most critical scholars, and some others, read the sequence as Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece, with the little horn as the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175–164 BC), who persecuted the Jews and defiled the temple. Chapter 8 clearly does describe Antiochus as a "little horn" coming out of Greece (8:9, 21–23), which gives that view weight. Against it, Daniel's own book treats Media and Persia as one kingdom (5:28; 6:8; 8:20). Many readers see both at once: Antiochus as a real fulfilment and a preview of a final enemy still to come.
- **Commentators:** Henry identifies the lion as "the Chaldean monarchy." On the fourth beast he admits "the learned are not agreed," and gives both the Roman view and the view that it is Syria under the Seleucids, with the little horn as "Antiochus Epiphanes, the last of the ten." JFB holds the Roman view and notes that "no particular beast is specified as the image of the fourth." Like many Protestants of his day, he adds that "Popery seems to be a fulfilment of the prophecy in many particulars," though he still expects a personal Antichrist. Guzik also takes the four as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome. He reads the ten horns as ten kings of a revived Roman empire at the end of history and the little horn as the Antichrist.

#### The Ancient of Days Reigns (vv9–12)
- **"Thrones were placed, and the Ancient of Days took his seat" (v9).** The scene moves from the stormy sea to the calm of heaven's courtroom. "Ancient of Days" (*attiq yomin*) means one who is advanced in days, the eternal one, who was there before any empire. The plural "thrones" suggests a court of assessors, and Revelation shows thrones around God's throne (Rev 4:4; 20:4).
- **"White as snow… like pure wool" (v9).** White speaks of purity and the dignity of age, wisdom rather than weakness. John sees the risen Jesus with hair "white, like white wool, like snow" (Rev 1:14). He takes language Daniel used of the Ancient of Days and applies it to Christ.
- **"His throne was fiery flames; its wheels were burning fire" (v9).** A throne with wheels recalls Ezekiel's vision of God's chariot-throne (Ezek 1:15–21; 10). Fire is the sign of God's holy presence and judgment (Exod 19:18; Deut 4:24). **"A stream of fire issued"** (v10) pictures judgment pouring out from him.
- **"A thousand thousands served him" (v10).** The numbers are a way of saying "beyond counting" (compare Deut 33:2; Rev 5:11).
- **"The court sat in judgment, and the books were opened" (v10).** God keeps records (Exod 32:32; Ps 56:8; Mal 3:16), and nothing done on earth is forgotten. Revelation 20:12 uses almost the same words for the final judgment.
- **"Because of the sound of the great words that the horn was speaking" (v11).** The boasting goes on even as the court sits. Then, in one short line, **"the beast was killed."** All that terror ends without a fight.
- **"Their lives were prolonged for a season and a time" (v12).** The earlier beasts lost their power but were not wiped out at once. Their peoples and cultures carried on inside the empires that followed. The fourth beast, by contrast, is utterly destroyed.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God "is called the Ancient of days, because he is God from everlasting to everlasting," and on the books, "the judgment is set, publicly and openly." JFB identifies the Ancient of Days with the Father: "He is the Judge here." Guzik agrees, since the son of man is brought to him as a distinct figure. He takes the wheels as a sign of God's ceaseless activity.

#### The Son of Man Is Given Dominion (vv13–14)
- **"With the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man" (v13).** In Aramaic, *bar enash* means "a human being." After four beasts comes someone human, the picture of true humanity as God made it to rule (Gen 1:26–28; Ps 8:4–6). The beasts rose from the sea. He comes with the clouds of heaven, and in the Old Testament it is God who rides on the clouds (Ps 104:3; Isa 19:1). So this figure is human and yet more than human.
- **"He came to the Ancient of Days and was presented before him" (v13).** He comes *to* God, not *down* to earth. This is an enthronement scene in heaven. Henry and others connect it with the ascension (Acts 1:9).
- **"To him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom" (v14).** It is given, not seized. It is universal: **"all peoples, nations, and languages,"** the very phrase used of the empires of Nebuchadnezzar and Darius (3:4; 6:25). The verb "serve" here is the one used elsewhere in the Aramaic of Daniel for serving God (3:12, 17–18, 28; 6:16, 20). And it is permanent: it **"shall not pass away."**
- **Jesus' favourite title.** "The Son of Man" is the name Jesus uses for himself more than any other in the Gospels. At his trial he quotes this verse to the high priest: "You will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven" (Mark 14:62). The high priest tears his robes and calls it blasphemy. He understood exactly what Jesus was claiming.
- **Who is he?** Jewish and Christian interpreters have long read the figure as the Messiah. Some modern scholars read him as a symbol for "the saints of the Most High" (v18, 27), Israel vindicated. The two are less opposed than they look. In v27 the kingdom is given to the people, and "his kingdom" belongs to one ruler. The king and his people share the kingdom.
- **Commentators:** Henry says he comes "swiftly, irresistibly… with the clouds of heaven," and refers the scene to Christ's ascension. JFB calls him "Head of restored humanity." Guzik ties the title to Jesus' own use of it and holds that the kingdom will be fully seen on earth after Christ returns.

#### Daniel's Vision Interpreted (vv15–28)
- **"My spirit within me was anxious" (v15).** Even with a vision of God's victory, Daniel is shaken. He goes to **"one of those who stood there,"** one of the attending angels, and asks for "the truth."
- **"The saints of the Most High shall receive the kingdom" (v18).** "Saints" (*qaddishin*) means "holy ones," people set apart for God. In Daniel's day it meant faithful Israel. The New Testament widens it to all who belong to Christ (Rom 1:7; Eph 2:19). The kingdom the son of man receives (v14), the saints also receive (vv18, 22, 27).
- **"Claws of bronze" (v19).** A new detail appears in the retelling. Daniel's focus is the fourth beast and especially the horn, which **"seemed greater than its companions"** (v20), though it started small.
- **"This horn made war with the saints and prevailed over them" (v21).** God's people are not promised exemption from suffering. The horn wins for a time, **"until the Ancient of Days came"** (v22).
- **"Ten kings… and another shall arise after them" (v24).** The angel explains the horns as kings. The tenth is followed by one who is **"different"** and puts down three.
- **Four marks of the little horn (v25).** He speaks against the Most High. He **"shall wear out the saints,"** a word used of clothes worn thin, picturing slow, grinding persecution. He **"shall think to change the times and the law,"** trying to rewrite the calendar and worship God gave. Antiochus IV did exactly this in 167 BC: he banned the Sabbath, the festivals and circumcision, and set up pagan worship in the temple (1 Macc 1:41–61).
- **"A time, times, and half a time" (v25).** Most take "time" as a year, so this means three and a half years. It is half of seven, the number of completeness, so the persecution is real but cut short. The figure returns in 12:7 and in Revelation as "1,260 days" and "forty-two months" (Rev 11:2–3; 12:6, 14; 13:5). Antiochus's defilement of the temple lasted about three years before it was cleansed in 164 BC, the event Hanukkah remembers.
- **"But the court shall sit in judgment" (v26).** The scene of vv9–10 returns. Persecution ends not by human revolt but by God's verdict.
- **"The kingdom… shall be given to the people of the saints of the Most High" (v27).** "The greatness of the kingdoms under the whole heaven" passes to God's people. **"His kingdom shall be an everlasting kingdom"** most naturally refers to the Most High, with his people sharing his reign (compare 2 Tim 2:12; Rev 5:10; 22:5).
- **"I kept the matter in my heart" (v28).** Daniel is pale and troubled. He does not fully understand, but he holds on to what he has seen. Compare Mary, who "treasured up all these things, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "a time, times, and half" as "literally three and a half years" of persecution under the final Antichrist, and rejects the old year-for-a-day reckoning. He adds that "Christ and His people are one in suffering, and one in glory." Guzik also takes it as three and a half years, the second half of the seventieth week of 9:27. He stresses that the saints *receive* the kingdom as a gift when Christ returns and do not win it by conquest. Henry, who leaves both the Roman and the Seleucid readings open, allows that an earlier fulfilment may also picture later ones. The Tyndale notes leave the length of this period open and stress that it is limited and will end suddenly.

#### Pointing to Christ
The beasts rise from the sea and devour. The son of man comes with the clouds and receives. Jesus took this title as his own, lived it out in a life of service, and told his disciples the Son of Man came "not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). The one who will receive all dominion first let the beasts of this world do their worst to him. Then he rose, ascended, and was given "all authority in heaven and on earth" (Matt 28:18). Daniel saw the saints worn down and then given the kingdom. That is the pattern of the cross and resurrection, and Jesus promises his people a share in it (Luke 12:32; Rev 3:21).
