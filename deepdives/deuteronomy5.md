*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Chapters 1–4 were Moses' first sermon, retelling Israel's history and urging loyalty to the God who "spoke out of the midst of the fire." Chapter 4 ended with a heading: "This is the law that Moses set before the people." Now the second and longest sermon begins (chapters 5–26), and Moses starts where God started at Sinai: with the Ten Commandments. They were first given in Exodus 20. Here Moses repeats them to a new generation, with a few telling differences, and then recalls how the people begged him to stand between them and God. The chapter is the foundation for everything that follows in Deuteronomy. The laws in chapters 6–26 largely unpack these ten words.

#### The Ten Commandments (vv1–21)
- **"Hear, O Israel" (v1).** Moses gathers "all Israel." The call to hear (*shema*) means listen and obey. **"Learn them and be careful to do them."** The commands are to be studied and lived, not just heard once.
- **"The LORD our God made a covenant with us in Horeb" (v2).** The Hebrew idiom is literally "cut a covenant," probably because covenants were sealed by cutting sacrificial animals (Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18).
- **"Not with our fathers did the LORD make this covenant, but with us, who are all of us here alive today" (v3).** Strictly speaking the covenant *was* made with their parents, most of whom have now died. Moses means "not only with our fathers, but with us." The covenant is not a museum piece. Every generation stands at Sinai and must answer for itself. Jews at Passover still say "this is what the LORD did for *me* when I came out of Egypt" (Exod 13:8).
- **"The LORD spoke with you face to face… while I stood between the LORD and you" (vv4–5).** "Face to face" here means directly, in person, not that they saw a form, since "you saw no form" (4:12). The people heard God's voice themselves, but they were too afraid to go up the mountain, so Moses stood between as go-between. Both are true: God spoke to them directly, and they needed a mediator.
- **"I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (v6).** The commandments begin not with a demand but with who God is and what he has done. Grace comes first, then obedience. Israel did not obey in order to be freed. They were freed, and so they obey.
- **How to count the ten.** The Bible calls them "the Ten Words" (4:13; 10:4) but doesn't number them. Jewish tradition counts v6 as the first "word" and joins "no other gods" and "no images" as the second. Most Protestant churches count "no other gods" and "no images" as separate commandments (first and second) and the two "covet" clauses as one (tenth). Catholic and Lutheran tradition joins "no other gods" with "no images" and divides the coveting command into two. All agree on the content.
- **"You shall have no other gods before me" (v7).** "Before me" is literally "before my face," that is, in my presence, alongside me. In a world full of gods, Israel was to have one. Nothing is to compete with God for first place.
- **"You shall not make for yourself a carved image" (v8).** This forbids images of other gods, and also images of the LORD himself, as chapter 4 explained: "you saw no form" (4:15–19). God cannot be pictured, controlled or carried around like an idol.
- **"A jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate me" (v9).** "Jealous" (*qanna*) is used only of God, the right zeal of a husband for his wife. This does not mean children are punished for their parents' sin while innocent themselves. Deuteronomy 24:16 says plainly that "fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor children… because of their fathers" (also Ezek 18:20). It describes how sin's effects run through families, and notice: "of those who hate me." The children who follow their parents' rejection of God share in it. Three or four generations is the span of a household living together.
- **"But showing steadfast love to thousands of those who love me" (v10).** The contrast is the point. Judgment reaches three or four generations, but God's love reaches "thousands," probably thousands of generations (7:9). "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. Mercy far outweighs judgment.
- **"You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain" (v11).** Literally "lift up the name… to emptiness." It covers swearing false oaths in God's name (Lev 19:12), using his name as a magic word or to manipulate, and treating it lightly. Bearing God's name as his people and then living as if he doesn't matter also falls under it.
- **"Observe the Sabbath day, to keep it holy" (v12).** Exodus 20:8 says "Remember." Deuteronomy says "Observe" (*shamar*, "keep" or "guard"). Jewish tradition holds that both were spoken at once, which is why the Sabbath is both remembered and guarded. "Sabbath" comes from a verb meaning to stop or cease.
- **"That your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (v14).** The Sabbath is for everyone: children, servants, foreigners and even animals. No one in Israel could be worked seven days a week. In the ancient world this was remarkable.
- **"You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt" (v15).** This is the biggest change from Exodus. There, the reason for the Sabbath is creation: God rested on the seventh day (Exod 20:11). Here, the reason is redemption: God freed you from slavery, so give rest to those who work for you. Both reasons stand together. The God who made the world and the God who rescued Israel calls his people to rest and to let others rest. Most Christians have kept the first day of the week as the Lord's Day, celebrating the resurrection (Acts 20:7; Rev 1:10), and Christians differ on how directly the Sabbath command applies to them (Col 2:16–17; Heb 4:9–10).
- **"Honor your father and your mother… that your days may be long" (v16).** This is the first command with a promise attached, as Paul notes (Eph 6:2–3). Deuteronomy adds "that it may go well with you." "Honor" (*kabed*) comes from the word for heavy or weighty: give parents weight, take them seriously, care for them in old age (Mark 7:10–13).
- **"You shall not murder" (v17).** The verb (*ratsach*) refers to unlawful killing of a person. The same law allows killing in war and as a judicial penalty, so this is not a ban on all killing. It is the same word used in Numbers 35 for the murderer. Jesus traces murder back to anger and contempt (Matt 5:21–22).
- **"And you shall not commit adultery… And you shall not steal… And you shall not bear false witness… And you shall not covet" (vv18–21).** In Deuteronomy each of these is joined with "And," which Exodus does not have. It ties the last commands together as one chain: life, marriage, property, reputation and the heart all belong to the neighbor God has given. **"Steal"** (*ganav*) is the same verb used for kidnapping in 24:7, and some Jewish interpreters read this command as mainly about stealing people. **"False witness"** first means lying testimony in court, which could get someone executed (19:16–21), but it extends to any lie that harms a neighbor.
- **"You shall not covet your neighbor's wife. And you shall not desire your neighbor's house, his field" (v21).** Two changes from Exodus 20:17. First, the wife comes first, set apart from the property list, and Deuteronomy uses two verbs: "covet" (*chamad*) for the wife and "desire" (*'avah*) for the house. Second, "his field" is added, fitting a people about to receive land. **Coveting** is the only commandment aimed purely at the heart. *Chamad* is the root used of the tree in Eden, "desirable to make one wise" (Gen 3:6), and Achan's confession, "I coveted them and took them" (Josh 7:21). Coveting is where other sins begin. Paul said this command was the one that exposed his own sin (Rom 7:7).
- **Commentators:** On v3, JFB lists several readings, the main one being that the covenant included the present generation as well as their fathers. On the Sabbath, JFB calls Deuteronomy's "a different reason," but not a conflicting one, and thinks Moses stressed it so that servants would actually receive their rest. Henry says the Egypt reason "was typical of our redemption by Jesus Christ." He also notes the small differences in wording from Exodus and says, "It is more necessary that we tie ourselves to the things, than to the words unalterably." JFB argues that because "wife" and "house" are swapped between Exodus and Deuteronomy, both belong to "one and the same commandment," which supports counting the coveting clauses as one. Guzik says "visiting the iniquity" describes the generational outworking of sin among those who hate God, not guilt transferred to the innocent. He reads "face to face" as an idiom, as in Exodus 33:11, and notes that "before me" is literally "to my face." He says the word "covet" literally means "to pant after," but the lexicon gives *chamad* as "desire, take pleasure in," so the image is his, not the word's. Guzik also holds that Christians are not bound to a particular Sabbath day, since Christ is their rest, though regular rest is still wise; other Christians, especially in the Reformed tradition, see the Lord's Day as the Christian Sabbath. JFB treats vv7–11 and 17–20 only in a general note.

#### Moses Intercedes (vv22–33)
- **"These words the LORD spoke… with a loud voice; and he added no more" (v22).** The Ten Commandments are unique. God spoke these, and only these, directly to the whole people. Everything else came through Moses. **"He wrote them on two tablets of stone."** The stone showed they were permanent.
- **"You came near to me, all the heads of your tribes, and your elders" (v23).** The leaders came on the people's behalf, terrified by the burning mountain (Exod 20:18–21).
- **"This day we have seen God speak with man, and man still live" (v24).** They realized something amazing: God had spoken directly to them and they had not died.
- **"Now therefore why should we die? For this great fire will consume us" (v25).** But they did not want to risk it again. **"Who is there of all flesh, that has heard the voice of the living God… and has still lived?" (v26).** The "living God" contrasts with dead idols that cannot speak (4:28).
- **"Go near and hear… and speak to us… and we will hear and do it" (v27).** The people ask Moses to be their mediator. They promise to obey whatever he relays. Sadly, they made the same promise at Sinai, "All that the LORD has spoken we will do" (Exod 19:8; 24:3, 7), and broke it within weeks at the golden calf.
- **"They are right in all that they have spoken" (v28).** God approves their request. Their fear was the right response to his holiness. A sinful people could not stand in God's direct presence. They needed someone to stand between. This is also the basis for the promise of a coming prophet like Moses (18:15–18), which explicitly recalls this day at Horeb.
- **"Oh that they had such a heart as this always, to fear me and to keep all my commandments" (v29).** One of the most moving verses in the Old Testament: God longs for his people. The Hebrew is literally "Who will give that…?", a cry of desire. Their words were right; the question was whether their hearts would stay that way. Deuteronomy later admits that they would not, "to this day the LORD has not given you a heart to understand" (29:4), and promises that one day "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart" (30:6). Jeremiah and Ezekiel pick up this hope: a new covenant written on the heart (Jer 31:33; Ezek 36:26–27).
- **"Return to your tents. But you, stand here by me" (vv30–31).** The people go home, and Moses stays to receive "the whole commandment and the statutes and the rules." That is what the rest of Deuteronomy contains.
- **"You shall not turn aside to the right hand or to the left" (v32).** A picture of a road. Obedience is walking straight on God's path, not wandering off on either side, whether by adding rules or loosening them (4:2). Joshua receives the same charge (Josh 1:7).
- **"That you may live, and that it may go well with you" (v33).** The commands are for Israel's good. God is not a tyrant laying burdens on his people but a Father showing them the road to life.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Moses recalls "the consternation caused by the terror with which the law was given," and on v29 writes, "It would be well with many, if there were always such a heart in them." JFB says God both can give such a heart and has promised to do so, and reads the wish as concerning the nation. JFB treats vv23–28 only by a cross-reference to Exodus 20:19, and does not cover vv30–33. Guzik reads v29 as a sigh of divine desire and insists every command flows from love for Israel's good. He contrasts Sinai's "stay back" with Hebrews 12, where believers come not to a burning mountain but to Mount Zion and to "Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant" (Heb 12:18–24).

#### Pointing to Christ
At Horeb the people heard God's voice, trembled, and begged for someone to stand between them and him. God said they were right, and later promised a prophet like Moses who would speak his words (18:15–18). The New Testament sees that promise fulfilled in Jesus (Acts 3:22–23), the "one mediator between God and men" (1 Tim 2:5). God's longing in v29, "Oh that they had such a heart as this always," is answered in the new covenant, where God himself writes his law on the heart and gives his Spirit (Jer 31:33; 2 Cor 3:3). Jesus did not cancel these commandments. He kept them perfectly, showed their depth (Matt 5:17–48), and summed them up in love for God and neighbor (Matt 22:37–40). And the Sabbath's two reasons, creation and redemption, meet in him: the one through whom all things were made is the one who says, "Come to me… and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).
