*Setting:* Judah in the years before the Babylonian invasions, probably the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC). In chapter 15 Jeremiah complained that he "sat alone" because God's hand was on him (15:17). Chapter 16 shows how deep that loneliness went. God tells him not to marry, not to go to funerals, and not to go to feasts. His whole life becomes a sign: what he gives up now, the whole nation will lose soon. Then, without warning, the chapter flashes forward to a new exodus greater than the first, before returning to judgment, and it ends with a vision of the nations coming to God and confessing that their idols were lies.

#### Famine, Sword, and Death (vv1–13)
- **"You shall not take a wife, nor shall you have sons or daughters in this place" (v2).** This was a startling command. In Israel marriage and children were expected of every man, usually by his early twenties, and children were seen as the LORD's blessing (Ps 127:3–5). Some have pointed out that biblical Hebrew has no word for "bachelor." Remaining single would have marked Jeremiah as strange and drawn questions. That was the point. His empty house would preach.
- **Other prophets' family lives as signs.** Hosea's marriage to an unfaithful wife and the names of his children were messages (Hos 1–3). Isaiah's sons had prophetic names (Isa 7:3; 8:3, 18). Ezekiel was told not to mourn when his wife died (Ezek 24:15–24). Jeremiah's sign is the absence of a family.
- **"They shall die of deadly diseases… as dung on the surface of the ground" (v4).** The reason is not that marriage is bad. It is that children born "in this place" in these years would die by plague, sword and famine, unburied and unmourned. Jesus said something similar about the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70: "Blessed are the barren and the wombs that never bore" (Luke 23:29). Paul advised the unmarried in Corinth to remain as they were "in view of the present distress" (1 Cor 7:26), a passage built on the same kind of reasoning.
- **"Do not enter the house of mourning" (v5).** The phrase is literally "house of the *marzeach*." The word appears elsewhere only in Amos 6:7, where it means a feast or banquet. Texts from Ugarit and elsewhere describe the *marzeach* as a gathering, sometimes connected with funerals and mourning for the dead, with eating and drinking. Here it is the funeral meal where friends gathered to comfort the family. Jeremiah must stay away. Refusing to comfort mourners would have looked heartless. It was meant to shock.
- **"For I have taken away my peace from this people, my steadfast love and mercy" (v5).** Three of God's richest gifts: *shalom* (peace, wholeness), *chesed* (steadfast covenant love) and *rachamim* (compassion, from the word for womb). These are what had kept Judah alive. God is withdrawing them, so there is no point offering human comfort.
- **"No one shall… cut himself or make himself bald for them" (v6).** Gashing the skin and shaving the head were common mourning rites in the ancient Near East (41:5; 47:5; 48:37). The Law forbade Israel to practice them (Lev 19:28; Deut 14:1), yet they were clearly common in Judah. When the judgment falls, there will be too many dead for any of these rites, permitted or not.
- **"No one shall break bread for the mourner… nor give him the cup of consolation" (v7).** Friends brought food and wine to a grieving household, since the family would not cook in the days of mourning (2 Sam 3:35; 12:17; Ezek 24:17). The "cup of consolation" was wine given to comfort those who had lost a parent. Even these small kindnesses will disappear.
- **"You shall not go into the house of feasting" (v8).** Jeremiah is barred not only from funerals but from celebrations. No weddings, no feasts. He is cut off from both sides of community life.
- **"I will silence… the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" (v9).** This line comes three times in Jeremiah (7:34; 16:9; 25:10). The sound of a wedding was the sound of life going on. **"Before your eyes and in your days."** It will not be a distant future. The same generation will see it. In 33:11 the phrase will return as a promise: the voice of the bridegroom and the bride will be heard again.
- **"Why has the LORD pronounced all this great evil against us? What is our iniquity?" (v10).** The question may be genuine confusion or wounded innocence. Either way, people who had lived with idolatry for generations no longer saw it as sin. Deuteronomy 29:24–26 had predicted exactly this question and the answer.
- **"Because your fathers have forsaken me… and because you have done worse than your fathers" (vv11–12).** God does not blame the present generation for their ancestors' sin alone. They inherited it and then outdid it, each one following "his stubborn, evil will." The judgment is not unfair inheritance. It is a long debt that the present generation added to.
- **"I will hurl you out of this land" (v13).** The verb is used of throwing a spear (1 Sam 18:11; 20:33). **"There you shall serve other gods day and night."** A bitter irony. They wanted other gods. In exile they will have nothing else, and the gods of Babylon will be imposed on them (Deut 4:27–28; 28:36, 64). Daniel and his friends show what that pressure looked like (Dan 3; 6). **"For I will show you no favor."**
- **Commentators:** Henry draws a principle from Jeremiah's self-denial: "Those that would convince others of and affect them with the word of God must make it appear, even in the most self-denying instances, that they do believe it themselves." JFB links the command to stay single with 1 Corinthians 7 and Luke 23:29, and explains the "cup of consolation" as the wine friends sent to the mourning-feast. Guzik, citing Ryken, notes that Hebrew has no word for "bachelor," and quotes Cundall that for a man as tender as Jeremiah this amounted to a "self-imposed excommunication." On v13 JFB calls the punishment "retribution in kind": they forsook God for idols at home, and in exile they "were not allowed to serve God, if they wished it."

#### The LORD Will Restore Israel (vv14–21)
- **"The days are coming… when it shall no longer be said, 'As the LORD lives who brought up the people of Israel out of the land of Egypt'" (v14).** The Exodus was the great act by which Israel knew God. People swore oaths by it: "As the LORD lives, who brought us out of Egypt." God says a day is coming when that oath will be replaced, because he will do something even greater.
- **"As the LORD lives who brought up the people of Israel out of the north country" (v15).** The "north country" is Babylon, since armies and travelers came from Mesopotamia along the Fertile Crescent and entered Israel from the north. **"And out of all the countries where he had driven them."** The promise reaches beyond the return from Babylon in 538 BC (Ezra 1). Only some exiles came back then, and Israel remained scattered "in all the countries." Many interpreters see a further fulfillment still to come. These two verses appear again almost word for word in 23:7–8, after the promise of the righteous Branch.
- **Why does a promise of hope interrupt judgment here?** The Tyndale notes put it simply: judgment does not cancel future hope, and hope does not cancel the certainty of judgment. Calvin (cited by JFB) read it the other way round: the bondage will be so severe that rescue from it will be greater than rescue from Egypt. Both are true. The promise only makes sense because the exile will be real.
- **"I am sending for many fishers… and afterward… many hunters" (v16).** Back to judgment. Fishermen catch with nets in the open. Hunters search the hills and "the clefts of the rocks," where people hide in fear (Isa 2:19–21). Nobody will escape. Amos and Habakkuk use the same picture of conquerors catching people with hooks and nets (Amos 4:2; Hab 1:14–17).
- **Fishers of men.** Jesus called Galilean fishermen with the words "I will make you fishers of men" (Mark 1:17). He was likely not quoting this verse, since here fishers bring judgment. But JFB notes that Ezekiel 47:9–10 uses fishing in a good sense, of life spreading from the temple, and the image that once meant capture for judgment becomes in the gospel a net for salvation.
- **"For my eyes are on all their ways" (v17).** No one hides from the hunters, because no one hides from God (Ps 139:7–12; Prov 15:3).
- **"But first I will doubly repay their iniquity" (v18).** The word "double" (*mishneh*) can mean twice as much, but it can also mean a matching or full equivalent, like a duplicate copy. Isaiah 40:2 uses it when Jerusalem has received "double for all her sins," that is, the full penalty. The point is that the debt will be paid in full before restoration comes. "First" ties this back to v15: the exile must come before the return.
- **"They have polluted my land with the carcasses of their detestable idols" (v18).** A powerful insult. Idols are lifeless, so Jeremiah calls them corpses. Dead bodies made a place unclean (Num 19:11–16), and the land was God's "inheritance." Leviticus 26:30 had warned that God would "cast your dead bodies upon the dead bodies of your idols."
- **"O LORD, my strength and my stronghold, my refuge in the day of trouble" (v19).** Suddenly Jeremiah breaks into a short psalm of trust, in the language of David (Ps 18:1–2; 59:16). The lonely prophet, without wife, children or friends, finds his shelter in God.
- **"To you shall the nations come from the ends of the earth" (v19).** The vision widens to the whole world. The same nations whose gods Judah copied will one day come to Judah's God. This is the hope of Isaiah 2:2–4 and 45:14–24, Micah 4:1–3 and Zechariah 8:20–23.
- **"Our fathers have inherited nothing but lies, worthless things in which there is no profit" (v19).** The nations confess that what their ancestors handed down was empty. "Worthless things" is *hevel*, breath or vapor, the word for idols in 10:3, 15. **"Can man make for himself gods? Such are not gods!" (v20).** The confession sums up chapter 10 in one line.
- **"This once I will make them know my power and my might, and they shall know that my name is the LORD" (v21).** "Them" may be the nations, who will see God's power in judging and restoring his people, or Judah, who will learn through the exile what they would not learn by warning. "They shall know that I am the LORD" is the refrain of the Exodus story (Exod 6:7; 7:5; 14:4) and of Ezekiel, who uses it more than seventy times. Through judgment and restoration God will make his name known, as he did in Egypt.
- **Commentators:** Henry admits that in vv14–21 mercy and judgment are "so interwoven" that it is hard to know where some passages belong, "and some seem to look as far forward as the times of the gospel." He reads "double" as "not double to what it deserves, but double to what they expect," and calls idols "carcases of detestable things," as loathsome to God as dead bodies are to us. JFB notes that the Babylonians were known as hunters and the Egyptians as fishermen, and argues that "from all the lands" shows the return from Babylon was only a partial fulfillment, with a fuller gathering still in view. Guzik observes that the hope of v14 bursts in right after God says "I will show you no favor," as though God could not leave the darkness without a promise.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah gave up marriage, funerals and feasts to be a living sign of what was coming. Jesus, too, had no wife or home of his own (Matt 8:20) and gave himself wholly to his mission. Yet he went into both the house of mourning and the house of feasting, weeping at Lazarus's tomb and turning water into wine at a wedding (John 11:35; 2:1–11). The voice of the bridegroom that Jeremiah said would be silenced returns in him: John the Baptist rejoiced "greatly at the bridegroom's voice" (John 3:29). The new exodus greater than Egypt came through his death and resurrection, the "departure" (literally "exodus") he would accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31). And through his fishers of men, the nations have been coming from the ends of the earth, confessing that their fathers inherited lies and turning "to God from idols" (1 Thess 1:9).
