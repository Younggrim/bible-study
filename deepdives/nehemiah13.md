*Setting:* Jerusalem, around 432 BC or a little later. Nehemiah served twelve years as governor (445–433 BC; 5:14), then went back to the Persian court as he had promised the king (2:6; 13:6). When he returned after "some time," he found that much of what the people had sworn in the covenant (ch 10) had been abandoned. An enemy of Judah was living in a room in the temple. The Levites had gone home because the tithes had stopped. Jerusalem's markets were open on the Sabbath. Jewish men had married women from nearby peoples, and even the high priest's grandson had married Sanballat's daughter. Chapter 13 records how Nehemiah confronted each problem. These are the same failings the prophet Malachi condemns, and many scholars think he preached in this same period. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading, "Nehemiah's Final Reforms." The subheadings below follow its four reforms, each of which ends with a short prayer: "Remember me, O my God."

#### Nehemiah's Final Reforms: The Assembly and the Temple Chamber (vv1–9)
- **"On that day they read from the Book of Moses" (v1).** "On that day" is a loose connection, meaning about that time. It may refer back to the dedication (12:44) or, as JFB thinks, to a reading after Nehemiah's return. As in chapter 8, the people heard the Law read aloud and acted on what they heard.
- **"No Ammonite or Moabite should ever enter the assembly of God" (v1).** This is Deuteronomy 23:3–6. Ammon and Moab, descendants of Lot (Gen 19:36–38), had refused Israel food and water in the wilderness and hired Balaam to curse them (Num 22–24). "Enter the assembly" probably meant taking full part in Israel's worship and public life. Ruth was a Moabite who became the great-grandmother of David, which shows that the law was not understood to shut out every person of Moabite birth who turned to the LORD. Jewish interpreters later explained this in different ways, but the point here is Israel's identity as the people of God, not the rejection of converts.
- **"Yet our God turned the curse into a blessing" (v2).** This echoes Deuteronomy 23:5. Balaam opened his mouth to curse Israel and blessed them instead (Num 23–24). The aside shows God's protection, even in a passage about exclusion.
- **"They separated from Israel all those of foreign descent" (v3).** The Hebrew word is *'erev*, "mixed company." It is the same word used for the "mixed multitude" that left Egypt with Israel (Exod 12:38). Those who were attached to Israel without belonging to its covenant were set apart.
- **"Eliashib the priest, who was appointed over the chambers… and who was related to Tobiah" (v4).** Eliashib was probably the high priest of 3:1 and 12:10, though some think this was a different priest of the same name, responsible for the temple storerooms. Tobiah was the Ammonite official who had opposed the wall from the start (2:10, 19; 4:3, 7; 6:17–19). The irony is plain. The Law read in v1 excluded Ammonites, and an Ammonite was living inside the temple courts.
- **"A large chamber where they had previously put the grain offering, the frankincense, the vessels, and the tithes" (v5).** The temple had storerooms around its courts. One of them, emptied of the offerings that fed the Levites, singers and gatekeepers, had become a private apartment for Tobiah, probably as a base when he was in Jerusalem.
- **"In the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes king of Babylon I went to the king" (v6).** That was 433 BC. Persian kings had conquered Babylon and used the title "king of Babylon" among others (compare Ezra 5:13). "After some time" is vague. It may mean a year or more.
- **"I was very angry, and I threw all the household furniture of Tobiah out of the chamber" (v8).** Nehemiah did not negotiate. He emptied the room himself. This is a striking picture of zeal for God's house, like Jesus overturning the tables in the temple: "Zeal for your house will consume me" (John 2:15–17; Ps 69:9).
- **"They cleansed the chambers, and I brought back there the vessels" (v9).** The rooms were ritually purified, then restored to their proper use. The plural "chambers" suggests Tobiah's use had spread into more than one room.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "with a particular indignation, he expelled Tobiah out of the lodgings he had got in the court of the temple." JFB says Eliashib "was allied unto Tobiah," and that Nehemiah spent "twelve years as governor" before returning to Persia. Guzik notes that the real test of a revival is what is still standing years later. He compares Nehemiah's action to Jesus cleansing the temple and reminds readers that love is not the same as being nice. The Tyndale notes add that Eliashib's family was tied by marriage to both Tobiah and Sanballat (v28).

#### The Tithes and the Levites (vv10–14)
- **"The portions of the Levites had not been given to them" (v10).** The people had promised, "We will not neglect the house of our God" (10:39). Now the tithes had stopped. With no food coming in, "the Levites and the singers, who did the work, had fled each to his field." They had to farm to survive, and the temple's daily worship suffered. Malachi preached against the same failure: "Will man rob God? Yet you are robbing me… In your tithes and contributions" (Mal 3:8–10).
- **"Why is the house of God forsaken?" (v11).** "Forsaken" (*'azav*) is the same verb as "neglect" in the covenant oath (10:39). Nehemiah throws their own promise back at the officials responsible for collecting the tithes. He then brought the Levites back "and set them in their stations."
- **"Then all Judah brought the tithe" (v12).** When the leaders acted, the people followed. JFB says they "willingly brought in their tithes as formerly."
- **"Shelemiah the priest, Zadok the scribe, and Pedaiah of the Levites, and… Hanan" (v13).** Nehemiah appointed a team that included a priest, a scribe, a Levite and an assistant from the singers' family. That arrangement shared the oversight and guarded against one person controlling the stores. "They were considered reliable," literally "faithful." The Tyndale notes compare the choosing of the seven in Acts 6:1–6, trusted men chosen to distribute food fairly.
- **"Remember me, O my God, concerning this, and do not wipe out my good deeds" (v14).** "Good deeds" is the plural of *chesed*, "steadfast love" or "loyalty." Nehemiah speaks of his acts of loyal love for God's house. "Wipe out" means to blot out, the word Moses used when he asked to be blotted out of God's book (Exod 32:32). Nehemiah asks God not to let his work be forgotten, even if the people forget it.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Nehemiah "secured the maintenance of the priests and Levites to them more firmly than it had been." Guzik observes that failing to support God's work is a quiet way of forsaking him. JFB notes that Nehemiah rebuked those in charge and restored the temple service.

#### The Sabbath (vv15–22)
- **"Treading winepresses on the Sabbath" (v15).** It was harvest time. People were pressing grapes, loading donkeys and bringing grain, wine, figs and "all kinds of loads" into Jerusalem on the Sabbath. Jeremiah had warned the city before the exile: "bear no burden on the Sabbath day or bring it in by the gates of Jerusalem" (Jer 17:21–27). The people had promised not to buy on the Sabbath (10:31).
- **"I warned them on the day when they sold food" (v15).** Nehemiah's first step was a warning, not punishment.
- **"Tyrians also, who lived in the city, brought in fish" (v16).** Tyre was the great Phoenician trading city on the coast. Its merchants lived in Jerusalem and sold dried or salted fish from the Mediterranean. Foreigners were not bound by the Sabbath, but the people of Judah were buying from them, "in Jerusalem itself!"
- **"I confronted the nobles of Judah" (v17).** Nehemiah went to the leaders. Those with power to stop the trade had allowed it.
- **"Did not your fathers act in this way, and did not our God bring all this disaster on us?" (v18).** The prophets had tied Sabbath-breaking to the exile (Jer 17:27; Ezek 20:12–24). Nehemiah warns that repeating the sin of their fathers would bring "more wrath on Israel." The past was a warning to them.
- **"As soon as it began to grow dark at the gates… before the Sabbath" (v19).** Hebrew days began at sunset, so the Sabbath started on Friday evening. Nehemiah ordered the gates shut at dusk and kept them shut until it ended. He placed his own servants on guard. The same gates he had built to keep enemies out now kept out the Sabbath trade.
- **"The merchants… lodged outside Jerusalem once or twice" (v20).** The traders camped outside the walls, waiting to sell when the gates opened or hoping to tempt people out. "If you do so again, I will lay hands on you" (v21). That was a plain threat of force, and it worked. "From that time on they did not come on the Sabbath."
- **"I commanded the Levites that they should purify themselves and come and guard the gates" (v22).** Nehemiah's servants were a short-term fix. The Levites were to guard the gates permanently, making Sabbath-keeping a sacred duty, not only a political order.
- **"Spare me according to the greatness of your steadfast love" (v22).** "Spare" means to pity or have compassion. Nehemiah asks God to remember his work, but he rests finally on God's *chesed*, not on his own record.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that "religion is never in the throne while sabbaths are trodden under foot," and that Nehemiah "would not punish it till he had laid open the evil of it." He adds that "the greatest of men are not too high to be told of their faults," and that "if reformers will but put on resolution, more may be done towards the breaking of bad customs than they can imagine." On v22: "God's mercy is what we must depend upon, and not any merit of our own." Guzik says the root problem was priorities, since buying and selling are not wrong in themselves. He points to Colossians 2:16–17 to say that Christians are not bound by Israel's Sabbath law in the same way, but are still called to put God before money. JFB describes the shut gates, the guards and the merchants waiting outside the walls.

#### Mixed Marriages and the Priesthood (vv23–31)
- **"Jews who had married women of Ashdod, Ammon, and Moab" (v23).** Ashdod was a former Philistine city on the coast west of Judah. In the Persian period it was a provincial capital near Judah's border. Ammon and Moab lay east of the Jordan. These marriages broke the first specific promise of the covenant (10:30) and repeated the crisis Ezra had faced (Ezra 9–10).
- **"Half of their children spoke the language of Ashdod, and they could not speak the language of Judah" (v24).** "The language of Judah" is Hebrew, as in 2 Kings 18:26. The language of Ashdod is uncertain. It may have been a local dialect or a form of Aramaic. Children who could not speak Hebrew could not understand the Law when it was read, so within a generation the faith itself would be lost. The language problem showed the spiritual problem.
- **"I confronted them and cursed them and beat some of them and pulled out their hair" (v25).** This is the most startling verse in the book. "Cursed" may mean a formal rebuke or a pronouncement of God's judgment. Pulling out hair or beard was a public disgrace (2 Sam 10:4–5; Isa 50:6). The same verb (*marat*) describes Ezra pulling out *his own* hair in grief over the same sin (Ezra 9:3). Readers have judged Nehemiah differently. Some see an angry man going too far. Others see a governor using the punishments of his time to stop a disaster. The text reports it without comment.
- **"I made them take an oath in the name of God" (v25).** The text does not say that Nehemiah dissolved existing marriages, as Ezra had (Ezra 10:3, 11). It says he made the people swear not to arrange such marriages for their children.
- **"Did not Solomon king of Israel sin on account of such women?" (v26).** Solomon was Israel's wisest king and "beloved by his God," a play on his other name, Jedidiah, "beloved of the LORD" (2 Sam 12:24–25). Yet his foreign wives "turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kgs 11:1–8). If they could lead Solomon astray, they could lead anyone astray. The concern was never ancestry as such. It was the worship of other gods coming into Israel through marriage (Deut 7:3–4). Malachi makes the same charge: Judah "has married the daughter of a foreign god" (Mal 2:11).
- **"One of the sons of Jehoiada, the son of Eliashib the high priest, was the son-in-law of Sanballat the Horonite" (v28).** Sanballat was governor of Samaria and Nehemiah's chief enemy (2:10; 4:1–2; 6:1–14). The high priest's grandson, in line for the high priesthood, had married his daughter. The Law required the high priest to marry "a virgin of his own people" (Lev 21:14). "I chased him from me" means Nehemiah drove him out of Jerusalem.
- **Josephus and the Samaritan temple.** Josephus, writing about 500 years later, tells of a priest named Manasseh, brother of the high priest Jaddua, who married the daughter of a Sanballat and became priest of a rival temple the Samaritans built on Mount Gerizim. He places this a century later, in Alexander the Great's day. Scholars debate whether he confused the dates or whether there was more than one Sanballat (papyri found at Wadi Daliyeh show later governors of Samaria by that name). Either way, the Samaritans' temple on Gerizim became a lasting division. The Samaritan woman raised it with Jesus: "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain" (John 4:20).
- **"They have desecrated the priesthood and the covenant of the priesthood and the Levites" (v29).** God had made a "covenant of a perpetual priesthood" with Phinehas because of his zeal (Num 25:12–13). Malachi calls it "my covenant with Levi" and accuses the priests of corrupting it (Mal 2:4–8). Nehemiah does not take revenge. He hands them over to God: "Remember them."
- **"Thus I cleansed them from everything foreign" (vv30–31).** The book closes with a summary of Nehemiah's work. He set the priests and Levites back in their duties and provided for the wood offering and the firstfruits (10:34–35).
- **"Remember me, O my God, for good" (v31).** These are the last words of the book, and they echo Nehemiah's earlier prayer (5:19). There is no triumphant ending. The wall stands, but the people have already fallen back once, and the book ends with a tired servant asking God to remember him.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the children spoke "half in the speech of Ashdod," which shows how much children take from their mothers. He says that "when we would reclaim people from sin we must show them the sinfulness of it in the glass of the commandment." On v25 he writes that "Nehemiah plucked off their hair, in a holy indignation at the sinners," and contrasts Ezra, who tore his own hair in grief. On Solomon: "when he married strange wives, his wisdom could not secure him from their snares." He mentions Josephus's link to the Gerizim temple and says Nehemiah, whose public service would likely be forgotten, looked to God as his true rewarder. JFB calls the children's speech "a mongrel dialect imbibed from their mothers" and says the men's hair was torn "with violence," adding pain to disgrace. Guzik sees the marriages as the most dangerous of the three sins, because they reached the next generation. He notes that within about a decade the people had returned to all three sins they had sworn off in chapter 10. He concludes that vows and laws cannot change the heart, and only God's grace can (Rom 8:3).

#### Pointing to Christ
Nehemiah's book ends with a good man doing his best and finding it was not enough. The walls were strong and the covenant was signed, but the people's hearts drifted as soon as their leader was away. Nehemiah could throw out Tobiah's furniture, shut the gates and pull out hair, but he could not change a heart. The book closes with a prayer, "Remember me, O my God, for good." The Old Testament ends with its history at this point, waiting. Malachi, preaching to the same people, promises that "the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple" (Mal 3:1). Jesus came and cleansed the temple as Nehemiah had, but he also gave his own body as the true temple (John 2:19–21). He made a covenant that writes the law on the heart (Jer 31:33; Heb 8:10). The thief on the cross prayed almost what Nehemiah prayed: "Jesus, remember me." The answer came at once: "Today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:42–43).
