*Setting:* Shiloh, a few years after chapter 2, still around 1100 BC. Samuel is no longer a toddler but a boy, perhaps about twelve (the age the Jewish historian Josephus gives, and JFB repeats, though the text does not say). He has grown up at the tabernacle serving the aging priest Eli, while Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas have robbed the offerings and corrupted the sanctuary. An unnamed man of God has already announced that Eli's family will lose the priesthood (2:27–36). Chapter 3 is the night God calls Samuel. It is one of the best-known scenes in the Old Testament, and it marks a turning point: after a long silence, God begins to speak to Israel again, and he chooses a child to speak through.

#### The LORD Calls Samuel (vv1–21)
- **"The boy Samuel was ministering to the LORD in the presence of Eli" (v1).** This is the third time the narrator says it (2:11, 18). The phrase keeps setting Samuel's quiet faithfulness against the noise of Eli's sons.
- **"The word of the LORD was rare in those days" (v1).** "Rare" (*yaqar*) is the word for something scarce and therefore precious, like a costly jewel. "There was no frequent vision" is literally "no vision breaking out" or "spreading abroad." In the days of the judges God had spoken through Deborah and a few others, but for a long stretch there had been almost nothing. Amos later warns of a famine "of hearing the words of the LORD" (Amos 8:11–12). Israel's spiritual darkness was partly the silence of God.
- **"Eli, whose eyesight had begun to grow dim so that he could not see" (v2).** Eli's failing eyes match his failing spiritual sight. He is still the high priest, but he cannot see what God is doing, and he cannot see clearly what his own sons are doing to God's house.
- **"The lamp of God had not yet gone out" (v3).** The golden lampstand stood in the Holy Place and was to burn "from evening to morning" (Exod 27:20–21; Lev 24:3). So the scene is in the last dark hours before dawn. The detail also works as a picture. Israel's light is flickering low, but God has not let it go out.
- **"Samuel was lying down in the temple of the LORD, where the ark of God was" (v3).** The ark was kept in the inner room, the Most Holy Place, which no one but the high priest could enter, once a year. Samuel was not sleeping beside the ark. He slept somewhere in the sanctuary complex, probably a room near the Holy Place, where he could tend the lamp and open the doors in the morning (v15). The phrase "where the ark of God was" tells us he was close to the place of God's presence.
- **"Here I am!" (vv4–5).** Samuel answers with *hinneni*, "here I am," the same word Abraham used when God called him (Gen 22:1, 11), and Moses at the burning bush (Exod 3:4). It means "I'm listening, ready." Then Samuel *runs* to Eli. He assumes the only person who would call him at night is the old priest, perhaps because Eli needed help in his blindness. His eagerness to obey a man prepares him to obey God.
- **"I did not call, my son; lie down again" (vv5–6).** It happens twice. Eli is gentle with the boy, calling him "my son," something we never hear him say warmly to Hophni or Phinehas.
- **"Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD" (v7).** This is not the same as Eli's sons, who "did not know the LORD" (2:12). They had turned their backs on him. Samuel simply had not yet received a word from God directly: "the word of the LORD had not yet been revealed to him." He served faithfully, but he had never heard God's voice. Faithful service and personal encounter are not the same thing, and God is about to give him the second.
- **"Then Eli perceived that the LORD was calling the boy" (v8).** On the third call Eli finally understands. He is slow, but not completely blind. Here he does something right, even humbly right: he points the boy away from himself to God.
- **"Speak, LORD, for your servant hears" (v9).** Eli teaches Samuel the right posture before God: available, humble ("your servant") and ready to obey. In Hebrew "hear" (*shama'*) often means both "listen" and "obey." It may also be an echo of Samuel's own name, which sounds partly like *shama'* (1:20).
- **"The LORD came and stood, calling as at other times, 'Samuel! Samuel!'" (v10).** "Came and stood" means this was more than a voice. God himself was present in some way. Verse 15 calls it a "vision," and v21 says "the LORD appeared." The doubled name marks a moment of deep personal calling, as with "Abraham, Abraham" (Gen 22:11), "Moses, Moses" (Exod 3:4) and "Saul, Saul" (Acts 9:4).
- **"Speak, for your servant hears" (v10).** Samuel leaves out "LORD." Perhaps he was unsure, perhaps overawed. The Tyndale notes suggest either reverence or uncertainty. Either way, he takes the place of a servant and waits.
- **"The two ears of everyone who hears it will tingle" (v11).** The first word God gives Samuel is terrible news. "Tingle" (*tsalal*) is the sound and shiver of a shock that makes the ears ring. The same phrase announces the fall of Jerusalem centuries later (2 Kgs 21:12; Jer 19:3). The disaster about to strike Israel at Shiloh (ch 4) will be remembered for generations (Ps 78:60–64; Jer 7:12–14).
- **"I will fulfill against Eli all that I have spoken" (v12).** This confirms the message of the man of God in 2:27–36. God gives Eli two witnesses: a stranger, and a boy he raised.
- **"Because his sons were blaspheming God, and he did not restrain them" (v13).** Eli's sin is named precisely. He "knew" (2:22–24), and "he did not restrain them." The verb means to rebuke with force, to hold back. Eli had the authority, as high priest and father, to remove them from service. He settled for words. There is a textual note here. The traditional Hebrew text reads "his sons were cursing themselves" or "bringing a curse on themselves." Ancient Jewish scribes are known to have changed a few phrases that spoke of cursing God, out of reverence, and the Greek translation reads "cursing God." The ESV follows that (with a footnote). The verb (*qalal*) is the same "make light" verb as 2:30: "those who despise me shall be lightly esteemed." They made God light, and God will make their house light.
- **"Shall not be atoned for by sacrifice or offering forever" (v14).** The sacrifices of Leviticus covered unintentional sins and sins confessed and repented of (Lev 4–6). There was no sacrifice for "high-handed" sin, deliberate and defiant rebellion (Num 15:30–31). Eli's sons had despised the very sacrifices that could have covered them. This does not mean no member of the family could ever be forgiven personally. It means the sentence on the house would not be lifted.
- **"Samuel lay until morning; then he opened the doors of the house of the LORD" (v15).** He does his ordinary chores the morning after the most extraordinary night of his life. "Samuel was afraid to tell the vision to Eli." How do you tell the man who raised you that God has condemned his house?
- **"May God do so to you and more also if you hide anything" (v17).** This is a standard oath formula (Ruth 1:17; 1 Kgs 2:23), probably once accompanied by a gesture, like cutting an animal: "may God do this to you, and worse." Eli insists on the whole truth.
- **"So Samuel told him everything and hid nothing from him" (v18).** His first act as a prophet is to deliver a hard word in full, without softening it. That is what a true prophet does (Jer 23:28; Acts 20:27).
- **"It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him" (v18).** Readers have long disagreed on how to hear Eli's reply. Many hear humble submission, like Job's "the LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away" (Job 1:21). Others hear resignation: he accepts the sentence but still does nothing about his sons. Both may be true. Eli bows to God's justice, which is right, yet nothing in the text says he repented or removed his sons.
- **"The LORD was with him and let none of his words fall to the ground" (v19).** Words that "fall to the ground" are like arrows that drop short of the target, or seed that dies. None of Samuel's did. This was the test of a true prophet in the law: does what he says come to pass? (Deut 18:21–22).
- **"All Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew" (v20).** Dan in the far north and Beersheba in the far south were about 150 miles apart, and the phrase means "the whole land." "Established" is *ne'eman*, the same word as the "faithful" priest of 2:35: firm, reliable, confirmed. Samuel is the first fulfillment of that promise.
- **"The LORD appeared again at Shiloh… by the word of the LORD" (v21).** The chapter ends where it began, but reversed. It opened with the word of the LORD rare. It closes with the LORD revealing himself again, and doing so "by the word." In the Hebrew Bible the first line of chapter 4, "And the word of Samuel came to all Israel," completes this sentence.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the rarity of prophecy made it the more precious," and on the lamp, that Samuel had gone to bed "before any of the lamps in the branches of the candlestick went out." He calls Samuel "a young priest, though by birth a Levite only," and "a young prophet, which was more," and sees the chapter as "reviving, if not commencing, prophecy in Israel." Guzik and JFB both mention Josephus's age of twelve, and Guzik says plainly that the text does not give it. Guzik draws careful lessons on hearing God: inner impressions must be tested by Scripture, God tends to confirm his word more than once, and even a child raised in God's house needs to know the LORD personally. He suggests tentatively that the LORD who "stood" may have been an appearance of the Son before his incarnation, which is a possibility, not something the text says. JFB takes Eli's reply as "meek and unmurmuring submission," while Guzik leaves it open whether it was submission or fatalism. Henry also commends Eli for giving the boy "the best advice he could" even though it meant being passed over himself.

#### Pointing to Christ
In a time when God's word was rare and the priesthood had failed, God spoke to a boy and made him the one through whom his word came to all Israel. The writer to the Hebrews begins with this same pattern on a larger scale: "Long ago… God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:1–2). Samuel heard the word. Jesus *is* the Word (John 1:1, 14), and none of his words fall to the ground: "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35). And Samuel's simple answer, "Speak, for your servant hears," is the answer of every true disciple. Jesus said, "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me" (John 10:27).
