*Setting:* The title reads "To the choirmaster: according to The Gittith. A Psalm of David." *Gittith* is related to Gath, the Philistine city where David once lived in exile (1 Sam 27), or to *gat*, the Hebrew word for a winepress. It may mean a tune or instrument from Gath, or a joyful harvest melody sung at the treading of grapes. It also heads Psalms 81 and 84, both songs of celebration. No occasion is given. Psalms 3–7 were prayers from a world full of enemies, slander and sickness. Psalm 8 lifts its eyes to the night sky and the world as God first made it. It is the first psalm that is pure praise, with no request in it at all, and it reads like a hymn on Genesis 1: God the Creator, the heavens, the creatures, and humanity made to rule as his image. The New Testament quotes it four times and finds its full meaning in Jesus (Matt 21:16; 1 Cor 15:27; Eph 1:22; Heb 2:6–8). The psalm begins and ends with the same line, wrapping the whole song in God's name.

#### How Majestic Is Your Name (v1)
- **"O LORD, our Lord" (v1).** Two different Hebrew words stand behind the English. The first, in small capitals, is *YHWH*, God's covenant name revealed to Moses (Exod 3:14–15). The second is *Adonai*, "master" or "sovereign." The God who bound himself to Israel by name is also the ruler of the whole world. Notice too "*our* Lord." The song is sung by a people who belong to him.
- **"How majestic is your name in all the earth!" (v1).** "Majestic" (*addir*) means mighty, glorious, splendid. It is used of powerful kings (136:18), noble people (16:3), and the thundering waves of the sea (93:4). In the Bible a "name" is not just a label. It is who someone is and what they are known for. God's character is on display "in all the earth," not only in Israel's temple.
- **"You have set your glory above the heavens" (v1).** The sky is the highest thing a person can see, yet God's glory is higher still. The heavens display his glory (19:1), but they cannot contain it (1 Kings 8:27).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "thy name" as God's perfections, his revealed character, and says God has set his glory high as if the earth were "too small a stage" for it. Henry sees the repeated opening and closing line as a claim and its proof: the psalm opens by stating God's excellence and closes by repeating it after showing why it is true. Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's warm paraphrase of the double name: Jehovah, "our Prop, our Stay, our Support."

#### Out of the Mouth of Babies (v2)
- **"Out of the mouth of babies and infants" (v2).** "Infants" are nursing children, literally "sucklings." Nursing in Israel could last until a child was two or three (1 Sam 1:22–24), so these may be toddlers old enough to babble praise. The point is the contrast: the weakest voices in the world.
- **"You have established strength because of your foes, to still the enemy and the avenger" (v2).** God builds a fortress out of children's praise. The "enemy and the avenger" are those who oppose God and nurse grudges against his rule. They are silenced not by an army but by the simple worship of the smallest people. This is God's way all through Scripture: "God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Cor 1:27). David himself, the shepherd boy, brought down Goliath of Gath.
- **Jesus quotes this verse (Matt 21:16).** On Palm Sunday week, children in the temple shouted "Hosanna to the Son of David!" The chief priests were furious and demanded that Jesus stop them. He answered with this verse: "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise." He quotes the ancient Greek translation, which reads "praise" where the Hebrew has "strength." The two ideas belong together: the children's praise *is* the strength that silences God's enemies. And the claim is enormous. The psalm is about praise to the LORD, and Jesus applies it to praise given to himself, casting the chief priests in the role of "the enemy."
- **Commentators:** Henry says God makes use of "the weakest of the children of men, by them to serve his own purposes," and links the verse to the children's hosannas. JFB explains "ordained" as "founded, or prepared, and perfected," and says the "avenger" is one eager to find fault with God's government. He notes that the New Testament puts the effect (praise) in place of its cause (strength). Guzik reads Jesus' use of the verse as a claim to be God, and passes on Spurgeon's picture of Satan put to shame by children's songs.

#### What Is Man? (vv3–4)
- **"When I look at your heavens, the work of your fingers" (v3).** David the shepherd spent many nights under open skies. With no city lights, thousands of stars would have been visible. And the vast sky, to him, is the work of God's *fingers*, as if creating the universe were fine needlework rather than heavy labor. Elsewhere Scripture speaks of God's "hand" or "arm" for great deeds. Here even fingers are enough.
- **"The moon and the stars, which you have set in place" (v3).** The sun is not mentioned, which fits a night scene. Israel's neighbors worshiped the sun, moon and stars as gods. Genesis 1 and this psalm put them in their place: they are things God made and arranged (Gen 1:16; Deut 4:19).
- **"What is man that you are mindful of him" (v4).** "Man" here is *enosh*, a word that often stresses human weakness and mortality (103:15, "as for man, his days are like grass"). Set beside the stars, a human being seems tiny. Yet God is "mindful" of him. The verb (*zakar*) means to remember, to keep in mind and act on it. It is the word used when God "remembered Noah" (Gen 8:1).
- **"And the son of man that you care for him?" (v4).** "Son of man" (*ben adam*, "son of Adam") is a Hebrew way of saying "a human being," in parallel with the first line. "Care for" (*paqad*) means to visit, attend to, look after. The KJV has "visitest him." It is the word used when the LORD "visited Sarah" and gave her a son (Gen 21:1). David is not asking a gloomy question about how small we are. He is amazed that the Maker of galaxies pays personal attention to people.
- **"Son of man" in the rest of the Bible.** Daniel later saw "one like a son of man" coming with the clouds to receive an everlasting kingdom (Dan 7:13–14). Jesus made "the Son of Man" his favorite title for himself. The phrase in Psalm 8 is ordinary, but it becomes the doorway through which the New Testament sees Jesus in this psalm.
- **A different question.** Job asks almost the same question bitterly: "What is man, that you make so much of him... visit him every morning and test him every moment?" (Job 7:17–18). For Job in his pain, God's attention felt like scrutiny. In Psalm 8 it is pure grace.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the splendor of the heavens illustrates God's condescension, that the great Creator regards "frail man," and reads "visitest" as showing favor. Henry gives thanks that God made the heavenly bodies serve humanity. Guzik passes on Kidner's comment that David's question expresses not doubt but "only astonishment" that God should be mindful and care.

#### Crowned With Glory and Honor (vv5–8)
- **"Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings" (v5).** The Hebrew word is *elohim*. It is the ordinary word for God, but it can also mean "gods" or heavenly beings, as in 82:1 and 6. So translations differ: "a little lower than God" (NRSV, CSB) or "a little lower than the angels" (KJV). The ancient Greek translation read "angels," and Hebrews 2:7 quotes that Greek version. The ESV's "heavenly beings" keeps both possibilities in view. Either way, the psalm places humanity just under the heavenly realm. That reflects Genesis 1:26–27: people are made in God's image, to represent him on earth.
- **"And crowned him with glory and honor" (v5).** "Glory" (*kavod*) and "honor" (*hadar*) are royal words, the same words used of God's own majesty and of a king's splendor (21:5; 96:6). Humans are not gods, but God has given them a kind of royalty. This is a high view of humanity. Every person, whatever their condition, carries a dignity God gave.
- **"You have given him dominion over the works of your hands" (v6).** This picks up God's command in Genesis 1:28: "fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the heavens." Dominion in Scripture is not permission to exploit. It is stewardship under a higher King, ruling as a gardener rules a garden (Gen 2:15).
- **"You have put all things under his feet" (v6).** Putting enemies under one's feet was a picture of conquest (110:1; Josh 10:24). Here it means full authority over creation.
- **"All sheep and oxen... the beasts of the field... the birds... the fish of the sea" (vv7–8).** The list moves outward from the farm to the wild, to the sky and to the sea, following the order of the creatures in Genesis 1. **"Whatever passes along the paths of the seas"** probably refers to sea creatures large and small. Unlike in much of the ancient world, animals are not sacred or to be worshiped. They are given into human care.
- **But the world does not look like this.** Psalm 8 describes humanity as God made it. Genesis 3 tells of the fall, and the crown slipped. Thorns, death and violence followed, and today people often seem ruled by the creation rather than ruling it well. Hebrews 2:8 says exactly this: "At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him."
- **"But we see him... Jesus" (Heb 2:9).** Hebrews reads Psalm 8 as finally fulfilled in Christ. He was made "for a little while lower than the angels" when he became a man. The Greek of Hebrews can mean "a little" in rank or "for a little while" in time, and Hebrews uses it in the time sense. He was then "crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death," and through him God is "bringing many sons to glory" (Heb 2:10). Paul likewise says God "put all things in subjection under his feet" (1 Cor 15:27; Eph 1:22), with death the last enemy to be destroyed. What Adam lost, the last Adam regains, and he shares it with his people.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives thanks to God for "placing him but little lower then the angels" and giving him dominion "over the creatures in this lower world." He says the psalm "is, in the New Testament, applied to Christ and the work of our redemption," and traces Christ's humiliation and exaltation through it. JFB places man "next in dignity to angels," calls glory and honor marks of royal dignity, and sees the words fulfilled finally in Christ as the "second Adam." Guzik sets out both readings of *elohim*. He notes the ancient translators and Hebrews read "angels," and he passes on Kidner's comment that dominion over nature "takes second place to his calling as servant and worshipper."

#### How Majestic Is Your Name (v9)
- **"O LORD, our Lord, how majestic is your name in all the earth!" (v9).** The last line repeats the first word for word. The psalm is framed by God's glory. Humanity's high place sits in the middle, but it is held inside praise of God. That structure is a lesson: human dignity is real, but it is a gift, and its purpose is to make God's name great.
- **What changed between v1 and v9?** Nothing in the words, but the singer now knows more of *why* the name is majestic. It is majestic in the stars, in the children's praise and above all in God's gracious care for frail people.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes the psalm closes with the same admiration it opened with. Guzik says the psalm is really about God more than man: God made man, made him glorious and made him for a high destiny. He quotes F. B. Meyer: "He made us to have dominion by the word of creation." Henry urges readers to move from seeing God's glory in creation and providence to seeing it in the kingdom of grace.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 8 is about humanity, and the New Testament insists that the human being who fits it perfectly is Jesus. He is the "son of man" who was made for a little while lower than the angels, who silenced his enemies with the praise of children, and who is now crowned with glory and honor with all things being put under his feet (Heb 2:6–9; 1 Cor 15:27). The crown humanity lost in Eden, Jesus wore first as a crown of thorns. Because he tasted death for everyone, he can bring his people back to the glory they were made for. The Maker of the stars was mindful of us in the most personal way possible: he became one of us.
