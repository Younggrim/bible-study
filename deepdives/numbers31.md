*Setting:* The plains of Moab, in the last months of Moses' life. In chapter 25, Midianite and Moabite women drew Israel into sexual immorality and the worship of Baal of Peor, and a plague killed 24,000. God then told Moses to "harass the Midianites and strike them down" (25:17). This chapter carries that out. It is the last thing Moses does as war leader before his death, and the only full account of a battle in Numbers. It is also one of the hardest chapters in the Bible for modern readers. It describes a war of judgment, ordered by God, with killing on a scale that troubles believers today, and it should. The chapter needs to be read slowly, in its setting, without either explaining it away or pretending it is easy.

#### Vengeance on Midian (vv1–54)
- **"Avenge the people of Israel on the Midianites. Afterward you shall be gathered to your people" (v2).** The word "vengeance" (*neqamah*) in Scripture usually means just retribution, the righting of a wrong by the proper authority, not personal spite. Moses frames it as "the LORD's vengeance on Midian" (v3). God says "Vengeance is mine" (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19), and that is exactly why individuals are told not to take it. Here God carries it out through Israel as a nation under his direct rule. Moses' last task before death is to judge the people who tried to destroy Israel's faith.
- **Who were the Midianites?** They were desert tribes descended from Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:2), spread over a wide area east and south of Canaan. Moses had married into a Midianite family (Exod 2:16–21), and his father-in-law had been a friend to Israel (Exod 18). But these Midianites had joined Balak in hiring Balaam (22:4, 7), and then set out to seduce Israel (25:6, 18). The war targets these groups near Moab, not every Midianite. Generations later Midian is strong enough to oppress Israel (Judg 6–8).
- **"A thousand from each of the tribes… twelve thousand" (vv4–5).** A small force from a nation of 600,000 fighting men (26:51). Every tribe shares equally in the task, and the small number makes clear that the victory is God's.
- **"Phinehas the son of Eleazar the priest, with the vessels of the sanctuary and the trumpets for the alarm" (v6).** Phinehas, whose zeal stopped the plague at Peor (25:7–13), goes with the army as priest. The "vessels of the sanctuary" may mean the ark, which went before Israel in battle (10:35; Josh 6:6), or other holy objects. The silver trumpets were blown in war to call on God's help: "you shall be remembered before the LORD your God, and you shall be saved from your enemies" (10:9). This marks it as a holy war, God's battle more than Israel's.
- **"Killed every male" (v7).** This means every fighting man of the groups they attacked. The five kings named in v8, Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur and Reba, are called "princes of Midian" in Joshua 13:21, where they are also described as allies of Sihon. **Zur** was the father of Cozbi, the Midianite woman killed with Zimri (25:15).
- **"They also killed Balaam the son of Beor with the sword" (v8).** The diviner who had blessed Israel four times and gone home (24:25) is found among the Midianites. He had wished, "Let me die the death of the upright" (23:10). He died by the sword among Israel's enemies. Joshua 13:22 adds that Israel killed "Balaam… the one who practiced divination."
- **Captives, plunder and burned towns (vv9–12).** The army brings back women, children and animals, and burns the settlements, ordinary practice in ancient war. They bring it all to Moses and Eleazar at the camp.
- **"Moses was angry with the officers" (v14).** **"Have you let all the women live? Behold, these, on Balaam's advice, caused the people of Israel to act treacherously against the LORD in the incident of Peor" (vv15–16).** This is the only place Scripture tells us that Balaam stood behind the disaster at Peor. Having failed to curse Israel, he showed Midian how to make Israel curse itself. The New Testament remembers him for exactly this: he "taught Balak to put a stumbling block before the sons of Israel" (Rev 2:14; also 2 Pet 2:15; Jude 11). The women spared by the army were the very ones who had led Israel astray, and the danger they posed was spiritual, not military.
- **"Kill every male among the little ones, and kill every woman who has known man" (v17).** This is the hardest verse in the chapter, and there is no way to make it comfortable. It is helpful to say what can honestly be said. First, the judgment fell on the women who had taken part in the seduction at Peor (v16) and on the male line that would carry on Midian's hostility. In ancient tribal society, sons grew up to avenge their fathers. Second, this was a specific, one-time judgment ordered by God through Moses, not a pattern for Israel's ordinary wars. Deuteronomy 20:10–15 required Israel to offer peace first to distant cities and to spare women and children. Third, Scripture never presents this as a model for God's people today. The church's warfare "is not against flesh and blood" (Eph 6:12), and Jesus rebuked his disciples when they wanted to call down fire on a village that rejected him (Luke 9:54–55). Christians have long wrestled with chapters like this. Some stress that God, as the giver of life and the judge of all, has the right to take it, and that this was judgment on real guilt. Others stress that these texts belong to a unique moment in God's dealings with Israel and are overshadowed by the cross. Many hold both. What believers should not do is read the passage without feeling its weight.
- **"All the young girls who have not known man… keep alive for yourselves" (v18).** These girls had not taken part at Peor. They were taken into Israelite households, as servants, and in time many would marry into Israel. This was not a license for abuse. The law in Deuteronomy 21:10–14 governed how an Israelite could marry a captive woman: she was given a month to mourn her parents, she became a full wife, and if he later rejected her he had to let her go free and could not sell her or treat her as a slave. Ancient law codes rarely protected captives like this.
- **"Encamp outside the camp seven days… purify yourselves and your captives on the third day and on the seventh day" (v19).** Everyone who had killed or touched a dead body was unclean and had to follow the purification law of chapter 19, with the water for impurity. Even a war God commanded left the soldiers unclean. Killing, even when just, was not treated as an ordinary thing.
- **"Everything that can stand the fire, you shall pass through the fire" (vv22–23).** Eleazar adds a new rule: metal objects, gold, silver, bronze, iron, tin and lead, are purified by fire and then water. Everything else is washed. Mentioning iron is a reminder that Israel's neighbors already had iron goods. Nothing taken from an idolatrous people could come into God's camp without being cleansed.
- **"Divide the plunder into two parts between the warriors… and all the congregation" (v27).** The 12,000 who fought receive half; the whole congregation shares the other half. David later makes the same rule: "as his share is who goes down into the battle, so shall his share be who stays by the baggage" (1 Sam 30:24–25). The victory belonged to the whole people.
- **"Levy for the LORD a tribute… one out of five hundred" (v28).** From the soldiers' half, one in five hundred goes to the priests. From the people's half, one in fifty goes to the Levites (v30). The people, who did not risk their lives, give ten times more.
- **The numbers (vv32–47).** 675,000 sheep, 72,000 cattle, 61,000 donkeys and 32,000 girls. These are enormous figures for a force of 12,000. Some scholars think the Hebrew word for "thousand" (*eleph*) can sometimes mean a clan unit, which would make the numbers much smaller. Others take them at face value, noting that Midian's wealth was in flocks and herds. The lists show the precision of the division: every half and every tribute is worked out exactly.
- **"There is not a man missing from us" (v49).** Not one Israelite soldier was lost. It reads as a sign that God fought the battle.
- **"We have brought the LORD's offering… to make atonement for ourselves" (v50).** The officers bring gold jewelry, armlets, bracelets, signet rings, earrings and beads, the portable wealth desert peoples wore (Judg 8:24–26). "Make atonement" may echo the census law of Exodus 30:12, where each man counted gave a ransom "that there be no plague among them." Having counted their men, the officers bring a ransom in gratitude. **16,750 shekels** is around 400 pounds of gold.
- **"A memorial for the people of Israel before the LORD" (v54).** The gold was placed in the tabernacle as a lasting reminder of what God had done.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the chapter "the history of a holy war," and on Moses' anger says, "It is dangerous to let them live; they will be still tempting the Israelites to uncleanness." He calls the absence of casualties "very extraordinary, and perhaps cannot be paralleled in any history," and says the officers' offering "was gotten honestly by a divine warrant." JFB says Moses' anger "arose in reality from a pious and enlightened regard to the best interests of Israel." It calls Balaam "an object of merited vengeance" and treats the lack of casualties as "an astonishing miracle." JFB holds that the "holy instruments" "must mean the 'trumpets,'" against those who think of the ark. Guzik acknowledges that the judgment is harsh and that vengeance makes modern readers uneasy, but argues that God's vengeance is just while personal vengeance is forbidden, and that Israel's unique role cannot be claimed by individuals today. He draws the warning that Israel's greatest danger was spiritual seduction, not enemy armies, and that the compromises believers tolerate can do more damage than open opposition. The Tyndale notes suggest the battle was fought only against the Midianite groups near Moab, and that it is not impossible for a well-prepared army to suffer no losses.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter shows that God takes seriously what destroys his people's faith. Balaam's plan failed on the hilltops but almost succeeded in the tents, and God judged it. The chapter also shows that judgment, even when just, leaves defilement that must be cleansed, and that victory comes from God, not numbers. The New Testament points to a different kind of war. Jesus did not take vengeance on his enemies; he bore it in their place, and prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). Final judgment belongs to him alone (John 5:22; Rev 19:11), and until that day his people "do not wage war according to the flesh" (2 Cor 10:3–4). The danger Balaam represented still comes the same way, through enticement to compromise (Rev 2:14). The protection is the same too: belonging wholly to the God who fights for his people.
