*Setting:* Chapter 4 ended with the bride opening her garden to her husband: "Let my beloved come to his garden." Chapter 5 opens with his answer (v1), the high point at the very center of the Song. Then the mood turns. In a second night scene (vv2–8), much like 3:1–4 but darker, he knocks at her door, she is slow to open, and when she does he is gone. She searches the city and the watchmen beat her. When the daughters of Jerusalem ask what is so special about him (v9), she answers with the Song's only full description of the man, head to foot (vv10–16). The Tyndale notes observe that right after the joy of union comes the pain of separation, a reminder that married love is not always easy. Many read vv2–8 as a dream ("I slept, but my heart was awake"), and the questions and answers seem to run on to 6:3. For the main ways the Song is read, see the Setting of chapter 1. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song. It labels the speakers, and the headings below follow those exchanges.

#### I Came to My Garden (v1)
- **"I came to my garden, my sister, my bride" (v1).** He answers her invitation (4:16). "My" appears eight times in the verse: my garden, my myrrh, my spice, my honeycomb, my honey, my wine, my milk. What she called "his garden" he now calls "mine." The marriage is complete. The Tyndale notes: there is no guilt or shame in the deep pleasure of husband and wife.
- **"I gathered my myrrh with my spice, I ate my honeycomb with my honey, I drank my wine with my milk" (v1).** These pick up the images of 4:11–14. The pleasures he praised, he now enjoys. The verbs are all past tense, a finished act.
- **"Eat, friends, drink, and be drunk with love!" (v1).** Someone else speaks. The ESV gives it to "Others," the wedding guests, who cheer the couple on. Some think it is the man inviting the guests to the wedding feast. Others hear the voice of God himself, the unseen host of the wedding, blessing the union. Guzik notes that commentators disagree on the speaker and sees heaven's approval in it either way. "Be drunk with love" means be filled with it, and the word for "love" (*dodim*) is the same as in 1:2. Some scholars, counting the Hebrew lines, place it at the very center of the Song.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "See how ready Christ is to accept the invitations of his people." JFB sees the Last Supper here, "a pledge of the future marriage," and notes that feasts were often held in gardens.

#### The Knock in the Night (vv2–8)
- **"I slept, but my heart was awake" (v2).** Half asleep, half dreaming. The Tyndale notes: a good description of a dream, her mind and feelings active while she sleeps. JFB: "Not total sleep."
- **"A sound! My beloved is knocking. 'Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my perfect one'" (v2).** "A sound!" is literally "a voice!" as in 2:8. He uses four names for her, each tender. "Perfect one" is *tammati*, from *tam*, "complete, blameless," the word used of Job (Job 1:1). **"For my head is wet with dew, my locks with the drops of the night."** Nights in Israel's hill country can be heavy with dew (Judg 6:38). He has been out in the cold and wants to come in. He knocks and asks. He doesn't force the door. Guzik passes on the suggestion that Jesus' words to Laodicea echo this verse: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him" (Rev 3:20).
- **"I had put off my garment; how could I put it on? I had bathed my feet; how could I soil them?" (v3).** Her excuses are flimsy. She has undressed for bed and washed her feet, and floors were dirt or stone. Getting up is a bother. Guzik, with Carr, calls it unwillingness, not impossibility. Many couples know the moment: one is eager, the other tired, and the moment passes.
- **"My beloved put his hand to the latch, and my heart was thrilled within me" (v4).** Doors were locked with a wooden bolt on the inside. There was a hole through which a hand could reach to lift it with a key (JFB describes a wooden key with pegs). He reaches for the latch, and her feelings stir. "My heart was thrilled" is literally "my inner parts were moved" for him. She wants him after all.
- **"I arose to open to my beloved, and my hands dripped with myrrh, my fingers with liquid myrrh, on the handles of the bolt" (v5).** Either she had perfumed herself for him, or he had left myrrh on the latch as a sign of his love before he left. JFB takes it the first way: perfume was the bride's best welcome. Guzik takes it the second way, as a love note, and draws a lesson for husbands: answer a rebuff with love, not demands.
- **"I opened to my beloved, but my beloved had turned and gone" (v6).** Too late. "Turned and gone" is two verbs in Hebrew, abrupt, like a door closing. **"My soul failed me when he spoke."** Literally "my soul went out." It may mean "when he spoke I melted," remembering his voice at the door, or "when he turned away," as some translations read. **"I sought him, but found him not; I called him, but he gave no answer."** The words of 3:1–2 again, now worse. That time she found him. This time silence.
- **"The watchmen found me as they went about in the city; they beat me, they bruised me, they took away my veil, those watchmen of the walls" (v7).** In 3:3 the watchmen let her pass. Now they treat her as a woman of the streets and beat her. The "veil" may be a light shawl or mantle. Taking it was a public humiliation, and JFB calls it "the greatest indignity to an Eastern lady." The Tyndale notes: in her dream the protectors become threats, and their blows mirror how wounded she feels. Guzik reads it as her guilt over her slow response.
- **"I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if you find my beloved, that you tell him I am sick with love" (v8).** The refrain of 2:7 and 3:5 changes. Before, she warned them not to awaken love too soon. Now she begs them to help her find it. In 2:5 she was "sick with love" because he was near. Now she is sick because he is gone. Guzik draws the same contrast.
- **Commentators:** Henry turns the scene to the believer's drowsiness: "Christ knocks to awaken us, knocks by his word and Spirit, knocks by afflictions and by our consciences." He adds, "Making excuses is making light of Christ," and comfortingly, "Every desertion is not despair." JFB reads the watchmen as harsh religious leaders, and the bolt as the sins that close the heart against Christ.

#### What Is Your Beloved More Than Another? (v9)
- **"What is your beloved more than another beloved, O most beautiful among women?" (v9).** The daughters ask twice. The question may be teasing, curious or a challenge: why are you so desperate for this one man? They call her "most beautiful among women," as he did in 1:8, which Guzik suggests may be partly sarcastic. Guzik passes on Meyer's point that her distress made them see this was no ordinary love. Their question gives her the chance to tell them, and she does.

#### My Beloved Is Radiant and Ruddy (vv10–16)
- **"My beloved is radiant and ruddy, distinguished among ten thousand" (v10).** This is the only full description of the man in the Song. It mirrors his descriptions of her (4:1–7) and moves from head to feet. Guzik, with Carr, notes that detailed descriptions of a man are rare in ancient love poetry. "Radiant" (*tsach*) means dazzling, glowing with health. "Ruddy" (*'adom*) is the word used of David (1 Sam 16:12). Lamentations uses both of Jerusalem's princes: "purer than snow, whiter than milk; their bodies were more ruddy than coral" (Lam 4:7). The Tyndale notes take it as health and a deep tan, not race. **"Distinguished"** is *dagul*, from the word for "banner" (2:4): he stands out like a flag above an army of ten thousand. JFB: "a standard bearer."
- **"His head is the finest gold; his locks are wavy, black as a raven" (v11).** Gold means precious and glowing. His hair is thick and dark. The whole description has the feel of a statue: gold, ivory, gems, marble. The Tyndale notes point out that ancient images of gods were made of exactly these materials. To her, he is magnificent.
- **"His eyes are like doves beside streams of water, bathed in milk, sitting beside a full pool" (v12).** She returns his compliment of 1:15 and 4:1. The whites of his eyes are clear as milk, and the irises sit like doves by a pool. Clean, bright and gentle.
- **"His cheeks are like beds of spices, mounds of sweet-smelling herbs. His lips are lilies, dripping liquid myrrh" (v13).** His cheeks are probably bearded, and the beard perfumed, as the Tyndale notes suggest (Ps 133:2). His lips are lovely, and his kisses fragrant. The liquid myrrh is the same as on the door bolt (v5).
- **"His arms are rods of gold, set with jewels. His body is polished ivory, bedecked with sapphires" (v14).** "Arms" is literally "hands," perhaps rounded fingers with gold rings. "Body" is literally "belly," his torso, smooth as an ivory panel. "Sapphires" are probably lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone flecked with gold, since true sapphire was hardly known then. The Tyndale notes give "beryl" (a green stone) for "jewels."
- **"His legs are alabaster columns, set on bases of gold" (v15).** Strong and steady like pillars of white marble or alabaster. **"His appearance is like Lebanon, choice as the cedars."** Tall, majestic, impressive (Ps 92:12). The Tyndale notes: the cedars of Lebanon were large, majestic and fragrant.
- **"His mouth is most sweet, and he is altogether desirable" (v16).** "Mouth" is literally "palate": his speech and kisses are sweetness itself. The Tyndale notes read it as her wish to kiss him. "Altogether desirable" (*machamaddim*) is a plural form, "all of him is desirable," or "he is all delights." The KJV's "he is altogether lovely" has become a classic phrase of Christian devotion.
- **"This is my beloved and this is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem" (v16).** "Friend" (*re'a*) is the masculine form of the word he uses for her, "my companion" (1:9). He is not only her lover but her friend. After the pain of the night she ends not with complaint but with praise. Guzik, with Carr, says friendship runs deeper than physical attraction and keeps a marriage alive.
- **Commentators:** Henry and JFB both read the colors of v10 of Christ: Henry: "He is white in the spotless innocence of his life, ruddy in the bleeding sufferings he went through at his death." JFB: "The chief of sinners needs the 'chiefest' of Saviours." JFB gives the literal sense of v16 as "His palate is sweetness, yea, all over loveliness," and adds on "my friend": "for He loves me." Henry admits the imagery reflects the customs of its time, so some details may be lost on modern readers. Guzik passes on Watchman Nee's reading of each feature as a picture of Jesus, and Spurgeon's lament that the world so seldom sees how lovely Jesus is. He ends with a pointed question for married couples: if he is so wonderful, why was she so slow to open the door?

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter swings from the joy of union to the ache of absence, and back to wonder. It is first about the ups and downs of real married love, and the lessons are practical: don't make excuses when love knocks, and don't lose sight of how precious your spouse is. But the scene of a lover standing outside in the night, wet with dew, knocking and saying "Open to me," is unforgettable, and the risen Christ uses almost the same words to a lukewarm church: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (Rev 3:20). He does not force the door. The bride's portrait of her beloved, "distinguished among ten thousand… altogether desirable," has given believers words to describe the Lord they love: the one who is "the radiance of the glory of God" (Heb 1:3), who was "pierced for our transgressions" (Isa 53:5), and who calls those he died for not servants but "friends" (John 15:13–15). "This is my beloved and this is my friend."
