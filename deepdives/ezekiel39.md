*Setting:* This chapter finishes the vision of Gog that began in chapter 38, given to Ezekiel among the exiles in Babylonia some time after Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC. Chapter 38 described a vast army from the ends of the earth invading a restored Israel living at peace, and God destroying it with earthquake, panic, plague, hail and fire. Chapter 39 retells the defeat in shorter form, then lingers on what comes after: Israel burning the weapons, burying the dead to cleanse the land, and the birds and beasts summoned to a gruesome "feast." It closes by stepping back from the vision to the exiles' own situation. God explains why Israel went into exile, and promises to bring them all home and pour out his Spirit on them. Those last verses tie the whole message of restoration in chapters 34–39 together, and prepare for the vision of a new temple that begins in chapter 40.

#### Prophecy Against Gog (vv1–24)
- **"Behold, I am against you, O Gog" (v1).** The chapter opens as chapter 38 did (38:2–3). The repetition is deliberate: the second panel retells the first, with more attention to the aftermath.
- **"I will turn you about and drive you forward" (v2).** "Drive you forward" translates a rare Hebrew verb that appears only here. The KJV guessed "leave but the sixth part of thee," and older commentators built a good deal on that. Modern lexicons generally take it as "lead along" or "drive." The point is the same as the hooks in 38:4: God steers Gog to his ruin.
- **"I will strike your bow from your left hand, and will make your arrows drop out of your right hand" (v3).** An archer held the bow in the left hand and drew with the right. God disarms him in mid-battle. The great army never fires a shot.
- **"I will give you to birds of prey of every sort and to the beasts of the field" (v4).** To be left unburied for scavengers was the deepest disgrace in the ancient world. David said the same to Goliath (1 Sam 17:44–46). The image prepares for vv17–20.
- **"I will send fire on Magog and on those who dwell securely in the coastlands" (v6).** The judgment reaches back to Gog's homeland and to the distant "coastlands" (*iyyim*), the islands and shores of the Mediterranean, Ezekiel's way of saying "the far ends of the earth." Those who felt safe far away are not safe. Some readers have tried to identify modern nations here, but the text gives no basis for it.
- **"My holy name I will make known in the midst of my people Israel, and I will not let my holy name be profaned anymore" (v7).** This takes up 36:20–23. Israel's exile made God's name look weak. Gog's defeat will make it clear to Israel and to the nations that he is **"the Holy One in Israel."** Isaiah's favorite title for God is "the Holy One of Israel." Ezekiel's "in Israel" stresses that God is present among his people.
- **"Behold, it is coming and it will be brought about… That is the day of which I have spoken" (v8).** The prophetic past tense, "it is done," makes a future event certain.
- **"They will make fires of the weapons and burn them… for seven years" (vv9–10).** The weapons listed are those of an ancient army: shields, bucklers, bows, arrows, clubs and spears, much of it made of wood or with wooden shafts. Israel will not need to cut firewood for seven years. Seven years, like the seven months that follow, signals completeness: the war is over for good, and the tools of war become fuel for cooking and warmth. Isaiah has a similar picture of swords beaten into plowshares (Isa 2:4) and of warriors' boots and bloody garments burned (Isa 9:5). Some modern readers try to fit this to modern weapons, but Ezekiel uses the language his hearers knew.
- **"They will seize the spoil of those who despoiled them, and plunder those who plundered them" (v10).** Gog came "to seize spoil and carry off plunder" (38:12). The plunderer is plundered.
- **"I will give to Gog a place for burial in Israel, the Valley of the Travelers, east of the sea" (v11).** "The sea" is probably the Dead Sea, so the valley would be in the highlands east of it, in modern Jordan. "Travelers" is *ovrim*, "those who pass through," which may be a play on the Abarim, the mountain range east of the Dead Sea where Moses looked over the promised land (Num 27:12; Deut 32:49). The exact location is not known. **"It will block the travelers"** probably means the graves will fill the valley so that the road through it is closed.
- **"It will be called the Valley of Hamon-gog" (v11).** *Hamon* means "multitude" or "horde." It is the word used again and again of Egypt's proud multitude in chapters 30–32. The name means "Valley of Gog's Horde." Some scholars also hear a play on *Ge-Hinnom*, the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, the place of child sacrifice that later gave its name to Gehenna, the place of judgment.
- **"For seven months the house of Israel will be burying them, in order to cleanse the land" (v12).** The reason is ritual purity. Under the law, contact with a corpse made a person unclean for seven days (Num 19:11–16), and an unburied body defiled the land (Deut 21:23). Ezekiel is a priest, and he cares that the land where God will dwell be clean. The scale is staggering: the whole nation needs seven months to bury the dead.
- **"It will bring them renown on the day that I show my glory" (v13).** The honor goes to Israel not for fighting, since God did that, but for the humble work of burying and cleansing.
- **"They will set apart men to travel through the land regularly… At the end of seven months they will make their search" (v14).** After the national effort, a permanent team keeps searching. **"When… anyone sees a human bone, then he shall set up a sign by it" (v15).** A passer-by marks a bone with a little stone pile or post, and the professional buriers collect it. Not even one bone may be left to defile the land. The care is almost clinical, and the point is that God's land will be completely clean.
- **"(Hamonah is also the name of the city.)" (v16).** This aside means "Multitude-town," a settlement named after the burial ground, perhaps where the buriers lived. It is not known from any other source.
- **"Speak to the birds of every sort and to all beasts of the field: 'Assemble and come… to the sacrificial feast that I am preparing for you'" (v17).** The scene is deliberately shocking. At a normal sacrificial meal, worshipers ate the meat of animals offered to God (Deut 12:7; 1 Sam 9:12–13). Here everything is turned upside down: God is the host, the birds and beasts are the guests, and the enemy soldiers are the sacrifice. Isaiah describes the same kind of "sacrifice" when God judges Edom (Isa 34:6), and Zephaniah calls the day of the LORD "a sacrifice" (Zeph 1:7).
- **"You shall eat the flesh of the mighty… of rams, of lambs, and of he-goats, of bulls, all of them fat beasts of Bashan" (v18).** The soldiers and princes are described as sacrificial animals. Bashan, east of the Sea of Galilee, was famous for its fattened cattle (Amos 4:1; Ps 22:12). **"Drink blood till you are drunk" (v19)** is especially jarring, since the law forbade people to eat blood (Lev 17:10–12). This is not a meal for people. It is a picture of total judgment.
- **"You shall be filled at my table with horses and charioteers" (v20).** Revelation takes this scene up almost word for word. An angel calls the birds to "the great supper of God, to eat the flesh of kings… and the flesh of horses and their riders" (Rev 19:17–18). Revelation sets beside it a very different meal: "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Rev 19:9). There are two suppers, and everyone attends one of them.
- **"I will set my glory among the nations… and the nations shall see my judgment" (v21).** Here the vision begins to give its meaning. The defeat of Gog shows God's glory to the world.
- **"The house of Israel shall know that I am the LORD their God, from that day forward" (v22).** After Gog, there will be no going back to idols.
- **"The nations shall know that the house of Israel went into captivity for their iniquity" (v23).** This is the key to the whole section. The nations thought Israel's God had failed to protect them (36:20). Gog's defeat will show the truth: God was able all along. Israel went into exile not because God was weak, but **"because they dealt so treacherously with me."**
- **"I hid my face from them and gave them into the hand of their adversaries" (vv23–24).** "Hiding the face" is the opposite of the priestly blessing, "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). Moses warned that God would hide his face if Israel turned to other gods (Deut 31:17–18). That is what happened in 586 BC. Twice in two verses the phrase appears.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in Gog's destruction "God crowns his favour to his people Israel," and that God's mercy to Israel "shines very brightly after the scattering of that black cloud." He finds the scale of the defeat in three pictures: the weapons burned, the dead buried, and the birds feasting. JFB takes "seven" as "the sacred number," implying "the completeness of the cleansing, and the people's zeal for purity." He places the valley east of the Dead Sea, says the dead were so many that "nothing but a deep valley would suffice for their corpses," and explains the feast from the fact that "anciently worshippers feasted on the sacrifices." Like Henry, JFB reads Gog partly in light of Antiochus IV and the Maccabean wars. Guzik, citing Wiersbe, says Ezekiel describes weapons his hearers could picture, and with Vawter and Hoppe stresses that Israel contributes nothing to the victory and only cleans up afterward. He thinks Revelation borrows Ezekiel's imagery for two different end-time scenes (Rev 19 and 20) rather than presenting one fulfilment.

#### The Lord Will Restore Israel (vv25–29)
- **"Now I will restore the fortunes of Jacob and have mercy on the whole house of Israel" (v25).** The word "now" brings the vision back from the distant future to the exiles' present. Before Gog, before anything else, God will bring his people home. "Restore the fortunes" (*shuv shevut*) is an idiom that can mean "bring back the captives" or, more broadly, "turn things around." **"The whole house of Israel"** includes the northern tribes, as in 37:16–22.
- **"I will be jealous for my holy name" (v25).** God's jealousy, which once burned against Israel, now works for them (36:5–6; Joel 2:18).
- **"They shall forget their shame" (v26).** The Hebrew consonants can be read as "they shall bear their shame," which is how the standard text reads, or as "forget," following a slight change. The ESV follows "forget." Ezekiel elsewhere says restored Israel will remember its sin with shame (16:61–63; 36:31). Both are true in different ways: forgiven people do not carry the old disgrace, and they never forget the grace that removed it.
- **"When they dwell securely in their land with none to make them afraid" (v26).** These are the words of the covenant of peace (34:25–28).
- **"I sent them into exile among the nations and then assembled them into their own land" (v28).** Both exile and return were God's doing. The God who scattered is the God who gathers (Jer 31:10). **"I will leave none of them remaining among the nations anymore."** Not one will be left behind, as not one bone was left in the land (v15).
- **"I will not hide my face anymore from them, when I pour out my Spirit upon the house of Israel" (v29).** This is the last word of the section, and it is the reverse of vv23–24. The hidden face is turned toward them again, and the reason is the Spirit. The verb "pour out" is used for liquids. God's wrath had been "poured out" on Jerusalem (7:8; 22:22). Now his Spirit is. Joel uses the same picture: "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" (Joel 2:28), and Peter quotes Joel at Pentecost to explain what had just happened (Acts 2:16–18).
- **How was this fulfilled?** The return under Zerubbabel, Ezra and Nehemiah was real but partial: many stayed in Babylon, the northern tribes mostly did not return as tribes, and God's glory did not fill the second temple as Ezekiel describes. The pouring out of the Spirit came at Pentecost, first on Jews gathered in Jerusalem "from every nation under heaven" (Acts 2:5), and then on Gentiles too. Christians differ on whether the gathering of "the whole house of Israel" points to a future turning of the Jewish people to Christ (Rom 11:25–27), to the church of Jews and Gentiles together as one Israel of God, or to both.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "bring again the captivity" as restoration "from calamity to prosperity," still awaiting a future, universal fulfilment, and calls the outpouring of the Spirit "the sure forerunner of their conversion." Guzik sees the return under Ezra and Zerubbabel as only a small, imperfect fulfilment, with the complete restoration still to come (Rom 11:26). Citing Taylor, he notes that the prophetic past tense makes the promise of the Spirit as certain as if it had already happened. Henry calls vv23–29 a declaration of "God's gracious purposes concerning his people Israel."

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel's Gog chapters end not with a battlefield but with a face turned toward God's people and the Spirit poured out on them. That is the order of the gospel. Jesus bore the hidden face of God on the cross, crying "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46), so that those who trust him would never be forsaken. Raised and exalted, he "received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit" and "poured out this that you yourselves are seeing and hearing" (Acts 2:33). The great supper of God in Revelation 19 echoes the grim feast of vv17–20, but for those who belong to the Lamb another table is set: the marriage supper, where the guests are not the defeated enemies of God but his redeemed and forgiven people.
