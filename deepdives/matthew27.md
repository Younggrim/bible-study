*Setting:* Jerusalem, Friday of Passover week and the Saturday after, spring of AD 30 (some date it AD 33). Through the night Jesus has been arrested in Gethsemane, tried before Caiaphas and the council, condemned for blasphemy, beaten and mocked, while Peter denied him three times in the courtyard (ch 26). The council can condemn, but under Roman rule it cannot carry out an execution (John 18:31). So at dawn the case moves to the Roman governor. This chapter is the center of Matthew's Gospel: the King of the Jews is rejected by his own leaders, sentenced by a pagan judge, mocked by soldiers, crucified between rebels, and laid in a sealed tomb. Matthew keeps showing two things at once. Jesus is innocent, and everything happens just as Scripture and Jesus himself said it would.

#### Jesus Delivered to Pilate (vv1–2)
- **"When morning came… took counsel against Jesus to put him to death" (v1).** The night hearing (26:57–68) was irregular, and Jewish custom frowned on capital trials at night. A dawn meeting gave the verdict a formal stamp (Luke 22:66–71). The real work of the morning was turning a religious charge, blasphemy, into a political one that Rome would care about: a man claiming to be king (Luke 23:2).
- **"They bound him and led him away and delivered him over to Pilate the governor" (v2).** "Delivered over" is the word Jesus used when he predicted this: "the Son of Man will be delivered over to the chief priests and scribes… and deliver him over to the Gentiles" (20:18–19). Pontius Pilate was prefect of Judea from AD 26 to 36. He usually lived in Caesarea on the coast and came to Jerusalem at the feasts to keep order. Josephus and Philo remember him as stubborn and harsh, a man who more than once offended Jewish customs and answered protests with force (compare Luke 13:1). A stone found at Caesarea in 1961 bears his name and title.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Matthew Henry both see the morning meeting as a formality covering what was already decided. Guzik draws on Philo for Pilate's reputation for cruelty and lists the charges brought to him (Luke 23:2). Henry sets the whole chapter in that light: it is "a very affecting story," yet "it is gospel, it is good news, that Jesus Christ was thus delivered for our offences."

#### Judas Hangs Himself (vv3–10)
- **"When Judas… saw that Jesus was condemned, he changed his mind" (v3).** The verb is *metamelomai*, "to regret." Many teachers contrast it with *metanoeō*, the usual word for true repentance, and the Tyndale notes do. The contrast is real in this story, since Judas's regret ends in despair, not in turning back to God. But the lexicon warns that the distinction is hard to hold by word use alone: Matthew uses *metamelomai* for the son who "changed his mind and went" into the vineyard (21:29, 32). What shows the difference here is not the word but where Judas went with his guilt.
- **"I have sinned by betraying innocent blood" (v4).** The first witness to Jesus' innocence in this chapter is the man who sold him. Innocence runs through the whole account: Pilate's wife calls him "that righteous man" (v19), Pilate asks "What evil has he done?" (v23), and he washes his hands of "this man's blood" (v24).
- **"What is that to us? See to it yourself" (v4).** The priests who paid him do not care. They were happy to use him and now leave him alone with his guilt. Pilate will say almost the same words to them: "see to it yourselves" (v24).
- **"Throwing down the pieces of silver into the temple" (v5).** The word is *naos*, often used of the sanctuary itself rather than the wider courts. Judas may have flung the coins toward the holy place, a desperate attempt to give the money back to someone, anyone.
- **"He went and hanged himself" (v5).** Acts 1:18–19 says Judas "falling headlong… burst open." The two accounts can fit together if his body fell after the hanging, perhaps when the rope or branch gave way, and many have read them that way since early times. Matthew tells what Judas did; Luke in Acts tells what became of his body.
- **"It is not lawful to put them into the treasury, since it is blood money" (v6).** Here is the irony at its sharpest: men who arranged the death of an innocent man worry about the purity of their offering box. The law barred money from unclean sources from the temple (Deut 23:18). Jesus had said they "strain out a gnat and swallow a camel" (23:24).
- **"The potter's field as a burial place for strangers" (v7).** "Strangers" means foreigners, pilgrims or Gentiles who died in Jerusalem with no family tomb. A potter's field was probably land where clay had been dug out, worthless for farming. Tradition places it in the Hinnom Valley south of the city, the valley of Jeremiah 19. Acts gives its Aramaic name, *Akeldama*, "Field of Blood" (Acts 1:19).
- **"Spoken by the prophet Jeremiah" (vv9–10).** The words come mainly from Zechariah 11:12–13, where the shepherd of God's flock is paid off with "thirty pieces of silver," the price of a slave (Exod 21:32), and the LORD says, "Throw it to the potter," calling it sarcastically "the lordly price at which I was priced by them." Why does Matthew name Jeremiah? The main answers: Matthew blends Zechariah with Jeremiah's own potter and field passages (Jer 18:1–4; 19:1–13, where the prophet buys a potter's flask and goes to the Hinnom Valley; 32:6–15, where he buys a field), and he names the major, better-known prophet, as Mark 1:2 names Isaiah for a blend of Malachi and Isaiah. Or, as an old Jewish tradition had it, Jeremiah stood first in the scroll of the Prophets and his name could stand for the whole. Either way, the point is the same: Israel's leaders once valued God's shepherd at a slave's price, and they have done it again.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up Judas: "he repented, despaired, and was ruined," and warns that "Sin will soon change its taste." He says the priests treated "the hire of a traitor" as like "the hire of a whore," which could not go into the temple (Deut 23:18). JFB calls Judas's grief "the sorrow of the world, which worketh death" (2 Cor 7:10). On the Jeremiah question JFB calls it "one more of critical interest than real importance" and gives Lightfoot's view that Jeremiah headed the prophetic books. Guzik lists three possible explanations and, like the Tyndale notes, draws the line between being sorry for the consequences of sin and being sorry for the sin itself.

#### Jesus Before Pilate (vv11–14)
- **"Are you the King of the Jews?" (v11).** To a Roman this was the only charge that mattered. A self-made king was a rebel against Caesar. "You" is emphatic in Greek, and the question may carry surprise: this bound, bruised Galilean, a king?
- **"You have said so" (v11).** The same reply Jesus gave Judas (26:25) and Caiaphas (26:64). It is a yes, but a guarded one. He is the King, though not in the sense Pilate fears. John fills in the conversation: "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:33–38).
- **"He gave no answer" (vv12–14).** Jesus answers Pilate's direct question but stays silent before the stream of accusations. "Not even to a single charge" stresses it. Isaiah had said of the servant, "like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7). The silence is not weakness. It is the calm of a man who will not defend himself because he has come to die.
- **"The governor was greatly amazed" (v14).** Pilate had seen many prisoners plead, lie and rage. He had not seen this.
- **Herod's part.** Luke alone records that Pilate, learning Jesus was a Galilean, sent him to Herod Antipas, who was in the city for the feast. Herod mocked him and sent him back (Luke 23:6–12). Matthew moves straight to the second hearing.
- **Commentators:** Guzik dwells on the dignity of Jesus' silence, citing Spurgeon on gentleness joined with majesty. Henry, like the Tyndale notes, sees in the silence the lamb of Isaiah 53:7. JFB passes over these verses here.

#### The Crowd Chooses Barabbas (vv15–23)
- **"The governor was accustomed to release… any one prisoner" (v15).** No source outside the Gospels mentions this Passover custom, but all four Gospels do (Mark 15:6; John 18:39), and releasing a prisoner at a feast fits what is known of Roman gestures of goodwill. Passover celebrated freedom from Egypt, so a release would be popular.
- **"A notorious prisoner called Barabbas" (v16).** Mark and Luke say he was in prison for insurrection and murder (Mark 15:7; Luke 23:19), and John calls him a *lēstēs*, a word used for bandits and violent rebels (John 18:40). To some in the crowd he may have been a hero of resistance.
- **"Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ?" (v17).** The ESV footnote notes that some manuscripts read "Jesus Barabbas" in vv16–17. The early writer Origen knew this reading and disliked it, which may explain why scribes dropped it. Many scholars think it is original. *Bar-abba* is Aramaic for "son of the father." If the reading stands, Pilate was offering a choice between two men named Jesus: Jesus son of the father, a killer, and Jesus called Christ, the Son of the Father. Whether or not the name was there, the exchange is the gospel in miniature: the guilty man walks free and the innocent one dies in his place.
- **"He knew that it was out of envy" (v18).** Pilate is no fool. He can see this is about rivalry, not rebellion. That makes his final decision worse.
- **"Have nothing to do with that righteous man" (v19).** Pilate sat on the *bēma*, the raised judge's seat. Roman governors' wives did not usually travel with them, but by this time the practice was allowed. Later tradition names her Procula or Claudia Procula, and the Greek Orthodox Church honors her as a saint, but the text gives no name. Dreams have guided events in Matthew before: Joseph's dreams (1:20; 2:13, 19, 22) and the wise men's (2:12). Now a Gentile woman is warned by God about Jesus, while his own leaders push for his death.
- **"The chief priests and the elders persuaded the crowd" (v20).** This crowd, gathered early at the governor's residence, was not the same as the pilgrims who cheered on Palm Sunday. It was a crowd that could be worked. The leaders steered the crowd and the crowd steered Pilate.
- **"Let him be crucified!" (vv22–23).** Pilate's question, "What evil has he done?" is the right one. No one answers it. They only shout louder. Crucifixion was the cruelest Roman death, kept for slaves and rebels, and for Jews it carried the curse of Deuteronomy 21:23: "a hanged man is cursed by God."
- **Commentators:** Henry says of Barabbas's crimes of robbery, sedition and murder that "Barabbas was guilty of all three," the very things falsely laid against Jesus. Guzik pictures Barabbas in his cell hearing his name shouted and then "Crucify him," and realizing that Jesus would hang on the cross meant for him. The Tyndale notes contrast the pagan wife who speaks up for Jesus with the crowd that does not. JFB does not comment on vv10–50, so its notes pick up again at v51.

#### Pilate Delivers Jesus to Be Crucified (vv24–26)
- **"He took water and washed his hands before the crowd" (v24).** Only Matthew records this. The gesture echoes the law for an unsolved murder, when the elders of the nearest town washed their hands and declared, "Our hands did not shed this blood" (Deut 21:6–9), and Psalm 26:6, "I wash my hands in innocence." Pilate borrows a Jewish sign of innocence, but water cannot wash away a judge's responsibility. He had the power to release Jesus (John 19:10) and chose not to. The church's creed has remembered him for two thousand years with the words "suffered under Pontius Pilate."
- **"I am innocent of this man's blood; see to it yourselves" (v24).** Again Jesus' innocence is spoken by someone condemning him. And Pilate echoes the priests' words to Judas (v4). Everyone tries to hand the guilt to someone else.
- **"His blood be on us and on our children!" (v25).** This was a known formula for accepting responsibility for a death (2 Sam 1:16; Josh 2:19; compare Acts 5:28). This verse has been terribly misused through history to justify hatred and violence against Jewish people. That reading is wrong. "All the people" means the crowd present that morning, not every Jew then or since. Jesus, his disciples and the first church were Jewish. Peter preached to people in this very city about the one they crucified and called them to repent and be forgiven (Acts 2:36–38; 3:17–19). Matthew likely sees the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, within the lifetime of "our children," as the judgment Jesus warned of (23:35–38). And in God's grace the blood they called down in guilt is the very blood "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28).
- **"Having scourged Jesus" (v26).** Roman flogging used a whip of leather thongs weighted with bits of bone or metal. It shredded the back, and some victims died under it. There was no Jewish limit of forty lashes here (Deut 25:3). Isaiah 53:5 had said, "with his wounds we are healed."
- **Commentators:** Henry says Pilate's washing was an attempt to shift guilt that only fixed it on him, and he reads the crowd's cry as a real imprecation of "wrath and vengeance upon themselves and their posterity," adding, "it is ill jesting with that which, sooner or later, will come in earnest." Guzik calls the hand-washing empty, and like the Tyndale notes he warns strongly against using v25 to justify any persecution of Jews, pointing to God's promise in Genesis 12:3. He also draws on a 1986 medical study of the crucifixion for what the scourging did to the body.

#### Jesus Is Mocked (vv27–31)
- **"The governor's headquarters… the whole battalion" (v27).** The headquarters is the *praetorium*, the governor's residence. Scholars debate whether this was the Antonia Fortress beside the temple, as the Tyndale notes hold, or Herod's former palace on the city's western side, which many now think more likely. "Battalion" is *speira*, a Roman cohort, nominally about 600 men, though the lexicon notes it could mean a smaller unit. These troops were mostly non-Jewish auxiliaries recruited from the region, men with little love for Jews.
- **A mock coronation (vv28–29).** A soldier's scarlet cloak stands in for the purple of royalty. A twisted crown of thorns mimics the laurel wreath on an emperor's head. A reed serves as a scepter. They kneel and say "Hail, King of the Jews!" as men hailed Caesar. Every detail is meant as a joke, and every detail is true. Thorns entered the world with the curse (Gen 3:18), and the King wears them.
- **"They spit on him and took the reed and struck him on the head" (v30).** The Jewish council had mocked him as a prophet (26:67–68). Now the Gentiles mock him as a king. Both sides of humanity join in. Isaiah's servant said, "I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting" (Isa 50:6).
- **"Put his own clothes on him" (v31).** Condemned men usually walked to execution naked or nearly so. Returning his clothes may have been a concession to Jewish sensibilities in the city.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the soldiers gathered the whole cohort for the sport, and that sin done together in a crowd is still sin. He suggests the account may have come from a soldier who later believed. Henry treats the mockery of Christ's kingship as part of the shame he bore for sinners, and adds that despite it, "Christ crucified is the Power of God."

#### The Crucifixion (vv32–44)
- **"A man of Cyrene, Simon by name" (v32).** Cyrene was a Greek city in North Africa, in modern Libya, with a large Jewish community (Acts 2:10; 6:9). Simon was likely a pilgrim in for Passover. "Compelled" is the word for Roman forced service, the same word Jesus used: "if anyone forces you to go one mile" (5:41). The condemned man normally carried the crossbeam himself. Jesus, after the flogging, apparently could not. Mark adds that Simon was "the father of Alexander and Rufus" (Mark 15:21), names his readers evidently knew, and a Rufus greets the church in Rome (Rom 16:13). It seems the man pressed into carrying the cross later took it up as a disciple.
- **"Golgotha (which means Place of a Skull)" (v33).** An Aramaic name. "Calvary" comes from the Latin word for skull, *calvaria*. Why it was called that is unknown: perhaps a skull-shaped rock, perhaps a place of executions. It lay outside the city wall (Heb 13:12) near a road where passersby could see (v39). The Church of the Holy Sepulchre has marked the traditional site since the fourth century. The so-called Garden Tomb site is more recent and less likely.
- **"Wine to drink, mixed with gall" (v34).** Mark says "wine mixed with myrrh" (Mark 15:23), a drug that dulled pain. Matthew's word "gall" echoes Psalm 69:21: "They gave me poison for food, and for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink." Jesus tasted it and refused. He would go through this with a clear mind.
- **"They divided his garments among them by casting lots" (v35).** The execution squad had the right to the victim's clothes. Psalm 22:18 had said, "they divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots." John adds the seamless tunic (John 19:23–24). Matthew's description of the crucifixion itself is just a few words. No Gospel dwells on the physical horror. Everyone in the first century knew what it meant.
- **"This is Jesus, the King of the Jews" (v37).** The charge was written on a board, the *titulus*. John says it was in Aramaic, Latin and Greek and that Pilate refused to change it (John 19:19–22). The four Gospels give slightly different wordings, which is natural if the sign was in three languages and each writer summarizes it. It was meant as a warning to anyone with royal ambitions. It was also the plain truth. The magi had asked for "he who has been born king of the Jews" (2:2), and now it is written over his cross.
- **"Two robbers were crucified with him" (v38).** The word is *lēstēs* again, the word used for Barabbas, so they may have been rebels from the same uprising. "He was numbered with the transgressors" (Isa 53:12). Luke records that one of them later repented (Luke 23:39–43).
- **"Wagging their heads" (v39).** Psalm 22:7: "All who see me mock me; they make mouths at me; they wag their heads."
- **"You who would destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, save yourself!" (v40).** They throw his own words back (26:61; John 2:19), not knowing that in three days he will do exactly that with the temple of his body.
- **"If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross" (v40).** The same "if you are the Son of God" that Satan used in the wilderness (4:3, 6). The last temptation is the oldest one: prove who you are by saving yourself.
- **"He saved others; he cannot save himself" (v42).** The leaders meant it as an insult. It is the truest sentence in the scene. He could have come down, but he would not, because he stayed to save others. "Let him come down now… and we will believe" was false too: when he rose from the grave, they did not believe (28:11–15).
- **"He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (v43).** Almost word for word Psalm 22:8: "He trusts in the LORD; let him deliver him." The leaders mock Jesus in the words of the very psalm he is fulfilling.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Simon's burden a picture of discipleship: "All that will approve themselves disciples indeed, must follow Christ." Guzik notes that a cross was a one-way trip, so to take up one's cross (16:24) means to go out to die. He suggests Simon's sons became leaders in the church, and he lists the four things the mockers threw at Jesus: his work as Savior, his kingship, his trust in God and his sonship. The Tyndale notes, like Guzik, connect v40 to the wilderness temptation, and observe that every insult told the truth.

#### The Death of Jesus (vv45–56)
- **"From the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land until the ninth hour" (v45).** Noon to three in the afternoon, counting from sunrise. It cannot have been an eclipse, because Passover fell at the full moon, when the moon is on the far side of the earth from the sun. Later writers, Thallus and Phlegon, quoted by Julius Africanus, apparently mention a darkness about this time, though their words survive only secondhand. In Scripture darkness signals judgment: the plague before the first Passover (Exod 10:21–23), and Amos 8:9, "I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight." "All the land" can mean the land of Israel or the whole earth.
- **"Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" (v46).** The opening line of Psalm 22, the psalm the mockers have just been unknowingly quoting. Matthew gives "Eli," the Hebrew form, where Mark gives "Eloi," the Aramaic (Mark 15:34). This is the only time in the Gospels that Jesus addresses God in prayer without calling him "Father."
- **"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (v46).** What did Jesus mean? Two truths must be held together. First, the forsakenness was real. The Son who "knew no sin" was made "to be sin" (2 Cor 5:21) and became "a curse for us" (Gal 3:13), bearing in himself God's judgment on sin, and he felt it as abandonment. Second, he still says "*my* God." It is a cry of faith, not of unbelief, and Psalm 22, which begins in desolation, ends in praise and in the nations turning to the LORD (Ps 22:22–31). Jewish readers used the first line of a psalm to call up the whole. The Trinity was not broken in two; the Son bore what we deserved, and the Father turned his face from the sin the Son carried.
- **"This man is calling Elijah" (v47).** "Eli" sounds like "Elijah" (*Eliyahu*). Many Jews believed Elijah would come in times of need and before the day of the LORD (Mal 4:5). Whether the bystanders misheard or twisted his words on purpose, they missed that the Elijah who was to come had already come in John the Baptist (11:14; 17:12).
- **"A sponge, filled with sour wine" (v48).** Sour wine, *oxos*, was the cheap vinegary drink of soldiers and laborers. John says Jesus had just said, "I thirst" (John 19:28–29). It may have been a small kindness, or more mockery (Ps 69:21). The others say "Wait," enjoying the show.
- **"Jesus cried out again with a loud voice and yielded up his spirit" (v50).** Crucified men usually died slowly, exhausted and unable to breathe. Jesus dies with a loud cry, strong to the end. John tells us the cry: "It is finished" (John 19:30), and Luke the prayer: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46). "Yielded up" (*aphēken*, "let go, sent away") is the language of a free act. He had said, "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18).
- **"The curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (v51).** The inner curtain hung before the Most Holy Place, where only the high priest entered once a year with blood (Lev 16). Josephus describes the temple curtains as huge, richly embroidered hangings. "From top to bottom" means no human hand tore it. God opened the way. Hebrews explains it: by Christ's blood we have "confidence to enter the holy places… by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20). It also hints that the temple's role was ending; within forty years it would be destroyed.
- **"The earth shook, and the rocks were split" (v51).** In the Old Testament the earth shakes when God comes to act and to judge (Exod 19:18; Ps 18:7; Joel 3:16).
- **"Many bodies of the saints… were raised" (vv52–53).** Only Matthew records this, and it raises honest questions. The Greek can be read so that the tombs opened at Jesus' death, but the saints came out "after his resurrection," keeping Jesus as "the firstfruits" of those raised (1 Cor 15:20). Most interpreters read it this way, and JFB does too. Who they were and what happened to them afterward is not said. Some think they were raised to die again, like Lazarus, others that they went to glory with the risen Christ. A few modern scholars read the passage as apocalyptic imagery rather than a reported event, but Matthew tells it plainly as something that happened, adding that they "appeared to many." The point is clear: the death of Jesus breaks the power of death (Ezek 37:12–13; Dan 12:2).
- **"Truly this was the Son of God!" (v54).** A Roman centurion commanded about a hundred men, and he and his squad had carried out the execution. The first people in Matthew to confess Jesus as Son of God after his death are Gentile soldiers, and the gospel will go out to "all nations" (28:19). The Greek has no article ("a son of God" is grammatically possible), and Luke gives his words as "Certainly this man was innocent!" (Luke 23:47). Perhaps the centurion understood little. But Matthew means his readers to hear the full truth in his words, as the disciples had confessed it (14:33; 16:16).
- **"Many women… looking on from a distance" (vv55–56).** The men had fled (26:56). The women stayed. Mary Magdalene, from whom Jesus had cast out seven demons (Luke 8:2), is named first in nearly every list. "Mary the mother of James and Joseph" may be the "other Mary" of v61. "The mother of the sons of Zebedee" had once asked for her sons to sit at Jesus' right and left in his kingdom (20:20–21). Now she sees who is at his right and left (v38). These women will be the witnesses of his death, his burial and his empty tomb.
- **Commentators:** Henry on the darkness: "Never were there three such hours since the day that God created man upon the earth." On the cry of dereliction he writes that "God forsook him, not as he forsook Saul, leaving him to an endless despair," but withdrew the sense of his favor while Jesus bore wrath for sin, and he notes that Jesus still said "my God." On the Elijah mistake: "Many of the reproaches cast upon the word of God and the people of God, take rise from gross mistakes." JFB says the torn curtain shows "the way into the holiest of all" opened, and argues that the risen saints came out after Christ's resurrection and did not die again, a point Guzik and Henry leave open. Guzik stresses that the tear ran from top to bottom, from God's side, and wonders whether the "great many of the priests" who later believed (Acts 6:7) included men who saw it. He also points out that the centurion says Jesus "was" the Son of God, when in fact he still is.

#### Jesus Is Buried (vv57–61)
- **"When it was evening" (v57).** The Sabbath began at sunset, and the law said a hanged body must not stay on the tree overnight (Deut 21:22–23). Romans often left crucified bodies up for days as a warning, and many were thrown into common graves. Joseph had little time.
- **"A rich man from Arimathea, named Joseph, who also was a disciple" (v57).** Arimathea was probably a town in the hill country northwest of Jerusalem. Mark and Luke say he was a respected member of the council who had not agreed with its decision (Mark 15:43; Luke 23:50–51), and John says he had been a secret disciple "for fear of the Jews" (John 19:38). Now he steps out in the open at the worst possible moment. Isaiah had said the servant would be "with a rich man in his death" (Isa 53:9).
- **"He went to Pilate and asked for the body" (v58).** It took courage for a council member to ask for the body of a man executed as a rebel. Pilate's quick agreement fits his belief that Jesus was no real threat. Mark adds that Pilate first checked that Jesus was already dead (Mark 15:44–45).
- **"A clean linen shroud… his own new tomb, which he had cut in the rock" (vv59–60).** John adds that Nicodemus brought about seventy-five pounds of spices (John 19:39). Rock-cut tombs had a low entrance and benches or niches inside, and wealthy families used them for generations. A "new" tomb, where no one had yet been laid (John 19:41), means there could be no confusion later about whose body was missing. The "great stone" was likely a disc rolled in a channel across the entrance.
- **"Mary Magdalene and the other Mary were there, sitting opposite the tomb" (v61).** They saw exactly where he was laid. On Sunday morning they will not go to the wrong tomb (28:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ has more secret disciples than we are aware of," and he sees God's providence in a rich man being ready to bury him. Guzik stresses that the new tomb rules out any mix-up, and he describes how such tombs and their rolling stones worked. JFB refers the burial to its notes on John.

#### The Guard at the Tomb (vv62–66)
- **"The next day, that is, after the day of Preparation" (v62).** "Preparation" was the name for Friday, the day to prepare for the Sabbath. Matthew avoids saying "the Sabbath" outright, perhaps because of the irony: the chief priests and Pharisees are doing business with a Gentile governor on the holy day.
- **"That impostor said… 'After three days I will rise'" (v63).** The enemies remember what the disciples had forgotten. Jesus had said it plainly and often (16:21; 17:23; 20:19), and the leaders had heard about "the sign of Jonah" (12:40). "After three days" and "on the third day" were interchangeable ways of speaking (compare 16:21).
- **"Lest his disciples go and steal him away" (v64).** They fear a fraud. In fact they are arranging the best evidence against one. In 28:11–15 Matthew tells how the story that the disciples stole the body was spread, and that it was still being told in his day. This little scene shows that even the enemies of Jesus agreed the tomb was empty; they disagreed only on why.
- **"You have a guard of soldiers" (v65).** The Greek can be read as "you have a guard," meaning use your own temple police, or as an order, "take a guard." The word is *koustōdia*, borrowed from the Latin *custodia*, which suggests Roman soldiers, and 28:14 has the guards answerable to the governor. Most readers think Pilate gave them Roman troops.
- **"Sealing the stone and setting a guard" (v66).** A cord across the stone, fixed with clay or wax and stamped, so any tampering would show. A stone, a seal and a squad of soldiers: every human precaution was taken. None of it could hold him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ's enemies, even when they have gained their point, are still in fear of losing it again." JFB notes that "that deceiver… said, while he was yet alive" is their own testimony that Jesus really died, and that God used their seal and their watch to guard the body and serve his own purposes. Guzik lists the three barriers, stone, seal and guard, and observes that the more carefully the tomb was secured, the stronger the witness of its emptiness.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everyone in this chapter tries to get rid of Jesus or to get rid of the guilt for doing so. Judas throws down the silver, the priests hand off the blame, Pilate washes his hands, the crowd shouts, the soldiers laugh, and the leaders seal the stone. Yet at every step the truth comes out of their mouths: innocent blood, the righteous man, the King of the Jews, he saved others, truly the Son of God. Barabbas walks free while Jesus takes the cross meant for him, and that exchange is the gospel. The Son cries out in forsakenness so that the curtain can be torn and sinners welcomed into the presence of God. The sealed tomb is not the end of the story. It is the setting for the morning that follows.
