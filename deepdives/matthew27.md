*Setting:* Jerusalem, Friday of Passover week and the Saturday after, spring of AD 30 (some date it AD 33). Through the night Jesus has been arrested, tried before Caiaphas and the council, condemned for blasphemy and mocked, while Peter denied him three times (ch 26). The council can condemn, but under Roman rule it cannot execute (John 18:31), so at dawn the case moves to the Roman governor. This is the center of Matthew's Gospel: the King of the Jews is rejected by his leaders, sentenced by a pagan judge, mocked, crucified between rebels and laid in a sealed tomb. Matthew keeps showing two things at once: Jesus is innocent, and everything happens as Scripture and Jesus himself said it would.

#### Jesus Delivered to Pilate (vv1–2)
- **"When morning came… took counsel against Jesus" (v1).** The night hearing was irregular, and a dawn meeting gave the verdict a formal stamp (Luke 22:66–71). The real work was turning a religious charge, blasphemy, into a political one Rome would care about: a man claiming to be king (Luke 23:2).
- **"Delivered him over to Pilate the governor" (v2).** "Delivered over" is the word Jesus used when he predicted this (20:18–19). Pontius Pilate was prefect of Judea from AD 26 to 36. He lived in Caesarea and came to Jerusalem at the feasts to keep order. Josephus and Philo remember him as stubborn and harsh (compare Luke 13:1). A stone found at Caesarea in 1961 bears his name and title.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Matthew Henry both treat the morning meeting as a formality covering what was already decided, and Guzik draws on Philo for Pilate's cruelty. Henry frames the whole chapter: it is "a very affecting story," yet "it is gospel, it is good news, that Jesus Christ was thus delivered for our offences."

#### Judas Hangs Himself (vv3–10)
- **"He changed his mind" (v3).** The verb is *metamelomai*, "to regret." Many teachers, the Tyndale notes among them, contrast it with *metanoeō*, the usual word for repentance. The lexicon warns that the distinction is hard to hold by word use alone: Matthew uses *metamelomai* for the son who "changed his mind and went" into the vineyard (21:29). What shows the difference is not the word but where Judas took his guilt: to the priests and a rope, not to God.
- **"I have sinned by betraying innocent blood" (v4).** The first witness to Jesus' innocence in this chapter is the man who sold him. Pilate's wife (v19) and Pilate himself (vv23–24) will add their voices.
- **"What is that to us? See to it yourself" (v4).** The men who paid him leave him alone with his guilt. Pilate will say the same words to them (v24). Everyone tries to pass the blame along.
- **"Throwing down the pieces of silver into the temple" (v5).** The word is *naos*, often the sanctuary itself, so Judas may have flung the coins toward the holy place.
- **"He went and hanged himself" (v5).** Acts 1:18 says that "falling headlong he burst open." Since early times many have read the two together: his body fell after the hanging. Matthew tells what Judas did, and Acts what became of his body.
- **"It is not lawful to put them into the treasury" (v6).** Men who arranged an innocent man's death worry about the purity of the offering box (Deut 23:18). Jesus had said they "strain out a gnat and swallow a camel" (23:24).
- **"The potter's field as a burial place for strangers" (v7).** "Strangers" means foreigners or pilgrims who died in Jerusalem with no family tomb. A potter's field was probably land dug out for clay. Tradition puts it in the Hinnom Valley, the valley of Jeremiah 19. Acts gives its Aramaic name, *Akeldama* (Acts 1:19).
- **"Spoken by the prophet Jeremiah" (vv9–10).** The words come mainly from Zechariah 11:12–13, where God's shepherd is paid off with thirty pieces of silver, a slave's price (Exod 21:32), and the LORD says, "Throw it to the potter." Why Jeremiah? Either Matthew blends Zechariah with Jeremiah's own potter and field passages (Jer 18:1–4; 19:1–13; 32:6–15) and names the better-known prophet, as Mark 1:2 names Isaiah for a blend of Malachi and Isaiah; or, by an old Jewish tradition, Jeremiah stood first in the scroll of the Prophets and his name could stand for it. Either way, Israel's leaders have again priced God's shepherd at a slave's wage.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up Judas: "he repented, despaired, and was ruined," and warns, "Sin will soon change its taste." The priests, he says, treated "the hire of a traitor" like "the hire of a whore." JFB calls Judas's grief "the sorrow of the world, which worketh death" (2 Cor 7:10) and calls the Jeremiah question "one more of critical interest than real importance," giving Lightfoot's view that Jeremiah headed the prophetic books. Guzik, like the Tyndale notes, separates being sorry for sin's consequences from being sorry for the sin.

#### Jesus Before Pilate (vv11–14)
- **"Are you the King of the Jews?" (v11).** To a Roman this was the only charge that mattered: a self-made king was a rebel against Caesar. "You" is emphatic, perhaps with surprise: this bruised Galilean, a king?
- **"You have said so" (v11).** The reply he gave Judas (26:25) and Caiaphas (26:64): a guarded yes. He is the King, though not as Pilate fears (John 18:36).
- **"He gave no answer… not even to a single charge" (vv12–14).** "Like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7). Pilate had seen prisoners plead, lie and rage, and he "was greatly amazed." Luke alone adds that Pilate sent him to Herod Antipas in between (Luke 23:6–12).
- **Commentators:** Guzik dwells on the dignity of Jesus' silence, citing Spurgeon on gentleness joined to majesty. JFB passes over vv10–50 entirely, so its notes resume at v51.

#### The Crowd Chooses Barabbas (vv15–23)
- **"The governor was accustomed to release… any one prisoner" (v15).** No source outside the Gospels mentions this custom, but all four do (Mark 15:6; John 18:39), and it suits Passover, the feast of freedom.
- **"A notorious prisoner called Barabbas" (v16).** Mark and Luke say he was jailed for insurrection and murder (Mark 15:7; Luke 23:19). To some in the crowd he may have been a hero of resistance.
- **"Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ?" (v17).** The ESV footnote notes that some manuscripts read "Jesus Barabbas" in vv16–17. Origen knew the reading and disliked it, which may be why scribes dropped it, and many scholars think it original. *Bar-abba* is Aramaic for "son of the father." If the reading stands, Pilate offered two men named Jesus: a killer called son of the father, and the Son of the Father. Either way the exchange is the gospel in miniature: the guilty man goes free and the innocent one dies in his place.
- **"He knew that it was out of envy" (v18).** Pilate could see this was rivalry, not rebellion, which makes his decision worse.
- **"Have nothing to do with that righteous man" (v19).** Pilate sat on the *bēma*, the judge's seat. Later tradition names his wife Procula, but the text does not. God has spoken through dreams before in Matthew (1:20; 2:12–13). Now a Gentile woman speaks up for Jesus while his own leaders press for his death.
- **"The chief priests and the elders persuaded the crowd" (v20).** This early-morning crowd at the governor's residence was not the pilgrim crowd of Palm Sunday. The leaders steered the crowd, and the crowd steered Pilate.
- **"Why? What evil has he done?" (v23).** It is the right question, and no one answers it. They only shout louder for crucifixion, the death for slaves and rebels and, for Jews, the death under God's curse (Deut 21:23).
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that of the crimes Jesus was falsely charged with, "Barabbas was guilty of all three." Guzik pictures Barabbas in his cell hearing his name shouted and realizing Jesus would hang on the cross meant for him. The Tyndale notes contrast the pagan wife who speaks for Jesus with the crowd that does not.

#### Pilate Delivers Jesus to Be Crucified (vv24–26)
- **"He took water and washed his hands" (v24).** Only Matthew records it. It echoes the law for an unsolved murder, when elders washed their hands and said, "Our hands did not shed this blood" (Deut 21:6–9). But water cannot wash away a judge's responsibility. Pilate had power to release Jesus (John 19:10), and the creed still says Jesus "suffered under Pontius Pilate."
- **"His blood be on us and on our children!" (v25).** A known formula for accepting responsibility for a death (2 Sam 1:16; Acts 5:28). The verse has been terribly misused to justify hatred of Jewish people, and that reading is wrong. "All the people" is the crowd present that morning. Jesus, his disciples and the first church were Jewish, and Peter soon called people in this very city to repent and be forgiven (Acts 2:36–38; 3:17–19). Matthew likely sees the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, within the lifetime of "our children," as the judgment Jesus warned of (23:35–38). And the blood they called down in guilt is the blood "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28).
- **"Having scourged Jesus" (v26).** Roman flogging used leather thongs weighted with bone or metal and had no Jewish limit of forty lashes (Deut 25:3). Some died under it. "With his wounds we are healed" (Isa 53:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the crowd's cry as calling down "wrath and vengeance upon themselves and their posterity," adding, "it is ill jesting with that which, sooner or later, will come in earnest." Guzik calls the hand-washing empty, and like the Tyndale notes he warns strongly against using v25 to justify persecution of Jews, pointing to Genesis 12:3.

#### Jesus Is Mocked (vv27–31)
- **"The governor's headquarters… the whole battalion" (v27).** The *praetorium* was the governor's residence, either the Antonia Fortress beside the temple (as the Tyndale notes hold) or Herod's palace on the west side, which many scholars now prefer. "Battalion" is *speira*, a cohort, nominally about 600 men, though the lexicon notes it could mean a smaller unit. They were mostly non-Jewish auxiliaries from the region.
- **A mock coronation (vv28–29).** A soldier's scarlet cloak for royal purple, a crown of thorns for an emperor's wreath, a reed for a scepter, and "Hail, King of the Jews!" as men hailed Caesar. Every detail is a joke, and every detail is true. Thorns came with the curse (Gen 3:18), and the King wears them.
- **"They spit on him… and struck him on the head" (v30).** The council mocked him as a prophet (26:67–68). Now Gentiles mock him as a king. "I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting" (Isa 50:6).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that they gathered the whole cohort for the sport, and that sin done in a crowd is still sin. He suggests the account may have come from a soldier who later believed. Henry treats the mockery of Christ's kingship as part of the shame he bore, and insists that "Christ crucified is the Power of God."

#### The Crucifixion (vv32–44)
- **"A man of Cyrene, Simon by name" (v32).** Cyrene, in modern Libya, had a large Jewish community (Acts 2:10). "Compelled" is the word for Roman forced service (5:41). After the flogging Jesus apparently could not carry the crossbeam. Mark calls Simon "the father of Alexander and Rufus" (Mark 15:21), names his readers knew, and a Rufus is greeted in Rome (Rom 16:13). The man pressed into carrying the cross seems to have taken it up as a disciple.
- **"Golgotha (which means Place of a Skull)" (v33).** An Aramaic name. "Calvary" comes from the Latin *calvaria*, skull. It lay outside the wall (Heb 13:12) by a road (v39). The Church of the Holy Sepulchre has marked the traditional site since the fourth century.
- **"Wine… mixed with gall" (v34).** Mark says myrrh (Mark 15:23), a drug that dulled pain. Matthew's "gall" echoes Psalm 69:21. Jesus tasted and refused it. He would go through this with a clear mind.
- **"They divided his garments… by casting lots" (v35).** "They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots" (Ps 22:18). The crucifixion itself gets a few words. No Gospel dwells on the horror; everyone then knew what it meant.
- **"This is Jesus, the King of the Jews" (v37).** The written charge, in three languages (John 19:19–20), which explains the slightly different wordings in the Gospels. Meant as a warning, it was the plain truth. The magi had sought "he who has been born king of the Jews" (2:2).
- **"Two robbers were crucified with him" (v38).** The word *lēstēs* was also used of Barabbas (John 18:40), so they may have been rebels. "He was numbered with the transgressors" (Isa 53:12). Luke records that one later repented (Luke 23:39–43).
- **"If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross" (v40).** The same words Satan used in the wilderness (4:3, 6). The last temptation is the oldest: save yourself. They also throw back his saying about rebuilding the temple in three days, not knowing he will do just that with the temple of his body (John 2:19–21).
- **"He saved others; he cannot save himself" (v42).** Meant as an insult, it is the truest sentence in the scene. He could have come down. He stayed in order to save others. Their promise to believe was empty; when he rose, they did not (28:11–15).
- **"He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (v43).** Nearly word for word Psalm 22:8. With "wagging their heads" (v39; Ps 22:7), the mockers quote the very psalm Jesus is fulfilling.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Simon's burden a picture of discipleship: "All that will approve themselves disciples indeed, must follow Christ." Guzik notes that a cross was a one-way trip, so taking up one's cross (16:24) means going out to die, and he lists four things mocked: Jesus as Savior, as King, as one trusting God and as Son of God. The Tyndale notes, like Guzik, tie v40 to the wilderness temptation.

#### The Death of Jesus (vv45–56)
- **"Darkness over all the land until the ninth hour" (v45).** Noon to three. It cannot have been an eclipse, because Passover fell at full moon. In Scripture darkness signals judgment: the plague before the first Passover (Exod 10:21–23) and Amos 8:9, "I will make the sun go down at noon."
- **"Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" (v46).** The first line of Psalm 22, the psalm the mockers have been quoting. Matthew gives the Hebrew "Eli" where Mark has Aramaic "Eloi" (Mark 15:34). It is the only recorded prayer in which Jesus does not say "Father."
- **"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (v46).** Two truths belong together. The forsakenness was real: the sinless Son was made "to be sin" (2 Cor 5:21) and "a curse for us" (Gal 3:13), and he bore God's judgment as abandonment. Yet he still says "*my* God." It is a cry of faith, and Psalm 22 moves from desolation to praise and the nations turning to the LORD (Ps 22:22–31). The Trinity was not broken; the Son bore what sinners deserved.
- **"This man is calling Elijah" (vv47–49).** "Eli" sounds like *Eliyahu*. Many expected Elijah before the day of the LORD (Mal 4:5), not seeing that he had come in John the Baptist (17:12). The sour wine (*oxos*) was the cheap drink of soldiers. John says Jesus had said, "I thirst" (John 19:28). The others say "Wait," enjoying the show.
- **"Cried out again with a loud voice and yielded up his spirit" (v50).** The crucified usually died slowly, too weak to breathe. Jesus dies with a loud cry: "It is finished" (John 19:30) and "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46). "Yielded up" (*aphēken*, "let go") is a free act: "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18).
- **"The curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (v51).** The inner curtain hung before the Most Holy Place, entered only by the high priest once a year with blood (Lev 16). "From top to bottom" means God tore it. By Christ's blood believers now enter "through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20). It also hints the temple's role was ending; within forty years it was destroyed.
- **"Many bodies of the saints… were raised" (vv52–53).** Only Matthew records this. The Greek can be read so that the tombs opened at Jesus' death but the saints came out "after his resurrection," keeping Jesus as "the firstfruits" (1 Cor 15:20). Most interpreters read it so. Who they were and what became of them is not said. A few modern scholars take it as symbolic imagery, but Matthew tells it plainly as an event, adding that they "appeared to many." Jesus' death breaks the power of death (Ezek 37:12–13).
- **"Truly this was the Son of God!" (v54).** The first in Matthew to confess this after his death are Gentile soldiers, a foretaste of "all nations" (28:19). The Greek lacks the article, and Luke gives the words as "Certainly this man was innocent!" (Luke 23:47), so the centurion may have understood little. Matthew means his readers to hear the full truth (14:33; 16:16).
- **"Many women… looking on from a distance" (vv55–56).** The men had fled (26:56). The women stayed. Mary Magdalene, freed from seven demons (Luke 8:2), is named first. The mother of Zebedee's sons once asked for her sons to sit at Jesus' right and left (20:21). Now she sees who is at his right and left (v38). These women will witness his death, burial and empty tomb.
- **Commentators:** Henry on the darkness: "Never were there three such hours since the day that God created man upon the earth." On the cry, "God forsook him, not as he forsook Saul, leaving him to an endless despair," and he notes Jesus still said "my God." On the Elijah mistake: "Many of the reproaches cast upon the word of God and the people of God, take rise from gross mistakes." JFB says the torn veil makes manifest "the way into the holiest of all," and holds that the saints came out after Christ's resurrection and did not die again, a point Guzik and Henry leave open. Guzik stresses the tear from God's side and wonders whether the priests who later believed (Acts 6:7) had seen it.

#### Jesus Is Buried (vv57–61)
- **"When it was evening" (v57).** The Sabbath began at sunset, and the law forbade leaving a hanged body overnight (Deut 21:22–23). Romans often left the crucified up for days or threw them in common graves.
- **"A rich man from Arimathea, named Joseph" (v57).** A council member who had not agreed with its decision (Luke 23:50–51) and a secret disciple "for fear of the Jews" (John 19:38). He steps into the open at the worst moment. Isaiah said the servant would be "with a rich man in his death" (Isa 53:9).
- **"His own new tomb, which he had cut in the rock" (v60).** Nicodemus brought about seventy-five pounds of spices (John 19:39). A new tomb, where no one had been laid (John 19:41), rules out any later confusion about whose body was missing. The "great stone" was likely a disc rolled in a channel.
- **"Sitting opposite the tomb" (v61).** The women saw exactly where he was laid. On Sunday they will not go to the wrong tomb (28:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ has more secret disciples than we are aware of," and he sees providence in a rich man ready to bury him. Guzik stresses that the new tomb rules out a mix-up. JFB refers the burial to its notes on John.

#### The Guard at the Tomb (vv62–66)
- **"After the day of Preparation" (v62).** Friday was "Preparation" for the Sabbath. Matthew quietly notes that the chief priests and Pharisees are doing business with a Gentile governor on the holy day.
- **"That impostor said… 'After three days I will rise'" (v63).** The enemies remember what the disciples forgot (16:21; 17:23; 20:19).
- **"Lest his disciples go and steal him away" (v64).** They fear a fraud and arrange the best evidence against one. Matthew later tells how the stolen-body story was spread (28:11–15). Even Jesus' enemies agreed the tomb was empty; they argued only about why.
- **"You have a guard of soldiers" (v65).** The Greek can mean "you have a guard" (use your temple police) or "take a guard." The word *koustōdia* is the Latin *custodia*, and 28:14 makes the guards answerable to the governor, so most readers think they were Roman.
- **"Sealing the stone and setting a guard" (v66).** A cord across the stone fixed with stamped clay or wax would show any tampering. Stone, seal and soldiers: every human precaution, and none could hold him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ's enemies, even when they have gained their point, are still in fear of losing it again." JFB notes that "while he was yet alive" is their own testimony that Jesus really died, and that God used their seal and watch for his purposes. Guzik observes that the tighter the tomb was secured, the stronger the witness of its emptiness.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everyone in this chapter tries to get rid of Jesus or of the guilt for it: Judas throws down the silver, the priests pass the blame, Pilate washes his hands, the soldiers laugh, the leaders seal the stone. Yet the truth keeps coming from their own mouths: innocent blood, that righteous man, the King of the Jews, he saved others, truly the Son of God. Barabbas walks free while Jesus takes the cross meant for him. The Son cries out in forsakenness so that the curtain can be torn and sinners welcomed into God's presence. The sealed tomb is not the end of the story. It is the setting for the morning that follows.
