*Setting:* "A Psalm of David, when he was in the wilderness of Judah." The wilderness of Judah is the dry, rocky country that drops steeply from the hills around Bethlehem and Hebron down to the Dead Sea, a land of bare ridges, deep ravines and very little water. David knew it well. He hid there from Saul, in the forest of Hereth, the wilderness of Ziph and En-gedi (1 Sam 22:5; 23:14–15; 24:1). He crossed it again years later, fleeing from his son Absalom, when the king and his people went out "toward the wilderness" and reached the Jordan "hungry and weary and thirsty in the wilderness" (2 Sam 15:23; 17:29). Guzik and Henry favor the Saul years. JFB favors Absalom's rebellion, partly because David calls himself "the king" in v11. Either way, he is cut off from the sanctuary in Jerusalem, and that is what hurts most. Henry: "As the sweetest of Paul's epistles were those that bore date out of a prison, so some of the sweetest of David's psalms were those that were penned… in a wilderness." It became one of the best-loved psalms of the early church. Chrysostom, cited by Spurgeon through Guzik, records that the early fathers appointed it to be sung every morning. The psalm moves from thirst (vv1–4) to satisfaction (vv5–8) to confidence about the end of his enemies (vv9–11).

#### My Soul Thirsts for You (vv1–4)
- **"O God, you are my God" (v1).** It is not repetition. The first is *'Elohim*, God in his greatness. The second, *'Eli*, "my God," is personal. Guzik notes that in a world of many gods, this is a declaration of loyalty. Henry: "mine by creation… mine by covenant and my own consent." Everything in the psalm flows from this sentence. Morgan, cited by Guzik, says it is the first thing needed for triumph.
- **"Earnestly I seek you" (v1).** The verb is *shachar*, which is related to the noun for dawn (the word in 57:8, "I will awake the dawn"). The King James translates it "early will I seek thee." Lexicons give both senses, to seek early and to seek eagerly or diligently. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, keeps both. The early church heard "early" and made this its morning psalm. Isaiah uses the same verb: "My soul yearns for you in the night; my spirit within me earnestly seeks you" (Isa 26:9), a parallel JFB notes.
- **"My soul thirsts for you; my flesh faints for you" (v1).** "Soul" and "flesh" together mean the whole person, inside and out (Tyndale notes; JFB). David is physically thirsty in the desert, and his body's thirst becomes a picture of his soul's. The verb "faints" (*kamah*) appears only here in the Old Testament and means to long or yearn so hard it aches. Psalm 42:2 has the same thirst: "My soul thirsts for God, for the living God." Jesus promised, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37).
- **"As in a dry and weary land where there is no water" (v1).** A precise description of the Judean wilderness in the dry season. JFB: "weary" is literally the word, describing "moral destitution" as well as his outward state. The Tyndale notes say David was in a spiritual wasteland as well as a physical one, cut off from God's people and the sanctuary. Henry: "This world is a weary land." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says the land was dry, but there was no desert in David's heart.
- **"So I have looked upon you in the sanctuary, beholding your power and glory" (v2).** David remembers worship in the tabernacle, where the ark was kept, the place of God's presence. The verb "looked upon" (*chazah*) is often used of seeing in a vision. He is not saying he saw God's face. He saw God's power and glory displayed in worship. "So" may mean "in this same longing" or "in the same way," so now in the wilderness he looks for God as he once did in the sanctuary. Henry takes it both ways: to see God again in the sanctuary, and to see him here and now, since "a closet may be turned into a little sanctuary." He adds that David does not say "as I have seen them," but "as I have seen thee."
- **"Because your steadfast love is better than life, my lips will praise you" (v3).** One of the great lines of the Bible. *Chesed* is God's covenant love, faithful and kind. "Life" is *chayyim*, a plural word that can mean life with everything that makes it good. David is in danger of losing his life, and he says God's love is worth more. The Tyndale notes: the highest quality of life is found in relationship with God (4:7). Paul says the same in his own way: "to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Phil 1:21). Brooks, cited by Guzik, notes that people grow tired of life, but no one ever grows tired of God's favor.
- **"So I will bless you as long as I live; in your name I will lift up my hands" (v4).** Lifting the hands was the normal posture of prayer and praise in Israel (28:2; 134:2; 1 Tim 2:8), with open palms turned upward, the posture of someone waiting to receive. "In your name" means in keeping with who God has shown himself to be. JFB: "in praise of Thy perfections."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Gracious souls look down upon the world with a holy disdain and look up to God with a holy desire." On v3 he writes, "It is better, a thousand times, to die in God's favour than to live under his wrath." Guzik lists ways people treat life as their highest value and shows how David ranks God's love above all of them. JFB notes that "thus" in v4 is literally "Truly," and reads vv1–2 as David's desire for "God's perfections as displayed in his worship."

#### My Soul Clings to You (vv5–8)
- **"My soul will be satisfied as with fat and rich food" (v5).** The thirsty man now speaks of a feast. "Fat and rich food" is literally "fat and fatness," the King James "marrow and fatness." In Israel the fat portions of the sacrifices were the best and belonged to God (Lev 3:16: "All fat is the LORD's"). David is saying communion with God satisfies like the richest meal there is. Isaiah uses the same picture for the feast God will one day spread for all peoples (Isa 25:6). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, compares God's love to the richest food.
- **"My mouth will praise you with joyful lips" (v5).** The satisfied soul spills out into song. JFB: "Full spiritual blessings satisfy his desires, and acts of praise fill his thoughts and time."
- **"When I remember you upon my bed, and meditate on you in the watches of the night" (v6).** Israel divided the night into three "watches" of about four hours each (Judg 7:19; Lam 2:19). To think of God through the watches is to lie awake through the long hours of the night, perhaps from fear in the open desert, and to fill them with God. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes how the phrase captures how slowly those hours pass. "Meditate" (*hagah*) is the word of Psalm 1:2, to murmur, mull over, go over again and again. The Tyndale notes: in the darkness, God is the light.
- **"For you have been my help, and in the shadow of your wings I will sing for joy" (v7).** Past help gives present joy. The shadow of God's wings appears for the third time in a few psalms (57:1; 61:4). Here it is not just protection but a place to sing. Guzik thinks the idea of presence fits better here than the idea of shelter.
- **"My soul clings to you" (v8).** The verb is *davaq*, to cling, stick, hold fast. It is the word for a man holding fast to his wife (Gen 2:24), for Ruth clinging to Naomi (Ruth 1:14), and for Israel holding fast to the LORD (Deut 10:20; Josh 23:8). The King James has "My soul followeth hard after thee," which carries the idea of following so closely there is no gap. Chandler, cited by Guzik, describes the sense as being glued together. Meyer, cited by Guzik, says there should be no needless distance between the believer and the Master.
- **"Your right hand upholds me" (v8).** The clinging goes both ways. David holds on to God, and God's right hand holds David. Isaiah: "I will uphold you with my righteous right hand" (Isa 41:10). The believer's grip is weak. God's is not.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls v6 David's "secret communion with God," and v7–8 his "joyful dependence." In his opening overview he says the psalm "speaks its own genuine language" to a devout heart. JFB connects v8 with God's power that sustains him (17:8; 60:5), so that "past favors assure him of future, and hence he presses earnestly near to God." Guzik notes that David here gives thanks for past help instead of crying out for rescue.

#### The King Shall Rejoice in God (vv9–11)
- **"But those who seek to destroy my life shall go down into the depths of the earth" (v9).** The enemies come in only at the end, and briefly. They "seek" David's life, the same verb he used in v1. He seeks God. They seek him. "The depths of the earth" means the grave, the world of the dead (Tyndale notes). JFB reads it as death, "appointed to it" (compare 35:8).
- **"They shall be given over to the power of the sword" (v10).** Literally "they shall pour him out by the hands of the sword," a vivid phrase for dying in battle. Absalom's army was cut down in the forest of Ephraim (2 Sam 18:6–8), and Saul fell by the sword on Gilboa (1 Sam 31:4).
- **"They shall be a portion for jackals" (v10).** The King James has "foxes." The Hebrew word *shu'al* covers both, and JFB and Kidner (cited by Guzik) prefer "jackals," which scavenge battlefields in packs. In Israel, to be left unburied and eaten by animals was the most shameful death imaginable (Jer 22:19). The Tyndale notes link jackals to desolation and abandonment. The men who hunted David in the wilderness will end up as food for its wild animals.
- **"But the king shall rejoice in God" (v11).** David speaks of himself in the third person, as kings often did. If the psalm comes from the Saul years, he is calling himself king by faith, trusting God's promise and Samuel's anointing (1 Sam 16:13). Poole, cited by Guzik, notes the third-person form. If it comes from Absalom's rebellion, he is the king, driven from his throne. Either way, his joy is "in God," not in the throne.
- **"All who swear by him shall exult" (v11).** To "swear by" God was to take oaths in his name, which meant acknowledging him as the true God and binding oneself to him (Deut 6:13; Isa 65:16). It describes those loyal to the LORD. Guzik takes it as those who trust God and put their confidence in him.
- **"For the mouths of liars will be stopped" (v11).** The enemies of Psalms 62 and 63 are liars (62:4). Their mouths will finally be shut. JFB links it directly to 62:4: "treacherous foes shall be for ever silenced." Paul uses the same language of the whole world standing silent before God's judgment: "every mouth may be stopped" (Rom 3:19).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this section "his holy triumph in God over his enemies." JFB says all who reverence God "shall share a glorious part." Kidner, cited by Guzik, observes that the God of steadfast love is also strong in justice.

#### Pointing to Christ
David in the wilderness, thirsty, hunted, longing for God's presence, is a picture of Jesus, who was led by the Spirit "into the wilderness" of Judea for forty days and was hungry (Matt 4:1–2). There he refused bread from the tempter because "man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matt 4:4), the same conviction as "your steadfast love is better than life." On the cross Jesus said, "I thirst" (John 19:28), entering the dry and weary land so that others could drink. Now he stands and cries out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37). He is the true King who "shall rejoice in God," and he spreads the feast of fat things that satisfies the soul (Isa 25:6–8). Those who cling to him find that his right hand upholds them, and that "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28).
