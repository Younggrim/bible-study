*Setting:* Ezekiel, a priest exiled to Babylon in 597 BC, has just seen the glory of the LORD by the Chebar canal in the summer of 593 BC: storm and fire, four living creatures, blazing wheels, and above them a throne with "a likeness with a human appearance" (1:26). Chapter 1 ended with Ezekiel flat on his face, hearing "the voice of one speaking" (1:28). Chapter 2 tells what the voice said. This is the heart of Ezekiel's call, which runs on into 3:15. God commissions him to speak to his own people, warns him they will not listen, tells him three times not to be afraid, and hands him a scroll to eat. The call echoes those of Isaiah (Isa 6) and Jeremiah (Jer 1), but it stands out for how bluntly God describes the audience: some form of the word "rebel" appears again and again, seven times in ten verses. Back in Jerusalem the city still stood, and most exiles expected to go home soon. Ezekiel's first job is to tell them the opposite.

#### Ezekiel's Call (vv1–10)
- **"Son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you" (v1).** This is the first of about ninety-three times God calls Ezekiel "son of man" (*ben 'adam*), and God never addresses him by his own name. In Hebrew, "son of" often means "one belonging to the class of," so "son of man" simply means "human being," "mortal." After the overwhelming vision of chapter 1, the title reminds Ezekiel of the gap between himself and the glory he has seen. He is a man of dust (*'adam* is related to *'adamah*, "ground," Gen 2:7), standing before the living God. Psalm 8:4 uses the phrase the same way: "what is man… and the son of man that you care for him?" Only one other person in the Old Testament is called this by God: Daniel, once (Dan 8:17).
- **"Stand on your feet" (v1).** God does not want Ezekiel to stay face down. Falling before God's glory was right (1:28), but God raises his servant up to listen and to serve. The same happened to Daniel (Dan 10:10–11) and to John on Patmos (Rev 1:17).
- **"As he spoke to me, the Spirit entered into me and set me on my feet" (v2).** Ezekiel could not get up on his own. God commands, and God supplies the strength to obey. The word of God and the Spirit of God come together. This is a small preview of what God will later do for the whole nation in the valley of dry bones: "I will cause breath to enter you… and they stood on their feet" (37:5, 10). "Spirit" here is *ruaḥ*, the same word for the driving Spirit in the vision (1:12, 20). The Hebrew has no article ("spirit entered into me"), but the context makes plain this is God's Spirit, not merely a renewed human spirit. **"And I heard him speaking to me."** Only when he stands can he truly hear.
- **"I send you to the people of Israel, to nations of rebels" (v3).** "Nations" is *goyim*, the word normally used for the Gentile nations, the outsiders. Applied to Israel, it is a deliberate insult: God's own people have become like the pagan nations around them. Some translations smooth it to "a rebellious nation," but the plural is in the Hebrew. **"They and their fathers have transgressed against me to this very day."** Ezekiel will trace that history at length in chapters 16, 20 and 23. Rebellion was not a recent lapse. It ran back through every generation to Egypt.
- **"The descendants also are impudent and stubborn" (v4).** Literally "hard of face and strong of heart." A hard face is a brazen, shameless look that will not blush (Jer 3:3; 5:3). A strong heart here means a will set like stone. In chapter 3 God promises to make Ezekiel's own forehead harder than theirs, "like emery harder than flint" (3:8–9).
- **"You shall say to them, 'Thus says the Lord GOD'" (v4).** This is the prophet's whole authority. He is a messenger, not an author. "Lord GOD" renders *'Adonai YHWH*, Ezekiel's favorite name for God, used over two hundred times in the book. It joins God's title as Master to his covenant name.
- **"Whether they hear or refuse to hear… they will know that a prophet has been among them" (v5).** Success is not measured by results. Ezekiel's task is to speak faithfully. When the warnings come true, they will not be able to say they were not warned (33:33). "They will know" anticipates the phrase that runs through the whole book, more than seventy times: "then they will know that I am the LORD." **"(For they are a rebellious house)"**: "house of rebellion" (*bet meri*) is Ezekiel's nickname for Israel, a play on "house of Israel." It appears about a dozen times in the book, three times in this chapter alone.
- **"Be not afraid of them, nor be afraid of their words, though briers and thorns are with you and you sit on scorpions" (v6).** Three times in one verse: do not fear. Ezekiel's own people will be painful to live among, like thorns that scratch and scorpions that sting. Scorpions were, and are, common in that region. Jeremiah heard the same "do not be afraid of them" at his call (Jer 1:8, 17), and Jesus told his disciples not to fear those who can kill the body (Matt 10:28). **"Nor be dismayed at their looks"**: the hard faces of v4 will glare at him.
- **"You shall speak my words to them, whether they hear or refuse to hear" (v7).** The same phrase as v5, repeated so Ezekiel cannot miss it. The message does not change depending on the audience's mood.
- **"But you, son of man, hear what I say to you. Be not rebellious like that rebellious house" (v8).** The test begins with the prophet. Before Ezekiel can call others to listen, he must listen. The danger for a prophet sent to a rebellious people is to share their rebellion, perhaps by softening the message, or by refusing a hard assignment, as Jonah did.
- **"Open your mouth and eat what I give you" (v8).** The command comes before Ezekiel knows what he will be given. Obedience is required in advance. There is a striking contrast with Eden: Adam ate what God told him not to eat (Gen 3:6). Ezekiel, a "son of Adam," must eat what God tells him to eat, even when it is bitter.
- **"A hand was stretched out to me, and behold, a scroll of a book was in it" (v9).** Ezekiel does not say whose hand. It may be the hand of the figure on the throne, or of one of the cherubim, who had human hands (1:8; 10:7). A scroll was the usual form of a book: sheets of papyrus or leather sewn into a long strip and rolled up. Jeremiah had his prophecies written on a scroll in these same years (Jer 36).
- **"It had writing on the front and on the back" (v10).** Scrolls were normally written on one side only, the inside. Writing on both sides meant the message was so full there was no room for anything more, and nothing for Ezekiel to add. Zechariah sees a flying scroll of curses written on both sides (Zech 5:1–3), and John sees a scroll in God's right hand "written within and on the back," sealed with seven seals (Rev 5:1).
- **"There were written on it words of lamentation and mourning and woe" (v10).** Three words for grief: *qinim*, funeral laments (the Talmud calls the book of Lamentations *Qinot*), *hegeh*, moaning, and *hi*, a cry of woe, a word found only here. This scroll is the content of Ezekiel's ministry for the next seven years. Chapters 1–24 are almost all judgment against Jerusalem, until news of the city's fall arrives (33:21). Only then does the message turn to comfort and restoration (chs 33–48). Chapter 3 tells what happened when he ate it: "it was in my mouth as sweet as honey" (3:3). God's word, even when its message is grief, is sweet to the one who receives it from God's hand.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry stresses that "as Ezekiel had not strength of his own, the Spirit entered into him." On v5, God's word will be honored whether it proves "a savour of life unto life, or of death unto death" (2 Cor 2:16). On v6: "Those who will do any thing to purpose in the service of God, must not fear men." Guzik counts "son of man" as appearing ninety-three times in Ezekiel and distinguishes the prophet's use, which marks his humanity, from Jesus' use, which draws on the heavenly figure of Daniel 7:13. He reads the call to stand as a move from lying prostrate to attentive service, explains *goyim* as a deliberate insult putting Israel on the level of the pagans, and insists that Ezekiel was responsible for faithful delivery, not for results. He also describes eating the scroll as a picture of truly taking in God's word: deliberately, inwardly, and digesting it. JFB agrees that "nations" is the right rendering, since Israel was "altogether heathenized with idolatries." He calls "impudent" literally "hard-faced," says the eating was "not literal eating, but such an appropriation of its unsavory contents" that the message became part of Ezekiel, and explains that the scroll was written on both sides because "so full was God's message of impending woes." On v2 JFB writes: "The divine word is ever accompanied by the Spirit."

#### Pointing to Christ
God calls Ezekiel "son of man" to remind him he is only human. Jesus took that same title as his favorite name for himself, more than eighty times in the Gospels. In his mouth it carried both meanings: he was truly human, a son of Adam, sharing our weakness, and he was also the heavenly "one like a son of man" of Daniel 7:13–14, who receives an everlasting kingdom. Like Ezekiel, Jesus was sent to "a rebellious house" that would not listen (Matt 23:37; John 1:11), and he spoke the Father's words whether they heard or refused (John 12:49–50). Unlike Ezekiel, he never needed the warning "Be not rebellious." He always did what pleased the Father (John 8:29). And where Ezekiel ate a scroll of "lamentation and mourning and woe," Christ bore the woe of that scroll in his own body on the cross, so that the book God now opens before his people, the scroll of Revelation 5 in the hands of the Lamb who was slain, ends not in mourning but in a new song.
