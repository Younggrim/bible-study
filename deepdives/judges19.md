*Setting:* This is the start of the book's second appendix story (chapters 19–21), like the first set "in those days, when there was no king in Israel" (v1). It probably happened early in the judges period: Phinehas, Aaron's grandson, is still serving as priest in 20:28. The first appendix showed Israel's worship going wrong (chapters 17–18). This one shows its moral life collapsing, first in one household, then in one town, and then across the whole nation in civil war. It is among the darkest chapters in the Bible, and it is meant to be. The writer tells it with very little comment and lets the details speak. Its echoes are deliberate: an Israelite town behaves exactly like Sodom (Gen 19), and the town is Gibeah, which will later be the home of Israel's first king, Saul (1 Sam 10:26). Centuries later Hosea could say that Israel had "deeply corrupted themselves as in the days of Gibeah" (Hos 9:9; 10:9).

#### A Levite and His Concubine (vv1–21)
- **"A certain Levite… in the remote parts of the hill country of Ephraim" (v1).** Like the Levite of chapters 17–18, he lives in the hill country of Ephraim and is connected to Bethlehem. He is never named, and neither is anyone else in the chapter. The anonymity makes the story feel like it could have happened anywhere in Israel.
- **"Who took to himself a concubine" (v1).** A concubine was a wife of secondary rank, often without a dowry and with fewer rights. She was still a real wife: the Levite is called "her husband" (v3) and her father his "father-in-law" (v4). The law of Moses tolerated the arrangement, but every concubine story in Genesis and Judges brings trouble (Gen 16; 35:22; Judg 8:31).
- **"His concubine was unfaithful to him" (v2).** The Hebrew verb is the ordinary word for prostitution or sexual unfaithfulness. Some ancient Greek manuscripts read instead that "she became angry with him," and several scholars prefer that, partly because a woman who had committed adultery would hardly flee to her father's house where she could be punished. Either way, the marriage broke and she went home. Four months is a long time. It suggests the separation looked permanent.
- **"To speak kindly to her and bring her back" (v3).** Literally "to speak to her heart," a tender idiom. Boaz speaks this way to Ruth (Ruth 2:13), and God uses it of wooing back unfaithful Israel (Hos 2:14). For a moment the story looks like it may be about reconciliation.
- **"He came with joy to meet him" (v3).** The girl's father is delighted. Hospitality in the ancient Near East was a sacred duty: a host fed and protected his guest, and pressing a guest to stay was a mark of honor.
- **The long goodbye (vv4–9).** Three days, then a fourth, then a fifth. Each morning the Levite rises to leave, and each time his father-in-law says "Strengthen your heart," and "let your heart be merry," and keeps him eating and drinking. The scene is almost comic, and the narrator dwells on it on purpose. The generous hospitality of Bethlehem in Judah is the backdrop against which the next town's cruelty will stand out. But the delay also has a cost. When they finally leave on the fifth afternoon, the day is too far gone to reach home.
- **"Opposite Jebus (that is, Jerusalem)" (v10).** Jebus was about six miles north of Bethlehem. Israel had not taken it. Judah and Benjamin both failed to drive out the Jebusites (1:8, 21), and it stayed a foreign city until David captured it (2 Sam 5:6–9). The note "that is, Jerusalem" was added for readers who knew it by its later name.
- **"We will not turn aside into the city of foreigners" (v12).** The servant wisely suggests stopping before dark (v11). The Levite refuses. He assumes he will be safer among fellow Israelites than among Canaanites. The story will prove him terribly wrong. It is the bitterest irony in the chapter: the foreign city would probably have treated them better.
- **Gibeah or Ramah (vv13–14).** Both were towns of Benjamin, a few miles north of Jerusalem along the ridge road. Gibeah is about four miles from Jebus. Ramah, a little farther, will be Samuel's home town (1 Sam 7:17), and Gibeah will be Saul's.
- **"No one took them into his house" (v15).** Travelers without relatives in a town would sit in the open square near the gate and wait for someone to offer lodging. To leave them sitting there at nightfall was a serious failure. The law repeatedly told Israel to care for the sojourner (Lev 19:33–34; Deut 10:19).
- **"An old man… from the hill country of Ephraim" (v16).** The only person who helps is not a Benjaminite but an outsider, himself "sojourning" in Gibeah. The narrator adds pointedly, "The men of the place were Benjaminites."
- **"I am going to the house of the LORD" (v18).** The Levite may mean the sanctuary at Shiloh (18:31). The Greek translation reads "to my house," which fits v29 ("he entered his house"), and some scholars think that was the original. He mentions his supplies: straw, feed, bread and wine. He is not asking for charity, only for a roof.
- **"Only, do not spend the night in the square" (v20).** The old man's urgency is ominous. He knows his town. Like Lot in Sodom, who "pressed them strongly" to come in (Gen 19:2–3), he brings them inside, feeds the animals and washes their feet.
- **Commentators:** Guzik treats the concubine's leaving as adultery and sees in the Levite's journey a picture of pursuing reconciliation even after unfaithfulness, though he notes concubinage itself fell short of God's design. JFB calls a concubine union "a secondary or inferior relationship" that still had "the true essence of marriage," mentions the reading that she left after quarrels, and says that in Arab towns a leading man "usually comes out and urgently invites the strangers," so Gibeah's coldness showed "the bad character of the people." JFB also observes that passing by Jebus for Gibeah proved the worse choice. Henry outlines the chapter as a contrast between a father's "kind entertainment" in Bethlehem and Gibeah's neglect.

#### Gibeah's Crime (vv22–30)
- **"Worthless fellows" (v22).** Literally "sons of Belial," a Hebrew phrase meaning worthless, lawless men. The same phrase describes Eli's sons (1 Sam 2:12). By New Testament times "Belial" had become a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15).
- **"Bring out the man… that we may know him" (v22).** The words are almost exactly those of the men of Sodom (Gen 19:5). "Know" here means sexual violation. The narrator wants every reader to hear Genesis 19: Israel has become Sodom. In Sodom, angels struck the attackers blind. In Gibeah, no one intervenes.
- **"No, my brothers, do not act so wickedly… do not do this vile thing" (v23).** "Vile thing" is *nebalah*, a strong word for an outrageous act of folly, used of rape elsewhere (Gen 34:7; 2 Sam 13:12). The old man is right to call it that. But his solution is monstrous.
- **"Here are my virgin daughter and his concubine" (v24).** Lot made the same offer (Gen 19:8). The old man holds to the duty of protecting his male guest while offering two women who are also under his roof. The text does not approve. It simply shows how a twisted sense of honor can sacrifice the weak to protect the strong.
- **"The man seized his concubine and made her go out to them" (v25).** "The man" is the Levite. The husband who went to "speak to her heart" now pushes her out the door to save himself. They abused her "all night until the morning."
- **"Fell down at the door of the man's house where her master was" (v26).** The text now calls him "her master," not "her husband." She lies at the door all night. Where he slept, the text does not need to say.
- **"Her hands on the threshold" (v27).** One of the most painful images in Scripture: she had reached the door and could go no further. He opens the doors "to go on his way," apparently intending to leave without her.
- **"Get up, let us be going.' But there was no answer" (v28).** His first words to her since Bethlehem are an order. The Hebrew leaves it unclear whether she was already dead, and the silence is deliberate. The Greek translation adds "for she was dead." The Hebrew lets the question hang over what the Levite does next.
- **"Divided her… into twelve pieces, and sent her throughout all the territory of Israel" (v29).** One piece for each tribe. It was a summons: a grisly call to every tribe to come and act. Saul, from this same Gibeah, will later cut up a pair of oxen and send the pieces through Israel to rally the tribes against the Ammonites (1 Sam 11:7). Saul's message was a threat to the tribes' livestock. The Levite's was a human body.
- **"Such a thing has never happened… consider it, take counsel, and speak" (v30).** The nation is horrified, and rightly so. Some Greek manuscripts have a longer verse in which the Levite tells his messengers what to say. The three verbs set up chapter 20. The question the rest of the book will answer is whether Israel can respond to evil without becoming more evil itself.
- **No one is the hero.** The men of Gibeah are guilty of rape and murder. But the narrator also lets the reader see the Levite's cowardice and coldness, the old man's twisted bargain, and a town that left strangers in the square. The only character with no voice, and the only one with no blame, is the woman. When the Levite tells his story in 20:4–7, he will leave out his own part.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the attackers "ungovernable men" and says their sin exceeded Sodom's, since they lived within reach of the memory of Sodom's ruin. He thinks the old man's offer was wrong though partly excusable, and he sees the concubine's death as judgment on her earlier unfaithfulness: "Lust was her sin, and lust was her punishment." Most modern readers, Guzik included, do not put it that way. Guzik holds every character guilty except the concubine, including the Levite, whose concern for justice came only after he had sacrificed her. JFB says plainly, "Both men ought to have protected the women in the house," and argues that "the want of a regular government warranted an extraordinary step" in sending the pieces. On v30 Henry draws out the three verbs as a model for deciding hard things: "Consider of it, take advice, and speak your minds." The Tyndale notes point to Ephesians 5:25–28 as the measure of a husband: to give himself up for his wife, not to give her up to save himself.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter shows what happens when everyone does what is right in his own eyes: the strong sacrifice the weak to save themselves. The Levite pushed his wife out the door to spare his own life. Christ did the opposite. He is the husband who "loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Eph 5:25), who went out into the night and took the violence on himself so that his bride could be safe. The woman's hands on the threshold of a closed door are a picture of a world with no refuge. Jesus is the door that is opened (John 10:9), and he will not leave anyone who comes to him lying outside.
