*Setting:* Ecclesiastes is the third of the wisdom books traditionally linked to Solomon, after Proverbs and the Song. Where Proverbs gives confident rules for a good life, Ecclesiastes asks the hard questions that the rules do not settle: why the righteous suffer, why the wicked prosper, why everything we build is forgotten, why death takes the wise and the fool alike. The speaker calls himself *Qoheleth*, "the Preacher" or "the Teacher," from the Hebrew *qahal*, "assembly": the one who gathers people and speaks to them. He is "the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (v1), and from the early church through the Reformation readers took him to be Solomon, who reigned about 970–930 BC. Jewish tradition held that Solomon wrote the Song as a young man, Proverbs in middle age and Ecclesiastes when old. Matthew Henry called it Solomon's "recantation-sermon," written after his fall into idolatry (1 Kgs 11). Many modern scholars, including some conservatives, think the book was written later by a wise man who speaks in Solomon's voice to test life's pleasures and achievements through the one man who had them all. They point to late features of the Hebrew, and to the fact that Solomon's name never appears and "I… have been king" (v12) sounds like someone looking back. Guzik and JFB hold to Solomon, and the book's message is the same either way. Its closing words give the key: "Fear God and keep his commandments" (12:13). Until then, the Preacher deliberately looks at life "under the sun," as it appears from the ground, to show how empty it is without God.

#### All Is Vanity (vv1–11)
- **"The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (v1).** *Qoheleth* is a feminine form in Hebrew, used for an office or role, the way "the Speaker" names a role in a parliament. The Greek translation rendered it *Ekklēsiastēs*, "one who addresses an assembly" (from *ekklēsia*, the word the New Testament uses for "church"), which gives the book its English name. "Son of David" can mean any descendant, but the details that follow (vv12, 16; 2:4–9) fit Solomon best.
- **"Vanity of vanities… All is vanity" (v2).** The key word of the book, used about thirty-eight times. *Hebel* means "breath" or "vapor": the puff of steam from a cup, the mist that burns off in the morning. It is the name of Abel, the son whose life was cut short (Gen 4:2). The lexicon gives literal "vapour, breath" and figurative "what is evanescent, unsubstantial, worthless." Different verses lean toward different senses: brief (Ps 144:4), empty and useless (Isa 49:4), baffling, beyond our grasp. "Vanity" in older English meant "emptiness," not pride. "Vanity of vanities" is the Hebrew way of saying "the utmost," like "holy of holies" or "King of kings": the most fleeting of all fleeting things.
- **"What does man gain by all the toil at which he toils under the sun?" (v3).** This is the book's question. "Gain" (*yitron*) is a business word, "profit" or "what is left over," used only in Ecclesiastes. After the books are balanced, what is left? "Under the sun" is the Preacher's phrase for life in this world, seen from below, without looking above. It appears nearly thirty times. Jesus asked the same question from the other side: "What does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his soul?" (Mark 8:36).
- **"A generation goes, and a generation comes, but the earth remains forever" (v4).** The irony is sharp. In the creation story man is put over the earth (Gen 1:28), but the earth outlasts every man who rules it. "Forever" here means "for as long as we can see," not eternity in the full sense.
- **"The sun… hastens to the place where it rises" (v5).** "Hastens" is literally "pants," like a runner gasping as he hurries back to the starting line, only to run the same race again tomorrow. JFB notes the runner image. The same sun that the psalmist sees rejoicing "like a strong man runs its course with joy" (Ps 19:5) here only plods through its rounds.
- **"Around and around goes the wind… All streams run to the sea, but the sea is not full" (vv6–7).** Wind and water go in endless circles. The Preacher is not giving a science lesson, though ancient observers did know that water returns (JFB notes evaporation). His point is that creation is restless and repetitive, always moving and never arriving. "Wind" is *ruach*, which also means "breath" or "spirit," and will return in "a striving after wind" (v14).
- **"All things are full of weariness… the eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing" (v8).** Like the sea that never fills, human desire never fills either. There is always another thing to see and hear. Proverbs says the same of the grave and of human eyes (Prov 27:20). The Tyndale notes draw the link between the sea and the human heart.
- **"There is nothing new under the sun" (vv9–10).** He does not mean there are no new inventions. He means human life, with its loves, ambitions, wars and failures, runs in the same grooves in every age. What looks new is usually something old we have forgotten.
- **"There is no remembrance of former things" (v11).** That is why things seem new: we forget. And we will be forgotten in turn. The Tyndale notes say we cannot count on anyone remembering what we achieved. The section ends where it began, with generations passing (v4), mirroring the very cycles it describes. The Tyndale notes point out that structure.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the book as an argument from the premise of life without God. Its statements are true from that viewpoint, and the Preacher presses them so readers will see that viewpoint's emptiness. He also notes that the book's real question is not whether God exists but whether God matters. JFB is careful that created things are not vain in themselves. They become vain when "put in the place of God and made the end, instead of the means." Henry sums up vv4–7 as "the shortness of human life and the multitude of births and burials," set against a world that keeps turning.

#### The Vanity of Wisdom (vv12–18)
- **"I the Preacher have been king over Israel in Jerusalem" (v12).** Now he begins his report, an experiment in finding meaning. He had the wisdom (1 Kgs 4:29–34), the money and the time to try everything. If he could not find lasting gain under the sun, no one could.
- **"I applied my heart to seek and to search out by wisdom all that is done under heaven" (v13).** "Seek" and "search out" are two different verbs. Deane, cited by Guzik, distinguishes digging deep from surveying wide. He studied human life both ways.
- **"It is an unhappy business that God has given to the children of man to be busy with" (v13).** God is named for the first time, and the first thing said of him is that he gave people a task that wearies them. The urge to understand life is God-given, but under the sun it is never satisfied. The Tyndale notes connect the hard truths of this book with the fall (Gen 3:17–19): the ground is cursed and toil is painful. Paul says God "subjected" creation "to futility" (Rom 8:20). The Greek word there, *mataiotēs*, is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for *hebel* in this book.
- **"All is vanity and a striving after wind" (v14).** A second refrain joins the first. "Striving after wind" may mean "chasing the wind," trying to catch what cannot be held, or "feeding on wind," living on what cannot nourish. Either way the picture is futile effort. JFB notes Maurer's rendering "the pursuit of wind," against the KJV's "vexation of spirit."
- **"What is crooked cannot be made straight, and what is lacking cannot be counted" (v15).** A proverb, perhaps a saying already known. Wisdom can describe the world's twistedness, but it cannot fix it. Some things are bent past straightening, and some gaps cannot be filled. He returns to it in 7:13: "Who can make straight what he has made crooked?" JFB adds that what man cannot straighten, "God, the chief good, alone can do," and points to Isaiah 40:4.
- **"I have acquired great wisdom, surpassing all who were over Jerusalem before me" (v16).** "All who were over Jerusalem" could include the Jebusite and Canaanite kings before David (Gen 14:18; Josh 10:1). If Solomon is speaking, there was only one Israelite king before him. Kidner, cited by Guzik, argues that the claim to surpass predecessors fits Solomon better than any later king.
- **"I applied my heart to know wisdom and to know madness and folly" (v17).** He studied both sides, the wise life and the foolish one, to compare them. JFB defines the "madness" as vaunting extravagance. Chapter 2 tells what happened when he tried folly himself.
- **"For in much wisdom is much vexation, and he who increases knowledge increases sorrow" (v18).** The more one understands the world, the more one sees its brokenness and one's inability to mend it. Ignorance can be a kind of comfort. Knowledge takes that comfort away. The verse does not condemn wisdom (the book praises it, 2:13; 7:12). It says wisdom alone cannot make anyone happy.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Solomon's study of nature and politics "the first example of the vanity of human knowledge," and concludes, "A great scholar cannot be happy unless he be a true saint." JFB reads the "sore travail" of v13 as the laborious study of human affairs, and v18 as the pain of seeing how crooked those affairs are. Guzik, quoting Moffatt through Eaton, puts v18 in modern terms: the more one understands, the more one aches. But Guzik also sees love in the "unhappy business" of v13. God made people restless so they would look beyond the sun to him, as in Augustine's "our heart is restless until it rests in you."

#### Pointing to Christ
Ecclesiastes asks, "What does man gain?" (v3), and under the sun the answer is "nothing that lasts." The New Testament answers from beyond the sun. The one who said "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25) is the one thing that is not *hebel*. "Something greater than Solomon is here" (Matt 12:42), and he is the wisdom of God (1 Cor 1:24, 30). Where the Preacher finds "nothing new," God says in Christ, "Behold, I am making all things new" (Rev 21:5), and "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Cor 5:17). Where the eye is never satisfied, Jesus offers water after which one "will never be thirsty again" (John 4:13–14). And because Christ is risen, Paul can say what Ecclesiastes never could: "your labor is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Cor 15:58). The creation that was "subjected to futility" waits for its freedom (Rom 8:20–21).
