*Setting:* The title reads "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments; according to The Sheminith. A Psalm of David." *Sheminith* means "the eighth." It may mean an eight-stringed instrument or, more likely, a lower register or octave, since 1 Chronicles 15:21 sets lyres "according to the Sheminith" beside harps "according to Alamoth," which probably means a higher one. No occasion is given. Psalms 3–5 were about enemies; Psalm 6 is mostly about the psalmist's own body and soul. He is sick, exhausted, weeping through the nights, and afraid that God is angry with him. Enemies appear only near the end. Some think the illness came as discipline for sin, though the psalm never names any. Since the early church, Psalm 6 has been counted as the first of the seven "penitential psalms" (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143), prayed in seasons of repentance. It moves from anguish to sudden confidence, and one word ties the whole psalm together: "troubled" (*bahal*), used of the psalmist's bones (v2), his soul (v3), and finally his enemies (v10).

#### O LORD, How Long? (vv1–3)
- **"O LORD, rebuke me not in your anger, nor discipline me in your wrath" (v1).** He does not ask God to stop correcting him. He asks God not to correct him *in anger*. There is a difference between a father's discipline and a judge's wrath. Jeremiah prayed the same way: "Correct me, O LORD, but in justice; not in your anger, lest you bring me to nothing" (Jer 10:24). Psalm 38 opens with nearly the same words, and there the psalmist confesses his sin plainly (38:1–4, 18). Here no sin is named, but his fear of God's anger suggests a troubled conscience.
- **"Be gracious to me, O LORD, for I am languishing" (v2).** His only plea is grace, undeserved kindness. "Languishing" (*umlal*) is a word for withering, like a plant drying up in drought (Isa 24:4; Joel 1:10–12). He is fading.
- **"Heal me, O LORD, for my bones are troubled" (v2).** "Bones" stands for the whole body, its frame and strength. "Troubled" (*bahal*) means shaken, terrified, thrown into panic. It is the word used in 2:5, where God terrifies the rebel nations in his fury. Now the psalmist feels that terror in his own bones. Physical illness and spiritual distress are bound together. He asks God to "heal," and the God of Israel had called himself "the LORD, your healer" (Exod 15:26).
- **"My soul also is greatly troubled" (v3).** "Soul" (*nefesh*) is the whole inner self. The trouble has gone from the body to the soul, and "greatly" (*meod*) shows how deep it goes. Jesus echoed this line before the cross: "Now is my soul troubled. And what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour'?" (John 12:27). In the Greek Old Testament the wording is very close.
- **"But you, O LORD—how long?" (v3).** The sentence breaks off. He cannot finish it. "How long" becomes one of the most common cries in the Psalms (13:1–2; 35:17; 79:5; 90:13). It is not unbelief. It is faith that God will act, struggling with the fact that he has not acted yet. The martyrs in heaven still pray it (Rev 6:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry says David dreaded God's wrath more than any outward suffering, and that the soul's pain was worse than the body's because he linked his sickness to God's displeasure. JFB says the psalmist "owns his ill desert in begging a relief from chastisement," compares him to a withering plant, and notes that "how long?" is left unfinished because of strong emotion. Guzik draws a contrast with believers after the cross: God's anger against sin fell on Christ, so his discipline of his children now comes from correcting love, not anger (Heb 12:5–11). He passes on Spurgeon's point that God's chastisements are sweetened by knowing they come from covenant love.

#### Turn, O LORD (vv4–5)
- **"Turn, O LORD, deliver my life" (v4).** "Turn" (*shuvah*) is the word for returning or repenting. God seems to have turned away. The psalmist asks him to turn back. Remember this verb, because it comes back in v10.
- **"Save me for the sake of your steadfast love" (v4).** He gives no reason in himself. His only argument is God's *chesed*, his loyal covenant love. That is the right ground for anyone who has nothing to offer. It is the same plea David makes in Psalm 51: "according to your steadfast love… blot out my transgressions" (51:1).
- **"For in death there is no remembrance of you; in Sheol who will give you praise?" (v5).** *Sheol* is the Old Testament name for the realm of the dead, the grave and what lies beyond it, pictured as a silent, shadowy place below (88:10–12; Isa 38:18–19). This verse is not denying life after death, which the Old Testament only glimpses at this point (16:10–11; 49:15; Job 19:25–27). It is saying that the dead cannot join the worship of God's people on earth, telling of his deeds in the assembly. The psalmist's argument is that if he dies now, God loses a worshipper. Keep me alive, and I will praise you.
- **Light on the grave.** Later revelation fills in what the psalmist could only partly see. Christ "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10). For believers, death is now "to be away from the body and at home with the Lord" (2 Cor 5:8), where praise does not stop.
- **Commentators:** Henry says David appeals to God's compassion, "for thy mercies' sake," and to God's glory, since life gives more opportunity to honor him. JFB says there is "no incredulity as to a future state" here. The contrast is between life on earth and the unseen world of the dead. Guzik agrees that this is not a denial of the afterlife, and quotes Kidner: "David's conscience is uneasy, and he must appeal to grace to temper the discipline he deserves." He also points out the psalms that glimpse rescue from Sheol (16:10; 17:15; 49:15; 73:24).

#### I Am Weary with My Moaning (vv6–7)
- **"I am weary with my moaning" (v6).** He has groaned until he is worn out. Grief itself has become exhausting.
- **"Every night I flood my bed with tears; I drench my couch with my weeping" (v6).** Literally "I make my bed swim." It is deliberate exaggeration, poetry stretching to describe sorrow too big for plain words. Night is often worst for the sick and the grieving. Psalm 4 ended with peaceful sleep; here the psalmist cannot sleep at all. The Psalms are honest that both experiences belong to the life of faith.
- **"My eye wastes away because of grief" (v7).** His eyes are red, swollen and dim from weeping and sleeplessness. In Hebrew thought dim eyes were a sign of failing strength (13:3; 38:10). "Grief" (*ka'as*) can also mean vexation or provocation, sorrow with an edge of indignation.
- **"It grows weak because of all my foes" (v7).** This is the first mention of enemies. Whether they caused the illness, gloated over it, or used it to attack him, their presence made his suffering heavier. People who mock a sick man's faith appear in other psalms too (41:5–9).
- **Commentators:** Henry says David wept so much in private that he "made his bed to swim," and argues that true penitents mourn in secret and that sorrow for sin should run deep. JFB calls v6 a strong figure of speech showing both the abundance and the intensity of his grief, and says "consumed" may mean "has failed," with grief mixed with indignation. Guzik reads the swimming bed as poetic exaggeration, read naturally as poetry, and suggests that David was so low that his enemies no longer roused him to fight.

#### The LORD Has Heard (vv8–10)
- **"Depart from me, all you workers of evil" (v8).** The tone changes all at once. Nothing outward seems to have changed. But the psalmist knows God has heard, and so he turns on those who were waiting for his downfall. "Workers of evil" (*poale aven*) is the same phrase as in 5:5. Jesus took these words on his own lips for the last day: "I will declare to them, 'I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness'" (Matt 7:23; compare Luke 13:27). What the psalmist said to his enemies, the King will say to everyone who refuses him.
- **"For the LORD has heard the sound of my weeping" (v8).** Tears have a voice. He wept without words, and God heard it as prayer. Elsewhere David says, "You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle" (56:8).
- **"The LORD has heard my plea; the LORD accepts my prayer" (v9).** Three times in two verses he says God has heard or accepted. "Plea" (*techinnah*) is a prayer for grace, from the same root as "be gracious" in v2. What he asked for at the start has been granted.
- **"All my enemies shall be ashamed and greatly troubled" (v10).** Here is the third "troubled." In v2 and v3 the psalmist's bones and soul were "greatly troubled"; now that terror passes to his enemies, with the same word, *bahal*, and the same "greatly," *meod*. The shame and panic are turned around.
- **"They shall turn back and be put to shame in a moment" (v10).** "Turn back" is the same verb as "Turn, O LORD" in v4 (*shuv*). He asked God to turn toward him; now his enemies turn back in retreat. **"In a moment"** answers "how long?" in v3. The waiting felt endless, but the reversal, when it comes, is sudden.
- **Commentators:** Henry links v8 to the Lord's own words, "Depart from me, all you workers of iniquity," and says God's mercies should make us more resolved to have nothing to do with sin. On v8 he writes, "Silent tears are not speechless ones," and says God "gives, and does not upbraid with former grants." He reads v10 either as a prayer for the enemies' repentance or a prediction of their fall. JFB says that, sure of being heard, the psalmist suddenly defies his enemies and no longer fears them. Guzik passes on Spurgeon: "Let us learn to think of tears as liquid prayers." He adds that God is not moved by emotion for its own sake but honors a heart that pours itself out to him.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Son of David also had a troubled soul (John 12:27), offered up "prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death," and "he was heard because of his reverence" (Heb 5:7). But where the psalmist prayed "rebuke me not in your anger," Jesus went under the wrath of God for sins not his own, so that those who trust him are disciplined as children and never condemned (Rom 8:1). The psalmist asked to be spared from Sheol so that he could go on praising God. Jesus went down into death and came back, and in him death no longer silences praise. One day he will speak v8 himself to the workers of evil. To those who come weeping to him, he promises that God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Rev 21:4).
