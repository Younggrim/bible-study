*Setting:* Bethel, about 930 BC, at the opening ceremony of Jeroboam's new religion. Chapter 12 ended with the northern kingdom's new king going up to the altar he had built at Bethel, on the feast day "he had devised from his own heart," to offer sacrifices before his golden calf (12:32–33). Chapter 13 picks up at that very moment. An unnamed prophet arrives from Judah and denounces the altar. The first half of the chapter is about God's word against Jeroboam. The second half is one of the strangest stories in the Old Testament: the prophet who delivered that word is deceived by another prophet, disobeys, and is killed by a lion. The two halves belong together. Both are about whether God's word will be obeyed, by a king and by a prophet.

#### A Man of God Confronts Jeroboam (vv1–10)
- **"A man of God came out of Judah by the word of the LORD to Bethel" (v1).** He is never named. "Man of God" is a title for a prophet. He comes from Judah, the very kingdom Jeroboam feared and was trying to wall off. JFB: "Who this prophet was cannot be ascertained." Jewish tradition and the historian Josephus later named him Jadon, possibly the "Iddo the seer" of 2 Chronicles 9:29, but that is only a guess. "By the word of the LORD" comes again and again in this chapter, at least seven times in the Hebrew. It is the chapter's theme.
- **"O altar, altar" (v2).** He speaks not to the king but to the altar, as though the stones could hear better than Jeroboam. Henry: "In threatening the altar, the prophet threatens the founder and worshippers."
- **"A son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name" (v2).** This is one of the most remarkable predictions in Scripture. About three centuries later, King Josiah of Judah did exactly this. He tore down the Bethel altar, took bones from the tombs, burned them on it to defile it, and spared only the tomb of this very prophet (2 Kgs 23:15–18). JFB counts "three hundred sixty years," following an older chronology. Modern dates put it nearer 300 years, from about 930 to about 622 BC. The Tyndale notes compare Isaiah naming Cyrus long before he lived (Isa 44:28; 45:1). Some scholars think the name was added after the fact. The narrator plainly presents it as a true prophecy, and Kings repeatedly shows God's word spoken long before and fulfilled exactly (compare Josh 6:26 with 1 Kgs 16:34).
- **"He shall sacrifice on you the priests of the high places... and human bones shall be burned on you" (v2).** Burning human bones on an altar made it permanently unclean (Num 19:16). The priests who "make offerings" on it would one day become the offering.
- **"This is the sign... the altar shall be torn down, and the ashes that are on it shall be poured out" (v3).** The distant prophecy is backed by a sign that same day. The law required the ashes from a true altar to be carried out carefully to a clean place (Lev 6:10–11). The Tyndale notes say ashes spilled on the ground made the altar and its sacrifices unclean.
- **"Jeroboam stretched out his hand from the altar, saying, 'Seize him.' And his hand... dried up" (v4).** "Dried up" means withered, stiff and useless. The hand that pointed at God's messenger is frozen in place. Guzik notes that God judged Jeroboam at the very point of his sin. At the same moment the altar splits and the ashes spill out (v5), just as foretold.
- **"Entreat now the favor of the LORD your God, and pray for me" (v6).** "Your God," not "my God." Jeroboam wants his hand back, not God. Guzik observes that wanting something from God is not the same as repentance. The prophet prays, and God heals him. The Tyndale notes say this mercy should have reminded Jeroboam he could still turn and serve God rightly. He doesn't (v33).
- **"Come home with me, and refresh yourself, and I will give you a reward" (v7).** If force fails, try friendship. In the ancient Near East, eating at a king's table meant approval and alliance (compare 2 Sam 9:7). The Tyndale notes make the point. Had the prophet accepted, it would have looked as if Judah's prophet endorsed Bethel's altar.
- **"If you give me half your house, I will not go in with you" (v8).** A brave and clear refusal, even with a large reward on offer. The reason: "You shall neither eat bread nor drink water nor return by the way that you came" (v9). God had told him to treat Bethel as an unclean place. JFB says the ban was "because all the people had become apostates from the true religion." Taking a different road home may have been so no one could find him or follow him, or simply as a sign that the message was final. Either way, it was his clear instruction.
- **"So he went another way" (v10).** So far he has obeyed perfectly.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that God sent a messenger from the south, perhaps because there was no faithful voice left in the north, and through Poole that God's kindness in healing was meant to lead Jeroboam to repent. Henry adds a line that prepares for what follows: "Those have not learned self-denial, who cannot forbear one forbidden meal."

#### The Prophet's Disobedience (vv11–34)
- **"Now an old prophet lived in Bethel" (v11).** We are not told much about him, and that is part of the puzzle. He seems to be a genuine prophet, since God later speaks through him (v20). But he lives at Bethel and has said nothing about Jeroboam's calves. His sons were apparently at the ceremony. JFB: "If this were a true prophet, he was a bad man." Clarke, cited by Guzik, thinks he had been faithful once and had fallen.
- **"Found him sitting under an oak" (v14).** The man of God had stopped to rest. He was still near Bethel, perhaps tired and hungry after a long walk and no food. Clarke, via Guzik, suggests he was faint from fasting. He had refused the king's palace, but he had not gone far.
- **"I also am a prophet as you are, and an angel spoke to me by the word of the LORD" (v18).** The man of God repeats God's command (vv16–17), but the old prophet has a counterclaim. He is a fellow prophet, an older one, and claims an angel brought him new orders. "But he lied to him." The narrator states it flatly. Why did he lie? The text does not say. Suggestions include wanting to keep up his own standing, wanting the honor of hosting the famous visitor, testing him, or simply wanting to prove that the man of God was no better than himself. JFB thinks self-interest drove it. Guzik says the reason is not fully explained.
- **"So he went back with him and ate bread in his house and drank water" (v19).** He had refused a king's reward but gave way to a fellow prophet. Henry says it well: "Believers are most in danger of being drawn from their duty by plausible pretences of holiness." Paul warns: "Even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed" (Gal 1:8). The man of God had a direct word from God. He should have held to it unless God himself clearly changed it. Guzik, citing Morgan, notes that God does not contradict himself.
- **"The word of the LORD came to the prophet who had brought him back" (v20).** Now the real word comes, through the liar. God sometimes speaks through unworthy messengers, as he did through Balaam (Num 22–24) and the high priest Caiaphas (John 11:49–52).
- **"Your body shall not come to the tomb of your fathers" (v22).** Being buried with one's family was deeply important in Israel. Jacob and Joseph both demanded it (Gen 49:29; 50:25). To die away from home and lie in a stranger's tomb was a disgrace. Guzik passes on Wiseman's note on this.
- **"A lion met him on the road and killed him" (v24).** Lions lived in the thickets of Israel and Judah in Old Testament times (Judg 14:5; 1 Sam 17:34; 2 Kgs 17:25). JFB notes the area near Bethel was known for wild animals (2 Kgs 2:24). But this was no ordinary attack. "The donkey stood beside it; the lion also stood beside the body." A lion that kills but does not eat, a donkey that does not run, and passers-by who are not attacked: everyone could see the hand of God (v28). JFB cites "the undevoured carcass, the untouched ass" as signs of it.
- **Why such a harsh judgment?** Readers have long found this hard. The man of God was deceived, while the liar goes unpunished, and Jeroboam, the main sinner, keeps his throne for now. Several things help. First, the man of God's whole mission was to show that God's word must be obeyed exactly. His disobedience undercut his own message, and his death proved it. Second, he was a prophet with a direct command, and those who carry God's word are held to a higher standard (James 3:1). Guzik connects it to judgment beginning "at the household of God" (1 Pet 4:17). Third, the story becomes a warning to Jeroboam and all Bethel: if God would not overlook this from his own prophet, how much less from the king. The text does not say the man of God lost his soul. The Tyndale notes stress the seriousness of disobeying a clear command. Some, like Clarke, compare it to the "sin that leads to death" (1 John 5:16), a physical death as discipline.
- **"Alas, my brother!" (v30).** The old prophet goes and gets the body, brings it back, and lays it in his own tomb. The Tyndale notes say such tombs were usually cut into the soft limestone near the town. He mourns with the traditional cry for the dead (Jer 22:18). Whatever his earlier motives, his grief seems real. Guzik suggests he recognized a courage he himself lacked.
- **"Lay my bones beside his bones" (v31).** He wants to be buried with the man of God. JFB suggests he hoped his bones would not be disturbed, which is exactly what happened. When Josiah came and burned the bones from the tombs at Bethel, he saw this grave and said, "Let him be; let no man move his bones." So the old prophet's bones were spared too (2 Kgs 23:17–18).
- **"The saying that he called out... shall surely come to pass" (v32).** The liar becomes a witness. He now confirms that the prophecy against Bethel's altar is true. "The cities of Samaria" is the name the region later took from Omri's capital, built about fifty years after this (16:24). The Tyndale notes say this shows the writer looking back from a later time.
- **"After this thing Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way" (v33).** After a withered hand, a split altar, a healing and a public death by lion, nothing changed. He ordained anyone who wanted to be a priest: literally "whoever wished, he filled his hand," the old phrase for ordaining priests (Exod 28:41). Guzik passes on Trapp's image of God's hammers beating on cold iron.
- **"This thing became sin to the house of Jeroboam, so as to cut it off and to destroy it" (v34).** The promise of a lasting dynasty (11:38) is lost. Chapter 14 announces his family's end, and chapter 15 shows it fulfilled (15:29–30).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "They that yield to him as a tempter, will be terrified by him as a tormentor," pointing to the old prophet who first tempted and then pronounced judgment. Also, "God warns all whom he employs, strictly to observe their orders." Guzik lists what made the lie convincing: the old man seemed respectable, shared the same calling, claimed an angel, used the Lord's name and offered only simple food. He passes on Meyer's counsel that a direct command from God must not be traded for another believer's suggestion. JFB thinks the old man's talk of an angel was meant "to conceal a double meaning."

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter shows how hard it is for even a faithful messenger to keep God's word perfectly. The man of God faced down a king and then fell to a fellow prophet's lie. Christ is the faithful prophet who never wavered. In the wilderness he was hungry, alone and offered bread with Scripture quoted at him, and he answered, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matt 4:4). He said, "My food is to do the will of him who sent me" (John 4:34). And the old prophet, a sinner who lay down beside the righteous man's bones and was spared when judgment came, is a strange picture of the gospel itself. Those who are joined to the true Man of God, buried with him in his death (Rom 6:4), are kept safe in the day of judgment.
