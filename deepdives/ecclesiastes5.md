*Setting:* The Preacher has been surveying life in society: oppression with no comforter, envy behind hard work, the lonely man who works with no one to leave his money to, and the fickle crowd (ch 4). Now he turns, for the first time, to direct commands. Chapter 5 opens with the most practical section in the book so far: how to come before God (vv1–7). Its center is "God is in heaven and you are on earth" (v2) and its end is "God is the one you must fear" (v7). Then he turns back to money, the thing people most often put in God's place. Wealth does not satisfy (vv10–12), it can vanish overnight (vv13–17), and no one takes it with them. But the chapter ends with the fullest of the book's "enjoyment" passages: the power to enjoy what one has is itself "the gift of God" (vv18–20). In Hebrew Bibles 5:1 is counted as 4:17, so the verse numbers there run one behind. For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### Fear God (vv1–7)
- **"Guard your steps when you go to the house of God" (v1).** Literally "guard your foot." Watch where you are going and how. The "house of God" is the temple in Jerusalem (1 Kgs 8). JFB links the phrase to Moses and Joshua, told to remove their sandals on holy ground (Exod 3:5; Josh 5:15). The Preacher has said little about God so far. Now the first thing he tells his readers to do is to come to God carefully.
- **"To draw near to listen is better than to offer the sacrifice of fools" (v1).** "Listen" (*shama'*) in Hebrew means both "hear" and "obey," as in "Hear, O Israel" (Deut 6:4). Kidner, cited by Guzik, makes the point. Samuel told Saul: "to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams" (1 Sam 15:22). The "sacrifice" here (*zevach*) was the offering that ended in a meal shared by the worshipers. Eaton, cited by Guzik, notes it could slide into a careless party. The fool goes through the motions of worship and does not even know he is doing wrong. JFB: "The warning is against mere ceremonial self-righteousness." The prophets said the same (Isa 1:11–17; Hos 6:6; Amos 5:21–24).
- **"Be not rash with your mouth… for God is in heaven and you are on earth. Therefore let your words be few" (v2).** This is the heart of the section. The distance between Creator and creature should make us careful with words. Jesus warned against heaping up "empty phrases… for they think that they will be heard for their many words" (Matt 6:7). The prophets of Baal shouted all day. Elijah prayed a few sentences and fire fell (1 Kgs 18:26–38). Guzik draws that contrast.
- **"For a dream comes with much business, and a fool's voice with many words" (v3).** A proverb. Just as a busy, worried day produces restless dreams, so a fool produces a stream of words. Proverbs says, "When words are many, transgression is not lacking" (Prov 10:19). The Tyndale notes point to that verse.
- **"When you vow a vow to God, do not delay paying it… Pay what you vow" (v4).** A vow was a voluntary promise to give God something, often if he answered a prayer: Jacob at Bethel (Gen 28:20–22), Hannah for a son (1 Sam 1:11). This verse is nearly a quotation of the Law: "If you make a vow to the LORD your God, you shall not delay fulfilling it" (Deut 23:21). The Tyndale notes say vows were optional, but once made they were binding. Jephthah's rash vow is the Bible's darkest example of speaking too quickly (Judg 11:30–40).
- **"It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not pay" (v5).** Deuteronomy says the same: "if you refrain from vowing, you will not be guilty of sin" (Deut 23:22). No one has to make promises to God. But a promise to God is not a mood. It is a debt. Jesus taught that his followers should be people whose plain yes and no can be trusted (Matt 5:33–37).
- **"Do not say before the messenger that it was a mistake" (v6).** "Messenger" (*mal'akh*) is also the Hebrew word for "angel." Eaton, cited by Guzik, notes that Hebrew draws no line between the two. Most likely it means a priest or temple official, whom Malachi calls "the messenger of the LORD of hosts" (Mal 2:7). The worshiper who failed to pay a vow might come and say, "It was a mistake," as if it were a slip of the tongue. The Law did provide a sin offering for rash, unintended vows (Lev 5:4–6), but this is the man who meant it and changed his mind. Some ancient translations read "before God." JFB takes it as the priest, and applies it further to Christ, "the angel of the covenant."
- **"Why should God be angry at your voice and destroy the work of your hands?" (v6).** Careless words with God are not harmless. Ananias and Sapphira lied about what they had promised and died (Acts 5:1–11).
- **"For when dreams increase and words grow many, there is vanity; but God is the one you must fear" (v7).** The section ends where it was heading. Empty religious talk is *hebel*, vapor. The answer is the fear of God: reverence, awe, taking him seriously. The Tyndale notes trace it to "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (Prov 1:7). It is also where the whole book ends (12:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the section as the remedy for all the vanity so far: true religion, rightly practiced in hearing the word, prayer, vows, and not trusting dreams. JFB says "hear" means "to be ready to hear (obey)," and that obedience to God's moral law matters more than ceremony. Guzik calls broken vows a sin God's people often overlook, and urges three things: don't vow hastily, keep the vows you make, and confess those you have broken. He passes on J. Edwin Orr's advice to keep public prayers short.

#### The Vanity of Wealth and Honor (vv8–20)
- **"If you see in a province the oppression of the poor and the violation of justice… do not be amazed at the matter" (v8).** "Province" (*medinah*) is a district under a governor. The Preacher is a realist. In any government, injustice will happen. Do not be shocked by it.
- **"For the high official is watched by a higher, and there are yet higher ones over them" (v8).** This is read in two ways. On one reading, each official watches the one below him for his own gain, protecting his share, and the poor are squeezed at every level. The Tyndale notes favor this reading as fitting the Preacher's realism, and Guzik reads it the same way, as bureaucracy that enables oppression. On the other, the officials check each other, and the "higher" over them all is God, who sees and will judge. JFB holds the second. Samuel had warned Israel what kings would take (1 Sam 8:11–18).
- **"But this is gain for a land in every way: a king committed to cultivated fields" (v9).** This verse is very hard to translate, and versions differ widely. The ESV takes it as a positive picture: a land does well when its king cares for agriculture, the source of everyone's food. Others read it as "even the king profits from the field," meaning everyone, top to bottom, takes a cut from what the farmer grows. The Tyndale notes give the second. Clarke, cited by Guzik, notes that even the king depends on the field. Kidner, also cited by Guzik, says the Preacher offers no utopian fix.
- **"He who loves money will not be satisfied with money" (v10).** Here is the turn to wealth. Money is like the sea of 1:7 that is never full. Paul says "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils," and those who crave it "pierce themselves with many pangs" (1 Tim 6:9–10). Notice he says "loves money." The problem is not having it but loving it. The Tyndale notes add that people are spiritual as well as physical, so possessions cannot fill them.
- **"When goods increase, they increase who eat them" (v11).** More income means more expenses and more people living off it: staff, family, advisers, tax collectors. Solomon's own court ate an enormous daily ration (1 Kgs 4:22–23). Trapp, cited by Guzik, pictures the hangers-on. In the end the owner's only advantage is to look at it.
- **"Sweet is the sleep of a laborer, whether he eats little or much, but the full stomach of the rich will not let him sleep" (v12).** The working man is tired and sleeps well. The rich man lies awake. "Full stomach" can be "abundance" more widely: he is kept awake by too much food, or by worry over too much money. Either way the laborer is better off.
- **"Riches were kept by their owner to his hurt, and those riches were lost in a bad venture" (vv13–14).** A "grievous evil," literally "a sick evil." He saved and hoarded, and one bad deal wiped it all out. "Bad venture" is literally "an evil business," the same phrase as the "unhappy business" of 1:13. Now he has a son and nothing to give him.
- **"As he came from his mother's womb he shall go again, naked as he came" (v15).** Job said this as he lost everything: "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return" (Job 1:21). Psalm 49:17 says the same, and JFB cites it. Paul: "we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world" (1 Tim 6:7). Even if the money is not lost in a bad deal, death strips it away.
- **"What gain is there to him who toils for the wind?" (v16).** The book's question again (1:3). Working only for wealth is chasing wind, and the Tyndale notes say it breeds frustration and anger.
- **"All his days he eats in darkness in much vexation and sickness and anger" (v17).** Eating in darkness means a life without joy. Some think it also means literally: too stingy to light a lamp. He has money and no happiness, and the strain shows in his body and temper. JFB reads "wrath" as fretfulness.
- **"What I have seen to be good and fitting is to eat and drink and find enjoyment in all the toil… the few days of his life that God has given him, for this is his lot" (v18).** The fourth enjoyment passage (2:24; 3:12–13, 22), and the fullest so far. "Good and fitting" is literally "good, which is beautiful," the same word as "beautiful in its time" (3:11). Over and over in these verses God is the giver: the days of life, the lot, the wealth, the power to enjoy it. "Lot" (*cheleq*) is the share God hands out, as land was shared among the tribes.
- **"Everyone also to whom God has given wealth and possessions and power to enjoy them… this is the gift of God" (v19).** Here is the key. Having money is one gift. Being able to enjoy it is another, and many who have the first lack the second. Chapter 6 opens with exactly that case (6:1–2). Paul tells the rich to set their hope "on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy" (1 Tim 6:17).
- **"He will not much remember the days of his life because God keeps him occupied with joy in his heart" (v20).** The person who receives life as a gift does not brood over how short it is. "Keeps him occupied" may mean God "answers" him with joy. The same Hebrew verb can mean "answer" or "occupy," and JFB notes the alternatives. Either way, joy given by God fills his days.
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline lists what wealth cannot do: it does not satisfy (v10), brings no lasting gain (v11), causes restlessness (v12), often brings harm (v13), does not last (v14), and "must be left behind when we die" (vv15–16). His answer is "the comfortable use of that which God has given us," with an eye on the Giver. JFB on v18–19: the portion is God's gift, "to be used, not abused," and God's own share must not be held back from it. Guzik reads vv18–20 as practical advice under the sun: make the best of things and enjoy what is in hand. He notes that the New Testament adds what the Preacher lacked: treasure stored in heaven through generous giving (Matt 6:19–21).

#### Pointing to Christ
"God is in heaven and you are on earth" (v2). The Preacher's distance is real, and it calls for few words and deep reverence. The gospel does not cancel that reverence, but it closes the distance. The God in heaven came to earth. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). Because of him, believers can "with confidence draw near to the throne of grace" (Heb 4:16), and Jesus taught his disciples to say, "Our Father in heaven" (Matt 6:9). Every human vow is fragile, but Christ is the faithful one: "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20). The rich man goes out "naked as he came" (v15). Jesus, who was rich, became poor and was stripped naked on a cross, "so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). And the joy that God "keeps him occupied with" (v20) is what Jesus came to give: "that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be full" (John 15:11).
