*Setting:* Proverbs is Israel's book of wisdom: how to live well in God's world, day to day. The title names Solomon, who reigned about 971–931 BC and "spoke 3,000 proverbs" (1 Kgs 4:32). But the book itself names other contributors too: "the wise" (22:17; 24:23), Agur (30:1) and King Lemuel's mother (31:1). Some of Solomon's proverbs were collected by Hezekiah's scribes more than two centuries after him (25:1). So the book grew over time, and it probably reached its final form in Hezekiah's day or later. Chapters 1–9 are an introduction. They are not short sayings but longer talks, mostly a father speaking to his son, urging him to choose wisdom before he meets the short proverbs of chapters 10 onward. Chapter 1 sets out the book's purpose, states its motto, warns against a gang of violent men and then lets Wisdom herself speak in the streets.

#### The Beginning of Knowledge (vv1–7)
- **"The proverbs of Solomon" (v1).** "Proverb" is *mashal*, a word with a wide range. It can mean a short wise saying, but also a comparison, a parable, a taunt or a poem. The root idea may be "likeness": a proverb sets two things side by side so that one sheds light on the other ("Like cold water to a thirsty soul, so is good news from a far country," 25:25). Proverbs are not promises or laws. They describe how life usually works under God, and they take wisdom to apply (26:4–5 gives two opposite proverbs back to back on purpose).
- **"Son of David, king of Israel" (v1).** The book sits in the world of a royal court. Solomon asked God for wisdom to govern (1 Kgs 3:9), and kings across the ancient Near East collected instruction for their sons and officials. Egypt has several such books, and one of them, the *Instruction of Amenemope*, is very close to Proverbs 22:17–24:22. Most scholars think Israel's wise men knew and reworked material like this, but always under "the fear of the LORD."
- **A pile of words for wisdom (vv2–6).** "Wisdom" (*chokmah*) is skill. The same word describes the craftsmen who built the tabernacle (Exod 31:3, 6). In Proverbs it is skill at living. Around it cluster "instruction" (*musar*, discipline or training, sometimes painful correction), "insight," "wise dealing," "prudence" (shrewdness, the ability to see through a trap) and "discretion." The heaping up of terms shows wisdom is many-sided.
- **"In righteousness, justice, and equity" (v3).** Wisdom in the Bible is never mere cleverness. It is moral. A clever crook is a fool in Proverbs' terms.
- **"To give prudence to the simple, knowledge and discretion to the youth" (v4).** The "simple" (*peti*) is a key figure in the book. The word is usually linked to a root meaning "open," and the simple are open-minded in the bad sense: gullible, easily led, they "believe everything" (14:15). They are not yet fools, just undecided. The same root appears as "entice" in v10, a sharp wordplay. The open mind is exactly the mind that gets enticed.
- **"Let the wise hear and increase in learning" (v5).** The book is not only for beginners. The wise get wiser by listening (9:9).
- **"A proverb and a saying, the words of the wise and their riddles" (v6).** "Riddles" (*chidot*) are puzzling sayings that need thought, the same word used for the queen of Sheba's "hard questions" (1 Kgs 10:1). Proverbs teaches its readers how to read proverbs.
- **"The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (v7).** This is the motto of the whole book, repeated at the end of the introduction (9:10) and echoed in Job 28:28 and Psalm 111:10. "Fear" (*yirʾah*) here is not terror but reverent awe that takes God seriously and trusts and obeys him (Deut 10:12–13). "Beginning" (*reshit*) can mean the first step or the chief and controlling part. Both meanings fit. Without God at the start, knowledge goes wrong. Note too that it is "the LORD," God's covenant name *YHWH*. Israel's wisdom starts with the God who made a covenant with them, not with a general idea of a higher power.
- **"Fools despise wisdom and instruction" (v7).** The opposite of fearing God is not ignorance but contempt. "Fools" here is *ʾevilim*, the stubborn, morally thick kind of fool.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those know enough who know how to fear God." He says the proverbs "are of use to all, but are designed especially for the simple," the young and untaught. Guzik stresses that proverbs are general truths, not guarantees, and separates knowledge (gathering facts) from wisdom (using them rightly). On "beginning" he follows Derek Kidner, who calls it the first and controlling principle. JFB glosses the "simple" as "one easily led to good or evil" and "beginning" as "first part, foundation." The Tyndale notes picture the simple as an empty jar that wisdom and folly compete to fill, and that matches the lexicon well.

#### The Enticement of Sinners (vv8–19)
- **"Hear, my son, your father's instruction, and forsake not your mother's teaching" (v8).** This is the first of about a dozen talks in chapters 1–9 that start with "my son." In Egyptian instructions it is almost always the father who speaks. Proverbs names the mother too (6:20; 31:1). "Teaching" is *torah*, the word used for God's law. Faithful parents' instruction passes on God's ways in the home (Deut 6:6–7). The address is to a son because it pictures a young man about to enter adult life, but the wisdom is for all.
- **"A graceful garland for your head and pendants for your neck" (v9).** Wisdom is pictured as jewelry. It makes a person attractive and honored, the way a victor's wreath or a gold chain of office did (Gen 41:42).
- **"My son, if sinners entice you, do not consent" (v10).** The first danger the father names is not sex or laziness but peer pressure to join violent crime. "Entice" is *patah*, the root behind "simple." He also expects his son to be tempted. The question is not whether, but how he will answer.
- **The gang's pitch (vv11–14).** The father puts their words in their mouths so the son can hear how it sounds. "Let us lie in wait for blood; let us ambush the innocent without reason." They offer excitement, wealth ("we shall fill our houses with plunder") and belonging: **"throw in your lot among us; we will all have one purse."** That last line is the hook. A young man is offered a crew to belong to.
- **"Like Sheol let us swallow them alive" (v12).** Sheol is the grave, the realm of the dead, pictured as a mouth that is never full (27:20; 30:16). The gang casts itself as death. JFB: they mean to "utterly destroy the victim and traces of the crime."
- **"Do not walk in the way with them" (v15).** "Way" and "path" are the book's master picture. Life is a road, and there are only two (4:18–19; Ps 1:6).
- **"Their feet run to evil, and they make haste to shed blood" (v16).** Isaiah uses almost the same words of Israel's sin (Isa 59:7), and Paul quotes the line in Romans 3:15 as part of his proof that "none is righteous." What the father describes as a gang is, in the end, a picture of every human heart without God.
- **"In vain is a net spread in the sight of any bird" (v17).** This proverb is debated. The most common reading is that a bird that sees the net spread will avoid it, yet these men walk straight into a trap they ought to see. Another reading is that the bird sees the scattered grain but not the net, as the gang sees only the plunder. Either way, the next verse is clear.
- **"These men lie in wait for their own blood" (v18).** The same words as v11, turned around. They think they are ambushing others. They are ambushing themselves (Ps 7:15–16; 9:15).
- **"Greedy for unjust gain; it takes away the life of its possessors" (v19).** The root sin underneath is greed. Ill-gotten gain kills the one who holds it.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Both the father and the mother must do all they can for the good education of their children." Guzik, drawing on Duane Garrett, notes that the gang's real offer is belonging and easy money, and that the father teaches his son to hear what the wicked *mean*, not just what they say. He adds the irony that Solomon's own marriages later became the trap that caught him. JFB reads v17 to mean that people warned of danger should flee it as birds "instinctively avoid visibly spread nets."

#### The Call of Wisdom (vv20–33)
- **"Wisdom cries aloud in the street" (v20).** Now Wisdom herself speaks, pictured as a woman. The Hebrew noun *chokmah* is feminine, which suits the picture, and in chapter 9 she will stand opposite "the woman Folly" (9:13). The form here is plural, *chokmot*, "wisdoms." JFB reads it as the plural of fullness: wisdom in all its greatness.
- **Where she speaks (vv20–21).** Not in a temple or a school but in "the street," "the markets," "the head of the noisy streets" and "the entrance of the city gates," where business was done and courts sat (Ruth 4:1). Wisdom is not hidden or kept for an elite. She shouts where ordinary people live and work, competing with the gang's voice.
- **"How long, O simple ones… scoffers… fools?" (v22).** There are three kinds of people who lack wisdom, and they get worse in order. The **simple** (*petayim*) are naïve and drifting, and they "love being simple." The **scoffers** (*letsim*) mock wisdom openly (Ps 1:1, "the seat of scoffers"). The **fools** (*kesilim*) are dull and set in their ways and "hate knowledge." The simple can still be taught. The longer they wait, the more they become the others.
- **"If you turn at my reproof, behold, I will pour out my spirit to you" (v23).** "Turn" is *shuv*, repent. "Spirit" (*ruach*) can mean breath, thoughts or spirit. Here it is paired with "my words," so it may mean "pour out my thoughts." But the language echoes the prophets' promise of the Spirit poured out (Joel 2:28; Isa 44:3). Wisdom offers far more than advice. She offers herself.
- **Wisdom sounds like a prophet (vv24–25).** "I have called and you refused… stretched out my hand and no one has heeded" is the language of the prophets and of God himself (Isa 65:2, 12; Jer 7:13). Paul quotes Isaiah 65:2, "All day long I have held out my hands," of God's patience with Israel (Rom 10:21).
- **"I also will laugh at your calamity" (v26).** This is hard to read. It is not cruelty. It is the laughter of justice when the scoffers' own scoffing comes back on them, like God laughing at the nations that rage against him (Ps 2:4). The "storm" and "whirlwind" (v27) picture sudden disaster.
- **"Then they will call upon me, but I will not answer" (v28).** There is a "too late." People who refuse wisdom in calm days may find that in the crisis the opportunity is gone (Isa 55:6, "while he may be found"). This is not a statement about anyone being shut out of God's mercy if they repent. It describes a fool who wants rescue from consequences, not wisdom itself.
- **"Because they… did not choose the fear of the LORD" (v29).** The fear of the LORD is something people choose. The motto of v7 returns. Rejecting wisdom is rejecting God.
- **"They shall eat the fruit of their way" (v31).** Judgment here is not arbitrary. Folly carries its own consequences. God lets people have what they chose (Rom 1:24, 26, 28; Gal 6:7).
- **"The simple are killed by their turning away… but whoever listens to me will dwell secure" (vv32–33).** "Turning away" (*meshuvah*) is from the same root as "turn" in v23. Turn toward Wisdom and live, or turn away and die. The chapter ends on an open door: "whoever listens to me will dwell secure… without dread of disaster."
- **Commentators:** Henry identifies the speaker with Christ: "Christ himself is Wisdom, is Wisdoms, for in him are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3). On v23 he hears a promise of "the Spirit in abundance, rivers of living water." On v26 he writes, "Now God pities their folly, but he will then laugh at their calamity." JFB takes "my spirit" as "whether of wisdom personified, or of Christ, a divine agent," and reads vv26–27 to mean that God will refuse help and add "derision." Guzik, following Kidner, stresses that Wisdom calls in public to ordinary people, and he traces the line from simple to fool to scoffer. Most modern scholars read Wisdom in chapter 1 as a personification, a figure of speech for God's wisdom, rather than as a separate divine person. The fuller Christian reading leans more on chapter 8.

#### Pointing to Christ
The father pleads with his son to choose the right road, and Wisdom calls in the streets for the simple to turn. In the Gospels Jesus speaks the way Wisdom does here. He stands up in public and cries out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37–39), and he promises the Spirit. He also weeps over a city that would not come to him: "How often would I have gathered your children together… and you were not willing!" (Luke 13:34). Paul calls Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24), in whom "are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3). Fearing the LORD starts there, and whoever listens to him will "dwell secure."
