*Setting:* Right after the disaster at Aphek (ch 4), around 1100–1050 BC. Israel's army has been routed, Eli and his sons are dead, and the ark of the covenant, the throne of the LORD of hosts, has been carried off by the Philistines. Phinehas's widow named her baby Ichabod, "The glory has departed" (4:21). From the human side it looks as if Israel's God has lost. Chapter 5 follows the ark into enemy country and shows the opposite. Israel is not mentioned once. The LORD needs no army and no priest to defend his honor. He fights alone, in the enemy's own temple and cities.

#### The Philistines and the Ark (vv1–12)
- **"They brought it from Ebenezer to Ashdod" (v1).** Ashdod was one of the five chief Philistine cities (6:17), a few miles inland from the Mediterranean on the coastal highway, roughly 30 miles south of the battlefield. Its later Greek name was Azotus (Acts 8:40).
- **"Into the house of Dagon and set it up beside Dagon" (v2).** In the ancient world, a victorious nation would set the captured god of its enemy in its own temple, as a trophy and a sign of submission. Placing the ark "beside Dagon" says: your God now serves ours. Dagon was a major god of the Philistines. Samson pulled down his temple at Gaza (Judg 16:23–30). For a long time Dagon was described as a fish-god, half man and half fish, because his name looks like Hebrew *dag*, "fish." Most scholars now think the name comes from *dagan*, "grain," and that Dagon was a god of crops and fertility. He was worshiped across the region centuries before the Philistines arrived. In texts from Ugarit, Baal is called "son of Dagan."
- **"Dagon had fallen face downward on the ground before the ark of the LORD" (v3).** This is the posture of worship, a servant bowing before his master. The god meant to stand over the captured God is found on his face before him. "So they took Dagon and put him back in his place." The irony is sharp: a god who has to be picked up and set back in place by his worshipers. Isaiah mocks idols in just this way. They must be carried and propped up so they will not topple (Isa 40:20; 46:1–7).
- **"The head of Dagon and both his hands were lying cut off on the threshold" (v4).** The second morning is worse. In ancient warfare the victor cut off the head and hands of a defeated enemy as proof of victory, as the Philistines later did to Saul (31:9), and David to Goliath (17:51). The head is the seat of thought and the hands the means of action. Dagon is left without power to think or act. "Only the trunk of Dagon was left to him" is literally "only Dagon was left," that is, only the stump. The Philistines thought they had defeated Israel's God. Their own god lies beheaded on his own temple floor.
- **"Do not tread on the threshold of Dagon in Ashdod to this day" (v5).** The Philistines turned the humiliation into a ritual. Instead of asking what it meant, they stepped over the place where their god fell and treated it as sacred. Zephaniah later condemns those "who leap over the threshold" (Zeph 1:9), probably a related superstition. "To this day" means the custom was still kept when the book was written.
- **"The hand of the LORD was heavy against the people of Ashdod" (v6).** "Heavy" is *kabed*, the same root as the "glory" (*kabod*) that Israel thought had departed (4:21). God's glory has not vanished. Its weight now falls on Philistia. And notice the contrast of hands. Dagon has none left, while "the hand of the LORD" is heavy (vv6, 7, 9, 11). The Philistines remembered how "the gods" had struck Egypt (4:8). Now they feel it themselves, a new round of plagues.
- **"He terrified and afflicted them with tumors" (v6).** The Hebrew word means swellings. The traditional reading (*'ophalim*) suggests "mounds," and the Jewish scribes' marginal reading (*tehorim*) was understood as "hemorrhoids," which is where the KJV's "emerods" comes from. Many scholars now suspect the swollen lymph glands (buboes) of bubonic plague, which is spread by fleas on rats, comes in deadly epidemics and causes panic. That would also explain the golden mice or rats in 6:4–5. The Greek translation adds a line here about mice swarming in the land, which some think was lost from the Hebrew. The diagnosis is uncertain. The point is not: God struck the city with a painful and deadly disease.
- **"His hand is hard against us and against Dagon our god" (v7).** The men of Ashdod see clearly: two events, one cause. But they do not ask how to be reconciled to Israel's God. They just want him somewhere else.
- **"They sent and gathered together all the lords of the Philistines" (v8).** "Lords" (*seren*) is a word used only for Philistine rulers. Many scholars connect it with the Greek *tyrannos*, "tyrant," fitting the Philistines' origins in the Aegean world. The five lords together decide to try again: "Let the ark… be brought around to Gath." Perhaps, they think, the trouble is in Ashdod, not in the ark. Gath was inland, near the hills of Judah. It would be home to Goliath (17:4).
- **"A very great panic… young and old" (v9).** The result at Gath is the same, or worse. The disease struck everyone, from the least to the greatest. The word for "panic" (*mehumah*) is used of the confusion God sends on his enemies in holy war (Deut 7:23; 1 Sam 14:20).
- **"They have brought around to us the ark of the God of Israel to kill us" (v10).** By the time it reaches Ekron, the northernmost Philistine city and the nearest to Israel's border, the people cry out before anything has happened. The ark's reputation has run ahead of it.
- **"Let it return to its own place" (v11).** The lords meet a second time. This time Ekron demands the ark go home. There is "a deathly panic," literally "a panic of death," and "the hand of God was very heavy there." The trophy has become a curse that no city will keep.
- **"The cry of the city went up to heaven" (v12).** Seven months of this (6:1). The cry echoes the outcry of Egypt at the last plague (Exod 12:30), and the cry of Israel in Egypt that went up to God (Exod 2:23). But here the cry of the Philistines is a cry of pain, not of repentance.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the Philistines set the ark in Dagon's house as "a trophy of victory, in honour of Dagon their god," but "down he comes before the ark, directly towards it." When the priests stood their god up again, "they had better have let him alone when he was down." On the plague, he suggests "the disease was epidemical, and perhaps, among them, a new disease," and notes that each city passed on the ark "instead of returning it, as they should have done, to its own place." JFB says Dagon "lay like a vanquished enemy," and that "to this day" shows the custom was still practiced in Samuel's later years. JFB also notes that a painful disease of this kind was not rare in the Philistine plain. Guzik still describes Dagon as half man, half fish, which older writers assumed but most scholars now doubt. He favors bubonic plague over hemorrhoids, and he draws the lesson that the priests found it easier to glue their god back together than to change their lives. He also observes that the same presence of God that would have been a blessing became a curse to those who would not bow, "a fragrance from death to death" (2 Cor 2:15–16).

#### Pointing to Christ
The ark went into enemy territory alone, apparently defeated and taken captive, and there it toppled the enemy's god and broke his power from the inside. That is a picture of what happened at the cross. Jesus was handed over to his enemies and seemed utterly defeated. Yet by his death he "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Col 2:15). "The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8). Like Dagon, every rival to Christ will one day be found face down before him: "at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Phil 2:10). The Philistines could not live with God's presence, so they sent it away. The gospel offers what they never asked for: a way to be brought near to the holy God without being destroyed, through the one who bore God's heavy hand for us.
