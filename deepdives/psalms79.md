*Setting:* "A Psalm of Asaph." Psalm 79 is a community lament over the worst disaster in Israel's history: Jerusalem in ruins, the temple defiled, the dead lying unburied in the streets. Most interpreters, Henry and JFB included, connect it with the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, when Nebuchadnezzar's army burned the temple and the city (2 Kgs 25:8–10; Jer 52:12–14). If so, "Asaph" means the guild of temple singers descended from David's musician, which continued for centuries (2 Chr 35:15; Ezra 2:41). A later Jewish book, 1 Maccabees, quotes vv2–3 about a massacre in 162 BC (1 Macc 7:16–17), and a few scholars have dated the psalm to that era, but most think 1 Maccabees is borrowing an older psalm to describe a new horror. Psalm 79 is a twin of Psalm 74, which also mourns the temple's destruction. Coming right after Psalm 78, which retold Israel's long record of rebellion, it reads like the end of that story: the judgment the history warned of has fallen. Yet the psalm does not end in despair. It moves from complaint, to confession, to bold petition, to a vow of praise.

#### The Nations Have Come into Your Inheritance (vv1–4)
- **"O God, the nations have come into your inheritance" (v1).** The first word of complaint is about God's loss, not Israel's. The land, the city and the people are his "inheritance," his own possession (Deut 32:9). The Tyndale notes point out that the word may mean the land, the temple or the people, and here it likely includes all three. "The nations" (*goyim*) are pagan peoples, outsiders to the covenant. The irony is bitter: in Psalm 78 God drove out the nations to plant Israel (78:55). Now the nations have come back in.
- **"They have defiled your holy temple; they have laid Jerusalem in ruins" (v1).** "Ruins" is literally "heaps of rubble." Micah had warned it would happen: "Jerusalem shall become a heap of ruins" (Mic 3:12; Jer 26:18). Lamentations, written about the same event, mourns that "the enemy has stretched out his hands over all her precious things" and the nations have entered the sanctuary (Lam 1:10).
- **"They have given the bodies of your servants to the birds of the heavens for food" (v2).** In the ancient world, being left unburied was the ultimate disgrace. The law warned of exactly this as a covenant curse: "your dead body shall be food for all birds of the air" (Deut 28:26; Jer 7:33). Notice that the dead are still called "your servants" and "your faithful" (*chasidim*, loyal ones). Judgment fell on the nation, but God's own people were among those who died, and the psalmist reminds God that they are his.
- **"Poured out their blood like water all around Jerusalem, and there was no one to bury them" (v3).** Blood was meant to be poured out on the altar, and water poured out freely without a second thought. Here human lives have been spilled like both. 1 Maccabees 7:17 quotes this verse.
- **"We have become a taunt to our neighbors" (v4).** The neighbors are the small nations around Judah: Edom, Moab, Ammon, the Philistines. Instead of helping, they mocked and even joined in. "Remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem, how they said, 'Lay it bare'" (137:7; Obad 10–14; Ezek 25:1–17). Psalm 44:13 uses almost the same words.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The heathen have come into thy inheritance, to plunder that, and lay it waste," and of the dead, "When they had killed them they would let none bury them." Guzik, following VanGemeren, notes that even under deserved judgment the land is still God's inheritance and the people still his servants and saints. He cites Kidner on the special shame of being left unburied. JFB says the psalm "probably depicts the desolations of the Chaldeans" and sends readers to Psalm 74 for the same scene.

#### How Long, O LORD? (vv5–7)
- **"How long, O LORD? Will you be angry forever?" (v5).** The psalmist does not ask why. He knows why: the nation had sinned (v8). He asks how long. This is the language of faith under pressure, the same cry heard in 13:1–2, 74:10, 89:46, and at the end of history from the martyrs under the altar (Rev 6:10). Henry gives the sense: "How long, Lord, wilt thou be angry? Shall it be for ever?"
- **"Will your jealousy burn like fire?" (v5).** God's jealousy is his rightful zeal for his covenant people's whole love, the reaction of a faithful husband to an unfaithful wife (Deut 4:24, "the LORD your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God"; Zeph 1:18). The people's idolatry had provoked that jealousy (78:58).
- **"Pour out your anger on the nations that do not know you" (v6).** Verses 6–7 appear almost word for word in Jeremiah 10:25. Either Jeremiah borrowed from the psalm or the psalm from Jeremiah. Henry thinks the "weeping prophet borrows two verses out of it." "Pour out your anger" plays on v3: they poured out blood, now let God pour out wrath. The plea is not that God would go easy on sin, but that the nations which "do not know" God and "do not call upon" his name would not escape while his own repentant people are crushed.
- **"For they have devoured Jacob and laid waste his habitation" (v7).** "Habitation" can mean a dwelling or a pasture. The enemies are pictured as wild beasts that have eaten up the flock.
- **Is this a right prayer?** Prayers for judgment on enemies are hard for Christians who are told to love their enemies (Matt 5:44). Several things help. The psalmist leaves vengeance to God instead of taking it himself (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19). He is asking for justice against those who slaughtered the innocent and mocked God. And the request lines up with what God had promised: Babylon itself would be judged for its cruelty (Jer 25:12–14; 50:17–18), and it fell to the Medes and Persians in 539 BC.
- **Commentators:** JFB gives the logic of v6: "Though we deserve much, do not the heathen deserve more for their violence to us." Henry takes the same line further: "This prayer is in effect a prophecy, in which the wrath of God is revealed from heaven" against the ungodly. Guzik, citing Maclaren and Morgan, says that crying "How long?" to God is itself an act of faith, because the psalmist is still talking to him.

#### For the Glory of Your Name (vv8–10)
- **"Do not remember against us our former iniquities" (v8).** The Hebrew can mean "the iniquities of former times" (JFB: "literally, 'iniquities of former times'") or "the iniquities of our forefathers." Kings says Judah fell because of the sins of Manasseh, "which the LORD would not pardon" (2 Kgs 24:3–4). The people admit that generations of sin have piled up (Lam 5:7). At the same time, the prophets insisted each generation was judged for its own sin (Jer 31:29–30; Ezek 18:20), and the plea in v9 confesses "our sins" too. Henry: "When God pardons sin he blots it out and remembers it no more."
- **"Let your compassion come speedily to meet us, for we are brought very low" (v8).** "Compassion" (*rachamim*) is God's tender, motherly mercy, the very thing Psalm 77:9 feared God had shut up. The picture is of mercy running to meet them, like the father running to the prodigal son (Luke 15:20).
- **"Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of your name… for your name's sake!" (v9).** The appeal is not to Israel's goodness but to God's reputation. God's "name" means his revealed character, what he has made known of himself. If his people are destroyed, the nations will think their God was weak. Moses prayed this way (Num 14:13–16), and so did Daniel in exile (Dan 9:18–19). JFB says such pleading for God's glory is what "often distinguish[es] the prayers of Old Testament saints." Henry: "Help us for the glory of thy name; pardon us for thy name's sake."
- **"Deliver us, and atone for our sins" (v9).** "Atone" is *kipper*, to cover or make atonement, the word used of the sacrifices (Lev 16:30). But the temple and altar are in ruins. There is nowhere left to offer a sacrifice. So the psalmist asks God himself to provide the atonement. Guzik draws out the point: only God can supply what no altar now could (Gen 22:8, 14).
- **"Why should the nations say, 'Where is their God?'" (v10).** This is the taunt the psalmist cannot bear (42:3, 10; 115:2; Joel 2:17). JFB: "This ground of pleading often used."
- **"Let the avenging of the outpoured blood of your servants be known among the nations" (v10).** In Israel's law a murdered person's nearest relative was the "avenger of blood" (Num 35:19). The psalmist asks God to act as next of kin for his slain servants. The law also promised: "he avenges the blood of his children" (Deut 32:43). "Before our eyes" asks to see it happen in their own lifetime.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the turn from complaint to confession: the people now admit their guilt and appeal only to God's glory. He explains the kinsman-avenger custom behind v10. The Tyndale notes say that both the nation's long history of sin and the people's own sins brought the exile, and that the pagan nations still answer for the bloodshed.

#### The Groans of the Prisoners (vv11–13)
- **"Let the groans of the prisoners come before you" (v11).** Thousands were marched off to Babylon (2 Kgs 25:11). The word "groans" recalls Egypt: "God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant" (Exod 2:24). The psalmist asks God to do again what he did then. Psalm 102:20 uses the same words: "to hear the groans of the prisoners."
- **"According to your great power, preserve those doomed to die!" (v11).** "Your great power" is literally "the greatness of your arm," the arm that redeemed Israel from Egypt (77:15). "Those doomed to die" is literally "sons of death," people under a death sentence. Henry: "They were appointed to die, as sheep for the slaughter."
- **"Return sevenfold into the lap of our neighbors the taunts with which they have taunted you, O Lord!" (v12).** "Seven" signifies completeness (Gen 4:15, 24; Lev 26:18). "Lap" means the fold of a robe at the chest, which people used as a pocket to carry grain; Jesus uses the same picture: "good measure, pressed down… will be put into your lap" (Luke 6:38). Notice whose honor is at stake: the neighbors taunted "you, O Lord." Mocking God's people was mocking God. JFB: "The figure denotes retaliation."
- **"But we your people, the sheep of your pasture, will give thanks to you forever" (v13).** "Sheep of your pasture" is a favorite phrase of the Asaph psalms (74:1; 95:7; 100:3). It ties back to the end of Psalm 78, where God shepherded Israel and gave them a shepherd king. Even with the flock scattered, they are still his sheep. The last line echoes 78:4: "from generation to generation we will recount your praise." The psalm that began in rubble ends with a vow to keep telling God's story.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls v13 a plea "taken from the readiness of his people to praise him," and adds, "Those lives that are entirely devoted to God's praise are assuredly taken under his protection." Guzik, citing Boice, reads the ending as faith looking ahead to a day when the people will praise God again. He takes "sevenfold" as a figure for full measure and adds Spurgeon's point that those who mock God should bear the shame of it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 79 asks God to atone for sins when there was no altar left, to hear the groans of prisoners doomed to die, and to act for the glory of his name. God answered in a way the psalmist could not have imagined. He provided the atonement himself: Jesus "was delivered up for our trespasses" (Rom 4:25), and through him the sins of former times are remembered no more (Heb 8:12). Jesus came "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18), and by his death he delivers "all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Heb 2:15). The cry "How long?" is still prayed by the martyrs in heaven (Rev 6:10), and the answer is the return of Christ, when God's name will be vindicated before every nation. Meanwhile the sheep of his pasture belong to the Good Shepherd, and they keep recounting his praise from generation to generation.
