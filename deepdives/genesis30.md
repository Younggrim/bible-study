*Setting:* Jacob is still in Haran, in upper Mesopotamia, working for his uncle Laban. He was tricked into marrying Leah, then given Rachel a week later in exchange for seven more years of work (29:21–30). He loves Rachel more, and the LORD, seeing that Leah was unloved, has given Leah four sons: Reuben, Simeon, Levi and Judah (29:31–35). Rachel has none. This chapter picks up in the middle of that rivalry and runs to the end of Jacob's second seven years and beyond. Within a few years Jacob has eleven sons and a daughter, born to four women. Most of the sons give their names to tribes of Israel. Then Jacob asks to go home, and he and Laban strike a deal over speckled sheep and goats that turns into another contest of wits. It is not a tidy chapter. People use children as weapons and bargain over mandrakes and sheep. Through all of it, God is building the family he promised at Bethel: "your offspring shall be like the dust of the earth" (28:14).

#### Jacob's Children (vv1–24)
- **"Give me children, or I shall die!" (v1).** Rachel "envied her sister." It is the same verb used of the Philistines envying Isaac (26:14) and later of Joseph's brothers envying him (37:11). In that world a childless wife lived under a cloud. Children carried the family name, inherited the land and cared for parents in old age, and barrenness was often read as God's disfavor (compare 1 Sam 1:6–7). The Tyndale notes say it was something like death for a woman. There is a hard irony in Rachel's cry. She will die giving birth to her second son (35:16–19).
- **"Am I in the place of God?" (v2).** Jacob's anger is understandable but harsh, and his theology is right: it is God "who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb." His grandfather Abraham prayed for Abimelech's household to be healed of barrenness (20:17), and his father Isaac "prayed to the LORD for his wife, because she was barren" (25:21). Jacob prays for nothing. Many years later Joseph, the son Rachel longed for, will use almost the same words to his brothers: "Am I in the place of God?" (50:19).
- **"Here is my servant Bilhah" (v3).** Rachel repeats exactly what Sarah did with Hagar (16:2). Ancient marriage contracts from Mesopotamia and laws such as Hammurabi's (§§144–147) deal with a barren wife giving her female servant to her husband so that the children would count as hers. "Give birth on my behalf" is literally, as the ESV footnote says, "on my knees," apparently a sign that the child was received into the family as Rachel's own (compare 48:12; 50:23). "That even I may have children through her" is literally "that I may be built up," which in Hebrew sounds like the word for "son" (ESV footnote). Sarah used the same expression (16:2). It ended badly then too.
- **Dan (vv5–6).** "God has judged me." The ESV footnote says Dan sounds like the Hebrew for "judged." The Tyndale notes take it as "vindicated." Rachel feels God has ruled in her favor. Jacob's later blessing plays on the same name: "Dan shall judge his people" (49:16).
- **Naphtali (vv7–8).** "With mighty wrestlings I have wrestled with my sister and have prevailed." The ESV footnote says the Hebrew is "with wrestlings of God," which may mean wrestlings of great intensity, or wrestling about God's favor. The Tyndale notes observe that Rachel's names, unlike Leah's first four, show no faith, only a bitter struggle with her sister. "Prevailed" is a striking word. It anticipates the night her husband wrestles with God and is told, "you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed" (32:28).
- **Leah answers in kind (v9).** Leah already has four sons, but when she sees she has "ceased bearing," she gives Jacob her own servant Zilpah. The contest has become two teams.
- **Gad (vv10–11).** "Good fortune has come!" The lexicon gives *gad* as "fortune," and notes that Gad was also the name of a god of luck worshiped in Phoenicia and Aram (compare Isa 65:11, where "Fortune" is a pagan deity). The Tyndale notes suggest the names show Leah's Aramean background, without implying she worshiped such a god. The written Hebrew text reads "with fortune," and the margin reading (followed by the ESV) is "fortune has come." The KJV's "a troop cometh" follows a different explanation that the lexicon does not support.
- **Asher (vv12–13).** "Happy am I! For women have called me happy." The ESV footnote ties Asher to the Hebrew for "happy," the word that opens Psalm 1, "Blessed is the man." Leah's joy is now about what other women think of her. It is worth setting beside Mary's words, "all generations will call me blessed" (Luke 1:48). Mary's joy was in what God had done for her.
- **The mandrakes (vv14–15).** Reuben, now old enough to wander the fields at "wheat harvest" (about May), finds mandrakes and brings them to his mother. The mandrake is a low Mediterranean plant with a thick, forked root that can look like a human figure, and small yellow, fragrant fruit. The Hebrew word *duday* is related to *dod*, "love," and the lexicon notes that mandrakes were believed to stir desire and help conception (compare Song 7:13). Rachel wants them for that reason. Leah's reply is the first time she speaks to Rachel directly in the story, and it is bitter: "Is it a small matter that you have taken away my husband?"
- **"I have hired you with my son's mandrakes" (v16).** The household has sunk to this. Two sisters trade nights with their husband for a handful of plants, and Jacob goes along. "Hired" is *sakar*, the word for wages. It sets up the next son's name, and later the wages dispute with Laban (vv28–33), where the same root returns.
- **"God listened to Leah" (v17).** This is the turning point of the episode. Rachel got the mandrakes, but Leah got the child. The text does not credit the plant. "God listened," and that is the reason. The Tyndale notes stress that children here come from God, not from superstition or social customs.
- **Issachar (v18).** "God has given me my wages." The name sounds like "wages" or "hire" (ESV footnote). Leah connects it, oddly, to giving her servant to Jacob, as if God had rewarded that. The narrator simply records her view.
- **Zebulun (vv19–20).** "God has endowed me with a good endowment; now my husband will honor me." The ESV footnote ties Zebulun to "honor." The lexicon agrees, giving the verb *zabal* as "probably exalt, honour," and explaining that older translations such as the KJV took it as "dwell with me." Either way, Leah's old hope comes back after six sons: that Jacob will finally value her.
- **"Afterward she bore a daughter and called her name Dinah" (v21).** No explanation is given for her name. It looks like the feminine form of Dan, "judgment." Dinah is mentioned because her story comes in chapter 34. She is probably not Jacob's only daughter (37:35; 46:7), but she is the only one named.
- **"Then God remembered Rachel" (v22).** In the Bible, God "remembering" is not recalling something he had forgotten. It means turning to act on someone's behalf. God "remembered Noah" and the waters went down (8:1). He "remembered Abraham" and rescued Lot (19:29). Later he "remembered" Hannah (1 Sam 1:19). "God listened to her and opened her womb." The mandrakes had not done it. Years of rivalry and a borrowed servant had not done it. God did.
- **"God has taken away my reproach" (v23).** "Reproach" means public shame. Elizabeth, the mother of John the Baptist, says the same of her own late pregnancy: the Lord has acted "to take away my reproach among people" (Luke 1:25).
- **Joseph (v24).** The name carries a double wordplay, as the ESV footnote explains. It means "May he add," and sounds like the Hebrew for "taken away" (v23). Rachel is thankful and asking for more in the same breath: "May the LORD add to me another son!" Notice that she uses the covenant name, the LORD. The prayer will be answered with Benjamin, at the cost of her life (35:17–18).
- **A family of twelve, nearly.** By the end of this section Jacob has eleven sons. The order of their births, and which mother bore each, will shape everything that follows: Reuben's place as firstborn, Joseph's favored status, and the jealousy of the brothers (ch 37). The tribes of Israel begin in a household full of envy, bargaining and favoritism. God's promise did not wait for a better family.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls envy "grieving at the good of another, than which no sin is more hateful to God," and approves of Jacob's rebuke: "Faithful reproofs show true affection." On the servants he observes that Rachel wanted "children whom she had a right to rule" more than children "she had more reason to love." JFB calls Rachel's conduct sinful and, on the servant wives, says, "The evil lies in the system." On Dinah, JFB notes "the inferior value set on a daughter" in the plain announcement of her birth. Guzik notes that the wives were using their children as pieces in a household power struggle, with no real winner. He also cites Leviticus 18:18, which later forbade marrying two sisters, as a lesson drawn from this home. He gives Gad as "troop" or "good fortune," and Zebulun as "dwelling." The lexicon supports "fortune" and "honor," as the ESV has them, and treats "troop" and "dwelling" as older explanations. Guzik also describes the mandrake as a "love apple." The lexicon's link between *duday* and love-producing supports the idea, though "love apple" is a later English name. JFB has no notes on vv10–19 or 22–24.

#### Jacob's Prosperity (vv25–43)
- **"Send me away, that I may go to my own home" (v25).** As soon as Joseph is born, Jacob asks to leave. His fourteen years of service are complete (v26). "Home and country" means Canaan, the land God promised to bring him back to (28:15), even though he has not lived there for fourteen years. He asks for "my wives and my children for whom I have served you." Under the customs of the day, Laban could probably argue that a son-in-law who had paid with work still owed him, and that the family belonged to Laban's household (compare 31:43). Jacob is asking to leave as a free man.
- **"I have learned by divination that the LORD has blessed me because of you" (v27).** "Learned by divination" is *nachash*. Elsewhere it means reading omens, which the law later forbids (Lev 19:26; Deut 18:10). Joseph's silver cup is described as one used for divining (44:5). For this verse, though, the lexicon allows the sense "observe the signs," and JFB reads it as learning "from long observation." The ESV footnote offers "have become rich" as another possible reading. Either way, even Laban can see that Jacob's God, whom he calls "the LORD," is behind his wealth. It is the promise of 12:3 at work: those who host Abraham's family are blessed through them.
- **"Name your wages, and I will give it" (v28).** Laban said almost the same thing fourteen years earlier (29:15), and it cost Jacob two marriages and fourteen years. Jacob knows better now. Later he will say Laban "changed my wages ten times" (31:7, 41).
- **"It has increased abundantly" (v30).** The verb is *parats*, "break through, burst out," and the lexicon lists it under the sense "increase." It is the same verb God used at Bethel: "you shall spread abroad to the west and to the east" (28:14). It returns at the end of the chapter: "the man increased greatly" (v43). The promise is breaking out in Jacob's flocks before it breaks out in a nation. "The LORD has blessed you wherever I turned" is literally "at my foot." Joseph will later bring the same kind of blessing on Potiphar's household (39:5).
- **"You shall not give me anything" (v31).** Jacob refuses a wage that Laban could change or withhold. Instead he proposes to take only the unusual animals. In that region sheep were normally white and goats normally black or dark brown. Black sheep and speckled or spotted goats were the exceptions. The Tyndale notes compare Jacob to Abraham letting Lot choose first (13:9). On the face of it, Jacob is taking the leftovers.
- **"My honesty will answer for me later" (v33).** "Honesty" is literally "righteousness." Jacob, whose name is linked with cheating (27:36), now offers his record as his guarantee. Any normally colored animal found in his flock could be counted as stolen. It was an easy deal to check.
- **"Good! Let it be as you have said" (v34).** Laban agrees, and "that day" he removes every speckled, spotted and black animal, every one "that had white on it," gives them to his sons and moves them "three days' journey" away (vv35–36). He leaves Jacob a flock that should produce almost no animals of the agreed colors. It is a breach of the spirit of the deal on the very day it was made. This is the first mention of Laban's sons. Their resentment will come out in 31:1.
- **The peeled sticks (vv37–39).** Jacob takes fresh branches of poplar, almond and plane, peels "white streaks" in them and sets them in the watering troughs where the animals mate. The flocks then bear "striped, speckled, and spotted" young. Many ancient peoples believed that what a mother saw during conception could mark her offspring. JFB explains the method that way, as working "through the imagination," and the Tyndale notes describe it as a traditional belief. Modern genetics finds no such effect.
- **A play on Laban's name.** The Hebrew word for "white" is *laban*, the same as Laban's name. "Poplar" is *libneh*, which the lexicon says was named for its white gum. Jacob "peeled white (*laban*) streaks... exposing the white (*laban*)." The Tyndale notes draw the conclusion: Jacob beat "Mr. White" at his own game. "Almond" is *luz*, the old name of Bethel (28:19), which may be a quiet reminder of who really stood behind Jacob.
- **The stronger and the feebler (vv40–42).** Jacob also breeds selectively. He uses the sticks only when the strong animals are mating, so their offspring are his, and he lets the weaker ones breed for Laban. He also places his own flocks so they face the striped and dark animals in Laban's flock. These steps reflect real skill in animal breeding. Animals that look plain can carry hidden traits for spotting, and careful mating brings those out.
- **So what really happened?** Genesis gives its own answer in the next chapter. Jacob tells his wives that God showed him in a dream that the rams mating were striped, spotted and mottled, and said, "I have seen all that Laban is doing to you" (31:10–12). Jacob concludes, "God has taken away the livestock of your father and given them to me" (31:9). Interpreters weigh this in different ways. Some think the sticks were Jacob's own superstitious scheme, and God blessed him despite it. Others, like JFB, think the method was suggested by "a divine intimation." Still others think Jacob's breeding was skilled and God simply prospered it. All agree on the main point: the text credits God, not the sticks.
- **"Thus the man increased greatly" (v43).** "Large flocks, female servants and male servants, and camels and donkeys" is nearly the same list used for Abraham's wealth (12:16; 24:35). The fugitive who crossed the Jordan with only a staff (32:10) has become a wealthy man. Isaac's blessing, "plenty of grain and wine" (27:28), and God's promise at Bethel are both coming true, in Laban's backyard and in spite of Laban.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jacob "was willing to depart without any provision, except God's promise," and that he "referred his cause to God, rather than agree for stated wages with Laban, whose selfishness was very great." His summary: "The Lord will one way or another plead the cause of the oppressed." JFB thinks Laban learned Jacob's value "from long observation" rather than by omens, and draws the lesson that "good men are blessings to the places where they reside." JFB explains that Eastern shepherds were often paid with a share of the flock, and that eastern sheep were mostly white and goats black, so the speckled ones were rare. It has no notes on vv29–30, 33–36 or 39–43. Guzik reads the divination as real occult practice, which goes further than the lexicon requires. He admits that no one knows exactly how the sticks worked, and he leans toward the view that Jacob did what he knew and God blessed it. His applications are practical: work hard to make an employer prosper, do not make wealth the main goal, and trust God with the outcome.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is a picture of a family grasping for blessing. Rachel grasps for children, Leah for her husband's love, Laban for profit, and Jacob for a flock of his own. Every scheme is undercut by the same line: "God listened," "God remembered," "God has taken away the livestock of your father and given them to me." The tribes of Israel were not born from a model household. They came by grace into a family full of envy. That is how the people of God have always been formed. The promise that began in this tangled home led to one son of Judah, Leah's fourth son, who would fulfil it. The "reproach" Rachel felt lifted is the kind of shame Christ came to bear: he "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Heb 12:2). And where Jacob was finally paid in sheep, Christ "became poor," so that his people "by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9).
