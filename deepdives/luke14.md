*Setting:* About AD 29–30, on the journey to Jerusalem, probably in Perea, east of the Jordan. Chapter 13 ended with Jesus refusing to be hurried by Herod and lamenting over Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets. Chapter 14 is set mostly at a single Sabbath meal in the home of a leading Pharisee. Meals in that world were displays of rank: who was invited, who sat where and who could repay. Jesus heals a sick man before the meal, then uses the table itself to teach about humility, about whom to invite, and about God's great banquet, where the invited guests make excuses and the poor fill the hall. On the road afterward he turns to the crowds and tells them what it costs to follow him.

#### Healing of a Man on the Sabbath (vv1–6)
- **"A ruler of the Pharisees" (v1).** He was a leading Pharisee, perhaps a member of a local council. This is Luke's third Pharisee meal (7:36; 11:37). Sabbath dinners after synagogue were often festive occasions with guests. The food was cooked the day before, since cooking counted as work.
- **"They were watching him carefully" (v1).** The verb means to watch closely, even to spy. The same word appears in 6:7 and 20:20. Jesus is a guest under observation.
- **"A man before him who had dropsy" (v2).** Dropsy (*hydrōpikos*, from the word for water) is the swelling of tissues with fluid, now called edema, usually a sign of serious heart, liver or kidney disease. Only Luke the doctor uses this medical word. Whether the man was placed there to trap Jesus or just wandered in, he stands "before him" where everyone can see.
- **"Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath, or not?" (v3).** Jesus asks the question before they can. Nothing in the law of Moses forbade healing on the Sabbath. The ban came from tradition, which allowed medical help only when life was in danger.
- **"But they remained silent" (v4).** They cannot say yes without approving what they planned to condemn, and they cannot say no without looking heartless in front of a suffering man.
- **"He took him and healed him and sent him away" (v4).** "Took" means he took hold of him, a physical touch. "Sent him away" spares the man from being caught in the argument that follows.
- **"A son or an ox that has fallen into a well" (v5).** Some manuscripts read "a donkey or an ox," which matches 13:15 and looks like a scribe smoothing the saying. "Son" is the harder and probably original reading, and the ESV follows it with a footnote. The argument is the same either way. Anyone would pull a child or an animal out of a well on the Sabbath without a second thought. Later rabbinic writings record debates over just this case, and some stricter groups at Qumran forbade lifting an animal out of a pit on the Sabbath.
- **"They could not reply" (v6).** The silence of v4 becomes a forced silence. They have no answer.
- **Commentators:** JFB thinks the man was not a guest but came in "before the guests sat down," perhaps hoping for a cure. Henry notes that "good men have often been persecuted for doing that which even their persecutors" would do themselves, and that Sabbath meals especially should be "guarded against all manner of excess." Guzik suggests the man may have been placed there as a setup, and points out that the objection rested on tradition, not on any command of God.

#### The Parable of the Wedding Feast (vv7–14)
- **"When he noticed how they chose the places of honor" (v7).** Guests reclined on couches around U-shaped tables, and the places nearest the host were the most honored. Guests jostled for them. Jesus watched, and then spoke.
- **"When you are invited by someone to a wedding feast" (v8).** JFB notes that Jesus tactfully moves the setting to a wedding so as not to criticize this host's guests directly. The advice comes almost word for word from Proverbs 25:6–7: "It is better to be told, 'Come up here,' than to be put lower in the presence of a noble."
- **"Then you will begin with shame to take the lowest place" (v9).** In an honor-and-shame culture, being moved down in public was deeply humiliating. By then all the middle seats would be taken, and the only seat left would be the lowest.
- **"Friend, move up higher" (v10).** Honor received is better than honor grabbed. Jesus is not teaching a trick for getting the best seat. The parable is about the kingdom, where God decides who sits where.
- **"Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted" (v11).** This reversal runs through Luke, from Mary's song (1:52) to the Pharisee and the tax collector (18:14). The passive verbs mean God does the humbling and exalting. Jesus himself is the great example: he "humbled himself" to the cross, and "therefore God has highly exalted him" (Phil 2:8–9).
- **"Do not invite your friends or your brothers or your relatives or rich neighbors" (v12).** Jesus now speaks to the host. The Greek tense suggests "do not make a habit of inviting only." He is not banning family dinners. He is exposing a system in which hospitality was an investment, given to people who would return the favor and raise the host's standing.
- **"Invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind" (v13).** These were the people with nothing to give back. The Qumran community's rules barred the lame, the blind and the crippled from its gatherings. Jesus puts them at the top of the guest list. The same four groups appear in v21, God's own guest list.
- **"You will be repaid at the resurrection of the just" (v14).** Generosity to those who cannot repay is not wasted. God himself repays (Prov 19:17). "The resurrection of the just" speaks of the day when the righteous are raised (Dan 12:2; Acts 24:15).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the aim was "not so much to inculcate mere politeness," notes that "to be lowest is only ignominious to him who affects the highest," and calls v11 a saying of "proverbial terseness," "apples of gold in a setting of silver." Henry: "Pride will have shame, and will at last have a fall," and "the way to rise high is to begin low." Guzik warns that a show of humility can itself become a performed pride, and reads "do not invite" as "do not habitually invite."

#### The Parable of the Great Banquet (vv15–24)
- **"Blessed is everyone who will eat bread in the kingdom of God!" (v15).** Jewish hope pictured the age to come as a great feast (Isa 25:6–8). The guest's remark sounds pious. He probably assumed he would be there. Jesus' parable answers him: the question is not whether the feast will be wonderful but who will actually come.
- **"A man once gave a great banquet and invited many" (v16).** In that culture, a host sent invitations in advance, and guests accepted. When the meal was ready, a servant went out with a second summons: "Come, for everything is now ready" (v17). Esther 6:14 shows the custom. Refusing at the second call, after accepting the first, was a grave insult.
- **"Everything is now ready" (v17).** The time of preparation, the Law and the Prophets and the ministry of John, is over. With Jesus, the feast has begun.
- **"They all alike began to make excuses" (v18).** "All alike" suggests they acted as one, almost as if they had agreed.
- **"I have bought a field, and I must go out and see it" (v18).** No one bought land without seeing it first. The excuse is thin, and the field would still be there tomorrow.
- **"I have bought five yoke of oxen, and I go to examine them" (v19).** Five pairs of oxen meant a large farm, perhaps a hundred acres. Again, no one bought oxen without testing them. He does not even say "I must." He simply prefers his oxen.
- **"I have married a wife, and therefore I cannot come" (v20).** The law excused a newly married man from war for a year (Deut 24:5), but this was a dinner. The wedding would not have been scheduled on the day of a village feast. He does not even ask to be excused.
- **What the excuses share.** Property, business and family are all good things. None of the three is evil. They simply matter more to these guests than the host does. This is the thorny soil again: choked "by the cares and riches and pleasures of life" (8:14).
- **"Bring in the poor and crippled and blind and lame" (v21).** The very people of v13. The host does what Jesus told the Pharisee to do. In Jesus' ministry, these were the tax collectors, sinners and outcasts who were responding while the religious leaders refused (7:29–30; 15:1–2).
- **"Still there is room" (v22).** God's house is large, and his generosity is not used up.
- **"Go out to the highways and hedges and compel people to come in" (v23).** The servant now goes outside the town, to the roads and hedgerows where travelers and outsiders were found. Many see here the mission to the Gentiles, which Acts describes (Acts 13:46–48). "Compel" does not mean force. In that culture, a poor stranger invited to a rich man's feast would politely refuse, and had to be urged and taken by the arm. Sadly, Augustine used this verse to justify the state forcing heretics back into the church, and others later used it to defend coercion. The parable itself pictures insistent, gracious persuasion.
- **"None of those men who were invited shall taste my banquet" (v24).** "I tell you" is plural. Jesus seems to step out of the story and speak directly to the guests at the table. Those who think they are sure of a place may find themselves outside.
- **Matthew's version.** Matthew 22:1–14 tells a similar parable set at a king's wedding feast for his son, with servants killed and a city burned, and a guest thrown out for lacking a wedding garment. Many think Jesus told the story more than once, in different forms for different audiences.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the guest's remark "seemingly pious," like Balaam wishing to "die the death of the righteous" without living the life. It says the three excuses "all come to the same result": "We have other things to attend to, more pressing just now." It explains "compel" as overcoming the outcasts' doubts about their fitness, so they come as they are, and adds: "Grace no more than nature will endure a vacuum." Henry: "Christ in the gospel, as he keeps a good house, so he keeps an open house," and "grace despised is grace forfeited, like Esau's birthright." Guzik notes the excuses were foolish on their face, sees the Gentiles in the highways and hedges, and warns, as Barclay did, against the use of "compel" to justify coercion.

#### The Cost of Discipleship (vv25–33)
- **"Great crowds accompanied him, and he turned and said to them" (v25).** JFB suggests the crowds may have been pilgrims heading to Jerusalem for Passover. Jesus is not pleased simply to draw a crowd. He turns and tells them the truth.
- **"If anyone comes to me and does not hate his own father and mother" (v26).** In Hebrew idiom, "love" and "hate" can mean "prefer" and "put second." Jacob "loved Rachel more than Leah," and the next verse says Leah was "hated" (Gen 29:30–31 in the Hebrew). God says "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated" (Mal 1:2–3). Matthew 10:37 gives the sense: "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me." Jesus does not cancel the command to honor parents (18:20; Mark 7:9–13). He demands first place, above family and "even his own life." No rabbi asked that. Only God can rightly claim it.
- **"Whoever does not bear his own cross and come after me" (v27).** A man carrying a cross was on a one-way road to execution. In 4 BC the Roman governor Varus crucified about two thousand Jews near Jerusalem after a revolt, according to Josephus, so the image was well known. Jesus has said it before (9:23). Here the stress is on what it means to be his disciple at all.
- **"Desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost" (v28).** A tower might be a farm watchtower over a vineyard (Isa 5:2) or a larger building. A half-built foundation would stand as a joke for years: "This man began to build and was not able to finish" (v30).
- **"What king, going out to encounter another king in war" (v31).** A king with ten thousand men facing twenty thousand must decide in time whether to fight or seek terms. Some readers see the stronger king as God, so that the point is to seek peace with him. JFB rejects that reading. The main point is the same in both pictures: think it through before starting.
- **Counting the cost.** Jesus is not putting people off. He is making sure that those who come know what they are joining. He is not asking people to clean up their lives before coming. He is asking them to come knowing that he will claim everything.
- **"Any one of you who does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple" (v33).** "Renounce" (*apotassō*) means to say goodbye to, to take leave of. Luke used it in 9:61 for saying farewell at home. Disciples hold everything as given back to them by their Lord, to be used as he directs. Some, like the rich ruler (18:22), are told to sell everything. Others, like Zacchaeus, give generously and keep the rest (19:8–9). All must let go of ownership.
- **Commentators:** JFB's verdict on the two parables: "Better not begin… than begin and not finish." Henry: "Begin low, and lay the foundation deep, lay it on the rock, and make sure work, and then aim as high as heaven," and "nothing is more shameful than for those that have begun well in religion to break off." Guzik reads "hate" as comparative, gives the Varus crucifixions as background, and warns against two errors: demanding that people clean themselves up before coming to Christ, and implying that Christ will not help them change afterward.

#### Salt Without Taste Is Worthless (vv34–35)
- **"Salt is good" (v34).** Salt seasoned and preserved food and was used in sacrifices (Lev 2:13). In small amounts it was even spread on soil as fertilizer. Jesus told his disciples they are "the salt of the earth" (Matt 5:13).
- **"If salt has lost its taste" (v34).** Pure sodium chloride does not lose its saltiness. But salt in the ancient world was often gathered from the Dead Sea area, mixed with other minerals. Damp could leach out the real salt, leaving a tasteless residue that looked like salt and did nothing. The verb (*mōrainō*) also means "to become foolish," so it can describe a disciple who has lost the very thing that made him useful.
- **"Of no use either for the soil or for the manure pile. It is thrown away" (v35).** Even as fertilizer, it is useless. A disciple who keeps the name but loses the commitment of vv26–33 is like that salt.
- **"He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (v35).** The same call as at the end of the sower parable (8:8). The words are for the crowds, and they call for a decision.
- **Commentators:** Henry applies the salt saying especially to ministers, who must keep their savor if they are to be of any use. Guzik says a believer must keep the character of Christ, the very thing that makes salt salt. JFB only refers to Matthew 5 and Mark 9. The Tyndale notes explain the Dead Sea salt mixture.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 14 shows Jesus at a table where everyone is watching him, and he uses it to describe the kingdom. He heals a man nobody wanted at the meal. He tells guests to take the lowest seat, and he is the one who would take the lowest place of all, at the cross, before God exalted him. He tells the host to invite the poor and the lame, because that is what God does: his great banquet is ready, and he sends his servant into the streets and the hedges to fill his house with people who could never pay him back. The only thing that keeps anyone out is refusing to come. And he asks for everything, father and mother and life itself, because he is giving everything himself, carrying his own cross ahead of those he calls to carry theirs.
