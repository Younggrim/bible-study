*Setting:* Amos, from Tekoa in Judah, has been preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760 BC, in the reign of Jeroboam II. Chapters 3–6 laid out the charges: injustice in the courts, luxury built on the backs of the poor, worship God hates, and pride in military success. Now the book turns to five visions (7:1–9:10), each beginning "This is what the Lord GOD showed me." The Tyndale notes suggest comparing them with the five warnings of 4:6–11. In the first two visions Amos prays and God holds back. In the third, God stops listening to prayers for Israel. Between the third and fourth visions comes the only story in the book: a confrontation at Bethel between Amos and the king's priest. It shows exactly why the judgment can no longer be turned back.

#### Warning Visions (vv1–9)
- **"This is what the Lord GOD showed me" (v1).** The prophets are sometimes called "seers" because God showed them things, and Amos's book was introduced as "the words… which he saw" (1:1). These visions come with no explanation of how they came, only what he saw.
- **"He was forming locusts" (v1).** "Forming" (*yatsar*) is the potter's word, used of God shaping Adam (Gen 2:7) and the mountains (4:13). Henry: God formed these locusts as "instruments of his wrath." Locust swarms were one of the worst disasters an ancient farmer could face. JFB says the word means young locusts, in their crawling stage, which do the most damage.
- **"When the latter growth was just beginning to sprout… after the king's mowings" (v1).** The king seems to have had the right to the first cutting of hay or grain, a kind of tax to feed his horses and army (compare 1 Kings 4:7; 18:5). The Tyndale notes say the later crop fed the farmer and his family. In Israel this "latter growth" came after the spring rains, and there would be nothing more until autumn. If locusts ate it, people would starve. JFB calls the king's share "tyrannically exacted," and Guzik notes the irony: the royal court had already taken its portion, so only the poor would suffer.
- **"O Lord GOD, please forgive! How can Jacob stand? He is so small!" (v2).** Amos the fierce preacher of judgment is also a man of prayer. Henry: "He that foretold the judgment in his preaching to the people, yet deprecated it in his intercessions for them." "Small" is striking. Israel felt powerful under Jeroboam (6:13). Before God, it was tiny and fragile. Amos does not argue that Israel deserves mercy. He appeals to its weakness. Guzik notes, drawing on Hubbard, that the name "Jacob" recalls the younger, smaller brother whom God had chosen anyway. Henry: "Jacob is small, very small already, weakened and brought low by former judgments."
- **"The LORD relented concerning this: 'It shall not be'" (v3).** God answered the prayer. "Relented" is *nacham*, as in Jonah 3:10 and Joel 2:13. Abraham (Gen 18:22–33) and Moses (Exod 32:11–14) prayed for guilty people the same way, and God listened. Henry explains: "He did not change his mind, for he is one mind and who can turn him?" JFB says the change was in the outward effect, not in God's mind. The point is that God really does answer prayer. He had planned from the start to hold back judgment through the prayers of his prophet. Guzik stresses how much depended on Amos's intercession.
- **"The Lord GOD was calling for a judgment by fire" (v4).** The second vision is worse. A fire, probably a picture of scorching drought and heat, so fierce that **"it devoured the great deep."** The "great deep" (*tehom rabbah*) is the vast underground and ocean waters, the same phrase used in the flood (Gen 7:11). The Tyndale notes suggest it is poetic for Israel's water sources. JFB reads the fire as war. If the deep dries up, the springs fail, and the land itself burns. **"And was eating up the land."** Literally "the portion," perhaps Israel's inheritance from God.
- **"O Lord GOD, please cease! How can Jacob stand? He is so small!" (v5).** This time Amos does not ask God to forgive, only to stop. He knows forgiveness is not being sought by Israel itself. Still, God relents again (v6): **"This also shall not be."**
- **"The Lord was standing beside a wall built with a plumb line, with a plumb line in his hand" (v7).** The third vision is different. No disaster, just the Lord standing by a wall holding a builder's tool. The wall was "built with a plumb line," meaning it was built straight. Israel had once been built true by God. Now God comes back to check it.
- **"Amos, what do you see?" "A plumb line" (v8).** God asks a question, as he will again in 8:2. Prophets like Jeremiah had similar visions with questions (Jer 1:11–13). The Hebrew word for "plumb line," *'anak*, appears only here in the Bible. The traditional reading is a lead weight on a string, used to test whether a wall is vertical. Many scholars now think it means "tin," from an Akkadian word, which might picture tin weapons or a wall about to be torn down. The Tyndale notes add that *'anak* sounds like *'anachah*, "groaning," hinting at the grief judgment would bring. The ESV keeps "plumb line," and its sense fits the context well: God measures Israel by his straight standard.
- **"Behold, I am setting a plumb line in the midst of my people Israel" (v8).** Israel was measured against God's straight line and found crooked, leaning, about to fall. JFB: the judgment would be measured by exact justice. Guzik says it plainly: by God's standard, Israel was crooked. Isaiah uses the same image: "I will make justice the line, and righteousness the plumb line" (Isa 28:17; compare 2 Kings 21:13).
- **"I will never again pass by them" (v8).** This is the turning point. To "pass by" means to overlook, to let something go. In the first two visions Amos prayed and God held back. Now he will not. And this time, notice, Amos does not pray. Once the plumb line shows the wall is crooked, there is nothing left to say. Amos will hear the same sentence in the next vision (8:2).
- **"The high places of Isaac shall be made desolate, and the sanctuaries of Israel shall be laid waste" (v9).** "High places" were local hilltop shrines all over the land. "Sanctuaries" means the major shrines, Bethel and Dan. The Tyndale notes say every trace of Israel's false worship, from village shrines to the royal temple, would be destroyed. "Isaac" here is spelled in an unusual way (*Yischaq*, with a different first consonant), and appears only here and in v16 as a name for the nation. Isaac means "he laughs." JFB suggests Amos may be hinting that the shrines named for the patriarchs would be laughed at. Guzik notes it recalls the nation's ancestors, and the break with the covenant past.
- **"I will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword" (v9).** This is the first time Amos names the king. Jeroboam II himself died peacefully (2 Kings 14:29). His son Zechariah reigned only six months before he was assassinated, ending the dynasty of Jehu (2 Kings 15:8–10). JFB and the Tyndale notes both point to that fulfillment. Israel then had six kings in about thirty years, four of them murdered, until Assyria finished it.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws comfort from the first two visions: God's servants may still pray even against threatened judgment, and God hears. He also insists God's "relenting" does not mean God changes his mind. JFB reads the "latter growth" as Israel's brief revival under Jeroboam, and the plumb line as exact justice reaching "the very center." Guzik emphasizes the power of Amos's prayer and his appeal to Israel's smallness. The Tyndale notes connect these visions to the five warnings of chapter 4, and point out that the third vision allows no intercession.

#### Amos Accused (vv10–17)
- **"Then Amaziah the priest of Bethel sent to Jeroboam" (v10).** This story is placed here for a reason. Right after God says he will no longer "pass by" Israel, we see why: Israel's chief priest tries to silence God's word. Amaziah was head of the royal shrine at Bethel, the shrine of the golden calf set up by Jeroboam I (1 Kings 12:28–33). JFB calls him "chief priest of the royal sanctuary to the calves at Beth-el." He sends a message to the king, probably in Samaria about twenty miles to the north.
- **"Amos has conspired against you in the midst of the house of Israel" (v10).** "Conspired" (*qashar*) is the word for a political plot or coup, used of Jehu's revolt (2 Kings 10:9) and later of the murder of Jeroboam's son (2 Kings 15:10). Amaziah accuses Amos of treason, not false teaching. Guzik notes that when people can't answer a true prophet's message, they often accuse him of sedition instead. There was a precedent: Elisha had sent a prophet to anoint Jehu, who overthrew the old dynasty (2 Kings 9). A king of Jehu's line knew prophets could topple thrones.
- **"The land is not able to bear all his words" (v10).** An unintended compliment. Amos's words were shaking the nation. It was not Amos the land could not bear, but the truth.
- **"For thus Amos has said, 'Jeroboam shall die by the sword'" (v11).** That is not quite what Amos said. Amos said the *house* of Jeroboam would fall by the sword, and he framed it as God's act, "I will rise against" (v9). Amaziah turns it into a personal threat. The Tyndale notes say he misunderstood or misrepresented Amos, who was calling for repentance, not rebellion. Amaziah also leaves out the reason for the judgment, and leaves out God.
- **"O seer, go, flee away to the land of Judah, and eat bread there, and prophesy there" (v12).** "Seer" (*chozeh*) was a respectable title (2 Sam 24:11), but here JFB says it is used with contempt. "Eat bread there" means "earn your living there." Amaziah assumes Amos is a professional who prophesies for pay, as many court prophets did (Mic 3:5, 11). The Tyndale notes suggest he thought Judah would appreciate Amos's message and pay him for it.
- **"But never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom" (v13).** This is the most revealing line in the chapter. Amaziah says Bethel belongs to the king. He never says it belongs to God. "Temple of the kingdom" is literally "house of the kingdom," the state church. The name Bethel means "house of God," but Amaziah has made it the house of the king. God's word was not welcome in it. The Tyndale notes say Amaziah was protecting his job.
- **"I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son" (v14).** The Hebrew has no verb, so it could be "I am no prophet" or "I was no prophet." Either way, Amos is not saying he isn't a prophet now, since he says in the next verse that God told him to prophesy. He means he did not belong to the professional class. "A prophet's son" means a member of the prophetic guilds, the "sons of the prophets" trained under leaders like Elijah and Elisha (2 Kings 2:3–5; 4:38). JFB: "that is, disciple." Amos had no training, no title and no salary.
- **"But I was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs" (v14).** "Herdsman" (*boqer*) comes from the word for cattle, as the Tyndale notes say, so he raised livestock. Sycamore figs grew in the warm lowlands, not in the high hills of Tekoa (1 Kings 10:27), so Amos probably worked seasonally in the Jordan Valley or the western foothills. "Dresser" (*boles*) refers to the practice of nicking or pinching each fig so it would ripen sweeter. JFB notes the fruit was like the fig "but inferior," food for the poor and for animals. The Tyndale notes add that it was often gathered for cattle feed. Amos had an honest trade. He did not need Amaziah's money.
- **"But the LORD took me from following the flock" (v15).** These words echo the call of David, whom God "took… from following the sheep" (2 Sam 7:8; Ps 78:70–71). Amos did not choose to be a prophet. God took him. Guzik notes that God called him in the middle of ordinary work, and kept using that work in his message, with all its lions, locusts and harvest carts. **"Go, prophesy to my people Israel."** Amaziah says Bethel is the king's. Amos says Israel is God's: "*my* people."
- **"Now therefore hear the word of the LORD" (v16).** Amos does not back down. He quotes Amaziah's own words back to him: **"Do not prophesy against Israel, and do not preach against the house of Isaac."** "Preach" is literally "drip," as in letting words drip or flow (Mic 2:6, 11), perhaps a mocking word for a preacher who drones on. Amaziah's command was exactly what Amos had condemned in 2:12: "you… commanded the prophets, saying, 'You shall not prophesy.'"
- **"Your wife shall be a prostitute in the city" (v17).** This is a hard prophecy. It describes what happened to the families of the powerful when a city fell. Soldiers raped the women, and women left without husbands or property were sometimes forced into prostitution to survive. JFB takes it as the wife being "forced by the enemy." God is not wishing it on her. He is announcing what the invasion Amaziah refuses to hear about will do to his own household.
- **"Your sons and your daughters shall fall by the sword, and your land shall be divided up with a measuring line" (v17).** His children will die, and his estate will be surveyed and handed out to foreigners. Assyria did exactly this, resettling conquered lands with other peoples (2 Kings 17:24).
- **"You yourself shall die in an unclean land" (v17).** For a priest, dying in a foreign land was the final irony. Foreign lands were "unclean" because they were full of idols, and food there was unclean (Hos 9:3–4). JFB notes that Israel had already polluted its own land with idols, so it would be sent to one. **"And Israel shall surely go into exile away from its land."** Amaziah had reported this line to the king as treason (v11). Amos repeats it word for word. Guzik notes the boldness: Amos would not soften the very sentence that got him into trouble.
- **What happened to Amos?** The book doesn't say. There is no evidence that he was harmed. A later tradition claims Amaziah's son killed him, but that story is very late and not reliable. Most likely Amos went home to Tekoa, and his words, written down, outlived both Amaziah and Jeroboam's dynasty.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls Amaziah a hireling priest and notes how often truth is answered with charges of disloyalty. JFB calls Bethel the place set apart "for the king's own devotions," and explains "prophet's son" as a disciple in a prophetic school. Henry: "Great pretenders to sanctity are commonly the worst enemies to those who are really sanctified." Of Amos's call he says, "God made him a prophet, and a prophet to them," and of Amaziah's sentence, "Those that cannot bear general woes may expect woes of their own." The Tyndale notes add that Amos had no financial motive and that the oracle against Amaziah came because he tried to silence God's word.

#### Pointing to Christ
Twice Amos stood between God and a guilty people and prayed, and God held back. Jesus is the greater intercessor, who "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through him (Heb 7:25), and who prayed for the very people who crucified him (Luke 23:34). The plumb line showed that Israel's wall was crooked beyond repair. Jesus is the one life that measured perfectly straight against God's line, and he took the judgment the crooked wall deserved. Like Amos, he was a working man from a small town with no rabbinic credentials (John 7:15), accused of conspiring against the government (Luke 23:2), and told by the religious authorities to stop. Like Amos, he kept speaking the word of God, and the house of God he cleansed, he called "my Father's house" (John 2:16), not the king's.
