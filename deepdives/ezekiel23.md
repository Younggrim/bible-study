*Setting:* Babylonia, probably 591–589 BC. Ezekiel is among the exiles taken from Jerusalem in 597 BC, and the city itself has only a few years left before Babylon's final siege (24:1–2). The undated messages of chapters 20–23 build one long case against Jerusalem: chapter 20 retold Israel's history as rebellion, chapter 21 drew the sword, and chapter 22 listed the city's crimes. Chapter 23 closes the case with a parable of two sisters, Oholah and Oholibah, who stand for Samaria and Jerusalem, the capitals of the northern and southern kingdoms. It follows the same marriage picture as chapter 16, but the focus shifts. Chapter 16 dwelt on idols. Chapter 23 dwells on politics: the alliances with Assyria, Babylon and Egypt that Israel and Judah trusted instead of the LORD. The language is deliberately shocking, some of the harshest in the Bible. It was meant to make a complacent people feel how God sees betrayal.

#### Oholah and Oholibah (vv1–49)
- **"There were two women, the daughters of one mother" (v2).** One mother is the one people of Israel. After Solomon died, around 930 BC, the nation split into two kingdoms: Israel in the north with its capital at Samaria, and Judah in the south with Jerusalem. The parable treats them as sisters with one family history and one husband. JFB traces the "one mother" back to Sarah.
- **"They played the whore in Egypt; they played the whore in their youth" (v3).** The unfaithfulness did not begin with the kings. It began in Egypt, before the exodus. Ezekiel has already said that Israel would not give up "the idols of Egypt" even when God was rescuing them (20:7–8), and Joshua told the people to put away the gods their fathers served "in Egypt" (Josh 24:14). The golden calf at Sinai (Exod 32) was the first public proof.
- **"Oholah… and Oholibah" (v4).** Both names are built on *'ohel*, "tent," the word used for the tabernacle, the tent where God met his people. *Oholah* means "her tent." *Oholibah* means "my tent is in her." Most commentators read the difference as a comment on worship. Samaria had a sanctuary of her own making: Jeroboam's calf shrines at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:26–33). Jerusalem had the temple God himself chose. The names sound almost alike, which fits sisters who end up living alike.
- **"They became mine, and they bore sons and daughters" (v4).** This is covenant language. God married his people at Sinai, and the "sons and daughters" are the citizens of each kingdom. Guzik notes that the picture has limits. The LORD did not literally have two wives. The story is a parable, built to show that Judah walked in the steps of Israel. The Tyndale notes add that God made his covenant in spite of his people's character, not because of it.
- **"She lusted after her lovers the Assyrians" (v5).** Israel's kings sought Assyria's favor for well over a century. The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III, now in the British Museum, shows King Jehu bowing to the ground before the Assyrian king around 841 BC. Menahem paid Assyria a thousand talents of silver to keep his throne (2 Kings 15:19–20), and Hoshea became an Assyrian vassal (2 Kings 17:3). Hosea, who preached in the north in those years, used the same picture: "Ephraim has hired lovers" (Hos 8:9).
- **"Clothed in purple, governors and commanders… horsemen riding on horses" (v6).** What attracted Samaria was not Assyria's gods at first but its glamour: the uniforms, the officers, the cavalry. Assyria was the first empire to use cavalry on a large scale. The parable describes a woman dazzled by handsome men in uniform. In politics, that meant a small kingdom dazzled by power. Guzik observes that the attraction was purely on the surface, fleshly and material, with nothing spiritual in it.
- **"She defiled herself with all the idols of everyone after whom she lusted" (v7).** Alliances in the ancient world were sealed with oaths in the names of both nations' gods, and a weaker partner often had to honor the stronger partner's gods. Political trust and spiritual adultery came as one package. The word "all" appears three times in vv6–7: she held nothing back.
- **"Therefore I delivered her into the hands of her lovers" (v9).** Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 BC, and its people were deported across the empire (2 Kings 17:5–6). The lovers she courted became the army that destroyed her. "She became a byword among women" (v10) is literally "she became a name": her fate was a warning everyone knew, more than a hundred years before Ezekiel spoke.
- **"Her sister Oholibah saw this, and she became more corrupt than her sister" (v11).** This is the heart of the charge. Jerusalem watched Samaria's fall and learned nothing. Jeremiah made the same point: "faithless Israel has shown herself more righteous than treacherous Judah" (Jer 3:11; compare Ezek 16:51). Judah had the temple, the line of David and the warning of history. More light meant more guilt.
- **"She lusted after the Assyrians" (v12).** King Ahaz of Judah sent silver and gold to Tiglath-pileser of Assyria, calling himself "your servant and your son," and then copied an altar he saw in Damascus for the temple in Jerusalem (2 Kings 16:7–18). Isaiah had begged him to trust the LORD instead (Isa 7:1–17). **"They both took the same way" (v13).**
- **"She saw men portrayed on the wall, the images of the Chaldeans portrayed in vermilion" (v14).** Babylonian palaces and temples were decorated with painted wall reliefs and glazed brick. Vermilion is a bright red pigment. Envoys from Judah would have seen such images of officers in rich belts and flowing turbans (v15). Jeremiah condemned King Jehoiakim for painting his own palace "with vermilion" (Jer 22:14). Judah's love affair with Babylon began with the eyes. Henry and the older Puritans liked to note that sin often enters by what is looked at and admired.
- **"Babylonians whose native land was Chaldea" (v15).** Abraham came from "Ur of the Chaldeans" (Gen 11:31). God called him out of that land and its gods. Now his descendants were running back to it.
- **"Sent messengers to them in Chaldea" (v16).** JFB stresses that Judah did the courting, not Babylon. Hezekiah proudly showed his treasures to envoys from Babylon (2 Kings 20:12–19), and Isaiah warned that Babylon would one day carry them off. Later kings switched back and forth between Babylon and Egypt as it suited them.
- **"After she was defiled by them, she turned from them in disgust" (v17).** This is the pattern of the last kings of Judah. Jehoiakim submitted to Nebuchadnezzar and then rebelled (2 Kings 24:1). Zedekiah swore loyalty to Babylon and then broke his oath to seek Egypt's help (17:13–15). The Hebrew verb for "turned in disgust" (*yaqa'*) pictures being wrenched loose or torn away. **"I turned in disgust from her" (v18)** uses the same word of God. She tore herself from Babylon. God tore himself from her.
- **"Remembering the days of her youth, when she played the whore in the land of Egypt" (vv19–21).** In the final years Judah turned to Egypt, hoping Pharaoh would rescue it from Babylon (Jer 37:5–8). The prophets had warned for decades against that hope: "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses" (Isa 31:1). The crude imagery of v20 deliberately mocks Egypt's reputation for power: Judah was attracted to strength, and Ezekiel describes that attraction in the coarsest terms available. The Tyndale notes observe that when Jerusalem thought of Egypt it did not remember the exodus, the great rescue, but its old sins there. JFB draws the lesson that memory can be dangerous when it lingers fondly on past sins.
- **"I will stir up against you your lovers from whom you turned in disgust" (v22).** The judgment matches the sin. The nations Judah courted will be the nations that destroy her. Feinberg, quoted by Guzik, puts it as a rule of Scripture: the source of sinful pleasure becomes the source of punishment.
- **"Pekod and Shoa and Koa" (v23).** Pekod (Akkadian *Puqudu*) was an Aramean tribe east of the Tigris that appears in Assyrian and Babylonian records, and Jeremiah names it in an oracle against Babylon (Jer 50:21). Shoa and Koa are less certain, though many scholars link them with the Sutu and Qutu, peoples of the same region. The names also make a grim wordplay. In Hebrew, *Pekod* sounds like "punishment," and *Shoa* and *Koa* sound like a cry for help and a shriek. The whole Babylonian world is coming, with the very Assyrians Judah once courted now serving in its armies.
- **"They shall judge you according to their judgments" (v24).** God hands the sentence over to Babylon, which will judge by its own brutal standards (Jer 39:5–7; 52:9–11). **"They shall cut off your nose and your ears" (v25)** reflects a real ancient punishment. Mutilation is attested in Assyrian and Egyptian law for adultery and other crimes, and Assyrian kings boasted of cutting off the noses and ears of captives. JFB notes the grim fit: the face she adorned with jewelry would be disfigured.
- **"I will direct my jealousy against you" (v25).** God's jealousy is not petty envy. It is the burning love of a husband whose covenant has been betrayed (Exod 20:5; 34:14). Babylon's fury is the instrument. God's jealousy is the reason.
- **"Thus I will put an end to your lewdness" (v27).** Judgment has a purpose. After the exile, Israel never again fell into open idolatry on the scale of the years before it. JFB observes that the captivity left the Jewish people with a lasting horror of idols.
- **"Into the hands of those whom you hate" (v28).** Love affairs built on using each other end in hatred. Wright, quoted by Guzik, points to Amnon, who hated Tamar more than he had desired her (2 Sam 13:15). The alliance was never real love. Judah wanted protection, and the empires wanted tribute.
- **"I will give her cup into your hand" (v31).** The cup is a common Old Testament picture of God's judgment, poured out and drunk to the bottom (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15–29). The short poem in vv32–34 describes the cup of Samaria passed to her sister: "deep and large," it holds so much that it brings "drunkenness and sorrow… horror and desolation." She will "drain it out, and gnaw its shards," chewing the broken pieces of the cup, then "tear [her] breasts" in grief.
- **"Because you have forgotten me and cast me behind your back" (v35).** This is the root of everything. To cast something behind the back is to treat it as worthless and get it out of sight. The same charge was laid against Jeroboam (1 Kings 14:9) and against the generation that killed the prophets (Neh 9:26).
- **"Son of man, will you judge Oholah and Oholibah?" (v36).** The last part of the chapter drops much of the picture language and states the crimes plainly, as in 20:4 and 22:2. Ezekiel acts as prosecutor. **"They have committed adultery, and blood is on their hands" (v37):** idolatry and murder, the two charges of chapter 22.
- **"They have even offered up to them for food the children whom they had borne to me" (v37).** The children were God's ("borne to me"), and they were burned as offerings to idols, especially Molech, in the Valley of Hinnom just outside Jerusalem (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31). The law named this as one of the worst of all crimes (Lev 18:21; 20:2–5).
- **"On the same day they came into my sanctuary to profane it" (vv38–39).** This is what makes the sin unbearable. People left the valley where their children had burned and walked up to the temple for worship as though nothing had happened. Jeremiah preached the same charge at the temple gate: "Will you steal, murder, commit adultery… and then come and stand before me in this house?" (Jer 7:9–10). Henry pictures them coming to God's house with their children's blood on their hands, "not to ask pardon for what they had done" but expecting to be accepted. Taylor, quoted by Guzik, notes that both sisters are charged with defiling the one sanctuary in Jerusalem, a reminder that God still counted the northern tribes as part of one people.
- **"For them you bathed yourself, painted your eyes, and adorned yourself" (v40).** The picture returns: a woman preparing to receive lovers. Women of the ancient Near East outlined their eyes with dark kohl, and Jezebel did so before Jehu arrived (2 Kings 9:30). Jeremiah used the same image of Judah dressing up for lovers who would kill her (Jer 4:30). The "messengers" sent "to men… from afar" are Judah's diplomatic missions (v16; Isa 57:9).
- **"A table spread before it on which you had placed my incense and my oil" (v41).** The incense and oil were God's gifts and belonged in his worship (16:18–19; Hos 2:8). Judah served them to her lovers. **"The sound of a carefree multitude" (v42)** suggests an idol feast, noisy and relaxed. "Drunkards… from the wilderness" follows the Hebrew margin. The written text can be read "Sabeans" (so the KJV), desert traders from Arabia. Either way, Judah would welcome anyone.
- **"Her who was worn out by adultery" (v43).** The Hebrew *balah* means "to wear out" and is used of worn-out sacks, sandals and clothes (Josh 9:4–5). Sarah used it of her own old age (Gen 18:12). The once-desirable sister is now an old prostitute, used up, and still the lovers come. Sin promised her glamour and left her worn out.
- **"Righteous men shall pass judgment on them with the sentence of adulteresses" (v45).** The law's penalty for adultery and for murder was death (Lev 20:10; Gen 9:6). Commentators differ on who the "righteous men" are. JFB takes them as the Babylonians, righteous only in carrying out God's just sentence, not in their own character. Others take the phrase as any just judge who looked at the facts. Either way, the verdict is fair.
- **"The host shall stone them and cut them down with their swords" (v47).** The legal penalty for an adulteress was stoning (16:40; Deut 22:21–24). Taylor, quoted by Guzik, points out how closely this matches a siege, with sling-stones and catapults battering the walls and fire set to the houses. Jerusalem's houses were in fact burned in 586 BC (2 Kings 25:9).
- **"That all women may take warning" (v48).** The "women" are other nations, and also every reader. Samaria became a warning Jerusalem ignored. Jerusalem now becomes a warning to everyone after her (1 Cor 10:6, 11). **"And you shall know that I am the Lord GOD" (v49).** As so often in Ezekiel, the goal of judgment is that God be known for who he is.
- **Commentators:** Henry, Guzik and JFB all read the names the same way: Samaria's worship was "of their own devising," while the temple at Jerusalem was "the place which God himself had chosen" (Henry). Henry sums up the chapter as a warning against idolatry and against "confidence in an arm of flesh, and sinful leagues and confederacies with wicked people," and draws the lesson from v11 that "those may justly expect God's judgments upon themselves who do not take warning by his judgments upon others." He notes how the adulteress keeps changing lovers, from Assyria to Babylon to Egypt. JFB calls forgetting God "the root of all sin" (v35) and says the cup is drained so greedily that "there shall be no evil left which thou shalt not taste" (v34). Guzik addresses the language directly: the coarseness was not used to entertain or to sound daring, but to shock hearers out of complacency and to show God's own revulsion, and its rarity in Scripture is part of its force. He also stresses that having the temple did not protect Jerusalem. Oholibah, "my tent is in her," fell anyway because privilege without faithfulness counts for nothing.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is a hard story about a people who kept looking for a stronger protector than God and kept being betrayed by every one they chose. It ends with a cup "of horror and desolation" (v33) passed from one guilty sister to the other and drunk to the dregs. In Gethsemane Jesus took up that image of the cup of God's judgment: "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me" (Matt 26:39). He drank the cup the unfaithful bride deserved, so that she could be washed rather than stripped. Paul describes Christ loving the church and giving himself up for her, "that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle" (Eph 5:25–27). The worn-out adulteress of Ezekiel 23 becomes, in the end, "a bride adorned for her husband" (Rev 21:2), not because she cleaned herself up but because the faithful Husband paid for her.
