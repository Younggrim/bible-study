*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapters 40–48 dealt with Cyrus, the fall of Babylon and the folly of idols, and ended with the call "Go out from Babylon" (48:20). From chapter 49 on, Cyrus and Babylon are not named again. The focus moves to two figures: the Servant of the LORD, and Zion, the mother city waiting for her children. The chapter opens with the second Servant Song (vv1–13), in which the Servant speaks for himself for the first time. Then Zion complains that God has forgotten her, and God answers with one of the most tender passages in the Bible.

#### The Servant of the LORD (vv1–7)
- **"Listen to me, O coastlands, and give attention, you peoples from afar" (v1).** In 42:1–4 God spoke *about* the Servant. Now the Servant speaks, and he addresses the far nations. His message is for the whole world.
- **"The LORD called me from the womb, from the body of my mother he named my name" (v1).** Before he was born, his calling and name were fixed. Jeremiah was told something similar (Jer 1:5), and Paul says God "set me apart before I was born" (Gal 1:15). Before Jesus was conceived, the angel told Mary and Joseph his name (Matt 1:21; Luke 1:31).
- **"He made my mouth like a sharp sword… he made me a polished arrow" (v2).** The Servant's weapon is his word. A sword cuts close up, and an arrow reaches far. Isaiah 11:4 says the coming king will strike "with the rod of his mouth." In Revelation a sharp two-edged sword comes from the mouth of the risen Christ (Rev 1:16; 19:15), and Hebrews says the word of God is "sharper than any two-edged sword" (Heb 4:12).
- **"In the shadow of his hand he hid me… in his quiver he hid me away" (v2).** "Hid" is said twice. The weapon is kept ready but out of sight until the right moment. Jesus lived about thirty years in Nazareth before his public ministry, largely unknown.
- **"You are my servant, Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (v3).** Here the Servant is called "Israel." Yet in vv5–6 his task is to bring Israel back to God, so he cannot simply be the nation. Most Christian readers, and the Tyndale notes, take him as the true Israel, the one faithful Israelite who does what the nation was called to do and failed to. Matthew sees the same pattern when he applies "Out of Egypt I called my son," said of Israel, to Jesus (Matt 2:15; Hos 11:1). Some Jewish interpreters read the whole song of the nation or of the prophet.
- **"But I said, 'I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing and vanity'" (v4).** The Servant is honest about discouragement. His work looks like a failure. "Vanity" is *tohu*, emptiness (44:9). Jesus came to his own and his own did not receive him (John 1:11). By the cross nearly all his followers had fled.
- **"Yet surely my right is with the LORD, and my recompense with my God" (v4).** The Servant does not measure his work by visible results. His "right" (*mishpat*, justice or vindication) and his reward are in God's hands. Faithfulness, not success, is what he is asked for. Peter says Jesus "continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23).
- **"He who formed me from the womb to be his servant, to bring Jacob back to him" (v5).** Part of the mission is to Israel itself, to bring God's scattered and wandering people back. "For I am honored in the eyes of the LORD, and my God has become my strength." The discouraged Servant finds his worth in God's eyes, not people's.
- **"It is too light a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob" (v6).** This is the turning point of the song. Restoring Israel is a great task, but it is "too light," too small, for this Servant. **"I will make you as a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth."** Simeon quotes this when he holds the baby Jesus: "a light for revelation to the Gentiles" (Luke 2:32). Paul and Barnabas quote it in Pisidian Antioch to explain why they are turning to the Gentiles: "For so the Lord has commanded us" (Acts 13:47). Those who belong to the Servant share his mission.
- **"To one deeply despised, abhorred by the nation, the servant of rulers" (v7).** Here is the first clear hint of what chapter 53 will spell out: the Servant will be rejected. "Abhorred by the nation" probably means by his own people. "The servant of rulers" means someone at the mercy of the powerful. Jesus stood before Annas, Caiaphas, Herod and Pilate.
- **"Kings shall see and arise; princes, and they shall prostrate themselves" (v7).** The rejected one will be honored by kings. This anticipates 52:15, "kings shall shut their mouths because of him." It rests on God's faithfulness: "because of the LORD, who is faithful… who has chosen you."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v1 of Christ named before birth ("By an angel he called him Jesus"). He calls v4 Isaiah's own complaint first, then the comfort "of all faithful ministers, when they see little success of their labours." JFB explains "Israel" in v3 by the meaning of the name, the one who strives with God and prevails (Gen 32:28), and calls the Servant "the ideal Israel, the representative man of the nation." Guzik holds that the song speaks of the Messiah. On v4 he points out that the New King James does not capitalize "I" there, treating it as the prophet's words, while he applies it to Jesus' discouragements. Guzik quotes Grogan: Israel has light but needs restoring, while the Gentiles need both light and salvation.

#### The Restoration of Israel (vv8–26)
- **"In a time of favor I have answered you; in a day of salvation I have helped you" (v8).** God answers the Servant's prayer. Paul quotes this line and adds: "Behold, now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Cor 6:2). The day promised here has arrived in Christ, and it is the time to respond.
- **"I will keep you and give you as a covenant to the people" (v8).** This repeats 42:6. The Servant himself is the covenant. He will "establish the land" and "apportion the desolate heritages," like Joshua dividing the land among the tribes (Josh 13–19). The return home is pictured as a new conquest of Canaan.
- **"Saying to the prisoners, 'Come out,' to those who are in darkness, 'Appear'" (v9).** This repeats 42:7. In Nazareth Jesus read the parallel passage, "to proclaim liberty to the captives," and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled" (Luke 4:18–21).
- **"They shall not hunger or thirst, neither scorching wind nor sun shall strike them" (v10).** The road home crosses desert, but the people are fed and watered like a flock. **"He who has pity on them will lead them, and by springs of water will guide them."** It is Psalm 23 on a national scale (40:11). Revelation quotes this verse of the great multitude from every nation: "They shall hunger no more… the sun shall not strike them… the Lamb… will guide them to springs of living water" (Rev 7:16–17).
- **"I will make all my mountains a road" (v11).** The mountains in the way are still "my mountains." God turns obstacles into roads (40:3–4).
- **"These from the north and from the west, and these from the land of Syene" (v12).** "West" is literally "from the sea," the Mediterranean. The traditional Hebrew text reads "Sinim," which some older interpreters, including JFB, took to be China. The great Isaiah scroll from the Dead Sea reads "Syene," modern Aswan on Egypt's southern border, and the ESV follows it. There was in fact a colony of Jews at Elephantine, an island at Aswan, in the 400s BC. Their letters have survived. The point either way is that God's people will come home from every direction.
- **"Sing for joy, O heavens, and exult, O earth… For the LORD has comforted his people" (v13).** The song ends where the whole section began: "Comfort, comfort my people" (40:1). Creation is called to celebrate (44:23).
- **"But Zion said, 'The LORD has forsaken me; my Lord has forgotten me'" (v14).** After the song comes Zion's honest despair. Jerusalem, pictured as a mother bereaved of her children, has heard the promises and cannot believe them. This is the same voice as 40:27, "My way is hidden from the LORD," and as Lamentations 5:20. God does not rebuke her. He answers.
- **"Can a woman forget her nursing child, that she should have no compassion on the son of her womb?" (v15).** "Compassion" (*racham*) is related to the word for womb (*rechem*). It is the love of a mother for the child she carried. That is the strongest bond people know. **"Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you."** Sadly, mothers sometimes do. During sieges mothers even ate their children (Lam 4:10). God's love is more reliable than the strongest human love. God is not called "mother" in Scripture, but he uses a mother's love to show what his own is like (66:13).
- **"Behold, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands" (v16).** "Engraved" (*chaqaq*) means cut or carved, permanent and not written in ink. In the ancient world people sometimes marked their hands with a name or sign of devotion (44:5). God does the reverse: he has Zion's name cut into his hands, where he cannot help seeing it. Christian readers have long thought of the risen Jesus showing his disciples the nail marks in his hands (John 20:20, 27).
- **"Your walls are continually before me" (v16).** Jerusalem's walls lay in ruins. God keeps them in view as an architect keeps his plans, ready to rebuild.
- **"Your builders make haste; your destroyers… go out from you" (v17).** The ESV again follows the Dead Sea scroll, "builders." The traditional Hebrew text reads "your children," a word that differs only in its vowels in Hebrew. Both fit: the returning children are the builders. Nehemiah later records the walls rebuilt in fifty-two days (Neh 6:15).
- **"You shall put them all on as an ornament; you shall bind them on as a bride does" (v18).** Zion's returning children will be like jewels on a bride. "As I live, declares the LORD" is an oath. God swears by his own life.
- **"Surely now you will be too narrow for your inhabitants" (v19).** The empty land will be too crowded. The "children of your bereavement," those born while Zion thought she had none, will complain, "The place is too narrow for me; make room" (v20).
- **"Who has borne me these? I was bereaved and barren, exiled and put away" (v21).** Zion is astonished. She thought herself a childless widow, and suddenly her home is full of children. Paul applies a parallel text (54:1) to the church, children of the "Jerusalem above," Jews and Gentiles alike (Gal 4:26–27). The early church's surprise at Gentile conversions (Acts 10:45; 15:3) fits this scene.
- **"I will lift up my hand to the nations, and raise my signal to the peoples" (v22).** God signals the nations as a commander raises a flag (11:12). They carry Zion's children home "in their arms" (literally in their bosom) and "on their shoulders," as Cyrus's decree helped the exiles return with supplies (Ezra 1:4, 6).
- **"Kings shall be your foster fathers, and their queens your nursing mothers… and lick the dust of your feet" (v23).** It is the reversal of the exile. The powers that crushed Israel will care for its children. "Lick the dust" is a vivid ancient picture of deep submission, a court gesture before a king (Ps 72:9). It is poetic language, not a literal act. **"Those who wait for me shall not be put to shame."** This echoes 40:31.
- **"Can the prey be taken from the mighty, or the captives of a tyrant be rescued?" (v24).** Zion's last objection: no one takes prey back from a warrior. The traditional Hebrew text has "captives of a righteous one," which is hard to make sense of. The ESV follows the Dead Sea scroll and ancient translations, "tyrant," matching v25.
- **"Even the captives of the mighty shall be taken… for I will contend with those who contend with you" (v25).** God himself takes up Zion's fight. Jesus used the same picture of his own work: "no one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the strong man" (Mark 3:27; Luke 11:21–22). The cross is where the strong man was bound (Col 2:15).
- **"I will make your oppressors eat their own flesh… drunk with their own blood" (v26).** It is a grim picture taken from siege warfare and civil war (9:20). The oppressors destroy themselves. The goal is that "all flesh shall know that I am the LORD your Savior, and your Redeemer, the Mighty One of Jacob."
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads v16 in the light of Jesus' nail-scarred hands. That is a devotional connection rather than what the verse meant at first, but a natural one. JFB mentions a custom of Jewish pilgrims marking their hands with a picture of the city and temple. Henry answers Zion's complaint by saying God "takes himself to be reflected upon if Zion say, The Lord has forsaken me." JFB takes Sinim as China. Guzik reports the views that it is Aswan or China, and Grogan's suggestion that Isaiah left it deliberately vague. The Dead Sea scroll's "Syene" has settled it for most modern translations. JFB sees v21 fulfilled in part in the surprise of the early church at Gentile converts. Guzik ties v24–25 to Jesus binding the strong man. Guzik also notes that v23 has been used to justify a state church and kissing the pope's feet. He and Clarke both treat the language as poetry for great honor.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Servant called from the womb, hidden like an arrow, despised by his own nation and yet honored by kings, is Jesus. He knew what it was to seem to labor in vain, and he left his vindication with his Father. Raising up Israel was "too light" a thing for him. He came to be "a light for the nations," and through his followers his salvation is reaching "the end of the earth" (Acts 13:47). To everyone who feels forgotten, his answer is the scarred hands of the risen Lord: "I will not forget you."
