*Setting:* James, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, is writing to Jewish believers scattered outside Judea, probably in the 40s or 50s AD. Chapter 3 ended by contrasting two kinds of wisdom: one "earthly, unspiritual, demonic," full of jealousy and selfish ambition, and one "from above," pure and peaceable, which yields "a harvest of righteousness… sown in peace by those who make peace" (3:15–18). Chapter 4 picks up right where that left off. If wisdom from above makes peace, why are these churches full of fighting? James traces the quarrels to their source, calls the double-minded back to God, warns against speaking against one another, and then turns to merchants making confident plans as if tomorrow belonged to them.

#### Warning Against Worldliness (vv1–10)
- **"What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you?" (v1).** The Greek words are military: *polemoi* and *machai*, "wars and battles." James is not describing a polite disagreement over the carpet. These are bitter, ongoing feuds inside the church, the opposite of the "peace" that closed chapter 3.
- **"Your passions are at war within you" (v1).** "Passions" is *hēdonē*, "pleasure," the word English borrows for "hedonism." James uses it again in v3, so it frames the paragraph. The war *between* believers comes from a war *inside* each of them: desires for pleasure that act like an army camped in the body ("in your members," literally). Paul describes the same inner battle (Rom 7:23; Gal 5:17), and Peter speaks of "passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul" (1 Pet 2:11).
- **"You desire and do not have, so you murder" (v2).** The word is shocking, and James means it to be. Some scholars think a few readers had been drawn into the violent Zealot movement of the time, when Jewish nationalists were assassinating opponents. Most read it in light of Jesus' teaching that anger is murder in the heart (Matt 5:21–22), which James has echoed before (1:20; 3:6). Either way, the line runs from wanting, to not getting, to destroying the person in the way.
- **"You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight and quarrel" (v2).** "Covet" here is *zēloō*, the root of the "jealousy" (*zēlos*) James condemned in 3:14, 16. Jealousy is the thread tying the two chapters together.
- **"You do not have, because you do not ask" (v2).** A quiet sentence in the middle of a storm. Instead of grasping, they could have asked God, the one "who gives generously to all without reproach" (1:5). Jesus said, "Ask, and it will be given to you" (Matt 7:7), and James is likely echoing that.
- **"You ask wrongly, to spend it on your passions" (v3).** Some did pray, but their prayers were just another way to feed the same desires. "Spend" is the verb used of the prodigal son, who "squandered" his inheritance (Luke 15:14), and "passions" is *hēdonē* again. Prayer is not a tool for getting God to fund our cravings. John says we receive when we ask "according to his will" (1 John 5:14).
- **"You adulterous people!" (v4).** Literally "adulteresses" (*moichalides*), feminine. The Old Testament prophets pictured God as Israel's husband and her idolatry as adultery (Jer 3:6–10; Ezek 16; Hosea 1–3), and Jesus called his own generation "adulterous" (Matt 12:39). The church is the bride, and friendship with the world is unfaithfulness to her Husband. Later manuscripts read "adulterers and adulteresses," probably because a scribe took the word literally and added the masculine. The oldest manuscripts have only the feminine, which the ESV follows with "adulterous people."
- **"Friendship with the world is enmity with God" (v4).** "The world" here is not the planet or the people in it, whom God loves (John 3:16). It is human life organized without God: its values, ambitions and way of measuring worth (1 John 2:15–17). In the ancient world "friend" could mean an exclusive alliance, as when Pilate was called "Caesar's friend" (John 19:12). Abraham was "a friend of God" (2:23). James says one cannot hold both alliances, just as Jesus said one cannot serve God and money (Matt 6:24). Note the wording: "whoever *wishes* to be a friend of the world." The choice of loyalty, not only the act, puts a person on the wrong side.
- **"He yearns jealously over the spirit that he has made to dwell in us" (v5).** This is one of the hardest verses in the letter, for two reasons. First, no Old Testament verse says these words. James seems to be summing up a theme of Scripture rather than quoting one line, the theme of God as a jealous God who will not share his people (Exod 20:5; 34:14; Deut 32:16). Second, the Greek can be read several ways, and the ESV footnotes show them.
- **Three readings of v5.** (1) *God yearns jealously over the human spirit he placed in us* (the ESV text): God made us and wants us wholly for himself, which fits the adultery picture of v4 best. (2) *The Holy Spirit, whom God made to dwell in us, yearns jealously* for our undivided love, as many older English versions read it. (3) *The spirit God placed in us yearns enviously.* "Jealously" is literally "toward envy" (*pros phthonon*), and everywhere else in the New Testament *phthonos* is a bad word, the "envy" that handed Jesus over to Pilate (Matt 27:18). On this reading James is saying the human spirit leans toward envy, which is why we need the "more grace" of v6. Careful scholars hold all three. The first two are close in meaning: God is a jealous lover of his people. The verb "yearns" (*epipotheō*) is a word of deep longing, used of Paul's longing for his friends (Phil 1:8) and of a newborn's longing for milk (1 Pet 2:2).
- **"But he gives more grace" (v6).** Whatever the exact sense of v5, the answer is the same. God's demand is total, and so is his supply. Where his jealousy might seem crushing, his grace is greater.
- **"God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (v6).** A quotation of Proverbs 3:34 in its Greek (Septuagint) form. Peter quotes the same verse (1 Pet 5:5), and both then say "humble yourselves" and "resist the devil," which suggests this was a familiar early Christian pattern of teaching. "Opposes" is a military word, to line up in battle against. Pride sets God himself against a person.
- **Ten commands in four verses (vv7–10).** The Greek has a run of sharp, urgent imperatives: submit, resist, draw near, cleanse, purify, be wretched, mourn, weep, let your laughter be turned, humble yourselves. It reads like an Old Testament prophet's call to repentance (Joel 2:12–13).
- **"Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (v7).** Submission comes first. James tied jealous, selfish wisdom to the demonic in 3:15, so it is no surprise that the devil appears here. The promise is plain: the devil is not stronger than a believer who stands under God (Eph 6:11–13; 1 Pet 5:8–9). Jesus resisted him with Scripture in the wilderness, and he left (Matt 4:11).
- **"Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you" (v8).** The language of the temple, where priests "drew near" to God (Exod 19:22). Under the old covenant only priests could come close, after washing. Now the call goes to sinners. This is not a bargain where we earn his presence. It is an invitation, and a promise that he is willing (Zech 1:3; Mal 3:7; Heb 10:22).
- **"Cleanse your hands, you sinners, and purify your hearts, you double-minded" (v8).** Hands stand for what we do, hearts for what we want (Ps 24:3–4: "He who has clean hands and a pure heart"). "Double-minded" is *dipsychos*, literally "two-souled," the same rare word James used of the doubting man in 1:8. The divided loyalty of v4 is now named for what it is.
- **"Let your laughter be turned to mourning" (v9).** James is not against joy. He opened the letter with "count it all joy" (1:2). He is against the careless laughter of people who do not see their sin. Jesus said, "Blessed are those who mourn" and "woe to you who laugh now" (Matt 5:4; Luke 6:25). Godly grief over sin leads to repentance and life (2 Cor 7:10).
- **"Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you" (v10).** The section ends where v6 began. The way up is down, which is the shape of Jesus' own life (Phil 2:8–9) and a saying he repeated often (Luke 14:11; 18:14).
- **Commentators:** All three see the inner war of v1 as the root of the outer quarrels. Henry: "Worldly-mindedness is enmity to God," and he adds that "an enemy may be reconciled, but enmity never can be reconciled," meaning the love of the world itself must go. On v4 Guzik and JFB both note that the oldest manuscripts read only "adulteresses." JFB says of v1 that the passions are like soldiers "encamped within" the soul. On v5 they differ. Guzik takes it as the Holy Spirit's jealous longing for whole-hearted devotion. JFB reads it as the human spirit inclined to envy, drawn from the general sense of several passages, and thinks the "jealous Spirit" reading is a stretch. Guzik's illustration for v8 is memorable: when God seems distant, he is not the one who moved. JFB reads "he will flee" as a firm divine promise, and calls pride "peculiarly satanic."

#### Do Not Judge One Another (vv11–12)
- **"Do not speak evil against one another, brothers" (v11).** The verb is *katalaleō*, literally "to talk down," to run someone down behind their back or to their face. Peter uses the same word for pagans slandering Christians (1 Pet 2:12). James has already spent a whole chapter on the tongue (3:1–12). Here he names one of its commonest sins inside the church. Note "brothers": he softens his tone after the hard words of vv1–10, but the point is just as sharp.
- **"Speaks evil against the law and judges the law" (v11).** The law says, "You shall not go around as a slanderer among your people… you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev 19:16–18). James called that command "the royal law" (2:8). A person who runs down a brother is in effect saying that law does not apply to him. He sets himself above it, as its judge rather than its doer, and "doer" is one of James's key words (1:22–25).
- **"There is only one lawgiver and judge" (v12).** The Greek puts "one" first for emphasis. The one who wrote the law is the only one with the right to pass final sentence under it, and he alone "is able to save and to destroy" (Deut 32:39; Matt 10:28). The tone echoes Jesus: "Judge not, that you be not judged" (Matt 7:1).
- **"But who are you to judge your neighbor?" (v12).** Paul asks the same question in Romans 14:4. James is not forbidding all moral discernment. He will soon tell believers to bring a wandering brother back (5:19–20), and Jesus told his followers to recognize false prophets by their fruit (Matt 7:15–20). What he forbids is condemning speech: the critical, superior spirit that takes God's seat.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Our lips must be governed by the law of kindness, as well as truth and justice." He ties the evil-speaking to the same pride that caused the fighting. JFB says it "springs from the same self-exalting spirit" as the quarrels of v1, and notes that the best manuscripts read "lawgiver *and judge*" and "who are you to judge your *neighbor*," as the ESV has it. Guzik connects these verses to 1 John 4:20: a person who claims to love God while despising a brother is deceiving himself.

#### Boasting About Tomorrow (vv13–17)
- **"Come now, you who say" (v13).** The phrase (*age nun*) is a sharp call to attention, used again in 5:1 for the rich. James now addresses traveling merchants. Jewish traders were spread across the Roman world, and long trading trips were a common way to make money. The plan in v13 is detailed: a time ("today or tomorrow"), a place ("such and such a town"), a length ("a year") and a goal ("make a profit").
- **What is wrong with the plan?** Not the planning or the business. Scripture praises the diligent planner (Prov 21:5; 31:16). The fault is the assumption underneath: that the future is theirs to arrange. God is missing from every clause. Proverbs 27:1 says, "Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring."
- **"What is your life? For you are a mist" (v14).** "Mist" is *atmis*, vapor or steam, the puff of breath on a cold morning or the steam rising off a pot. It appears and is gone. Scripture often pictures human life this way (Job 7:7; Ps 39:5; 102:3). Jesus told of a rich man who planned bigger barns and died that night: "Fool! This night your soul is required of you" (Luke 12:16–21). James's merchants are in the same danger.
- **"If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that" (v15).** Both living and doing depend on God. Paul spoke this way himself (Acts 18:21; 1 Cor 4:19; 16:7). Christians later wrote "D.V." on letters and notices, from the Latin *Deo volente*, "God willing." James is not handing out a phrase to tack on. He wants a heart that actually holds plans with an open hand. A person can say "if the Lord wills" and still be proud, and another can make plans without the words and be truly humble.
- **"You boast in your arrogance. All such boasting is evil" (v16).** "Arrogance" (*alazoneia*) is the pretentious boasting of someone claiming more than they have. John uses the same word for "the pride of life" (1 John 2:16). It is the opposite of the humility that vv6–10 just called for. James can speak well of the right kind of boasting, the poor brother boasting in his exaltation (1:9).
- **"Whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, for him it is sin" (v17).** This sounds like a proverb, perhaps a known saying James applies. It sums up the whole letter: knowing without doing is not enough (1:22–25; 2:14–17). The merchants know life is short and God is sovereign. If they keep living as if they don't, that is sin. Sins of omission are as real as sins of action (Luke 12:47; Matt 25:41–46).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Our times are not in our own hands, but at the disposal of God," and on v17, "Omissions are sins which will be brought into judgment, as well as commissions." Guzik insists James is not against planning, only against planning that leaves God out. He sees proud independence as the root of sin from Lucifer and Adam onward. JFB notes the manuscripts differ slightly in vv13–15 ("today *and* tomorrow"; "we shall *both* live and do") without changing the sense. He warns that good feelings never acted on "evaporate," so conviction must be acted on to stay alive.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every warning in this chapter finds its opposite in Jesus. He did not grasp at what he wanted, but "emptied himself" and "humbled himself" to the point of death, and God "highly exalted him" (Phil 2:7–9), the very pattern of v10. He refused the devil's offer of "all the kingdoms of the world," the ultimate friendship with the world, and the devil left him (Matt 4:8–11). He lived every day as "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42), the perfect "if the Lord wills." And he is the one Lawgiver and Judge of v12 who is "able to save." That is why the call to "draw near to God" can be made to sinners and the double-minded: Jesus has opened the way, and the grace God gives is always "more."
