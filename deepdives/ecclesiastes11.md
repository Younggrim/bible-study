*Setting:* The Preacher is nearing the end of his search. Chapter 10 was a string of proverbs about wisdom and folly, leaders and laziness. Chapter 11 turns to how to live when the future cannot be known. He has said again and again that "man does not know" what is coming (8:7; 9:12; 10:14). Some would take that as a reason to hold back, play it safe and wait for certainty. He draws the opposite conclusion: because no one knows, be generous, take reasonable risks, work steadily, and enjoy the light while it lasts. The second half of the chapter speaks to the young, and it flows straight into the great poem on old age and death in chapter 12. The ESV heading for 11:1–10 is "Cast Your Bread upon the Waters." For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### Cast Your Bread upon the Waters (vv1–10)
- **"Cast your bread upon the waters, for you will find it after many days" (v1).** This is one of the best-known lines in the book, and it has been read in two main ways. Some take it as trade: send your grain overseas by ship, and in time it comes back as profit. Solomon's fleets did just that (1 Kgs 10:22). Guzik leans this way, with Eaton's note that ancient trade ran on trust. Others take it as generosity: give freely, even when the gift seems thrown away, and it will come back. Jewish tradition and many Christian interpreters read it so, and Matthew Henry, Trapp and Clarke follow. JFB mentions the custom of sowing seed from boats onto the Nile floodwaters, but that practice is not well attested and few modern scholars accept it. Either reading teaches the same posture: let go of what you have, without a guarantee, and trust that it is not wasted.
- **"Give a portion to seven, or even to eight" (v2).** "Seven, or even eight" is a Hebrew way of saying "as many as you can, and then one more" (Prov 30:15–18; Amos 1:3; Mic 5:5). If v1 is about trade, this is diversifying: don't put all your goods on one ship. If it is about generosity, it means be generous widely. Both fit the reason given: "you know not what disaster may happen on earth." The Tyndale notes take vv1–2 as a call to invest resources instead of hoarding them, even when outcomes cannot be controlled. Jesus told his disciples to "make friends for yourselves by means of unrighteous wealth," so that when it fails they are welcomed in (Luke 16:9).
- **"If the clouds are full of rain, they empty themselves on the earth" (v3).** Some things simply happen according to the way God made the world. A full cloud rains. No one can stop it or bring it on early.
- **"Where the tree falls, there it will lie" (v3).** Some things, once done, cannot be undone. A fallen tree does not get back up. Jerome and some later preachers read this as a picture of death: as a person dies, so he stays, with no second chance after death. JFB takes it that way. Guzik passes on Wright's caution that the verse itself does not teach that, though other passages speak to the question (Heb 9:27; Luke 16:26). In context the point is the limits of human control.
- **"He who observes the wind will not sow, and he who regards the clouds will not reap" (v4).** A farmer sowing seed by hand had good reason to watch the wind, since a strong wind blew the seed away. At harvest, rain could ruin cut grain. But a farmer who waits for perfect conditions will never plant or reap at all. Guzik passes on Meyer's picture of letting a whole autumn go by while waiting for ideal weather, and Spurgeon's warning that endless hesitation can mask unbelief, fear or laziness. Proverbs agrees: "The sluggard does not plow in the autumn" (Prov 20:4).
- **"As you do not know the way the spirit comes to the bones in the womb" (v5).** The Hebrew word *ruach* means both "wind" and "spirit." The ESV puts "spirit" in the text and "wind" in the footnote. Some translations make it two separate examples: no one knows the path of the wind, or how bones grow in the womb (NIV, KJV). Either way, the link to v4's "wind" is deliberate. How a child is formed was a deep mystery to the ancient world, and remains a wonder even now. The psalmist praised God for knitting him together "in my mother's womb" (Ps 139:13–16).
- **"So you do not know the work of God who makes everything" (v5).** If people cannot understand how a baby is formed, they cannot expect to understand all God is doing. This repeats the conclusion of 3:11 and 8:17. Guzik connects it to Jesus' words to Nicodemus: "The wind blows where it wishes... So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit" (John 3:8). The same Greek word, like the Hebrew, means both wind and spirit.
- **"In the morning sow your seed, and at evening withhold not your hand" (v6).** Work from morning to evening, keep at it, because "you do not know which will prosper, this or that, or whether both alike will be good." Not knowing is a reason to work harder, not to quit. Paul uses the same farming picture: "whoever sows bountifully will also reap bountifully" (2 Cor 9:6), and "let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap" (Gal 6:9). The Talmud read "sow your seed" as having children. Guzik passes on Eaton's judgment that this doesn't fit the context. JFB reads morning and evening as early and late in life.
- **"Light is sweet, and it is pleasant for the eyes to see the sun" (v7).** A rare, simple delight in this book. "Under the sun" has usually meant life cut off from heaven's view. Here the sun is just good. To see the sun means to be alive (Job 3:16; Ps 58:8). Life itself is a gift to be enjoyed.
- **"If a person lives many years, let him rejoice in them all" (v8).** All of them, not just the good ones, and not just the young ones. But "let him remember that the days of darkness will be many." The "days of darkness" are old age and, beyond that, death, the long dark of Sheol (Job 10:21–22). Remembering them is not meant to spoil joy now. It is a reason to take joy now. "All that comes is vanity" (*hebel*), a vapor, as brief as breath.
- **"Rejoice, O young man, in your youth" (v9).** He now speaks directly to the young. Enjoy these years. "Let your heart cheer you" means let your heart be glad. "Walk in the ways of your heart and the sight of your eyes" sounds surprising, because Numbers 15:39 warns Israel not to follow "after your own heart and your own eyes." But the next line sets the boundary.
- **"But know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment" (v9).** This is not a threat meant to kill joy. It is the frame that makes joy safe. Enjoy youth as someone who will give account to God. The Tyndale notes say young people should enjoy their youth fully while remembering they will answer to God. JFB goes further and reads the first half as irony, "not advice, but warning," as if to say, "Go on, follow your heart, but know the judgment is coming." Most interpreters think the encouragement to joy is sincere, since the Preacher has commended joy all through the book (2:24; 3:12; 5:18; 8:15; 9:7). Judgment is a theme he returns to at the very end (3:17; 12:14).
- **"Remove vexation from your heart, and put away pain from your body" (v10).** "Vexation" is the word for grief or irritation in 1:18 and 7:3. Put away what troubles the heart and harms the body. Some read "pain" (literally "evil") as the sins of youth that damage the body. JFB takes it as the lusts that lead to sorrow. Others read it as needless anxiety. The ESV's wording allows both. The young should not waste their best years in worry or in self-destruction.
- **"For youth and the dawn of life are vanity" (v10).** "Dawn of life" (*shacharut*) is from a word for dawn, though some connect it with "blackness," the black hair of youth before it turns gray. Either way, youth is *hebel*, a breath. It passes quickly. That is exactly why the next verse says, "Remember also your Creator in the days of your youth" (12:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v1 as charity: "Give freely, though it may seem thrown away and lost. It is not lost, but well laid out." On sowing he adds, "Wherever we are, we may find good work to do, if we have but hearts to do it." On vv7–8, "Here is a caution to think of death, even when life is most sweet." Guzik reads vv1–2 as a business venture and passes on Spurgeon's picture of full clouds pouring out rain as Christ pouring himself out for his people. JFB calls "seven" "the perfect number" and "eight" a call to give to many more, and says "life is enjoyable, especially to the godly." The Tyndale notes sum up vv1–6: every worthwhile activity carries risk, so act wisely and trust God with the outcome.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Preacher says to cast bread on the waters and trust it will be found "after many days." Jesus lived that generosity to the full. He gave himself away, and his life looked lost, a grain of wheat falling into the earth to die. But "if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). The Preacher does not know "the work of God who makes everything" (v5). Jesus told Nicodemus that the Spirit, like the wind, blows where he wills, bringing new birth that no one can explain (John 3:8). And the judgment that the young are warned of (v9) is the judgment Christ himself will carry out (John 5:22; Acts 17:31), and has already borne for those who trust him. So believers can rejoice in their days, light and dark alike, without fear: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Rom 8:1).
