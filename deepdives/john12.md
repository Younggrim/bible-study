*Setting:* The apostle John, writing late in the first century, reaches the turning point of his Gospel. The raising of Lazarus (ch 11) has made Jesus more popular than ever and moved the Sanhedrin to plan his death, and he has withdrawn to Ephraim. Now, about AD 30 or 33, he returns for his last Passover. Bethany sits about two miles east of Jerusalem on the far slope of the Mount of Olives, and the road into the city was crowded with pilgrims. Chapter 12 closes Jesus' public ministry. After it he speaks only to his disciples (chs 13–17) until his arrest. John sets three scenes side by side, an anointing, a royal entry and the arrival of Gentiles, then explains why so many still did not believe and records Jesus' last public appeal.

#### Mary Anoints Jesus at Bethany (vv1–8)
- **"Six days before the Passover" (v1).** John counts down to the cross. Depending on how the days are reckoned, this is the Friday or Saturday before Passover week, and the next day's entry (v12) is the Sunday Christians call Palm Sunday. The word "therefore" ties the visit to ch 11: Jesus walks back into the region where a death warrant is waiting.
- **"Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those reclining with him" (v2).** The family is just as Luke 10:38–42 drew them: Martha busy serving, Mary at Jesus' feet. Now Martha serves without complaint, and Lazarus, who was four days dead, sits at the table as living proof. Guests at a formal meal lay on couches, propped on the left elbow with their feet stretched away from the low table, which is how Mary could reach his feet.
- **"A pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard" (v3).** The "pound" (*litra*) was a Roman pound, about 11–12 ounces, a large amount. Nard was an aromatic oil from a plant of the Himalayas, carried to the Mediterranean at great cost. "Pure" translates *pistikos*, a rare word found only here and in Mark 14:3. The lexicon gives "trustworthy, genuine," which fits perfume that had not been watered down, though some scholars think it may name a variety of plant. Either way, this was the real thing.
- **"Wiped his feet with her hair" (v3).** A respectable Jewish woman did not let her hair down in front of men, and the feet were the lowliest part of the body, the part a slave washed. Mary sets aside both her dignity and her savings. Mark 14:3 and Matthew 26:7 say the woman poured the oil on his head. John mentions the feet. Both are likely true of one generous act. Luke 7:36–50 tells a different anointing, earlier in Galilee, by a sinful woman in a Pharisee's house.
- **"The house was filled with the fragrance" (v3).** A small detail from someone who was there. Mark 14:9 adds Jesus' promise that the act would be told wherever the gospel is preached, and in a sense the fragrance has filled the world.
- **"Why was this ointment not sold for three hundred denarii?" (v5).** A denarius was a laborer's daily wage, so three hundred was close to a year's income once Sabbaths and feast days are left out. Matthew and Mark say several disciples grumbled. John names the one who started it.
- **"He was a thief" (v6).** Only John gives this. Judas kept the "moneybag" (*glōssokomon*, originally a box for a musician's mouthpieces, later any money box) and "used to help himself" to it. The verb can mean "carry" or "carry off," and here it clearly means taking. His concern for the poor was cover. The two disciples are set side by side: Mary gives everything to Jesus, and Judas takes what he can from him. Mark tells the anointing and then, in the very next verse, has Judas go to the chief priests (Mark 14:10).
- **"So that she may keep it for the day of my burial" (v7).** The Greek is compressed and hard to render. It may mean she kept the perfume until now for this, or that her act anticipates his burial. Either way, Jesus reads her gift as preparation for his death. Whether Mary fully understood is not said, but she seems to have grasped more than the Twelve, who still expected a throne.
- **"The poor you always have with you" (v8).** This echoes Deuteronomy 15:11, where the very next words command open-handed generosity to the poor. Jesus is not dismissing the poor. He is saying that this moment, with him, before his death, will not come again.
- **Commentators:** All three contrast Mary and Judas. Guzik notices that Mary went to the feet, not the head, a mark of humility, and suggests she understood his coming death because she had spent time listening at those feet. JFB calls Judas "treasurer to Jesus and the Twelve," a trust that hid his character for a time. Matthew Henry frames the whole chapter as a series of honors paid to Jesus in his humiliation, beginning with how "Mary did him honour, by anointing his feet at the supper in Bethany."

#### The Plot to Kill Lazarus (vv9–11)
- **"Not only on account of him but also to see Lazarus" (v9).** A man raised from the dead was the talk of the festival. JFB's phrase captures it: they came "to see dead Lazarus alive."
- **"The chief priests made plans to put Lazarus to death as well" (v10).** The chief priests were mostly Sadducees, who denied the resurrection (Acts 23:8). A living Lazarus was a walking refutation of their theology and a magnet drawing people to Jesus. Their solution was to kill the evidence. Jesus had said, in the story of the rich man and Lazarus, that some would not be persuaded "even if someone should rise from the dead" (Luke 16:31). Here a man named Lazarus has risen, and the response is a second murder plot.
- **"Many of the Jews were going away and believing in Jesus" (v11).** "Going away" may mean leaving the leaders' side, the authority they had followed until now. A miracle cannot force faith, but it does force a choice.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws out the Sadducee angle: the living man contradicted what the priests taught, so they wanted him gone. JFB puts it plainly that they meant to kill Lazarus to stop the movement toward Jesus. The lesson Henry and Guzik both draw is that those whom Christ raises share in the hostility aimed at him.

#### The Triumphal Entry (vv12–19)
- **"The next day" (v12).** All four Gospels tell this story (Matt 21:1–11; Mark 11:1–11; Luke 19:29–40). John's version is the shortest and adds what the others don't: the crowd came out from the city because of Lazarus (vv17–18), and the disciples understood it only later (v16).
- **"Branches of palm trees" (v13).** Only John mentions palms. Palms grew around Jericho rather than Jerusalem, and they had become a symbol of Jewish victory and independence. When Simon Maccabeus retook the Jerusalem citadel in 141 BC, the people entered "with praise and palm branches" (1 Macc 13:51), and later Jewish rebels put palms on their coins. Waving palms was close to waving a national flag.
- **"Went out to meet him" (v13).** The word for meeting (*hypantēsis*) was used for a city's leading citizens going out to escort a visiting king or general into town.
- **"Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord" (v13).** The words come from Psalm 118:25–26, part of the Hallel psalms sung at the great feasts. "Hosanna" is Hebrew *hoshi'a na*, "Save, please!" By this time it was also a shout of praise, but the original plea is still in it. "Even the King of Israel" is the crowd's addition. Nathanael had called Jesus that at the start (1:49), and the title will be nailed above the cross (19:19).
- **"Jesus found a young donkey and sat on it" (v14).** The other Gospels tell how the animal was fetched. John goes straight to its meaning. Conquerors rode war horses. Jesus answers the palm-waving crowd with an animal of peace, the mount of the humble king in Zechariah 9:9–10, who will "cut off the chariot" and "speak peace to the nations." Solomon rode David's mule to his coronation (1 Kings 1:33–40), so a king on such an animal was not absurd, but it was not a warrior's arrival.
- **"Fear not, daughter of Zion" (v15).** Zechariah begins "Rejoice greatly." John's "Fear not" may come from similar promises (Isa 40:9; Zeph 3:14–16, where "the King of Israel, the LORD, is in your midst"). John often blends Old Testament texts this way. The point is the same: Zion's king is coming, and he comes to save, not to crush.
- **"When Jesus was glorified, then they remembered" (v16).** John is honest that the disciples did not get it at the time (compare 2:22). Only after the cross, the resurrection and the Spirit's coming (14:26) did they see how the Scriptures fit. Understanding often comes looking back.
- **"The crowd that had been with him… continued to bear witness" (v17).** Two crowds meet: those who saw Lazarus raised, and the festival crowd coming out from the city. The testimony of eyewitnesses spreads the news.
- **"Look, the world has gone after him" (v19).** The Pharisees mean "everybody" in a fit of exaggeration. John hears more: Jesus came for "the world" (3:16–17), and in the very next verse the world arrives in the form of Greeks. Like Caiaphas in 11:50, the enemies say more than they know.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the palms and the "Hosanna" as hopes for political rescue from Rome, which Jesus redirects with the donkey of Zechariah 9:9. JFB notes that by one reckoning this was the tenth of Nisan, the day each household set apart its Passover lamb (Exod 12:3), though the dating is not certain. JFB skips comment on vv13–15 and vv17–19. Henry counts the crowd's welcome as one more honor done to Christ while he was "in his humiliation."

#### Some Greeks Seek Jesus (vv20–26)
- **"Some Greeks" (v20).** These were Gentiles (*Hellēnes*), not Greek-speaking Jews, who are called by a different word in Acts 6:1. They had come "to worship at the feast," so they were God-fearers or converts who worshiped Israel's God. They could go only as far as the Court of the Gentiles in the temple. JFB agrees: "Not Grecian Jews, but Greek proselytes to the Jewish faith."
- **"They came to Philip, who was from Bethsaida" (v21).** Philip and Andrew are the only two of the Twelve with Greek names, and Bethsaida lay near the Gentile region east of the Jordan. "Sir, we wish to see Jesus" is a request that has been carved on many pulpits since, a reminder of what listeners really want.
- **"Philip went and told Andrew" (v22).** Andrew is always bringing people to Jesus: his brother Peter (1:41), the boy with the loaves (6:8–9) and now these Greeks.
- **"The hour has come" (v23).** Until now Jesus has said his hour had "not yet come" (2:4; 7:30; 8:20). The arrival of the Gentiles sets the clock. The "hour" is his death, resurrection and return to the Father, which John calls his glorification. John never tells us whether the Greeks got their meeting. Jesus' answer is that they will "see" him best from the cross, when he draws all people (v32).
- **"Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies" (v24).** A seed kept in the jar stays one seed. Buried, it breaks open and becomes a plant full of grain. Jesus' death will not end his work but multiply it, and the harvest will include Greeks. Paul uses the same picture for resurrection (1 Cor 15:36–37).
- **"Whoever hates his life… will keep it" (v25).** "Hate" is a Hebrew way of saying "prefer less" (compare Luke 14:26 with Matt 10:37). The word for life here is *psychē*, one's earthly life and self. The word for "eternal life" is *zōē*. Clutching the one loses the other. This saying appears in some form in all four Gospels, more than almost any other.
- **"Where I am, there will my servant be also" (v26).** Following Jesus means going where he goes, and he is going to a cross. But it also means being honored by the Father. The servant who walks the path shares its end (17:24).
- **Commentators:** Guzik says the hour of glory is the cross, which the world saw as shame, and explains "hating" one's life as holding it loosely compared with following Christ. JFB reads Jesus' answer as saying his death would open salvation to the Gentiles. Henry counts the Greeks' "longing desire to see him" as another honor and observes that they asked to see Jesus, not the feast or the temple.

#### The Son of Man Must Be Lifted Up (vv27–36a)
- **"Now is my soul troubled" (v27).** The verb *tarassō* means stirred, shaken, agitated. It is the word used of Jesus at Lazarus's tomb (11:33) and when he speaks of his betrayer (13:21). John has no Gethsemane prayer scene, but this is its equivalent. The words echo Psalm 6:3 and 42:6. Jesus is not calm about the cross. He feels its horror fully.
- **"'Father, save me from this hour'? But for this purpose I have come" (v27).** Most translations take the first prayer as a question Jesus considers and sets aside. Some read it as a real prayer, like "let this cup pass" (Mark 14:36), followed by submission. Either way, he chooses the Father's glory over his own escape.
- **"Father, glorify your name" (v28).** This is the heart of the Lord's Prayer ("hallowed be your name") prayed under pressure.
- **"A voice came from heaven" (v28).** The Synoptic Gospels record the Father's voice at the baptism and the transfiguration. This is the third time, and the only one in John. "I have glorified it" looks back on Jesus' life and signs. "I will glorify it again" looks ahead to the cross and resurrection.
- **"It had thundered… an angel has spoken" (v29).** The same sound, heard three ways. Some heard noise. Some heard something spiritual. Only those with ears for it heard words. Paul's companions on the Damascus road had a similar experience (Acts 9:7; 22:9).
- **"This voice has come for your sake" (v30).** Jesus did not need reassurance. The crowd needed to know the Father stood behind him.
- **"Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out" (v31).** "The ruler of this world" is Satan (14:30; 16:11). At the cross the world judges Jesus, but in truth the world is judged and its ruler thrown out. The cross looks like Satan's victory and is his defeat (Col 2:15; Heb 2:14).
- **"When I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself" (v32).** The verb *hypsoō* means "lift up" and also "exalt" (Acts 2:33). John uses it three times of Jesus (3:14; 8:28; 12:32), and v33 confirms he means the cross. The cross is the Son's lowest point and his throne at once. Isaiah 52:13 in the Greek Old Testament says the Lord's Servant will be "lifted up and glorified," the same two verbs, at the start of the song John quotes in v38. "All people" does not mean every person will be saved. It means people of every kind, Greeks included, not Jews only.
- **"We have heard from the Law that the Christ remains forever" (v34).** "The Law" here means the Scriptures broadly. They may have had Psalm 89:36 ("his offspring shall endure forever") or Isaiah 9:7 in mind. A Messiah who dies made no sense to them. "Who is this Son of Man?" is the right question asked in the wrong spirit.
- **"The light is among you for a little while longer" (v35).** Jesus does not argue. He warns. The light will not stay forever. "Believe in the light, that you may become sons of light" (v36). "Sons of" is a Hebrew way of saying "people marked by." Paul uses the same phrase (1 Thess 5:5; Eph 5:8).
- **Commentators:** JFB treats "save me from this hour" as a real petition, immediately yielded to the Father's will. Guzik sees the troubled soul as the true agony of the cross and notes that "lifted up" carries both crucifixion and exaltation. He also observes that the crowd knew the promises of a triumphant Messiah but not the prophecies of his suffering. Henry counts the voice from heaven as the Father's own honor to the Son.

#### The Unbelief of the People (vv36b–43)
- **"He departed and hid himself from them" (v36b).** This is Jesus' last public moment in John. The light withdraws, just as he warned in v35.
- **"Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe" (v37).** John has recorded seven signs, from water into wine to Lazarus. This verse is his sober summary of the public ministry, echoing 1:11: "his own people did not receive him."
- **"Lord, who has believed what he heard from us?" (v38).** The quotation is Isaiah 53:1, the opening of the song of the Suffering Servant. Rejection of the Messiah was not a surprise. Scripture foretold it.
- **"Therefore they could not believe" (v39).** John then quotes Isaiah 6:10, the commission Isaiah received when he saw the Lord in the temple: his preaching would harden a people already turned away. Jesus applied the same text to his parables (Mark 4:12), and Paul applied it to the Jews of Rome (Acts 28:26–27). Scripture holds two truths here. People refused to believe (v37), and God gave them over to their refusal (v40). John does not turn this into a theory. He means that unbelief is serious, that rejecting the light leads to darkness, and that God's purposes were not defeated by it.
- **"Isaiah… saw his glory and spoke of him" (v41).** In Isaiah 6 the prophet saw "the Lord, high and lifted up," the King, the LORD of hosts. John says he saw Jesus' glory. It is one of the clearest statements in the New Testament that the Son shared the glory of Israel's God before he became man (compare 1:14; 17:5). Notice that John has just quoted both Isaiah 53, the Servant "lifted up," and Isaiah 6, the Lord "lifted up." He sees them as one person.
- **"Many even of the authorities believed in him, but… did not confess it" (v42).** Being "put out of the synagogue" meant losing community, trade and family ties (9:22). Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea were secret believers like this, though both came into the open at Jesus' burial (19:38–39).
- **"They loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God" (v43).** The word for glory (*doxa*) is the same as in vv23, 28 and 41. The chapter is about glory: Jesus seeks the Father's, Isaiah saw Christ's, and these men settled for their neighbors'. Jesus had already named this problem (5:44).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the hardening a judicial act of God against people who had already closed their eyes, and reads Isaiah 6 as a vision of the Son's glory. Guzik warns that persistent rejection can harden a heart until belief no longer comes, and that secret discipleship cannot last forever. Henry describes the rulers as men "whose consciences witnessed for him, though they had not courage to own it."

#### Jesus Came to Save the World (vv44–50)
- **"Jesus cried out and said" (v44).** Since v36 says Jesus hid himself, John probably gathers here a summary of what Jesus had proclaimed publicly, rather than a new speech. Nearly every line repeats earlier themes, a closing statement for chs 1–12.
- **"Whoever sees me sees him who sent me" (v45).** Jesus is the Father's perfect representative (1:18; 14:9). To trust him is to trust God, and to reject him is to reject God.
- **"I have come into the world as light" (v46).** This picks up 1:4–9 and 8:12. The purpose of light is rescue: "so that whoever believes in me may not remain in darkness."
- **"I did not come to judge the world but to save the world" (v47).** This echoes 3:17. Jesus' first coming was a mission of rescue, not condemnation.
- **"The word that I have spoken will judge him on the last day" (v48).** Rejecting him still has a judge: the very words he spoke. Moses warned that God would hold accountable anyone who refused to listen to the prophet like him (Deut 18:18–19).
- **"The Father who sent me has himself given me a commandment" (v49).** Jesus claims no independent authority. His every word comes from the Father (5:19; 7:16; 8:28). That is exactly why his words carry the Father's weight.
- **"His commandment is eternal life" (v50).** The Father's command to the Son is not a burden but life for the world. The public ministry ends on the word "life," as John's whole Gospel was written "that by believing you may have life in his name" (20:31).
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads these verses as a summary of Jesus' great themes: his unity with the Father, his role as light, and his saving purpose, with judgment coming through the very words he spoke. JFB gives only a brief framing note here and no verse-by-verse comment. Henry sums it up as Jesus "asserting his divine mission, and the account he gave of his errand into the world."

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 12 shows Jesus received three ways. Mary pours out a year's wages at his feet. The crowd shouts "Hosanna" and wants a king on its own terms. The leaders want him dead and Lazarus too. Through it all Jesus walks toward the hour he came for. He is the grain of wheat that must fall into the earth, the King on a donkey who brings peace, the Servant "lifted up" whom Isaiah saw in the temple. His soul is troubled, and still he prays, "Father, glorify your name." The Greeks who wished to see Jesus see him best on the cross, where he draws all kinds of people to himself and casts out the ruler of this world. His last public word is life.
