*Setting:* Judah in the last decades before the exile, with most of the chapter probably from the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), though Matthew Henry places the Sabbath sermon at the end in the days of Josiah's reform. This is a collection of several short pieces, not one speech. It opens with a picture of sin carved into the nation's heart, then turns to wisdom poetry about two kinds of trust, a famous saying about the deceitful heart, and a proverb about ill-gotten wealth. Jeremiah then prays again for rescue, another of his "confessions" (11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18). The chapter closes with a sermon preached at the city gates about keeping the Sabbath holy, the clearest test of whether Judah would put God before profit.

#### The Sin of Judah (vv1–13)
- **"The sin of Judah is written with a pen of iron; with a point of diamond it is engraved" (v1).** An iron stylus with a hard point (the Hebrew *shamir* may mean diamond, emery or flint) was used to cut letters into stone (Job 19:24). What is carved in rock is permanent. Judah's sin is not chalk on a slate. It is cut into the heart.
- **"On the tablet of their heart" (v1).** God's law had been written on tablets of stone (Exod 31:18) and was meant to be written on the heart as well (Deut 6:6; Prov 3:3). Instead, the heart carries the inscription of sin. This is the problem Jeremiah 31:33 answers: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts." Paul draws on both passages when he calls believers "a letter from Christ… written… not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts" (2 Cor 3:3).
- **"And on the horns of their altars" (v1).** The corners of an altar had raised projections called horns. On the Day of Atonement the priest put blood on the horns of the altar to cleanse it (Lev 16:18). Here the horns carry the record of sin instead of the blood that cleanses it. Whether these are altars to the LORD corrupted by false worship or altars to Baal, the place meant for atonement has become evidence against them.
- **The Greek version omits vv1–4.** The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation, lacks these verses, though other Greek versions include them. Verses 3–4 repeat 15:13–14 almost exactly, which may be why a translator or scribe left them out. The Greek text of Jeremiah is about one-eighth shorter than the Hebrew overall, and scholars still debate why.
- **"While their children remember their altars and their Asherim" (v2).** The next generation is learning the idolatry. The Asherim were wooden poles or carved trees honoring Asherah, the Canaanite mother goddess linked with fertility, often set up beside an altar to Baal (Judg 6:25–26; 2 Kgs 21:3, 7). **"Beside every green tree and on the high hills"** is Jeremiah's standard phrase for the hilltop shrines (2:20; 3:6).
- **"Your wealth and all your treasures I will give for spoil as the price of your high places" (v3).** "On the mountains in the open country" can also be read "O my mountain in the field," a name for Jerusalem. Either way, the wealth gathered at the shrines will become plunder.
- **"You shall loosen your hand from your heritage" (v4).** The verb is the one used for the Sabbath year and the year of release, when land was left fallow and debts were cancelled (Exod 23:11; Deut 15:2). Judah had not given the land its rest (2 Chr 36:21). Now she will be forced to let go of it altogether.
- **"Cursed is the man who trusts in man and makes flesh his strength" (v5).** "Strength" is literally "arm" (ESV footnote), the arm a man relies on in battle. "Flesh" means human weakness, set against God's power (Isa 31:3: "The Egyptians are man, and not God, and their horses are flesh, and not spirit"). Judah's kings kept looking to Egypt for rescue from Babylon (2:36–37; 37:5–10). **"Whose heart turns away from the LORD."** Trusting in people is not the problem in itself. The problem is trust that takes the place of God.
- **"He is like a shrub in the desert" (v6).** The Hebrew *ʿarʿar* is probably a dwarf juniper or a similar stunted desert bush. There is a sound-play between *ʿarʿar* and *ʾarur*, "cursed," in v5. This plant grows in the salt flats around the Dead Sea, where Sodom once stood (Deut 29:23). **"And shall not see any good come."** Even when rain does fall, the bush in salt ground cannot take it in.
- **"Blessed is the man who trusts in the LORD, whose trust is the LORD" (v7).** The repetition is emphatic. The LORD is not only the one he trusts but the very content of his trust.
- **"He is like a tree planted by water, that sends out its roots by the stream" (v8).** This is a close echo of Psalm 1:3. The difference is in what each adds. Psalm 1 speaks of delight in God's law. Jeremiah speaks of trust in God himself, and he adds what happens under pressure: **"does not fear when heat comes… is not anxious in the year of drought."** Heat and drought still come to the tree by the river. Coming right after the drought of chapter 14, the point is sharp. Its roots reach water no drought can dry up, so "it does not cease to bear fruit."
- **"The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick" (v9).** "Deceitful" is *ʿaqov*, from the same root as Jacob's name, the one who grabbed the heel and tripped up his brother (Gen 25:26; 27:36; see 9:4). The heart trips us up from behind. "Desperately sick" (*ʾanush*) means incurable, the word used for wounds that will not heal (15:18; 30:12, 15). The KJV's "desperately wicked" catches the moral sense, but the Hebrew is a medical picture: the heart is terminally ill.
- **"Who can understand it?" (v9).** Not even its owner. People can sincerely believe they trust God while their hearts lean on something else (vv5–8). Proverbs 28:26 says, "Whoever trusts in his own mind is a fool."
- **"I the LORD search the heart and test the mind" (v10).** "Mind" is literally "kidneys" (ESV footnote), the seat of deepest feeling. What people cannot read in themselves, God reads perfectly (Ps 139:23–24; 1 Sam 16:7). **"To give every man according to his ways."** The New Testament repeats this principle (Rom 2:6; Rev 2:23, where Jesus says, "I am he who searches mind and heart").
- **"Like the partridge that gathers a brood that she did not hatch" (v11).** The bird is probably a sand partridge, named in Hebrew for its call (1 Sam 26:20). There was a popular belief that the partridge stole other birds' eggs and hatched them, and that the chicks then left her when they grew. Whether or not that is true of partridges, the proverb's point is plain: wealth gained unjustly does not stay. **"In the midst of his days they will leave him, and at his end he will be a fool."** Jesus' parable of the rich fool makes the same point (Luke 12:20). Jehoiakim, who built his palace with unpaid labor, may be in view (22:13–19).
- **"A glorious throne set on high from the beginning is the place of our sanctuary" (v12).** After the false objects of trust (people, the self, money), Jeremiah lifts his eyes to the true one. The temple was the earthly footstool of God's heavenly throne (3:17; 14:21), but God's throne existed "from the beginning," before any temple was built.
- **"O LORD, the hope of Israel" (v13).** The same title as 14:8. **"Those who turn away from you shall be written in the earth."** Names cut in stone last. Names written in the dust are soon blown away. That is the opposite of v1, where sin is engraved permanently. Some have wondered whether Jesus had this verse in mind when he wrote on the ground before the accusers of the woman caught in adultery (John 8:6–8), but John does not say what he wrote, and the link is only a guess.
- **"For they have forsaken the LORD, the fountain of living water" (v13).** This repeats 2:13. Living water is running water from a spring, fresh and constant, compared with stale water in a cistern. To leave God is to leave the source of life itself.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "deceitful" comes from the root of Jacob's name, so the people followed their forefather's deceit "but not [his] faith," and that "desperately wicked" means "incurable." JFB also points out that the Septuagint omits vv1–4. Henry calls vv5–11 "excellent doctrine… of general concern and use to us all," and warns that trusting in man is leaning on "a broken reed" that will "run into his hand and pierce it" (Isa 36:6). JFB, quoting Calvin, says Jeremiah "first removed the weeds (false trusts), so that there might be room for the good grain." On the partridge, JFB sees no need "to make Scripture allude to an exploded notion, as if it were true," while Guzik, citing Harrison, explains the popular belief behind the proverb. Guzik also notes the Hebrew sound-play between the desert shrub and "cursed."

#### Jeremiah Prays for Deliverance (vv14–18)
- **"Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed; save me, and I shall be saved" (v14).** Right after saying the heart is incurable (v9), Jeremiah asks God to heal him. Only the God who searches the heart can heal it. The repetition says that if God heals, the healing is real and complete. Unlike the false prophets who "healed the wound… lightly" (6:14), God's healing goes all the way down. **"For you are my praise."** God himself is the subject of his praise and his reason for confidence (Deut 10:21).
- **"Where is the word of the LORD? Let it come!" (v15).** The people mock him. Years have gone by and the disaster he predicted has not arrived. According to Deuteronomy 18:22, a prophet whose word does not come true is false. They are using that test against him. Peter describes scoffers in the last days asking the same kind of question (2 Pet 3:3–4).
- **"I have not run away from being your shepherd, nor have I desired the day of sickness" (v16).** Jeremiah defends himself. He has not deserted his post as God's shepherd for the people, and he has not longed for the disaster he announced. He warned of it because he had to, not because he wanted it. "The day of sickness" is literally "the incurable day," the same root as v9. **"You know what came out of my lips."** Everything he said was spoken in God's presence.
- **"Be not a terror to me; you are my refuge in the day of disaster" (v17).** In 15:18 Jeremiah feared God might be a dry brook. Now he asks that God not become a terror to him, and he names God his refuge. The day of disaster for Judah will be a day of shelter for him.
- **"Let those be put to shame who persecute me, but let me not be put to shame" (v18).** This prayer, like 11:20 and 15:15, asks God to vindicate his servant. **"Destroy them with double destruction!"** "Double" probably means full and complete, as in 16:18. Jeremiah hands his enemies to God rather than taking matters into his own hands. The New Testament goes further and calls believers to bless those who persecute them (Rom 12:14), but it keeps the principle that vengeance belongs to God (Rom 12:19).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "Where is the word of the LORD?" as the people's mockery because the threatened judgment had not yet come, and notes Amos 5:18 and Isaiah 5:19 as parallels. On v16, Guzik takes Jeremiah's claim as a sincere defense of his motives: he faithfully followed God as a shepherd and never wished disaster on his people. The Tyndale notes add that the people may have been treating Jeremiah as a false prophet under the test of Deuteronomy 18.

#### Keep the Sabbath Holy (vv19–27)
- **"Go and stand in the People's Gate, by which the kings of Judah enter" (v19).** The exact gate is unknown. It may be the main gate used by king and people alike, perhaps one near the palace. Jeremiah is to go from there to "all the gates of Jerusalem," wherever traders brought goods into the city. Gates were the busiest public places, where markets, courts and business all happened.
- **"Do not bear a burden on the Sabbath day or bring it in by the gates of Jerusalem" (v21).** The Sabbath command forbade ordinary work on the seventh day (Exod 20:8–11; Deut 5:12–15). Carrying loads of produce and goods into the city to sell was work, and on a busy market day it was good business. **"Take care for the sake of your lives."** This was not a small matter of ritual. It was part of the covenant sign between God and Israel (Exod 31:13–17).
- **Why the Sabbath?** The Sabbath was a weekly confession that God, not human labor, was Israel's provider. In Deuteronomy it reminds them that they were once slaves who could never rest (Deut 5:15). Refusing to stop trading one day in seven was a way of saying that money mattered more than God. It is the same issue as vv5–11 in another form: trusting in what our own hands can produce.
- **"Yet they did not listen… but stiffened their neck" (v23).** An ox that stiffens its neck refuses the yoke. The fathers had not kept the Sabbath either (Ezek 20:13, 16).
- **"Then there shall enter by the gates of this city kings and princes who sit on the throne of David" (v25).** The offer is real. If they keep the Sabbath, the royal line will continue, the city will stand "forever," and worshipers will stream in from every region: Benjamin to the north, the Shephelah (the low foothills to the west), the hill country around Jerusalem, and the Negeb, the dry south (v26). They will bring every kind of offering, including **"thank offerings,"** the sacrifice of gratitude. It is a picture of a nation whose life revolves around worship instead of trade.
- **"I will kindle a fire in its gates, and it shall devour the palaces of Jerusalem and shall not be quenched" (v27).** The same gates that carried the forbidden loads will burn. This happened in 586 BC, when the Babylonians "burned the house of the LORD and the king's house and all the houses of Jerusalem" (2 Kgs 25:9; Jer 52:13). Chronicles says the land lay desolate "until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths" (2 Chr 36:21). Amos 2:5 uses the same phrase.
- **After the exile.** The lesson stuck for a while. When the exiles returned, Nehemiah found merchants again bringing goods into Jerusalem on the Sabbath, and he shut the gates, reminding them, "Did not your fathers act in this way, and did not our God bring all this disaster on us and on this city?" (Neh 13:15–22). He was almost quoting this chapter.
- **Commentators:** Henry suggests this sermon was "probably sent in the days of Josiah, for the furtherance of that work of reformation," since such generous promises seem to fit an earlier time, before judgment was certain. He also notes that it had to be proclaimed first at the king's gate, because the rulers must be told their duty first, as Nehemiah later rebuked the nobles (Neh 13:17). JFB and the Tyndale notes connect the fire of v27 with the burning of Jerusalem in 586 BC. Some modern scholars have questioned whether this passage comes from Jeremiah because of its focus on the Sabbath, but its form matches his other gate sermons (7:2; 22:1–5), and the promise-and-warning pattern is his.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah says sin is carved into the heart and the heart is incurable, then prays, "Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed." Only God can answer that prayer, and he answers it in the new covenant, writing his law on hearts that once bore the inscription of sin (Jer 31:33; Heb 8:10). Jesus is the one who searches mind and heart (Rev 2:23), and he is also the healer of hearts (Luke 4:18). He is the fountain of living water whom Judah forsook, crying out at the feast, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37–38), and the one who trusts in him is the tree planted by the stream. And he is the Lord of the Sabbath (Mark 2:28), who gives the true rest that the Sabbath pictured: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28; Heb 4:9–10).
