*Setting:* Psalm 113 has no title or named author. It is the third psalm in a row to open with "Praise the LORD!" (*Hallelu-Yah*), and it begins a group of six psalms, 113–118, that Jewish tradition calls the "Egyptian Hallel" (*hallel* means "praise"). They are sung at Passover, the feast that remembers the exodus from Egypt. By long custom Psalms 113–114 are sung before the Passover meal and 115–118 after it. That custom is recorded in the Mishnah, written down around AD 200, and most scholars think it was already in place in Jesus' day. If so, when Jesus and his disciples "sang a hymn" and went out to the Mount of Olives after the Last Supper (Matt 26:30; Mark 14:26), this group of psalms is very likely what they sang. Psalm 113 is a short hymn with one big idea: the God who sits higher than the heavens bends down to lift the lowest people on earth. It borrows its central lines from Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2, and its picture of God raising the humble runs on into Mary's song in Luke 1. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Praise the Name of the LORD (vv1–3)
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v1).** The psalm opens and closes with the same word (vv1, 9), so the whole psalm is wrapped in praise. *Hallelu* is a command to a group, "all of you, praise," and *Yah* is a short form of God's personal name, Yahweh. The ESV prints "LORD" in small capitals wherever the Hebrew has that name.
- **"Praise, O servants of the LORD" (v1).** "Praise" comes three times in one verse. The repetition is the point: this is urgent, not polite. "Servants" could mean the priests and Levites who led worship at the temple (134:1; 135:1–2), but here it most naturally means all God's people. Israel as a whole is called his servant (Isa 41:8). Those who serve God have the most reason to praise him, since they know him best.
- **"Praise the name of the LORD!" (v1).** In the Bible a name is more than a label. God's "name" is God as he has made himself known: his character, his reputation, what he has done (Exod 3:13–15; 34:5–7). To praise his name is to praise him for who he has shown himself to be, not for a God of our own imagining.
- **"From this time forth and forevermore!" (v2).** The praise stretches across time. God never becomes less worthy of it, so it never needs to stop.
- **"From the rising of the sun to its setting" (v3).** Now the praise stretches across space, from the far east to the far west. The phrase means "everywhere on earth," and it also works as "all day long." The prophet Malachi uses the same words for a coming day when the nations will honor God's name: "from the rising of the sun to its setting my name will be great among the nations" (Mal 1:11). A psalm sung by Israel at its own national feast already looks out to the whole world.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the repeated commands as a sign of "earnestness and zeal," takes "servants of the Lord" as "all the people of God," and explains "name" as God's "perfections." Henry says God's own people have the most cause to praise him, because they know him and receive his favor, and that "God's name ought to be praised in every place, from east to west." Guzik makes the same point as Henry about servants and adds that a believer with open eyes keeps finding fresh reasons to praise, so the "forevermore" is not a burden but a natural overflow.

#### Who Is Like the LORD Our God? (vv4–6)
- **"The LORD is high above all nations, and his glory above the heavens!" (v4).** The nations had their own gods, and the great empires thought of their kings as near-divine. The psalm sets the LORD above all of them. His glory is not just higher than the nations. It is higher than the sky itself (57:5; 108:5).
- **"Who is like the LORD our God?" (v5).** This is a question with no answer, and it is meant to have none. Israel asked it at the Red Sea: "Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?" (Exod 15:11). The name Micah means "Who is like Yah?" and Michael means "Who is like God?" The question is not only about God's power. The next lines show that what makes him unlike any other god is how far he is willing to come down.
- **"Who is seated on high, who looks far down" (vv5–6).** In Hebrew these two lines mirror each other. Literally: "who makes himself high to sit, who makes himself low to look." The second verb is *shaphel*, "to be low" or "to bring low," and in this form it means "to make oneself low." The same verb is used in Hannah's song: "he brings low and he exalts" (1 Sam 2:7). The picture is of a king rising from his throne and bending down.
- **"On the heavens and the earth" (v6).** The surprise is that God has to stoop even to look at the heavens. The sky that seems so high to us is far beneath him (Isa 40:22; 57:15). And yet he does not stay distant. Ancient thinkers often pictured the highest god as too exalted to concern himself with small human troubles. The psalm says the opposite: his greatness is exactly what makes his care for the small so remarkable (138:6).
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline pairs "the elevations of his glory and greatness" with "the condescensions of his grace and goodness," which, he says, "very much illustrate one another." He sees the stooping most fully in the Son of God coming down from heaven to save the lost. JFB says the same in a line: "God's exaltation enhances His condescension." Guzik ties v6 to Psalm 8:4, where David wonders that God is mindful of man at all, and passes on Spurgeon's point that the philosophers of the ancient world imagined a God who did not care, while Israel's God bends down.

#### He Raises the Poor from the Dust (vv7–9)
- **"He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap" (v7).** These lines are taken almost word for word from Hannah's song (1 Sam 2:8). The "ash heap" (*'ashpot*) was the town dump outside the walls, where refuse and dung were thrown and burned. Jerusalem's "Dung Gate" (Neh 2:13) takes its name from the same word. The poorest beggars and outcasts sat there. Job, struck with sores, sat among the ashes (Job 2:8). This is the lowest place a person could be.
- **"To make them sit with princes, with the princes of his people" (v8).** The ESV repeats "princes" to match the Hebrew, which says it twice for emphasis. God does not just pull the poor out of the dump. He seats them among the nobles. The word for princes, *nedivim*, comes from a root meaning willing or generous, and it was used for people of high rank. Scripture shows the pattern again and again: Joseph from prison to Pharaoh's right hand (Gen 41:41), David from the sheepfolds to the throne (78:70–71), the shepherds of Bethlehem told the news first (Luke 2:8–11).
- **"He gives the barren woman a home" (v9).** Literally "he causes the barren of the house to dwell." In the ancient world a woman who could not have children often lived with shame and insecurity. Her place in the household could feel temporary (Gen 16:4; 30:1, 23; 1 Sam 1:6–7). God gives her a settled place.
- **"Making her the joyous mother of children" (v9).** Hannah is in the background again. She was barren, mocked by her rival, and God gave her Samuel and then more children (1 Sam 1:20; 2:21). The pattern runs through Scripture: Sarah (Gen 21:1–2), Rebekah (Gen 25:21), Rachel (Gen 30:22–23), Samson's mother (Judg 13), and Elizabeth (Luke 1:7, 24–25). Each time the birth moves God's plan forward. Isaiah uses the same picture for God's people after exile: "Sing, O barren one… for the children of the desolate one will be more than the children of her who is married" (Isa 54:1). Paul applies that verse to the church (Gal 4:27).
- **The promise is not a formula.** The psalm celebrates what God does, often and dramatically. It does not promise that every childless woman will bear children or that every poor person will become rich. Many faithful people have not. What it shows is the kind of God he is: one who notices the overlooked and has the power to reverse their story, in this life or in the next (Luke 6:20–21).
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v9).** The psalm ends where it began. The God who is high above the heavens and the God who lifts a beggar off the dump are the same God, and both are reasons for the same *Hallelujah*.
- **Mary's song.** When Mary learned she would bear the Messiah, her song echoed Hannah's and this psalm: "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate; he has filled the hungry with good things" (Luke 1:52–53).
- **Commentators:** All three note the borrowing from Hannah's song. JFB says God's condescension "is illustrated as often in raising the worthy poor and needy to honor," and calls barrenness "a disgrace" in that culture and "a type of a deserted Church," pointing to Isaiah 54:1. Henry sees in these verses God taking people who are "by nature beggars, and even traitors" and making them his children, and notes that "the apostles were sent from fishing to be fishers of men." He adds that God's comforts "are generally the more welcome when long delayed, and no longer expected." Guzik connects v8 to Ephesians 2:5–6, where believers dead in sin are raised and seated with Christ in the heavenly places, and notes the poignancy that Jesus likely sang these words on the night before he was laid in the dust of death.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 113 asks "Who is like the LORD our God?" and answers by showing a God who comes down. That is the shape of the gospel. Christ "was in the form of God" and yet "made himself nothing… humbling himself by becoming obedient to the point of death" (Phil 2:6–8). He was born to a poor couple, laid in a feeding trough, and announced to shepherds. On the night he was betrayed he most likely sang this psalm with his friends, then went out to be lowered further than anyone, into death and the grave. And God raised him from the dust and seated him at his right hand (Eph 1:20). Those who belong to him share that same reversal: raised from the ash heap of sin and seated with him among the princes of his people (Eph 2:4–7).
