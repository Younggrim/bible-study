*Setting:* The title reads "To the choirmaster: for the flutes. A Psalm of David." The word for flutes, *nechilot*, appears only here. Most think it means wind instruments, though some ancient translators guessed it meant "inheritances." No occasion is given. Like Psalm 3 it is a morning prayer (v3), and like Psalms 3–4 it comes from a man surrounded by enemies who lie about him and plot against him. Some connect it with Absalom's rebellion and the treachery of counselors like Ahithophel (v10, "let them fall by their own counsels"), but the psalm itself names no one. Its heart is the contrast between two kinds of people and their place before God: the wicked, who "shall not stand before your eyes" (v5), and the psalmist, who "will enter your house" (v7) only because of God's steadfast love. The psalm alternates between them, and it is built around that "But I."

#### My King and My God (vv1–3)
- **"Give ear… consider… give attention" (vv1–2).** Three ways of asking God to listen. **"My groaning"** (*hagig*) comes from the same family of words as "meditate" in 1:2: a low murmuring, sighing or musing. David brings both his spoken words and his wordless inner sighs (compare Rom 8:26, where the Spirit intercedes "with groanings too deep for words").
- **"My King and my God" (v2).** David was Israel's king, yet he calls God "my King." The human king knew he ruled under a greater one (compare 1 Chr 28:5, "the throne of the kingdom of the LORD"). This is the first time "King" is used of God in the Psalms, and it frames everything that follows: the psalmist appeals to the royal Judge.
- **"For to you do I pray" (v2).** He has nowhere else to turn and does not want to turn anywhere else.
- **"O LORD, in the morning you hear my voice" (v3).** The day began with God. Morning was the time of the daily sacrifice at the tabernacle (Exod 29:38–39), and many psalms connect it with prayer and with God's fresh mercies (59:16; 88:13; 143:8; Lam 3:22–23).
- **"In the morning I prepare a sacrifice for you and watch" (v3).** "Prepare" is *arak*, to set in order, lay out. It is used for arranging wood and pieces of a sacrifice on the altar (Lev 1:7–8; 6:12), setting out the bread of the Presence (Exod 40:23), drawing up an army in battle lines, and laying out a legal case (Job 23:4). The Hebrew does not actually say what David sets in order. The ESV supplies "a sacrifice," other versions supply "my prayer" or "my case." David may be picturing prayer as his morning offering, carefully laid out. **"And watch"** (*tsaphah*) is the word for a sentry on a city wall scanning the horizon (Hab 2:1; Mic 7:7). Prayer is not tossed into the air and forgotten. He prays, then looks out for God's answer.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "David's prayers were not his words only, but his meditations," and "meditation is the best preparative for prayer, so prayer is the best issue of meditation." He insists "Morning prayer is our duty," because we are fresh before the day's business crowds in, and he says after praying we should look up for God's reply. JFB explains "direct" (KJV) as "literally, 'set in order,' as the showbread was placed or set in order." Guzik makes the same point about *arak*: ordering a prayer as a priest lays out the morning sacrifice, not aiming it like an arrow. Henry, by contrast, compares it to an archer aiming at a target, so the two read the KJV's "direct" differently, and the Hebrew favors JFB and Guzik.

#### Evil May Not Dwell With You (vv4–6)
- **"You are not a God who delights in wickedness" (v4).** This is why the psalmist can pray against liars with confidence: God is on the side of truth. The gods of the nations were thought to be as fickle and corrupt as people. Israel's God is holy.
- **"Evil may not dwell with you" (v4).** "Dwell" (*gur*) means to stay as a guest. In the ancient world a host had to protect a guest under his roof. God will not take evil in as his guest. Psalm 15 asks, "O LORD, who shall sojourn in your tent?" and answers, the one "who walks blamelessly" and "speaks truth in his heart" (15:1–2).
- **Six descriptions of the wicked (vv4–6).** Wickedness, evil, the boastful, evildoers, those who speak lies, the bloodthirsty and deceitful. The **"boastful"** (*holelim*) are the arrogant, people swollen with self-praise. **"Evildoers"** (*poale aven*) are "workers of iniquity," those who make wrongdoing their trade. The list stresses speech. Three of the six involve lies and deceit, because that is what the psalmist is suffering.
- **"You hate all evildoers… the LORD abhors the bloodthirsty and deceitful man" (vv5–6).** These are strong words, and they make modern readers uneasy. The Psalms say plainly that God hates evildoers (11:5), and Scripture also says God loved the world and gave his Son for it (John 3:16) and takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezek 18:23). Christians have held these together in different ways. Many say God's hatred is his settled, holy opposition to people in their rebellion, not a spiteful emotion, and that his love moves to rescue the very people his justice stands against (Rom 5:8–10). The psalm does not soften it: evil is not a small thing to God.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God has no pleasure in wickedness, "though covered with a cloak of religion," and that "if we regard iniquity in our heart, he will not hear our prayers" (Ps 66:18). JFB explains the "foolish" (KJV) as "vainglorious and insolent," and says "iniquity" especially means a lack of moral principle. Guzik passes on Boice's point that drawing near to God makes us more sensitive to sin, which is one way to tell whether we are really praying.

#### Lead Me in Your Righteousness (vv7–8)
- **"But I, through the abundance of your steadfast love, will enter your house" (v7).** The "But I" is the turning point. The wicked will not stand before God, but the psalmist will enter his house. Not because he is better than they are, but through "the abundance of your steadfast love" (*rov chasdeka*). *Chesed* is God's loyal covenant love, the love he pledged to his people and keeps. Compare v10, where the enemies are cast out "because of the abundance of their transgressions." The same word, *rov*, "abundance," is used of both. Abundant sin shuts people out; abundant mercy lets the psalmist in.
- **"I will bow down toward your holy temple in the fear of you" (v7).** "Temple" is *hekal*, "palace," the royal hall of God. Solomon's temple had not yet been built in David's day, but the same word is used for the tabernacle at Shiloh (1 Sam 1:9; 3:3), so it fits a psalm of David. He bows "toward" it, since only priests entered the holy place. Later Israelites prayed facing the temple even from far away (1 Kings 8:29–30; Dan 6:10). Coming in by mercy does not make him casual. He comes in "the fear of you," with reverent awe.
- **"Lead me, O LORD, in your righteousness because of my enemies" (v8).** His enemies are watching for him to slip. So he asks God to lead him in a way that will show God's righteousness and leave them nothing to accuse.
- **"Make your way straight before me" (v8).** A straight or level road is one without obstacles or detours (Isa 40:3–4; Prov 3:6). He wants God's way, not his own, made plain.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls God's mercy the foundation of our hopes: David relies on "the multitude of God's mercy," not his own merit. On v8 he notes that enemies watching us are a reason for greater care in how we live. JFB explains "temple" as "palace," God's residence, and "toward" as "not in; the high priest alone was allowed to enter." Guzik passes on an older writer's remark that enemies often drive believers to pray more and watch their own conduct more closely.

#### No Truth in Their Mouth (vv9–10)
- **"For there is no truth in their mouth" (v9).** The reason for the prayer in v8: the enemies cannot be trusted at all.
- **"Their inmost self is destruction" (v9).** Inside they are ruin. Their hearts are pits of destruction (*havvot*), whatever their faces show.
- **"Their throat is an open grave; they flatter with their tongue" (v9).** An open grave gives off the stench of death, and it swallows what goes in and never gives it back. Their words reek of the death inside them, and their flattery is a trap: "they flatter with their tongue," literally "they make their tongue smooth." Jesus said, "Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks" (Matt 12:34).
- **Paul quotes this verse about all of us.** In Romans 3:13 Paul strings together Old Testament verses to prove that "all, both Jews and Greeks, are under sin." One of them is this line: "Their throat is an open grave; they use their tongues to deceive." What David said of his enemies, Paul applies to every human being. The reader who stands with the psalmist must also find himself among the people described in v9, and needs the "abundance of your steadfast love" of v7 just as much.
- **"Make them bear their guilt, O God" (v10).** Literally "declare them guilty" (*ha'ashimem*). This is the language of a law court. **"Let them fall by their own counsels."** Let their own schemes trip them. That is what happened to Ahithophel, whose rejected advice led to his suicide (2 Sam 17:23), and to Absalom. **"For they have rebelled against you."** David's case is not personal. They are rebels against God.
- **Praying against enemies.** Psalm 3 celebrated God striking enemies; here, for the first time, the psalmist asks outright for their downfall, and many more psalms follow (35; 69; 109; 137). Christians, told to "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matt 5:44), have read these prayers in a few ways. Most see them as appeals to God's justice rather than personal revenge: the psalmist hands judgment over to God instead of taking it himself (Rom 12:19). Many add that they are prayers against unrepentant evil, and that the same God who judges also saves. Jesus prayed for those who crucified him (Luke 23:34), and also warned of judgment on those who refuse him.
- **Commentators:** Henry says their throat is "an open sepulchre," insatiable like the grave. He reads v10 not as revenge but as recognizing that sin brings its own ruin, and that their sins had filled up the measure of their guilt. JFB calls the grave "a dwelling-place of corruption, emitting moral putridness," and renders "destroy" (KJV) as "condemn" them as guilty, reading the prayer as a judicial sentence. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's warning to beware of flatterers, and treats v10 as a prayer for just judgment.

#### Let All Who Take Refuge in You Rejoice (vv11–12)
- **"Let all who take refuge in you rejoice" (v11).** The psalm widens from "I" to "all," as Psalm 3 did (3:8). "Take refuge" (*chasah*) picks up the last line of Psalm 2: "Blessed are all who take refuge in him" (2:12).
- **"Let them ever sing for joy" (v11).** "Sing for joy" (*ranan*) is a ringing shout of gladness. The morning that began with groaning (v1) ends in song.
- **"Spread your protection over them" (v11).** The verb (*sakak*) means to cover or screen, as a bird's wings shelter its young (91:4) or as the cherubim's wings overshadowed the ark (Exod 25:20). **"Those who love your name"** are those who love God himself, since his name is his revealed character (Exod 34:5–7).
- **"You bless the righteous, O LORD; you cover him with favor as with a shield" (v12).** In 3:3 God was a shield "about me," using the word for a small round shield (*magen*). Here the word is *tsinnah*, the large body shield, like the one carried before Goliath (1 Sam 17:7), covering a soldier from head to foot. "Cover" (*atar*) means to surround, even to crown. God's favor, his gracious acceptance, surrounds the righteous on every side.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God surrounds the righteous with favor "as with a shield" on every side, unlike a shield in battle, which guards only one side. JFB explains "favour" as "acceptance," the favor God shows to an acceptable offering and worshipper (Lev 7:18; 19:7), which ties the end of the psalm back to the morning offering of v3. Guzik pictures a large shield covering every part of the believer, and tells how Luther, asked where he would find shelter if his protectors abandoned him, answered, "Under the shelter of heaven."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 5 asks who may enter God's house, and answers: no one by right, and the psalmist only by the abundance of God's steadfast love. Paul takes v9 and shows that the throat like an open grave belongs to all of us (Rom 3:13). Then he announces how sinners can still enter: "justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Rom 3:24). Jesus is the one in whose mouth there was truth, who was "the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). He made the way straight, opened the house of God, and tore the curtain that kept worshippers bowing "toward" the holy place from outside (Matt 27:51; Heb 10:19–22). Everyone who takes refuge in him is covered with God's favor as with a shield, and the morning prayer of the psalm becomes the church's daily confidence.
