*Setting:* Jerusalem, Thursday and early Friday of Passover week, about AD 30. Jesus has spent his days teaching in the temple and his nights on the Mount of Olives (21:37–38). The leaders want him dead but fear the crowds. Chapter 22 covers less than a day: a traitor's deal, the Passover meal, Jesus' prayer in agony, his arrest, Peter's denials, and at dawn, the council.

#### The Plot to Kill Jesus (vv1–2)
- **"The Feast of Unleavened Bread... which is called the Passover" (vv1–2).** Passover remembered the night God struck the firstborn of Egypt and passed over the houses marked with a lamb's blood (Exod 12). The Passover meal was eaten on the evening that began the 15th of Nisan, in March or April, and it opened the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread. By Jesus' day people used the two names for the whole festival. Jerusalem swelled with pilgrims, and the leaders, who have sought Jesus' death since 19:47, fear a riot.

#### Judas to Betray Jesus (vv3–6)
- **"Then Satan entered into Judas called Iscariot" (v3).** After tempting Jesus in the wilderness, the devil left him "until an opportune time" (4:13). This is that time. John says the devil had already put the idea in Judas's heart, and that he was a thief who helped himself from the money bag (John 12:6; 13:2). Satan's work does not cancel Judas's own choice. He goes, confers and consents.
- **"They were glad, and agreed to give him money" (v5).** Matthew gives the sum: thirty pieces of silver (Matt 26:15), the price the law set for a slave killed by an ox (Exod 21:32) and the wage paid to the rejected shepherd in Zechariah 11:12–13.
- **Commentators:** JFB traces stages in Judas's fall, from covetousness given room by being trusted with the bag, through theft, to Satan entering him. Henry: "It is hard to say whether more mischief is done to Christ's kingdom, by the power of its open enemies, or by the treachery of its pretended friends." Guzik insists that Satan's entering does not remove Judas's responsibility.

#### The Passover with the Disciples (vv7–13)
- **"The day of Unleavened Bread, on which the Passover lamb had to be sacrificed" (v7).** The lambs were slaughtered in the temple on the afternoon of the 14th of Nisan, thousands of them, and the meal was eaten that evening. John's Gospel seems to place the crucifixion at the time the lambs were being killed (John 18:28; 19:14), which raises a well-known question of dates. Proposals include different calendars or John using "Passover" for the whole festival. Either way, all four Gospels connect Jesus' death with Passover.
- **"A man carrying a jar of water" (v10).** Carrying water was usually women's work, so a man with a water jar would stand out. Jesus may have arranged a sign in advance, or known by divine insight. Either way, the secrecy kept the location from Judas until the meal was under way.

#### Institution of the Lord's Supper (vv14–23)
- **"I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer" (v15).** The Greek doubles the idea, a Hebrew way of saying "with desire I have desired." He knows what is coming and longs for this meal anyway.
- **"Until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God" (v16).** Passover remembered the exodus and looked forward to God's final redemption. Jesus says its meaning will be fulfilled in the kingdom, at the great banquet he has spoken of (13:29; 14:15).
- **"He took a cup... 'Take this, and divide it among yourselves'" (vv17–18).** The Passover meal included four cups of wine at set points, each tied to one of God's promises in Exodus 6:6–7. This first cup in Luke is probably one of the earlier cups. Luke is the only Gospel that mentions two cups, one before and one after the bread.
- **"This is my body, which is given for you" (v19).** The bread was unleavened, flat and broken by hand. At Passover the head of the house explained the meaning of each food (Exod 12:26–27). Jesus gives the bread a new meaning: his own body given in death for them. "For you" is the language of a substitute.
- **"Do this in remembrance of me" (v19).** Passover was a "memorial" (Exod 12:14). In biblical thinking, remembering is not just recalling. It is bringing a past saving act of God into the present by faith. From now on God's people will remember a greater exodus.
- **"This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood" (v20).** This is the cup "after they had eaten," the third cup, sometimes called the cup of blessing (1 Cor 10:16). Moses sealed the old covenant with blood: "Behold the blood of the covenant" (Exod 24:8). Jeremiah promised a "new covenant" in which God would write his law on hearts and "remember their sin no more" (Jer 31:31–34). Jesus says that covenant begins with his blood. Paul gives nearly the same words, which he "received from the Lord" (1 Cor 11:23–25).
- **A textual note.** A few manuscripts, chiefly Codex Bezae and some old Latin copies, lack the second half of v19 and all of v20. Nearly all modern scholars and translations accept the longer text as original, since the manuscript support for it is overwhelming.
- **Readings differ on the Supper.** Christians have long disagreed about what Jesus meant by "This is my body." Roman Catholics hold that the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood, Lutherans that he is truly present in them, Reformed churches that he is spiritually present to faith, and many Baptists see the Supper mainly as a memorial and proclamation (1 Cor 11:26). All agree it is commanded by the Lord and points to his death for sinners.
- **"The hand of him who betrays me is with me on the table" (v21).** Sharing food was a bond of friendship. Psalm 41:9 foretold a close friend who ate his bread lifting his heel against him. Luke places this after the bread and cup, which suggests Judas received them.
- **"The Son of Man goes as it has been determined, but woe to that man" (v22).** God's plan and human guilt stand side by side. Peter will preach the same: Jesus was "delivered up according to the definite plan" of God, yet killed "by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the meal "the point of transition between two economies and their two great festivals." Henry: "He takes his leave of all passovers," since "now in the kingdom of God the substance was come." Guzik notes that no Gospel account of the supper mentions the lamb, perhaps keeping the focus on Jesus as the sacrifice, and he warns against treating the bread and cup as bare symbols.

#### Who Is the Greatest? (vv24–30)
- **"A dispute also arose among them" (v24).** It is hard to read. At the table where Jesus has just spoken of his body given for them, the disciples argue about rank, perhaps over seating. They had argued this before (9:46). John records that Jesus washed their feet at this meal (John 13:3–17), which may be his answer to this very argument.
- **"Those in authority over them are called benefactors" (v25).** "Benefactor" (*euergetēs*) was an official title of honor given to kings and wealthy patrons who funded public works, often stamped on coins and carved on monuments. The title made power look like kindness.
- **"I am among you as the one who serves" (v27).** At a meal the one reclining is greater than the waiter. Yet Jesus, the Lord of the table, has taken the servant's place. Mark records the fuller saying: the Son of Man came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).
- **"I assign to you, as my Father assigned to me, a kingdom" (v29).** The verb is the word for making a covenant or a will (*diatithemai*), related to "covenant" (*diathēkē*) in v20. Within hours of his arrest, Jesus is handing out a kingdom, and the Twelve will sit on thrones as leaders of renewed Israel, whether in the church or in the age to come (Rev 21:14).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the title of benefactor "a title which the vanity of princes eagerly coveted," and on v29 asks, "Who is this that dispenses kingdoms... within an hour or two of His apprehension?" Guzik suggests the argument came around the time of the foot washing, and that the world's great men want credit for their gifts.

#### Jesus Foretells Peter's Denial (vv31–34)
- **"Simon, Simon, behold, Satan demanded to have you" (v31).** The doubled name signals tender seriousness (10:41). "Demanded" is *exaiteomai*, "to ask for and obtain." The lexicon gives the sense "obtained you by asking." The picture recalls Job, where Satan had to ask God's permission to test him (Job 1:6–12). The "you" here is plural: all the disciples will be shaken like grain in a sieve, to see what falls through.
- **"But I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail" (v32).** Now "you" is singular. Jesus prays for Peter in particular. "Fail" means to die out completely. Peter's courage will fail. His faith will not.
- **"When you have turned again, strengthen your brothers" (v32).** Jesus speaks of Peter's restoration as certain before his fall has happened. His failure will be turned into a ministry of strengthening others. Peter's first letter shows him doing exactly this (1 Pet 5:8–10).
- **"I am ready to go with you both to prison and to death" (v33).** He means it. Years later he would go to prison (Acts 12:3–5), and tradition holds he died as a martyr in Rome. But tonight his promise depends on his own strength.
- **"The rooster will not crow this day, until you deny three times that you know me" (v34).** The Jewish day began at sunset, so "this day" means before dawn. Mark records "before the rooster crows twice" (Mark 14:30), a more detailed version of the same warning.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "demanded" as "asked and obtained," as with Job, and says Peter's faith would not fail "entirely; for partially it did fail." Henry: "Nothing more certainly forebodes a fall, in a professed follower of Christ, than self-confidence," but believers "are kept by the power of God, and the prayer of Christ." Guzik notes that Satan had to ask permission.

#### Scripture Must Be Fulfilled in Jesus (vv35–38)
- **"But now let the one who has a moneybag take it" (v36).** On earlier missions, villages had welcomed and fed them (9:3; 10:4). Things are about to change. Their Master will be treated as a criminal, and they will face hostility, not hospitality. They must be ready to provide for themselves.
- **"Let the one who has no sword sell his cloak and buy one" (v36).** The saying is vivid: danger will be so great that a sword seems more needed than a cloak, which also served as a blanket at night. Most take it as a picture of the coming crisis, not an order to arm.
- **"He was numbered with the transgressors" (v37).** From Isaiah 53:12, the suffering servant. This is the only time in the Gospels Jesus quotes that chapter directly about himself. He will be arrested as a criminal and crucified between two (23:32–33).
- **"Look, Lord, here are two swords." "It is enough" (v38).** The disciples take him literally. Two short swords, probably large knives, would be useless against an armed crowd. Most interpreters read "It is enough" as "Enough of this!" an end to a conversation they have misunderstood. A few take it as "two are enough" to fulfill the prophecy of being counted with lawbreakers. When a disciple uses one of these swords a few hours later, Jesus stops him (vv49–51).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the apostles took him to mean real weapons, "but he spake only of the weapons of the spiritual warfare." Guzik also reads the reply as Jesus closing the subject, not approving of swords.

#### Jesus Prays on the Mount of Olives (vv39–46)
- **"As was his custom, to the Mount of Olives" (v39).** Judas knew this habit (John 18:2). Luke does not name Gethsemane ("oil press," Mark 14:32), an olive grove on the western slope facing the city.
- **"Pray that you may not enter into temptation" (v40).** Luke opens and closes the scene with these words (v46). "Temptation" (*peirasmos*) means testing that could lead to falling away. Jesus taught them to pray, "lead us not into temptation" (11:4).
- **"Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me" (v42).** In the Old Testament, the "cup" is often the cup of God's wrath poured out on sin (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15). Jesus does not shrink from pain alone. He shrinks from bearing God's judgment against sin. His humanity is real. He does not want to drink it.
- **"Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done" (v42).** In the garden of Eden, Adam said, in effect, "my will." In this garden, the second Adam says, "yours." This is the prayer he taught them: "Your will be done" (Matt 6:10).
- **"There appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him" (v43).** Only Luke records this. The angel does not take the cup away. He gives strength to drink it.
- **"Being in agony he prayed more earnestly" (v44).** "Agony" (*agōnia*) is the word for an athlete's struggle in a contest, intense strain and anguish. Even the Son of God's prayer grows more intense under pressure.
- **"His sweat became like great drops of blood" (v44).** The word for drops is *thrombos*, literally "clots" or "lumps." "Like" can mean his sweat fell like drops of blood, heavy and thick, or that it was mixed with blood. A rare condition called hematidrosis, in which blood vessels near the sweat glands break under extreme stress, has been described in modern medicine. Luke may mean either. The point is the depth of his anguish.
- **The manuscript question.** Some important early manuscripts lack vv43–44, while other early witnesses include them. Second-century writers already knew them, and scholars debate whether a scribe added them or removed them. The ESV includes them with a footnote. Nothing in them conflicts with the rest of the Gospels, which also show his deep distress (Mark 14:33–34; Heb 5:7).
- **"He... found them sleeping for sorrow" (v45).** Only Luke the doctor explains why: grief had exhausted them. He is gentle with them. "Why are you sleeping? Rise and pray" (v46).
- **Commentators:** JFB: "'I will suffer,' is the grand result of Gethsemane: 'It is finished' is the shout that bursts from the Cross." Henry: "When next you dwell in imagination upon the delights of some favourite sin, think of its effects as you behold them here!" Guzik reads the cup as God's wrath against sin and contrasts this garden with Adam's.

#### Betrayal and Arrest of Jesus (vv47–53)
- **"A crowd, and the man called Judas, one of the twelve, was leading them" (v47).** Luke again: "one of the twelve." John says there was a detachment of soldiers and temple officers with torches and weapons (John 18:3).
- **"Judas, would you betray the Son of Man with a kiss?" (v48).** A disciple greeted his teacher with a kiss, and in the dark it identified the right man. Jesus names the horror of it: the sign of love used to hand him over. Joab killed Amasa while greeting him with a kiss (2 Sam 20:9–10).
- **"No more of this!... And he touched his ear and healed him" (vv50–51).** John names Peter as the swordsman and Malchus as the servant (John 18:10), and only Luke the doctor says it was the right ear. Jesus' last miracle before the cross is a healing for an enemy who came to arrest him. He does what he taught: "love your enemies, do good to those who hate you" (6:27).
- **"Have you come out as against a robber, with swords and clubs?" (v52).** The word can mean a bandit or an armed rebel, the same word used of Barabbas in John 18:40. They treat a teacher as a dangerous rebel, the "transgressor" of v37.
- **"This is your hour, and the power of darkness" (v53).** They could not arrest him in the daylight before the people. Now it is night in every sense. Jesus allows it, for a short time. The hour belongs to darkness, but the plan belongs to God (v22).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that by healing Malchus Jesus "gave an illustrious example of his own rule of doing good to those that hate us," and that the hour of darkness was short, "and such the triumphs of the wicked always will be."

#### Peter Denies Jesus (vv54–62)
- **"Bringing him into the high priest's house" (v54).** The high priest that year was Caiaphas, but his father-in-law Annas, a former high priest, still held great influence. John says Jesus was taken first to Annas (John 18:13). Wealthy houses were built around an open courtyard.
- **Three denials (vv55–60).** Peter sits by the courtyard fire, and its light shows his face. A slave girl, one of the least powerful people in the house, says he was with Jesus: "Woman, I do not know him." Someone else says he is one of them: "Man, I am not." An hour later another points to his Galilean accent (Matt 26:73): "Man, I do not know what you are talking about."
- **"The Lord turned and looked at Peter" (v61).** Only Luke records this. Jesus may have been led across the courtyard, or seen him through an open doorway. Luke calls him "the Lord" at the moment Peter has denied knowing him. The look is not described as angry. It brings Peter to remember.
- **"He went out and wept bitterly" (v62).** Peter fell, but he did not fall away. The difference between Peter and Judas is not the size of their sin but what they did afterward. Peter's tears were the beginning of his return, and Jesus' prayer (v32) was being answered.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Jesus' look "a significant look" that conveyed grace to Peter's heart: "It was not the mere look from Christ, but the Divine grace with it, that restored Peter." Guzik stresses that Peter fell but did not fall away.

#### Jesus Is Mocked (vv63–65)
- **"Mocking him as they beat him" (v63).** These are the guards holding him during the night. Jesus had foretold he would be "mocked and shamefully treated and spit upon" (18:32). Isaiah's servant gave his back to those who struck him (Isa 50:6).
- **"Prophesy! Who is it that struck you?" (v64).** They blindfold him and play a cruel game, mocking his reputation as a prophet. The irony is sharp: his prophecy about Peter has just been fulfilled in the same courtyard.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those that condemned Jesus for a blasphemer, were the vilest blasphemers." Guzik sees in the beating a picture of human hostility toward God, and notes that Jesus endured it without using his power to retaliate.

#### Jesus Before the Council (vv66–71)
- **"When day came, the assembly of the elders" (v66).** Jewish law, as later recorded in the Mishnah, required capital trials to be held by day. Luke records this formal morning session of the council, the Sanhedrin, after the night hearings. Later rules also forbade trials on feast days and required a day between a trial and a guilty verdict. Scholars debate how strictly those rules applied in Jesus' time, but the haste and the predetermined outcome are plain.
- **"If I tell you, you will not believe, and if I ask you, you will not answer" (vv67–68).** They showed this already, refusing to answer his question about John (20:7).
- **"From now on the Son of Man shall be seated at the right hand of the power of God" (v69).** Jesus joins Daniel 7:13 and Psalm 110:1. "The power" is a reverent way of saying "God." The man on trial says he will soon be the judge.
- **"Are you the Son of God, then?" "You say that I am" (v70).** His answer is a yes, but a careful one. They understand it as a claim to be far more than a human messiah.
- **"What further testimony do we need?" (v71).** They have the charge they wanted. But blasphemy is a religious charge, and Rome would not execute a man for it. When they bring him to Pilate, they will change it to a political one (23:2).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus pointed them to his second coming "for the full proof of his being the Christ," and "owns himself to be the Son of God, though he knew he should suffer for it." Guzik lists the rules of Jewish law the trial broke.

#### Pointing to Christ
At the table Jesus takes the old Passover and fills it with himself: his body given, his blood poured out, a new covenant. In the garden he accepts the cup of wrath he will drink for others. At the arrest he heals an enemy. In the courtyard he looks at a friend who has just denied him, and the look brings him home. Before the council, the prisoner says he will sit at God's right hand. The one "numbered with the transgressors" is the servant who "bore the sin of many" (Isa 53:12).
