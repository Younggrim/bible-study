*Setting:* Late in Ahab's reign, around 855–853 BC. Chapter 20 ended with Ahab going home "vexed and sullen" after a prophet condemned him for sparing Ben-hadad. This chapter opens with the same mood over a much smaller matter: a neighbor's vineyard. The setting moves from the battlefield to a small town, Jezreel, where Ahab had a second palace in the fertile valley of the same name. The story is about land, law and murder. In Israel, land was not simply property. God owned the land and gave each family its share as an inheritance, never to be sold away for good (Lev 25:23). A king who forgets that, and a queen who never believed it, set up one of the darkest crimes in the Old Testament. Elijah returns to confront them.

#### Naboth's Vineyard (vv1–16)
- **"Naboth the Jezreelite had a vineyard in Jezreel, beside the palace" (v1).** Jezreel sat on a ridge at the eastern end of the Jezreel Valley, with springs, rich soil and a view down toward the Jordan. Excavations there have uncovered a large royal enclosure from this period. The phrase "Ahab king of Samaria" uses the capital's name for the whole northern kingdom.
- **"Give me your vineyard, that I may have it for a vegetable garden" (v2).** Ahab wants to turn a vineyard into a vegetable plot. Vines took years to mature, and a vineyard was a family's long-term wealth. Ahab's offer sounds fair: a better vineyard or a cash price. By the standards of the day, it was not a bad deal. The trouble is what the land meant to Naboth. There may be an echo too: in Deuteronomy, Egypt is the land of the vegetable garden (Deut 11:10), while the promised land is a land of vines.
- **"The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers" (v3).** "Inheritance" (*nachalah*) is the key word. The land had been divided by lot among Israel's tribes and families under Joshua (Josh 13–21). The law said, "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine" (Lev 25:23), and family land was to stay in the family and tribe (Num 36:7). Even land sold in hard times returned to its family at the Jubilee. Naboth is not being stubborn or greedy. He swears by the LORD. He is being faithful. JFB says he acted from "a conscientious regard to the divine law." Henry: "he must obey God rather than men."
- **"Ahab went into his house vexed and sullen" (v4).** The exact words of 20:43. He lies on his bed, turns his face to the wall and refuses to eat. The king of Israel is sulking like a child over a garden. Notice that Ahab, unlike Jezebel, knows he cannot simply take the land. Israel's king was under the law too (Deut 17:18–20). He just doesn't like it. Henry: "Discontent is a sin that is its own punishment and makes men torment themselves."
- **"Give me your vineyard for money" (v6).** When Ahab tells Jezebel, he leaves out the crucial words. Naboth had said "the inheritance of my fathers." Ahab reports only "I will not give you my vineyard." He makes Naboth's faithfulness sound like mere rudeness.
- **"Do you now govern Israel?" (v7).** This is mockery: "Are you the king or not?" Jezebel grew up in the court of Tyre, where Phoenician kings did what they pleased. To her, a king who lets a farmer refuse him is weak. **"I will give you the vineyard."** The "I" is emphatic. She will do what he lacks the nerve to do.
- **"She wrote letters in Ahab's name and sealed them with his seal" (v8).** A royal seal, pressed into clay or wax, made a document official. Many seals from ancient Israel have been found, including one engraved with the name "Yzbl," which some scholars link to Jezebel, though that is debated. Using his seal makes the order the king's own. Ahab let her take it. The letters go to "the elders and the leaders" of Jezreel, the town's council and nobles.
- **"Proclaim a fast" (v9).** A public fast was called when a town believed disaster was coming because of some hidden sin (Judg 20:26; 1 Sam 7:6; Joel 1:14). The point was to find the guilty person and remove the curse. After years of drought and war, a fast would not have seemed strange. Jezebel uses the forms of religion to commit murder. **"Set Naboth at the head of the people"**: give him a place of honor at the assembly, so his fall would be all the more public.
- **"Two worthless men" (v10).** Literally "sons of *belial*," a Hebrew phrase meaning "worthless," "good for nothing," scoundrels (Deut 13:13; 1 Sam 2:12). In later Jewish writing Belial became a name for Satan, and Paul uses it that way: "What accord has Christ with Belial?" (2 Cor 6:15). The law required two witnesses for a capital charge (Deut 17:6; 19:15). Jezebel knows the law well enough to fake it.
- **"You have cursed God and the king" (v10).** The Hebrew literally says "blessed God and the king." Scribes in several places used "bless" as a polite substitute so as not to write "curse God" (also Job 1:5, 11; 2:9). Cursing God carried the death penalty by stoning (Lev 24:15–16), and cursing a ruler was also forbidden (Exod 22:28). Charging both makes the case airtight: blasphemy and treason at once.
- **"They took him outside the city and stoned him to death" (v13).** Executions took place outside the town (Lev 24:14; Num 15:36). The elders did exactly as the letters said (v11). No one in Jezreel refused, or if anyone did, we are not told. Later we learn that Naboth's sons were killed too (2 Kgs 9:26). With no heirs left, the land would pass to the crown, which is how the scheme was meant to work.
- **"Naboth has been stoned; he is dead" (v14).** The report goes to Jezebel, not Ahab. Everyone knew who was really in charge.
- **"Arise, take possession of the vineyard" (v15).** Jezebel repeats "arise" from v7. She speaks of Naboth with icy contempt: "not alive, but dead." **"As soon as Ahab heard that Naboth was dead, Ahab arose"** (v16). He asks no questions. His silence is consent. He goes down to claim the vineyard.
- **Commentators:** All three agree Naboth's refusal was faithfulness, not stubbornness. Guzik, drawing on Poole, lays out the laws against permanently selling family land, and portrays Ahab as spineless and childish, noting Adam Clarke's dry remark that a king ruling most of Israel could be miserable over one small vineyard. Guzik also notes Maclaren's three kinds of wickedness in the story: weak Ahab, strong Jezebel and the elders who simply went along. JFB says the fast was called to "give an external appearance of justice to their proceedings" and calls the witnesses "worthless fellows who had been bribed to swear a falsehood." The Tyndale notes connect the death of Naboth's sons to Achan's family (Josh 7:24–25).

#### The LORD Condemns Ahab (vv17–24)
- **"Then the word of the LORD came to Elijah the Tishbite" (v17).** Elijah has not been seen since chapter 19. Jezebel arranged everything quietly by letter, but God saw it. **"Arise, go down"** (v18): the same word "arise" Jezebel used twice. She told Ahab to arise and take. God tells Elijah to arise and confront.
- **"Have you killed and also taken possession?" (v19).** In Hebrew this is two words, and it sums up the crime. Ahab did not throw a stone, but God holds him responsible. He is the king, the seal was his, and he took the profit.
- **"In the place where dogs licked up the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick your own blood" (v19).** Dogs in the ancient Near East were mostly scavengers roaming the streets, not pets. Being left to dogs was the worst disgrace, the denial of burial. The punishment fits the crime. When Ahab dies in battle, his blood is washed from his chariot at the pool of Samaria and "the dogs licked up his blood" (22:38). Because of Ahab's repentance (v29), the fullest fulfillment falls on his son Joram, whose body is thrown onto Naboth's own field at Jezreel (2 Kgs 9:25–26). JFB notes it was fulfilled "not in Jezreel, but in Samaria," and fully in the son. The prophecy is about the same judgment, worked out over two generations.
- **"Have you found me, O my enemy?" (v20).** Ahab's first words show his guilty conscience. He knows why Elijah has come. He sees God's prophet as his enemy, as he called him "troubler of Israel" before (18:17).
- **"I have found you, because you have sold yourself to do what is evil" (v20).** "Sold yourself" is the language of selling oneself into slavery. Ahab wanted to buy Naboth's land, and instead sold himself. Paul uses the same picture for the power of sin: "sold under sin" (Rom 7:14).
- **"I will cut off from Ahab every male, bond or free" (v21).** The Hebrew for "male" is a coarse phrase, literally "one who urinates against the wall," used in these curses on dynasties (14:10; 16:11). "Bond or free" probably means every last one, whatever his position. The dynasty of Omri will end like the dynasties of Jeroboam (15:29) and Baasha (16:11–12), each wiped out after its founder's son.
- **"The dogs shall eat Jezebel within the walls of Jezreel" (v23).** Some manuscripts read "in the plot of ground at Jezreel." Either way, this is fulfilled exactly when Jehu has Jezebel thrown from a window there, and the dogs leave only her skull, feet and hands (2 Kgs 9:30–37). Jehu himself remembers Elijah's words when it happens (2 Kgs 9:36).
- **"Anyone belonging to Ahab who dies in the city the dogs shall eat" (v24).** The same curse was spoken over the houses of Jeroboam and Baasha (14:11; 16:4). No family tomb, no honor, no memory.
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses Elijah's courage, and holds Ahab responsible as husband, king and the one who profited, even though Jezebel planned it. He passes on Poole's point that being tempted is no excuse. On "my enemy," Guzik draws on Meyer: the prophet was Ahab's truest friend, but to someone living in sin a faithful friend looks like an enemy. JFB calls the dogs "a righteous retribution of Providence." Guzik discusses the apparent gap between the prophecy and Ahab's death in Samaria and, with Clarke, favors the view that the judgment passed to Joram, whose body ended up on Naboth's field.

#### Ahab's Repentance (vv25–29)
- **"There was none who sold himself to do what was evil... like Ahab, whom Jezebel his wife incited" (v25).** The narrator steps out of the story to give his verdict, set off in the ESV with parentheses. Ahab was the worst. Jezebel "incited" him, a word used of stirring up or luring (compare 1 Chr 21:1). She pushed, but he chose.
- **"As the Amorites had done, whom the LORD cast out" (v26).** "Amorites" stands for all the peoples of Canaan whose idolatry and practices led God to drive them out (Gen 15:16; Lev 18:24–28). The warning is unmistakable: Israel under Ahab has become like the nations God removed from the land, and may lose the land the same way. The very sin of the chapter, stealing a family's inheritance, is a sin against the land itself.
- **"He tore his clothes and put sackcloth on his flesh and fasted" (v27).** This is surprising. Ahab, who sulked twice, now humbles himself in grief. He "went about dejectedly," literally "softly" or "quietly," the walk of a mourner. Sackcloth "on his flesh" means against the skin, not over his robes. Whether this was deep or shallow, the text does not say. It does not record him giving back the vineyard or rebuking Jezebel. But God takes it seriously.
- **"Have you seen how Ahab has humbled himself before me?" (v29).** God points it out to Elijah, almost as if to say, "Did you notice?" It is remarkable that God, who had just pronounced the harshest judgment, responds at once to the worst king's humbling. The word "humbled" (*kana'*) is used later of Hezekiah, Manasseh and Josiah humbling themselves before God (2 Chr 32:26; 33:12; 34:27).
- **"I will not bring the disaster in his days; but in his son's days" (v29).** The judgment is delayed, not cancelled. This does not mean the son is punished for the father's sin while himself innocent. Ahab's sons Ahaziah and Joram followed in his ways (22:52; 2 Kgs 3:2), and the judgment falls when it does because the dynasty never changed. The delay shows how ready God is to show mercy (Ezek 18:23).
- **Commentators:** JFB says Ahab "was not obdurate, like Jezebel," and that his sorrow was real enough for God to delay the judgment. Guzik is less impressed. He sees the repentance as real but shallow, passing on Poole's point that Ahab never returned the land or confronted Jezebel, and Trapp's that God gave a temporary reprieve for a temporary repentance. But Guzik also stresses how far God's mercy reaches: if God responded even to Ahab's humbling, he will not turn away anyone who comes to him humbly. The Tyndale notes call Ahab a complicated man, rightly condemned but able to repent when rebuked.

#### Pointing to Christ
Naboth is one of the clearest pictures in the Old Testament of what would be done to Jesus. An innocent man who would not betray his Father's inheritance was accused by false witnesses of blasphemy against God and the king, condemned in a show of religious law, and taken outside the city to be killed (Matt 26:59–66; John 19:12; Heb 13:12). Jesus told a parable about tenants who killed the heir to seize the vineyard (Mark 12:1–9), and the religious leaders knew he meant them. The difference is that Naboth's blood cried out for justice. Jesus' blood "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24), the word of forgiveness. And the God who noticed Ahab's humbling, and delayed judgment, is the God who sent his Son so that judgment would fall on him instead of on everyone who humbles themselves before him (1 Pet 5:6).
