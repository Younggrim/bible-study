*Setting:* Jerusalem in the later years of Josiah or the early years of Jehoiakim (roughly 620–605 BC). Chapters 2–3 laid out God's case against his faithless people and ended with a call to return and the words of a confession: "Behold, we come to you." Chapter 4 opens by telling them what real return would require (vv1–4), then breaks into alarm. A trumpet sounds, a lion leaves its thicket, and an army sweeps down "from the north." The enemy is not named here. Older scholars sometimes suggested the Scythians, raiding horsemen from the steppes, but most now see Babylon, which is named in 20:4. The chapter is some of the most vivid war poetry in the Bible. In the middle of it Jeremiah cries out in his own pain, and he sees a vision of the whole earth undone.

#### Faithless Israel Called to Repentance (vv1–4)
- **"If you return, O Israel… to me you should return" (v1).** The ESV heading began at 3:6, and these verses finish that section. God answers the confession of 3:22–25 with a condition. Return must be to God himself, not just to religion. **"If you remove your detestable things"**: the idols must actually go. **"And do not waver"**: no wandering back again.
- **"If you swear, 'As the LORD lives,' in truth, in justice, and in righteousness" (v2).** An oath by the living God was the way Israel confirmed its loyalty (Deut 10:20). But it had to be honest. Jeremiah 5:2 complains that they say "As the LORD lives" and swear falsely. The three words cover the inner life ("truth") and conduct toward others ("justice," "righteousness").
- **"Then nations shall bless themselves in him, and in him shall they glory" (v2).** This is God's promise to Abraham: "in your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed" (Gen 22:18). Israel was never meant to keep God to itself. A people truly turned to God would draw the nations to him. Their unfaithfulness was not only their own loss but the world's. Paul echoes the last line: "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1 Cor 1:31, from Jer 9:24).
- **"Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns" (v3).** Fallow ground was a field left unplowed, which grew hard and overgrown. A farmer who scattered seed over it would get nothing but thorns (Matt 13:7). Hosea used the same picture (Hos 10:12). Repentance means breaking up the hard ground of the heart before the seed of God's word can grow.
- **"Circumcise yourselves to the LORD; remove the foreskin of your hearts" (v4).** Circumcision was the sign of the covenant (Gen 17:10–14). Every man in Judah bore it. But the sign was meant to point to a heart given to God. Moses had already said so (Deut 10:16), and promised that one day God would do it himself: "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart" (Deut 30:6). Paul builds on this: "circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter" (Rom 2:29; Col 2:11).
- **"Lest my wrath go forth like fire, and burn with none to quench it" (v4).** The warning that turns the chapter. If they do not change, the fire of judgment cannot be put out.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the heart in v3 well: "An unconvinced unhumbled heart is like fallow-ground, ground untilled, unoccupied." On v4 he says, "Do that in sincerity which was done in profession by your circumcision." JFB takes v2 to mean that the conversion of the nations would follow Israel's, as the nations "bless themselves in (by) Him." Guzik stresses that the plowing of v3 is costly: repentance has to break the hard ground, not just scatter a little seed on top.

#### Disaster from the North (vv5–18)
- **"Blow the trumpet through the land" (v5).** The trumpet was the *shofar*, a ram's horn, the alarm signal of war (Amos 3:6). Villagers in the open country were to run for the walled cities. **"Raise a standard toward Zion"** (v6): a signal flag on a hill to point the refugees toward Jerusalem.
- **"I bring disaster from the north" (v6).** The boiling pot of 1:13–14 is now tipping. "Disaster" is *raʿah*, the same word as "evil." God brings evil, in the sense of calamity, on a people who have done evil.
- **"A lion has gone up from his thicket, a destroyer of nations has set out" (v7).** Lions lived in the thick brush of the Jordan valley (49:19). The lion picture fits Babylon, whose great Processional Way was lined with lions in glazed brick, and Daniel's vision shows Babylon as a lion with eagle's wings (Dan 7:4). "Destroyer of nations" says this lion's target is not Judah alone.
- **"Put on sackcloth, lament and wail" (v8).** Sackcloth was rough goat-hair cloth worn in mourning. **"The fierce anger of the LORD has not turned back from us."** Jeremiah says "us." He stands with his people under the judgment.
- **"Courage shall fail both king and officials" (v9).** Literally "the heart" of king and officials will perish. The priests and prophets who promised peace will be stunned speechless.
- **"Ah, Lord GOD, surely you have utterly deceived this people" (v10).** One of Jeremiah's most startling prayers. The prophets of the day kept saying, "It shall be well with you" (6:14; 23:17). Jeremiah cries out as if God himself had told the lie. Most read this as Jeremiah's anguished complaint: God allowed false prophets to speak, and the people gladly believed them (1 Kings 22:19–23 shows God permitting a lying spirit). Jeremiah later knows exactly where the false promises came from: "the prophets are prophesying lies in my name. I did not send them" (14:14). The cry is not doctrine. It is honest prayer, the kind the Psalms allow.
- **"A hot wind from the bare heights in the desert… not to winnow or cleanse" (v11).** Farmers threshed grain and then tossed it into a light breeze, which blew the chaff away and let the grain fall (Ps 1:4). This wind is the sirocco, the scorching east wind off the desert that withers everything. It is **"too full"** for winnowing. It will not separate good from bad. It will flatten everything. **"Daughter of my people"** is a tender name for the nation, as if speaking of a beloved child.
- **"He comes up like clouds; his chariots like the whirlwind; his horses are swifter than eagles" (v13).** The invader rolls in like a storm. The words echo the covenant curse: "a nation… swooping down like the eagle" (Deut 28:49), and Habakkuk's description of the Chaldeans (Hab 1:8). The people's cry is short: **"Woe to us, for we are ruined!"**
- **"O Jerusalem, wash your heart from evil, that you may be saved" (v14).** Even now, with the enemy in sight, the door is open. Washing the heart echoes Isaiah 1:16 and Psalm 51:10. **"How long shall your wicked thoughts lodge within you?"** "Lodge" means stay overnight, like a guest who will not leave.
- **"A voice declares from Dan and proclaims trouble from Mount Ephraim" (v15).** Dan was the northernmost town of Israel, on the road any invader from the north would take. The hill country of Ephraim was the old heart of the northern kingdom, only about ten miles north of Jerusalem. The pictured news is getting closer by the hour: first from the far border, then from just over the hill. There is irony too. Dan was where Jeroboam had set up a golden calf (1 Kings 12:29). Judgment comes down the same road the idolatry took.
- **"Warn the nations… besiegers come from a distant land" (v16).** "Besiegers" is literally "watchers" or "guards." **"Like keepers of a field"** (v17): like the watchmen who camped around a field at harvest to guard it, the army camps around Jerusalem so that no one can get out.
- **"Your ways and your deeds have brought this upon you" (v18).** The same truth as 2:17 and 2:19. **"It has reached your very heart."** The bitterness of judgment goes as deep as the sin did.
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies the lion as Nebuchadnezzar and its "thicket" as Babylon. Guzik notes that scholars have debated whether the enemy was Scythian, Assyrian or Babylonian, and judges Babylon the best fit. On v10 JFB explains that God allows the false prophets to deceive people who have already chosen error, and Henry is blunt: "Thus they cheated themselves and then wickedly complained that God had cheated them." The Tyndale notes say Jeremiah himself was confused by the promises of peace. On v14 Henry writes, "No reformation is saving but that which reaches the heart," and Guzik cites Spurgeon's picture of sinful thoughts as lodgers who must be put out of the house. JFB identifies the hot wind of v11 as the simoom, the desert storm wind, and sees divine justice in the warning coming from Dan and Bethel, where Jeroboam's calves stood.

#### Anguish over Judah's Desolation (vv19–31)
- **"My anguish, my anguish! I writhe in pain!" (v19).** Literally "My bowels, my bowels!" In Hebrew the inner organs were the seat of deep feeling, as English speaks of the heart. **"Oh the walls of my heart! My heart is beating wildly"**: the prophet feels his heart pounding in his chest. This is why Jeremiah is called "the weeping prophet" (9:1; 13:17). He does not announce judgment from a distance. He feels it before it comes.
- **"Crash follows hard on crash" (v20).** Literally "destruction upon destruction." **"Suddenly my tents are laid waste, my curtains in a moment"**: the image of a nomad's camp torn down in an instant. The tent curtains are gone.
- **"How long must I see the standard and hear the sound of the trumpet?" (v21).** The war signals of vv5–6 have become a constant torment.
- **"For my people are foolish; they know me not" (v22).** God answers the prophet's cry by explaining the cause. The words are a parent's grief: "stupid children." **"They are 'wise'—in doing evil! But how to do good they know not."** They have the skill and cunning of the wise, all turned to wrongdoing (Rom 16:19 reverses it: "wise as to what is good and innocent as to what is evil").
- **"I looked on the earth, and behold, it was without form and void" (v23).** The Hebrew is *tohu wabohu*, the exact phrase of Genesis 1:2, found together elsewhere only in Isaiah 34:11. Jeremiah sees creation run backwards. **"To the heavens, and they had no light"** undoes day one. The mountains quake (v24), there is **"no man"** (v25), which undoes day six, the birds have fled, undoing day five, and the fruitful land is a desert, undoing day three (v26). It is poetic, not a literal prediction of the end of the world. But it shows how total the judgment on Judah will feel. When God's people forsake him, their world returns to chaos.
- **"I looked… I looked… I looked… I looked" (vv23–26).** Four times, each line starting the same way, like four camera shots of a ruined world. The last ends with the reason: **"before the LORD, before his fierce anger."**
- **"Yet I will not make a full end" (v27).** In the middle of total desolation, a small word of mercy. God will judge, but he will not wipe his people out. The same promise returns in 5:10, 5:18 and 30:11. It is the thread of hope that leads to the remnant and the return from exile.
- **"For this the earth shall mourn, and the heavens above be dark" (v28).** Mourners dressed in black or dark clothing, and the sky itself will look like a mourner. **"I have spoken; I have purposed; I have not relented, nor will I turn back."** The verb "turn back" is *shuv*, the key word of chapter 3. Because the people would not turn back, God will not turn back from judgment.
- **"At the noise of horseman and archer every city takes to flight" (v29).** The people run into the thickets and climb up among the rocks, as Israel did in the days of the Philistines (1 Sam 13:6). The cities stand empty.
- **"And you, O desolate one, what do you mean that you dress in scarlet" (v30).** Judah is pictured as a woman dressing up to seduce: scarlet clothes, gold jewelry, eyes painted larger with black eye-paint made from antimony (as Jezebel did before she died, 2 Kings 9:30). This probably points to Judah's diplomacy, trying to charm Egypt or other allies into helping. **"Your lovers despise you; they seek your life."** The nations she courts will turn on her.
- **"A cry as of a woman in labor, anguish as of one giving birth to her first child" (v31).** A first labor is the hardest and most frightening. The seductress of v30 is now a woman gasping in pain, **"stretching out her hands"** for help that does not come. **"Woe is me! I am fainting before murderers."** The chapter that began with the offer, "if you return," ends with this cry.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v19 as the grief of a godly man in an evil world: "A good man, in such a bad world as this is, cannot but be a man of sorrows." JFB sees a rising sequence in the verse, from the bowels to the wall around the heart to the heart itself. All three connect vv23–26 with Genesis 1:2. JFB says the land is "reduced to the primeval chaos," and Henry calls it "confusion and emptiness, stripped of all its beauty." Guzik points beyond it to the greater judgment Christ bore. On v27 Henry offers a lasting comfort: "every end of our comforts is not a full end, however we may be ready to think it so," and JFB says God leaves "some hope of restoration." On v30 JFB takes the painted eyes as Judah's attempt to win Egypt's help, and notes that the Hebrew reads "eyes," not "face" as in the KJV. Henry says the paint, "though it beautifies the face for the present, really rends it."

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah weeps over a city that will not repent, feels its ruin in his body, and cries, "My anguish, my anguish!" Six centuries later Jesus wept over the same city: "Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace!" (Luke 19:41–44). Jeremiah saw creation undone, the earth "without form and void" and the heavens without light. At the cross darkness covered the land at midday (Mark 15:33), as the Son bore the judgment that undoes the world. And Jesus is the one who gives what Jeremiah called for and could not give: a circumcised heart, done "by the Spirit, not by the letter" (Rom 2:29). In him the promise of v2 comes true, and the nations "bless themselves in him, and in him… glory."
