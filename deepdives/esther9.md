*Setting:* About 473 BC, nearly a year after Haman cast his lot (3:7) and nine months after Mordecai's counter-decree went out (8:9). Two royal edicts are in force at once. One allows anyone to attack the Jews on the thirteenth of Adar; the other allows the Jews to gather and defend themselves on that same day. Haman is dead and Mordecai now holds his office. Chapter 9 tells what happened on the day itself and then explains how it became the feast of Purim, which Jews still keep every year in February or March. This is also the chapter modern readers find hardest, with its numbers of dead, and it needs to be read carefully, on its own terms.

#### The Jews Destroy Their Enemies (vv1–19)
- **"On the very day when the enemies of the Jews hoped to gain the mastery over them, the reverse occurred" (v1).** "The reverse occurred" (literally "it was turned upside down") is the theme of the whole book in two Hebrew words. Every part of Haman's plan has been reversed, and now the day itself is too. Notice the verb is passive: it "was turned." The book does not say who turned it, but the reader knows.
- **"To lay hands on those who sought their harm" (v2).** The Jews do not go hunting. They gather "in their cities" to meet those who come against them. The enemies are described three ways: "those who hated them" (v1), "those who sought their harm" (v2), "their enemies" (v5). The fighting is against people who chose to act on Haman's decree even after Mordecai's was published.
- **"The fear of them had fallen on all peoples… the fear of Mordecai had fallen on them" (vv2–3).** Two kinds of fear. Ordinary people fear the Jews, as the Canaanites feared Israel (Josh 2:9). Officials fear Mordecai, the king's new chief minister. The officials, who under the first decree would have led the slaughter (3:12–13), now help the Jews.
- **"The man Mordecai grew more and more powerful" (v4).** The phrase recalls David, who "grew stronger and stronger" while Saul's house "became weaker and weaker" (2 Sam 3:1). Here too a house rises and an enemy's house falls.
- **"Did as they pleased to those who hated them" (v5).** This echoes the king's word to Haman: "to do with them as it seems good to you" (3:11). The power Haman was given over the Jews has passed to them.
- **500 men in Susa the citadel (v6).** "The citadel" was the fortified royal district, so these were probably Haman's supporters at court: his household, allies and officials. The Tyndale notes suggest this was close to an attempted revolt.
- **The ten sons of Haman (vv7–10).** Most of the names are Persian. Haman boasted of "the number of his sons" (5:11); now they are named one by one, and the list ends with their father's title, **"the enemy of the Jews."** In Jewish scrolls of Esther these ten names are written in a narrow column down the page, and the reader of the scroll at Purim traditionally says them all in one breath.
- **"But they laid no hand on the plunder" (v10).** Said three times in this chapter (vv10, 15, 16). The decree allowed them to take plunder (8:11), and they refused. Two reasons stand out. First, they wanted it clear that this was self-defense, not robbery; Haman's men were promised Jewish property (3:13), but the Jews would not profit from blood. Second, the reader of 1 Samuel 15 remembers that Saul lost his kingdom because he took the plunder of Amalek instead of devoting it to God (1 Sam 15:9, 19). Now a Benjaminite descended from Kish leads a people who will not repeat that sin. The narrator does not spell it out, but the repetition seems to point there.
- **"What then have they done in the rest of the king's provinces!" (v12).** The king seems more impressed than troubled. He turns at once to Esther with his usual offer: **"Now what is your wish?"**
- **"Let the Jews who are in Susa be allowed tomorrow also" (v13).** This request has troubled many readers, who see it as vengeful. Other readers point out what the text implies: enemies were still active in the capital, and some may have hidden until the one-day permission ran out. JFB argues this way. **"Let the ten sons of Haman be hanged."** They were already dead (v10). Hanging their bodies on stakes was a public display, a warning to anyone still planning to act on Haman's decree. Saul's own body was displayed the same way by the Philistines (1 Sam 31:10).
- **300 more killed in Susa on the fourteenth (v15).** Again, no plunder.
- **75,000 in the provinces (v16).** Across an empire of 127 provinces stretching from India to Ethiopia, this was a very large number, but spread over a huge area. The Greek version reads 15,000. The Tyndale notes add that the Hebrew word for *thousand* can sometimes mean a clan or military unit, which would make the number far smaller; but that suggestion is debated, and the plain reading is 75,000. The text stresses that these were people who "hated them," and that the Jews "gathered to defend their lives" and **"got relief from their enemies."** The word "relief" is literally "rest" (*nuach*), the same word used for the rest God gave Israel from its enemies in the land (Josh 21:44; 2 Sam 7:1).
- **How to read the violence.** Christians have read this differently. Some see the Jews' action as justified self-defense against people who were determined to kill them, with God's judgment on Amalek in the background (Exod 17:14; Deut 25:19). Others hold that the narrator reports it without approving every part. The text itself never calls it God's command. It records a defensive war on a single fixed day against armed attackers, carried out under royal law, with no plunder taken. What it does not do is celebrate the killing: the feast is held on the day after, the day of "rest."
- **"On the fourteenth day they rested and made that a day of feasting and gladness" (vv17–19).** The Jews in the provinces fought on the 13th and celebrated on the 14th. The Jews of Susa fought on the 13th and 14th and celebrated on the 15th. That is why, to this day, most Jews keep Purim on the 14th of Adar, while those in walled cities, Jerusalem above all, keep "Shushan Purim" on the 15th. **"Gifts of food to one another"** became a lasting Purim custom.
- **Commentators:** JFB defends Esther against "the charge of being actuated by a cruel and vindictive disposition," arguing that the enemies "adroitly concealing themselves for the first day might have returned on the next," and that displaying the sons' bodies was meant "to brand them with public infamy." He also suggests that many of the dead were Amalekites, fulfilling the old prophecies against that people. Matthew Henry stresses that "the enemies of the Jews were the aggressors," that both sides had months of warning, and that "if they would have sat still… not a hair of their head would have fallen." He adds that the officials helped the Jews not out of kindness but because "the fear of Mordecai fell upon them." Guzik acknowledges that many criticize Esther for the second day, and answers that she, like Joshua, would not settle for less than full victory. He quotes Spurgeon: the conflict with Amalek "which began with Joshua in the desert was to be finished by Mordecai in the king's palace."

#### The Feast of Purim Inaugurated (vv20–32)
- **"Mordecai recorded these things and sent letters" (v20).** Mordecai writes to every Jew "near and far" to fix the feast as a yearly practice. Some older Jewish tradition took "recorded these things" to mean Mordecai wrote the book of Esther itself. Most scholars think the book was written later, though perhaps using his records.
- **"The fourteenth day… and also the fifteenth day… year by year" (v21).** Mordecai makes both days official for everyone, so that no community feels its date is the wrong one.
- **"Turned for them from sorrow into gladness and from mourning into a holiday" (v22).** The same idea of reversal again. Psalm 30:11 says it in prayer: "You have turned for me my mourning into dancing."
- **"Gifts of food to one another and gifts to the poor" (v22).** Purim is not only a party. Care for the poor is written into it. Jews still keep both customs: sending portions of food to friends (*mishloach manot*) and giving to the needy.
- **"So the Jews accepted what they had started to do" (v23).** The feast began among the people before Mordecai's letter made it official. Then vv24–25 summarize the whole story in a few lines. Notice what the summary says in v25: "when it came before the king." The Hebrew is literally "when she came," likely meaning when Esther came before the king, though the ESV takes it more generally. The plan that Haman "devised against the Jews" returned **"on his own head."**
- **"They called these days Purim, after the term Pur" (v26).** *Purim* is the plural of *pur*, the lot Haman cast (3:7). The name of the feast remembers that Haman thought chance or the gods had chosen the day of destruction. Instead it became a day of rescue. Proverbs puts the theology in one line: "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Prov 16:33).
- **"The Jews firmly obligated themselves and their offspring and all who joined them" (v27).** "All who joined them" includes Gentiles who became Jews (8:17). Purim is the only major festival in the Hebrew Bible not given through Moses. These verses explain why it was nevertheless binding: the people themselves took it on, with the authority of Mordecai and the queen.
- **"These days should never fall into disuse… nor should the commemoration of these days cease" (v28).** It never has. Purim has been kept for about 2,400 years, often in times of persecution, when the story of a people saved from extermination meant a great deal.
- **Esther's letter (vv29–32).** **"Queen Esther, the daughter of Abihail"** writes with "full written authority," confirming Mordecai's "second letter." She is now an author and lawgiver for her people, and the chapter ends with her command. The letters go out "in words of peace and truth" (v30), a phrase Zechariah uses: "love truth and peace" (Zech 8:19), in the same verse where he promises that Israel's fasts will become "seasons of joy and gladness."
- **"With regard to their fasts and their lamenting" (v31).** Purim keeps a memory of the fasting in chapter 4 as well as the feasting. Jews observe the "Fast of Esther" on the 13th of Adar, the day before Purim.
- **"It was recorded in writing" (v32).** Haman's decree was written and sealed; so was Mordecai's; now the feast too is written down. The last word belongs to the record that will outlast the empire.
- **Commentators:** JFB describes Jewish Purim customs of his day: the scroll read in synagogue, the names of Haman's sons arranged "like so many bodies on a gibbet," and noise made whenever Haman's name is read. He notes that commentators differ on whether "these things" (v20) means the letters or a full written account. Matthew Henry notes that the KJV reads v25 as "when Esther came before the king." Assuming, as many did, that Mordecai wrote the book, he contrasts it with Nehemiah, who "at every turn, takes notice of divine Providence," while Mordecai, writing "at Shushan the palace, where policy reigned more than piety," never names God. Henry prefers Nehemiah's way, but concludes that "men may be truly devout though they do not abound in the shows and expressions of devotion." Guzik commends the principle of setting days aside to remember God's deliverance, since believers forget his works too easily.

#### Pointing to Christ
Purim celebrates a day "turned" from death to life, when a decree of destruction was overruled and a people sentenced to die were given rest. Christians have their own day like that. On the cross, what looked like the enemy's victory was "turned upside down" into the defeat of sin and death, and the risen Christ turns his people's "mourning into dancing" (Ps 30:11; John 16:20). Purim's mix of feasting and giving to the poor fits that pattern, too. Those who have been rescued are meant to share what they have been given. The rest the Jews found from their enemies was real but temporary; the writer of Hebrews says there remains a fuller "Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Heb 4:9), won by Christ.
