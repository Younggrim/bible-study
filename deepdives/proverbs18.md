*Setting:* The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 18 is above all a chapter about words: the fool who only wants to air his opinion, the gossip whose stories are swallowed like sweets, the one who answers before he hears, the first speaker in a lawsuit, and the tongue that holds death and life. Around that core sit sayings on isolation and friendship, pride and humility, and at the very center the only place in Proverbs where "the name of the LORD" appears, a strong tower for the righteous (v10). The chapter opens with a man who cuts himself off from others and closes with a friend who sticks closer than a brother.

#### The Loner, the Fool and Deep Waters (vv1–5)
- **"Whoever isolates himself seeks his own desire; he breaks out against all sound judgment" (v1).** The Hebrew word is *niphrad*, "one who separates himself." The ESV and JFB read it as selfishness: the man who walks away from people does so to please himself, and he "breaks out" (the verb of a quarrel bursting out in 17:14 and 20:3) against anyone's sound advice. JFB: he "seeks selfish gratification" and rushes against "all wisdom." The older King James reading ("Through desire a man, having separated himself, seeketh and intermeddleth with all wisdom") took it the other way, as a scholar withdrawing to study, and Matthew Henry follows it: "If we would get knowledge and grace, we must try all methods of improving ourselves." The Hebrew is hard and both readings have defenders, but most modern translations and commentators side with the ESV. Proverbs everywhere else prizes counsel from many advisers (11:14; 15:22), so cutting oneself off is a warning sign.
- **"A fool takes no pleasure in understanding, but only in expressing his opinion" (v2).** "Expressing" is literally "uncovering" or "revealing" his heart. The fool doesn't want to learn, only to be heard. JFB: his heart "takes pleasure in revealing his folly." Guzik passes on the observation that this form of the verb appears elsewhere only when drunken Noah uncovered himself in his tent (Gen 9:21), a picture of how embarrassing such self-exposure is. James puts it simply: "be quick to hear, slow to speak" (Jas 1:19).
- **"When wickedness comes, contempt comes also, and with dishonor comes disgrace" (v3).** Sin and shame travel together. The wicked person brings contempt for others with him, and in the end receives contempt himself. Henry: "As soon as sin entered, shame followed," which is the story of Adam and Eve hiding in the garden (Gen 3:7–10). JFB: "So surely are sin and punishment connected."
- **"The words of a man's mouth are deep waters; the fountain of wisdom is a bubbling brook" (v4).** The two lines can be read as parallel (a wise person's words are deep and keep flowing) or as a contrast (people's words are often deep in the sense of hidden and murky, but wisdom flows clear). 20:5 uses "deep water" for hidden purposes in the heart. The Tyndale notes take the general point: words come up from a person's character as water rises from a spring. JFB: wise speech is "an exhaustless stream of benefit." Henry: "The well-spring of wisdom in the heart of a believer, continually supplies words of wisdom." Jesus promised that from the believer's heart "will flow rivers of living water" (John 7:38).
- **"It is not good to be partial to the wicked or to deprive the righteous of justice" (v5).** "Be partial" is literally "lift up the face," the idiom for favoritism in court. "Not good" is understatement. Compare 17:15 and 24:23. Henry: "The merits of a cause must be looked to, not the person." The law said the same: "You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great" (Lev 19:15).
- **Commentators:** On v1 Henry, reading the King James, finds a seeker of wisdom, while JFB and Guzik see a selfish loner. Guzik adds that God made people for community, reflecting a God who is himself a fellowship of Father, Son and Spirit. All three read v4 as speech flowing from what fills the heart.

#### A Fool's Mouth and a Gossip's Morsels (vv6–9)
- **"A fool's lips walk into a fight, and his mouth invites a beating" (v6).** His words pick quarrels, and in a world where a father or a judge might punish with a rod (10:13; 19:29), they invite one. JFB treats vv6–7 together: "Their rash language ensnares them."
- **"A fool's mouth is his ruin, and his lips are a snare to his soul" (v7).** "Soul" (*nephesh*) here means his whole life, not only his inner self. A snare is a bird trap. The fool sets his own trap and walks into it. Henry: "What mischief bad men do to themselves by their ungoverned tongues!" Paul's catalog of human sin dwells on throat, tongue, lips and mouth (Rom 3:13–14).
- **"The words of a whisperer are like delicious morsels; they go down into the inner parts of the body" (v8).** "Delicious morsels" translates a word found only here and in 26:22, where the whole proverb is repeated. It comes from a verb meaning "to swallow greedily," so the picture is of tidbits gulped down. The King James rendered it "wounds," a guess, and the lexicon supports the ESV; JFB too prefers "sweet morsels." Gossip tastes good, so people take it in, and once it is "down" it stays, shaping how they see the person it was about. A fool mostly ruins himself (v7). A whisperer ruins others (16:28). Henry: "How base are those that sow contention!"
- **"Whoever is slack in his work is a brother to him who destroys" (v9).** The lazy man and the vandal are family. One lets things fall apart, the other knocks them down, and the result is the same ruin. Henry: "Omissions of duty, and in duty, are fatal to the soul, as well as commissions of sin." JFB notes the "destroyer" may be a waster, the prodigal who squanders what others built. Guzik ties it to James 4:17: knowing the right thing and failing to do it is sin.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the gossip proverb appears twice in the book (here and 26:22) because people need the reminder, and that gossip is believed even when it is unconfirmed, though Scripture requires two or three witnesses before a charge is accepted (Deut 19:15; 1 Tim 5:19). Henry and Guzik both read v9 as saying that neglect is a sin, not a neutral failing.

#### The Strong Tower and the Imagined Wall (vv10–12)
- **"The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous man runs into it and is safe" (v10).** Ancient towns had a fortified tower, the last refuge when enemies broke in. At Thebez the townspeople all fled into the "strong tower" and shut themselves in (Judg 9:51). God's "name" means God himself as he has made himself known, his character, as he proclaimed it to Moses: "merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exod 34:5–7). "Is safe" is the verb *sagab*, "to be set on high," out of reach. JFB: "set on high, out of danger." The lexicon confirms this sense here. "Runs" matters too. The tower doesn't help those who only admire it from outside. Compare Psalm 18:2 and 61:3 ("a strong tower against the enemy"), and Joel 2:32: "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved," which Peter and Paul both apply to calling on Jesus (Acts 2:21; Rom 10:13).
- **"A rich man's wealth is his strong city, and like a high wall in his imagination" (v11).** The first line repeats 10:15 word for word, but here the second line punctures it. The rich man's wall is high "in his imagination." The word for "high wall" is the same *sagab* root as "safe" in v10. Side by side, the two verses ask which tower is real. JFB calls trust in riches "a vain trust." The Tyndale notes: money's power is limited (11:4). Jesus' parable of the rich fool, who built bigger barns the night his life was required of him, is this proverb in story form (Luke 12:16–21).
- **"Before destruction a man's heart is haughty, but humility comes before honor" (v12).** The first line restates 16:18, the second repeats 15:33. The proud heart is a forecast of a fall, the humble one a forecast of honor. The Tyndale notes add that pride blocks change because it can't take criticism. Guzik is careful that humility is not running oneself down but seeing oneself as God does.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the name of the LORD is not a magic word but God's revealed character, and suggests running to the particular attribute a trouble calls for: his mercy in guilt, his strength in weakness. Henry reads the tower in light of Christ, God's power made known through him. JFB and the Tyndale notes both stress that vv10–11 are set side by side for contrast.

#### Listening Before Answering (vv13–17)
- **"If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame" (v13).** Answering before the other person finishes is a sign of self-importance. JFB: it "evinces self-conceit, and ensures shame." Job's friends spoke long and confidently without understanding his case, and God rebuked them (Job 42:7).
- **"A man's spirit will endure sickness, but a crushed spirit who can bear?" (v14).** A strong inner life can carry a weak body. But when the spirit itself is crushed, what is left to carry it? The Tyndale notes: an emotional or spiritual burden can be harder than physical illness. Compare 15:13 and 17:22. Henry asks the sober question this raises, how unbearable the soul's misery will be for those under God's judgment. Guzik is gentle here: depression is real, and the proverb calls for kindness to those who carry it. Hope for the crushed comes from outside: "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (Ps 34:18).
- **"An intelligent heart acquires knowledge, and the ear of the wise seeks knowledge" (v15).** Heart and ear work together: the ear listens and the heart understands. The wise are never finished learning (1:5). This is the opposite of the fool of v2. Henry: knowledge must pass from the head into the heart.
- **"A man's gift makes room for him and brings him before the great" (v16).** "Gift" here (*mattan*) is a general word, not the word for "bribe" (*shochad*) in 17:8 and 17:23. Giving gifts to a superior was normal courtesy in the ancient world: Jacob sent gifts ahead to Esau (Gen 32:20) and to the Egyptian governor (Gen 43:11), and Saul worried about having nothing to bring the prophet Samuel (1 Sam 9:7). JFB thinks the verse simply observes what happens, without approving of buying access. Henry turns it around: we come before the greatest King "without money and without price" (Isa 55:1).
- **"The one who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him" (v17).** A courtroom proverb. The first story always sounds convincing until cross-examination. JFB: "One-sided statements are not reliable." The Tyndale notes: hear both sides. This is why Israel's law required judges to "inquire diligently" (Deut 19:18) and why Nicodemus objected, "Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing?" (John 7:51). Henry applies it inward: we see ourselves more truly when we listen to our critics.
- **Commentators:** On v16 Guzik follows the distinction between a gift and a bribe and notes that no gift is needed to approach Christ. Guzik adds on v17 that Christians should hold their beliefs in a way that can survive honest examination. The Tyndale notes and Guzik both treat v14 as a word about emotional and spiritual suffering, not only illness.

#### Lots, Offended Brothers and the Tongue (vv18–21)
- **"The lot puts an end to quarrels and decides between powerful contenders" (v18).** Lots were marked stones or sticks drawn or thrown to reach a decision. Israel used them to divide the land (Josh 18:6–10) and the apostles to choose Judas' replacement (Acts 1:26). Because "its every decision is from the LORD" (16:33), both sides could accept the result. JFB: "properly used," the lot is "a right mode of settling disputes." Henry: "It was customary sometimes to refer matters to God, by casting lots, with solemn prayer." After Pentecost the lot disappears from the New Testament. Believers now settle disputes through Scripture, prayer and wise counsel in the church (1 Cor 6:1–6).
- **"A brother offended is more unyielding than a strong city, and quarreling is like the bars of a castle" (v19).** The Hebrew of the first line is difficult, but the ESV's sense is widely followed. The closer the relationship, the harder the break is to heal. Strong cities and barred gates were nearly impossible to take. Jacob and Esau were estranged for twenty years (Gen 27–33). Absalom and Amnon's feud ended in murder (2 Sam 13). JFB: family feuds are the hardest to heal, so "great care should be used to avoid them." Henry: grace makes forgiveness easy, and corruption makes it hard.
- **"From the fruit of a man's mouth his stomach is satisfied" (v20).** What we say comes back to us and we live on it, for good or ill. Compare 12:14 and 13:2. Henry takes "the belly" as the inner person: what fills the heart decides whether we are at peace.
- **"Death and life are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruits" (v21).** "Power" is literally "the hand of the tongue." Words can kill (false witness sent Naboth to his death, 1 Kgs 21:13) and give life (12:18; 15:4). "Those who love it" are those who love to talk, and they will eat the results of their talking, sweet or bitter. JFB glosses "death and life" as "the greatest evil and good." James develops the same thought: the tongue is small but sets a forest on fire (Jas 3:5–10). Jesus: "by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned" (Matt 12:37).
- **Commentators:** Guzik quotes the rabbinic saying that an evil tongue kills three: the speaker, the one spoken of, and the listener. Henry and JFB agree that the lot was a godly practice when used with prayer and in earnest. The Tyndale notes connect v21 to the book's many warnings about careless speech.

#### A Wife, the Poor and a True Friend (vv22–24)
- **"He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD" (v22).** The wording echoes Wisdom's own promise: "whoever finds me finds life and obtains favor from the LORD" (8:35). A good wife is a gift of the same kind as wisdom. This goes back to Eden: "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Gen 2:18). The ancient Greek and Latin translations add "good" before "wife," and JFB notes this. The Hebrew doesn't have it, but other proverbs make clear the saying assumes a good wife (12:4; 19:14; 21:9; 31:10). Henry: a good wife is a great blessing and a sign of God's favor.
- **"The poor use entreaties, but the rich answer roughly" (v23).** An observation, not a recommendation. Poverty makes people plead, and wealth can make people curt. JFB: the rich man is put up with only "because rich," which shows how wrong it is to judge people by money. Henry: before God's throne all of us are the poor man, and must come with humble requests. Jesus treated blind Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46–52) with the same care he gave a nobleman (John 4:46–53).
- **"A man of many companions may come to ruin, but there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother" (v24).** A wordplay: "companions" is *re'im*, and "come to ruin" is a verb from a similar-sounding root (*ra'a*, "to break"). The King James, misreading that verb, gave the famous "A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly." The point is not quantity. Many casual acquaintances can't save a person, but one true friend can. "Sticks" (*dabeq*, "clings") is the same verb used of a husband holding fast to his wife (Gen 2:24) and of Ruth clinging to Naomi (Ruth 1:14). The Tyndale notes: real friends show themselves in hard times. Jonathan, who loved David "as his own soul," is the model (1 Sam 18:1–3).
- **Commentators:** JFB suggests the writer may have had in mind a friend greater than any human one, "Friend of sinners," and Henry says Christ "never forsakes those who trust and love him." Guzik ends the chapter the same way. The verse is first about human friendship, but all three see where it points.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 18 sets two towers side by side: the rich man's high wall that exists "in his imagination" (v11), and the name of the LORD, a strong tower where the righteous are set safe on high (v10). The New Testament says that name has been given to Jesus, the "name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9), and "there is salvation in no one else" (Acts 4:12). In a chapter full of careless, wounding, hasty words, Jesus is the one whose words are "spirit and life" (John 6:63), who did not answer before he heard but "knew what was in man" (John 2:25), and who kept silent before his accusers rather than defend himself (Isa 53:7). And when many companions fail, as the disciples did when they "all left him and fled" (Mark 14:50), he remains the friend who sticks closer than a brother: "I have called you friends" (John 15:15).
