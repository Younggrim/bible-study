*Setting:* Forty years have passed since Moses fled Egypt (Acts 7:30). He is about eighty, a shepherd with a wife and son, living among the Midianites. In Egypt the king who wanted him dead has died, and Israel's groaning has risen to God, who "heard… remembered… saw… knew" (2:23–25). Now God acts, and he begins by speaking to one man in the desert. The call of Moses runs from 3:1 to 4:17. In it Moses raises five objections, and each time God answers with something about himself. The ESV gives the whole of chapter 3 a single heading.

#### The Burning Bush (vv1–22)
- **"Moses was keeping the flock of his father-in-law" (v1).** The verb form suggests this was his ongoing job. The prince of Egypt now tends someone else's sheep, work Egyptians despised (Gen 46:34). God often trains his leaders as shepherds first: Jacob, Moses, David (Ps 78:70–72). Moses will spend the next forty years shepherding a far more difficult flock.
- **"To the west side of the wilderness… Horeb, the mountain of God" (v1).** "West side" is literally "behind" or "beyond" the wilderness, the far side of the grazing land. Horeb probably means "dry" or "desolate" and is another name for Mount Sinai (compare 19:11 with Deut 5:2). Calling it "the mountain of God" looks ahead to what will happen there. The traditional site is Jebel Musa in the southern Sinai Peninsula, though scholars propose several others, including sites in northwest Arabia, the region of Midian.
- **"The angel of the LORD appeared to him in a flame of fire" (v2).** By v4 the speaker is "the LORD" and "God," and in v6 he says, "I am the God of your father." The angel of the LORD is often identified with God himself (Gen 16:7–13; Judg 13:21–22). Many Christian interpreters since the early church have seen him as the Son appearing before his incarnation. Others read him as a messenger so fully representing God that he speaks in God's own name. Stephen simply says "an angel appeared to him… in a flame of fire in a bush" and then "the voice of the Lord came" (Acts 7:30–31).
- **"The bush was burning, yet it was not consumed" (v2).** The word for bush, *seneh*, is a thorn bush or bramble, the most ordinary plant in the desert. *Seneh* also sounds like *Sinai*, and many think the mountain's name echoes this bush. Fire in the Bible often signals God's holy presence: the smoking firepot at Abraham's covenant (Gen 15:17), the pillar of fire (13:21), and Sinai wrapped in fire (19:18). God's presence burns, but it doesn't destroy what he chooses to dwell in. Deuteronomy 33:16 later calls God "him who dwells in the bush."
- **"I will turn aside to see this great sight" (v3).** Moses is curious, and God waits for that: "When the LORD saw that he turned aside" (v4). Only then does he call.
- **"Moses, Moses!" "Here I am" (v4).** A doubled name marks moments of personal calling: "Abraham, Abraham!" (Gen 22:11), "Jacob, Jacob" (Gen 46:2), "Samuel! Samuel!" (1 Sam 3:10). "Here I am" (*hinneni*) is the answer of someone ready to listen, the same answer Abraham and Isaiah gave (Isa 6:8).
- **"Take your sandals off your feet, for… holy ground" (v5).** This is the first time in the Bible that a place is called holy. "Holy" (*qodesh*) means set apart, belonging to God, wholly other. The dirt is not special. God's presence makes it so. In the ancient Near East, as in many Middle Eastern cultures today, removing one's sandals showed respect, and priests later served barefoot in the tabernacle. Joshua hears the same command at Jericho (Josh 5:15).
- **"The God of your father, the God of Abraham… Isaac… Jacob" (v6).** God introduces himself by his promises. This is not a new god of the desert but the God who made a covenant with Moses' ancestors. Jesus quotes this verse to answer the Sadducees, who denied the resurrection: God says "I *am* the God of Abraham," not "I was," so "he is not God of the dead, but of the living" (Matt 22:31–32). **"Moses hid his face, for he was afraid to look at God."** This is the right instinct. Later Moses will ask to see God's glory (33:18–23).
- **"I have surely seen… heard… I know… I have come down to deliver" (vv7–8).** These are the four verbs of 2:24–25 again, plus one more. God "comes down" in the Bible to see and act (Gen 11:5; 18:21). "Deliver" is *natsal*, to snatch out, as a shepherd snatches a lamb from a lion's mouth (1 Sam 17:35). God calls them **"my people"** (v7), the first time he does so in Exodus. They were Pharaoh's slaves. They are his.
- **"A good and broad land, a land flowing with milk and honey" (v8).** Canaan is pictured as rich pastureland (milk from goats and sheep) and blossoming fields. "Honey" may mean bee honey or the thick syrup made from dates and figs. To slaves making bricks in the Delta it was a picture of plenty. **The six peoples** (Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, Jebusites) show that the land was crowded and contested. God's promise does not ignore the difficulty.
- **"Come, I will send you to Pharaoh" (v10).** God could deliver Israel alone, but he chooses to send a person. "I have come down" (v8) and "I will send you" (v10) belong together.
- **"Who am I?" (v11).** Objection one. Forty years earlier Moses acted on his own without being asked. Now, asked by God, he refuses. God does not answer "Who am I?" by building up Moses' confidence. He answers with himself: **"But I will be with you" (v12).** The Hebrew is *ehyeh immak*, "I will be with you." The same verb, *ehyeh*, becomes God's name two verses later.
- **"This shall be the sign… you shall serve God on this mountain" (v12).** It is a strange sign because it comes *after* obeying. Moses must step out first, and the sign will confirm afterward that God sent him. "Serve" is *avad*, the same word used for Israel's slavery in 1:13–14. Israel will move from serving Pharaoh to worshipping God at this very mountain (19:1–2).
- **"What is his name?" (v13).** Objection two. In the ancient world, a god's name was thought to reveal his character and power, and Israel would want to know which god sent Moses and what he was like. Some think the people had forgotten God's name in Egypt. Others think Moses is asking what new thing the name would reveal.
- **"I AM WHO I AM" (v14).** In Hebrew, *ehyeh asher ehyeh*. The ESV footnote gives "I will be what I will be," and both are possible, since Hebrew verbs of this kind can point to present or future. It is a name and also a refusal to be pinned down. God exists by himself, depends on no one, does not change, and will prove who he is by what he does. The Greek translation renders it "I am the one who is" (*egō eimi ho ōn*), and Revelation echoes it: the one "who is and who was and who is to come" (Rev 1:4).
- **"The LORD… This is my name forever" (v15).** "LORD" in small capitals stands for the four Hebrew letters *YHWH*, almost certainly from the same verb "to be." Out of reverence, later Jewish readers stopped saying it aloud and read *Adonai*, "Lord," instead. The pronunciation was lost, and "Yahweh" is scholars' best reconstruction. "Jehovah" is a late blend of the consonants of *YHWH* with the vowels of *Adonai*. The name was already used in Genesis (Gen 4:26; 12:8). What is new here is its meaning, unfolded in the exodus (see 6:2–3).
- **"Gather the elders of Israel" (v16).** These were the heads of families and clans, Israel's natural leaders even in slavery. God's message to them, "I have observed you," uses *paqad*, the verb Joseph used on his deathbed: "God will surely *visit* you" (Gen 50:24–25). Joseph's dying hope is now coming true.
- **"Let us go a three days' journey… that we may sacrifice" (v18).** Some see this as a trick, since God's plan was a full departure. But the request is real and modest, and it exposes Pharaoh: he will refuse even a few days for worship. The exodus was always about worship as well as freedom (see 4:23; 8:1). Egyptian texts also record workers being given days off for religious festivals, so the request itself was not unheard of.
- **"The king of Egypt will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand" (v19).** The Hebrew reads literally "not even by a mighty hand." The ESV follows the Greek and Latin versions in reading "unless by a mighty hand," which fits v20. Either way, God knows from the start that Pharaoh will resist, so the refusals to come aren't surprises. **"I will stretch out my hand and strike Egypt with all the wonders" (v20)** previews the plagues.
- **"You shall not go empty… So you shall plunder the Egyptians" (vv21–22).** God told Abraham that his descendants would "come out with great possessions" (Gen 15:14), and the law later forbids sending a freed slave away "empty-handed" (Deut 15:13). The KJV's "borrow" is misleading. The verb *sha'al* simply means "ask," and the Egyptians will give willingly (12:35–36). It is back pay for generations of unpaid labor. "Plunder" is *natsal*, the same verb as "deliver" in v8, in a different form: God *delivers* his people, and his people *strip* Egypt. Much of this gold and silver will later go into the tabernacle (35:22), though some of it will go into the golden calf first (32:2–4).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls shepherding "a poor employment for a man of his parts and education," yet "Moses saw more of God in a desert than ever he had seen in Pharaoh's court." He reads the bush as "an emblem of the church now in bondage in Egypt, burning in the brick-kilns, yet not consumed." Guzik shares that reading and adds a second, Christ bearing the fire of judgment without being destroyed. Guzik also identifies the angel of the LORD as the pre-incarnate Christ. JFB, more cautiously, says that under this symbol "the Divine Being was present," and identifies the bush as "the wild acacia or thorn." On the sandals, JFB calls their removal "a confession of personal defilement and conscious unworthiness," while Henry compares it to taking off one's hat. On v11 Henry observes, "The more fit any person is for service commonly the less opinion he has of himself," and on v14 that God "has his being of himself, and has no dependence upon any other." Guzik and Henry both defend the plundering as just wages, not theft. Henry says God "has many ways of balancing accounts between the injured and the injurious." The Tyndale notes add that God's answers to Moses' objections are really statements about God.

#### Pointing to Christ
The God of the burning bush is the God who comes down to deliver and goes with the one he sends. "I will be with you" (v12) and "I AM" (v14) come together in Jesus. Matthew's Gospel opens with "Immanuel… God with us" (Matt 1:23) and closes with "I am with you always" (Matt 28:20). Jesus also takes the divine "I am" on his own lips: "Before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58), and his hearers understood the claim well enough to pick up stones. The fire that burned in the bush without consuming it is the holy God dwelling among people, which is what he does in Jesus, who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14).
