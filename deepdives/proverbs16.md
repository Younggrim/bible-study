*Setting:* The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 16 sits at the center of the collection, and many scholars think it was arranged with care. It opens with a cluster of sayings about the LORD (vv1–9), where his name appears in almost every verse, then turns to the king (vv10–15), then to wisdom, speech and troublemakers. It begins and ends on the same thought: people make plans and cast lots, and the LORD decides the outcome (vv1, 9, 33). From here on there are fewer simple "but" contrasts and more sayings that build a thought or compare two things.

#### Man Plans, the LORD Directs (vv1–9)
- **"The plans of the heart belong to man, but the answer of the tongue is from the LORD" (v1).** "Plans" is a word for setting things in order, like arranging troops for battle. People prepare and rehearse what they will say, but the final word that comes out, and what happens because of it, is in God's hands. JFB: "the efficient ordering is from God." Balaam planned to curse Israel and could only speak blessing (Num 23:12). Jesus promised his disciples words in their hour of need (Luke 12:11–12). The Tyndale notes connect James 4:13–16, "If the Lord wills."
- **"All the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the spirit" (v2).** People are very good at seeing their own motives as clean. "Weighs" (*token*) is the word for measuring something on a balance. Egyptian art showed the dead person's heart weighed against a feather on the scales of judgment. Proverbs says the LORD alone does the weighing, and he weighs not just actions but the "spirit," the motive behind them. The saying is nearly repeated in 21:2. The Tyndale notes point to 1 Samuel 16:7: "the LORD looks on the heart." Paul: "I am not aware of anything against myself, but I am not thereby acquitted. It is the Lord who judges me" (1 Cor 4:4).
- **"Commit your work to the LORD, and your plans will be established" (v3).** "Commit" is literally "roll" (*gol*), as in rolling a heavy stone off one's shoulders onto someone else. The same picture appears in Psalm 37:5: "Commit your way to the LORD" and Psalm 55:22: "Cast your burden on the LORD." Guzik uses exactly that image: hand the work over like setting down a heavy load. This is not a promise that every plan will succeed as designed, but that work entrusted to God will be made firm in his way (Ps 127:1).
- **"The LORD has made everything for its purpose, even the wicked for the day of trouble" (v4).** "For its purpose" (*lammaʿanehu*) can mean "for its answer," fitting each thing to its proper end, or, as the KJV, "for himself." Nothing escapes God's purpose, not even the wicked. This does not say God makes people wicked. It says even their wickedness ends where justice puts it. JFB: sin and suffering are "indissolubly united." Guzik insists this does not make God the author of evil. The Tyndale notes connect Romans 9 and 2 Peter 2:9. Pharaoh is the classic example: his hardness served God's purpose to show his power (Exod 9:16; Rom 9:17).
- **"Everyone who is arrogant in heart is an abomination to the LORD; be assured, he will not go unpunished" (v5).** "Be assured" is "hand to hand" again (11:21), a pledge. The Tyndale notes: the proud think they can succeed without God (v1).
- **"By steadfast love and faithfulness iniquity is atoned for" (v6).** "Atoned for" (*kipper*) is the word for atonement in the sacrifices (Lev 16). "Steadfast love and faithfulness" (*chesed weʾemet*) are the great pair from Exodus 34:6, God's own character. So whose love is meant? JFB suggests it may refer to God, who makes atonement. Others read it as human loyalty and faithfulness, the evidence of a repentant life that God accepts (compare Dan 4:27). Guzik notes that either way it does not mean people can earn atonement. The Old Testament never thought sacrifices worked without a changed heart (Ps 51:16–17), and the New Testament shows where God's steadfast love and faithfulness finally made atonement: at the cross (Rom 3:25). The second line gives the practical result: the fear of the LORD turns people away from evil.
- **"When a man's ways please the LORD, he makes even his enemies to be at peace with him" (v7).** A general pattern: God can turn enemies into friends, as with Jacob and Esau (Gen 33:4) or Abimelech and Isaac (Gen 26:26–31). JFB rightly notes the exception: persecution. Jesus was perfectly pleasing to God and was crucified (John 15:18–20).
- **"Better is a little with righteousness than great revenues with injustice" (v8).** Close to 15:16. The Tyndale notes cross-reference it.
- **"The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps" (v9).** The section ends where it began (v1). Planning is good. Proverbs urges it (15:22; 21:5). But the LORD directs each step. JFB: "directeth" means "establisheth." Guzik: plan carefully, and hold plans humbly. Joseph's brothers planned evil, and God used it for good (Gen 50:20). Jeremiah: "it is not in man who walks to direct his steps" (Jer 10:23).
- **Commentators:** JFB and Guzik both refuse to read v4 as making God the cause of sin. Guzik and JFB part slightly on v6: JFB inclines to God's mercy making atonement, while Guzik focuses on guarding against the idea of earning it. The Tyndale notes tie v6 to God's covenant character in Exodus 34.

#### The King (vv10–15)
- **"An oracle is on the lips of a king; his mouth does not sin in judgment" (v10).** "Oracle" (*qesem*) is surprising. Everywhere else it means pagan divination, which the law forbade (Deut 18:10; 1 Sam 15:23). Here it is used positively: a king's verdict should carry the weight of a divine decision. The Tyndale notes point to Solomon's judgment, when Israel "perceived that the wisdom of God was in him to do justice" (1 Kgs 3:28). The second line is less a description than an ideal. Many kings did sin in judgment. JFB: it shows the importance of "equity in decisions."
- **"A just balance and scales are the LORD's; all the weights in the bag are his work" (v11).** Merchants kept stone weights in a pouch at the belt (Deut 25:13; Mic 6:11). Honest weights belong to God, he set the standard. The Tyndale notes count this message four times in the collection (11:1; 20:10, 23). It sits among the king sayings because the king was responsible for honest measures in the land (2 Sam 14:26 mentions "the king's weight").
- **"It is an abomination to kings to do evil, for the throne is established by righteousness" (vv12–13).** These assume a good king. The Tyndale notes observe that many of Israel's kings did the opposite, like Ahab (1 Kgs 21), and point forward to the righteous King of Isaiah 9:6–7. A good king loves honest advisers, not flatterers.
- **"A king's wrath is a messenger of death, and a wise man will appease it" (v14).** An ancient king could order death with a word (Esth 7:8–10; Dan 2:12). The wise know how to calm a ruler's anger, as Abigail calmed David (1 Sam 25:23–35).
- **"In the light of a king's face there is life, and his favor is like the clouds that bring the spring rain" (v15).** A shining face means favor (Num 6:25). The "spring rain" (*malqosh*) is the latter rain of March and April, which swelled the grain just before harvest. JFB notes it was especially valuable. Without it the crop failed.
- **Commentators:** The Tyndale notes treat vv10–15 as a unit, with the king standing for God's power on earth, and stress that these sayings describe what a king should be. JFB reads the "light of countenance" as favor and the latter rain as the most precious rain of the year.

#### Wisdom, Humility and Gracious Words (vv16–24)
- **"How much better to get wisdom than gold!" (v16).** "Get" is *qanah*, the verb of 4:5–7 and 8:22. The Tyndale notes: wisdom can lead to wealth, but money cannot buy wisdom.
- **"The highway of the upright turns aside from evil" (v17).** The upright have a well-built road, and its direction is away from evil. JFB: "the habitual course of the righteous." "Guards his way" is the watchman's verb again.
- **"Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall" (v18).** One of the most quoted lines in the Bible, often shortened to "pride goes before a fall." "Destruction" is literally "breaking," and "fall" (*kishalon*) is stumbling. JFB: "sliding." Pride walks with its head so high it doesn't see the edge. Guzik: pride opposes God, and so opposes the wisdom that starts with fearing him. Examples crowd in: Pharaoh, Haman, Nebuchadnezzar (Dan 4:30–33), Uzziah (2 Chr 26:16). Compare 11:2; 18:12; 29:23.
- **"It is better to be of a lowly spirit with the poor than to divide the spoil with the proud" (v19).** "Divide the spoil" is what victorious armies did after a battle. JFB: it means "conquer." Better to lose with the humble than win with the proud. Mary sang of God scattering the proud and lifting the humble (Luke 1:51–53).
- **"Whoever gives thought to the word will discover good, and blessed is he who trusts in the LORD" (v20).** "The word" may mean any good instruction, but JFB reads it as God's word, and the parallel line, trust in the LORD, supports that. Careful thought and simple trust belong together.
- **"Sweetness of speech increases persuasiveness" (vv21, 23).** "Persuasiveness" (*leqach*) is "teaching" or "learning," the word for the father's instruction in 1:5 and 4:2. Pleasant words make teaching go further. JFB: eloquent speech that persuades and teaches. Truth spoken harshly is often not heard.
- **"Good sense is a fountain of life to him who has it, but the instruction of fools is folly" (v22).** The wise have a spring inside. Fools, when they try to teach, can only pass on more folly.
- **"Gracious words are like a honeycomb, sweetness to the soul and health to the body" (v24).** Honey was the sweetest thing an Israelite knew, and was also used as medicine. Kind words do both: they taste good and they heal. "Health" is literally "healing for the bones." JFB: kind words soothe the mind and help bodily health. Jonathan's eyes "brightened" after tasting honey (1 Sam 14:27). God's words are "sweeter also than honey" (Ps 19:10).
- **Commentators:** Guzik on v18 links pride directly to rejecting the fear of the LORD. JFB on v18 reads the "fall" as sliding, the slow slip that pride doesn't notice. The Tyndale notes connect v18 back to v5: the proud rely on their own strength and harm themselves.

#### Troublemakers, Gray Hair and the Lot (vv25–33)
- **"There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death" (v25).** Word for word the same as 14:12. Repetition in Proverbs is a way of underlining. Guzik: self-deception is dangerous, so trust God's word over feelings. Placed here after v2 ("pure in his own eyes"), it is a reminder that sincerity is not the same as being right.
- **"A worker's appetite works for him; his mouth urges him on" (v26).** Hunger is a good motivator. "Appetite" is *nephesh*. Paul uses the same logic: "If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" (2 Thess 3:10). JFB: diligence is owed to oneself.
- **"A worthless man plots evil, and his speech is like a scorching fire" (v27).** "Worthless man" is "a man of *beliyyaʿal*," a word for good-for-nothing troublemakers (1 Sam 2:12; 1 Kgs 21:10), later used as a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15, "Belial"). "Plots" is literally "digs," as one digs a pit for someone to fall in. JFB: he "diggeth up evil." His words burn like fire. James: "the tongue is a fire" (Jas 3:6).
- **"A dishonest man spreads strife, and a whisperer separates close friends" (v28).** "Whisperer" (*nirgan*) is a gossip, a murmuring talebearer (JFB). "Close friends" may be literally "a friend" or "a chief friend." Gossip can split even the closest friendship. Compare 17:9 and 26:20: "where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases."
- **"A man of violence entices his neighbor and leads him in a way that is not good" (v29).** Like the gang of 1:10–19. Violence recruits.
- **"Whoever winks his eyes plans dishonest things; he who purses his lips brings evil to pass" (v30).** Body language gives away the schemer (6:13; 10:10). JFB reads it as shutting the eyes in deep thought and pressing the lips in firm purpose. The point is the same: watch for the signs of a plotter.
- **"Gray hair is a crown of glory; it is gained in a righteous life" (v31).** In Israel old age was honored (Lev 19:32), and long life was a blessing of the righteous path (3:2, 16). Gray hair is a crown "found in the way of righteousness." Guzik: age alone does not make someone admirable. It must be a righteous life. The Tyndale notes connect the other crowns of Proverbs (4:9; 12:4; 17:6).
- **"Whoever is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city" (v32).** Self-control is a greater victory than military conquest. The great conquerors of history often could not rule their own tempers. Alexander the Great killed his friend Cleitus in a drunken rage. Guzik: this is a battle fought in the heart. The Tyndale notes: patience is worth more than force. Paul lists self-control as fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:23).
- **"The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (v33).** Lots were stones or marked pieces shaken in a fold of clothing (the "lap") and thrown out. Israel used them to divide the land (Josh 14:2), choose a king (1 Sam 10:20–21), find a guilty man (Josh 7:14–18; Jonah 1:7) and assign temple duties (1 Chr 24:5). The high priest's Urim and Thummim may have worked similarly (Exod 28:30). The Tyndale notes: these were not for gambling but a way of seeking God's decision. The apostles cast lots to replace Judas (Acts 1:26), the last time this is recorded in Scripture, before the Spirit was poured out. Henry: "Divine Providence orders and directs those things which to us are perfectly casual and fortuitous," and "nothing comes to pass by chance, nor is an event determined by a blind fortune." JFB: "Seemingly the most fortuitous events are ordered by God." Guzik adds a caution: this is not permission to treat every chance event as a message from God.
- **Commentators:** Henry, JFB and Guzik all read v33 as a statement of God's providence over the smallest events, and Guzik guards against turning it into a method of guidance. On v31 Guzik and JFB both stress that gray hair is honorable when it goes with righteousness.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 16 says the LORD weighs the spirit (v2), makes atonement through steadfast love and faithfulness (v6), and directs every step and every lot (vv9, 33). All three meet at the cross. Human beings planned evil against Jesus, "yet it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (Isa 53:10). Peter put it plainly: he was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God" (Acts 2:23). There God's steadfast love and faithfulness atoned for iniquity once for all (Rom 3:25–26; 1 John 4:10). Even the soldiers' dice at the foot of the cross fell where Scripture said they would (Ps 22:18; John 19:24). And Jesus is the King whose lips never sin in judgment (v10; Isa 11:3–4), who, unlike proud kings, humbled himself and was raised (Phil 2:8–9).
