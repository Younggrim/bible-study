*Setting:* Paul writes to Timothy, his younger co-worker, probably around AD 62–64. The most common reconstruction is that Paul was released from the house arrest in Rome that closes Acts (Acts 28:30), travelled again in the eastern Mediterranean, and left Timothy behind in Ephesus while he went on to Macedonia (v3). The early church received the letter as Paul's. Many modern scholars think the three "Pastoral Letters" (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus) were written later in Paul's name, because their vocabulary and church concerns differ from his earlier letters. Others answer that a different situation, an older apostle and a trusted secretary explain the differences. Years earlier Paul had warned the Ephesian elders that "fierce wolves" would come in and that men from among their own leaders would twist the truth (Acts 20:29–30). That is now happening, and this letter is Timothy's written authority to deal with it.

#### Greeting (vv1–2)
- **"An apostle of Christ Jesus by command of God" (v1).** Timothy hardly needed reminding who Paul was. This is a semi-official letter that would be read aloud in the church, and Paul's opening backs his delegate with apostolic authority. Paul usually says "by the will of God." "By command" is stronger: he was put under orders.
- **"God our Savior" (v1).** This title for the Father is characteristic of the Pastoral Letters (2:3; 4:10; Titus 1:3). In the Greek world "savior" was claimed by gods and by the emperor. Paul gives it to the God of Israel, and in the same letters to Jesus as well (v15; Titus 2:13).
- **"Christ Jesus our hope" (v1).** Christian hope is not wishful thinking. It is a person. Later in the letter Paul contrasts this with setting hope on riches (6:17).
- **"My true child in the faith" (v2).** "True" means genuine, legitimate. Timothy came from Lystra, with a Jewish mother and a Greek father (Acts 16:1), and he had travelled with Paul for over a decade. Acts doesn't say Paul converted him, and he may already have been a disciple when Paul met him, but Paul was certainly his father in the faith in every sense that mattered (Phil 2:20–22).
- **"Grace, mercy, and peace" (v2).** Paul's letters to churches say "grace and peace." Only the letters to Timothy add "mercy" (some manuscripts of Titus 1:4 have it as well). A man facing a hard assignment needs mercy as well as grace.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes the addition: "'Mercy' is grace of a more tender kind," and suggests that ministers in particular need fresh mercy. Guzik points out that the three personal letters share this fuller greeting. JFB reads "by the commandment" as meaning Paul was not acting by his own choice; "a necessity was laid on him."

#### Warning Against False Teachers (vv3–11)
- **"Remain at Ephesus" (v3).** The note is urgent: "as I urged you." Timothy seems to have wanted to move on. Ephesus was a large, wealthy, idol-filled city, and the trouble was inside the church, not outside.
- **"Charge certain persons" (v3).** "Charge" is *parangellō*, to give an order, and its noun returns in vv5 and 18. In classical Greek writers the noun can mean a military order. Paul does not name the teachers yet. "Certain persons" is deliberately cool.
- **"Any different doctrine" (v3).** One Greek word, *heterodidaskaleō*, "to teach otherwise." Paul's concern is not that people think, ask questions or disagree on small matters. It is teaching that departs from the apostolic gospel (v11).
- **"Myths and endless genealogies" (v4).** What exactly these were is debated. Most scholars think they were Jewish in flavour: imaginative stories built out of Old Testament family lists, or claims of special status from one's ancestry. Some see an early form of the later Gnostic teaching about chains of spiritual beings. Titus 1:14 speaks of "Jewish myths," which leans toward the first view. Either way, they led to speculation and argument, not to trust in God.
- **"The stewardship from God that is by faith" (v4).** "Stewardship" is *oikonomia*, household management. It anticipates the letter's big picture: the church is "the household of God" (3:15), and God's way of running his household is by faith, not by clever theories.
- **"The aim of our charge is love" (v5).** This is the key to the whole letter. The purpose of sound teaching is not winning arguments but love, flowing from three sources: a pure heart (undivided motives), a good conscience (nothing hidden) and sincere faith. "Sincere" is literally "unhypocritical," faith that isn't playing a part.
- **"Swerving from these… wandered away into vain discussion" (v6).** "Swerving" means missing the mark, like an archer. Having lost the target of love, they drift into empty talk.
- **"Desiring to be teachers of the law" (v7).** Here is the irony. They want the prestige of teaching the law of Moses but don't understand what they say or "the things about which they make confident assertions."
- **"The law is good, if one uses it lawfully" (v8).** Paul is not against the law. He says the same in Romans 7:12. The question is what the law is *for*.
- **"Not laid down for the just but for the lawless" (v9).** The law's job is to restrain and expose sin. It was never meant as a ladder for already righteous people to climb, or as raw material for speculation.
- **The list (vv9–10).** It loosely follows the Ten Commandments in order. The first three pairs are sins against God (commandments 1–4). Then come: those who strike their fathers and mothers (the fifth commandment; the word means "father-beaters," even killers), murderers (sixth), the sexually immoral and men who practise homosexuality (seventh), enslavers (eighth, since kidnapping was the theft of a person, Ex 21:16), and liars and perjurers (ninth).
- **"Men who practice homosexuality" (v10).** The Greek is *arsenokoitēs*, built from "male" and "bed." It appears in the New Testament only here and in 1 Corinthians 6:9, and the two parts echo the Greek wording of Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13. Most interpreters across church history read it as referring to male same-sex intercourse. Some recent writers argue for a narrower meaning (exploitative or commercial sex), but the lexicons and the Leviticus background favour the broader reading.
- **"Enslavers" (v10).** *Andrapodistēs* means a slave-dealer or kidnapper, someone who captured free people and sold them. The Roman slave trade ran on such men. Paul puts them in the same list as murderers.
- **"Contrary to sound doctrine" (v10).** "Sound" is *hygiainō*, the root of our "hygiene": healthy teaching. It is a favourite word of the Pastoral Letters (6:3; 2 Tim 4:3; Titus 1:9). False teaching makes the church sick.
- **"The gospel of the glory of the blessed God" (v11).** The law exposes sin. The gospel shows God's glory. Paul says this gospel was "entrusted" to him, like a deposit put in his keeping, a word picture he returns to at the end (6:20).
- **Commentators:** JFB thinks the "genealogies" were not "merely such civil genealogies as were common among the Jews" but speculative lists of the kind later Gnostics built, while Henry speaks of "Judaizing teachers" who mixed fables with the gospel. Guzik lists several possibilities and is content to call them distractions. All three agree with the Tyndale notes that the law exposes sin rather than making anyone righteous. Henry sums up: love is "the end of the commandment," so whatever weakens love works against the law's purpose. JFB adds that the teachers' error "was not in maintaining the obligation of the law, but in abusing it," and reads "men-stealers" as slave dealers, which the lexicon supports. Guzik stresses that having moral standards is not legalism; trusting our conduct to make us right with God is.

#### Christ Jesus Came to Save Sinners (vv12–20)
- **"I thank him who has given me strength" (v12).** Talk of the gospel entrusted to him (v11) sets Paul thanking God. Notice the order: Christ strengthened him, judged him faithful and appointed him. Paul did not volunteer himself into ministry.
- **"A blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent" (v13).** Each word gets worse. He spoke against Christ, hunted Christians (Acts 8:3; 9:1–2) and, "insolent," treated them with violent contempt. In Acts 26:11 he admits he tried to force believers to blaspheme.
- **"I had acted ignorantly in unbelief" (v13).** This doesn't mean ignorance earned him mercy. It explains why his sin was not the defiant, knowing rejection that the Old Testament distinguishes from unintentional sin (Num 15:27–31). Jesus prayed the same over his executioners: "they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34).
- **"The grace of our Lord overflowed for me" (v14).** The verb is *hyperpleonazō*, "super-abounded," a word found only here in the New Testament. Paul likes "hyper" words for grace (Rom 5:20).
- **"The saying is trustworthy" (v15).** This formula appears five times, all in the Pastoral Letters (3:1; 4:9; 2 Tim 2:11; Titus 3:8). It seems to mark sayings already known and repeated in the churches, short summaries of the faith. This one sounds like Jesus' own words: "the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10).
- **"Of whom I am the foremost" (v15).** "Foremost" is *prōtos*, first or chief. The verb is present tense: not "I was," but "I am." Paul is not wallowing in his past. He is saying that, standing before God, he knows himself only as a forgiven sinner. The nearer he grew to Christ, the more clearly he saw it (compare 1 Cor 15:9; Eph 3:8).
- **"As an example" (v16).** The word means a pattern or prototype, a first sketch. If Christ could show "perfect patience" to the chief persecutor, no one who comes to him is beyond reach.
- **"To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God" (v17).** Paul's testimony turns into worship. "King of the ages" was a Jewish title for God, and every term contrasts with earthly kings and with the emperors who claimed divine honours. The KJV's "the only wise God" follows later manuscripts. The oldest read "the only God," and "wise" probably came in from Romans 16:27.
- **"In accordance with the prophecies previously made about you" (v18).** At Timothy's commissioning, prophetic words were spoken over him, probably when the elders laid hands on him (4:14; 2 Tim 1:6), much as at Antioch in Acts 13:1–3. Paul tells him to fight with those words in hand.
- **"Wage the good warfare" (v18).** The language is military: a soldier's campaign, not one skirmish. "Good" means noble, worthy.
- **"Holding faith and a good conscience" (v19).** The two belong together. "Rejecting" is a strong verb, to thrust away. When people push away a clear conscience, faith goes with it. The image of the shipwreck comes naturally from Paul, who had been shipwrecked at least four times (2 Cor 11:25; Acts 27).
- **"Hymenaeus and Alexander" (v20).** Hymenaeus is named again in 2 Timothy 2:17–18, teaching that the resurrection had already happened. An Alexander the coppersmith later does Paul "great harm" (2 Tim 4:14), probably the same man. Whether he is the Alexander of the Ephesian riot (Acts 19:33) is uncertain, as the name was common.
- **"Handed over to Satan" (v20).** The same phrase appears in 1 Corinthians 5:5. It means putting a person outside the church, back into the world where Satan holds sway (1 John 5:19). The goal is not destruction but correction: "that they may learn." Church discipline aims at restoration.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives the heart of v15: "the chief of sinners may become the chief of saints." JFB draws out the hope for everyone else: "where there was mercy for him, there is mercy for all." On v19 JFB offers a fine picture: "Faith is like a very precious liquor; a good conscience is the clean, pure glass that contains it." Henry agrees: those who "put away a good conscience will soon make shipwreck of faith." Guzik adds that a shipwreck is not necessarily beyond rescue. On v20 JFB thinks the sentence may have carried physical effects too, as with sickness in 1 Corinthians 11:30, while Guzik and the Tyndale notes read it as removal from the church's protection. All agree the aim was the offenders' repentance.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter's centre is one plain sentence: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners." The false teachers had built a religion of clever theories and borrowed law. Paul answers with his own story. The man who hunted Christians became the proof that Jesus' patience has no bottom. Sound doctrine, in the end, is not a system to master. It is the news of a Saviour who came for people who know they need saving, and love is what that news produces.
