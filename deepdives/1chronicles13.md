*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. David has just been made king by all Israel and has taken Jerusalem (chapters 11–12). His first act as king of the whole nation, in the Chronicler's telling, is to bring the ark of the covenant to his new capital. The ark was a gold-covered wooden chest, about four feet long, holding the stone tablets of the Ten Commandments. Its lid, the mercy seat, with two golden cherubim, was the place where God said he would meet his people (Exod 25:10–22). It was the center of Israel's worship and the sign of God's presence. Decades earlier the Philistines had captured it in battle, then sent it back when plagues broke out among them (1 Sam 4–6). Since then it had sat in a private house in the village of Kiriath-jearim, almost forgotten through Saul's reign. 2 Samuel tells this story after David's palace and his Philistine wars (2 Sam 5–6). The Chronicler moves it earlier, ahead of both (chapter 14), so that David's first concern as king is the worship of God. The attempt begins in joy and ends in death. The full story, and the lesson David learned, is finished in chapter 15.

#### The Ark Brought from Kiriath-Jearim (vv1–4)
- **"David consulted with the commanders of thousands and of hundreds, with every leader" (v1).** David does not act alone. He brings the leaders and then "all the assembly of Israel" (v2) into the decision. Guzik notes what is missing: the text never says David asked the LORD. Compare how carefully David inquires of God before his battles in the next chapter (14:10, 14).
- **"If it seems good to you and from the LORD our God" (v2).** David wants both the people's agreement and God's approval. JFB reads this as David taking the people's consent as a sign of God's will. That is reasonable as far as it goes, but this chapter shows that a popular decision is not the same as an obedient one.
- **"Let us send abroad to our brothers who remain in all the lands of Israel" (v2).** "Send abroad" is the Hebrew verb *parats*, literally "break out" or "spread out." It is a word to watch. It returns, painfully, in v11.
- **"The priests and Levites in the cities that have pasturelands" (v2).** The Levite towns listed in 6:54–81. David wants the tribe responsible for the ark to be there. The tragedy is that they were invited but not put in charge of carrying it.
- **"Let us bring again the ark of our God to us, for we did not seek it in the days of Saul" (v3).** "Seek" is *darash*, the word used for Saul's failure in 10:13–14. Saul did not seek the LORD, and under Saul no one sought the ark. David means to reverse that. JFB says the ark had lain in "culpable neglect."
- **"The thing was right in the eyes of all the people" (v4).** Unanimous agreement. The aim was good. Guzik's point runs through the whole chapter: the goal of putting God's presence at the center of national life was right; the method was the problem.

#### Uzzah and the Ark (vv5–14)
- **"From the Nile of Egypt to Lebo-hamath" (v5).** "Nile" here is *Shihor*, probably not the great river but a stream on Egypt's border, perhaps the Wadi el-Arish, as JFB suggests. Lebo-hamath was a pass far to the north in Syria. These are the ideal borders of the promised land, reached again under Solomon (2 Chr 7:8). All Israel, from end to end.
- **"To Baalah, that is, to Kiriath-jearim that belongs to Judah" (v6).** A hill town about eight miles west of Jerusalem. Its older name, Baalah, points to an old Canaanite shrine. After the Philistines returned the ark, it was taken to "the house of Abinadab on the hill," and his son Eleazar was set apart to look after it (1 Sam 7:1–2). It stayed there through Samuel's judgeship and Saul's whole reign. Guzik estimates about seventy years.
- **"The ark of God, which is called by the name of the LORD who sits enthroned above the cherubim" (v6).** The ark was God's footstool, and the space above the cherubim was pictured as his throne (28:2; Ps 99:1). This is the reason for everything that follows. The ark was not a sacred object to be used. It was where the LORD of the universe had put his name.
- **"They carried the ark of God on a new cart" (v7).** This is the mistake. God's law said the ark must be carried on poles, on the shoulders of Kohathite Levites, and covered first by the priests (Exod 25:12–15; Num 4:5–6, 15; 7:9). A new cart is exactly how the Philistines had sent it back (1 Sam 6:7–8). For pagans who did not know God's law, that was all they could do. For Israel, it was copying the world instead of reading the Word. A "new" cart was meant as honor. It was still the wrong way.
- **"Uzzah and Ahio were driving the cart" (v7).** They belonged to Abinadab's family, his sons or descendants. They had lived with the ark in the house for years. Familiarity may have been part of the danger.
- **"David and all Israel were celebrating before God with all their might, with song and lyres and harps and tambourines and cymbals and trumpets" (v8).** Wholehearted, joyful, musical worship, the kind the Chronicler loves. Nothing in this verse is wrong in itself. That is what makes the next verse so shocking. Guzik, drawing on Spurgeon, notes that the joy was real but the awe was missing: worship is measured by whether it pleases God, not by how it feels.
- **"When they came to the threshing floor of Chidon, Uzzah put out his hand to take hold of the ark, for the oxen stumbled" (v9).** 2 Samuel 6:6 calls the place "the threshing floor of Nacon." Uzzah's reaction was instinctive: the cart lurched and he reached out to steady the ark.
- **"And the anger of the LORD was kindled against Uzzah, and he struck him down… and he died there before God" (v10).** Many readers find this the hardest verse in the chapter. Uzzah seems to have meant well. But the law was explicit: the Kohathites "must not touch the holy things, lest they die" (Num 4:15). Even the men of Beth-shemesh had died for looking into the ark (1 Sam 6:19). The Tyndale notes put it simply: touching the ark violated its holiness. 2 Samuel 6:7 adds "because of his error." Several things went wrong at once. The ark was on a cart that should never have carried it. Uzzah, who should never have been driving it, touched what no one but a priest could touch. And he acted as if God's throne needed a human hand to keep it from falling. Guzik, following Alexander Maclaren, says Uzzah treated the ark like any other valuable. Henry links Uzzah with Nadab and Abihu, who died for offering "unauthorized fire" (Lev 10:1–3): "God will be sanctified in those that come nigh unto him."
- **"And David was angry because the LORD had broken out against Uzzah" (v11).** "Broken out" is *parats* again. David had said, "Let us break out (send abroad) to our brothers" (v2), and now God has broken out against them. David's anger may have been at God, or at the disaster, or at himself; the Hebrew does not say. Henry asks pointedly, "If God be angry with us, shall we dare to be angry with him?" Guzik suggests David's anger came from confusion, because he had not yet understood what went wrong.
- **"That place is called Perez-uzza to this day" (v11).** "The breaking out against Uzzah." The name stood as a warning. The word will return twice more: at Baal-perazim, where God "broke through" David's enemies (14:11), and when David admits, "the LORD our God broke out against us, because we did not seek him according to the rule" (15:13). The same God who breaks out against his enemies can break out against careless worshipers.
- **"And David was afraid of God that day, and he said, 'How can I bring the ark of God home to me?'" (v12).** Joy turned to fear. The Tyndale notes suggest David may have felt himself unfit to have the ark near him. It is the right question, asked in the wrong spirit at first. Guzik observes that David should have feared his own disobedience rather than fearing God as if God were unpredictable. In chapter 15 David finds the answer in the Law.
- **"Took it aside to the house of Obed-edom the Gittite" (v13).** "Gittite" usually means a man from Gath, the Philistine city, and some take Obed-edom to be a Philistine who had joined David, like Ittai the Gittite (2 Sam 15:19–22). But Chronicles later names an Obed-edom among the Levite musicians and gatekeepers (15:18, 24; 16:38; 26:4–8). Gath-rimmon was a Levite town (Josh 21:24–25; 1 Chr 6:69), so "Gittite" may mean a man from there. Guzik, following Adam Clarke, takes him as a Levite. If so, the ark finally rested with the right family.
- **"The ark of God remained with the household of Obed-edom in his house three months. And the LORD blessed the household of Obed-edom and all that he had" (v14).** The same ark that brought death at Perez-uzza brought blessing to Obed-edom's house. The difference was not in the ark but in how it was treated. Chronicles later says of Obed-edom's large family of gatekeepers, "for God blessed him" (26:5). 2 Samuel 6:12 tells us David heard about this blessing, and that news sent him back to try again, this time the right way (chapter 15). Henry says the ark "is a guest that nobody shall lose by."
- **Commentators:** Henry draws together joy and reverence. God's people are to rejoice in him, but "to rejoice with trembling" (Ps 2:11), because "the God with whom we have to do is a jealous God." Guzik's applications are practical: good intentions and sincere enthusiasm do not replace obedience to what God has said, and how we serve God matters as much as what we set out to do. He also points out that the names Uzzah ("strength") and Ahio ("brotherly") suit a big, impressive production that never stopped to ask God. JFB says little on vv7–14, simply referring readers to 2 Samuel 6. On the place-name in v6, he prefers the reading "the LORD… who is worshipped there" for the phrase about God's name.

#### Pointing to Christ
The ark was God's throne on earth, and no one could handle it casually and live. Uzzah's death shows how serious it is for sinners to come near a holy God, a truth that the joyful music of v8 had forgotten. The New Testament does not soften that holiness. It shows how it is met. The ark's lid, the mercy seat, was where the high priest sprinkled blood once a year to cover Israel's sin (Lev 16:14–15). Paul uses that very word for Jesus: God "put forward [him] as a propitiation," a mercy seat, "by his blood" (Rom 3:25). In Christ, God's presence came near in human flesh, and people touched him. A sick woman reached out her hand and touched his garment, and instead of death, power went out from him to heal her (Luke 8:44–48). Through his blood believers now "draw near with confidence to the throne of grace" (Heb 4:16), yet still, as Hebrews says, "with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire" (Heb 12:28–29).
