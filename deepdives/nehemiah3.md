*Setting:* Jerusalem, summer of 445 BC. Nehemiah has arrived as governor with the king's letters, inspected the broken walls by night, and called the people to "rise up and build" (2:17–18). Before telling how the enemies fought the work (ch 4), he pauses to record who built what. Chapter 3 is a work roster, about forty crews spaced around the whole circuit of the walls. It begins at the Sheep Gate near the temple, moves counterclockwise (west along the north wall, down the west side, around the southern tip and up the east side) and ends back at the Sheep Gate. It can look like a list of hard names, but it is a list of ordinary people doing God's work side by side, and God saw fit to keep their names.

#### Rebuilding the Wall (vv1–32)
- **What the list is for.** This is not a blueprint. It is a record of honor and accountability. Every crew had a section, every section had a name, and the city's safety depended on every gap being closed. The words "next to him" and "after him" repeat about thirty times. The verb "repaired" is from *chazaq*, "to make strong," the same root as "strengthened their hands" in 2:18. Some scholars think the list came from Nehemiah's work records or from a document like it, kept in the archives.
- **"Eliashib the high priest rose up with his brothers the priests" (v1).** The list starts with the spiritual leaders, at the gate nearest the temple. Eliashib was the grandson of Jeshua, the high priest of the first return (12:10). The Sheep Gate, in the northeast near the temple, was probably where sacrificial animals were brought in. John 5:2 places the pool of Bethesda "by the Sheep Gate" in Jesus' day. Sadly, the same Eliashib later gave Tobiah, Nehemiah's enemy, a room in the temple courts (13:4–7).
- **"They consecrated it and set its doors" (v1).** Only this gate is said to be consecrated, set apart to God, perhaps because it stood next to the temple. It is a way of saying the whole work belonged to the LORD. The full wall is dedicated in 12:27–43.
- **The Tower of the Hundred and the Tower of Hananel (v1).** These towers guarded the north wall, the side of Jerusalem without a valley to protect it. Jeremiah promised that the city would be rebuilt "from the Tower of Hananel to the Corner Gate" (Jer 31:38). Nehemiah's builders were working inside a promise.
- **"The men of Jericho" (v2).** Crews came from towns all over Judah: Jericho, Tekoa (v5), Gibeon and Mizpah (v7), Zanoah (v13), Beth-haccherem (v14), Beth-zur (v16) and Keilah (v17). Jerusalem's walls protected the whole province, and the whole province built them.
- **The Fish Gate (v3).** On the north side, where merchants, some of them from Tyre (13:16), brought fish from the coast and the Sea of Galilee. "They laid its beams and set its doors, its bolts, and its bars": the gates were the hardest and most important work, since the timber from the king's forest (2:8) went into them.
- **Meremoth son of Uriah (v4).** The priest who received Ezra's treasure (Ezra 8:33). He repaired a second section later (v21), one of several who did more than one share. **Meshullam son of Berechiah** (v4) is a sad note: his daughter later married Tobiah's son (6:18).
- **"But their nobles would not stoop to serve their Lord" (v5).** Literally "did not bring their necks to the work." The picture is an ox refusing the yoke. "Their Lord" can be read as God, or as "their lords," the leaders in charge of the work. Either way, the leaders of Tekoa thought themselves above it. Their own townsmen did a double share anyway (v27). Tekoa, about ten miles south of Jerusalem, was the prophet Amos's hometown (Amos 1:1). The Tyndale notes suggest that Geshem the Arab, who controlled the region to the south, may have pressured its nobles.
- **The Gate of Yeshanah (v6).** "Yeshanah" means "old," so many versions call it the Old Gate. It may also mean the gate on the road to the town of Jeshanah to the north (2 Chr 13:19).
- **"Gibeon and of Mizpah, the seat of the governor of the province Beyond the River" (v7).** The meaning is unclear. These towns may have been under the direct authority of the regional governor, or the crews worked up to the residence the governor used when he visited Jerusalem.
- **"Goldsmiths" and "one of the perfumers" (v8).** These were craftsmen, not builders. Men whose hands were used to fine metal and spices hauled stone. Goldsmiths and merchants appear again at the end (vv31–32).
- **"As far as the Broad Wall" (v8).** In 1970–71 archaeologists excavated a massive wall about 23 feet thick in Jerusalem's Jewish Quarter, built in Hezekiah's day around 700 BC (compare 2 Chr 32:5; Isa 22:10). Most scholars think Nehemiah's smaller city did not include that western hill, so the Broad Wall may be named here as a landmark at the point where his wall turned.
- **"Ruler of half the district of Jerusalem" (vv9, 12).** Judah was divided into districts, some split in halves, each with its own official. These were men of standing, and they took up tools.
- **"Opposite his house" (vv10, 23, 28–30).** Some people repaired the stretch right in front of their own homes. It was efficient, and it was motivating: no one builds carelessly the wall that will protect his own family.
- **Malchijah son of Harim (v11).** A Malchijah of the sons of Harim was among those who had married foreign wives in Ezra's day (Ezra 10:31). If it is the same man, he is now building beside his people. The name is common, so this is possible but not certain.
- **The Tower of the Ovens (v11).** Probably near the bakers' quarter (Jer 37:21).
- **"Shallum… he and his daughters" (v12).** The only women named in the roster. Shallum may have had no sons, and his daughters took their place. The text makes nothing of it, which makes it all the more striking.
- **"A thousand cubits of the wall" (v13).** About 1,500 feet. Hanun and the men of Zanoah, a town about thirteen miles southwest, rebuilt the Valley Gate and a long stretch of wall, perhaps because that part was less damaged.
- **The Dung Gate (v14).** At the city's southern tip, leading to the Hinnom Valley, where rubbish was burned. One man, Malchijah son of Rechab, took responsibility for the least attractive gate in the city.
- **The Fountain Gate and the Pool of Shelah (v15).** Near the spring-fed pools at the south end. Shelah is probably Siloam, "Sent," fed by Hezekiah's tunnel (2 Kgs 20:20; John 9:7). "The king's garden" was in the valley there, and "the stairs that go down from the city of David" may be the stepped street that archaeologists have uncovered on the slope.
- **"Nehemiah the son of Azbuk" (v16).** A different Nehemiah, ruler of half the district of Beth-zur, near Hebron. He built "opposite the tombs of David." David was buried in the city of David (1 Kgs 2:10), and in Peter's day his tomb was still known (Acts 2:29). "The house of the mighty men" may have been a barracks for the royal guard, or a memorial to David's champions (2 Sam 23:8).
- **The Levites, the priests and the temple servants (vv17, 22, 26).** Every class of temple worker took part. The temple servants "living on Ophel," the hill just south of the temple, built near their own quarter.
- **"The house of Eliashib the high priest" (vv20–21).** Baruch son of Zabbai repaired "another section" (v20). The standard Hebrew text adds a word usually read "zealously," given to no one else in the chapter. Many scholars think it is a copying slip, because it looks very like the word "after him," and the ESV leaves it out.
- **The east side (vv16–32).** This part of the wall was new. As 2:14 hinted, the old eastern wall lay under a mass of collapsed terraces, so Nehemiah's wall seems to have run along the crest of the ridge instead. That is why the descriptions shift from gates to houses, buttresses, a "corner" and "the tower projecting from the upper house of the king" (v25), near the old royal palace and "the court of the guard," where Jeremiah was once held (Jer 32:2).
- **The Water Gate (v26).** On the east, toward the Gihon Spring. In front of this gate Ezra would later read the Law to the whole people from morning until midday (8:1–3).
- **The Horse Gate and the East Gate (vv28–29).** The Horse Gate stood near the palace, at "the horses' entrance" where the wicked queen Athaliah was executed (2 Kgs 11:16; Jer 31:40). Shemaiah, "the keeper of the East Gate," left his own gate to help elsewhere.
- **"Meshullam the son of Berechiah repaired opposite his chamber" (v30).** "Chamber" is a single room. A man with only one room still did what he could, and his small share was recorded with everyone else's.
- **The Muster Gate and back to the Sheep Gate (vv31–32).** "Muster Gate" (*Miphkad*) may mean the gate of inspection or appointment, perhaps where troops gathered or the temple's guard was posted. The list ends where it began. The circle is closed, and "the goldsmiths and the merchants" fill the last gap. People with the most to lose from a city open to raiders helped close it.
- **Commentators:** Henry, noting the man who "repaired over against his chamber," draws the lesson that "If every one will sweep before his own door, the street will be clean; if every one will mend one, we shall all be mended," and writes, "Let not nobles think any thing below them." JFB explains that each family worked on the side of the city nearest its home village, and that Shallum's daughters may have been heiresses who "undertook to defray the expenses." He reads "Meah" (v1) as a measurement of a hundred cubits rather than the name of a tower. The ESV takes it as "the Tower of the Hundred," which most scholars now prefer. Guzik draws from the chapter a list of lessons about leadership. Nehemiah gave each person responsibility, began with the spiritual work at the temple, joined high and low, and did not rule out people with past failures. Guzik also points out that Jesus himself was a builder (Mark 6:3).

#### Pointing to Christ
A broken city is rebuilt one section at a time by priests and perfumers, rulers and daughters, villagers and goldsmiths, each beside the next, until the circle is closed. The New Testament uses the same picture for the church. Christ is the cornerstone, and believers are "being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" (Eph 2:20–22). Each is a "living stone" (1 Pet 2:5). Each has a gift, and none is too great or too small to take part (1 Cor 12:12–27). Paul warns that "each one's work will become manifest" (1 Cor 3:10–15). Nehemiah kept the builders' names. The Lord who builds his church keeps a better record, and he will not forget the one who repaired opposite a single room.
