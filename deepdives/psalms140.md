*Setting:* Psalm 140 is the first of five laments (Psalms 140–144) in the last collection of David's psalms. After the wonder and trust of Psalm 139, the mood shifts to danger. The heading reads "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David," so it was sung in public worship. No occasion is named. Matthew Henry, like many older interpreters, places it in the years when King Saul was hunting David (1 Sam 18–26). Others think of Doeg the Edomite, who informed on David and murdered the priests of Nob (1 Sam 21–22), or of Absalom's rebellion. JFB says plainly that which enemy is meant "is not important," and that is fair: the psalm describes a type of enemy rather than naming one. The striking thing is how much it is about speech. The enemies plot in their hearts, sharpen their tongues like snakes, and slander. Their weapons are words and traps. The psalm moves from a cry for rescue (vv1–5), to trust in God (vv6–8), to a prayer for judgment (vv9–11), and ends in quiet confidence (vv12–13). It also brings back the musical word *Selah*, which appears three times here (vv3, 5, 8). It has not appeared since Psalm 89. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### Deliver Me from Evil Men (vv1–3)
- **"Deliver me, O LORD, from evil men; preserve me from violent men" (v1).** The psalm opens with no praise and no introduction, just a cry for help. "Deliver" (*challetseni*) means rescue, pull out. "Preserve" (*tintsereni*) means guard, keep watch over. "Violent men" is literally "a man of violences" (*'ish chamasim*). *Chamas* is the violence that filled the earth before the flood (Gen 6:11). It includes injustice and cruelty, not only physical attack.
- **"Who plan evil things in their heart and stir up wars continually" (v2).** Their violence starts inside, in the heart, the place of planning. Jesus said the same: "out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder" (Matt 15:19). "Stir up wars" could also be read "gather together for war," as Trapp notes through Guzik. They are not men who stumble into conflict. They keep it going.
- **"They make their tongue sharp as a serpent's" (v3).** Snakes do not actually strike with their tongues. The flicking forked tongue simply looked dangerous, and the picture fits words that wound. Spurgeon, as Guzik reports, notes the point. The enemies sharpen their words the way a warrior sharpens a sword (Ps 52:2; 57:4; 64:3).
- **"Under their lips is the venom of asps" (v3).** "Asp" (*'akhshuv*) occurs only here in the Bible, so the exact kind of snake is unknown. The point is poison. Paul quotes this line, from the Greek translation, in Romans 3:13, in a chain of Old Testament verses showing that "all, both Jews and Greeks, are under sin" (Rom 3:9). What David says about his enemies, Paul says is true of all humanity apart from grace. James adds that the tongue is "full of deadly poison" (Jas 3:8).
- **"Selah" (v3).** The word's meaning is uncertain. Most think it marks a pause, a musical interlude or a moment to reflect. Here it follows the picture of poisoned words, as if to say: stop and consider how deadly speech can be.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the enemies are sharpened "like a serpent," not literally serpent-like, but alike in cunning and venom, and he links the description to Psalms 10:7 and 58:4. Henry describes the enemies as subtle, malicious, united and proud, and reads David's sufferings as a pattern of Christ's, who also suffered before he was exalted. Guzik notes Paul's use of v3 in Romans 3:13, and passes on Horne's observation that slander usually comes before persecution: people are first talked about, then attacked.

#### Guard Me from the Hands of the Wicked (vv4–5)
- **"Guard me, O LORD, from the hands of the wicked; preserve me from violent men" (v4).** The second stanza repeats the first, with small changes. "Guard" (*shomreni*) is the word used again and again in Psalm 121: "The LORD will keep you from all evil" (121:7). The hands of the wicked carry out what their hearts planned (v2) and their tongues spoke (v3).
- **"Who have planned to trip up my feet" (v4).** Literally "to push my steps." They want to make him stumble and fall, morally or physically.
- **"The arrogant have hidden a trap for me" (v5).** The images are from hunting. A "trap" (*pach*) was a bird-snare, a spring trap. "Cords" and a "net" were for catching larger animals. "Snares" (*moqshim*) were baited traps. All of them were hidden "beside the way," along the path someone would walk. David pictures himself as hunted game. Saul did literally hunt him "like one hunts a partridge in the mountains" (1 Sam 26:20).
- **"The arrogant" (v5).** The enemies are proud. They think no one will hold them to account (Ps 10:4, 11).
- **"Selah" (v5).** A second pause, this time over the danger itself.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the snares and nets stand for threatening dangers. Henry says simply, "those are safe whom God preserves." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's reminder that Saul set many traps for David and God brought him through every one, and VanGemeren's point that the wicked may plan and act for a time, but God will not let lawlessness rule forever.

#### You Are My God (vv6–8)
- **"I say to the LORD, You are my God" (v6).** The turning point. Surrounded by enemies, David says the simplest and strongest thing a believer can say: "You are my God." It is a confession of trust and of loyalty. It sets the LORD apart from every rival and claims him as David's own (Ps 16:2; 31:14).
- **"Give ear to the voice of my pleas for mercy, O LORD!" (v6).** "Pleas for mercy" (*tachanunay*) are requests for grace, for favor not earned. David does not plead his own merit.
- **"O LORD, my Lord, the strength of my salvation" (v7).** Two names stand together: *YHWH*, God's personal covenant name, and *'Adonai*, "Master" or "Lord." The study notes point out that the combination stresses God's sovereign power (also 68:20; 141:8). "The strength of my salvation" means the power that saves me. God is not only the one who rescues. He is the strength behind the rescue.
- **"You have covered my head in the day of battle" (v7).** This looks back on past protection. "Day of battle" is literally "day of weapons" or "day of armor," as JFB notes: the day when armor is put on. God himself was David's helmet. The verb "covered" (*sakak*) comes from the same root as the word Psalm 139:13 uses for God knitting David together in the womb. The God who wove him in the womb shields him in battle. Paul would later speak of "the helmet of salvation" (Eph 6:17; Isa 59:17).
- **"Grant not, O LORD, the desires of the wicked; do not further their evil plot, or they will be exalted!" (v8).** David asks God not to let the wicked succeed. Success would make them proud. JFB reads it this way: they "will be exalted if permitted to prosper." Failure would humble them.
- **"Selah" (v8).** The third pause. It marks the end of David's prayer for protection and the start of his prayer against the enemies.
- **Commentators:** JFB gives only a cross-reference on v6 (Ps 5; 16:2) and explains "day of battle" as the day armor is used. Henry finds four encouragements in vv6–7: David's relationship with God, his access to God, his confidence in God's help and his memory of God's past protection. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's picture of God as David's armor-bearer, and notes that God hears not only the words of prayer but its "voice," the cry behind it.

#### Let Burning Coals Fall upon Them (vv9–11)
- **"As for the head of those who surround me, let the mischief of their lips overwhelm them!" (v9).** God "covered" David's head (v7). Now David prays that the enemies' heads will be "covered" by their own evil words. JFB draws out the contrast. "The head" may mean the leader of the enemies, perhaps Saul, as Guzik suggests. Or it may mean the head of each enemy. Either way, the prayer is that their slander will come back on them (Ps 7:16; Prov 26:27).
- **"Let burning coals fall upon them! Let them be cast into fire, into miry pits, no more to rise!" (v10).** The pictures are of total judgment: fire from above, like the fire on Sodom (Gen 19:24; Ps 11:6), and pits from which there is no climbing out. JFB calls them "figures for utter destruction." Kidner, as Guzik reports, takes them as largely metaphorical: burning coals answering burning words, pits answering the traps the enemies dug. The study notes see in them a foreshadowing of the New Testament's teaching on final judgment (Matt 10:28; Mark 9:43–48).
- **"Let not the slanderer be established in the land" (v11).** "Slanderer" is literally "a man of tongue" (*'ish lashon*). A liar may thrive for a while, but David prays that such a man will not put down roots. Proverbs says, "Truthful lips endure forever, but a lying tongue is but for a moment" (Prov 12:19). Henry quotes it here.
- **"Let evil hunt down the violent man speedily!" (v11).** The hunters become the hunted. The trap-setters of v5 are now pursued by their own evil. Henry says, "Evil pursues sinners" (Prov 13:21).
- **How to read prayers like this.** Psalms that ask God to punish enemies are called "imprecatory psalms" (from a Latin word for calling down a curse). Psalm 140 is one. David does not take revenge himself. He hands it to God. This is the same David who twice spared Saul's life when he could have killed him (1 Sam 24; 26). Paul teaches Christians, "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Rom 12:19), and then adds, "if your enemy is hungry, feed him... you will heap burning coals on his head" (Rom 12:20, quoting Prov 25:21–22). In Paul's words the "burning coals" come through kindness, not destruction.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the slanderer "will not be tolerated" and takes the fire and pits as figures for complete ruin. Henry says, "Pride goes before destruction." Guzik notes that Romans 12:17 forbids returning evil for evil, yet he sympathizes with David's longing for justice. He passes on Trapp's image that the judgments of the wicked are "feathered from themselves," like an arrow fletched with the bird's own feathers.

#### The LORD Will Maintain the Cause of the Afflicted (vv12–13)
- **"I know that the LORD will maintain the cause of the afflicted" (v12).** The psalm ends in calm certainty. "I know" is not a feeling but a settled conviction, grounded in who God is. "Maintain the cause" (*din*) is a courtroom word: God will plead the case of the poor and win it. "The afflicted" (*'ani*) and "the needy" (*'evyonim*) are the poor and powerless, often in the Psalms the faithful who suffer for their faithfulness (Ps 9:18; 12:5).
- **"And will execute justice for the needy" (v12).** God is the judge who cannot be bribed or fooled (Deut 10:17–18). The slanderers may have the ear of the powerful, but the needy have the ear of God.
- **"Surely the righteous shall give thanks to your name" (v13).** The lament that began with a cry ends with thanksgiving. The same verb opened Psalm 138: "I give you thanks."
- **"The upright shall dwell in your presence" (v13).** Literally "sit before your face." This is the opposite of the enemies' fate. They fall into pits, "no more to rise." The upright sit securely before God. JFB says "shall dwell" means sitting securely under God's protection. Psalm 139 said there is nowhere to flee from God's presence (139:7). For the upright, God's presence is not a threat but a home.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God "is, and will be, the patron of oppressed innocence, much more of persecuted piety." JFB says the righteous will have "cause for praise" after all their troubles. Guzik passes on VanGemeren's description of "I know" as a victory cry, and Kidner's link to Revelation 22:3–4, where God's servants "will see his face."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 140 is the prayer of a man hunted by liars and violent men, who trusts God to cover his head and judge his enemies. Jesus prayed this kind of prayer in his own life. Men "plotted together in order to arrest Jesus by stealth" (Matt 26:4), laid verbal traps for him (Matt 22:15), and brought false witnesses against him (Mark 14:56). He did not answer slander with slander: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return... but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). Paul uses v3 to show that the poison of the tongue is in every human heart (Rom 3:13), including the reader's. The good news that follows in Romans is that Christ bore the judgment that such sin deserves (Rom 3:21–26). The fire and pits of v10 are real warnings, but the cross makes a way out. In Christ the afflicted have an advocate who will "maintain the cause" of his people (1 John 2:1), and the upright will one day "dwell" in God's presence and see his face (Rev 22:4).
