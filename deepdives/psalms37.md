*Setting:* "Of David." This is a wisdom psalm, closer to Proverbs than to a prayer. David never speaks to God in it. He speaks to God's people, as an older man passing on what a long life has taught him: "I have been young, and now am old" (v25). The question is the one Psalm 36 raised and Psalm 73 will raise again: what should a believer do when wicked people prosper and the righteous seem to lose? The psalm is an acrostic. Roughly every second verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, which makes it easy to memorize and gives it the feel of a collection of sayings rather than a story. It circles back on itself on purpose. "Fret not" comes three times (vv1, 7, 8), "inherit the land" five times (vv9, 11, 22, 29, 34), and "cut off" five times (vv9, 22, 28, 34, 38). Jesus quoted v11 in the Beatitudes: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5).

#### Fret Not Yourself (vv1–11)
- **"Fret not yourself because of evildoers" (v1).** "Fret" is *charah*, a common Hebrew word for anger that burns or is kindled. It is the word used when Cain "was very angry" (Gen 4:5). The form here means to heat oneself up. David is not talking about mild worry but about a slow burn inside at the unfairness of it all. "Not yourself" matters: this is something a person does to himself.
- **"Be not envious of wrongdoers" (v1).** Envy is the hidden root. Watching the wicked succeed, part of us wants what they have. Proverbs repeats the warning almost word for word (Prov 24:19; 3:31), and Asaph confesses he nearly slipped this way: "I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked" (73:3).
- **"They will soon fade like the grass" (v2).** In Israel, green grass and wild plants spring up after the winter rains and are scorched brown within days when the hot east wind blows. The success of the wicked is real, but it is seasonal. Isaiah uses the same picture for all human glory (Isa 40:6–8).
- **"Trust in the LORD, and do good" (v3).** Here is the first remedy. Trust is the inward part, doing good the outward part, and they belong together. Fretting tends to make us either passive or bitter. Trust keeps a person working.
- **"Dwell in the land and befriend faithfulness" (v3).** "Dwell in the land" means stay where God has put you, settle down, do not run off. "Befriend faithfulness" is a hard phrase in Hebrew. It can mean "feed on faithfulness" (God's faithfulness as food, so KJV "verily thou shalt be fed") or "pursue faithfulness" (be faithful yourself, as a shepherd tends a flock). The ESV's "befriend" leans to the second. Both are true to the psalm.
- **"Delight yourself in the LORD, and he will give you the desires of your heart" (v4).** This is not a blank check. The one who truly delights in God finds that God himself becomes the deepest desire of the heart, and that desire God always grants. Delight also reshapes the other desires over time. The verb is the same one used in Isaiah 58:14: "then you shall take delight in the LORD." It is a command, so delight is something we can choose to turn toward, not only a feeling that happens to us.
- **"Commit your way to the LORD" (v5).** "Commit" is *galal*, "to roll." It is the word for rolling a stone away from a well (Gen 29:3, 10). The picture is of rolling a load too heavy to carry off your own back and onto God. Proverbs 16:3 uses the same verb: "Commit your work to the LORD." Peter's "casting all your anxieties on him" says the same (1 Pet 5:7).
- **"Trust in him, and he will act" (v5).** Literally just "he will do." The object is left open on purpose. Whatever needs doing, he will do it.
- **"He will bring forth your righteousness as the light" (v6).** A person slandered or wronged often cannot clear his own name. God will. Like dawn growing to full noon, the truth about the righteous will come out. That may happen in this life, or it may wait for the day of judgment, but it will happen.
- **"Be still before the LORD and wait patiently for him" (v7).** "Be still" is literally "be silent." It is the silence of a person who stops arguing his case and lets God be judge. JFB renders it "Be silent to the Lord." "Wait patiently" uses a verb for waiting with longing, even writhing, the way someone waits for news (the same root is used for labor pains). Patience here is not indifference.
- **"Refrain from anger… Fret not yourself; it tends only to evil" (v8).** The third "fret not," and now David gives the reason. Burning resentment at the wicked does not stay pure. It leads to evil, to speaking rashly, taking revenge or giving up on God. James says the same: "the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (Jas 1:20).
- **"Those who wait for the LORD shall inherit the land" (v9).** "The land" (*'erets*) can mean the land of Canaan or the whole earth. For Israel the inheritance was the land promised to Abraham, divided by lot among the tribes (Josh 13–21). The promise is that the faithful, not the grabbers, will hold it in the end.
- **"In just a little while, the wicked will be no more" (v10).** "A little while" from God's view can be a long while from ours. But David's point is that it is short compared with forever. "Though you look carefully at his place, he will not be there": someone goes looking for the great man who once dominated the town and finds an empty house.
- **"But the meek shall inherit the land" (v11).** "Meek" is *'anav*, the humble and lowly, often the poor and afflicted, who do not push themselves forward but depend on God. Moses is called "very meek" (Num 12:3). Jesus quotes this line almost exactly (Matt 5:5). In Greek the Septuagint translates "the land" with *gē*, which can mean land or earth, and Jesus' use widens the promise to the whole renewed creation. Paul speaks of Abraham's offspring as "heir of the world" (Rom 4:13).
- **"Abundant peace" (v11).** *Shalom* is more than the end of fighting. It is wholeness, prosperity and well-being. JFB notes that peace here "includes prosperity."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the psalm an exposition of "some of the hardest chapters in the book of Providence." On v3 he writes, "The life of religion is a believing trust in the Lord," and warns that trusting God while neglecting duty "is not trusting God, but tempting him." On v4: "To delight in God is as much a privilege as a duty." Guzik, following Kidner, notes that "commit" literally means "roll," and the lexicon confirms it. Guzik reads v4 as God gradually shaping our desires to match his, and passes on Spurgeon's comment that sheep outnumber wolves, so the meek will one day fill the land. JFB reads "commit" as laying down as a burden "what you have to do and cannot."

#### The Lord Laughs at the Wicked (vv12–22)
- **"The wicked plots against the righteous and gnashes his teeth at him" (v12).** Gnashing teeth is the snarl of a wild animal, an image of hatred too strong for words (35:16; Acts 7:54, where the council gnashed their teeth at Stephen). JFB: "in beastly rage."
- **"But the Lord laughs at the wicked" (v13).** The same picture as Psalm 2:4, where God laughs at kings who plot against his Anointed. It is not cruelty. It is the calm of one who knows how the story ends. "He sees that his day is coming," the day of judgment, long delayed but sure. If God is not alarmed, his people need not be either.
- **"Their sword shall enter their own heart" (v15).** The weapons of the wicked turn back on them. Haman built a gallows for Mordecai and was hanged on it (Esth 7:10). Saul, who hunted David with the sword, died on his own sword (1 Sam 31:4). Psalm 7:15–16 makes the same point.
- **"Better is the little that the righteous has than the abundance of many wicked" (v16).** This is a classic wisdom "better than" saying (Prov 15:16; 16:8). JFB notes that the word for "abundance" can carry the idea of noise and tumult, the turmoil that often comes with great wealth. A little with God's blessing and a quiet conscience is worth more than a fortune without them.
- **"The arms of the wicked shall be broken, but the LORD upholds the righteous" (v17).** "Arms" means strength. The wicked trust their own arm. The righteous are held up by another's.
- **"The LORD knows the days of the blameless" (v18).** "Knows" in Hebrew is more than awareness. It is caring attention (1:6: "the LORD knows the way of the righteous"). He knows how many days they have and what each day holds. "Blameless" (*tamim*) means whole or complete, a life of integrity, not sinless perfection.
- **"In the days of famine they have abundance" (v19).** Famine was a real threat in a land that depended on rain. Elijah was fed by ravens and by a widow's jar that did not run out in the drought (1 Kgs 17). The promise is not that the righteous never suffer hard times but that God provides for them in hard times.
- **"Like the glory of the pastures; they vanish—like smoke" (v20).** The Hebrew word *kar* can mean "pasture" or "lamb," and the word for glory can mean "preciousness" or "fat." So the KJV reads "as the fat of lambs," fat burned up on the altar that rises as smoke and is gone. The ESV reads it as the beauty of spring meadows, which wither as fast as the grass in v2. Either way, the wicked disappear without a trace.
- **"The wicked borrows but does not pay back" (v21).** JFB reads this partly as a result: the wicked grow so poor they *cannot* repay, while the righteous have enough to give. It is also a matter of character. One kind of person takes, the other gives. The righteous are "generous," a word that means to show favor or be gracious.
- **"Those blessed by the LORD shall inherit the land, but those cursed by him shall be cut off" (v22).** The blessing and curse of Deuteronomy (Deut 28) and the promise to Abraham (Gen 12:3) stand behind this line. "Cut off" is covenant language for being removed from the people and the land (Exod 12:15; Lev 20:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those that rest on the Rock of ages, have no reason to envy the wicked." He adds that "God knows the believer's days," both the troubles and their length. Guzik ties v13 to Psalm 2 and stresses that God's calm should end our agitation, and on v16 he passes on an old saying that a crust with God's blessing beats a feast with his curse. JFB notes that the turning of the sword on its owner is "often mentioned" in Scripture and says nothing specific on the "fat of lambs" reading.

#### The Lord Upholds His Hand (vv23–31)
- **"The steps of a man are established by the LORD" (v23).** The word for man here is *geber*, a common word for a man, from a root meaning "to be strong." Clarke, cited by Guzik, makes much of the "strong man," but the lexicon uses it for men in general, so the point should not be pressed. The plain sense is that God steadies the walk of the one who walks with him (Prov 16:9; 20:24).
- **"When he delights in his way" (v23).** "His" is ambiguous. It can mean God delights in the man's way, or the man delights in God's way. Both are true, and both have been read here.
- **"Though he fall, he shall not be cast headlong" (v24).** The righteous do stumble, in trouble and in sin. But a fall is not a final fall. "The LORD upholds his hand" is the picture of a father holding a toddler's hand. The child may trip but never hits the ground. Proverbs 24:16: "the righteous falls seven times and rises again." Peter fell hard and was raised up again (Luke 22:31–32).
- **"I have been young, and now am old, yet I have not seen the righteous forsaken" (v25).** This is David's personal testimony, not a promise that no believer will ever be poor. Wisdom sayings state general truths in absolute form, like Proverbs 22:6. David had once been hungry himself, fleeing from Saul (1 Sam 21:3), so he knew hard times. His point is that God does not abandon the righteous, and over a lifetime he had seen God provide for them and their families. Faithful believers have lived and died poor (Heb 11:37), and Jesus himself had nowhere to lay his head. The verse is about God's faithfulness, not a guarantee of comfort.
- **"He is ever lending generously, and his children become a blessing" (v26).** The righteous person who has been provided for keeps giving. The Law commanded Israel to lend to the poor without interest (Deut 15:7–8). "His children become a blessing": JFB, "literally, for a blessing." The family is not a burden on others but a source of good.
- **"Turn away from evil and do good; so shall you dwell forever" (v27).** The same words as 34:14, which Peter quotes (1 Pet 3:11). God's care never makes his people careless. The promise of security comes with a call to live rightly.
- **"For the LORD loves justice; he will not forsake his saints" (v28).** "Saints" is *chasidim*, the faithful ones, those who live in loyal love (*chesed*) toward God. The reason he will not forsake them is his own character. He loves justice.
- **"The righteous shall inherit the land and dwell upon it forever" (v29).** "Forever" stretches beyond one lifetime. Israel's hold on the land was lost in the exile, and later Jewish and Christian readers came to see the final fulfilment in the new heavens and new earth (Heb 11:13–16; Rev 21:1–7).
- **"The mouth of the righteous utters wisdom" (v30).** "Utters" is the verb translated "meditate" in Psalm 1:2. What a person mutters over in his heart comes out in his speech (Luke 6:45).
- **"The law of his God is in his heart; his steps do not slip" (v31).** Here is the secret of the steady walk of v23. God's word inside keeps the feet on the path (119:11, 105). This is the new-covenant promise: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jer 31:33).
- **Commentators:** Henry on v27: "We must leave our sins; learn to do well, and cleave to it." On v28, "God forsakes not his saints in affliction." Guzik handles v25 carefully. He says wisdom literature states general truths that allow exceptions, and he sets Clarke, who claimed never to have seen an exception, beside Spurgeon, who had himself helped the begging children of godly parents. JFB skips v25 entirely. On v31 Guzik passes on Trapp's line that the righteous carry a Bible in head and heart.

#### Wait for the LORD and Keep His Way (vv32–40)
- **"The wicked watches for the righteous and seeks to put him to death" (v32).** A righteous life is not an untroubled life. The wicked lie in wait like a hunter (10:8–9). Saul watched David's house to kill him (1 Sam 19:11; Ps 59 title).
- **"Or let him be condemned when he is brought to trial" (v33).** Even if an earthly court condemns the righteous, God's verdict is the one that stands. JFB: the plots fail because God acquits the righteous. Romans 8:33–34: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies."
- **"Wait for the LORD and keep his way" (v34).** The two halves belong together: hope and obedience. Waiting is not sitting idle. It is staying on God's road while his timing unfolds. "You will look on when the wicked are cut off": the righteous will see the outcome they could not see while they were fretting.
- **"Spreading himself like a green laurel tree" (v35).** David gives another piece of testimony. The Hebrew phrase is unusual, literally "like a luxuriant native tree," a tree growing in its own soil, rooted and spreading. The KJV's "green bay tree" became a proverb. The ancient Greek read "cedars of Lebanon." The picture is the opposite of the righteous tree of Psalm 1:3. This tree looks strong but has no root in God.
- **"But he passed away, and behold, he was no more" (v36).** "I sought him, he could not be found," echoing v10. The great man, the one everyone feared, is suddenly gone, and no one can find a trace.
- **"Mark the blameless and behold the upright" (v37).** The wicked man draws every eye. David says look somewhere else. Pay attention to the quiet person of integrity.
- **"There is a future for the man of peace" (v37).** "Future" is *'acharit*, "the end" or "the latter part." It can mean how a life turns out, a happy close (Prov 23:18; Jer 29:11), and the lexicon notes it can also mean posterity, descendants. The KJV reads "the end of that man is peace." The KJV's "perfect man" is the same word as "blameless" in v18. The righteous man's story ends well. The wicked man's "future… shall be cut off" (v38). He has no good end and no lasting line.
- **"The salvation of the righteous is from the LORD" (v39).** The last word is not about the righteous but about God. They are not saved because they are good. They are saved because God saves. "Stronghold" is a fortress or place of refuge, the same word as 27:1 ("the stronghold of my life").
- **"Because they take refuge in him" (v40).** The final line returns to trust, where v3 began. The meek, the patient, the generous, the blameless, the upright: all of them are simply people who take refuge in God. "Delivers" comes twice: he delivers them, he delivers them from the wicked.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Duty is ours, and we must mind it; but events are God's." On v37 he writes, "None are perfect in themselves, but believers are so in Christ Jesus," and on v39, "The salvation of the righteous will be the Lord's doing." Guzik notes that the flourishing tree reverses Psalm 1, and he too stresses that the righteous are saved by God, not by their goodness. JFB reads "the end" of v37 as reward or expectation, and "time of trouble" as "straits."

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus took this psalm's central promise onto his own lips: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5). And he lived it. He called himself "gentle and lowly in heart" (Matt 11:29). When the wicked plotted against him, gnashed their teeth and brought him to trial, he did not fret or fight back but "continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). He was condemned by an earthly court, but God brought forth his righteousness like the noonday by raising him from the dead. Because of him, the meek who take refuge in God will inherit not just a strip of land but a renewed earth, and their end will truly be peace.
