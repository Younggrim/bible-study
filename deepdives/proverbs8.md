*Setting:* Part of the father's talks in Proverbs 1–9 (see Proverbs 1 on authorship). Chapter 7 ended in darkness, with a naïve young man following the forbidden woman "as an ox goes to the slaughter" down to "the chambers of death" (7:22, 27). Chapter 8 answers her. Wisdom, pictured as a noble woman, stands in broad daylight at the busiest places in town and calls to everyone. She first spoke in 1:20–33, where her tone was a warning. Here it is an invitation, and the speech climbs to the most famous poem in the book: Wisdom was with the LORD before the world began, beside him as he made it. Chapter 9 will then set Wisdom's house and Folly's house side by side and ask the reader to choose.

#### The Blessings of Wisdom (vv1–36)
- **"Does not wisdom call? Does not understanding raise her voice?" (v1).** The question expects a yes. The forbidden woman whispered at dusk on a corner (7:8–9). Wisdom shouts in the open. God's wisdom is not hidden or reserved for insiders, a point Isaiah makes too: "I did not speak in secret" (Isa 45:19).
- **Heights, crossroads, gates (vv2–3).** These were the busiest spots in an ancient town: high places where people could see and hear, the crossroads where travelers chose their path, and the city gates. The gate was the town square, where elders heard court cases and merchants did business (Ruth 4:1–11). Wisdom goes where decisions are made. The Tyndale notes point out that the forbidden woman worked "now in the street, now in the market" (7:12). The two women compete for the same people in the same places.
- **"To you, O men, I call… to the children of man" (v4).** Two different words: *ʾishim*, men in general, and "children of *ʾadam*," humanity. The call is universal, not limited to Israel's scholars or priests.
- **"O simple ones, learn prudence; O fools, learn sense" (v5).** She calls the very people who most need her, the "simple" (*petaʾim*, the naïve and impressionable, like the young man of 7:7) and "fools" (*kesilim*, the dull and self-confident). "Prudence" (*ʿormah*) is shrewdness used well. The same root describes the serpent as "crafty" in Genesis 3:1. Wisdom does not ask the naïve to stay innocent. She makes them shrewd enough not to be fooled.
- **"Noble things… what is right… truth" (vv6–7).** "Noble things" is literally "leading things" or "princely things." "Wickedness is an abomination to my lips" uses the word *toʿebah*, something God finds detestable (6:16). JFB says the wickedness meant is "specially falsehood, as opposed to truth."
- **"Nothing twisted or crooked in them" (v8).** "Twisted" and "crooked" are the words used for the wicked's paths in 2:15. Wisdom's words are the opposite of the seductress's "smooth talk" (7:21): straight, honest, nothing hidden.
- **"They are all straight to him who understands" (v9).** Wisdom's words are not riddles, but they are clear to people who are willing to learn. "Those who find knowledge" assumes a search (2:4).
- **"Take my instruction instead of silver… for wisdom is better than jewels" (vv10–11).** This repeats 3:14–15 almost word for word, and v11 sounds like the father speaking about Wisdom rather than Wisdom speaking. "Jewels" (*peninim*) may be red coral or pearls. The point is a choice: if it comes to it, take wisdom and leave the money. Solomon himself asked for wisdom rather than riches, and God gave him both (1 Kgs 3:9–13).
- **"I, wisdom, dwell with prudence" (v12).** Wisdom lives with good sense the way people live with neighbors. "Discretion" (*mezimmah*) is careful planning, a word that elsewhere can mean scheming (12:2), here put to good use.
- **"The fear of the LORD is hatred of evil" (v13).** The motto of the book is that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge (1:7). Here we learn what that fear looks like in practice: it hates what God hates. The list, "pride and arrogance and the way of evil and perverted speech," matches the things the LORD hates in 6:16–19, which also begin with "haughty eyes." Reverence for God that does not turn away from evil is not the real thing (Job 28:28).
- **"I have counsel and sound wisdom; I have insight; I have strength" (v14).** "Sound wisdom" (*tushiyyah*) is practical, effective wisdom that gets results. These four words echo Isaiah's description of the Messiah, on whom rests "the Spirit of counsel and might" (Isa 11:2).
- **"By me kings reign, and rulers decree what is just" (vv15–16).** Good government depends on wisdom, and in Israel the king was meant to rule by God's wisdom (Deut 17:18–20). The Tyndale notes point to Solomon's judgment between the two mothers (1 Kgs 3:16–28). This is not a promise that every king is wise. It says that any just rule anywhere comes from this source, which is why Paul can say the authorities are "instituted by God" (Rom 13:1).
- **"I love those who love me, and those who seek me diligently find me" (v17).** "Seek diligently" (*shachar*) is related to the word for dawn. The KJV has "seek me early," and some have taken it to mean seeking at first light or early in life. The lexicons give "seek earnestly," and JFB says "early" may simply mean "diligently." Either way, the promise is that the search is not in vain. Jesus said the same: "seek, and you will find" (Matt 7:7).
- **"Riches and honor… enduring wealth and righteousness" (vv18–21).** Wisdom does bring good things, but notice which ones. "Enduring wealth" is wealth that lasts, and it is paired with righteousness. Her "fruit" (v19) is better than gold. She walks "in the way of righteousness," so her gifts never come through crooked means. "Granting an inheritance to those who love me" (v21) uses the Hebrew *yesh*, "substance" or "what truly is," an unusual word for wealth. JFB calls these "enduring sources of happiness in moral possessions." Proverbs does not promise every wise person will be rich, and it says plainly elsewhere that a little with righteousness is better than much with injustice (16:8).
- **"The LORD possessed me at the beginning of his work" (v22).** Now the poem reaches back before creation. The verb *qanah* normally means "get, acquire" or "buy" (it is the word for acquiring wisdom in 4:5, 7). In a few poetic places it is used of God as "Creator" or "maker" of heaven and earth (Gen 14:19, 22; Deut 32:6), and the lexicon lists Proverbs 8:22 among those. So translations divide: "possessed" (ESV, KJV), "created" (NIV text, NRSV) or "brought me forth" (NIV footnote). The point of the line is not the origin of wisdom but its precedence. Wisdom was already there before God did anything.
- **The ancient debate.** In the 300s, Arius argued from the Greek translation, "the Lord created me," that the Son of God is a created being, the first and greatest of God's creatures. The church at Nicaea (AD 325) rejected that teaching. Many defenders of the faith replied that "possessed" is the better reading, or that wisdom, as an attribute of God, could never have had a beginning: God was never without his wisdom. The verse is poetry personifying an attribute of God, and building a doctrine of Christ's origin on one verb in it misreads the genre.
- **"Ages ago I was set up, at the first" (v23).** "Set up" (*nasak*) is the word for installing a king (Ps 2:6, "I have set my King on Zion"). JFB has "ordained, or inaugurated." Wisdom is given a royal place from the start.
- **"When there were no depths I was brought forth" (vv24–25).** "Brought forth" (*chul*) is the language of birth, used of the mountains being "brought forth" in Psalm 90:2. The poem uses every image it can, possessing, installing, birthing, to say one thing: wisdom is older than the oceans, the springs, the mountains and the hills.
- **"Before he had made the earth with its fields, or the first of the dust of the world" (v26).** "The first of the dust" may mean the earliest bits of soil or the sum total of the world's dust. Either way, before there was a single grain of earth, Wisdom was there.
- **"When he drew a circle on the face of the deep" (v27).** This is the horizon, the line where sky meets sea, pictured as God tracing a boundary with a compass (Job 26:10; Isa 40:22). JFB says the image follows "the popular idea" of the earth's shape. The verse is about God marking boundaries, not giving a science lesson.
- **"When he assigned to the sea its limit, so that the waters might not transgress his command" (v29).** In the myths of Israel's neighbors, creation meant a god battling the chaotic sea. Here the sea is no rival at all. God simply tells it where to stop (Job 38:8–11; Jer 5:22). JFB takes "his command" as "the shore, that is, of the sea." Creation is ordered, not a truce.
- **"Then I was beside him, like a master workman" (v30).** The word *ʾamon* is rare and has two main readings. The lexicon (BDB) gives "artificer, architect, master-workman," followed by the ancient Syriac and Latin, the Greek translation and the ESV. Medieval Jewish scholars and the KJV read it as "one brought up with him," a child or ward. JFB takes it as "an object of special and pleasing regard." The "master workman" reading fits the creation context: God built the world with wisdom (3:19). The "child" reading fits the next lines, where Wisdom plays and delights.
- **"I was daily his delight, rejoicing before him always" (v30).** "Rejoicing" (*sachaq*) can mean laughing, playing or dancing. It is the word used of David dancing before the ark (2 Sam 6:5, 21) and of children playing in the streets (Zech 8:5). Creation was not grim labor. It was joy, God delighting in his wisdom and wisdom delighting before him.
- **"Rejoicing in his inhabited world and delighting in the children of man" (v31).** The poem ends where it began, with people. Wisdom's delight in creation narrows to humanity, the very "children of man" she was calling in v4. The one who was there at creation is the one who now calls at the gates.
- **"And now, O sons, listen to me: blessed are those who keep my ways" (v32).** "Blessed" (*ʾashre*) is the first word of the Psalms (Ps 1:1): "how happy," "how well off." Wisdom's speech turns from creation back to the reader. Because she made the world's pattern, living by her is living with the grain of the universe.
- **"Watching daily at my gates, waiting beside my doors" (v34).** The picture is a student, or a lover, or a servant waiting at a doorpost for the door to open. Compare the young man of chapter 7 wandering toward the wrong door. JFB says this denotes "a most sedulous attention."
- **"For whoever finds me finds life and obtains favor from the LORD" (v35).** "Finds" is repeated. Life and God's favor go together. Proverbs 18:22 uses almost the same words of a man who finds a good wife.
- **"He who fails to find me injures himself; all who hate me love death" (v36).** "Fails to find" is the verb *chataʾ*, normally "to sin," but its basic meaning is to miss a target (Judg 20:16). JFB: "missing me" is the opposite of "finding." Missing wisdom is not neutral. It injures the soul, and those who hate her are choosing death, whether they mean to or not. The chapter ends on the same two roads as chapter 7, but now life is offered first.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry divides the chapter in three. In vv1–21 he hears "divine revelation" as "the word and wisdom of God," by which God "instructs, and governs, and blesses, the children of men." In vv22–31 he reads Wisdom as "the eternal Word and wisdom, the Logos," "the second person in the Godhead," and says many of the ancients read it the same way. JFB is more cautious. It grants that "many regard the passage as a description of the Son of God," but argues the chapter is personification: "though described as with God, wisdom is not asserted to be God," and "God's attributes are part of Himself." Guzik takes a middle line. He rejects Arius's use of v22, prefers "possessed," and points to the many places in Proverbs where *qanah* means to acquire, but he warns against reading the chapter as a one-for-one description of Christ. He does believe Jesus is the wisdom of God, as the New Testament says. His application is that a world made by wisdom works best for those who live by it, and that wisdom is found by people who seek it on purpose, every day. The Tyndale notes agree with JFB that Wisdom here is not a separate person but God's own wisdom, and point out that God needs no outside counselor (Isa 40:13–14).

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 8 is about God's wisdom, not a prediction in code, but the New Testament writers read it and saw Jesus. John's opening line echoes it: the Word was "in the beginning… with God," and "all things were made through him" (John 1:1–3). Paul calls Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24), the one "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3), "the firstborn of all creation," by whom "all things were created" (Col 1:15–16). What Wisdom claims in poetry, that she was beside God before the world began, Jesus claims plainly as a person: "the glory that I had with you before the world existed" (John 17:5). He is not a creature, as Arius thought, but the eternal Son who stood in the streets and called, "Come to me" (Matt 11:28), and who delighted in "the children of man" enough to become one of them. Whoever finds him finds life (John 17:3; 1 John 5:12).
