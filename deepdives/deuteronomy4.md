*Setting:* The plains of Moab, in the last weeks of Moses' life, about forty years after the Exodus. Chapters 1–3 retold the journey from Sinai (Horeb) to the edge of Canaan: the failure at Kadesh, the long wandering, the victories over Sihon and Og, and God's refusal to let Moses cross the Jordan. Now the history turns into a call to action. "And now, O Israel, listen" (v1). This chapter is the climax of Moses' first sermon, and it is one of the richest theological passages in the Old Testament. It explains why Israel must not make images of God, warns of exile if they do, promises mercy if they return, and proclaims that "the LORD is God… there is no other." In the shape of an ancient treaty, this is the point where the history gives way to the call to loyalty. The chapter ends with a short narrative note about three cities of refuge, and then a heading that introduces the main body of the law.

#### Moses Commands Obedience (vv1–14)
- **"Listen to the statutes and the rules… and do them, that you may live" (v1).** "Listen" (*shema*) means hear and obey, not just hear. Life in the land depends on it. **"Statutes and rules"** is a common pair in Deuteronomy for the whole body of God's instruction.
- **"You shall not add to the word that I command you, nor take from it" (v2).** God's word is complete for its purpose. Adding human rules to it, or quietly dropping the parts we dislike, both put man in charge of what God says. The principle repeats in 12:32 and Proverbs 30:6, and the last chapter of the Bible closes with a similar warning (Rev 22:18–19). Jesus rebuked the Pharisees for doing exactly this, making void the word of God through their tradition (Mark 7:8–13).
- **"Your eyes have seen what the LORD did at Baal-peor" (v3).** Only months before, Israel had joined in the sexual rites of Baal of Peor and 24,000 died (Num 25). **Baal** was the Canaanite storm and fertility god, worshiped under many local names. **"But you who held fast to the LORD your God are all alive today" (v4).** "Held fast" is *dabaq*, to cling, the word used of a husband clinging to his wife (Gen 2:24). The survivors are standing in front of Moses as living proof.
- **"That will be your wisdom and your understanding in the sight of the peoples" (v6).** Israel's obedience would be a witness. The nations would see a people whose life was shaped by good laws and say, "Surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people." Wisdom in the Bible begins with obeying God (Prov 1:7).
- **"What great nation is there that has a god so near to it… whenever we call upon him?" (v7).** Israel's greatness was not its size or army but God's nearness. Other nations' gods were distant, needing rituals and bribes to get their attention. The LORD hears whenever his people call.
- **"Statutes and rules so righteous as all this law" (v8).** Other ancient law codes existed, such as Hammurabi's, and there are real similarities. But Israel's law protected the poor, the foreigner and the slave in ways those codes did not, and it flowed from the character of a righteous God, not from a king's will.
- **"Keep your soul diligently, lest you forget" (v9).** The great danger is forgetting. **"Make them known to your children and your children's children."** Faith is passed on through families. Deuteronomy repeats this again and again (6:7, 20–25; 11:19).
- **"The day that you stood before the LORD your God at Horeb" (v10).** Moses speaks to the new generation as if they had been there. Many were, as children, and the rest stood there in their parents. The covenant was made with the whole people, across generations (5:3; 29:14–15).
- **"The mountain burned with fire to the heart of heaven, wrapped in darkness, cloud, and gloom" (v11).** God revealed himself and hid himself at the same moment. The fire showed his presence. The darkness kept his glory from consuming them (Exod 19:16–19; 20:18–21).
- **"You heard the sound of words, but saw no form; there was only a voice" (v12).** This is the key to the whole chapter. At Sinai God spoke but did not show a shape. Israel's relationship with God was to be built on his word, not on an image.
- **"His covenant… the Ten Commandments, and he wrote them on two tablets of stone" (v13).** "Ten Commandments" is literally "ten words." The two tablets may have been two complete copies, one for each party, as was done with ancient treaties. Both were kept in the ark (Exod 25:16).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v2 as forbidding "the introduction of any heathen superstition or forms of worship" and the neglect of what was commanded. It adds that the law itself was "a temporary economy" for the church's infancy, which is a Christian reading looking back from the New Testament. On v7, Henry says God is "ready to give an answer of peace to every prayer of faith." On v6, both JFB and Guzik note that some ancient thinkers admired Israel's laws and borrowed from them. Guzik stresses that faith rests on real events that people saw, and connects this to the eyewitness testimony of the resurrection (1 John 1:1–3). JFB has no notes on vv11, 13 or 14.

#### Idolatry Forbidden (vv15–31)
- **"Since you saw no form… beware lest you act corruptly by making a carved image" (vv15–16).** Because God showed no form, any image of him is a lie about him. This is not just a rule against worshiping other gods. It forbids making an image even of the true God. Israel learned this painfully with the golden calf, which Aaron called "the LORD" (Exod 32:4–5).
- **"Male or female… animal… bird… creeping thing… fish" (vv16–18).** The list runs through creation in roughly the reverse order of Genesis 1, and it covers the gods of Egypt and Canaan: human-shaped gods, bulls, falcons, snakes, fish. Paul echoes this list exactly when he describes humanity exchanging "the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things" (Rom 1:23).
- **"The sun and the moon and the stars, all the host of heaven" (v19).** Sun, moon and star worship was everywhere in the ancient world, including Egypt and later Babylon. Israel later fell into it (2 Kgs 21:3–5; Jer 8:2; Ezek 8:16). **"Things that the LORD your God has allotted to all the peoples."** This phrase is debated. Some read it as saying God gave the heavenly bodies to all humanity as lights and signs of the seasons (Gen 1:14), so they belong to everyone and are not gods. Others connect it to 32:8–9, where God divided the nations and kept Israel for himself, and think it means God allowed other nations to go their own way for a time (Acts 14:16; 17:30). Both readings agree that Israel was not to worship them.
- **"The iron furnace, out of Egypt" (v20).** An iron-smelting furnace was the hottest fire people knew. Egypt was a place of suffering, but also of refining. God brought Israel out "to be a people of his own inheritance." Israel *is* God's inheritance (32:9).
- **"I must die in this land… But you shall go over" (vv21–22).** Moses mentions his own exclusion for the third time (1:37; 3:26). The point now is a warning: if even Moses fell short and paid for it, how seriously must the people take God's commands?
- **"The LORD your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God" (v24).** God's holiness is like fire: it purifies and it destroys. "Jealous" (*qanna*) is used only of God in the Old Testament. It is not petty envy. It is the right, fierce love of a husband who will not share his wife with another lover. Hebrews quotes this verse: "our God is a consuming fire" (Heb 12:29).
- **"When you… have grown old in the land" (v25).** The danger will come not in the hard years but in the comfortable ones, when the miracles are a distant memory (6:10–12; 8:11–14).
- **"I call heaven and earth to witness against you today" (v26).** Ancient treaties named the gods as witnesses. Israel had no other gods, so Moses calls on creation itself (30:19; 31:28; Isa 1:2).
- **"The LORD will scatter you among the peoples" (v27).** This was fulfilled in the Assyrian exile of the northern kingdom (722 BC) and the Babylonian exile of Judah (586 BC). **"There you will serve gods of wood and stone… that neither see, nor hear, nor eat, nor smell" (v28).** A grim irony: if Israel wants idols, it will be sent to lands full of them. Psalm 115:4–8 and Isaiah 44:9–20 mock idols in the same way.
- **"But from there you will seek the LORD your God and you will find him, if you search after him with all your heart and with all your soul" (v29).** The great promise of the chapter. Exile is not the end. Jeremiah quotes this verse almost word for word in his letter to the exiles in Babylon: "You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart" (Jer 29:13). Daniel's prayer (Dan 9) and Nehemiah's (Neh 1:8–9) rest on it.
- **"When you are in tribulation… in the latter days, you will return" (v30).** "The latter days" (*acharit hayyamim*) means "the end of the days," the future after the judgment. The prophets later use the same phrase for the age when God would restore his people and send the Messiah (Isa 2:2; Hos 3:5). "Return" (*shuv*) is the Old Testament's word for repentance.
- **"For the LORD your God is a merciful God. He will not leave you or destroy you or forget the covenant" (v31).** The consuming fire of v24 and the merciful God of v31 are the same God. His jealousy and his mercy both come from covenant love.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls v20's furnace a "furnace for smelting iron," a picture of Israel's bondage and affliction, while the Tyndale notes add the idea of refining. On idols, Henry asks, "shall we serve those that were made to serve us?" On v29 he writes, "Afflictions engage and quicken us to seek God." Guzik warns that no leader stands above God's word, as Moses' exclusion shows. He passes on Thompson's point that God's jealousy is not like human jealousy but a holy zeal, which agrees with the Tyndale notes. He reads the scattering as fulfilled in the Babylonian exile and stresses that God's mercy stays open even then. JFB says calling heaven and earth as witnesses is figurative. JFB has no notes on vv21–25, 27, 29 or 31.

#### The LORD Alone Is God (vv32–40)
- **"Ask now of the days that are past… since the day that God created man" (v32).** Moses invites Israel to search all of history and all of space. Has anything like this ever happened?
- **"Did any people ever hear the voice of a god speaking out of the midst of the fire… and still live?" (v33).** Hearing God's voice should have been fatal (5:24–26; Exod 33:20). Israel heard and lived.
- **"Has any god ever attempted to go and take a nation for himself from the midst of another nation" (v34).** Ancient peoples had many stories of gods, but none of a god who freed a nation of slaves from a superpower "by trials, by signs, by wonders, and by war, by a mighty hand and an outstretched arm." Those last two phrases are the language of the Exodus (Exod 6:6) and become a refrain in Deuteronomy.
- **"That you might know that the LORD is God; there is no other besides him" (v35).** This is plain monotheism: not just "worship only the LORD," but "there is no other god." Isaiah says it again and again: "I am the LORD, and there is no other" (Isa 45:5–6, 18, 21–22).
- **"Out of heaven he let you hear his voice… on earth he let you see his great fire" (v36).** God's voice came from heaven and his fire stood on earth. He is God in both places, as v39 will say.
- **"Because he loved your fathers and chose their offspring" (v37).** This is the first time Deuteronomy says God *loved*, and it explains everything. Israel was not chosen because it was great or good, but because God loved (7:7–8). **"With his own presence"** is literally "with his face." God himself went with them (Exod 33:14).
- **"Know therefore today, and lay it to your heart, that the LORD is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath; there is no other" (v39).** Knowing this is not just a matter of the mind. It must be "laid to the heart," shaping how one lives. Rahab the Canaanite later says almost exactly this: "the LORD your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath" (Josh 2:11).
- **"That it may go well with you and with your children after you" (v40).** Obedience is not a burden laid on Israel to earn God's love. It is the way of life in the land that love has given.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Israel's laws are "above the statutes and judgments of any of the nations." He also sees Sinai's fire as "an earnest of the day of judgment, in which the Lord Jesus shall be revealed in flaming fire" (2 Thess 1:7–8). Guzik stresses that Israel was chosen for a role in God's plan and not saved by ancestry (Matt 3:9), and that obedience is the only fitting answer to such love. JFB has no notes on vv32–40.

#### Cities of Refuge (vv41–43)
- **"Then Moses set apart three cities in the east beyond the Jordan" (v41).** The sermon pauses for a brief narrative note. Numbers 35 required six cities of refuge, places where someone who had killed accidentally could flee from the avenger of blood and have a fair hearing. Moses designates the three on the east side now, since that land was already in Israel's hands. The other three would be named after the conquest (Josh 20:7).
- **"Bezer… Ramoth in Gilead… Golan in Bashan" (v43).** One for each eastern tribe: **Bezer** in Reuben's tableland, **Ramoth-gilead** in Gad's territory, and **Golan** in Manasseh's region of Bashan. The modern Golan Heights take their name from this town.
- **Why here?** The Tyndale notes suggest the placement fits the promise of long life in the land just made (v40): the cities protect innocent life. It also shows Moses doing what he could before he died, setting up justice for the land he would not enter.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes Moses acted on the part of the command he could carry out now rather than waiting for everything to be in place. JFB simply cross-references Joshua 20.

#### Introduction to the Law (vv44–49)
- **"This is the law that Moses set before the people of Israel" (v44).** A new heading. The history and the call to loyalty are done. Now comes the main body of the covenant, from chapter 5 to chapter 26. "Law" is *torah*, which comes from a verb meaning "to teach." It is instruction from a Father, not just a legal code.
- **"The testimonies, the statutes, and the rules" (v45).** Three words for God's instruction. "Testimonies" are the solemn terms of the covenant, the witness of what God requires.
- **"In the valley opposite Beth-peor, in the land of Sihon" (v46).** The setting is restated: the very place of Israel's recent sin (v3) and its recent victories (vv46–47).
- **"As far as Mount Sirion (that is, Hermon)" (v48).** The Hebrew text here actually reads "Sion" (not the Zion of Jerusalem), an otherwise unknown name for Hermon. The ESV follows the Syriac version and reads "Sirion," the name used in 3:9. Either way, it is the great mountain in the north.
- **Commentators:** Guzik mentions the "Sion" reading and links it to Sirion in 3:9 and Psalm 29:6.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel saw no form at Sinai, only heard a voice, and was forbidden to make any image of God. The New Testament answers that ban not by relaxing it but by fulfilling it. "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (John 1:18). Christ is "the image of the invisible God" (Col 1:15), the one true image, not made by human hands. The God who spoke from the fire has now "spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:1–2). And the promise of v29, that those scattered far away would seek God and find him, is how Paul describes God's purpose for all nations: "that they should seek God… yet he is actually not far from each one of us" (Acts 17:27). The "God so near" of v7 has come nearer still in Jesus, Immanuel, "God with us."
