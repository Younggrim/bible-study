*Setting:* Genesis 1–11 has told the story of the whole human race: a good creation, the fall, Cain, the flood and Babel, each one a fresh break between people and God. Chapter 11 ended with the nations scattered in confusion and with one family in Haran, in what is now southern Turkey: Terah, his son Abram, Abram's barren wife Sarai and his orphaned nephew Lot. Terah set out for Canaan but stopped halfway and died there. Genesis 12 is the turning point of the book. The camera narrows from all nations to one man, and God starts his answer to Babel. The events are usually placed sometime in the early second millennium BC, roughly 2000–1800 BC, though exact dates are debated. Genesis was written for Israel, and Israel heard in this chapter its own beginning: called out, promised a land, and later rescued from Egypt.

#### The Call of Abram (vv1–9)
- **"Now the LORD said to Abram" (v1).** No introduction, no reason given, no hint that Abram was looking for God. Joshua 24:2 says Terah's family "served other gods." The call comes out of nowhere, which is the point: it is grace. Stephen says "the God of glory appeared" to Abram while he was still in Mesopotamia, before Haran (Acts 7:2–4). That is why older translations like the KJV read "the LORD *had* said." The call in Ur is repeated or recalled here, now that Terah has died.
- **"Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house" (v1).** The Hebrew *lek-leka* is literally "go for yourself" or "take yourself off." The three steps narrow in, from the wide to the close: homeland, clan, and then the father's household, the most painful of all in a culture where the household was a person's security, inheritance and identity. Leaving it meant giving up everything that made a man safe.
- **"To the land that I will show you" (v1).** He is not told where. Hebrews puts it plainly: "he went out, not knowing where he was going" (Heb 11:8). The same phrase comes back at the climax of Abraham's story, when God tells him to take Isaac "to the land of Moriah" and offer him on a mountain "of which I shall tell you" (22:2). The life of faith begins and peaks with a journey toward a place God has not yet named.
- **"I will make of you a great nation" (v2).** The reader already knows the problem: "Sarai was barren; she had no child" (11:30). The first promise is impossible from the start. Everything about Abram's story will turn on whether God can do what nature cannot.
- **"And make your name great" (v2).** This is the direct answer to Babel. The builders said, "Let us make a name for ourselves" (11:4) and ended in confusion. Here God says "*I* will make your name great." The same promise is later given to David (2 Sam 7:9). The name the world grabs for, God gives.
- **"So that you will be a blessing" (v2).** In Hebrew this is a command form ("be a blessing"), not just a result. Abram is blessed for a purpose beyond himself. The word "bless" (*barak*) appears five times in vv2–3. In Genesis 3–11 the key word was "curse" (*arar*): the serpent, the ground, Cain, Canaan. Now blessing takes over the story, and it begins here.
- **"I will bless those who bless you, and him who dishonors you I will curse" (v3).** Two different Hebrew words stand behind "dishonors" and "curse." The first is *qalal*, whose root meaning is "to be light" and so "to treat as of little account" or "to despise." The second is *arar*, the strong word for a formal curse. Even to treat Abram lightly brings God's curse. Notice also the numbers: "those who bless" is plural, "him who dishonors" is singular. God expects the blessing to reach many and the opposition to be the exception. Balaam later finds this out the hard way (Num 24:9).
- **"In you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (v3).** "Families" points back to the clans of the nations listed in chapter 10 (10:5, 32). The scattered world of Babel is the target of the promise. The Hebrew verb (Niphal of *barak*) can be read "shall be blessed" or "shall bless themselves," that is, use Abram's name when they pray for blessing. The lexicon lists the second meaning for this form, and some modern versions follow it. The ancient Greek translation and the New Testament read it as "shall be blessed," and the later repetitions of the promise (18:18; 22:18; 28:14) make plain that the nations really receive something through Abram's line. Paul calls this verse the gospel announced in advance: "Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham" (Gal 3:8). Peter applies it to Jesus (Acts 3:25–26).
- **"So Abram went, as the LORD had told him" (v4).** There is no recorded reply, no questions, no bargaining. He simply goes. **"And Lot went with him."** Lot is fatherless, and Abram is his natural guardian. Whether bringing him was faithful care or a half-step of obedience (God had said to leave "kindred") is debated. Lot's presence will certainly cause trouble later (ch 13; ch 19).
- **"Seventy-five years old" (v4).** This is not a young man starting out. Abram lives to 175 (25:7), so by the standards of his time he was in mid-life, settled and prosperous. The promise of a nation comes to a childless man who has waited decades already, and he will wait 25 more years for Isaac (21:5).
- **"The people that they had acquired in Haran" (v5).** Literally "the souls (*nephesh*) they had made." This means servants and household members, a sizable group. Jewish tradition, followed by Matthew Henry and the Tyndale notes, reads it as converts Abram had won to the true God, since "make souls" sounds like making disciples. The word itself simply means "persons," so this is a reasonable inference rather than what the text says. Either way, Abram arrives as the head of a large household, not a lone wanderer.
- **"The place at Shechem, to the oak of Moreh" (v6).** Shechem lay in the central hill country, in a pass between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, about 30 miles north of Jerusalem. It became a key site in Israel's history: Jacob settled there (33:18), Joshua renewed the covenant there (Josh 24), and Jesus met the Samaritan woman nearby at Jacob's well (John 4:5). The "oak" (*elon*) is better a terebinth, a large shade tree. BDB gives "Moreh" as "teacher," so it was "the teacher's tree," most likely a spot where Canaanites sought oracles. Abram worships the LORD at a place where others sought guidance from pagan gods.
- **"At that time the Canaanites were in the land" (v6).** This short line is a cold splash of water. Abram has arrived in the promised land, and someone else lives there. The promise does not mean empty ground. It means God will give it in his own time (15:16).
- **"Then the LORD appeared to Abram" (v7).** This is the first time in the Bible God is said to "appear" to someone. And the promise sharpens: not just "a land I will show you," but "To your offspring I will give this land." "Offspring" (literally "seed") is a word that runs through Genesis from 3:15 onward. Paul notices that it is singular and points it to Christ (Gal 3:16).
- **"So he built there an altar" (v7).** Abram owns no land, and he builds no house. He builds an altar. It was his public claim, in the middle of Canaanite territory, that this land and his life belonged to the LORD. Abram's story is marked by altars (12:7, 8; 13:18; 22:9).
- **"With Bethel on the west and Ai on the east" (v8).** Bethel ("house of God," though at this time the town was called Luz, 28:19) is about 12 miles north of Jerusalem. Ai means "the ruin" or "the heap." Abram pitches his tent between the two. The name Bethel here is the one Israel later knew. Jacob will meet God at the same place (28:10–22).
- **"Called upon the name of the LORD" (v8).** The same phrase is used of Seth's line in 4:26. It means public worship and prayer by name, and it may include proclaiming that name to others. The Tyndale notes take it that way: Abram announcing the LORD in a land full of other gods. The ESV's plain "called upon" leaves both ideas open.
- **"Still going toward the Negeb" (v9).** The Negeb (*negeb*, "south" or "dry country") is the semi-desert south of the hill country. Abram never settles. He "pitched his tent," and Hebrews draws the lesson: he "went to live in the land of promise, as in a foreign land, living in tents… For he was looking forward to the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:9–10).
- **Commentators:** All three see the call as a test and a gift. Henry says Abram was tried "whether he could trust God further than he saw him," since God promised only "a land that I will show thee," not yet one he would give. On v3 he writes, "Jesus Christ is the great blessing of the world, the greatest that ever the world was blessed with." Guzik stresses the many "I will" statements and contrasts the human plans of chapter 11 with God's plans in chapter 12. He reads bringing Lot as only partial obedience. Henry instead thinks Lot came willingly, "influenced by Abram's good example," and the text itself makes no judgment. Guzik also applies v3 at length to modern nations and their treatment of Jewish people, an application many share but which goes beyond what the verse itself says. JFB calls the altar at Shechem "an open profession of his religion," and describes Shechem as "a pastoral valley then unoccupied," which sits awkwardly beside the text's own note that "the Canaanites were in the land." Henry is closer to the verse: "The children of this world have commonly more of it than God's children." On vv7–8 Henry adds the line most often quoted from this chapter: "wherever he had a tent God had an altar." JFB has no comment on vv2–4 and vv8–9.

#### Abram and Sarai in Egypt (vv10–20)
- **"Now there was a famine in the land" (v10).** Abram obeyed, arrived and worshiped, and then the land of promise could not feed him. Canaan depends on rain, while Egypt was watered by the Nile, so in drought people went down to Egypt. An Egyptian tomb painting at Beni Hasan, from around 1900 BC, shows a group of Semitic travelers from Canaan arriving in Egypt with their families and goods. Famine is the first test after the promise, and it will come again for Isaac (26:1) and Jacob (42:1–5).
- **"Abram went down to Egypt to sojourn there" (v10).** The text doesn't say God told him to go, and it doesn't say he prayed or built an altar. Many readers notice this silence and see Abram acting on his own judgment. Others point out that going where there was food was ordinary prudence, and that God never condemns the trip itself. "Sojourn" means to live as a resident foreigner, not to settle. Abram went south and west, not back toward Haran, which Henry takes as a sign he was not turning back.
- **"I know that you are a woman beautiful in appearance" (v11).** Sarai was about 65 (she was ten years younger than Abram, 17:17). With lifespans of around 127 years (23:1), she was roughly in mid-life, and the text plainly says she was striking.
- **"Then they will kill me, but they will let you live" (v12).** Abram's fear is not foolish. Powerful men in that world could take an attractive foreign woman, and a husband was in the way while a brother could be bargained with. The irony is sharp: Pharaoh later kills Israel's sons and keeps the daughters alive (Exod 1:22). Abram imagines here what his descendants will one day suffer.
- **"Say you are my sister" (v13).** It was a half-truth. Sarai really was Terah's daughter by another mother (20:12). But it was told to make people believe something false, and it put Sarai at risk to protect Abram. His reason is frank: "that it may go well with me because of you." The man who will bless all nations is willing to let his wife be taken to save himself. Abram does it again in Gerar (ch 20), and Isaac copies him (26:7). Genesis later reveals it was a habit Abram and Sarai agreed on wherever they went (20:13).
- **"The woman was taken into Pharaoh's house" (v15).** "Pharaoh" is a title, from the Egyptian for "great house," not a name. Genesis never names this king. Sarai enters the royal harem. The promised mother of the promised offspring is now in the palace of a foreign king, and the promise of v2 hangs by a thread. Abram's plan has not saved the promise, it has endangered it.
- **"For her sake he dealt well with Abram" (v16).** The list of animals and servants reads like a bride-price. Abram is getting rich off the very thing his lie set in motion, and the text lets that sit uncomfortably. Some of these gifts will cause trouble later: wealth leads to strife with Lot (13:5–7), and Hagar, Sarai's Egyptian servant (16:1), may well have come from this visit, though the text doesn't say. **"Camels"** has drawn scholarly debate, since large-scale camel herding in Canaan shows up in the archaeological record much later. Small numbers of domesticated camels are attested earlier in some regions, and a wealthy man might have a few. The list may also describe the gifts in terms later readers knew.
- **"But the LORD afflicted Pharaoh and his house with great plagues" (v17).** The word for "plagues," *nega*, means a stroke or blow, often a disease sent as a judgment. It is the same word God uses in Exodus 11:1: "Yet one plague more I will bring upon Pharaoh." The text doesn't say what the plagues were or how Pharaoh learned the reason. The point is clear: God steps in, not because Abram earned it, but "because of Sarai, Abram's wife." The promise depends on God, not on Abram's cleverness. Psalm 105:14–15 looks back on this: "he rebuked kings on their account, saying, 'Touch not my anointed ones.'"
- **"What is this you have done to me?" (v18).** The pagan king asks the man of God the question God asked Eve: "What is this that you have done?" (3:13). Pharaoh is the one acting with integrity here, and Abram has no answer. He says nothing at all in vv18–20.
- **"Here is your wife; take her, and go" (v19).** Pharaoh's "go" echoes God's "go" in v1, but in disgrace. Abram, who was called to be a blessing to the nations, has brought a curse on the first nation he met.
- **"They sent him away with his wife and all that he had" (v20).** The Hebrew *shalach*, "send away," is the same verb used throughout Exodus ("Let my people go," Exod 5:1). The whole episode previews Israel's story: a famine drives them to Egypt (47:13), their lives are threatened, God strikes Pharaoh with plagues, and they leave with Egyptian wealth (Exod 12:35–36). Israel would hear this and know that the God who brought Abram out of Egypt would bring them out too.
- **Commentators:** All three treat the lie as sin and refuse to excuse it. Henry says Abram "so concealed a further truth as in effect to deny it," and he sums up the lesson: "The grace Abram was most eminent for was faith; and yet he thus fell through unbelief." He asks, "what will become of the willows, when the cedars are thus shaken?" JFB calls the scheme "culpable" and "a reliance on worldly policy more than a trust in the promise." Guzik likewise insists that a half-truth told to deceive is still a lie, and that Abram found it easier to trust God's far-off promises than to trust him for today's needs. Guzik adds that God was protecting the line through which the Messiah would come. Henry is unusually kind to Pharaoh, observing that Abram "found Pharaoh to be a better man than he expected," and calls the plagues "happy chastisements that hinder us in a sinful way." JFB suggests Pharaoh's dismissal may have kept Abram from settling in Egypt and forgetting the promise, a point Henry also makes. Henry closes the chapter by noticing the same parallel to the exodus as the Tyndale notes. JFB has no comment on v17.

#### Pointing to Christ
Genesis 12 gives the promise that the rest of the Bible unfolds: one man's offspring will bring blessing to every family on earth. Abram is not the hero who earns it. Within a few verses he goes from faithful altar-builder to frightened liar, and the promise survives only because God guards it. That is the pattern of the whole story. Paul says the "offspring" in view is ultimately one person, Christ (Gal 3:16), and that in him the blessing of Abraham comes to the Gentiles (Gal 3:14). Where Abram went down to Egypt and put his bride at risk to save himself, Jesus, the true offspring, also went down to Egypt (Matt 2:15), and then gave himself up to save his bride (Eph 5:25). Abram left his father's house for a land he could not see. Jesus left his Father's glory to bring the nations of Babel home.
