*Setting:* This chapter belongs to the first of the two "oracles" that make up Zechariah 9–14 (9:1). It is undated, but it speaks to the same small community of returned exiles in Judah, sometime after 518 BC. Chapter 9 ended with the LORD saving his people "as the flock of his people" (9:16). Chapter 10 picks up that shepherd picture. The people have been scattered like sheep because their leaders failed them and because they trusted household idols and fortune-tellers instead of the LORD. God promises to punish the false shepherds, raise up true leadership from Judah, and bring home not only Judah but the long-lost northern tribes, "the house of Joseph," gathering his scattered people from every land in a new exodus. The ESV treats the chapter as one section.

#### The Restoration for Judah and Israel (vv1–12)
- **"Ask rain from the LORD in the season of the spring rain" (v1).** Israel had no great river like Egypt's Nile or Babylon's Euphrates. Its farms depended entirely on rain (Deut 11:10–17). The autumn rains softened the ground for plowing and sowing. The spring rains (*malqosh*, the "latter rain") in March and April filled out the grain before harvest. If they failed, the crop failed. Haggai had told this same community that drought came because they neglected God's house (Hag 1:10–11). Now the simple command is: ask.
- **"From the LORD who makes the storm clouds" (v1).** The Canaanites credited rain to Baal, the storm god, "the rider on the clouds." Elijah's contest on Carmel was about exactly this question: who sends the rain? (1 Kgs 17:1; 18:41–45). The answer here is plain. The LORD makes the clouds. **"To everyone the vegetation in the field"**: his gift is for all.
- **"For the household gods utter nonsense" (v2).** The Hebrew is *teraphim*, small figurines of household gods or ancestors kept in the home and consulted for guidance. Rachel stole her father's (Gen 31:19), and Michal used one to fool Saul's men (1 Sam 19:13). Josiah removed them in his reforms (2 Kgs 23:24). "Nonsense" is *'awen*, emptiness or wickedness. The word recalls "Beth-aven," "house of nothing," Hosea's mocking name for Bethel (Hos 4:15).
- **"The diviners see lies; they tell false dreams and give empty consolation" (v2).** Diviners claimed to read the future through omens, and the Law banned them outright (Deut 18:10–14). Jeremiah had fought prophets who told "lying dreams" and said "peace, peace" when there was no peace (Jer 23:25–32; 6:14). The word for "empty," *hevel*, is the "vanity" of Ecclesiastes: breath, vapor, nothing.
- **"Therefore the people wander like sheep; they are afflicted for lack of a shepherd" (v2).** Literally they "pull up their tents" like nomads. When true guidance is missing, people drift. Moses had prayed that Israel would not be "as sheep that have no shepherd" (Num 27:17), and Micaiah saw Israel "scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no shepherd" (1 Kgs 22:17).
- **"My anger is hot against the shepherds, and I will punish the leaders" (v3).** In the ancient world "shepherd" was a standard title for kings and rulers. "Leaders" is literally "male goats" (*'attudim*), the strong he-goats that walk at the front of a flock. Ezekiel 34 is the great chapter against shepherds who fed themselves instead of the sheep and "pushed with side and shoulder" against the weak (Ezek 34:2–21). Who these shepherds were in Zechariah's day is not stated. They may be foreign rulers over Judah, Judah's own leaders, or both. The theme becomes the center of chapter 11.
- **"The LORD of hosts cares for his flock… and will make them like his majestic steed in battle" (v3).** "Cares for" is the verb *paqad*, "to visit" or "attend to." It is the same verb as "punish" in the line before. God visits the shepherds in judgment and visits the flock in care. Then the picture changes sharply: helpless sheep become a royal war horse, the proud horse a king rides into battle.
- **"From him shall come the cornerstone, from him the tent peg, from him the battle bow, from him every ruler" (v4).** "From him" means from Judah. The leaders will no longer be imposed from outside but raised up from within. Each image is a picture of strength and stability:
- **The cornerstone (v4).** (*pinnah*) was the main stone at a building's corner that set the line for the walls. "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (Ps 118:22), and God laid in Zion "a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation" (Isa 28:16).
- **The tent peg (v4).** (*yated*) held a tent fast against the wind, or, as a peg driven into a wall, held whatever was hung on it. Isaiah used it for Eliakim, a faithful official: "I will fasten him like a peg in a secure place… and they will hang on him the whole honor of his father's house" (Isa 22:23–24).
- **The battle bow (v4).** stands for military strength, which God has already said he will supply (9:13).
- **"Every ruler" (v4).** translates *noges*, a word that normally means "oppressor" or "taskmaster." It is the word for Pharaoh's slave drivers (Exod 3:7; 5:6). Here it seems to mean a ruler who exercises power over the enemy. Some read it the old way: every oppressor will depart from Judah. The ESV takes it positively.
- **"They shall be like mighty men in battle… because the LORD is with them" (v5).** The reason for victory is not their skill but God's presence. **"They shall put to shame the riders on horses"**: Judah's foot soldiers beat the enemy's cavalry, as the chariots and horses of Egypt were overthrown at the sea (Exod 15:1).
- **"I will strengthen the house of Judah, and I will save the house of Joseph" (v6).** "Joseph" means the northern tribes, led by Ephraim and Manasseh, Joseph's sons. They had been carried away by Assyria in 722 BC (2 Kgs 17:6) and never returned as a people. Their restoration was a long-standing promise (Jer 31:18–20; Ezek 37:15–22). Zechariah does not give up on them.
- **"They shall be as though I had not rejected them" (v6).** One of the most tender lines in the chapter. God's compassion (*racham*, the womb-love of a parent) will restore them so fully that it will be as if the rejection had never happened. **"I am the LORD their God and I will answer them"**: in 7:13 God would not answer. Now he will.
- **"Ephraim shall become like a mighty warrior, and their hearts shall be glad as with wine" (v7).** Joy runs through three generations: the fighters, their hearts and "their children" who see it and are glad. Their joy is "in the LORD," not merely in victory.
- **"I will whistle for them and gather them in" (v8).** The verb *sharaq* is a whistle or hiss, the call a shepherd uses for his flock or a beekeeper for bees. It is a striking reversal. Isaiah said the LORD would "whistle for the fly… of Egypt, and for the bee… of Assyria" to come and judge Israel (Isa 7:18; 5:26). The same whistle that once summoned Israel's invaders now summons Israel home. **"For I have redeemed them"**: *padah*, to ransom, to buy back from slavery (Deut 7:8).
- **"They shall be as many as they were before" (v8).** In Egypt Israel "multiplied and grew exceedingly strong" (Exod 1:7). The promise to Abraham of numberless descendants still stands (Gen 22:17).
- **"Though I scattered them among the nations" (v9).** The Hebrew verb is *zara'*, "to sow" seed. God did not throw his people away. He planted them among the nations, and seed is sown to grow. Hosea used the same picture with the name Jezreel, "God sows" (Hos 2:23). **"In far countries they shall remember me"**: dispersion produced remembrance, as Moses foresaw (Deut 30:1–3). Many scattered Jews did keep the faith and spread knowledge of the true God, and they were in synagogues across the empire when the apostles brought the gospel (Acts 2:5–11).
- **"I will bring them home from the land of Egypt, and gather them from Assyria" (v10).** Egypt and Assyria stand for the two great places of bondage and dispersion: the first slavery and the northern exile. Isaiah 11:11–16 makes the same promise of a second exodus. Assyria as an empire had fallen ninety years before Zechariah, so the names are used as symbols of the old oppressors.
- **"To the land of Gilead and to Lebanon, till there is no room for them" (v10).** Gilead was the fertile hill country east of the Jordan, settled by Gad, Reuben and half of Manasseh and lost early to Assyria (2 Kgs 15:29). Lebanon was the far north. The returned people will fill the whole promised land and overflow it.
- **"He shall pass through the sea of troubles and strike down the waves of the sea" (v11).** The Hebrew is compressed, literally "he shall pass through the sea, distress." The picture is the Red Sea crossing: God strikes the waves and leads his people through (Exod 14:21–22; Isa 51:10). **"All the depths of the Nile shall be dried up"**: the river that was Egypt's life and pride is dried up, as in Isaiah 11:15 and 19:5. **"The pride of Assyria… the scepter of Egypt"**: the power of both oppressors is broken.
- **"I will make them strong in the LORD, and they shall walk in his name" (v12).** The chapter ends where v6 began: God strengthens his people. "Walk in his name" means to live by his character and authority, under his protection. Micah used the same words: "we will walk in the name of the LORD our God forever and ever" (Mic 4:5).
- **How it was fulfilled.** Interpreters read the regathering in different ways. Some see a partial fulfilment in the growth of Judah after the exile and in the Maccabean victories, and its completion in Christ's gathering of Jews and Gentiles into one flock. Many dispensationalists see the modern return of Jews to the land as a step toward a final, literal fulfilment when Israel turns to Jesus. Most agree that the full gathering is still ahead.
- **Commentators:** Henry urges asking rain of God rather than of "clouds or stars," and says "The idols have spoken vanity," which left the people "because there was no shepherd" to guide them. He reads the gathering of vv8–12 as fulfilled when Jews and Gentiles were united in the gospel church, and notes that strength from God does not excuse believers from working: they must "walk up and down in his name." Guzik treats the four images of v4 as titles of Jesus: cornerstone, tent peg, battle bow and ruler over every ruler, drawing on Psalm 118, Isaiah 22 and Revelation 19. He takes v1 as an invitation to bold prayer and sees the modern return of Jews to Israel as a partial fulfilment, with the full one when Israel believes in Jesus. JFB reads "goodly horse" as God making Judah a strong warrior, and the Messiah as the final fulfilment of v4. He compares the whistle to a beekeeper calling bees. The Tyndale notes see the *teraphim* as perhaps ancestor figures used to summon the dead, which the Law forbade.

#### Pointing to Christ
The people wandered "like sheep… for lack of a shepherd," and when Jesus saw the crowds "he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (Matt 9:36; Mark 6:34). He is the shepherd God promised, the one who said "I am the good shepherd" and "I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also" (John 10:11, 16). From Judah came the cornerstone. Peter applied Psalm 118 to Jesus: "the stone that was rejected by you, the builders, which has become the cornerstone" (Acts 4:11). He is the peg on which "the whole honor of his father's house" hangs. He is the one who redeems, *padah*, buying his people out of slavery with his own life (Mark 10:45), and who gathers his scattered children "from east and west" into one family (Matt 8:11; John 11:52). Those who belong to him are strong in the Lord and walk in his name.
