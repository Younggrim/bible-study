*Setting:* This is the fourth of the six "woes" in chapters 28–33. The scheming that Isaiah exposed in 29:15 now comes into the open. Around 705–701 BC, after the Assyrian king Sargon II died, Hezekiah joined a revolt against Assyria, and Judah's officials sent envoys loaded with gifts south across the desert to Egypt to buy military help. Egypt at the time was ruled by the Cushite (Nubian) 25th Dynasty, a power that promised much. Isaiah calls the whole mission a disaster. God had offered a better way, "in returning and rest you shall be saved," and they refused it. Yet the chapter does not end in judgment. It turns to one of the tenderest passages in Isaiah, "the LORD waits to be gracious to you," and closes with God himself destroying Assyria, which happened in 701 BC (37:36).

#### Do Not Go Down to Egypt (vv1–7)
- **"Ah, stubborn children… who carry out a plan, but not mine" (v1).** "Ah" is the woe-cry again. God calls them "children," a reminder of the relationship they are betraying (1:2), and "stubborn," the word for the rebellious son of Deuteronomy 21:18. **"Who make an alliance, but not of my Spirit."** The word for alliance can mean "pour out a drink offering," the libation that sealed a treaty. Their plan was not from God, and its covenant was not made in his presence. **"That they may add sin to sin."** Distrusting God compounds their earlier sins.
- **"Who set out to go down to Egypt, without asking for my direction" (v2).** Literally "without asking my mouth." Israel's kings were meant to consult the LORD before war (1 Kings 22:5). This was also the very thing God had warned kings against: going back to Egypt for horses (Deut 17:16). Egypt was the house of slavery God had rescued them from. **"To take refuge in the protection of Pharaoh and to seek shelter in the shadow of Egypt"** uses language that belongs to God. Elsewhere his people shelter in "the shadow of your wings" (Ps 36:7; 91:1).
- **"Therefore shall the protection of Pharaoh turn to your shame" (v3).** Egypt did send an army in 701 BC, under the Cushite prince Tirhakah (37:9), and Sennacherib's annals boast that he defeated an Egyptian force at Eltekeh, near the coast. It did not save Judah.
- **"His officials are at Zoan and his envoys reach Hanes" (v4).** Zoan (Tanis) was a royal city in the northeastern Nile Delta (19:11). Hanes is probably Heracleopolis, farther south in Middle Egypt, though the Tyndale notes say its location is uncertain. The envoys have reached deep into Egypt, and **"everyone comes to shame through a people that cannot profit them" (v5).**
- **"An oracle on the beasts of the Negeb" (v6).** Isaiah gives the delegation an oracle title of its own, as if it were a foreign nation. The Negeb is the dry south of Judah, and beyond it lay the Sinai desert. The envoys probably took this harsh route to avoid the coast road, which Assyria controlled. **"The lioness and the lion, the adder and the flying fiery serpent"** recall the wilderness wanderings, when fiery serpents bit Israel (Num 21:6; Deut 8:15). Judah is walking back through the desert of the Exodus, the wrong way, toward Egypt.
- **"They carry their riches on the backs of donkeys… to a people that cannot profit them."** The picture is a long caravan of donkeys and camels carrying Judah's treasury to Egypt to pay for protection. It is a vivid picture of wasted wealth.
- **"Egypt's help is worthless and empty; therefore I have called her 'Rahab who sits still'" (v7).** Rahab (not the woman of Jericho, a different word) was a mythical sea monster of chaos, a name often used for Egypt (51:9; Ps 87:4; 89:10). The name means something like "arrogance" or "storming." Isaiah gives it a mocking twist: *Rahab hem shabet*, "Rahab sits still," or "the do-nothing dragon," as Guzik paraphrases it. Egypt roars but stays on the couch. JFB renders it "Arrogance that sitteth still." The Tyndale notes call it a "harmless dragon."
- **Commentators:** Henry sets the chapter against "the approaching danger of Jerusalem" from Sennacherib and calls vv1–7 a reproof of those who ran to Egypt. Guzik notes that the real sin was not asking God first, which added sin to sin. JFB has no real note on v5 and only gives a cross-reference to Jeremiah 2:36, where Jeremiah later says the same about Egypt a century on.

#### A Rebellious People (vv8–17)
- **"Go, write it before them on a tablet and inscribe it in a book" (v8).** God tells Isaiah to put the message in writing, so that later generations would see that it was true (8:1, 16). JFB thinks the tablet was a short public version and the scroll the full message. **"As a witness forever."** The written word outlasts the people who refused to hear it.
- **"They are a rebellious people, lying children, children unwilling to hear" (v9).** Three times God calls them his children. "Instruction" is *torah*, God's teaching.
- **"Who say to the seers, 'Do not see,' and to the prophets, 'Do not prophesy to us what is right'" (v10).** They do not reject prophets as such. They want a different message. **"Speak to us smooth things, prophesy illusions."** Henry: "They must speak to them smooth things, must flatter them in their sins." Paul warned Timothy of the same thing: people who "will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions" (2 Tim 4:3).
- **"Let us hear no more about the Holy One of Israel" (v11).** That was Isaiah's favorite name for God, and they were tired of it. They wanted a God who would not make demands. Isaiah's response is to use the name three times in the next few verses (vv11, 12, 15).
- **"Because you despise this word and trust in oppression and perverseness" (v12).** They rejected God's word and relied instead on crooked dealings. The Tyndale notes connect it with both the Egyptian scheme and the injustice at home that paid for it.
- **"Like a breach in a high wall, bulging out and about to collapse… suddenly, in an instant" (v13).** A mudbrick or stone wall with a weak spot bulges slowly for a long time, and then falls all at once. Their sin is that weak spot. **"Like that of a potter's vessel that is smashed so ruthlessly that… not a shard is found" (v14).** Normally even broken pottery was useful. A shard could scoop embers from the fire or water from a cistern. This collapse will be so complete that nothing useful is left.
- **"In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength" (v15).** This is the heart of the chapter. "Returning" (*shuvah*) is repentance, turning back to God. JFB adds that it also meant turning back from the Egypt mission. "Rest" (*nachat*) is to settle down, stop striving. "Quietness" is calm, the opposite of panic (7:4). "Trust" (*bitchah*) is confident reliance. God's way of salvation looked like doing nothing, and that is exactly why it was so hard for them. Henry: "in returning to God and reposing in him as our rest." **"But you were unwilling."**
- **"No! We will flee upon horses"; therefore you shall flee away (v16).** Isaiah plays on words. "We will speed on horses" (*nanus*), "so you will speed away" (*tenusun*). "We will ride on swift ones" (*qal*), "so your pursuers will be swift" (*yiqqallu*). They will get their speed, all of it in retreat. Horses were Egypt's great export (31:1; 1 Kings 10:28), and the psalmist warns that "the war horse is a false hope for salvation" (Ps 33:17).
- **"A thousand shall flee at the threat of one" (v17).** This reverses the covenant blessing. God had promised that if Israel obeyed, "five of you shall chase a hundred" (Lev 26:8; Deut 32:30). Now the numbers run the other way. **"Like a flagstaff on the top of a mountain."** Jerusalem was left like a single pole on a bare hill. That is a fair description of 701 BC, when Sennacherib claimed to have taken 46 of Judah's walled cities and left Jerusalem standing alone (1:8).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv8–17 a warning to those who rejected God's advice. Guzik sees each word in v15, returning, rest, quietness and trust, as a mark of real faith, and notes that the fleeing on horses reverses the blessing of Leviticus 26. JFB reads the bulging wall as Judah's unstable policy toward Egypt and v15 as God's call to stop the arms race and wait on him.

#### The Lord Will Be Gracious (vv18–33)
- **"Therefore the LORD waits to be gracious to you" (v18).** After vv8–17 we expect "therefore judgment." Instead comes grace. They would not wait for God (v15), but God waits for them. "Waits" is *chakah*, to wait patiently, to linger. **"He exalts himself to show mercy"**: God rises, as from his throne, to have compassion. **"For the LORD is a God of justice."** His justice does not cancel his mercy. It means he will put things right. **"Blessed are all those who wait for him."** It is the same verb: those who wait for God share his patience. Henry: "He will wait till you return to him and seek his face, and then he will be ready to meet you with mercy."
- **"You shall weep no more. He will surely be gracious to you at the sound of your cry" (v19).** God responds to the first cry, "as soon as he hears it." Hezekiah's prayer during the siege was answered in exactly this way (37:14–21).
- **"Though the Lord give you the bread of adversity and the water of affliction" (v20).** Siege rations were bread and water, and little of both. God does not promise to remove all hardship. **"Yet your Teacher will not hide himself anymore, but your eyes shall see your Teacher."** The word *moreh* can be read as plural, "teachers" (as the KJV has it), meaning God's true prophets, no longer silenced. Henry takes it that way: "God will find a time to call the teachers out of their corners again." The ESV reads it as singular, "your Teacher," meaning God himself, who had seemed to hide his face (8:17). Both fit. Guzik adds that hardship often opens ears that comfort closed.
- **"Your ears shall hear a word behind you, saying, 'This is the way, walk in it'" (v21).** The image is a shepherd or guide walking behind a flock, calling out when one starts to wander to the right or the left. The people who told God's prophets to "leave the way" (v11) will now be guided back onto it. JFB takes the word as conscience guided by the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised that the Spirit "will guide you into all the truth" (John 16:13).
- **"You will defile your carved idols overlaid with silver… You will say to them, 'Be gone!'" (v22).** True repentance shows in throwing out the idols, as Hezekiah did in his reforms (2 Kings 18:4), and treating them as unclean, like a soiled cloth.
- **"He will give rain for the seed… your livestock will graze in large pastures" (vv23–24).** These are the covenant blessings of rain and harvest (Lev 26:4–5; Deut 28:12). Even the farm animals eat well: **"seasoned fodder, which has been winnowed with shovel and fork."** Normally animals ate rough straw. In this new abundance, they get the cleaned grain that people usually ate.
- **"Brooks running with water, in the day of the great slaughter, when the towers fall" (v25).** Water on every hill, a picture of plenty in a dry land, comes on the same day God brings down his enemies' strongholds.
- **"The light of the moon will be as the light of the sun, and the light of the sun will be sevenfold" (v26).** Seven is the number of completeness. This is creation renewed and filled with God's glory (24:23; 60:19–20). **"In the day when the LORD binds up the brokenness of his people, and heals the wounds inflicted by his blow."** The same God who struck them heals them, just as Hosea said: "he has torn us, that he may heal us" (Hos 6:1).
- **"Behold, the name of the LORD comes from afar, burning with his anger" (v27).** God's "name" stands for God himself, present and acting. He comes like a thunderstorm: smoke, fiery lips, breath like a flood **"that reaches up to the neck" (v28)**. Isaiah had used that exact image of Assyria flooding Judah (8:8). Now God's breath floods Assyria. **"To sift the nations with the sieve of destruction, and to place on the jaws of the peoples a bridle that leads astray."** The nations are shaken like grain in a sieve, and led off course like a horse with a bridle (37:29, where God says to Sennacherib, "I will put my hook in your nose and my bit in your mouth").
- **"You shall have a song as in the night when a holy feast is kept" (v29).** Passover was celebrated at night (Exod 12:42). JFB compares the night of Assyria's defeat to the night God brought Israel out of Egypt. **"As when one sets out to the sound of the flute to go to the mountain of the LORD, to the Rock of Israel."** Pilgrims climbing to Jerusalem for the festivals walked with flutes and singing (Ps 122:1). The night of the Assyrian defeat will feel like a festival.
- **"The LORD will cause his majestic voice to be heard… with a cloudburst and storm and hailstones" (v30).** God's "arm" coming down is the image of the Exodus (Exod 6:6; 15:16). **"The Assyrians will be terror-stricken at the voice of the LORD, when he strikes with his rod" (v31).** Assyria had been God's rod for punishing Israel (10:5). Now God picks up the rod against Assyria.
- **"Every stroke of the appointed staff… will be to the sound of tambourines and lyres" (v32).** The instruments silenced in judgment (24:8) play again, as God's people celebrate his victory, as Miriam did at the Red Sea (Exod 15:20).
- **"A burning place has long been prepared; indeed, for the king it is made ready" (v33).** "Burning place" is *tophteh*, a form of Topheth, the site in the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem where children were burned as sacrifices to the god Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31). There may be a pun: Molech means "king," and here the pyre is ready for "the king," the king of Assyria. The pyre is **"deep and wide, with fire and wood in abundance; the breath of the LORD, like a stream of sulfur, kindles it."** The valley's Hebrew name, *Ge Hinnom*, later became *Gehenna*, the word Jesus used for hell (Matt 5:22; 10:28). Sennacherib himself was not burned there. He went home and was murdered by two of his sons while worshiping in his god's temple (37:37–38). But his army was destroyed before Jerusalem, and the fire of Topheth stands for God's final judgment on all who set themselves against him.
- **Commentators:** Henry divides vv18–33 into promises to those who trusted God and a prophecy of "the total defeat of the Assyrian army," and reads v21 as God's people being told, "This is the way, walk you in it." Guzik stresses that God's delays have a merciful purpose and that being uncomfortable but listening to God is better than being comfortable and out of step with him. He connects Topheth with Sennacherib's death at the hands of his sons. JFB compares the song in the night to Passover and sees in Topheth a picture of both Assyria's end and, later, hell.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah wanted protection it could buy and a message it could enjoy. God offered rest it could only receive by trust. "In returning and rest you shall be saved." Jesus makes the same offer to the weary and burdened (Matt 11:28–29), and the cross is where God's waiting grace and his justice meet (Rom 3:26). He is the Teacher who no longer hides, the Good Shepherd whose voice says "this is the way" (John 10:4; 14:6). And the fire of Topheth, which Jesus called Gehenna, is the judgment he himself bore so that those who wait for him would sing the song in the night.
