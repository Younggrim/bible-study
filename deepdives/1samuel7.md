*Setting:* The ark has come back from Philistine territory to Beth-shemesh, where seventy men died for treating it carelessly (6:19). The frightened townspeople asked the people of Kiriath-jearim, up in the hills, to take it. Chapter 7 covers a span of twenty years and more. Samuel, missing from the story since 4:1, steps back in, now a grown man and Israel's recognized prophet. The chapter is the mirror image of chapter 4. There, Israel fought the Philistines near a place called Ebenezer, trusted in the ark, and lost everything. Here, Israel repents, Samuel prays, God fights, and a new stone named Ebenezer marks the victory. It is the high point of Samuel's leadership, just before the people ask for a king (ch 8).

#### The Ark at Kiriath-jearim (vv1–2)
- **"Brought it to the house of Abinadab on the hill" (v1).** Kiriath-jearim, "town of forests," was a former Gibeonite town (Josh 9:17) on the border of Judah and Benjamin. The ark is kept not in a tabernacle but in a private house. Shiloh was apparently destroyed after the battle at Aphek (Jer 7:12; Ps 78:60), and the ark never returned there. David will later fetch it from "the house of Abinadab, which was on the hill" (2 Sam 6:3).
- **"They consecrated his son Eleazar to have charge of the ark" (v1).** "Have charge of" is the word used for the Levites' duty to guard the tabernacle (Num 3:7–8). Whether Abinadab's family were Levites is not said. The Tyndale notes think they likely were, while JFB thinks Eleazar was not a Levite and was simply set apart as a keeper.
- **"Some twenty years, and all the house of Israel lamented after the LORD" (v2).** The ark stayed far longer than twenty years: through Saul's whole reign until early in David's. So "twenty years" probably measures the time until the assembly at Mizpah below. "Lamented after" is an unusual verb (*nahah*), meaning to wail or mourn. Israel had the ark back, but not the LORD's favor. They were still under Philistine pressure, and they began to feel their distance from God. That longing is where repentance begins.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Eleazar "was not a Levite, and was therefore only set apart" as keeper, and notes that "a much longer period elapsed" before the ark left Kiriath-jearim. Guzik suggests that having the ark nearby had not made Israel right with God, and that this is why they mourned.

#### Samuel Judges Israel (vv3–17)
- **"If you are returning to the LORD with all your heart" (v3).** Samuel's first recorded words to the nation are a call to repentance. "Return" (*shuv*) is the great Old Testament word for repentance: to turn around and go back. Samuel does not let them stop at feelings. A returning heart will show itself in what they get rid of.
- **"Put away the foreign gods and the Ashtaroth" (v3).** Baal was the Canaanite storm god, believed to send the rain that made crops grow. The Ashtaroth (plural of Ashtoreth, also called Astarte) were images of his partner, a goddess of fertility, love and war. Their worship often involved sexual rites. Israel had not abandoned the LORD. They had added these gods alongside him, hedging their bets for rain and harvest. That is why Samuel says "serve him *only*." Jacob told his household the same thing at Bethel (Gen 35:2), and Joshua at Shechem (Josh 24:14, 23).
- **"So the people of Israel put away the Baals and the Ashtaroth" (v4).** This time they actually do it. In Judges, Israel cried out to God many times, but rarely do we read that they put their idols away before God acted (Judg 10:16 is one exception).
- **"Gather all Israel at Mizpah, and I will pray to the LORD for you" (v5).** Mizpah means "watchtower." It probably stood on a hill about 8 miles north of Jerusalem in Benjamin, and it was a traditional gathering place for the tribes (Judg 20:1). "I will pray" is the Hebrew verb *palal*. Eli had asked, "If someone sins against the LORD, who can intercede (*palal*) for him?" (2:25). Here is the answer: Samuel. He is remembered later as one of Israel's great intercessors, alongside Moses (Ps 99:6; Jer 15:1), and he will say, "Far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you" (12:23).
- **"Drew water and poured it out before the LORD" (v6).** This rite appears nowhere else in the Old Testament. Its meaning is not explained. It may picture hearts poured out in repentance like water (Lam 2:19, "pour out your heart like water"), or utter helplessness, since spilled water cannot be gathered up again (2 Sam 14:14). It may also be a kind of offering. Along with fasting, it shows grief and humility before God.
- **"We have sinned against the LORD" (v6).** Simple, direct confession, with no excuses. Compare David's "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Sam 12:13).
- **"Samuel judged the people of Israel at Mizpah" (v6).** Samuel is now the nation's judge. In his case "judging" means leading, settling disputes and calling the people back to God's law, rather than leading armies.
- **"When the Philistines heard… they went up against Israel" (v7).** A huge assembly of Israelites looked to the Philistines like a rebellion in the making. They attack while Israel is fasting and praying. "They were afraid," but this time their fear sends them to prayer rather than to a lucky charm.
- **"Do not cease to cry out to the LORD our God for us" (v8).** In chapter 4 they asked, "Let us bring the ark… that it may save us." Now they ask Samuel to pray "that he may save us." The object of their trust has changed from a sacred box to the LORD himself.
- **"A nursing lamb… as a whole burnt offering" (v9).** The law required a lamb to be at least eight days old for sacrifice (Lev 22:27), so this is a very young animal. A whole burnt offering was completely consumed on the altar, all of it given to God, as atonement and as total surrender (Lev 1:4). Samuel was a Levite (1 Chr 6:28), not a descendant of Aaron. With the priesthood at Shiloh in ruins, he acts as Israel's priest, prophet and judge all at once. "And the LORD answered him."
- **"The LORD thundered with a mighty sound that day against the Philistines" (v10).** While the sacrifice is still burning, God acts. This fulfills Hannah's song: "against them he will thunder in heaven" (2:10). The same word is used. Thunder, storm and panic are how God fought for Israel in other battles (Exod 14:24; Josh 10:10–11; Judg 5:20–21). There is also irony here. The Philistines and Israel's backsliders had looked to Baal, the storm god, for thunder and rain. Now the LORD thunders, and it is the Philistines who are thrown "into confusion."
- **"Pursued the Philistines… as far as below Beth-car" (v11).** Beth-car is otherwise unknown. Israel's part is only to chase an enemy God has already routed.
- **"Called its name Ebenezer; for he said, 'Till now the LORD has helped us'" (v12).** *Eben ha'ezer* means "the stone of help." It deliberately answers chapter 4, where Israel camped at Ebenezer and lost the ark (4:1; 5:1). The defeat at the first Ebenezer is turned into victory at the second. It is probably not the same spot. This stone stood near Mizpah, and the earlier Ebenezer was near Aphek. "Shen" means "tooth," perhaps a crag. "Till now" (*'ad-hennah*) can mean "up to this time" or "as far as this place." Both are true. God's help reached all the way to here and to now, and the stone was a promise that it would go on.
- **"The Philistines were subdued and did not again enter the territory of Israel" (v13).** This seems at odds with later chapters, where Philistine garrisons sit in Israel's land (10:5; 13:3). The point is that the Philistines made no further major invasion while Samuel led the nation. "The hand of the LORD was against the Philistines all the days of Samuel," the same "hand" that was heavy on them in chapter 5. Their pressure returns in Saul's reign, when Israel trusts a king more than God.
- **"From Ekron to Gath" (v14).** The border towns the Philistines had taken were recovered, in the strip of land nearest Ekron and Gath. "Peace also between Israel and the Amorites." "Amorites" here means the remaining Canaanite peoples in the hill country. When Israel was right with God, peace followed on every side.
- **"Samuel judged Israel all the days of his life" (v15).** Even after Saul is crowned, Samuel continues as judge and prophet.
- **"On a circuit year by year to Bethel, Gilgal, and Mizpah" (v16).** All three lay in Benjamin and southern Ephraim, within about 25 miles of each other. Each had a long history: Bethel, where God met Jacob (Gen 28); Gilgal, Israel's first camp in the land (Josh 4–5); and Mizpah, the place of this assembly. Samuel was a traveling judge, bringing justice to the people rather than making them come to him.
- **"He would return to Ramah… and he built there an altar to the LORD" (v17).** Samuel ends where his story began, in his parents' hometown (1:1, 19). With no central sanctuary, he built an altar there for the worship of the LORD, as the patriarchs did.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Samuel first tried "to separate between them and their idols, for there reformation must begin," and that "he made intercession with a sacrifice." JFB thinks the water ceremony "seems to have been a symbolical act," and that at Mizpah Samuel "entered on the duties of a civil magistrate." He describes Ebenezer as "a huge stone pillar" with its name "thought to have been written on the face of it," and says the victory "secured peace and independence to Israel for twenty years," though the text gives no such figure. Guzik ties the water to Lamentations 2:19, sees the nursing lamb as a picture of the Lamb of God (John 1:29), and stresses that the thunder at Mizpah was the very victory Israel had wanted from the ark in chapter 4. They got it when they trusted God instead of the box. He also reads "till now" as looking both back and forward: past help is a pledge of future help. JFB passes over vv14–17 without comment.

#### Pointing to Christ
Eli asked, "If someone sins against the LORD, who can intercede for him?" (2:25). Chapter 7 gives a first answer: a man who stood between a guilty people and a holy God, offered a lamb as a whole burnt offering, and prayed until God answered. Samuel was a good intercessor, but he was a sinner himself, and he eventually died. The full answer is Jesus. He is both the priest who prays and the Lamb who is offered. "He always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25), and "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7). Israel's victory came not from the ark, their weapons or their strength, but from God's thunder while the sacrifice was burning. The church's victory likewise rests on a sacrifice already offered. Every believer can look back on the cross and the help God has given, set up a stone, and say, "Till now the LORD has helped us."
