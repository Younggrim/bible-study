*Setting:* About 913–911 BC. Rehoboam has died after seventeen years, and his son Abijah, the favorite whom he chose over older brothers (11:20–22), takes the throne. The border war that never stopped under Rehoboam (12:15) flares into a pitched battle. Kings gives Abijah eight verses and a harsh verdict: "he walked in all the sins that his father did before him, and his heart was not wholly true to the LORD" (1 Kgs 15:1–8). Chronicles passes no overall verdict and instead tells, at length, the one day of his reign that mattered most: a battle where Judah, outnumbered two to one and surrounded, cried to the LORD and won. The heart of the chapter is Abijah's speech on a mountainside, the Chronicler's clearest statement of what made Judah, not the north, the true heir of Israel.

#### Abijah Reigns in Judah (vv1–22)
- **"In the eighteenth year of King Jeroboam, Abijah began to reign" (v1).** This is the only place in Chronicles where a king of Judah is dated by a king of Israel. The Chronicler normally ignores northern kings, but this chapter is about the two kingdoms facing each other.
- **Abijah or Abijam?** Kings calls him Abijam (1 Kgs 15:1). Abijah means "my father is Yah," that is, the LORD. Some scholars think Abijam means "father of the sea" or even contains the name of Yam, a Canaanite sea god, and that Kings preserves a less flattering form. Others think it is simply a spelling variant. The text does not explain the difference.
- **"He reigned for three years in Jerusalem" (v2).** Probably parts of three years, about 913–911 or 911–908 BC by different reckonings.
- **"Micaiah the daughter of Uriel of Gibeah" (v2).** In 11:20 and 1 Kings 15:2 his mother is "Maacah the daughter of Absalom." Micaiah is probably a variant of Maacah, and "daughter of Absalom" means granddaughter: her mother Tamar was Absalom's daughter, and her father was Uriel. Gibeah was Saul's hometown in Benjamin, and JFB suggests the name "implies that Uriel was connected with the house of Saul."
- **"400,000 chosen men… 800,000 chosen mighty warriors" (v3).** The numbers, and the 500,000 dead in v17, are very large. They are close to the totals in David's census (2 Sam 24:9). Faithful interpreters handle this three ways. Some take them as written, since the whole fighting manpower of each kingdom was called up. Some think the Hebrew *'eleph*, "thousand," here means a military unit or clan group, which would shrink the totals greatly. Some suspect copying errors in numbers, which were easily miscopied. On any reading the point is the same: Judah was outnumbered two to one.
- **"Abijah stood up on Mount Zemaraim that is in the hill country of Ephraim" (v4).** Zemaraim was a town on Benjamin's northern border near Bethel (Josh 18:22), in the disputed zone between the kingdoms. Like Jotham shouting his parable from Mount Gerizim (Judg 9:7), Abijah speaks from a height to the army below. His speech is the longest in Chronicles by a king of Judah.
- **"Hear me, O Jeroboam and all Israel!" (v4).** He speaks to the northern soldiers as Israelites. The goal is not only victory but to call them home.
- **"The LORD God of Israel gave the kingship over Israel forever to David and his sons" (v5).** Abijah's first argument: the north has rejected God's chosen king. The promise to David was forever (2 Sam 7:12–16; 1 Chr 17:11–14). For the Chronicler, the kingdom of Israel is really "the kingdom of the LORD" entrusted to David's line (v8; 1 Chr 28:5; 29:23).
- **"By a covenant of salt" (v5).** Salt was added to every grain offering as "the salt of the covenant with your God" (Lev 2:13), and God gave the priests their portion by "a covenant of salt forever" (Num 18:19). In the ancient Near East, eating salt together sealed friendship and loyalty. Salt does not decay and preserves what it touches, so a "covenant of salt" means an unbreakable, permanent covenant. The phrase appears only these three times in the Bible.
- **"Jeroboam… a servant of Solomon… rose up and rebelled against his lord" (v6).** True as far as it goes (1 Kgs 11:26–27). Abijah leaves out the other half: God himself, through Ahijah, gave Jeroboam the ten tribes because of Solomon's idolatry (1 Kgs 11:29–39), and the Chronicler himself said the split was "from God" (10:15; 11:4).
- **"Certain worthless scoundrels gathered about him" (v7).** Literally "empty men, sons of *belial*." *Beliya'al* means worthlessness or good-for-nothingness, and later became a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15). Abijah blames the split on troublemakers.
- **"When Rehoboam was young and irresolute" (v7).** "Young" here means inexperienced, since Rehoboam was forty-one (12:13), and "irresolute" is literally "soft of heart," timid. It is a generous way for a son to describe the father who answered his people harshly (10:13–14). Abijah's speech is partly propaganda. His history is selective, and the Chronicler lets that show.
- **"You think to withstand the kingdom of the LORD in the hand of the sons of David, because you are a great multitude and have with you the golden calves" (v8).** Abijah's second argument begins with mockery. Jeroboam trusts numbers and idols. Those calves were set up at Bethel and Dan with the words "behold your gods" (1 Kgs 12:28).
- **"Whoever comes for ordination with a young bull or seven rams becomes a priest of what are not gods" (v9).** "Ordination" is literally "to fill his hand," the Hebrew idiom for consecrating a priest (Exod 29:9; Judg 17:5, 12). In the north, anyone who could pay could become a priest (1 Kgs 13:33). The offering echoes, but cheapens, the seven-day ordination of Aaron (Exod 29:1, 35). And the gods they serve are "not gods" at all (Jer 2:11; 5:7).
- **"But as for us, the LORD is our God, and we have not forsaken him" (v10).** The contrast is sharp: "you have forsaken him" (v11), "we have not." The Chronicler's key word *'azab*, "forsake," again.
- **The temple's daily worship (v11).** Abijah lists exactly what the law required: burnt offerings every morning and evening (Exod 29:38–42), incense (Exod 30:7–8), the showbread (Lev 24:5–9) and the lampstand kept burning (Exod 27:20–21). Ordinary, daily, unspectacular faithfulness. "We keep the charge of the LORD our God."
- **Is Abijah a hypocrite?** Kings says his heart was not true to God (1 Kgs 15:3), and the next chapter shows that Asa had to tear down high places and foreign altars (14:3), so Judah's worship was not as pure as Abijah claims. But his point is still true: Jerusalem had the right priests, the right altar and the right king. The Chronicler is not praising Abijah's private life. He is recording a true confession spoken at a decisive moment, and showing God honoring it.
- **"God is with us at our head, and his priests with their battle trumpets" (v12).** The law commanded priests to blow silver trumpets in battle so that "you may be remembered before the LORD your God, and you shall be saved from your enemies" (Num 10:8–9). Judah is going to war as God's people, with God in front.
- **"O sons of Israel, do not fight against the LORD, the God of your fathers, for you cannot succeed" (v12).** The speech ends with a plea, not a threat. Gamaliel will say much the same about the apostles: if this is of God, "you might even be found opposing God!" (Acts 5:39).
- **"Jeroboam had sent an ambush around" (v13).** While Abijah preached, Jeroboam maneuvered. Half his larger army slipped around behind Judah. **"The battle was in front of and behind them" (v14).**
- **"They cried to the LORD, and the priests blew the trumpets" (v14).** Exactly what Numbers 10:9 commanded. Surrounded, Judah does the one thing left.
- **"The men of Judah raised the battle shout. And when the men of Judah shouted, God defeated Jeroboam" (v15).** The battle shout (*teru'ah*) recalls the walls of Jericho (Josh 6:20). The text does not say how God struck the northern army, by panic, confusion or some other means. It simply says God did it. The Chronicler keeps naming God as the actor: "God defeated… God gave them into their hand" (vv15–16).
- **"Because they relied on the LORD, the God of their fathers" (v18).** This is the explanation for everything. "Relied" (*sha'an*) means to lean on, to put your weight on something. It becomes a key word in the next three chapters: Asa will rely on the LORD against a huge army (14:11) and later rely on the king of Syria instead, and be rebuked for it (16:7–8).
- **"Bethel with its villages and Jeshanah… and Ephron" (v19).** Abijah took the border towns around Bethel, the site of one of Jeroboam's golden calves, about 12 miles north of Jerusalem. The irony is plain: the shrine of the calves fell to the king who mocked them. The text does not say what Abijah did with the shrine. Bethel was back in Israel's hands later and was still a royal shrine in Amos's day (Amos 7:13).
- **"The LORD struck him down, and he died" (v20).** Jeroboam actually outlived Abijah by a year or two, since Asa began to reign in Jeroboam's twentieth year (1 Kgs 15:9) and Jeroboam reigned twenty-two years (1 Kgs 14:20). The Chronicler tells his death here to close the story of the conflict. "The LORD struck him" probably means a disease or sudden death from God, not death in battle.
- **"Abijah grew mighty. And he took fourteen wives and had twenty-two sons and sixteen daughters" (v21).** A large family is a sign of blessing in Chronicles, though like his father's harem it breaks Deuteronomy 17:17. These figures likely cover his whole life, not just three years as king.
- **"The story of the prophet Iddo" (v22).** "Story" is *midrash*, its first appearance in the Bible. Here it probably means a written study or account. Later the word became the name for Jewish commentary on Scripture. Iddo's works, cited also for Solomon and Rehoboam (9:29; 12:15), are lost.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry writes, "The battle is not always to the strong nor the cause to the majority," and praises Abijah for speaking before fighting: "It is good to try reason before we use force." On Abijah's mixed character he adds, "Many that have little religion themselves yet have so much sense and grace as to value it in others." Guzik, through G. Campbell Morgan, calls the speech a strange mix of misrepresentation and religion, and notes that Jeroboam set his ambush during the talks. He quotes F. B. Meyer that enemies may surround us on every side, but no one can shut off the way up to God. Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's suggestion that each number has an extra zero, and Poole's view that the numbers stand and the slaughter shows God fought against Israel. JFB thinks the armies were swollen by camp followers, since "Oriental armies are mere mobs," and says Abijah wanted Bethel to "wrest from profanation" a place of "sacred associations." JFB also notes Jeroboam "lived, indeed, two years after the death of Abijah."

#### Pointing to Christ
Abijah staked everything on two facts: God had promised the throne to David's son forever, and God had given one true way to approach him through his appointed priests. Both promises reach their goal in Jesus, the Son of David whose kingdom has no end (Luke 1:32–33) and the great High Priest who serves in the true sanctuary (Heb 8:1–2). Judah was surrounded front and back and won only because they cried out and relied on the LORD. The same is true for anyone who comes to Christ. "Do not fight against the LORD… for you cannot succeed" (v12) is not only a warning. It is an invitation to come over to the side of God's King.
