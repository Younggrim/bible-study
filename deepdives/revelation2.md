*Setting:* John is still on Patmos, probably about AD 95. The risen Jesus, who walks among the lampstands (1:13), now dictates seven short letters, four in this chapter and three in the next. They go to real churches along a circular road in the Roman province of Asia (western Turkey), in the order a messenger would travel: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, then Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea. These churches face emperor worship, trade guilds with pagan feasts, hostile neighbours and their own drift. Each letter follows a pattern:
- a greeting that picks up a feature of Christ from the vision in chapter 1;
- "I know," followed by praise (usually);
- a rebuke and a call to repent (usually);
- "He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches";
- a promise "to the one who conquers," which looks ahead to the new creation in chapters 21–22.

The four reading approaches meet the letters differently:
- **Preterist and idealist readers** take them as letters to first-century churches whose conditions recur in every age.
- **Historicist readers** often see seven ages of church history, from the apostolic church (Ephesus) to a lukewarm last age (Laodicea).
- **Futurist readers** take the letters as real first-century letters. Some dispensationalists also see the church ages in them.

Almost everyone agrees on the first two points: these were real churches, and "the churches" (plural) means every church listens in.

#### To the Church in Ephesus (vv1–7)
- **"Him who holds the seven stars in his right hand, who walks among the seven golden lampstands" (v1).** "Holds" is a firm grip. The churches belong to Christ, and he is not absent from them. He *walks* among them, inspecting and caring. Ephesus was the leading city of the province, a great port and banking centre, and home of the vast temple of Artemis (Acts 19). Paul spent about three years there. Timothy later led the church, and tradition says John himself lived there in old age.
- **"I know your works, your toil and your patient endurance" (v2).** "I know" opens every letter. Jesus sees the good before he names the problem. "Patient endurance" is *hypomonē*, staying under a load without giving up. "Toil" is labour to the point of exhaustion.
- **"Have tested those who call themselves apostles and are not" (v2).** Paul had warned the Ephesian elders that "fierce wolves" would come (Acts 20:29–30), and Timothy had fought false teachers there (1 Tim 1:3). The church listened. It checked teachers' claims against the apostles' teaching and exposed the false ones.
- **"You have abandoned the love you had at first" (v4).** This is the heart of the letter. The church is orthodox, hardworking and brave, yet the warmth has gone. The text doesn't say whether the lost love is love for Christ, for one another, or both. Most take it as both, since in John's writings they can't be pulled apart (1 John 4:20–21). Paul's letter to Ephesus once praised their "love toward all the saints" (Eph 1:15). A church can win the battle for truth and lose its heart along the way.
- **"Remember… repent, and do the works you did at first" (v5).** There are three steps: *remember* where you were, *repent* (turn around), and *return* to the first works. Love is restored by doing again what love once did.
- **"I will come to you and remove your lampstand" (v5).** The threat is not loss of salvation for individuals. It is that the church would stop being a church, a light that has gone out. This "coming" is a visit in judgment now, not the Second Coming. The site of Ephesus is abandoned today. Its harbour silted up and its church faded over the centuries, though the letter's warning is about faithfulness, not geography.
- **"You hate the works of the Nicolaitans, which I also hate" (v6).** Little is known about them. Irenaeus (about AD 180) linked them to Nicolas, one of the seven chosen in Acts 6:5, but that is tradition, not certainty. From 2:14–15 they seem to have allowed eating at idol feasts and sexual immorality. Jesus hates their *works*, not the people.
- **"To the one who conquers I will grant to eat of the tree of life… in the paradise of God" (v7).** "Conquers" is *nikaō*, to win a victory. In Revelation, believers conquer the way the Lamb did, by faithfulness even to death (12:11). The tree of life was guarded after the fall (Gen 3:22–24). It reappears by the river of the new Jerusalem (22:2). "Paradise" is a Persian loanword for a walled garden.
- **Commentators:** All three agree the sin is cooled love inside a sound church. JFB puts it sharply: "Their warmth of love had given place to a lifeless orthodoxy." Henry notes that "the first affections of men towards Christ… are usually lively and warm," so cooling is a real decline and not just maturity. Guzik says leaving first love is a choice, not an accident. He says the first works to return to are things like prayer and time in the Word. On v6, JFB reminds readers: "We should hate men's evil deeds, not hate the men themselves." Guzik and JFB both pass on the idea that "Nicolaitan" comes from Greek words for "conquer the people." JFB even calls it a Greek form of "Balaam." The lexicon gives only "a Nicolaitan," with no such meaning, so treat that as a suggestion and not a settled fact.

#### To the Church in Smyrna (vv8–11)
- **"The first and the last, who died and came to life" (v8).** This picks up 1:17–18 and God's title in Isaiah 44:6. Smyrna, a beautiful port north of Ephesus, had been destroyed and rebuilt, a city that "died and came to life." It still exists as Izmir. It was proudly loyal to Rome and built one of the first temples to the goddess Roma (195 BC), which made emperor worship a constant pressure.
- **"I know your tribulation and your poverty (but you are rich)" (v9).** "Poverty" here is *ptōcheia*, real destitution, not just being short of money. Christians who wouldn't join guild feasts or honour Caesar could lose work and property (Heb 10:34). Jesus calls them rich (Matt 6:20; Jas 2:5). Laodicea is the reverse: rich in its own eyes and poor in his (3:17).
- **"Those who say that they are Jews and are not, but are a synagogue of Satan" (v9).** "Slander" is *blasphēmia*. Some members of the local synagogue were denouncing Christians to the Roman authorities. Judaism had legal standing, and Christians who were pushed out of the synagogue lost that cover. John, himself a Jew, is not condemning Jewish people as a whole. He is naming the behaviour of one local group that was handing believers over to persecution, the same charge Jesus brought in John 8:44. "Satan" means "accuser," and that is what they were doing.
- **"Do not fear… the devil is about to throw some of you into prison… for ten days you will have tribulation" (v10).** Prison in Rome's world was usually a holding cell before trial or execution, not a sentence in itself. Behind the human accusers stands the devil, yet the time is limited. "Ten days" most likely means a short, set period (Dan 1:12–15). Some historicists once read it as ten imperial persecutions or ten years under Diocletian, but most interpreters today, futurist and otherwise, take it as a short, limited time.
- **"Be faithful unto death, and I will give you the crown of life" (v10).** The crown is *stephanos*, the victor's wreath at the games, not a king's diadem. Smyrna was known for its games, and for a "crown" of fine public buildings around its hill. About sixty years later, Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna and by tradition a disciple of John, was burned alive in its stadium. According to the earliest account of his death, he said he had served Christ eighty-six years and could not blaspheme his King.
- **"Will not be hurt by the second death" (v11).** The second death is the lake of fire, the final judgment (20:14; 21:8). Those who might face the first death for Jesus are safe from the second. Smyrna and Philadelphia are the only two churches with no rebuke.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the suffering is "but for a set time, and a short time: Ten days". JFB adds that "the time of trial shall be short, the duration of your joy shall be for ever." JFB also explicitly rejects reading the ten days as ten Roman persecutions. Guzik calls Smyrna a poor church that is rich, the mirror image of Laodicea. He tells the story of Polycarp's martyrdom as the letter lived out. He also notes that Smyrna's name comes from myrrh, the embalming spice, and that the church gave off its fragrance by being crushed.

#### To the Church in Pergamum (vv12–17)
- **"Him who has the sharp two-edged sword" (v12).** Pergamum was the old capital of the province. The Roman governor there held the "right of the sword," the power of execution. Jesus reminds the church who really holds that power: his word judges (1:16; Heb 4:12). The city also had a great library, and "parchment" (*pergamēnē*) is named after it.
- **"Where Satan's throne is" (v13).** Several things may lie behind this:
  - the huge altar of Zeus on the acropolis, shaped like a throne;
  - the temple of Asclepius, the healing god whose symbol was a serpent;
  - Pergamum's role as the first city in Asia with a temple to a living emperor (Augustus, 29 BC).

  Emperor worship is probably the main point, since that is what got Christians killed.
- **"Antipas my faithful witness, who was killed among you" (v13).** Jesus gives Antipas his own title from 1:5, "faithful witness" (*martys*). This word later came to mean "martyr" because so many witnesses died. Nothing else is known about Antipas. Later tradition says he was roasted inside a bronze bull, but that comes from much later.
- **"The teaching of Balaam, who taught Balak to put a stumbling block before the sons of Israel" (v14).** Balaam couldn't curse Israel (Num 22–24), so he advised Balak to seduce Israel into idol feasts and immorality at Peor instead (Num 25:1–3; 31:16). What a curse couldn't do, compromise did. In Pergamum the pressure came through trade guilds and civic festivals, where a meal in a temple dining room often came with worship and sometimes with prostitution. Paul handles the same issue in 1 Corinthians 8–10.
- **"So also you have some who hold the teaching of the Nicolaitans" (v15).** "So also" probably links the two: the Nicolaitans were a Balaam-like group. Ephesus hated their works. Pergamum tolerated them.
- **"Therefore repent. If not, I will come to you soon and war against them with the sword of my mouth" (v16).** The whole church must repent of tolerating the error, but the sword is turned against the false teachers ("them"). Balaam himself died by the sword (Num 31:8).
- **"Some of the hidden manna… a white stone, with a new name written on the stone" (v17).** Those who refuse idol meat will eat heaven's food. A jar of manna was kept in the ark (Exod 16:33). Jewish tradition held that the ark was hidden before the Babylonian exile and that manna would return in the Messiah's day. Jesus calls himself the true bread from heaven (John 6:32–35). The white stone is uncertain. Several customs are possible:
  - Jurors voted "not guilty" with a white stone.
  - Victors and guests received a token for admission to a feast.
  - Stones were worn as amulets with secret names.

  All point to acceptance and belonging. The new name, known only to its owner, speaks of a personal relationship with Christ (Isa 62:2).
- **Commentators:** Henry traces a downward path: "Corrupt doctrines and a corrupt worship often lead to a corrupt conversation" (in his day "conversation" meant way of life). He reads the white stone as "absolution from the guilt of sin" and the hidden manna as the Spirit's comfort in communion with Christ. JFB sets the manna "in contrast to the idol-meats" and notes that the whole church, "not only the Nicolaitanes," is told to repent. JFB also reads the white stone as a diamond, which few follow. Guzik stresses that a hard place to live is never an excuse for compromise. He lists the possible meanings of the white stone and says the new name matters more: it speaks of closeness with Jesus.

#### To the Church in Thyatira (vv18–29)
- **"The Son of God, who has eyes like a flame of fire, and whose feet are like burnished bronze" (v18).** This is the only use of "Son of God" in Revelation, and it fits. Thyatira's guardian god was Apollo, honoured as a son of Zeus, and coins showed the emperor as son of a god. Jesus is the true Son. The bronze feet may glance at the city's metalworkers. Thyatira, the smallest of the seven cities, was a garrison and trade town full of guilds (wool, dyeing, leather, bronze). Lydia, the seller of purple cloth, came from here (Acts 16:14).
- **"Your love and faith and service and patient endurance… your latter works exceed the first" (v19).** This is the opposite of Ephesus. Thyatira is growing in love and service, but it is weak on truth. Each church has the strength the other lacks.
- **"You tolerate that woman Jezebel, who calls herself a prophetess" (v20).** Queen Jezebel brought Baal worship into Israel and led Ahab into it (1 Kgs 16:31; 21:25). Most take "Jezebel" as a code name for a real woman teaching in the church. Her message was probably that believers could join guild feasts and their immorality without harm, perhaps even that they should experience such things. Some manuscripts read "your wife Jezebel." A few take that as the leader's wife, but most translations, the ESV included, follow the manuscripts without "your."
- **"I gave her time to repent, but she refuses" (v21).** God's patience comes before his judgment (2 Pet 3:9), but it doesn't last forever.
- **"I will throw her onto a sickbed… and I will strike her children dead" (vv22–23).** "Sickbed" is *klinē*, a bed or couch. The same word was used for the couches people lay on at banquets. The bed of immorality and the guild feast becomes a bed of sickness. "Her children" probably means her committed followers, not literal children. Even for them the door is still open: "unless they repent" (v22).
- **"I am he who searches mind and heart" (v23).** In the Greek this is "kidneys and hearts," the seat of feelings and will. It echoes God's claim in Jeremiah 17:10, now made by Jesus. "All the churches will know," so Thyatira is a lesson for everyone.
- **"What some call the deep things of Satan" (v24).** The false teachers probably boasted of "deep things," secret knowledge. Jesus calls them by their real name: Satan's depths, not God's (1 Cor 2:10). Some link this to later Gnostic groups, who prized secret knowledge. Others think the teachers claimed believers could enter Satan's world unharmed.
- **"I do not lay on you any other burden. Only hold fast what you have until I come" (vv24–25).** This echoes the Jerusalem council (Acts 15:28–29), which asked Gentile believers to avoid idol food and immorality, "no greater burden." Faithful believers don't need new rules, only steadiness.
- **"Authority over the nations… rule them with a rod of iron" (vv26–27).** This quotes Psalm 2:8–9, the Messiah's coronation psalm. "Rule" is *poimainō*, to shepherd, which the lexicon confirms. The iron rod is the shepherd's club that protects the flock and smashes what threatens it. Jesus shares with his people the authority he received from the Father (3:21; 20:4).
- **"I will give him the morning star" (v28).** The morning star (Venus) shines just before dawn. In 22:16 Jesus says, "I am… the bright morning star." The final gift is not just authority but Christ himself.
- **Commentators:** Henry notices that "their last works were better than the first". JFB sees the woman in the church as the old queen at Ahab's side: "The symbolical Jezebel was to the Church of Thyatira what Jezebel, Ahab's 'wife,' was to him." He adds a warning: "The deeper the Church penetrated into heathenism, the more she herself became heathenish". JFB notes that searching the heart is "God's peculiar attribute… given to Christ." Henry ("Christ is the morning-star") and JFB both take v28 as the gift of Christ himself. Guzik agrees and calls it better than the authority promised before it. He suggests "Jezebel" worked through the trade-guild feasts. He reads "sickbed" as a play on banquet couches, which the lexicon supports.

#### Pointing to Christ
Each letter starts with Jesus and ends with Jesus. He is the one who holds and walks among the churches. He died and came to life, he holds the sword, and his eyes burn like fire. He is the tree of life, the hidden manna and the morning star. He sees busy, orthodox churches that have lost their love, poor churches that are rich, brave churches that tolerate compromise, and loving churches that tolerate error. He tells each one the truth. Every rebuke comes with a way back, and every promise to "the one who conquers" depends on the Lamb who conquered first.
