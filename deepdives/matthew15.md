*Setting:* Galilee and beyond, around spring and summer of AD 29. Chapter 14 ended with Jesus healing crowds in Gennesaret, where people needed only to touch the tassel of his cloak. Now a delegation of religious experts arrives from Jerusalem, and the question is not about healing but about purity: who is clean and who is unclean. Jesus answers that real uncleanness comes from the heart, not from unwashed hands. Then, as if to show it, he travels into Gentile territory and praises the faith of a Canaanite woman, the kind of person strict Jews considered unclean. The chapter ends with a second feeding miracle, probably among Gentiles on the far side of the lake. The movement of the chapter is outward, from Jerusalem's rules to bread for the nations.

#### Traditions and Commandments (vv1–9)
- **"Pharisees and scribes came to Jesus from Jerusalem" (v1).** Jerusalem was the center of religious authority. This was an official delegation, probably sent to investigate the Galilean teacher everyone was talking about.
- **"The tradition of the elders" (v2).** Over the centuries Jewish teachers had built up a large body of oral teaching to explain how to apply the written law of Moses to daily life. The Pharisees held it to be authoritative, and many believed it had come down from Moses alongside the written law. It was later written down in the Mishnah (about AD 200), which has a whole section on handwashing.
- **"They do not wash their hands when they eat" (v2).** This was not about hygiene. The law required priests to wash before serving (Exod 30:17–21). The Pharisees extended that purity to ordinary meals in ordinary homes, washing hands in a set way to remove any uncleanness picked up during the day. Mark explains the custom for his Gentile readers (Mark 7:3–4).
- **"Why do you break the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition?" (v3).** Jesus does not answer the question. He turns it around. They accuse the disciples of breaking tradition. He accuses them of breaking God's law.
- **"Honor your father and your mother" (v4).** Jesus quotes the fifth commandment (Exod 20:12) and its penalty (Exod 21:17). Honoring parents included supporting them financially in old age (1 Tim 5:4, 8).
- **"What you would have gained from me is given to God" (v5).** Mark gives the Hebrew word: *Corban*, "an offering" (Mark 7:11). A son could declare his property dedicated to God, which put it off limits to his parents. Often he could still use it himself, and the money might never reach the temple at all. Some teachers held that such a vow could not be undone even if it left parents in need. A religious loophole was used to dodge a plain command.
- **"For the sake of your tradition you have made void the word of God" (v6).** "Made void" means to cancel or annul, as a court sets aside a contract. Tradition is not wrong in itself, and Paul tells churches to hold to the traditions the apostles taught (2 Thess 2:15). The problem comes when human rules are placed on a level with God's word, and then above it.
- **"This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (v8).** Jesus quotes Isaiah 29:13. Isaiah spoke to Jerusalem in his day, when worship was full of ceremony and empty of love. "Hypocrites" (v7) is the actors' word again (6:2): the outward performance does not match the heart.
- **"Teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" (v9).** The danger is not having rules but treating human rules as if they were God's commands.
- **Commentators:** JFB states the principle fairly: "To dedicate property to God is indeed lawful and laudable, but not at the expense of filial duty." Henry defines hypocrites as those who "only make a lip-labour of religion and religious worship," and adds that those who enforce unlawful rules share the guilt of those who invented them. Guzik stresses that Jesus did not condemn all tradition, only ranked it below God's word.

#### What Defiles a Person (vv10–20)
- **"Hear and understand" (v10).** Jesus turns from the experts to the crowd. What he says next is for everyone.
- **"It is not what goes into the mouth that defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth" (v11).** "Defile" means to make ceremonially unclean, unfit to come before God. This saying goes beyond handwashing to the whole system of food laws. Mark adds a comment: "Thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). The early church took years to grasp it fully (Acts 10:9–16; Rom 14:14). Matthew, writing for Jewish Christians, does not spell that out, but the principle is the same.
- **"The Pharisees were offended" (v12).** The word is *skandalizō*, to stumble. The disciples seem worried. Jesus is not.
- **"Every plant that my heavenly Father has not planted will be rooted up" (v13).** Israel was God's planting (Isa 5:1–7; 60:21). Jesus implies that these leaders and their teaching were not planted by God. Compare the weeds in 13:24–30.
- **"Let them alone; they are blind guides" (v14).** Jesus does not mean "do not argue with them" so much as "do not follow them." The Pharisees saw themselves as "a guide to the blind" (Rom 2:19). A blind guide leads others into the same ditch he falls into.
- **"Are you also still without understanding?" (v16).** Peter asks for an explanation, and Jesus' answer has a note of disappointment. Even the disciples had absorbed the idea that food could make a person unclean before God.
- **"Whatever goes into the mouth passes into the stomach and is expelled" (v17).** Jesus is blunt. Food goes through the body and out. It does not touch the heart.
- **"Out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false witness, slander" (v19).** In the Bible the heart is the center of the whole person: mind, will, desires. The list follows the order of the second half of the Ten Commandments: murder, adultery, theft, false witness. It starts with "evil thoughts," because every sin begins there (James 1:14–15). Jeremiah had said "the heart is deceitful above all things" (Jer 17:9).
- **"But to eat with unwashed hands does not defile anyone" (v20).** The section ends where it began. The real problem is not dirty hands but a dirty heart, and no washing ritual can cleanse that. Only God can create a clean heart (Ps 51:10; Ezek 36:26).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls v14 a "Striking expression of the ruinous effects of erroneous teaching!" and of v19, "How appalling is this black catalogue!" Henry says it is not the washing that Christ condemns, but "the opinion it is built upon," the idea that it is a religious duty. Guzik, following R.T. France, explains "heart" as the whole person as they really are, and sees in these verses the first step toward setting aside the food laws, as in Acts 10.

#### The Faith of a Canaanite Woman (vv21–28)
- **"He withdrew to the district of Tyre and Sidon" (v21).** These were Phoenician cities on the Mediterranean coast, north of Galilee, in what is now Lebanon. It was Gentile territory. Jesus has just said that food and contact do not defile, and now he goes where strict Jews would not. Elijah once went to this region and stayed with a widow of Zarephath, near Sidon (1 Kings 17:8–16; Luke 4:25–26).
- **"A Canaanite woman" (v22).** Mark calls her "a Gentile, a Syrophoenician by birth" (Mark 7:26). Matthew uses the old name, Canaanite, recalling Israel's ancient enemies whom they had been told to drive out. Yet Canaanite women are already in Jesus' family tree: Tamar and Rahab (1:3, 5).
- **"Have mercy on me, O Lord, Son of David" (v22).** A Gentile woman uses the messianic title that the leaders refuse to use. She knows who he is.
- **"But he did not answer her a word" (v23).** This is surprising. Jesus normally responds at once. His silence seems to be a test, drawing out her faith, the way a parent may wait before answering a child's request to see if the child really means it.
- **"Send her away, for she is crying out after us" (v23).** The disciples want her gone. They may mean "give her what she wants so she will leave," or simply "get rid of her."
- **"I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (v24).** This is the same limit Jesus gave the Twelve (10:6). His mission during his earthly ministry was first to Israel, in keeping with God's promises. The Gentiles would come in after the cross and resurrection (28:19).
- **"Lord, help me" (v25).** She kneels in front of him. Her prayer is three words long.
- **"It is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs" (v26).** Jews sometimes called Gentiles "dogs," an insult, since dogs were unclean scavengers. Jesus uses a gentler word, *kynarion*, which the lexicon defines as "little dog" or "puppy." It suggests a house dog under the table rather than a street dog. Some think the diminutive had lost much of its force by this time, so its softening should not be pressed too far. Still, the picture is of a household: children at the table, pets beneath it. Mark adds "Let the children be fed *first*" (Mark 7:27), which leaves room for the dogs later.
- **"Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table" (v27).** Her answer is brilliant and humble. She accepts the picture and turns it into a plea. She does not ask to take the children's place. She asks for crumbs, and she believes a crumb from this table is enough to heal her daughter.
- **"O woman, great is your faith!" (v28).** Only two people in Matthew are praised for great faith, and both are Gentiles: the centurion (8:10) and this woman. Both are healed at a distance, and in both cases a servant or child is healed "at that very moment." Meanwhile the experts from Jerusalem went away offended.
- **Readings of this story.** Some readers find Jesus' words hard. Most interpreters see a deliberate test, like God's testing of Abraham or Jacob's wrestling. Jesus' silence and his reply draw out a faith that would not give up, and Jesus both rewards it and holds it up as an example. The story also shows the way the gospel would move: to Israel first, then to the nations.
- **Commentators:** Henry praises her humility: "A humble, believing soul, that truly loves Christ, takes every thing in good part," and treats the delay as a test that her faith passed. Guzik lists the marks of her great faith: unexpected, tested, persistent, quick-witted and focused on real need. He follows F.F. Bruce in taking the "little dogs" as household pets, which the lexicon's "little dog, puppy" allows. JFB defers to its notes on Mark.

#### Jesus Heals Many (vv29–31)
- **"He went up on the mountain and sat down there" (v29).** Mark says Jesus came back through the Decapolis, the mainly Gentile region on the east and south side of the lake (Mark 7:31). Matthew does not name the place, but the details suggest Gentile crowds.
- **"The lame, the blind, the crippled, the mute" (v30).** "Crippled" (*kyllos*) can mean maimed or deformed. They "put them at his feet," probably because there were so many.
- **"The mute speaking, the crippled healthy, the lame walking, and the blind seeing" (v31).** Again Isaiah 35:5–6 is being fulfilled (11:5).
- **"And they glorified the God of Israel" (v31).** This phrase suggests the crowd were Gentiles, praising Israel's God as the one at work. Gentiles are getting more than crumbs.
- **Commentators:** Guzik places the scene in the Decapolis and notes the "God of Israel" point. He cites Bruce's suggestion that "crippled" may mean lost limbs, which would mean Jesus restored limbs. The word can mean maimed, so it is possible, though the text does not say so directly. JFB defers to Mark.

#### Jesus Feeds the Four Thousand (vv32–39)
- **"I have compassion on the crowd because they have been with me now three days" (v32).** This time Jesus raises the problem himself. The people had stayed three days, and whatever food they had brought was gone.
- **"Where are we to get enough bread in such a desolate place?" (v33).** It is surprising that the disciples ask this so soon after the five thousand. It may show how slowly faith grows, or it may be that they did not expect Jesus to do the same thing among Gentiles. Either way, Jesus will bring it up later (16:9–10).
- **"Seven, and a few small fish" (v34).** He asks what they have, and uses it.
- **"Having given thanks he broke them and gave them to the disciples" (v36).** "Given thanks" (*eucharisteō*), the word behind "Eucharist," is used here, where 14:19 had "said a blessing." Both describe a Jewish blessing before a meal.
- **"Seven baskets full" (v37).** The basket here (*spyris*) is a different word from the one at the five thousand. The lexicon describes it as a flexible mat-basket for carrying provisions, and it could be large: Paul was let down over the wall of Damascus in one (Acts 9:25).
- **"Four thousand men, besides women and children" (v38).** Some have suggested this is a second telling of the same event. But Matthew and Mark both tell both stories, and Jesus himself refers to both, with their different numbers of loaves and baskets (16:9–10; Mark 8:19–20). The differences are many: five loaves and seven, five thousand and four thousand, twelve baskets and seven, one day and three, Jewish crowd and probably Gentile crowd.
- **The meaning of two feedings.** Many see a pattern. The first feeding, with twelve baskets, was for Israel. The second, after the Canaanite woman and in Gentile country, shows the same bread offered to the nations. Some note that seven was a number of completeness and was linked with the nations, though that is an interpretation, not something the text says.
- **"He got into the boat and went to the region of Magadan" (v39).** No one knows for sure where Magadan was. Some manuscripts read "Magdala," the hometown of Mary Magdalene, on the west shore. Mark says "Dalmanutha" (Mark 8:10), also unknown.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Christ told his disciples about his compassion before he acted, and that "They did all eat, and were filled." Guzik lists the many differences between the two feedings and links the second to the messianic banquet that includes Gentiles (8:11). He cites John Trapp's claim that "satisfied" was a word for fattening animals. The verb (*chortazō*) did originally refer to feeding livestock, so the point is fair. JFB defers to Mark.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Pharisees washed their hands, and their hearts stayed unclean. Jesus touched lepers, ate with sinners and walked into Gentile land, and his holiness made others clean. The chapter shows that the real problem is the heart, and that no tradition can fix it. Only the one who gives "a new heart" (Ezek 36:26) can. And the bread from his table, which the Canaanite woman begged a crumb of, turns out to be enough for thousands of Gentiles too. The Son of David came first to the lost sheep of Israel, and through his death the children's bread was given to the whole world.
