*Setting:* About AD 29, in Galilee and across the lake. Chapter 7 ended at a Pharisee's dinner with a forgiven woman and the question "Who is this, who even forgives sins?" (7:49). Chapter 8 opens with Jesus on the road again, now with the Twelve and a group of women who support the work. The first half is about hearing: the parable of the sower, the lamp, and Jesus' true family, "those who hear the word of God and do it." The second half shows the power behind that word in four mighty works: over a storm, over a legion of demons, over twelve years of sickness and over death itself. Fear and faith run through every scene.

#### Women Accompanying Jesus (vv1–3)
- **"Through cities and villages, proclaiming and bringing the good news" (v1).** This is a second tour of Galilee, now with the Twelve.
- **"Some women who had been healed" (v2).** Rabbis did not normally travel with women disciples. Luke names women more than any other Gospel writer, and here they are part of Jesus' traveling company. Each had a story of healing or deliverance behind her.
- **"Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out" (v2).** "Magdalene" means "from Magdala," a fishing town on the lake's west shore north of Tiberias, where a first-century synagogue was excavated in 2009. "Seven" suggests a severe bondage. Nothing in the text says she was a prostitute. That idea came centuries later from wrongly merging her with the woman of 7:37. She stays faithful to the end: at the cross, at the tomb and as the first witness of the risen Lord (24:10; John 20:1–18).
- **"Joanna, the wife of Chuza, Herod's household manager" (v3).** Chuza managed the estates or household of Herod Antipas, the ruler who imprisoned John. So the gospel had reached into Herod's court, and a woman of standing chose to travel with a wandering teacher. Joanna is also at the empty tomb (24:10).
- **"Who provided for them out of their means" (v3).** The Son of God, who fed thousands, lived on gifts from people he had helped. "Provided" is *diakoneō*, "to serve," the root of "deacon." Women were not on the edges of Jesus' ministry. They funded it.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The greatest of sinners must not despair of pardon; and the worse any have been before their conversion the more they should study to do for Christ after." JFB calls Jesus "the Prince of itinerant preachers," marvels that he lived on his people's gifts, and says "it is a great wrong to this honored woman" to identify Mary Magdalene with the sinner of 7:37. Guzik also warns against assuming her past was sexual sin, and the Tyndale notes say there is no basis for it.

#### The Parable of the Sower (vv4–8)
- **"A sower went out to sow his seed" (v5).** Farmers scattered seed by hand as they walked, often before plowing it in. So some seed landed where no one meant it to go.
- **"Along the path… trampled underfoot" (v5).** Footpaths ran through the fields. The soil there was packed hard, and the seed lay on top for birds to take.
- **"On the rock… because it had no moisture" (v6).** Much of Galilee has a limestone shelf a few inches under the surface. Seed there sprouts quickly in the warm, thin soil, but its roots hit rock and it dries out. Luke alone mentions the lack of moisture.
- **"Among thorns" (v7).** Thorn roots stay in the ground after the surface is cleared and grow back faster than the grain, crowding it out.
- **"Yielded a hundredfold" (v8).** Matthew and Mark list thirtyfold, sixtyfold and a hundredfold. Luke gives only the highest.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws from the path that "where the word of God is heard carelessly there is commonly a contempt put upon it too." Guzik prefers calling it the parable of the soils, since the seed and the sower never change. Only the ground differs. JFB refers the reader to its notes on Mark 4 and adds nothing here.

#### The Purpose of the Parables (vv9–10)
- **"To you it has been given to know the secrets of the kingdom of God" (v10).** "Secrets" is *mystēria*, "mysteries": not riddles but truths that can be known only when God reveals them. "Has been given" is a gift. Disciples do not earn understanding by cleverness.
- **"So that 'seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not understand'" (v10).** This is from Isaiah 6:9–10, God's commission to Isaiah to preach to a people who would not listen, a word of judgment. Parables work like a door. Those who come asking, as the disciples do here (v9), are let in. Those who are not interested hear a story and walk away unchanged, and their hardness is confirmed. The New Testament uses this text repeatedly to explain why many rejected Jesus (John 12:40; Acts 28:26–27). Luke's quotation is shorter than Mark's and leaves out the line about turning and being forgiven.
- **Commentators:** Guzik describes parables as doorways that invite the interested to step through, and defines a mystery as truth known only by revelation.

#### The Parable of the Sower Explained (vv11–15)
- **"The seed is the word of God" (v11).** The seed is always good, and the sower always sows. Every difference lies in how the word is heard. In Acts, the church grows as "the word of God increased" (Acts 6:7; 12:24).
- **"Then the devil comes and takes away the word… so that they may not believe and be saved" (v12).** Only Luke adds "so that they may not believe and be saved." The enemy's goal is to stop faith before it starts. Hearing without receiving leaves the word lying on the surface.
- **"Receive it with joy. But these have no root" (v13).** Joy is good, but emotion is not root. Luke says "in time of testing" where Mark has "tribulation or persecution." The word for testing, *peirasmos*, can mean trial or temptation. Under pressure, a faith with no depth falls away.
- **"Choked by the cares and riches and pleasures of life" (v14).** Only Luke includes "pleasures." Cares are the worries of the poor, riches the snare of the wealthy, and pleasures a danger for anyone. None of these is dramatic sin. They simply crowd out the word over time, so "their fruit does not mature."
- **"Hold it fast in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patience" (v15).** "Honest and good" (*kalos kai agathos*) was a Greek phrase for a person of fine character. Here it describes a heart opened by the word. "Hold it fast" means to keep, retain. "Patience" is *hypomonē*, steady endurance under pressure. Fruit takes time. Luke's sower ends with perseverance.
- **Which soil am I?** Interpreters differ on whether the rocky and thorny ground pictures people never truly saved or believers who fall away. Reformed readers usually take the first view, pointing to the fact that only the good soil bears fruit (Matt 7:16–20; 1 John 2:19). Wesleyan and some other readers take the second, as a warning that faith can be lost. Both agree the parable calls hearers to examine themselves and keep the word.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that the devil is always on time when the word is preached, and he reads the lack of moisture as a lack of the Spirit's work. He rejects a fatalistic reading. Hearts can be cultivated by turning from what hardens them. Henry stresses the need for "patience" and perseverance, since fruit does not come at once. JFB again refers to its notes on Mark.

#### A Lamp Under a Jar (vv16–18)
- **"No one after lighting a lamp covers it with a jar" (v16).** A household lamp was a small clay dish of olive oil with a wick, set on a stand or in a wall niche to light the room. The secrets just given to the disciples (v10) are not meant to stay secret. They are a light meant to shine "so that those who enter may see."
- **"Nothing is hidden that will not be made manifest" (v17).** What is now told privately will one day be known by all. This is a promise that the gospel will spread and a warning that every heart will be shown for what it is.
- **"Take care then how you hear" (v18).** Matthew and Mark say "what you hear." Luke stresses "how." The way the word is received decides what happens next.
- **"To the one who has, more will be given" (v18).** This is a rule of spiritual growth. Those who take in the word and hold it receive more. Those who do not lose even "what he thinks that he has," a religious knowledge that was never really theirs.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Ministers and Christians are to be lights in the world, holding forth the word of life." JFB calls v18 "very weighty" and explains the last clause as what a person has "hanging loosely on him, and not appropriated." Guzik makes a similar point, that what someone seems to have can be an illusion, and stresses that receiving truth brings the duty to share it.

#### Jesus' Mother and Brothers (vv19–21)
- **"His mother and his brothers came to him" (v19).** Mark places this scene just after his family tried to "seize him" because people said he was out of his mind (Mark 3:21, 31–35). Luke tells it more gently.
- **"His brothers."** The Gospels name four brothers, James, Joses, Judas and Simon, and mention sisters (Mark 6:3). Most Protestants take them as younger children of Joseph and Mary. Eastern Orthodox tradition holds they were Joseph's children from an earlier marriage. Roman Catholic tradition, following Jerome, holds they were cousins. Both of the latter views protect a belief in Mary's lifelong virginity. The Greek word *adelphos* normally means a full or half brother but can stretch to close relatives.
- **"My mother and my brothers are those who hear the word of God and do it" (v21).** Jesus is not rejecting his family. Mary herself is Luke's model of one who hears and keeps God's word (1:38; 2:19, 51). He is saying that the closest bond with him comes through hearing and doing, and that door is open to anyone. Two of these brothers, James and Jude, later believed and wrote New Testament letters (Acts 1:14; 1 Cor 15:7).
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads "brothers" as literal siblings, arguing against perpetual virginity. The Tyndale notes favor the same view while giving the Orthodox alternative. Henry stresses the honor Christ gives to those who obey the word, calling them his nearest relations.

#### Jesus Calms a Storm (vv22–25)
- **"Let us go across to the other side of the lake" (v22).** Jesus gave them a destination. That promise matters later in the story.
- **"As they sailed he fell asleep" (v23).** This is the only time the Gospels say Jesus slept. He was truly human and truly tired. His sleep in the storm is also the picture of trust from Psalm 4:8: "In peace I will both lie down and sleep."
- **"A windstorm came down on the lake" (v23).** The lake lies about 700 feet below sea level, ringed by hills. Cool air from the heights can pour down the ravines and whip the water into violent waves in minutes. "Came down" fits that exactly. "They were filling with water" is literally "they were being swamped." These were experienced fishermen, and they knew they could die.
- **"Master, Master, we are perishing!" (v24).** The doubled name shows panic.
- **"He awoke and rebuked the wind and the raging waves" (v24).** Jesus "rebukes" the storm the way he rebuked a demon (4:35) and a fever (4:39). In the Old Testament, stilling the sea belongs to God (Ps 89:9; 107:25–30, "he made the storm be still"). Jonah slept in a storm because he was running from God. Jesus sleeps in one as Lord of it.
- **"Where is your faith?" (v25).** Luke's version is gentler than Mark's "Have you still no faith?" They did have faith, enough to wake him. But they had forgotten his word: they were going to the other side.
- **"Who then is this, that he commands even winds and water, and they obey him?" (v25).** Their fear moves from the storm to the one in the boat.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Many that have true faith have it to seek when they have occasion to use it." Guzik notes that the disciples were in the storm because they obeyed, unlike Jonah, and suggests Jesus' question pointed to his promise to reach the other side. JFB notes only that "filling" means the boat was taking on water.

#### Jesus Heals a Man with a Demon (vv26–39)
- **"The country of the Gerasenes, which is opposite Galilee" (v26).** This is the east shore, Gentile land in the Decapolis, the "Ten Cities" with Greek culture. Manuscripts differ among "Gerasenes," "Gadarenes" and "Gergesenes," and the Gospels vary too. Gerasa was some 30 miles from the lake and Gadara about 6, so the names probably describe the wider district. Many place the scene near modern Kursi, where steep slopes come down close to the water.
- **"For a long time he had worn no clothes… not lived in a house but among the tombs" (v27).** Tombs were cut into the hillsides. To a Jew they were unclean, the haunt of the dead. Naked, homeless, among the dead, he was as cut off as a person could be.
- **"What have you to do with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God?" (v28).** The demons know exactly who Jesus is. "Most High God" was a title Gentiles often used for Israel's God (Gen 14:18–20; Dan 3:26). Their knowledge does not save them (James 2:19).
- **"Bound with chains and shackles, but he would break the bonds" (v29).** People had tried to control him. No one could. Only Jesus sets him free.
- **"What is your name?… 'Legion'" (v30).** A Roman legion held up to 6,000 soldiers. The name may be a boast meant to intimidate, or it may express how many were tormenting him.
- **"Not to command them to depart into the abyss" (v31).** The abyss (*abyssos*) is the prison of evil spirits (Rev 9:1–2; 20:1–3). They know a day of judgment is coming (Matt 8:29, "before the time").
- **"A large herd of pigs… rushed down the steep bank into the lake and drowned" (vv32–33).** Pigs were unclean under the law, which marks this as Gentile territory. Why did Jesus allow it? The text does not say. Many see a visible proof that the demons had really left, and a picture of what they intended for the man: destruction. A man is worth more than a herd of pigs.
- **"Sitting at the feet of Jesus, clothed and in his right mind" (v35).** Every detail reverses verse 27. "At the feet" is the place of a disciple (10:39). "In his right mind" is *sōphroneō*, to be of sound mind, self-controlled.
- **"They were afraid… asked him to depart" (vv35, 37).** They were not afraid of the madman, whom they had lived with for years. They were afraid of the one who set him free. They also counted the cost of the lost pigs. They choose their old life over the power of God among them.
- **"Return to your home, and declare how much God has done for you" (v39).** He begs to go with Jesus. Jesus says no, and makes him the first missionary to the Decapolis. Notice the wording: Jesus says "how much God has done," and the man tells "how much Jesus had done." Luke lets the two stand side by side.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those lose their Saviour, and their hopes in him, that love their swine better," and on v35, "If God has possession of us, he preserves to us the government and enjoyment of ourselves." Guzik reads "Legion" as an attempt to intimidate Jesus, notes that the demons' correct theology is not saving faith, and suggests that sending the man home taught him to trust Jesus without his physical presence. JFB only refers to Matthew and Mark.

#### Jesus Heals a Woman and Jairus's Daughter (vv40–56)
- **"The crowd welcomed him, for they were all waiting for him" (v40).** One shore sent him away. This one welcomes him.
- **"A man named Jairus, who was a ruler of the synagogue" (v41).** A synagogue ruler was a respected lay leader who arranged services and looked after the building. For him to fall at Jesus' feet in public cost him something, since some synagogue leaders were already opposed to Jesus.
- **"An only daughter, about twelve years of age" (v42).** At twelve a Jewish girl was on the edge of womanhood, nearly old enough to be betrothed. Luke links this story to the next with the number twelve: a girl who had lived twelve years, and a woman who had suffered twelve years.
- **"A discharge of blood for twelve years" (v43).** Probably a chronic menstrual bleeding. Under Leviticus 15:25–30 it made her ritually unclean, and anyone she touched was unclean too. For twelve years she had been excluded from worship and kept at arm's length by everyone around her.
- **"Though she had spent all her living on physicians" (v43).** Some important early manuscripts lack this phrase, and the ESV footnotes it. Mark 5:26 says bluntly that she "was no better but rather grew worse." If Luke wrote it, the doctor tells the story more kindly to his profession.
- **"Touched the fringe of his garment" (v44).** The "fringe" is probably one of the tassels (*kraspedon*) that the law told Israelite men to wear on the corners of their cloak as a reminder of God's commands (Num 15:38–40). Her touch should have made him unclean. Instead his cleanness flows into her.
- **"Who was it that touched me?" (v45).** Peter thinks it a strange question, with crowds crushing him on every side.
- **"Power has gone out from me" (v46).** This healing was not a magic trick drawn from his clothing. Jesus knew, and he willed it.
- **"She came trembling… declared in the presence of all the people" (v47).** Why make her speak? She would have gone away healed but hiding, perhaps thinking she had stolen a cure. Now her healing is public, so her community knows she is clean. And she meets Jesus face to face.
- **"Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace" (v48).** This is the only time in the Gospels Jesus calls someone "daughter." "Made you well" is *sōzō*, the same word as "saved" in 7:50. It covers both the body's healing and the soul's rescue.
- **"Your daughter is dead; do not trouble the Teacher any more" (v49).** While Jesus stopped for one woman, another life ran out. The delay that healed one seemed to cost the other.
- **"Do not fear; only believe, and she will be well" (v50).** "Only believe" is the shortest sermon in Luke. Fear and faith are set against each other, as in the storm.
- **"Peter and John and James, and the father and mother" (v51).** These three form an inner circle, present again at the Transfiguration (9:28) and in Gethsemane (Mark 14:33).
- **"She is not dead but sleeping… they laughed at him" (vv52–53).** Jesus does not deny she died. Luke says her spirit returned (v55). He means that for him, death is like sleep, something from which he wakes people. The New Testament often speaks of believers' death as sleep (John 11:11–14; 1 Thess 4:13–14). The mourners, likely including professional wailers, laugh.
- **"Taking her by the hand he called, saying, 'Child, arise'" (v54).** Jesus touches a corpse, which by law should defile him, and gives life instead.
- **"He directed that something should be given her to eat" (v55).** She is truly alive and hungry.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that among the throng, the question is "rather ask who touched Thee not." It says "the voluntary, living contact of faith" is what alone draws healing from him, and that making her speak was a "great trial to the shrinking modesty of the believing woman," yet he meant it for her good. Henry: "There may be trembling where yet there is saving faith," and on v50, "our faith in Christ should be bold and daring." On v40 JFB adds that "the tide of His popularity was now fast rising." Guzik contrasts the crowd's casual contact with a touch of faith, and lists reasons Jesus called her forward, including assurance for her and encouragement for Jairus as he waited.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 8 shows the word of God and the Lord who speaks it. The seed is the word, and everything turns on how it is heard. Then the one who sows that word commands the storm, as only God does in the Psalms. He walks into the tombs to free a man no chains could hold. He lets an unclean woman touch him and calls her "daughter." He takes a dead girl's hand and says "Child, arise." Unclean, demonized, sick and dead, every barrier gives way before him. The chapter keeps asking "Who then is this?" The answer is the Son of the Most High God, who will one day go into death himself and come out again, so that everyone who trusts him can hear "Do not fear; only believe."
