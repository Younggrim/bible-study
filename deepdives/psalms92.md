*Setting:* Psalm 92 is the only psalm whose title links it to a particular day: "A Psalm. A Song for the Sabbath." The author and date are not given. Henry thought David the likeliest writer, while others place it in the temple worship of a later time. The Mishnah, the early collection of Jewish tradition (about AD 200), records that the Levites sang a set psalm each day of the week at the morning offering in the temple, and that Psalm 92 was the Sabbath psalm. It calls it "a song for the time to come, for the day that is all Sabbath," a picture of the age when God's rest is complete. A later Jewish tradition even imagined Adam singing it on the first Sabbath. The psalm sits in Book Four of the Psalms, just after Psalm 91's promises of protection and just before the "LORD reigns" psalms (93–100). Psalm 91 spoke of the "Most High" and promised to show God's salvation. Psalm 92 opens by praising the "Most High" (v1) and thanks God for what he has done. The covenant name *Yahweh* ("the LORD") appears exactly seven times in the psalm, perhaps a deliberate touch in a song for the seventh day. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanza breaks.

#### It Is Good to Give Thanks (vv1–4)
- **"It is good to give thanks to the LORD" (v1).** "Good" means fitting and right, and also pleasant. Praise is not a duty owed reluctantly. It is the right response and it brings joy (Ps 147:1: "it is good to sing praises to our God; for it is pleasant"). "Give thanks" (*hodot*) also means to confess or acknowledge, to say openly what God has done.
- **"To sing praises to your name, O Most High" (v1).** "Your name" means God himself as he has made himself known. "Most High" (*Elyon*) is the God over all other powers, the title Psalm 91 opened with.
- **"Your steadfast love in the morning, and your faithfulness by night" (v2).** "Steadfast love" (*hesed*) is God's loyal covenant love, and "faithfulness" is his reliability. The two often appear together (Ps 89:1–2; Lam 3:22–23: "his mercies… are new every morning; great is your faithfulness"). Morning and night together mean all day, every day. In the temple, a lamb was offered every morning and every evening (Exod 29:38–42), and the Levites stood "every morning, thanking and praising the LORD, and likewise at evening" (1 Chr 23:30). On the Sabbath the offering was doubled (Num 28:9–10).
- **Why morning for love and night for faithfulness?** It may simply be poetry, the two words split across the two halves of a line. But many have seen a pattern: the morning brings new mercies to receive, and the night brings a day to look back on and see that God kept his word.
- **"To the music of the lute and the harp, to the melody of the lyre" (v3).** The "lute" here is literally "ten," a ten-stringed instrument. The "harp" and "lyre" were the two main stringed instruments of Israel's worship, played by the Levites (1 Chr 15:16; 25:1). "Melody" (*higgayon*) also appears as a musical note in Psalm 9:16 and means a murmuring or meditative sound. Worship engages the hands and the mind as well as the voice.
- **"For you, O LORD, have made me glad by your work" (v4).** Here is the reason for the praise. **"The works of your hands"** may mean creation, which the Sabbath celebrates (Gen 2:2–3; Exod 20:11). It may mean God's acts of rescue and judgment in history, which the rest of the psalm describes. Deuteronomy ties the Sabbath to the exodus (Deut 5:15). Both fit a Sabbath song.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "praise, the business of the sabbath, is here recommended," and adds, "we must give him thanks every morning for the mercies of the night and every night for the mercies of the day." He wants music in worship to be "grave," not "gay, and apt to dissipate the spirits." JFB reads "morning… night" as worship done "diligently and constantly." He takes the instruments to mean that "all proper aid must be used." Guzik says the Sabbath is a day of delight in God, not merely of rest. He notes that by evening there is a whole day's experience of God's faithfulness to give thanks for.

#### How Great Are Your Works (vv5–9)
- **"How great are your works, O LORD! Your thoughts are very deep!" (v5).** God's works are visible, but the thoughts behind them run deeper than anyone can see. Compare Isaiah 55:8–9 and Paul's outburst, "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!" (Rom 11:33).
- **"The stupid man cannot know; the fool cannot understand this" (v6).** "Stupid" (*ba'ar*, KJV "brutish") is related to the word for cattle. It describes someone with no more spiritual understanding than an animal, who sees only what is in front of him. Asaph used the same word of himself when he was bitter about the wicked prospering: "I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you" (Ps 73:22). The "fool" (*kesil*) is the person in Proverbs who refuses wisdom (Prov 1:22). The problem is not intelligence but direction.
- **"Though the wicked sprout like grass and all evildoers flourish, they are doomed to destruction forever" (v7).** This is what the fool cannot see. In Israel, grass springs up quickly after the winter rains and withers within weeks in the summer heat (Ps 37:2; 90:5–6; Isa 40:6–8). The success of the wicked is real, but it is fast and shallow. The verb "flourish" will be used again in vv12–13, of the righteous, but with a very different end.
- **"But you, O LORD, are on high forever" (v8).** This is the shortest line in the psalm and stands exactly at its centre, with seven verses before and seven after. Everything turns on it. The wicked perish "forever" (v7). God is on high "forever." The word "forever" links the two and shows the difference.
- **"For behold, your enemies, O LORD, for behold, your enemies shall perish" (v9).** The repetition builds up like a drumbeat. It echoes an old Canaanite poem from Ugarit that says, "Behold, your enemies, O Baal, behold, your enemies you shall smite." The psalmist may be deliberately borrowing a pagan hymn's language and giving the victory to the true God. The enemies are called **"your enemies"**: the wicked are opposed not just to the psalmist but to God. **"All evildoers shall be scattered,"** like chaff or a defeated army.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls God's works "great and unsearchable" and says "those are fools, they are brutish, who do not know… how great God's works are." He pictures the wicked springing up "as the grass in spring (so numerous, so thickly sown, so green, and growing so fast)." JFB, citing Psalm 73:22, takes the "brutish man" as what the psalmist himself is like "when amazed by the prosperity of the wicked." Guzik links the brutish man to humanity's calling in Psalm 8 to look up to God. He contrasts the wicked, who get their best in this life, with believers, whose worst may come here.

#### You Have Exalted My Horn (vv10–11)
- **"You have exalted my horn like that of the wild ox" (v10).** A horn is a picture of strength and dignity (1 Sam 2:1, 10; Ps 75:10). The "wild ox" (*re'em*) was the aurochs, a huge wild bull, now extinct, that once roamed the Near East and was famous for its strength (Num 23:22; Job 39:9–12). The Greek translation rendered it "unicorn," "one-horn," and the KJV followed: "like the horn of an unicorn."
- **"You have poured over me fresh oil" (v10).** Anointing with oil was a sign of honour, joy and refreshment (Ps 23:5; 45:7; Luke 7:46), and of being set apart for service, as kings and priests were. "Fresh" (*ra'anan*) is literally "green" or "flourishing," the same word used in v14 for the righteous who stay "green." The oil that refreshes the psalmist's head is the same life that keeps the old tree green.
- **"My eyes have seen the downfall of my enemies" (v11).** The Hebrew is very compressed: "my eye looks on my watchers, my ears hear of those who rise against me." The psalmist has seen God vindicate him. This is not personal revenge. It is the same truth as v9 applied to one person: God's enemies, and those who attack God's people, will not stand.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the contrast in these verses: "God's enemies shall be cut off, but David shall be exalted." JFB says that to exalt the horn "is to increase power," and oil is "a figure for refreshment," still common in the East in his day. Guzik reads the fresh oil as renewed blessing and anointing for service.

#### The Righteous Flourish (vv12–15)
- **"The righteous flourish like the palm tree" (v12).** The date palm grows straight and tall, stays green all year, lives a long time, and bears sweet fruit for decades. It survives in the desert because its roots reach deep water. Palms were carved on the walls and doors of Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 6:29, 32), and palm branches were waved at the Feast of Booths (Lev 23:40) and at Jesus' entry into Jerusalem (John 12:13).
- **"And grow like a cedar in Lebanon" (v12).** The cedars of Lebanon were the greatest trees the Israelites knew, tall, fragrant, long-lived and resistant to rot. The temple was built of them (1 Kgs 5:6; 6:9–18). Grass springs up fast and is gone (v7). Palm and cedar grow slowly and last.
- **"They are planted in the house of the LORD; they flourish in the courts of our God" (v13).** Trees did not grow inside the temple courts, so the picture is poetic. The righteous are rooted in God's presence (compare Ps 1:3; 52:8: "I am like a green olive tree in the house of God"; Jer 17:7–8). Their life does not come from the surface but from where they are planted.
- **"They still bear fruit in old age; they are ever full of sap and green" (v14).** "Full of sap" is literally "fat," full of richness and life. Physical strength fails with age, but a life rooted in God keeps producing fruit. Caleb at eighty-five (Josh 14:10–11), Simeon and Anna in the temple (Luke 2:25–38), and Paul saying "our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day" (2 Cor 4:16) are all examples.
- **"To declare that the LORD is upright" (v15).** The point of the fruitful life is not to bring the righteous credit but to show what God is like. **"He is my rock"** recalls Moses' song: "The Rock, his work is perfect… a God of faithfulness and without iniquity, just and upright is he" (Deut 32:4). **"There is no unrighteousness in him"** answers the question that troubles the "stupid man" of v6. The prosperity of the wicked may make God seem unjust, but in the end the psalm affirms the opposite.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that the palm "has a stately body," and that God's trees "shall grow higher, like the cedars." He notes, "Trees are not usually planted in a house; but God's trees are said to be planted in his house," and draws attention to the promise "that they shall bring forth fruit in old age." He concludes that "all the righteous shall be fruitful and flourishing." JFB lists the trees' "vigorous growth, longevity, utility, fragrance, and beauty" as a picture of the godly. Guzik notes that fruitfulness in old age comes from God's inner renewal, not from holding on to youthful strength. He also says the fruit is meant to point to the Lord's uprightness, not to the person bearing it. The Tyndale notes connect the palms and cedars with the carvings and timber of the temple itself.

#### Pointing to Christ
A Sabbath song looks forward, as the rabbis said, to "the day that is all Sabbath." Hebrews says "there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" and that it is entered through faith in Christ (Heb 4:9–10). Jesus called himself "lord of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28) and offered rest to the weary (Matt 11:28). Psalm 92's righteous man is anointed with fresh oil and exalted over his enemies. That fits Jesus, the Anointed One, whom "God… anointed… with the oil of gladness beyond your companions" (Heb 1:9). He too was cut down, and for a time it looked as if the wicked had flourished. Then God raised him, and he became the root in whom his people are planted, a vine whose branches "bear much fruit" (John 15:5) and stay green to the end. His resurrection is the final proof that "the LORD is upright… and there is no unrighteousness in him."
