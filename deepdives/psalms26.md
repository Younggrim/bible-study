*Setting:* "Of David." No occasion is named. David appears to have been accused of something, and he brings his case before God, the only judge who can see the heart. Henry pictures him "putting himself upon a solemn trial, not by God and his country, but by God and his own conscience." The psalm is full of the language of worship: the altar, washing hands, God's house, the place where his glory dwells, the great assembly. Some think it was a prayer a worshipper would pray at the entrance to the sanctuary, like the questions of Psalms 15 and 24 ("Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?"). It is carefully framed. It begins "I have walked in my integrity" (v1) and nearly ends "I shall walk in my integrity" (v11). Psalm 25 closed with "May integrity and uprightness preserve me" (25:21). Psalm 26 shows what that integrity looks like, while still ending, as Psalm 25 did, with a plea to be redeemed.

#### Vindicate Me, O LORD (vv1–3)
- **"Vindicate me, O LORD, for I have walked in my integrity" (v1).** "Vindicate" is literally "judge me" (*shaphat*), meaning "decide my case, and clear my name." David does not take matters into his own hands. He brings his case to court. "Integrity" (*tom*) means wholeness, completeness, a life not split in two. It is the same root as "blameless" in 18:23 and 19:13. It does not mean sinless. Psalm 25, just before, confessed sin three times. It means undivided loyalty, a heart that is all for God.
- **"I have trusted in the LORD without wavering" (v1).** Literally "I shall not slip." His integrity is not the root of his confidence. His trust in God is. Integrity is how that trust shows up in the way he walks.
- **"Prove me, O LORD, and try me; test my heart and my mind" (v2).** Three testing words, like 17:3. "Prove" (*bachan*) is to examine. "Try" (*nasah*) is to put to the test. "Test" (*tsaraph*) is to refine by fire, as a smelter does with silver. "Mind" is literally "kidneys," the Hebrew picture of the deepest feelings and motives (7:9; 16:7). David invites God to search the parts of him no one else can see. Psalm 139 ends with the same prayer: "Search me, O God, and know my heart!" (139:23–24).
- **"For your steadfast love is before my eyes, and I walk in your faithfulness" (v3).** Here is what keeps him steady. He keeps God's steadfast love (*chesed*) in front of him like a landmark, as in 16:8, "I have set the LORD always before me." "Faithfulness" (*emet*) is God's truth and reliability. David walks in the light of who God is.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "Judge—decide on my case; the appeal of innocence," and integrity is "freedom from blemish." He notes that the psalmist's "confidence of perseverance results from trust in God's sustaining grace." Guzik stresses that David asks God to vindicate him rather than defending himself, and passes on Boice's point that he cares more for God's reputation than his own. He also passes on Spurgeon's advice that a dull faith is revived by thinking more about God's lovingkindness.

#### I Do Not Sit with Men of Falsehood (vv4–5)
- **"I do not sit with men of falsehood" (v4).** "Falsehood" (*shav*) is the same word for emptiness and vanity as in 24:4. These are empty, worthless people, or people devoted to empty things, including idols. To "sit" with people meant to share their company, their plans and their way of life. Psalm 1:1 began the whole Psalter this way: "Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked… nor sits in the seat of scoffers."
- **"Nor do I consort with hypocrites" (v4).** "Hypocrites" is literally "those who hide themselves," people who keep their real intentions concealed, like plotters who show one face and hide another.
- **"I hate the assembly of evildoers" (v5).** "Assembly" (*qahal*) is the word for a gathered congregation. There are two assemblies in this psalm: the assembly of evildoers here, and "the great assembly" where David will bless the LORD (v12). He belongs to one and refuses the other.
- **Not hating people, but refusing their way.** This does not mean David refused to speak to sinners or show them kindness. He spared Saul and wept for Absalom. It means he would not join them, take their counsel or make them his close circle. Paul quotes a proverb: "Bad company ruins good morals" (1 Cor 15:33).
- **Commentators:** Henry says these verses show David's "rooted antipathy to sin and sinners," and his avoidance of the wicked was evidence of his sincerity. JFB treats vv4–8 as "the fruits of divine grace" in David's life, "especially in his avoiding the wicked and his purposes of cleaving to God's worship." Guzik passes on Poole's comment that "sitting" implies ease and settled companionship, and Clarke's description of the "hypocrites" as hidden ones and secret plotters.

#### I Love the Habitation of Your House (vv6–8)
- **"I wash my hands in innocence" (v6).** Priests had to wash their hands and feet at the bronze basin before going near the altar, "so that they may not die" (Exod 30:19–21). The elders of a town washed their hands over a sacrifice to declare that they were innocent of an unsolved murder: "Our hands did not shed this blood" (Deut 21:6–7). David uses the gesture as a picture of a clean life. Pilate later washed his hands before the crowd to declare himself innocent of Jesus' blood (Matt 27:24), the most famous empty use of the gesture in history. Washing hands means nothing unless the heart is clean (24:4).
- **"And go around your altar, O LORD" (v6).** A worshipper walking around the altar in procession. The Bible gives no details of such a rite, but later Jewish practice at the Feast of Booths included processions circling the altar, and the psalm may reflect an early form of that. The point is clear: David is not hanging back. He comes close to the place of sacrifice.
- **"Proclaiming thanksgiving aloud, and telling all your wondrous deeds" (v7).** "Wondrous deeds" (*niphla'ot*) are God's great saving acts, like the plagues and the Exodus (Exod 3:20). David does not come to the altar to show off his integrity. He comes to tell about God.
- **"O LORD, I love the habitation of your house" (v8).** Here is the heart of the psalm. It is not only that David avoids the wicked. He loves God's house. In David's day this was the tabernacle, the tent where the ark rested. "Habitation" can mean a dwelling, or a refuge.
- **"And the place where your glory dwells" (v8).** "Dwells" comes from *mishkan*, the word for the tabernacle itself, God's "dwelling" among his people. The glory (*kavod*) filled the tabernacle when it was finished (Exod 40:34–35). Psalm 24 had just pictured the King of glory entering. Psalm 27:4 will say, "One thing have I asked of the LORD… that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life."
- **Commentators:** JFB: "wash mine hands—expressive symbol of freedom from sinful acts (compare Mt 27:24)," and "honour dwelleth—conveys an allusion to the Holy of Holies." He reads "habitation" as "where Thy house rests, as the tabernacle was not yet permanently fixed." Henry notes that hypocrites may attend worship too, "as the proud Pharisee went up to the temple to pray," but that loving God's house as David did "is a good sign of sincerity." He suggests David may have been driven away from the sanctuary at the time by persecution (1 Sam 20:27; 26:19), and imagines him saying, "though I cannot come to the habitation of thy house, I love it; my heart is there." Guzik says David's "innocence" means a clean conscience before God, not sinlessness, and that obedience is sustained by delight in God's presence.

#### Do Not Sweep My Soul Away (vv9–10)
- **"Do not sweep my soul away with sinners" (v9).** "Sweep away" is literally "gather" (*asaph*). It is the verb used for dying and being "gathered to one's people" (Gen 25:8). David is asking not to share the fate of the wicked, in life or in death. If God judges sinners, let him not be gathered up in the same harvest. Balaam said, "Let me die the death of the upright" (Num 23:10).
- **"Nor my life with bloodthirsty men" (v9).** Literally "men of blood," people willing to kill to get what they want (5:6; 55:23).
- **"In whose hands are evil devices, and whose right hands are full of bribes" (v10).** "Evil devices" (*zimmah*) are schemes, plans laid out in advance. The right hand, the hand of power and action, is full of bribes. God's law said, "you shall not take a bribe, for a bribe blinds the clear-sighted and subverts the cause of those who are in the right" (Exod 23:8). These are people in positions of power using it corruptly. David's hands are washed in innocence (v6). Theirs are stuffed with bribes. The contrast is the whole point.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "Gather not" as "Bring me not to death," and says the enemies' "whole conduct is that of violence and fraud." Henry says David "deprecates the doom of the wicked." Guzik notes that sin offers its own bribes, pleasure, comfort, attention, and passes on Gurnall's comment that a soul walking in integrity takes bribes neither from men nor from sin itself.

#### My Foot Stands on Level Ground (vv11–12)
- **"But as for me, I shall walk in my integrity" (v11).** The psalm returns to its opening words, but the tense has changed. In v1, "I have walked." Now, "I shall walk." Looking back, God has kept him. Looking forward, he resolves to keep going.
- **"Redeem me, and be gracious to me" (v11).** Here is the key that keeps this psalm from being self-righteous. Right after resolving to walk in integrity, David asks to be redeemed and shown grace. "Redeem" (*padah*) means to ransom, to buy free, the same word that closed Psalm 25. Integrity is not his ransom. He still needs a redeemer.
- **"My foot stands on level ground" (v12).** "Level ground" (*mishor*) is a plain, a flat place where there is no stumbling. The same word can mean uprightness or fairness (45:6; 67:4). The man who said "I shall not slip" in v1 now finds his foot on firm, even ground.
- **"In the great assembly I will bless the LORD" (v12).** "Assembly" is plural in Hebrew, the gathered congregations of God's people. David refuses one assembly (v5) and joins the other. He ends where all the psalms of trust end, praising God in the company of his people (22:22, 25).
- **Commentators:** JFB: "even place—free from occasions of stumbling—safety in his course is denoted." Henry reads v12 as David's resolution "fixed for God and godliness, not to be shaken by the temptations of the world," and his hope that though "now perhaps banished from public ordinances," he would again praise God in the congregation. Guzik links the level ground back to "I shall not slip" in v1, and passes on Spurgeon's comment that the psalm moves from a minor key to a major one.

#### Pointing to Christ
David's claim of integrity was real but limited, which is why he still prayed "redeem me, and be gracious to me." Only one person could pray the first half of this psalm without needing the second. Jesus could say, "Which one of you convicts me of sin?" (John 8:46). He did not sit with men of falsehood, yet he ate with sinners to call them home (Luke 15:1–2). He loved his Father's house so much that zeal for it consumed him (John 2:17). He was falsely accused before men whose hands were full of bribes and who had paid for his betrayal (Matt 26:15). Pilate washed his hands, but it was Jesus who was truly innocent. And he was swept away with sinners, crucified between two criminals (Luke 23:33), so that those who trust him would not be. Because he was the innocent one, sinners who could never make David's claims on their own can be redeemed, shown grace, and stand on level ground in the great assembly.
