*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Chapters 17–20 called all Israel to be holy. Chapters 21–22 now turn to the priests, Aaron and his sons, who stood closest to God's presence in the tabernacle. Not long before, two of Aaron's sons, Nadab and Abihu, died for offering "unauthorized fire" (10:1–2), so everyone knew how serious the priestly calling was. The rule of thumb in this chapter is simple: the nearer someone stands to God, the higher the standard. Ordinary priests have stricter rules than ordinary Israelites, and the high priest has stricter rules than any other priest.

#### Holiness and the Priests (vv1–24)

**Mourning, marriage and family (vv1–9)**
- **"No one shall make himself unclean for the dead" (v1).** Touching a corpse, or even being in the same tent, made a person unclean for seven days (Num 19:11–16). An unclean priest could not serve. Death was the clearest sign of sin's curse (Gen 2:17), and the priests served the living God, so they were to keep away from it.
- **"Except for his closest relatives" (vv2–3).** A priest could mourn and bury his mother, father, son, daughter, brother, or **"virgin sister… who has had no husband,"** because she had no husband to see to her burial. The wife is not named, but most interpreters think she is assumed, since she is closer than any of these (compare Ezekiel, a priest, mourning his wife's death in Ezek 24:15–18, where God's command not to mourn was a special sign).
- **"He shall not make himself unclean as a husband among his people" (v4).** The Hebrew is hard. *Baal* can mean "husband," "master" or "chief man." It may mean a priest must not defile himself for his in-laws, or that as a leader among the people he must not defile himself for others. Translations differ.
- **No bald patches, shaved beards or cuts (v5).** These were pagan mourning rites (19:27–28; Deut 14:1). The priests above all were to grieve in a way that showed faith in the living God. Paul says believers grieve, but not "as others do who have no hope" (1 Thess 4:13).
- **"The bread of their God" (vv6, 8).** The offerings are called God's "food" or "bread." This does not mean God was hungry (Ps 50:12–13). It is the language of a meal shared at a king's table. The priests served at that table, so they had to be holy.
- **"They shall not marry a prostitute… or a woman divorced" (v7).** An ordinary priest could marry a widow, but not a prostitute, a woman **"defiled"** (the word probably means one who had lost her virginity outside marriage), or a divorced woman. The priest's family was on display before the whole nation, and his marriage had to be above question. Paul's standards for church leaders follow a similar logic: "the husband of one wife… he must manage his own household well" (1 Tim 3:2–5).
- **"You shall sanctify him" (v8).** The people were responsible for helping their priests stay holy, by honoring them and not drawing them into anything defiling. **"For I, the LORD, who sanctify you, am holy."** This refrain, "the LORD who sanctifies," runs through chapters 21–22 (21:15, 23; 22:9, 16, 32).
- **"The daughter of any priest… shall be burned with fire" (v9).** A priest's daughter who became a prostitute dishonored her father and his office in a public way. Canaanite religion had cult prostitutes, and this may be partly in view. The penalty matches 20:14. As there, some think burning followed execution.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "God's ministers must be examples to others of patience under affliction," and that "The children of ministers ought, of all others, to take heed of doing any thing that is scandalous." JFB says the restrictions guarded "the purity and dignity of the sacred office," and reads v5 as the priests modelling grief "not as those who have no hope." JFB also notes the "husband" or "chief man" uncertainty in v4. Guzik observes that the ban covered graves and tombs as well as corpses, and draws the principle that priests in either covenant are to set their affections on what is pure.

**The high priest (vv10–15)**
- **"On whose head the anointing oil is poured" (v10).** The high priest was anointed with the holy oil poured on his head (8:12; Ps 133:2) and dressed in special garments (Exod 28). His title here is literally "the great priest among his brothers."
- **"Shall not let the hair of his head hang loose nor tear his clothes" (v10).** Loose hair and torn clothes were the normal signs of grief (10:6; Gen 37:34). The high priest could do neither, even for his own father or mother (v11). At Jesus' trial, the high priest Caiaphas "tore his robes" and cried "blasphemy" (Matt 26:65). He broke this law at the very moment he condemned the true High Priest.
- **"He shall not go out of the sanctuary" (v12).** This probably does not mean he lived there. It means he may not leave his duties to join a funeral procession. His consecration to God came before even his family ties.
- **"A virgin of his own people" (vv13–14).** The high priest could not marry even a widow. His wife had to be a virgin from Israel, most likely from a priestly family. **"That he may not profane his offspring" (v15).** The high priesthood passed from father to son, so the line had to be beyond question.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "the high dignity of his office demanded a corresponding superiority in personal holiness," and it applies the same principle to ministers' households (1 Tim 3:2; Titus 1:6). Guzik also points to Caiaphas tearing his robes at Jesus' trial. The Tyndale notes add that the high priest's composure in grief kept him always ready to serve.

**Priests with a blemish (vv16–24)**
- **"None of your offspring… who has a blemish may approach" (v17).** A descendant of Aaron with a physical defect could not offer sacrifices. The list (vv18–20) includes blindness, lameness, a disfigured face, a limb too long, an injured foot or hand, a hunched back, dwarfism, an eye defect, skin diseases and damaged testicles. Some of the Hebrew terms are rare, and translations vary on several of them.
- **Why this rule?** The same word for "blemish" (*mum*) is used for animals unfit for sacrifice (22:20–25). The one who offered and the thing offered were both to picture wholeness. In the ancient world, the gods were often served by priests chosen for their perfect appearance, and Israel's rule is similar on the surface. But the Bible's reason is not that God values able bodies more. The priest at the altar was a living picture of the perfection that God's holiness requires.
- **"He may eat the bread of his God, both of the most holy and of the holy things" (v22).** This is the key verse. A priest with a blemish was not cast out. He still belonged to the priestly family, he still ate the holiest food, and he could still do other work around the sanctuary. He simply could not **"go through the veil or approach the altar"** (v23). His standing with God was not in question.
- **A larger story.** In Israel, lameness and blindness could also bar people from places of honor (2 Sam 5:8). Jesus turned this upside down. He healed the blind and lame *in the temple* (Matt 21:14), and in his parable the master tells his servant to bring in "the poor and crippled and blind and lame" to his banquet (Luke 14:21). Isaiah foresaw a day when even the eunuch would have "a monument and a name" in God's house (Isa 56:3–5), and Acts records the Ethiopian eunuch's baptism (Acts 8:26–39).
- **"So Moses spoke to Aaron… and to all the people of Israel" (v24).** Even the rules for priests were told to everyone. The people needed to know what holiness their priests were held to.
- **Commentators:** Henry is gentle here: "There is many a healthful beautiful soul lodged in a crazy deformed body." He says the sins of Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas were "worse than any of the blemishes here mentioned," and concludes, "let all that are made to our God spiritual priests be before him holy and without blemish." JFB sees in the unblemished priest a picture of Christ, "holy, innocent, unstained" (Heb 7:26). Guzik reads the physical rule as pointing to the deeper need to be free from spiritual defects before God, and stresses that the blemished priest still shared the holy food, so he was barred from public service, not from fellowship with God.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every priest in this chapter fell short somewhere. The ordinary priest had to avoid death, the high priest had to stay inside the sanctuary, and the blemished priest could not go near the altar. Jesus is the High Priest this chapter was waiting for: "holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners" (Heb 7:26). He did not avoid death. He went into it and broke its power, so that death no longer defiles those who belong to him. He is also the perfect sacrifice, "a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Pet 1:19). And because of him, people with every kind of weakness are welcome at the table. All who believe are made "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9), and he presents them to God "without blemish" (Col 1:22), not by their bodies, but by his blood.
