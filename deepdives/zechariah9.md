*Setting:* Chapters 9–14 are the second half of Zechariah and sound very different from the first. There are no dates, no night visions and no interpreting angel, no mention of Zerubbabel, Joshua or the temple building. Instead come two long "oracles" (9:1; 12:1), poems and prophecies that range from the near future to the end of history. Many scholars think these chapters come from a later stage of Zechariah's ministry, perhaps after 480 BC when Greece had become a rising power. Others since the 1700s have argued that they come from a different author, and some critics date them to the Greek period. Many conservative scholars see nothing in the text that requires a second author, and the book has always come down as one. Whatever the date, the Gospel writers quote these chapters again and again in telling the story of Jesus' final week. Chapter 9 opens with judgment sweeping down the coast from Syria to Philistia, which many see fulfilled in Alexander the Great's march of 332 BC, with Jerusalem spared. Then comes one of the best-known prophecies in the Bible: Zion's king, coming humble and riding on a donkey.

#### Judgment on Israel's Enemies (vv1–8)
- **"The oracle of the word of the LORD" (v1).** The word *massa'* comes from the verb "to lift" or "to carry." It means a weighty pronouncement, often of judgment, and the KJV translates it "burden." It appears again at 12:1 and at Malachi 1:1, which may mark the last three units of the Minor Prophets.
- **"Against the land of Hadrach and Damascus is its resting place" (v1).** Hadrach appears nowhere else in the Bible, but Assyrian records name a region called Hatarikka near Hamath in northern Syria. Damascus, the capital of Aram (Syria), is where God's word comes to "rest," that is, lands and stays. The oracle moves in the order an army would march: down from Syria, along the Phoenician coast, into Philistia.
- **"For the LORD has an eye on mankind and on all the tribes of Israel" (v1).** The Hebrew is literally "to the LORD the eye of man," which can be read two ways. The ESV takes it as God watching all people. Others read it as "the eyes of mankind are toward the LORD," as people turn to him in the crisis. Either way, God sees all nations, not only Israel.
- **"Tyre and Sidon, though they are very wise" (v2).** These were the great Phoenician port cities, in what is now Lebanon, famous for trade, shipbuilding and purple dye. Their "wisdom" was commercial and political shrewdness. Ezekiel mocked Tyre's prince for thinking his wisdom made him a god (Ezek 28:2–5).
- **"Tyre has built herself a rampart" (v3).** There is a wordplay here: "rampart" is *matsor*, and Tyre is *Tsor*. Tyre's main city stood on an island half a mile offshore, ringed with walls said to rise 150 feet on the landward side. Nebuchadnezzar besieged it for thirteen years and never fully took it (Ezek 29:18). **"Heaped up silver like dust, and fine gold like the mud of the streets"** describes its enormous wealth.
- **"The Lord will strip her… and she shall be devoured by fire" (v4).** In 332 BC Alexander the Great did what no one else had done. He built a causeway from the mainland to the island, using rubble from the old mainland city, took Tyre after a seven-month siege and burned it. Thousands were killed and some 30,000 sold as slaves. The causeway is still there, silted into a permanent isthmus.
- **"Ashkelon shall see it, and be afraid; Gaza too" (v5).** The Philistine cities lay further south on the coast road to Egypt. When Tyre fell, they knew they were next. Gaza resisted Alexander for two months and was then destroyed, its king (named Batis by ancient historians) dragged to death behind a chariot. "The king shall perish from Gaza" fits closely. Amos and Zephaniah had spoken against the same cities (Amos 1:6–8; Zeph 2:4–7).
- **"A mixed people shall dwell in Ashdod" (v6).** The word *mamzer* appears elsewhere only in Deuteronomy 23:2, of a child of an illegitimate union. Here it means a population of mixed origins. After the conquests the Philistines lost their identity as a people and disappear from history.
- **"I will take away its blood from its mouth, and its abominations from between its teeth" (v7).** A vivid picture of the Philistines eating meat with the blood still in it and unclean sacrificial food (Lev 17:10–14; Isa 65:4). God will tear these things out of their mouths. The judgment becomes a purging.
- **"It too shall be a remnant for our God… like a clan in Judah, and Ekron shall be like the Jebusites" (v7).** A remarkable turn. Israel's oldest enemies, the Philistines, will have a remnant who belong to God and are counted among Judah's clans. The Jebusites were the Canaanites of Jerusalem whom David conquered and who were then absorbed into Israel. Araunah the Jebusite sold David the site of the temple (2 Sam 24:18–24). Luke records Philip preaching the gospel in Gaza's region and in Ashdod (Acts 8:26, 40).
- **"Then I will encamp at my house as a guard" (v8).** God himself stands guard over his temple and people. As the conqueror sweeps past, Jerusalem is spared. Alexander did pass by without destroying Jerusalem. The Jewish historian Josephus tells a story that the high priest Jaddua came out in his robes to meet him, and that Alexander bowed and offered sacrifice in the temple (Antiquities 11.329–339). Most historians doubt the details, but the city was not harmed.
- **"No oppressor shall again march over them, for now I see with my own eyes" (v8).** "Now I see" echoes v1. God has his eye on everything, and especially on his own. The promise reaches beyond Alexander, since other oppressors did come, to the final peace described in the rest of the book.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read vv1–8 as fulfilled in Alexander's conquests, and both mention Josephus's account of Alexander sparing Jerusalem. JFB places Hadrach as "a part of Syria, near Damascus," and says Tyre lay "in the conqueror's way on his march along the Mediterranean to Egypt." Henry sees in v7 "an intimation of mercy to some of them, in their conversion," and so do the Tyndale notes, which connect it with Philip's preaching in Acts 8:40. The Tyndale notes also point out the *matsor*/*Tsor* wordplay.

#### The Coming King of Zion (vv9–13)
- **"Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Shout aloud, O daughter of Jerusalem!" (v9).** The tone changes completely. A conqueror has just swept down the coast with war horses and siege engines. Now another king comes. Zephaniah used nearly the same call: "Sing aloud, O daughter of Zion; shout, O Israel!… The King of Israel, the LORD, is in your midst" (Zeph 3:14–15).
- **"Behold, your king is coming to you" (v9).** Judah had had no king since 586 BC. This is the son of David promised in the Branch passages (3:8; 6:12), and "to you" makes it personal. He comes to his people.
- **"Righteous and having salvation is he" (v9).** "Righteous" (*tsaddiq*) means he rules justly (Jer 23:5). "Having salvation" translates *nosha'*, a passive form that means literally "saved" or "delivered," in the sense of victorious, a king whom God has brought through. The same form describes a king "saved" by God rather than by his army (Ps 33:16). He brings salvation because God has given him victory.
- **"Humble and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey" (v9).** "Humble" is *'ani*, which usually means poor, lowly or afflicted. Kings of the time rode horses or chariots into cities as conquerors. A donkey was a working animal, the mount of peace rather than war. Yet it was not a mean animal in Israel. Judges' sons rode them (Judg 10:4), David's sons rode mules (2 Sam 13:29), and Solomon rode to his coronation on David's mule (1 Kgs 1:33). Jacob's blessing on Judah spoke of the ruler who would come, "binding his foal to the vine and his donkey's colt to the choice vine" (Gen 49:10–11). "A colt, the foal of a donkey" is Hebrew parallelism, the same animal described twice to stress that it is young.
- **The triumphal entry.** All four Gospels describe Jesus entering Jerusalem on a donkey colt the week of his death, and Matthew and John quote this verse (Matt 21:4–5; John 12:14–16). John adds that the disciples did not understand it at first, but after Jesus was glorified "they remembered that these things had been written about him." Matthew mentions both the donkey and her colt, reading the parallel lines literally, and some think the mother came along to keep the unbroken colt calm. Jesus chose this deliberately. He planned the entry, sending for the animal in advance (Mark 11:1–6), and enacted the prophecy.
- **"I will cut off the chariot from Ephraim and the war horse from Jerusalem; and the battle bow shall be cut off" (v10).** It is God who disarms his own people. The kingdom will not be won by weapons. Ephraim (the north) and Jerusalem (the south) are named together, a reunited Israel. Isaiah and Micah saw the same future: swords beaten into plowshares (Isa 2:4; 9:5; Mic 4:3; 5:10).
- **"He shall speak peace to the nations" (v10).** The king conquers by a word, and the word is *shalom*. Alexander subdued the nations by force. This king makes peace with them.
- **"His rule shall be from sea to sea, and from the River to the ends of the earth" (v10).** These are the exact words of Psalm 72:8, Solomon's prayer for the ideal son of David. "The River" is the Euphrates, the boundary promised to Abraham (Gen 15:18). From there the rule extends to the whole world.
- **One coming or two?** The Gospels quote v9 and not v10. Many interpreters see v9 fulfilled at Jesus' first coming, while the worldwide peace of v10 awaits its completion at his return. Others see v10 already under way as the gospel spreads to the nations. Both agree the king is Jesus.
- **"Because of the blood of my covenant with you, I will set your prisoners free from the waterless pit" (v11).** "Blood of my covenant" recalls Sinai, where Moses threw the blood on the people and said, "Behold the blood of the covenant" (Exod 24:8). A waterless pit was a dry cistern used as a prison. Joseph was thrown into one (Gen 37:24), and so was Jeremiah (Jer 38:6). The prisoners are the Jews still scattered in exile. On the night before his death Jesus took the cup and said, "This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many" (Matt 26:28).
- **"Return to your stronghold, O prisoners of hope" (v12).** A beautiful phrase. They are prisoners, but prisoners bound to hope, because God has made a promise. The "stronghold" is Zion, and behind Zion, God himself (Ps 18:2; Nah 1:7). **"I will restore to you double"**: a double portion, the share of the firstborn, more than was lost (Isa 61:7).
- **"I have bent Judah as my bow; I have made Ephraim its arrow" (v13).** Having disarmed them in v10, God now uses his people themselves as his weapons. The fight is his.
- **"Against your sons, O Greece" (v13).** The Hebrew is *Yavan*, the name for the Ionian Greeks (Gen 10:2). Daniel had already seen Greece as the coming world power after Persia (Dan 8:21). Even in Zechariah's lifetime the Persians were fighting the Greeks, at Marathon in 490 BC and in Xerxes' invasion of 480 BC. Most interpreters see a fulfilment in the Maccabean revolt of the 160s BC, when Jewish fighters defeated the Greek Seleucid kings who tried to stamp out their faith, a victory still remembered at Hanukkah.
- **Commentators:** All three read v9 as a prophecy of Jesus' entry into Jerusalem. Henry writes that "all his acts of government will be exactly according to the rules of equity, for he is just," and that his kingdom spreads by "the speaking of peace to the heathen," not by the sword. He calls sin a prison, "a pit in which was no water," from which Christ's blood sets free. JFB names the king "Prince of peace," calls the exiles "prisoners of hope" because of God's covenant, and refers v13 to the struggle against Antiochus Epiphanes. Guzik assigns v9 to Christ's first coming and v10 to his second, reads the stronghold as both Zion and the LORD himself, and contrasts the humble entry with the triumphs of Roman generals. He takes the doubled phrase "a colt, the foal of a donkey" as stressing a fine, royal animal. The lexicon gives the words only as "colt" and "she-donkey," and the parallel more likely stresses that the animal was young.

#### The LORD Will Save His People (vv14–17)
- **"Then the LORD will appear over them, and his arrow will go forth like lightning" (v14).** God appears as a divine warrior, as at Sinai with lightning and the trumpet (Exod 19:16–19), and as in the old hymns of Israel's battles (Ps 18:7–15; Hab 3:3–11). **"The whirlwinds of the south"** are the fierce storms that blew in from the desert. God came "from Teman" in the south in Habakkuk 3:3.
- **"They shall devour, and tread down the sling stones" (v15).** The images are harsh. The enemy's sling stones are trampled underfoot as harmless. Victory is pictured as a feast, the people drinking and shouting "as if drunk with wine."
- **"Be full like a bowl, drenched like the corners of the altar" (v15).** The bowls in the temple caught the blood of sacrifices, which was then splashed on the corners of the altar (Exod 27:3; Lev 4:7). The picture is of battle as a sacrifice to God, a common prophetic image (Isa 34:6; Jer 46:10). It is shocking, and meant to be.
- **"On that day the LORD their God will save them, as the flock of his people" (v16).** The warrior is also the shepherd. "Flock" introduces the shepherd theme that runs through chapters 10, 11 and 13.
- **"Like the jewels of a crown they shall shine on his land" (v16).** The people who were trampled now sparkle like gems in a crown. Isaiah had promised Zion would be "a crown of beauty in the hand of the LORD" (Isa 62:3). In 6:11–14 a crown was made for the Branch. Now his people are the jewels.
- **"How great is his goodness, and how great his beauty!" (v17).** The Hebrew "his" could refer to God or to the people or land. Most take it as a cry of praise to God. The chapter that began with Tyre's wealth ends with something better: grain and new wine, God's own goodness in the ordinary food that makes young men and women flourish (Joel 2:19, 24).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the victories of vv13–15 as pointing beyond the Maccabees to "the greater deliverance in Christ," and the crown stones as God's people prized and displayed. JFB likewise sees the Maccabean wars as a foretaste of a final deliverance and calls the people "the stones of a crown." Guzik reads the battle images as partly fulfilled in the Maccabean period and finally in the last days, and the grain and wine of v17 as pictures of millennial plenty. The Tyndale notes add that the image of God hovering over his people in v14 may play on the winged sun disk that Persian art showed protecting the Persian king.

#### Pointing to Christ
The contrast in this chapter is the gospel in miniature. Alexander came down the coast on a war horse, and cities burned. Jesus came down the Mount of Olives on a borrowed donkey, and the crowds threw their cloaks on the road. He was righteous, and he brought salvation not by killing his enemies but by dying for them. Days after riding in, he lifted the cup and called it "my blood of the covenant" (Matt 26:28), the blood that sets prisoners free from the waterless pit. He speaks peace to the nations, "for he himself is our peace" (Eph 2:14–17). His rule now runs from sea to sea as the gospel goes out, and one day every chariot and battle bow will be done away with. Those he saves become the jewels of his crown.
