*Setting:* The plains of Moab, east of the Jordan across from Jericho, in the eleventh month of the fortieth year after the Exodus, around 1400 BC on the traditional dating (some scholars prefer the 1200s). Numbers ended here, with Israel camped on the edge of Canaan. Moses is 120 years old and knows he will not cross the river (Num 20:12). Deuteronomy is his farewell: a series of sermons to the new generation, most of whom were children or not yet born when God gave the law at Sinai. The Hebrew title is *Devarim*, "words," from its opening line. The English name comes from the Greek translation of 17:18, where the king is to write "a copy of this law." The Greek rendered that *deuteronomion*, "second law." It is not a new law but the same covenant, retold and preached to a new generation. The book is also shaped like an ancient treaty between a great king and his people: preamble, history, laws, blessings and curses, and witnesses. Chapters 1–3 are the history section, and this chapter goes straight to the most painful memory in Israel's story.

#### The Command to Leave Horeb (vv1–8)
- **"These are the words that Moses spoke to all Israel" (v1).** Moses speaks to the whole nation, not just the leaders. The place names in v1 (Suph, Paran, Tophel, Laban, Hazeroth, Dizahab) are mostly unknown today. They may trace the route of the wilderness years or mark the area around the camp. **"The Arabah"** is the deep rift valley running from the Sea of Galilee down to the Gulf of Aqaba, here the stretch just north of the Dead Sea.
- **"It is eleven days' journey from Horeb… to Kadesh-barnea" (v2).** One of the saddest verses in the Bible, all the more for being stated so plainly. Horeb is Deuteronomy's usual name for Mount Sinai. The trip to the edge of the promised land took eleven days. Because of unbelief, it took thirty-eight more years to get from Kadesh to here (2:14).
- **"In the fortieth year, on the first day of the eleventh month" (v3).** Aaron died in the fifth month of this year (Num 33:38). Moses will die before the crossing, which comes in the first month of the next year (Josh 4:19). These sermons are given in Moses' last weeks.
- **"After he had defeated Sihon… and Og" (v4).** The two recent victories east of the Jordan (Num 21:21–35) are a fresh reminder that God fights for Israel. Sihon and Og will be retold in chapters 2–3.
- **"Moses undertook to explain this law" (v5).** "Explain" (*ba'ar*) means to make plain or distinct. Its only other uses are for writing the law "very plainly" on stones (27:8) and for writing a vision "plainly" on tablets (Hab 2:2). Deuteronomy is preaching: the law laid out clearly so a new generation can understand and obey it. "Law" is *torah*, which means instruction or teaching, not just legal rules.
- **"You have stayed long enough at this mountain" (v6).** Israel spent nearly a year at Sinai (Num 10:11). That time was necessary, for receiving the law and building the tabernacle, but it was never meant to be permanent. God's word was "move on."
- **"As far as the great river, the river Euphrates" (v7).** God lists the regions of the land: the hill country, the Arabah, the Shephelah ("lowland," the foothills between the Judean hills and the coast), the Negeb in the south, the coast, and Lebanon to the north. The promise reaches as far as the Euphrates, the borders first given to Abraham (Gen 15:18). Israel came closest to this under David and Solomon (1 Kgs 4:21).
- **"See, I have set the land before you. Go in and take possession" (v8).** The land is already given. Israel's part is to go in and take it. The Tyndale notes point out that "possess" here carries the sense of taking up an inheritance. It is what God "swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob."
- **Commentators:** Henry says God appointed Moses "to leave this legacy to the church," giving this review and exhortation "purely by divine direction." Guzik explains the name "second law" and notes the book's treaty structure. He takes the "eleven days" as a picture of how unbelief can turn a short walk with God into a long detour. JFB likewise stresses that the long wandering was caused not by distance but by banishment for rebellion. JFB suggests "beyond the Jordan" may mean "on the bank of the Jordan."

#### Leaders for Each Tribe (vv9–18)
- **"I am not able to bear you by myself" (v9).** Moses recalls the appointment of leaders. This combines two episodes: Jethro's advice to appoint judges (Exod 18:13–27) and God's sharing of Moses' Spirit with seventy elders (Num 11:14–17), where Moses used almost these same words.
- **"You are today as numerous as the stars of heaven" (v10).** The promise to Abraham (Gen 15:5) has come true in Israel's growth. Moses' burden is itself evidence of God's faithfulness. **"May the LORD… make you a thousand times as many" (v11).** His complaint turns into a blessing. He is not resentful of the crowd but overwhelmed by it.
- **"The weight and burden of you and your strife" (v12).** The three words, weight, burden and strife, are honest. Leading people includes carrying their quarrels.
- **"Choose… wise, understanding, and experienced men" (v13).** The people nominate and Moses appoints. The qualities are character and judgment, not wealth or status. "Experienced" means "known," men whose lives were known and respected in their tribes.
- **"Commanders of thousands, commanders of hundreds, commanders of fifties, commanders of tens" (v15).** These units were also standard military divisions (1 Sam 8:12). The same men led in war and judged in peace.
- **"Judge righteously between a man and his brother or the alien" (v16).** Foreigners living among Israel had the same right to justice.
- **"You shall not be partial in judgment" (v17).** Literally "do not recognize faces." A judge must decide as though he could not see who stood in front of him, rich or poor, important or not. **"Hear the small and the great alike."** **"You shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God's."** That last phrase is the foundation of justice in Israel. Judges acted on God's behalf. Jehoshaphat later reminds his judges: "you judge not for man but for the LORD" (2 Chr 19:6).
- **"The case that is too hard for you, you shall bring to me" (v17).** This anticipates the central court of 17:8–13.
- **Commentators:** Guzik links the leaders to the seventy elders of Numbers 11 and treats Exodus 18 as an earlier, separate event. He stresses that "the judgment is God's" means judges answer to God. JFB connects the passage to Jethro's suggestion and the growing population. JFB has no separate notes on vv11–18.

#### Israel's Refusal to Enter the Land (vv19–33)
- **"All that great and terrifying wilderness that you saw" (v19).** Moses speaks to the new generation as if they had been there: "you saw." In a sense they had, as children. The covenant is passed from one generation to the next as their own story.
- **"Go up, take possession… Do not fear or be dismayed" (v21).** The command was clear and the promise was made. "Do not fear" is one of Deuteronomy's great refrains (1:29; 3:2, 22; 7:18; 20:1; 31:6, 8).
- **"Then all of you came near me and said, 'Let us send men before us'" (v22).** Numbers 13:1–2 says God told Moses to send the spies. Deuteronomy adds that the idea started with the people. Both are true: the people asked, Moses agreed, and God permitted it and gave directions. Moses' point here is that the request itself already showed a reluctance to simply trust God's word.
- **"It is a good land that the LORD our God is giving us" (v25).** Moses recalls only the good part of the report. He passes over the ten spies' fearful words, except as the people repeated them (v28).
- **"Yet you would not go up, but rebelled" (v26).** Moses calls their refusal what it was: rebellion against God's command, not caution.
- **"Because the LORD hated us" (v27).** This is the heart of it. They looked at everything God had done for them, the plagues, the sea, the manna, and concluded that God hated them and had brought them out to die. Unbelief does not only doubt God's power. It misjudges his heart.
- **"Our brothers have made our hearts melt" (v28).** Fear spreads. **"The cities are great and fortified up to heaven"** is an exaggeration that sounds like panic. **The Anakim** were a people known for their great height (Num 13:33). Some think Goliath and his kin were among their descendants who survived in Philistia (Josh 11:22).
- **"The LORD your God who goes before you will himself fight for you" (v30).** Moses had said the same at the Red Sea: "The LORD will fight for you" (Exod 14:14). What God did then he could do again.
- **"The LORD your God carried you, as a man carries his son" (v31).** One of the tenderest images of God in the Old Testament. In the wilderness God was a father carrying a child too small to walk. Hosea uses the same picture (Hos 11:1–3), and Paul quotes the verse in his sermon at Antioch, where some manuscripts of Acts 13:18 read that God "carried them" in the wilderness.
- **"Yet in spite of this word you did not believe the LORD your God" (v32).** The verb is *'aman*, the same word used when Abraham "believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness" (Gen 15:6). Abraham believed and was counted righteous. His descendants did not believe and could not enter. **"Who went before you… in fire by night and in the cloud by day" (v33).** God had gone ahead of them every day, finding them places to camp. He was asking them to follow him one more step.
- **Commentators:** Henry, JFB and Guzik all read v22 as showing the spies were the people's idea. JFB says the proposal "emanated from the people through unbelief," and Henry says God "gave them up to their counsels." Guzik sees this as clarifying Numbers 13:2. Henry sums up: "Thus *they could not enter in because of unbelief*" (Heb 3:19). On v31 he says they were "carried in the arms of divine grace with as much care and tenderness as were ever shown to any child." Guzik notes that Moses leaves out the ten spies' bad report, perhaps because it was too painful to repeat. JFB has no separate notes on vv23–27 or 29–33.

#### The Penalty for Israel's Rebellion (vv34–46)
- **"Not one of these men of this evil generation shall see the good land" (v35).** God's oath against that generation (Num 14:21–23, 28–35). A whole generation shut out, not because the land was too hard, but because they would not trust him.
- **"Except Caleb the son of Jephunneh… because he has wholly followed the LORD" (v36).** "Wholly followed" is literally "filled after," the same phrase as Numbers 14:24 and 32:12. Caleb will receive Hebron, the very land he had walked through as a spy (Josh 14:9–14).
- **"Even with me the LORD was angry on your account" (v37).** Moses mentions his own exclusion here, though the event that caused it came much later, at Meribah (Num 20:10–13). He sets it here because the cause was the same: the people's rebellion drove him to his own sin. He is not shifting the blame. He is showing that he, too, is part of this sad story. He says it again in 3:26 and 4:21.
- **"Joshua the son of Nun, who stands before you, he shall enter. Encourage him" (v38).** Joshua had been Moses' assistant for forty years, literally "the one who stands before you." His name means "the LORD saves," and in Greek it is the same name as Jesus.
- **"Your little ones, who you said would become a prey… they shall go in" (v39).** The bitter irony: the children the parents claimed to be protecting by refusing to go in are the very ones God brings in. **"Who today have no knowledge of good or evil"** means children too young to be held responsible.
- **"We have sinned… We ourselves will go up and fight" (v41).** Too late, the people change their mind. They thought it would be "easy to go up." But obeying God's old command after he has given a new one is not obedience.
- **"Do not go up or fight, for I am not in your midst" (v42).** The only thing that ever made Israel strong was God's presence. Without it, they were just another army.
- **"Presumptuously went up" (v43).** The verb (*zud*) means to act proudly or insolently. Its root sense is to boil or seethe (it is used of Jacob boiling stew in Gen 25:29), pride boiling over. Both refusing to go and going without God are rebellion. One trusts in fear; the other trusts in self.
- **"Chased you as bees do" (v44).** A vivid image: a swarm pouring out, stinging, chasing. The Amorites (Numbers 14:45 also names Amalekites and Canaanites) drove them back to **Hormah**, whose name sounds like the word for "destruction" (*charam*). Numbers 21:1–3 later records Israel's victory at the same place.
- **"You returned and wept before the LORD, but the LORD did not listen" (v45).** Tears for the consequences are not the same as repentance. Esau's tears are a similar warning (Heb 12:17). **"You remained at Kadesh many days" (v46).** The phrase is deliberately vague, as if Moses does not want to count the wasted time.
- **Commentators:** On v37, JFB treats "the LORD was angry with me for your sakes" as a parenthesis, and refers to Psalm 106:32–33, which says it "went ill with Moses on their account." Henry notes they "went presumptuously up to the hill," defying the warning as they had earlier defied the promise, and that God would not listen because they had refused to listen to him. Guzik reads their weeping in v45 as sorrow over the consequences rather than over having grieved God, a shallow repentance. Guzik also takes the word for "explain" in v5 as coming from the idea of digging out, but the lexicon gives the meaning as "make plain, distinct" and notes the link to digging is doubtful. JFB does not mention the bees or comment on v38.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is a sermon about a door that was open and a generation that would not walk through it. Hebrews takes up exactly this story and warns, "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts" (Heb 3:7–19), concluding that "they were unable to enter because of unbelief." Moses could lead the people to the edge but could not bring them in. That job fell to Joshua, whose name in Greek is Jesus. And the Father who "carried you, as a man carries his son" is fully shown in the Son, who calls himself the Good Shepherd who carries his sheep home on his shoulders (Luke 15:5; John 10:11). Where Israel said "the LORD hated us," the cross answers that God so loved the world that he gave his Son (John 3:16).
