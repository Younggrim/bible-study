*Setting:* Jerusalem and then Galilee, from Sunday dawn after the crucifixion to some days or weeks later, spring of AD 30 (some date it AD 33). Jesus died on Friday afternoon and was laid in Joseph of Arimathea's new tomb, and the women watched where he was put (27:61). On Saturday the chief priests had the stone sealed and a guard posted, because they remembered his words, "After three days I will rise" (27:63–66). The Sabbath has now passed. Matthew's account of the resurrection is the shortest of the four Gospels, but it is shaped with care: an empty tomb, an angel's message, the risen Jesus meeting the women, an official cover-up, and a final scene on a mountain in Galilee where Jesus sends his followers to all nations. The Gospel that opened with "Immanuel… God with us" (1:23) closes with "I am with you always."

#### The Resurrection (vv1–10)
- **"After the Sabbath, toward the dawn of the first day of the week" (v1).** Sunday morning, just as light was coming. The first Christians made "the first day of the week" their day of worship because of this morning (Acts 20:7; 1 Cor 16:2), later calling it "the Lord's day" (Rev 1:10). The Greek *opse sabbatōn* can mean "late on the Sabbath," as the KJV has it, but here it plainly means "after the Sabbath," at dawn.
- **"Mary Magdalene and the other Mary went to see the tomb" (v1).** These are the women who stood by the cross and sat opposite the tomb (27:56, 61). Mark and Luke say they brought spices to anoint the body (Mark 16:1; Luke 24:1). Matthew says simply that they came "to see," perhaps because he has already told of the guard and seal, which would have stopped any anointing. They come expecting a corpse. In that culture a woman's testimony counted for little in court (Josephus says as much), so no one inventing a story would have chosen women as the first witnesses. The Tyndale notes make this point, and it is one of the strongest marks of an honest account.
- **"A great earthquake, for an angel of the Lord descended" (v2).** Only Matthew tells this. The earth shook when Jesus died (27:51) and shakes again when he rises. "Angel of the Lord" recalls the messenger who spoke to Joseph at the beginning of the Gospel (1:20; 2:13).
- **"Rolled back the stone and sat on it" (v2).** The stone was not moved to let Jesus out. He was already gone; the risen Lord later appears in locked rooms (John 20:19). It was moved to let witnesses in. And the angel *sat* on it: the great sealed stone that the authorities had secured becomes an angel's seat.
- **"His appearance was like lightning, and his clothing white as snow" (v3).** Language like the vision of the Ancient of Days (Dan 7:9) and like Jesus at the transfiguration (17:2).
- **"The guards trembled and became like dead men" (v4).** The irony is intended. The men set to guard a dead man become like dead men, and the dead man is alive. "Trembled" (*eseisthēsan*) is from the same root as "earthquake" (*seismos*, v2): the earth shook and so did the soldiers.
- **"Do not be afraid, for I know that you seek Jesus who was crucified" (v5).** The "you" is emphatic in Greek, setting the women against the guards. The guards should fear; the women need not. "Who was crucified" is a perfect participle in Greek, carrying the sense "the crucified one." The risen Jesus is still the one who was crucified (Rev 5:6).
- **"He is not here, for he has risen, as he said" (v6).** Three short clauses carry the heart of the gospel. "As he said" points back to the times Jesus foretold it (16:21; 17:23; 20:19). The disciples should not have been surprised. **"Come, see the place where he lay."** The resurrection is something to be looked into, not just believed in the dark. John tells how Peter and John ran to the tomb and saw the folded grave cloths (John 20:3–8).
- **"Go quickly and tell his disciples… he is going before you to Galilee" (v7).** Jesus had promised this the night he was arrested: "after I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee" (26:32). The disciples who deserted him are not written off. They are the first to be told. "Going before" is shepherd language; the shepherd struck down (26:31) now leads his scattered flock again.
- **"With fear and great joy" (v8).** Both at once. Awe at something beyond them, joy at what it means. They "ran."
- **"Jesus met them and said, 'Greetings!'" (v9).** The word is *chairete*, the ordinary Greek greeting, literally "rejoice." The KJV's "All hail" makes it sound grander than it is. After the angel's lightning, the risen Lord greets them in the most everyday way, like "Good morning."
- **"Took hold of his feet and worshiped him" (v9).** They touch real feet. This is no ghost or vision (Luke 24:39). And they worship him, and he accepts it. In Matthew people have bowed to Jesus before (2:11; 14:33), but now worship is the natural response to the risen Lord. Jesus had told Satan, "You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve" (4:10).
- **"Go and tell my brothers" (v10).** After Peter's denial and the others' flight, Jesus calls them "my brothers." It is a word of forgiveness, said before they have even seen him. John records the same: "go to my brothers" (John 20:17). Hebrews says he "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Heb 2:11).
- **How the accounts fit together.** The four Gospels tell the Easter morning differently: the number of women, one angel or two, sitting or standing, the order of who saw what. The differences are real, and careful readers have offered harmonies since early times. They are the kind of differences expected from independent witnesses who agree on the central facts: the tomb was empty early on the first day, women found it, angels announced the resurrection, and Jesus appeared. A story made up together would have been tidier.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads the earthquake as the earth that "resigned him, leaped for joy in his exaltation," and the moved stone as proof that Christ did not break prison "but had a fair and legal discharge, obtained from heaven." On the urgent errand he writes, "Public usefulness to others must be preferred before the pleasure of secret communion with God ourselves." Guzik also stresses that the stone was rolled away to let witnesses in, not to let Jesus out, and he separates resurrection from resuscitation: Lazarus came back to die again, while Jesus rose to a new and lasting life. On "Greetings," Guzik follows France that it is a homely word, and Henry calls it a greeting "wishing all health to those we meet." Both see grace in "my brothers." Henry adds that it makes them "brethren one to another." JFB does not comment on vv1–10 here.

#### The Report of the Guard (vv11–15)
- **"Some of the guard went into the city and told the chief priests" (v11).** While the women run to tell the disciples, the soldiers run to tell the priests. Two reports of the same event go into the city. The guards report to the priests, not to Pilate, which may suggest they were temple guards, or Roman soldiers placed at the priests' disposal (27:65) who knew they were in trouble.
- **"They gave a sufficient sum of money to the soldiers" (v12).** The story began with silver paid to Judas (26:15) and ends with silver paid to soldiers. Money buys the betrayal, and money buys the lie. The bribe broke the law against false witness (Exod 20:16; 23:8).
- **"His disciples came by night and stole him away while we were asleep" (v13).** The story undercuts itself. If they were asleep, how did they know who took the body? And soldiers who slept on duty could face death, so they would hardly admit it unless someone powerful promised to protect them. Grave robbery was common enough that the claim could be believed. A stone inscription from Nazareth, an imperial decree probably from the first century, threatens death for anyone who disturbs tombs or moves bodies; some scholars think it may reflect stories like this one, though its date and origin are debated.
- **"We will satisfy him and keep you out of trouble" (v14).** "Satisfy" here means persuade, and likely with more money. The leaders who claimed to be protecting the people from fraud (27:64) now create one.
- **"This story has been spread among the Jews to this day" (v15).** Matthew wrote perhaps thirty to fifty years later, and the story was still told. About AD 160 Justin Martyr wrote that Jewish teachers were still saying Jesus' disciples "stole him by night from the tomb." The striking thing is what the story admits: the tomb was empty. No one claimed the body was still there. The question was only how it got out.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the guards "Simple, unsophisticated soldiers!" for thinking their report would be welcome, and says the large sum was paid because "the whole case of the Jewish authorities was now at stake." It calls the cover story "clumsy and incredible" and marvels that it lasted so long, citing Justin Martyr. Henry says the soldiers got "probably a great deal more than they gave to Judas." Guzik lists what the stolen-body story would require, every soldier asleep and none woken by a great stone rolling, and argues that the swoon, theft and hallucination theories each take more faith than the Gospel account.

#### The Great Commission (vv16–20)
- **"The eleven disciples went to Galilee, to the mountain to which Jesus had directed them" (v16).** "Eleven" is a quiet reminder of Judas. Matthew does not record the Jerusalem appearances that Luke and John tell (Luke 24; John 20), and he moves straight to Galilee, where Jesus' ministry began and where he had said they would see him. Which mountain is not said. Mountains matter in Matthew: the temptation (4:8), the Sermon on the Mount (5:1), the transfiguration (17:1). Like Moses on Sinai, Jesus gives his final word from a mountain, but this time it is the risen Lord who speaks with all authority.
- **"They worshiped him, but some doubted" (v17).** An honest line. The verb is *distazō*, used elsewhere in the New Testament only of Peter sinking in the waves: "why did you doubt?" (14:31). The lexicon gives "doubt, hesitate." It is not settled unbelief but wavering, a mind divided. Who doubted? Some think "some" means some of the eleven, others that more were there, perhaps the "more than five hundred brothers at one time" of 1 Corinthians 15:6. The Greek allows either. Either way Matthew does not hide it, and Jesus does not send the doubters away. He comes closer (v18).
- **"Jesus came and said to them" (v18).** He approaches them. The answer to hesitant worship is not a rebuke but the presence and word of Jesus.
- **"All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (v18).** This echoes Daniel 7:14, where one like a son of man is "given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him." Satan once offered him "all the kingdoms of the world" for a bow (4:8–9). Jesus refused, went the way of the cross, and now receives far more from the Father. Note the repeated "all" in these verses: all authority, all nations, all that I have commanded, always (literally "all the days").
- **"Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" (v19).** "Therefore" ties the mission to his authority. The main verb is "make disciples" (*mathēteusate*). "Go" is a participle in Greek, but in this kind of construction it shares the force of the command, so the ESV's "Go" is right. It is not merely "as you happen to go." A disciple (*mathētēs*) is a learner and follower, someone who belongs to Jesus and learns to live his way. The commission is not just to win decisions but to make followers.
- **"All nations" (v19).** Earlier Jesus sent the twelve only "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (10:5–6; 15:24). Now the door is thrown open, as Matthew has hinted all along: Gentile women in the genealogy (1:3–6), the magi (2:1–12), the centurion (8:10–11), the Canaanite woman (15:28), the centurion at the cross (27:54). God promised Abraham that in him "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). Here is how it happens.
- **"Baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (v19).** Literally "*into* the name." Baptism marks a person as belonging to the one named, entering a new allegiance (compare "baptized into Moses," 1 Cor 10:2; "baptized into Christ," Gal 3:27). Note that "name" is singular, yet it names three: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. This is one of the clearest statements of the Trinity in the New Testament: one God, three persons. Acts often speaks of baptism "in the name of Jesus" (Acts 2:38). Most see that as describing Christian baptism as distinct from other kinds, not as a different formula, and the early church manual called the Didache (about AD 100) already uses the threefold name. Christians differ on whether baptism is for believers only or also for their children; this verse is read by both sides, and it does not settle the question on its own.
- **"Teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you" (v20).** Not just to know, but to *keep*. Matthew's Gospel is built around five great blocks of Jesus' teaching (chs 5–7, 10, 13, 18, 24–25), and the church now has the task of passing them on. Until now only Jesus has been called the teacher in Matthew. Now his disciples teach in his name.
- **"Behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age" (v20).** The "I" is emphatic. "Always" is literally "all the days," every one of them, ordinary days and hard ones. This closes a frame that opened in the first chapter: "Immanuel… God with us" (1:23), and runs through the middle: "where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them" (18:20). Jesus does not send his people out and then leave them. "The end of the age" is the day he returns (13:39–40; 24:3). Matthew ends without an ascension scene. The last thing heard is the promise.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Even among those that worship there are some that doubt," but Christ does not cast them off. He says the charge is "to disciple all nations," and calls baptism "a sacrament, that is, it is an oath," a pledge of allegiance. On the closing promise: "I am with you, to bear you up, to plead your cause." JFB says none of the eleven doubted, only some of the five hundred if they were present. That is possible, but the Greek does not require it, and the Tyndale notes allow that the eleven themselves may have felt both worship and doubt. JFB prefers "make disciples" to the KJV's "teach," since teaching comes in v20 with a different word, and reads "into the name." It stresses that the "I" is emphatic, and says the closing "Amen" found in some manuscripts is doubtful. Guzik notes that Jesus sent imperfect men, that making disciples is a process and not an instant, and that the singular "name" points to the unity of the three persons. He follows Carson that "always" means "the whole of every day."

#### Pointing to Christ
Matthew began with the promise that Jesus would be "God with us," and ends with Jesus saying it himself. In between is the cross, and here is its answer: the crucified one is not in the tomb. The guards become like dead men while the dead man lives. Money tries to bury the truth, but the empty tomb cannot be bribed. The risen Jesus calls deserters "my brothers," comes near to those who hesitate, and sends them, with all authority behind them and his presence with them every day, to bring all nations to learn from him. The commission is as large as his authority, and the promise is as long as the age.
