*Setting:* "Of Solomon." This is one of only two psalms linked with Solomon (with Psalm 127). The Hebrew preposition can mean "by," "for" or "about," so readers divide. JFB argues Solomon wrote it, and Guzik thinks this is the most natural reading. He suggests Solomon may even have gathered Book Two of the Psalms and written this as its close. Henry follows an older view that David wrote it for Solomon near the end of his life, when he had Solomon proclaimed king (1 Kgs 1:30–40), and so takes v20 as David's last word. Either way, it is a royal psalm: a prayer for the king of David's line, that he would rule with God's own justice, care for the poor, reign over all nations, and bring blessing to the whole earth. No king of Israel, not even Solomon at his best, fully matched the prayer. The Tyndale notes say its righteousness and dominion foreshadow Jesus, the Son of David, and JFB notes that both older Jewish interpreters and most Christian ones refer it to the Messiah. The Aramaic paraphrase of the Psalms (the Targum) reads v1 as "Give the King Messiah your judgments." Psalm 72 closes Book Two of the Psalter, and its final verses (vv18–20) close the whole book. Isaac Watts built the hymn "Jesus Shall Reign Where'er the Sun" on it.

#### Give the King Your Justice (vv1–4)
- **"Give the king your justice, O God, and your righteousness to the royal son!" (v1).** The psalm begins as a prayer, and everything after it is the hoped-for result. "The king" and "the royal son" are the same person, a king who is also heir of the dynasty. JFB: "a very proper title for Christ." "Justice" (*mishpatim*, plural) means right judgments, decisions in real cases. A king in Israel was first of all a judge (1 Kgs 3:9, 28). The king does not have justice of his own; it must be given him by God. That is what Solomon asked for at Gibeon: "an understanding mind to govern your people, that I may discern between good and evil" (1 Kgs 3:9).
- **"May he judge your people with righteousness, and your poor with justice!" (v2).** "Your people… your poor": they belong to God, not the king. The Tyndale notes observe that every ancient Near Eastern king claimed to stand for justice, but Israel's king was held to a higher standard because the LORD was his model.
- **"Let the mountains bear prosperity for the people, and the hills, in righteousness!" (v3).** "Prosperity" is *shalom*, peace and well-being of every kind. JFB notes that mountains are not usually fruitful, so making them "bear" peace like a crop shows how abundant it will be. Justice in the land is pictured as making the land itself flourish (Isa 32:16–17). In the Bible, injustice and a cursed land go together, and so do righteousness and a fruitful one. Solomon's name (*Shelomoh*) sounds like *shalom*.
- **"May he defend the cause of the poor… give deliverance to the children of the needy, and crush the oppressor!" (v4).** "Children of the needy" is a Hebrew way of saying "the needy," as Calvin, cited by Guzik through Spurgeon, explains. A good king takes the side of those who cannot defend themselves and breaks the power of those who exploit them.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the psalm "must refer to the kingdom of the Messiah… because there are many passages in it which cannot be applied to the reign of Solomon," whose reign began in peace but ended in trouble and unrighteousness (1 Kgs 11). Even "the Jewish expositors understand it of the kingdom of the Messiah." He calls Christ "the poor man's King." JFB: "a prayer which is equivalent to a prediction." Guzik ties v1 to Solomon's prayer for wisdom (1 Kgs 3:5–9).

#### Like Rain on the Mown Grass (vv5–7)
- **"May they fear you while the sun endures, and as long as the moon, throughout all generations!" (v5).** The Hebrew says "they will fear you," meaning the people will revere God because of the king's just rule. The Greek Old Testament reads "may he endure," a prayer for the king's long life (compare vv15, 17). The Tyndale notes mention both. Either way, the reign is meant to last as long as sun and moon (89:36–37).
- **"May he be like rain that falls on the mown grass, like showers that water the earth!" (v6).** In Israel the hay was cut in spring, and the rain that followed made the stubble grow again. A good king is that kind of rain: he refreshes and renews. The word for "mown grass" (*gez*) can also mean fleece, and the Greek Old Testament read "fleece," so some early Christians connected the verse with Gideon's fleece (Judg 6:37–40). The lexicon gives "mown grass" or "land to be mown" as the meaning here. David's last words use the same picture: a just ruler is "like the sun shining forth on a cloudless morning, like rain that makes grass to sprout from the earth" (2 Sam 23:3–4).
- **"In his days may the righteous flourish, and peace abound, till the moon be no more!" (v7).** "Flourish" is literally "sprout," like the grass. JFB reads "righteousness" instead of "the righteous," the reading of the Greek Old Testament and a few Hebrew manuscripts. "Till the moon be no more" means forever.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v6 of "the graces and comforts of his Spirit," falling "not on that which is cut down, but that which is left growing, that it may spring again." Horne, cited by Guzik, connects the rain with the Holy Spirit poured out at Pentecost. Clarke, cited by Guzik, argues the word means pastured rather than mown grass, but the lexicon does not support that sense. Henry notes that Solomon built the temple and worship flourished "for some time, under his government, but it did not last long," so "this therefore must point at Christ's kingdom."

#### From Sea to Sea (vv8–11)
- **"May he have dominion from sea to sea, and from the River to the ends of the earth!" (v8).** "The River" in the Old Testament usually means the Euphrates. God promised Abraham the land "from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates" (Gen 15:18; Exod 23:31), and Solomon's rule did reach from the Euphrates to the border of Egypt (1 Kgs 4:21, 24). But "from sea to sea… to the ends of the earth" goes further. JFB: "'ends of the earth' is never used of Palestine, but always of the world." Zechariah repeats this line about the humble king riding on a donkey (Zech 9:9–10).
- **"May desert tribes bow down before him, and his enemies lick the dust!" (v9).** The desert nomads, the hardest people to rule, bow. "Lick the dust" means lie face down in complete submission (Isa 49:23; Mic 7:17). Henry hears an echo of the serpent's curse, "dust you shall eat" (Gen 3:14).
- **"May the kings of Tarshish and of the coastlands render him tribute; may the kings of Sheba and Seba bring gifts!" (v10).** Tarshish was a distant port in the far west of the Mediterranean, possibly in Spain. Jonah tried to flee there (Jonah 1:3). "Coastlands" means the islands and shores of the Mediterranean. Sheba was in south-west Arabia (modern Yemen), the home of the queen who visited Solomon with gold and spices (1 Kgs 10:1–10). Seba was probably in Africa, south of Egypt (Gen 10:7; Isa 43:3). West, south and east: the ends of the known world come with tribute. In Solomon's day, "the whole earth sought the presence of Solomon to hear his wisdom… Every one of them brought his present" (1 Kgs 10:24–25).
- **"May all kings fall down before him, all nations serve him!" (v11).** This goes beyond anything Solomon ruled. It is the hope of Psalm 2:8–12, the nations given as the king's inheritance. The wise men from the east who brought gold, frankincense and myrrh to the child Jesus (Matt 2:11) recall these verses and Isaiah 60:6, where Sheba brings "gold and frankincense."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "no sea, no river, is named, that it might, by these proverbial expressions, intimate the universal monarchy of the Lord Jesus." He applies v10 to Solomon (2 Chr 9:23–24) and to the wise men, "men of the first rank in their own country." He adds, "Before the Lord Jesus we must all either bow or break." VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, likewise reads v8 as universal rule, not a map of specific borders.

#### He Delivers the Needy When He Calls (vv12–14)
- **"For he delivers the needy when he calls, the poor and him who has no helper" (v12).** Here is why the nations bow. They are not conquered by force but drawn by his goodness. JFB: these "are not the conquests of arms, but the influences of humane and peaceful principles" (Isa 9:7; 11:1–9). The king's greatness is measured by how he treats the person with no one else to help.
- **"He has pity on the weak and the needy, and saves the lives of the needy" (v13).** "Pity" (*chus*) means to look with compassion, to spare. The Tyndale notes say the king is not only judge but father to the poor (68:5).
- **"From oppression and violence he redeems their life" (v14).** "Redeems" (*ga'al*) is the kinsman-redeemer word, as Maclaren, cited by Guzik, notes. The king acts as family to those who have none. "Oppression" (*tok*) is fraud or deceit, and "violence" (*chamas*) is open force. Trapp, cited by Guzik, comments on the pairing.
- **"Precious is their blood in his sight" (v14).** The lives of the poor, which others treat as cheap, are costly in the king's eyes. Their deaths matter to him and will be answered for (116:15).
- **Commentators:** Henry applies the two words to Satan, who attacks "both as an old serpent working by deceit… and as a roaring lion working by violence." He says Christ "is not prodigal of their blood." He also notes that the poor are protected "because they have no helper and it is for his honour to help them and because they cry unto him."

#### May People Be Blessed in Him (vv15–17)
- **"Long may he live; may gold of Sheba be given to him!" (v15).** The traditional cry to a king, "Long live the king!" (1 Kgs 1:31, 34, 39). Sheba's gold was famous (1 Kgs 10:10).
- **"May prayer be made for him continually, and blessings invoked for him all the day!" (v15).** His people pray for him. The New Testament also calls believers to pray "for kings and all who are in high positions" (1 Tim 2:1–2). Henry asks how this fits Christ, since he does not need our prayers, and answers that Old Testament believers prayed for his coming, and we now pray for his kingdom to come.
- **"May there be abundance of grain in the land; on the tops of the mountains may it wave" (v16).** Grain growing even on hilltops, where the soil is thin. "Abundance" (*pissah*) appears only here, and its meaning is uncertain; the KJV took it as "a handful of corn." **"May its fruit be like Lebanon"**: waving like the great forests of cedar in Lebanon, the finest timber of the ancient world (1 Kgs 5:6). Poole, cited by Guzik, pictures the heads of grain rustling like cedars. **"May people blossom in the cities like the grass of the field"**: the cities fill with people as the land fills with crops.
- **"May his name endure forever, his fame continue as long as the sun!" (v17).** "Continue" is a rare verb, possibly meaning "sprout" or "have offspring." The king's name keeps growing like a family line. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, sets the empires of the Caesars, Charlemagne and Napoleon, whose names faded, against the name of Jesus.
- **"May people be blessed in him, all nations call him blessed!" (v17).** This is the promise to Abraham: "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3; 22:18). The psalm sees that promise coming true through the king of David's line. Paul says it is fulfilled in Christ, through whom "the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles" (Gal 3:8, 14).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v16 as the gospel: "A handful of that seed, sown in the mountainous and barren soil of the Gentile world, produced a wonderful harvest gathered in to Christ." On v17 he writes, "This plainly refers to the promise made unto the fathers that in the Messiah all the nations of the earth should be blessed." JFB takes the grain and the growing cities as spiritual blessing "set forth by material," and as the growth of the church. Guzik, with the Tyndale notes, links v17 to Abraham. Guzik also notes, following Kidner, that the New Testament never quotes this psalm directly, but its themes match the messianic chapters of Isaiah (11; 60–62) so closely that it should be read the same way.

#### Blessed Be His Glorious Name (vv18–20)
- **"Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who alone does wondrous things" (v18).** This doxology closes not only Psalm 72 but all of Book Two (Pss 42–72). Each of the five books of Psalms ends with a doxology like this (41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52; 106:48; and Ps 150 as the close of the whole). The king can do great things only because God "alone does wondrous things."
- **"May the whole earth be filled with his glory! Amen and Amen!" (v19).** The great hope of the Bible, also stated in Numbers 14:21, Isaiah 6:3 and Habakkuk 2:14. The kingdom of the ideal king is the means by which God's glory fills the earth. The double "Amen" seals it.
- **"The prayers of David, the son of Jesse, are ended" (v20).** An editor's note marking the end of a collection. It cannot mean that David wrote nothing later in the Psalter, since Psalms 86, 101, 103, 108–110 and 138–145 are all headed "of David," as the Tyndale notes point out. Most likely, an earlier collection of David's prayers once ended here, before later books were added. "Ended" (*kalu*) means completed. JFB: "the word never denotes fulfilment." Guzik notes the humble title, "son of Jesse," which reminds the reader that a greater Son of David is coming.
- **Commentators:** Henry follows the old view that "This was the last psalm that ever he penned… he penned it when he lay on his death-bed," and calls v20 his dying wish: "Let God be glorified, let the kingdom of the Messiah be set up… and I have enough." JFB, more cautiously, calls v20 "evidently, from its prosaic style, an addition" by the collector. Henry ties the double Amen to the Lord's Prayer: "Hallowed be thy name; thy kingdom come."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 72 asks for a king no ordinary king could be: perfectly just, tender to the poor, ruling to the ends of the earth, living as long as the sun, and bringing Abraham's blessing to every nation. Solomon began well and ended badly, and every later son of David fell short. The prayer waited for Jesus. He was born king (Matt 2:2), received gold from the east (Matt 2:11), and announced good news to the poor (Luke 4:18). He counts the blood of the weak precious, because he shed his own for them. His kingdom spreads not by the sword but by mercy, and its harvest grows on unlikely ground. One day "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever" (Rev 11:15), and the whole earth will be filled with his glory.
