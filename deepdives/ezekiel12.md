*Setting:* Babylonia, probably late 592 or 591 BC, not long after Ezekiel told the exiles everything he had seen in the temple vision of chapters 8–11 (11:25). That vision ended with God's glory leaving Jerusalem and a promise that the exiles, not the people left in the city, were Israel's future. Chapter 12 opens a long new section (chs 12–24) of sign-acts, parables and oracles that hammer one point until Jerusalem falls: the city is doomed and no one should hope otherwise. Here Ezekiel acts out a refugee's escape by night, and then God answers two popular sayings by which people waved away the prophets' warnings. In Jerusalem King Zedekiah sat on the throne as a vassal Babylon had installed. Within about three years he would break his oath to Nebuchadnezzar and rebel (2 Kings 24:20; Ezek 17:13–15), the siege would begin, and in July 586 BC he would try to escape the city by night, exactly as this chapter describes.

#### Judah's Captivity Symbolized (vv1–28)
- **One heading, four messages.** The ESV gives the chapter a single heading. It holds four separate words from the LORD, each beginning "The word of the LORD came to me" (vv1, 17, 21, 26): the exile's baggage (vv1–16), the bread of trembling (vv17–20), and two proverbs answered (vv21–25, 26–28).
- **"Who have eyes to see, but see not, who have ears to hear, but hear not" (v2).** This is the language of Isaiah's call (Isa 6:9–10) and of Jeremiah (Jer 5:21). The trouble is not that the people cannot understand but that they will not. Jesus quoted the same idea to explain why he taught in parables (Matt 13:13–15). Ezekiel's audience in Babylon is called "a rebellious house" just like the people in Jerusalem (2:5–8). The exiles were not more righteous than the ones left behind. They too clung to the hope of a quick return home.
- **"Prepare for yourself an exile's baggage" (v3).** Literally "vessels of exile," the few things a deportee could carry: a mat, a bowl, a waterskin, a cloak. Assyrian palace reliefs show long lines of captives with bundles on their shoulders. The exiles watching Ezekiel had carried bags like that themselves when they were marched out of Judah five years earlier. **"Perhaps they will understand."** God still hopes. The sign is meant to break through, not merely to condemn.
- **"By day in their sight" (vv3–7).** The phrase "in their sight" appears seven times in vv3–7. The whole thing is staged for an audience. Ezekiel packs during the day so everyone sees the bundle, then leaves at evening.
- **"Dig through the wall, and bring your baggage out through it" (v5).** Houses in Mesopotamia were built of sun-dried mud brick, which could be dug through by hand. Thieves did exactly this (Job 24:16). Ezekiel acts out someone so desperate to escape a besieged city that he cannot use the gate, and breaks out in secret.
- **"You shall cover your face that you may not see the land" (v6).** The covered face could mean disguise, shame, or grief at leaving home forever. Verse 12 adds another meaning: the escaping prince will cover his face, and verse 13 explains why that is grimly fitting. **"For I have made you a sign for the house of Israel."** The word is *mopheth*, a portent or warning sign. Isaiah and his children were "signs and portents in Israel" (Isa 8:18). Ezekiel's own life has become the message.
- **"And I did as I was commanded" (v7).** Ezekiel obeys to the letter, with no protest. It must have looked absurd. A grown man, a priest, digging through his own wall with his hands at dusk and crawling out with a bundle on his shoulder.
- **"Has not the house of Israel… said to you, 'What are you doing?'" (v9).** The sign worked to the extent of getting people to ask. The next morning God gives Ezekiel the explanation.
- **"This oracle concerns the prince in Jerusalem" (v10).** There is a pun in the Hebrew. "Oracle" is *massa'*, which also means "burden," something carried, and "prince" is *nasi'*, from the same verb, "to lift" or "carry." The burden is on the one who will soon carry his own burden on his shoulder (v12). Ezekiel calls Zedekiah "prince," not "king." Many exiles still counted the captive Jehoiachin as the real king (Ezekiel dates his book by Jehoiachin's exile, 1:2), and Zedekiah ruled by Babylon's appointment. Ezekiel's word choice quietly makes the same point.
- **"I am a sign for you: as I have done, so shall it be done to them" (v11).** Ezekiel's neighbors in Babylon had already been through deportation. Their relatives in Jerusalem were about to go through it too.
- **"The prince who is among them shall lift his baggage upon his shoulder at dusk, and shall go out" (v12).** This is fulfilled in almost every detail. When the Babylonians broke into Jerusalem in July 586 BC, "all the men of war fled by night by the way of the gate between the two walls, by the king's garden," and Zedekiah fled toward the Jordan valley (2 Kings 25:4; Jer 39:4; 52:7). Henry sums it up: "The judgments of God can turn a prince into a porter."
- **"I will spread my net over him, and he shall be taken in my snare" (v13).** Babylon caught him on the plains of Jericho, but God says the net is his. Nebuchadnezzar was only the hunter's tool (17:20; 32:3).
- **"I will bring him to Babylon… yet he shall not see it, and he shall die there" (v13).** This was a riddle when it was spoken. Jeremiah had told Zedekiah, "your eyes shall see the eyes of the king of Babylon" (Jer 32:4; 34:3). Ezekiel says he will go to Babylon but not see it. Both came true. Zedekiah was brought before Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah and looked him in the eyes. There he watched his sons executed, then "they put out the eyes of Zedekiah and bound him in chains and took him to Babylon" (2 Kings 25:6–7), where he died in prison (Jer 52:11). The covered face of v12 becomes a covered face for life. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the first century AD, says that Zedekiah heard both prophecies, thought they contradicted each other, and so believed neither (Antiquities 10.7.2). Whether Josephus had a source beyond the two books is uncertain, but the story fits: an apparent contradiction became an excuse not to listen.
- **"I will scatter toward every wind all who are around him" (v14).** His bodyguard and army scattered when he was caught (2 Kings 25:5). This echoes the third part of Ezekiel's hair, scattered to the wind with a sword behind it (5:2, 12).
- **"But I will let a few of them escape… that they may declare all their abominations among the nations" (v16).** God spares a few, not as a reward but as witnesses. Wherever they go, they will explain why Jerusalem fell: not because the LORD was weak, but because Israel was unfaithful. The nations will learn that the God of Israel is holy and just.
- **"Eat your bread with quaking, and drink water with trembling and with anxiety" (v18).** The second sign is short. Ezekiel eats an ordinary meal while shaking, perhaps with visible trembling, like a man who does not know if he will be alive tomorrow. It recalls the rationed siege bread of 4:16. **"The people of the land" (v19)** here means the exiles listening to him. The message concerns **"the inhabitants of Jerusalem in the land of Israel."** Ezekiel is telling his neighbors what their families back home will go through.
- **"On account of the violence of all those who dwell in it" (v19).** Once more the reason is given: violence, injustice and bloodshed (7:23; 8:17; 9:9). The land will be **"stripped of all it contains"**, emptied of its people and goods. Henry draws the lesson: "The decay of virtue in a nation brings on a decay of every thing else."
- **"What is this proverb… 'The days grow long, and every vision comes to nothing'?" (v22).** A proverb (*mashal*) is a popular saying. This one sneered at the prophets. Isaiah, Micah, Jeremiah and others had been warning of disaster for over a century, and Jerusalem still stood. The prophets of doom kept talking, the years kept passing, and nothing happened. God's patience had been mistaken for proof that he did not mean it. The ESV footnote points out that "you" is plural: this was a saying going around among the exiles too.
- **"I will put an end to this proverb… The days are near, and the fulfillment of every vision" (v23).** God answers the proverb with a new one, using almost the same words in reverse. Literally, "the days are near, and the word of every vision." Where they said the days were long and the vision empty, he says the days are near and every vision will be fulfilled.
- **"There shall be no more any false vision or flattering divination" (v24).** "Flattering" means smooth or slippery (*ḥalaq*), telling people what they wanted to hear. Divination meant trying to read the future by omens, lots or signs. Jerusalem was full of prophets promising peace (Jer 23:16–17; 28:1–4). Once judgment comes, their smooth words will be exposed for what they are. This verse leads into chapter 13, which is a full attack on the false prophets.
- **"I will speak the word that I will speak, and it will be performed" (v25).** God's word is not just a forecast. It is an act. He speaks and it happens (Isa 55:10–11). **"In your days, O rebellious house."** Not in some distant future, but in their own lifetimes.
- **"The vision that he sees is for many days from now, and he prophesies of times far off" (v27).** This is the second saying, and it is subtler. These people did not deny the prophecy. They postponed it. "Maybe it's true, but not in our time." Hezekiah had once responded to a prophecy of exile with relief that it would not come in his own lifetime (2 Kings 20:19; Isa 39:8).
- **"None of my words will be delayed any longer" (v28).** God's answer is the same to both: the time is now. Within five or six years, both sayings were silenced by the fall of the city.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the sign as God teaching through the eyes when the ears had failed, and says "If but one soul be awakened to consider, our care and pains will be well bestowed." JFB says the people's blindness was "the result of perversity, not incapacity." Guzik and JFB both identify the prince as Zedekiah, and Guzik explains the title as a quiet refusal to call him king while Jehoiachin lived. JFB calls the covered face "a humiliation for a king!" and passes on Josephus's claim that Ezekiel "sent a copy of this prophecy to Zedekiah." Henry reconciles Ezekiel with Jeremiah exactly: "he did see the king of Babylon at Riblah," but not the city of Babylon. On the proverbs, Henry writes, "Their putting the evil day far from them does but provoke God to bring it the sooner upon them." JFB calls the first proverb an "infidel scoff" and the second "carnal security," and links them to later scoffing at Christ's return. Guzik notes that God's patience had become the people's excuse, and passes on Spurgeon's warning against postponing turning to God.

#### Pointing to Christ
Zedekiah escaped through a hole in the wall at night, carrying his own bundle, and was caught by God's net. He went to Babylon blind. Centuries later another King of the line of David went out of Jerusalem carrying a burden on his shoulder. Jesus carried his own cross out of the city (John 19:17), not to escape judgment but to take it. Zedekiah was blinded. Jesus opened the eyes of the blind (John 9:39), giving sight to "a rebellious house" that had eyes but did not see. And the scoffers of Ezekiel's day still have their successors. Peter warns of people who say, "Where is the promise of his coming?" and he answers as Ezekiel did: the Lord is not slow but patient, "not wishing that any should perish," and the day will come (2 Pet 3:3–10).
