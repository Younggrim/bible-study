*Setting:* Moses is still on Mount Sinai receiving the plans for the tabernacle. The house and its main furniture were described in chapters 25–27, the priests' clothing in chapter 28, and their ordination in chapter 29, which ended with God's great purpose: "I will dwell among the people of Israel" (29:45). Chapter 30 adds a set of further items that all have to do with the priests' ongoing service: a small gold altar for incense, a tax paid by every Israelite man, a bronze basin for washing, and the recipes for the holy anointing oil and incense. Each one keeps the same point in front of the reader. God lives among his people, and coming near him must be done his way.

#### The Altar of Incense (vv1–10)
- **"An altar on which to burn incense" (v1).** Why is this altar described here and not with the lampstand and table in chapter 25? The Tyndale notes suggest it is placed here because it belongs with the priests' daily work described in vv7–10. Others note that chapters 25–29 were about how God comes to meet his people, and the incense altar is about how they answer him in prayer.
- **"A cubit… its length, and a cubit its breadth… two cubits… its height" (v2).** A cubit was about 18 inches (45 cm), so the altar was a small stand, about 18 inches square and 3 feet (90 cm) high. Like the bronze altar it had **horns** at the corners, made **of one piece with it**. It was acacia wood **overlaid with pure gold** (v3), which is why it is often called "the golden altar" (39:38; Rev 8:3). It had rings and poles for carrying (vv4–5), like the ark, the table and the bronze altar. Everything in the tabernacle was made to move with the people.
- **"In front of the veil that is above the ark… in front of the mercy seat" (v6).** The altar stood in the Holy Place, the first room, right before the curtain that hid the Most Holy Place. Smoke from it would rise toward the ark on the other side, where God's presence was enthroned. Hebrews 9:4 lists "the golden altar of incense" along with the ark in the inner room. Some think the Greek word there means a censer rather than the altar. Others think Hebrews links the altar with the inner room because of what it served, not where it stood. Exodus is clear that it stood outside the veil.
- **"Every morning when he dresses the lamps he shall burn it, and… at twilight" (vv7–8).** Incense went up at the same times as the daily lambs (29:38–42), morning and evening, every day. Lamp and incense went together: as the priest trimmed the wicks and refilled the oil, he burned the incense. The word **"regular"** is *tamid*, "continual," the same word used for the daily offering. This is the scene in Luke 1, when the priest Zechariah "was chosen by lot to enter the temple of the Lord and burn incense," while "the whole multitude of the people were praying outside at the hour of incense" (Luke 1:9–10).
- **Incense and prayer.** Scripture itself makes the link. David prays, "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you" (Ps 141:2). In Revelation the elders hold "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (Rev 5:8), and an angel offers incense "with the prayers of all the saints on the golden altar before the throne" (Rev 8:3–4). Practically, incense also filled the tent with a sweet smell in a place where animals were slaughtered every day.
- **"You shall not offer unauthorized incense on it" (v9).** "Unauthorized" is *zarah*, "strange" or "foreign," incense made any other way than the recipe in vv34–38. The same word describes the "unauthorized fire" that Nadab and Abihu offered, and they died for it (Lev 10:1–2). No sacrifices, grain or drink offerings were to go on this altar. It was for incense only.
- **"Aaron shall make atonement on its horns once a year" (v10).** On the Day of Atonement, blood was put on this altar's horns (Lev 16:18–19 describes the altar cleansing; Lev 4:7 shows blood put on the incense altar's horns for a priest's sin). Even the place of prayer needed cleansing. The sins of the people who prayed there defiled it. **"It is most holy to the LORD."**
- **Commentators:** Henry says the placement, facing the mercy seat behind the veil, teaches that we pray by faith toward a throne we cannot see, and he writes that "prayer is the true incense." He draws a practical rule: "we must keep up stated times for prayer every day, morning and evening, at least." He also sees the tending of the lamps as Scripture-reading that goes with prayer. Henry reads the bronze altar as Christ's death and the golden altar as his intercession in heaven. JFB likewise calls incense "a symbol or emblem of prayer" and the priest a type of Christ's intercession, and notes that "Aaron" here stands for the priesthood, so that in later times any priest might burn it (as Zechariah did). Guzik's point is that the blood on the altar's horns shows that prayer rests on atonement. We do not pray to be saved. We pray because of the cross.

#### The Census Tax (vv11–16)
- **"When you take the census… each shall give a ransom for his life" (v12).** "Ransom" is *kopher*, the price paid to redeem a life. The same word appears in Exodus 21:30, where a man whose ox killed someone could pay a ransom instead of dying. A census in the ancient world was usually taken for war or taxes. Counting people meant claiming them. This law reminds Israel that the people belong to God, not to the leader doing the counting.
- **"That there be no plague among them" (v12).** This is the background to David's census in 2 Samuel 24, which brought a plague that killed 70,000. The text there does not say exactly what David did wrong, but pride in his own strength seems to be at the heart of it. Many think he also ignored this ransom.
- **"Half a shekel according to the shekel of the sanctuary (the shekel is twenty gerahs)" (v13).** The shekel was a weight, not a coin, about 0.4 ounce (11.5 grams). Half a shekel of silver was a modest sum, affordable for anyone. "The shekel of the sanctuary" was the standard weight kept at the tabernacle, so no one could cheat with light weights.
- **"From twenty years old and upward" (v14).** Twenty was the age for military service (Num 1:3). This was a count of men able to fight.
- **"The rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less" (v15).** Every life was worth the same ransom, because every person needed the same redemption. Nobody could buy more standing with God, and nobody was too poor to be counted. Peter makes a related point: we were ransomed "not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19).
- **"Give it for the service of the tent of meeting" (v16).** The silver was used for the tabernacle's foundations: 603,550 men paid, and their silver went into the sockets that held up the frames and the veil (38:25–28). Israel's house of worship literally stood on ransom money. Later this became a regular temple tax (2 Chr 24:6, 9; in Nehemiah's day a third of a shekel, Neh 10:32). In Jesus' time it was the "two-drachma tax," and Jesus told Peter to pay it for both of them from the coin in a fish's mouth, though as the Son he owed nothing (Matt 17:24–27).
- **"That it may bring the people of Israel to remembrance before the LORD" (v16).** Like the names on the high priest's shoulders (28:12), the silver was a standing reminder before God of his ransomed people.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees God's impartiality in the equal amount: "the rich have as much need of Christ as the poor, and the poor are as welcome to him as the rich." He is careful to add that "money indeed cannot make an atonement for the soul," though it can serve the worship that points to the one who does. He also notes the debate over whether this was a one-time payment or yearly, and links it to the tax Christ paid. JFB treats it as a required ransom, not a free-will gift, and also connects it to the tax Jesus paid. Guzik adds a caution: the flat half shekel was a ransom, not a model for Christian giving, which the New Testament describes in proportion to what one has (1 Cor 16:2).

#### The Bronze Basin (vv17–21)
- **"A basin of bronze, with its stand of bronze, for washing" (v18).** No size or shape is given, unlike every other piece of furniture. We learn later that it was made from the bronze mirrors of "the ministering women who ministered in the entrance of the tent of meeting" (38:8). Polished bronze was what ancient people used for mirrors. Solomon's temple later had a great bronze "sea" and ten smaller basins (1 Kgs 7:23–39).
- **"Between the tent of meeting and the altar" (v18).** The basin stood in the courtyard, between the bronze altar and the door of the tent. A priest coming from the altar, where sin was dealt with by blood, had to wash before going into the Holy Place.
- **"Aaron and his sons shall wash their hands and their feet" (v19).** At ordination they were washed all over (29:4). This was the daily washing. Their hands handled the sacrifices, and their feet walked barefoot on the dust of the courtyard and the holy ground of God's tent (compare 3:5).
- **"So that they may not die" (vv20–21).** Said twice. Washing was not a matter of personal cleanliness. It was a matter of life and death in the presence of a holy God. Psalm 24:3–4 asks who may stand in God's holy place and answers, "He who has clean hands and a pure heart."
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws the line to John 13, where Jesus tells Peter that one who has bathed needs only to wash his feet. There is a once-for-all cleansing, and there is the daily cleansing from the dirt picked up along the way, which Guzik connects to the washing of the word (Eph 5:26). JFB thinks the basin's "foot" was probably a lower trough for washing hands and feet rather than a pedestal, and notes that under the gospel all believers are priests (1 Pet 2:9), so the call to clean hands is for every Christian.

#### The Anointing Oil and Incense (vv22–38)
- **"Take the finest spices" (v23).** **Liquid myrrh**, literally "flowing myrrh," was a gum from a thorny shrub of Arabia and East Africa, prized as perfume. It was one of the gifts of the magi (Matt 2:11) and was used at Jesus' burial (John 19:39). **Cinnamon** and **cassia** come from the bark of trees that grow in South and Southeast Asia, so they would have been imported through long trade routes. **Aromatic cane** was probably a fragrant reed (compare "sweet cane from a distant land," Jer 6:20).
- **"500 shekels… 250… 250… 500… and a hin of olive oil" (vv23–24).** Using about 0.4 ounce (11.5 g) per shekel, the spices weighed roughly 38 pounds (17 kg) in all, and a hin was about a gallon (3.7 liters). The spices were likely steeped in the oil, or boiled with it, and then strained out, leaving a richly scented oil. This was a costly blend.
- **"Blended as by the perfumer" (v25).** Perfume-making was a skilled trade. Egypt was famous for it, and Israel had lived there for centuries.
- **"Anoint the tent of meeting and the ark… the table… the lampstand… the altar of incense… the altar of burnt offering… the basin" (vv26–28).** Every piece is named. When Moses carried this out, the tabernacle and everything in it were anointed and made holy (40:9–11; Lev 8:10–11). **"Whatever touches them will become holy" (v29)**, the same phrase used of the altar in 29:37.
- **"You shall anoint Aaron and his sons" (v30).** The same oil that made the furniture holy made the priests holy. They belonged to God as much as the altar did.
- **"It shall not be poured on the body of an ordinary person, and you shall make no other like it" (v32).** This oil was not for the cosmetics trade or for personal pleasure. **"Shall be cut off from his people" (v33)** means removed from the community, either by death or by being expelled. Interpreters differ on which, and on whether God himself or the community carried it out. The severity shows how seriously God guarded what was set apart for him.
- **"Stacte, and onycha, and galbanum… with pure frankincense" (v34).** **Stacte** (*nataf*, "drops") was a resin that oozed in drops, perhaps from the storax tree or a type of myrrh. **Onycha** (*shecheleth*) appears only here. Most scholars, following the Greek and Latin translations, think it was the shell flap of a kind of sea snail, which gives off a strong smell when burned. **Galbanum** was a gum with a sharp, bitter smell. On its own it was not pleasant, but mixed with the others it deepened and fixed the scent. **Frankincense**, a white resin from trees in southern Arabia, was the most famous incense of the ancient world, also brought by the magi.
- **"Seasoned with salt, pure and holy" (v35).** Salt helped the incense burn and kept it pure. Salt also had covenant meaning: every grain offering was to be seasoned with "the salt of the covenant with your God" (Lev 2:13).
- **"Beat some of it very small, and put part of it before the testimony" (v36).** The incense was ground fine so it would burn evenly and give out its full fragrance.
- **"You shall not make for yourselves… Whoever makes any like it to use as perfume shall be cut off" (vv37–38).** Like the oil, the incense was holy and could not be copied for personal use. Fragrance in God's house was not a luxury for the priests to take home.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "that which is God's peculiar must not be used as a common thing," and warns against treating sacred things lightly. He mentions the Jewish tradition that Moses' oil lasted for centuries, but prefers the view that it was made again as needed. On the incense being beaten very small, he sees a picture of Christ, bruised as a sweet-smelling sacrifice. JFB describes each ingredient and admits Israel could have learned perfume-making in Egypt, but stresses that this recipe came from God. JFB puts the spices at about 120 pounds, much higher than most modern estimates, which depend on the weight of the shekel. Guzik sees the oil as a picture of the Holy Spirit and draws the warning that the Spirit's work must not be faked with human substitutes or used for personal gain. He adds that the incense may also have covered the smell of so many sacrifices.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every item in this chapter finds its meaning in Jesus. He is the ransom paid for every life alike, rich and poor, "the Son of Man came… to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). He is the priest who lives forever at the golden altar, where "he always lives to make intercession" (Heb 7:25), so that the prayers of his people rise as incense before God. He washes his people's feet and cleanses them by his word. And he is the Anointed One himself. The name "Christ" means "anointed," and his anointing was not with myrrh and cassia but with the Spirit without measure (Luke 4:18; John 3:34). Through him that holy anointing now rests on all who belong to him (2 Cor 1:21–22).
