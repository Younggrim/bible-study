*Setting:* This chapter answers the prayer that closed chapter 14. During a terrible drought Jeremiah had pleaded for Judah: "Do not spurn us, for your name's sake… We set our hope on you" (14:21–22). God's reply is the hardest word yet: not even Moses and Samuel could turn him now. The judgment is fixed. Then the chapter turns inward. Jeremiah, worn down by years of rejection, curses the day he was born and accuses God of being a dried-up stream. God answers him too, not with comfort alone but with a call to repent and a renewal of the promises made when Jeremiah was first called (1:18–19). The setting is probably Jehoiakim's reign (609–598 BC).

#### The LORD Will Not Relent (vv1–9)
- **"Though Moses and Samuel stood before me" (v1).** These two were Israel's great intercessors. Moses stood between God and the people after the golden calf and again at Kadesh, and God relented (Exod 32:11–14; Num 14:13–20). Samuel prayed at Mizpah and the Philistines were routed (1 Sam 7:8–10; 12:23). Psalm 99:6 names them together: "Moses and Aaron… Samuel also… They called to the LORD, and he answered them." Ezekiel makes a similar point with Noah, Daniel and Job (Ezek 14:14). The problem is not that Jeremiah prays poorly. No one's prayers could change this outcome.
- **"My heart would not turn toward this people" (v1).** Literally "my soul." **"Send them out of my sight, and let them go!"** Like a ruler dismissing people from his court. The phrase anticipates the exile.
- **"Where shall we go?" (v2).** When they ask, the answer is a grim four-part sentence: pestilence (literally "death," which here means plague, as in 18:21), sword, famine and captivity. Each group goes to the fate marked out for it. Zechariah 11:9 has the same chilling pattern.
- **"Four kinds of destroyers" (v3).** The sword kills, then dogs drag the bodies, and birds and wild animals finish them. In Israel, being left unburied for scavengers was the ultimate disgrace (Deut 28:26; 1 Kgs 14:11). The curse covers not only death but dishonor after death.
- **"A horror to all the kingdoms of the earth" (v4).** Moses had warned of this (Deut 28:25). The phrase means a sight that makes other nations shudder.
- **"Because of what Manasseh the son of Hezekiah… did in Jerusalem" (v4).** Manasseh reigned fifty-five years (about 697–642 BC), the longest reign in Judah's history. He rebuilt the pagan altars his father Hezekiah had torn down, put an idol in the temple, practiced sorcery, burned his own son as an offering, and "shed very much innocent blood, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another" (2 Kgs 21:1–16). Kings says that the final fall came "on account of the sins of Manasseh… and also for the innocent blood that he had shed… and the LORD would not pardon" (2 Kgs 23:26; 24:3–4). Mentioning his famous godly father Hezekiah sharpens the tragedy.
- **But Manasseh repented.** Chronicles records that in his later years, as a captive in Babylon, Manasseh humbled himself, prayed, and was restored (2 Chr 33:10–16). God forgave him personally. Yet the damage he did to the nation lasted. A generation had been trained in his ways, and even Josiah's reform could not undo it (2 Kgs 23:26). One man's forgiveness does not erase the harm his sin did to others. And the people who suffered in Jeremiah's day were not punished for Manasseh's sin alone. They had made it their own (v6).
- **"Who will have pity on you, O Jerusalem?" (v5).** No one will turn aside even to ask, "How are you?", literally "to ask about your *shalom*." The city that refused God's peace will have no one to wish it peace.
- **"You have rejected me… you keep going backward" (v6).** Judah has not stood still. She has gone in reverse (7:24). **"I am weary of relenting."** God had held back judgment many times (26:19; Exod 32:14). "Relenting" (*nicham*) means to change course, to have pity, to hold back a threatened punishment. Scripture speaks this way about God in human terms. He does not lose patience the way people do, but there comes a point where his long patience, repeatedly ignored, gives way to the judgment he had warned of (Rom 2:4–5).
- **"I have winnowed them with a winnowing fork in the gates of the land" (v7).** Winnowing meant tossing threshed grain into the wind with a fork, so the chaff blew away. "The gates of the land" may mean its borders, the exits through which the people will be blown into exile. John the Baptist uses the same picture of judgment (Matt 3:12).
- **"I have made their widows more in number than the sand of the seas" (v8).** A shocking reversal. God had promised Abraham descendants like the sand of the sea (Gen 22:17). Now it is the widows who are uncountable. **"A destroyer at noonday."** In the heat of midday armies usually rested. An attack at noon was unexpected.
- **"She who bore seven has grown feeble" (v9).** Seven sons was the picture of the fullest blessing a mother could have (1 Sam 2:5; Ruth 4:15). Now she loses them all and faints away. **"Her sun went down while it was yet day."** Darkness at noon: her joy ends while it should still be at its height (Amos 8:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Manasseh was named "the son of Hezekiah because his relation to so good a father was a great aggravation of his sin," and that "reprieves are not pardons." JFB says the effects of Manasseh's sins remained "even after he himself has received repentance and forgiveness," and "How much evil one bad man can cause!" Guzik cites Kidner's similar point: Manasseh found personal forgiveness, but his legacy lived on. On "weary of relenting," JFB explains that God speaks "in condescension to human modes of thought." JFB and the Tyndale notes both read "death" in v2 as deadly plague.

#### Jeremiah's Complaint (vv10–21)
- **"Woe is me, my mother, that you bore me" (v10).** Jeremiah turns from the nation's doom to his own misery. Job cursed the day of his birth (Job 3), and Jeremiah will do so even more bitterly in 20:14–18. God had told him he was set apart before he was born (1:5). Now he wishes he had not been.
- **"A man of strife and contention to the whole land" (v10).** He did not go looking for a fight. He was simply faithful, and the whole land fought him. **"I have not lent, nor have I borrowed, yet all of them curse me."** Lending and borrowing were the classic sources of bitter quarrels, especially when interest was charged (Exod 22:25). Jeremiah has done nothing to earn this hatred.
- **"Have I not set you free for their good?" (v11).** The ESV footnote says the Hebrew of this verse is uncertain. The written Hebrew text and the reading tradition differ by one letter. One reading says "I have set you free (or strengthened you) for good," and another, as the KJV takes it, "it shall be well with your remnant," that is, with the rest of your life. **"Have I not pleaded for you before the enemy?"** can also be read "I will make the enemy plead with you." That second reading came true: King Zedekiah later begged Jeremiah to pray for the city (21:1–2; 37:3), and after the city fell, the Babylonian commander treated Jeremiah with kindness and set him free (39:11–14; 40:1–6).
- **"Can one break iron, iron from the north, and bronze?" (v12).** This short verse is also obscure. It may mean that Judah cannot break the hard iron army coming from the north, Babylon. Or it may be a promise to Jeremiah: God made him "an iron pillar, and bronze walls" (1:18), and no one will break him. Both fit the chapter. The best iron of the time came from the region of the Black Sea, far to the north.
- **"Your wealth and your treasures I will give as spoil, without price" (v13).** This is addressed to the nation. God will hand over Judah's treasures for nothing, as though they were worthless. Verses 13–14 appear again, almost word for word, in 17:3–4.
- **"I will make you serve your enemies in a land that you do not know" (v14).** The standard Hebrew text reads "I will make you pass over with your enemies" (so the KJV). The ESV follows some Hebrew manuscripts and ancient versions that read "serve," matching 17:4. The two Hebrew words differ by one similar-looking letter, and the sense is the same: exile.
- **"O LORD, you know; remember me and visit me" (v15).** Jeremiah pleads his case. **"Take vengeance for me on my persecutors."** As in 11:20 and 12:3, he asks God, not himself, to deal with his enemies. **"In your forbearance take me not away."** God is being patient with the wicked. Jeremiah asks that this patience not give them enough time to kill him. **"For your sake I bear reproach."** Psalm 69:7 says almost the same, a psalm the New Testament applies to Jesus (John 2:17; Rom 15:3).
- **"Your words were found, and I ate them" (v16).** Jeremiah remembers the early joy of his calling. God had put his words in Jeremiah's mouth (1:9). Ezekiel and John were later told to eat a scroll (Ezek 3:1–3; Rev 10:9–10). "Were found" may also recall the Book of the Law found in the temple in Josiah's day (2 Kgs 22:8–13), early in Jeremiah's ministry. **"Your words became to me a joy and the delight of my heart, for I am called by your name."** God's word was once his food and his gladness. Psalm 119:103 says the same: "How sweet are your words to my taste."
- **"I did not sit in the company of revelers… I sat alone, because your hand was upon me" (v17).** Being God's spokesman cut Jeremiah off from ordinary life. He could not join the parties of people whose doom he had to announce. In 16:2–9 God will forbid him even to marry, mourn or feast. **"You had filled me with indignation."** He carried God's anger at sin, and it made him lonely (6:11).
- **"Why is my pain unceasing, my wound incurable?" (v18).** He uses the very words he had used for the nation's wound (10:19; 14:17; 30:12). **"Will you be to me like a deceitful brook, like waters that fail?"** This is the boldest line in the book. A "deceitful brook" was a wadi, a stream bed that runs full in the rainy season and is bone dry in summer. A traveler counting on it would find nothing (Job 6:15–20). In 2:13 God called himself "the fountain of living waters." Now Jeremiah asks if that fountain has turned out to be a dry wadi. It is close to accusing God of lying.
- **"If you return, I will restore you, and you shall stand before me" (v19).** God does not answer the accusation with sympathy alone. He calls his prophet to repent. The word "return" (*shuv*) is the same word Jeremiah has preached to the nation again and again (3:12, 14, 22). Now the preacher must take his own medicine. To "stand before" God was the language of a servant in a king's presence, and of a prophet (1 Kgs 17:1). Jeremiah's office is at stake.
- **"If you utter what is precious, and not what is worthless, you shall be as my mouth" (v19).** He must separate his true message from the bitter, self-pitying words he has just spoken, the way a refiner separates precious metal from dross (6:29–30). Then he will again be God's "mouth," the way Moses' brother Aaron was to be Moses' mouth (Exod 4:16).
- **"They shall turn to you, but you shall not turn to them" (v19).** A play on the same verb. Jeremiah is to *turn* to God. The people may *turn* to him for help, but he must never *turn* to their ways to be accepted.
- **"I will make you to this people a fortified wall of bronze" (v20).** God renews the exact promise of Jeremiah's call (1:18–19): "They will fight against you, but they shall not prevail over you, for I am with you… to deliver you." Nothing new is promised. The old promise is enough. It is a striking answer: the cure for Jeremiah's despair is not a fresh word but trust in the first one.
- **"I will deliver you… and redeem you from the grasp of the ruthless" (v21).** "Redeem" (*padah*) is the word for ransoming someone, the word used of God's rescue from Egypt (Deut 7:8). Jeremiah was never spared opposition, but he was never destroyed by it. He outlived the kings who persecuted him.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jeremiah was "not a man striving, but a man striven with," and notes that the Prince of Peace himself was "a sign spoken against" (Luke 2:34). He gently calls the cry of v10 an "infirmity," while hoping it was meant as no more than a lament. JFB describes Jeremiah as "of a peculiarly sensitive temperament," yet enabled by the Spirit to speak at the cost of being wounded, and he reads v11 as fulfilled when the Babylonians treated Jeremiah well (39:11–12; 40:4–5). JFB also links "for thy sake I have suffered rebuke" to Psalm 69 and to Christ. Guzik passes on Kidner's observation that God gives Jeremiah no release from his calling, only a renewal of it, and Spurgeon's remark that working for God is hard, but working and waiting is harder still. The Tyndale notes are frank that Jeremiah, worn down by persecution, was beginning to think like the rebellious people he preached to, which is why God calls him to return.

#### Pointing to Christ
Not even Moses and Samuel could stand between God and Judah. But a greater intercessor has stood in the gap: "Christ Jesus is the one who died… who indeed is interceding for us" (Rom 8:34), and "he always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25). Jeremiah bore reproach for God's sake and was hated without cause, words Jesus applied to himself (John 15:25; Ps 69:4). Unlike Jeremiah, Jesus never accused his Father of failing him, though he cried out in deeper desolation than any prophet. And Jeremiah's fear that God might be a dry brook is answered in him: "whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again" (John 4:14).
