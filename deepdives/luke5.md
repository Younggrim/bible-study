*Setting:* About AD 28–29, early in Jesus' Galilean ministry. Chapter 4 ended with Jesus rejected at Nazareth, then teaching and healing in Capernaum, a fishing town on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee, and insisting he "must preach the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns as well" (4:43). He has already stayed in Simon's house and healed Simon's mother-in-law (4:38–39), so Simon knows him. Chapter 5 shows the kingdom gathering people: fishermen, a leper, a paralyzed man and a tax collector. It also starts the long conflict with the Pharisees and scribes, built around three questions: who can forgive sins, why Jesus eats with sinners, and why his disciples do not fast.

#### Jesus Calls the First Disciples (vv1–11)
- **"The lake of Gennesaret" (v1).** This is Luke's name for the Sea of Galilee, taken from the fertile plain of Gennesaret on its northwest shore. Luke, who had seen the Mediterranean, never calls it a "sea." It is a freshwater lake about 13 miles long and 8 wide, sitting nearly 700 feet below sea level, with a fishing industry that fed the region and exported salted fish.
- **"To hear the word of God" (v1).** Luke describes Jesus' teaching with the phrase the Old Testament used for the prophets' message, and that Acts will use for the apostles' preaching (Acts 4:31; 6:7). The crowd is not only curious. They are hungry for God's word.
- **"Washing their nets" (v2).** Night fishing on the lake was done with nets, and cleaning and mending them came after every shift. These men are finished for the day, tired and empty-handed.
- **"He sat down and taught the people from the boat" (v3).** Rabbis sat to teach (4:20). A boat a little offshore kept the crowd from pressing in, and the water carried his voice to the people on the slope of the shore.
- **"Put out into the deep and let down your nets" (v4).** Everything about this sounds wrong to a fisherman. Fish on this lake were caught at night in the shallows, and by day they went deep. The heavy nets were not made for deep water. And these men had just worked all night and caught nothing.
- **"Master, we toiled all night and took nothing! But at your word" (v5).** "Master" is *epistatēs*, a word only Luke uses in the New Testament, for one in charge or a superior. It is respectful, but not yet the "Lord" of v8. Simon states the facts honestly and then obeys anyway. "At your word" is the hinge of the story. His trust rests not on how things look but on who has spoken.
- **"Their nets were breaking… so that they began to sink" (vv6–7).** The Greek tenses picture nets starting to tear and boats starting to go under. The catch is more than the fishermen can handle.
- **"Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord" (v8).** Simon's title for Jesus jumps from "Master" to "Lord." He does not say "thank you" for the fish. Seeing God's power up close exposes his own sin, as Isaiah cried "Woe is me!" when he saw the Lord (Isa 6:5). Luke now calls him "Simon Peter" for the first time.
- **"Do not be afraid" (v10).** The answer to Simon's fear is not that he is not sinful. It is that Jesus will not depart. Grace keeps the sinner close and gives him a job.
- **"From now on you will be catching men" (v10).** "Catching" is *zōgreō*, from "alive" and "catch": to take alive. Fish are caught to die. People caught for the kingdom are caught to live. Jeremiah 16:16 used fishing for God gathering people, though there in judgment. Jesus turns the image toward rescue.
- **Parallel accounts.** Matthew 4:18–22 and Mark 1:16–20 tell of Jesus calling these men by the lake, without the miraculous catch. Many think Luke records a separate, later moment, or tells the same call with fuller detail. John 1:35–42 shows Peter and Andrew had met Jesus even earlier. The calling came in stages. A similar catch happens after the resurrection (John 21:1–11), when Peter again throws himself at Jesus' feet, this time to be restored.
- **"They left everything and followed him" (v11).** They walk away from the biggest catch of their lives. In a culture where a son carried on his father's trade and family business, this was costly (Mark 1:20 adds that they left Zebedee in the boat with the hired men).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads this as the third stage of a "progressive" call, after John 1 and Matthew 4, and takes "Master" as proof of "a relationship already formed." It reads Peter's cry not as wanting Jesus gone but as "How shall I abide this blaze of glory?" Henry calls the catch Christ's payment for the loan of the boat, and says his "recompences for services done to his name are abundant, they are superabundant." Guzik dwells on Peter's obedience against his own expertise and fatigue, and makes the point that a vision of Christ's holiness should drive a sinner toward him, not away. He also sees the partner boat as a picture of needing others to bring in a great harvest.

#### Jesus Cleanses a Leper (vv12–16)
- **"A man full of leprosy" (v12).** Luke the doctor alone says "full," an advanced case. Biblical "leprosy" covered a range of serious skin diseases, not only Hansen's disease (Lev 13). The law required a sufferer to tear his clothes, cover his lip, cry "Unclean, unclean" and live outside the camp (Lev 13:45–46). Rabbis later added rules about how far away a leper had to stand. Many treated the disease as a sign of God's judgment (Num 12:10; 2 Kings 5:27). This man was cut off from worship, family and touch.
- **"Lord, if you will, you can make me clean" (v12).** He has no doubt that Jesus can. He is unsure Jesus wants to. That is a common doubt among those who feel unclean.
- **"Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him" (v13).** Touching a leper made a person unclean under the law. Jesus could have healed with a word, as he did elsewhere. Instead he touches first. The man likely had not felt a human touch in years. Here the direction of contagion is reversed: Jesus is not made unclean, and the leper is made clean.
- **"I will; be clean" (v13).** Jesus answers the man's exact words. His will matches his power. "Immediately" the disease leaves. Under the law only God cured leprosy (2 Kings 5:7, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?").
- **"Go and show yourself to the priest… as Moses commanded" (v14).** Leviticus 14 lays out a detailed ritual for someone healed of leprosy: inspection, two birds, washing, shaving, and offerings on the eighth day. It was rarely if ever needed. Jesus sends the man back into the community through the proper door, and does not set aside the law. "For a proof to them" means evidence to the priests, and perhaps a testimony that something new had come.
- **"Tell no one" (v14).** Jesus often asked for silence (4:41; 8:56). Crowds wanting a wonder-worker could choke his real mission, and popular hopes for a political Messiah were dangerous. Mark 1:45 says the man disobeyed and spread the news, so Jesus could no longer openly enter a town.
- **"But he would withdraw to desolate places and pray" (v16).** The Greek describes a habit: he kept on withdrawing. Luke shows Jesus praying more than any other Gospel does, at his baptism (3:21), before choosing the Twelve (6:12), at the Transfiguration (9:29) and on the Mount of Olives (22:41). The busier he became, the more he prayed.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws from v12 that "we must refer ourselves to the good-will of Christ," confident in his power and leaving the outcome to him. On v16 he notes that Jesus prayed alone: "Secret prayer must be performed secretly." Guzik observes that the leper doubted Christ's willingness rather than his power, and contrasts this man's inability to stay quiet with believers who are told to speak and stay silent. JFB says nothing here beyond cross-references to Matthew 8 and Mark 1.

#### Jesus Heals a Paralytic (vv17–26)
- **"Pharisees and teachers of the law… from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem" (v17).** This is the first time Pharisees appear in Luke. They were a lay movement devoted to careful keeping of the law and its traditions. "Teachers of the law" were the trained scholars, mostly Pharisees. A delegation from as far as Jerusalem, about 80 miles south, means Jesus is now being watched.
- **"The power of the Lord was with him to heal" (v17).** "The Lord" here is God, whose power worked through Jesus. Luke links power to the Spirit (4:14).
- **"They went up on the roof and let him down… through the tiles" (v19).** Galilean houses had flat roofs reached by outside stairs, made of beams covered with branches and packed clay. Mark 2:4 says they "dug through." Luke mentions tiles, probably describing it in terms his Greek-speaking readers would picture, or the house had a tiled section. Either way, they are tearing open someone's roof while Jesus is teaching below.
- **"When he saw their faith" (v20).** Faith is seen here in what these friends do. Their faith carried the paralyzed man to Jesus. Whether the man himself believed is not said, but Jesus honors the faith of those who brought him.
- **"Man, your sins are forgiven you" (v20).** Everyone expected healing. Jesus speaks to the deeper need first. This does not mean the paralysis was a punishment for a specific sin (John 9:2–3 warns against that conclusion). It means sin is a person's greatest problem, and Jesus came to deal with it.
- **"Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v21).** Their theology is right. A person can forgive a wrong done to him, but only God can forgive sin as such, since all sin is finally against him (Ps 51:4; Isa 43:25). Their mistake is not their logic but their conclusion. If only God can forgive sins and Jesus does, then either he is blaspheming or he is who he implies.
- **"When Jesus perceived their thoughts" (v22).** They had questioned "in their hearts." Jesus answers what was never said aloud. Scripture treats knowing hearts as God's work (1 Kings 8:39; Jer 17:10).
- **"Which is easier…?" (v23).** Saying "your sins are forgiven" is easier, because no one can check it. Saying "rise and walk" is harder, because everyone will see at once whether it works. So Jesus does the visible thing to prove the invisible one.
- **"That you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (v24).** This is the first time Jesus calls himself "the Son of Man" in Luke. The phrase can simply mean "a human being" (Ps 8:4; Ezek 2:1), which let Jesus use it without stirring political hopes. But it also points to Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds and receives everlasting dominion from God. "On earth" matters: forgiveness, which belongs to heaven, is now being given on earth by this man.
- **"Picked up what he had been lying on and went home, glorifying God" (v25).** The bed that carried him he now carries. Luke ends many healings with people "glorifying God" (7:16; 13:13; 17:15; 18:43). The point of the miracle is God's glory.
- **"We have seen extraordinary things today" (v26).** "Extraordinary" is *paradoxa*, the root of "paradox": things contrary to expectation. "Today" echoes 4:21. Salvation is not only future.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ claims one of the prerogatives of the King of kings when he undertakes to forgive sin." JFB calls the presence of leaders from every region "the highest testimony yet borne" to Jesus' growing influence, and passes on Bengel's line on v24: "The bed had borne the man; now the man shall bear the bed!" Guzik agrees with the scribes that only God forgives, and draws the conclusion they would not. He also explains "Son of Man" from Daniel 7 as a messianic title without the nationalist weight of "Messiah."

#### Jesus Calls Levi (vv27–32)
- **"A tax collector named Levi, sitting at the tax booth" (v27).** Capernaum sat on a trade road near the border between Herod Antipas' Galilee and his brother Philip's territory. Levi probably collected tolls on goods for Antipas. Tax collectors bid for the right to collect and kept whatever they could take above the required amount. They were widely seen as cheats and as collaborators with Gentile rulers, and the rabbis ranked them with robbers. Matthew 9:9 calls him Matthew, and tradition holds that he wrote the first Gospel.
- **"Follow me" (v27).** Two words. There is no recorded test, no probation, no demand that he clean up first.
- **"Leaving everything, he rose and followed him" (v28).** A fisherman could go back to fishing, and Peter later did (John 21:3). A tax collector who walked off his post could not easily get it back. Levi's step was final.
- **"Levi made him a great feast in his house" (v29).** His first act as a disciple is to throw a party and invite the only friends he had: "tax collectors and others." He wants them to meet Jesus. "Reclining at table" was the posture at a formal meal, lying on couches around a low table.
- **"Why do you eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners?" (v30).** In that culture eating together meant acceptance and friendship. Pharisees avoided meals with people who were careless about the law, to protect their own purity. "Sinners" meant both people known for immoral living and ordinary people who ignored Pharisaic rules. Note that they complain to the disciples, not to Jesus. This "grumbling" (*gongyzō*) is the word for Israel's complaining in the wilderness (Exod 16:7–8 in the Greek Old Testament), and Luke will use it again (15:2; 19:7).
- **"Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick" (v31).** A doctor who stays away from sick people is not doing his job. Jesus' answer is gently ironic. The "well" are those who think they are well.
- **"I have not come to call the righteous but sinners to repentance" (v32).** Only Luke adds "to repentance." Jesus is not approving how Levi's friends live. He welcomes them in order to change them. Welcome comes first, and repentance follows. The verse is Luke's whole Gospel in brief (compare 19:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees Levi's call as proof that "there is no heart too hard for the Spirit and grace of Christ to work upon." Guzik describes how despised tax collectors were and treats Levi's feast as the instinct of every new believer to bring friends to Jesus. He also lists the ways sick people avoid doctors, by denying the illness or trying home cures, as a picture of how people avoid Christ. JFB notes only that "their scribes" shows Luke writing with Gentile readers in mind.

#### A Question About Fasting (vv33–39)
- **"The disciples of John fast often and offer prayers" (v33).** The law required fasting only on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29). Pious Jews added more, and some Pharisees fasted twice a week, on Mondays and Thursdays (18:12). John the Baptist's followers shared his austere life. Jesus' disciples were feasting at Levi's house, and to the critics that looked careless.
- **"Can you make wedding guests fast while the bridegroom is with them?" (v34).** A Jewish wedding lasted up to a week, and guests were excused from religious duties like fasting during it. In the Old Testament God is Israel's bridegroom (Isa 62:5; Hos 2:19–20). Jesus quietly puts himself in that role. John the Baptist used the same picture of Jesus (John 3:29).
- **"The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away" (v35).** This is the first hint in Luke of Jesus' death. "Taken away" is a forceful word. The joy is real, but it will be interrupted, and then fasting will fit. The early church did fast (Acts 13:2–3; 14:23).
- **The new garment (v36).** In Luke's version, someone ruins a new garment to patch an old one. The new cloth is wasted, and the patch does not match. Matthew and Mark stress that unshrunk cloth tears the old when washed. Either way, Jesus did not come to patch the old system with a bit of new material.
- **New wine and old wineskins (vv37–38).** Wine was stored in goatskins. New wine was still fermenting and expanding. An old, stretched, brittle skin would split. New wine needs fresh skins that can stretch. The life Jesus brings will not fit inside rigid old forms of religion.
- **"No one after drinking old wine desires new, for he says, 'The old is good'" (v39).** Only Luke has this line, and it is dry and wry. People used to the old taste do not want the new. It explains why the Pharisees resist. Some manuscripts read "the old is better," but the better-supported reading is "good." Jesus is not saying the old is better. He is describing the people who think it is.
- **Is the old covenant being thrown out?** Jesus himself says he came to fulfill the Law, not abolish it (Matt 5:17). The point is not that the Old Testament was bad. The point is that the kingdom's arrival brings something new that human traditions cannot contain.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus "insisted most upon that which is the soul of fasting, the mortification of sin," and sees in vv36–39 Christ's care not to load new disciples with burdens too heavy for them yet. JFB warns of "the danger of mixing" the old and new, where "the rent is made worse" and "the new wine is spilled," and reads v39 as patience with those raised under the old: they need time, and the new wine "will itself in time become old." Guzik sees the faint shadow of the cross in the bridegroom "taken away," and argues that Jesus fulfills the law rather than discarding it, while refusing to be squeezed into the traditions built around it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Each scene in Luke 5 shows what kind of Savior Jesus is. He tells fishermen where to fish and does not leave when a sinner begs him to. He touches a man no one would touch. He forgives sins on earth with the authority of heaven and proves it by making a paralyzed man walk. He eats with tax collectors because he came as a physician for the sick. He is the bridegroom at a wedding feast who will one day be taken away, the death that makes the forgiveness of v20 possible. The religion of patches and old wineskins cannot hold him. He brings new wine.
