*Setting:* Mahanaim in Gilead and the Jordan crossing near Gilgal, around 976 BC, in the days just after the battle in the forest of Ephraim. Absalom is dead, killed by Joab against David's orders, and his army has scattered (18:14–17). David sits in the room above the gate weeping, "O my son Absalom" (18:33). The war is won, but the kingdom is not yet restored. The tribes that followed Absalom have to decide whether to take David back, and David has to decide how to treat those who betrayed or cursed him. This chapter is a road back to Jerusalem, lined with people David must deal with: a blunt general, a reluctant tribe, a man who cursed him, a slandered friend and a generous old man. (The ESV follows English numbering here. In the Hebrew Bible, 18:33 is 19:1, so each verse below is one number lower than in Hebrew.)

#### Joab Rebukes David (vv1–8a)
- **"The victory that day was turned into mourning for all the people" (v2).** Returning armies were normally met with songs and dancing (1 Sam 18:6). Instead the men hear that the king is grieving for the rebel they just defeated.
- **"The people stole into the city that day as people steal in who are ashamed when they flee in battle" (v3).** "Stole" is the same verb used of Absalom, who "stole the hearts" of Israel (15:6). The winners slink home like losers.
- **"The king covered his face" (v4).** A sign of mourning and shame (15:30). He will not look at anyone, and the men who saved him cannot see his face.
- **"You have today covered with shame the faces of all your servants, who have this day saved your life" (v5).** Joab does not soften his words. He lists who was saved: David, his sons and daughters, his wives and concubines. The Tyndale notes observe that this is the only time Joab openly rebukes David (compare 24:3).
- **"Because you love those who hate you and hate those who love you" (v6).** Joab exaggerates to make his point, but it lands. **"If Absalom were alive and all of us were dead today, then you would be pleased."** It is harsh, and it comes from the man who killed Absalom, but it is not false.
- **"If you do not go, not a man will stay with you this night" (v7).** Joab warns that the army will desert him. Whether this is a prediction or a veiled threat, David hears it. Joab holds great power, and David knows it.
- **"Then the king arose and took his seat in the gate" (v8).** The gate was where the king sat to judge and be seen (15:2). David puts his grief aside and does his duty. **"All the people came before the king."** The army is restored to its king.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Joab's rebuke "was right and necessary, but it was made with harshness." He was one of those who "spoil their important services by the insolence of their manners." JFB also traces David's later instruction to Solomon about Joab (1 Kings 2:5–6) partly to this moment. Guzik reads David's grief as excessive, feelings mastering duty, and praises David for acting on what was right rather than what he felt. He observes that we never again hear David cry "O Absalom." Matthew Henry says Joab "thought it no time to dally with him."

#### David Returns to Jerusalem (vv8b–15)
- **"Now Israel had fled every man to his own home" (v8).** Absalom's army is gone. The northern and eastern tribes are leaderless.
- **"The king delivered us from the hand of our enemies and saved us from the hand of the Philistines" (v9).** The tribes argue among themselves. They remember what David did for them (5:17–25; 8:1). **"Absalom, whom we anointed over us, is dead in battle"** (v10). They had anointed Absalom, which was a solemn act. Now their king is dead, and they are embarrassed and uncertain. **"Why do you say nothing about bringing the king back?"**
- **"Why should you be the last to bring the king back to his house?" (v11).** David does not march back as a conqueror. He waits to be invited. But his own tribe, Judah, is silent. Absalom had launched his revolt at Hebron, in Judah (15:10), and the men of Judah may have been afraid. David sends the priests Zadok and Abiathar to the elders of Judah with a personal appeal: **"You are my brothers; you are my bone and my flesh"** (v12). The northern tribes had used those same words when they made David king at Hebron (5:1).
- **"Say to Amasa… you are… commander of my army from now on in place of Joab" (v13).** This is a bold move. Amasa had commanded Absalom's army (17:25). He was also David's nephew. Making him commander would show that David would not punish Absalom's supporters, and it would win Judah. It also demotes Joab, who had disobeyed David by killing Absalom. The move will backfire badly (20:8–10).
- **"He swayed the heart of all the men of Judah as one man" (v14).** It works. Judah sends word: "Return, both you and all your servants."
- **"Judah came to Gilgal to meet the king and to bring the king over the Jordan" (v15).** Gilgal, near Jericho, was where Israel first camped after crossing the Jordan under Joshua (Josh 4:19) and where Saul's kingship was renewed (1 Sam 11:14–15). The Tyndale notes suggest David's reign is being renewed at the same place.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the kingdom was "completely disorganized," with royalists, former rebels and those indifferent to David, so David was right not to hurry back "as a conqueror." He calls the offer to Amasa "a dextrous stroke of policy." Guzik stresses that David would not force himself on the people, and sees in it a picture of Christ, who reigns over willing hearts. Henry thinks Judah was perhaps "remiss and careless" rather than hostile, needing someone to stir them up.

#### David Pardons His Enemies (vv16–43)
- **"Shimei the son of Gera, the Benjaminite, from Bahurim, hurried to come down" (v16).** The man who cursed David and threw stones at him on the road out (16:5–13) rushes to be first on the road back in. **"A thousand men from Benjamin"** come with him (v17), a reminder that Shimei is a man of some standing in Saul's tribe, and that Benjamin's goodwill matters.
- **Ziba and his household (v17).** Ziba arrives with his fifteen sons and twenty servants, rushing to help ferry the king's household across the river. He is eager to be seen before Mephibosheth can tell his side.
- **"Your servant knows that I have sinned" (v20).** Shimei confesses plainly. Whether he means it or is simply saving his life, the text does not say. **"The first of all the house of Joseph."** "Joseph" here stands for the northern tribes, after Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh (compare Amos 5:6). Benjamin was often counted with them.
- **"Shall not Shimei be put to death for this, because he cursed the LORD's anointed?" (v21).** Abishai, again (16:9). Cursing the king was a serious crime (Exod 22:28; 1 Kings 21:10).
- **"What have I to do with you, you sons of Zeruiah, that you should this day be as an adversary to me?" (v22).** "Adversary" is *śāṭān*, the ordinary Hebrew word for an opponent or accuser. It is the same word used of the one who stood in Balaam's way (Num 22:22), and later of Satan. David treats Abishai's zeal as an obstacle. **"Shall anyone be put to death in Israel this day?"** A day of restoration is not a day for revenge. Saul once said much the same after his first victory (1 Sam 11:13).
- **"You shall not die.' And the king gave him his oath" (v23).** David spares him. Years later, on his deathbed, David tells Solomon not to hold Shimei guiltless (1 Kings 2:8–9). Solomon confines him to Jerusalem, and Shimei is executed only after he breaks that condition (1 Kings 2:36–46). David's oath protected Shimei for David's lifetime.
- **"He had neither taken care of his feet nor trimmed his beard nor washed his clothes" (v24).** Mephibosheth has been in mourning since the day David left. A man hoping to be king, as Ziba had claimed (16:3), would not look like this.
- **"My servant deceived me… For your servant is lame" (v26).** Mephibosheth had told Ziba to saddle a donkey so he could go with the king. Ziba left without him and then slandered him. A lame man could not follow on foot.
- **"My lord the king is like the angel of God; do therefore what seems good to you" (v27).** He does not demand his rights. **"All my father's house were but men doomed to death before my lord the king, but you set your servant among those who eat at your table"** (v28). He remembers the grace of chapter 9. What claim does a man who has been shown such kindness have?
- **"You and Ziba shall divide the land" (v29).** This verdict is puzzling. Either David cannot tell who is lying, or he does not want to deal with the matter now, or he wants to keep Ziba's support. Some think he simply goes back to the original arrangement, Mephibosheth as owner and Ziba as manager (9:9–10). The text does not explain, and many readers feel Mephibosheth was not fully vindicated.
- **"Oh, let him take it all, since my lord the king has come safely home" (v30).** Mephibosheth's answer shows his heart. He cares about the king, not the land. Readers have always found his answer the most convincing evidence that he was telling the truth.
- **"Barzillai was a very aged man, eighty years old… a very wealthy man" (v32).** He had supplied David at Mahanaim (17:27–29). He comes down from Rogelim to see the king safely over the river.
- **"Come over with me, and I will provide for you with me in Jerusalem" (v33).** David offers him a place at court. The one who fed David will now be fed by David.
- **"Can I discern what is pleasant and what is not? Can your servant taste what he eats?" (v35).** Barzillai declines with gentle humor. At eighty he cannot enjoy court food or court music, and he would only be a burden. **"Let me die in my own city near the grave of my father and my mother"** (v37). Being buried with one's family mattered deeply (Gen 47:29–30; 50:25).
- **"Here is your servant Chimham" (v37).** He asks the honor for Chimham, probably his son. David agrees, and later tells Solomon to keep showing kindness to Barzillai's sons (1 Kings 2:7). Jeremiah 41:17 mentions a place near Bethlehem called "Geruth Chimham," perhaps land David gave him.
- **"The king kissed Barzillai and blessed him" (v39).** This kiss is warm and real. Compare the cold kiss for Absalom (14:33) and Absalom's political kisses at the gate (15:5).
- **"Why have our brothers the men of Judah stolen you away?" (v41).** The northern tribes are offended. They had first raised the idea of bringing David back (v10), but Judah escorted him across the Jordan without them. "Stolen" is that word again (15:6; 19:3).
- **"The king is our close relative" (v42).** Judah answers that David is one of them, and insists they have gained nothing from it.
- **"We have ten shares in the king" (v43).** The northern tribes count themselves as ten against Judah's one (Benjamin and Simeon aside). It is the same ten-and-two division that splits the kingdom after Solomon (1 Kings 11:31; 12:16). **"But the words of the men of Judah were fiercer."** The quarrel leads straight into another revolt in chapter 20.
- **Commentators:** On Shimei, Matthew Henry says David's "experience of God's mercy in restoring him to his kingdom… inclined him to show mercy." Guzik makes a similar point: David could forgive because he was secure in the throne God gave him, and insecurity often drives revenge. JFB calls the pardon magnanimous. The Tyndale notes are more cautious: Shimei might have been sincere or simply saving his life. On Mephibosheth, JFB says the reception given to him "was less creditable to David" and that the sincerity of his grief "cannot be doubted." Henry reads the verdict as Mephibosheth keeping ownership with Ziba as tenant, so that "Mephibosheth is where he was," though Ziba "goes away unpunished." Guzik praises Mephibosheth for caring about the king more than his rights. On Barzillai, Henry says the old "learn of Barzillai to be dead to the delights of sense," and JFB suggests David gave Chimham land near Bethlehem. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both see the quarrel of vv41–43 as an early sign of the later split between Judah and Israel.

#### Pointing to Christ
The returning king meets three kinds of people: one who cursed him and asks for mercy, one who was slandered and asks for nothing but the king's presence, and one who served him freely and wants no reward. David's welcome is mixed. He forgives Shimei but later wants him punished, and he never quite clears Mephibosheth. The true King does better. Jesus forgives those who cursed him (Luke 23:34), fully vindicates those who are falsely accused (Rom 8:33–34), and rewards even a cup of cold water given in his name (Matt 10:42). Mephibosheth's words are the heart of a disciple: "let him take it all, since my lord the king has come safely home." Those who love Christ long for his return more than for anything they might gain from it (2 Tim 4:8).
