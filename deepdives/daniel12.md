*Setting:* This is the end of Daniel's last vision (chapters 10–12) and of the book, still "in the third year of Cyrus" (10:1), about 536 BC, by the Tigris river. Daniel is in his mid-eighties. Chapter 11 traced the wars of the kings of north and south, the persecution under Antiochus IV, and a final tyrant who "shall come to his end, with none to help him" (11:45). There is no break in the angel's speech, so chapter 12 follows straight on: "at that time." What comes after the worst trouble is not just rescue but resurrection. This is the clearest statement of bodily resurrection and eternal judgment in the Old Testament. The book then closes with Daniel asking "how long?", being told he will not see the end, and receiving one last personal promise.

#### The Time of the End (vv1–13)
- **"At that time shall arise Michael, the great prince who has charge of your people" (v1).** "At that time" ties this to the end of the tyrant in 11:40–45. Michael was introduced in 10:13, 21 as the angel who fights for Israel. "Arise" (literally "stand up") may mean he stands up to act, as a champion steps forward. Revelation shows Michael and his angels defeating the dragon (Rev 12:7–9).
- **"A time of trouble, such as never has been since there was a nation till that time" (v1).** Jeremiah used similar language for a coming "time of distress for Jacob; yet he shall be saved out of it" (Jer 30:7). Jesus quotes this verse almost word for word: "then there will be great tribulation, such as has not been from the beginning of the world until now" (Matt 24:21; Mark 13:19). He sets it in his teaching on the fall of Jerusalem and the end of the age. Some interpreters apply Daniel's words first to the persecution under Antiochus and then fully to the final days. Others apply them only to the end.
- **"Your people shall be delivered, everyone whose name shall be found written in the book" (v1).** Deliverance is not for everyone born an Israelite but for those written in God's book. The idea of a book of the living runs through Scripture: Moses asked to be blotted out of it (Exod 32:32), the psalmist prays that the wicked be removed from it (Ps 69:28), and Revelation calls it "the Lamb's book of life" (Rev 13:8; 20:12–15; 21:27). Jesus told his disciples to rejoice "that your names are written in heaven" (Luke 10:20).
- **"Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (v2).** "Sleep" is a common biblical picture of death (Ps 13:3; John 11:11; 1 Thess 4:13–14), and "dust" recalls Genesis 3:19: "to dust you shall return." Daniel is told the reverse: those returned to dust will wake. Isaiah had already said, "Your dead shall live; their bodies shall rise. You who dwell in the dust, awake and sing" (Isa 26:19). Daniel 12:2 goes further by naming two destinies.
- **"Some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (v2).** This is the first time the Old Testament uses the phrase "everlasting life." The word for "contempt" (*dera'on*, "abhorrence") is found only here and in the last verse of Isaiah, of the dead bodies of the rebels: "they shall be an abhorrence to all flesh" (Isa 66:24). Jesus draws the same line: "an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment" (John 5:28–29; compare Matt 25:46; Acts 24:15).
- **"Many," not "all."** Why does Daniel say "many"? Some take "many" in the Hebrew sense of "the great number," meaning everyone (compare Rom 5:15, 19, where "the many" means all). Others think Daniel is focused on his own people, or on those who died in the great persecution, both faithful and faithless. A few read it as a resurrection of the righteous only, with "some to shame" meaning those who remain in the dust. The New Testament's clear teaching that all will be raised (John 5:28–29) fills out what Daniel states in part.
- **"Those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky above" (v3).** "The wise" are the *maskilim* of 11:33–35, who taught many and suffered for it. Here their reward is described: they will shine. **"Those who turn many to righteousness, like the stars forever and ever."** The phrase "turn many to righteousness" echoes Isaiah's Servant, who "shall… make many to be accounted righteous" (Isa 53:11), and the words are close in Hebrew. The persecuted teachers share in the Servant's work and in his glory. Jesus uses the same picture: "Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (Matt 13:43).
- **"Shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end" (v4).** In the ancient world, an important document was rolled up, sealed, and kept safe as an authentic record until it was needed (Jer 32:9–14; Isa 8:16). The message is to be preserved, not hidden forever. Its full meaning will be clearer when the events arrive. By contrast John is told, "Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near" (Rev 22:10).
- **"Many shall run to and fro, and knowledge shall increase" (v4).** This verse is often quoted to mean that travel and science will grow at the end of history. The phrase "run to and fro" more likely means searching restlessly, as in Amos 8:12, where people "run to and fro, to seek the word of the LORD, but they shall not find it." So it may mean many will search for understanding and, with the book unsealed, knowledge of God's plan will grow. One early Greek translation (Theodotion's) reads "until many are taught and knowledge increases." Some see a darker meaning: people searching without finding, as in Amos. Either way, it is not a prophecy about cars and computers.
- **"Two others stood, one on this bank… and one on that bank" (v5).** Two angels stand on either side of the Tigris, perhaps as the two witnesses required to confirm a matter (Deut 19:15). **"The man clothed in linen"** of 10:5 is above the waters.
- **"How long shall it be till the end of these wonders?" (v6).** "Wonders" here means extraordinary, terrible events, the things the chapter has described. As in 8:13, an angel asks the question believers ask.
- **"He raised his right hand and his left hand toward heaven and swore" (v7).** People swore oaths with one raised hand (Gen 14:22; Deut 32:40). Raising both makes the oath doubly solemn. He swears **"by him who lives forever."** Revelation 10:5–7 echoes this scene: an angel standing on sea and land raises his hand and swears there will be no more delay.
- **"A time, times, and half a time" (v7).** The same period as 7:25, usually understood as three and a half years. It is half of seven: real and painful, but cut short by God. **"When the shattering of the power of the holy people comes to an end."** The end comes when God's people have been brought to the point of having no strength left. Then God acts.
- **"I heard, but I did not understand" (v8).** The great interpreter of dreams admits he does not understand. He asks again, **"what shall be the outcome of these things?"**, and the answer is not a fuller explanation.
- **"Go your way, Daniel, for the words are shut up and sealed" (v9).** He is told, in effect, that he has received what he needs. Peter says the prophets "searched and inquired carefully" about the sufferings and glories they foretold, and "it was revealed to them that they were serving not themselves but you" (1 Pet 1:10–12).
- **"Many shall purify themselves and make themselves white and be refined, but the wicked shall act wickedly" (v10).** The same three verbs as 11:35. The time of trouble divides people. It refines some and hardens others. **"None of the wicked shall understand, but those who are wise shall understand."** Understanding is not a matter of intelligence but of the heart (compare Hos 14:9; Matt 13:11–15).
- **"From the time that the regular burnt offering is taken away… there shall be 1,290 days" (v11).** Three numbers are now given for the period of desolation: 1,150 or 2,300 (8:14), 1,290 (here) and 1,335 (v12). At about 30 days to a month, 1,290 days is 43 months, roughly three and a half years plus one month. Explanations differ. Some suggest the extra month is a lunar calendar's leap month. Others match the numbers to events in Antiochus's time: the desecration of the temple, its cleansing, and Antiochus's death some weeks later. Others see stages at the end of history: Christ's return, the setting up of his kingdom, and the full blessing. Some take them as symbolic, saying in effect that the time may run a little longer than expected, so keep waiting.
- **"Blessed is he who waits and arrives at the 1,335 days" (v12).** This is the only beatitude in Daniel. Whatever the numbers mean exactly, the point is clear: blessing comes to those who keep waiting, even past the point where it seemed the end should have come. Jesus said the same: "the one who endures to the end will be saved" (Matt 24:13).
- **"But go your way till the end. And you shall rest and shall stand in your allotted place at the end of the days" (v13).** The book closes with a personal word to an old man. He will not see the end. He will **"rest,"** which means die, in peace. Then he will rise: "stand" is the same verb used of Michael in v1, and here it means standing up alive at the resurrection. **"Your allotted place"** is literally "your lot," the language of Joshua dividing the promised land by lot (Josh 18:10). Daniel was taken from his land as a boy and never went back. He is promised a share in the inheritance that no exile can take away.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls these verses comforts "prescribed as cordials for the support of God's people in those times of trouble." He calls v2 "a distinguishing resurrection of those that sleep in the dust," and says the wise "shall shine for ever and ever, shall never set, never be eclipsed." On v13 he writes, "Go thy way, Daniel, satisfied with what thou hast," and "We must every one of us stand in our lot at the end of the days." JFB applies the time of trouble "partially" to Antiochus and "fully" to the last times, and calls Michael "the guardian angel of Israel" and "not the Lord Jesus." On v2 he takes "many" as the first resurrection of the righteous rather than all people. Guzik, by contrast, reads v2 as the resurrection of all, though not at the same time. He takes "run to and fro" as eager searching after knowledge, especially of prophecy, not as modern travel. He offers a tentative scheme for the 1,290 and 1,335 days after Christ's return, while admitting they are hard to pin down. Like Henry, he applies "go your way" to every believer: follow Christ and leave the unknown to God, as Jesus told Peter (John 21:22).

#### Pointing to Christ
Daniel saw the dead rising from the dust, some to everlasting life. Jesus made that promise personal: "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live" (John 11:25). He went down into the dust himself and was the first to rise, "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Cor 15:20). The wise will shine like stars, but only because the one who is "the bright morning star" (Rev 22:16) first shone into the darkness. Daniel was told to go his way, rest, and stand in his lot at the end. Jesus gives the same hope to everyone written in his book: "I go to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2).
