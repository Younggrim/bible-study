*Setting:* The covenant made at Sinai was broken almost as soon as it was sealed. The people made a golden calf, Moses smashed the tablets, and God said he would not go up among them (chs 32–33). But Moses pleaded, and God promised, "My presence will go with you" (33:14). Moses then asked to see God's glory, and God promised to make his goodness pass before him and proclaim his name (33:18–23). Chapter 34 is the fulfilment. God reveals his name in the words that the rest of the Old Testament quotes more than any other description of him. Then he renews the covenant, rewrites the Ten Commandments, and sends Moses down with a face so bright the people are afraid to come near.

#### Moses Makes New Tablets (vv1–9)
- **"Cut for yourself two tablets of stone like the first" (v1).** The first tablets were "the work of God" (32:16). This time Moses has to cut the stone himself, though God will still do the writing. **"Which you broke"**: God reminds Moses, without rebuke, of what happened. A renewed covenant does not pretend the break never occurred.
- **"No one shall come up with you" (v3).** Not even Joshua this time (compare 24:13). The same boundaries as the first giving of the law apply (19:12–13, 21–24): no people, no animals grazing near the mountain. God's holiness has not changed.
- **"The LORD descended in the cloud and stood with him there" (v5).** God keeps the promise of 33:19–22. He comes down, stands with Moses, and **"proclaimed the name of the LORD."** In Hebrew thought a name is not just a label. It sums up who someone is.
- **"The LORD, the LORD" (v6).** The personal name *YHWH* is repeated twice for emphasis, the name God first explained at the burning bush: "I AM WHO I AM" (3:14). There it stressed God's self-existence and power. Here it is filled out with his character.
- **"A God merciful and gracious" (v6).** "Merciful" (*rachum*) is related to the Hebrew word for "womb," and many hear in it the tender love a mother has for her child (compare Isa 49:15). "Gracious" (*channun*) means showing favor freely, giving kindness that is not earned. Both words were just displayed: Israel deserved destruction and was spared.
- **"Slow to anger" (v6).** Literally "long of nostrils," a Hebrew picture of anger as heated breath. God's anger takes a long time to burn. It is real, but it is not quick.
- **"Abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (v6).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, covenant love, loyal kindness that keeps its commitments. "Faithfulness" is *'emet*, reliability or truth. John may be echoing this pair when he writes that the Word who "dwelt among us" was "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).
- **"Keeping steadfast love for thousands" (v7).** This could mean thousands of people or, as Deuteronomy 7:9 puts it, "a thousand generations." Either way, God's love far outweighs his judgment.
- **"Forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (v7).** Three words for wrongdoing. "Iniquity" (*'avon*) is twistedness or guilt. "Transgression" (*pesha'*) is rebellion, breaking with authority. "Sin" (*chatta'ah*) is missing the mark. Together they cover every kind of wrong. "Forgiving" is literally "lifting" or "carrying away."
- **"But who will by no means clear the guilty" (v7).** God's mercy is not indifference. He does not pretend guilt does not exist. How a holy God can both forgive sin and not clear the guilty is a puzzle the Old Testament leaves open, and the cross answers it: God is "just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:26).
- **"Visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children… to the third and the fourth generation" (v7).** This does not mean God punishes innocent children for their parents' sins. Ezekiel 18:20 says plainly that "the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father." In 20:5 the phrase is limited to "those who hate me," children who carry on their parents' sin. It also describes how sin's effects spread through families. Three or four generations might all live under one roof. The contrast is striking: judgment to three or four generations, love to thousands.
- **The most quoted verses about God.** These words are repeated or echoed again and again: by Moses (Num 14:18), the Levites (Neh 9:17), the psalmists (Ps 86:15; 103:8; 145:8), the prophets (Joel 2:13; Nah 1:3), and Jonah, who complained that God was exactly this merciful to Nineveh (Jonah 4:2). This is how Israel learned to describe their God.
- **"Moses quickly bowed his head toward the earth and worshiped" (v8).** He asked to see God's glory. What he received was a sermon on God's character, and his response was worship.
- **"Please let the Lord go in the midst of us, for it is a stiff-necked people" (v9).** Moses uses the very word God used against them (32:9; 33:3, 5). God said, "I will not go up among you, for you are stiff-necked." Moses says, "Go among us, *because* we are stiff-necked." Their sinfulness is the reason they need God, not a reason for him to stay away. **"Pardon our iniquity and our sin"**: note "our." Moses includes himself. **"Take us for your inheritance"**: make us your own possession again (19:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in the name a pardon flowing from God's own good pleasure, not Israel's merit. He writes that God "is not only good to Israel, but good to all," and that "even the goodness of God must be looked upon by us with a profound veneration and holy awe." JFB contrasts the burning bush, where God revealed his self-existent majesty, with this passage, where he reveals his grace, and notes the cloud shows that much of God stayed veiled from mortal sight. Guzik uses this passage, with Psalm 86:15, to answer the idea of a harsh Old Testament God and a loving New Testament God. He says "gracious" comes from a root meaning to bend or stoop down in kindness. The lexicon gives the meaning "show favour, be gracious" and does not support the "stooping" idea, though the sense of undeserved kindness is right. Guzik also takes "third and fourth generation" as a common way of saying "continuing on."

#### The Covenant Renewed (vv10–28)
- **"Behold, I am making a covenant" (v10).** God answers Moses' prayer by renewing the covenant. He does not negotiate terms with Israel. He sets them. **"I will do marvels, such as have not been created in all the earth."** The word "created" is the same verb used in Genesis 1:1. What God will do for Israel will be like a new act of creation.
- **"Take care, lest you make a covenant with the inhabitants of the land" (v12).** The instructions that follow (vv11–26) repeat parts of the Book of the Covenant (chs 21–23), especially the laws about worship. After the golden calf, they focus on the danger that led to it: other gods. Ancient treaties called on the gods of both parties as witnesses, so a treaty with Canaanites would mean honoring their gods. It would become **"a snare."**
- **"Tear down their altars and break their pillars and cut down their Asherim" (v13).** "Pillars" were standing stones used in Canaanite worship. "Asherim" were wooden poles or trees associated with the goddess Asherah, a fertility goddess and the consort of El, the chief god. Asherah worship tempted Israel for centuries (Judg 6:25–26; 1 Kgs 14:15; 2 Kgs 23:4–6).
- **"The LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God" (v14).** "Jealous" (*qanna'*) is used only of God in the Bible. It is not petty envy. It is the right jealousy of a husband who will not share his wife with another lover. That is why the next verses use the language of **"whore after their gods"** (vv15–16). Idolatry is adultery against the God who married Israel at Sinai (Jer 3:1–10; Hos 2).
- **"You take of their daughters for your sons" (v16).** The concern is not ethnic but religious. Intermarriage with pagan Canaanites would draw Israel into their worship, as it did with Solomon (1 Kgs 11:1–8). Ruth the Moabite, who said "your God my God" (Ruth 1:16), was welcomed. Paul applies a similar principle to marriage between believers and unbelievers (2 Cor 6:14).
- **"You shall not make for yourself any gods of cast metal" (v17).** After the calf of "cast metal" (32:4, 8), this needs no comment.
- **The feasts and firstborn (vv18–26).** The **Feast of Unleavened Bread** (v18) was held in **Abib**, the month of new grain, around March–April, remembering the exodus (12:15–20; 13:3–10). **"All that open the womb are mine"** (v19): firstborn animals were sacrificed, and firstborn sons were redeemed, bought back, because God spared Israel's firstborn in Egypt (13:11–16). A donkey, which could not be sacrificed, was redeemed with a lamb (v20). **"None shall appear before me empty-handed."**
- **"In plowing time and in harvest you shall rest" (v21).** The Sabbath held even in the busiest seasons, when stopping work felt most costly. That is exactly when resting showed trust.
- **The three pilgrim feasts (vv22–23).** The **Feast of Weeks**, at the wheat harvest, seven weeks after Passover, later called Pentecost (Acts 2:1). The **Feast of Ingathering** at the end of the harvest year in autumn, also called Booths or Tabernacles (Lev 23:33–43). **"Three times in the year shall all your males appear before the Lord GOD."**
- **"No one shall covet your land, when you go up to appear before the LORD" (v24).** If all the men went to worship three times a year, the land would be left open to raiders. God promises to guard it himself. Worship would cost them nothing they could not trust God to keep.
- **"You shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk" (v26).** This law appears three times (23:19; Deut 14:21), and its reason is not given. Views differ. Some think it forbade a Canaanite ritual, though the ancient text once cited for this is now read differently by most scholars. Others see a principle of respect for life: the milk meant to nourish the young should not be used to cook it (compare Deut 22:6–7). Jewish tradition built on this verse the practice of keeping meat and dairy separate.
- **"Write these words" (v27).** Moses writes down the covenant terms of vv10–26.
- **"Forty days and forty nights. He neither ate bread nor drank water" (v28).** This was Moses' second forty-day stay (24:18), and Deuteronomy says he fasted this way both times (Deut 9:9, 18). Going without water that long is beyond human strength. It was sustained by God. Elijah traveled forty days to this same mountain (1 Kgs 19:8), and Jesus fasted forty days in the wilderness (Matt 4:2).
- **"He wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the Ten Commandments" (v28).** This is the first time the phrase appears, literally "the ten words." Who is "he"? Grammatically it could be Moses, but verse 1 said God would write, and Deuteronomy 10:2–4 says the LORD wrote them. Most take "he" to be God. Some scholars have called vv14–26 a second, "ritual" set of ten commands, but the text itself identifies the ten words with the commandments of chapter 20 (Deut 10:4).
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that God dictates the covenant rather than bargaining over it, and treats the law about boiling a kid as a ban on imitating pagan practice. He calls Moses' forty-day fast a miracle never repeated or recommended in Scripture. JFB says "these words" in v27 are the laws of vv11–26, while God himself wrote the Ten Commandments on the new tablets, and points to Deuteronomy 9:18 for the reason Moses fasted: he lay before God in intercession for the people's sin.

#### The Shining Face of Moses (vv29–35)
- **"Moses did not know that the skin of his face shone" (v29).** "Shone" is *qaran*, a verb formed from *qeren*, "horn." It means to send out rays, like horns of light. Jerome's Latin Vulgate translated it "his face was horned," which is why medieval art, including Michelangelo's famous statue, shows Moses with horns. The light was not his own. It was reflected glory, the afterglow of being with God, like the moon shining with the sun's light.
- **"Because he had been talking with God" (v29).** Moses had asked to see God's glory (33:18). Without knowing it, he came down carrying some of it. He was not aware of it, and that fits the man Numbers calls "very meek, more than all people" (Num 12:3).
- **"They were afraid to come near him" (v30).** The people who were not afraid to make a golden calf are afraid of Moses' face. Guilty people fear the light (John 3:20).
- **"Moses called to them… and Moses talked with them" (vv31–32).** First Aaron and the leaders, then all the people. He gave them **"all that the LORD had spoken."** The glory did not keep him from his work.
- **"He put a veil over his face" (v33).** The pattern is clear in vv34–35: Moses went into God's presence unveiled, came out and spoke God's words with his face uncovered, and then veiled his face until he went in again. Why the veil? The text does not say. Some think it was a kindness to the frightened people. Paul gives an inspired reading in 2 Corinthians 3:7–18: the glory on Moses' face was being brought to an end, and the veil kept Israel from gazing at the end of that fading glory (2 Cor 3:13). Paul goes on: "a veil lies over their hearts" when the old covenant is read, but "when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed" (3:15–16).
- **"Whenever Moses went in before the LORD… he would remove the veil" (v34).** Before God, Moses had nothing to hide. Paul draws the point for every believer: "we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Cor 3:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that "the Israelites could not look him in the face but they must there read his commission," and draws a gentle lesson from Moses not knowing: "Their friends see much of God in them, but they themselves are ready to think they have no grace." He says, "This veil signified the darkness of that dispensation." JFB similarly calls the shining face a badge of Moses' office as God's ambassador, attributes the people's fear to their sense of guilt, and sees the veil as a sign of the dim, partial character of the old covenant. Guzik also explains the "horns" mistake and follows Paul in reading the veil as hiding a fading glory. He compares the old covenant to a bright moon and the new to the noonday sun. He notes that the only other person described with a shining face is Stephen (Acts 6:15), and that Moses did not stay on the mountain basking in the experience. He went down to serve.

#### Pointing to Christ
God answered Moses' request to see his glory by proclaiming his name: merciful, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness, forgiving, yet by no means clearing the guilty. That name is lived out in Jesus. In him the Word "dwelt among us… full of grace and truth" (John 1:14), and at the cross God's mercy and justice meet, so that sin is truly forgiven and truly paid for. Moses' face reflected a glory that faded. On another mountain, Jesus' face "shone like the sun" (Matt 17:2), not with borrowed light but his own, and Moses stood beside him (Matt 17:3). Now "God… has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6), and those who look to him, unveiled, are slowly changed into his likeness.
