*Setting:* This chapter continues the answer to the delegation from Bethel, given in December 518 BC (7:1). They had asked whether to keep mourning the fall of Jerusalem with fasts (7:3). Chapter 7 answered with a hard look back: the fasts remembered a disaster caused by refusing God's call to justice and mercy. Chapter 8 turns from warning to promise. Ten times the chapter says "Thus says the LORD of hosts," and each time a new promise follows: God's return to Zion, a city full of old people and children, the scattered brought home, a land made fruitful, a curse turned to blessing, fasts turned to feasts and the nations streaming to Jerusalem. The returned community was small, poor and still in the middle of a building project. These promises gave them a reason to keep their hands strong. Some were fulfilled in the following centuries, and the largest reach beyond them into the work of Christ and the age to come. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading.

#### The Coming Peace and Prosperity of Zion (vv1–23)
- **"I am jealous for Zion with great jealousy" (v2).** The Hebrew *qin'ah* means zeal or jealousy. Human jealousy is usually envy, but God's jealousy is the fierce, protective love of a husband for his wife (Exod 34:14; Hos 2). It is a love that will not share its beloved and will not let her be harmed. The same words opened the visions (1:14). The lexicon connects the root, perhaps, with the flush that comes into a face under strong feeling, though that is only a guess about the word's origin. **"With great wrath"** shows the other side: the same love that cherishes Zion burns against those who hurt her.
- **"I have returned to Zion and will dwell in the midst of Jerusalem" (v3).** Ezekiel had watched God's glory leave the temple before its destruction (Ezek 10–11) and promised its return (Ezek 43:1–5). Zechariah speaks as though the return has already begun ("I have returned"). Ezekiel's vision of the new city ended with its name: "The LORD is there" (Ezek 48:35).
- **"Jerusalem shall be called the faithful city" (v3).** The Hebrew is literally "the city of truth" (*'emet*, faithfulness, reliability). Isaiah had mourned "how the faithful city has become a whore," and promised that afterward "you shall be called the city of righteousness, the faithful city" (Isa 1:21, 26). **"The holy mountain"**: Zion would be set apart for God again.
- **"Old men and old women shall again sit in the streets… boys and girls playing in its streets" (vv4–5).** This is one of the most tender pictures in the prophets. War, famine and exile take the old and the young first (Lam 2:11, 21). A city where elderly people sit in the open squares, leaning on their canes, and children play in the streets is a city at peace. Long life was one of the covenant's blessings (Deut 4:40; Isa 65:20). In Zechariah's day Jerusalem was thinly populated and largely unwalled. Nehemiah, seventy years later, still had to draw lots to move people into the city (Neh 11:1–2).
- **"If it is marvelous in the sight of the remnant… should it also be marvelous in my sight?" (v6).** The word *pala'* means to be wonderful, extraordinary, beyond what is possible. It is the word God used to Abraham when Sarah laughed: "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" (Gen 18:14), and Jeremiah's: "Nothing is too hard for you" (Jer 32:17, 27). Something may look impossible to a small, discouraged remnant. It is not impossible to God.
- **"I will save my people from the east country and from the west country" (v7).** Literally "from the land of the rising of the sun and from the land of its going in," that is, from everywhere. Only a fraction of the exiles had returned, and Jews were already scattered from Persia to Egypt and beyond.
- **"They shall be my people, and I will be their God, in faithfulness and in righteousness" (v8).** This is the heart of God's covenant, repeated from Exodus to Revelation (Exod 6:7; Jer 31:33; Ezek 36:28; Rev 21:3). "In faithfulness and in righteousness" describes both sides: God will be faithful to them, and they will be faithful to him.
- **"Let your hands be strong" (v9).** This phrase opens and closes the next paragraph (vv9, 13), framing it. It means "take courage and get to work" (Judg 7:11; 2 Sam 2:7). "The prophets who were present on the day that the foundation… was laid" refers to Haggai and Zechariah, who preached when the work restarted in 520 BC (Ezra 5:1–2; Hag 2:18).
- **"Before those days there was no wage for man or any wage for beast" (v10).** Haggai had described the same years: crops failed, money disappeared "into a bag with holes" (Hag 1:6), drought struck (Hag 1:10–11). **"Neither was there any safety from the foe"**: hostile neighbors opposed the work (Ezra 4:1–5), and **"I set every man against his neighbor"** shows even the community was divided. God was behind the hardship, using it to call them back.
- **"There shall be a sowing of peace" (v12).** Literally "the seed of peace," perhaps meaning seed that grows in peaceful conditions. Vine, ground and sky all cooperate: "the heavens shall give their dew." This reverses Haggai 1:10, "the heavens above you have withheld the dew." It also echoes the covenant blessings of Leviticus 26:4–6.
- **"As you have been a byword of cursing among the nations… so will I save you, and you shall be a blessing" (v13).** When Judah fell, the nations used her name as a curse: "May you end up like Judah." Jeremiah had warned of this (Jer 24:9; 29:18). God promises a complete reversal. The words recall the promise to Abraham: "I will bless you… so that you will be a blessing… in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:2–3). "House of Judah and house of Israel" names both the southern and northern tribes, reunited.
- **"As I purposed to bring disaster to you… and I did not relent… so again have I purposed in these days to bring good" (vv14–15).** The word *zamam*, "purposed," is repeated. God was as determined to judge as he now is to bless. If his threats came true, his promises will too. **"Fear not"** is repeated from v13.
- **"Speak the truth to one another… judgments that are true and make for peace" (v16).** The promises come with duties, and they are the same duties as 7:9–10. Blessing is not earned by obedience, but it is meant to produce it. "In your gates" means the city gates, where elders sat as judges (Deut 21:19; Ruth 4:1–11). Paul quotes v16 almost word for word: "Having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor" (Eph 4:25).
- **"Love no false oath, for all these things I hate, declares the LORD" (v17).** This picks up the false swearing condemned by the flying scroll (5:3–4). Proverbs 6:16–19 lists things the LORD hates, including "a lying tongue" and "a heart that devises wicked plans."
- **"The fast of the fourth month and the fast of the fifth and the fast of the seventh and the fast of the tenth" (v19).** At last the direct answer to the question of 7:3. The four fasts marked the stages of Jerusalem's fall: the tenth month, the start of Babylon's siege (2 Kgs 25:1); the fourth month, the breach of the walls (2 Kgs 25:3–4; Jer 39:2); the fifth, the burning of the temple (2 Kgs 25:8–9); and the seventh, most likely the murder of Gedaliah (2 Kgs 25:25). The delegation asked about one fast. God answers about all four.
- **"Shall be… seasons of joy and gladness and cheerful feasts" (v19).** Not "you may stop fasting," but "your fasts will become feasts." God's answer is far bigger than their question. Days of mourning will become days of celebration, because what they mourned will be undone. **"Therefore love truth and peace"** closes the answer with the same two words that run through the chapter (vv3, 8, 10, 12, 16).
- **"Peoples shall yet come, even the inhabitants of many cities" (v20).** The vision widens to the nations. Bethel's small delegation came "to entreat the favor of the LORD" (7:2). Now whole cities do the same (vv21–22).
- **"Let us go at once… I myself am going" (v21).** People urge one another: "Come, I'm going too." The words echo Isaiah and Micah: "many peoples shall come, and say: 'Come, let us go up to the mountain of the LORD'" (Isa 2:3; Mic 4:2). It is a picture of contagious faith.
- **"Ten men from the nations of every tongue shall take hold of the robe of a Jew" (v23).** "Ten" is a round number for many, perhaps ten to one. The verb *chazaq* means to grip firmly, the word used when Moses caught the serpent by its tail (Exod 4:4) and when David seized a lion by its beard (1 Sam 17:35). The "robe" is the *kanaph*, the corner or hem of the garment, where Israelites wore tassels to remind them of God's commands (Num 15:38–39). Ruth asked Boaz to spread his *kanaph* over her (Ruth 3:9), and the sick reached for the fringe of Jesus' garment (Matt 9:20; 14:36).
- **"Let us go with you, for we have heard that God is with you" (v23).** The nations are drawn not by Israel's power or wealth but by God's presence among them. That was always Israel's calling: to be a people so marked by God's presence that the nations would ask about him (Deut 4:6–8). The chapter ends where it began. God dwells in Zion (v3), so the world comes to Zion.
- **How these promises are fulfilled.** Christians read them in different ways. Some take them as fulfilled in part after the exile, when Jerusalem grew again, and finally in the church, where Gentiles of every tongue join God's people through a Jewish Messiah. Many dispensational interpreters expect a future, literal fulfillment in a restored Jerusalem during Christ's earthly reign. Others hold that both are true, with the church a firstfruits of a final restoration. All agree that the promise reaches its goal in the new Jerusalem, where "the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the chapter's turning point in v2: "The sins of Zion were her worst enemies." On vv4–5 he writes, "It is a graceful thing to a city to see abundance of old people in it," and of the children, "It is their pleasant playing age; let us not grudge it to them." On v19: "when God turns judgments into mercies we must turn fasts into festivals." On v23: "It is good being with those who have God with them." Guzik connects the grip of v23 with Exodus 4:4 and 1 Samuel 17:35, which the Hebrew supports, and reads the nations clinging to a Jew as clinging finally to Jesus the Jewish Messiah. He expects a full fulfillment in Christ's millennial reign. JFB also reads the restoration of vv7–8 and v13 as not yet complete, but sees the conversion of the nations as the climax. The Tyndale notes point out the "let your hands be strong" frame around vv9–13.

#### Pointing to Christ
The ten men take hold of a Jew because "God is with you." The Jew whose garment the world has reached for is Jesus, *Immanuel*, "God with us" (Matt 1:23). In the Gospels a sick woman pushed through a crowd to touch the edge of his robe and was healed (Mark 5:27–29). After his resurrection Greeks came saying, "Sir, we wish to see Jesus" (John 12:21), and the apostles took his name to "every tongue." In him the curse becomes blessing: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law… so that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles" (Gal 3:13–14). He is the one who turns fasts into feasts. While the bridegroom was taken away, his people would fast, but he promised to drink new wine with them in his Father's kingdom (Matt 9:15; 26:29). The city where old men sit and children play is the city he is preparing, where "death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning" (Rev 21:4).
