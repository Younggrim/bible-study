*Setting:* God has renewed his promises with seven "I will" statements (6:6–8), but Israel, crushed by slavery, would not listen, and Moses protested once more that he has "uncircumcised lips" (6:12, 30). Chapter 7 answers that objection and begins the contest. From here to chapter 12, the LORD confronts Pharaoh, who was treated as a god, and the gods of Egypt, in ten plagues. The point, repeated again and again, is that Egypt and Israel "shall know that I am the LORD" (7:5, 17). The plagues come in three sets of three, followed by the tenth. In each set, the first plague is announced to Pharaoh in the morning by the water, the second is announced at the palace, and the third comes without warning. The first plague begins this pattern.

#### Moses and Aaron Before Pharaoh (vv1–13)
- **"I have made you like God to Pharaoh" (v1).** The Hebrew is bolder: literally "I have made you God (*elohim*) to Pharaoh." Pharaoh claimed divine status. Now a Hebrew shepherd stands before him with real divine authority. **"Aaron shall be your prophet."** This shows what a prophet is: one who speaks another's words. Moses receives the words from God and Aaron delivers them, as God arranged in 4:15–16.
- **"I will harden Pharaoh's heart" (v3).** This verb is *qashah*, "to make hard or stubborn." In Egyptian belief, a person's heart was the center of thought and will, and after death it was weighed against the feather of truth (*ma'at*). A "heavy" heart meant guilt. The Bible's hardening language would have rung loud in Egypt. The text says God hardens (here, 9:12; 10:1), Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15, 32), and Pharaoh's heart "was hardened" (7:13, 22). Both are true. God confirms in judgment what Pharaoh chooses in pride.
- **"Signs and wonders" (v3).** These two words become the Bible's standard pair for God's mighty acts in the exodus (Deut 6:22; Ps 135:9) and are later used of Jesus and the apostles (Acts 2:22, 43).
- **"I will… bring my hosts, my people" (v4).** "Hosts" is a military word, armies. Brickmakers will march out of Egypt like an army in ranks (12:41).
- **"The Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD" (v5).** Pharaoh said "I do not know the LORD" (5:2). The plagues are God's answer to that, and the purpose is not only judgment but knowledge. Some Egyptians will come to fear God's word (9:20), and "a mixed multitude" leaves with Israel (12:38).
- **"Moses was eighty years old, and Aaron eighty-three" (v7).** Their age is noted at the moment their real work begins. Forty years in Egypt and forty in Midian were preparation. Moses lives to 120 (Deut 34:7).
- **"Prove yourselves by working a miracle" (v9).** Pharaoh asks for credentials, as a king would of any envoy claiming to speak for a god.
- **"It became a serpent" (v10).** The word here is not the "snake" of 4:3 (*nachash*) but *tannin*, which can mean a large serpent, a dragon or a sea monster, and some suggest a crocodile. The prophets later picture Pharaoh himself as "the great dragon (*tannin*) that lies in the midst of his streams" (Ezek 29:3). The sign is pointed: the creature that stands for Egypt's power is under the LORD's control.
- **"The wise men and the sorcerers… the magicians of Egypt" (v11).** "Magicians" (*chartummim*) is an Egyptian loanword for the priestly scholars who handled sacred texts and spells. The same men appear in Joseph's story (Gen 41:8). Paul gives the traditional names of two of them, "Jannes and Jambres," who "opposed Moses" (2 Tim 3:8). **"By their secret arts."** How they did it is not said. Some think it was sleight of hand: Egyptian snake charmers could make a cobra go stiff like a rod by pressing on its neck. Others think it was real demonic power (compare 2 Thess 2:9). The text doesn't settle it.
- **"Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs" (v12).** Note that it says "staff," not "serpent," as if to stress that God's staff swallows the symbols of Egypt's power. "Swallowed" (*bala*) returns at the Red Sea: "the earth swallowed them" (15:12).
- **"Still Pharaoh's heart was hardened" (v13).** "Was hardened" is *chazaq*, "made strong," stubborn. **"As the LORD had said."** Pharaoh's resistance does not catch God off guard.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Moses "was to act in this business as God's representative," and calls the brothers' age "a pledge that they had not been readily betrayed into a rash or hazardous enterprise." On the magicians, JFB thinks they produced "an illusion on the senses" but that their rods became "probably real serpents," which suggests real power at work. Guzik thinks the magicians' power was demonic and makes the point that a wonder can show something is supernatural without showing it is true. The Tyndale notes allow either demonic power or sleight of hand. Henry: Aaron's serpent "swallowed up the others… which was sufficient to have convinced Pharaoh on which side the right lay," because "great is the truth, and will prevail."

#### The First Plague: Water Turned to Blood (vv14–25)
- **"Pharaoh's heart is hardened" (v14).** Here the word is *kaved*, "heavy," the word Moses used of his own "heavy" mouth (4:10). In Egyptian terms, a heavy heart was a guilty one. The same root also gives *kavod*, "glory." Pharaoh's heart grows heavy so that God will "get glory" (literally "be made heavy") over him (14:4).
- **"Go to Pharaoh in the morning, as he is going out to the water" (v15).** Perhaps Pharaoh went to the Nile for a ritual, or simply to inspect the river. God sends Moses to meet him at the source of Egypt's life. **"Take in your hand the staff that turned into a serpent."** The staff carries yesterday's sign as a reminder.
- **"Let my people go, that they may serve me" (v16).** The demand never changes. The first word is "serve" (*avad*). Egypt has made Israel *serve* Pharaoh. God says they were made to *serve* him.
- **"Strike the water… and it shall turn into blood" (v17).** The Nile was Egypt's life. Nearly all its people lived in the narrow green band along it, and without its yearly flood there would be no farming. Egyptians worshiped the river's flood as the god Hapi, and Osiris was linked with it as well. To turn the river to blood is to strike the heart of Egypt, both its food supply and its faith.
- **"Their rivers, their canals, and their ponds… even in vessels of wood and… stone" (v19).** The plague reaches every place water was kept, down to the jars where Nile water was stored and allowed to settle. "Rivers" means the branches of the Nile in the Delta, and "canals" means the irrigation channels.
- **"All the water in the Nile turned into blood" (v20).** Some suggest God used natural means, such as red earth and algae carried by an unusually high flood, which can kill fish and make the river foul. Others insist on literal blood. The text presents it as God's direct judgment, announced in advance, starting when Moses lifts the staff and ending when God allows. Whatever the means, the timing and control are God's. Henry sees justice in it: Egypt had stained the river with the blood of Hebrew baby boys (1:22).
- **"The fish… died, and the Nile stank" (v21).** Fish were a main food for ordinary Egyptians (Num 11:5). "Stank" echoes 5:21, where the foremen complained that Moses had made them "stink" before Pharaoh. Now Egypt itself stinks.
- **"But the magicians of Egypt did the same" (v22).** With all the water already turned, they would have had to use water from wells dug by the river. They could add to the disaster but not undo it. Pharaoh needs only a small excuse not to believe.
- **"He did not take even this to heart" (v23).** Literally "he did not set his heart even to this." He goes home, and the people do the digging. **"All the Egyptians dug along the Nile for water" (v24).** Groundwater filtered through the sand gave them something to drink.
- **"Seven full days passed" (v25).** The plague lasts a full week. The text doesn't say Moses prayed for it to end, as he does later. The number suggests a complete period of judgment.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the chapter a contest "between the power of the great God and the power of a proud prince," and sees the fit of the judgment: "they had stained the river with the blood of the Hebrews' children, and now God made that river all bloody." He mocks the magicians for making more blood only "to show their art," when what Egypt needed was clean water. JFB agrees that "nothing could more humble the pride of Egypt than this dishonor brought on their national god," and thinks the magicians' copy was small, working on water "dug among the sands." Guzik lists the Nile deities Hapi, Khnum and Osiris and argues that each plague humbled an Egyptian god, as God said he would (12:12). He adds that the magicians could make things worse but never undo the harm.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses' first public sign turned water into blood, bringing death to the river and its fish. John tells us that Jesus' first sign turned water into wine at a wedding, bringing joy and life (John 2:1–11). "The law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (John 1:17). Pharaoh would not take God's warnings to heart. Jesus calls people not to harden their hearts when they hear God's voice (Heb 3:7–8, 15). And the God who struck the false gods of Egypt is the God who in Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them" (Col 2:15).
