*Setting:* The tabernacle tent and its inner furniture are finished (36:8–37:29). Now Bezalel's team moves outside to the courtyard: the great bronze altar where sacrifices were burned, the bronze basin where priests washed, and the linen fence that enclosed the whole area. All of these were described earlier (27:1–19; 30:17–21), and the account repeats them with small additions. The chapter ends with something the instructions did not contain, an audit. Moses has the materials counted, and the totals show both how much the people gave and how carefully it was handled.

#### Making the Altar of Burnt Offering (vv1–7)
- **"The altar of burnt offering of acacia wood. Five cubits… five cubits… three cubits" (v1).** About 7½ feet square and 4½ feet high (2.3 by 2.3 by 1.4 m), by far the largest piece of furniture. It stood in the courtyard just inside the gate, the first thing anyone saw on entering. No one reached God's presence without passing the place of sacrifice.
- **"Horns for it on its four corners… of one piece with it… overlaid it with bronze" (v2).** The horns received sacrificial blood (29:12), and a person fleeing for his life might grab them for refuge (1 Kgs 1:50; 2:28). The inner furniture was overlaid with gold. This altar, which bore the fire and the blood, was covered in bronze, a tougher metal for the hardest work.
- **"The pots, the shovels, the basins, the forks, and the fire pans" (v3).** The tools of a working altar: pots for ashes, shovels for coals, basins for catching blood, forks for turning meat, and fire pans for carrying coals.
- **"A grating, a network of bronze, under its ledge, extending halfway down" (v4).** Exactly how the grating worked is not clear. It may have held the fire and let ash fall through, or it may have been an outer frame that strengthened the altar.
- **"He made it hollow, with boards" (v7).** A hollow box was light enough to carry on poles (vv5–7). Many think that at each camp it was filled with earth or stones, in keeping with the older law that an altar be made of earth or unhewn stone (20:24–25), though the text does not say so.

#### Making the Bronze Basin (v8)
- **"He made the basin of bronze and its stand of bronze" (v8).** This is where the priests washed their hands and feet before serving, "so that they may not die" (30:17–21).
- **"From the mirrors of the ministering women" (v8).** This detail appears only here. Mirrors in the ancient world were made of polished bronze or copper. Egyptian women prized them highly. These women gave up something personal and valuable, an object used to look at oneself, to make a basin used for cleansing before God.
- **"Who ministered in the entrance of the tent of meeting" (v8).** The verb is *tsava'*, usually used of serving in an army, and also of the Levites' service (Num 4:23; 8:24). It is used of women only here and in 1 Samuel 2:22, where Eli's wicked sons slept with "the women who were serving at the entrance to the tent of meeting." What these women did is not explained. They may have done practical work, gathered for prayer, or kept watch at the entrance. Since the tabernacle had not yet been finished, "tent of meeting" here may refer to the earlier tent outside the camp (33:7), or the phrase may describe their later role. Like Anna, who "did not depart from the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day" (Luke 2:37), they were women devoted to God's house.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Egyptian women carried mirrors even to their temples, and that the Hebrew women gave up "those valued accompaniments of the female toilet." It thinks they were not priestesses but devout women, and that giving up their mirrors was a sign of turning from worldly concerns. Guzik draws a lesson from the mirrors: self-absorption must give way to looking to God. He links the basin to washing "with the word" (Eph 5:26) and to James's picture of Scripture as a mirror (Jas 1:23–25). He also passes on R. Alan Cole's note that the verb for the women's service is military, which the lexicon supports.

#### Making the Court (vv9–20)
- **"For the south side the hangings of the court were of fine twined linen, a hundred cubits" (v9).** The courtyard was 100 cubits by 50 (vv9–13), about 150 by 75 feet (46 by 23 m), roughly half the size of an American football field. The white linen fence was five cubits high (27:18), about 7½ feet (2.3 m), too tall to see over. The tabernacle itself stood inside it, in the western half.
- **Pillars, bases, hooks and fillets (vv10–12, 17).** The fence hung on sixty pillars standing in **bronze bases**, with **hooks** and **fillets of silver**. "Fillets" were probably silver bands or connecting rods along the tops of the pillars. The **capitals**, the tops of the pillars, were overlaid with silver (v17). From outside, the courtyard was a long wall of white linen trimmed with silver.
- **"For the front to the east, fifty cubits" (v13).** The entrance faced east, toward the sunrise. Eden's entrance was guarded on the east by cherubim (Gen 3:24). Here, on the east side, the way back to God was opened.
- **"The screen for the gate of the court was embroidered… twenty cubits long" (v18).** The gate was 30 feet wide (9 m), a colorful curtain of blue, purple and scarlet in the middle of the white wall, hung on **four pillars** (v19), with plain linen hangings of fifteen cubits on each side (vv14–15). There was only one way in, and it was wide and easy to find. Jesus said, "I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved" (John 10:9).
- **"All the pegs for the tabernacle and for the court all around were of bronze" (v20).** Even the tent pegs are recorded. The smallest parts held up the whole.

#### Materials for the Tabernacle (vv21–31)
- **"These are the records of the tabernacle, the tabernacle of the testimony" (v21).** It is called "the tabernacle of the testimony" because it housed the tablets of the covenant, the testimony (25:16). The records were kept **"under the direction of Ithamar the son of Aaron the priest."** Ithamar was Aaron's youngest son. He later oversaw the Gershonite and Merarite Levites who carried the tabernacle's curtains and frames (Num 4:28, 33).
- **Bezalel and Oholiab (vv22–23).** The two master craftsmen are named again, Bezalel who **"made all that the LORD commanded Moses,"** and Oholiab, **"an engraver and designer and embroiderer."** The chapter has come full circle from 31:1–6 and 35:30–35.
- **"Twenty-nine talents and 730 shekels" of gold (v24).** A talent was 3,000 shekels, so this was about 87,730 shekels, roughly a ton of gold (about 1,000 kg). The Tyndale notes trace this wealth back to the Egyptians, who gave the Israelites whatever they asked as they hurried them out (12:35–36).
- **"A hundred talents and 1,775 shekels" of silver (v25).** Here the arithmetic checks out exactly. **603,550 men** (v26) paying **"a beka a head (that is, half a shekel)"** gives 301,775 shekels, and 100 talents (300,000 shekels) plus 1,775 shekels is 301,775 shekels. That is about 3¾ tons (3,400 kg) of silver. A *beka* means "a split," half a shekel. Stone weights marked "beka" have been found by archaeologists in Israel. The number 603,550 is the same as the census total in Numbers 1:46. Most take this to mean the ransom money was collected when that count was made, and it is reported here because this is where the silver was used.
- **"A hundred bases for the hundred talents, a talent a base" (v27).** Each base weighed a talent, about 75 pounds (34 kg) of silver. The 96 bases under the frames and the four under the veil's pillars (26:19–25, 32) all came from the ransom money of 30:11–16. The holy house literally rested on silver paid "as a ransom for his life" (30:12). The leftover 1,775 shekels went into hooks, capitals and fillets for the court pillars (v28).
- **"The bronze that was offered was seventy talents and 2,400 shekels" (v29).** About 212,400 shekels, some 2½ tons (2,400 kg). It went into the altar, the bases of the entrance and court, and all the pegs (vv30–31).
- **Why record all this?** The numbers show the size of the people's generosity, more than seven tons of metal from a nation of former slaves in the desert. They also show integrity. Every shekel given to God was counted and accounted for. Paul shows the same care with the collection for Jerusalem: "We aim at what is honorable not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of man" (2 Cor 8:20–21).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the repeated descriptions show "the exact conformity of the execution to the order," and puts the court at about 150 by 75 feet. It suggests the hooks on the court pillars were used for hanging sacrifices and priestly items, a guess the text does not confirm. JFB tries to give the gold and silver values in British pounds of its day. It explains the wealth by the Egyptian spoil, the plunder taken from Amalek, and likely trade with neighboring peoples. Henry outlines the chapter in three parts, the altar and basin, the court, and the account of the metals, and adds little comment. Guzik notes Ithamar's oversight as an example of careful handling of money given to God, and he too traces the wealth to Egypt's payments (12:36).

#### Pointing to Christ
Anyone who came through the one gate of the courtyard met two things before anything else: an altar of sacrifice and a basin of water. Blood and washing come first, and no one reaches God's presence any other way. Jesus is both. He is the sacrifice "offered once to bear the sins of many" (Heb 9:28), and the one whose blood "cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). He is also the one door into God's house (John 10:9). And the whole structure rested on ransom silver, every Israelite counted and paid for at the same price. Believers are ransomed "not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19), and on that foundation God builds his dwelling.
