*Setting:* Jerusalem and the Jordan valley, the day after David's flight, around 976 BC. Absalom holds the capital and has publicly taken his father's concubines on the palace roof, on Ahithophel's advice (16:20–22). David is camped near the Jordan fords, weary and exposed, with only his household, his bodyguard and a few hundred loyal men. Two counselors stand before Absalom: Ahithophel, whose advice was treated "as if one consulted the word of God" (16:23), and Hushai, David's friend, sent back to undermine him (15:32–37). David had prayed on the Mount of Olives, "O LORD, please turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness" (15:31). This chapter shows how God answered that prayer, through a debate in a council room, a hiding place in a well and a gift of food from unexpected friends.

#### Hushai Saves David (vv1–29)
- **"Let me choose twelve thousand men, and I will arise and pursue David tonight" (v1).** Ahithophel's plan is fast, simple and deadly. Strike now, while David is "weary and discouraged" (v2), before he can cross the Jordan and gather support. Twelve thousand may mean twelve units, one from each tribe, a symbolic national force.
- **"I will strike down only the king" (v2).** Kill one man, and the war is over. There will be no civil war and no long fighting between tribes. Ahithophel even calls David "the king," a revealing slip.
- **"I will bring all the people back to you as a bride comes home to her husband" (v3).** The Hebrew of this verse is difficult. The ESV follows the Greek Old Testament here, picturing the nation returning to Absalom like a bride. The Hebrew may read something like "the return of all depends on the man you seek." Either way the point is the same: with David dead, the people will settle down under Absalom.
- **"The advice seemed right in the eyes of Absalom and all the elders of Israel" (v4).** It was good advice. Most modern readers and the Tyndale notes agree that, militarily, Ahithophel was right.
- **"Call Hushai the Archite also, and let us hear what he has to say" (v5).** Why ask for a second opinion when everyone agrees? The text will give the reason in v14. Absalom may have wanted to look careful, or simply to hear himself flattered. Behind it is God's hand.
- **"This time the counsel that Ahithophel has given is not good" (v7).** Hushai cannot say Ahithophel is usually wrong, since everyone knows he is usually right. So he says, in effect, "Not this time."
- **"Like a bear robbed of her cubs in the field" (v8).** A she-bear robbed of her cubs was a proverbial picture of rage (Prov 17:12; Hos 13:8). Hushai plays on Absalom's memory of his father the warrior, the man who killed a lion and a bear as a boy (1 Sam 17:34–36) and fought from caves and strongholds as an outlaw. **"He will not spend the night with the people"** and is hiding "in one of the pits" (v9). In truth David was exhausted and close by, but Hushai paints a fearsome old soldier.
- **"Whoever hears it will say, 'There has been a slaughter'" (v9).** Hushai warns that one early setback would spread panic. Even "the valiant man, whose heart is like the heart of a lion, will utterly melt with fear" (v10). He feeds Absalom's fears.
- **"All Israel be gathered to you, from Dan to Beersheba, as the sand by the sea for multitude, and that you go to battle in person" (v11).** Now he feeds Absalom's pride. "From Dan to Beersheba" means the whole land, north to south. "As the sand by the sea" echoes God's promise to Abraham (Gen 22:17). And Absalom himself will lead them. Ahithophel's plan made Ahithophel the hero. Hushai's plan makes Absalom the hero. It also takes time, which is exactly what David needs.
- **"We shall light upon him as the dew falls on the ground" (v12).** Dew settles everywhere, silently, leaving nothing untouched. **"All Israel will bring ropes to that city, and we shall drag it into the valley, until not even a pebble is to be found there" (v13).** The exaggerated, colorful language is part of the persuasion. It sounds overwhelming, and it is mostly hot air.
- **"The counsel of Hushai the Archite is better than the counsel of Ahithophel" (v14).** For the first time, someone has out-advised Ahithophel. Hushai's plan is worse but sounds better, because it appeals to vanity and fear.
- **"For the LORD had ordained to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel, so that the LORD might bring harm upon Absalom" (v14).** This is one of the few places in these chapters where the narrator tells us plainly what God is doing. The writer calls Ahithophel's advice "good," and still it fails, because God has decided against Absalom. "Ordained" is literally "commanded." The same God who let David reap the fruit of his sin is still keeping his promise to David's house (7:15). God works through people's free choices, Hushai's skill and Absalom's pride, and he is the one who decides the outcome (Prov 19:21; 21:30).
- **"Do not stay tonight at the fords of the wilderness, but by all means pass over" (v16).** Hushai does not trust the decision to last. Ahithophel might yet change Absalom's mind. So he sends an urgent warning through the priests Zadok and Abiathar, as arranged (15:35–36).
- **"Jonathan and Ahimaaz were waiting at En-rogel" (v17).** En-rogel was a spring just outside Jerusalem, usually placed south of the city where the Kidron and Hinnom valleys meet (Josh 15:7; 1 Kings 1:9). A servant girl, coming to draw water or wash clothes, would not draw attention. She carries the message from the priests to their sons.
- **"A young man saw them and told Absalom" (v18).** The plan is discovered. The two young men run.
- **"A man at Bahurim, who had a well in his courtyard. And they went down into it" (v18).** Bahurim is the same village where Shimei cursed David (16:5). Even in a town of Saul's supporters, God has people loyal to David. The "well" was probably a dry cistern, a pit cut into the rock to collect rainwater.
- **"The woman took and spread a covering over the well's mouth and scattered grain on it" (v19).** Grain was often spread out to dry, so it looked ordinary. Like Rahab hiding Israel's spies under flax on her roof (Josh 2:4–6), an unnamed woman risks her life and sends the searchers the wrong way. **"They have gone over the brook of water" (v20).** The Hebrew phrase is unusual, and no one is sure exactly what she meant. It worked.
- **"By daybreak not one was left who had not crossed the Jordan" (v22).** The warning comes in time. David's whole company crosses the river in the dark. The danger of Ahithophel's plan is past.
- **"He set his house in order and hanged himself" (v23).** Ahithophel sees at once that the delay means Absalom will lose, and that as the brains of the rebellion he would be executed. He goes home to Giloh, puts his affairs in order and takes his life. He is buried "in the tomb of his father." David's prayer is answered in full: Ahithophel's counsel becomes folly, first for Absalom and then for Ahithophel himself. The only other person in the Bible who hangs himself is Judas, another trusted friend who betrayed his master (Matt 27:5). Many Christians have read Psalm 41:9 and 55:12–14 with both men in mind.
- **"Then David came to Mahanaim" (v24).** Mahanaim, east of the Jordan in Gilead, means "two camps." There Jacob saw the angels of God on his way home, afraid of his brother (Gen 32:1–2). It had been Ish-bosheth's capital (2:8), with good walls and loyal country around it.
- **"Absalom had set Amasa over the army instead of Joab" (v25).** Amasa was David's nephew and Joab's cousin. His mother Abigal was sister to Zeruiah, Joab's mother. The ESV reads "Ithra the Ishmaelite," following 1 Chronicles 2:17 ("Jether the Ishmaelite"); the Hebrew of this verse says "the Israelite." **"Abigal the daughter of Nahash"** is puzzling, since 1 Chronicles 2:16 calls Zeruiah and Abigail David's sisters. They may have been half-sisters through their mother, whose first husband was named Nahash. Amasa will matter again (19:13; 20:8–10).
- **"Israel and Absalom encamped in the land of Gilead" (v26).** Absalom does as Hushai advised: he gathers the army, crosses the Jordan and leads it himself.
- **Shobi, Machir and Barzillai (v27).** Three men from east of the Jordan come to David's aid. **Shobi the son of Nahash from Rabbah** was probably a brother of Hanun, the Ammonite king who had insulted David's envoys (10:1–4). David had once tried to show kindness to Nahash's house, and now a son of Nahash shows kindness to David. **Machir the son of Ammiel from Lo-debar** had sheltered Mephibosheth before David took him in (9:4–5). David's kindness to Jonathan's son seems to have won him a friend. **Barzillai the Gileadite** was a very old and very rich man who will appear again when David returns (19:31–39). David never forgot him (1 Kings 2:7).
- **"Beds, basins, and earthen vessels, wheat, barley, flour, parched grain, beans and lentils, honey and curds and sheep and cheese" (vv28–29).** The list is long and homely, everything a tired army needs. **"The people are hungry and weary and thirsty in the wilderness."** God answers David's prayers not only with a clever spy but with bread and beds. Psalm 23:5 says, "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies."
- **Commentators:** All three agree that Ahithophel's plan was the wiser one. JFB calls it proof of "deep political sagacity," and Guzik says it was bold, likely to work and would have spared Israel a long civil war. Guzik sees "only the king" (v2) as an unknowing admission of who the true king was. Matthew Henry shows how Hushai "gained his point by humouring his revenge, as well as his pride," and he sets the Lord's control over all of it at the center: God "turns all men's hearts as the rivers of water." He also compares Ahithophel's plan to kill one man with Caiaphas's plan to kill Jesus, "that second Ahithophel" (John 11:49–50). On Ahithophel's suicide, JFB and Guzik both link him with Judas. Guzik says suicide is sin but adds that it should not be treated as unforgivable. JFB thinks Shobi was governor of Ammon under David and Machir possibly Bathsheba's brother. The first is a fair guess. The second rests on the name alone: 1 Chronicles 3:5 calls Bathsheba's father Ammiel (Eliam in 11:3, the same two parts reversed). It is possible but unproven.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ahithophel's plan was to strike one man so that "all the people will be at peace" (v3). Caiaphas said almost the same about Jesus: "it is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish" (John 11:50). John says he spoke more truly than he knew. The difference is everything. Ahithophel meant one man's death to secure a usurper's power. God meant the death of the one true King to bring his scattered people home in peace (John 11:51–52). The trusted friend who betrayed David hanged himself. So did the friend who betrayed Jesus. But Jesus, unlike David, did not escape across the river. He went to his death willingly, and God raised him up, defeating the counsel of all his enemies.
