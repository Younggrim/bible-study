*Setting:* The oracles against the nations now reach Damascus, capital of Aram (Syria), Israel's northern neighbor. The time is the crisis of chapter 7, about 735–732 BC. King Rezin of Damascus and King Pekah of Israel, the northern kingdom also called Ephraim, had joined forces against Assyria and tried to force Judah into their alliance by attacking Jerusalem. Ahaz of Judah, instead of trusting God, paid Assyria's Tiglath-pileser III to come and rescue him (2 Kgs 16:7–9). Isaiah had promised that both enemy kings would soon be gone (7:16; 8:4). This oracle is headed "Damascus," but most of it is about Israel, which tied its fate to Syria. Henry puts it well: the chapter is entitled "the burden of Damascus" yet "reads the doom of Israel too." It ends with a short, vivid scene of many nations storming against Zion and vanishing overnight, which most readers connect with Assyria's defeat at Jerusalem in 701 BC.

#### An Oracle Concerning Damascus (vv1–14)
- **"Damascus will cease to be a city and will become a heap of ruins" (v1).** Damascus was the strongest power in Syria for two centuries and often fought Israel (1 Kgs 20; 2 Kgs 8–13). In 732 BC Tiglath-pileser III took it, killed Rezin, and deported its people to Kir (2 Kgs 16:9). The Tyndale notes date the fulfillment there. Assyria made Damascus a provincial outpost, and as an independent kingdom it never rose again.
- **Fulfilled or still future?** Damascus was rebuilt and is today one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. So readers ask how it "ceased to be a city." Most commentators answer that the prophecy was fulfilled in 732 BC: the royal city and its kingdom ceased (v3, "the kingdom from Damascus"), and its ruins were later built over, as happened to many ancient cities. Some modern prophecy teachers expect a further, total destruction of Damascus in the future. The text itself ties the oracle to Damascus and Ephraim falling together, which happened in the 730s and 720s BC.
- **"The cities of Aroer are deserted; they will be for flocks" (v2).** Several towns were called Aroer. The best-known was on the Arnon in Moab's territory, east of the Dead Sea, and the Tyndale notes take it that way. Syria had overrun that region under King Hazael a century earlier (2 Kgs 10:32–33). So Damascus's conquests, too, will become sheep pasture. Empty towns where sheep lie down with "none to make them afraid" is a picture of total depopulation.
- **"The fortress will disappear from Ephraim" (v3).** Ephraim was the leading tribe of the northern kingdom, and its name stands for the whole kingdom. Its "fortress" was chiefly Samaria, the capital, which fell to Assyria in 722 BC after a three-year siege (2 Kgs 17:5–6).
- **"The remnant of Syria will be like the glory of the children of Israel" (v3).** This is bitter irony. Syria's glory will be like Israel's glory, which is about to fade (v4). Two allies, one fate.
- **"The glory of Jacob will be brought low, and the fat of his flesh will grow lean" (v4).** Israel is pictured as a well-fed man wasting away with illness. "Glory" (*kavod*) comes from a root meaning "heavy, weighty." The weighty one becomes thin.
- **"As when the reaper gathers standing grain… in the Valley of Rephaim" (v5).** The Valley of Rephaim was a fertile plain just southwest of Jerusalem, well known to Isaiah's Jerusalem audience. After harvest, the field was bare except for a few stalks. God's law said to leave gleanings for the poor (Lev 19:9–10), which is how Ruth found food (Ruth 2). Israel will be harvested down to its gleanings.
- **"As when an olive tree is beaten—two or three berries in the top of the highest bough" (v6).** Olives were harvested by beating the branches with sticks (Deut 24:20). A few olives always stayed out of reach at the very top. That is the remnant: tiny, but real. The Tyndale notes point out that the title used here, "the LORD God of Israel," shows that even in judgment he is still Israel's God. Henry: "The body of the people were carried into captivity, but here and there one was left behind."
- **"In that day man will look to his Maker, and his eyes will look on the Holy One of Israel" (v7).** Judgment produces repentance in the remnant. The verb "look" is repeated four times in vv7–8: they will look to God and not look to their altars. "Maker" points to God as Creator and also as the one who made Israel a nation (Deut 32:6). The Tyndale notes allow that "man" may be the Israelite remnant or humanity in general. Some Israelites did return to the Lord after Samaria fell: Hezekiah's Passover drew northerners to Jerusalem (2 Chr 30:10–11, 18).
- **"He will not look to… the Asherim or the altars of incense" (v8).** The Asherim were wooden poles or sacred trees honoring Asherah, a Canaanite fertility goddess linked with Baal. Israel had set them up beside its altars, mixing the Lord's worship with Canaanite religion (1 Kgs 16:33; 2 Kgs 17:10). The "altars of incense" may be small incense stands used in household or local shrines. The key phrase is "the work of his hands… what his own fingers have made." Isaiah contrasts the Maker with things made (2:8; 44:9–20).
- **"Like the deserted places of the wooded heights and the hilltops" (v9).** The Hebrew is difficult. The Greek Septuagint reads "like the deserted places of the Amorites and the Hivites," and the Tyndale notes follow that reading. Either way the point is a reversal: the Canaanite towns left empty when Israel arrived under Joshua will be matched by Israel's towns left empty when Israel is driven out. The land would treat Israel as it treated the Canaanites (Lev 18:28).
- **"For you have forgotten the God of your salvation and have not remembered the Rock of your refuge" (v10).** This is the root of it all. Israel's sin is not mainly ignorance but forgetting. Moses warned of this exact danger in a good land (Deut 8:11–14; 32:18, "You were unmindful of the Rock that bore you"). "Rock" pictures God as a high, safe place in a land of cliffs and caves (Ps 18:2). Guzik observes that the enemy often wins not through dramatic sins but through simple neglect and distraction.
- **"Though you plant pleasant plants and sow the vine-branch of a stranger" (v10).** "Pleasant plants" (*na'amanim*) may refer to quick-growing garden pots used in the cult of Adonis or a similar fertility god, whose name had the title *Na'aman*, "pleasant one." Many scholars suggest this. Worshippers forced plants to sprout quickly as a charm for fertility, then they withered. "The vine-branch of a stranger" means foreign cuttings: imported religion, and perhaps imported alliances like the one with Damascus. JFB reads them as costly "strange slips" from far away.
- **"Though you make them grow on the day that you plant them… yet the harvest will flee away" (v11).** Fast growth, no harvest. Whatever looked fruitful outside the true God ends "in a day of grief and incurable pain." Compare Jesus' seed on rocky ground that springs up quickly and withers (Mark 4:5–6).
- **"Ah, the thunder of many peoples… like the roaring of mighty waters!" (v12).** The scene changes abruptly. "Ah" (*hoy*) is a cry, sometimes a "woe." Now the threat is not Damascus but a vast army made up of many nations, the Assyrian empire's mixed forces. The sea and great waters are a biblical picture of chaos and of hostile nations (Ps 46:2–3; 93:3–4). Isaiah already pictured Assyria as the River overflowing its banks (8:7–8).
- **"But he will rebuke them, and they will flee far away, chased like chaff… and whirling dust" (v13).** One word from God stops the sea, as Jesus rebuked the wind and the waves (Mark 4:39; Ps 65:7). Chaff on a hilltop threshing floor blows away in the wind (Ps 1:4). "Whirling dust" is tumbleweed, rolling before a storm.
- **"At evening time, behold, terror! Before morning, they are no more!" (v14).** This is a precise picture of what happened in 701 BC. Sennacherib's army surrounded Jerusalem, and "that night the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians. And when people arose early in the morning, behold, these were all dead bodies" (37:36; 2 Kgs 19:35). The Tyndale notes point to that event, and JFB says it was "fulfilled to the letter."
- **"This is the portion of those who loot us" (v14).** The chapter ends with "us." Isaiah speaks as one of God's people, sure that all who plunder them will meet this end.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the chapter's content belongs in time before later oracles, since Damascus fell under Ahaz (2 Kgs 16:9). He sees three movements: ruin of strong cities, mercy to a remnant, and the overthrow of the Assyrian army. On the remnant he writes that "it is our duty at all times to have respect to God," looking to him as Maker and Holy One. JFB thinks the oracle was given early in Hezekiah's reign and also foresees a later overthrow of Damascus beyond Tiglath-pileser's. Guzik reads vv1–3 as fulfilled by Assyria in 2 Kings 15:29 and 16:9. He also draws out the lesson of v10: forgetting God is a serious sin, often committed by neglect.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel's tragedy was trusting what it could make and forgetting the Rock of its refuge. The remnant's recovery is simple: "man will look to his Maker." The New Testament says that Rock was Christ (1 Cor 10:4), and that the Maker became visible: "whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). The roaring nations that vanished before morning preview his rule over every power that rages against his people. He rebukes the sea with a word, and those who look to him are never put to shame.
