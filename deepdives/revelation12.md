*Setting:* The seventh trumpet has just sounded, heaven has announced that "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ," and the ark of the covenant has been seen in God's temple (11:15–19). Now Revelation starts again from a new angle. Chapters 12–14 step behind the seals and trumpets to show the war underneath all of history: the dragon against the woman, her child and her other children. Chapter 12 tells who the real enemy is, why he is so angry, and why he has already lost. Chapter 13 then shows the two beasts he works through. For a small church under pressure from Rome, this chapter explained their suffering. They were not losing. They were being attacked by a defeated enemy who "knows that his time is short."

The approaches read the chapter differently, mainly on who the woman is and when the war in heaven happens:
- **Futurists** like Guzik see the woman as Israel. The flight into the wilderness and the 1,260 days are the last three and a half years before Christ returns, when Satan, thrown out of heaven, turns on the Jewish people.
- **Historicists** like Henry and JFB see the woman as the church through history. The dragon is pagan Rome, its fall is the triumph of Christianity under Constantine, and the flood is later invasions or heresies.
- **Preterists** see the first-century church, born out of Israel, fleeing persecution, perhaps the flight of Jerusalem's Christians to Pella before AD 70.
- **Idealists** see the whole church age, from Christ's ascension to his return, pictured in one scene: the church protected and fed by God while the devil rages.

All agree on the core: Satan is real, he hates Christ and his people, he was beaten at the cross, and he cannot finish what he started.

#### The Woman and the Dragon (vv1–6)
- **"A great sign appeared in heaven" (v1).** This is the first time Revelation calls a vision a "sign" (*sēmeion*). John is telling readers plainly that what follows is a symbol pointing to something else. "In heaven" means in the sky of the vision, where both the woman and the dragon appear.
- **"A woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars" (v1).** This picture comes from Joseph's dream, where the sun, moon and eleven stars bowed to him, and Jacob took them as himself, his wife and his sons (Gen 37:9–10). So the sun, moon and twelve stars point to the family of Israel. The Old Testament often pictures God's people as a woman, a bride or a mother (Isa 54:1–6; Jer 2:2). She is clothed in heaven's light. Later in the book she has a dark opposite, the prostitute dressed in purple and gold, who is Babylon (17:4).
- **Who is the woman?** There are three main views:
  - **Israel**, the nation the Messiah came from (Rom 9:5). Guzik holds this, mainly because of Joseph's dream and the Exodus imagery that follows.
  - **The one people of God** in both testaments: Israel before Christ and the church after him. The Tyndale notes take this view, and so does JFB ("Israel first, and then the Gentile Church"). Henry sees her as the church.
  - **Mary**, the mother of Jesus. This reading is strong in Roman Catholic and Orthodox tradition, which also notices that the ark (11:19) is seen just before the woman appears. Most Protestant interpreters think Mary may be in the background but is not the main point, because the woman later flees to the wilderness for 1,260 days and has other children who are persecuted (v17).
- **"Crying out in birth pains" (v2).** The prophets used labor pain for Israel's long suffering before God's deliverance: "Like a pregnant woman who writhes and cries out in her pangs" (Isa 26:17; Mic 4:9–10; 5:3). Israel waited centuries for the Messiah. Jesus used the same image for the disciples' sorrow turning to joy (John 16:21).
- **"A great red dragon, with seven heads and ten horns, and on his heads seven diadems" (v3).** "Dragon" is *drakōn*, a great serpent or sea monster. In the Greek Old Testament it often translates *tannin*, the monster of the sea. God's enemies were pictured this way: Pharaoh is "the great dragon that lies in the midst of his streams" (Ezek 29:3), and God will "slay the dragon that is in the sea," Leviathan (Isa 27:1). Red suggests violence and bloodshed. The seven heads and ten horns come from Daniel's fourth beast (Dan 7:7, 24), and the beast of chapter 13 will have the same features. The **diadems** are royal crowns, claims to rule. The dragon has diadems, but Christ has "many diadems" (19:12).
- **"His tail swept down a third of the stars of heaven and cast them to the earth" (v4).** This echoes Daniel 8:10, where a horn "threw down to the ground some of the host and some of the stars." Many read the stars as angels who followed Satan in his rebellion and became demons, the "angels" of v7. Others read them as God's people attacked and brought low, as in Daniel. "A third" is the same limited fraction as in the trumpets (8:7–12).
- **"So that when she bore her child he might devour it" (v4).** The history of God's people is full of attempts to wipe out the line of the promised Seed: Pharaoh killing the Hebrew baby boys (Exod 1:22), Athaliah murdering the royal family of Judah (2 Kings 11:1), Haman's plot (Esth 3). The plainest example is Herod killing the boys of Bethlehem (Matt 2:16). Behind all of them John shows the dragon, the "serpent" whose enmity with the woman's offspring God announced in the garden (Gen 3:15).
- **"A male child, one who is to rule all the nations with a rod of iron" (v5).** This is Psalm 2:9, the psalm of God's anointed King. The child is plainly Jesus. "Rule" here is the verb for shepherding (*poimainō*). He guards his flock and breaks the enemy (2:27; 19:15).
- **"Her child was caught up to God and to his throne" (v5).** The whole earthly life of Jesus is skipped, from the manger to the ascension in one breath. "Caught up" is *harpazō*, to snatch away or seize. It is the same verb Paul uses for believers "caught up" to meet the Lord (1 Thess 4:17). The dragon waited to devour the child, but the child ended up on the throne. The cross itself, where the devil seemed to win, is part of how Jesus was taken beyond his reach (Acts 2:32–33).
- **"The woman fled into the wilderness, where she has a place prepared by God" (v6).** In the Bible the wilderness is a place of danger and of protection. Israel was fed with manna there (Exod 16), Elijah was fed there by ravens and an angel (1 Kings 17:3–6; 19:4–8), and God promised to "allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her" (Hos 2:14). The place was "prepared by God". She did not find it by chance.
- **"Nourished for 1,260 days" (v6).** This is the same period as the 42 months and 1,260 days of chapter 11, and the "time, and times, and half a time" of v14 and Daniel 7:25. Futurists read it as the last three and a half years. Idealists and the Tyndale notes read it as the whole time between Christ's ascension and his return. Historicists like Henry's era often read it as 1,260 years. Guzik sees a long gap between v5 and v6, with the ascension in v5 and the end-time flight in v6. He mentions the view that the place is Petra in Jordan, while presenting it only as a suggestion.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the woman as the church, clothed with "the imputed righteousness of the Lord Jesus Christ," with the moon of the world under her feet and the twelve stars as the twelve apostles' teaching. He sees the dragon's seven heads as Rome's seven hills, "placed on seven hills, as Rome was," and the dragon as "a red dragon for fierceness and cruelty." He opens the chapter by saying it is "not a prediction of things to come, but rather a recapitulation and representation of things past." JFB agrees with Henry on the church but adds that the twelve stars "are the twelve tribes of Israel." Guzik argues the opposite: the woman cannot be the church, because the church came from Christ and not the other way around. All three agree that the child is Christ and the dragon is Satan.

#### Satan Thrown Down to Earth (vv7–17)
- **"War arose in heaven, Michael and his angels fighting against the dragon" (v7).** Michael is one of the few angels named in Scripture. In Daniel he is "the great prince who has charge of your people" (Dan 12:1; 10:13, 21). Jude calls him "the archangel," who would not even rebuke the devil on his own authority but said, "The Lord rebuke you" (Jude 9). Some older interpreters take Michael as a figure for Christ, and Henry reads "Michael and his angels" as Christ and his followers. Most modern readers, Guzik included, see Michael as an angel, and note that the Lord does not even need to fight this battle himself.
- **"There was no longer any place for them in heaven" (v8).** Before this the accuser had access to God's court. In Job, Satan comes "among" the sons of God to bring charges (Job 1:6; 2:1), and in Zechariah he stands at the right hand of Joshua the high priest "to accuse him" (Zech 3:1). Now he is shut out.
- **When is the war?** This is where readers differ most:
  - **At the cross and ascension.** Jesus said, as the cross drew near, "Now will the ruler of this world be cast out" (John 12:31). He also saw "Satan fall like lightning from heaven" when his disciples cast out demons (Luke 10:18). The hymn in vv10–11 ties the victory to "the blood of the Lamb." Many idealists, preterists and the Tyndale notes read it this way, and so in part does JFB, who says Satan was "cast out judicially" after Christ's ascension.
  - **At the middle of the coming tribulation.** Guzik and most futurists see a future event that ends Satan's access to heaven and starts his last fury on earth. They point to v12: "his time is short."
  - **The fall of pagan Rome.** Henry and historicists link the dragon's defeat to the end of state idolatry in the empire, "extirpated out of the empire in the time of Constantine."
  - **The original fall of Satan** before creation. This is popular in Christian tradition and in Milton's *Paradise Lost*, though the setting here, right after the child is taken up, makes it a less natural reading.
- **"That ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world" (v9).** John lays out the dragon's full identity, so no one can miss it. "Ancient serpent" goes back to Eden (Gen 3:1). "Devil" (*diabolos*) means slanderer. "Satan" is Hebrew for adversary or accuser. "Deceiver" is present tense: he keeps on misleading the world. His first weapon is the lie (John 8:44). His angels fall with him.
- **"Now the salvation and the power and the kingdom of our God and the authority of his Christ have come" (v10).** A loud voice in heaven breaks out in song, as heaven often does in Revelation when the meaning of a vision needs to be spelled out. It echoes 11:15. "Now" ties the victory to the Christ-child's enthronement in v5.
- **"The accuser of our brothers… who accuses them day and night before our God" (v10).** "Accuser" (*katēgōr*) is a courtroom word, the prosecutor who brings charges. The Old Testament scenes show Satan doing exactly this. His charges are often true: believers really do sin. That is what makes them so heavy. But the case cannot stand, because "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies" (Rom 8:33–34), and "we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous" (1 John 2:1).
- **"They have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, for they loved not their lives even unto death" (v11).** "Conquered" (*nikaō*) is the same word as "the one who conquers" in each of the seven letters (2:7, 11, 17). Here is how believers conquer. There are three parts:
  - **"The blood of the Lamb"**: Christ's death answers every accusation. The guilt is real, but it has been paid. "By" here means "because of": the blood is the ground of victory, not a phrase to recite.
  - **"The word of their testimony"**: they kept speaking the truth about Jesus. That answers the deceiver.
  - **"They loved not their lives even unto death"**: they would rather die than deny Christ. Death cannot frighten those who share in the Lamb's victory, and the devil has no weapon left. Jesus said, "Whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matt 16:25). Antipas at Pergamum was one of them (2:13).
- **"Rejoice, O heavens… But woe to you, O earth and sea" (v12).** Heaven celebrates because the accuser is gone. Earth must brace itself, because he has landed there. Many link this cry of woe with the third woe announced in 11:14. It also explains why there is so much evil in the world. Why is the devil so angry? **"Because he knows that his time is short."** His rage is the rage of a defeated enemy with little time left. For suffering Christians this is real comfort. The fury they face is a sign that the enemy is losing, not winning.
- **"He pursued the woman who had given birth to the male child" (v13).** He can no longer reach the child, so he goes after the mother. Jesus told Saul on the road to Damascus, "Why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 9:4). To attack his people is to attack him.
- **"The two wings of the great eagle so that she might fly… into the wilderness" (v14).** God told Israel at Sinai, "I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself" (Exod 19:4), and pictured himself as an eagle carrying its young (Deut 32:11). "They shall mount up with wings like eagles" (Isa 40:31). The woman's escape is a new exodus. God himself carries her out. The Tyndale notes point out that the protection is spiritual and does not mean believers will escape all suffering or death. Some futurists have guessed at literal aircraft, which Guzik mentions only as speculation.
- **"A time, and times, and half a time" (v14).** This is Daniel's phrase (Dan 7:25; 12:7), the same as 1,260 days in v6. Repeating it from v6 shows that vv13–17 tell the same flight again in more detail.
- **"The serpent poured water like a river out of his mouth after the woman, to sweep her away with a flood" (v15).** The dragon is now a sea monster spewing water, the chaos waters God tamed at creation (Gen 1:2, 9). In the Psalms, enemies are often pictured as floods that would have "swept us away" if the Lord had not been on our side (Ps 124:2–5). Notice the water comes from his **mouth**. Many read it as a flood of lies, slander, false teaching or persecution, all things that come from the mouth.
- **"The earth came to the help of the woman, and the earth opened its mouth and swallowed the river" (v16).** One mouth answers another. This echoes the Exodus: Moses sang of Pharaoh's army, "the earth swallowed them" (Exod 15:12). It also recalls the ground opening to swallow Korah's rebels (Num 16:32). God uses his own creation to protect his people. The water flows into the desert and soaks away.
- **"Then the dragon became furious with the woman and went off to make war on the rest of her offspring" (v17).** He can't destroy the woman, so he goes after her children one by one. "Offspring" is literally "seed," the very word of Genesis 3:15: "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring." Readers identify the "rest of her offspring" as:
  - **Individual Christians** in every place, while the woman is the church as a whole. Many idealists and the Tyndale notes read it this way.
  - **Gentile believers** during the tribulation, distinct from Israel. Guzik holds this.
- **"Those who keep the commandments of God and hold to the testimony of Jesus" (v17).** This is what marks the woman's children. It is the same description John gives of himself (1:2, 9) and of the saints in 14:12. Obedience and testimony go together, and the devil hates both.
- **"And he stood on the sand of the sea" (v17).** Some translations put this sentence as 12:18. Later manuscripts read "*I* stood" and make it the start of 13:1, as in the KJV. The older manuscripts, followed by the ESV, read "*he* stood." The dragon takes his position on the seashore, waiting. In the next verse a beast rises out of the sea. The Tyndale notes contrast this scene with the Lamb standing on Mount Zion in 14:1: the dragon stands on shifting sand, the Lamb on the rock.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the stars swept down as "turning the ministers and professors of the Christian religion out of their places." He reads the overcoming of v11 in terms of the martyrs of the early church, who valued Christ's cause above their lives. On v15 he gives two readings: "The flood of water is explained by many to mean the invasions of barbarians," or a flood of heresy, and he notes that "ungodly men, for their worldly interests, protected the church." JFB states the heart of v11 well: "Had that blood not been shed, Satan's accusations would have been unanswerable." JFB says Michael is "the prince, or presiding angel of the Jewish nation," and takes "the great eagle" as "the world power," probably Rome, which sheltered the church. It reads the flood as the barbarian tribes that swept over the empire. Guzik reads the passage as Satan's last persecution of Israel, with the earth swallowing the flood as God's protection of the Jewish people, and he links the woman's flight to Jesus' warning to "flee to the mountains" (Matt 24:16). He stresses that the blood of the Lamb is not a magic formula but the answer to every accusation. He says the "love" of v11 is *agapē*, a love chosen by the will. The verb is *agapaō*, and the lexicon allows that sense, though it adds that the sharp line some draw between *agapaō* and *phileō* is debated.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter tells the gospel as a war story. A serpent waits to devour a child, and the child is snatched up to the throne. The accuser loses his place in God's court, not because believers are innocent but because of "the blood of the Lamb." The dragon's rage is real, but it is the rage of an enemy who knows his time is short. From the first promise in Eden, that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head (Gen 3:15), to this scene, the Bible tells one story, and its hero is the child the dragon could not destroy. His people conquer the way he did: by his blood, by bearing witness to him, and by trusting him even unto death.
