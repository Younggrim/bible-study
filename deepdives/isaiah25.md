*Setting:* This is the second part of the "Isaiah Apocalypse" (chs 24–27). Chapter 24 ended with the old world staggering and falling, and with the LORD reigning on Mount Zion before his elders (24:23). Chapter 25 answers with a song. The prophet, speaking for the redeemed, thanks God for bringing down a proud city and protecting the poor. Then comes one of the most hopeful scenes in the whole Old Testament: a feast for all peoples on God's mountain, where he will remove the shroud over the nations, swallow up death forever and wipe away every tear. Paul quotes it at the climax of his great chapter on resurrection (1 Cor 15:54), and Revelation borrows its words for the new creation (Rev 21:4). The chapter closes by setting proud Moab beside that feast, as a warning that the invitation can be refused.

#### God Will Swallow Up Death Forever (vv1–12)
- **"O LORD, you are my God; I will exalt you; I will praise your name" (v1).** The song begins personally, "my God," like the songs of 12:1–6. Praise here is a decision, Guzik notes, grounded in what God has done, not in passing feelings.
- **"You have done wonderful things, plans formed of old, faithful and sure" (v1).** "Wonderful" is *pele'*, the word in "Wonderful Counselor" (9:6). "Faithful and sure" is literally "faithfulness, firmness" (*'emunah 'omen*), two forms of the root that gives us "amen." God's plans were made long ago and he keeps them. The same contrast ran through chapter 22: Jerusalem did not look to "him who planned it long ago" (22:11).
- **"You have made the city a heap… the foreigners' palace is a city no more" (v2).** Which city? It is never named, like "the wasted city" of 24:10. Some take it as Babylon, as JFB does, calling it "type of the seat of Antichrist." Others suggest a Moabite city, given v10. Most modern readers take it as the city of man in general, every proud human system built without God. Guzik takes this view. **"It will never be rebuilt"** sets it against the city of God, which stands forever (26:1).
- **"Therefore strong peoples will glorify you" (v3).** God's judgment wins the honor even of tough, ruthless nations. The Tyndale notes think of Assyria and Egypt, which Isaiah already pictured worshiping with Israel (19:23–25).
- **"A stronghold to the poor… a shelter from the storm and a shade from the heat" (v4).** In a land of sudden downpours and blistering sun, a shelter and a shade meant survival. Isaiah uses both images for God's care (4:6; 32:2). The poor and needy are those with no defense of their own, often God's oppressed people. JFB takes them as the Jewish exiles.
- **"The breath of the ruthless is like a storm against a wall" (v4).** The rage of tyrants beats against God's people like rain on a wall, but the wall stands. **"As heat by the shade of a cloud, so the song of the ruthless is put down" (v5).** A passing cloud cools the desert heat, and God silences the victory chants of the oppressor just as easily. The "song" of the ruthless gives way to the song of the redeemed.
- **"On this mountain the LORD of hosts will make for all peoples a feast" (v6).** "This mountain" is Zion (24:23), the mountain to which all nations stream in 2:2–4. The guest list is "all peoples," not just Israel. In the ancient world kings held great banquets to celebrate their coronation or a victory. This is the coronation feast of the LORD as king.
- **"Rich food full of marrow, aged wine well refined" (v6).** "Rich food" is literally "fat things," the choicest portions, which in the sacrifices belonged to God (Lev 3:16). Now he serves them to his guests. "Well-aged wine" is wine "on the lees." The word *shemarim* means dregs, and BDB explains it here as wine "matured by resting undisturbed on the lees," then strained clear. It is the best vintage. JFB: "the oldest and most generous wine." The same word comes up later about Moab, which Jeremiah says was "settled on his dregs," self-satisfied and never poured out (Jer 48:11). Moab appears at the end of this chapter as the guest who would not come.
- **The feast in the New Testament.** Jesus takes up this picture: "many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 8:11). He tells parables of a great banquet where the invited refuse and the poor are brought in (Luke 14:15–24), and at the Last Supper he says he will not drink wine again until he drinks it new in his Father's kingdom (Matt 26:29). Revelation calls it "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Rev 19:9).
- **"He will swallow up on this mountain the covering that is cast over all peoples" (v7).** The "covering" and "veil" are probably a funeral shroud. Mourners covered their heads (2 Sam 15:30; Jer 14:3–4), and the dead were wrapped. Over the whole human race hangs the shroud of death. JFB also sees in it "the mist of ignorance as to a future state," and Paul uses a related image of a veil over the heart that is removed in Christ (2 Cor 3:14–16). Henry: "There is a veil spread over all nations, for they all sit in darkness."
- **"He will swallow up death forever" (v8).** The verb is *bala'*, "swallow, gulp down." In Canaanite myth Mot, the god of death, was a monster with an enormous throat who swallowed everything. The grave is pictured that way in Scripture too: "Sheol has enlarged its appetite" (5:14; Prov 1:12). Here the swallower is swallowed. God gulps down death itself. "Forever" (*lanetsach*) can also mean "in victory," and that is how Paul renders it: "Death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Cor 15:54). Guzik stresses that this is resurrection to a life that cannot die again, not a mere return to the old life. Henry: "The grave seemed to swallow him up, but really he swallowed it up."
- **"The Lord GOD will wipe away tears from all faces" (v8).** This is one of the most tender images of God in Scripture. He does not only end sorrow. He personally wipes each face. John repeats it twice in Revelation (Rev 7:17; 21:4).
- **"The reproach of his people he will take away from all the earth" (v8).** God's people have long been mocked as weak and foolish for trusting him (Ps 79:4; 1 Cor 4:13). That shame will end, because the world will see they were right. **"For the LORD has spoken"** seals the promise, as in 24:3.
- **"Behold, this is our God; we have waited for him, that he might save us" (v9).** The redeemed point and say, "This is the one!" Waiting (*qavah*) is a key word in Isaiah. It means hopeful, expectant trust, like a cord pulled tight (8:17; 40:31). Ancient Christian writers heard this verse as people recognizing the God who came in flesh, and JFB records Jerome's view that the people point to the one "whom unbelievers regarded as only a man." **"Let us be glad and rejoice in his salvation"**: the word for salvation is *yeshu'ah*, from the same root as the name Jesus.
- **"The hand of the LORD will rest on this mountain" (v10).** His hand rests on Zion in protection and blessing. In contrast, **"Moab shall be trampled down in his place, as straw is trampled down in a dunghill."** The image is crude on purpose: straw trodden into a manure pit. Moab, Israel's proud neighbor across the Dead Sea (chs 15–16), stands for every nation that trusts itself. Isaiah has already named Moab's sin: "his arrogance, his pride, and his insolence" (16:6).
- **"As a swimmer spreads his hands out to swim" (v11).** Who is swimming? Two readings are held, and JFB lists both. Most take it as Moab, flailing in the muck, trying to save himself while God pushes him down, which is how the Tyndale notes read it. Calvin and some others take it as God spreading out his hands to strike, the way a swimmer sweeps the water aside. Either way, **"the LORD will lay low his pompous pride together with the skill of his hands."** Moab's best efforts cannot keep him afloat.
- **"The high fortifications of his walls he will bring down… to the dust" (v12).** The verbs pile up: bring down, lay low, cast to the ground. The fortified city of v2 ends in dust, and the poem returns to where it began.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the feast as "the grace of the gospel," a "feast of fat things and full of marrow," fulfilled in Christ and completed at the resurrection, and says of v8, "The hope of this should now wipe away all excessive tears." Guzik places the banquet at the marriage supper of the Lamb and ties the removal of the veil to 2 Corinthians 3. JFB explains the "wines on the lees" as wine left on its sediment to gain strength and then "well refined" of all dregs. He reads v7 as Israel converted first and the nations after, a view the text itself does not spell out. All three take the "city" of v2 and Moab of v10 as figures for God's enemies more widely.

#### Pointing to Christ
Death is the great swallower, and every human grave says so. Isaiah promises the day God swallows it instead. Jesus went into death's mouth and came out the other side, and Paul quotes this verse to say what that means for everyone joined to him: "Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is your victory?" (1 Cor 15:54–55). The feast on the mountain is already set in the gospel invitation, the Lord's Supper is a foretaste of it, and one day the Host himself will wipe the tears from every face at his table. The only question the chapter leaves is whether, like Moab, one stays outside.
