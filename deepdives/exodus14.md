*Setting:* A few days after the first Passover. Israel has left Rameses, camped at Succoth and then at Etham "on the edge of the wilderness" (13:20), led by the pillar of cloud and fire. God has deliberately taken them the long way, away from the fortified coast road (13:17–18). Now he gives a strange order: turn back and camp with the sea in front of you. This is the climax of the exodus. Everything the plagues have been building toward, "the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD," is settled at the sea. Israel will look back on this night for the rest of its history, and the New Testament will see in it a picture of baptism and of salvation itself (1 Cor 10:1–2).

#### Crossing the Red Sea (vv1–31)
- **"Turn back and encamp in front of Pi-hahiroth, between Migdol and the sea" (v2).** God sends Israel backwards, into a dead end with the sea in front and desert on either side. None of the three place names can be located today with confidence. **Migdol** means "tower" or "fort," likely one of Egypt's border forts. **Baal-zephon** means "lord of the north," the name of a Canaanite storm god, probably a shrine or landmark named after him. Fittingly, the sea crossing takes place in front of a storm god's shrine, and it is the LORD who rules wind and water.
- **"They are wandering in the land; the wilderness has shut them in" (v3).** The move is bait. From Pharaoh's palace it will look as if the Hebrews are lost. "Wandering" means confused, milling about.
- **"I will get glory over Pharaoh and all his host" (v4).** "Get glory" is from *kaved*, to be heavy or weighty. Pharaoh made his heart *kaved*, heavy; now God will make himself *kaved*, glorious, over him. The Tyndale notes explain that God's "glory" means his weight, his reality, compared with which all of Egypt's power is thin. **"And they did so."** For now Israel obeys without seeing why.
- **"What is this we have done, that we have let Israel go from serving us?" (v5).** Within days the funerals are over and grief turns to regret. Egypt has lost its labor force. Without real repentance, the old self-interest returns.
- **"Six hundred chosen chariots and all the other chariots of Egypt" (v7).** The chariot was the most feared weapon of the age, light and fast, usually with a driver and a warrior. **"Officers over all of them"** translates *shalishim*, which may come from the word for "three," and some older interpreters took it as a third man in each chariot. Most now take it as "officers" or elite troops. Either way, this is the best army in the world chasing a column of families on foot.
- **"The people of Israel were going out defiantly" (v8).** Literally "with a high hand," a phrase for open, confident action. Israel walks out in full view, not sneaking away. The irony is sharp: they leave with heads high in v8 and panic in v10.
- **"The Egyptians were marching after them, and they feared greatly" (v10).** They see the dust of chariots coming. **"And the people of Israel cried out to the LORD."** That much was right.
- **"Is it because there are no graves in Egypt…?" (v11).** Then the fear turns to sarcasm. Egypt was the land of tombs and pyramids, so the jab is bitter: "Weren't there enough graves back there?" This is the first of many complaints in the wilderness (15:24; 16:2–3; 17:2–3; Num 14:2–4). **"Leave us alone that we may serve the Egyptians" (v12).** They rewrite their own past. The Tyndale notes sum up the attitude well: the security of slavery feels safer than the risk of faith.
- **"Fear not, stand firm, and see the salvation of the LORD" (v13).** Moses does not know how God will act, but he knows that he will. "Stand firm" means hold your ground, don't run. "Salvation" is *yeshu'ah*, rescue or deliverance, the word that the name Jesus (Hebrew *Yeshua*) is built on. Jehoshaphat's army heard almost the same words (2 Chr 20:17).
- **"The Egyptians whom you see today, you shall never see again."** A bold promise, and literally true by the next morning.
- **"The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent" (v14).** God as warrior is a theme that rises here and runs through Scripture (15:3; Deut 1:30; Josh 10:14). "Be silent" means stop the panic and the complaining. The battle is not theirs.
- **"Why do you cry to me? Tell the people of Israel to go forward" (v15).** Moses has been praying, though we were not told so. God's answer is not a rebuke of prayer but a call to move. There is a time to wait and a time to walk. Israel is told both "stand firm" and "go forward," and the two belong together: trust God, and then obey.
- **"Lift up your staff, and stretch out your hand over the sea and divide it" (v16).** The same staff that struck the Nile (7:20) now opens the sea. **"On dry ground"** is repeated four times in the chapter (vv16, 21, 22, 29), as if to say: not mud, not wading.
- **"I will harden the hearts of the Egyptians so that they shall go in after them" (v17).** For the last time in the story, the hardening is mentioned, now on the whole army. They will chase Israel into a place no sane army would go.
- **"The angel of God… moved and went behind them" (v19).** The guide becomes a rear guard. "The angel of God" and "the pillar of cloud" are named side by side, the same presence described two ways. Elsewhere this angel speaks as God himself (3:2–6).
- **"There was the cloud and the darkness. And it lit up the night" (v20).** The Hebrew is compressed, and translations differ, but the sense is clear: to Egypt the cloud was darkness, to Israel it gave light. The same presence that protects God's people blocks his enemies.
- **"The LORD drove the sea back by a strong east wind all night" (v21).** God uses a wind, as with the locusts (10:13), but the result is beyond any natural event. **"The waters were divided."** Psalm 74:13 says, "You divided the sea by your might."
- **"The waters being a wall to them on their right hand and on their left" (v22).** The song in chapter 15 calls it waters that "stood up in a heap" and "congealed in the heart of the sea" (15:8). Some scholars have argued that a strong, steady wind over a shallow lake could push water back. The Tyndale notes answer that this might lower a lake, but it does not produce dry ground with walls on both sides. The text means a miracle, worked through means God chose.
- **Where was it?** The *yam suph* ("Sea of Reeds") has been placed at Lake Ballah, Lake Timsah, the Bitter Lakes, the northern Gulf of Suez, or even the Gulf of Aqaba. JFB thought the site "has not yet been, and probably never will be, satisfactorily fixed," and that is still the honest answer. As the Tyndale notes say, the first readers knew exactly where it was, even if we do not.
- **"The Egyptians pursued and went in after them" (v23).** Pharaoh's army follows Israel down the same path. What was a road for one was a grave for the other.
- **"In the morning watch the LORD… looked down" (v24).** The morning watch was the last watch of the night, roughly 2 to 6 a.m. **"Looked down… in the pillar of fire and of cloud"** pictures God glancing from the cloud, and the glance alone is enough. Psalm 77:16–19 adds thunder, lightning and rain to the scene. **"Threw the Egyptian forces into a panic."** The verb is used of God confusing armies in holy war (Josh 10:10; Judg 4:15).
- **"Clogging their chariot wheels so that they drove heavily" (v25).** The Hebrew reads "turned aside" or "removed" the wheels; the ancient Greek and Samaritan versions read "bound" or "clogged." Either way, the chariots bogged down or broke on the seabed. "Heavily" is *kaved* once more. Pharaoh's heavy heart ends in heavy wheels.
- **"Let us flee from before Israel, for the LORD fights for them against the Egyptians."** Egypt finally says what Moses said in v14. The knowledge God promised (v4) comes, too late.
- **"The sea returned to its normal course when the morning appeared" (v27).** Daylight shows the full reversal. **"The LORD threw the Egyptians into the midst of the sea."** The verb means to shake off, as one shakes something out of a garment. Pharaoh had thrown Hebrew babies into the Nile (1:22). Now his army is thrown into the sea.
- **"Not one of them remained" (v28).** The text does not say whether Pharaoh himself drowned, and Egyptian records say nothing about it, which is unsurprising, since Egypt rarely recorded defeats. Psalm 136:15 says God "overthrew Pharaoh and his host in the Red Sea," which may mean his army rather than his own body.
- **"Israel saw the Egyptians dead on the seashore" (v30).** They saw the end of their oppressors with their own eyes. The fear of v10 is gone.
- **"The people feared the LORD, and they believed in the LORD and in his servant Moses" (v31).** "Saw… feared… believed" mirrors "saw… feared greatly" in v10. The fear of the enemy turns into the fear of the LORD. Sadly, this faith would be tested within three days (15:22–24). **"His servant Moses."** "Servant of the LORD" is a title of great honor, later given to Joshua, David and the Servant of Isaiah 42 and 53.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes Israel's fear: "when without are fightings, it cannot be otherwise but that within are fears." He reads Moses' word as "Stand still, and think not to save yourselves either by fighting or flying," and says of the sea: "God can bring his people through the greatest difficulties, and force a way where he does not find it." He pictures the Egyptians "struck with a panic-fear," their "wheels dropped off, and the axle-trees failed," and calls the crossing "a type of baptism," following Paul in 1 Corinthians 10:2. JFB glosses Pi-hahiroth as "the mouth of the defile, or pass" and Migdol as "a fortress or citadel," and says the cloud gave "light (a symbol of favor) to the people of God, and darkness (a symbol of wrath) to their enemies." He argues that no ebb tide could make walls of water on both sides. JFB also suggests the word for "east" primarily means "previous," so the wind might be one that blew beforehand. The lexicon does not support that: the word here, *qadim*, simply means "east" or "east wind," often a scorching wind off the desert. Guzik notes the tension between "stand still" and "go forward" and says there is a time to pray and a time to act, and that prayer can even become a way to put off obeying. He links the Passover and the sea as two halves of one redemption, much as the cross and the empty tomb belong together.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel stood trapped between an army and the sea with no way out, and God made a way where there was none. Paul says the fathers "were all baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea" (1 Cor 10:2). The crossing is a picture of what God does for his people in Christ: we pass through death and come out the other side, while the enemy that enslaved us is left behind for good. Jesus went down into death itself, not between walls of water but into the grave, and rose on the third day. Sin and death, the oppressors we could not escape, are overthrown in him (Col 2:15; Heb 2:14–15). "The LORD will fight for you" is the gospel in one line. We contribute nothing to our rescue but the need of it, and like Israel on the far shore, we are called to "see the salvation of the LORD" and believe.
