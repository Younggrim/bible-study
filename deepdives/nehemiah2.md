*Setting:* Spring of 445 BC, at the Persian winter palace in Susa. Four months earlier, in Chislev, Nehemiah heard that Jerusalem's walls lay broken and its gates burned, and he has been fasting and praying ever since (1:4–11). His prayer ended with a request that God would "grant him mercy in the sight of this man," the king, and a quiet note: "Now I was cupbearer to the king." Chapter 2 is the answer. Behind it lies real danger. Only a few years earlier this same Artaxerxes had ordered the rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls stopped, "until a decree is made by me" (Ezra 4:21). Nehemiah is about to ask him to reverse his own order.

#### Nehemiah Sent to Judah (vv1–8)
- **"In the month of Nisan, in the twentieth year" (v1).** Nisan is March–April 445 BC, about four months after the news came (1:1). Nehemiah waited and prayed until the moment came. Some interpreters connect this decree with Daniel's prophecy of seventy weeks, which counts from "the going out of the word to restore and build Jerusalem" (Dan 9:25). Others date that word to Cyrus (538 BC) or to Ezra's commission (458 BC). Faithful readers differ on the reckoning.
- **"When wine was before him, I took up the wine and gave it to the king" (v1).** This was Nehemiah's daily duty. The Greek writer Xenophon describes how Persian cupbearers served: rinsing the cup, tasting a little from their own hand, then offering it to the king with graceful fingertips. Persian palace reliefs show such servants. Being trusted with the king's cup meant being trusted with his life.
- **"Now I had not been sad in his presence" (v1).** Servants were expected to show a cheerful face before the king. Esther 4:2 notes that no one could even enter the king's gate in mourning clothes. A sad face could suggest discontent or even disloyalty. After four months of grief, Nehemiah could no longer hide it.
- **"This is nothing but sadness of the heart." Then I was very much afraid (v2).** The king noticed and named it. Nehemiah's fear was reasonable. Kings could be suspicious of a sad servant who handled their wine, and the request he was about to make touched an order the king himself had given.
- **"Let the king live forever!" (v3).** The standard court greeting (Dan 2:4; 6:21). Then comes his honest answer, carefully worded. He does not mention Jerusalem by name or the walls the king had stopped. He speaks of "the city, the place of my fathers' graves." In the ancient world, honoring ancestors' tombs was a sacred duty, one a Persian king would respect.
- **"What are you requesting?" So I prayed to the God of heaven (v4).** Between the king's question and his answer, Nehemiah prays. There is no time for a long prayer, only a silent cry of the heart. Months of prayer lay behind this moment, and now a few seconds of prayer meet it. Nehemiah prays like this repeatedly through the book (4:4–5; 5:19; 6:9, 14; 13:14).
- **"That you send me to Judah, to the city of my fathers' graves, that I may rebuild it" (v5).** He asks politely ("if it pleases the king," "if your servant has found favor"), but plainly. He does not only want permission. He wants to be *sent*, with the king's authority behind him.
- **"(the queen sitting beside him)" (v6).** Nehemiah adds this detail as an aside. Persian kings did not usually have their wives at public banquets, so this was probably a private meal. The Greek historian Ctesias names Artaxerxes' queen as Damaspia. Her presence may have helped, though the text does not say so.
- **"How long will you be gone, and when will you return?" (v6).** The king's question shows he valued Nehemiah and wanted him back. It also shows the request was granted. Nehemiah "had given him a time." He did not tell us what time, but 5:14 says he served as governor for twelve years (445–433 BC). He may have asked for a shorter period at first and been extended.
- **"Let letters be given me" (vv7–8).** Nehemiah had a plan ready. He asks for safe-conduct letters to the governors west of the Euphrates ("Beyond the River"), and a letter to "Asaph, the keeper of the king's forest." "Forest" is *pardes*, a Persian loanword for a royal park or enclosed garden. It is the source of the English word "paradise." The park's location is unknown. Lebanon, famous for cedar, is possible, though some suggest a royal park nearer Jerusalem, perhaps near Bethlehem. Asaph has a Hebrew name and may have been a Jew.
- **"Beams for the gates of the fortress of the temple, and for the wall of the city, and for the house that I shall occupy" (v8).** The "fortress" (*birah*) was a citadel guarding the temple's north side, where the ground was most open to attack. Centuries later Herod built the Antonia Fortress on or near the same spot. Nehemiah had thought through gates, wall and his own residence. Four months of prayer had included four months of planning.
- **"For the good hand of my God was upon me" (v8).** The same phrase that marked Ezra's journey (Ezra 7:6, 9, 28). Nehemiah does not credit his own tact or position. God moved the king's heart (Prov 21:1).
- **Commentators:** JFB describes the Persian cupbearer's ceremony from Xenophon and notes that signs of mourning were "highly unbecoming" before the king (Esth 4:2). JFB suggests the queen was Esther and that her influence won the request. That cannot be right: Esther was the wife of Xerxes, Artaxerxes' father. Henry says Nehemiah "lifted up his heart to that God who understands the language of the heart," and that "approaches to the throne of grace are never out of season." Guzik makes the point that Nehemiah's four months of prayer were also four months of planning, since he had answers ready for every question.

#### Nehemiah Inspects Jerusalem's Walls (vv9–20)
- **"The king had sent with me officers of the army and horsemen" (v9).** Thirteen years earlier, Ezra was "ashamed to ask the king for a band of soldiers" (Ezra 8:22). Nehemiah accepted one. Ezra was a priest who had made a public claim about God's protection. Nehemiah was a royal governor traveling on the king's business. Neither is criticized. Faith can trust God either with or without an escort.
- **Sanballat the Horonite (v10).** "Horonite" probably means he came from Beth-horon, about twelve miles northwest of Jerusalem, though some (including JFB) link it with Horonaim in Moab. His name is Babylonian, *Sin-uballit*, "the moon-god Sin gives life," though his sons had names honoring the LORD. A letter from the Jewish colony at Elephantine in Egypt, dated 407 BC, mentions "Sanballat governor of Samaria" and his sons Delaiah and Shelemiah. He was the most powerful official in the region north of Judah, and a stronger Jerusalem threatened his influence.
- **Tobiah "the Ammonite servant" (v10).** Tobiah is a Hebrew name meaning "the LORD is good." "Servant" may mean a royal official, as in "servant of the king," a title found on seals of the period, and probably not a slave. He was connected by marriage with leading Jewish families (6:17–18) and with the priest Eliashib (13:4–7). A powerful family named Tobiah ruled from Transjordan for centuries afterward. "Ammonite" may describe the territory he governed east of the Jordan.
- **"It displeased them greatly that someone had come to seek the welfare of the people of Israel" (v10).** The phrase "displeased them greatly" is literally "it was evil to them, a great evil." The narrator lets their motive show. Their trouble was not with Nehemiah personally but with Israel's good.
- **"Was there three days" (v11).** Like Ezra (Ezra 8:32), Nehemiah rested and took stock before acting.
- **"I arose in the night, I and a few men with me. And I told no one" (v12).** Nehemiah inspected before he announced. He kept his plans quiet, perhaps to keep enemies and their friends inside the city from organizing against him before he had a plan. "What my God had put into my heart to do" shows that he saw the plan itself as God's gift.
- **The route (vv13–15).** He went out by the Valley Gate, on the western side of the old City of David, the narrow ridge south of the temple. He went south past the Dragon Spring (perhaps En-rogel, or the "Jackal's Well") to the Dung Gate at the city's southern tip, which led out to the Hinnom Valley where rubbish was dumped. Then he turned north to the Fountain Gate and the King's Pool, near the Pool of Siloam. There "there was no room for the animal… to pass" (v14). Then he went up "by the valley," the Kidron on the east side, and came back the way he had come. The Jerusalem of Nehemiah's day was much smaller than later Jerusalem, covering mainly the eastern ridge and the temple mount.
- **"No room for the animal" (v14).** Excavations on the eastern slope of the City of David have found collapsed stone terraces and houses from the Babylonian destruction of 586 BC, a tumbled mass of rubble. Nehemiah's new wall on that side seems to have run higher up the slope, along the ridge, leaving the old ruin outside. He saw for himself just how bad it was.
- **"I had not yet told the Jews, the priests, the nobles, the officials, and the rest who were to do the work" (v16).** The list is the whole community. All of them would do the work. Nehemiah had the overall picture before he called them together.
- **"You see the trouble we are in" (v17).** Nehemiah says "we," not "you." He has only just arrived, but he joins himself to them, as he did in prayer (1:6). "That we may no longer suffer derision" names the deeper problem: the broken walls made God's people, and so God's name, a joke among the nations.
- **"I told them of the hand of my God that had been upon me for good, and also of the words that the king had spoken" (v18).** Two reasons to build: God was in this, and the king had authorized it. Nehemiah gave them both a spiritual and a practical basis for confidence.
- **"Let us rise up and build." So they strengthened their hands for the good work (v18).** The people answer with his own words. "Strengthened their hands" is a Hebrew idiom for taking courage and committing to a task (Ezra 6:22; Judg 9:24). After decades of discouragement, one man's prayer, plan and testimony moved a whole city.
- **Geshem the Arab (v19).** A third enemy appears. Geshem was a powerful Arab ruler. A silver bowl found in Egypt, at Tell el-Maskhuta, is inscribed by "Qainu son of Geshem, king of Kedar," probably the same man's son. Kedar was a leading Arab people who controlled trade routes south of Judah. So Judah was now ringed by hostile officials: Sanballat to the north, Tobiah to the east, Geshem to the south.
- **"They jeered at us and despised us… Are you rebelling against the king?" (v19).** Mockery comes first, then accusation. The charge of rebellion was dangerous, because it had worked before (Ezra 4:12–16). This time it was empty: Nehemiah had the king's letters.
- **"The God of heaven will make us prosper" (v20).** Nehemiah does not show his letters or argue. He points to God. "We his servants will arise and build" contrasts with Tobiah "the servant": these men serve the king, but Nehemiah and the people serve the God of heaven.
- **"You have no portion or right or claim in Jerusalem" (v20).** Three words: no share of land, no legal right and no "memorial" (*zikkaron*), no remembrance or place in its worship. The leaders of the first return gave a similar answer when their neighbors offered to help build the temple: "You have nothing to do with us in building a house to our God" (Ezra 4:3). Jerusalem belongs to the people who belong to its God.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls Tobiah "a freed slave, elevated to some official dignity," and treats both men as leaders of the Samaritan party. Most scholars now read "servant" as an official title, not slavery. JFB's description of the route follows nineteenth-century maps that placed the Valley Gate at today's Jaffa Gate. Archaeology now places Nehemiah's city on the eastern ridge, so that part of JFB is out of date. Henry writes that Nehemiah "does not undertake to do it without them," and "By stirring up ourselves and one another to that which is good, we strengthen ourselves and one another for it." Of the enemies he writes, "The enmity of the serpent's seed against the cause of Christ is confined to no age or nation." Guzik notes that Nehemiah counted the cost before he started, seeing the damage honestly, and that he answered mockery with a statement of faith instead of a debate.

#### Pointing to Christ
Nehemiah leaves the palace, sent by the king, to rebuild a city lying in disgrace. He comes quietly, sees the ruins for himself in the dark, and then says "we," taking the city's shame as his own. Jesus was sent by the Father from a far greater throne. He came to a people in ruins, saw their condition fully, and identified with them completely, "made like his brothers in every respect" (Heb 2:17). Like Nehemiah, he faced mockery and the charge of rebellion against Caesar (Luke 23:2). And he builds a city that no enemy can claim, "the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:10). Those who oppose it "have no portion" there. Those who belong to him are "fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Eph 2:19).
