*Setting:* For seven days and nights Job has sat on the ash heap with his three friends, and nobody has said a word (2:13). Now Job breaks the silence, and the book changes. The prose story of chapters 1–2 gives way to Hebrew poetry, which runs all the way to 42:6. The calm Job who said "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away" (1:21) now pours out raw grief. This chapter is a lament, a cry of pain addressed to no one in particular and to God at the same time. It does three things: Job curses the day he was born (vv1–10), wishes he had died at birth (vv11–19), and asks why life is given to people who long to die (vv20–26). It is one of the darkest chapters in the Bible, and Scripture includes it on purpose.

#### Job Laments His Birth (vv1–26)
- **"After this Job opened his mouth and cursed the day of his birth" (v1).** The word for "cursed" here is *qalal*, the ordinary word for cursing, not the polite substitute *barak* used in 1:5, 1:11 and 2:5, 9. Satan said Job would curse God. Job curses something, but not God. He curses his birthday. The Tyndale notes point out that his words are closer to sad complaint than a real curse, and that they are aimed at the condition of his life, not at his Creator.
- **"Let the day perish on which I was born, and the night that said, 'A man is conceived'" (v3).** Job wants both ends of his existence undone: the night he was conceived and the day he was born. The night is pictured as a person announcing the news, as if it had been proud of it. Jeremiah uses almost the same words in his own darkest hour: "Cursed be the day on which I was born!" (Jer 20:14–18). Elijah, too, asked to die (1 Kgs 19:4). Job is in the company of prophets here, not unbelievers.
- **"Let that day be darkness!" (v4).** This is creation run backward. In Genesis 1:3 God said, "Let there be light." Job says, in effect, "Let there be darkness." The words in Hebrew are close: *yehi or*, "let there be light," and *yehi choshek*, "let there be darkness." He wants his day erased from creation, as if God had never spoken it into being.
- **"May God above not seek it" (v4).** Job asks that God would not look for that day or care about it. The irony is that Job longs, later in the book, for exactly the opposite: that God would seek him out and answer him (23:3–9).
- **"Gloom and deep darkness" (v5).** "Deep darkness" is *tsalmaveth*, the word in "the valley of the shadow of death" (Ps 23:4). The Tyndale notes connect it with the gloom of the place of the dead (10:21–22; 38:17). Job piles up darkness words: gloom, deep darkness, clouds, blackness, thick darkness (v6). The language is meant to feel smothering.
- **"Let it not come into the number of the months" (v6).** Job wants the calendar itself to skip his birthday, so it never comes round again.
- **"Let that night be barren; let no joyful cry enter it" (v7).** A birth was a time for shouts of joy. Job wants that night to have been empty: no conception, no celebration.
- **"Let those curse it who curse the day, who are ready to rouse up Leviathan" (v8).** The ancient world had professional cursers, people hired to put a curse on a day or a person, like Balaam (Num 22–24). The Tyndale notes make that connection. **Leviathan** (*livyathan*) is a great sea creature, sometimes a crocodile (41:1), sometimes a whale-like beast God made to play in the sea (Ps 104:26), and in poetry a dragon of chaos that God crushed (Ps 74:14; Isa 27:1). The lexicon lists Job 3:8 under the mythological dragon that was thought to swallow the sun and cause eclipses. Job is using the imagery of his world: "Let the sorcerers who claim they can wake the monster that eats the light swallow that day whole." He is not endorsing magic. He is reaching for the strongest picture of darkness he knows. At the end of the book, God himself will describe Leviathan at length (ch 41), and Job will learn that even the creature people feared most is just one of God's creatures.
- **"Nor see the eyelids of the morning" (v9).** A lovely phrase for the first rays of dawn, as if the morning were opening its eyes. The "stars of its dawn" are the morning stars, which the Tyndale notes identify as Venus and Mercury, shining just before sunrise (38:7). Job wants that night to wait and wait for a morning that never comes.
- **"Because it did not shut the doors of my mother's womb" (v10).** This is the reason for the whole curse. The night let him be born, so it let him see trouble. The Tyndale notes observe that "shutting the womb" usually refers to conception (1 Sam 1:5–6), but here it refers to birth itself.
- **"Why did I not die at birth?" (v11).** The second stanza turns from cursing to questions. The Tyndale notes count seven "why" questions across vv11–23. "Why" is the cry of the psalms of lament: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Ps 22:1). Job never asks "Why me?" in a self-pitying way. He asks why life itself has to go on.
- **"Why did the knees receive me?" (v12).** In the ancient world a newborn was placed on the knees of the father, or sometimes a grandparent, to show that he was accepted into the family (Gen 50:23). The breasts were the mother's nursing. Job wishes nobody had received him or fed him.
- **"Then I would have lain down and been quiet; I would have slept" (v13).** Job pictures death as rest and sleep. Notice the four words: lain down, quiet, slept, at rest. He longs for peace more than anything. Old Testament believers had only a dim view of life after death. They spoke of *Sheol*, the place of the dead, as a shadowy realm of silence. Job will reach further later in the book: "I know that my Redeemer lives… in my flesh I shall see God" (19:25–26). Here, though, he simply wants the noise of suffering to stop.
- **"With kings and counselors of the earth who rebuilt ruins for themselves" (vv14–15).** In death Job would lie among the greatest people of history. "Rebuilt ruins" may mean kings who restored ancient cities to make a name for themselves, or it may mean they built great tombs and monuments that are now ruins. The princes "who filled their houses with silver" may have been buried with their treasure, or the line may simply point out that their wealth did them no good in the end. The Tyndale notes favor the second reading. Either way, death levels everyone. Job, once a prince himself (1:3; 29:25), would lie down as their equal.
- **"As a hidden stillborn child" (v16).** A baby born dead and buried quietly, never seeing "the light." It is a shocking wish, and Job means it to be.
- **"There the wicked cease from troubling, and there the weary are at rest" (v17).** Job sees the grave as a place where everything that troubles people in this life stops. Raging oppressors fall silent. Worn-out workers rest. Prisoners no longer hear "the voice of the taskmaster" (v18), the same word for the slave drivers of Egypt (Exod 3:7). "The small and the great are there, and the slave is free from his master" (v19). This picture of the dead is not the whole truth. The Bible later shows that the grave is not the end for either the wicked or the righteous (Dan 12:2; John 5:28–29). But it is a true picture of how death looks to someone in unbearable pain: a release.
- **"Why is light given to him who is in misery, and life to the bitter in soul?" (v20).** The third stanza widens to all sufferers. "Light" is life, as it often is in Scripture (33:30; John 1:4). "Bitter in soul" is a phrase used of Hannah in her longing (1 Sam 1:10) and of the desperate men who joined David in the cave (1 Sam 22:2). Job now speaks for everyone who has felt this.
- **"Who long for death, but it comes not, and dig for it more than for hidden treasures" (v21).** The picture is of treasure hunters digging with great effort. Job says sufferers search for death more eagerly than that. Yet he never takes his own life, and never considers it. He wants God to end it (6:8–9). His longing to die is spoken to God, which is itself a kind of faith.
- **"A man whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in" (v23).** For the first time in the chapter, Job names God directly as the one behind his trouble. "Hedged in" (*sakak*) is a word for fencing or closing off. In 1:10 Satan said God had put a "hedge" around Job (a related word, *suk*) to protect him. Job, knowing nothing about that conversation, feels the same hedge from the inside, and to him it is a prison wall. What was protection now feels like a trap. He can't see his way forward or out.
- **"My sighing comes instead of my bread, and my groanings are poured out like water" (v24).** Sighs come before meals, or take the place of them. His groans pour out like a river that won't stop. This is the language of real depression and physical misery, and the Bible does not hurry past it.
- **"For the thing that I fear comes upon me" (v25).** Job seems to say that he always feared disaster might come, even when life was good, and now it has. His early-morning sacrifices for his children (1:5) show a man who knew how fragile his blessings were. Some readers take the verse as present tense: one dreaded thing after another keeps arriving.
- **"I am not at ease, nor am I quiet; I have no rest, but trouble comes" (v26).** The last verse echoes the words of v13 in reverse: the quiet and rest he longs for in death are exactly what he doesn't have in life. "Trouble" (*rogez*) is turmoil or agitation. The chapter ends without resolution, without any turn to hope, the way some psalms of lament do (Ps 88). That honesty is part of the Bible's comfort. God allows his people to speak like this.
- **Did Job sin here?** Faithful readers disagree. Some, like Henry, see real impatience here, sin with his lips that Job later repents of (42:6). Others note that God later says Job spoke rightly of him, unlike the friends (42:7), and that James calls him a model of steadfastness (Jas 5:11). The Tyndale notes take the second view: Job's outburst does not mean his integrity failed. Both views agree on the main thing: Job cursed his birthday, but he never cursed God.
- **Commentators:** Henry is the sternest of the three. He sees impatience in the chapter and calls the wish never to have been born "a vain wish, for it is impossible to make that which has been not to have been." He draws a sharp line between wanting to die "that we may be with Christ" and wanting it only "that we may be quiet in the grave," and concludes that grace teaches us, "in the midst of life's greatest comforts, to be willing to die." He also notes that death is "an irresistible leveller." Guzik is gentler. He reads the chapter as poetic overstatement from a man in agony, stresses that Job speaks *to* God while the friends speak only *about* him, and treats Job's picture of the dead as limited understanding that God's later revelation corrects (2 Tim 1:10). JFB sums it up: "Job cursed his birthday, but not his God." On v8 JFB thinks Leviathan is a real animal that magicians claimed to rouse. Guzik reads it as the chaos monster of ancient poetry. The lexicon supports Guzik's reading for this verse. JFB reads v26 as a fresh trouble still to come, the friends' suspicion that he is a hypocrite, which the next chapters bring.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job's "why?" is answered most deeply on the cross, where Jesus took up the opening line of a lament psalm: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34; Ps 22:1). Jesus did not stand above the darkness Job describes. He entered it. Job wished for rest in the grave; Jesus went into the grave and came out of it, and so he can say to all who are "weary and heavy laden," "I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28). Job pictured death as a place where prisoners are freed and slaves released, but only as a sad escape. In Christ that freedom is real and living: he "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10). And because Jesus prayed with "loud cries and tears" (Heb 5:7), believers in their own dark nights can pray as honestly as Job did, and know they are heard.
