*Setting:* After oracles against Babylon, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Edom and Arabia, chapter 22 turns without warning to Jerusalem itself. God's own city appears in a list of nations under judgment, because it has started to act like them. The background is the Assyrian crisis of Hezekiah's reign, around 705–701 BC. Hezekiah rebelled against Assyria, Sennacherib invaded, took 46 walled towns of Judah, and surrounded Jerusalem (chs 36–37; 2 Kings 18–19). The city frantically repaired its walls and secured its water supply. When the Assyrian army withdrew, Jerusalem celebrated. Isaiah weeps instead, because the city trusted its own preparations and never turned to God. Many readers think the prophecy also looks ahead to 586 BC, when Babylon finished what Assyria began. The chapter closes with two royal officials, Shebna and Eliakim, one proud and one called "my servant."

#### An Oracle Concerning Jerusalem (vv1–25)
- **"The oracle concerning the valley of vision" (v1).** The title is a riddle, like "the wilderness of the sea" in 21:1. Verse 5 shows it means Jerusalem. The city sits on hills, but it is ringed by higher hills and cut by the Kidron, Tyropoeon and Hinnom valleys. It was also the place of vision, where God spoke through his prophets. JFB calls it "the seat of divine revelations and visions." The Tyndale notes see irony too: the city prided itself on spiritual sight while its people were blind (6:10). Guzik observes how strange it is to find Jerusalem in a series of oracles against foreign nations, and concludes that Judah had taken on its neighbors' ways.
- **"You have gone up, all of you, to the housetops" (v1).** Houses had flat roofs used as living space. People climbed up to watch, to shout, to celebrate. Commentators differ on the mood. Most modern scholars, and the Tyndale notes, see a party after the Assyrian retreat: "tumultuous city, exultant town" (v2). JFB and Guzik read the rooftops as panic, people scanning the horizon for the enemy. The city's noise may well have been both, fear turning to relief.
- **"Your slain are not slain with the sword or dead in battle" (v2).** There is no glory in how they died. They died of hunger and disease in a siege, or were cut down running away. Sieges cut a city off from its fields and crowded it with refugees, and sickness followed.
- **"All your leaders have fled together; without the bow they were captured" (v3).** They were taken without a fight. This matches 586 BC closely: King Zedekiah and his soldiers slipped out of Jerusalem by night and were caught on the plains of Jericho (2 Kings 25:4–6). JFB notes that this verse "applies better to the attack of Nebuchadnezzar."
- **"Look away from me; let me weep bitter tears" (v4).** While the city cheers, the prophet mourns. "The daughter of my people" is a tender name for Jerusalem's people, used often by Jeremiah (Jer 8:21–22; 9:1). Isaiah, like Jeremiah after him and Jesus later (Luke 19:41), weeps over the city he warns.
- **"The Lord GOD of hosts has a day of tumult and trampling and confusion" (v5).** In Hebrew the three nouns sound alike (*mehumah umebusah umebukhah*), a pile-up of noise. This is a "day" of the LORD, the term Isaiah uses for God's decisive judgment (2:12). "Shouting to the mountains" may be the cries of the besieged echoing off the hills, or the noise of the attackers on them.
- **"Elam bore the quiver… and Kir uncovered the shield" (v6).** Elam lay east of Babylon, and Kir was the far land to which Assyria had deported the Arameans of Damascus (2 Kings 16:9; Amos 1:5; 9:7). Its site is unknown. Assyria's army drew soldiers from its subject peoples, so JFB takes these as contingents in Sennacherib's army. Guzik reads them as Babylon's allies a century later. "Uncovered the shield" means taking off the leather cover a shield wore on the march, getting ready for battle.
- **"Your choicest valleys were full of chariots" (v7).** The valleys that defined the "valley of vision" fill up with the enemy. Sennacherib's own annals boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage."
- **"He has taken away the covering of Judah" (v8).** The "covering" is Judah's protection, the ring of fortified towns that shielded the capital. Sennacherib took them all. Judah stood exposed.
- **"You looked to the weapons of the House of the Forest" (v8).** Solomon's "House of the Forest of Lebanon" was a great hall lined with cedar pillars (1 Kings 7:2–5). It held gold shields (1 Kings 10:17) and served as an armory and treasury (Isa 39:2). The people's first move in danger was an arms inventory.
- **"You collected the waters of the lower pool… You made a reservoir between the two walls" (vv9, 11).** This fits Hezekiah's works exactly. He blocked the springs outside the city so the Assyrians would have no water, and brought the Gihon spring's water inside through a tunnel cut in the rock (2 Kings 20:20; 2 Chr 32:3–5, 30). That tunnel, about a third of a mile long, can still be walked today, and an inscription found inside it describes the two digging crews meeting in the middle. The reservoir at its outlet is generally identified with the Pool of Siloam. JFB says these preparations "exactly answer to those in 2Ch 32:4, 5, 30."
- **"You counted the houses… and you broke down the houses to fortify the wall" (v10).** Houses were surveyed and some torn down for stone. In the 1970s archaeologists in Jerusalem's Jewish Quarter uncovered a massive wall, about 23 feet thick, dated to Hezekiah's time. It runs straight across the foundations of houses that were demolished to make room for it. It is now called the Broad Wall.
- **"But you did not look to him who did it, or see him who planned it long ago" (v11).** This is the heart of the chapter. The verb "look" repeats: they "looked" to the weapons (v8), "saw" the breaches (v9), but did not "look" to God. Isaiah does not condemn the engineering. Hezekiah's tunnel was a good thing. He condemns trusting it in place of God, who had planned these events long before (37:26). Notably, the Tyndale notes point out that the verbs are plural. Isaiah is rebuking the people as a whole, not singling out Hezekiah, who did in the end turn to the LORD (37:14–20).
- **"The Lord GOD of hosts called for weeping and mourning, for baldness and wearing sackcloth" (v12).** Shaving the head and wearing rough goat-hair cloth were signs of grief and repentance (15:2; Joel 1:13). God's invitation in a crisis was to humble themselves.
- **"Behold, joy and gladness, killing oxen and slaughtering sheep" (v13).** They did the opposite and threw a feast. "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" is the motto of despair dressed as celebration. Paul quotes it as the logical end of a life with no resurrection (1 Cor 15:32), and Jesus' parable of the rich fool says the same (Luke 12:19–20). Henry: "They made a jest of dying."
- **"Surely this iniquity will not be atoned for you until you die" (v14).** "Atoned" is the Hebrew *kaphar*, the word for covering sin by sacrifice. The issue is not that one sin is too big for God. They refused the very repentance God called them to in v12, so there was nothing left to atone for them but judgment. Henry compares it to the sin of Eli's house, which "shall never be expiated with sacrifice and offering" (1 Sam 3:14). The Tyndale notes see the exile of 586 BC foreshadowed here. JFB has no note on this verse.
- **"Go to this steward, to Shebna, who is over the household" (v15).** The prophecy turns from the city to one man. "Over the household" was the title of the king's chief minister, something like a prime minister (1 Kings 4:6; 18:3). "Steward" is *sokhen*, from a verb meaning "be of use, serve," a reminder that the office was service. God's "this steward" is curt, even scornful. Henry suggests "he was a foreigner (for we never read of the name of his father)," and later Jewish tradition accused him of plotting with Assyria. Both are guesses. The text says only that he was proud.
- **"That you have cut out here a tomb for yourself… on the height" (v16).** Wealthy families cut tombs into rock cliffs, and Shebna was carving a showpiece. In the village of Silwan, across the Kidron from the City of David, a rock-cut tomb from this period carries a damaged Hebrew inscription for "…yahu, who is over the house." Many scholars think it may be the tomb of Shebna, whose name could be short for Shebanyahu. It cannot be proved, but the fit is striking. "What have you to do here, and whom have you here?" means: you have no family roots here, no right to this monument.
- **"Whirl you around and around, and throw you like a ball into a wide land" (v18).** The picture is a ball, or a bundle, wound tight and flung. The man who carved a grave to be remembered will die abroad with no grave at all. "Your glorious chariots" shows the luxury he lived in, and he is "you shame of your master's house."
- **Shebna demoted (v19).** History shows this happening at least in part. When the Assyrian envoys arrive, Eliakim is "over the household" and Shebna is "the secretary," a lower post (36:3; 2 Kings 18:18). His exile is not recorded, but Scripture never mentions him again.
- **"I will call my servant Eliakim the son of Hilkiah" (v20).** "My servant" is a title of honor that Isaiah gives to David, to Israel and above all to the coming Servant of the LORD (42:1). Shebna served himself. Eliakim, whose name means "God raises up," will serve God.
- **"I will clothe him with your robe, and will bind your sash on him" (v21).** These are the official robe and sash of the office. **"He shall be a father to the inhabitants of Jerusalem"** is the heart of good leadership: care, not self-display.
- **"I will place on his shoulder the key of the house of David" (v22).** Keys in this period were large wooden bars and could be carried on the shoulder, but here the key is a symbol of authority. The steward controlled access to the palace and to the king. "He shall open, and none shall shut; and he shall shut, and none shall open." The risen Jesus applies these exact words to himself: he is the one "who has the key of David, who opens and no one will shut" (Rev 3:7). Jesus also gave Peter "the keys of the kingdom" (Matt 16:19), and many interpreters hear this verse behind that promise. JFB calls Eliakim "plainly a type of the God-man Christ," and Henry and Guzik both make the same link.
- **"I will fasten him like a peg in a secure place" (v23).** The word is *yated*, a peg or pin. BDB lists both tent-pegs and pegs driven into a wall for hanging things, and gives this verse as a figure for Eliakim. Zechariah uses the same word for a coming ruler (Zech 10:4), and Ezra for God's people's "secure hold" in his holy place (Ezra 9:8). He will become "a throne of honor," a seat of glory, for his family.
- **"They will hang on him the whole honor of his father's house… every small vessel, from the cups to all the flagons" (v24).** Here the picture turns. Relatives hang on Eliakim like pots on a peg, every cup and jug of the extended family looking for a position.
- **"The peg that was fastened in a secure place will give way" (v25).** Who is this peg? Two readings are held. Henry and JFB take it as Shebna, the peg that seemed secure, falling with everyone who depended on him. But "fastened in a secure place" is the exact phrase just used of Eliakim (v23), so many modern scholars, and the Tyndale notes, think it is Eliakim himself: even a good man gives way when a whole family's weight hangs on him, and even he cannot save the house of David from exile (39:6–7). Guzik takes it of Shebna but draws a warning from it either way: no human authority is unlimited.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the chapter's sin: "They did not depend upon him for a blessing upon their endeavours." He divides the chapter between the city's misconduct in its distress and the change of officers at Hezekiah's court, and says of v23 that "our Lord Jesus is as a nail in a sure place." Guzik likens Shebna to the rich fool of Luke 12, who planned for himself and was not rich toward God. On the peg, he notes that cups and flagons differ in size, but each is safe only because of what it hangs on. JFB reads the two walls and pools against 2 Chronicles 32, and takes the key on the shoulder as "figurative for sustaining the government," echoing 9:6.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jerusalem counted weapons and dug tunnels "but did not look to him who did it." Shebna carved a grave to be remembered and was flung away. Eliakim was a faithful servant, and even he could only bear so much weight. The chapter leaves a gap that only one Servant fills. Jesus holds "the key of David," and what he opens no one can shut (Rev 3:7). The government rests on his shoulder (9:6). He is the peg in a sure place that will never give way, so even the smallest cup can hang its whole weight on him.
