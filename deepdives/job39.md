*Setting:* God's first speech from the whirlwind continues. Chapter 38 walked Job through the building of the earth, the sea, dawn, death, weather and stars, and ended with lions and ravens (38:39–41). Chapter 39 stays with the animal world. God shows Job a gallery of wild creatures: mountain goats, the wild donkey, the wild ox, the ostrich, the war horse, the hawk and the eagle. Almost none of them are useful to people. Most are untamed, some are strange, and one is comic. That is the point. God made, feeds and runs a world full of creatures that have nothing to do with human beings, and he is plainly delighted with them. Job had been sure the universe should revolve around his case. God shows him how much of it revolves around other things entirely.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv1–12)
- **"Do you know when the mountain goats give birth?" (v1).** These are the ibex of the rocky desert cliffs, shy animals that give birth in remote places no one sees (Ps 104:18). "The calving of the does" refers to deer, among the most timid of creatures. A shepherd counts the months of his ewes and helps with the lambing. No one does that for ibex. God does.
- **"Can you number the months that they fulfill?" (vv2–3).** "Crouch" is the posture of an animal in labor. "Are delivered of their young" is literally "send out their pains" or "birth pangs." It is a tender picture: God watches over every wild birth no human ever witnesses. Jesus says the same of sparrows: "not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father" (Matt 10:29).
- **"They go out and do not return to them" (v4).** The young grow strong in the open and leave home. No one raised them but God.
- **"Who has let the wild donkey go free?" (v5).** Two different Hebrew words for wild donkey appear in this verse. The wild donkey (onager) was proverbial for freedom and stubbornness. The angel described Ishmael as "a wild donkey of a man" (Gen 16:12). Zophar had sneered that a stupid man would get understanding when "a wild donkey's colt is born a man" (11:12). God answers by admiring the wild donkey.
- **"I have given the arid plain for his home and the salt land for his dwelling place" (v6).** The wild donkey lives in desolate country, salt flats and steppes, and thrives there. God gave him that home. Land humans call worthless, God calls a gift.
- **"He scorns the tumult of the city; he hears not the shouts of the driver" (v7).** The domestic donkey works under a driver's shouts. The wild one has never heard them. The Tyndale notes say this is the first in a series of animals that "scorn" or "laugh at" what others fear (vv18, 22; 41:29). God has made creatures that are utterly free of human control.
- **"Is the wild ox willing to serve you?" (v9).** The wild ox (*re'em*) is the aurochs, the huge wild ancestor of cattle, now extinct. The last aurochs died in Poland in 1627. Bulls stood about six feet at the shoulder. The Tyndale notes say it was the strongest land animal in early Canaan and was never tamed. The Bible uses it as a symbol of strength (Num 23:22; Deut 33:17; Ps 92:10). The Greek Old Testament translated *re'em* with a word meaning "one-horned," which is how the KJV came to say "unicorn." The lexicon is clear that the word means a wild ox.
- **"Will he spend the night at your manger? Can you bind him in the furrow with ropes?" (vv9–10).** The picture is almost funny: Job trying to tie a rope to an aurochs and make it pull a plough. A tame ox "knows its owner" (Isa 1:3). This one knows no owner but God.
- **"Will you depend on him because his strength is great?" (vv11–12).** Its power is enormous, but it is useless to Job. He could never trust it to bring in his harvest. Strength alone does not make a thing serve human purposes.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the lesson from God's tender care for these creatures: it shows how little reason Job had to charge God with unkindness, and calls him to submit to the God "on whom they all depend." JFB notes that the young goats grow up without human care and that the wild donkey's "loosed bands" mean freedom within the laws God set. It says the *re'em* is a buffalo-like animal, not a mythical unicorn, and that "after thee" (v10) pictures an animal willingly following the ploughman, which this one never will. Guzik identifies the wild ox as the aurochs and enjoys the humor of the scene. He also draws from the goats a lesson in trust: Job knows nothing about these births, yet they go well, so he can trust the same God with what he cannot see in his own life.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv13–18)
- **"The wings of the ostrich wave proudly" (v13).** Suddenly the tone becomes playful. The ostrich flaps its great wings, but it cannot fly. The word for ostrich here, *renanim*, comes from a root meaning "to cry out," perhaps from the bird's loud call. The KJV translated it "peacocks."
- **"But are they the pinions and plumage of love?" (v13).** This is a Hebrew pun. The word translated "love" is *chasidah*, which is also the Hebrew name for the stork. The stork was named "the loyal one" or "the kind one" from *chesed*, faithful love, because of its devotion to its young. So the line can mean "are these the feathers of a stork?" and "are these the feathers of a loving mother?" at the same time. The answer to both is no.
- **"She leaves her eggs to the earth" (vv14–15).** Ostriches lay their eggs in a shallow scrape in the sand. To an observer, the nest looks unguarded, where any passing foot could crush it. Lamentations uses the ostrich as a picture of cruelty: "my people has become cruel, like the ostriches in the wilderness" (Lam 4:3).
- **"She deals cruelly with her young, as if they were not hers" (v16).** The Tyndale notes give three reasons the ostrich got this reputation. She leaves the eggs on the ground. She runs from the nest when a predator comes, probably to lead it away. And several females lay in one shared nest, so many of the eggs really are not hers. Modern observers know ostriches are more attentive parents than they look. The Bible here is describing how the bird appears, in the language of a poem, not writing a zoology textbook.
- **"God has made her forget wisdom" (v17).** This is the striking line. God says he made a creature *without* much sense, and he is not embarrassed by it. The Tyndale notes say people in the ancient Near East believed the ostrich was a bird God had not given wisdom, and an Arab proverb said "more foolish than an ostrich." The ostrich does not fit any tidy rule about how creatures should behave. Yet God made her and she thrives. Job's friends had a tidy rule for everything. God's world does not run on their rules.
- **"When she rouses herself to flee, she laughs at the horse and his rider" (v18).** And then the surprise. This foolish bird can outrun a horse. Ostriches can sprint at around 40 miles an hour. What she lacks in wisdom, God gave her in speed. She "laughs," the same scorn as the wild donkey's (v7).
- **Commentators:** Henry follows the KJV and treats v13 as both peacock and ostrich: "Fine feathers make proud birds." He says the ostrich "is hardened against her young ones," lacking natural affection because she lacks wisdom, yet she "scorneth the horse and his rider." JFB is more careful about the bird's habits. It says the ostrich does lay and hatch with care, and in hot climates the eggs need less constant warmth, so the text describes how she *appears* to people, while a sure instinct from God guides her. It notes the Arab proverb "foolish as an ostrich," and adds a sharp point: her apparent folly still serves God's purpose, "as Job's sufferings" do. Guzik calls this passage the closest thing to humor in the book, and suggests some creatures exist partly for God's delight and ours.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv19–30)
- **"Do you give the horse his might? Do you clothe his neck with a mane?" (v19).** Now the war horse, the most magnificent animal of the ancient world. Horses were mainly used for chariots and cavalry, not farm work. JFB notes they are not listed among Job's livestock (1:3; 42:12). The KJV's "clothed his neck with thunder" reflects a Hebrew word that probably means "quivering" or "a trembling mane."
- **"Do you make him leap like the locust? His majestic snorting is terrifying" (v20).** Locusts and horses were compared in the ancient world because their heads look alike (Joel 2:4; Rev 9:7). The horse springs forward like a locust and snorts like a storm.
- **"He paws in the valley and exults in his strength; he goes out to meet the weapons" (v21).** The horse is eager for battle. Most animals flee danger. This one runs toward it.
- **"He laughs at fear and is not dismayed" (v22).** Another creature that laughs, this time at fear itself. "He does not turn back from the sword."
- **"Upon him rattle the quiver, the flashing spear, and the javelin" (v23).** The sound of the rider's weapons clattering on his back only excites him.
- **"With fierceness and rage he swallows the ground" (v24).** He eats up the distance at a gallop. "He cannot stand still at the sound of the trumpet." The trumpet was the ram's horn (*shofar*), blown to signal the attack (Josh 6:4–5; Judg 7:18–20).
- **"When the trumpet sounds, he says 'Aha!'" (v25).** The horse neighs as if shouting for joy. "He smells the battle from afar, the thunder of the captains, and the shouting." Where Job has been afraid and broken, this animal is fearless, and God made it so. The courage Job lacks, God has poured into a horse.
- **Warnings about horses.** Elsewhere Scripture warns against trusting in this very power: "The war horse is a false hope for salvation" (Ps 33:17), and "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (Ps 20:7). God made the horse's strength, but strength is not salvation.
- **"Is it by your understanding that the hawk soars and spreads his wings toward the south?" (v26).** Many hawks migrate south for the winter along the great flyway over Israel and the Jordan valley, one of the busiest migration routes in the world. No one taught them the way. Jeremiah notes that birds know their seasons while God's people do not know his law (Jer 8:7).
- **"Is it at your command that the eagle mounts up and makes his nest on high?" (v27).** The word *nesher* is broad. The lexicon says it can mean the eagle or the griffon vulture, and in some places only vulture fits. The bird nests on cliffs and crags, out of reach of every enemy (Jer 49:16; Obad 4). Elsewhere it is the picture of God carrying his people: "I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself" (Exod 19:4).
- **"From there he spies out the prey; his eyes behold it from far away" (v29).** Eagles and vultures can spot food from great heights. Job had compared his days to "the eagle swooping on the prey" (9:26).
- **"His young ones suck up blood, and where the slain are, there is he" (v30).** The speech ends on a grim note. The magnificent bird feeds its young on carrion. God does not hide the hard side of his world. Life feeds on death, and he governs that too. Jesus echoes this line: "Wherever the corpse is, there the vultures will gather" (Matt 24:28; Luke 17:37). In Jesus' saying it is a picture of how unmistakable his return will be.
- **Commentators:** Henry turns the horse into a warning about presumptuous sinners who rush into danger and ignore God's warnings, as the horse "mocks at fear." JFB reads the horse's neighing at the trumpet as showing its love of battle, notes that the eagle spots its prey by sight rather than smell, and says Jesus partly quotes v30 in Matthew 24:28. It suggests the "slain" in v30 points to the vulture, which belongs to the same family in Hebrew usage. JFB has no comment on v22. Guzik says even a trained horse can break free of its rider, so Job cannot claim to control even what he has tamed. He closes by stressing that God comes to Job as a loving teacher, not a judge, and that God's presence is itself the answer Job needed.

#### Pointing to Christ
God never tells Job why he suffered. He shows him ibex giving birth in secret, a donkey that has never heard a driver, an ox no one can harness, a silly bird that outruns horses, a horse that laughs at swords, and an eagle on a crag. The message is that God knows, feeds and governs a world far wider and wilder than Job imagined, and that he does it with care and delight. Jesus taught the same lesson from the same book of nature: "Look at the birds of the air... your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not of more value than they?" (Matt 6:26). The God who watches over every unseen birth (v1) is the Father who counts the hairs of his children's heads (Matt 10:30). And the God who made the fearless war horse sent his Son, not on a charger to battle, but "humble and mounted on a donkey" (Zech 9:9; Matt 21:5), to win the real battle by dying. One day he will ride out on a white horse (Rev 19:11), but the victory was won first at the cross.
