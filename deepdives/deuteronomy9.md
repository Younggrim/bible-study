*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, as his second sermon (chapters 5–26) continues. Chapter 7 told Israel that God chose them out of love, not because they were many. Chapter 8 warned them never to say, "My power… has gotten me this wealth." Chapter 9 closes the last door to boasting: they must not say the land was given because they were good. To prove it, Moses tells the most shameful story in Israel's history, the golden calf at Mount Sinai (also called Horeb), which happened only weeks after they had heard God speak the Ten Commandments. The full account is in Exodus 32–34. Here Moses retells it from his own point of view, as the man who lay on his face before God for forty days to keep the nation alive. The story runs on into chapter 10, where God gives the tablets again.

#### Not Because of Righteousness (vv1–12)
- **"Hear, O Israel: you are to cross over the Jordan today" (v1).** The same opening as the Shema (6:4) marks a new section. "Today" means this is the moment, not literally the same day. The crossing came some weeks later (Josh 3).
- **"Cities great and fortified up to heaven" (v1).** The phrase is borrowed from the spies' report forty years earlier (1:28). Canaanite cities did have massive walls, and some city gates and ramparts of this era still stand in places such as Hazor and Shechem. Moses does not pretend the enemy is small.
- **"Who can stand before the sons of Anak?" (v2).** The Anakim were a people known for their height, living around Hebron (Num 13:22, 33). This seems to have been a common saying, almost a proverb. It was the sight of them that had made the first generation lose heart.
- **"He who goes over before you as a consuming fire is the LORD your God" (v3).** The God who appeared as fire on Sinai (4:24) now goes ahead of them into battle. **"So you shall drive them out and make them perish quickly."** God's part and Israel's part go together. He destroys, and they drive out. Joshua shows both: sometimes God fought directly with hailstones (Josh 10:11), and sometimes Israel's army did the work.
- **"Do not say in your heart… 'It is because of my righteousness'" (v4).** This is the third time Moses has warned about what Israel might "say in your heart." First fear: "These nations are greater than I" (7:17). Then pride in strength: "My power… has gotten me this wealth" (8:17). Now pride in goodness. The last is the hardest to see in oneself.
- **"Because of the wickedness of these nations… and that he may confirm the word that the LORD swore" (vv4–5).** Two reasons are given, and neither is Israel. The Canaanites are being judged for their own sin (Gen 15:16; Lev 18:24–25), and God is keeping his promise to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Israel is the instrument of judgment and the receiver of a promise, not a reward for good behavior.
- **"For you are a stubborn people" (v6).** Literally "stiff of neck" (*qesheh oreph*). The picture is an ox that won't bend its neck to the yoke. God himself used these words at Sinai (Exod 32:9; 33:3, 5). Here the words come in v6 and again in v13, framing the history that follows.
- **"Remember and do not forget how you provoked the LORD" (v7).** Chapter 8 told them to remember God's goodness. Now they must remember their own failures. Both kinds of memory are needed: one keeps us grateful, the other keeps us humble. **"From the day you came out of the land of Egypt until you came to this place, you have been rebellious."** The whole forty years, end to end.
- **"Even at Horeb" (v8).** Of all places. At the very mountain where they had heard God's voice and promised to obey (5:27), they made an idol. "Horeb" is Deuteronomy's usual name for Mount Sinai.
- **"Forty days and forty nights. I neither ate bread nor drank water" (v9).** Moses was on the mountain receiving the law (Exod 24:18). Going forty days without water is beyond what a body can do, so this was a time when God himself sustained him. Elijah later went forty days to the same mountain (1 Kgs 19:8), and Jesus fasted forty days in the wilderness (Matt 4:2).
- **"Written with the finger of God" (v10).** The tablets were God's own writing (Exod 31:18; 32:16). They held "all the words that the LORD had spoken with you" on "the day of the assembly," that is, the Ten Commandments. In Luke 11:20 Jesus says he casts out demons "by the finger of God," the same personal, direct power.
- **"Your people whom you have brought from Egypt have acted corruptly" (v12).** Notice the pronoun. God calls them "*your* people whom *you* brought out," speaking to Moses, as if disowning them (Exod 32:7). "Acted corruptly" (*shichet*) means to ruin or spoil. **"Turned aside quickly."** Weeks after promising "All the words that the LORD has spoken we will do" (Exod 24:3). **"A metal image"** (*massekah*) is an idol cast by pouring molten metal into a mould.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sums up the section: "Thus boasting is for ever excluded," and on v3 he balances grace and effort: "We must do our endeavour in dependence upon God's grace." JFB notes that "even in Horeb" is the sense of v8, the place where Israel should have behaved best. On vv4–6 it says God acted "not for the sake of the Israelites, but for His own sake." JFB says nothing on vv2–3 or vv9–11. Guzik links the warning against self-righteousness to the gospel: no believer earns right standing with God, and all stand on Christ's righteousness. He reads the call to remember past rebellions as aimed at humility, not despair, the "poor in spirit" of Matthew 5:3.

#### The Golden Calf (vv13–29)
- **"Let me alone, that I may destroy them… And I will make of you a nation mightier and greater than they" (v14).** God offers Moses what he once offered Abraham: to start again with him. "Let me alone" is striking. God speaks as if Moses' prayer could hold back his hand, and in effect invites him to intercede. Moses refuses the offer of his own greatness.
- **"The mountain was burning with fire. And the two tablets of the covenant were in my two hands" (v15).** The scene is vivid: God's holy fire above, the covenant document in Moses' hands, and below, the people dancing around a calf.
- **"You had made yourselves a golden calf" (v16).** The calf was probably a young bull, a common symbol of strength and fertility in Egypt and Canaan. Egypt worshipped the Apis bull. In Canaan the bull was linked with Baal and El. Aaron proclaimed "a feast to the LORD" (Exod 32:5), so the people may have thought of the calf as an image of the LORD himself, or as the pedestal on which he stood. Either way it broke the second commandment, which they had just heard. Centuries later King Jeroboam set up two golden calves with the same words (1 Kgs 12:28).
- **"I took hold of the two tablets and threw them out of my two hands and broke them before your eyes" (v17).** This was not a loss of temper but a public act with a meaning. In the ancient world, breaking a treaty tablet declared the treaty void. Israel had broken the covenant, and Moses showed it in a way no one could miss.
- **"Then I lay prostrate before the LORD as before, forty days and forty nights" (v18).** A second forty-day fast, this time not to receive the law but to plead for those who broke it. Exodus 32:30–32 records part of his prayer: "if you will forgive their sin—but if not, please blot me out of your book."
- **"For I was afraid of the anger and hot displeasure" (v19).** The verb for "afraid" (*yagor*) is a rare, strong word for dread. Moses knew God's anger was real. **"But the LORD listened to me that time also."** "Also" recalls other times God heard Moses' prayers for the people (Exod 15:25; 17:4–6; Num 11:2).
- **"The LORD was so angry with Aaron that he was ready to destroy him. And I prayed for Aaron also" (v20).** This detail is not in Exodus. The high priest-to-be, the man who made the calf with his own hands, would have died without his brother's prayers. Even Israel's priest needed a mediator.
- **"Burned it with fire and crushed it, grinding it very small, until it was as fine as dust" (v21).** The calf was probably a wooden core overlaid with gold, which would explain how it could be both burned and ground. Moses threw the dust into the stream from the mountain, and Exodus 32:20 adds that he made the people drink it. Whatever else that meant, the god they had worshipped ended up as nothing. Later kings who cleansed the land did the same to idols (2 Kgs 23:6, 12).
- **"At Taberah also, and at Massah and at Kibroth-hattaavah" (v22).** Three place names that are themselves memorials to sin. **Taberah** means "burning," where fire broke out on the grumbling camp (Num 11:1–3). **Massah** means "testing," where they demanded water (Exod 17:7). **Kibroth-hattaavah** means "graves of craving," where they gorged on quail and many died (Num 11:31–34). The map of the wilderness was a map of Israel's rebellion.
- **"When the LORD sent you from Kadesh-barnea… you rebelled… and did not believe him" (v23).** The worst failure of all was the refusal to enter the land (Num 13–14; Deut 1:26–33). Underneath every other sin was this one: they "did not believe him."
- **"You have been rebellious against the LORD from the day that I knew you" (v24).** A painful summary from a man who had given forty years to them.
- **"So I lay prostrate before the LORD for these forty days" (v25).** This returns to the second forty days of v18 and now gives the content of the prayer. JFB is right that this is the same intercession, not a third one.
- **Moses' prayer (vv26–29).** It is a model of pleading with God, and it is worth noticing what it rests on. It doesn't argue that the people are better than they look. Moses openly admits "the stubbornness of this people, or their wickedness or their sin" (v27). Instead he appeals to **God's ownership**: "your people and your heritage" (vv26, 29), a direct answer to "your people" in v12. He appeals to **God's past work**: "whom you have redeemed through your greatness." He appeals to **God's promises**: "Remember your servants, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob" (v27). When God "remembers," it means he acts on a commitment. And he appeals to **God's reputation**: Egypt would say the LORD "was not able" to finish what he started, or that he "hated them" (v28). Every argument is about God, none about Israel.
- **"Your heritage" (vv26, 29).** The word *nachalah* means an inheritance, a family's land handed down and never to be sold. Israel is about to receive the land as its inheritance. Moses reminds God that Israel is *his* inheritance.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry treats the breaking of the tablets as a sign that Israel had forfeited the covenant's privileges. On v7: "We are apt to forget our provocations, especially when the smart of the rod is over." On v21: "It was infinite mercy that accepted the destruction of the idol instead of the destruction of the idolaters." He notes that Moses "was obliged to own their stubbornness" and pleaded only God's mercy. JFB says the tablets were broken "in righteous indignation, from zeal to vindicate" God's honor, not from temper, and that Aaron was "overborne by the tide of popular clamor." It takes the brook in v21 to be the stream from the smitten rock near Sinai, which is possible but more than the text says. JFB passes over vv22–24 and vv26–29. Guzik gives three reasons for grinding the calf to dust: to show the idol was nothing, to destroy it completely, and to make the people feel the consequences of their sin. He sets out Moses' four grounds for prayer, God's redemption, his promises, his glory and his people, as a pattern for praying on solid ground rather than wishful thinking. All three agree that the chapter's purpose is to leave Israel no room to boast.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel's hope rested on one man lying face down before God, refusing to let go until the people were spared, even offering to be blotted out in their place (Exod 32:32). Moses could not actually take their judgment; he could only plead. Jesus is the mediator Moses pointed to. He too fasted forty days. He did not merely ask to be blotted out for his people; on the cross he bore their sin. "He always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25), and his prayer rests on the same ground as Moses': not our righteousness, but God's promise and God's glory. This chapter's message, "not because of your righteousness," is the gospel's message too: "not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy" (Titus 3:5).
