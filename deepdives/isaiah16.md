*Setting:* Chapter 16 continues the oracle against Moab begun in chapter 15, with no break in the Hebrew. Moab, Judah's neighbor and distant relative across the Dead Sea, has been struck suddenly. Its cities have fallen overnight, its refugees are fleeing south, and its people are wailing at the shrines of their god Chemosh. Isaiah has wept for them (15:5). Now the scene shifts. Moabite refugees reach the edge of Judah and ask for shelter, and the question is whether Moab will humble itself before the God of Zion and the king on David's throne. It does not, because of its pride, and the chapter ends with a dated warning: judgment within three years. Most scholars connect this to Assyrian campaigns in the region in the late 700s BC.

#### An Oracle Concerning Moab, continued (vv1–14)
- **"Send the lamb to the ruler of the land" (v1).** Moab was sheep country. When it was subject to Israel, its king Mesha paid a huge tribute in lambs and wool (2 Kgs 3:4). After Ahab died, Mesha stopped paying. Isaiah tells Moab to send the tribute again, this time to Jerusalem, "the mount of the daughter of Zion." It is a call to submit to the king of Judah and, through him, to Judah's God. Henry reads it as earnest advice: "Send him the lamb, the best you have," and applies it to giving ourselves to Christ as "a living sacrifice."
- **"From Sela, by way of the desert" (v1).** *Sela* means "rock" or "cliff." Some take it as the Edomite stronghold later called Petra, south of Moab, where refugees had fled. The Tyndale notes are cautious and call it an unknown remote site. Either way the picture is refugees far from home being urged to send to Zion.
- **"Like fleeing birds, like a scattered nest… at the fords of the Arnon" (v2).** The Arnon is the deep gorge that formed Moab's northern border. "The daughters of Moab" are its women, or its towns, fluttering helplessly at the river crossings like birds knocked from a nest.
- **"Give counsel; grant justice; make your shade like night at the height of noon" (vv3–4).** These are Moab's words to Judah, a plea for asylum. In the blazing noon sun of the desert, shade is life. "Do not reveal the fugitive" means do not hand the refugees over to their pursuers. Guzik sees here a model for how God's people should treat those who come to them for refuge. The law had already said not to hand over a runaway who seeks shelter (Deut 23:15–16).
- **"When the oppressor is no more" (v4).** The ESV reads the end of v4 as the start of a promise. A day is coming when the destroyer has vanished.
- **"Then a throne will be established in steadfast love" (v5).** In the middle of a lament over a pagan nation, Isaiah sets a throne. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. "Faithfulness" (*'emet*) is his reliability. This is the language of God's promise to David that his throne would last forever (2 Sam 7:15–16; Ps 89:14).
- **"In the tent of David one who judges and seeks justice and is swift to do righteousness" (v5).** "Tent of David" pictures David's royal house, perhaps humbled to a tent instead of a palace (compare Amos 9:11, "the booth of David that is fallen," which James applies to Christ gathering the Gentiles in Acts 15:16–17). The ruler here is the same King of 9:7 and 11:3–5, who judges the poor with righteousness. Readers differ on the near reference. Henry applies it to Hezekiah: Moab should "make Hezekiah your friend." The Tyndale notes, JFB and Guzik point to the Messiah, with JFB placing it "in the latter days." Many see Hezekiah as a partial, near fulfillment and Christ as the full one. The offer to Moab is real: shelter is found under David's throne.
- **"We have heard of the pride of Moab—how proud he is!" (v6).** Here comes the answer, and it hinges on pride. The Hebrew piles up four words from the same root (*ga'on*, *ge'*, *ga'avah*), roughly "pride, proud, proudness," and adds "insolence." Moab's pride was proverbial (Jer 48:29 repeats the line with six terms). "His idle boasting is not right" means his big talk is empty. Henry: "They are a proud people, and therefore they will not take good counsel when it is given them." Moab will not bow to Zion even to save its life. Guzik notes that a small nation can be as proud as a great one.
- **"Let Moab wail for Moab… for the raisin cakes of Kir-hareseth" (v7).** Kir-hareseth was Moab's southern fortress capital (Kerak). Raisin cakes were pressed cakes of dried grapes, a delicacy and often part of festivals and pagan worship (Hos 3:1). Older versions read "foundations," from a similar-looking word; JFB takes it as "ruins." The ESV's "raisin cakes" leads straight into the vineyard picture that follows.
- **"The vine of Sibmah… reached to Jazer and strayed to the desert… passed over the sea" (v8).** The Heshbon region was famous wine country. Sibmah's vines are pictured spreading north to Jazer, east to the desert and west across the Dead Sea, a way of saying Moab's wine was known everywhere. "The lords of the nations have struck down its branches." Invading armies cut down vineyards to break a people (compare 2 Kgs 3:19, 25). The Tyndale notes contrast this with Israel, God's vine, which "shall… fill the whole world with fruit" (27:6).
- **"I weep with the weeping of Jazer… I drench you with my tears" (v9).** The prophet's grief returns, even stronger than in 15:5.
- **"In the vineyards no songs are sung… no treader treads out wine" (v10).** The grape harvest was the happiest time of year, with singing and shouting as workers trod grapes in the stone presses (Judg 9:27; Jer 25:30). Now there is silence. "I have put an end to the shouting." God is the one who does it.
- **"My inner parts moan like a lyre for Moab" (v11).** "Inner parts" are literally the bowels, which Hebrew treats as the seat of deep feeling, much as English uses "heart" (Jer 4:19). Henry pictures the sound "like that of the strings of a harp when it is played upon," a low, throbbing sorrow. JFB calls the bowels the seat of "yearning compassion." Guzik compares Isaiah's grief to Jesus weeping over Jerusalem.
- **"When he wearies himself on the high place… he will not prevail" (v12).** Moab prays hard, to the point of exhaustion, but to the wrong god. Compare the prophets of Baal at Carmel, who cried out all day "but there was no voice" (1 Kgs 18:26–29). Henry: Moab "shall spend his spirits and strength in vain in praying to his idols." The lesson runs through Isaiah: idols cannot save (44:17–20; 45:20).
- **"This is the word that the LORD spoke concerning Moab in the past. But now…" (vv13–14).** Isaiah marks off an earlier oracle (15:1–16:12) from a new, dated word. Some scholars think the earlier oracle came from an older prophet and Isaiah adopted it, since Jeremiah also reuses it. The text simply says God had spoken this before and now sets a date.
- **"In three years, like the years of a hired worker" (v14).** A hired laborer under contract counts the days exactly, not one day more or less. Henry: "a servant that is hired for a certain term keeps account to a day." The Tyndale notes say the exact events are unknown, though the most likely setting is an Assyrian campaign. Sargon II campaigned in the region around 715–711 BC, and Moab paid tribute to Sennacherib in 701 BC. Moab never recovered its glory. A remnant survived (Neh 13:23), and later Moab disappeared as a nation, absorbed by Arab peoples.
- **"Those who remain will be very few and feeble" (v14).** The "great multitude" Moab trusted in will shrink to a handful.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv1–5 as the prophet's "good counsel" to Moab, to be kind to God's people and submit, and identifies the throne of v5 as Hezekiah's: "though the throne of the ten tribes be sunk… yet the throne of David shall be established in mercy." He also hears God's own voice in v4: "They are outcasts, but they are my outcasts." JFB notes the debate over who speaks in vv3–4 (Moabite refugees asking Judah, or advice to Moab) and applies v5 to the Messiah. Guzik also sees the Messiah in v5. JFB adds that the ruins of Heshbon, Elealeh, Medeba and Dibon could still be seen in his day. Guzik cites a suggestion that Sargon devastated Moab around 715 BC, while admitting the exact date cannot be confirmed.

#### Pointing to Christ
The door Moab refused is the door Christ holds open. Shelter was offered under "a throne… established in steadfast love… in the tent of David," and the King on that throne "judges and seeks justice." Pride kept Moab out. The gospel asks the same humbling of every nation: come to the Son of David as one who needs refuge. Ruth the Moabitess once did exactly that, taking refuge under the wings of Israel's God (Ruth 2:12), and she became an ancestor of David and of Jesus. In him, Moab's outcasts too can find a home.
