*Setting:* About AD 28–29. Jesus has just been baptized in the Jordan, the Spirit has come on him, and the Father has called him "my beloved Son" (3:21–22). Luke then traced his family line back to "Adam, the son of God" (3:38). Chapter 4 shows the Son tested and then sent. First comes forty days in the Judean wilderness, west of the Jordan and the Dead Sea, a barren land of rock and ravines. Then Jesus returns north to Galilee, where he preaches in his hometown of Nazareth, is rejected there and moves on to Capernaum on the Sea of Galilee. Luke places the Nazareth sermon at the very start of the ministry as a kind of mission statement, and almost every theme of his Gospel appears in it.

#### The Temptation of Jesus (vv1–13)
- **"Full of the Holy Spirit… led by the Spirit in the wilderness" (v1).** Luke mentions the Spirit twice in one verse and again in v14 and v18. The Spirit does not lead Jesus away from testing but into it. Jesus faces the devil not as a man left to himself but as one filled with God's Spirit, as believers are meant to.
- **"For forty days, being tempted by the devil" (v2).** "Devil" (*diabolos*) means "slanderer" or "accuser," the Greek word for the Hebrew *satan*, "adversary." Forty recalls Israel's forty years of testing in the wilderness (Deut 8:2) and Moses' and Elijah's forty-day fasts (Exod 34:28; 1 Kings 19:8). Luke's wording suggests Jesus was tempted throughout the forty days, with these three tests coming at the end.
- **"He ate nothing… he was hungry" (v2).** A plain human detail. The Son of God was truly human and felt real hunger (Heb 4:15).
- **"If you are the Son of God" (v3).** The Greek form of "if" assumes the condition for argument's sake: "since you are the Son of God." The devil is not doubting it. He is trying to make Jesus use his sonship on his own terms. The phrase follows straight on from the genealogy's last line, "Adam, the son of God." Adam was tested in a garden with every kind of food and fell. Israel, called God's "son" (Exod 4:22; Hos 11:1), was tested in the wilderness and complained for bread. Jesus is tested in a wilderness, starving, and stands.
- **"Command this stone to become bread" (v3).** There is nothing wrong with bread, and Jesus will make bread for thousands (9:12–17). The temptation is to meet a real need in a way that steps outside his Father's will and his calling to share fully in human weakness.
- **"Man shall not live by bread alone" (v4).** All three of Jesus' answers come from Deuteronomy 6–8, Moses' sermon to Israel about their own wilderness years. Deuteronomy 8:3 says God let Israel hunger and fed them with manna "that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but… by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD." Jesus trusts the Father to provide.
- **"All the kingdoms of the world in a moment of time" (v5).** No mountain shows every kingdom, and Luke does not mention one. It was a vision. Luke's order differs from Matthew's: Matthew ends on the mountain (Matt 4:8–10), Luke at the temple in Jerusalem. Luke's Gospel is shaped around a journey to Jerusalem (9:51), and the climax of the temptation there fits that shape. The Gospels did not always tell events in strict time order.
- **"It has been delivered to me, and I give it to whom I will" (v6).** Only Luke records this boast. It is partly true. Jesus later calls Satan "the ruler of this world" (John 12:31; 14:30), and John says the whole world "lies in the power of the evil one" (1 John 5:19). But the authority was "delivered" to him, which means it is not his by right, and God remains Lord over all.
- **"If you, then, will worship me, it will all be yours" (v7).** The offer is the crown without the cross. Jesus will receive all authority (Matt 28:18), but by obedience and suffering, not by bowing to the enemy.
- **"You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve" (v8).** From Deuteronomy 6:13. Worship and service belong to God alone.
- **"The pinnacle of the temple" (v9).** Probably the corner of the temple platform high above the Kidron Valley, at the southeast, or the royal colonnade on the south, where the drop was well over a hundred feet. Josephus says looking down from there made a person dizzy.
- **"For it is written" (vv10–11).** Now the devil quotes Scripture too, Psalm 91:11–12. He leaves out the words "in all your ways," and he twists a promise of God's care for those who trust him into a dare. Scripture taken out of its sense can be used against God's purpose.
- **"You shall not put the Lord your God to the test" (v12).** From Deuteronomy 6:16, which goes on: "as you tested him at Massah," where Israel demanded, "Is the LORD among us or not?" (Exod 17:7). Throwing himself down to force God's hand would be distrust, not faith. Jesus answers Scripture misused with Scripture rightly understood.
- **"He departed from him until an opportune time" (v13).** The devil will return. Luke says later that "Satan entered into Judas" (22:3), and in Gethsemane Jesus calls the hour of his arrest "the power of darkness" (22:53). The final temptation will be the same one: "If you are the Christ… save yourself" (23:35–39).
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that the first temptation used a legitimate need to suggest an illegitimate means, and that Satan's use of Psalm 91 shows quoting the Bible does not guarantee speaking its truth. He stresses that Jesus fought with the Word and the Spirit, resources open to believers too, and that steady resistance keeps the enemy at bay. JFB's notes on Luke here simply refer readers to its comments on Matthew 4. The Tyndale notes trace the parallels with Adam and with Israel in the wilderness.

#### Jesus Begins His Ministry (vv14–15)
- **"In the power of the Spirit to Galilee" (v14).** He went into the wilderness "full of the Spirit" and comes out "in the power of the Spirit." Luke skips about a year of ministry that John records in Judea and Samaria (John 1–4), and focuses on Galilee, the northern region where most of Jesus' public ministry took place.
- **"He taught in their synagogues, being glorified by all" (v15).** Synagogues were meeting places for prayer and the reading and teaching of Scripture in every Jewish town. A visiting teacher could be invited to speak. The early response is wide approval. It will not last.

#### Jesus Rejected at Nazareth (vv16–30)
- **"Nazareth, where he had been brought up" (v16).** A small village in the hills of lower Galilee, perhaps a few hundred people, so obscure that it is never mentioned in the Old Testament, Josephus or early rabbinic writings. Nathanael asked, "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" (John 1:46).
- **"As was his custom, he went to the synagogue on the Sabbath day" (v16).** Jesus had a habit of public worship. Paul followed the same custom (Acts 17:2).
- **"He stood up to read… the scroll of the prophet Isaiah" (vv16–17).** A synagogue service included prayers, a reading from the Law, a reading from the Prophets and often a sermon. Readers stood to read out of respect for Scripture and sat to teach. Each book was a separate scroll, and Isaiah was a long one. "Found the place" may mean he chose the passage himself.
- **"The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me" (v18).** The text is Isaiah 61:1–2, with a line from Isaiah 58:6 ("to set at liberty those who are oppressed") woven in. In Isaiah the speaker is the one anointed by God to bring the exiles good news. "Anointed" is the root of "Messiah" and "Christ." Jesus has just been anointed with the Spirit at his baptism (3:22; Acts 10:38).
- **"Good news to the poor… liberty to the captives… recovering of sight to the blind" (v18).** The poor, the captive, the blind and the oppressed run through Luke's Gospel: shepherds at the manger (2:8), a blind beggar at Jericho (18:35–43), Zacchaeus set free from greed (19:1–10), a woman bound by Satan for eighteen years (13:16). Jesus' work touches bodies and lives, and also sets people free from sin; the word for "liberty" (*aphesis*) is the same word translated "forgiveness" in 3:3.
- **"To proclaim the year of the Lord's favor" (v19).** This points to the Year of Jubilee, every fiftieth year in Israel, when debts were cancelled, slaves freed and family land returned (Lev 25:8–55). The prophets used it as a picture of God's great rescue. Jesus says the true Jubilee has come.
- **Where he stopped.** Isaiah 61:2 continues, "and the day of vengeance of our God." Jesus stops in mid-sentence. Many see in this the difference between his two comings: his first coming brings the time of favor, and the day of judgment is held back for his return (2 Cor 6:2; Acts 17:30–31).
- **"He rolled up the scroll… and sat down. And the eyes of all… were fixed on him" (v20).** Sitting was the teacher's posture. Everyone waits for the sermon. Luke slows the scene with each small action, building the suspense.
- **"Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (v21).** Perhaps the shortest sermon summary in the Bible, and the boldest. Not "someday," but "today." "Today" is a key word in Luke: "unto you is born this day" (2:11), "today salvation has come to this house" (19:9), "today you will be with me in paradise" (23:43).
- **"All spoke well of him… Is not this Joseph's son?" (v22).** At first they admire his "gracious words," literally "words of grace." But the question shows the limit of their faith. They knew him as the carpenter's boy. How could the local son be the anointed one?
- **"Physician, heal yourself" (v23).** A common proverb in the ancient world. Here it means "do for your own people what you did elsewhere." They want a show of miracles in their own town, as proof and perhaps as a privilege.
- **"No prophet is acceptable in his hometown" (v24).** "Acceptable" (*dektos*) is the same word as "favor" in v19, "the *acceptable* year of the Lord." He came proclaiming the year of God's welcome, and his hometown will not welcome him.
- **Elijah and Elisha (vv25–27).** Jesus reminds them of two prophets whose miracles went to outsiders. During the great drought, Elijah was sent not to an Israelite widow but to a widow in Zarephath, near Sidon in Phoenicia, the homeland of Jezebel (1 Kings 17:8–24). Elisha healed no Israelite leper but Naaman, commander of the army of Syria, Israel's enemy (2 Kings 5). "Three years and six months" matches James 5:17; 1 Kings 18:1 says "in the third year," probably counting from Elijah's stay at Zarephath rather than from the start of the drought.
- **"All in the synagogue were filled with wrath" (v28).** The point stung. God's grace was not owed to them because they were insiders. If they would not receive the prophet, God would bless Gentiles instead. Luke's second volume shows exactly that (Acts 13:46; 28:28).
- **"The brow of the hill… so that they could throw him down the cliff" (v29).** Nazareth sits on a hillside, and cliffs drop nearby. Throwing a person down from a height was sometimes the first step in stoning, which the Law prescribed for false prophets (Deut 13:5; 18:20). From admiration to attempted murder in one service.
- **"But passing through their midst, he went away" (v30).** Luke does not say how. Whether by miracle or by sheer authority, they could not touch him, because his hour had not come (John 7:30; 8:59). Some see a foreshadowing here: rejected by his own and taken out to die, he will pass through death itself.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Jesus chose a passage on "the sublime object of His whole mission," links the "acceptable year" to the Jubilee and sees Father, Son and Spirit together in v18. It reads "gracious words" as both the richness of his message and the sweetness of his manner, and calls the escape "evidently in a miraculous way, though perhaps quite noiselessly." Henry notes that the hearers' admiration was mixed with contempt for his humble family, and that he escaped because "his hour was not yet come." Guzik points out that Jesus stopped before "the day of vengeance," which he takes as marking the gap between Christ's first and second comings, and that the crowd's rejection revealed more about them than about him.

#### Jesus Heals a Man with an Unclean Demon (vv31–37)
- **"He went down to Capernaum" (v31).** Down in both senses: from the hills of Nazareth to the lakeshore, about 700 feet below sea level. Capernaum was a busy fishing town on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee, on a trade road, with a customs post (5:27). It became Jesus' base (Matt 4:13). The remains of a white limestone synagogue there date from a few centuries later, but beneath it lie the basalt foundations of an earlier one, possibly the very building of this story.
- **"His word possessed authority" (v32).** Other teachers quoted earlier rabbis and traditions. Jesus taught as one who had the right to say what God meant.
- **"The spirit of an unclean demon" (v33).** "Unclean" means defiled and defiling, the opposite of holy. Luke, writing for Greeks, for whom "demon" could mean any spirit, good or bad, adds "unclean" to make clear what kind.
- **"Ha! What have you to do with us… Have you come to destroy us?" (v34).** "Us": one demon speaks for the whole kingdom of darkness, which knows that Jesus' coming means its end.
- **"I know who you are—the Holy One of God" (v34).** A true confession from a hostile mouth. Some think the demon was trying to gain power over Jesus by naming him, as people then believed knowing a spirit's name gave power over it. If so, it failed.
- **"Be silent and come out of him!" (v35).** Literally "be muzzled." Jesus does not accept testimony from demons, and he uses no rituals, spells or names of power, only his word. Luke the physician notes that the man was thrown down but left unharmed.
- **"With authority and power he commands the unclean spirits" (v36).** The crowd connects the authority of his teaching with the authority of his command. The same word does both.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the irony that demons recognized Jesus as the Holy One of God when his own town did not. The Tyndale notes explain the ancient belief about names and power and observe that exorcists of the day used elaborate rituals, which makes Jesus' plain word so striking. JFB gives no separate note on these verses.

#### Jesus Heals Many (vv38–41)
- **"Simon's house… Simon's mother-in-law" (v38).** Luke mentions Simon here before telling of his call (5:1–11), as if his readers already knew him. Peter was married, and his wife later traveled with him (1 Cor 9:5). Remains of a first-century house near the Capernaum synagogue, later turned into a meeting place and then a church, are thought by many archaeologists to be Peter's house, though this cannot be proved.
- **"Ill with a high fever" (v38).** Mark says simply "a fever" (Mark 1:30). Ancient Greek medical writers distinguished "great" and "small" fevers, and Luke's phrase may reflect a doctor's precision, though ordinary writers used it too.
- **"He stood over her and rebuked the fever" (v39).** "Rebuked" is the same verb used of the demon (v35) and of the winds and waves (8:24). Luke shows Jesus confronting sickness as an enemy. That does not mean every illness is demonic; Luke describes this as a fever, not a possession. **"Immediately she rose and began to serve them":** no recovery time. The healing was complete, and her response was service.
- **"When the sun was setting" (v40).** The Sabbath ended at sunset. Carrying the sick on the Sabbath was considered work, so people waited until it was over, then came in crowds.
- **"He laid his hands on every one of them" (v40).** Only Luke says this. Jesus did not heal the crowd in a sweeping gesture. He touched each one.
- **"You are the Son of God!… he would not allow them to speak, because they knew that he was the Christ" (v41).** Luke links "Son of God" and "Christ" as titles for the Messiah (Ps 2:2, 7). Jesus silences the demons. He would reveal who he was in his own way and time, and such witnesses would only discredit the truth and stir false hopes of a political Messiah.
- **Commentators:** Guzik cites Barclay's view that Luke uses medical language for the fever. That is possible but not certain, since the phrase was also used outside medical writing. Guzik also notes that she rose at once to serve. The Tyndale notes point out that the same verb "rebuked" is used of the demon and the fever, showing Jesus' authority over both. JFB refers readers to its notes on Matthew 8.

#### Jesus Preaches in Synagogues (vv42–44)
- **"When it was day, he departed and went into a desolate place" (v42).** Mark adds that he went to pray (Mark 1:35). After a night of healing crowds, Jesus seeks solitude with his Father. Luke often shows him withdrawing to pray (5:16; 6:12).
- **"Would have kept him from leaving them" (v42).** Nazareth drove him out. Capernaum wants to keep him for itself. Both reactions try to fit Jesus into their own plans.
- **"I must preach the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns as well" (v43).** "Must" (*dei*) is Luke's word for God's purpose (2:49; 9:22; 24:7, 26). The "kingdom of God," God's rule breaking into the world through Jesus, is mentioned here for the first time in Luke and becomes the center of his preaching. Miracles served the message; they were not the whole of it.
- **"For I was sent for this purpose" (v43).** This echoes "he has sent me" in v18. The mission announced at Nazareth sets the course for everything that follows.
- **"Preaching in the synagogues of Judea" (v44).** Since Jesus was in Galilee, Luke seems to use "Judea" in its broad sense of "the land of the Jews," as he does elsewhere (1:5; 7:17; 23:5). Some manuscripts read "Galilee," likely a scribe's attempt to smooth the difficulty.
- **Commentators:** JFB says duty alone made him refuse requests "so grateful to His spirit." Guzik stresses that preaching, not miracle-working, was Jesus' primary work. The Tyndale notes explain "Judea" as the land of the Jews in general.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 4 shows who the Son is and what he came to do. In the wilderness he succeeds where Adam and Israel failed, trusting his Father's word rather than seizing bread, power or proof. In Nazareth he reads Isaiah's promise of an anointed one who brings good news to the poor and freedom to captives, and says, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled." He is that anointed one, the Christ. His own town rejects him, as his own nation later will, and the good news goes out to outsiders like the widow of Zarephath and Naaman the Syrian. Then, in Capernaum, his word drives out demons and fevers, and his hands rest on each sick person in turn. The year of the Lord's favor has begun, and he must take it to other towns as well.
