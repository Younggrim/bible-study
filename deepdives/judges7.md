*Setting:* Gideon has been called (ch 6). He tore down his father's altar to Baal, the Spirit of the LORD clothed him, he sounded the trumpet, and men came from Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun and Naphtali (6:34–35). Twice God answered his fleece. Now the armies face each other in the Jezreel Valley, the broad plain in northern Israel that cuts from the coast toward the Jordan. Chapter 7 is the heart of Gideon's story. God deliberately makes Israel's army smaller and smaller until no one could mistake who won the battle.

#### Gideon's Three Hundred Men (vv1–18)
- **"Jerubbaal (that is, Gideon)" (v1).** The new name from 6:32 ("Let Baal contend against him") now stands first. The man who pulled down Baal's altar leads the army.
- **"The spring of Harod… the hill of Moreh" (v1).** Harod sits at the foot of Mount Gilboa on the south side of the valley. Moreh is a hill a few miles north across the plain. The Tyndale notes explain that this ground guarded the pass from the Jezreel Valley down to the Jordan crossings near Beth-shan. Israel is on the high ground, with Midian spread out in the valley below. Saul will die on this same mountain against the Philistines (1 Sam 31:1).
- **A name that fits (vv1, 3).** *Harod* comes from the Hebrew verb *charad*, "to tremble." Verse 3 sends home everyone "fearful and trembling" (*chared*). Gideon's army camps at "Trembling Spring," and the trembling are sent away.
- **"The people with you are too many for me" (v2).** Israel has 32,000 men. Judges 8:10 puts Midian's force at about 135,000, so Israel is already outnumbered more than four to one. God's problem is not that Israel is too weak but that it is too strong: "lest Israel boast over me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me.'" The danger God guards against is pride, not defeat (compare Deut 8:17; 1 Sam 14:6).
- **"Whoever is fearful and trembling, let him return home" (v3).** This was already the law of Israel's warfare (Deut 20:8), so that fear would not spread through the ranks. Gideon himself was fearful (6:15, 27; 7:10), so he is sending home men much like himself. Two out of three go, 22,000 men, and 10,000 remain.
- **"Mount Gilead" (v3).** Gilead is normally the land east of the Jordan, which does not fit a camp at Gilboa. Some think this was a local name for a hill near Harod. Others think an early copyist confused it with Gilboa, or that the phrase is an idiom for "go home quickly." The text does not settle it.
- **"The people are still too many" (v4).** Now God does the choosing: "I will test them for you there." Gideon does not have to pick.
- **Lapping and kneeling (vv5–6).** 300 men scoop water to their mouths with their hands and lap it "as a dog laps." Everyone else kneels with face to the water. What did the test mean? Some see the lappers as alert men who kept their eyes up and weapons ready. Others see them as men in a hurry. The Tyndale notes see no military virtue at all: the test simply produced a small number. The text never praises the 300. The point is the number, not the men.
- **"With the 300 men who lapped I will save you" (v7).** Now the odds are something like 450 to one. Gideon has nothing left to trust but God's word.
- **"Took provisions in their hands, and their trumpets" (v8).** The 300 keep the supplies and the rams'-horn trumpets of the men going home, which is how they end up with 300 trumpets and 300 jars (v16). "The camp of Midian was below him in the valley."
- **"If you are afraid to go down, go down… with Purah your servant" (vv9–10).** God gives the command and the promise ("I have given it into your hand") and then, kindly, makes room for Gideon's fear. He does not scold. He offers a companion and one more piece of reassurance, this time without being asked. Gideon goes, which says the fear was real.
- **"Like locusts in abundance… as the sand that is on the seashore" (v12).** From the edge of the camp Gideon sees the size of it. The camels again are "without number." "Sand on the seashore" is the language God used of Israel's own future numbers (Gen 22:17). Here it describes the enemy.
- **The barley loaf dream (vv13–14).** A soldier dreams that a round loaf of barley bread rolls into camp and flattens a tent. Barley was cheap bread, the food of poor farmers, and Israel under Midian had been reduced to exactly that. The tent was the nomad's home and symbol. His comrade reads it at once: "This is no other than the sword of Gideon the son of Joash." The enemy already knows Gideon's name and already fears him. In the ancient world dreams were taken seriously as messages from the gods, and in the Old Testament God sometimes speaks even through pagans' dreams (Gen 41; Dan 2). Gideon arrived in the right spot at the right minute to hear it.
- **"He worshiped" (v15).** Before he gives an order, Gideon bows. The Hebrew word means to bow down low. Then the frightened man becomes the encourager: "Arise, for the LORD has given the host of Midian into your hand." He has moved from "if you are with me" to "the LORD has given."
- **Trumpets, jars and torches (vv16–18).** Three companies of 100 are each given a trumpet, an empty clay jar and a torch hidden inside the jar. Normally only a few men in a large army carried trumpets and torches, so 300 of each would suggest a huge force. "Look at me, and do likewise" (v17) is simple leadership by example. The battle cry gives the credit in the right order: "For the LORD and for Gideon."
- **Commentators:** All three see the shrinking army as God guarding his glory. Matthew Henry: "God provides that the praise of victory may be wholly to himself." JFB ties v3 to the law of Deuteronomy 20:8 and sees God teaching Israel dependence on him. On the drinking test they differ: Guzik reads the lappers as more alert, with their eyes on their surroundings, JFB reads them as quick men who drank like travelers on the move, and the Tyndale notes say the choice seems arbitrary and was simply a way to cut numbers. Guzik also draws the lesson that abundance can make dependence on God harder, pointing to Paul's thorn (2 Cor 12:7–10). On the dream, Henry calls the barley cake one of "the foolish things of the world" chosen "to confound the wise," and JFB notes barley was "usually the food of the poor, and of beasts." Guzik points out how Gideon's encouragement, once he had it, spread to his men.

#### Gideon Defeats Midian (vv19–25)
- **"At the beginning of the middle watch" (v19).** Israel divided the night into three watches. The middle watch began about 10 p.m. The guards had "just set the watch," so the new sentries were barely awake to the dark, and the sleeping camp had settled in for the night.
- **Smashed jars, blazing torches, blaring trumpets (vv19–20).** In a moment the hills on three sides burst with light and sound. The men held torches in the left hand and trumpets in the right, so they had no hand free for a sword. "A sword for the LORD and for Gideon!" was shouted by men who were not using swords at all. The only swords drawn that night were Midian's.
- **"Every man stood in his place around the camp" (v21).** Israel did not charge. The 300 simply stood still while the camp panicked. Picture tethered camels bolting, men waking in the dark to noise on all sides, and no idea who was friend or enemy.
- **"The LORD set every man's sword against his comrade" (v22).** This is the decisive act, and it is God's. The same thing happens when Jonathan attacks the Philistines (1 Sam 14:20) and when Jehoshaphat's choir sings (2 Chr 20:22–23). Israel blows trumpets, and God fights.
- **The flight east (v22).** The army runs southeast down the valley toward the Jordan fords: Beth-shittah ("house of the acacia"), Zererah, and Abel-meholah, later Elisha's hometown (1 Kgs 19:16). Some Hebrew manuscripts read "Zeredah" for Zererah. They were trying to get back across the river to home ground.
- **"The men of Israel were called out" (v23).** Men from Naphtali, Asher and Manasseh join the chase. JFB thinks these were many of the same men sent home the day before, who had not gone far. The 300 broke the army, and now the wider people share in the pursuit.
- **"Capture the waters… and also the Jordan" (v24).** Gideon calls on Ephraim, the large tribe to the south, to seize the river crossings and trap the fleeing army. Ehud did the same against Moab (3:28), and Jephthah will do it against Ephraim itself (12:5–6). Beth-barah's location is unknown, but it was one of the fords.
- **Oreb and Zeeb (v25).** The names mean "Raven" and "Wolf," fitting names for desert raiders. The Tyndale notes point out that these were commanders, not the two kings, who are still at large (8:5). The places where they died were named after them, so later Israelites could point to the rock and the winepress and remember. The "winepress of Zeeb" is a sharp echo: Gideon was first found hiding in a winepress from Midian (6:11), and now a Midianite prince dies in one. Isaiah later looks back on "the slaughter of Midian at the rock of Oreb" as the model of God's sudden rescue (Isa 10:26; see also Isa 9:4 and Ps 83:11).
- **"Across the Jordan" (v25).** Gideon has already crossed the river in pursuit, which leads straight into chapter 8 and Ephraim's complaint.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sums up the plan as "not to fight them, but to frighten them," and calls the story one that "shines very brightly in the book of the wars of the Lord." Guzik says the battle cry was not pride but good tactics, since the Midianites already dreaded Gideon's name (v14). He also mentions Origen's old allegory that the trumpets are preaching and the torches holy living, which is an illustration, not the meaning of the text. Guzik reads Gideon's call to Ephraim as wisdom, letting many share the work once God had begun it. JFB says the jars were cheap earthenware that broke easily and kept the torches hidden until the signal, and calls Oreb and Zeeb "appropriate designations of Arab leaders."

#### Pointing to Christ
God shrank Gideon's army until no one could boast, and then won the battle while the 300 stood still holding lights. Paul uses the same pattern for the gospel: "we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (2 Cor 4:7), and God chose what is weak and low "so that no human being might boast" (1 Cor 1:27–29). The barley loaf that flattened Midian's tent was poor man's bread. Jesus fed the five thousand from five barley loaves (John 6:9) and then called himself "the bread of life." He won his greatest victory through weakness, on a cross, and the people who belong to him share the spoils of a battle they did not fight.
