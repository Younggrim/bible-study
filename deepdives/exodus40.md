*Setting:* Israel has been camped at Mount Sinai for about nine months (19:1). The covenant was given, broken at the golden calf, and renewed (chs 32–34). The people have given their gold, silver and skill, and every piece of the tabernacle has been made and inspected (39:32–43). Now God tells Moses to put it all together and set it up. The book that opened with Israel enslaved in Egypt, building cities for Pharaoh, ends with the LORD himself moving in among his people.

#### The Tabernacle Erected (vv1–33)
- **"On the first day of the first month you shall erect the tabernacle" (v2).** God had made this month "the beginning of months" at the Passover (12:2). The tabernacle goes up on New Year's Day, almost exactly a year after Israel left Egypt on the fifteenth of the first month (Num 33:3). A new year and a new way of life begin together. Numbers picks up the story a month later (Num 1:1).
- **Inside out (vv3–8).** The order of setup starts with the ark in the Most Holy Place, then the table, lampstand and incense altar in the Holy Place, then the altar of burnt offering and basin outside, and finally the courtyard fence. A worshiper approached from the outside in, gate, altar, basin, tent. God builds his house from the inside out, starting where his presence would rest.
- **"You shall screen the ark with the veil" (v3).** The veil hid the ark even from the priests serving in the Holy Place. Only the high priest went behind it, once a year (Lev 16). "The testimony" (*'edut*) placed in the ark (v20) means the two stone tablets of the covenant, which the Tyndale notes describe as a standing witness to the covenant Israel had entered.
- **"Anoint the tabernacle and all that is in it, and consecrate it… so that it may become holy" (v9).** Anointing oil marked something as belonging to God. The same verb, *qadash*, "make holy, set apart," is used for the furniture and for Aaron. The altar of burnt offering is singled out as **"most holy"** (v10), because it was where blood was shed and sin dealt with.
- **Aaron and his sons washed, clothed and anointed (vv12–15).** God gives the order here, but it is carried out in Leviticus 8. **"A perpetual priesthood throughout their generations"** (v15) meant it was to last as long as that covenant order stood. Hebrews argues that the Levitical priesthood was always meant to give way to a priest "after the order of Melchizedek" (Heb 7:11–17).
- **"This Moses did; according to all that the LORD commanded him, so he did" (v16).** This is the summary line, and then the detail follows. Through vv19–32 the phrase **"as the LORD had commanded Moses"** sounds seven times, once after each step. Guzik counts it at least nineteen times across chapters 36–40. The Tyndale notes set it against Aaron's excuse after the calf: "I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf" (32:24). God's presence is received on his terms, not manufactured on ours.
- **Moses does it with his own hands (vv18–33).** Moses lays the bases, raises the frames, spreads the tent, sets the bread on the table, lights the lamps, burns incense and offers the first burnt offering and grain offering (vv23, 25, 27, 29). Until Aaron is ordained (Lev 8–9), Moses acts as priest. He is the one who saw the pattern on the mountain (25:9, 40), so he is the one who sets it up.
- **"The bread… before the LORD… the lamps before the LORD" (vv23, 25).** The bread of the Presence was literally "bread of the face." The table, the light and the incense were all set out in God's presence, as a family table is set in a home. Israel's God was not an idol to be fed (Ps 50:12–13), but he chose to live among them as the host of their life.
- **"Moses and Aaron and his sons washed their hands and their feet" (v31).** Even Moses washes. No one, however close to God, approached the altar unwashed (30:20–21).
- **"So Moses finished the work" (v33).** As in 39:32, "finished" (*kalah*) echoes Genesis 2:2, where God "finished his work." The work that began with God's word on the mountain (25:1) ends with the word done.
- **Slavery and service.** Exodus opened with Israel's "hard service" (*'avodah*) under Pharaoh (1:14; 2:23). The same word describes "all the work" of the tabernacle (39:42). The people have not stopped working. They have changed masters, from building store cities for Pharaoh to building a house for the LORD, and this time they gave freely.
- **Commentators:** JFB reckons the work began within six months of leaving Egypt and notes that the oil was "not poured over the heads" of Aaron's sons, a pouring kept for the high priest (Lev 8:12; Ps 133:2). It says Moses arranged the furniture with "scrupulous fidelity" to the pattern. Henry draws a lesson from the timing: Israel did not wait until they reached Canaan to build God a house, because "a tabernacle for God is very needful, even in a wilderness." Guzik uses the one-year mark to ask whether believers are actually growing year by year, and he says the tabernacle had to be both built and assembled according to God's pattern, with no improvements.

#### The Glory of the LORD (vv34–38)
- **"The cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (v34).** This is the climax of the whole book. The cloud that led Israel out of Egypt (13:21–22) and settled on Sinai (24:15–18) now rests on the tent. The Tyndale notes put it well: God's goal was never only to free Israel from slavery but to bring them to himself. "Glory" is *kavod*, literally "weight" or "heaviness," the felt reality of God's presence.
- **After the golden calf.** In chapter 33 God said he would not go up among them, "lest I consume you on the way" (33:3). Moses pleaded, "If your presence will not go with me, do not bring us up from here" (33:15). This verse is the answer. The sinful people Moses interceded for now have God living in their camp.
- **"Moses was not able to enter… because the cloud settled on it" (v35).** "Settled" is *shakan*, "to dwell," the root of *mishkan*, "tabernacle," the "dwelling place." The man who spoke with God "face to face" (33:11) cannot go in. The same thing happens when Solomon dedicates the temple: "the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud" (1 Kgs 8:10–11). Leviticus begins the next scene: "The LORD called Moses and spoke to him from the tent of meeting" (Lev 1:1). God himself will explain how sinners may draw near.
- **"Whenever the cloud was taken up… the people of Israel would set out" (v36).** The tabernacle was a portable house. Wherever the cloud moved, Israel moved. Numbers 9:15–23 describes this in detail: sometimes the cloud stayed two days, sometimes a month or a year.
- **"Cloud… by day, and fire… by night, in the sight of all the house of Israel" (v38).** God's presence was visible to everyone, not only to the priests. The Tyndale notes point out that "the house of Israel" echoes the start of the book, the "sons of Israel" who went down to Egypt as one family (1:1). That family, once slaves, now travels with God in its midst.
- **An open ending.** Exodus closes on the road, "throughout all their journeys." The glory has come, but the land has not been reached. Israel is a people on the way with God, which is where every believer still stands.
- **Commentators:** All three see the filling glory as God's seal on the work. Guzik says the people's obedience did not earn the glory but made room to receive it, and that without God's presence any building, church or home is only a structure. He also sees here God's answer to Moses' prayer in 33:14–15, and he passes on R. Alan Cole's point that a journey implies an arrival. JFB says the glory filled the tent without singeing a curtain, and it contrasts Moses, who "could not endure the unclouded effulgence," with believers who now approach God through Christ. Henry: "what Moses could not do, our Lord Jesus has done." He also notes how Israel rested when the cloud rested and moved when it moved.

#### Pointing to Christ
The book ends with God's glory dwelling in a tent among his people. John begins his Gospel with the same picture: "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). "Dwelt" is *skēnoō*, "to pitch a tent," the word the Greek Old Testament uses for *shakan*. In Jesus, God's glory pitched its tent in human flesh. Moses could not enter where the glory rested, but Christ entered "once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12) and opened the way for others to follow. The story Exodus begins ends in Revelation: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them" (Rev 21:3).
