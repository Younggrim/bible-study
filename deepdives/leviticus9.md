*Setting:* Israel is camped at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Leviticus 8 told of the seven-day ordination of Aaron and his sons, when they stayed at the entrance of the tent of meeting day and night. Now comes "the eighth day," when the new priests begin their work. Everything so far has been building toward this moment: the tabernacle built (Exod 40), the offerings explained (Lev 1–7), and the priests installed (Lev 8). The question hanging over the chapter is whether God will actually come and meet with his people in the way he promised (Exod 29:42–46).

#### The LORD Accepts Aaron's Offering (vv1–24)
- **"On the eighth day" (v1).** The seven days of ordination are done (8:33–35). In the Bible the eighth day often marks a new beginning after a completed week: circumcision on the eighth day (12:3), the eighth day ending the Feast of Booths (23:36), and the leper's restoration on the eighth day (14:10). The early church saw the day of Christ's resurrection, the first day of a new week, in the same light.
- **"And the elders of Israel" (v1).** The leaders of the tribes are called in as well as the priests. This is a national event. The priesthood serves the whole people, and the people need to see it begin.
- **Aaron's offerings: "a bull calf for a sin offering and a ram for a burnt offering" (v2).** The week of ordination did not make Aaron sinless. He begins, as he did in chapter 8, with an offering for his own sin. Some commentators note that the animal is a calf, the same Hebrew word (*'egel*) used for the golden calf Aaron made only months earlier (Exod 32:4). The text does not draw that line itself, but a reader of Exodus can hardly miss it.
- **The people's offerings (vv3–4).** Israel brings a goat for a sin offering, a calf and a lamb for a burnt offering, an ox and a ram for peace offerings, and a grain offering. That is every main offering in chapters 1–7 except the guilt offering. The order matters: sin is dealt with first, then the whole worshiper is given to God (burnt offering), and then there is fellowship and a shared meal (peace offering). The Tyndale notes observe that together these offerings covered sin and reaffirmed the covenant.
- **"For today the LORD will appear to you" (v4).** This is the goal of the whole system. The offerings are not ends in themselves. They clear the way for God to come near.
- **"This is the thing that the LORD commanded you to do, that the glory of the LORD may appear to you" (v6).** The word for glory is *kabod*, from a root meaning "heavy" or "weighty." BDB lists Leviticus 9:6 and 9:23 among the great appearances of God's glory in the wilderness, alongside the cloud on Sinai (Exod 24:16–17) and the cloud that filled the finished tabernacle (Exod 40:34–35). The promise is tied to obedience: do what God said, and he will show himself.
- **"Make atonement for yourself and for the people" (v7).** Before Aaron can stand for others, he has to be right with God himself. Hebrews picks up exactly this point: the high priest "is obligated to offer sacrifice for his own sins just as he does for those of the people" (Heb 5:3).
- **Aaron's sin offering (vv8–11).** For the first time Aaron himself does the work Moses did in chapter 8. His sons bring the blood, he puts it on the horns of the altar and pours the rest at its base, and he burns the fat. **"The flesh and the skin he burned up with fire outside the camp" (v11).** This follows the rule in 4:11–12 and 6:30: when the blood of a sin offering was for the priest, the priest could not eat the meat. A man cannot eat the offering made for his own sin. Hebrews 13:11–12 sees Jesus suffering "outside the gate" in the same pattern.
- **Aaron's burnt offering (vv12–14).** The ram is handed to him "piece by piece," washed and burned whole. Aaron's sons assist at every step. From the beginning, the priests serve as a team.
- **The people's offerings presented (vv15–21).** The goat is offered "like the first one" (v15), the burnt offering "according to the rule" (v16), and a handful of the grain offering is burned **"besides the burnt offering of the morning" (v17).** The daily lamb offered every morning and evening (Exod 29:38–42) has already begun, and these special offerings are added to it, not in place of it. Then the ox and ram of the peace offerings are killed, their fat burned, and **"the breasts and the right thigh Aaron waved"** (v21), the portions that would become the priests' share (7:30–34).
- **"Aaron lifted up his hands toward the people and blessed them" (v22).** The words are not given here, but most readers connect this with the priestly blessing God gives in Numbers 6:24–26: "The LORD bless you and keep you…" Lifting the hands was the posture of blessing. **"He came down from offering"** reflects the raised altar, which priests approached by a ramp rather than steps (Exod 20:26).
- **"Moses and Aaron went into the tent of meeting, and when they came out they blessed the people" (v23).** What they did inside is not said. Many readers think they prayed, and Moses may have been showing Aaron his duties in the Holy Place, such as the incense and lamps. Then came the second blessing, from both of them.
- **"The glory of the LORD appeared to all the people" (v23).** The promise of vv4 and 6 is kept. Not only Moses on the mountain, but everyone, saw it.
- **"Fire came out from before the LORD and consumed the burnt offering" (v24).** The offerings had already been burning since morning. Now God's own fire consumes what was left, all at once. It was a visible sign that he accepted the sacrifices, the atonement and the new priesthood. The same sign comes at other turning points: Gideon (Judg 6:21), David at the threshing floor (1 Chr 21:26), Solomon's temple (2 Chr 7:1) and Elijah on Carmel (1 Kings 18:38). Jewish tradition holds that this fire was the one kept burning on the altar continually (6:13).
- **"They shouted and fell on their faces" (v24).** The verb is *ranan*, "to give a ringing cry." BDB lists this verse among its uses for crying out in joy and exultation. Joy and reverent fear arrive together: a shout, and then faces in the dust. The people are not driven away. They worship.
- **The next chapter.** The joy of this day is followed almost at once by disaster, when two of Aaron's sons offer "unauthorized fire" and fire comes out "from before the LORD" again, this time in judgment (10:1–2). The same phrase is used on purpose. The fire that accepts is also the fire that consumes.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both stress that Aaron needed atonement after a whole week of consecration. Guzik reads this as proof that animal sacrifices could never finish the work, and JFB contrasts Aaron with Christ, who "knew no sin." JFB also suggests the calf may allude to Aaron's golden calf, which is a reasonable guess but not stated in the text. Henry divides the chapter simply: a meeting appointed between God and his priests, Aaron's offerings, and God's acceptance "of their persons, by showing them his glory" and of the sacrifices "by consuming them with fire from heaven." On v23 he notes that "God's manifestations of himself, of his glory and grace, are commonly given in answer to prayer," and on v24 that "a gracious fear makes us bow before him," not run from him. Guzik lists other places in Scripture where God's fire showed he accepted an offering and ties fire to God's presence and to the Spirit's work (Matt 3:11; Acts 2:3).

#### Pointing to Christ
Everything in this chapter, the right animals, the right order, the careful handling of blood, was aimed at one thing: that "the glory of the LORD may appear." Aaron had to offer first for himself, and his sacrifices would have to be repeated every morning and evening for centuries. Jesus is the High Priest who needed no offering for himself, and who "offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins" (Heb 10:12). In him the glory Israel glimpsed at the tabernacle came to live among us: "we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father" (John 1:14). And like Aaron coming out to bless the people, the risen Christ "lifting up his hands… blessed them" as he left his disciples (Luke 24:50).
