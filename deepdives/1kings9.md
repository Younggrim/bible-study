*Setting:* About 946 BC, some twenty-four years into Solomon's reign. The temple took seven years and the palace thirteen (6:38; 7:1), and both are now finished. Chapter 8 told of the temple's dedication and Solomon's great prayer. Chapter 9 gives God's answer, which is both a promise and a warning, and then a set of short reports on Solomon's dealings with Hiram of Tyre, his labor force, his building projects, his worship and his navy. On the surface it is a picture of a king at the height of his power. Read closely, it also shows small cracks that will widen in chapters 10–11. Second Chronicles 7:11–8:18 covers the same ground.

#### The LORD Appears to Solomon (vv1–9)
- **"As soon as Solomon had finished building… all that Solomon desired to build" (v1).** The word "desired" (*chesheq*, longing or delight) will appear again in v19: "whatever Solomon desired to build." It is the language of a man who loves his projects. Verse 10 dates this appearance to the end of the twenty years of building, which suggests God spoke some years after the dedication (ch 8), not the same night. Some commentators place it right after the dedication and read vv1 and 10 as a summary. Either way it comes when the work is done, a moment when a successful man can drift.
- **"The LORD appeared to Solomon a second time, as he had appeared to him at Gibeon" (v2).** The first appearance, in a dream at Gibeon, came at the start of his reign, when he asked for wisdom (3:4–15). This is the second and last. Chapter 11 will count it against him: the LORD "had appeared to him twice" (11:9).
- **"I have heard your prayer… I have consecrated this house" (v3).** Solomon built the house and dedicated it, but only God can make it holy. **"My eyes and my heart will be there for all time"** answers Solomon's request that God's eyes be "open night and day toward this house" (8:29). God gives more than was asked: not only his eyes but his heart. Second Chronicles 7:12–16 gives a fuller version, including the famous promise: "if my people who are called by my name humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and will forgive their sin and heal their land" (2 Chr 7:14).
- **"If you will walk before me, as David your father walked, with integrity of heart and uprightness" (v4).** "Integrity" is *tom*, wholeness or completeness, and "uprightness" is *yosher*, straightness. David was far from sinless (2 Sam 11), so the standard is not perfection. It is a whole heart that keeps returning to God and never turns to other gods. That was David's distinguishing mark (15:5; 11:4).
- **"Then I will establish your royal throne over Israel forever" (v5).** The promise to David's line stands (2 Sam 7:16), but each king's share in it is tied to faithfulness, as Solomon himself prayed (8:25). Psalm 89:30–37 holds both together: God will punish disobedient sons, but he will not break his covenant with David.
- **"But if you turn aside from following me, you or your children" (v6).** The "you" changes from singular to plural in the Hebrew here. The warning now covers the king, his sons and the whole nation. The specific sin named is the worst one: "go and serve other gods and worship them."
- **"I will cut off Israel from the land… and the house… I will cast out of my sight" (v7).** This repeats the covenant curses of Deuteronomy almost word for word: Israel will become "a proverb and a byword among all peoples" (Deut 28:37). The house God has just said he will watch "for all time" can still be thrown away if the people abandon him. A holy building is no protection for an unholy people. Jeremiah would make the same point centuries later to people who chanted "the temple of the LORD" as a charm (Jer 7:4, 12–14).
- **"This house will become a heap of ruins… everyone passing by it will be astonished and will hiss" (v8).** The Hebrew is literally "this house will be high (*ʿelyon*)." The ESV, following ancient translations, reads "a heap of ruins," which takes only a small change in the Hebrew. If the standard text is kept, the sense may be "this house, which is now so exalted, will become an object of horror." Hissing or whistling was a gesture of shock and scorn (Jer 19:8; Lam 2:15).
- **"Why has the LORD done thus to this land and to this house?" (vv8–9).** The question and its answer are taken from Deuteronomy 29:24–26. Even the nations will understand that Israel's ruin is judgment for unfaithfulness. That is exactly what happened in 586 BC, when the Babylonians burned this temple (2 Kgs 25:9). The book of Kings was most likely finished in exile, and its first readers would have recognised their own story here.
- **Commentators:** Henry: Solomon dedicated the house, but God alone "hallowed" it, and if the people forsake him, "this house will not save you." He adds, "Their sin will be read in their punishment." Guzik calls the moment after a great achievement a perilous hour, and passes on the Puritan John Trapp's remark that Solomon's fall began with loving his buildings too much. Guzik also warns that a magnificent temple could tempt Israel to trust the building instead of God. JFB places the vision on the night after the dedication and treats vv2–9 as a parenthesis.

#### Solomon's Other Acts (vv10–28)
- **"At the end of twenty years" (v10).** Seven years for the temple and thirteen for the palace (6:38; 7:1), counted from Solomon's fourth year (6:1).
- **"King Solomon gave to Hiram twenty cities in the land of Galilee" (v11).** Hiram had supplied timber and gold, and Solomon had paid him yearly in wheat and oil (5:10–11). The towns were apparently further payment, or security for a debt. They lay in the far north near the border with Tyre, in Asher's territory. Giving away part of the land God had given Israel's tribes was a serious step, since the land was an inheritance, not a royal asset to trade (Lev 25:23; compare Naboth's vineyard in ch 21).
- **"What kind of cities are these…? So they are called the land of Cabul" (v13).** Hiram calls Solomon **"my brother,"** the standard language of treaty partners, but he is not pleased. The name *Kabul* is a real town in Asher (Josh 19:27). The Hebrew lexicon explains Hiram's jibe as a play on sounds, "like nothing" or "as good as nothing." Others connect it to a root meaning "bound," as in land given in pledge. Second Chronicles 8:2 says Solomon later rebuilt cities Hiram had given *him*, so the towns may have been returned, or there was a further exchange.
- **"Hiram had sent to the king 120 talents of gold" (v14).** A talent was roughly 75 pounds (about 34 kg), so this was around four and a half tons of gold, a huge sum. Whether it was a gift, a loan or payment for the towns is not stated, and readers differ.
- **"The account of the forced labor" (v15).** The word is *mas*, the labor gang or corvée, the compulsory work that subjects owed a king. Samuel had warned Israel that a king would take their sons and daughters to work for him (1 Sam 8:11–17). Chapter 5 described Israelite workers sent to Lebanon in shifts (5:13–14). This section stresses the permanent slave labor drawn from the remaining Canaanite peoples. In the end the burden of Solomon's labor demands is what splits the kingdom (12:4, 18).
- **"The Millo and the wall of Jerusalem" (v15).** "Millo" probably comes from the word for "fill." It was likely a system of stone terraces or fill on the slope of the City of David, holding up buildings above. The huge "stepped stone structure" excavated on the eastern slope of the City of David may be part of it, though its date is debated.
- **"Hazor and Megiddo and Gezer" (v15).** These three cities guarded the main routes through the land: Hazor in the north toward Damascus, Megiddo at the pass into the Jezreel Valley, and Gezer on the road from the coast up to Jerusalem. Archaeologists have found nearly identical six-chambered gates at all three. Many date them to Solomon's time, and the match with this verse is famous, though some scholars place the gates a century later. The question is still argued.
- **"Pharaoh king of Egypt had… captured Gezer… and had given it as dowry to his daughter" (v16).** Solomon's marriage to Pharaoh's daughter (3:1) came with a wedding gift: a city that the Israelites had never fully taken (Josh 16:10; Judg 1:29). An Egyptian relief from Tanis showing a pharaoh, probably Siamun, striking a foreign enemy may record this campaign. That an Egyptian king gave a daughter to a foreign ruler at all shows how strong Solomon was. Egypt usually refused such marriages.
- **"Lower Beth-horon and Baalath and Tamar in the wilderness" (vv17–18).** Beth-horon guarded the western pass toward Jerusalem. Tamar was south of the Dead Sea toward Edom. The written Hebrew text has "Tamar," but the Jewish scribes' reading notes and 2 Chronicles 8:4 have "Tadmor," the oasis city later called Palmyra in the Syrian desert. The ESV follows "Tamar," which fits "in the land of Judah." This is a well-known case where the margin and the text differ.
- **"Store cities… cities for his chariots… cities for his horsemen" (v19).** Supply depots and chariot bases. Deuteronomy's law for kings warned against multiplying horses, wives and gold (Deut 17:16–17). Chapters 10–11 will show Solomon doing all three, and this verse is the first sign.
- **"All the people who were left of the Amorites, the Hittites…" (vv20–21).** These were the Canaanite peoples Israel had not driven out under Joshua and the judges (Judg 1:27–36). Solomon made them a permanent slave labor force, and **"so they are to this day"** again points to a written source from before the exile.
- **"But of the people of Israel Solomon made no slaves" (v22).** Israelites served as soldiers, officials and officers instead. The text says they were not made permanent slaves, but 5:13 shows they were still drafted for rotating work, and 12:4 shows how heavy that felt. Both are true: no Israelite was a slave, yet the people still bore a "heavy yoke."
- **"550 who had charge of the people" (v23).** Second Chronicles 8:10 gives 250, and 1 Kings 5:16 gives 3,300 foremen. The different figures probably count different levels or groups of supervisors. Added together, 3,300 plus 550 equals 3,850, the same total as Chronicles' 3,600 (2 Chr 2:18) plus 250. That neat match suggests the two books are simply grouping the same officials differently.
- **"Pharaoh's daughter went up from the city of David to her own house" (v24).** She had lived in the City of David until her palace was finished (3:1; 7:8). Second Chronicles 8:11 gives the reason she had to move: Solomon said, "My wife shall not live in the house of David… for the places to which the ark of the LORD has come are holy." He sensed that a foreign wife, probably still devoted to her own gods, did not belong near the ark. He did not see where that would lead (11:1–8).
- **"Three times a year Solomon used to offer up burnt offerings and peace offerings" (v25).** At the three pilgrim feasts, Unleavened Bread (Passover), Weeks and Booths (Deut 16:16). The phrase "on the altar that he built" means the king provided and led the sacrifices. The priests performed them. The Hebrew also mentions burning incense, which some readers take as the king overstepping, as Uzziah later did (2 Chr 26:16–21). It more likely means Solomon sponsored the offerings. **"So he finished the house"** marks the temple as fully in use. This is the last good word about Solomon's worship. In chapter 11 his altars are for other gods.
- **"A fleet of ships at Ezion-geber, which is near Eloth on the shore of the Red Sea" (v26).** Ezion-geber was at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, beside modern Eilat. The "Red Sea" here is the Hebrew *Yam Suph*, the "Sea of Reeds," which can name both the gulf east of Sinai and the waters Israel crossed in the Exodus. This was Israel's first real seagoing venture. Edom had been conquered by David (2 Sam 8:14), which gave Israel the port.
- **"Hiram sent with the fleet his servants, seamen who were familiar with the sea" (v27).** The Phoenicians of Tyre were the great sailors of the ancient world. Israel was a land people, and Solomon needed their expertise. Later, King Jehoshaphat tried to rebuild this trade, and his ships were wrecked at Ezion-geber (22:48).
- **"They went to Ophir and brought from there gold, 420 talents" (v28).** Roughly 16 tons. "Gold of Ophir" was proverbial for the finest gold (Job 28:16; Ps 45:9; Isa 13:12). An inscribed potsherd found at Tell Qasile, near Tel Aviv, reads "gold of Ophir to Beth-horon, thirty shekels," which shows Ophir was a real source. Where it was is unknown. The main proposals are southwestern Arabia, the African coast (Somalia), and India. The three-year voyages of 10:22 suggest a long way. Chronicles gives 450 talents (2 Chr 8:18), a difference in one letter-numeral that could easily arise in copying.
- **Commentators:** Guzik questions Solomon trading away towns of the promised land, and regards enslaving the Canaanites as a compromise with the command to drive them out. He also gives the meaning "good-for-nothing" for Cabul, which fits the lexicon's "as good as nothing." JFB says the towns were "inhabited by Canaanite heathens," and that Hiram, a sea-trader, disliked them for being inland. JFB identifies the feasts of v25 as "the passover, pentecost, and feast of tabernacles," and explains Ophir as "a general name, like the East or West Indies with us," for distant southern lands. JFB identifies Baalath with Baalbek in Lebanon and follows Tadmor (Palmyra), while the Tyndale notes connect Baalath with Kiriath-jearim and place Tamar south of the Dead Sea. Henry heads this section "the interchanging of grateful kindnesses between Solomon and Hiram," reading the friendship more warmly than Guzik.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's answer to Solomon shows both sides of his covenant: a promise that will not fail and a warning that cannot be ignored. No son of David kept the condition of v4 perfectly, and the warning of vv7–9 came true in the ruins of 586 BC. But the promise did not die. One Son of David walked before God "with integrity of heart and uprightness" with no failure at all, and his throne is established forever (Luke 1:32–33). Jesus also spoke of the temple's coming ruin (Mark 13:2), and offered himself as the place where God's eyes and heart are for all time. Solomon used foreign slaves to build his kingdom. Christ builds his house from people of every nation, set free and made "living stones" (1 Pet 2:5).
