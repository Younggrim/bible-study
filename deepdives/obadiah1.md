*Setting:* Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, one chapter of 21 verses, and it is addressed to Edom, not Israel. Edom was the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Gen 25:23–30; 36:1). Its land lay south and southeast of the Dead Sea, a region of red sandstone mountains and cliffs. From the womb the brothers struggled (Gen 25:22), and the nations kept up the feud: Edom refused Israel passage in the wilderness (Num 20:14–21), fought Saul, was conquered by David and kept breaking free (2 Chr 21:8–10; 25:11–12). Yet God told Israel, "You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deut 23:7). Obadiah's charge is that Edom betrayed that brotherhood on the day Jerusalem fell. **When was it written?** Nothing in the book gives a date, so the answer depends on which fall of Jerusalem verses 10–14 describe. Most scholars, with JFB and the Tyndale notes, link it to the Babylonian destruction in 586 BC, when Edom joined in the looting (Ps 137:7; Lam 4:21–22; Ezek 35:5, 15). Some, Guzik among them, place it after the Philistine and Arab raid on Jerusalem under Jehoram, about 845 BC (2 Chr 21:16–17), which would make Obadiah one of the earliest writing prophets. Jeremiah 49:7–22 repeats much of Obadiah 1–9, and Joel 2:32 may quote Obadiah 17, but scholars disagree about who borrowed from whom. The message is the same either way: God sees how his people are treated, and pride and cruelty will not have the last word.

#### Edom Will Be Humbled (vv1–9)
- **"The vision of Obadiah" (v1).** "Vision" is a standard title for a prophet's message from God (Isa 1:1; Nah 1:1). It means what the prophet was given to see and say, not necessarily a dream. Obadiah means "servant of the LORD." About a dozen men in the Old Testament share the name. Jewish tradition identified this prophet with Ahab's God-fearing steward (1 Kings 18:3–16), but nothing in the book supports that. We know nothing about him except this message.
- **"We have heard a report from the LORD, and a messenger has been sent among the nations" (v1).** The picture is of diplomacy before a war. An envoy goes round the nations gathering allies: "Rise up! Let us rise against her for battle!" The nations think they are making their own alliance. In fact the LORD is the one who sent the message. The Tyndale notes point out the irony that these same nations will face judgment too (vv15–16).
- **"I will make you small among the nations" (v2).** The verbs are in the Hebrew "prophetic perfect," spoken as if already done. Edom, proud and high, will be made small.
- **"The pride of your heart has deceived you, you who live in the clefts of the rock" (v3).** This is the heart of Edom's sin. Edom's cities were built into red sandstone cliffs and mountain strongholds reached by narrow passes. "The rock" is *sela*, possibly a pun on the Edomite stronghold Sela (2 Kings 14:7). Later the Nabateans carved the famous city of Petra into this same landscape. Pride "deceived" them. They looked at their walls of rock and thought they were safe.
- **"Who will bring me down to the ground?" (v3).** It is the question of every proud heart. God answers it in the next verse.
- **"Though you soar aloft like the eagle, though your nest is set among the stars, from there I will bring you down" (v4).** The eagle builds on the highest crags. "Among the stars" is deliberate exaggeration, echoing the king of Babylon who said "I will ascend to heaven; above the stars of God I will set my throne" (Isa 14:13–15). Height is no protection from God. "Declares the LORD" seals it.
- **Thieves and grape gatherers (v5).** Thieves take what they can carry and leave the rest. Harvesters leave gleanings, which the law required them to leave for the poor (Deut 24:19–21). Edom will not be so lucky. "How you have been destroyed!" breaks into the middle of the sentence like a cry of lament.
- **"How Esau has been pillaged, his treasures sought out!" (v6).** Using "Esau" instead of "Edom" reminds the reader of the brother story (Tyndale). Edom was rich: it controlled the King's Highway, the trade route from Arabia to Damascus, and had copper mines. Its hidden treasures in rocky storehouses would be searched out.
- **"All your allies have driven you to your border" (v7).** Literally "men of your covenant" and "men of your peace." Edom had betrayed its brother, and now it would be betrayed by its friends. "Those who eat your bread have set a trap beneath you" uses table fellowship, the deepest bond of trust in the ancient world (Ps 41:9). History matched it. After the Babylonians, Arab tribes, and later the Nabateans, pushed the Edomites out of their homeland, and by the 300s BC Edom's old land was Nabatean.
- **"Destroy the wise men out of Edom" (v8).** Edom was famous for wisdom: "Is wisdom no more in Teman?" (Jer 49:7). Job's friend Eliphaz was "the Temanite" (Job 2:11). Their wisdom could not see what was coming: "you have no understanding" (v7).
- **"O Teman… Mount Esau" (v9).** Teman was a major region or city of Edom, named for a grandson of Esau (Gen 36:11). "Mount Esau" stands for the whole mountainous land of Edom. Note that the book will end with a different mountain: Mount Zion (v21).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "God can easily lay those low who magnify and exalt themselves; and will do it." He adds that "carnal security ripens men for ruin, and makes the ruin worse when it comes." JFB explains the rock dwellings, cities "consisted of houses mostly cut in the rocks," and reads "nest among the stars" as "the loftiest hills which seem to reach the very stars." Guzik notes that Edom's pride rested on three things, its fortress, its wisdom and its alliances, and that each is exactly what God says he will break. He also notes the close parallel with Jeremiah 49.

#### Edom's Violence Against Jacob (vv10–14)
- **"Because of the violence done to your brother Jacob" (v10).** The word is *hamas*, violence or cruel wrong, the same word used of the corrupt world before the flood (Gen 6:11). "Your brother Jacob" is the key phrase. JFB notes the point of using "Jacob" here: not just his brother, but "his twin brother." "Shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever."
- **"On the day that you stood aloof… you were like one of them" (v11).** The first charge is that Edom stood by. "Strangers carried off his wealth and foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem." Casting lots was how conquerors divided spoil and captives (Joel 3:3; Nah 3:10). Edom did not lift a hand. To God, standing aside was the same as joining in.
- **The "do not" verses (vv12–14).** Eight times the prophet says "do not." The Hebrew form is a command, as if Obadiah is standing on the day itself shouting at Edom to stop. Older versions such as the KJV render it "thou shouldest not have looked," as a rebuke for what was done. The ESV keeps the vivid present. Either way, they show how sin grows step by step.
- **"Do not gloat… do not rejoice… do not boast" (v12).** First came the heart and mouth. "Gloat" is literally "look on," staring at a brother's misery with pleasure. Proverbs warns, "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls" (Prov 24:17), and this was not even an enemy but a brother. Psalm 137:7 records what Edom said: "Lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations!"
- **"Do not enter the gate… do not loot his wealth" (v13).** Then came the feet and hands. Edom moved from watching to entering and from entering to plundering.
- **"Do not stand at the crossroads to cut off his fugitives; do not hand over his survivors" (v14).** Finally Edom blocked the escape routes and turned refugees over to the enemy. People fleeing south from Jerusalem would have headed for the desert roads through Edomite territory, and they found the road blocked by their own relatives.
- **"The day" (vv11–14).** The word "day" rings through these verses: the day of his misfortune, of their ruin, of distress, of calamity. The Tyndale notes count it twelve times in vv8–15. Edom had its "day" over Jacob. In v15 a greater day is coming.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the charge: "Their barbarous conduct towards Judah and Jerusalem, is charged upon them." Guzik traces how the sin grows, from standing by to gazing, rejoicing, boasting, entering, looting, ambushing and handing over, and he presses the point that doing nothing when a brother suffers can be a grave sin in itself. He quotes Trapp on the way sin proceeds by degrees. JFB glosses the "strangers" of v11 as the armies that took Jerusalem. The Tyndale notes add that God's law required help for a neighbor in trouble (Deut 22:1–4), and how much more for a brother.

#### The Day of the LORD Is Near (vv15–18)
- **"The day of the LORD is near upon all the nations" (v15).** The "day of the LORD" is the time God steps in to judge and to save. It can mean a near event, like the fall of Edom or the locust plague in Joel (Joel 1:15), and it also points to the final judgment of all the nations. Here Edom's judgment is the first sign of the greater day.
- **"As you have done, it shall be done to you; your deeds shall return on your own head" (v15).** This is the principle of fitting justice (Lev 24:19–20; Ps 7:16; Gal 6:7). Edom cut off fugitives, so Edom will be cut off. It cheered at Jerusalem's day of disaster, so its own day will come.
- **"As you have drunk on my holy mountain, so all the nations shall drink continually" (v16).** The "you" here probably means Judah, who drank God's cup of judgment when Jerusalem fell. Or it may mean Edom, which held drunken victory celebrations on Zion. The image of the cup comes from Jeremiah 25:15–29: the cup of God's wrath passes from nation to nation. Judah drank it first, and all the nations will drain it, "and shall be as though they had never been."
- **"But in Mount Zion there shall be those who escape, and it shall be holy" (v17).** The great "but." Edom's mountain fortress will fail, but God's mountain will be a refuge. Joel 2:32 says almost the same: "in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be those who escape, as the LORD has said." Joel may be quoting Obadiah. "It shall be holy" means it will never again be trampled by strangers (Joel 3:17).
- **"The house of Jacob shall possess their own possessions" (v17).** God's people will receive back what was taken, the land promised to Abraham (Gen 12:7; 17:8).
- **"The house of Jacob shall be a fire, and the house of Joseph a flame, and the house of Esau stubble" (v18).** "Jacob" and "Joseph" together mean the whole people, Judah and the northern tribes reunited (JFB: "the two kingdoms"). Stubble, the dry stalks left after harvest, burns in moments (Isa 5:24; Mal 4:1). The Tyndale notes add that the Babylonian king Nabonidus campaigned against Edom in about 553 BC.
- **"No survivor for the house of Esau" (v18).** History bore this out over the following centuries. Driven from their homeland, Edomites moved into southern Judah, which became known as Idumea. They were conquered and forcibly converted to Judaism by the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus around 125 BC. Herod the Great, who tried to kill the infant Jesus, was an Idumean. After the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70, the Edomites disappear from history as a people.
- **Commentators:** Guzik ties v15 to God's promise to Abraham: "him who dishonors you I will curse" (Gen 12:3). He traces Edom's later history and points to the Edomites' end in the Jewish revolt against Rome (AD 66–70) as the prophecy's fulfilment. JFB links the fire image to Isaiah 10:17, where "the light of Israel will become a fire." Henry looks past the history to the gospel: "Blessed be the Divine Saviour and Judge on Mount Zion!" The Tyndale notes stress that Edom's cliffs fail while the LORD's mountain stands as a true refuge.

#### The Kingdom of the LORD (vv19–21)
- **"Those of the Negeb shall possess Mount Esau" (v19).** The prophet looks to every point of the compass. The people of the Negev, the dry south, take Edom. Those in the Shephelah, the western foothills, take the Philistine coast. The north, Ephraim and Samaria, lost to Assyria in 722 BC, is restored. Benjamin spreads across the Jordan into Gilead. It is the promised land restored and enlarged.
- **"As far as Zarephath" (v20).** Zarephath was a Phoenician town between Tyre and Sidon, where Elijah stayed with the widow (1 Kings 17:9). The exiles of the northern kingdom would return and settle all the way up the coast.
- **"The exiles of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad" (v20).** Sepharad's location is uncertain. Most scholars now think it is Sardis in Asia Minor (western Turkey), whose name appears as *Sparda* in Persian and *SPRD* in an Aramaic inscription. Later Jewish tradition took it to be Spain, which is why Jews of Spanish descent are called Sephardic. JFB, following Jerome, mentions the Bosphorus. Wherever it was, the point is that even the most distant exiles will come home.
- **"Saviors shall go up to Mount Zion to rule Mount Esau" (v21).** "Saviors" (*moshi'im*) means deliverers, the word used for the judges God raised up to rescue Israel (Judg 3:9, 15; Neh 9:27). They are not rivals to God but his agents. The two mountains, Esau and Zion, face each other for the last time, and Zion rules.
- **"And the kingdom shall be the LORD's" (v21).** The book's last line lifts everything higher than land and borders. This is not finally about Israel defeating Edom. It is about God's reign over all the earth (Ps 22:28; Zech 14:9; Rev 11:15). God will be king.
- **Edom in the rest of Scripture.** Amos 9:11–12 says that when David's fallen tent is rebuilt, Israel will "possess the remnant of Edom and all the nations who are called by my name." When the apostles met in Jerusalem, James quoted that passage from the Greek translation, which reads "the remnant of mankind" (Acts 15:16–17), to show that Gentiles were welcome in the church. The name "Edom" and the Hebrew for "mankind" (*adam*) look alike in Hebrew. The prophecy that Zion would rule Edom came to mean Gentiles brought under Christ's gracious rule.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the end of the book through the gospel: "When Christ is come, and not till then, shall the kingdom be the Lord's in the full sense of the term." JFB likewise takes the kingdom to be "under Messiah," citing Daniel 2:44 and Revelation 11:15, and it takes the "saviours" to be leaders like the old judges. Guzik explains that "saviors" means deliverers, not rival saviors to God, and says the purpose of the book is to comfort God's suffering people that he will act on their behalf. The Tyndale notes see the scene as partly fulfilled in Israel's history and pointing on to the end of time.

#### Pointing to Christ
Obadiah is about two brothers and two mountains. Esau's mountain is proud, rocky and high, and it falls. Zion's mountain is a place of refuge where the escaped find safety, and it stands. The book ends with deliverers on Zion and the kingdom belonging to the LORD. Jesus is the greater Deliverer who went up to Zion. There he did not take revenge on the brothers who betrayed him. He was handed over, like the fugitives at the crossroads, and he died for his enemies. Through him, people from "the remnant of Edom" and every nation are brought into the kingdom (Acts 15:17). Pride says "who will bring me down?" The Son of God came down, of his own will, to raise up the humble (Luke 1:52).
