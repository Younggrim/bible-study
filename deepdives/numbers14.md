*Setting:* Kadesh, on the southern edge of Canaan, about a year and a half after the exodus. The twelve spies have just come back after forty days in the land. All of them agreed it was rich, "flowing with milk and honey," and ten of them said it could not be taken (13:27–33). Only Caleb spoke up for going in. This chapter is what happened that night and the next morning. It is the darkest moment in Numbers, the one later Scripture keeps returning to (Deut 1:19–46; Ps 95:8–11; 106:24–27; 1 Cor 10:5–11; Heb 3:7–4:11). A generation that had walked out of Egypt through the sea turns back at the border, and is sentenced to die in the desert.

#### The People Rebel (vv1–12)
- **"All the congregation raised a loud cry, and the people wept that night" (v1).** They had wept for meat in 11:4–10. Now they weep in despair over the land itself. Jewish tradition later linked this night of weeping to the ninth of Av, the date on which both temples were later destroyed, a way of saying this was the night Israel's sorrows began. That is tradition, not the text, but it shows how seriously Israel remembered it.
- **"Would that we had died in the land of Egypt! Or… in this wilderness!" (v2).** Keep this sentence in mind. God will take them at their word (v28–29).
- **"Our wives and our little ones will become a prey" (v3).** In ancient warfare, women and children were taken as plunder, so the fear was not imaginary. But they blame God: "Why is the LORD bringing us into this land, to fall by the sword?" They speak as if God had brought them out of Egypt to kill them. The children they claim to be protecting are the very ones who will enter the land (v31).
- **"Let us choose a leader and go back to Egypt" (v4).** This is a complete reversal of the exodus. Back to the land of slavery, without Moses, without God's presence. Nehemiah 9:17 remembers that "they… appointed a leader to return to their slavery in Egypt," which suggests the plan got as far as naming someone.
- **"Moses and Aaron fell on their faces" (v5).** In front of the whole assembly. It is the posture of a suppliant, whether pleading with the people or, more likely, crying out to God. They have no other answer to give.
- **Joshua and Caleb "tore their clothes" (v6).** Tearing clothes was a sign of deep grief or horror, often at blasphemy (Matt 26:65). Joshua now stands with Caleb, who had spoken alone in 13:30.
- **"An exceedingly good land" (v7).** The Hebrew doubles the word: "very, very good." It echoes God's verdict on creation (Gen 1:31). The land is a gift, not a trap.
- **"If the LORD delights in us, he will bring us into this land" (v8).** Their confidence does not rest on Israel's army. It rests on God's favor. The only thing that can undo it is the people's own rebellion: **"Only do not rebel against the LORD"** (v9).
- **"They are bread for us" (v9).** That is, we will eat them up. They turn the spies' own phrase around. The ten said the land "devours its inhabitants" (13:32). Joshua and Caleb say it is the Canaanites who will be devoured.
- **"Their protection is removed from them" (v9).** "Protection" is *tsel*, literally "shadow" or "shade," a common picture of protection in a hot land (Ps 91:1; 121:5). The Canaanites' shelter is gone, because God had been patiently waiting until their sin was "complete" (Gen 15:16), and that time had come. **"The LORD is with us"** is the whole difference.
- **"All the congregation said to stone them with stones" (v10).** Truth spoken in faith makes the crowd more violent. Before they can act, **"the glory of the LORD appeared at the tent of meeting to all the people."** God steps in visibly, in the bright cloud of his presence (Exod 40:34), and the stones never fly.
- **"How long will this people despise me?" (v11).** "Despise" is *na'ats*, to spurn or treat with contempt. It is a strong word, used of people who reject God's counsel (Prov 1:30) and of those who blaspheme (Ps 10:3). God names the root sin: **"how long will they not believe in me, in spite of all the signs."** The problem was not the giants. It was unbelief, after the plagues, the sea, the manna and the water from the rock.
- **"I will make of you a nation greater and mightier than they" (v12).** This is the same offer God made at the golden calf (Exod 32:10). He would wipe the slate clean and start again with Moses, as he once started with Abraham. The question is what Moses will do with the offer.
- **Commentators:** Guzik identifies the roots of the weeping as resentment that conquest would not be easy, blaming God, and letting fear overrule faith, and he notes how quickly unbelief spread through the whole camp. He treats the plan to go back to Egypt as a rejection of the whole work of redemption. Henry calls the chapter "that fatal quarrel between God and Israel." JFB explains "their defence is departed" as "the favor of God was now lost to those whose iniquities were full," and says God's glory appeared "seasonably… on this great emergency to rescue His ambassadors." JFB reads v12 as "not a final decree, but a threatening," which fits Moses' response.

#### Moses Intercedes for the People (vv13–19)
- **Moses refuses the offer.** For the second time, he turns down the chance to become the father of a new nation (Exod 32:10–13). He does not argue that Israel deserves mercy. He never mentions their merits at all.
- **"Then the Egyptians will hear of it" (vv13–16).** His first argument is God's own name. The nations knew that the LORD was with Israel: **"you, O LORD, are seen face to face, and your cloud stands over them"** (v14). If Israel dies in the desert, they will conclude **"the LORD was not able to bring this people into the land"** (v16). Moses asks God to act so that his reputation in the world is not damaged. Rahab later shows that the nations really were watching (Josh 2:9–11).
- **"Please let the power of the Lord be great as you have promised" (v17).** Moses' second argument is God's own word. Here, God's "power" is shown not in destroying but in forgiving.
- **"The LORD is slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" (v18).** Moses quotes almost word for word what God proclaimed about himself on Sinai after the golden calf (Exod 34:6–7). "Steadfast love" is *hesed*, God's loyal, covenant love that keeps going when it has every reason to stop. This self-description is one of the most often repeated passages in the Old Testament (Neh 9:17; Ps 86:15; 103:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2).
- **"But he will by no means clear the guilty" (v18).** Moses includes this line too. He is not asking God to pretend sin did not happen. He is asking for pardon that is consistent with justice. **"Visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children"** describes how the effects of sin pass down through a family. In a moment, Israel's children will "suffer for your faithlessness" (v33), though they will not be condemned for it.
- **"Please pardon… just as you have forgiven this people, from Egypt until now" (v19).** His final argument is God's past mercy. God had forgiven them again and again, so Moses asks him to do it once more.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls Moses' refusal of God's offer a sign of his greatness, and stresses that Moses simply asks God to act according to his revealed character. Henry sums up the prayer: "The prayer of his petition is, in one word, Pardon." The Tyndale notes point out that Moses used the same argument at the golden calf (Exod 32:11–14), and that he knew God would not let the guilty go unpunished.

#### God Promises Judgment (vv20–38)
- **"I have pardoned, according to your word" (v20).** The prayer is answered. Moses' intercession actually changes what happens. The nation will not be destroyed.
- **"But truly, as I live, and as all the earth shall be filled with the glory of the LORD" (v21).** Pardon is not the end of the matter. God swears an oath by his own life. Moses had worried about God's glory among the nations, and God answers that his glory will fill the whole earth (Hab 2:14; Isa 11:9). It will do so through a people who will learn that he keeps his word in both mercy and judgment.
- **"Put me to the test these ten times" (v22).** Ten may be a round number meaning "over and over" (Gen 31:7; Job 19:3). Jewish tradition actually counted ten specific rebellions from the Red Sea to Kadesh (Exod 14:11–12; 15:24; 16:2–3, 20, 27; 17:2–3; 32:1–6; Num 11:1, 4; 14:2). Either way, Kadesh was not a single slip. It was the last of a pattern.
- **"Shall see the land that I swore to give to their fathers" (v23).** The adults who rejected the land will not see it. Forgiveness spared the nation but did not cancel the consequence. That is a hard truth that runs through Scripture: David was forgiven, but the child died (2 Sam 12:13–14).
- **"My servant Caleb, because he has a different spirit and has followed me fully" (v24).** "Followed me fully" is literally "filled after me," a picture of wholehearted, complete loyalty. Caleb's "different spirit" is not a personality type but a heart that took God at his word. Forty-five years later, at eighty-five, he will claim Hebron, the very place where the giants lived (Josh 14:6–14). Joshua is not named here but is named alongside him in v30 and v38.
- **"Turn tomorrow and set out for the wilderness by the way to the Red Sea" (v25).** The way to Canaan is now closed. They are to turn south and east, toward the Gulf of Aqaba. "Red Sea" here is the Hebrew *yam suph*, "sea of reeds," which can name either gulf on each side of the Sinai peninsula. The note that the Amalekites and Canaanites live in the valleys looks ahead to the disaster of vv40–45.

- **"How long shall this wicked congregation grumble against me?" (v27).** Their complaints were directed at Moses and Aaron (v2), but God says they were really against him. The New Testament draws the same line: grumbling against God's leaders can be grumbling against God (1 Cor 10:10).
- **"What you have said in my hearing I will do to you" (v28).** This is the sharpest irony in the chapter. They had said, "Would that we had died in this wilderness" (v2). Their wish is granted. **"Your dead bodies shall fall in this wilderness"** (v29).
- **"From twenty years old and upward" (v29).** This is the military census of chapter 1, the men who were counted as able to go to war, and who refused to. The tribe of Levi was not counted in that census (1:47–49), and some think that is why Eleazar the priest, Aaron's son, is still alive at the conquest (Josh 14:1).
- **"Your little ones, who you said would become a prey, I will bring in" (v31).** The children their parents used as an excuse for disobedience become the heirs of the promise. Deuteronomy 1:39 adds that these children "have no knowledge of good or evil" that day. God holds the adults responsible.
- **"Your children shall be shepherds in the wilderness forty years and shall suffer for your faithlessness" (v33).** The children are not condemned, but they share the consequences, wandering with their parents until the last of them dies. "Faithlessness" is literally "your harlotries," Scripture's picture for unfaithfulness to God as a betrayal of a marriage covenant.
- **"Forty days, a year for each day" (v34).** Forty years includes the year and a half already spent, so about thirty-eight more remain (Deut 2:14). Every day the spies spent looking at the land becomes a year of not entering it. **"You shall know my displeasure"** is a rare Hebrew word that may mean "my opposition" or "my turning away," what it is like to have God against you.
- **"The men… who… brought up a bad report… died by plague before the LORD" (vv36–37).** The ten spies did not wait thirty-eight years. Their judgment came at once. Leaders who lead others astray carry heavier responsibility (Jas 3:1).
- **"Only Joshua… and Caleb… remained alive" (v38).** The same plague, in the same camp, passed over two men. Of the whole generation that left Egypt as adults, they alone will enter Canaan. Even Moses and Aaron will fall short, for their own failure later at Meribah (20:12).
- **Commentators:** Guzik insists that prayer truly matters here: God's answer came "according to your word." Henry notes that God's mercy made the sentence lighter, but it still stood: "they shall not all be cut off," yet the decree is confirmed by an oath. JFB says "ten times" means "very frequently," and applies the filling of the earth with God's glory, in its full sense, to the future spread of the gospel. JFB explains that Joshua was not named in v24 because he was serving with Moses and no longer counted "in the ranks of the people." Guzik links this sentence to Psalm 95 and Hebrews 3–4, which use it to warn believers against hardening their hearts, and notes that even Moses would not enter. He points to the ten spies' quick death as a warning to anyone who discourages others from trusting God. Henry closes his reading of the chapter with the warning of Hebrews 4:11 not to "fall after the same example of unbelief." JFB notes that some of the older generation, such as the priests, did not join in the murmuring, and reads "you shall know my displeasure" as "my breach of promise," meaning God withholds the blessings of a covenant they had broken. The Tyndale notes put it simply: their disobedience brought on them the very thing they feared would come from obeying.

#### Israel Defeated in Battle (vv39–45)
- **"The people mourned greatly" (v39).** The news of the sentence breaks them. But grief over consequences is not the same as repentance.
- **"Here we are. We will go up… for we have sinned" (v40).** Yesterday they would not go up when God said go. Today they insist on going when God has said don't. They say the right words, "we have sinned," but the action shows they still mean to do what they want. True repentance accepts God's word, including his "no."
- **"Why now are you transgressing the command of the LORD, when that will not succeed?" (v41).** Going forward was now as much rebellion as going back had been. The command had changed (v25), and obedience means following God's word as it is now, not as it was yesterday.
- **"The LORD is not among you" (v42).** This was the one thing Joshua and Caleb had counted on: "the LORD is with us" (v9). Without it, the plan is just a military gamble against a stronger enemy.
- **"Neither the ark of the covenant of the LORD nor Moses departed out of the camp" (v44).** The ark, the throne of God's presence, normally went before the army (10:33–35). Here it stays behind. Israel will learn a similar lesson the opposite way at Aphek, when they carry the ark into battle like a good-luck charm and lose it (1 Sam 4:3–11).
- **"Defeated them and pursued them, even to Hormah" (v45).** **Hormah** means "destruction" or "devoted to destruction." Deuteronomy 1:44 says the Amorites chased them "as bees do." Later, in faith, Israel will defeat the Canaanites at this same place (21:1–3), so the name of their defeat becomes the name of their victory.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls the people's confession shallow: sorry for the loss, not for the sin. He also points out that the time to act in faith had passed, and that regret could not reopen it. Henry's outline names this as the rebuke of those who "try to advance anyway." JFB says their mourning did not last and their rash march showed continued rebellion, and he notes that Hormah took its name from the slaughter. The Tyndale notes say Moses' words in v43 sum up the whole chapter: because they had abandoned the LORD, the LORD would not be with them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses stood between a guilty people and a holy God, refused glory for himself, and pleaded God's own name and steadfast love, and God pardoned "according to your word." Jesus is the greater mediator who does not merely plead but bears the judgment himself (1 Tim 2:5; Heb 7:25). Hebrews takes this chapter as a direct warning: "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts," for they "were unable to enter because of unbelief" (Heb 3:15, 19). A rest is still offered, entered not by an army climbing a hill but by trusting the one whose name, like Joshua's, means "the LORD saves" (Heb 4:1–10).
