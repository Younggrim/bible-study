*Setting:* The story steps back in time. Chapter 28 ended with Saul at En-dor on the night before the battle, with the Philistines already camped at Shunem in the Jezreel Valley (28:4). This chapter returns to David at an earlier point, when the Philistine armies were first mustering at Aphek, far to the south. Achish has told David he must march with him against Israel and has made him his bodyguard (28:1–2). David has been living a double life in Philistine territory for sixteen months, lying to Achish about his raids (27:7–12). Now his lies have walked him into an impossible position. If he fights Israel, he can never be Israel's king. If he turns on the Philistines, he betrays the man who sheltered him. God gets him out of it, through the suspicion of Philistine commanders.

#### The Philistines Reject David (vv1–11)
- **"The Philistines had gathered all their forces at Aphek" (v1).** Aphek was a town on the coastal plain, at the source of the Yarkon River east of modern Tel Aviv, where the main road north began. It was a natural assembly point. It was also where the Philistines had gathered at the start of the book, when they defeated Israel and captured the ark (4:1). From here the army would march north about forty miles to the Jezreel Valley. JFB places this Aphek in the north near Jezreel instead, but most modern scholars identify it with the coastal site.
- **"The Israelites were encamped by the spring that is in Jezreel" (v1).** This is the spring at the foot of Mount Gilboa, probably the spring of Harod where Gideon once tested his men by how they drank (Judg 7:1). Saul's army was on the slopes above it (28:4; 31:1).
- **The order of events.** Chapter 29 happens before chapter 28. The Philistines muster at Aphek (29:1), send David home, and move north to Jezreel (29:11) and Shunem (28:4). Saul visits the medium the night before the battle. Meanwhile David is marching south to Ziklag, a three-day journey (30:1). The narrator arranges the scenes to set the two kings side by side. While Saul is at En-dor hearing his doom, David is being sent away from the battle that will kill Saul, and toward the crisis that will bring him back to God.
- **"The lords of the Philistines were passing on by hundreds and by thousands" (v2).** The Philistines were led by five "lords" (*seranim*), the rulers of their five main cities: Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Gath and Ekron (6:17). They marched out in units, as in a review. David and his men came at the rear, with Achish, as his personal guard.
- **"What are these Hebrews doing here?" (v3).** "Hebrews" is what foreigners called the Israelites (4:6, 9; 14:11). The commanders see six hundred Israelite soldiers marching in their army against Israel and rightly ask what they are doing.
- **"I have found no fault in him to this day" (v3).** Achish's defense is warm and sincere, and completely mistaken. David had been lying to him the whole time (27:10–12). The irony is painful. The Philistine king thinks David has become one of them. The Tyndale notes point out that he defends David three times (vv3, 6, 9).
- **"Lest in the battle he become an adversary to us" (v4).** "Adversary" is the Hebrew *satan*, used here in its ordinary sense of an opponent who turns against you. The commanders had seen this happen before. In Saul's early battle at Michmash, "Hebrews" who had been with the Philistines switched sides in the middle of the fight (14:21).
- **"How could this fellow reconcile himself to his lord? Would it not be with the heads of the men here?" (v4).** They suspect David would win back Saul's favor by bringing him Philistine heads. Their fear was not unreasonable. David had once carried Goliath's head to Saul (17:54, 57) and brought him two hundred Philistine foreskins (18:27).
- **"Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands" (v5).** This is the third time the song appears (18:7; 21:11). In Gath it nearly got David killed. At Aphek it gets him sent home. The song that made Saul jealous now saves David from fighting Saul.
- **"As the LORD lives, you have been honest" (v6).** A Philistine king swears by the God of Israel. He may be speaking David's language out of courtesy. "To me it seems right that you should march out and in with me." He would gladly have kept David. "The lords do not approve of you."
- **"But what have I done?" (v8).** David protests. Is he sincere, or putting on a show? The question has been asked for centuries, and the text does not say. He asks to "fight against the enemies of my lord the king." Achish hears "my lord the king" as Achish himself. But "my lord the king" is exactly what David called Saul (24:8; 26:17). Many readers think David chose ambiguous words on purpose. Whatever was in his heart, he is now arguing for the right to go to a battle against his own people.
- **"As blameless in my sight as an angel of God" (v9).** Achish uses a high compliment. Later, Israelites will say the same of David as king (2 Sam 14:17, 20; 19:27). Still, the commanders' decision stands.
- **"Rise early in the morning… and depart as soon as you have light" (v10).** "Your lord" here probably means Saul, since David's men had once been Saul's subjects. Achish wants David gone before the army moves.
- **"So David set out with his men early in the morning" (v11).** He goes south to Ziklag, and the Philistines go north to Jezreel. The battle that will end Saul's life goes on without him. David will not be anywhere near Gilboa, and no one will ever be able to say he raised his hand against the LORD's anointed or fought against Israel.
- **God's hand behind it all.** God is not named in this chapter except in Achish's oath. Yet the reader sees his care everywhere. David trapped himself by his own choices (27:1). God freed him, not through a prophet or a miracle, but through the suspicion of pagan generals. He kept David from a sin David seemed willing to commit, and kept his future throne from being stained by Israelite blood.
- **Commentators:** Henry is sharp: "It is hard to come near to the brink of sin, and not to fall in," and "David scarcely ever had a greater deliverance than when dismissed from such insnaring service." He credits God, who "inclined the princes of the Philistines to oppose David's being employed in the battle." JFB calls the outcome "a happy circumstance in the overruling providence of God" and adds that "David is not free from censure." It also notes that the Philistine government "had constitutional checks," since the king had to yield to the lords. Guzik observes that the pagan commanders saw clearly what David had stopped seeing, that he did not belong in an army marching against God's people. He finds it sad that Achish could vouch for David as one of his own. He suggests that Achish's oath by the LORD is courtesy rather than faith, and urges gratitude when God keeps us back from a sin we were ready to commit.

#### Pointing to Christ
David is rescued not by his own wisdom but by God's quiet providence, working even through people who do not know him. Scripture shows this again and again: "the king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will" (Prov 21:1). It reaches its height at the cross, where Herod, Pilate and the rulers did "whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place" (Acts 4:27–28). David was kept from fighting his own people. Jesus, David's greater Son, went willingly to suffer at the hands of his own people and of the Gentiles, so that both could be brought to God. And where David needed rescue from a trap of his own making, Jesus "was tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin" (Heb 4:15), and now "is able to help those who are being tempted" (Heb 2:18).
