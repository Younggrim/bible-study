*Setting:* The plains of Moab by the Jordan, opposite Jericho, as Moses' life draws to its close. Chapter 34 drew the borders of Canaan and named the men who would divide it among the tribes. But one tribe was left out of the division on purpose. The Levites, set apart to serve at the tabernacle, were to have no territory of their own, because "I am your portion and your inheritance" (18:20, 23–24). So how would they live, and where? This chapter answers that, and then turns to one of the most humane institutions in the Old Testament law: the cities of refuge, where a person who had killed someone by accident could run for safety.

#### Cities for the Levites (vv1–8)
- **"Give to the Levites… cities for them to dwell in" (v2).** The Levites receive towns to live in and pasture around them for their animals, but no tribal region. In the wilderness they camped around the tabernacle at the center of Israel (ch 2). In the land, they are scattered among all the tribes, so that every part of Israel has teachers of God's law close by (Deut 33:10; 2 Chr 17:8–9).
- **"A thousand cubits… two thousand cubits" (vv4–5).** A cubit was about 18 inches, so a thousand cubits is about 1,500 feet and two thousand about 3,000 feet. The two numbers have puzzled readers for centuries. One common explanation: verse 4 measures outward from the city wall, while verse 5 gives the length of each side of the square of land, with the city in the middle. Another is that there was an inner ring for gardens and an outer ring for grazing. Either way the point is clear: the Levites had a modest, fixed amount of land, enough to keep animals but not to build estates.
- **The Sabbath day's journey.** Later Jewish teachers used the 2,000 cubits of v5 as the limit for how far one could walk on the Sabbath. That is the "Sabbath day's journey" of Acts 1:12, the distance from the Mount of Olives to Jerusalem.
- **"Forty-eight" cities (vv6–7).** Six of these were cities of refuge and forty-two were ordinary Levite towns. Joshua 21 lists them all, scattered through every tribe. Most were small, and the Levites lived alongside the other Israelites in them.
- **"From the larger tribes you shall take many, and from the smaller tribes… few" (v8).** Each tribe gives in proportion to what it receives, the same principle Paul uses for giving: "according to what a person has, not according to what he does not have" (2 Cor 8:12).
- **The curse turned to blessing.** Jacob had said of Simeon and Levi, "I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel" (Gen 49:7), a judgment for their violence at Shechem (Gen 34). Levi was indeed scattered, but because the tribe stood with Moses at the golden calf (Exod 32:26–29), the scattering became a ministry. They were spread out as God's servants throughout the land.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v4 as an inner zone of 1,000 cubits for outbuildings, gardens and vineyards, and v5 as a further "common for the pasturing of cattle," so that the two verses "measure different things." Guzik makes the Levites a picture of believers today, spread throughout the world as salt and light rather than gathered in one Christian enclave.

#### Cities of Refuge (vv9–34)
- **"The manslayer who kills any person without intent" (v11).** The word for "without intent" (*bishgagah*) is the same word used for "unintentional" sins in 15:22–29. The law cares not only about what happened but why. A man who killed without meaning to was not a murderer, and the law treated him differently.
- **"A refuge from the avenger" (v12).** The **avenger of blood** (*go'el ha-dam*) was the dead person's nearest male relative. The same word *go'el* means "redeemer" elsewhere: the kinsman who buys back a relative's land or frees him from slavery (Lev 25:25, 48), as Boaz does for Ruth (Ruth 3:12–13). His duty was to protect his family, and when one of them was killed, that meant seeking justice on the killer (Gen 9:6). Blood feuds like this existed across the ancient world. God's law did not invent the avenger but put limits on him, so that a hot-tempered relative could not kill someone innocent of murder (Deut 19:6).
- **"Until he stands before the congregation for judgment" (v12).** The refuge was not a hiding place from justice. It bought time for a fair trial. The community, usually through the elders, would hear the case and decide.
- **"Three cities beyond the Jordan, and three… in the land of Canaan" (v14).** Joshua 20:7–8 names them: Kedesh in Galilee, Shechem in the central hills and Hebron in the south, west of the river; Bezer, Ramoth-gilead and Golan to the east. They were spread out so that no one in Israel was more than a day or so from one. Deuteronomy 19:3 adds that the roads to them were to be kept in good order. Jewish tradition later says signposts marked "Refuge" stood at the crossroads.
- **"For the stranger and for the sojourner" (v15).** Foreigners living in Israel were protected by the same law. There was one standard of justice for everyone (Lev 24:22).
- **Murder by weapon (vv16–18).** Iron, stone or wood, any object "that could cause death," used to kill, makes the killer a murderer. The Hebrew word for murderer here (*rotseach*) comes from the same verb as the sixth commandment, "You shall not murder" (Exod 20:13). Using a deadly weapon counts as evidence of intent.
- **"The avenger of blood shall himself put the murderer to death" (vv19, 21).** After a guilty verdict, the family's avenger carries out the sentence. Ancient Israel had no police force or state executioner. Justice was carried out within the community under the elders' oversight.
- **Murder by intent (vv20–21).** Pushing "out of hatred," throwing something while "lying in wait," striking "in enmity": these words point to the heart. Malice, planning and hatred make a killing murder even without a weapon.
- **Manslaughter (vv22–23).** "Pushed him suddenly without enmity… without seeing him dropped it on him… though he was not his enemy." Deuteronomy 19:5 gives the classic example: two men cutting wood, and an axe head flies off the handle and kills one. The law sees the difference between a tragedy and a crime.
- **"The congregation shall judge… rescue the manslayer… restore him to his city of refuge" (vv24–25).** If the hearing finds the death accidental, the community protects the man from the avenger and sends him back to the city of refuge. He is not executed, but he is not simply sent home either.
- **"He shall live in it until the death of the high priest" (v25).** This is the most striking detail of the law. The manslayer is free to go home only when the high priest dies. Why? A human life had still been lost, and "blood pollutes the land" (v33). Most interpreters think the high priest's death served as a kind of atonement for the blood shed, closing the matter for the whole nation. His death marked the end of an era, and with it the manslayer's guilt was considered settled. The phrase "anointed with the holy oil" underlines his role as the people's representative before God.
- **"If the manslayer shall at any time go beyond the boundaries" (vv26–27).** The refuge only protected those inside it. If the manslayer wandered out, the avenger could kill him without guilt. Safety was found by staying within the walls.
- **"After the death of the high priest the manslayer may return" (v28).** The exile ends, and he can go home to his family and land. The avenger's claim is cancelled.
- **"On the evidence of witnesses… not… on the testimony of one witness" (v30).** No one could be executed on one person's word. Deuteronomy 17:6 and 19:15 repeat the rule, and Jesus and Paul both cite it (Matt 18:16; 2 Cor 13:1). It protected the accused against false charges and personal grudges.
- **"You shall accept no ransom for the life of a murderer" (v31).** In many ancient law codes a killer could pay the victim's family and walk free. Israel's law refused that. A human being made in God's image could not be paid for with money (Gen 9:6). Interestingly, the law allowed ransom in other cases, such as the owner of an ox that gored someone (Exod 21:29–30), but never for deliberate murder.
- **"No ransom for him who has fled to his city of refuge" (v32).** Even the manslayer could not buy his way home early. Wealth did not shorten the sentence.
- **"Blood pollutes the land… no atonement… except by the blood of the one who shed it" (v33).** The word "pollute" (*chanaph*) means to profane or make godless. Unpunished bloodshed infects the land itself, as Abel's blood "cried out" from the ground (Gen 4:10). The only cleansing for murder is justice.
- **"For I the LORD dwell in the midst of the people of Israel" (v34).** This is the reason behind the whole chapter. The land is holy because God lives in it among his people. Injustice there is not just a crime against a neighbor; it is an offense in God's own house.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the high priest "so great a blessing to his country" that his death brought release to the exiles. He stresses that the manslayer's safety "was not owing to the strength of its walls" but to God's appointment, and says the gospel's way of salvation is "couched under the type and figure of the cities of refuge." He also notes that blood defiles not only the murderer's conscience but the land itself. JFB explains the *go'el* as the closest relative, a very ancient custom open to abuse by hasty avengers, and links the release at the high priest's death to a time of national mourning. It notes the law gave no "possibility of deliverance by any pecuniary satisfaction." Guzik notes the word for "accidentally" is the same as "unintentional" in chapter 15, which the Hebrew supports, and that *go'el* is often translated "redeemer." His main application is fleeing to Christ as refuge. He points out one important difference: the cities protected only the innocent, while Christ welcomes the guilty who come to him. He also lists parallels: the refuge was near, open to all, and protected only those who stayed inside. JFB has no notes on vv9–15 individually.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews picks up this chapter's language when it speaks of "we who have fled for refuge" laying hold of the hope set before us (Heb 6:18). The parallels are hard to miss: a refuge near at hand, open to Israelite and foreigner alike, safe only for those who stay within it. But the gospel goes further than the law. The cities sheltered only the innocent, while Christ shelters the guilty. And the manslayer went free when the high priest died. Jesus is the High Priest whose death sets his people free for good, and who, unlike Aaron's sons, "always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:24–25). Where Abel's blood cried out for justice, Christ's blood "speaks a better word" (Heb 12:24): it cleanses what no other blood could.
