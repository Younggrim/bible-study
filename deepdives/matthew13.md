*Setting:* Capernaum and the shore of the Sea of Galilee, around AD 29, "that same day" as the clash with the Pharisees in chapter 12. The leaders have accused Jesus of working by Satan's power, his family has come to take him home, and he has said his true family is whoever does his Father's will. Now he teaches in a new way. This is the third of Matthew's five great teaching blocks, a collection of seven or eight parables about the kingdom of heaven. A parable (*parabolē*, "something set alongside") is a story or picture from everyday life set beside a spiritual truth. The parables answer questions that chapter 12 raised: if the kingdom has come, why do so many reject it, why are evil people still here, and why does it look so small? The chapter ends with Jesus rejected in his hometown, a living example of the hard soil.

#### The Parable of the Sower (vv1–9)
- **"He got into a boat and sat down" (v2).** Teachers sat to teach (5:1). The boat gave Jesus room from the crush of the crowd, and the water carried his voice to the people standing on the sloping beach. A cove near Capernaum, sometimes called the Bay of Parables, forms a natural amphitheater.
- **"A sower went out to sow" (v3).** Farmers in Galilee often scattered seed by hand and then plowed it in. Seed fell wherever it fell.
- **The path, rocky ground, thorns (vv4–7).** Footpaths ran through and alongside fields, and the soil there was trampled hard. "Rocky ground" was not soil full of stones but a thin layer of soil over limestone bedrock, common in Galilee. Seed sprouted quickly in the warm, shallow earth and died just as quickly. Thorn roots lay hidden in the soil and grew back faster than the grain.
- **"Some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty" (v8).** An ordinary harvest might yield something like seven to ten times what was sown. Isaac's hundredfold was a mark of God's blessing (Gen 26:12). The good soil yields a harvest beyond expectation.
- **"He who has ears, let him hear" (v9).** Everyone has ears. Not everyone truly listens.
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that the sower and the seed are the same in every case. The difference is the soil, so the parable calls each hearer to ask what kind of soil they are. JFB refers to its notes on Mark for this parable, reading it as showing that the word's effect depends on the state of the heart.

#### The Purpose of the Parables (vv10–17)
- **"Why do you speak to them in parables?" (v10).** Jesus had used picture language before (5:13–16; 7:24–27), but here he begins teaching the crowds mainly in parables. JFB notes that this follows the leaders' charge that his works were Satan's (12:24).
- **"To you it has been given to know the secrets of the kingdom of heaven" (v11).** "Secrets" (*mystēria*) does not mean puzzles for the clever. In the Bible a "mystery" is something God has kept hidden and now reveals (Dan 2:28; Rom 16:25–26). Here it means the surprising truth that the kingdom has come in Jesus, but not yet in its final, visible form. Understanding it is a gift: "it has been given."
- **"To the one who has, more will be given… from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away" (v12).** Those who receive the truth Jesus gives will receive more. Those who reject it lose even what they had. The same principle comes back in the parable of the talents (25:29).
- **"Seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not hear" (v13).** The crowd's blindness is not a lack of information. They have seen miracles and heard teaching.
- **"The prophecy of Isaiah is fulfilled" (vv14–15).** When Isaiah was called, God told him his preaching would make the people's hearts dull, because they had already turned away (Isa 6:9–10). The same pattern happens with Jesus. The New Testament quotes this passage several times to explain Israel's rejection of the gospel (John 12:40; Acts 28:26–27). Note the last line: "lest they should… turn, and I would heal them." God still wants to heal. Their refusal is the problem.
- **Parables both reveal and conceal.** To those who come to Jesus wanting to understand, parables make truth vivid and memorable. To those who have hardened themselves, they remain riddles. In that sense parables are both a judgment and a mercy: they do not force more truth on people who would only reject it, and they leave the door open for anyone who will ask.
- **"Many prophets and righteous people longed to see what you see" (v17).** Abraham, Moses, Isaiah and David looked forward to what the disciples now saw in front of them (1 Pet 1:10–12).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "A parable is a shell that keeps good fruit for the diligent, but keeps it from the slothful." JFB reads the hardening as God's just response to the people's own refusal: they "closed their eyes." Guzik likens parables to the sun, which melts wax and hardens clay: the same message softens one heart and hardens another.

#### The Parable of the Sower Explained (vv18–23)
- **"The word of the kingdom" (v19).** The seed is the message about the kingdom, the good news Jesus preaches.
- **The path: "does not understand it, the evil one comes and snatches away" (v19).** The birds are the devil. The word never gets in, because the heart is hard and does not take it in.
- **Rocky ground: "receives it with joy… has no root… when tribulation or persecution arises… immediately he falls away" (vv20–21).** A quick, happy response is not a guarantee of real faith. Faith without roots shows its shallowness when following Jesus costs something. "Falls away" (*skandalizō*) means to stumble and fall.
- **Thorns: "the cares of the world and the deceitfulness of riches choke the word" (v22).** Two opposite things choke faith: worry, which comes from having too little, and riches, which come from having plenty. Jesus warned about both in the Sermon on the Mount (6:19–34). Riches are "deceitful" because they promise security and satisfaction they cannot give.
- **Good soil: "hears the word and understands it" (v23).** In Matthew, understanding means taking the word in so it changes a life. The proof is fruit: obedience, character, and other people reached. The amount varies (a hundredfold, sixty, thirty), but every true hearer bears fruit.
- **What kind of soil?** Christians have long debated whether the rocky and thorny soils describe true believers who fall away, or people who were never truly converted. The parable's point is practical rather than technical. Only the good soil produces fruit, and that is the kind of hearing Jesus calls for.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives hope to every hearer: "Man's heart is like soil, capable of improvement, of bearing good fruit." The Tyndale notes connect the thorns to Matthew's repeated warnings about wealth. Guzik describes the four soils as hard, shallow, crowded and fruitful.

#### The Parable of the Weeds (vv24–30)
- **"His enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat" (v25).** The weed (*zizanion*) is almost certainly darnel, a grass that looks almost exactly like wheat until it heads out. Its seeds often carry a fungus that is poisonous. Sowing darnel in an enemy's field was a known act of spite. Roman law even had a penalty for it.
- **"While his men were sleeping" (v25).** This is not a rebuke for laziness. It simply means the enemy came at night, unseen.
- **"Do you want us to go and gather them?" (v28).** The servants want to fix the problem at once. But by the time the weeds can be told apart, their roots are tangled with the wheat's. Pulling one would tear up the other.
- **"Let both grow together until the harvest" (v30).** Separation will happen, but not yet, and not by the servants. At harvest the reapers will gather the weeds first and burn them, and gather the wheat into the barn.
- **Commentators:** Henry quotes the master's warning: "Nay, lest while ye gather up the tares, ye root up also the wheat with them." JFB stresses that separating them early would destroy wheat too.

#### The Mustard Seed and the Leaven (vv31–33)
- **"A grain of mustard seed… the smallest of all seeds" (vv31–32).** The black mustard seed was proverbially tiny in Jewish speech ("as small as a mustard seed"). Some seeds are smaller, but Jesus is speaking as his hearers did, about the smallest seed a Galilean farmer would sow. The plant can grow to ten feet or more in a season, big enough for birds to perch in.
- **"Becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches" (v32).** The words echo the great tree in Daniel 4:12 and the cedar in Ezekiel 17:23 and 31:6, pictures of a kingdom that shelters the nations. The kingdom began with a carpenter from Nazareth and twelve ordinary men. It will grow into something vast.
- **"Leaven that a woman took and hid in three measures of flour" (v33).** Leaven was a lump of old fermented dough kept back from the last batch and worked into the new. "Three measures" is about fifty pounds of flour, enough bread for perhaps a hundred people, the amount Sarah used to bake for the Lord's visit (Gen 18:6). A small lump, hidden, works through the whole mass.
- **Is the leaven good or bad?** Elsewhere in the Bible leaven often stands for corruption (16:6; 1 Cor 5:6–8), and some interpreters, including Guzik, read both parables negatively: the mustard tree is unnaturally large, the birds are the evil one (as in v19), and the leaven is false teaching spreading in the church. Most interpreters, including Henry, JFB and the Tyndale notes, read them positively. Jesus says "the kingdom of heaven is like" these things, and the point of both pairs is small beginnings and great results. A picture can mean different things in different settings, as a lion pictures both Satan (1 Pet 5:8) and Christ (Rev 5:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "A grain of mustard-seed is small, but however it is seed, and has in it a disposition to grow," and that leaven "works silently and insensibly." JFB calls it "the all-penetrating and assimilating quality of the Gospel." Guzik takes the minority view described above, seeing both parables as warnings about unhealthy growth and corruption in the visible church.

#### Prophecy and Parables (vv34–35)
- **"He said nothing to them without a parable" (v34).** This refers to this stage of his teaching to the crowds, not his whole ministry.
- **"I will open my mouth in parables" (v35).** The quote is Psalm 78:2. The psalm's title names Asaph, whom Scripture calls a seer (2 Chron 29:30), which explains "the prophet." Asaph told Israel's history as a "parable," drawing lessons from God's past dealings. Jesus fulfils that pattern: he brings out what "has been hidden since the foundation of the world."
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the psalm holds truths "not fully brought to light till the Gospel day."

#### The Parable of the Weeds Explained (vv36–43)
- **"He left the crowds and went into the house" (v36).** The explanation is private, for disciples who ask.
- **"The field is the world" (v38).** This is important. The field is not the church but the world, so the parable is first about why God allows the wicked to live alongside his people until the end. Still, the church has often applied it to itself too, as a warning against trying to make a perfectly pure church by force. Augustine used it against groups that split the church over the purity of its members.
- **"The enemy who sowed them is the devil" (v39).** Evil in the world does not come from the sower. "An enemy has done this" (v28).
- **"The Son of Man will send his angels, and they will gather out of his kingdom all causes of sin and all law-breakers" (v41).** Jesus calls it "his kingdom" and the angels "his angels." He is the Lord of the final harvest. "Law-breakers" is *anomia*, lawlessness, the word in 7:23.
- **"The fiery furnace… weeping and gnashing of teeth" (v42).** The language recalls Nebuchadnezzar's furnace (Dan 3:6). Jesus speaks of judgment in the plainest, most sobering terms.
- **"Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (v43).** This echoes Daniel 12:3: "those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky." The end is not only judgment but glory.
- **Commentators:** JFB warns that the parable should not be used to excuse leaving openly scandalous members in the church, since discipline is commanded elsewhere (18:15–17; 1 Cor 5). Guzik notes that some earlier interpreters read the field as the church, and holds that God, not the church, will do the final separating.

#### The Parable of the Hidden Treasure (v44)
- **"Treasure hidden in a field" (v44).** With no banks for ordinary people, and with armies passing through Palestine often, people buried valuables (25:25). Owners died or fled, and the treasure was forgotten. The Dead Sea Scrolls include a "Copper Scroll" listing hidden treasure sites.
- **"Covered up… buys that field" (v44).** Was this honest? Under Jewish custom, finders could in some cases keep what they found, and Jesus is not teaching ethics here. The point is the man's response.
- **"In his joy" (v44).** He sells everything, not grudgingly, but gladly. He knows he is getting far more than he gives. That is what finding the kingdom does.

#### The Parable of the Pearl of Great Value (vv45–46)
- **"A merchant in search of fine pearls" (v45).** Pearls came from the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf and India, and the finest were worth fortunes. This man is a dealer, looking for quality.
- **"Sold all that he had and bought it" (v46).** The two parables differ in one way: one man stumbles on treasure, the other searches for it. People come to the kingdom both ways. In both, the response is the same: everything for the one thing (Phil 3:7–8).
- **Who is the buyer?** Most interpreters see the buyer as the disciple who gives up everything for the kingdom. Some, including Guzik, see the buyer as Jesus, who gave everything to buy his people, since no one can buy salvation. Both are true teachings, but the setting, which is about how people respond to the kingdom, favors the first reading.
- **Commentators (vv44–46):** Henry reads the treasure Christ-centered: "Jesus Christ is the true Treasure," and "A true Christian is a spiritual merchant, that seeks and finds this pearl of price." JFB contrasts the accidental finder of v44 with a man whose business it was to seek. Guzik holds that the buyer is Christ and the treasure is the believer.

#### The Parable of the Net (vv47–50)
- **"A net that was thrown into the sea and gathered fish of every kind" (v47).** A dragnet was dragged between two boats or from the shore, catching everything. The fishermen among the disciples knew the work well. Under the food laws some fish were unclean (Lev 11:9–12), and they were thrown away.
- **"So it will be at the end of the age" (v49).** The point is the same as the weeds: mixed now, sorted later. The emphasis falls on the end: "the angels will come out and separate the evil from the righteous."
- **Commentators:** Henry says "Ministers are fishers of men, employed in casting and drawing this net." JFB treats the bad fish as parallel to the weeds. Guzik draws the point that the church will not turn the whole world into the kingdom before the end.

#### New and Old Treasures (vv51–52)
- **"Have you understood all these things?" "Yes" (v51).** Their answer is a bit too confident, given how often they misunderstand later (15:16; 16:8–11). But they have begun.
- **"Every scribe who has been trained for the kingdom of heaven" (v52).** "Trained" is literally "discipled." A scribe was an expert in the Scriptures. Jesus pictures a new kind of scribe who knows both the old (the Law and the Prophets) and the new (the kingdom Jesus brings), and brings out both like a homeowner bringing treasures from a storeroom. Many think Matthew, a careful record keeper, saw himself here. His Gospel does exactly this, showing again and again how Jesus fulfils the Old Testament.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "Those who are to instruct others, have need to be well instructed themselves." JFB calls it bringing out "old truths in ever new forms, aspects, applications."

#### Jesus Rejected at Nazareth (vv53–58)
- **"His hometown" (v54).** Nazareth, a small village in the hills about twenty miles southwest of Capernaum, where Jesus grew up.
- **"Where did this man get this wisdom and these mighty works?" (v54).** They do not deny his wisdom or his miracles. They cannot fit them with the man they knew.
- **"Is not this the carpenter's son?" (v55).** "Carpenter" (*tektōn*) means a builder or craftsman in wood or stone. Mark says "Is not this the carpenter?" (Mark 6:3), so Jesus himself had worked the trade. Nearby Sepphoris was being rebuilt in his youth, and some suggest he and Joseph worked there, though that is a guess.
- **"His brothers James and Joseph and Simon and Judas… all his sisters" (vv55–56).** See the note on 12:46–50 for the main views. James later led the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13; Gal 1:19), and Judas is the Jude who wrote the letter.
- **"They took offense at him" (v57).** The same word as v21, *skandalizō*. They stumbled over his ordinariness.
- **"A prophet is not without honor except in his hometown" (v57).** It became a proverb. Familiarity can make people blind.
- **"He did not do many mighty works there, because of their unbelief" (v58).** Mark says "he could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people" (Mark 6:5). It is not that Jesus' power ran out. Miracles were signs meant to call for faith, and where people refused to believe, he did not put on a display.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums it up: "Familiarity breeds contempt." JFB calls the question of the brothers and sisters "exceedingly difficult" and leaves it open. Guzik takes them as Mary's later children, the plain reading in his view. He also says unbelief limited what Jesus did there, and quotes the Puritan John Trapp on how poisonous unbelief is.

#### Pointing to Christ
The parables are about the kingdom, and the kingdom is about the King. Jesus is the sower scattering the word, the Son of Man who sowed good seed in the world and will send his angels at the harvest. He is the treasure worth everything and the pearl of great price. Many hear him and miss him, from the crowd on the beach to the neighbors in Nazareth who could not see past the carpenter. But the mustard seed was planted. A few years later the seed went into the ground at the cross, and from that death came a harvest no one could count (John 12:24).
