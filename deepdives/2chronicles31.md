*Setting:* Judah, in the first year of Hezekiah, about 715 BC. In a few months the young king has reopened and cleansed the temple (ch 29) and held a great Passover that drew worshipers from the broken northern tribes, the first time all Israel had celebrated together since Solomon (ch 30). This chapter turns from the feast to what keeps a reform alive afterward: idols removed across the land, priests and Levites organized again, and the people's gifts collected, stored and fairly shared. It is less dramatic than the Passover, but without it the Passover would have been a single good day. 2 Kings 18:4 sums up the same reform in one verse and adds that Hezekiah broke Moses' bronze serpent, which the people had been worshiping. The ESV prints one heading for the whole chapter, "Hezekiah Organizes the Priests," so the later headings below simply follow the chapter's natural breaks.

#### Hezekiah Organizes the Priests (vv1–4)
- **"All Israel who were present went out… and broke in pieces the pillars and cut down the Asherim" (v1).** The people did this, not just the king's officers. They went home from the feast and tore down what they had once worshiped. "Pillars" were standing stones set up as symbols of a god, often Baal. The "Asherim" were wooden poles or carved trees for the goddess Asherah. "High places" were local hilltop shrines, and some of them had been used for the LORD's worship, which Deuteronomy forbade (Deut 12:2–5, 13–14). Henry's outline: "All the remnants of idolatry were destroyed and abolished."
- **"In Ephraim and Manasseh" (v1).** The clean-up spread north, beyond Hezekiah's borders. With Assyria having crushed the northern kingdom, no strong local king was left to stop it. Guzik passes on Clarke's guess that the northern king Hoshea allowed it, which only fits if this was before Samaria's fall, and Clarke himself admits he is unsure. A century later Josiah would have to do this all again (34:6–7), which shows how quickly idols return.
- **"Every man to his possession" (v1).** Each family went home to its inherited land. The phrase recalls the days of Joshua, when the tribes went home after the covenant at Shechem (Josh 24:28).
- **"Hezekiah appointed the divisions of the priests and of the Levites" (v2).** David had organized the priests into twenty-four rotating "divisions" or courses (1 Chr 24:1–19), and the Levites into groups for gatekeeping, music and other work (1 Chr 23–26). Guzik notes Payne's point that the Hebrew says "*the* divisions," David's established ones. Hezekiah is not inventing a new system but restarting the old one. A thousand years later this same system was still running: Zechariah, John the Baptist's father, was serving "in the order of his division" when the angel came to him (Luke 1:5, 8).
- **"To minister in the gates of the camp of the LORD" (v2).** "Camp" is wilderness language. In Numbers the tabernacle stood at the center of Israel's camp, with Levites camped around it (Num 2–3). The Chronicler uses it for the temple, linking Hezekiah's day with Moses'.
- **"The contribution of the king from his own possessions was for the burnt offerings" (v3).** Hezekiah gave first, and from his own estate, not the people's taxes. JFB: "his own proportion was announced in the first instance," following the example of David and Solomon (1 Chr 29:3; 2 Chr 8:12–13). The offerings listed are the regular calendar in Numbers 28–29: every morning and evening, every Sabbath, every new moon and every feast.
- **"That they might give themselves to the Law of the LORD" (v4).** Literally "be strong in the Law." This is the reason for the gifts. If the priests and Levites had to farm to survive, they could not teach and serve. That is exactly what happened in Nehemiah's day, when the gifts stopped and the Levites "fled each to his field" (Neh 13:10). Paul argues the same principle for gospel ministers (1 Cor 9:13–14).

#### The People Give in Abundance (vv5–10)
- **"As soon as the command was spread abroad, the people… gave in abundance" (v5).** The response was immediate and generous.
- **"Firstfruits of grain, wine, oil, honey" (v5).** The first part of each harvest belonged to God (Exod 23:19; Num 18:12–13). "Honey" (*devash*) can mean bees' honey but also a thick syrup made from dates or grapes. JFB notes the margin's "dates." Honey was not allowed on the altar (Lev 2:11), but it could be given to the priests as food.
- **"The tithe of everything" (v5).** A tithe is a tenth. Under the law Israel gave a tenth of produce and herds to the Levites, who in turn gave a tenth of that to the priests (Num 18:21–28; Lev 27:30–32). The Tyndale notes suggest the heaps of animal tithes may have been payments in money or goods, since Deuteronomy let people exchange their tithe for money when the journey was long (Deut 14:24–26).
- **"Laid them in heaps… In the third month they began… and finished them in the seventh month" (vv6–7).** The third month was the wheat harvest, celebrated at the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost). The seventh month was the end of the fruit and grape harvest, celebrated at the Feast of Booths. JFB: "The wheat harvest occurred in the third month." So for four months, through the whole harvest season, the gifts kept coming.
- **"They blessed the LORD and his people Israel" (v8).** The king does not credit his own leadership. He blesses God first and then the people, as David did when the people gave for the temple (1 Chr 29:10–20).
- **"Azariah the chief priest, who was of the house of Zadok" (v10).** Zadok's family had held the high priesthood since Solomon (1 Kings 2:35). JFB thinks this might be the same Azariah who confronted King Uzziah for burning incense (26:17–20), which is possible if he served a long time. The name was common in the priestly family (1 Chr 6:4–14).
- **"We have eaten and had enough and have plenty left, for the LORD has blessed his people" (v10).** After years of neglect under Ahaz, the priests not only have food but a surplus. Azariah does not praise the people's generosity so much as God's blessing on their harvests, which made the generosity possible. The Tyndale notes compare the people bringing so much for the tabernacle that Moses had to tell them to stop (Exod 36:3–7). The later prophet Malachi promised the same pattern: "Bring the full tithes into the storehouse… and thereby put me to the test… if I will not… pour down for you a blessing until there is no more need" (Mal 3:10).
- **Commentators:** Guzik uses this section to teach on Christian giving. He explains the Old Testament tithe from Numbers 18 and Malachi 3, then argues that the New Testament does not command a tithe but calls for giving that is regular, planned, proportional and cheerful, with ten percent as a good starting point rather than a ceiling. JFB explains the four months as the span from wheat harvest to the end of the vintage. Henry's outline sees the king's gift, the order to the people and the people's generous response as one connected work.

#### Storerooms and Faithful Stewards (vv11–19)
- **"Prepare chambers in the house of the LORD" (v11).** Storerooms were built around the temple for this purpose (1 Kings 6:5; 1 Chr 28:12). Abundance needs organizing, or it goes to waste or to thieves.
- **"They faithfully brought in the contributions" (v12).** "Faithfully" (*be'emunah*) comes three times in this section (vv12, 15, 18). It is the key word. Money for God's work needs trustworthy people. Compare the honest workmen under Joash and Josiah (2 Kings 12:15; 22:7).
- **Conaniah and Shimei, with ten overseers (vv12–13).** A chief officer, a second-in-command and a team, all named and "by the appointment of Hezekiah the king and Azariah the chief officer." Accountability ran to both the king and the high priest. Paul handled the collection for Jerusalem the same way, with named men, "for we aim at what is honorable not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of man" (2 Cor 8:20–21).
- **"Kore the son of Imnah… keeper of the east gate, was over the freewill offerings" (v14).** The east gate faced the temple's entrance and was the main gate, so its keeper was senior. The "most holy offerings" were portions that only priests could eat (Lev 6:17–18).
- **"In the cities of the priests… old and young alike" (v15).** Most priests and Levites lived in towns spread across the land (Josh 21), and served in Jerusalem only during their division's week. Six men took the gifts out to them, so those at home were not forgotten. The Tyndale notes explain that all Levites received shares, not only those on duty.
- **"Males from three years old and upward" (v16).** The verse is hard. It seems to mean that boys from age three, perhaps after they were weaned, were enrolled with their fathers who served in the temple and received food there. JFB reads it as excluding only children under three. Some scholars suggest the text originally read "thirty," the old starting age for Levites (Num 4:3; 1 Chr 23:3), since the numbers could be confused when copied. The ESV follows the Hebrew.
- **"The Levites from twenty years old and upward" (v17).** David lowered the Levites' starting age from thirty to twenty, since they no longer carried the tabernacle and more men were needed for temple work (1 Chr 23:24–27). Guzik passes on Clarke's note of the same.
- **"With all their little children, their wives, their sons, and their daughters… for they were faithful in keeping themselves holy" (v18).** Whole families were supported, because the men gave their lives to God's service. JFB: "for in their set office they sanctified themselves." The reason for the support is their faithfulness.
- **"The fields of common land belonging to their cities" (v19).** These were pasture lands around the Levites' towns (Num 35:2–5). Even priests living in the countryside were named and provided for. Nobody was missed.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws on Selman: careful planning and accountability are what let wholehearted worship continue. He also stresses that the families, not just the men on duty, were cared for. JFB summarizes vv11–18 as storerooms built, records kept by family and gifts sent out to the priests' cities, with provision for wives, daughters and servants. Henry outlines it as officers "appointed to distribute the gifts fairly."

#### Hezekiah's Faithfulness (vv20–21)
- **"He did what was good and right and faithful before the LORD his God" (v20).** Three words. "Faithful" is the same word used of the stewards (vv12, 15, 18). The king was as trustworthy as the men he appointed. JFB calls him one of the most illustrious of Judah's kings.
- **"Seeking his God, he did with all his heart, and prospered" (v21).** "Seek" is the Chronicler's great word for real faith (7:14; 15:2; 26:5). "Prospered" is not a promise of wealth but of success in God's work, the same promise given to Joshua for keeping the law (Josh 1:7–8) and to Uzziah while he sought God (26:5). The next verse (32:1) is a warning against reading this too simply: "After these things and these acts of faithfulness, Sennacherib king of Assyria came and invaded Judah." Faithfulness does not keep trouble away. It prepares a person to face it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik concludes that Hezekiah's success was not luck but the fruit of wholehearted devotion, and notes Morgan's reading of "seeking his God" alongside Jesus' call to "seek first the kingdom of God" (Matt 6:33). Henry closes the chapter by commending Hezekiah's sincerity in everything he undertook.

#### Pointing to Christ
After the joy of Passover came the ordinary work of tearing down idols, organizing the service and sharing out the gifts faithfully, so that God's servants could give themselves to his law. The chapter is a picture of what love for God looks like once the feast is over. It also points beyond itself. The priests needed the people's gifts to keep serving, and their service had to be repeated every morning and evening. Jesus is the priest who "has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily," because he offered himself "once for all" (Heb 7:27). He is also the faithful steward over God's house (Heb 3:2–6). Hezekiah did everything "with all his heart." The Son did his Father's will perfectly, and his people's giving now flows from his greater gift: "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor" (2 Cor 8:9).
