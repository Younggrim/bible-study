*Setting:* Psalm 100 is one of the best-loved psalms, five short verses. Its title, "A Psalm for giving thanks" (*mizmor letodah*), appears nowhere else in the Psalter. The word *todah* means thanksgiving, and it was also the name of the thank offering, a sacrifice brought to celebrate God's goodness and shared in a meal (Lev 7:11–15). Jewish tradition holds that the psalm was sung as the thank offering was presented, and many scholars agree it was meant for worshipers entering the temple courts. The author and date are unknown. It closes the group of "The LORD reigns" psalms (93–99). Those psalms announced God's kingship, his coming to judge, and his holiness. Psalm 100 answers with one great invitation: all the earth should come in and worship. It also gathers up phrases from the psalms before it, especially Psalm 95 ("the people of his pasture," 95:7). The church has sung it for centuries as the *Jubilate Deo* ("Rejoice in God") in morning prayer, and in William Kethe's 1561 paraphrase "All People That on Earth Do Dwell," sung to the tune still called "Old Hundredth." The psalm has two matching halves. Each gives a call to worship (vv1–2, v4) followed by the reason (v3, v5). There are seven commands in all. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow those two halves.

#### Know That the LORD, He Is God (vv1–3)
- **"Make a joyful noise to the LORD, all the earth!" (v1).** "Make a joyful noise" (*hari'u*) is the shout raised when a king appears among his people, or after a victory, the same word as in 95:1 and 98:4, 6. "All the earth" (literally "all the land") widens the call beyond Israel to every nation. The psalms before it have explained why: the LORD reigns over all of them.
- **"Serve the LORD with gladness!" (v2).** "Serve" (*avad*) is the word for a servant's work, and also for worship: the priests' service in the temple, and Israel's call to leave Egypt "that they may serve me" (Exod 7:16). In Egypt Israel had served Pharaoh with bitter labor (Exod 1:13–14). Now the whole earth is invited to serve the LORD, and to do it "with gladness" (*simchah*). The King does not want grudging slaves. Deuteronomy warns of the curse that comes "because you did not serve the LORD your God with joyfulness and gladness of heart" (Deut 28:47).
- **"Come into his presence with singing!" (v2).** "Singing" (*renanah*) is a ringing cry of joy. To "come into his presence" was to enter the temple, where God had put his name. Worship is not only shouting from far off but coming near.
- **"Know that the LORD, he is God!" (v3).** Here is the first reason, and it is a command to know. "Know" in Hebrew means more than knowing a fact. It means recognizing, acknowledging, experiencing. The words recall Elijah on Mount Carmel, when the people fell on their faces and cried, "The LORD, he is God; the LORD, he is God" (1 Kgs 18:39), and Moses: "the LORD is God; there is no other besides him" (Deut 4:35, 39). The nations knew many gods. The psalm says: know which one is God.
- **"It is he who made us, and we are his" (v3).** There is a famous textual question here. The Hebrew words for "not" (*lo'*) and "his" (*lo*) sound the same and differ by one letter. The written Hebrew text reads "and not we ourselves" (KJV: "it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves"). The Jewish scribes' marginal reading, the *qere*, says "and we are his." The ESV follows the marginal reading and puts "and not we ourselves" in a footnote, as do most modern translations. Both say something true: we did not make ourselves, and because God made us, we belong to him.
- **"Made us."** This may mean God made human beings, but in this context it probably also means he made Israel into his people, as in Deuteronomy 32:6 ("Is not he your father, who created you, who made you and established you?") and Isaiah 43:1. Either way, the Maker owns what he makes.
- **"We are his people, and the sheep of his pasture" (v3).** This repeats Psalm 95:7 with the order changed. A shepherd does not only own his sheep. He feeds, leads and protects them (Ps 23; Isa 40:11). The nations invited in v1 are being invited to become part of that flock.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the call to "serve the Lord with gladness" "a prediction that in gospel-times there should be special occasion for joy," and says "gospel-worshippers should be joyful worshippers." On v3 he writes, "Knowledge is the mother of devotion and of all obedience: blind sacrifices will never please a seeing God." He mentions the alternative reading, "He made us, and his we are," and draws from it that God is "our rightful owner." JFB says that "to the obligations of a creature and subject is added that of a beneficiary" (Ps 95:7). Guzik observes that unlike the psalms before it, this one does not open by declaring God's reign but goes straight to the invitation, which reaches far beyond Israel. He lists three reasons for praise in v3: God is God, he is Creator, and he has made a people and cares for them as a shepherd.

#### Enter His Gates with Thanksgiving (vv4–5)
- **"Enter his gates with thanksgiving" (v4).** The picture moves to the temple itself. Worshipers came up through the city gates and the temple gates into the open courts where the altar stood. Only priests went into the holy place, but the people gathered in the courts. "Thanksgiving" (*todah*), the word in the title, may mean words of thanks, or the thank offering they carried in. Psalm 24:7–10 and 118:19–20 also picture worshipers passing through the gates.
- **"And his courts with praise!" (v4).** "Praise" (*tehillah*) is the word that gives the book of Psalms its Hebrew name, *Tehillim*. The temple courts were normally the place of Israel's worship. Here the invitation is to "all the earth" (v1), a hint of Isaiah's vision of a house of prayer for all peoples (Isa 56:7).
- **"Give thanks to him; bless his name!" (v4).** To "give thanks" (*hodu*) is to acknowledge publicly what God has done. To "bless his name" is to speak well of him as he has made himself known. The last two of the psalm's seven commands round off the call.
- **"For the LORD is good" (v5).** The second reason. It is simple, and it is the foundation. The gods of the nations were thought to be unpredictable, selfish and easily angered. The LORD is good, and his goodness does not change. This short line became one of Israel's great refrains of worship: "Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good" (1 Chr 16:34; 2 Chr 5:13; 7:3; Ezra 3:11; Ps 106:1; 107:1; 118:1; 136:1).
- **"His steadfast love endures forever" (v5).** *Hesed*, God's loyal covenant love, has no end. When the ark came into Solomon's temple and the priests sang these words, "the house of the LORD was filled with a cloud" (2 Chr 5:13). Psalm 136 repeats the line twenty-six times.
- **"And his faithfulness to all generations" (v5).** "Faithfulness" (*emunah*) is reliability. God's love lasts forever, and his promises hold from parents to children to grandchildren. The psalm that began with "all the earth" ends with "all generations," the whole world and the whole of time.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in all worship "we come into God's presence," but "in public worship especially" we "enter into his gates and into his courts." He tells his readers to "take it as a favour to be admitted into his service," and calls thanksgiving a "golden thread" that "must run through every duty" (Heb 13:15). JFB reads the gates and courts as "of course, figurative" for "His public worship," and says the reason for praise is "God's eternal mercy and truth." Guzik adds that thanksgiving has a corporate side, belonging to God's people together and not only to private prayer, and that in Christ not only the gates and courts but the way into the Most Holy Place is open (Heb 10:19).

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 100 invites every nation to enter God's courts. In the Jerusalem temple, Gentiles could go only as far as an outer court, and a barrier warned them not to come further. Paul says that in Christ "the dividing wall of hostility" has been broken down, so that Gentiles "are no longer strangers and aliens" but members of God's household (Eph 2:14, 19). Jesus calls himself both the gate and the good shepherd: "I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture," and "I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also… So there will be one flock, one shepherd" (John 10:9, 16). Through him people from every land become "his people, and the sheep of his pasture," and the thank offering the psalm was sung with is now "a sacrifice of praise to God… the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15).
