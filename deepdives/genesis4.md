*Setting:* Adam and Eve are now outside the garden, east of Eden, with the cherubim guarding the way back (3:24). God has promised that the woman's offspring will crush the serpent's head (3:15), and Eve has been named "mother of all living" (3:20). Chapter 4 shows what sin does in the very next generation. In chapter 3 sin broke the bond between people and God. Here it breaks the bond between brothers, and then it spreads through a whole family line, from one murder to a boast about killing. Yet the chapter ends with a new son, a new line of hope, and people calling on the name of the LORD. Genesis now follows two families side by side: Cain's line, which builds cities and culture without God, and Seth's line, through which the promise will travel.

#### Cain and Abel (vv1–26)
- **"Adam knew Eve his wife" (v1).** "Knew" is a Hebrew way of speaking of sexual union. It is more than a polite phrase: it describes a personal, committed knowing between two people.
- **"I have gotten a man with the help of the LORD" (v1).** "Cain" (*Qayin*) sounds like the verb *qanah*, "to get, acquire" or "produce." Eve's words are short and unusual in Hebrew, literally "I have gotten a man, with the LORD." Most translate "with the help of the LORD." A few older interpreters, including Luther, read it as "I have gotten a man, the LORD," as if Eve thought her son was the promised deliverer of 3:15. Most scholars think the grammar doesn't require that, but either way Eve speaks with hope. She is clinging to God's promise, and her firstborn will become a murderer.
- **"His brother Abel" (v2).** "Abel" (*Hevel*) means "breath" or "vapor," the same word as "vanity" in Ecclesiastes ("vanity of vanities," Eccl 1:2). He is given no recorded words, and his life passes like a breath. The word "brother" comes seven times in vv2–11, pressing the horror of what follows.
- **"Abel was a keeper of sheep, and Cain a worker of the ground" (v2).** Both jobs are honorable. Cain carries on his father's calling to "work the ground" (2:15; 3:23). The story is not about farming versus herding.
- **"An offering of the fruit of the ground… the firstborn of his flock and of their fat portions" (vv3–4).** "Offering" is *minchah*, which means a gift or tribute in general and later the grain offering in particular. In Genesis 4 it is used for *both* brothers' offerings. So the difference isn't simply that one was grain and one was animal. Grain offerings were fully acceptable in Israel's law (Lev 2). The text quietly points to the difference: Abel brought the **firstborn** and the **fat portions**, the very best. Cain brought "of the fruit," some of it, with no mention of firstfruits.
- **"The LORD had regard for Abel and his offering" (v4).** Notice the order: God looked at *Abel*, then *his offering*. The man came before the gift. Hebrews 11:4 explains: "By faith Abel offered to God a more acceptable sacrifice than Cain." 1 John 3:12 adds that Cain's "own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous." Readers have suggested several reasons God rejected Cain's offering:
  - **Cain's heart:** he gave carelessly and without faith. This is the most common reading, and it fits Hebrews and 1 John.
  - **The kind of offering:** only a blood sacrifice could deal with sin, as some, including JFB, argue. But the text calls both offerings *minchah*, and neither is said to be for sin.
  - **The quality:** Abel gave the first and best, Cain did not.
  - These overlap. Faith shows itself in what we give.
- **How did they know?** The text doesn't say. Many, like JFB and Guzik, guess that God sent fire, as he later did for Aaron (Lev 9:24), Gideon (Judg 6:21) and Elijah (1 Kings 18:38).
- **"Cain was very angry, and his face fell" (v5).** His anger is aimed at God, and it shows on his face. He doesn't ask what he did wrong.
- **"Why are you angry?" (v6).** God doesn't reject Cain. He comes to him, as he came to Adam ("Where are you?"), with questions that invite him to look at himself. The door to repentance is open.
- **"If you do well, will you not be accepted?" (v7).** "Accepted" is literally "lifted up," a play on Cain's "fallen" face. If he does right, his face will be lifted.
- **"Sin is crouching at the door" (v7).** This is the first time the word "sin" (*chatta'th*) appears in the Bible, and it appears as a wild animal. "Crouching" (*rabats*) is used of a lion lying in wait (49:9). Some scholars hear an echo of the Akkadian *rabitsu*, a demon believed to lurk at doorways, though the Hebrew word itself just means to lie stretched out. JFB reads *chatta'th* here as "sin offering," as if a sacrifice lay at the door ready for Cain to use. The Hebrew word can mean both "sin" and "sin offering," but most translations and scholars read "sin" because of what follows.
- **"Its desire is for you, and you must rule over it" (v7).** These words echo 3:16, "your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you," with the same two Hebrew words, *teshuqah* ("desire") and *mashal* ("rule"). Sin wants to own Cain. God tells him he must master it, and so he can. It's the first warning in the Bible about temptation, and it puts responsibility on Cain.
- **"Cain spoke to Abel his brother" (v8).** The Hebrew text seems to break off: "Cain said to Abel his brother…" with no words given. The ancient Greek, Samaritan, Syriac and Latin versions all fill it in: "**Let us go out to the field.**" The ESV keeps the Hebrew and mentions the other reading in a footnote. Either way, the murder is planned.
- **"Cain rose up against his brother Abel and killed him" (v8).** The first death in the Bible is not old age but murder, and of a brother. Jesus calls Abel the first martyr, "the blood of righteous Abel" (Matt 23:35). Sin has gone from a stolen fruit to a shed life in one generation.
- **"Where is Abel your brother?" (v9).** God asked Adam "Where are *you*?" Now he asks Cain "Where is your *brother*?" Sin against God always becomes sin against others.
- **"I do not know; am I my brother's keeper?" (v9).** It's a lie followed by a sneer. "Keeper" is the same root (*shamar*) as Adam's calling to "keep" the garden (2:15) and Abel's work as a "keeper" of sheep (v2). The answer the whole Bible gives is yes: love your neighbor as yourself (Lev 19:18).
- **"The voice of your brother's blood is crying to me from the ground" (v10).** "Crying" (*tsa'aq*) is the word for the cry of the oppressed calling for justice, the same root used for Israel's cry in Egypt (Exod 3:7). Murder pollutes the land itself (Num 35:33). "Blood" is plural in Hebrew, "bloods." A Jewish tradition in the Mishnah says it means his blood and the blood of all the descendants who would never be born.
- **"You are cursed from the ground" (v11).** Adam was not cursed directly; the ground was (3:17). Cain is the first person God curses. The ground "opened its mouth" to drink his brother's blood, and now it will refuse him its strength. **"A fugitive and a wanderer"** (v12) is in Hebrew a rhyming pair, *na' wanad*, restless and homeless.
- **"My punishment is greater than I can bear" (v13).** The word for "punishment" (*avon*) also means "iniquity" or "guilt." Some translate "my iniquity is too great to be forgiven." Either way, Cain grieves the sentence, not the sin. There is self-pity here, not repentance.
- **"From your face I shall be hidden" (v14).** For Cain, leaving the land means leaving God's presence. Israel would later hear the same warning: unfaithfulness would mean exile from the land and from God's presence (Lev 26:33).
- **"Whoever finds me will kill me" (v14).** Who else is out there? Genesis 5:4 says Adam "had other sons and daughters," and many generations may be passing in these few verses. Most readers, including Guzik and the Tyndale notes, conclude that Cain's wife (v17) was a sister or close relative, before later laws forbade such marriages (Lev 18:9). Some scholars instead see this as a sign that the story assumes a wider population without explaining it.
- **"The LORD put a mark on Cain" (v15).** The text doesn't say what the mark was. It isn't a curse but protection: God promises that anyone who kills Cain will suffer **sevenfold**, meaning full and complete vengeance. Even for a murderer, God shows mercy. The old idea that the "mark of Cain" was dark skin has no basis in the text and was used to justify slavery and racism. It should be rejected outright.
- **"Cain went away from the presence of the LORD and settled in the land of Nod, east of Eden" (v16).** "Nod" means "wandering," from the same root as "wanderer" in v12. So he "settled" in the "Land of Wandering." Like his parents, he moves east, away from God (3:24).
- **"He built a city" (v17).** The man sentenced to wander builds a city, perhaps for safety, perhaps in defiance. He names it after his son **Enoch**, his own legacy. The first city in the Bible is built by a murderer, and the Bible's story moves from this city to Babel (11:4) to Babylon and at last to the city God builds (Heb 11:10; Rev 21:2).
- **Cain's line (vv18–22).** The names run from Enoch to Lamech, seventh from Adam. Several names in this list look like names in Seth's line in chapter 5 (Enoch, Irad/Jared, Methushael/Methuselah, Lamech). Some scholars think the two lists share a common tradition. Others see the parallel as deliberate, showing two very different families.
- **"Lamech took two wives" (v19).** This is the first recorded polygamy, a departure from "a man… and his wife… one flesh" (2:24). Genesis never approves of it, and the stories of polygamous households that follow (Abraham, Jacob, Elkanah, David) are full of rivalry and pain.
- **Jabal, Jubal and Tubal-cain (vv20–22).** Herding with tents, music with **lyre and pipe**, and metalwork in **bronze and iron** all start in Cain's line. Culture, art and technology are gifts that reflect the creativity of people made in God's image, and Scripture never condemns them in themselves. Israel's worship used music and its tabernacle used skilled metalwork (Exod 31:1–5). But here they grow in a family that has walked away from God. Progress doesn't make people better. **Naamah** ("lovely") is the only daughter named in this list, and her mention is unusual.
- **"Lamech said to his wives" (vv23–24).** This is the first poem in the Bible after Adam's love song (2:23), and it's a song of revenge. Lamech kills a young man for merely wounding him and boasts about it. **"If Cain's revenge is sevenfold, then Lamech's is seventy-sevenfold."** God's protection for Cain becomes Lamech's excuse for unlimited vengeance. Jesus reverses this exactly. Peter asks if forgiving seven times is enough, and Jesus answers, "seventy-seven times" (Matt 18:21–22). Lamech's limitless revenge is answered by limitless forgiveness.
- **"She bore a son and called his name Seth" (v25).** "Seth" (*Shet*) sounds like the verb "appointed" (*shat*): "God has appointed for me another offspring." "Offspring" is *zera*, "seed," the same word as in 3:15. Eve sees God keeping his promise. The line of hope didn't die with Abel.
- **"Enosh" (v26).** The name means "man" or "mortal," often with a sense of human weakness (Ps 8:4; 103:15). It's a humble name for a humble time.
- **"At that time people began to call upon the name of the LORD" (v26).** In the rest of Genesis, "calling on the name of the LORD" means worship: prayer and sacrifice at an altar (12:8; 13:4; 26:25). In contrast to Cain's line, with its cities and boasts, Seth's line is marked by prayer. Some ancient Jewish interpreters read the verse as "began to profane the name," but that reading is not supported by the Hebrew grammar most scholars accept.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in this one family "both the world and the church," and "that great distinction of mankind into saints and sinners." He says God accepted Abel first and then his gift: "The tree must be good, else the fruit cannot be pleasing to the heart-searching God," and "Abel offered in faith, and Cain did not." On v10: "Murder is a crying sin, none more so," and "Abel's blood cried for vengeance, Christ's blood cries for pardon." On v26 he urges, "The worse others are the better we should be, and the more zealous." JFB reads Eve's words as grateful, "by the help of the Lord," and thinks God showed acceptance by fire. It argues v7 means a "sin offering" lay at the door, a minority reading, as noted above. It suggests Lamech's song is an old poem in which he claims self-defense. Guzik also notes that grain offerings were allowed under the Law, so the problem was Cain's heart, not the kind of offering. He calls Cain's anger spiritual pride. He says Cain regretted his punishment but never his sin, and he calls v26 possibly the first revival. He also notes the contrast between Lamech, seventh from Adam through Cain, and Enoch, seventh from Adam through Seth, who "walked with God" (5:24; Jude 14). The Tyndale notes make the same contrast.

#### Pointing to Christ
Abel was a shepherd who brought a lamb and was killed by his brother out of jealousy. Jesus is the Good Shepherd and the Lamb, handed over by his own people "out of envy" (Matt 27:18). Abel's blood cried out from the ground for justice. Hebrews says believers have come "to Jesus… and to the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24). Abel's blood calls for judgment. Christ's blood speaks pardon. Lamech boasted of seventy-sevenfold revenge, and Jesus calls his people to seventy-sevenfold forgiveness because he forgave them first. The "appointed seed" in Seth's line runs through Noah, Abraham and David to the offspring of the woman who would crush the serpent's head.
