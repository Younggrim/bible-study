*Setting:* For thirty-five chapters Job has argued with his three friends and then listened to Elihu. Again and again Job has asked for one thing: that God would answer him. "Let the Almighty answer me!" (31:35). He wanted to lay out his case and hear the charge against him (13:22–23; 23:3–7). Elihu has just said that no one can find God and that it would be madness to ask for a hearing (37:19–23). Now, with no warning and no introduction, God speaks. This is the first time since chapter 2 that God has said a word, and the first time in the whole book that he speaks to Job. His two speeches run from 38:1 to 41:34, with a short reply from Job in between (40:3–5). What is surprising is what God does *not* say. He never mentions the heavenly scene of chapters 1–2, never explains Job's suffering, and never answers Job's legal questions. Instead he takes Job on a tour of creation, asking question after question.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv1–3)
- **"Then the LORD answered Job" (v1).** The divine name *Yahweh* ("the LORD") has barely appeared since the prologue. Job and his friends used general names for God (*El*, *Eloah*, *Shaddai*). Now the narrator uses God's covenant name, the name he gave Moses at the burning bush (Exod 3:14–15). The God who speaks is not a distant force but the personal God who knows Job by name and called him "my servant" (1:8).
- **"Out of the whirlwind" (v1).** The word is *se'arah*, a storm wind or tempest. It is the wind that carried Elijah to heaven (2 Kings 2:11) and the storm out of which Ezekiel saw God's glory (Ezek 1:4). In 9:17 Job said God "crushes me with a tempest," using the same word. Now God comes in a tempest, and it does not crush him. It speaks to him. The storm Elihu described in 36:27–37:22 has arrived, and God is in it.
- **"Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge?" (v2).** "Counsel" is God's plan or purpose. Job's words have clouded it. The Tyndale notes point out that Eliphaz and Elihu made similar charges (15:2; 34:35; 35:16), and that Job later admits it, repeating this very question (42:3). Notice what God does not say. He does not say Job is wicked or that he is being punished for sin. The charge is ignorance, not guilt. Some think the question is aimed at Elihu, who has just finished speaking, but v1 says the LORD "answered Job," and Job takes it to himself (42:3).
- **"Dress for action like a man" (v3).** Literally "gird up your loins like a mighty man." A man tucked his long robe into his belt to work, run or fight (1 Kings 18:46; Luke 12:35). God treats Job as a serious opponent. He does not shout him down or brush him aside. "I will question you, and you make it known to me": Job wanted to question God (13:22). Now the roles are reversed. Job will be the one answering, and the questions are about the world, not about Job's case.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the judge upon the bench must have the last word," and God's aim is to humble Job and so bring him to repentance. He passes on Bishop Patrick: God carries on Elihu's line of argument, but in a style that surpasses it "as much as thunder does a whisper." JFB calls Job to be a "hero, ready for battle," and reads v2 as Job's words obscuring rather than throwing light on God's ways, while noting that God will in the end vindicate Job. Guzik finds comfort in the fact that God came at all. He thinks v2 may have been aimed at Elihu, but stresses that every speaker talked without knowing what the reader knows from chapters 1–2. He also sees "dress like a man" as God giving Job dignity, not crushing him.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv4–11)
- **"Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?" (v4).** This is the first question and it sets the tone for all of them. Job was not there. Creation is pictured as a building project. God is the architect and builder.
- **"Who determined its measurements... who stretched the line upon it?" (v5).** A builder measures with a cord before laying stone (Zech 1:16). "Surely you know!" is gentle irony. Of course Job does not.
- **"On what were its bases sunk, or who laid its cornerstone?" (v6).** The earth has footings and a cornerstone like a temple. Job had said God "hangs the earth on nothing" (26:7), and here God asks what holds it up. The cornerstone image returns later for the Messiah: "the stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (Ps 118:22; Eph 2:20).
- **"When the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy" (v7).** When a building's foundation was laid, there was a celebration (Ezra 3:10–11, at the second temple). At creation the celebration was cosmic. The "sons of God" are the angels, the same phrase as in 1:6 and 2:1, where they gathered before the LORD and Satan came among them. The "morning stars" stand in parallel with them. The Tyndale notes say the stars are described as if they were persons, like the angels. The angels watched creation with joy. Job, by contrast, was not even born.
- **"Who shut in the sea with doors when it burst out from the womb?" (v8).** Now the sea. In the ancient Near East the sea was a picture of chaos, and Canaanite and Babylonian myths told of gods battling the sea monster. Here the sea is not a rival god but a newborn baby.
- **"When I made clouds its garment and thick darkness its swaddling band" (v9).** God dresses the infant sea in clouds and wraps it in darkness like a midwife wrapping a newborn (Ezek 16:4; Luke 2:7). It is a startling picture: the raging ocean is, to God, a baby in a blanket.
- **"Thus far shall you come, and no farther" (vv10–11).** God sets limits, bars and doors. The Tyndale notes describe the sea here as a dangerous creature kept under control. The "proud waves" are stopped by a word (Ps 104:9; Jer 5:22; Prov 8:29). This answers something Job said in his pain: "Am I the sea, or a sea monster, that you set a guard over me?" (7:12). God does set boundaries on the chaos, and the boundaries are for the world's good. Jesus will do the same with a word on Galilee (Mark 4:39).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the "measures" and "line" as an architect's plan, showing the earth "formed on an all-wise plan," and takes the morning stars as a symbol of the angels, linking v7 to the shouts when Zerubbabel's temple was founded. It pictures the sea "wrapped in clouds as its swaddling bands." Guzik notes that the angels at creation were witnesses who rejoiced, not advisers, and ties vv8–11 to the second and third days of Genesis 1. Henry groups these as the founding of the earth and the bounds set to the sea, proofs of how little Job knows.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv12–21)
- **"Have you commanded the morning since your days began?" (v12).** Every sunrise is under orders. Job has lived many years and never once told the dawn to come.
- **"That it might take hold of the skirts of the earth, and the wicked be shaken out of it" (v13).** The dawn grabs the edges of the earth like a person grabbing a blanket and shakes it, and the wicked tumble out like crumbs. Thieves and adulterers work in the dark (24:13–17). When morning comes, they scatter. This is a quiet answer to Job's complaint that God lets the wicked go free (21:7–17). God's world has a built-in check on evil, even if it is not the full judgment.
- **"It is changed like clay under the seal" (v14).** A seal rolled or pressed on soft clay left a sharp image. At dawn the shapeless dark earth takes form: hills, trees and roads appear. "Its features stand out like a garment": the Tyndale notes explain that the morning sun brings out color, shape and texture, like the folds of a robe.
- **"From the wicked their light is withheld" (v15).** For the wicked, darkness is their "light," their working hours (24:17). Dawn takes it away. "Their uplifted arm," raised to strike, "is broken."
- **"Have you entered into the springs of the sea?" (v16).** Ancient people believed great springs fed the ocean from below (Gen 7:11). No human had walked "in the recesses of the deep."
- **"Have the gates of death been revealed to you?" (v17).** Death is pictured as a city with gates (Ps 9:13; Matt 16:18). "Deep darkness" is *tsalmaveth*, often rendered "shadow of death" (Ps 23:4). Job had talked a great deal about death and the grave (3:11–19; 10:21–22; 17:13–16). God asks whether he has actually seen it. He has not. God has.
- **"Have you comprehended the expanse of the earth?" (v18).** Job does not even know how big the world is. "Declare, if you know all this."
- **"Where is the way to the dwelling of light... the place of darkness?" (vv19–20).** Light and darkness are pictured as having homes they return to. Could Job lead them home?
- **"You know, for you were born then, and the number of your days is great!" (v21).** This is open sarcasm, the sharpest line of the speech. Eliphaz used a similar jab: "Are you the first man who was born?" (15:7). The Tyndale notes compare Wisdom in Proverbs 8:25, who was there "before the hills." Job was not. The sarcasm is not cruelty. It is a father's firm smile at a child who has been talking beyond his years.
- **Commentators:** JFB pictures the dawn shaping the earth like clay under a rolling seal, while its beauty "stands forth as a garment," and says the uplifted arm of the wicked is broken by fear of the light. On v17 it notes that the gates of death are hidden from the living but "naked before God" (compare 26:6). Guzik groups dawn, sea springs, death and the breadth of the earth as a list of things Job simply does not know. Henry lists "the morning light" and "the hidden depths of sea and earth" as two of God's proofs.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv22–38)
- **"Have you entered the storehouses of the snow... the hail?" (v22).** Elihu had pictured storm "chambers" (37:9). God says he keeps snow and hail in storehouses like a king's armory (Ps 135:7; Jer 10:13).
- **"Which I have reserved for the time of trouble, for the day of battle and war" (v23).** Weather is God's weapon. Hail stones killed more of the Amorites than Israel's swords did (Josh 10:11). Thunder routed the Philistines (1 Sam 7:10). The Tyndale notes add Revelation 16:21, where huge hailstones fall at the end.
- **"Where the light is distributed... where the east wind is scattered" (v24).** The east wind blew in from the desert, hot and dry, withering crops (Gen 41:6; Jonah 4:8).
- **"Who has cleft a channel for the torrents of rain and a way for the thunderbolt?" (v25).** God cuts channels in the sky for the downpour as a farmer cuts irrigation ditches.
- **"To bring rain on a land where no man is" (vv26–27).** This is one of the most important lines in the chapter. God sends rain on deserts where no human lives, so that grass sprouts where no one will ever see it. Creation is not all about people. God delights in his world for its own sake. Job and his friends had assumed everything centers on human beings and their deeds. God's world is bigger than that.
- **"Has the rain a father, or who has begotten the drops of dew?" (v28).** Canaanite religion said Baal fathered the rain. God's question implies the answer: rain has no divine father. God made it. "From whose womb did the ice come forth?" (v29). Rain comes from a father, ice from a womb. Both pictures mean the same thing: God is the source.
- **"The waters become hard like stone" (v30).** Water becomes solid. To people in a warm climate ice was a marvel.
- **"Can you bind the chains of the Pleiades or loose the cords of Orion?" (v31).** The Pleiades are a tight cluster of stars; "chains" may picture them as a string of beads. Orion is a striding figure with a belt of three stars. The Hebrew name for Orion is *Kesil*, spelled like the word for "fool," and some scholars think it reflects an old story of a giant bound in the sky. Job had praised God as "Maker of the Bear and Orion, the Pleiades" (9:9), and Amos says the same (Amos 5:8). The Pleiades rise in spring and Orion in winter, so some read v31 as about the seasons.
- **"Can you lead forth the Mazzaroth in their season?" (v32).** The ESV leaves *Mazzaroth* untranslated because no one is certain what it means. It may be a particular constellation, or the twelve signs of the zodiac, or the planets. "The Bear with its children" is usually taken as the Big Dipper, with its stars as the "children," though some think it is another cluster. The point is not astronomy. Job cannot move a single star. Ancient nations believed the stars ruled human destiny. Here they are simply God's flock, led out on time (Isa 40:26).
- **"Do you know the ordinances of the heavens?" (v33).** The sky runs by laws God set (Jer 31:35–36; 33:25). "Can you establish their rule on the earth?" Seasons and calendars on earth follow the heavens.
- **"Can you lift up your voice to the clouds?" (vv34–35).** Can Job shout for rain and get a flood? Can he send out lightning bolts and have them report back, "Here we are"? That is a servant's answer to a master (Isa 6:8). Lightning answers to God that way. It does not answer to Job.
- **"Who has put wisdom in the inward parts or given understanding to the mind?" (v36).** The two Hebrew words here are rare and uncertain. The second, *sekvi*, appears only here. The lexicon suggests it may mean some sign in the sky, and Jewish tradition later used the same word for "rooster." The Tyndale notes explain one reading: the first word may mean "ibis," a bird the Egyptians believed knew when the Nile would flood, and the second "rooster," which rabbis thought could sense coming rain. Then the question is: who taught the birds to read the weather? The ESV takes the traditional reading, wisdom in the human heart. Either way, all wisdom, animal or human, comes from God (Prov 2:6; Jas 1:5).
- **"Who can tilt the waterskins of the heavens?" (vv37–38).** The clouds are leather water jugs that God tips over. Rain falls on cracked, dusty ground until "the clods stick fast together" and the earth becomes workable soil.
- **Commentators:** JFB on v23 says God reserves hail "against the time of trouble," when he chastises people, and on vv26–27 that rain on empty land shows God has a wise design even where no one benefits, and that the desert's beauty pleases him. It explains v31's "sweet influences" (KJV) as the joy of spring linked to the Pleiades. Guzik compares the rain no human can command to grace, which no one can control or earn. He thinks *Mazzaroth* is probably the zodiac, following Adam Clarke. On v36 he says God is the designer of the human mind, and that real scientific progress still leaves a vast gap between human and divine wisdom. He adds that God's tone with the stars is playful rather than humiliating. Henry lists rain, frost and lightning, the stars, and "the making of the soul" (v36) as further proofs of Job's ignorance.

#### The LORD Answers Job (vv39–41)
- **"Can you hunt the prey for the lion?" (v39).** Now the speech turns to animals, and it continues through chapter 39. Lions were common in the region then (Judg 14:5; 1 Sam 17:34). Does Job feed them? God does: "The young lions roar for their prey, seeking their food from God" (Ps 104:21).
- **"When they crouch in their dens" (v40).** Notice that God provides even for predators. The world God describes is wild and dangerous, and he does not apologize for it. It is still his.
- **"Who provides for the raven its prey, when its young ones cry to God for help?" (v41).** Ravens were unclean birds under the law (Lev 11:15), scavengers, not beautiful or valued. Yet God hears the cries of their chicks. Psalm 147:9 says the same, and Jesus points to it: "Consider the ravens: they neither sow nor reap... and yet God feeds them. Of how much more value are you than the birds!" (Luke 12:24). The speech ends this chapter not with power but with care.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the raven, though feared and disliked, is still cared for by God "as for all His creatures." Guzik says God's care for lions and ravens shows how far Job is from being able to provide for creation, underlining the gap between them. Henry lists God's "provision for lions and ravens" as the last of his proofs in this chapter.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job asked for an answer and God came, but not with the answer Job expected. God never explains the suffering. He shows Job who he is: the builder of the earth, the midwife of the sea, the one who commands dawn, keeps hail for battle, waters empty deserts and feeds raven chicks. That is the answer underneath every other answer. Jesus speaks of the same God: the Father who feeds the ravens and dresses the grass (Luke 12:24–28), and Jesus himself stops the waves with "Peace! Be still!" (Mark 4:39), doing what God claims in v11. Paul says that "all things were created through him and for him... and in him all things hold together" (Col 1:16–17). The one who "laid the foundation of the earth" (v4) is the one Hebrews says "laid the foundation of the earth in the beginning" (Heb 1:10, quoting Ps 102:25 of the Son). Job never learned the reason for his trial, but he met the Lord of creation. Believers in suffering now know more than Job did: that same Lord took on flesh, entered the "gates of death" (v17), and came back holding their keys (Rev 1:18).
