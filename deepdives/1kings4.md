*Setting:* Solomon's reign at its height, roughly 965–930 BC. Chapter 3 ended with Israel in awe of the wisdom God put in the king. Chapter 4 shows that wisdom in a different setting: not one hard court case, but the running of a whole state. It is a list chapter, of cabinet officials, tax districts, daily food supplies and stables, and then a closing hymn to Solomon's learning. Lists like these were probably drawn from royal archives, such as "the Book of the Acts of Solomon" (11:41). They are not dull filler. They show God's promises to Abraham coming true: a people as many as the sand, land from Egypt to the Euphrates, peace on every side. But the careful reader will also notice the forced labor, the horses and the heavy demands of the royal table, which Samuel had warned about (1 Sam 8:11–17) and which would split the kingdom after Solomon died (12:4). In Hebrew Bibles, vv21–34 are numbered 5:1–14, so verse numbers differ in some translations and commentaries.

#### Solomon's Officials (vv1–19)
- **"King Solomon was king over all Israel" (v1).** A simple sentence, but it matters. After civil war under Ish-bosheth, Absalom's revolt and Sheba's rebellion (2 Sam 2–4; 15–20), the twelve tribes are united under one king. This unity will not outlive Solomon.
- **"Azariah the son of Zadok was the priest" (v2).** Listed first, ahead of the army. "Son" here likely means grandson. First Chronicles 6:8–9 gives Zadok, Ahimaaz, Azariah. JFB thinks "the priest" (*kohen*) here means a chief minister, "the highest in office next the king," since the title could be used for top royal officials (2 Sam 8:18 calls David's sons "priests"). The Tyndale notes take it as the high priest, since Zadok was old.
- **"Elihoreph and Ahijah the sons of Shisha were secretaries; Jehoshaphat the son of Ahilud was recorder" (v3).** The secretaries handled royal letters and records. Their father (probably the Seraiah or Sheva of 2 Sam 8:17; 20:25) held the job under David, so this was a family office. Jehoshaphat had been David's recorder (2 Sam 8:16), the official who kept the court chronicle and probably managed protocol. JFB: the office was "of great importance in Oriental courts."
- **"Benaiah… was in command of the army; Zadok and Abiathar were priests" (v4).** Benaiah replaced Joab (2:35). Abiathar's name is surprising after 2:26–27. The list may come from the very start of the reign, or Abiathar kept the title while banished. JFB: "the latter had been banished to his country seat."
- **"Azariah the son of Nathan… Zabud the son of Nathan was priest and king's friend" (v5).** Two sons of a Nathan, very likely the prophet who stood by Solomon (ch 1). "King's friend" was an official title, a close personal adviser, as Hushai was to David (2 Sam 15:37). Azariah oversaw the twelve district governors of vv7–19.
- **"Adoniram the son of Abda was in charge of the forced labor" (v6).** Forced labor (*mas*) meant compulsory work crews for the king's building projects. Adoniram (also called Adoram) did the same under David (2 Sam 20:24) and was later stoned to death when Rehoboam sent him to the northern tribes (12:18). His name at the end of the list is a quiet warning.
- **"Twelve officers over all Israel, who provided food for the king and his household… for one month in the year" (v7).** A system of rotating tax districts. Each district supplied the court for one month. The districts did not exactly follow the old tribal borders. Several are defined by cities and regions instead, which may have helped weaken old tribal loyalties.
- **Ben-hur, Ben-deker, Ben-hesed, Ben-abinadab, Ben-geber (vv8–13).** Several men are named only by their father's name, "son of Hur," "son of Deker." Some think the left edge of the source document was damaged so the first names were lost. Others think these were simply how the men were known. Two governors married Solomon's daughters, Taphath and Basemath (vv11, 15), keeping power in the family.
- **The places (vv8–19).** The districts cover the whole land: the hill country of Ephraim, the coastal plain at Dor, the great fortress cities of Megiddo and Taanach in the Jezreel Valley, Beth-shean in the Jordan Valley, and across the river in Gilead and Bashan. "Sixty great cities with walls and bronze bars" (v13) are the strongholds of Argob that Israel took from Og (Deut 3:4). "The country of Sihon… and of Og" (v19) reminds the reader that this land was God's gift from the days of Moses (Num 21:21–35).
- **"And there was one governor who was over the land" (v19).** The Hebrew is unclear. It may mean one more officer over Judah, or a governor over the whole system. Notice that Judah, Solomon's own tribe, does not appear clearly among the twelve districts. Many scholars think Judah was taxed differently or exempted, which would explain the northern tribes' bitterness at the end of his reign: "Your father made our yoke heavy" (12:4).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees wisdom in Solomon's delegating: a wise leader does not try to do everything himself, and the twelve-month rotation spread the load. JFB says each officer "had the charge of a tribe or particular district." Henry's outline treats the officials as part of "the magnificence of Solomon's court." The Tyndale notes add that Samuel had warned about forced labor, and that Israel's leaders later complained of it to Rehoboam.

#### Solomon's Wealth and Wisdom (vv20–34)
- **"Judah and Israel were as many as the sand by the sea. They ate and drank and were happy" (v20).** This is the language of God's promise to Abraham: "I will surely multiply your offspring as… the sand that is on the seashore" (Gen 22:17). The writer means it to be noticed. For a moment the promise looks complete. Henry: "They were cheerful in the use of their plenty."
- **"Solomon ruled over all the kingdoms from the Euphrates to the land of the Philistines and to the border of Egypt" (v21).** These were the borders promised to Abraham, "from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates" (Gen 15:18; Deut 11:24). Solomon did not annex all of this land. Many smaller kingdoms David had conquered paid tribute and kept their own kings (2 Sam 8). JFB: "All the petty kingdoms between the Euphrates and the Mediterranean were tributary to him." The great empires of Egypt and Assyria were weak at this time, leaving room for Israel to be the leading power in the region.
- **"Thirty cors of fine flour and sixty cors of meal, ten fat oxen… a hundred sheep, besides deer, gazelles" (vv22–23).** A cor was a large dry measure, about six bushels or 220 liters by the ESV footnote, though estimates vary. This was enough to feed thousands each day: the royal family, the harem, officials, servants, guards and guests. Estimates of how many it fed range widely, perhaps 15,000 or more. JFB stresses it was "not for the king's table only." It is a picture of abundance, and also of how much the court consumed from the people.
- **"Dominion over all the region west of the Euphrates from Tiphsah to Gaza" (v24).** Tiphsah was Thapsacus, a crossing point on the Euphrates in northern Syria. Gaza was the southernmost Philistine city on the coast near Egypt. "West of the Euphrates" is literally "beyond the River," a phrase used from the point of view of Mesopotamia. Some scholars think it shows the final writer was in exile in Babylon.
- **"He had peace on all sides around him" (v24).** Solomon's name comes from *shalom*. God had told David his son would be "a man of rest… I will give peace and quiet to Israel in his days" (1 Chr 22:9).
- **"Every man under his vine and under his fig tree, all the days of Solomon" (v25).** One of the Bible's best-loved pictures of peace. Every family has its own land and sits in the shade of its own vines and fig trees without fear of raiders. "From Dan even to Beersheba" means from the far north to the far south. The prophets took up the picture for God's future kingdom: "they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree, and no one shall make them afraid" (Mic 4:4; Zech 3:10).
- **"Solomon also had 40,000 stalls of horses for his chariots, and 12,000 horsemen" (v26).** The parallel in 2 Chronicles 9:25 reads 4,000 stalls, which fits Solomon's 1,400 chariots (10:26) much better. Most scholars think a copyist's error in transmitting numbers crept in here, as the Tyndale notes and Guzik say. More important is the law of the king: "he must not acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt" (Deut 17:16). Solomon bought horses from Egypt (10:28–29). Henry notes that "God had commanded that their king should not multiply horses." The same verse that describes the glory hints at the disobedience.
- **"Those officers supplied provisions… They let nothing be lacking" (v27).** The district system worked. "Barley also and straw for the horses and swift steeds" (v28) shows how far the system reached, even to feeding cavalry horses. Guzik uses this verse's phrase "each according to his duty" to encourage faithful service in one's own place.
- **"God gave Solomon wisdom and understanding beyond measure, and breadth of mind like the sand on the seashore" (v29).** "Breadth of mind" is literally "breadth of heart," a capacity to take in everything. "Like the sand" echoes v20. The people are as many as the sand, and the king's wisdom is as wide. God gave both. JFB: "high powers of mind, great capacity for receiving."
- **"Surpassed the wisdom of all the people of the east and all the wisdom of Egypt" (v30).** "The people of the east" probably means the Arabs, Edomites and Mesopotamians, known for wisdom (Job 1:3; Jer 49:7). Egypt had a long tradition of wisdom writing, such as *The Instruction of Amenemope*, which has close parallels to Proverbs 22:17–24:22. Solomon's wisdom was not cut off from the wider world. It drew on it and surpassed it.
- **"Wiser than Ethan the Ezrahite, and Heman, Calcol, and Darda, the sons of Mahol" (v31).** These were famous wise men of the time. Ethan and Heman are named in the titles of Psalms 89 and 88. First Chronicles 2:6 names Ethan, Heman, Calcol and Dara as sons of Zerah of Judah. "Sons of Mahol" may name their father, or may mean "sons of the dance," a guild of musicians. JFB says of Calcol and Darda simply, "the other two are not known."
- **"He also spoke 3,000 proverbs, and his songs were 1,005" (v32).** Only a portion survive. The book of Proverbs contains several hundred proverbs connected to him (Prov 1:1; 10:1; 25:1). Two psalms carry his name (Ps 72; 127), and the Song of Songs is called "Solomon's" (Song 1:1). JFB also counts Psalm 132.
- **"He spoke of trees, from the cedar that is in Lebanon to the hyssop that grows out of the wall" (v33).** From the grandest tree in the region to a small plant growing in cracks of stone: everything in between. JFB: "all plants, from the greatest to the least." He also studied animals, birds, reptiles and fish, the same four groups as in Genesis 1. Proverbs shows this kind of study: the ant (Prov 6:6–8), and the "four things on earth" that are small but wise (Prov 30:24–28, though that chapter is attributed to Agur). Solomon's wisdom included what we would call natural science, studied as part of God's creation.
- **"People of all nations came to hear the wisdom of Solomon" (v34).** This prepares for the queen of Sheba (10:1–13). Israel was meant to draw the nations to its God (Deut 4:6: "Surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people"). For a moment it did.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls this Israel's golden age, made possible partly by David's earlier victories and the weakness of neighboring empires. He also flags the horses as a breach of Deuteronomy 17:16, and judges the 4,000 of Chronicles more likely to be correct. He passes on a later Jewish legend of Solomon judging between a man and a serpent, which is not in the Bible. JFB credits Psalms 72, 127, 132 and the Song of Songs to Solomon. Henry ends his outline: "Thus great was Solomon, but our Lord Jesus was greater than he." The Tyndale notes see in v25 a hint of the messianic age.

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 4 is a picture of what God's kingdom looks like when it comes: a people as many as the sand, peace on every side, every family under its own vine and fig tree, plenty at the king's table, and the nations coming to hear the king's wisdom. It did not last. Solomon's heavy yoke, his horses and his foreign alliances undid it within a generation. But the prophets kept the picture and pointed it forward to the kingdom of the Messiah (Mic 4:1–4). Jesus is "greater than Solomon" (Matt 12:42), and in him "are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3). Unlike Solomon, he says, "my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matt 11:30). The nations do not merely come to hear him. They come to eat at his table, "from east and west," with Abraham (Matt 8:11).
