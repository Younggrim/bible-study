*Setting:* Still in Babylonia, about 593–592 BC. Ezekiel is in the middle of the long run of sign-acts that began in chapter 4: the clay model of Jerusalem under siege, the months spent lying on his side, the siege bread rationed by weight. Chapter 5 adds the last and most personal sign. He shaves his own head and beard with a sword and divides the hair three ways. Then, for the first time in the book, God explains in words why Jerusalem must fall. The answer is not that Babylon is too strong, but that Jerusalem, set by God at the center of the nations, has behaved worse than the nations around it. This chapter closes the first unit of signs (4:1–5:17). Its language leans heavily on the covenant curses of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28, and the exiles who heard it would have recognized the old warnings of Moses coming true.

#### Jerusalem Will Be Destroyed (vv1–17)
- **One section, two halves.** The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading. It falls into the sign itself (vv1–4) and God's explanation (vv5–17), which in turn has a charge (vv5–11) and a sentence (vv12–17). The phrase "Thus says the Lord GOD" marks the turns (vv5, 7, 8).
- **"Take a sharp sword. Use it as a barber's razor" (v1).** A sword (*ḥereb*) is a weapon of war, not a grooming tool. The instrument tells the meaning: the shaving will be done by an army. Isaiah used the same picture a century earlier, of Assyria as God's hired razor that "will shave the head and the hair of the feet, and will sweep away the beard also" (Isa 7:20).
- **"Pass it over your head and your beard" (v1).** In Israel shaving the head and beard was a sign of mourning (Isa 15:2; Jer 41:5) or of deep humiliation. When the Ammonites shaved off half the beards of David's envoys, it was an insult serious enough to start a war (2 Sam 10:4–5). For a priest it was worse still. The law forbade priests to shave their heads or the edges of their beards (Lev 21:5). Ezekiel, a priest, deliberately makes himself look disgraced and unclean. The man God has made the sign now carries the shame himself.
- **"Take balances for weighing and divide the hair" (v1).** The scales show that this judgment is measured, not random. Every hair is accounted for. God's anger is real, but it is never careless.
- **The three thirds (v2).** One third burned "in the midst of the city," probably meaning on the clay model of 4:1, once the acted siege is over. One third cut up with the sword "all around the city." One third tossed to the wind, with God's sword chasing after it. God himself explains the thirds in v12: plague and famine inside the walls, death by the sword around them, and scattering into exile with no safety even there. This is exactly what happened. Many died of starvation and disease in the siege (2 Kings 25:3; Lam 4:9), many were killed when the city fell (2 Kings 25:7, 18–21), and survivors were carried off or fled, some of them into Egypt, where Jeremiah warned the sword would overtake them (Jer 42:15–17; 44:27).
- **"Take from these a small number and bind them in the skirts of your robe" (v3).** The "skirts" (*kanaph*) are the hem or fold of the garment, where people tucked things to carry them. A few hairs are kept safe, a picture of a remnant God protects. The same word is used when Boaz is asked to spread his "wings" over Ruth (Ruth 3:9) and of God spreading his garment over Jerusalem as a bride (Ezek 16:8). The point is protection and belonging.
- **"Of these again you shall take some and cast them into the midst of the fire" (v4).** Even the remnant is not entirely safe. Some of those spared will still suffer. Many think this points to the people left in Judah under the governor Gedaliah after the fall. When Gedaliah was murdered, more bloodshed and flight followed (2 Kings 25:22–26; Jer 41–43). **"From there a fire will come out into all the house of Israel."** Judgment does not stay contained. It spreads to the whole people.
- **"This is Jerusalem. I have set her in the center of the nations" (v5).** Now God speaks plainly. The exiles may have hoped Ezekiel's model was Babylon. It was not. Geographically, the land of Israel sat on the land bridge between Egypt and Mesopotamia, at the crossroads of the ancient world. Theologically, God placed his people where the nations could see what life under the true God looked like (Deut 4:6–8). Later Ezekiel calls the land "the center of the earth," literally "the navel" (38:12). Medieval maps that put Jerusalem at the center of the world drew on this verse.
- **"She has rebelled against my rules by doing wickedness more than the nations" (v6).** The charge is not just that Jerusalem sinned, but that she sinned with more light than anyone and still did worse. "Rules" (*mishpatim*) and "statutes" (*ḥuqqot*) are the standard words for God's covenant law, used together throughout Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy. To reject them was to break the covenant.
- **"Because you are more turbulent than the nations" (v7).** The word (*hamon*, from a root meaning to roar or rage) suggests restless uproar, a people who will not be still under God's rule. **"And have not even acted according to the rules of the nations."** The pagan nations at least kept faith with their own false gods. Israel did not even do that much with the true one. Jeremiah made the same complaint: "Has a nation changed its gods, even though they are no gods? But my people have changed their glory" (Jer 2:11). A few Hebrew manuscripts and the Syriac leave out the word "not" here, which would make the verse say Israel copied the nations' ways, as 11:12 does say. The standard Hebrew text, followed by the ESV, has "not," and both are true of Israel in different ways.
- **"Behold, I, even I, am against you" (v8).** This is one of the most frightening sentences in the Bible. The phrase "I am against you" is a challenge to combat, and Ezekiel uses it repeatedly (13:8; 21:3; 26:3). God is no longer Jerusalem's defender. He is its opponent. **"In the sight of the nations."** The city chosen to display God's goodness before the world will now display his justice.
- **"What I have never yet done, and the like of which I will never do again" (v9).** This is the language of an unparalleled disaster, a way of saying "the worst ever" (compare Matt 24:21). The point is not to compare it with every other event in history but to say that the covenant curses would strike in full force.
- **"Fathers shall eat their sons in your midst, and sons shall eat their fathers" (v10).** This was one of the most terrible covenant curses: "You shall eat the flesh of your sons, and you shall eat the flesh of your daughters" (Lev 26:29; Deut 28:53–57). It had already happened during an earlier siege of Samaria (2 Kings 6:28–29), and Lamentations reports it in Jerusalem's fall: "The hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children" (Lam 4:10). Ezekiel adds the detail of sons eating fathers, so that nothing in the family is left sacred.
- **"As I live, declares the Lord GOD" (v11).** This is God's own oath, which Ezekiel records sixteen times. The living God swears by his own life, in contrast to idols that are lifeless. **"Because you have defiled my sanctuary with all your detestable things."** Here is the deepest charge, and chapter 8 will show what it means: idols and pagan rites inside the temple itself. **"Therefore I will withdraw."** The Hebrew verb (*'egra'*) normally means "diminish" or "cut back," and some take it as "I will shave you," tying back to the razor of v1. The ESV's "withdraw" fits what is coming: in chapters 10–11 the glory of the LORD leaves the temple. **"My eye will not spare, and I will have no pity"** becomes a refrain in these chapters (7:4, 9; 8:18; 9:10).
- **"A third part… a third part… a third part" (v12).** The hair sign explained, as above. The repetition is deliberate, like the tolling of a bell.
- **"Thus shall my anger spend itself… and satisfy myself" (v13).** The verb rendered "satisfy myself" is *niḥamti*, usually "I will be comforted" or "relieved." The picture is of anger brought to its full end, so that justice is complete and nothing more is owed. God does not take pleasure in the death of the wicked (18:23), but neither does he leave evil unanswered. **"They shall know that I am the LORD."** This book's great refrain appears here for the first time in judgment. **"In my jealousy."** *Qin'ah* is the burning zeal of a husband whose wife has betrayed him (Prov 6:34; Num 5:14). It is love wounded, not petty envy. God's jealousy is the other side of his covenant love (Exod 20:5; 34:14).
- **"A reproach and a taunt, a warning and a horror, to the nations" (vv14–15).** Four words pile up. The city meant to be a witness becomes a warning. Moses had foreseen exactly this: "all the nations will say, 'Why has the LORD done thus to this land?'" (Deut 29:24). **"I am the LORD; I have spoken"** appears three times in vv13–17 like a seal on a document.
- **"The deadly arrows of famine" (v16).** God as a warrior shooting arrows of famine, plague and disaster comes from the Song of Moses: "I will spend my arrows on them; they shall be wasted with hunger, and devoured by plague" (Deut 32:23–24). **"Break your supply of bread"** repeats 4:16 and Leviticus 26:26.
- **"Famine and wild beasts… pestilence and blood… the sword" (v17).** This is the standard list of covenant judgments (Lev 26:22–26), and Ezekiel will return to it as "my four disastrous acts of judgment" (14:21). When a land is emptied of people, wild animals move in (2 Kings 17:25). The chapter ends where it began, with the sword.
- **Commentators:** All three read the hair as Jerusalem's people and the thirds as famine and plague, sword, and scattering, and all three connect the few hairs in the robe, later partly burned, with the remnant under Gedaliah. Henry adds a sharp line: "Jerusalem had been the head, but, having degenerated, had become as the hair." Guzik notes that hair marked a priest's consecration, so Ezekiel's shaving struck at his priestly identity. He also hears in "I, even I, am against you" the cry of a challenger in single combat. On v5 Guzik and JFB both stress privilege as the measure of guilt, JFB saying God regarded Jerusalem as "center of the whole earth," and Henry adding that the city was placed there to be a light to the nations. On v7 JFB writes that Israel was not "as tenacious of the true religion as the nations have been of the false." JFB says God's being comforted in v13 is "expressed in condescension to man's conceptions," that is, it is God speaking in human terms. JFB also extends the unparalleled judgment of v9 to the Roman destruction of AD 70 and a final tribulation, which goes beyond what the text says. Guzik leaves open whether the "wild beasts" of v17 are literal animals or a figure for Babylon, and leans literal in light of 14:21. The Tyndale notes say the judgments flow directly from the curses of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28, because Israel had broken the covenant.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel took the shame of the judgment on his own head, shaved by a sword, before it fell on the city. He could show the people their judgment, but he could not take it away. When Jesus came to the same city, he wept over it and warned that it would again be surrounded (Luke 19:41–44). Then he went outside its walls and took the curse of the covenant on himself: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). The God who said "I, even I, am against you" is the same God who in Christ says "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31). And the few hairs tucked into the prophet's robe point to a remnant kept by grace, which Paul says is still there: "a remnant, chosen by grace" (Rom 11:5).
