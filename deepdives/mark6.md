*Setting:* Galilee and the Sea of Galilee, probably in the spring of AD 29, a year or so into Jesus' public ministry. In chapters 4 and 5 Jesus stilled a storm, freed a man from a legion of demons, healed a bleeding woman and raised a dead girl. Chapter 6 brings sharp contrasts. His hometown rejects him. He sends the twelve out to do what he has been doing. Mark breaks into that story to tell, in flashback, how Herod Antipas killed John the Baptist, a warning of what happens to God's messengers. Then come two of Jesus' best-known miracles, the feeding of the five thousand and the walking on the water, which John's Gospel places just before Passover (John 6:4). The chapter keeps pressing one question: who is this man, and why do even his closest friends not yet understand?

#### Jesus Rejected at Nazareth (vv1–6)
- **"He… came to his hometown" (v1).** Nazareth, a small village in the hills of lower Galilee, perhaps a few hundred people, about twenty-five miles southwest of Capernaum. He comes as a rabbi, "and his disciples followed him."
- **"Where did this man get these things?" (v2).** They cannot deny his wisdom or his "mighty works" (*dynameis*). Their question is where it came from. Not from Nazareth, they think, and not from a rabbinic school. The scribes had already given one answer: from Satan (3:22).
- **"Is not this the carpenter?" (v3).** The word is *tektōn*, a builder or craftsman who worked in wood and often stone. To his neighbors, Jesus was a manual laborer with no education. Matthew softens it to "the carpenter's son" (Matt 13:55).
- **"The son of Mary" (v3).** A man was normally named by his father: "son of Joseph." Calling him "son of Mary" may simply mean Joseph had died. But it could also be a sly insult, hinting at gossip about his birth (compare John 8:41). Joseph is never mentioned in Mark.
- **"Brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon… his sisters" (v3).** James later led the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13; Gal 1:19) and is traditionally the author of the letter of James. Judas (Jude) is traditionally the author of the letter of Jude. On how Christians understand these brothers and sisters, see the note on 3:31–35.
- **"They took offense at him" (v3).** The word is *skandalizomai*, "they stumbled over him." Familiarity got in the way. The very ordinariness of his life among them was the stumbling block.
- **"A prophet is not without honor, except in his hometown" (v4).** A proverb that has proved true in every age.
- **"He could do no mighty work there" (v5).** A startling sentence, which Matthew rephrases: "he did not do many mighty works there, because of their unbelief" (Matt 13:58). Jesus had not lost his power. He still healed "a few sick people." But his miracles were not shows to compel belief. They were signs of the kingdom given in response to faith. Where no one came to him in faith, there was little for him to do.
- **"He marveled because of their unbelief" (v6).** Jesus is amazed twice in the Gospels: at the faith of a Gentile centurion (Luke 7:9) and here at the unbelief of his own neighbors.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry explains "could do no mighty work" this way: "he could not, because people would not make application to him, nor sue for his favours." Guzik reads both "carpenter" and "son of Mary" as put-downs, the second citing Lane on rumors about Jesus' birth, and he takes the mention of sisters as evidence that Mary had other children. The Tyndale notes take "son of Mary" more simply as a sign that Joseph had died. JFB gives no comment here and refers to its notes on Luke 4.

#### Jesus Sends Out the Twelve Apostles (vv7–13)
- **"He went about among the villages teaching" (v6).** Rejected in one village, he goes on to others.
- **"Began to send them out two by two" (v7).** This is the second purpose of their calling: "to be with him and… send them out" (3:14). Two by two gave companionship and help, and fulfilled the law's requirement of two witnesses (Deut 19:15). The early church kept the pattern (Acts 13:2; 15:39–40).
- **"Nothing for their journey except a staff… sandals… not… two tunics" (vv8–9).** Travel light, trust God, and depend on the hospitality of the people they serve. "Bag" (*pēra*) could mean a beggar's bag; traveling Cynic philosophers carried one to collect donations. The apostles were not to look like them. Matthew and Luke record "no staff" (Matt 10:10; Luke 9:3), while Mark allows one; the Gospels summarize differently, but the point is the same: go with only what is at hand.
- **"Shake off the dust that is on your feet as a testimony against them" (v11).** Jews returning from Gentile lands sometimes shook the dust off their feet so as not to bring unclean soil into the land of Israel. To do this to a Jewish town was a strong sign: by rejecting the message, it had put itself outside God's people. Paul and Barnabas did it at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:51).
- **"Proclaimed that people should repent" (v12).** The same message as John's and Jesus' (1:4, 15).
- **"Anointed with oil many who were sick and healed them" (v13).** Olive oil was the common medicine of the day (Luke 10:34; Isa 1:6), and the anointing may also have been a sign of God's blessing and of the Spirit's work. James later tells church elders to pray over the sick, "anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (Jas 5:14). Christian traditions have practiced this in different ways ever since.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the apostles "had received, that they might give, had learned, that they might teach." He thinks the gift of miraculous healing that went with the anointing has ceased, "and therefore that sign must cease with it," a view many but not all Christians share. Guzik follows Liefeld that the forbidden "bag" was a beggar's or traveling philosopher's pouch, and notes that the word for "anointed" (*aleiphō*) was used for rubbing on oil medicinally, with Barclay citing the physician Galen. JFB refers this section to its notes on Matthew 10.

#### The Death of John the Baptist (vv14–29)
- **"King Herod heard of it" (v14).** This is Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great. When his father died in 4 BC, Rome divided the kingdom, and Antipas received Galilee and Perea as "tetrarch," a ruler of part of a province (Matt 14:1; Luke 3:19). He was never officially a king. The people may have called him that out of courtesy. Mark's "King Herod" may carry irony, since years later Antipas's request to Rome for the title of king led to his downfall.
- **"John the Baptist has been raised from the dead" (v14).** People were guessing who Jesus was: John come back, Elijah (Mal 4:5), or another of the prophets.
- **"John, whom I beheaded, has been raised" (v16).** Herod's guilty conscience speaks. The man he killed seems to be haunting him.
- **"For the sake of Herodias, his brother Philip's wife" (v17).** Here Mark goes back in time to explain. Herodias was a granddaughter of Herod the Great, so she had married her uncle Philip (called simply Herod in Josephus, a son of Herod the Great who lived as a private citizen, not the tetrarch Philip of Luke 3:1). Antipas fell for her on a visit, and both divorced their spouses to marry each other.
- **"It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife" (v18).** The law forbade marrying a brother's wife while the brother was alive (Lev 18:16; 20:21). John said it publicly, to the ruler's face, and kept saying it. The Jewish historian Josephus also describes John's arrest and death. He gives a political reason, that Antipas feared John's influence over the crowds might lead to revolt, and he says John was held at Machaerus, a fortress-palace east of the Dead Sea. The two accounts fit together: a preacher who denounces the ruler's marriage before huge crowds is a political danger too.
- **"Herodias had a grudge against him" (v19).** The word suggests she held it in, nursing it. Like Jezebel against Elijah (1 Kgs 19:2), the queen hated the prophet who would not keep quiet.
- **"Herod feared John… he was greatly perplexed, and yet he heard him gladly" (v20).** A man torn in two. He knew John was "righteous and holy." He liked listening to him. But he never acted on what he heard. Hearing gladly is not the same as obeying.
- **"An opportunity came when Herod on his birthday gave a banquet" (v21).**
- **"Herodias's daughter came in and danced" (v22).** Josephus names Herodias's daughter as Salome. Manuscripts differ here; some read "his daughter Herodias," which the ESV notes. She was probably in her early to mid teens. A princess dancing before a room of drinking men would have been shocking, and many think the dance was sensual. Herodias may have sent her in on purpose.
- **"Up to half of my kingdom" (v23).** A boastful phrase from the book of Esther (Esth 5:3, 6). Antipas did not have a kingdom to give; he governed at Rome's pleasure. But in front of his guests he plays the great king.
- **"The head of John the Baptist… on a platter" (vv24–25).** The girl asks her mother. Herodias does not hesitate.
- **"The king was exceedingly sorry, but because of his oaths and his guests" (v26).** "Exceedingly sorry" is *perilypos*, the same word Jesus uses of his own sorrow in Gethsemane (14:34). Herod is distressed but more afraid of losing face. A rash oath to do evil should be broken, not kept. Like Pilate later, he condemns a man he knows is innocent because of the crowd around him (15:15).
- **"He went and beheaded him in the prison" (v27).** The greatest of the prophets (Matt 11:11) dies at a drunken party, because a weak man made a foolish promise to a girl and her mother. Josephus records that a few years later Aretas IV, the Nabatean king whose daughter Antipas had divorced for Herodias, defeated Antipas's army, and many Jews saw it as God's judgment for John's death. In AD 39 Antipas, urged by Herodias, asked the emperor Caligula for the title of king. Instead he was accused of plotting, stripped of his lands and exiled to Gaul, and Herodias went with him.
- **"His disciples… took his body and laid it in a tomb" (v29).** The words echo what Joseph of Arimathea will do for Jesus (15:46). John, the forerunner, goes before Jesus in death too. Mark has already called his arrest a "handing over" (1:14), the word used for Jesus' betrayal.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "A guilty conscience needs no accuser or tormentor but itself." On Herod's listening: "He did not hear him with terror as Felix heard Paul, but heard him with pleasure," and yet stayed unrepentant. On the oath he says, "I can scarcely think he would have made such an unlimited promise, but that he knew what she would ask," a suspicion that goes beyond the text. JFB says the murdered prophet "haunted his guilty breast like a specter," calls him "king… only by courtesy," and blames Herod's "false shame." Guzik notes that *perilypos* is the word later used of Jesus in Gethsemane, and tells the rest of Antipas's story: defeat, exile and death.

#### Jesus Feeds the Five Thousand (vv30–44)
- **"The apostles returned to Jesus and told him all that they had done and taught" (v30).** Outside 3:14 this is the only place Mark calls them "apostles," fitting, since they have just been "sent." They report back to the one who sent them.
- **"Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while" (v31).** After hard work, Jesus gives them rest. "For many were coming and going, and they had no leisure even to eat."
- **"They ran there on foot from all the towns and got there ahead of them" (v33).** The boat crossed part of the lake; the crowd ran around the shore. Luke says the place was near Bethsaida (Luke 9:10), on the northeast side, outside Antipas's territory.
- **"He had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (v34).** The phrase comes from Moses, who prayed that Israel would not be "as sheep that have no shepherd" (Num 27:17), and from Ezekiel 34, where God condemns Israel's failed shepherds and promises, "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep" (Ezek 34:15, 23). The king of Galilee has just held a banquet that ended in a murder. Here is the true shepherd-king. His first act of compassion is to teach them "many things." Then he feeds them.
- **"You give them something to eat" (v37).** The "you" is emphatic. Jesus has just sent them out with his authority to heal and cast out demons. Now he tests whether they will think of his power. They think only of money. **"Two hundred denarii"** was about eight months' wages for a laborer.
- **"How many loaves do you have? Go and see" (v38).** **"Five, and two fish."** John says they came from a boy's lunch, small barley loaves, the bread of the poor, and two small dried fish (John 6:9). Jesus begins with what they have.
- **"Sit down in groups on the green grass… by hundreds and by fifties" (vv39–40).** "Green grass" fits the spring before Passover, the only season when those hills are green, a detail that matches John's timing. It also recalls Psalm 23: "He makes me lie down in green pastures." "Groups" is literally "garden beds," a picture of the colorful crowd arranged in rows on the hillside like plots of flowers. The groups of hundreds and fifties echo the way Moses organized Israel in the wilderness (Exod 18:21). God's people are being fed in the wilderness again.
- **"He looked up to heaven and said a blessing and broke the loaves and gave them to the disciples" (v41).** The usual Jewish blessing at a meal thanked God: "Blessed are you, LORD our God, King of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth." The four verbs, took, blessed, broke, gave, are the same ones Mark uses at the Last Supper (14:22). The meal looks back to manna in the wilderness and forward to the Lord's Supper and to the great banquet of the kingdom (Isa 25:6).
- **"They all ate and were satisfied" (v42).** Elisha fed a hundred with twenty loaves (2 Kgs 4:42–44). Jesus feeds thousands with five.
- **"Twelve baskets full of broken pieces" (v43).** A *kophinos* was a wicker basket a traveling Jew might carry. Twelve, one for each apostle, perhaps also for each tribe of Israel. There is more left over than there was at the start.
- **"Five thousand men" (v44).** The word is "males." Matthew adds "besides women and children" (Matt 14:21), so the whole crowd may have been much larger. This is the only miracle besides the resurrection recorded in all four Gospels.
- **Commentators:** Henry on rest: "There is no remaining rest for the people of God till they come to heaven," and on the crowd, they "had no shepherd, none to lead and guide them in the right way." JFB says the grass was "the rank grass of those bushy wastes" and that the seating let everyone see "at a glance the number fed." It also lists Jesus' reasons for withdrawing, rest and grief over John among them. Guzik follows Barclay that the word for "groups" pictures rows in a vegetable garden, and Lane that Jesus blessed God for the food, as Jewish custom did, rather than blessing the bread itself. He also draws the lesson that God begins with what we already have.

#### Jesus Walks on the Water (vv45–52)
- **"Immediately he made his disciples get into the boat" (v45).** "Made" is a strong word: he compelled them. John explains why: the crowd wanted to "take him by force to make him king" (John 6:15). Jesus sends the disciples away before they catch the fever, then dismisses the crowd himself.
- **"He went up on the mountain to pray" (v46).** The second of Mark's three scenes of Jesus at prayer (1:35; 14:32), each at a time when the easy road was offered to him.
- **"He saw that they were making headway painfully, for the wind was against them" (v48).** "Painfully" is the word for being tormented or tortured, the same word used by the demon in 5:7. They had been rowing for hours against the wind. **"About the fourth watch of the night"** was between 3 and 6 in the morning, using the Roman reckoning of four night watches, a hint that Mark was writing for Romans.
- **"He came to them, walking on the sea" (v48).** In the Old Testament, God alone "trampled the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8) and made "a path in the mighty waters" (Isa 43:16; Ps 77:19).
- **"He meant to pass by them" (v48).** A puzzling line. The best explanation lies in the Old Testament. When God showed his glory to Moses, he "passed by" him (Exod 33:19, 22; 34:6). When God appeared to Elijah, "the LORD passed by" (1 Kgs 19:11). Jesus is not trying to avoid them. He is showing himself to them as God showed himself to Moses and Elijah. Others read it as a test, as on the Emmaus road, when Jesus "acted as if he were going farther" (Luke 24:28).
- **"They thought it was a ghost" (v49).**
- **"Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid" (v50).** "It is I" is *egō eimi*, literally "I am." It is the normal way to say "It's me," but in this setting it echoes the name God gave himself to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exod 3:14), and God's repeated words in Isaiah, "I am he… fear not" (Isa 41:10; 43:10–13). Mark leaves it to his readers to hear both.
- **"He got into the boat with them, and the wind ceased" (v51).** Matthew adds that Peter walked on the water and sank (Matt 14:28–31). Mark, writing from Peter's preaching, leaves it out, perhaps because Peter did not care to tell it of himself, though that is only a guess.
- **"For they did not understand about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (v52).** A sobering verdict on the twelve. The word is the same one used of the Pharisees in 3:5. The feeding should have taught them who Jesus is: the God who feeds his people in the wilderness. Instead they were simply astonished. Even disciples can see miracles and miss their meaning. Mark keeps showing this until Jesus asks, "Do you not yet understand?" (8:21).
- **Commentators:** JFB, comparing Genesis 32:26 and Luke 24:28, reads "would have passed by them" as a test of their desire for him, not a real intention to leave them, and it calls v52 "What a singular statement!" Guzik follows Lane that Jesus sent the disciples off so they would not be swept up in the crowd's hopes of a warrior Messiah, and suggests, with Robertson, that Peter may have left his own sinking out of the story he told. The Tyndale notes, like many modern scholars, read "pass by" as language for God revealing his glory, as to Moses and Elijah. Henry notes that the disciples missed the lesson of the loaves, and so were amazed at what they should have expected.

#### Jesus Heals the Sick in Gennesaret (vv53–56)
- **"They came to land at Gennesaret" (v53).** They had set out for Bethsaida (v45), but the wind took them to Gennesaret, a fertile plain on the northwest shore, south of Capernaum.
- **"The people immediately recognized him" (v54).** In contrast to Nazareth, where his neighbors knew him and would not come to him, here strangers recognize him and run.
- **"They laid the sick in the marketplaces and implored him that they might touch even the fringe of his garment" (v56).** The "fringe" was the tassel the law told Israelite men to wear (Num 15:38–40). The woman in 5:27 had done this, and the story had spread. **"As many as touched it were made well."** "Made well" is *sōzō*, "saved." Mark ends the chapter with a picture of the kingdom: where people come to Jesus, they are healed.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the healings as a reminder not to make God too small. JFB comments on the landing place and the eagerness of the crowds to touch his garment.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter sets two kings side by side. Herod, who has no real kingdom, throws a lavish banquet for the powerful and ends it with the murder of God's prophet. Jesus, the true King, finds the crowds like sheep without a shepherd, teaches them and spreads a meal in the wilderness for thousands, with baskets left over. Then he comes across the water as only God can, passing by like the glory shown to Moses and saying, "I am; do not be afraid." Nazareth was offended by him, and his own disciples' hearts were still hard. Yet he keeps feeding, keeps coming to them in the storm, and keeps healing all who touch him. The shepherd who feeds the sheep will soon lay down his life for them, and the bread he breaks here points to his own body broken for them.
