*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Maskil of David, when Doeg, the Edomite, came and told Saul, 'David has come to the house of Ahimelech.'" A *maskil* is probably a psalm of instruction or skill, from the verb "to have insight" (see 47:7). The title takes us to one of the ugliest episodes in 1 Samuel (chs 21–22). David had fled from Saul with no food and no weapon. He came to the town of Nob, where the priests kept the tabernacle, and told the priest Ahimelech that he was on a secret mission for the king, which was a lie. Ahimelech gave him the holy bread and Goliath's sword (21:1–9). Watching was Doeg, an Edomite, Saul's chief herdsman, who happened to be "detained before the LORD" at the sanctuary that day (21:7). Edom descended from Esau, Jacob's brother, and the old rivalry ran deep. Later, when Saul accused his own men of conspiring with David, Doeg spoke up and reported what he had seen (22:9–10). It was technically true, but it painted an innocent priest as a traitor. Saul ordered the priests killed. His guards refused to lift a hand against "the priests of the LORD." Doeg did not refuse. He killed eighty-five priests, then struck down the whole town of Nob, "both man and woman, child and infant, ox, donkey and sheep" (22:17–19). Only one son of Ahimelech, Abiathar, escaped to David, who said, "I knew on that day, when Doeg the Edomite was there, that he would surely tell Saul. I have occasioned the death of all the persons of your father's house" (22:22). Psalm 52 is David's answer. It is a short psalm in three parts: a charge against the boaster (vv1–4), his sentence (vv5–7), and the contrast of the one who trusts God (vv8–9).

#### Why Do You Boast of Evil? (vv1–4)
- **"Why do you boast of evil, O mighty man?" (v1).** "Mighty man" (*gibbor*) is the word for a war hero, a champion (JFB: "literally, 'hero'"). It is bitter irony. Doeg's great feat was killing unarmed priests, women and babies. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's scorn for a "mighty man" who killed men who never touched a sword. Doeg apparently was proud of it, or at least of the favor it won him with Saul. Henry: "It is bad to do ill, but it is worse to boast of it and glory in it when we have done."
- **"The steadfast love of God endures all the day" (v1).** The second line answers the first. Doeg boasts. God's *chesed*, his loyal covenant love, keeps going "all the day," without a break. The word for God here is *El*, the short name that stresses might, as if to say: here is real might, against Doeg's pretend heroism. The boaster's moment will pass. God's love does not. This sets up the theme of the psalm: what lasts, and what does not.
- **"Your tongue plots destruction, like a sharp razor" (v2).** The weapon Doeg used first was not a sword but his tongue. A razor cuts quickly and cleanly, often before the victim notices. JFB: it cuts quietly but deeply. "Destruction" (*hawwot*) means ruin, calamity. Remember this word; it comes back in v7.
- **"You worker of deceit" (v2).** What Doeg said was factually true: David did come to Nob, and Ahimelech did help him. But he left out that Ahimelech knew nothing of David's flight and thought he was helping the king's loyal son-in-law (22:14–15). A half-truth told to destroy is still deceit. Henry: "The more there is of craft and contrivance in any wickedness the more there is of the devil in it."
- **"You love evil more than good, and lying more than speaking what is right. Selah" (v3).** It is not that Doeg slipped. He preferred evil. "Love… more than" is a comparison. Given the choice, he picked the lie.
- **"You love all words that devour, O deceitful tongue" (v4).** "Devour" is literally "swallow" (JFB), words that gulp people down whole. David speaks to the tongue itself, as if it had a life of its own. James says the same: "the tongue is a fire, a world of unrighteousness… a restless evil, full of deadly poison" (Jas 3:6, 8). The Tyndale notes list the many pictures the Psalms use for such speech: swords, arrows, a serpent's fangs (55:21; 57:4; 64:3; 140:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is bad to speak devouring words, but it is worse to love them either in others or in ourselves." Guzik, citing Maclaren, notes that the psalm traces violent deeds back to their source, the tongue, and with Boice suggests Doeg held on to what he knew until it would earn him favor. JFB reads "mighty man" as irony for Doeg's "might in slander."
- **David's own part.** David's lie to Ahimelech set the tragedy in motion, and he admitted it to Abiathar: "I have occasioned the death" (1 Sam 22:22). The psalm does not deny that. But it puts the blame where it finally belongs: on the man who chose to kill.

#### God Will Break You Down (vv5–7)
- **"But God will break you down forever" (v5).** "But God" turns the psalm, as in 49:15. Four strong verbs follow, each a picture of total ruin. "Break down" (*natats*) is the word for tearing down a pagan altar or a house (Exod 34:13; Lev 14:45). The Tyndale notes mention it is also used for smashing an unclean pot (Lev 11:35). Doeg will be demolished like a false altar.
- **"He will snatch and tear you from your tent" (v5).** "Snatch" (*chathah*) is the verb for scooping up hot coals (Prov 25:22; Isa 30:14). Maclaren pictures a coal dragged out of the fire with tongs. "Tear from your tent" means pull him out of his home as a tent peg is pulled out of the ground.
- **"He will uproot you from the land of the living. Selah" (v5).** The last picture is a tree pulled up by the roots. Keep it in mind for v8. Many of these verbs could be read as a prayer ("may God…"), as VanGemeren takes them. Either way, it is God's justice David looks for. He does not take revenge on Doeg himself.
- **"The righteous shall see and fear, and shall laugh at him" (v6).** In Hebrew "see" and "fear" sound alike (*yir'u… yira'u*), as the Tyndale notes point out. The righteous see God's judgment and are filled with awe. JFB: "regard with religious awe." Their laughter is not cruel glee at someone's suffering. It is the laughter that greets the collapse of something proud and foolish, the same laughter God has at the plotting nations (2:4). JFB: they laugh "for his folly." The Tyndale notes add that the godly rejoice in God's justice, not in the misfortune itself.
- **"See the man who would not make God his refuge" (v7).** "The man" is *gever*, which also means a strong man, an echo of "mighty man" in v1 (JFB: "literally, 'the mighty man' or 'hero'"). The hero is now a warning sign: look at what happens to such a man. His fault is named first. Not the murders, but the choice behind them: he "would not make God his refuge."
- **"But trusted in the abundance of his riches" (v7).** Doeg wanted wealth and position. Saul had asked his men, "Will the son of Jesse give every one of you fields and vineyards?" (1 Sam 22:7), and Doeg saw his chance. Psalm 49 warned about exactly this.
- **"And sought refuge in his own destruction!" (v7).** Here the word from v2 returns: *hawwah*, "destruction." The ESV catches the irony: the destruction he plotted for others was what he trusted in, and it became his own destruction. JFB reads it as trusting in his "mischief" in place of God. A refuge of evil is no refuge.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those wretchedly deceive themselves that think to support themselves in their power and wealth without God and religion." Guzik, citing Maclaren, says people who have suffered under cruel rulers understand the joy of v6 better than comfortable critics do, and quotes Caryl's warning that abundance makes trusting in riches all too easy. JFB notes that "so" (v5) implies God will repay Doeg in kind.

#### Like a Green Olive Tree (vv8–9)
- **"But I am like a green olive tree in the house of God" (v8).** The second "but" sets David against Doeg. Doeg will be uprooted (v5); David is like a tree planted where it cannot be uprooted. The olive is evergreen and one of the longest-lived trees in the world. Olive trees in Israel today are many centuries old, and they bear fruit for generations. "Green" means fresh, full of sap (JFB). Guzik cites Kidner on the picture of a tree in full sap in a sacred courtyard. There may have been trees in the tabernacle courts, and Psalm 92:13 speaks of the righteous "planted in the house of the LORD." Either way the point is that David's life is rooted in God's presence.
- **The irony of the place.** Doeg was in the house of God too. He was "detained before the LORD" at Nob the day he saw David (1 Sam 21:7). He stood in the sanctuary and was not changed by it. Being near God's house is not the same as being planted there.
- **"I trust in the steadfast love of God forever and ever" (v8).** The word from v1 returns: *chesed*. Doeg trusted in riches (v7). David trusts God's steadfast love. And it is that trust, not David's strength, that makes him like a living tree. Jeremiah uses the same picture: "Blessed is the man who trusts in the LORD… He is like a tree planted by water" (Jer 17:7–8; Ps 1:3).
- **"I will thank you forever, because you have done it" (v9).** "Done it" has no object in Hebrew. David thanks God for what he has done before it is visible, as though it were already finished. JFB supplies "preserved me." The judgment of v5 had not happened yet when David wrote. Faith speaks of it as done.
- **"I will wait for your name, for it is good, in the presence of the godly" (v9).** "Wait" (*qavah*) means to wait with hope, like a stretched cord. Boasters cannot wait. They grab what they want now. The one who trusts God can wait for God to act. "Your name" is God's revealed character, and it "is good," in sharp contrast to Doeg, who loved evil "more than good" (v3). And David will do this "in the presence of the godly," among God's people. The psalm that began with a lonely man surrounded by violence ends in the company of the faithful.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those that by faith and love dwell in the house of God shall be like green olive-trees there." JFB reads the house of God as "communion with God," and ties the olive tree to Psalm 1:3 and Jeremiah 11:16. Guzik, with Spurgeon, reads waiting on God's name as a patience that outlasts the boasting of the mighty.

#### Pointing to Christ
Doeg's story echoes into the New Testament. Jesus himself recalled David at Nob, eating the holy bread "in the time of Abiathar the high priest" (Mark 2:25–26), to show that he, the son of David, was Lord of the Sabbath. And Jesus knew what it was to be betrayed by a man who knew where he would be. Judas, like Doeg, went to the rulers with information, sold an innocent man for money (Matt 26:14–16), and stood near the holiest things without being changed by them. Jesus warned that "on the day of judgment people will give account for every careless word they speak" (Matt 12:36). He is also the true green olive tree. He prayed in an olive grove, Gethsemane, whose name means "oil press," and there he chose God's will over his own (Mark 14:32–36). Paul uses the olive tree for God's people, rooted in God's promises, with Gentiles grafted in to share "the nourishing root of the olive tree" (Rom 11:17). Those who trust Christ are planted in the house of God, and nothing can uproot them, because nothing "will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Rom 8:39).
