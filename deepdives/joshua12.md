*Setting:* The fighting is over for now. Chapter 11 closed with "the land had rest from war," and before the book turns to dividing the land (chapters 13–21), it pauses for a victory roll. Ancient kings, especially in Egypt, carved lists of conquered cities on temple walls to celebrate their campaigns. Joshua 12 is Israel's version, but the glory goes to the LORD. The chapter has two parts: the kings Moses defeated east of the Jordan about a year earlier (Num 21), and the thirty-one kings defeated under Joshua west of the Jordan. It is a list, and it reads like one, but every name was a fortified city that had once seemed impossible to take.

#### Kings Defeated by Moses (vv1–6)
- **"Beyond the Jordan toward the sunrise" (v1).** The east side of the river. The land runs "from the Valley of the Arnon to Mount Hermon." The Arnon is the deep gorge that enters the Dead Sea halfway down its eastern shore, Moab's northern border. Hermon is the snow-capped mountain about 25 miles north of the Sea of Galilee. Israel's conquests started at the Arnon (Num 21:13–24).
- **Why start with Moses?** The book could have listed only Joshua's victories. Instead it begins with the ones under Moses, so the two campaigns are seen as one work of the same God. It also reminds the western tribes that the two and a half tribes on the far side of the river are full members of Israel. That point will matter a great deal in chapter 22.
- **"Sihon king of the Amorites who lived at Heshbon" (vv2–3).** Sihon had taken this land from Moab (Num 21:26), and he refused Israel's request to pass peacefully (Num 21:21–23). His kingdom ran north from the Arnon to the Jabbok River, and west to the Jordan valley as far up as the "Sea of Chinneroth," the Sea of Galilee. Beth-jeshimoth was where Israel camped on the plains of Moab before crossing (Num 33:49), and Pisgah was the ridge from which Moses viewed the land before he died (Deut 34:1).
- **"Og king of Bashan, one of the remnant of the Rephaim" (v4).** The Rephaim were an ancient people remembered for their great size (Deut 2:11, 20–21). Deuteronomy 3:11 notes that Og's iron bed (or perhaps his basalt coffin) was about thirteen feet long. Bashan, the fertile high plateau east and northeast of the Sea of Galilee, was famous for its cattle and oak trees (Ps 22:12; Isa 2:13). Og is the only king in the chapter said to live in two cities, Ashtaroth and Edrei, a sign of a larger kingdom. Ashtaroth is named after the goddess Astarte. Matthew Henry guesses one was a summer palace and the other a winter one.
- **"Mount Hermon and Salecah and all Bashan to the boundary of the Geshurites and the Maacathites" (v5).** These two small peoples lived north and east of the Sea of Galilee. Israel did not drive them out (13:13). Much later, David married a princess of Geshur, and her son was Absalom (2 Sam 3:3).
- **"Moses, the servant of the LORD… gave their land for a possession" (v6).** "Servant of the LORD" is said of Moses twice in one verse. He gave Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh their land east of the Jordan (Num 32), on the condition that their fighting men would cross and help their brothers conquer the west. They kept that promise (1:12–18; 4:12).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the chapter repeats Moses' victories because "fresh mercies must not drown the remembrance of former mercies." He adds that what Moses did east of the Jordan served "as an example to Joshua" for the dividing to come. JFB calls the chapter "a recapitulation of the conquests" and treats vv1–6 in a single note. Guzik admits a list like this can feel tedious, but argues that it was anything but dull for families whose farms and futures depended on knowing which land was theirs.

#### Kings Defeated by Joshua (vv7–24)
- **"From Baal-gad in the Valley of Lebanon to Mount Halak" (v7).** The same north and south limits as 11:17. The Valley of Lebanon is the long valley between the Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon mountain ranges. Mount Halak, "the bare mountain," is in the far south, rising toward Seir, the hills of Edom.
- **"In the hill country, in the lowland, in the Arabah, in the slopes, in the wilderness, and in the Negeb" (v8).** Six kinds of land, and then six peoples: Hittites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites and Jebusites. The list echoes God's promise at the burning bush (Exod 3:8). What God named, he gave.
- **"The king of Jericho, one; the king of Ai… one" (v9).** The list begins with the two cities of chapters 6–8, in the order they fell. Each line ends with the word "one," like a tally mark scratched on a wall, one more victory counted.
- **The southern kings (vv10–16).** Jerusalem, Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish and Eglon are the five kings hanged at Makkedah (10:23–26). Gezer's king was defeated in the field (10:33), and Debir, Libnah and Makkedah fell in the southern sweep (10:28–39). Some names are new. Hormah and Arad, in the Negeb, recall an earlier victory near the end of the wilderness years (Num 21:1–3). Geder and Adullam were in the lowlands. Adullam's cave later sheltered David when he fled from Saul (1 Sam 22:1).
- **"The king of Bethel, one" (v16).** Bethel was close to Ai, and its men had joined in defending Ai (8:17), but the book never describes Bethel's capture. Judges 1:22–26 tells how the house of Joseph later took it, with the help of a man who showed them the way in. Here, as with Jerusalem and Gezer, a king defeated was not always a city settled. Jerusalem stayed a Jebusite city until David (15:63; 2 Sam 5:6–9), and Gezer stayed Canaanite until Solomon's day (16:10; 1 Kings 9:16).
- **The central kings (vv17–18).** Tappuah, Hepher, Aphek and Lasharon lie in the middle of the land and along the coastal plain of Sharon. Joshua tells no story of fighting in the central hills, around Shechem. Yet Israel gathered there for the covenant ceremony at Mount Ebal (8:30–35), and the land was evidently under Israel's control. The list fills in campaigns the story left out. Aphek, on the coastal plain, is where the Philistines later camped before capturing the ark (1 Sam 4:1).
- **The northern kings (vv19–24).** Madon, Hazor, Shimron-meron and Achshaph are the alliance of 11:1. Taanach and Megiddo guarded the passes through the Carmel ridge into the great Jezreel Valley, the most fought-over ground in the land. Judges 1:27 admits Manasseh could not drive their people out, and the battle against Sisera was fought "at Taanach, by the waters of Megiddo" (Judg 5:19). Megiddo's name lives on in "Armageddon," the "mount of Megiddo" of Revelation 16:16. Kedesh in Galilee later became a city of refuge (20:7), Jokneam sits at the foot of Mount Carmel, and Dor was a port on the coast.
- **"The king of Goiim in Galilee" (v23).** *Goiim* means "nations." Galilee was a mixed region from early on, and Isaiah would later call it "Galilee of the nations" (Isa 9:1), the verse Matthew quotes when Jesus begins his ministry there (Matt 4:15). The ESV here follows the Greek Septuagint. The Hebrew text reads "Goiim in Gilgal," a different Gilgal from Israel's camp, perhaps on the coastal plain. Either reading names a real city-state whose exact site is uncertain.
- **"The king of Tirzah, one" (v24).** Tirzah, a lovely city in the northern hills (Song 6:4), later became the first capital of the northern kingdom of Israel, until Omri built Samaria (1 Kings 14:17; 16:23–24).
- **"In all, thirty-one kings" (v24).** Thirty-one kings in a land roughly the size of New Jersey or Wales shows how divided Canaan was. Letters found at Amarna in Egypt show the same picture a little earlier: dozens of small city-kingdoms, each with its own king, quarreling with one another. A king in this world might rule one walled town and its farms. The number also tells how complete God's victory was. The Greek Septuagint's list differs at a few points and gives a smaller total, but the Hebrew counts thirty-one.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the list shows "God had been as good as his word," and notes that a land able to support so many kingdoms must have been fruitful. He says these kings "contented themselves with the government, each of them, of one city," small in territory but absolute over their people. He sees the chapter as both a conclusion to the wars and "a preface to the history of the dividing of Canaan." JFB calls the kingdoms "petty and insignificant" and says the towns that were not the scenes of great events "do not require a particular notice," so he passes over vv8–24. Guzik makes two points. Real names of real kings and cities show this is history, not legend. And a list like this is a record of what God has done, worth rehearsing, as believers do well to count what God's grace has already conquered in their lives. He adds that every ruling power was broken, yet each tribe still had ground to take. The Tyndale notes call the list a serious and joyful recital that honored God's name.

#### Pointing to Christ
A list of beaten kings sounds like an odd place to find Jesus, but the New Testament uses the same picture. Paul writes that Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them" at the cross (Col 2:15), and that he must reign "until he has put all his enemies under his feet" (1 Cor 15:25). Joshua's roll of thirty-one kings was real and costly, yet it was not the end: Jerusalem, Gezer and Megiddo would trouble Israel for centuries. The name Megiddo itself turns up again at the end of the Bible, at the last battle, where the Lamb conquers "for he is Lord of lords and King of kings" (Rev 17:14). On that day the list of defeated enemies will be complete, and none of them will rise again.
