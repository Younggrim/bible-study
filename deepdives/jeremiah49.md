*Setting:* This chapter gathers five short oracles against Judah's neighbors, part of Jeremiah's collection of prophecies against the nations (chs 46–51). The order runs roughly around Judah's borders: Ammon to the east across the Jordan, Edom to the southeast, Damascus to the north, the Arab tribes of the desert, and finally Elam, far to the east beyond Babylon. Most of these peoples were named in the list of nations who must drink the cup of God's wrath (25:15–26), and all of them had sent envoys or traded with Judah. Most of the oracles have no date. The Elam oracle is dated to 597 BC, and the Kedar oracle mentions Nebuchadnezzar by name. Several lines are shared with other prophets: the Edom oracle overlaps heavily with the book of Obadiah, and the Damascus oracle ends with a line from Amos. The chapter shows the LORD as judge of every nation, great and small, and three of the five oracles end with a surprising promise of mercy.

#### Judgment on Ammon (vv1–6)
- **Who were the Ammonites?** Like Moab, Ammon descended from Lot (Gen 19:38). Its land lay east of the Jordan, along the upper Jabbok River, and its capital Rabbah is modern Amman, the capital of Jordan, which still carries the name. Israel was told not to attack Ammon in Moses' day (Deut 2:19), but the two peoples fought often (Judg 11; 1 Sam 11). David captured Rabbah (2 Sam 12:26–31). Most recently Ammon's king Baalis had sent the assassin who murdered Gedaliah (40:14).
- **"Has Israel no sons? Has he no heir? Why then has Milcom dispossessed Gad" (v1).** When Assyria deported the tribes east of the Jordan in 733 BC (2 Kgs 15:29; 1 Chr 5:26), Ammon moved into the land of Gad. God asks: did Israel have no heirs, that Ammon could simply take it? Amos had condemned Ammon for "ripping open pregnant women in Gilead, that they might enlarge their border" (Amos 1:13). **Milcom** was Ammon's national god, "the abomination of the Ammonites," for whom Solomon built a shrine (1 Kgs 11:5, 33). The ESV footnote says the word may also be read "their king" (*malkam*). The Hebrew letters allow both. Either way, the god is pictured as the real owner who moved his people in.
- **"I will cause the battle cry to be heard against Rabbah of the Ammonites; it shall become a desolate mound" (v2).** A "mound" (*tel*) is the heap of ruins left by a destroyed city, the same word used for the many tells of archaeology. **"Then Israel shall dispossess those who dispossessed him."** The land will return to its rightful heirs.
- **"Wail, O Heshbon, for Ai is laid waste!" (v3).** This Ai is not the town Joshua took. It is an unknown place in Ammon, and the name simply means "ruin." Heshbon lay on the border of Moab and Ammon and changed hands more than once. **"Milcom shall go into exile, with his priests and his officials."** The same words were used of Chemosh (48:7). The god who dispossessed others will himself be carried away.
- **"Why do you boast of your valleys, O faithless daughter, who trusted in her treasures, saying, 'Who will come against me?'" (v4).** The ESV footnote says the Hebrew reads "boast of your valleys, your valley flows," probably meaning well-watered, fertile valleys. Ammon felt safe and rich. "Faithless" is literally "backturning," the same word Jeremiah used for Israel (3:14, 22).
- **"You shall be driven out, every man straight before him, with none to gather the fugitives" (v5).** A scattered people with no one to gather them back. Josephus says Nebuchadnezzar campaigned against Ammon and Moab in 582 BC, and Ammon fades from history as a nation afterward.
- **"But afterward I will restore the fortunes of the Ammonites, declares the LORD" (v6).** As with Moab (48:47), judgment is not God's last word. Ammonites still appear later, among them Tobiah the Ammonite who troubled Nehemiah (Neh 2:10). Christian readers have long seen the fuller answer in the gospel, which reaches every nation.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry recalls Amos 1:13, where Ammon "ripped up the women with child of Gilead, that they might enlarge their border," and writes, "God has many ways to terrify those who have been a terror to his people." On v6 he notes how history turns: "the wheel goes round." JFB reads "their king" as the idol Milcom, a "usurper-king" on land that belonged to the LORD. It sees a partial fulfillment of v2 under the Maccabees, who later fought the Ammonites. Guzik draws a lesson from Gad's lost land: believers have an inheritance in Christ that they should actually take up and not let others occupy.

#### Judgment on Edom (vv7–22)
- **Who were the Edomites?** Edom descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Gen 25:30; 36:1). Its land was the rugged mountains south and east of the Dead Sea, along the Arabah valley. The rivalry of Jacob and Esau ran through the whole history of the two nations. Edom refused Israel passage in the wilderness (Num 20:14–21). David conquered it (2 Sam 8:14), and it later broke free (2 Kgs 8:20). At Jerusalem's fall, Edom cheered and helped the Babylonians. Psalm 137:7 remembers: "Remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem, how they said, 'Lay it bare, lay it bare.'" Obadiah, Lamentations 4:21–22 and Ezekiel 25:12–14 all condemn Edom for it.
- **"Is wisdom no more in Teman? Has counsel perished from the prudent?" (v7).** Teman was a leading clan or region of Edom (Gen 36:11), and its name means "south." Edom was famous for its wise men. Eliphaz, the first of Job's counselors, was "the Temanite" (Job 2:11). Obadiah asks the same question: "Will I not… destroy the wise men out of Edom?" (Obad 8). Edom's famous wisdom could not see disaster coming.
- **"Flee, turn back, dwell in the depths, O inhabitants of Dedan!" (v8).** Dedan was an Arab trading people with an oasis to the southeast, in what is now northwestern Saudi Arabia. Its caravans passed through Edom. They are told to turn around and hide, so they are not caught in Edom's ruin. **"The calamity of Esau."** The prophet uses the ancestor's name for the nation.
- **"If grape gatherers came to you, would they not leave gleanings? If thieves came by night, would they not destroy only enough for themselves?" (v9).** Harvesters always left something behind for the poor (Lev 19:10), and thieves only take what they can carry. **"But I have stripped Esau bare; I have uncovered his hiding places" (v10).** God will leave nothing. Verses 9–10 are almost the same as Obadiah 5–6. Most scholars think one prophet drew on the other, or both drew on an older oracle. Which came first is debated.
- **"Leave your fatherless children; I will keep them alive; and let your widows trust in me" (v11).** In the middle of the harshest oracle, a sudden kindness. God himself will care for Edom's orphans and widows. He is the "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows" (Ps 68:5) even among Israel's enemies.
- **"If those who did not deserve to drink the cup must drink it, will you go unpunished?" (v12).** The "cup" is God's wrath, which Jeremiah was told to pass to the nations (25:15–29). If Judah, God's own people, had to drink it, Edom certainly will. Peter makes the same argument: "if it begins with us, what will be the outcome for those who do not obey the gospel of God?" (1 Pet 4:17).
- **"Bozrah shall become a horror, a taunt, a waste, and a curse" (v13).** Bozrah was Edom's capital, modern Buseirah in southern Jordan. Isaiah pictures God coming from Bozrah with garments stained red from judgment (Isa 63:1).
- **"I have heard a message from the LORD, and an envoy has been sent among the nations" (v14).** Verses 14–16 are almost word for word Obadiah 1–4. A herald calls the nations to war against Edom.
- **"The horror you inspire has deceived you, and the pride of your heart, you who live in the clefts of the rock" (v16).** The ESV footnote offers "of Sela," which means "rock" and was the name of an Edomite stronghold (2 Kgs 14:7). Edom's towns perched on high mountains and cliffs, and its people felt safe there. Centuries later the Nabataeans carved the famous rock city of Petra ("rock" in Greek) in the same region. **"Though you make your nest as high as the eagle's, I will bring you down from there."** No height is beyond God's reach (compare Amos 9:2).
- **"As when Sodom and Gomorrah and their neighboring cities were overthrown" (v18).** Sodom lay near the southern Dead Sea, close to Edom's border. The neighbors will share its fate: "no man shall dwell there."
- **"Like a lion coming up from the jungle of the Jordan against a perennial pasture" (v19).** The "jungle of the Jordan" was the thick undergrowth along the river, where lions lived in Bible times (12:5). When the river flooded, they were driven up into the pastures. The conqueror will come up like that lion. **"Who is like me? Who will summon me? What shepherd can stand before me?"** No ruler can call God to court or stand against him. Verses 19–21 are repeated almost exactly about Babylon in 50:44–46.
- **"Even the little ones of the flock shall be dragged away" (v20).** Even the smallest of Edom's people will be carried off. **"The sound of their cry shall be heard at the Red Sea" (v21).** Here the "Red Sea" (*Yam Suph*) is probably the Gulf of Aqaba, at Edom's southern tip, where Solomon's port of Ezion-geber stood (1 Kgs 9:26).
- **"One shall mount up and fly swiftly like an eagle and spread his wings against Bozrah" (v22).** The same words were used against Moab (48:40–41).
- **What became of Edom?** After the Babylonian period, Arab tribes and then the Nabataeans took over Edom's homeland. Many Edomites moved west into the dry south of Judah, which became known as Idumea. In the second century BC the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus conquered them and forced them to convert. Herod the Great was an Idumean. After the Roman destruction of AD 70 the Edomites vanish from history.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees Edom's doom as comfort to God's people, who had long suffered Edom's scorn. On v11 he says it is "an unspeakable comfort to the people of God, when they are dying," to leave their children with God. On v16: "They trusted to their strength, but neither shall that avail them." On v20: "When God has work to do of any kind he will soon find those that are able to engage in it." JFB stresses that Israel was punished as a son, while Edom will be judged as an enemy, and that the gleaning picture of v9 means the Babylonians will leave Edom nothing. Guzik notes that "dwell in the depths" (v8) has become a devotional phrase, while Jeremiah meant hiding in a hole from an invading army. He also ties Edom's lost wisdom to Eliphaz the Temanite, and the Tyndale notes add that Edom's rock fortress could be reached only through a narrow gorge.

#### Judgment on Damascus (vv23–27)
- **"Concerning Damascus" (v23).** Damascus was the capital of Aram (Syria), north of Israel. It had been Israel's great rival in the days of Ahab and Elisha (1 Kgs 20; 2 Kgs 6–8). Assyria had already conquered it in 732 BC (2 Kgs 16:9), so by Jeremiah's day it was a province, not a kingdom. **"Hamath and Arpad are confounded."** Hamath, on the Orontes River, and Arpad, near Aleppo, were Syrian cities further north. The Assyrians once boasted of conquering both (Isa 10:9; 36:19). They "melt in fear" at bad news, perhaps the Babylonian advance after Carchemish in 605 BC.
- **"They are troubled like the sea that cannot be quiet" (v23).** Syria had no sea of its own, and the image is of restless fear, like waves that never settle (compare Isa 57:20).
- **"Damascus has become feeble, she turned to flee" (v24).** The once proud city is like a woman in labor, gripped by pain.
- **"How is the famous city not forsaken, the city of my joy?" (v25).** The Hebrew is hard. It may be a citizen of Damascus speaking, "my joy" meaning the city its people loved. Or it may mean "how has the famous city not been abandoned," that is, why did its people not flee in time? Damascus was praised in the ancient world for its gardens, rivers and trade. Naaman boasted of its rivers (2 Kgs 5:12).
- **"I will kindle a fire in the wall of Damascus, and it shall devour the strongholds of Ben-hadad" (v27).** This line is taken almost word for word from Amos 1:4. Ben-hadad ("son of Hadad," the storm god) was the name of several kings of Damascus (1 Kgs 15:18; 20:1; 2 Kgs 13:3). There is no clear record of when this oracle was fulfilled. Babylon controlled Syria after 605 BC, and Josephus says Nebuchadnezzar campaigned in southern Syria (Coele-Syria) in 582 BC.
- **Commentators:** JFB hears in v25 the lament of a citizen over "the city of praise," a city celebrated for its beauty, and dates the fulfillment to Nebuchadnezzar's campaign five years after Jerusalem fell. Guzik notes that Damascus is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world and that, unlike other cities in these chapters, it is not said to become empty. Some of his sources think the fulfillment is not clearly known.

#### Judgment on Kedar and Hazor (vv28–33)
- **"Concerning Kedar and the kingdoms of Hazor that Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon struck down" (v28).** Kedar was a large Arab tribe of the Syrian and north Arabian desert, descended from Ishmael's second son (Gen 25:13). They lived in black goat-hair tents (Ps 120:5; Song 1:5) and traded sheep and goats (Isa 60:7; Ezek 27:21). The Babylonian Chronicle records that in his sixth year (599–598 BC) Nebuchadnezzar sent his army into the desert and "plundered extensively the possessions, animals and gods of the numerous Arabs." This is very likely the campaign the heading refers to.
- **"The kingdoms of Hazor."** This is not the famous Canaanite city of Hazor in Galilee (Josh 11). The Hebrew *hatsor* is related to *hatserim*, "unwalled settlements," the word Isaiah uses for "the villages that Kedar inhabits" (Isa 42:11). So "Hazor" probably means the Arab village peoples of the desert, as opposed to the tent nomads of Kedar, each group under its own sheikh.
- **"Rise up, advance against Kedar! Destroy the people of the east!" (v28).** "The people of the east" is an old name for the desert tribes (Judg 6:3; Job 1:3). **"Their tents and their flocks shall be taken, their curtains and all their goods; their camels shall be led away" (v29).** For nomads, these were everything. **"Terror on every side!"** Jeremiah's watchword again (6:25; 20:10; 46:5).
- **"Flee, wander far away, dwell in the depths, O inhabitants of Hazor!" (v30).** **"For Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon has made a plan against you."** The king makes his plan, but v28 calls it the LORD's command.
- **"A nation at ease, that dwells securely… that has no gates or bars, that dwells alone" (v31).** The desert tribes had no city walls because they had never needed them. They lived far from the empires' roads. "Dwells alone" was Balaam's phrase for Israel, set apart among the nations (Num 23:9). Isolation was no protection.
- **"Those who cut the corners of their hair" (v32).** Some Arab tribes shaved the hair at the temples, a custom the Greek historian Herodotus also describes. Israel was forbidden to do it (Lev 19:27). Jeremiah mentions the same people in 9:26 and 25:23. **"I will scatter to every wind."**
- **"Hazor shall become a haunt of jackals, an everlasting waste" (v33).** Jackals roam ruined places (compare 9:11; 10:22). A people who never had cities will lose even their villages.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry finds a lesson in v31: "Those are truly rich who have enough to supply their necessities, and know when they have enough." He adds that Nebuchadnezzar acted from greed, "Yet, how unrighteous soever he was in doing it, God was righteous in directing it," and on v32, "Privacy and obscurity are not always a protection and security." JFB agrees that Hazor here is a desert district, not the city in Galilee, and calls the "kingdoms" clans under sheikhs. Guzik passes on the view that Kedar and Hazor stand for the Arabs of the tents and the Arabs of the villages.

#### Judgment on Elam (vv34–39)
- **"Concerning Elam, in the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah king of Judah" (v34).** That is 597 BC, just after King Jehoiachin and the first large group of exiles were taken to Babylon (2 Kgs 24:10–17). Elam lay far east of Babylon, in what is now southwestern Iran, with its capital at Susa. It was one of the oldest civilizations of the region. Elamite soldiers had served in the Assyrian army against Judah (Isa 22:6). In the Greek translation, this oracle comes first in the collection, right after 25:13.
- **"I will break the bow of Elam, the mainstay of their might" (v35).** Elam's archers were famous across the ancient world (Isa 22:6). God will break the very thing they trusted most.
- **"I will bring upon Elam the four winds from the four quarters of heaven" (v36).** The winds come from every direction and scatter Elam's people "to all those winds." The image is complete dispersion. Elam would have no single conqueror to fear but would be battered from every side.
- **"I will send the sword after them, until I have consumed them" (v37).** The sword is God's, as in 47:6–7.
- **"I will set my throne in Elam and destroy their king and officials" (v38).** God will show himself as the true King in Elam. Babylon may have clashed with Elam soon after this oracle. The Babylonian Chronicle for 596 BC describes Nebuchadnezzar marching against a king, very likely Elam's, who withdrew in fear, though the text is broken. Later Elam was swallowed up by Persia. Susa became one of the capitals of the Persian empire, where Esther was queen and Nehemiah served the king (Esth 1:2; Neh 1:1). Daniel saw a vision "in Susa the citadel, which is in the province of Elam" (Dan 8:2). In that sense God did "set his throne" in Elam, ruling over the empire that would free his people.
- **"But in the latter days I will restore the fortunes of Elam, declares the LORD" (v39).** A third promise of mercy in this chapter. Cyrus, the Persian king who let the exiles return (Ezra 1:1–4), first ruled as king of Anshan, a part of old Elam. And on the day of Pentecost, the first list of nations who heard the gospel begins with "Parthians and Medes and Elamites" (Acts 2:9).
- **Commentators:** JFB says God will "show Myself King by My judgments" in Elam, and that the full restoration of v39 belongs to gospel times, noting that Elamites were among the first to hear the gospel at Pentecost. Henry's closing comment on the whole chapter is a sobering question: "When Israel was scarcely saved where shall all these appear?" Guzik observes that Elam's judgment is not tied to Nebuchadnezzar by name, and sees the Elamites at Pentecost as a glimpse of grace breaking through. The Tyndale notes link the promise to Cyrus and to Susa as a Persian capital.

#### Pointing to Christ
Five nations, five kinds of false security: Ammon's fertile valleys, Edom's wisdom and cliffs, Damascus's fame, the desert tribes' isolation, and Elam's bow. None of them held. Yet God also says, "Leave your fatherless children; I will keep them alive" (v11), and three times he promises to restore the very nations he judges. That mercy has a name. Jesus is the one in whom God's purpose reaches the nations, and on the day the risen Christ poured out his Spirit, Elamites heard the gospel in their own language, followed in Luke's list by Arabians (Acts 2:9–11). The cup of wrath that Edom had to drink, Christ drank in full (Mark 14:36), so that people from every nation, even Israel's oldest enemies, could be brought near.
