*Setting:* "A Psalm of David." It describes a thunderstorm rolling across the whole land: it starts over the Mediterranean, crashes into the mountains of Lebanon in the north, sweeps south to the wilderness of Kadesh, and ends with God enthroned above it all and blessing his people with peace. Henry passes on the guess that David wrote it during a real storm, as Psalm 8 seems to come from a moonlit night and Psalm 19 from a sunny morning. The phrase "the voice of the LORD" comes seven times, like seven claps of thunder, and the name "the LORD" (Yahweh) eighteen times in eleven verses. There is also a sharp edge to the poem. The Canaanites worshipped Baal as the storm god who rode the clouds and thundered, and texts found at Ugarit in Syria praise Baal in language strikingly close to this psalm. Many scholars think David deliberately takes that language and gives it to its rightful owner: the thunder belongs to the LORD, not to Baal. The Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) adds to the title a note linking it to the last day of the Feast of Booths, and in Jewish worship today it is sung each Friday evening to welcome the Sabbath. Psalm 28 ended praying, "Save your people… carry them forever." Psalm 29 shows how strong the God is who carries them.

#### Ascribe to the LORD Glory (vv1–2)
- **"Ascribe to the LORD, O heavenly beings" (v1).** "Ascribe" is literally "give" (*habu*), three times in two verses. Of course no one can give God glory he lacks. To "give" here means to recognize and declare what is already his (Deut 32:3; 1 Chr 16:28–29; Ps 96:7–8). "Heavenly beings" is literally "sons of God" or "sons of the mighty" (*bene elim*). Most interpreters, JFB included, take them to be angels, the court of heaven around God's throne (89:6–7; Job 1:6; 38:7). Henry, following older interpreters, read them as the princes and kings of the earth, which the KJV's "ye mighty" allows. Either way, the psalm begins by telling the most powerful beings that exist to bow.
- **The point against Baal.** In the Canaanite stories, the "sons of god" were a council of gods. The Tyndale notes suggest the psalmist calls on whatever powers people thought were real to look to the LORD as the only God. The psalm does not grant that other gods exist. It summons every power in heaven to give the glory to Israel's God.
- **"Glory and strength" (v1).** "Glory" (*kavod*) comes from a root meaning "weight." God's glory is his weightiness, the sheer worth and reality of who he is. The storm that follows shows both his glory and his strength. The psalm ends with that strength given to his people (v11).
- **"The glory due his name" (v2).** His name stands for who he has revealed himself to be (Exod 34:5–7). Worship is not flattery. It is giving God what he is owed.
- **"Worship the LORD in the splendor of holiness" (v2).** "Worship" (*shachah*) means to bow down, to fall on one's face. Boice, cited by Guzik, notes the pair: ascribing glory with the mind and bowing down with the will. "Splendor of holiness" (*hadrat qodesh*) is debated. It may mean the splendor that belongs to God's holiness, or it may mean the worshippers' "holy attire," the festive or priestly clothes worn to worship (the ESV footnote gives that reading, and 2 Chr 20:21 is similar). JFB reads it as "the loveliness of a spiritual worship," which the beauty of the sanctuary only pictured.
- **Commentators:** Henry notices that the call is repeated three times, which "intimates that the mighty men are backward to this duty." He adds that the call is not for anything God needs: "Who hath first given to him?" Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that neither angels nor men can add anything to God, only recognize what he already is. JFB reads "mighty" as "sons of the mighty," heavenly beings, as angels.

#### The Voice of the LORD Over the Waters (vv3–4)
- **"The voice of the LORD is over the waters" (v3).** "Voice" (*qol*) also means "sound" or "thunder." The Tyndale notes point out that the same word means thunder in Joel 3:16, and the "thunder" of the Egyptian plague is literally "voices" (Exod 9:23). At Sinai, God's coming was marked by "thunders" (literally "voices") and a trumpet blast (Exod 19:16–19). Thunder is the closest thing in creation to a sound that no human being can make or control (Job 37:2–5; 40:9).
- **"Over many waters" (v3).** The storm is first heard out at sea. For Israel, not a seafaring people, the sea was a picture of chaos and danger. In Canaanite myth, Baal fought and defeated Yam, the god of the sea. Here there is no battle. The LORD's voice is simply "over" the waters, as his Spirit hovered over the waters at creation (Gen 1:2). JFB takes "the waters" as the storm clouds (18:11), which is also possible.
- **"The God of glory thunders" (v3).** "The God of glory" connects back to v1–2. The glory angels are told to ascribe to God is heard on earth in the storm. Stephen used the same title for the God who appeared to Abraham (Acts 7:2).
- **"Powerful… full of majesty" (v4).** Literally "in power… in majesty." JFB notes the literal sense. God's voice does not just carry power; it is power.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives reasons why thunder especially is called God's voice: it comes from above, no one can direct or predict it, it reaches far, and God often spoke in thunder, "particularly at Mount Sinai." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's similar list and Boice's reminder that the thunder is only a picture of the real voice of God, which is far greater. He also notes the Canaanite background of Yam and Baal. JFB calls the voice an "audible exhibition of His power in the tempest, of which thunder is a specimen."

#### The Voice of the LORD Breaks the Cedars (vv5–6)
- **"Breaks the cedars of Lebanon" (v5).** The cedars of Lebanon were the greatest trees the ancient world knew, tall, strong and valued for palaces and temples (1 Kings 5:6; Isa 2:13). Solomon would build God's house from them. A lightning strike or a mountain gale can split such a tree to splinters. The strongest thing on earth breaks at a word from God.
- **"He makes Lebanon to skip like a calf" (v6).** Now the mountains themselves move. In the Exodus psalm, "the mountains skipped like rams" (114:4–6). Mountains were the picture of stability, and God's voice makes them leap like a young animal. JFB takes it as the swaying of forests in the wind. Others think of an earthquake. Either way, nothing on earth stands still before him.
- **"And Sirion like a young wild ox" (v6).** Sirion is the Sidonian (Phoenician) name for Mount Hermon (Deut 3:9), the highest mountain on Israel's northern border, snow-capped most of the year. The "wild ox" (*re'em*) was the aurochs, a huge, powerful wild bull now extinct. The KJV's "unicorn" came from the Greek translation's *monokerōs*, "one-horned," and is not a mythical horse.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "The tall and large cedars… are shivered, utterly broken." Guzik pictures a thunderbolt shattering a cedar and David thinking that God's voice is like that, and stronger still. He passes on Maclaren's note that Sirion is the Sidonian name for Hermon.

#### The Voice of the LORD Shakes the Wilderness (vv7–9)
- **"The voice of the LORD flashes forth flames of fire" (v7).** Literally "hews out flames of fire." JFB: the lightning flies "like flakes and splinters hewed from stone or wood." The thunder and the lightning come together, as they did at Sinai (Exod 19:16).
- **"The LORD shakes the wilderness of Kadesh" (v8).** "Shakes" is to writhe or whirl. The storm that began in the far north now reaches the far south. Kadesh is most often taken as Kadesh-barnea, the oasis in the southern wilderness where Israel camped during their forty years (Num 13:26; 20:1). The Tyndale notes take it this way: God's voice is heard throughout the land. Some scholars think it is Kadesh on the Orontes River in Syria, which would keep the whole storm in the north, but the southern reading fits the sweep of the poem and Israel's history. The wilderness where Israel once trembled at God's voice trembles again.
- **"The voice of the LORD makes the deer give birth" (v9).** The terror of the storm brings on labor in the does. Job 39:1–3 also speaks of God watching over the birth of deer. The ESV footnote gives another reading, "makes the oaks to shake," since the Hebrew words for "deer" and "oaks" look alike. That reading pairs neatly with "strips the forests bare" in the next line. Both readings are possible, and the point is the same: every living thing feels his voice.
- **"And strips the forests bare" (v9).** The wind tears the leaves from the trees and lays the forest floor open. JFB: "Terror-stricken animals and denuded forests close the illustration."
- **"And in his temple all cry, 'Glory!'" (v9).** This is the turning point. On earth, the storm brings terror. In God's temple, it brings worship. The angels told to ascribe glory in v1 now do it: "Glory!" Whether "his temple" is heaven, the earthly sanctuary, or creation as God's palace, the answer to the storm is praise. Isaiah heard the seraphim cry, "Holy, holy, holy… the whole earth is full of his glory!" (Isa 6:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry: in the world every man sees God's glory, "but it is only in the temple, in the church, that it is spoken of to his honour." JFB reads v9 as worshippers responding to the call of v2. He suggests "temple" here may be heaven, "or the whole frame of nature, as the angels are called on for praise." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's question whether believers can sing this psalm in a storm, and Boice's note that in the early church this psalm was often read during storms.

#### The LORD Sits Enthroned over the Flood (vv10–11)
- **"The LORD sits enthroned over the flood" (v10).** "Flood" is *mabbul*, a word used nowhere else in the Old Testament except in the Genesis account of Noah's flood (Gen 6–11). The lexicon (BDB) notes that elsewhere it is almost a proper name for that flood. So David looks at the rain pouring down and thinks of the greatest flood of all. Even then, when the waters covered the earth, the LORD was on his throne, judging the wicked and keeping Noah safe. Henry notes that God still "sits upon the flood, restraining the waters of Noah," according to his promise (Gen 9:11; Isa 54:9).
- **"The LORD sits enthroned as king forever" (v10).** The storm passes. The king does not. In Canaanite myth, Baal had to fight his way to a throne and could lose it. The LORD simply sits, and he sits forever.
- **"May the LORD give strength to his people!" (v11).** The psalm began by telling heavenly beings to give strength to the LORD (v1). It ends with the LORD giving strength to his people. The same power that breaks cedars is turned toward his own for their good (28:8).
- **"May the LORD bless his people with peace!" (v11).** The final word is *shalom*. A psalm full of thunder ends with peace. Morgan, cited by Guzik, sums it up: during the storm he gives strength, and after it he gives peace. This is the blessing of Numbers 6:24–26, "The LORD bless you… and give you peace."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "When the thunder of God's wrath shall make sinners tremble the saints shall lift up their heads with joy." He calls peace "a blessing of inestimable value, which God designs for all his people." JFB: over the raging elements God is enthroned, "directing and restraining," and quotes a hymn line: "This awful God is ours, our Father and our Love." Guzik passes on Maclaren and Boice on the rarity of the word "flood" and its tie to Noah.

#### Pointing to Christ
The voice of the LORD broke cedars and shook mountains. In the Gospels that same voice speaks in the person of Jesus. He stood in a boat on a stormy sea and said, "Peace! Be still!" and the wind and waves obeyed (Mark 4:39). The disciples asked, "Who then is this?" and Psalm 29 gives the answer: the LORD is the one whose voice is over the waters. When the Father spoke from heaven about his Son, some in the crowd said it had thundered (John 12:28–29). And on the cross, God's judgment fell like a storm on the one who took the place of his people, so that the last word for them could be the last word of this psalm. "Peace I leave with you," Jesus said, "my peace I give to you" (John 14:27). The King who sat enthroned over the flood saves his people through it, as he did Noah in the ark (1 Pet 3:20–21), and gives them strength and peace.
