*Setting:* Babylonia, probably 591 BC, among the Jewish exiles at the Chebar canal. Jerusalem still stood, with Zedekiah on its throne, and both in the city and among the exiles there were prophets promising that all would soon be well: Babylon would fall, the captives would come home, the temple would never be touched. Chapter 12 ended with God answering two popular sayings that brushed off the true prophets, and promising an end to "false vision or flattering divination" (12:24). Chapter 13 now goes after the people behind those smooth words. Verses 1–16 confront the men who prophesied peace. Verses 17–23 confront women who sold charms and spells. Jeremiah was fighting the same battle in Jerusalem at the same time (Jer 23:9–40; 28–29), and he even wrote to the exiles warning them about prophets in Babylon by name (Jer 29:8–9, 21–23).

#### False Prophets Condemned (vv1–23)
- **One heading, two targets.** The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading. The structure is careful. Each half opens with the command to prophesy against those who speak "from their own hearts" (vv2, 17), gives a woe (vv3, 18), names the sin, and then answers with "Therefore thus says the Lord GOD" and judgment (vv8, 20). Both halves end with the refrain "you shall know that I am the LORD" (vv14, 21, 23).
- **"Prophesy against the prophets of Israel, who are prophesying" (v2).** The irony is in the repetition. These men really were prophesying, in the sense that they spoke in God's name and drew a crowd. Ezekiel is told to bring them a word from the LORD, which is the one thing they never had. **"Hear the word of the LORD!"** is addressed to men who claimed to speak it every day.
- **"Who prophesy from their own hearts" (v2).** This is the test of a true prophet in one phrase. In Hebrew thinking the heart is the mind and will, not mainly the feelings. These men spoke what they themselves thought, hoped and wanted. Jeremiah says the same: "They speak visions of their own minds, not from the mouth of the LORD" (Jer 23:16).
- **"Woe to the foolish prophets who follow their own spirit, and have seen nothing!" (v3).** "Foolish" is *nabal*, the word for Nabal in 1 Samuel 25, whose name his own wife explained (1 Sam 25:25). In the Old Testament the *nabal* is not someone short on intelligence but someone morally and spiritually blind, the fool who "says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Ps 14:1). **"Their own spirit"** stands against the Spirit of the LORD who lifted Ezekiel and showed him visions (8:3; 11:5, 24). They had a spirit, but it was only their own.
- **"Like jackals among ruins" (v4).** The older translations have "foxes in the deserts." The Hebrew *shu'alim* can mean fox or jackal, and jackals that haunt ruined towns fit the picture best (Lam 5:18). A jackal does not rebuild anything. It lives off the rubble and scavenges what it can. These prophets thrived on the nation's collapse, gaining a following and an income while the walls crumbled around them.
- **"You have not gone up into the breaches, or built up a wall" (v5).** The picture is a city under attack. When a wall is breached, soldiers rush into the gap to hold it while others repair it. A true prophet stood in the gap by calling the people to repentance and praying for them, as Moses did when God threatened to destroy Israel (Ps 106:23). God will later look for such a man in Jerusalem and find none (22:30). **"In the day of the LORD"** here means the coming day of judgment on Jerusalem itself, not only the end of history.
- **"They say, 'Declares the LORD,' when the LORD has not sent them, and yet they expect him to fulfill their word" (v6).** "Declares the LORD" (*ne'um YHWH*) was the standard prophetic formula, a kind of signature. These men used it freely. The most chilling part is that they seem to have believed their own message. They expected God to make good on promises he never made. Self-deception is not innocence. **"Lying divinations"** links them to the pagan practice of reading omens (*qesem*), which the law forbade outright (Deut 18:10). Whatever their methods, the result was the same as pagan fortune-telling.
- **"Because you have uttered falsehood… behold, I am against you" (v8).** "Falsehood" is *shaw'*, emptiness, the same word used in the commandment against taking the LORD's name "in vain" (Exod 20:7). They took his name on their lips for nothing. The phrase "I am against you" is the most terrifying sentence a prophet could hear, since they claimed to be on his side.
- **Three exclusions (v9).** The punishment fits the crime. Men who promised the people a quick homecoming will themselves have no home. They will not sit in **"the council of my people"**, the gathering of elders who made decisions. They will not be **"enrolled in the register of the house of Israel"**, the family lists that proved who belonged to the covenant people, the kind of list later kept in Ezra 2 and Nehemiah 7. And they **"shall not enter the land of Israel."** Cut off from the community, the record and the land, they lose everything that made a man an Israelite.
- **"Saying, 'Peace,' when there is no peace" (v10).** This is almost word for word what Jeremiah said of the prophets and priests of Jerusalem: "They have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace" (Jer 6:14; 8:11). "Peace" (*shalom*) means wholeness and well-being, the blessing of the covenant. These prophets promised the blessing while ignoring the conditions.
- **"When the people build a wall, these prophets smear it with whitewash" (v10).** The word for "wall" here (*ḥayits*) appears only in this verse in the Old Testament and seems to mean a flimsy partition wall, not a city wall. The people throw up a weak, shoddy wall, their own plans for security, and the prophets cover it with a coat of whitewash (*tafel*) so it looks solid. The same Hebrew letters make an adjective meaning "tasteless" or "insipid" (Job 6:6), and Lamentations uses it for the prophets' visions that were "false and deceptive" (Lam 2:14). The older "untempered mortar" reflects a different reading of the word. Either way, the image is a cosmetic job on a wall with nothing holding it together.
- **Rain, hailstones and wind (vv11–13).** In Palestine and Mesopotamia many walls were mud brick. A plaster coat kept the rain off for a while, but a heavy winter storm would soak the bricks, turn them to mud and bring the wall down. The storm here is God's own judgment: "I will make a stormy wind break out in my wrath" (v13). **"O great hailstones"** is addressed directly to the hail, as if God were calling up his troops. Hail is a weapon of God in the Old Testament (Exod 9:23–25; Josh 10:11), and it returns in the judgment on Gog (38:22).
- **"Where is the coating with which you smeared it?" (v12).** When the wall lies in pieces, the bystanders will mock. All that remains of the prophets' reassurance is a pile of mud.
- **"Its foundation will be laid bare… you shall perish in the midst of it" (v14).** The storm does not only knock the wall over. It strips it down to the foundation, exposing how little was there. The prophets who plastered the wall will be crushed under it. **"The wall is no more, nor those who smeared it"** (v15) is God's epitaph over both. This happened in 586 BC, when the Babylonians broke the real walls of Jerusalem and the prophets of peace were proved wrong in the worst possible way. Jesus used a similar picture at the end of the Sermon on the Mount. A house built on sand looks fine until "the rain fell, and the floods came, and the winds blew" (Matt 7:27).
- **"Set your face against the daughters of your people" (v17).** Ezekiel now turns to women. The Old Testament honors true prophetesses, among them Miriam (Exod 15:20), Deborah (Judg 4:4) and Huldah, whom King Josiah consulted only a generation earlier (2 Kings 22:14). The New Testament adds Anna (Luke 2:36) and Philip's daughters (Acts 21:9). The problem is not that these women prophesied but that, like the men, they did it **"out of their own hearts."** They also seem to have practiced something closer to sorcery than prophecy.
- **"Who sew magic bands upon all wrists, and make veils for the heads" (v18).** The details are obscure, and translations differ widely. The KJV has "pillows to all armholes" and "kerchiefs." The word for "bands" (*keseth*) occurs only here and in v20. Later Hebrew used it for a cushion, which is where "pillows" came from, but most scholars now think of cloth bands or amulets tied on the wrist or arm, a practice known from Babylonian magic, where knotted cords were thought to bind a person or a spirit. The "veils" (*mispaḥoth*, also found only here) may have been head coverings worn by the client during a ritual. **"Persons of every stature"** probably means everyone, tall and short, young and old. The precise ritual is lost. What is clear is that these were spells sold to frightened people in a frightening time.
- **"In the hunt for souls" (v18).** The image is a bird-catcher with nets and snares, and it returns in v20: **"souls like birds."** "Soul" is *nephesh*, the whole living person, not a spirit separate from the body. These women trapped people's lives. **"Will you hunt down souls belonging to my people and keep your own souls alive?"** They thought their trade would keep them safe, and they were wrong.
- **"For handfuls of barley and for pieces of bread" (v19).** Barley was the cheaper grain, food for the poor and for animals. They were selling the name of God for almost nothing. The sin is that they **"profaned"** him, treating his holy name as one more ingredient in their magic. Micah had condemned the same thing: prophets who "cry 'Peace' when they have something to eat" (Mic 3:5).
- **"Putting to death souls who should not die and keeping alive souls who should not live" (v19).** By their spells and verdicts they pronounced life and death over people, and always the wrong way round. This is the opposite of Ezekiel's own calling as watchman, whose task was to warn the wicked so they could live and to keep the righteous from falling (3:17–21). **"By your lying to my people, who listen to lies."** The people are not let off. They wanted to hear it.
- **"I will tear them from your arms… I will let the souls whom you hunt go free" (v20).** God's answer is to rip off the charms and open the trap. He is the true rescuer of his people. The birds fly free.
- **"You have disheartened the righteous falsely… and you have encouraged the wicked" (v22).** Literally, they "made the heart of the righteous sad" and "strengthened the hands of the wicked." This is the sum of all false ministry. It frightens those God has not condemned, and it comforts those who should be warned. It cuts against God's own heart. He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked but wants them to turn and live (18:23, 32; 33:11). These women made turning seem unnecessary.
- **"You shall no more see false visions nor practice divination" (v23).** When Jerusalem falls, their trade is finished. The events themselves will expose them. **"I will deliver my people out of your hand. And you shall know that I am the LORD."** The chapter ends on rescue, not only judgment.
- **Commentators:** Henry introduces the chapter by noting that God's true prophets are "nowhere so sharp upon any sort of sinners as upon the false prophets," because they "did the greatest mischief to his people." On the wall he says, "Hopes of peace and happiness, not warranted by the word of God, will cheat men; like a wall well daubed, but ill built." JFB reads the "gaps" as the breach "made in the theocracy through the nation's sin" and the hedge as "the law of God." It calls the mortar "sand without lime, mud without straw," and sees the exclusion from the register as "literally fulfilled" in the lists of Ezra 2 and Nehemiah 7. JFB takes the "pillows" as symbols of the comfort the prophetesses promised, and the barley as "the paltry gain for which they bartered immortal souls." Its "immortal souls" reads more into *nephesh* than the word holds. Guzik points out that the women's charms were really sorcery dressed up as prophecy, and that "soul" here means the whole person. He presses the lesson that a comforting message has to be tested against God's word before the storm comes, not after. All three see the people as partly to blame for preferring pleasant lies. Henry: "It is ill with those who had rather hear pleasing lies than unpleasing truths."

#### Pointing to Christ
The false prophets never stood in the breach. They plastered the wall and walked away. Jesus is the one who went up into the breach for his people. He did not tell anyone that there was peace when there was none. He wept over Jerusalem because it did not know "the things that make for peace" (Luke 19:42), warned of the storm coming on it, and then made peace himself "by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20). He is the true foundation that no storm can lay bare (1 Cor 3:11), and the house built on his words stands when the rain falls (Matt 7:24–25). Where the women of Ezekiel's day hunted souls like birds, he came "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18). And unlike them, he never discouraged the righteous or comforted the wicked in their sin. He comforted sinners who turned and warned the self-righteous who did not.
