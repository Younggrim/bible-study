*Setting:* Samaria, 732–722 BC, and the decades that followed. The northern kingdom of Israel had lasted about two hundred years since it broke away from David's line under Jeroboam I (about 930 BC). It has been shrinking fast: Tiglath-pileser III took Galilee and Gilead in 733–732 BC (15:29), and what is left is little more than the hill country around Samaria. Its last king, Hoshea, came to power by assassination, with Assyria's approval. Hosea and Amos had warned for decades that exile was coming. This chapter tells how it came, in just six verses, and then stops the story to explain *why*, in one of the longest sermons the writer of Kings ever preaches. It ends with the strange new mixed people who filled the empty land, the beginnings of the Samaritans.

#### Hoshea Reigns in Israel (vv1–5)
- **"In the twelfth year of Ahaz… Hoshea the son of Elah began to reign… nine years" (v1).** About 732–722 BC. The synchronisms between Israel and Judah are hard to fit together in these years because of overlapping co-regencies, and scholars still debate them. Tiglath-pileser boasted that he set Hoshea on the throne after Pekah was overthrown (15:30).
- **"He did what was evil… yet not as the kings of Israel who were before him" (v2).** The writer gives a small, honest qualification without explaining it. Perhaps Hoshea did not actively push calf worship, or perhaps, as some suggest, the calf at Bethel had already been carried off (Hos 10:5–6) and Dan was in Assyrian hands. Either way, the kingdom did not fall because its last king was the worst. The verdict came on two centuries of accumulated sin.
- **"Shalmaneser king of Assyria… Hoshea became his vassal and paid him tribute" (v3).** Shalmaneser V succeeded his father Tiglath-pileser in 727 BC. A change of emperor was often the moment when vassals tested their chains.
- **"He had sent messengers to So, king of Egypt, and offered no tribute" (v4).** Hoshea gambled on Egypt. No pharaoh named So is known for certain. Suggestions include Osorkon IV, the city of Sais (the capital of a delta ruler, which would make it "to Sais, to the king of Egypt"), or an Ethiopian pharaoh. Egypt at this time was divided and weak, "a broken reed" as the Assyrians later sneered (18:21). Hosea had mocked exactly this policy: "calling to Egypt, going to Assyria" (Hos 7:11; 12:1).
- **"The king of Assyria shut him up and bound him in prison" (v4).** Hoshea was probably arrested when he came to explain himself, before the siege. Israel's last king simply disappears from history. Hosea's words came true: "Samaria's king shall perish like a twig on the face of the waters" (Hos 10:7).
- **"For three years he besieged it" (v5).** Samaria stood on a steep hill and had strong walls built by Omri and Ahab. Archaeologists have found the remains of its casemate walls. In ancient reckoning "three years" could be parts of three calendar years, about 725–722 BC. Assyrian armies first ravaged the surrounding countryside to cut off supplies, then sat down to starve the city out.

#### The Fall of Israel (v6)
- **"In the ninth year of Hoshea, the king of Assyria captured Samaria" (v6).** 722 BC. Shalmaneser died about this time, and his successor Sargon II claimed the conquest in his own inscriptions: "I besieged and conquered Samaria, led away as booty 27,290 inhabitants of it." Many scholars think Shalmaneser took the city and Sargon finished the deportation and boasted of it. The Bible's "king of Assyria" covers both.
- **"He carried the Israelites away to Assyria" (v6).** Assyrian deportation was a deliberate policy: break up rebellious peoples, mix them with others, and resettle them far away where they had no roots to fight for. Assyrian reliefs show families walking into exile with bundles on their backs. Amos had foretold being dragged away "with hooks" (Amos 4:2–3).
- **"In Halah, and on the Habor, the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes" (v6).** Halah was in the Assyrian heartland near Nineveh. The Habor (Khabur) is a tributary of the Euphrates in what is now northeastern Syria, and Gozan (Tell Halaf) stood on it. Documents found in that region a generation later include distinctly Israelite names, some of them built on *Yahu*, the short form of the LORD's name. The cities of the Medes lay far to the east, in what is now Iran.
- **Were the ten tribes "lost"?** Israel as a nation ended, and many exiles were absorbed into their new surroundings. But not every northerner was lost. Many faithful northerners had already moved south to Judah when Jeroboam set up his calves (2 Chr 11:13–16). Hezekiah later invited the survivors in the north to his Passover, and some came (2 Chr 30:10–11). In the New Testament Anna the prophetess is "of the tribe of Asher" (Luke 2:36). The prophets also promised that God would one day gather "the outcasts of Israel" with Judah (Isa 11:12; Ezek 37:15–22).

#### Exile Because of Idolatry (vv7–23)
- **"And this occurred because the people of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God" (v7).** Here the writer stops and preaches. From the outside the fall of Samaria looked like geopolitics: a small kingdom crushed by an empire. The writer says the real cause was spiritual. Henry: "It was destruction from the Almighty: the Assyrian was but the rod of his anger" (compare Isa 10:5).
- **"Who had brought them up out of the land of Egypt" (v7).** The sermon begins where the covenant began. The first commandment came with the reminder "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt… You shall have no other gods before me" (Exod 20:2–3). Israel's sin was ingratitude to their rescuer.
- **"Walked in the customs of the nations whom the LORD drove out" (v8).** The Canaanites lost the land because of their sins (Lev 18:24–28; Deut 9:5). God had warned that if Israel did the same, the land would "vomit you out… as it vomited out the nation that was before you" (Lev 18:28). God judges sin, not race.
- **"Did secretly against the LORD… things that were not right" (v9).** The Hebrew may mean "did secretly" or "covered over with words," dressing up wrong things to look acceptable. **"From watchtower to fortified city"** means everywhere, from the smallest farm outpost to the largest town.
- **"Pillars and Asherim on every high hill and under every green tree" (v10).** Pillars were standing stones; Asherim were wooden poles or trees for the goddess Asherah. "Every high hill and every green tree" is the language of Deuteronomy 12:2 for Canaanite worship sites.
- **"Yet the LORD warned Israel and Judah by every prophet and every seer" (v13).** God did not judge without warning. The northern kingdom heard Ahijah, Elijah, Elisha, Micaiah, Jonah, Amos and Hosea, among others. Their message was one word: **"Turn"** (*shuv*), the great prophetic call to repentance.
- **"But they would not listen, but were stubborn, as their fathers had been" (v14).** "Stubborn" is literally "they hardened their neck," like an ox refusing the yoke. It is God's own description of Israel at the golden calf in the wilderness: "a stiff-necked people" (Exod 32:9).
- **"They went after false idols and became false" (v15).** The Hebrew is a wordplay: *hevel*, "vapour, breath, emptiness," the word the Preacher uses for "vanity" in Ecclesiastes. Literally "they went after the emptiness and became empty." Jeremiah uses the same phrase (Jer 2:5). People become like what they worship (Ps 115:8; Hos 9:10).
- **The list of sins (vv16–17).** The writer gathers up the whole history of the north: **"two calves"** (1 Kgs 12:28), **"an Asherah"** (1 Kgs 16:33), **"all the host of heaven"** (worship of sun, moon and stars, popular in Assyrian religion), **"Baal"** (1 Kgs 16:31–32), **"burned their sons and their daughters as offerings"** (the Molech rites, Lev 18:21), and **"divination and omens"** (forbidden in Deut 18:10–12). **"Sold themselves to do evil"** is what was said of Ahab (1 Kgs 21:20, 25). It is the language of slavery: they sold themselves into it.
- **"The LORD was very angry with Israel and removed them out of his sight" (v18).** God's anger is not a loss of temper but his settled and just response to persistent evil (the Tyndale notes make this point carefully). "Out of his sight" means out of his land and the place of his presence. **"None was left but the tribe of Judah only"**: Judah here includes Benjamin and the faithful who had moved south.
- **"Judah also did not keep the commandments" (v19).** The writer looks ahead, probably writing after Judah's own exile in 586 BC. Judah had watched Israel fall and copied its sins anyway (Jer 3:6–10). The warning in this chapter is for Judah too, and for every reader.
- **"When he had torn Israel from the house of David" (v21).** The writer goes back to the beginning. God himself tore the kingdom from Solomon's son because of Solomon's idolatry (1 Kgs 11:11, 31). But **"Jeroboam drove Israel from following the LORD"**: the verb is strong, "pushed them away." From the very start the northern kingdom was built on a false religion meant to keep people from Jerusalem (1 Kgs 12:26–30). Every king after him is said to have "walked in the sins of Jeroboam," and now the whole kingdom ends on that note.
- **"As he had spoken by all his servants the prophets" (v23).** The word of God, spoken by Ahijah two centuries earlier, came true: "the LORD will… root up Israel out of this good land… and scatter them beyond the Euphrates, because they have made their Asherim" (1 Kgs 14:15–16).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "When the measure of sin is filled up, the Lord will forbear no longer," and "those that bring sin into a country or family, bring a plague into it." He also notes that "some of the poor Israelites were left in the land." JFB writes that "the divine patience was exhausted," and that captivity taught them "the difference between His service and that of their despotic conquerors" (compare 2 Chr 12:8). Guzik stresses that God sent prophet after prophet, so that Israel sinned against patient love, and he draws out the *hevel* wordplay: worship emptiness and you become empty. He also argues against the idea that the ten tribes were wholly lost. JFB identifies So with the Ethiopian Sabaco, while the Tyndale notes and Guzik prefer other options; no one knows for sure.

#### Assyria Resettles Samaria (vv24–41)
- **"The king of Assyria brought people from Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim" (v24).** This was the other half of Assyrian policy: bring in foreigners to fill the gap. Sargon records settling Arab tribes in Samaria in 715 BC, and later kings Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal added more (Ezra 4:2, 10). Babylon and Cuthah were in southern Mesopotamia. Hamath was on the Orontes River in Syria. Avva and Sepharvaim are uncertain, possibly also in Syria. **"Samaria"** now became the name of an Assyrian province, not just a city.
- **"The LORD sent lions among them" (v25).** With the land depopulated, wild animals multiplied (Exod 23:29; Lev 26:22). Lions had served as God's judgment in the north before (1 Kgs 13:24; 20:36). The settlers took it as a sign that they had offended "the god of the land."
- **"They do not know the law of the god of the land" (v26).** Ancient peoples assumed each god owned a territory and had to be served in his own way. To the Assyrians, the LORD was a local god to be kept happy. The truth is that he is God of all the earth, but he had chosen this land for his name.
- **"Send there one of the priests whom you carried away" (v27).** The priest went to live in **Bethel** (v28), the old centre of Jeroboam's calf worship. He was almost certainly a priest of that calf religion, not a Levite from Jerusalem (1 Kgs 12:31). He taught them "how they should fear the LORD," but it was the same corrupted worship that had brought judgment in the first place. JFB: he was "one of the calf-worshipping priests."
- **"But every nation still made gods of its own" (v29).** Each group set up its own gods in the old Israelite high-place shrines. **Succoth-benoth** (v30) is obscure; the name means "booths of daughters" in Hebrew, and may be a distorted form of a Babylonian goddess's name. **Nergal** was the Mesopotamian god of the underworld and plague, worshipped at Cuthah. **Ashima** may be a West Semitic goddess. Amos speaks of those who swear "by the Guilt of Samaria" (*'ashmat*), which some think is a pun on her name (Amos 8:14). **Nibhaz and Tartak** (v31) are unknown, perhaps Elamite or Syrian gods. **Adrammelech and Anammelech** received child sacrifice. The Tyndale notes observe that Jewish scribes may have deliberately mangled some of these names out of contempt.
- **"They feared the LORD but also served their own gods" (v33).** The writer plays on the word "fear." At first they "did not fear the LORD" (v25). Then they "feared the LORD" and also served their gods (vv32–33). And then, "to this day… they do not fear the LORD" (v34). Fear of the LORD mixed with other gods is not fear of the LORD at all. The word for mixing religions is *syncretism*.
- **"The LORD made a covenant with them and commanded them, 'You shall not fear other gods'" (vv35–39).** "Them" here means the children of Jacob, the Israelites who should have known better. The writer quotes the heart of the covenant four times over: "You shall not fear other gods… but you shall fear the LORD your God, and he will deliver you out of the hand of all your enemies" (v39; Deut 6:13–15). That single promise was the key to everything. Fear the LORD alone, and he will rescue you. Israel never believed it.
- **"So these nations feared the LORD and also served their carved images… to this day" (v41).** This mixed population, with some remaining Israelites, became the ancestors of the Samaritans. Later Jews called them "Cutheans," after Cuthah (v24). After the exile they offered to help rebuild the temple and were refused (Ezra 4:1–3), and the hostility between Jews and Samaritans ran deep by Jesus' day (John 4:9). Over time the Samaritans moved away from idols and kept only the five books of Moses, worshipping on Mount Gerizim. A small Samaritan community still exists near Nablus today.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The terror of the Almighty will sometimes produce a forced or feigned submission in unconverted men." He ends with the faithful remnant who have "chosen that good part which shall not be taken from" them (Luke 10:42). JFB calls the settlers' religion "a mongrel doctrine" and "a strange medley or compound of the service of God and the service of idols." He adds that "oral teaching was much better fitted for the superstitious people than instruction out of a written book." He assigns the settlement to Esarhaddon, which is true of a later wave (Ezra 4:2), though the first settlers came under Sargon. Guzik applies the passage to divided loyalties today: fearing God out of alarm while still holding onto one's own idols. He notes that the Samaritans had less light than Israel and that greater revelation brings greater accountability.

#### Pointing to Christ
2 Kings 17 is the funeral of a nation that kept God at a distance. The mixed people it leaves behind, the Samaritans, are the very people Jesus went out of his way to reach. He sat by Jacob's well in Samaria and told a Samaritan woman, "You worship what you do not know," and then promised that "the hour is coming, and is now here, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth" (John 4:22–23). Her town believed him and called him "the Savior of the world" (John 4:42). After his resurrection the gospel went first to Jerusalem, "and in all Judea and Samaria" (Acts 1:8; 8:5–25). The land that heard "you shall fear the LORD your God, and he will deliver you" welcomed the deliverer at last.
