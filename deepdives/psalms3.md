*Setting:* This is the first psalm in the Psalter with a title: "A Psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom his son." The titles are very old, though most scholars think they were added by those who collected the psalms rather than written with them. This one points to the darkest stretch of David's reign, around 980 BC, told in 2 Samuel 15–18. His son Absalom had spent four years winning the people's hearts, then had himself proclaimed king in Hebron. David's own counselor Ahithophel went over to him. David fled Jerusalem on foot, weeping as he climbed the Mount of Olives, his head covered and barefoot (2 Sam 15:30), while a man of Saul's family, Shimei, threw stones and cursed him as a man of blood getting what he deserved (16:5–8). Behind all of it was Nathan's word after the Bathsheba affair: "the sword shall never depart from your house" (2 Sam 12:10). The placing is striking. Psalm 2 promised that God's anointed King would triumph over raging nations. Psalm 3 shows that same king running for his life from his own son. It begins a run of psalms (3–7) in which the Lord's anointed cries out under attack. Psalm 3 is often read as a morning prayer (v5), and Psalm 4 as an evening one (4:8).

#### How Many Are My Foes (vv1–2)
- **"O LORD, how many are my foes!" (v1).** David goes straight to God. He does not start with strategy or self-pity, but with the name of the covenant God, *Yahweh*. The word "many" comes three times in two verses (*rabbu… rabbim… rabbim*): how many, many rising, many saying. The rebellion grew like a flood. "The conspiracy grew strong, and the people with Absalom kept increasing" (2 Sam 15:12).
- **"Many are rising against me" (v1).** These were not foreign enemies but his own people, his son, his trusted advisers. The pain of betrayal by those close to us runs all through David's psalms (41:9; 55:12–14).
- **"Many are saying of my soul, 'There is no salvation for him in God'" (v2).** "Of my soul" means "about me," the whole person. This was the worst blow. Shimei said it out loud: "The LORD has avenged on you all the blood of the house of Saul… See, your evil is on you" (2 Sam 16:8). The charge was that God had abandoned David for good. It had enough truth in it to hurt, since David knew this trouble came from his own sin. But the enemies drew the wrong conclusion. Discipline is not desertion. "Salvation" here is *yeshuah*, rescue or deliverance, the word that comes back at the very end (v8). The enemies say there is none for him in God; David will end by saying it belongs to God.
- **"Selah" (v2).** This word appears 71 times in the Psalms and 3 times in Habakkuk 3. Nobody knows for certain what it means. Most think it marks a pause, possibly a musical interlude or a cue for the singers to lift their voices. Here it falls right after the enemies' taunt, as if to let it hang in the air before the answer comes.
- **Commentators:** All three see the taunt of v2 as the deepest wound. JFB calls it the "bitterest reproach for a pious man," and Henry calls it a spiteful attempt to drive David to despair. Guzik thinks the enemies meant God was unwilling rather than unable to help, because they saw David's sin as deserving it, and he passes on Spurgeon: "It is the most bitter of all afflictions to be led to fear that there is no help for us in God." On "Selah," Henry calls it "a note commanding a solemn pause," and JFB says it probably marks "a rest or pause," with "something emphatic in the sentiment."

#### But You, O LORD (vv3–4)
- **"But you, O LORD" (v3).** The turn comes in one word: "But." David looks away from the crowd to the Lord. Nothing in his situation has changed yet. What has changed is where he is looking.
- **"A shield about me" (v3).** A shield (*magen*) was a small round shield carried into battle. A shield "about me," all around, is more than any soldier could carry. God told Abraham, "I am your shield" (Gen 15:1), and David uses the picture often (18:2; 28:7). With enemies on every side (v6), he needs protection on every side.
- **"My glory" (v3).** "Glory" (*kavod*) carries the idea of weight, honor and dignity. David had just lost his crown, his city and his reputation. He says God himself is his honor. A king without a throne still has God, and that is enough.
- **"And the lifter of my head" (v3).** David left Jerusalem with his head covered in grief and shame (2 Sam 15:30). To lift someone's head is to restore them, to give them back their dignity and hope. It is the picture of a king raising a prisoner who bows before him (Gen 40:13; 2 Kings 25:27). God would one day bring David home with his head lifted.
- **"I cried aloud to the LORD, and he answered me from his holy hill" (v4).** David had sent the ark back to Jerusalem with Zadok rather than taking it into exile with him: "If I find favor in the eyes of the LORD, he will bring me back" (2 Sam 15:25). Absalom now held Jerusalem. Yet God still answered "from his holy hill," the Zion of 2:6. Absalom could take the city, but he could not take God's throne. The verbs can be read as a past experience ("he answered") or a habit ("he answers"). Either way, prayer reached God when David was far from the temple.
- **Commentators:** Henry explains the three titles as safety ("a shield for me"), honor ("my glory"), so that David's worth rests on God when his earthly rank has fallen, and restoration ("the lifter up of my head"). He notes that God answers from his holy hill even though David is far from the ark. JFB calls the shield "a favorite and often-used figure for protection." Guzik observes that David's circumstances offered nothing to boast in or be lifted by, but his God did, and that though Absalom held Jerusalem, God still held his holy hill.

#### I Lay Down and Slept (vv5–6)
- **"I lay down and slept; I woke again, for the LORD sustained me" (v5).** This is one of the most remarkable lines in the Psalms. Ahithophel advised Absalom to send 12,000 men to attack David that very night, while he was "weary and discouraged" (2 Sam 17:1–2). God turned that advice aside through Hushai (17:14), and David crossed the Jordan safely (17:22). Whether or not this is the exact night the psalm remembers, a hunted man slept and woke up alive. Both the sleep and the waking were gifts. "Sustained" means to lean on and be held up. The Lord was the one who kept watch while David could not (121:3–4).
- **Sleep as an act of trust.** To lie down in danger is to admit that you cannot protect yourself. Worry keeps us awake because we think everything depends on us. David's sleep was faith in practice: "In peace I will both lie down and sleep; for you alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety" (4:8). Proverbs says the same: "when you lie down, your sleep will be sweet" (Prov 3:24).
- **"I will not be afraid of many thousands of people" (v6).** "Many thousands" is *rivevot*, "ten thousands," a word for a vast number. Absalom's army was huge (2 Sam 17:11). David does not deny it. He simply refuses to be ruled by fear of it. "The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?" (27:1). Paul asks the same question in a different key: "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31).
- **"Who have set themselves against me all around" (v6).** The enemy is "all around," but so is God's shield (v3). The psalm matches surrounding danger with surrounding protection.
- **Commentators:** Henry credits David's rest to God's sustaining care and calls his courage in v6 a holy boldness. JFB notes that "sustained" can be read "will sustain," as if it was the thought in David's mind when he lay down, and the reason for his calm. Guzik counts both falling asleep and waking up as signs of God's blessing, and passes on an old Puritan's remark that God was "a soft pillow indeed" for a man hunted by his own army. He also links v6 to Romans 8:31.

#### Salvation Belongs to the LORD (vv7–8)
- **"Arise, O LORD! Save me, O my God!" (v7).** "Arise" is the battle cry Moses used every time the ark set out ahead of Israel: "Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered" (Num 10:35). David has sent the ark back, but he still calls on the God of the ark to go into battle for him. Note the "but" of v2 answered: they said "no salvation for him in God"; he prays "save me, O my God."
- **"You strike all my enemies on the cheek; you break the teeth of the wicked" (v7).** A slap on the cheek was a public humiliation (1 Kings 22:24; Job 16:10). Breaking teeth leaves a wild beast harmless, unable to bite (58:6). The enemies are pictured as animals ready to devour, and God disarms them. The verbs are in the past tense in Hebrew. David may be recalling what God has done before, or he may be speaking of the future with such confidence that he describes it as done. The prayer does not ask David to take revenge. He leaves it to God, as he did in sparing Saul (1 Sam 24:12). He even ordered his men to deal gently with Absalom (2 Sam 18:5).
- **"Salvation belongs to the LORD" (v8).** This is the answer to v2. The enemies said salvation was not to be found in God; David says it is God's alone, his to give. Jonah prayed the same words from inside the fish (Jonah 2:9), and the great multitude in heaven sings them: "Salvation belongs to our God who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb!" (Rev 7:10).
- **"Your blessing be on your people!" (v8).** David ends by praying not for himself but for the nation, including the people who had followed Absalom. A true shepherd-king cares for the flock even when it has turned against him. When the war was won, David wept over his son: "O Absalom, my son, my son! Would I had died instead of you" (2 Sam 18:33).
- **Commentators:** Henry says God's promises of salvation drive us to prayer rather than replace it, and that the closing verse rests on two truths: God has power to save, and his blessing rests on his people, "salvation belongeth unto the Lord." JFB sees the enemies' teeth as those of wild beasts "ready to devour," and calls v8 "an ascription of praise to a delivering God." Guzik connects "Arise" to Numbers 10:35. He takes v8 to mean that salvation is no nation's or party's property but the Lord's, so everyone must come to him directly, and he notes that David's last thought is for the whole people.

#### Pointing to Christ
Long after David, another Son of David crossed the Kidron and went up the Mount of Olives at night, betrayed by a trusted friend (John 18:1–3; 2 Sam 15:23, 30). He too was surrounded by many who said God would not save him: "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now, if he desires him" (Matt 27:43). He was struck on the cheek (John 18:22). But where David lay down in sleep and woke because the Lord sustained him, Jesus lay down in death and rose on the third day, the true "I woke again." Even in a storm he could sleep in the boat (Mark 4:38), with the trust this psalm describes. His enemies thought there was no salvation for him in God. In fact, through his death and rising, salvation came for everyone who calls on him, and the blessing of v8 rests on his people.
