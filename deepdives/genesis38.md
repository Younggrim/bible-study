*Setting:* Joseph has just been sold into Egypt, and the last verse of chapter 37 left him in Potiphar's house. Now, just when we want to know what happens to Joseph, Genesis turns away to Judah. It is not a detour. Judah was the brother who said, "Come, let us sell him" (37:27). He is the fourth son of Leah, and since Reuben, Simeon and Levi have all disgraced themselves (34:25–31; 35:22), the leadership of the family, and the royal line, will pass to him (49:8–10). This chapter shows Judah at his worst and also the moment he begins to change. It also shows how fast Jacob's family was blending into the Canaanites around them, which helps explain why God moved them to Egypt, where they would live apart (43:32; 46:34). The events probably stretch over twenty years or more, so they overlap with Joseph's years in Egypt. The story is frank about sex and deceit. It is told plainly, and it ends in the family line of King David and of Jesus.

#### Judah and Tamar (vv1–30)
- **"Judah went down from his brothers" (v1).** Joseph was "brought down" to Egypt (39:1), and Jacob said he would "go down" to Sheol mourning (37:35). Now Judah "went down" too, leaving the family and the hill country for the lowlands to the west. **"At that time"** is a loose connection, not a precise date. JFB calls it "a formula… not to describe any precise period, but an interval near about it." Since Judah's grandsons are listed among those who went to Egypt (46:12), some think his marriage began before Joseph was sold.
- **"A certain Adullamite, whose name was Hirah" (v1).** Adullam was a town in the foothills (the Shephelah) southwest of Jerusalem. Centuries later David hid in "the cave of Adullam" (1 Sam 22:1). Judah settles in with Canaanite friends.
- **"Judah saw the daughter of a certain Canaanite… He took her" (v2).** "Saw" and "took" is the pattern of grasping desire that runs through Genesis (3:6; 6:2; 34:2). Abraham and Isaac had insisted their sons not marry Canaanites (24:3; 28:1), and Esau's Canaanite wives brought grief to his parents (26:34–35). Judah does it anyway. His wife is never named. She is "Shua's daughter," which is what 1 Chronicles 2:3 calls her: Bath-shua. JFB says Judah, like Esau, was "casting off the restraints of religion."
- **Er, Onan and Shelah (vv3–5).** Judah names the first son; his wife names the next two. **"Judah was in Chezib"** (v5). Chezib (probably the Achzib of Josh 15:44) sounds like the Hebrew word for "lie" or "deceive," and Micah later puns on it (Mic 1:14). In a chapter about deception, the place where Shelah was born, the son who is withheld, carries a fitting name.
- **"Er, Judah's firstborn, was wicked in the sight of the LORD, and the LORD put him to death" (vv6–7).** Judah chooses a wife for Er, Tamar. Her background is not given; most assume she was a Canaanite too. What Er did is not said. In Hebrew, *Er* (ʿr) spelled backwards is *ra'* (rʿ), "evil," a wordplay that is hard to miss. God, silent in chapter 37, is named here only as judge.
- **"Perform the duty of a brother-in-law" (v8).** This is the custom later called levirate marriage, from the Latin *levir*, "husband's brother." If a man died without a son, his brother took the widow as his wife, and the first son born counted as the dead man's son and heir, so his name and inheritance did not die out. The Law of Moses later made this formal and allowed a brother to refuse, at the cost of public shame (Deut 25:5–10). The book of Ruth shows a wider version of the same duty, carried out by Boaz. For a childless widow in that world, this custom was her security and her future.
- **"Onan knew that the offspring would not be his" (vv9–10).** "Whenever he went in" shows this was repeated, not a single act. Onan used Tamar for sex while making sure she never conceived. There was money in it too. If Er had no heir, Onan as the next son would get a larger share of Judah's estate. The narrator calls it "wicked in the sight of the LORD," and God put him to death as well. Christians have read this differently. Some, especially in older Catholic and Protestant teaching, took it as a ruling against contraception or any wasting of seed. Most interpreters today, Protestant and many others, see the sin as refusing the duty to his dead brother and exploiting Tamar for his own gain. The later law punished refusing the duty only with disgrace, which suggests God judged the deceit and greed behind Onan's actions, not just the act.
- **"Remain a widow in your father's house, till Shelah my son grows up" (v11).** The narrator tells us what Judah would not say aloud: "he feared that he would die, like his brothers." Judah blames Tamar, as if she were cursed, when the text says plainly that his sons died for their own wickedness. Sending her home leaves her in limbo. She is bound to Judah's family and cannot marry anyone else, yet Judah does not intend to give her Shelah. The Tyndale notes observe that Judah now fails at the same duty his son did.
- **"When Judah was comforted" (v12).** Judah's wife dies. After the time of mourning he goes up to **Timnah** for sheepshearing, a festival time of feasting and drinking (compare 1 Sam 25:2, 36; 2 Sam 13:23–28). JFB says the season was "spent in more than usual hilarity." Judah could be comforted. His father Jacob, grieving for Joseph, "refused to be comforted" (37:35).
- **Tamar takes off her widow's garments (vv13–14).** Clothing again marks a turning point, as it does all through the Joseph story (37:23; 39:12; 41:14). The narrator gives her reason: "she saw that Shelah was grown up, and she had not been given to him." She sits at **Enaim**, literally "the opening of the eyes," probably a place with two springs. It is a fitting name for the spot where Judah's eyes will finally be opened. She acts to secure an heir for her dead husband, the right Judah has denied her.
- **"He thought she was a prostitute, for she had covered her face" (v15).** The veil hid her identity. Judah does not recognize his own daughter-in-law. The narrator does not excuse him: he is a widower who goes looking for a prostitute on a road.
- **The goat and the pledge (vv16–18).** He offers "a young goat from the flock," the same animal his brothers used to fool their father with Joseph's robe (37:31), and the same animal Jacob used to fool Isaac (27:9). Tamar asks for a pledge until the goat arrives: **"Your signet and your cord and your staff."** The signet was a personal seal, often a small stone cylinder engraved with a design and worn on a cord around the neck. Rolled on clay, it left a mark like a signature. A man's staff was often carved to be recognizable as his. Together they were like handing over a modern ID card and signature. Tamar asked for the one thing that could prove who he was.
- **"She put on the garments of her widowhood" (v19).** She goes back to waiting. She is not looking for income. She wants a child for Er's line.
- **"Where is the cult prostitute?" (vv20–23).** Judah sends the goat with Hirah. The narrator used the common word for prostitute, *zonah* (v15). Hirah asks for a *qedeshah*, literally a "consecrated woman," which refers to women connected with Canaanite shrines and fertility worship. He may be using a more respectable-sounding word. Either way, the later law forbade both among Israel (Deut 23:17–18). **"Let her keep the things as her own, or we shall be laughed at" (v23).** Judah's main concern is his reputation. He would rather lose his seal and staff than be talked about.
- **"Let her be burned" (v24).** Three months later Tamar's pregnancy shows. Because she was promised to Shelah, her pregnancy is treated as adultery, and Judah, as head of the family, pronounces death at once. The later law prescribed burning for some sexual sins (Lev 20:14; 21:9). JFB notes that adultery was punished by burning in many ancient places. Judah is harsh toward the very sin he has committed himself. Henry: "It is a common thing for men to be severe against those very sins in others in which yet they allow themselves."
- **"Please identify whose these are" (v25).** Tamar does not accuse him publicly. She sends the seal, cord and staff with a quiet message, leaving him the chance to own up. Her words, *hakker-na*, "identify, please," are exactly what Judah and his brothers said to their father when they showed him Joseph's bloodied robe (37:32). The deceiver is now deceived, and the man who made his father "identify" a son's robe must now identify his own belongings.
- **"She is more righteous than I" (v26).** Judah "identified them" and confessed. The phrase can also be read "she is in the right, not I." Judah admits that he withheld Shelah and that Tamar, in her own way, was more faithful to the family than he was. This is the first time in the story that one of Jacob's sons admits he was wrong. It is the start of Judah's change, which comes to its height when he offers himself as a slave in place of Benjamin (44:18–34). The narrator adds that Judah "did not know her again." He did not continue the relationship.
- **Was Tamar right?** The text does not praise her method, and readers should not either. She deceived her father-in-law into sex. But Judah's verdict stands, and Scripture later remembers her with honor. When Boaz married Ruth, the townspeople prayed, "may your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah" (Ruth 4:12). Like Ruth after her, she was an outsider who would not let a dead man's name be lost.
- **Twins, a scarlet thread, and a breach (vv27–30).** The birth echoes Jacob and Esau (25:24–26). A hand comes out first, and the midwife ties a scarlet thread to mark the firstborn. Then the hand draws back and the other twin pushes past. **"What a breach you have made for yourself!"** gives the name **Perez**, "breach" or "breaking out." The one with the thread is **Zerah**, probably meaning "dawning" or "shining," perhaps for the bright red thread. Once again the younger comes first. From Zerah came Achan (Josh 7:1) and the famous wise men Ethan and Heman (1 Kgs 4:31; 1 Chr 2:6). From Perez came Boaz and then David (Ruth 4:18–22).
- **The twins replace the sons Judah lost.** Er and Onan died. Shelah, the son Judah protected, is not the line God uses (1 Chr 4:21). Through Tamar, Judah gets two sons, and the line of promise runs through the one born of the most unlikely union in the chapter.
- **Why this chapter is here.** Set beside chapter 39, it is a deliberate contrast. Judah, free, goes looking for a prostitute and falls. Joseph, a slave, is pursued by his master's wife and runs. Both stories involve a garment held as evidence (38:18, 25; 39:12–16). Both end with the truth coming out. Genesis is preparing us to see which of the two brothers will rule in Egypt, and how Judah will become the leader his father blesses at the end of the book.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the chapter as a picture of the family sliding into Canaanite ways and blames Judah for leaving Tamar stuck. He argues that Onan's sin was a selfish use of sex and a refusal of duty, and that the passage is not mainly about masturbation, which is not what Onan was doing. He also says the conception was not Judah's plan but was planned by Tamar and by God. Henry says of the hidden sin coming to light, "wickedness that has been most secretly committed, and most industriously concealed, yet sometimes is strangely brought to light." On the twins he writes that "like Jacob and Esau, [they] struggled for the birthright," and that "both these sons are named in the genealogy of our Saviour." JFB calls levirate marriage "the first instance of a custom" later written into Moses' law, and wonders why Judah gave the cord and staff as well as the signet, which alone seemed worth more than the goat. The Tyndale notes add that Hirah looked for a shrine prostitute when Judah had in fact gone to an ordinary one. JFB has no notes on vv9–11, vv13–14, v21, v26 or vv27–30.

#### Pointing to Christ
Matthew's genealogy of Jesus begins, "Judah the father of Perez and Zerah by Tamar" (Matt 1:3). Tamar is the first of four women Matthew names, with Rahab, Ruth and "the wife of Uriah," each with a story that a respectable family tree would leave out. Matthew leaves them in on purpose. The Messiah came through a family that sold a brother, went to prostitutes, and broke faith with widows. That does not excuse any of it. It shows grace. Jesus is "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" (Rev 5:5), and he came from this line not because it was pure but because it needed him. Judah's words, "She is more righteous than I," are the first real confession in the story of Jacob's sons. Jesus is the one truly righteous Son of Judah, who took the place of the unrighteous so that people like Judah and Tamar could be forgiven.
