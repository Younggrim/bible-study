*Setting:* Chapter 3 ended with the Preacher looking at corrupt courts (3:16) and at death, which makes man and beast seem alike (3:19–21). He trusted that "God will judge" (3:17), but he could not yet see it. Chapter 4 keeps looking at life in society, at "evil under the sun." He sees the tears of people crushed by those in power, the envy that drives hard work, the lonely man who works himself to death with no one to leave his money to, and the fickle crowd that cheers a new king and forgets him. In the middle stands one of the warmest passages in the book: "Two are better than one" (vv9–12). The chapter is built from short observations and proverbs, several of them starting with "better," a form common in wisdom writing (Prov 15:16–17; 16:8). For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### Evil Under the Sun (vv1–16)
- **"Again I saw all the oppressions that are done under the sun" (v1).** "Again" picks up from 3:16, the wickedness in the place of justice, as JFB notes. "Oppressions" is from *'ashaq*, to crush, extort or defraud: a boss who withholds wages (Deut 24:14), a creditor who strips the poor (Mic 2:2), a government that grinds people down. The Tyndale notes say oppression shows up in families, workplaces and governments alike.
- **"Behold, the tears of the oppressed, and they had no one to comfort them!" (v1).** He does not describe the injustice in the abstract. He looks at the tears. Then he says it twice: "no one to comfort them." JFB notes the repetition stresses suffering that goes on without relief. "On the side of their oppressors there was power." The weak have tears. The strong have power. Under the sun no one stands in the gap. The Law and the prophets demand care for the oppressed (Exod 22:21–24; Isa 1:17), and Eaton, cited by Guzik, notes how common that compassion is in the Old Testament.
- **"I thought the dead who are already dead more fortunate than the living" (v2).** "Thought" is literally "praised" or "congratulated." Looking at so much misery, he says the dead are lucky to be out of it. Job and Jeremiah said similar things in their worst hours (Job 3:11–22; Jer 20:14–18). JFB calls this "a profane sentiment if severed from its connection." In its setting it is the honest cry of someone who sees no justice and has not yet seen God's answer.
- **"But better than both is he who has not yet been" (v3).** Better still never to have been born, and never to have seen "the evil deeds that are done under the sun." This is the low point of the chapter. The Preacher does not argue it as a doctrine. He states how life looks when injustice is all one can see. Guzik notes that Jesus said this of only one man: "It would have been better for that man if he had not been born" (Matt 26:24).
- **"All toil and all skill in work come from a man's envy of his neighbor" (v4).** "Skill" is *kishron*, success or achievement (2:21). The engine behind much hard work is rivalry: keeping up with, and getting ahead of, the neighbor. That is what Paul calls "selfish ambition" (Phil 2:3). It can produce impressive results, but it is "a striving after wind," because the race never ends and the prize never satisfies. The Tyndale notes put it in modern terms: competition drives most people to succeed. The word for "envy" (*qin'ah*) is the same word as "jealousy" in Song 8:6. There it describes protective love. Here it describes rivalry.
- **"The fool folds his hands and eats his own flesh" (v5).** Proverbs describes the sluggard with the same folded hands (Prov 6:10; 24:33). If envy is one extreme, laziness is the other. The man who quits working "eats his own flesh." He destroys himself, starving or using up what he has. Deane, cited by Guzik, reads it as self-destruction. JFB takes it as the idle man becoming "a self-tormentor, never satisfied, his spirit preying on itself."
- **"Better is a handful of quietness than two hands full of toil and a striving after wind" (v6).** Here is the wise middle. Not folded hands (v5), not two hands grabbing (v4), but one hand, open, with rest. "Quietness" (*nachat*) means rest or calm. It is the same idea as Proverbs 15:16: "Better is a little with the fear of the LORD than great treasure and trouble with it." Paul says the same: "godliness with contentment is great gain" (1 Tim 6:6). The Tyndale notes call it moderation and contentment.
- **"One person who has no other, either son or brother, yet there is no end to all his toil" (vv7–8).** A man alone, with no family and no heir, who works without stopping and is never satisfied with what he has. He never stops to ask the obvious question: "For whom am I toiling and depriving myself of pleasure?" This is the envy of v4 taken to its end: work as an addiction, with no one to share it with and no one to leave it to. Kidner, cited by Guzik, calls it lonely, pointless busyness. Jesus told the story of a rich man like this, building bigger barns, to whom God said, "Fool! This night your soul is required of you, and the things you have prepared, whose will they be?" (Luke 12:16–21).
- **"Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their toil" (v9).** The lonely man (v8) leads into the opposite picture. Two people working together do more than twice what one can do alone. The Preacher gives four examples, and Guzik draws them out: work, a fall, cold, and attack.
- **"If they fall, one will lift up his fellow. But woe to him who is alone when he falls" (v10).** Travel in the ancient world was dangerous. A man who stumbled into a ravine or broke a leg on a mountain path might die if he was alone. The same is true in the spiritual life (Gal 6:1–2; Heb 3:13; 10:24–25).
- **"If two lie together, they keep warm, but how can one keep warm alone?" (v11).** Nights in the hills of Israel can be cold, and travelers slept together for warmth under one cloak. The picture certainly fits marriage, but it is wider than that: friends, companions on a journey. JFB says the image comes from marriage but applies to fellowship generally.
- **"Though a man might prevail against one who is alone, two will withstand him" (v12).** A robber can overpower a lone traveler. Two can fight back. David and Jonathan made a covenant of friendship that protected David's life (1 Sam 18:1–4; 20:42).
- **"A threefold cord is not quickly broken" (v12).** Having gone from one to two, the proverb goes to three. A rope made of three strands twisted together is much stronger than three strands apart. Eaton, cited by Guzik, notes that the saying was proverbial in the ancient world. The Preacher's point is that companionship multiplies strength, and the more the better. Christians often read the third strand as God, binding husband and wife together. That is a fair application, but it is not what the text itself says. Guzik notes that this reading is common and well applied. JFB suggests husband, wife and children.
- **"Better was a poor and wise youth than an old and foolish king who no longer knew how to take advice" (v13).** A short story, with the details hard to follow. Age and power are not the same as wisdom. An old king who can no longer be told anything is worse off than a poor young man who can. "Take advice" means "be warned." Rehoboam lost his kingdom by refusing to listen to the old men who advised him (1 Kgs 12:6–15). Solomon himself, in his old age, ignored God's warnings (1 Kgs 11:9–11).
- **"For he went from prison to the throne, though in his own kingdom he had been born poor" (v14).** Joseph rose from an Egyptian prison to rule Egypt (Gen 41:14, 41). David rose from the sheepfolds. The Hebrew is unclear about who went to prison and who was born poor, the youth or the old king. The ESV reads both as the youth.
- **"I saw all the living… along with that youth who was to stand in the king's place" (v15).** The crowds rush to the rising star. The Hebrew calls him "the second youth," which some take to mean a third figure, the next young man to replace the first. JFB thinks the first youth is Jeroboam, the servant who rebelled against Solomon and ruled the ten northern tribes (1 Kgs 11:26–40), and the second Rehoboam. The Tyndale notes and Guzik do not name anyone. The point does not depend on knowing who is meant.
- **"There was no end of all the people, all of whom he led. Yet those who come later will not rejoice in him" (v16).** Today the new king is the people's darling. Tomorrow another generation will come, and he will be forgotten or resented. Popularity is *hebel*, a breath, and politics is "a striving after wind." The Tyndale notes say it plainly: a ruler's popularity is temporary. Absalom stole the hearts of the people (2 Sam 15:6), and the same crowds that shouted "Hosanna" shouted "Crucify him."
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as a set of temptations and remedies: the temptation of the oppressed to "discontent and impatience" (vv1–3), the temptation to drop honest work for fear of envy (vv4–6), the folly of hoarding (vv7–8), the remedy of "society and mutual assistance" (vv9–12), and the instability of royal power, through both the prince's own folly and the people's fickleness (vv13–16). Guzik says vv4–6 together affirm hard work, condemn laziness and call for contentment. On vv13–16 he passes on Kidner's line that the young king has climbed to the top of human glory only to be stranded there. JFB, on v8: "The miser would not be able to give an account of his infatuation." JFB quotes a rabbinic saying on v9: "A man without a companion is like a left hand without the right."

#### Pointing to Christ
"They had no one to comfort them" (v1). Ecclesiastes leaves the oppressed weeping with no comforter, and it is honest to do so. Under the sun, that is often how it is. But Isaiah promised one who would come "to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives… to comfort all who mourn" (Isa 61:1–2), and Jesus read those words in Nazareth and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:18–21). He promised his followers "another Helper," the Spirit, whose name means "the one called alongside" (John 14:16). The lonely man of v8 works for no one. In Christ believers are made a body, members of one another (1 Cor 12:12–27), and "woe to him who is alone when he falls" (v10) is answered by the Shepherd who goes after the one who has fallen and carries it home (Luke 15:4–6). And unlike the youth whom the next generation forgot (v16), Christ's kingdom "shall never be destroyed" (Dan 2:44), and every generation will bow to him (Phil 2:10–11).
