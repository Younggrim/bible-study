*Setting:* Babylonia, around 591 BC, among the exiles. This is the longest chapter in Ezekiel and one of the hardest in the Bible to read aloud. It retells Jerusalem's whole history as the life story of one woman: a baby girl thrown out at birth, rescued by the LORD, raised, married, and dressed like a queen, who then gives herself and her husband's gifts to every passing lover. Hosea (Hos 1–3) and Jeremiah (Jer 2–3) had already pictured Israel's idolatry as adultery against God, but Ezekiel goes further and is far blunter. The bluntness is deliberate. The exiles believed Jerusalem was too precious to God to fall. Ezekiel wants them to feel how ugly her unfaithfulness looked to the husband who had loved her. Chapter 15 ended with the words "they have acted faithlessly" (15:8). Chapter 16 shows what that meant. Then, at the very end, it turns to grace.

#### The Lord's Faithless Bride (vv1–58)
- **"Make known to Jerusalem her abominations" (v2).** The chapter is addressed to the city, but the city stands for the nation, its people and its kings. This is a court case. Ezekiel is the prosecutor, and the charge sheet is history.
- **"Your father was an Amorite and your mother a Hittite" (v3).** This was an insult to people proud of descending from Abraham. Jerusalem really did begin as a Canaanite city. Long before Israel took it, it was the Jebusite stronghold of Jebus, and David captured it only around 1000 BC (2 Sam 5:6–9). Amorites and Hittites were among the peoples of Canaan whose sins God had judged (Gen 15:16; Deut 7:1). Ezekiel's point is moral more than ethnic. Jerusalem had nothing in her background to recommend her to God. Moses had told Israel the same thing: God did not choose them because they were many or great (Deut 7:7–8).
- **"Your cord was not cut, nor were you washed… nor rubbed with salt, nor wrapped in swaddling cloths" (v4).** These were the ordinary acts of care for a newborn in the ancient Near East. A midwife cut and tied the cord, washed the baby, rubbed the skin with salt (a practice still known in parts of the Middle East, thought to firm and cleanse the skin), and wrapped the child tightly in strips of cloth. This baby received none of it.
- **"You were cast out on the open field, for you were abhorred" (v5).** Abandoning unwanted babies, especially girls, was common in the ancient world. They were left outside to die or to be picked up by someone who might raise them as slaves. **"No eye pitied you."** The child had no claim on anyone.
- **"When I passed by you and saw you wallowing in your blood, I said to you in your blood, 'Live!'" (v6).** The phrase is repeated, for emphasis. Life comes by God's word alone, spoken to a helpless child who could do nothing for herself. Many readers have heard the gospel in this verse, and rightly. Paul uses the same order: "even when we were dead in our trespasses, [God] made us alive together with Christ" (Eph 2:5).
- **"I made you flourish like a plant of the field" (v7).** The girl grows into a young woman, still **"naked and bare"**, with no clothing, family or protection. This may picture Israel's growth in Egypt, where it multiplied but was poor and enslaved (Exod 1:7).
- **"I spread the corner of my garment over you and covered your nakedness" (v8).** This was the way to claim a bride. Ruth asked Boaz to do exactly this: "spread your wings over your servant, for you are a redeemer" (Ruth 3:9). The word for "corner" (*kanaph*) also means wing. **"I made my vow to you and entered into a covenant with you… and you became mine."** Most commentators see the covenant at Sinai here (Exod 19:5–6; 24:7–8), though the details fit Jerusalem in particular, the city God chose as the place for his name (1 Kings 9:3; Ps 132:13).
- **Washed, anointed, clothed and crowned (vv9–13).** The husband does everything the birth family failed to do and more. He washes off the blood, anoints her with oil, and dresses her in **"embroidered cloth… fine leather… fine linen… silk."** Several of these words belong to the tabernacle. "Fine leather" (*taḥash*) is the outer covering of the tabernacle in Exodus 26:14. Everywhere else in the Old Testament the word is used only for the tabernacle and its coverings. Embroidered work and fine linen are tabernacle and priestly materials (Exod 26:36; 28:39). The bride's beauty is bound up with the worship God gave her. Bracelets, necklace, nose ring and crown were the jewelry of a royal bride (Gen 24:47). She eats **"fine flour and honey and oil"**, the foods of a land flowing with milk and honey and also the stuff of offerings (Lev 2:1).
- **"You advanced to royalty… your renown went forth among the nations" (vv13–14).** This is Jerusalem in the days of David and Solomon, when the Queen of Sheba came to see her splendor (1 Kings 10:1–9). **"It was perfect through the splendor that I had bestowed on you."** All of it was a gift.
- **"But you trusted in your beauty" (v15).** Here the story turns. The first step in her fall was not lust but pride, trusting the gift instead of the Giver. Moses had warned of this exact danger: when Israel had eaten and was full, it would forget the LORD and say, "My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth" (Deut 8:11–17). **"Played the whore"** translates the verb *zanah*, which appears about twenty times in this chapter. It was the standard prophetic word for idolatry (Hos 1:2; Jer 3:1). It was also often literal, since Canaanite fertility worship could include ritual sex.
- **The gifts turned to idols (vv16–19).** Everything God gave her is turned against him. Her garments become decorations for shrines, his gold and silver become **"images of men"** (perhaps male idols), and his oil, incense, flour and honey are set before them as offerings. The repeated "my" ("my gold… my silver… my oil… my bread") stresses that she is giving away what belongs to her husband.
- **"You took your sons and your daughters, whom you had borne to me, and these you sacrificed to them" (v20).** This is the worst charge. Kings Ahaz and Manasseh burned their own sons as offerings (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6), and the practice spread at the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, where children were offered to Molech (Jer 7:31; 32:35). The law had called it an abomination (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31). God calls them **"my children"** (v21). The baby he rescued from death now gives her own children to death. **"Were your whorings so small a matter?"** Idolatry was never harmless. It ended in the murder of children.
- **"You did not remember the days of your youth" (v22).** This is the root of it all, forgetting. The word "remember" frames the chapter. Jerusalem did not remember (vv22, 43), but God will remember his covenant (v60), and then she will remember and be ashamed (vv61, 63).
- **"A vaulted chamber… a lofty place in every square" (vv24–25).** The word for "vaulted chamber" (*gav*) means a mound or raised platform, and "lofty place" (*ramah*) is a high place. These were shrines for false worship built on street corners in the city itself. Older translations and JFB render the first word "brothel" or "fornication chamber," following the Latin Vulgate. The Hebrew lexicon says that sense lacks good evidence. It was a raised shrine, which in this picture amounts to the same thing.
- **Egypt, Assyria and Chaldea (vv26–29).** The adultery now becomes political. Judah repeatedly looked to great powers for security instead of to God. It courted Egypt (Isa 30:1–3; 31:1), it bought help from Assyria (2 Kings 16:7–9, when Ahaz paid Tiglath-pileser III), and it sent envoys to Babylon (2 Kings 20:12–19; Ezek 23:14–16). Alliances in the ancient world came with the allies' gods, and Ahaz copied an Assyrian altar for the temple (2 Kings 16:10–16). **"You were not satisfied"** is said three times. Idolatry never delivers what it promises. **"The daughters of the Philistines, who were ashamed of your lewd behavior" (v27).** Even the pagan Philistines were shocked. Some scholars connect this with the Philistine cities that took Judean towns in Ahaz's day (2 Chr 28:18).
- **"How sick is your heart" (v30).** Some translators read "how weak," others "how inflamed with passion," since the Hebrew is unusual. The ESV takes it as a heart diseased by its own desire.
- **"You gave payment, while no payment was given to you" (vv31–34).** This is the low point. A prostitute at least earns money. Jerusalem paid her lovers to come. That was literally true. Judah sent tribute and gifts to Egypt and Assyria to buy help that never came (2 Kings 16:8; 18:14–16; Isa 30:6). **"You were different."** The irony is cutting. Israel was called to be different from the nations, holy and set apart. She was different only in being worse.
- **"I will gather all your lovers… and will uncover your nakedness to them" (vv35–39).** Now the sentence. In the ancient Near East, an adulterous wife could be publicly stripped as a sign of divorce and disgrace (Hos 2:3, 10). The nations she courted will turn against her. **"All those you loved and all those you hated"** may mean that Babylon, whom she once courted, and Edom and others whom she despised, will all join the attack, which is what happened in 586 BC (Ps 137:7; Obad 10–14). They will tear down her shrines and strip her of the jewels God gave her. She ends where she began, **"naked and bare"** (v39; compare vv7, 22).
- **"I will judge you as women who commit adultery and shed blood are judged" (v38).** The law prescribed death for both adultery (Lev 20:10; Deut 22:22) and murder (Gen 9:6). Jerusalem was guilty of both, idolatry and child sacrifice. **"They shall stone you and cut you to pieces with their swords" (v40).** Stoning was the legal penalty. The sword and the burning of houses (v41) are what an invading army does. The picture and the reality merge here: a city stormed, its houses burned (2 Kings 25:9).
- **"So will I satisfy my wrath on you, and my jealousy shall depart from you" (v42).** God's jealousy is not petty envy. It is the right anger of a husband betrayed by a wife he loved, the anger the second commandment speaks of (Exod 20:5). The verse also hints that the anger has an end. Once justice is done, God says, **"I will be calm and will no more be angry."**
- **"Like mother, like daughter" (v44).** The proverb sends the story back to v3. Jerusalem has turned out just like her Canaanite parents. **"Who loathed her husband and her children" (v45)** describes a family pattern of betrayal and child sacrifice.
- **"Your elder sister is Samaria… your younger sister… is Sodom" (v46).** In Hebrew "elder" and "younger" can also mean "bigger" and "smaller." Samaria, capital of the northern kingdom, was destroyed by Assyria in 722 BC for its idolatry (2 Kings 17:7–18). Sodom was the byword for wickedness, wiped out in Abraham's day (Gen 19). Samaria lay to the north ("on your left hand" in Hebrew, facing east), Sodom to the south. Calling Jerusalem their sister was the worst insult possible.
- **"This was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she… had pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy" (v49).** This verse is often quoted in arguments about Sodom. Ezekiel does not deny the sexual sin of Genesis 19. Verse 50 adds that **"they… did an abomination before me,"** and "abomination" (*to'evah*) is the word Leviticus uses for sexual sin (Lev 18:22). Jude 7 also names Sodom's sexual immorality. What Ezekiel adds is the root beneath the sin: pride, comfort, idleness, and indifference to the poor. Wealth and ease bred arrogance, and arrogance bred both cruelty and lust.
- **"Samaria has not committed half your sins" (v51).** Jerusalem had the temple, the priesthood, the Davidic king and prophets such as Isaiah and Jeremiah, and still did worse. More light means more guilt (Luke 12:48). **"You have made your sisters appear righteous."** Next to Jerusalem, Sodom looks almost respectable. Jesus said something similar about the towns that saw his miracles: "it will be more tolerable on the day of judgment for the land of Sodom than for you" (Matt 11:23–24).
- **"I will restore their fortunes, both the fortunes of Sodom… and… Samaria… and I will restore your own fortunes in their midst" (v53).** This is an astonishing turn. Sodom had been destroyed and was never rebuilt as a city. Readers take the promise in different ways. Some see the future inclusion of Gentile nations (the "daughters" of Sodom) in God's people. Others see a picture of grace reaching even the most hopeless, with Jerusalem's own restoration made just as surprising. What is clear is the humbling purpose: Jerusalem will be restored only alongside the sisters she despised, **"that you may bear your disgrace and be ashamed"** (v54).
- **"Was not your sister Sodom a byword in your mouth in the day of your pride?" (v56).** Jerusalem had used Sodom's name as an insult. Now the nations around her, **"the daughters of Syria… and… of the Philistines"** (v57), use Jerusalem's name the same way. (Some Hebrew manuscripts and the Syriac read "Edom" instead of "Syria," since the two words look almost the same in Hebrew. Edom fits the events of 586 BC well.)
- **Commentators:** Henry frames the chapter in five movements, from the foundling's rescue to God's mercy at the end, and he reads it as Israel's history from Egypt to exile. JFB notes that "the exposure of infants was common in ancient times," explains that salting was done "to make the skin firm," and sees the oil of v9 as an "emblem of the Levitical priesthood, the type of Messiah." On v49 JFB says God names "not merely her notorious lusts, but the secret spring of them," which agrees with the Tyndale notes, which list both Sodom's sexual sin and its pride and neglect of the poor. JFB also admits that "Sodom itself was not so restored" and takes v53 as still future. Guzik stresses that the decline began with pride in God's gifts, and he notes, as the Tyndale notes do, that the bride's clothing echoes the tabernacle. He applies it widely: any good thing God gives can become an idol when it is valued above him.

#### The Lord's Everlasting Covenant (vv59–63)
- **"I will deal with you as you have done, you who have despised the oath in breaking the covenant" (v59).** The verse sums up all that came before. An oath sealed a covenant. Jerusalem had treated hers with contempt, and justice requires that she bear the result.
- **"Yet I will remember my covenant with you in the days of your youth" (v60).** Everything turns on the word "yet." She forgot the days of her youth (v22, 43). God did not. In the Bible, God's remembering is not just recalling. It means acting on a commitment, as when "God remembered his covenant with Abraham" and moved to rescue Israel from Egypt (Exod 2:24).
- **"I will establish for you an everlasting covenant" (v60).** This is the new covenant that Jeremiah was announcing in Jerusalem at the same time (Jer 31:31–34; 32:40). Ezekiel returns to it as "a covenant of peace… an everlasting covenant" (37:26). The old covenant could be broken because it depended on Israel's faithfulness. The new one rests on what God will do.
- **"Then you will remember your ways and be ashamed" (v61).** Here the order is striking. Shame comes after grace, not before it. Jerusalem is not forgiven because she repents. She repents because she is forgiven. **"I give them to you as daughters, but not on account of the covenant with you."** Samaria and Sodom, the sisters she despised, will be given to her as daughters, but this will be a gift of God's new covenant, not something earned under the old.
- **"You shall know that I am the LORD" (v62).** Throughout Ezekiel, people come to know the LORD through his judgments. Here, for once, they come to know him through his mercy.
- **"That you may remember and be confounded, and never open your mouth again because of your shame, when I atone for you" (v63).** The verb is *kipper*, to atone or make covering, the word used for the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:30). God himself makes atonement for all she has done. The result is silence, not because she is crushed but because nothing is left to say. No excuse, no boasting, no complaint. Paul describes the same effect of grace: "every mouth may be stopped" (Rom 3:19), so that "no one may boast" (Eph 2:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry's whole comment on the ending is brief and warm: "After a full warning of judgments, mercy is remembered, mercy is reserved." He sees the promise partly fulfilled in the return from Babylon and more fully in the gospel. JFB calls shame "the fruit of repentance" and adds, "Nothing so melts into love and humility as the sense of the riches of God's pardoning grace." JFB and Guzik both read the everlasting covenant as the new covenant fulfilled in the Messiah, and the Tyndale notes agree that God's commitment cannot finally be undone by his people's sin. Guzik draws out the order of the passage: God's grace produces shame and humility, which is the true road to restoration.

#### Pointing to Christ
The story of the foundling is the story of every believer. Each of us was "dead in trespasses," with nothing to commend us, when God passed by and said, "Live." The husband who washes, clothes and adorns his bride is the picture Paul uses for Christ and the church: "Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word, so that he might present the church to himself in splendor" (Eph 5:25–27). But Ezekiel shows the cost. The faithless bride deserved to be stripped, exposed and put to death. At the cross, Jesus was the one stripped of his clothes and exposed to the crowd (John 19:23–24), bearing the judgment of a faithless people. That is how God "atones" for all his people have done (v63). The new covenant, sealed in Jesus' blood (Luke 22:20), is the "everlasting covenant" of v60, and its fruit is a people who remember what they were and are silenced by grace.
