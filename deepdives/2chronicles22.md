*Setting:* Judah and Israel in 841 BC, one of the most violent years in the Old Testament. Jehoram of Judah has just died of his terrible disease, unmourned (21:18–20). Raiders have killed all his older sons, so only the youngest is left (21:17). In the north, Ahab's son Joram (also spelled Jehoram) is king of Israel, and Ahab's daughter Athaliah is queen mother in Jerusalem. The two royal houses are tied together by marriage and by Baal worship. Elijah had announced that the house of Ahab would be wiped out (1 Kings 21:21–24), and Elisha has now sent a young prophet to anoint the army commander Jehu as the instrument of that judgment (2 Kings 9:1–10). Chronicles tells the story in twelve verses, mainly from Judah's side. The full drama of Jehu's revolt is in 2 Kings 9–11. The question from chapter 21 grows sharper: if David's house keeps marrying into Ahab's, will it share Ahab's end?

#### Ahaziah Reigns in Judah (vv1–9)
- **"The inhabitants of Jerusalem made Ahaziah, his youngest son, king" (v1).** The people of the capital acted, perhaps because there was no choice left. This is the son called Jehoahaz in 21:17: the same two name parts in reverse order, both meaning "the LORD has grasped." His older brothers had been killed by "the band of men that came with the Arabians to the camp." Jehoram killed his brothers, and his own sons were killed in turn.
- **"Twenty-two years old" (v2).** The standard Hebrew text here reads "forty-two," which cannot be right: it would make Ahaziah two years older than his father, who died at forty (21:20). The ESV follows some Greek manuscripts, the Syriac and 2 Kings 8:26, which all read twenty-two, and says so in a footnote. Hebrew wrote numbers with letters, and similar-looking numerals were easily confused in copying. This is a copyist's slip, not a contradiction in what the Chronicler wrote.
- **"He reigned one year" (v2).** The Tyndale notes date it to 841 BC. In ancient counting, part of a year was reckoned as a year, so the reign may have been only months.
- **"Athaliah, the granddaughter of Omri" (v2).** The Hebrew says "daughter of Omri," but "daughter" in Hebrew can mean any female descendant. She was Ahab's daughter (21:6) and Omri's granddaughter. Omri founded the northern dynasty and built Samaria. He was so powerful that Assyrian records went on calling Israel "the house of Omri" long after his line had ended. Naming him here sets Athaliah firmly in that idolatrous dynasty (1 Kings 16:25–26).
- **"His mother was his counselor in doing wickedly" (v3).** The queen mother was an official and powerful figure in Judah (compare Asa's removal of his grandmother Maacah in 15:16). A young king, newly crowned after disaster, leaned on the strongest person in the palace, and she was Jezebel's daughter.
- **"They were his counselors, to his undoing" (v4).** "They" are the house of Ahab, Athaliah's relatives and their people. The Chronicler loves the theme of counsel. Rehoboam lost ten tribes by taking the young men's advice (10:8), and here a king is ruined by listening to the wrong family. "To his undoing" translates a word for destruction or ruin.
- **"He even followed their counsel and went with Jehoram the son of Ahab… to make war against Hazael… at Ramoth-gilead" (v5).** Ramoth-gilead was a fortified Levitical city east of the Jordan (Josh 21:38) on the main trade road, constantly fought over by Israel and Aram (Syria). Ahaziah's grandfather Jehoshaphat had nearly died there beside Ahab (ch 18), and Ahab did die there. Now his grandson makes the same alliance at the same place. **Hazael** had recently murdered his master Ben-hadad and seized the throne of Aram, as Elisha had wept to foresee (2 Kings 8:7–15).
- **"He returned to be healed in Jezreel" (v6).** Jezreel, at the foot of Mount Gilboa, was the royal family's second residence. It was the town where Naboth had been murdered for his vineyard (1 Kings 21). "Ramah" here is a short form of Ramoth-gilead. Ahaziah went down to visit his wounded uncle, a family courtesy that put him in exactly the wrong place.
- **"It was ordained by God that the downfall of Ahaziah should come about through his going to visit Joram" (v7).** This is the Chronicler's key comment, and it is not in Kings. Literally, "from God was the downfall of Ahaziah." A sick-bed visit looks like chance, but the writer sees God's hand. Ahaziah had joined himself to Ahab's house in worship and war, so he was found with Ahab's house on the day of its judgment.
- **"Jehu the son of Nimshi, whom the LORD had anointed to destroy the house of Ahab" (v7).** Jehu was actually the grandson of Nimshi (2 Kings 9:2), another case of "son" meaning descendant. 2 Kings 9 tells the story: Jehu drives "furiously" to Jezreel, kills Joram with an arrow on Naboth's land, and has Jezebel thrown from a window. The Assyrian king Shalmaneser III's Black Obelisk, now in the British Museum, shows Jehu (or his envoy) bowing in tribute in 841 BC, the only surviving picture of an Israelite king.
- **A famous inscription.** The Tel Dan Stele, found in 1993–94 in northern Israel, is an Aramaic monument most scholars attribute to Hazael. Its broken lines claim the killing of "[Jo]ram son of [Ahab] king of Israel" and "[Ahaz]iah son of [Jehoram] king of the house of David." It is the oldest known mention outside the Bible of "the house of David." The Bible credits Jehu, while Hazael boasts of the deaths himself. Many scholars think Jehu and Hazael's war were bound together, and ancient kings often claimed credit for events that served them. Scripture's point is that behind both men stood God.
- **"The princes of Judah and the sons of Ahaziah's brothers" (v8).** Ahaziah's brothers were already dead (v1), so these "sons" are probably young relatives of the royal family. 2 Kings 10:12–14 tells of forty-two kinsmen of Ahaziah, on their way to visit the northern royals, whom Jehu caught and killed at a well. The family friendship with Ahab's house cost Judah almost all its remaining princes.
- **"Captured while hiding in Samaria… brought to Jehu and put to death" (v9).** 2 Kings 9:27–28 gives a fuller route: Ahaziah fled south, was shot near Ibleam, reached Megiddo and died there, and his servants took him to Jerusalem for burial. The two accounts are compressed differently. "Samaria" can mean the whole northern region as well as its capital city, and a likely sequence is that he hid for a time in Samaria, was found, was wounded as he fled, and died at Megiddo. Chronicles keeps only the outline: he hid, he was caught, he died.
- **"He is the grandson of Jehoshaphat, who sought the LORD with all his heart" (v9).** Even Jehu's men, or the men of Judah who received the body, gave him honorable burial for his grandfather's sake. 2 Kings 9:28 says he was buried with his fathers in the city of David, unlike his father Jehoram (21:20). A godly life went on blessing a family two generations later, even when the family did not deserve it. "Sought the LORD" is the Chronicler's favorite summary of true faith (1 Chr 16:11; 2 Chr 7:14; 15:2).
- **"The house of Ahaziah had no one able to rule the kingdom" (v9).** This is the lowest point of David's line so far. The king is dead, his brothers are dead, his nephews and kinsmen are dead, and his own sons are babies. The Tyndale notes observe that this almost gave David's house the same end as Saul's, which died out leaving no one to take the throne (1 Chr 10:6).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sums up the chapter in two lines: Ahaziah was the shame of his family as "a partaker, 1. In the sin, and, 2. In the destruction, of the house of Ahab," and Athaliah was its plague. JFB calls him a "facile king" who "surrendered himself wholly" to his mother and her relatives. On v2, Guzik and JFB agree that forty-two is a copying error, and both point to 2 Kings 8:26. JFB keeps the honor of v9 tied to "respect to his grandfather Jehoshaphat's memory." Guzik and JFB both work through the two accounts of Ahaziah's death and conclude that they can be reconciled, though Guzik admits the details are complicated. Guzik notes, following Selman, that the "sons of Ahaziah's brothers" were probably wider relatives.

#### Athaliah Reigns in Judah (vv10–12)
- **"She arose and destroyed all the royal family of the house of Judah" (v10).** Athaliah's son is dead, her mother Jezebel is dead, her brother Joram and seventy sons of Ahab are dead (2 Kings 10:1–11). Her family's power in the north is gone. In Judah she strikes first, killing her own grandsons to seize the throne. Her motives are not stated. They likely included self-protection, power, and the cause of Baal, whose temple and priest in Jerusalem appear in 23:17. If she had succeeded, there would have been no son of David left alive.
- **The only queen to reign.** Athaliah is the only woman who ever ruled Judah or Israel in her own right. The Tyndale notes point out that neither Kings nor Chronicles gives her the usual opening formula of age and length of reign. The writers treat her six years as a usurpation, not a reign in the line of David.
- **"But Jehoshabeath, the daughter of the king" (v11).** One "but" turns the story. She is called Jehosheba in 2 Kings 11:2. Her name means "the LORD is an oath" or "the LORD has sworn," and that is exactly the point here. God had sworn an oath to David (Ps 89:3–4, 35–36; 132:11), and the woman who saves his line carries the oath in her name. She was King Jehoram's daughter, most likely by a different wife than Athaliah, as the Tyndale notes suggest, so Athaliah was her stepmother.
- **"Wife of Jehoiada the priest" (v11).** Only Chronicles tells us this. A princess married to the high priest meant the palace and the temple were joined in one household. Jehoiada will lead the counterattack in chapter 23. God had put the right woman in the right family years before she was needed.
- **"Stole him away from among the king's sons who were about to be put to death" (v11).** Joash was a baby, about a year old. 24:1 says he became king at seven after six years in hiding. Jehoshabeath took him from among the children being killed, which means she acted at real risk in the middle of the slaughter.
- **"Put him and his nurse in a bedroom" (v11).** The phrase is literally "in the room of the beds," probably a storeroom for bedding and furniture in the palace or temple, not a sleeping room. It was a place no one would look, and later the child was moved to the temple itself.
- **"Six years, hidden in the house of God" (v12).** The temple complex had chambers built into its walls for priests and storage (1 Kings 6:5–6). The safest place in Judah was the house of the God whom Athaliah despised. The baby king grew up in the temple while a Baal-worshiping queen ruled outside. JFB explains that priestly families had rooms within the outer wall and that "Jehoiada and his family resided in one of these apartments."
- **The thread of promise.** At this moment the whole promise to David, and through it the promise of the Messiah, rests on one hidden child. The Bible tells this kind of story more than once: baby Moses hidden from Pharaoh's killing (Exod 2), baby Jesus carried to Egypt away from Herod's soldiers (Matt 2:13–18). In each case a ruler tries to destroy God's chosen line by killing children, and God preserves it through ordinary, courageous people.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that Athaliah "barbarously cut off all the seed-royal," and that the rescued child, "a child of a year old," preserved David's line because "no word of God shall fall to the ground." He sees God keeping a "lamp" for his anointed, the same image as 21:7. Guzik reads Athaliah's purge as an attack on the line of the Messiah and stresses that God's plan hung on one baby. He passes on Selman's description of the scene as a tale of two women: one destroys, one saves. Guzik also notes, from Josephus, that the hiding place was probably a storeroom. JFB, on v10, suggests she was "maddened" by the destruction of her family and resolved that David's house should share its fate.

#### Pointing to Christ
This short chapter brings David's line closer to extinction than at any time before the exile. A king dies, his kin are killed, and a grandmother murders her grandsons. Then one woman whose name means "the LORD has sworn" hides one child in the house of God. Satan's attempts to cut off the promised seed (Gen 3:15) run all through Scripture, and they all fail. The baby hidden in the temple foreshadows the baby carried to Egypt to escape Herod. Joash would one day come out of hiding and be crowned in the temple courts (ch 23). The greater Son of David came out of a grave, and he reigns forever.
