*Setting:* Directly after the Philistine commanders refused to let David march with them against Israel and Achish sent him home (29:6–11). David and his six hundred men have walked about three days south from Aphek to Ziklag, the Philistine town Achish gave him (27:6). Far to the north, the Philistines are closing in on Saul at Mount Gilboa, and Saul has heard from the medium's house that he will die tomorrow (28:19). At this very time, David's world falls apart. This chapter is the turning point of his wilderness years. Stripped of everything, he turns back to God, asks him for guidance for the first time since he went over to the Philistines, and acts like the king God made him to be. It is also a story of the Amalekites, the enemy Saul failed to destroy (ch 15), defeated by the man who will take Saul's place.

#### David's Wives Are Captured (vv1–15)
- **"The Amalekites had made a raid against the Negeb and against Ziklag" (v1).** While the Philistine armies, and David's men, were away in the north, the southern towns were undefended. The Amalekites, desert raiders from the Sinai and the Negeb, took their chance. They may also have been taking revenge for David's raids on them (27:8). They burned Ziklag.
- **"They killed no one, but carried them off" (v2).** This was not mercy. Women and children were valuable as slaves to sell or keep. Ironically, David had left no one alive in his raids (27:9, 11). The Amalekites, who were known for preying on the weak and weary (Deut 25:17–18), spared everyone, and that is what allowed David to get them all back.
- **"They wept until they had no more strength to weep" (v4).** These are hard men, soldiers, and they weep until they are exhausted. Their homes are ashes and their families gone. David's two wives, Ahinoam and Abigail, are among the captives (v5).
- **"The people spoke of stoning him" (v6).** David's men turn on him. They had followed him into Philistine territory, and while they were away on his business, everything they loved was taken. These are the same men who came to him "bitter in soul" at Adullam (22:2). Now they are bitter again, and they blame him.
- **"David was greatly distressed" (v6).** The Hebrew is almost exactly what Saul said to Samuel at En-dor the same night or near it: "I am in great distress" (28:15). The two kings face the same kind of darkness. Saul turned to a medium. David turns to God.
- **"But David strengthened himself in the LORD his God" (v6).** This is the hinge of the chapter. "Strengthened himself" comes from the verb *chazaq*, to be strong or firm, in a form that means "made himself strong." Jonathan once came and "strengthened his hand in God" (23:16). Now there is no Jonathan, no friend, no prophet and his own men want to kill him. David has to do it himself, and he does it "in the LORD his God." The text does not say how. The psalms he wrote in other dark times show the pattern: remembering what God had done, calling on him, and resting in his promises (Ps 27:13–14; 42:5; 56:3–4). The phrase "his God" is personal. Even after sixteen months of drifting, the LORD is still David's God.
- **"Bring me the ephod" (v7).** For the first time since he went over to the Philistines, David asks God for direction. Abiathar has been with him all along (22:20; 23:6). In chapter 27 David made his choices "in his heart." Now he brings them to the LORD.
- **"Shall I pursue after this band? Shall I overtake them?" (v8).** Two questions, and a full answer: "Pursue, for you shall surely overtake and shall surely rescue." The contrast with Saul is deliberate. Saul inquired, and "the LORD did not answer him" (28:6). David inquires and gets a clear promise.
- **"The brook Besor" (vv9–10).** The Besor is a wadi, a stream bed that runs with water only after winter rains, flowing northwest to the sea south of Gaza. Two hundred of David's men are "too exhausted" to go on. They had marched three days from Aphek and found their homes burned. David leaves them to guard the baggage (v24) and presses on with four hundred.
- **"They found an Egyptian in the open country" (v11).** A half-dead slave lying in the field becomes the key to the rescue. David's men give him bread, water, a piece of fig cake and raisins. Fig cakes were dense, sweet blocks of pressed figs, the kind of food Abigail had brought to David (25:18). They feed him before they know whether he can help them. "His spirit revived" (v12). He had had nothing to eat or drink for three days and three nights.
- **"My master left me behind because I fell sick" (v13).** The Amalekite treated a sick slave as worthless and left him to die. That cruelty becomes the Amalekites' undoing. God uses the person the world threw away.
- **"The Negeb of the Cherethites… Judah… the Negeb of Caleb" (v14).** The raiders had hit a wide area. The Cherethites were a people linked with the Philistines, probably from Crete. Later, Cherethites and Pelethites form David's royal bodyguard (2 Sam 8:18; 15:18). The "Negeb of Caleb" was the region around Hebron settled by Caleb's clan (Josh 14:13–14), where Nabal had lived (25:3).
- **"Swear to me by God that you will not kill me or deliver me into the hands of my master" (v15).** The slave asks for protection and freedom, and he gets it.
- **Commentators:** Henry finds hope in the depth of the disaster: "When things are at the worst with the church and people of God, then they begin to mend," and "Those who have taken the Lord for their God, may take encouragement from him in the worst times." JFB says David's "faith supplied him with inward resources of comfort and energy," and notes that the Amalekites' "apparent clemency did not arise from humane considerations." Guzik contrasts the words David said in his heart in 27:1, which led him into this mess, with the way he now speaks to himself about God, which helps bring him out. He stresses that David did not wait for someone else to strengthen him, but took hold of God's strength himself. Henry adds of the Egyptian: "A poor Egyptian lad, scarcely alive, is made the means of a great deal of good to David."

#### David Defeats the Amalekites (vv16–31)
- **"Eating and drinking and dancing" (v16).** The Amalekites are spread out across the countryside, celebrating a huge haul from both Philistine and Judean towns. They think they are safe. Like Nabal at his feast (25:36), they have no idea how close disaster is.
- **"From twilight until the evening of the next day" (v17).** David attacks at dusk and fights on through the next day. The Amalekite force was large, since even after the defeat "four hundred young men… mounted camels and fled." Camels were the desert raiders' fast animals (Judg 6:5; 7:12). Four hundred escaping is the same number as David's whole attacking force.
- **"David recovered all… Nothing was missing… David brought back all" (vv18–19).** The text piles up the words to make the point unmistakable. God's promise in v8 was "you shall surely rescue," and it came true to the last child. Not one wife, son or daughter was lost.
- **"This is David's spoil" (v20).** David also took the Amalekites' own flocks and herds, so the men came back with more than they lost. The same men who wanted to stone him that morning now praise him.
- **"He greeted them" (v21).** David meets the two hundred exhausted men at the Besor with a greeting of peace. He does not shame them.
- **"All the wicked and worthless fellows" (v22).** Literally "evil men and sons of Belial," the same term used of Nabal (25:17, 25) and of Eli's sons (2:12). They want to keep the spoil for those who fought. The tired men can take their families and go.
- **"You shall not do so, my brothers, with what the LORD has given us" (v23).** David calls them "brothers," but he corrects them firmly. The victory was not theirs to divide as they liked. "He has preserved us and given into our hand the band." What God gives is shared.
- **"As his share is who goes down into the battle, so shall his share be who stays by the baggage" (v24).** Those who guard the supplies are as much a part of the army as those who fight. A similar principle appears in Numbers 31:27, where Moses divided the spoil between those who fought and the rest of the people.
- **"He made it a statute and a rule for Israel from that day forward to this day" (v25).** The future king makes his first law, and it is a law of grace and fairness. The narrator notes that it was still in force when he wrote.
- **"A present for you from the spoil of the enemies of the LORD" (v26).** David sends gifts to "the elders of Judah." The word "present" is literally "blessing." This was generous and also wise. These towns had sheltered him in his years on the run (v31), some had been raided by Amalek, and their elders would soon make him king of Judah at Hebron (2 Sam 2:1–4).
- **The towns (vv27–31).** These are all towns in southern Judah and the Negeb. "Bethel" here is probably a southern town also called Bethul (Josh 19:4), not the famous Bethel in the north. Hormah was where Israel had once been defeated in the wilderness and later won a victory (Num 14:45; 21:3). The list ends with Hebron, the chief city of Judah, where Abraham was buried and where David will be crowned (2 Sam 2:4). The list is the map of David's wilderness years, now turned into the base of his coming kingdom.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns, "Sinners are nearest to ruin, when they cry, Peace and safety, and put the evil day far from them," and says of the selfish men, "Those are men of Belial indeed, who delight in putting hardships upon their brethren." He applies the shared spoil to every believer: "What God gives us, he designs we should do good with." JFB calls the sharing "the equitable rule—that all the soldiers should share alike," and says David's gifts to Judah's elders were "the dictate of an amiable and grateful heart." The Tyndale notes add that the gifts may also have been meant to win support for his kingship, and both readings can be true at once. Guzik observes that David won back more than he had lost, since he came home with the Amalekites' flocks as well as everything taken from Ziklag.

#### Pointing to Christ
At the lowest point of his life, David turns back to the LORD, is given a promise, pursues the enemy, and brings back everything that was taken, "small or great, sons or daughters." He then shares the victory with those who were too weak to fight. This is a picture of the gospel. Jesus, the Son of David, went down into the place of loss and defeated the enemy who held his people captive (Heb 2:14–15; Col 2:15). He promises that none of those the Father gives him will be lost (John 6:39). And the spoils of his victory are shared with all his people, the strong and the exhausted alike, not because they earned it but because it is "what the LORD has given us" (Eph 4:8). David strengthened himself in the LORD his God. Believers in every dark Ziklag can do the same, because God's promises in Christ are "Yes" (2 Cor 1:20).
