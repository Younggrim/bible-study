*Setting:* "A Prayer of David." Psalm 86 is the only psalm in Book 3 of the Psalter (73–89) with David's name on it, set among the songs of Asaph and the Sons of Korah. It is a personal lament under pressure from "insolent men" and "ruthless men" (v14), but no particular event is named. Henry thinks it "was not penned upon any particular occasion, but was a prayer he often used himself." One striking feature is how much of it is drawn from other Scripture. Line after line echoes earlier psalms (25:1; 27:11; 40:17; 54:3) and, above all, God's own description of himself to Moses after the golden calf: "merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exod 34:6), which appears in vv5 and 15. Some have called it a mosaic. That is not a weakness. It shows a man praying with the words of Scripture, which is how believers have always learned to pray. The title "Lord" (*Adonai*, "Master") appears seven times, and David calls himself "your servant" three times (vv2, 4, 16). The psalm moves from petition (vv1–7), to praise at its center (vv8–13), and back to petition (vv14–17).

#### Incline Your Ear (vv1–7)
- **"Incline your ear, O LORD, and answer me, for I am poor and needy" (v1).** "Incline your ear" pictures God bending down to listen, as an adult stoops to hear a child. The reason given is not David's worth but his need. "Poor and needy" (40:17; 70:5) means helpless, dependent, with nowhere else to turn. It is surprising language from a king, and that is the point: before God, even a king is a beggar.
- **"Preserve my life, for I am godly" (v2).** "Godly" is *chasid*, a loyal, faithful member of God's covenant people. It comes from *chesed*, God's steadfast love, and means someone who has received that love and responds to it. The KJV has "I am holy," which can sound like a boast. It is not a claim to be sinless, but a statement of whose side David is on. JFB: "or, 'godly,' as in Ps 4:3; 85:8." Guzik makes the same point, contrasting David's loyalty to God with his godless enemies (v14).
- **"Save your servant, who trusts in you—you are my God" (v2).** Three grounds in one verse: David belongs to God's people, he is God's servant, and he trusts him. "You are my God" is the heart of the covenant (Jer 31:33).
- **"Be gracious to me, O Lord, for to you do I cry all the day" (v3).** "Be gracious" means show undeserved favor. Persistent prayer is not nagging but trust that refuses to look elsewhere.
- **"Gladden the soul of your servant, for to you, O Lord, do I lift up my soul" (v4).** To "lift up my soul" (25:1; 143:8) means to offer one's whole self to God, longing and trusting. David asks for joy, not only rescue.
- **"For you, O Lord, are good and forgiving, abounding in steadfast love to all who call upon you" (v5).** Here is the foundation. "Forgiving" (*sallach*) is used only here in the Old Testament as an adjective: God is ready to forgive, quick to pardon. This is drawn from Exodus 34:6–7, where God described himself as "forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin." "To all who call upon you" opens the door wide (Joel 2:32; Rom 10:13).
- **"Give ear, O LORD, to my prayer… In the day of my trouble I call upon you, for you answer me" (vv6–7).** The opening request comes back, but now with confidence. David does not hope God might answer. He prays because God does answer.
- **Commentators:** Guzik says the humility of v1 is the foundation of the whole prayer, and that "godly" in v2 is about relationship, not perfection. He stresses that God in v5 is ready to forgive now, not after the sinner has waited or earned it. Henry notes that David "pleads God's goodness (Ps. 86:5, 15) and the malice of his enemies, Ps. 86:14."

#### There Is None Like You (vv8–13)
- **"There is none like you among the gods, O Lord, nor are there any works like yours" (v8).** This echoes the Song of the Sea: "Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?" (Exod 15:11). The "gods" are the idols and so-called deities of the nations. They have no works to compare with the LORD's, because they have no works at all (115:4–7).
- **"All the nations you have made shall come and worship before you, O Lord, and shall glorify your name" (v9).** In the middle of a personal prayer, David's vision widens to the whole world. Because God made all nations, all nations belong to him and will one day worship him (22:27; 66:4; Isa 66:23; Zech 14:16). The song of the redeemed in Revelation quotes this verse: "All nations will come and worship you, for your righteous acts have been revealed" (Rev 15:4). JFB: "The pious Jews believed that God's common relation to all would be ultimately acknowledged by all men."
- **"For you are great and do wondrous things; you alone are God" (v10).** The confession of Israel's faith (Deut 6:4; Isa 45:5). It is the reason for v9: if he alone is God, every nation will come to him in the end.
- **"Teach me your way, O LORD, that I may walk in your truth" (v11).** The prayer turns from asking God to change David's circumstances to asking God to change David. "Teach me your way" echoes 27:11. "Walk in your truth" means live by it, steadily.
- **"Unite my heart to fear your name" (v11).** One of the great prayers in the Psalms. "Unite" (*yached*) means make one, bring together. The opposite is a "double heart" (12:2), a divided loyalty that wants God and something else too. James calls it being "double-minded" (Jas 1:8; 4:8). David knows his heart pulls in different directions and asks God to bring it together in a single, reverent loyalty. The Tyndale notes connect it with the integrity and wholeness of a pure heart (73:1). God promised exactly this in the new covenant: "I will give them one heart" (Ezek 11:19; Jer 32:39). JFB: "unite my heart—fix all my affections."
- **"I give thanks to you, O Lord my God, with my whole heart" (v12).** The heart he asked to be united, he now gives whole. Praise is both the fruit of a united heart and one of the ways God unites it.
- **"For great is your steadfast love toward me; you have delivered my soul from the depths of Sheol" (v13).** "Sheol" is the realm of the dead. "The depths" (KJV "the lowest hell") means the lowest part of it. David may mean he was brought back from the brink of death, rescued from mortal danger, which was what Sheol usually meant to Old Testament believers (30:3). He speaks in the past tense, confident of rescue even before it comes, or remembering a past rescue as the pledge of the next one.
- **Commentators:** Henry on v11: "Let my heart be fixed for God, and firm and faithful to him, and fervent in serving him; that is a united heart." On v13 he applies the words more broadly: "Even the best saints owe it, not to their own merit, but to the mercy of God, that they are saved from the lowest hell," and he connects it with David's sin against Uriah, though the psalm itself does not mention that. Guzik notes that David's awareness of God's greatness leads him to ask for teaching, not to give up his request, and that praise itself helps unite the heart.

#### A Sign of Your Favor (vv14–17)
- **"O God, insolent men have risen up against me; a band of ruthless men seeks my life, and they do not set you before them" (v14).** This verse closely matches 54:3, written when the Ziphites betrayed David to Saul. "Insolent" means proud, arrogant. "Ruthless" means violent, terrifying. "They do not set you before them" is the root of their wickedness: they live as if God were not watching (36:1; Rom 3:18).
- **"But you, O Lord, are a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (v15).** "But you" sets God against the proud men. This is the fullest quotation of Exodus 34:6 in the Psalms, one of the verses the Old Testament quotes most often (Num 14:18; Neh 9:17; Ps 103:8; 145:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2). "Merciful" (*rachum*) is a mother's tender love. "Gracious" (*channun*) is favor freely given. "Slow to anger" is literally "long of nostrils," taking a long time to grow hot. "Steadfast love and faithfulness" (*chesed* and *'emet*) are the pair that "meet" in 85:10. David builds his plea on what God himself said about himself.
- **"Turn to me and be gracious to me; give your strength to your servant" (v16).** "Turn to me" asks God to face him, the opposite of hiding his face. "Your strength" is what David lacks.
- **"And save the son of your maidservant" (v16).** A son born to a female servant in the household was a servant from birth, belonging to the master's house, often the most loyal of servants (116:16; Exod 21:4). The phrase may also honor David's mother as a godly woman. Either way, David pleads that he has belonged to God all his life. JFB: "son… handmaid—homeborn servant." Henry: "The children of godly parents, who were betimes dedicated to the Lord, may plead it with him."
- **"Show me a sign of your favor, that those who hate me may see and be put to shame" (v17).** "Sign" (*'ot*) means a token or visible mark. The KJV has "a token for good." JFB: "literally, 'Make with me a token,'" by God's care. David is not demanding proof before he will believe. He already believes (vv5, 15). He asks God to make his favor visible so his enemies will see that God is with him. Henry: "make it to appear to others as well as to myself that thou art doing me good."
- **"Because you, LORD, have helped me and comforted me" (v17).** The psalm ends in the past tense again, help and comfort received. Faith speaks of what God will do as though it were already done.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lists signs of God's favor a believer may receive, such as answered prayer, joy, forgiveness and confidence, but cautions against demanding a sign as a condition of trust. He suggests the "son of your maidservant" may point to a godly mother. The Tyndale notes say that God is so great that even a glimpse of his goodness will shut the mouths of the wicked.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 86 is a prayer of the "poor and needy" servant who trusts God against proud men who seek his life. Henry notes that "many think that David penned this prayer as a type of Christ," and the shape fits. Jesus became poor for our sake (2 Cor 8:9), called himself the one who came "not to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45), was born to the Lord's maidservant ("Behold, I am the servant of the Lord," Luke 1:38), and was surrounded by men who did not set God before them. God delivered him from the depths of the grave and gave the world a sign of his favor in the resurrection. Through him the vision of v9 is coming true, as people from every nation come to worship. And in him we see the God of v15, "merciful and gracious… abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness," for "grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (John 1:17). The prayer "Unite my heart to fear your name" is one he answers by his Spirit, giving his people a new and undivided heart.
