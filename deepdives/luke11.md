*Setting:* About AD 29, somewhere on the long journey to Jerusalem that began in 9:51. Chapter 10 ended with Mary at Jesus' feet, listening, the "one thing" necessary. Chapter 11 opens with Jesus praying and his disciples asking him to teach them how. The first part of the chapter is about prayer, a Father who gives good gifts and the Holy Spirit. Then the mood darkens. Some accuse Jesus of driving out demons by Satan's power, others demand a sign, and at a Pharisee's dinner Jesus pronounces six woes on Pharisees and lawyers. The chapter ends with his opponents "lying in wait for him."

#### The Lord's Prayer (vv1–4)
- **"Jesus was praying in a certain place" (v1).** Luke keeps showing Jesus at prayer (3:21; 5:16; 6:12; 9:18, 28). The disciples ask after watching him. Seeing how Jesus prayed made them want to pray.
- **"As John taught his disciples" (v1).** Jewish teachers and movements often had their own characteristic prayers, a mark of the group's identity. The disciples want a prayer that belongs to Jesus' followers.
- **Luke's shorter form.** Luke's version is shorter than Matthew's (Matt 6:9–13), which Jesus gave in the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus probably taught it more than once, in slightly different words, as a pattern and not only a formula. Some later manuscripts of Luke add lines from Matthew ("Our Father in heaven," "your will be done," "deliver us from evil"), and the KJV includes them. The earliest manuscripts have the shorter form.
- **"Father" (v2).** Jesus almost certainly said the Aramaic *Abba*, the word a child used for a father, warm but respectful (Mark 14:36). Jews did call God Father (Isa 63:16), but addressing him so directly in prayer was unusual. Paul says the Spirit puts this same word on believers' lips (Rom 8:15; Gal 4:6). Jesus gives his disciples a share in his own way of speaking to God.
- **"Hallowed be your name" (v2).** "Hallowed" means treated as holy. God's "name" is his revealed character. The first request is not for anything the one praying needs. It is that God be honored as he truly is (Ezek 36:23).
- **"Your kingdom come" (v2).** A prayer for God's rule to arrive in full. It echoes the Jewish Kaddish prayer, which asked that God "establish his kingdom in your lifetime."
- **"Give us each day our daily bread" (v3).** The rare Greek word *epiousios*, translated "daily," appears almost nowhere outside this prayer. It may mean "needed for today" or "for the coming day." Either way it is a prayer of dependence, like Israel gathering manna one day at a time (Exod 16:4).
- **"Forgive us our sins, for we ourselves forgive everyone who is indebted to us" (v4).** Luke writes "sins" in the first line and "indebted" in the second. Aramaic used the same word for both, and sin was pictured as a debt owed to God. Forgiving others is not the price of God's forgiveness. It is the mark of someone who has received it (7:47; Matt 18:21–35).
- **"Lead us not into temptation" (v4).** God tempts no one to sin (James 1:13). The word *peirasmos* means testing or trial. The request asks God to keep his people from trials that would overwhelm them, and to keep them from falling. Jesus tells the disciples to pray it in Gethsemane (22:40, 46).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that although Christ was ready to teach, he waited to be asked: "though Christ is apt to teach, yet he will for this be enquired of." JFB reads "day by day" as extending the request to the needs of each day as it comes. Guzik stresses that the prayer's first concerns are God's name and kingdom, that daily bread means present provision rather than a stockpile, and that the differences from Matthew show it is a pattern, not a ritual to recite mechanically.

#### Ask, and It Will Be Given (vv5–13)
- **"Which of you who has a friend will go to him at midnight" (v5).** Travelers often moved in the cool of evening. Village hospitality was a matter of honor, not just for the host but for the whole village. To have nothing to set before a guest was shameful. Bread was baked daily, so by midnight a household might have none left.
- **"The door is now shut, and my children are with me in bed" (v7).** A peasant family often slept together on a mat in a single room. Getting up meant unbarring the door and waking everyone. Jesus' hearers would laugh: no neighbor would actually refuse.
- **"Because of his impudence he will rise" (v8).** The word is *anaideia*, "shamelessness." Many readers take it as the persistence of the man knocking. Others think it describes the sleeper, who gets up to avoid the shame of having been a bad neighbor. Either way, the story argues from the lesser to the greater. If a grumpy neighbor will get up, how much more will God, who is not grumpy and not asleep (Ps 121:4).
- **"Ask… seek… knock" (v9).** The Greek verbs are present tense: keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking. Prayer is meant to be ongoing, not a one-time attempt.
- **"A serpent… a scorpion" (vv11–12).** Some fish from the lake were snake-like, and a curled-up pale scorpion might look like an egg. No father would hand his son something deadly in place of food.
- **"If you then, who are evil… how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit" (v13).** Jesus assumes human sinfulness even in good fathers. Where Matthew 7:11 says "good things," Luke says "the Holy Spirit," the best gift of all. Luke highlights the Spirit throughout his two volumes, and in Acts the Spirit comes to those praying (Acts 1:14; 2:1–4; 4:31).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes midnight was "a most unseasonable hour of call," chosen for that reason, and reads *anaideia* as a shameless persistence that will not take no. It calls the Spirit the "Gift of gifts." Guzik argues that persistence works more on the one praying than on a reluctant God, and stresses that God's willingness to give the Spirit is never in doubt. The Tyndale notes give the alternative reading that the sleeper acts to avoid shame. Henry calls prayer "one of the great laws of natural religion."

#### Jesus and Beelzebul (vv14–23)
- **"A demon that was mute" (v14).** Matthew 12:22 says the man was also blind. Luke keeps the miracle short and turns to the reactions.
- **"He casts out demons by Beelzebul, the prince of demons" (v15).** No one denies the miracle. They argue about its source. "Beelzebul" probably meant "Baal the prince" or "lord of the house," from a Canaanite god. In 2 Kings 1:2 the god of Ekron is Baal-zebub, "lord of flies," likely a Jewish mocking twist of the name. By Jesus' day it was a name for Satan.
- **"Others, to test him, kept seeking from him a sign from heaven" (v16).** This is the second challenge, and Jesus answers it in vv29–32.
- **"Every kingdom divided against itself is laid waste" (v17).** Jesus answers with common sense. Satan does not tear down his own work. Civil wars destroy kingdoms, as Israel's own history showed.
- **"By whom do your sons cast them out?" (v19).** "Your sons" means their own followers. Jewish exorcists existed (Acts 19:13). If the critics accept their exorcisms as from God, they condemn themselves by calling Jesus' exorcisms satanic.
- **"By the finger of God" (v20).** Pharaoh's magicians, unable to copy the third plague, said: "This is the finger of God" (Exod 8:19). Matthew 12:28 says "by the Spirit of God." Luke's phrase recalls the exodus. A new deliverance is under way.
- **"Then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (v20).** The verb means it has already arrived. The exorcisms show God's rule breaking in now.
- **"When a strong man, fully armed, guards his own palace" (vv21–22).** Satan is the strong man, people held in bondage are his goods, and Jesus is the "stronger" one who overpowers him, strips his armor and divides the spoil. Isaiah asked, "Can the prey be taken from the mighty?" and God answered, "Even the captives of the mighty shall be taken" (Isa 49:24–25). Isaiah 53:12 says the servant "shall divide the spoil with the strong."
- **"Whoever is not with me is against me" (v23).** Compare 9:50, "the one who is not against you is for you." There Jesus warned against suspicion of others working in his name. Here he warns that no one can be neutral toward him. "Scatters" pictures a shepherd's flock or a harvest gathered or lost.
- **Commentators:** JFB distinguishes "the finger of God," God's power, from "the Spirit of God" in Matthew, the personal agent. It identifies the strong man as Satan and "his palace" as man, and on v23 says "neutrality in religion there is none." Henry: "Obstinate infidelity will never be at a loss for something to say in its own excuse." Guzik reads the strong man as Christ's victory over Satan and stresses that there is no neutral ground.

#### Return of an Unclean Spirit (vv24–26)
- **"Passes through waterless places seeking rest" (v24).** The desert was pictured as the haunt of evil spirits (Isa 13:21; 34:14). The spirit is restless outside a host.
- **"Finds the house swept and put in order" (v25).** Matthew adds "empty" (Matt 12:44). The house is clean but no one lives there. Reform without a new occupant leaves a person open.
- **"Seven other spirits more evil than itself" (v26).** Seven suggests completeness. Getting rid of evil is not enough. The heart must be filled by God's rule, with Jesus as master of the house. In context this warns the crowds who have seen demons driven out but remain neutral toward Jesus.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Hypocrisy is the high road to apostasy." JFB treats this as the reverse of the strong man picture: there the stronger man takes the house, here the old tenant returns. Guzik draws the point that a cleansed heart left empty invites worse evil, so it must be filled with Christ.

#### True Blessedness (vv27–28)
- **"Blessed is the womb that bore you" (v27).** Only Luke records this. It was a common way of honoring a great man, by praising his mother. Elizabeth said something similar to Mary (1:42), and Mary herself sang that "all generations will call me blessed" (1:48).
- **"Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and keep it!" (v28).** "Rather" can mean "yes, but more than that." Jesus does not deny his mother's blessedness. He points to what made Mary blessed in the first place, that she heard and believed (1:38, 45), and he opens that blessing to everyone. This echoes 8:21: "My mother and my brothers are those who hear the word of God and do it."
- **Commentators:** JFB contrasts Jesus' answer with later devotion that gives Mary a special place in heaven. Protestants commonly read it that way, while Catholic interpreters note that Mary is the model hearer and keeper of the word, so the verse honors her in the right way. Henry quotes the verse as its own lesson. Guzik says Jesus honors hearing and keeping God's word above physical kinship, without belittling his mother.

#### The Sign of Jonah (vv29–32)
- **"This generation is an evil generation. It seeks for a sign" (v29).** They have seen healings and exorcisms. Demanding more is not openness but unbelief (1 Cor 1:22).
- **"The sign of Jonah" (v30).** Jonah "became a sign to the people of Nineveh." He came out of the fish, as if back from death, and preached repentance. Matthew 12:40 spells out the three days and nights in the fish as a picture of Jesus' burial and resurrection. Luke stresses the preaching. Both meanings fit: Jesus' resurrection will be the sign, and his message of repentance is the call.
- **"The queen of the South" (v31).** She is the queen of Sheba, probably in southwest Arabia, modern Yemen. She traveled about 1,200 miles to hear Solomon's wisdom (1 Kings 10:1–10). "The ends of the earth" was how the far south seemed to Israel.
- **"Something greater than Solomon is here… something greater than Jonah is here" (vv31–32).** "Something" is neuter in Greek, possibly pointing to the kingdom present in Jesus. Solomon was the wisest king, and Jonah the prophet who turned a pagan city. Jesus is greater than both. On judgment day, a Gentile queen and Gentile Ninevites will testify against Israel's own people, who had far more and refused.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the sign of Jonah as a picture of the resurrection and stresses the phrase greater than. The Tyndale notes give both readings, the resurrection and Jonah's preaching. JFB refers only to Matthew.

#### The Light in You (vv33–36)
- **"No one after lighting a lamp puts it in a cellar" (v33).** The lamp is not hidden. God's light in Jesus is shining openly. The problem is not lack of light.
- **"Your eye is the lamp of your body" (v34).** In ancient thought the eye let light into the body, like a window. "Healthy" (*haplous*) means single, sound or sincere. "Bad" (*ponēros*) means diseased or evil. Hebrew idiom used a "bad eye" for stinginess or envy (Prov 28:22). A heart focused on God takes in light. A divided or envious heart stays dark however bright the lamp.
- **"Be careful lest the light in you be darkness" (v35).** The sharpest warning in the section. The crowds think they see. The religious leaders think they are full of light. They may be blind.
- **Commentators:** JFB says v36 shows "the clarity of the inward eye," a soul fully lit. Guzik argues that inner blindness, not lack of evidence, keeps people from seeing Jesus. Henry likewise makes a sincere, clear-sighted heart the condition of receiving the light.

#### Woes to the Pharisees and Lawyers (vv37–54)
- **"A Pharisee asked him to dine with him" (v37).** This is Luke's second Pharisee dinner (7:36; 14:1). The word for dinner here means a midday meal.
- **"He did not first wash before dinner" (v38).** This was not hygiene but ritual. Pharisaic tradition required pouring water over the hands before eating, to remove ceremonial impurity (Mark 7:3–4). The law of Moses required it only of priests in certain situations.
- **"You cleanse the outside of the cup… but inside you are full of greed and wickedness" (v39).** The rules for cleaning vessels were detailed. Jesus turns the picture on them: they are the cup, clean outside and filthy inside.
- **"Did not he who made the outside make the inside also?" (v40).** God made the whole person and cares about the heart (1 Sam 16:7).
- **"Give as alms those things that are within" (v41).** A hard phrase. It probably means: give from the heart, give of what is inside the cup, generously, to the poor. Inner generosity cleanses in a way that washing cannot.
- **"You tithe mint and rue and every herb" (v42).** The law required a tenth of grain, wine, oil and produce (Deut 14:22–23; Lev 27:30). The Pharisees tithed even tiny garden herbs. Rue was a bitter herb, and later rabbis said it did not need tithing, so these men went beyond even their own rules. Matthew lists "justice and mercy and faithfulness." Luke has "justice and the love of God," the two halves of the great commandment again (10:27). "Without neglecting the others" shows that Jesus is not against tithing.
- **"The best seat in the synagogues and greetings in the marketplaces" (v43).** The front seats faced the congregation. Public greetings with honorific titles were expected for scholars.
- **"Like unmarked graves" (v44).** Touching a grave made a person unclean for seven days (Num 19:16). Before Passover, Jews whitewashed tombs so pilgrims would not touch them by accident (Matthew 23:27 draws on this). An unmarked grave defiled people who never knew it. The Pharisees thought they made others holy. Jesus says they spread defilement without anyone noticing.
- **"Teacher, in saying these things you insult us also" (v45).** The "lawyers" were the scholars who interpreted the law, many of them Pharisees. They expected Jesus to apologize. He turns to them instead.
- **"You load people with burdens hard to bear" (v46).** The oral law multiplied rules for daily life. Jesus says these scholars laid heavy demands on others and would not lift a finger to help carry them. Contrast Jesus' "my burden is light" (Matt 11:30).
- **"You build the tombs of the prophets whom your fathers killed" (vv47–48).** Monuments to prophets were being built in Jesus' day. Honoring dead prophets while rejecting the living ones shows they are like their fathers, who killed the prophets while they were alive.
- **"The Wisdom of God said" (v49).** The quotation is not found in the Old Testament. It may be God's wisdom speaking through the pattern of Scripture as a whole, or Jesus speaking as God's Wisdom himself. In Matthew 23:34 Jesus says "I send you prophets." "Apostles" points ahead to the church in Acts.
- **"From the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah" (v51).** Abel is the first murder in the Bible (Gen 4:8). Zechariah son of Jehoiada was stoned "in the court of the house of the LORD," saying "May the LORD see and avenge!" (2 Chr 24:20–22). Chronicles is the last book in the Hebrew Bible, so these are the first and last murders in its order, A to Z. "Between the altar and the sanctuary" matches the temple courtyard.
- **"It will be required of this generation" (v51).** Jesus' generation would reject the greatest Prophet of all. Many see a fulfillment in the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 (19:41–44; 21:20–24).
- **"You have taken away the key of knowledge" (v52).** The key that opens the knowledge of God is Scripture rightly understood, pointing to Christ (24:27). The lawyers had locked it up with tradition, and stood in the doorway blocking others.
- **"Lying in wait for him, to catch him in something he might say" (v54).** The picture is of hunters setting a trap. From here, opposition grows, and the road leads to the cross.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the woes rest on the leaders' "heartless rigor" and inconsistency, notes that Luke has "the love of God" where Matthew has "mercy, and faith," and on v49 asks, "Could this be other than the Lord of Israel in the flesh?" It takes Zechariah as the one in 2 Chronicles 24, whose dying words were a call for the Lord to require his blood. Henry: "It is gross hypocrisy to condemn that in those who reprove us which yet we allow in those that flatter us." Guzik explains the handwashing ritual, notes that the word hypocrite meant an actor, and calls taking away the key of knowledge the worst offense, because it shut others out.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 11 shows the Son who prays and invites his disciples into his own way of speaking to God, "Father." He promises a Father who gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask. He is the stronger man who breaks into Satan's house and sets the captives free by the finger of God. He is greater than Solomon and greater than Jonah, and the sign he gives is his own death and resurrection. He is also the true light, and his woes expose religion that cleans the outside and leaves the heart dark. The key of knowledge that the lawyers took away is found in him, and the burden they would not lift, he carried to the cross.
