*Setting:* Jerusalem, soon after the spring of 597 BC. Nebuchadnezzar had just taken the city for the first time. He carried King Jeconiah (Jehoiachin), the queen mother, the officials, the soldiers and the skilled workers off to Babylon, about ten thousand people in all, and put Jeconiah's uncle Zedekiah on the throne (2 Kgs 24:10–17). The prophet Ezekiel was among those deported (Ezek 1:1–3). The people left behind drew a confident conclusion: the exiles were the ones God had rejected, and those who remained were the faithful ones who had kept the land. Ezekiel heard the same boast in Babylon: "Go far from the LORD; to us this land is given for a possession" (Ezek 11:15). This short vision turns that conclusion upside down.

#### The Good Figs and the Bad Figs (vv1–10)
- **"After Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon had taken into exile… Jeconiah" (v1).** Jeconiah is the Coniah of 22:24–30, the king just "hurled" into a foreign land. The chapter dates itself by his exile, and so do Ezekiel (Ezek 1:2) and later books (Esth 2:6). For the exiles, the clock started there.
- **"The officials of Judah, the craftsmen, and the metal workers" (v1).** Babylon took the people a city needed to govern itself, make weapons and repair walls (2 Kgs 24:14–16). The word translated "metal workers" is uncommon. It may mean smiths, or workers who fortified the walls. Either way, removing them made a future revolt harder. Babylon also gained skilled labor for its own building projects.
- **"The LORD showed me this vision" (v1).** The wording matches Amos's visions (Amos 7:1, 4, 7; 8:1). In Amos 8:1–2 a "basket of summer fruit" became a word of judgment. Jeremiah's own call had come through two ordinary sights, an almond branch and a boiling pot (1:11–14). It is uncertain whether he saw real baskets or saw them only in a vision. God may have used ordinary baskets he passed in the temple court, or shown them to him directly.
- **"Two baskets of figs placed before the temple of the LORD" (v1).** Israelites brought the firstfruits of the harvest to the sanctuary in a basket and set them "before the LORD" (Deut 26:2–4). So these baskets may have been offerings. Before the temple is where God's people present themselves to be judged by him.
- **"Very good figs, like first-ripe figs" (v2).** The first figs ripened in early summer on the previous year's growth. They were a prized delicacy, eaten fresh off the branch (Isa 28:4; Hos 9:10; Mic 7:1). **"So bad that they could not be eaten."** The second basket was rotten all the way through, fit only to be thrown out.
- **"What do you see, Jeremiah?" (v3).** The same question God asked at his call (1:11, 13). The prophet has to say it in his own words before God explains it. **"The good figs very good, and the bad figs very bad."** There is no middle basket.
- **"Like these good figs, so I will regard as good the exiles from Judah" (v5).** This would have shocked those left in Jerusalem. The ones who looked rejected are the ones God calls good. **"Whom I have sent away."** Not Nebuchadnezzar but God sent them, and he sent them "for good."
- **Good by grace, not by merit.** The exiles were not morally better. Ezekiel, who lived among them, found idolatry in their hearts too (Ezek 14:1–5; 20:30–31). The difference lies in what God chooses to do with each group. He will use exile to discipline and remake one group. The other is clinging to what God has already sentenced, and he will judge it.
- **"I will set my eyes on them for good" (v6).** God's eyes on a people can mean watchful care (Ps 33:18) or judgment (Amos 9:4, "for evil and not for good"). Here it is care. **"I will bring them back to this land."** About seventy years later this began to happen, when Cyrus of Persia allowed the exiles to return (Ezra 1:1–4).
- **"I will build them up, and not tear them down; I will plant them, and not pluck them up" (v6).** These are the very verbs of Jeremiah's call: "to pluck up and to break down… to build and to plant" (1:10). For thirty years his work had mostly been the first half. Now the second half begins.
- **"I will give them a heart to know me" (v7).** This is the heart of the chapter. God does not only command them to know him. He will give them the heart to do it. "Know" here means to know the LORD personally and to trust and obey him, not just to know facts about him. It looks forward to the new covenant: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts… they shall all know me" (31:33–34; 32:39–40; Ezek 36:26–27).
- **"They shall be my people and I will be their God" (v7).** This is the covenant formula, the summary of God's relationship with Israel from Sinai on (Exod 6:7; Lev 26:12). It recurs throughout Jeremiah (7:23; 11:4; 30:22; 31:33) and finds its last echo at the end of the Bible (Rev 21:3).
- **"For they shall return to me with their whole heart" (v7).** "Return" is *shub*, Jeremiah's key word for repentance (3:12, 14, 22). Notice the order: God gives the heart, and then they return. Repentance is real and wholehearted, but it is God's gift before it is their act. After the exile, Israel never again fell into the open idol-worship that had marked the monarchy.
- **"So will I treat Zedekiah the king of Judah, his officials, the remnant of Jerusalem" (v8).** This group thought the promises were theirs. Zedekiah's court is the same set of officials who later had Jeremiah beaten and thrown in a cistern (37:15; 38:4–6). Chapter 29 repeats this verdict in Jeremiah's letter to the exiles, using the same image of rotten figs (29:17).
- **"And those who dwell in the land of Egypt" (v8).** Some Judeans had already gone to Egypt, perhaps with Jehoahaz in 609 BC or as refugees seeking safety. After Jerusalem fell, a larger group fled there against Jeremiah's warning and took him with them. Chapters 42–44 record what became of them.
- **"A horror… a reproach, a byword, a taunt, and a curse" (v9).** Five words pile up, drawn from the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28:25, 37. A "byword" (*mashal*) is a proverb: their name would become a saying for disaster. "A curse" means people would use them as an example when cursing someone: "May you end up like them" (29:22).
- **"Sword, famine, and pestilence… until they shall be utterly destroyed" (v10).** The three killers of a siege (14:12; 21:7, 9). "From the land that I gave to them and their fathers." The land was a gift, and they were acting as if it were theirs by right.
- **What made the difference?** Chapters 21 and 27 give the practical answer. The way of life lay in accepting God's discipline and submitting to Babylon. The way of death lay in clinging to Jerusalem and fighting. The good figs were those God had placed under his discipline, and the bad figs were those who refused it.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the figs are called good and bad "not in an absolute, but a comparative sense," and that by exile "God bettered their condition," since it spared them the worst of the siege and brought them to repentance. Daniel and Ezekiel were among them. On v7 he says that repentance is the effect of God "giving a heart to know Him," not the cause of pardon. Henry adds that "it is God that puts his gold into the furnace, to be tried," and that their captivity was meant "to square the rough stones and make them fit for his building." He also notes that the bad figs' afflictions would harden them rather than humble them. Guzik quotes Morgan on how startling the comparison was, since it reversed what everyone assumed. He sees the return under Ezra and Nehemiah as a first fulfilment and the new heart of v7 as fully given only through Christ's new covenant (Luke 22:20). JFB and Henry both look for further fulfilment beyond the return from Babylon. Henry sees one in the Roman destruction of Jerusalem, and JFB in a future turning of the Jewish people.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus once went looking for figs on a tree full of leaves and found none, and the tree withered (Mark 11:12–14, 20–21). In his parable, a fruitless fig tree was given one more year (Luke 13:6–9). Like Jeremiah's baskets, his warnings were aimed at a city that looked secure and trusted in its temple. And like Jeremiah's good figs, his people are not good by nature. They are made good by a gift. What God promised the exiles, "I will give them a heart to know me… they shall be my people and I will be their God," Jesus brought about when he took the cup and called it "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20). The exiles' road to life went through loss. So does the gospel's road: the King himself was cast out of the city and then raised, so that those who share his rejection share his life.
