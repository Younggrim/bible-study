*Setting:* Late Thursday night into early Friday morning, about AD 30 or 33. Jesus has finished his farewell teaching and his prayer (chs 13–17). Now the "hour" that the whole Gospel has pointed to begins. In a few hours Jesus will be arrested, questioned by the former high priest, handed to the Roman governor and condemned. John tells the story differently from Matthew, Mark and Luke. He leaves out the agony in Gethsemane, Judas's kiss and the night session of the Sanhedrin, and he adds the interview with Annas, Jesus' long conversations with Pilate, and many small details only an eyewitness would know. Throughout, John shows Jesus in control. He is arrested because he allows it, questioned by men who are themselves on trial, and called a king by those who mock him.

#### Betrayal and Arrest of Jesus (vv1–11)
- **"Across the brook Kidron, where there was a garden" (v1).** The Kidron is a ravine between Jerusalem's east wall and the Mount of Olives. It runs with water only in the rainy season, which this was. John alone calls the place a "garden." Matthew and Mark give its name, Gethsemane, "oil press." A thousand years earlier, David crossed the Kidron weeping and fled up the Mount of Olives when his trusted counselor Ahithophel betrayed him (2 Sam 15:23, 30–31). Jesus has already quoted David's psalm about that kind of betrayal (13:18; Ps 41:9). Now the Son of David walks the same path.
- **"Jesus often met there with his disciples" (v2).** Jesus did not hide. He went to the place Judas knew, giving himself up to his hour (Luke 22:39).
- **"A band of soldiers and some officers" (v3).** The "band" (*speira*) was the word for a Roman cohort, nominally six hundred men, though here probably a detachment from the Antonia Fortress by the temple. Its commander in v12 is a *chiliarchos*, a tribune, "commander of a thousand." The "officers" were the temple police under the chief priests. Only John mentions Roman soldiers at the arrest. The authorities expected a fight, or wanted to be sure there would be no rescue.
- **"With lanterns and torches and weapons" (v3).** It was Passover, so the moon was full. They came with lamps anyway, armed to hunt down the light of the world (8:12) in the dark.
- **"Jesus, knowing all that would happen to him, came forward" (v4).** He does not wait to be found. He steps out to meet them and asks the question, "Whom do you seek?" The same question opens and closes John's Gospel: Jesus' first words in it are "What are you seeking?" (1:38), and the risen Jesus asks Mary, "Whom are you seeking?" (20:15).
- **"I am he" (v5).** In Greek it is simply *egō eimi*, "I am." In ordinary speech it meant "I'm the one." But John has taught his readers to hear more (8:24, 28, 58), the divine name of Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah 43:10.
- **"They drew back and fell to the ground" (v6).** At the words "I am," the arresting party collapses. In the Bible, people fall before the presence of God (Ezek 1:28; Dan 10:9; Rev 1:17). John shows that no one takes Jesus' life from him: "I lay it down of my own accord" (10:18). JFB calls it "the glorious effulgence of the majesty of Christ which overpowered them." John notes that Judas "was standing with them" (v5). He falls on the wrong side.
- **"If you seek me, let these men go" (v8).** The shepherd stands between the wolves and the sheep (10:11–12). Jesus gives himself so that his disciples go free, a small picture of his death.
- **"Of those whom you gave me I have lost not one" (v9).** John applies Jesus' words in the prayer (17:12; compare 6:39) to their physical safety that night. Keeping them alive now preserves them for faith and mission later.
- **"Simon Peter, having a sword, drew it" (v10).** The disciples had two swords between them (Luke 22:38). Only John names Peter as the swordsman and the servant as Malchus. John knew the high priest's household (v15), which may explain how he knew the name. Malchus probably flinched, and Peter took off his right ear. Luke, the physician, adds that Jesus touched the ear and healed it (Luke 22:51), the last healing before the cross, performed on an enemy.
- **"Put your sword into its sheath" (v11).** Peter promised to lay down his life (13:37), and in his way he meant it. But he is fighting the wrong battle with the wrong weapon. Jesus' kingdom does not advance by the sword (v36; Matt 26:52).
- **"Shall I not drink the cup that the Father has given me?" (v11).** In the Old Testament, "the cup" is often the cup of God's wrath against sin (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15). In Gethsemane, the other Gospels record Jesus praying for it to pass (Mark 14:36). John gives his settled answer. It is the Father's cup, and he will drink it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the Kidron would have carried blood from the temple altar at Passover, a reminder of the sacrifice ahead, and sees the garden as a second Eden where the second Adam takes on the enemy. He also passes on Calvin's comment that Peter tried to prove his faith with a sword when he could not prove it with his tongue. Henry calls Jesus' rebuke of Peter "a gentle reproof, because it was his zeal that carried him beyond the bounds of discretion." JFB reads Jesus' choice of the familiar garden as deliberate: he went where Judas would look.

#### Jesus Faces Annas and Caiaphas (vv12–14)
- **"Arrested Jesus and bound him" (v12).** Roman soldiers, their tribune and the Jewish officers all have a hand in it. Gentiles and Jews together, the world, arrest the Son of God (Acts 4:27). Henry: "Christ was bound, that he might bind us to duty and obedience."
- **"First they led him to Annas" (v13).** Annas was high priest from AD 6 to 15, when the Romans removed him. Five of his sons, a grandson and his son-in-law Caiaphas (high priest about AD 18–36) held the office after him. Many Jews still regarded him as the true high priest, since by the Law the office was for life, and he remained the power behind the office. Annas's family is linked in Jewish tradition with the temple markets Jesus disrupted (2:13–16). Only John records this hearing before Annas.
- **"Caiaphas, who was high priest that year" (v13).** "That year" means that fateful year, not that the office changed every year.
- **"Caiaphas… had advised… that one man should die for the people" (v14).** John reminds readers of 11:49–52. Caiaphas meant a political sacrifice to keep Rome happy. God meant a substitute who would die "for the people."
- **Commentators:** Guzik explains Annas as the real power behind the high priesthood, though not the officially recognized high priest. JFB treats these verses briefly, noting that Jesus was seized only after he had shown he was giving himself up willingly. Henry remarks that Caiaphas's high office did not make him good: "Many a man's advancement has lost him his reputation."

#### Peter Denies Jesus (vv15–18)
- **"Another disciple. Since that disciple was known to the high priest" (v15).** Most readers, JFB included ("our Evangelist himself, no doubt"), identify him as the beloved disciple, John. How a Galilean fisherman would be "known" (*gnōstos*, which can mean an acquaintance or even a relative) to the high priest is unclear. Some suggest John's family, who had hired servants (Mark 1:20), had business dealings with the priestly household. Others think the disciple was a Jerusalem follower. The text does not say.
- **"Brought Peter in" (v16).** The other disciple's connections get Peter through the gate, into the place where he will fall.
- **"You also are not one of this man's disciples, are you?" (v17).** The question is phrased in Greek to expect the answer "no." The challenge is mild, from a servant girl at a door, and Peter takes the easy way out.
- **"I am not" (v17).** In Greek, *ouk eimi*. In the garden Jesus said three times, "I am" (*egō eimi*). In the courtyard Peter says, "I am not." The contrast is deliberate.
- **"A charcoal fire, because it was cold" (v18).** Jerusalem sits about 2,500 feet up, and spring nights can be cold. The word for charcoal fire (*anthrakia*) appears only here and in 21:9, where the risen Jesus has a charcoal fire burning on the beach and restores Peter. Peter denies him by one charcoal fire and is restored by another.
- **"Peter also was with them, standing and warming himself" (v18).** He stands with the officers who arrested Jesus. John's camera keeps cutting between Jesus inside, standing firm, and Peter outside, falling.
- **Commentators:** Guzik points out the contradiction: Peter was bold with a sword before soldiers and cowardly before a servant girl. JFB calls following into the courtyard a fatal step, taken against Jesus' instruction to watch and pray. Henry and Guzik both see Peter's fall as a warning to the strong, who are most in danger when they trust their own courage.

#### The High Priest Questions Jesus (vv19–24)
- **"The high priest then questioned Jesus about his disciples and his teaching" (v19).** This is Annas, still called "high priest" by courtesy. He asks about the disciples, perhaps to see how large the movement was, and about the teaching, perhaps to catch Jesus as a false prophet (Deut 13:1–5).
- **"I have spoken openly to the world… I have said nothing in secret" (v20).** Jesus is no conspirator. His teaching was given in synagogues and the temple, in public. The Mishnah, written later, records that a Jewish court was meant to establish guilt by witnesses, not by forcing the accused to testify against himself (Deut 19:15). Whether those rules were in force in Jesus' day is debated, but his answer makes the same point: "Ask those who have heard me."
- **"One of the officers… struck Jesus with his hand" (v22).** The word means a slap or a blow with the palm or a rod. Isaiah's Servant "gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard" (Isa 50:6). Micah foresaw the ruler of Israel struck on the cheek with a rod (Mic 5:1).
- **"If what I said is wrong, bear witness about the wrong; but if what I said is right, why do you strike me?" (v23).** Jesus does not literally turn the other cheek here (Matt 5:39). He calmly and firmly protests injustice. Not retaliating is not the same as staying silent about what is wrong. Paul did the same before the Sanhedrin (Acts 23:2–5).
- **"Annas then sent him bound to Caiaphas the high priest" (v24).** The KJV reads "had sent," as if the questioning were by Caiaphas all along. Most modern versions, the ESV included, take it as the next step: Annas sent him on. JFB agrees, calling the Annas interview "a preliminary and non-official one." The formal hearing before Caiaphas and the Sanhedrin, with false witnesses and the charge of blasphemy, is told in Matthew 26:57–68 and Mark 14:53–65. John skips it, perhaps because his readers already knew it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik argues that Jewish legal protections, such as hearing witnesses for the defense first, were ignored that night, and that Jesus was asserting his legal rights. JFB reads Jesus' reply as a demand for proper witnesses and says his "if well" answer shows that Matthew 5:39 is not to be taken woodenly. JFB is also the source for reading v24 as "Annas sent Him [not 'had sent Him'] to Caiaphas."

#### Peter Denies Jesus Again (vv25–27)
- **"Now Simon Peter was standing and warming himself" (v25).** John picks up the courtyard scene exactly where he left it (v18). The trial and the denials happen at the same time.
- **"You also are not one of his disciples, are you?" (v25).** The same question from a group by the fire. Again Peter says, "I am not."
- **"A relative of the man whose ear Peter had cut off" (v26).** Only John gives this detail. This is the most dangerous question of the three: a family member of Malchus, who may have been in the garden. Matthew 26:73 adds that Peter's Galilean accent gave him away, and Matthew and Mark say he swore and called down curses.
- **"At once a rooster crowed" (v27).** Exactly as Jesus said (13:38). John does not describe Peter's tears. Luke adds that "the Lord turned and looked at Peter," and Peter went out and wept bitterly (Luke 22:61–62). John saves Peter's story for the end of the Gospel, where three denials are answered by three questions of love (21:15–17).
- **Commentators:** Guzik says it was Peter's courage that failed, not his love, and that his bitter weeping showed real devotion. JFB notes that the Gospels differ on who put the questions, which is natural in a crowded courtyard, and dwells on "this heart-piercing 'look' which his Lord gave him," tying it to Jesus' prayer that Peter's faith would not fail (Luke 22:32). Henry: "Dare to be brave, for truth will out."

#### Jesus Before Pilate (vv28–32)
- **"To the governor's headquarters. It was early morning" (v28).** The "headquarters" (*praitōrion*) was the governor's residence when in Jerusalem, probably Herod's old palace on the western hill, or possibly the Antonia Fortress. "Early morning" (*prōi*) was the Roman watch from 3 to 6 a.m. Roman officials began work at dawn. Pontius Pilate was prefect of Judea from AD 26 to 36. An inscription bearing his name and title was found at Caesarea in 1961. Josephus and Philo describe him as stubborn and harsh, and Luke 13:1 mentions Galileans "whose blood Pilate had mingled with their sacrifices."
- **"So that they would not be defiled, but could eat the Passover" (v28).** Entering a Gentile's house was thought to make a Jew ritually unclean. The irony is bitter: they guard their ceremonial purity while handing an innocent man over to be killed.
- **When was the Passover?** Matthew, Mark and Luke present the Last Supper as a Passover meal (Mark 14:12–16). Here, the next morning, the leaders have not yet eaten "the Passover," and in 19:14 it is "the day of Preparation of the Passover." Faithful scholars offer several explanations. Some think "eat the Passover" here means the festival sacrifices and meals of the following seven days of Unleavened Bread, which the Tyndale notes favor. Some think different Jewish groups followed different calendars that year. Others think John deliberately times Jesus' death with the slaughter of the Passover lambs. JFB admits the question has "occasioned immense research and learned treatises." What is clear in every Gospel is that Jesus died at Passover as the true Passover lamb (1:29; 1 Cor 5:7).
- **"If this man were not doing evil, we would not have delivered him over to you" (v30).** They have no charge ready. They want Pilate to sign off on their verdict without a trial of his own.
- **"It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death" (v31).** Rome kept the power of capital punishment for itself in provinces like Judea. There were exceptions, mob killings such as Stephen's stoning (Acts 7) and the temple rule that a Gentile entering the inner courts could be killed. But a formal execution needed the governor.
- **"To show by what kind of death he was going to die" (v32).** Jewish execution was by stoning. Roman execution of rebels and slaves was by crucifixion. Jesus had said he would be "lifted up" (3:14; 12:32–33). Handing him to Rome meant the cross. Deuteronomy 21:23 said a man hanged on a tree was under God's curse, and Paul saw in this that Christ became a curse for us (Gal 3:13).
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls it ironic that the leaders avoided ceremonial defilement while plotting to kill an innocent man, and links crucifixion to the curse of Deuteronomy 21 and to Jesus' words about being lifted up. JFB explains that crucifixion, being Roman, needed the governor's authority, while stoning was the Jewish method. JFB has no separate note on v31.

#### My Kingdom Is Not of This World (vv33–40)
- **"Are you the King of the Jews?" (v33).** In Greek the "you" is emphatic: "*You* are the King of the Jews?" Pilate looks at a bound, bruised Galilean and finds the charge absurd. But to Rome a "king" not appointed by Caesar was a rebel. Luke 23:2 gives the charge in full: forbidding taxes to Caesar and claiming to be "Christ, a king."
- **"Do you say this of your own accord, or did others say it to you?" (v34).** Jesus asks what kind of "king" Pilate means: a political rebel, as Rome would think, or the Messiah, as Israel would.
- **"Am I a Jew?" (v35).** Pilate's contempt shows. He wants nothing to do with Jewish religious quarrels.
- **"My kingdom is not of this world" (v36).** Jesus does not deny being a king. He says his kingdom is not "of" (*ek*, "from, out of") this world. JFB rightly says this is about its origin and nature, not its scope. It does not come from this world's power, and it is not built with this world's weapons. The proof is simple: his servants are not fighting, and he has just told Peter to put the sword away. His kingdom does affect this world, as he taught his disciples to pray, "your kingdom come… on earth" (Matt 6:10). Henry: "Its rise is not from this world."
- **"You say that I am a king" (v37).** This is a guarded "yes," meaning "Your word is accurate, but not in your sense." Jesus then states his mission: "For this purpose I was born and for this purpose I have come into the world," words that assume he existed before he was born, "to bear witness to the truth." Paul later remembers this "good confession" before Pilate (1 Tim 6:13).
- **"Everyone who is of the truth listens to my voice" (v37).** These are shepherd words (10:3, 16, 27). Jesus is inviting Pilate to listen.
- **"What is truth?" (v38).** Pilate does not wait for an answer. It may be cynicism, weariness or a politician's shrug. The irony is that the Truth in person (14:6) is standing in front of him. JFB calls it "the question of questions, which the thoughtful of every age have asked, but never man yet answered" apart from Christ.
- **"I find no guilt in him" (v38).** This is the first of three times Pilate declares Jesus innocent (19:4, 6). The Lamb of God is examined and found without blemish (Exod 12:5; 1 Pet 1:19).
- **"You have a custom that I should release one man for you at the Passover" (v39).** The custom is not recorded outside the Gospels, but Roman governors did sometimes grant amnesties, and the Passover celebrated Israel's release from slavery. Pilate tries to get out of the decision by offering them Jesus, calling him "the King of the Jews" to needle the priests.
- **"Not this man, but Barabbas!" (v40).** "Barabbas" means "son of the father" in Aramaic (*bar abba*). Some early manuscripts of Matthew 27:16–17 give his full name as "Jesus Barabbas," which would make Pilate's question sharper still: which Jesus do you want? John calls him a "robber" (*lēstēs*), the word Josephus uses for violent rebels and bandits. Mark 15:7 says he had committed murder in an uprising. The crowd chooses the kind of messiah who would have used the sword. A guilty man goes free because an innocent man takes his place.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that "king" to a Roman meant a political rival, while Jesus' kingdom comes from heaven and is founded on peace, and he draws the lesson that Christians should live by the humble power of the cross rather than worldly power. He also reads Barabbas as "son of the father," freed while the innocent Jesus died in his place. JFB reads Pilate's "Art thou a king?" as surprise and unease, not sarcasm, and links Jesus' witness to the truth with 1 Timothy 6:13. JFB has no note on v40.

#### Pointing to Christ
John shows that in the hours of his arrest and trial, Jesus is never a helpless victim. Soldiers fall back at his "I am," and he walks out to meet them, shields his friends, heals an enemy's ear and chooses to drink the Father's cup. Inside, he stands firm while Peter, warming himself at a charcoal fire, says three times, "I am not." Before Pilate, the prisoner calmly explains a kingdom no sword can build and no empire can stop. The Truth stands in front of Pilate, and Pilate asks, "What is truth?" Three times Jesus is declared innocent. Then the crowd chooses Barabbas, and the guilty "son of the father" goes free while the innocent Son of the Father goes to die in his place.
