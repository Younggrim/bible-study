*Setting:* About 966–946 BC, in Jerusalem. Chapter 6 described the building of the temple over seven years. Chapter 7 interrupts that account with Solomon's own palace complex, which took thirteen years (vv1–12). It then goes back to the temple and describes the bronze and gold furnishings made for it, especially the work of a master bronzesmith from Tyre named Hiram (vv13–51). The temple and palace stood together on the ridge north of the old City of David, the palace probably just south of the temple, so the king's house and God's house were neighbors. The details are many, and some of the technical Hebrew words are uncertain. Second Chronicles 3:15–4:22 covers the same furnishings, with some different numbers, which are noted below. The chapter ends with the temple complete and ready for the ark (ch 8).

#### Solomon Builds His Palace (vv1–12)
- **"Solomon was building his own house thirteen years" (v1).** The temple took seven (6:38). The two together took twenty years (9:10). What does the contrast mean? Readers disagree. Henry reads it generously: Solomon was "less eager and intent, in building his own house than in building God's," and was "impatient till the temple was finished." Guzik reads it as a warning sign that Solomon's own comfort and glory mattered to him, and points to Haggai 1:4: "Is it a time for you yourselves to dwell in your paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins?" The writer does not settle it, but placing the palace right in the middle of the temple account invites the comparison. The palace complex was also much bigger than the temple.
- **"The House of the Forest of Lebanon" (v2).** So named for its rows of cedar pillars, like a forest of tree trunks. It was 100 by 50 cubits, about 150 by 75 feet, and 45 feet high, larger than the temple building itself (60 by 20). It held 300 gold shields (10:17) and the gold drinking vessels (10:21), and Isaiah later refers to "the weapons of the House of the Forest" (Isa 22:8). So it seems to have been a great hall, treasury and armory. The details of the rows and chambers (vv2–5) are hard to reconstruct, since the Hebrew building terms are uncertain.
- **"The Hall of Pillars" (v6).** A colonnaded hall, 50 by 30 cubits, probably an entrance hall or waiting area before the throne room. Similar columned entrance halls have been found in palaces in Syria.
- **"The Hall of the Throne where he was to pronounce judgment, even the Hall of Judgment" (v7).** The king was the highest judge (3:9, 16–28). His great ivory throne overlaid with gold stood here (10:18–20). JFB: it was "appropriated to the transaction of public business." It was paneled in cedar "from floor to rafters."
- **"His own house… Solomon also made a house like this hall for Pharaoh's daughter" (v8).** His private living quarters were in a court behind the hall. His Egyptian queen got her own palace (3:1; 9:24). Chronicles says he moved her out of the City of David because the places where the ark had been were holy (2 Chr 8:11).
- **"Costly stones, cut according to measure, sawed with saws" (v9).** High-quality limestone blocks, cut to size and smoothed on both faces. Some foundation stones were "eight and ten cubits," 12 to 15 feet long (v10). Excavations at Megiddo, Hazor and Gezer, cities Solomon fortified (9:15), have found fine cut-stone masonry and gate structures that many archaeologists date to his time, though the dating is debated.
- **"Three courses of cut stone all around, and a course of cedar beams" (v12).** The same building style as the temple's inner court (6:36). Layers of wood between stone may have helped walls flex in earthquakes, as the Tyndale notes suggest.
- **Commentators:** Henry and Guzik differ on the thirteen years, as above. JFB agrees with Henry, saying the palace took "nearly double" the time because the temple had more preparation behind it (David's stockpiles) and more urgency. The Tyndale notes observe that the writer gives the palace only a few verses because his interest is the temple, where God dwells.

#### The Temple Furnishings (vv13–51)
- **"King Solomon sent and brought Hiram from Tyre" (v13).** Not King Hiram, but a craftsman with the same name. Chronicles calls him Huram-abi (2 Chr 2:13). "He was the son of a widow of the tribe of Naphtali" (v14), but Chronicles says "the son of a woman of the daughters of Dan" (2 Chr 2:14). JFB suggests she was from Dan by birth and married into Naphtali, or came from the town of Dan, which lay in Naphtali's territory. His father was a Tyrian bronze worker. So he was half Israelite and half Phoenician, as Guzik notes.
- **"Full of wisdom, understanding, and skill" (v14).** These are the same words used of Bezalel, who made the tabernacle furnishings (Exod 31:3; 35:31). In Hebrew, "wisdom" (*chokmah*) covers practical skill as well as good judgment. The Tyndale notes cite Proverbs 22:29: "Do you see a man skillful in his work? He will stand before kings." A craftsman's skill is a gift of God.
- **"He cast two pillars of bronze. Eighteen cubits was the height… a line of twelve cubits measured its circumference" (v15).** About 27 feet tall and 18 feet around, hollow, with walls "four fingers" (about 3 inches) thick (Jer 52:21). On top were bronze capitals 5 cubits high (v16), decorated with lattice, chainwork and two rows of bronze pomegranates, 200 for each (vv17–20), and lily-work (vv19, 22). The pillars seem to have stood free in front of the porch, not holding up the roof, as the Tyndale notes say. Free-standing pillars at temple entrances are known elsewhere in the region. Chronicles gives their height as 35 cubits (2 Chr 3:15), probably the two pillars combined, or a copying difference.
- **"He… called its name Jachin… and… Boaz" (v21).** *Jachin* means "he will establish," and *Boaz* probably means "in him is strength." Read together they may say, "God will establish [it] in strength." Boaz was also the name of David's great-grandfather (Ruth 4:21–22), though whether that is intended is unknown. The verb in Jachin is the same one used in God's promise to "establish" David's throne (2 Sam 7:12–13, 16). Every worshiper walking into the temple passed between two bronze reminders that God, not Solomon, makes the house and the kingdom stand. JFB: the names were "symbolical" of strength and stability, especially of God's spiritual kingdom, not the building, which was later destroyed. The Babylonians broke the pillars apart and carried the bronze away (2 Kgs 25:13; Jer 52:17).
- **"Then he made the sea of cast metal" (v23).** A huge bronze basin, 10 cubits (15 feet) across and 5 cubits (7.5 feet) high. Chronicles says it was for the priests to wash in (2 Chr 4:6), taking the place of the tabernacle's bronze basin (Exod 30:17–21). The priests washed before entering God's presence. The name "sea" may also echo the waters of creation, which God tamed and ruled (Gen 1:9; Ps 89:9), though that is an interpretation, not stated in the text.
- **"A line of thirty cubits measured its circumference" (v23).** Some have accused the Bible of saying pi equals 3. But the numbers are rounded, the brim flared out "like the flower of a lily" (v26), and the walls were a handbreadth (about 3 inches) thick, so the circumference measured under the brim would naturally be less than the diameter at the outer lip. There is no error here, just ancient builders' measurements.
- **"It stood on twelve oxen, three facing north, three facing west, three facing south, and three facing east" (v25).** The twelve oxen probably stood for the twelve tribes, arranged as Israel camped around the tabernacle (Num 2). Oxen were sacrificial animals and symbols of strength. Later King Ahaz removed the oxen and set the sea on a stone base, likely to pay tribute to Assyria (2 Kgs 16:17).
- **"It held two thousand baths" (v26).** A bath was a liquid measure, roughly 5–6 gallons (about 22 liters), so this was around 11,000–12,000 gallons, close to Guzik's estimate. Chronicles says "three thousand baths" (2 Chr 4:5). The Tyndale notes suggest different measuring standards. Others suggest the number in Chronicles includes the water when filled to the brim, or a copying difference.
- **"The ten stands of bronze" (v27).** Wheeled carts, four cubits square and three high, with panels decorated with "lions, oxen, and cherubim" (v29) and "palm trees" (v36). Each carried a bronze basin holding forty baths (about 230 gallons, v38). Chronicles says these were for rinsing the parts of the burnt offerings (2 Chr 4:6). The wheels (vv30–33), like chariot wheels, meant water could be moved around the courtyard. Similar wheeled bronze stands, much smaller, have been found in Cyprus, dating from a few centuries earlier. The technical terms in vv27–37 are some of the hardest in the Old Testament to translate.
- **"Five on the south side of the house, and five on the north… the sea at the southeast corner" (v39).** All in the courtyard in front of the temple, on either side of the great bronze altar (2 Chr 4:1).
- **"Hiram also made the pots, the shovels, and the basins" (v40).** The everyday tools of sacrifice: pots for carrying away ashes, shovels for the altar, basins for catching blood. The list in vv40–45 sums up all of Hiram's work.
- **"In the plain of the Jordan the king cast them, in the clay ground between Succoth and Zarethan" (v46).** Casting this much bronze took clay molds, fuel and wind for the furnaces. The Jordan Valley had all three. JFB says the soil "abounds with marl" (fine clay), suited to molds. Succoth was east of the Jordan near the Jabbok river (Gen 33:17; Judg 8:5). Zarethan was near Adam, where the Jordan stopped for Joshua (Josh 3:16). Archaeologists have found evidence of ancient metalworking in this area.
- **"Solomon left all the vessels unweighed, because there were so many of them" (v47).** There was too much bronze to count. David had gathered "bronze in quantities beyond weighing" (1 Chr 22:3, 14), much of it taken in his wars (1 Chr 18:8).
- **"The golden altar, the golden table for the bread of the Presence, the lampstands of pure gold, five on the south side and five on the north" (vv48–49).** These are the furnishings of the Holy Place, inside the temple. The golden altar was for incense (Exod 30:1–10). The table held twelve loaves, the "bread of the Presence," renewed every Sabbath (Lev 24:5–9). The tabernacle had one lampstand. Solomon made ten, lighting the larger room. Chronicles also mentions ten tables (2 Chr 4:8), so the "table" here may be the main one among several. Bronze outside, gold inside: the closer to the Most Holy Place, the more precious the metal.
- **"The sockets of gold, for the doors of the innermost part of the house" (v50).** Even the door hinges were gold.
- **"Solomon brought in the things that David his father had dedicated, the silver, the gold, and the vessels" (v51).** David had dedicated to God the plunder of his wars and gifts from other kings (2 Sam 8:10–12; 1 Chr 18:11; 26:26). Solomon stores them in the temple treasuries. The father gathered, the son built, and both gave to God. The chapter closes: "all the work… was finished."
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees in Jachin and Boaz a reminder to every worshiper that God establishes and strengthens, and suggests they may recall the pillars of cloud and fire of the Exodus. JFB calls the stands "trucks or four-wheeled carriages," and the Sea "an immense… vase," and explains the casting site by the Jordan clay. Henry's introduction defends the long lists: "The particular description of these things was not needless when it was written, nor is it now useless." The Tyndale notes add that the bread and the lampstand point to Christ, the bread of life (John 6:35) and the light of the world (John 8:12).

#### Pointing to Christ
Every object in the temple served one purpose: making it possible for sinful people to come near a holy God. The great altar and the carts were for sacrifice. The bronze sea was for washing. The bread of the Presence spoke of fellowship at God's table. The lampstands gave light in God's house. The New Testament says all of this was "a shadow of the good things to come" (Heb 10:1). Jesus is the sacrifice offered "once for all" (Heb 10:10), the one who washes his people (John 13:8; Titus 3:5), the bread of life, and the light of the world. The two pillars at the door proclaimed that God would establish his house in strength, and he has: "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone… a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation" (Isa 28:16; 1 Pet 2:6). And Jesus promises the believer who overcomes, "I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God. Never shall he go out of it" (Rev 3:12).
