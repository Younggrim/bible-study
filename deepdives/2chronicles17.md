*Setting:* About 872–869 BC. Asa has died after forty-one years, honored at his burial but sad at the end: he leaned on the king of Syria instead of the LORD, jailed the prophet who rebuked him, and in his final illness sought physicians rather than God (ch 16). His son Jehoshaphat, thirty-five years old (20:31), takes the throne and reigns twenty-five years, probably counting a short co-regency while Asa was ill. In the north, Ahab now rules Israel with his Phoenician queen Jezebel, who has brought in the worship of Baal (1 Kgs 16:29–33). Kings covers Jehoshaphat mainly as Ahab's ally (1 Kgs 22). Chronicles gives him four chapters (17–20), and this first one, found only in Chronicles, describes his reign at its best: a king who sought God, taught God's word to the nation, and was given peace, wealth and strength.

#### Jehoshaphat Reigns in Judah (vv1–6)
- **"Strengthened himself against Israel" (v1).** "Strengthened himself" (*chazaq*) is a favorite phrase in Chronicles for a king securing his rule (1:1; 12:13; 13:21). The threat from the north was real. Baasha's attempt to fortify Ramah was only a generation back (16:1), and Ahab's Israel was strong. Ironically, Jehoshaphat will soon make an alliance with that same Israel (18:1).
- **"The cities of Ephraim that Asa his father had captured" (v2).** Border towns in the hill country north of Jerusalem (15:8).
- **"The LORD was with Jehoshaphat, because he walked in the earlier ways of his father David" (v3).** The "earlier ways" are David's early years of devotion, before his adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah. Some ancient Greek manuscripts leave out "David," so that it reads "the earlier ways of his father," meaning Asa's early, faithful years before chapter 16. Either reading makes sense, and both make the same point: Jehoshaphat imitated a good beginning, not a bad ending.
- **"He did not seek the Baals" (v3).** Plural, the Baal gods in their many local forms. This is the first time the Chronicler names Baal worship in Judah's story. It was flourishing in Ahab's Israel at the time, with 450 prophets of Baal at Jezebel's table (1 Kgs 18:19). Elijah was prophesying in the north in these same years.
- **"But sought the God of his father and walked in his commandments, and not according to the practices of Israel" (v4).** Seeking God is again the measure. And for the first time Chronicles explicitly sets Judah's way against the northern kingdom's practices.
- **"Therefore the LORD established the kingdom in his hand" (v5).** The same promise God made to David for his son (1 Chr 17:11–12). **"All Judah brought tribute."** The word (*minchah*) means a gift or offering. Here it seems to be voluntary gifts of loyalty from his own people, not forced tribute. **"Great riches and honor"** is the phrase used of David and Solomon (1 Chr 29:28; 2 Chr 1:12; 18:1).
- **"His heart was courageous in the ways of the LORD" (v6).** Literally "his heart was lifted up," or "was high." Elsewhere this phrase means pride. Uzziah's heart "was lifted up, to his destruction" (26:16), and so was Hezekiah's (32:25). Only here is it used in a good sense. Jehoshaphat's heart rose high not in self-confidence but in confidence in God's ways. The ESV's "courageous" captures that well.
- **"He took the high places and the Asherim out of Judah" (v6).** Yet 20:33 and 1 Kings 22:43 say the high places were not removed. As with Asa (14:3; 15:17), the likeliest explanation is that he removed the shrines tied to other gods, while some local altars used for the LORD stayed, or that the people rebuilt what he tore down. The Chronicler himself admits in 20:33 that "the people had not yet set their hearts upon the God of their fathers." A king's reform could not change every heart.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry calls Jehoshaphat "one of the best that ever swayed the sceptre of Judah since David's head was laid," and "the good son of a good father, so that, as this time, grace ran in the blood." On v6: "He brought his heart to his work, and lifted up his heart in it," and "was bold and resolute in the ways of God." JFB reads "Baalim" as "a term used for idols generally" and says Jehoshaphat followed his ancestor's piety "in the early part of his reign." Guzik, quoting Trapp, takes David's "first ways" as his years before Bathsheba. He follows Adam Clarke in distinguishing idolatrous high places, which were removed, from those used for the LORD, which were left, and cites Selman that popular religion often lagged behind official reform.

#### Jehoshaphat's Teaching Mission (vv7–9)
- **"In the third year of his reign he sent his officials… to teach in the cities of Judah" (v7).** Possibly the third year counted from Asa's death, the first year of his sole reign. Jehoshaphat's first major policy after securing his borders was to teach God's word to the whole country. Azariah had told Asa that Israel once went "a long time… without a teaching priest and without law" (15:3). Jehoshaphat acts to fill that gap.
- **The team (vv7–8).** Five royal officials, nine Levites and two priests: sixteen men. The officials gave the mission the king's authority. The Levites and priests were the ones the law appointed to teach (Lev 10:11; Deut 33:10). Moses had also told the king himself to keep a copy of the law, read it all his days, and live by it (Deut 17:18–20). Here the king does more: he makes sure everyone hears it.
- **The names.** Most are found only here. Some names repeat (two Nethan- names, two -adonijah names), which is natural among related families. "Tobadonijah" looks like a combination of the two names before it, "Tobijah" and "Adonijah," and some scholars think a scribe accidentally repeated them. The Greek translation keeps it.
- **"Having the Book of the Law of the LORD with them" (v9).** They did not teach their own ideas. They carried the written text. Whether this meant the whole Pentateuch (Genesis to Deuteronomy) or mainly Deuteronomy, the law Moses commanded to be read to all Israel (Deut 31:9–13), the authority was the written word.
- **"They went about through all the cities of Judah and taught among the people" (v9).** This is a traveling Bible-teaching mission, centuries before Ezra read the law to the returned exiles while the Levites "helped the people to understand" (Neh 8:7–8). The Chronicler's own readers had seen Ezra and the Levites do exactly this.
- **Jehoshaphat's name.** It means "the LORD judges." Fittingly, his reign is marked by teaching God's law, and later by appointing judges throughout the land (19:4–11).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry approves that the king "will not lead them blindfold, no, not into a reformation, but endeavours to have them well taught," and adds, "Ministers, when they go to teach the people, should have their Bibles with them." He also says, "Teaching was part of the work for which they had their maintenance." JFB says teaching was normally the priests' job, "but extraordinary commissioners were appointed" for this mission, and leaves open whether the book was "the whole Pentateuch or only the book of Deuteronomy." Guzik cites Knapp that these sixteen teachers did more to impress the surrounding nations than an army, and Morgan that proclaiming God's word is the best service anyone can do a nation. Through Adam Clarke he compares the mission to John Wesley's traveling preachers.

#### Jehoshaphat's Greatness (vv10–19)
- **"The fear of the LORD fell upon all the kingdoms of the lands that were around Judah, and they made no war against Jehoshaphat" (v10).** The order matters. First the law was taught (v9), then the fear of the LORD fell on the nations (v10). The Chronicler links Judah's peace to its faithfulness, not to its forts. The same "fear of God" will fall on the nations after God's victory in 20:29.
- **"Some of the Philistines brought Jehoshaphat presents and silver for tribute" (v11).** The Philistines on the coastal plain had been subject to David (1 Chr 18:1) and later free. Now some return to paying tribute.
- **"The Arabians also brought him 7,700 rams and 7,700 goats" (v11).** Nomadic tribes of the desert south and southeast of Judah, the herdsmen Asa had plundered near Gerar (14:15). Their tribute was paid in flocks.
- **"Jehoshaphat grew steadily greater" (v12).** Literally "kept going and growing great," the same phrase used of David's rise (1 Chr 11:9).
- **"He built in Judah fortresses and store cities" (v12).** "Fortresses" here (*biraniyyot*) is a later word for citadels. Archaeologists have found a chain of fortresses across the Negev and Judah's wilderness from roughly this period, though dating any one of them to Jehoshaphat is not certain. Store cities held grain, oil and weapons.
- **The army (vv14–18).** Five commanders: three from Judah (300,000, 280,000 and 200,000) and two from Benjamin (200,000 and 180,000), totaling 1,160,000. That is about twice Asa's army (14:8), and as large as David's census of all Israel and Judah (1 Chr 21:5). As with the numbers in chapters 13–14, faithful readers take them in different ways: as the total of all men available for call-up, as counts of military units (since *'eleph* can mean a unit), or as figures enlarged in copying. Whatever the actual numbers, the Chronicler presents a strong kingdom blessed by God.
- **"Amasiah the son of Zichri, a volunteer for the service of the LORD" (v16).** Literally "who offered himself willingly to the LORD." The same verb (*nadab*) is used of the people who gave freely for the tabernacle and temple (Exod 35:29; 1 Chr 29:5–9) and of the volunteers who settled in Jerusalem after the exile (Neh 11:2). Amasiah is the only commander singled out this way. His military service was offered as worship.
- **"Armed with bow and shield" (v17).** Benjamin was famous for archers (14:8; 1 Chr 12:2).
- **"These were in the service of the king, besides those whom the king had placed in the fortified cities" (v19).** The field army was separate from the garrisons. Jehoshaphat's kingdom was at peace and well defended.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees the whole chapter as showing "his prosperity the reward of his piety and his piety the brightest grace and ornament of his prosperity." JFB thinks the Philistines either were already tributary or were "desirous of securing his valuable friendship," and places the Arabians among "the nomad tribes on the south of the Dead Sea." JFB totals the army at "one million one hundred and sixty thousand men, without including those who garrisoned the fortresses," taking the figures as written. Guzik reads Jehoshaphat's prosperity in light of Deuteronomy 28's blessings for obedience, and through Spurgeon turns Amasiah into a model of willing service: no one had to draft him, look for him, watch over him or lead him. Guzik adds that the real treasure of the kingdom was its devoted people, not its numbers. The Tyndale notes, unlike JFB, find the troop totals too high and suggest "thousand" here means smaller units.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jehoshaphat's best gift to his nation was not his army but his teachers, men who went from town to town with the Book of the Law. A greater Son of David "went throughout all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom" (Matt 9:35), and sent out his own disciples to teach all nations "to observe all that I have commanded you" (Matt 28:19–20). Like Amasiah, his followers serve not by draft but as volunteers, offering themselves as "a living sacrifice" (Rom 12:1). And the peace Jehoshaphat enjoyed for a time points to the peace Christ gives, which the world cannot give or take away (John 14:27).
