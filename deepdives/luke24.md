*Setting:* Jerusalem and its surroundings, Sunday after Passover, about AD 30, and then forty days later (Acts 1:3). Jesus was crucified on Friday and buried in Joseph's new tomb just before sunset. The women from Galilee saw where he was laid, prepared spices and rested on the Sabbath (23:55–56). Chapter 24 is one long day: the empty tomb at dawn, two disciples walking to Emmaus in the afternoon, and Jesus appearing to the gathered disciples that evening. It ends with a short account of his ascension, which Luke tells more fully at the start of Acts. The Gospel that began in the temple with a priest who could not speak a blessing (1:8–22) ends in the temple with disciples "blessing God."

#### The Resurrection (vv1–12)
- **"On the first day of the week, at early dawn" (v1).** Sunday, just as the light was coming. The women had waited through the Sabbath and came as soon as the law allowed. From then on, the first day of the week became the church's day of worship (Acts 20:7; 1 Cor 16:2; Rev 1:10, "the Lord's day").
- **"They found the stone rolled away" (v2).** Wealthy tombs were often closed with a large disk-shaped stone set in a sloping groove, easy to roll down into place and very hard to roll back up. Mark records the women asking on the way who would move it for them (Mark 16:3).
- **"They did not find the body of the Lord Jesus" (v3).** This is the first time Luke uses the full title "the Lord Jesus." It becomes a common title in Acts. The body is missing, and the one missing is the Lord.
- **"Two men stood by them in dazzling apparel" (v4).** They are angels (v23), described as men, as angels often are in Scripture (Gen 18:2; Acts 1:10). Luke's "two" matches the law's requirement of two witnesses (Deut 19:15). Mark and Matthew mention one angel, probably the one who spoke. "Dazzling" is the word for lightning flashing, used of Jesus' clothes at the transfiguration (9:29).
- **"Why do you seek the living among the dead?" (v5).** The women came looking for a corpse. JFB notes the angels do not say "the risen one" but "the living one." A cemetery is the wrong place to look for him.
- **"He is not here, but has risen" (v6).** The verb is passive, "he has been raised," with God as the one who raised him (Acts 2:24). This line is missing from a few Western manuscripts, but nearly all manuscripts include it.
- **"Remember how he told you, while he was still in Galilee" (vv6–7).** The women had been with Jesus in Galilee (8:1–3; 23:55) and had heard him say the Son of Man "must" be delivered, crucified and raised (9:22, 44; 18:31–33). "Must" (*dei*) again points to God's plan. The angels bring no new message. They remind the women of what Jesus said.
- **"And they remembered his words" (v8).** The first step toward faith is remembering what Jesus said.
- **"Mary Magdalene and Joanna and Mary the mother of James" (v10).** Mary from Magdala on the Sea of Galilee had been freed from seven demons (8:2). Joanna was the wife of Chuza, manager of Herod Antipas's household (8:3), so even Herod's court had a witness. Mary the mother of James is probably the mother of "James the younger" (Mark 15:40). "The other women" were with them.
- **Women as the first witnesses.** In that culture a woman's testimony was given little weight in court. The Jewish historian Josephus says women should not be accepted as witnesses. If early Christians had invented this story, they would not have made women the first witnesses. Most historians see this as strong evidence that the account is true to what happened.
- **"These words seemed to them an idle tale" (v11).** "Idle tale" (*lēros*) means nonsense, silly talk. The apostles did not expect a resurrection any more than the women did. Their unbelief makes their later witness more convincing. They were not people ready to believe anything.
- **"But Peter rose and ran to the tomb" (v12).** The man who denied Jesus three times runs to the tomb. John says he went with Peter and arrived first (John 20:3–10). "The linen cloths by themselves" means the grave wrappings were left lying there, which grave robbers would never have done. Peter "went home marveling," still trying to understand. A few Western manuscripts lack this verse, but the great majority include it.
- **Commentators:** Henry: the women "look rather to find their Master in his grave-clothes, than angels in their shining garments," and "these angels from heaven bring not any new gospel, but remind the women of Christ's words." He adds that Peter "now ran to the sepulchre, who so lately ran from his Master." JFB finds it remarkable "to hear angels quoting a whole sentence of Christ's to the disciples." Guzik notes that the women's surprise shows they were not expecting a resurrection, which counts against the idea that wishful thinking produced the story. He also reports a claim that the word in v11 was a medical term for the babbling of someone in a fever. The lexicon gives only "nonsense" or "silly talk," so that detail cannot be confirmed.

#### On the Road to Emmaus (vv13–35)
- **"Two of them were going to a village named Emmaus, about seven miles from Jerusalem" (v13).** Literally "sixty stadia," about seven miles. The site is uncertain. Several villages have been proposed. One manuscript tradition reads "160 stadia," which would fit Emmaus-Nicopolis, about twenty miles away, but that seems too far to walk there and back in one evening. The two were probably going home after Passover.
- **"Jesus himself drew near and went with them" (v15).** Only Luke tells this story, and it is one of the most loved passages in the Gospels. The risen Lord joins two ordinary disciples on a dusty road.
- **"Their eyes were kept from recognizing him" (v16).** The passive verb suggests God was holding their eyes. Mark summarizes the same event: he "appeared in another form to two of them, as they were walking into the country" (Mark 16:12). They recognize him only when God opens their eyes (v31).
- **"They stood still, looking sad" (v17).** "Looking sad" means gloomy, downcast in face. Jesus asks them to tell him what they are discussing, and lets them pour out their grief.
- **"Cleopas" (v18).** He is named only here. Some identify him with Clopas, whose wife Mary stood near the cross (John 19:25). If so, the second traveler may have been his wife. That is possible but uncertain. "Are you the only visitor to Jerusalem who does not know?" The crucifixion had happened in public during the busiest week of the year.
- **"A prophet mighty in deed and word" (v19).** Their summary of Jesus is accurate but incomplete. Moses was also called "mighty in his words and deeds" (Acts 7:22).
- **"But we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel" (v21).** "Redeem" means to set free by paying a price. They hoped Jesus would free Israel from Roman rule, as Zechariah and Anna had hoped at the start of the Gospel (1:68; 2:38). The past tense is heartbreaking: "we had hoped." They do not see that the cross they think ended his mission was the very price of redemption (Mark 10:45; 1 Pet 1:18–19).
- **"It is now the third day" (v21).** Jesus had said he would rise on the third day. They mention it without seeing its meaning.
- **"Some women of our company amazed us" (vv22–24).** They have heard the women's report and even that the tomb was empty, "but him they did not see." They have all the facts and still have no faith.
- **"O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken!" (v25).** "Foolish" here means "without understanding," not stupid. The problem is the heart as much as the head. They believed the parts of the prophets about a glorious Messiah, not "all" of it, including his suffering.
- **"Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?" (v26).** "Necessary" is *dei* again. The cross was not a detour on the way to glory. It was the road.
- **"Beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (v27).** "Interpreted" (*diermēneuō*) means to explain or expound. Luke does not list the passages, but the sermons in Acts show what the apostles learned: Psalm 16, Psalm 110, Psalm 118, Isaiah 53, Deuteronomy 18, and more (Acts 2:25–36; 3:22–26; 8:32–35). The whole Old Testament, read rightly, points to Christ (John 5:39, 46).
- **"He acted as if he were going farther" (v28).** He will not force his company on them. "They urged him strongly," as Abraham urged his three visitors to stay (Gen 18:3–5). Hospitality was a sacred duty.
- **"He took the bread and blessed and broke it and gave it to them" (v30).** The guest takes the host's role. The four verbs are the same as at the feeding of the five thousand (9:16) and the Last Supper (22:19). These two were not at the supper, but they may have been at the feeding, or seen him pray in this way before.
- **"Their eyes were opened, and they recognized him. And he vanished" (v31).** "Opened" echoes Genesis 3:7, where the eyes of Adam and Eve were opened to their shame. Here eyes are opened to the risen Lord. As soon as they know him, he is gone. They do not need to see him to believe any longer.
- **"Did not our hearts burn within us... while he opened to us the Scriptures?" (v32).** Their hearts were on fire before they recognized him, while he explained the Bible. Jeremiah described God's word as "a burning fire shut up in my bones" (Jer 20:9).
- **"They rose that same hour and returned to Jerusalem" (v33).** They had urged their guest to stay because it was late. Now they walk seven miles back in the dark.
- **"The Lord has risen indeed, and has appeared to Simon!" (v34).** Before they can tell their story, the others tell theirs. This private appearance to Peter is not described in any Gospel, but Paul lists it first among the resurrection appearances: "he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve" (1 Cor 15:5). The first apostle Jesus met alone was the one who had denied him.
- **"How he was known to them in the breaking of the bread" (v35).** "The breaking of bread" became an early name for the church's shared meal and the Lord's Supper (Acts 2:42, 46; 20:7). Luke may intend the link: the risen Lord is known in the Scriptures and at his table.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "where but two together are well employed in work of that kind, he will come to them, and make a third," and "a golden thread of gospel grace runs through the whole web of the Old Testament." He also says "they had found the preaching powerful, even when they knew not the preacher." JFB: "Ye believe in the glory; but these very sufferings are the predicted gate of entrance into it." On Peter, JFB says "the only one of the Eleven to whom He appeared alone was he... who had so shamefully denied Him." Guzik finds Jesus' question gracious, giving the two men room to voice their hearts, and calls their problem one of heart more than head. He argues from the use of the same verb in Acts 9:36, where it is used of translating a name, that Jesus kept close to the plain sense of the text. The lexicon gives "explain, expound" here and "translate" there, so the verb alone does not settle the method. Guzik also says this meal was not the Lord's Supper itself, while Henry sees a picture of Christ meeting his people at his table.

#### Jesus Appears to His Disciples (vv36–49)
- **"Jesus himself stood among them, and said to them, 'Peace to you!'" (v36).** The same evening (John 20:19). "Peace" (*shalom*) was the ordinary greeting, but on his lips it carried new meaning. These are the men who abandoned him. He greets them with peace, not reproach.
- **"Thought they saw a spirit" (v37).** "Spirit" here means a ghost. Many in that world believed the dead could appear as spirits. The disciples assume they are seeing Jesus' ghost, not Jesus himself in a body.
- **"See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Touch me" (v39).** The nail wounds identify him. John adds the wound in his side (John 20:20, 27). The risen Jesus is the same person who was crucified, not a replacement.
- **"A spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have" (v39).** The resurrection is bodily. He is not a vision or a phantom. His body is real, though changed. He can appear and vanish (vv31, 36), but he can also be touched. Paul calls it a "spiritual body" (1 Cor 15:44), meaning a body fully alive by the Spirit, not a body made of spirit. JFB notes he says "flesh and bones," not "flesh and blood," and links it to 1 Corinthians 15:50.
- **"They still disbelieved for joy" (v41).** It seemed too good to be true. JFB, following Bengel: "They did believe, else they had not rejoiced." Psalm 126:1 describes the same feeling: "we were like those who dream."
- **"He took it and ate before them" (vv42–43).** He eats broiled fish in front of them, a plain proof of a real body. Peter later preaches that the apostles "ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead" (Acts 10:41). Some later manuscripts add "and some honeycomb," which the KJV includes.
- **"Everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms" (v44).** These were the three parts of the Hebrew Bible: the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings, of which Psalms was the first and largest book. Jesus says the whole Old Testament is about him.
- **"He opened their minds to understand the Scriptures" (v45).** He opened the Emmaus disciples' eyes and the Scriptures (vv31–32). Now he opens their minds. Understanding the Bible is his gift.
- **"The Christ should suffer and on the third day rise" (v46).** This is the heart of the gospel, the same message Paul passed on "as of first importance" (1 Cor 15:3–4).
- **"Repentance for the forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations" (v47).** The message is now for everyone. Isaiah foretold that the servant would be "a light for the nations" (Isa 49:6). "In his name" means by his authority and through faith in him. Some manuscripts read "repentance and forgiveness," which means the same.
- **"Beginning from Jerusalem" (v47).** The city that crucified him is the first to hear the offer of forgiveness. Acts follows this plan: Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, and the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8).
- **"You are witnesses of these things" (v48).** A witness tells what he has seen. The word *martys* later came to mean someone who died for that testimony, a "martyr," as many of them did.
- **"I am sending the promise of my Father upon you" (v49).** The promise is the Holy Spirit, foretold by the prophets (Joel 2:28–29; Ezek 36:26–27) and poured out at Pentecost (Acts 2:1–4, 33). "Clothed with power from on high" pictures the Spirit put on like a garment, as Gideon was "clothed" with the Spirit (Judg 6:34). They must wait for him before they go.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls "he opened their minds" "a statement of unspeakable value," showing Christ's direct access to the human spirit, and he says it gives Christ's own sanction to the way the apostles later read the Old Testament. JFB also explains "beginning at Jerusalem" as proclaiming "for all time that there is mercy in Christ for the chief of sinners." Henry: "Even good men need to have their understandings opened." Guzik suggests the wounds remain as marks of identity and trophies of his victory, and that joy itself can make good news hard to believe.

#### The Ascension (vv50–53)
- **"He led them out as far as Bethany" (v50).** Bethany lay on the far slope of the Mount of Olives. Acts 1:12 says they returned "from the mount called Olivet." Luke here moves quickly from Easter to the ascension. Acts tells us forty days passed between them (Acts 1:3). In a two-volume work, Luke compresses the end of volume one and tells it in full at the start of volume two.
- **"Lifting up his hands he blessed them" (v50).** This is a priestly blessing, like Aaron lifting his hands to bless the people (Lev 9:22), or the high priest Simon in Sirach 50:20–21. The Gospel began with the priest Zechariah unable to bless the people (1:21–22). It ends with Jesus, the great high priest, blessing his people.
- **"While he blessed them, he parted from them and was carried up into heaven" (v51).** He leaves in the act of blessing. Elijah was taken up in a whirlwind (2 Kgs 2:11). Jesus is "carried up," a passive verb, taken by God. A few manuscripts lack "and was carried up into heaven," but most include it, and Acts 1:9–11 describes the same event.
- **"They worshiped him" (v52).** This is the first time in Luke that anyone worships Jesus. Only God is to be worshiped (4:8). The disciples now know who he is.
- **"Returned to Jerusalem with great joy" (v52).** The angels announced "good news of great joy" at his birth (2:10). Now the disciples have it. Their Master has left them, and they are full of joy, because he has not left them as before, but as the risen and reigning Lord.
- **"Continually in the temple blessing God" (v53).** The Gospel ends where it began, in the temple (1:9). The first Christians still worshiped there daily (Acts 2:46; 3:1). They are waiting for the promise of the Father, and the book of Acts begins with its arrival.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes Jesus "goes away in benedictions," and that Bethany means not the village itself but the descent toward it from the Mount of Olives. Henry: "He did not go away in displeasure, but in love, he left a blessing behind him," and "as he arose, so he ascended, by his own power." Henry also notes that the ascent began near the garden where his sufferings began: "Those that would go to heaven, must ascend thither from the house of sufferings and sorrows." Guzik says Jesus' blessing is not a wish but carries real power, and that the ascension marks a decisive turn from his earthly ministry to the work of the church.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke's last chapter answers the question his whole Gospel has raised. The crucified man is alive, in a real body, eating fish with his friends. The cross was not the failure of God's plan but its center: "the Christ should suffer and on the third day rise." The risen Lord opens the Scriptures and shows that all of them speak of him, sends his witnesses to all nations beginning with the city that killed him, and goes up into heaven with his hands raised in blessing. The disciples who had hoped he would redeem Israel now know that he has redeemed far more than that.
