*Setting:* Psalm 115 is the third psalm of the "Egyptian Hallel" (Psalms 113–118), and the first of the four that were sung after the Passover meal. Jesus and his disciples very likely sang it in the upper room before going out to Gethsemane (Matt 26:30). It has no title or named author. Its background seems to be a time of national humiliation, when the surrounding nations could mock Israel with "Where is their God?" (v2). Many scholars place it after the return from exile, when Israel was small and weak and had no king, and JFB reads v14 the same way. It is a psalm for public worship with several voices in it. The threefold call to "trust in the LORD" (vv9–11) and the blessing on "the house of Aaron" (vv10, 12) suggest that a leader, a choir or the priests sang parts in turn with the congregation. Much of vv4–11 reappears almost word for word in Psalm 135:15–20. Its theme is the contrast between the living God, who acts and speaks, and the idols of the nations, which can do nothing. In the ancient Greek and Latin Bibles it is joined to Psalm 114 as one psalm. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Not to Us, O LORD, but to Your Name Give Glory (vv1–3)
- **"Not to us, O LORD, not to us" (v1).** The psalm begins by refusing credit, twice. It is a prayer as well as praise: God, act for your people, but not so that we look good. Act so that your name is honored. The Latin of this verse, *Non nobis, Domine*, became a famous hymn of thanksgiving after victories, sung to give the glory to God rather than to the soldiers.
- **"For the sake of your steadfast love and your faithfulness!" (v1).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal, covenant love, the love that keeps its promises. "Faithfulness" is *'emet*, truth or reliability. The pair runs through the Old Testament (Exod 34:6), and John echoes it when he says Jesus was "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14, 17). The grounds of the prayer are not Israel's goodness but God's character.
- **"Why should the nations say, 'Where is their God?'" (v2).** That taunt appears again and again in Scripture (42:3, 10; 79:10; Joel 2:17). The nations could point to their gods: statues in their temples, made of gold and stone, carried in festival processions. Israel had no image at all. Its temple's innermost room held the ark of the covenant and no statue. To the nations that could look like having no God at all, especially when Israel was weak.
- **"Our God is in the heavens; he does all that he pleases" (v3).** The answer is calm. Israel's God cannot be seen because he is not a statue. He rules from heaven. "He does all that he pleases" means he is free, not obliged to act on anyone's timetable, and nothing can stop him (135:6; Dan 4:35; Eph 1:11). His silence is not absence or weakness.
- **Commentators:** Henry takes v1 as a check on pride in prayer: "Let no opinion of our own merits have any place in our prayers or in our praises," for "all the good we do, is done by the power of his grace." JFB says the vindication of God's mercy and faithfulness is itself the glory of his name. Guzik calls "not to us" the motto of a godly life and passes on Morgan's warning that believers often care more about their own welfare than about God's glory. He reads v2 as a skillful prayer: the psalmist asks God to act so the nations cannot conclude he has abandoned his people.

#### Their Idols Are Silver and Gold (vv4–8)
- **"Their idols are silver and gold, the work of human hands" (v4).** The idols were often costly. A wooden core might be covered with gold or silver plate (Isa 40:19; Jer 10:3–5, 9). The psalm grants their value as metal and denies everything else. However precious the material, a craftsman made it. God made the craftsman.
- **Mouths, eyes, ears, noses, hands, feet (vv5–7).** The psalm walks down the idol's body part by part, and the tone is mocking. Ancient temples held "mouth-opening" ceremonies in which priests ritually brought a statue to life so that it could eat, see and hear. The psalm says nothing has happened. The idol has a mouth and is silent, has eyes and is blind, and has a nose and cannot even smell the incense burned in front of it. Isaiah mocks idols in the same way, a man using half a log to cook his dinner and bowing down to the other half (Isa 44:9–20).
- **"They do not make a sound in their throat" (v7).** The verb is *hagah*, which means to make a low sound: a lion's growl, a dove's moan, a murmur. It is the word for "meditates" in Psalm 1:2, where the godly man murmurs God's law to himself. The idol cannot even manage a mumble, while the God of Israel speaks.
- **"Those who make them become like them" (v8).** This is the sharpest line in the psalm. People become like what they worship. Those who trust in something that cannot see, hear or feel grow blind, deaf and unfeeling to God themselves (Isa 6:9–10; Jer 2:5; Rom 1:21–23). The same law works in the other direction: those who look to the Lord "are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Cor 3:18).
- **Idols today.** Most readers will never bow to a statue, but the New Testament widens the word. Paul calls covetousness idolatry (Col 3:5), and John's last word to his readers is "keep yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:21). Anything trusted in place of God, whether money, success or a relationship, can be an idol. Such things promise much and in the end cannot speak, help or save.
- **Commentators:** JFB points to the parallel attacks on idols in Isaiah 40:18–20 and 44:9–20, explains that the word for sound in v7 means to "mutter," so the idols cannot make even an inarticulate noise, and adds that v8 covers everyone who trusts idols, not only those who carve them. Henry calls them "senseless things." Guzik passes on Kidner's comparison to the child who said the emperor had no clothes, and Maclaren's and Meyer's point that the same principle lifts believers who look to Christ into his likeness.

#### O Israel, Trust in the LORD! (vv9–11)
- **Three groups (vv9–11).** The call goes out to "Israel," the whole nation; to "the house of Aaron," the priests descended from Moses' brother; and to "you who fear the LORD." The third group may mean all the faithful, summing up the first two. But many scholars think it refers to Gentiles who worshiped Israel's God without becoming Jews, the people later called "God-fearers" (1 Kings 8:41–43; Isa 56:6–7; Acts 10:2; 13:16, 26). If so, the psalm already makes room for outsiders.
- **"Trust in the LORD!" (vv9–11).** "Trust" is *batach*, to lean on, rely on, feel safe in. It is the same word as v8, "all who trust in them." The question is not whether people will trust, since everyone leans on something, but what they lean on.
- **"He is their help and their shield" (vv9–11).** The refrain repeats three times. It is probably the congregation's or the choir's answer to the leader's call. "Help" is *'ezer*, the word for the woman as Adam's "helper" (Gen 2:18) and for God as Israel's helper (33:20; 121:1–2). It means real, strong help, not mere assistance. A "shield" protects in battle (3:3; 28:7). God was Abraham's shield from the beginning (Gen 15:1).
- **"Their," not "your" (vv9–11).** The shift from command ("trust") to third person ("their help") is natural in Hebrew poetry. It may be the response of another voice in the liturgy, affirming of the group just addressed: he is their help.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the repetitions show earnestness. Guzik says knowing the emptiness of idols should drive people to rely on the true God and quotes Peter's question, "Lord, to whom shall we go?" (John 6:68). He stresses that trust means actively leaning on God, not just agreeing that he can be trusted, and notes that the priests ought to lead the way in it. He reads "you who fear the LORD" as likely including Gentile God-fearers. Henry: "Wherever there is right fear of God, there may be cheerful faith in him."

#### The LORD Has Remembered Us; He Will Bless Us (vv12–15)
- **"The LORD has remembered us; he will bless us" (v12).** The past becomes the ground for the future. God has not forgotten his people, as the nations claimed. When the Bible says God "remembers," it means he acts on what he has promised (Gen 8:1; Exod 2:24). "Bless" appears five times in vv12–15. The three groups of vv9–11 each receive it in turn.
- **"Both the small and the great" (v13).** The "small" come first. No one is too unimportant for God's blessing, and no one is so great that he does not need it. Revelation uses the same phrase for those who fear God's name (Rev 11:18; 19:5).
- **"May the LORD give you increase, you and your children!" (v14).** This is a priestly blessing spoken over the people. If the psalm comes from after the exile, the prayer is pointed: the returned community was small and needed to grow again (Ezra 2:64; Zech 8:4–5). The nations prayed to their gods for children and good harvests. Israel asks the only one who can give them.
- **"Blessed by the LORD, who made heaven and earth!" (v15).** The idols were made. The LORD is the maker. This title, "maker of heaven and earth," closes the contrast with vv4–8, and it is a favorite phrase in the Psalms of Ascents that follow soon after (121:2; 124:8; 134:3).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the "increase" of v14 as "opposed to the decrease pending and during the captivity." Guzik passes on Trapp's line that God has, and therefore God will, and Boice's observation that the small are named first so they will not think themselves forgotten. Henry says it is wisdom to trust the living God, who, unlike dead images, gives real blessing.

#### We Will Bless the LORD (vv16–18)
- **"The heavens are the LORD's heavens, but the earth he has given to the children of man" (v16).** This does not mean God has given up the earth. "The earth is the LORD's" (24:1). It means he has entrusted it to humanity to live in, care for and rule under him, as he told Adam (Gen 1:26–28; Ps 8:6). God reigns in heaven (v3), and the earth is where people serve and praise him.
- **"The dead do not praise the LORD" (v17).** This sounds bleak, and several psalms say similar things (6:5; 30:9; 88:10–12). In much of the Old Testament, the place of the dead, Sheol, is pictured as "silence," where the voice of public worship is no longer heard. The point here is mainly practical: once a person is dead, they can no longer praise God among the living on earth, so the living must do it now. The Old Testament also has glimmers of hope beyond death (16:10–11; 49:15; Dan 12:2), and the New Testament brings that hope into full light through Christ's resurrection (2 Tim 1:10).
- **"But we will bless the LORD from this time forth and forevermore" (v18).** "But we" is emphatic. The dead are silent and the idols are silent, but God's people will not be. "Forevermore" pushes past the limit just set by v17. The same words closed the opening of Psalm 113 (113:2).
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v18).** The psalm ends with *Hallelu-Yah*.
- **Commentators:** JFB says God's people, as the living inhabitants of the earth, are given the task of praising him as monuments of his power, wisdom and goodness, and urges them to fulfill the purpose of their creation by praising him continually. Henry notes that "the dead body cannot praise God," and so believers should praise him while they live, though the souls of the faithful go on praising him after death. Guzik reads v16 as stewardship of the earth and points out the poignancy of Jesus singing "the dead do not praise the LORD" hours before he himself went down into the silence of death.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 115 sets the living God against the idols, which have eyes and do not see and mouths and do not speak. In Christ the invisible God made himself seen and heard. "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (John 1:18). Jesus opened blind eyes, unstopped deaf ears and loosened silent tongues, undoing in people what idolatry had done (Mark 7:37; Isa 35:5–6). On the night he was betrayed he likely sang "not to us, but to your name give glory," and he lived it more fully than anyone: "Father, glorify your name" (John 12:28). He sang that "the dead do not praise the LORD," then went down into death himself and came back out of the silence. Because he rose, "we will bless the LORD… forevermore" is no longer only a hope. It is a promise.
