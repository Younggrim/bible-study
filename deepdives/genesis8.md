*Setting:* For five months the waters have "prevailed" over the earth (7:24). Every land creature outside the ark is dead, and Noah's family and the animals are shut in by God's own hand (7:16), floating on a drowned world. Chapter 8 is the turning point. It begins with "God remembered Noah," which many scholars see as the center of the whole flood story, and it moves step by step from judgment back to life: the wind, the waters going down, the ark coming to rest, the birds sent out, the dry ground, and the family stepping out to start again. It ends with Noah's altar and God's promise to hold the world steady "while the earth remains." The Babylonian flood stories have similar scenes, including birds sent out and a sacrifice at the end, and the differences are telling. In the *Gilgamesh* epic the hungry gods crowd around the sacrifice "like flies." In Genesis the one God, who needs nothing, receives the offering and answers it with a promise.

#### The Flood Subsides (vv1–19)
- **"But God remembered Noah" (v1).** "Remember" (*zakar*) in the Bible doesn't mean that God had forgotten. It means he turns to act on behalf of someone, usually because of a promise. The same word is used when God "remembered his covenant with Abraham" in Egypt (Exod 2:24), when he "remembered" Rachel and Hannah and gave them children (Gen 30:22; 1 Sam 1:19), and in the thief's prayer, "Jesus, remember me" (Luke 23:42). Here God is keeping his word from 6:18. He remembers "all the beasts and all the livestock" too. His care reaches every creature on the ark.
- **"God made a wind blow over the earth" (v1).** "Wind" is *ruach*, the same word as "the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters" (1:2). The story is quietly retelling creation. Then, too, God's *ruach* moved over the waters, the waters were gathered back (1:9), and dry land appeared. The flood undid creation; now God does it again.
- **"The fountains of the deep and the windows of the heavens were closed" (v2).** This reverses 7:11 word for word. What God opened, he shuts. The rain didn't stop by chance.
- **"The ark came to rest on the mountains of Ararat" (v4).** "Came to rest" is *nuach*, the root behind Noah's name, "rest" (5:29). The ark of "Rest" comes to rest. **"Ararat"** is the Hebrew name for the land the Assyrians called Urartu, a mountain kingdom around Lake Van in what is now eastern Turkey, Armenia and northwestern Iran. The text says "mountains of Ararat," a region, not one named peak. The tall volcano now called Mount Ararat got that name much later. Claimed sightings of the ark there have never been confirmed.
- **"In the seventh month, on the seventeenth day" (v4).** Exactly five months after the flood began (7:11), matching the 150 days of 7:24 if months are counted as 30 days. On the first day of the tenth month **"the tops of the mountains were seen"** (v5), just as dry land "appeared" on the third day of creation (1:9).
- **"Noah opened the window of the ark… and sent forth a raven" (vv6–7).** A raven eats carrion and can live on floating debris, so it "went to and fro" and never needed to come back. It would later be listed as unclean (Lev 11:15). Ancient sailors were known to release birds to find land. In *Gilgamesh*, the flood hero sends out a dove, a swallow and a raven. Genesis shares the custom but tells it with its own details.
- **"The dove found no place to set her foot" (v9).** "Place to set her foot" is literally "a resting place" (*manoach*), another word from the root of Noah's name. The dove finds no *rest* outside, so she comes back to the man named Rest. **"He put out his hand and took her and brought her into the ark"** is a tender detail. Noah has been silent the whole story, but here we see him gently gathering in a tired bird.
- **"In her mouth was a freshly plucked olive leaf" (v11).** Olive trees don't grow on high peaks, so a fresh leaf meant the lower slopes were above water and plants were growing again. Life was coming back. Since then the dove with an olive branch has become a worldwide sign of peace, but in the text it is first a sign of new life after judgment. At Jesus' baptism the Spirit descends "like a dove" as he comes up out of the water (Matt 3:16), and many Christian writers have seen the two scenes as linked.
- **"She did not return to him anymore" (v12).** The waiting has its own rhythm: forty days, then seven, then seven again. Noah doesn't rush. He watches for signs and waits.
- **"The waters were dried… Noah removed the covering of the ark and looked" (v13).** The date is the first day of the first month of Noah's 601st year: a new year for a new world. "Removed the covering" probably means lifting part of the roof to look out. Even when he could see the dry ground, Noah stayed in the ark nearly two more months, until the earth was fully dry (v14) and God told him to go.
- **"In the second month, on the twenty-seventh day… the earth had dried out" (v14).** The flood began on the 17th day of the second month of Noah's 600th year (7:11), so Noah's family was in the ark a year and ten days. "Dried out" (*yabesh*) is related to the word for "dry land" at creation (1:9) and at the Red Sea (Exod 14:22).
- **"Go out from the ark" (v16).** The command matches "Go into the ark" (7:1). Noah went in at God's word, and he comes out at God's word, not at the dove's.
- **"That they may swarm on the earth, and be fruitful and multiply" (v17).** These are the blessing words of creation (1:22, 28). The animals go out **"by families"** (v19), in order, as they went in.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "God remembered Noah" is "an expression after the manner of men," and that it marks "the return of his mercy to mankind." He adds, "Those that remember God shall certainly be remembered by him." He makes the birds a picture of two kinds of people: "The carnal heart, like the raven, takes up with the world, and feeds on the carrions it finds there," while "The dove is an emblem of a gracious soul," which finds no rest in the world and returns "to Christ as to its ark." JFB notes the raven would stay away because "the smell of carrion would allure it," while the dove is "a bird flying low and naturally disposed to return." JFB thinks the dove brought the olive leaf "no doubt by supernatural impulse." The text doesn't say so. Guzik also reads "remembered" as God turning his active care back to Noah, not as a lapse of memory. He observes that the mountains of Ararat would have been a hard place to leave but a good place to keep the ark out of sight.

#### God's Covenant with Noah (vv20–22)
- **"Noah built an altar to the LORD" (v20).** This is the first altar in the Bible, and it is the first thing Noah does in the new world. No command to build it is recorded. Noah's obedience has turned into worship. "Burnt offerings" (*olot*, from a verb meaning "go up") were burned whole on the altar, so all of it went up to God (Lev 1). He offered **"some of every clean animal and some of every clean bird,"** using the extra clean animals God had told him to bring (7:2). This cost something. Each animal offered came from a small surviving stock.
- **"The LORD smelled the pleasing aroma" (v21).** "Pleasing aroma" is *reach nichoach*, the standard phrase in Leviticus for an offering God accepts (Lev 1:9). *Nichoach* means "soothing" or "quieting," and it sounds like Noah's name and "rest" again. Henry translates it "a savour of rest." The language is a human way of describing God's pleasure. Unlike the gods of Babylon, the LORD isn't fed by the sacrifice. He is pleased by the worship of a grateful man.
- **"The LORD said in his heart" (v21).** This echoes 6:6, where God was "grieved… to his heart." God's grief over the world now gives way to a settled resolve to preserve it.
- **"I will never again curse the ground because of man" (v21).** The verb here (*qalal*) is different from the one in 3:17, and it is the same verb used for the waters "subsiding" in vv8 and 11. The curse of 3:17 is not removed; thorns and death are still here. God promises not to add another curse like the flood. Lamech hoped Noah would bring "relief" from the cursed ground (5:29), and this is a first, partial answer.
- **"For the intention of man's heart is evil from his youth" (v21).** This is nearly the same diagnosis as 6:5, the reason God sent the flood. Now it is the reason he promises not to send another. The flood didn't change the human heart, and God knows it. So the world's future will rest on his patience, not on human goodness. The word "for" here can also be read "even though," as in some translations. Either way, God's mercy goes on in spite of human sin. Paul later speaks of the "forbearance" that "passed over former sins" until God dealt with them fully in Christ (Rom 3:25).
- **"While the earth remains, seedtime and harvest… day and night, shall not cease" (v22).** The promise comes in four pairs, a poem of steady rhythm. The days and seasons of 1:14, broken up by the flood, are restored. "While the earth remains" is a limit, too: this world won't last forever. Jeremiah points to this regular order as proof that God will keep his covenant with David (Jer 33:20–21). Every sunrise and every harvest is a sign that God is keeping his word.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Noah "begins with an altar for God: God, that is the first, must be first served," and "Those that have received mercy from God should be forward in returning thanks." He calls v22 "God's covenant of the day and of the night." JFB describes the altar as "a mound of earth, on which a sacrifice was offered," and says the offering "was acceptable as the most fragrant incense." Guzik stresses the cost of Noah's offering and links it to David, who refused to offer God what cost him nothing (2 Sam 24:24), and to the sacrifices believers now bring (Rom 12:1; Heb 13:15). Guzik suggests the seasons of v22 reflect a change in climate after the flood, linked to the water canopy he sees in 1:6–7, and that many extinctions came soon after. These are his own theories. The text simply promises that the seasons will go on.

#### Pointing to Christ
Lamech named his son "Rest" in hope that he would bring relief from the curse, and the whole chapter is full of that word: the ark comes to *rest*, the dove looks for a *resting* place and returns to Noah's hand, and God smells the *soothing* aroma of Noah's offering. But Noah could offer only animals, and the human heart stayed "evil from its youth." Paul writes that Christ "loved us and gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2). His sacrifice deals with the heart that the flood couldn't change. In him the weary find the resting place the dove found in Noah's hand: "Come to me… and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).
