*Setting:* The land has been divided among the tribes (19:51). Before the last piece of the settlement, the towns for the Levites (ch 21), God reminds Joshua of a command he had given through Moses: set apart cities of refuge. The law is given in Exodus 21:12–14, Numbers 35:9–34 and Deuteronomy 19:1–13. Moses had already named three cities east of the Jordan (Deut 4:41–43). Now that Israel has homes and towns of its own, the three west of the Jordan are named, and the system is put in place. It is a short chapter about a serious problem: what happens when someone kills another person by accident, in a world where the dead man's family is expected to take a life for a life.

#### The Cities of Refuge (vv1–9)
- **"Appoint the cities of refuge, of which I spoke to you through Moses" (v2).** The command is not new. What is new is that it can finally be carried out. Matthew Henry notes the timing: "As soon as ever God had given them cities of rest, he bade them appoint cities of refuge." A settled people would need a settled system of justice. JFB points out that unlike the sanctuaries of other ancient nations, these were "not temples or altars" but ordinary towns where people lived.
- **"The manslayer who strikes any person without intent or unknowingly" (v3).** Two phrases describe an accidental killing. "Without intent" is *bishgagah*, "by mistake" or "in error," the same word the law uses for unintentional sins in Leviticus 4 and Numbers 15. "Unknowingly" is literally "without knowledge." Deuteronomy 19:5 gives the classic example: two men chopping wood, and the axe head flies off the handle and kills one of them. The refuge was never for murderers. Numbers 35:16–21 and Deuteronomy 19:11–13 say plainly that a man who killed out of hatred or by lying in wait must be handed over and put to death.
- **"A refuge from the avenger of blood" (v3).** The "avenger" is the *go'el*, the nearest male relative of the dead person. The same word is used for the "kinsman-redeemer" who bought back family land or freed a relative from slavery (Lev 25:25, 48–49), the role Boaz plays for Ruth (Ruth 3:9–13). And God himself is called Israel's *Go'el*, "your Redeemer, the Holy One of Israel" (Isa 41:14), as Job says, "I know that my Redeemer lives" (Job 19:25). The *go'el* stood up for the family: he restored what was lost, and he answered for blood that was shed. In a land with no police force, this was how justice was done. God's law did not abolish the custom, which rested on the principle of Genesis 9:6, but put limits on it so that innocent blood would not be shed in revenge (Deut 19:10).
- **"Stand at the entrance of the gate of the city and explain his case to the elders" (v4).** The city gate was the town's courtroom and meeting place, where elders sat to judge (Ruth 4:1–2). The fugitive gives his account, and the elders take him in "and give him a place." He is not just tolerated. He becomes a resident.
- **"They shall not give up the manslayer into his hand" (v5).** The city must protect him against the avenger, "because he struck his neighbor unknowingly, and did not hate him in the past." The test of an accident was the past relationship: was there a grudge? Deuteronomy 19:4, 6 uses the same phrase.
- **"Until he has stood before the congregation for judgment" (v6).** The elders' first hearing gave temporary shelter. Then came a proper trial "before the congregation," probably back near the town where the death happened (Num 35:24–25). If he was found guilty of murder, he was handed over. If the killing was found to be accidental, he was returned to the city of refuge.
- **"Until the death of him who is high priest at the time" (v6).** The strangest part of the law. The manslayer had to stay inside the city, and if he left, the avenger could kill him without guilt (Num 35:26–28). Only when the high priest died was he free to go home. Why? Even an accidental killing left blood on the land (Num 35:33), and the manslayer's time in the city was a kind of exile. The death of the high priest, the man anointed to stand between Israel and God, closed the case for everyone. Numbers 35:25 notes that the high priest was "anointed with the holy oil." Many Christians from the early church on have seen this as a picture of Christ, the great high priest, whose death sets the guilty free.
- **A shorter Greek text.** The Greek Septuagint lacks most of vv4–6, including the elders at the gate and the high priest's death. The Hebrew text, which the ESV follows, includes them. Either way the same rules stand in Numbers 35 and Deuteronomy 19.
- **"So they set apart Kedesh… Shechem… Kiriath-arba (that is, Hebron)" (v7).** "Set apart" is *qadash*, "made holy" or "consecrated." These three cities stand in the north, center and south of the land west of the Jordan, each in its hill country: Kedesh in Naphtali in Galilee, Shechem in Ephraim, and Hebron in Judah. Deuteronomy 19:3 required that the land be divided into three parts and that the roads to these cities be kept in good repair, so that a man running for his life could get there. Jewish tradition later added that signposts marked "Refuge" stood at crossroads.
- **"Bezer… Ramoth in Gilead… Golan in Bashan" (v8).** The three cities east of the Jordan that Moses had chosen (Deut 4:41–43): Bezer in Reuben's tableland in the south, Ramoth in Gad's Gilead in the middle, and Golan in Manasseh's Bashan in the north. Ramoth-gilead later became a frontier fortress fought over by Israel and Aram, where King Ahab was killed (1 Kings 22). The modern Golan Heights take their name from Golan.
- **Levitical cities.** All six were also Levitical cities (21:13, 21, 27, 32, 36, 38). The Levites, who taught God's law, were the right people to protect those whose lives hung on careful judgment. Henry notes that the fugitive, cut off from going up to the tabernacle, would at least live among "the servants of God's house."
- **"For all the people of Israel and for the stranger sojourning among them" (v9).** The protection was not just for Israelites. A foreigner living in Israel had the same right of refuge (Num 35:15). God's law consistently required one standard of justice for native and foreigner (Lev 24:22).
- **The names of the cities.** Henry, while saying he does not "delight in quibbling upon names," gives their meanings: Kedesh, "holy"; Shechem, "shoulder"; Hebron, "fellowship"; Bezer, "fortification"; Ramoth, "heights"; Golan, which he takes as "joy." The first five are fair meanings of the Hebrew. The meaning of Golan is uncertain. The names make a pleasant meditation, but they were not chosen for that purpose. These were simply towns well placed across the land.
- **Commentators:** Henry argues that God would not have given so much attention to these cities in the law if they were not meant to picture "the relief which the gospel provides for poor penitent sinners," in Christ, "to whom believers flee for refuge (Heb. 6:18)." He notes that the cities were on hills, "to be seen afar off," and so placed that one could be reached from any corner of the land quickly: "God is a refuge at hand." JFB calls the institution "an important provision, tending to secure the ends of justice as well as of mercy," and says the high priest's death "became a type of that of the heavenly one (Heb 9:14, 15)." Guzik lists ways the cities picture Christ: they were within easy reach, open to all, a place to stay in rather than visit, the only safety from the avenger, and freedom came through a high priest's death. He then marks the key difference: the cities protected only the innocent, but in Christ even the guilty find refuge. The Tyndale notes point out that from anywhere in the land a person could reach one of these cities in about two days.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews draws on the language of refuge when it says that "we who have fled for refuge" have "strong encouragement to hold fast to the hope set before us" (Heb 6:18). The cities of refuge sheltered people whose hands were guilty of blood but whose hearts were not. The gospel goes further. Every sinner has a real debt, and the true Avenger is God's own justice. Yet God himself has become the Redeemer, the *Go'el*, who both answers for the blood and sets the guilty free. The manslayer in the city waited for the high priest to die. Jesus, the great high priest, has entered the holy places once for all "by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption" (Heb 9:12). Those who run to him are not just sheltered until a trial. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Rom 8:1).
