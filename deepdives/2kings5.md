*Setting:* About 850–845 BC, during the reign of Jehoram (Joram) son of Ahab over Israel, and probably Ben-hadad over Syria (Aram). Israel and Syria were enemies, fighting in pitched battles (1 Kgs 20; 22) and in smaller raids across the northern border. Elisha, the successor of Elijah, has become well known. The previous chapter showed him caring for widows, a farming family and hungry prophets. This chapter shows God's mercy reaching even further, to a foreign general, an enemy of Israel, who comes home believing in the LORD. And it shows the opposite: an Israelite, the prophet's own servant, who goes home a leper. Jesus later pointed to this story to show that God's grace has always reached outsiders (Luke 4:27).

#### Naaman Healed of Leprosy (vv1–14)
- **"Naaman, commander of the army of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master" (v1).** Naaman was Syria's top general, the king's right-hand man, wealthy and honored. His name means "pleasant" or "gracious." A Jewish legend, passed on by Trapp and cited by Guzik, made him the archer whose random arrow killed Ahab (1 Kgs 22:34). The text does not say so.
- **"Because by him the LORD had given victory to Syria" (v1).** This is a remarkable statement. The LORD gave victory to Israel's enemy. The God of Israel is not a local god. He rules the nations and their battles, even when they do not know him (compare Isa 10:5; 45:1–5; Amos 9:7).
- **"He was a mighty man of valor, but he was a leper" (v1).** In Hebrew the last phrase is one word, *metsora*, "leprous," at the very end of the sentence, after all his titles. Every honor comes with a "but." The Hebrew word for this disease, *tsara'at*, covered a range of serious skin diseases, not only the condition doctors call leprosy today (Hansen's disease). The law also used it for mold in houses and fabric (Lev 13–14). Naaman could still serve at court, so it was probably not yet advanced, or Syria did not isolate the sick as Israel's law required (Lev 13:45–46). Either way, it was disfiguring, feared and, as far as anyone knew, incurable. Henry: "There is many a sickly, crazy body under rich and gay clothing."
- **"The Syrians on one of their raids had carried off a little girl from the land of Israel" (v2).** "Raids" were bands of raiders who struck border villages, taking livestock and people. A little Israelite girl, torn from her family, is now a slave in a Syrian general's house. She is never named.
- **"Would that my lord were with the prophet who is in Samaria! He would cure him" (v3).** She could have been bitter. Instead she wishes her master well and points him to the one place help can be found. She speaks with total confidence. There is no record of Elisha curing leprosy before this (Luke 4:27 says no Israelite leper was cleansed in his day), so her faith rests on what she knows of God and his prophet. Henry: "Servants may be blessings to the families where they are, by telling what they know of the glory of God."
- **"I will send a letter to the king of Israel" (v5).** The king of Syria assumes a prophet works for the king, so he goes through official channels. Naaman takes an enormous payment: **"ten talents of silver, six thousand shekels of gold, and ten changes of clothing."** A talent was about 75 pounds, so ten talents was around 750 pounds of silver, and the gold was about 150 pounds. Fine clothing was a form of wealth too. It is a king's ransom.
- **"That you may cure him of his leprosy" (v6).** The letter is blunt and, from Israel's side, alarming.
- **"Am I God, to kill and to make alive...?" (v7).** Jehoram is right that only God can do this. The words echo Moses' song: "I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal" (Deut 32:39). But he does not think to send for God's prophet. He tears his clothes in panic and assumes it is a trap: **"he is seeking a quarrel with me."** If the king cannot cure him, Syria has a pretext for war. JFB reads it as "alarm and suspicion that this was merely made an occasion for a quarrel." The king of Israel has no idea there is a God in Israel who can help. The question of chapter 1, "Is there no God in Israel?", hangs over him too.
- **"Let him come now to me, that he may know that there is a prophet in Israel" (v8).** Elisha's message to the king carries a rebuke. The king of Israel acted as if there was no prophet. A foreign general will learn otherwise.
- **"Naaman came with his horses and chariots and stood at the door of Elisha's house" (v9).** The scene is almost comic: a general's entire procession parked outside a prophet's front door.
- **"Elisha sent a messenger to him" (v10).** Elisha does not even come out. It is a deliberate humbling. Naaman is used to being treated as the great man. Here, he is a leper at the door, like any other. **"Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and your flesh shall be restored, and you shall be clean."** "Clean" is the word for ritual purity in the law of Moses, used of lepers healed and declared clean (Lev 14:7–9). Seven is the number of completeness. The law of cleansing for leprosy included sprinkling seven times (Lev 14:7). JFB: "Seven was the symbol of the covenant."
- **"I thought that he would surely come out to me and stand and call upon the name of the LORD his God, and wave his hand over the place" (v11).** Naaman had pictured the whole scene in advance: the prophet coming out, a public prayer, a dramatic gesture over the diseased skin. He wanted a ceremony fit for his rank. Henry: "See in Naaman the folly of pride; a cure will not content him, unless he be cured with pomp and parade."
- **"Are not Abana and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel?" (v12).** Damascus sits in a fertile oasis watered by rivers flowing from the snows of Mount Hermon. The Abana is usually identified with the Barada, which runs through the city, and the Pharpar with the Awaj to the south. They were clear and cold. The Jordan, by contrast, is a muddy, winding river in a deep valley. To Naaman, washing in it is not just pointless but humiliating. **"He turned and went away in a rage."**
- **"My father, it is a great word the prophet has spoken to you" (v13).** His servants are wiser than he is. "My father" is an affectionate, respectful address. Their argument is simple: if he had told you to do something hard, you would have done it. Why not something easy? The difficulty was not the task but Naaman's pride. Henry: "we are to have our ears open to good advice, though brought by those below us." Twice in this story, Naaman's help comes from servants.
- **"He went down and dipped himself seven times in the Jordan" (v14).** "Went down" is literal, down from the hills to the river, and fitting, since he must go down in every sense. "Dipped" (*taval*) means to plunge or immerse. It is the word used in the Greek translation with *baptizō*, the word later used for baptism. He does it **"according to the word of the man of God."** The power is not in the river but in obeying God's word.
- **"His flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child, and he was clean" (v14).** The great general, the "great man" (v1), now has skin like a "little child," the same words used of the "little girl" in v2 (*na'arah qetannah*, and here *na'ar qaton*). The mighty man is made like the slave girl who pointed him to God. Jesus said, "Unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 18:3).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that "mighty man of valor" is a title given to heroes like Gideon and David, and sees the captive girl as an unwilling missionary God used despite her loss. He passes on Dilday's estimate that Naaman's gifts would be worth over a million dollars today. On Naaman's anger, Guzik says the problem was his expectations: he wanted something grand and was offended by something simple. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's image of Naaman having to defeat two enemies within, pride and arguing. JFB does not know which modern rivers Abana and Pharpar were ("uncertain which"). The Tyndale notes contrast the king of Syria's turn to Israel's prophet with Ahaziah's turn to the god of Ekron in chapter 1.

#### Gehazi's Greed and Punishment (vv15–27)
- **"He returned to the man of God, he and all his company" (v15).** He has to go back up the Jordan Valley, perhaps 25 miles or more. Like the one Samaritan leper who came back to thank Jesus (Luke 17:15–18), the foreigner returns. This time he comes in and **"stood before him."** This time Elisha sees him.
- **"Behold, I know that there is no God in all the earth but in Israel" (v15).** It is one of the great confessions of the Old Testament, from a pagan. He does not say Israel's God is the strongest of the gods. He says there is no other. Compare Rahab (Josh 2:11) and Nebuchadnezzar (Dan 4:34–37). **"Accept now a present from your servant."** The man who came demanding a cure now calls himself "your servant," five times in four verses.
- **"As the LORD lives, before whom I stand, I will receive none" (v16).** Elisha uses Elijah's oath again (1 Kgs 17:1). He refuses because God's grace cannot be paid for. Naaman must go home knowing he was healed for nothing, by the LORD's free kindness. The prophets often did accept gifts (1 Sam 9:7–8; 2 Kgs 4:42), so this is not a general rule. In this case, accepting would have made it look as though the healing could be bought.
- **"Two mule loads of earth" (v17).** People in the ancient world commonly believed each god was tied to his own land. Naaman wants Israelite soil to build an altar in Syria, so he can worship the LORD on the LORD's own ground. It is a new believer's mixed understanding, but his heart is clear: **"from now on your servant will not offer burnt offering or sacrifice to any god but the LORD."** JFB lists several possible motives and concludes "it is not easy to say" which one it was.
- **"When my master goes into the house of Rimmon to worship there, leaning on my arm" (v18).** Rimmon was the storm god of Damascus, known in Assyrian texts as Ramman or Hadad-Rimmon, "the thunderer" (Zech 12:11). The Hebrew spelling *Rimmon* also means "pomegranate," which may be a deliberate Israelite pun mocking the god's name, as with Baal-zebub ("lord of flies") in 1:2. As the king's chief officer, Naaman had to accompany him in state ceremonies. "Leaning on my arm" means serving as the king's chief aide (compare 7:2, 17). When the king bowed, Naaman would have to bow too. He asks the LORD's pardon in advance.
- **"Go in peace" (v19).** Elisha neither approves nor condemns. He sends him off with a blessing. Interpreters have long debated this. Some read Elisha as quietly allowing Naaman's outward duty, as long as his heart worshiped only the LORD. Others note that "go in peace" is simply a farewell and does not answer the question, leaving Naaman's conscience in God's hands. Some, including Adam Clarke and Trapp, have suggested Naaman was asking pardon for past idolatry, but the verbs point to the future. JFB says Elisha gives "simply... the parting benediction" without passing judgment. What is clear is that Naaman's conscience is now tender. Before, bowing to Rimmon was nothing. Now it troubles him. Henry: "Those who truly hate evil, will make conscience of abstaining from all appearances of evil."
- **"My master has spared this Naaman the Syrian" (v20).** Gehazi's words give him away. "This Naaman the Syrian": the contempt of an Israelite for a foreigner. Elisha's refusal looks to him like wasted opportunity. **"As the LORD lives, I will run after him."** He swears by the LORD to do something sinful, the same oath Elisha used to refuse the gift (v16), now used to grab it.
- **"He got down from the chariot to meet him" (v21).** The general, who had expected the prophet to come out to him (v11), now gets down from his chariot to meet the prophet's servant. The change in Naaman is complete. **"Is all well?"** (literally *shalom*?). "All is well," Gehazi lies (v22).
- **"Two young men of the sons of the prophets... a talent of silver and two changes of clothing" (v22).** Gehazi's story is clever: a request for others, not himself, in Elisha's name, and a modest sum compared with what Naaman brought. Naaman gladly gives twice as much, **"two talents"** (v23), about 150 pounds of silver, so heavy it takes two servants to carry.
- **"When he came to the hill, he took them from their hand and put them in the house" (v24).** "The hill" (*ophel*) was probably a raised, fortified part of the city, perhaps Samaria. Gehazi sends Naaman's servants back before they reach Elisha's house, and hides the silver.
- **"Where have you been, Gehazi?" "Your servant went nowhere" (v25).** A second lie, to his master's face. Like God's question to Adam, "Where are you?" (Gen 3:9), it gives him the chance to confess.
- **"Did not my heart go when the man turned from his chariot to meet you?" (v26).** Elisha saw it all in his spirit. **"Was it a time to accept money and garments, olive orchards and vineyards, sheep and oxen, male servants and female servants?"** Elisha lists more than Gehazi took, perhaps because he knows what Gehazi planned to buy with it: land, livestock, servants, a comfortable life. At a moment when God was showing a pagan his free grace, Gehazi made it look as if the prophet's God could be paid. He undid the witness of the whole miracle.
- **"The leprosy of Naaman shall cling to you and to your descendants forever" (v27).** "Cling" (*dabaq*) is the word used of Jehoram clinging to Jeroboam's sin (3:3). Gehazi took Naaman's silver, and he gets Naaman's leprosy with it. Henry: "If he will have Naaman's money, he shall have his disease with it." **"So he went out from his presence a leper, like snow."** "Like snow" describes the white, flaking skin of the disease (Exod 4:6; Num 12:10). The foreigner went home clean. The Israelite servant went out unclean. JFB calls the punishment "not too severe for the crime of Gehazi," which combined greed, lying and misusing the prophet's name. Gehazi appears again in 8:4–5, speaking to the king about Elisha's miracles, so the story there may come from before this, or he may have been allowed some contact despite his disease.
- **Commentators:** Guzik compares Naaman's return to the one thankful leper in Luke 17, and reads Elisha's refusal as protecting the free grace of God, not as a rule against supporting ministers. He sees the earth request as a new believer's mixed understanding. On "leaning on my arm," he passes on Wiseman's note that it means the king's chief aide, not literal support. On Gehazi, Guzik passes on Morgan's point that his greed spoiled the free witness of the captive girl and of Elisha, and Dilday's contrast: a foreigner healed by faith, an Israelite cursed by dishonesty. JFB reads Rimmon as a Syrian deity, "probably the sun, or the planetary system," but most modern scholars, like the Tyndale notes, identify him with the storm god Hadad.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus preached his first sermon in Nazareth on this story: "There were many lepers in Israel in the time of the prophet Elisha, and none of them was cleansed, but only Naaman the Syrian" (Luke 4:27). The crowd was so angry they tried to throw him off a cliff. The point they hated is the heart of the gospel: God's grace goes to outsiders and enemies, and it is free. Naaman had to stop trying to pay, stop expecting a ceremony fit for his rank, go down and wash as he was told, and become like a little child. That is how anyone comes to Christ. Jesus did for lepers what Elisha could only send word about: he touched them and said, "Be clean" (Mark 1:41). And in Gehazi's punishment there is a dark mirror of the cross. Gehazi took on another man's leprosy by greed. Jesus took on our uncleanness by love, "made... to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Cor 5:21).
