*Setting:* Genesis 1 told the story of creation from heaven's point of view: God speaking, the world taking shape in six days, and humanity, male and female, made in his image. Chapter 2 opens with the seventh day, the climax of that week, and then steps down to ground level. The camera narrows from the cosmos to one garden, one man, and the woman made for him. The name for God changes too, from "God" (*Elohim*) to "the LORD God" (*Yahweh Elohim*), the personal covenant name Israel would come to know at the burning bush (Exod 3:14–15). For Israel, hearing this after the exodus, the chapter answers questions they lived with every day: why they rested every seventh day, why work is good, what marriage is, and what life with God was meant to be before anything went wrong.

#### The Seventh Day, God Rests (vv1–3)
- **"The heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them" (v1).** "Host" usually means an army. Here it is everything that fills the heavens and earth: stars, birds, fish, animals and people. The work is complete, nothing is missing, and nothing needs fixing.
- **"He rested on the seventh day" (v2).** "Rested" is *shabath*, the root of "Sabbath." It means to stop or cease, not to recover from tiredness. God "does not faint or grow weary" (Isa 40:28). In the ancient world a god "rested" when he took his seat in a finished temple to rule. Many scholars read Genesis this way: God, having built his cosmic house, takes his throne in it (Ps 132:7–8, 13–14; Isa 66:1). Both ideas fit, completion and enthronement.
- **"God blessed the seventh day and made it holy" (v3).** This is the first thing in the Bible God calls holy. It isn't a place or a person but a time. God blessed animals (1:22), humans (1:28) and now a day.
- **No "evening and morning."** Every other day closes with "there was evening and there was morning." The seventh does not. Many see a hint that God's rest is open-ended and that he invites people into it. Hebrews 4:1–11 builds on exactly this verse: "there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God."
- **The Sabbath for Israel.** The fourth commandment grounds the Sabbath in this passage (Exod 20:8–11), and Deuteronomy adds the exodus (Deut 5:15). Israel's neighbors had unlucky days, but no weekly day of rest for everyone, including slaves, foreigners and animals. Christians differ on how the Sabbath applies now. Some see the Lord's Day as the Christian Sabbath. Others see the Sabbath fulfilled in Christ, with a weekly rest still wise (Col 2:16–17; Rom 14:5–6).
- **Commentators:** JFB says, "The institution of the Sabbath is as old as creation," and that God "ceased from working" rather than needing rest. Henry sees the Sabbath "made for man, to further his holiness and comfort." Guzik takes the missing "evening and morning" as a pointer to the eternal rest believers find in Jesus. He holds that Christians are not bound to keep the Sabbath law, since Christ fulfilled it, but that God made people to need a regular day of rest.

#### The Creation of Man and Woman (vv4–25)
- **"These are the generations of the heavens and the earth" (v4).** "Generations" is *toledoth*, "what came from." The phrase comes about ten times in Genesis (5:1; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10, 27; 25:12, 19; 36:1; 37:2) and marks the book's main sections. Each time it introduces what came *out of* something: here, what came out of the heavens and the earth, namely humanity and its story.
- **"The LORD God" (v4).** This is the first time the covenant name *Yahweh* (printed "LORD" in small capitals) appears. It's joined with *Elohim* about twenty times in chapters 2–3 and rarely elsewhere. The mighty Creator of chapter 1 is also the personal God who forms, plants, breathes and talks.
- **One account or two?** Many scholars see two creation accounts here from two sources, since chapter 2 tells events in a different order. Most traditional and evangelical readers, including the Tyndale notes, see chapter 2 as a close-up on day six, not a second creation story. Chapter 1 is the panorama, chapter 2 the zoom lens.
- **"No bush of the field… no small plant of the field" (v5).** Many read this as describing cultivated land, crops and fields, rather than all plant life. What's missing is rain and "a man to work the ground." The world is waiting for its gardener. Work is part of the design, not a result of sin.
- **"A mist was going up from the land" (v6).** The Hebrew word (*ed*) is rare. It may mean a mist or, as the Tyndale notes suggest, springs welling up from below. Some, like Guzik, use the verse to picture a world without rain before the flood, though the text says only that it hadn't rained *yet*.
- **"The LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground" (v7).** "Formed" (*yatsar*) is a potter's word (Jer 18:1–6; Isa 64:8). There's a wordplay: *adam* ("man") from the *adamah* ("ground"). We might say "a human from the humus." It is humbling and dignifying at once. We are dust, and we return to dust (3:19; Ps 103:14). But God himself shaped us with his hands.
- **"Breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (v7).** Chapter 1 had God speak creation into being. Here he stoops and breathes, face to face. "A living creature" (*nephesh chayyah*) is the same phrase used of animals in 1:20–24, so the phrase itself doesn't set humans apart. What sets them apart is how they come to life: God's own breath and his personal, intimate act. The KJV's "living soul" can mislead modern readers. *Nephesh* here means a whole living person, not a soul inside a body.
- **"A garden in Eden, in the east" (v8).** Eden is the region and the garden is in it. *Eden* sounds like the Hebrew for "delight," and the Greek translation calls it *paradeisos*, "paradise," a Persian word for a walled park. Jesus promised the thief, "today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43).
- **"Every tree that is pleasant to the sight and good for food" (v9).** God's world is both useful and beautiful. In the middle stand two trees:
  - **The tree of life**, which gives unending life (3:22). It reappears in Proverbs (3:18) and at the end of the Bible (Rev 22:2).
  - **The tree of the knowledge of good and evil.** Readers have long debated what this means. Most likely it is the power to decide for oneself what is good and evil, wisdom grabbed rather than received from God (3:5, 22). "Good and evil" can also mean "everything," in which case it means knowing everything, which only God does.
- **The four rivers (vv10–14).** One river flows out of Eden and splits into four "heads." The **Tigris** and **Euphrates** are well known and rise in the mountains of eastern Turkey. The **Pishon** and **Gihon** can't be identified with certainty. Havilah may be in Arabia, and Cush is usually Ethiopia, though here it may be a region in Mesopotamia. Proposals put Eden in eastern Turkey, at the head of the Persian Gulf, or elsewhere. Some, like Guzik, think the flood changed the landscape so much that the site can't be found. The point is that life flows out from God's garden to the whole earth. The same picture appears at the temple in Ezekiel 47 and in the river of life in Revelation 22.
- **Gold, bdellium and onyx (vv11–12).** These are odd details for a story, unless they're there on purpose. Gold covered the tabernacle and its furniture. Onyx stones were set on the high priest's shoulders with Israel's tribes engraved on them (Exod 28:9–12). Bdellium (a fragrant resin) is later compared to manna (Num 11:7). Many see Eden described as the first sanctuary, the place where God lived with people.
- **"To work it and keep it" (v15).** The two verbs, *abad* ("work, serve") and *shamar* ("keep, guard"), appear together later for the Levites' service at the tabernacle (Num 3:7–8; 8:26). Adam is a gardener and something like a priest. Work is part of paradise, not a punishment, and "keep" hints that the garden needs guarding.
- **"You may surely eat of every tree… but…" (vv16–17).** God's first words to a human being are generous: *every* tree, with one exception. The serpent will reverse the emphasis (3:1). The one command gives Adam a real choice to trust and obey.
- **"In the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (v17).** Adam and Eve didn't drop dead the day they ate. Readers explain this in several ways:
  - They died **spiritually** that day, cut off from God (Eph 2:1).
  - They began to die **physically** that day, mortal from then on (5:5).
  - "In the day" means simply "when," as in 2:4, and the sentence was certain even if it was carried out later.
  - Many combine these. Exile from the tree of life (3:22–24) makes death sure.
- **"It is not good that the man should be alone" (v18).** After seven "goods" in chapter 1, here is the first "not good." Nothing is wrong yet, and nothing is sinful. Creation is simply not finished. People are made for relationship.
- **"A helper fit for him" (v18).** "Helper" is *ezer*. In the Old Testament it most often describes God himself: "The God of my father was my help" (Exod 18:4), "My help comes from the LORD" (Ps 121:2), "He is our help and our shield" (Ps 33:20). So the word doesn't mean an assistant or a lesser person. "Fit for him" (*kenegdo*) literally means "as opposite him" or "corresponding to him," like a matching half. Christians differ on what this means for roles in marriage and the church. Complementarians see an ordered partnership, and egalitarians see full mutual partnership. Both agree the woman is equal in dignity and bears God's image fully (1:27).
- **Naming the animals (vv19–20).** God brings the animals "to see what he would call them," and whatever Adam called them stuck. In the ancient world naming was an act of authority, so Adam exercises the dominion of 1:28. The parade also shows Adam what he lacks: **"But for Adam there was not found a helper fit for him."** None of the animals is like him.
- **"A deep sleep… took one of his ribs" (v21).** "Deep sleep" (*tardemah*) is the same word for the sleep God sends on Abram before the covenant (15:12). God works while the man can only receive. "Rib" is *tsela*, which elsewhere almost always means "side," as in the sides of the ark or the tabernacle (Exod 25:12; 26:20). Some think God took part of Adam's side, not just a bone. The ESV, like most translations, keeps "rib."
- **"He made into a woman and brought her to the man" (v22).** "Made" is literally "built" (*banah*). God builds the woman, and then, like a father at a wedding, brings her to the man. This is the first wedding, and God gives the bride away.
- **"This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (v23).** These are the first recorded human words in the Bible, and they're a poem of joy. "At last!" after all those animals. "Bone of my bones" became an idiom for close kinship (29:14; 2 Sam 5:1).
- **"She shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man" (v23).** In Hebrew it's a sound-play: *ishshah* from *ish*. English happens to keep it: woman from man. Scholars think the two words probably come from different roots, but the point is the sound and the bond, not the dictionary. JFB renders it "man-ess."
- **"Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife" (v24).** The narrator steps back and draws a lesson for every generation. In a culture where a bride usually joined her husband's family, the man is told to *leave*: his first loyalty now belongs to his wife. "Hold fast" (*dabaq*) means to cling or stick. Ruth "clung" to Naomi (Ruth 1:14), and Israel is told to "hold fast" to the LORD (Deut 10:20). It is covenant loyalty, not just affection.
- **"They shall become one flesh" (v24).** This is a union of whole lives, including the sexual union. Jesus quotes this verse to teach that marriage is God's doing and should not be broken: "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Matt 19:4–6). Paul quotes it for sexual purity (1 Cor 6:16) and for Christ and the church (Eph 5:31–32).
- **"Both naked and were not ashamed" (v25).** Nothing to hide, from God or each other. "Naked" (*arummim*) sounds almost the same as "crafty" (*arum*) in the very next verse (3:1), describing the serpent. The storyteller sets up the fall with a wordplay. After it, nakedness will mean shame and exposure (3:7, 10).
- **Commentators:** Henry's most famous line comes from v21: the woman was "not made out of his head to rule over him, nor out of his feet to be trampled upon by him, but out of his side to be equal with him, under his arm to be protected, and near his heart to be beloved." He calls this "a marriage made in perfect innocency," adds "These two are one flesh; let them then be one soul," and says of v25, "Blushing is now the colour of virtue, but it was not then the colour of innocency." JFB reads "breath of life" as "literally, of lives, not only animal but spiritual life." It calls the tree of life "a sign and seal of immortal life" and says the garden placed Adam "on his probation." It doesn't comment on the rivers. Guzik notes that work belonged to Adam's perfect state and that a real command gave real freedom to love God by choice. He sees "helper" as a role of service and partnership, not inferiority, and calls Adam's sleep the first surgery with anesthesia. He also dismisses the folk belief that men have one fewer rib. The Tyndale notes see the garden as a garden-temple. They point out that Israel's neighbors told no story like this one of God's care for human companionship.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul calls Adam "a type of the one who was to come" (Rom 5:14), and this chapter shows why. A man is put in a garden to keep it and fails. Another man would pray in a garden (John 18:1) and stay faithful. God put the first Adam into a deep sleep and opened his side to make his bride. Christ, the last Adam, fell into the sleep of death, and from his pierced side came blood and water (John 19:34), and through that, his bride, the church (Eph 5:25–32). The rest God entered on the seventh day is the rest Jesus offers: "Come to me… and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28). And the garden where God lived with people, with a river and the tree of life, is where the Bible ends, opened again by the Lamb (Rev 22:1–2).
