*Setting:* This is the last chapter of the Song. Chapter 7 ended with the bride inviting her husband to the countryside and offering him "all choice fruits, new as well as old" (7:13). Chapter 8 gathers up the book's threads. Her refrain to the daughters of Jerusalem sounds a third and last time (v4; 2:7; 3:5). A voice asks "Who is that coming up from the wilderness?" as in 3:6. Then comes the Song's high point, the great statement about love in vv6–7, the only lines in the book that step back and say what love *is*. After that, short scenes tie off loose ends: her brothers' care for her when she was young (vv8–10), a riddle about Solomon's vineyard (vv11–12), and a parting exchange that leaves the lovers still longing (vv13–14). For the main ways the Song is read, see the Setting of chapter 1. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song. It labels the speakers, and the headings below follow those exchanges.

#### Oh That You Were Like a Brother (vv1–4)
- **"Oh that you were like a brother to me… If I found you outside, I would kiss you, and none would despise me" (v1).** In that culture a woman could kiss a brother in public, but not a husband or lover. The Tyndale notes explain the custom. She is not wishing he were literally her sibling. She wishes she could show her love openly, anywhere, without raising eyebrows. "Who nursed at my mother's breasts" stresses the closest kind of family tie, a full brother.
- **"I would lead you and bring you into the house of my mother — she who used to teach me" (v2).** This echoes 3:4, where she brought him "into my mother's house." Bringing him home means bringing him into the family's circle, an open, approved love, not a hidden one. Guzik draws that point. The Hebrew can also be read "and she will teach me," and Carr, cited by Guzik, notes the verb's usual sense is "teach." The ESV takes it as her mother's past instruction.
- **"I would give you spiced wine to drink, the juice of my pomegranate" (v2).** Spiced wine was the finest drink. The pomegranate has been a picture of her own beauty (4:3, 13; 6:7). She offers him her best, and herself.
- **"His left hand is under my head, and his right hand embraces me!" (v3).** Word for word from 2:6. What was then longing is now hers.
- **"I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (v4).** This is the third time she says it (2:7; 3:5). The Hebrew here is shorter and stronger, with no gazelles and does: literally "Why would you stir up…?" The Song ends its teaching to young women where it began: love is too powerful to rush. Let it wake in its own time, within the commitment that can hold it. The Tyndale notes read it as a charge to keep sexual love for marriage.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the refrain two ways, as letting a relationship mature before it bears fruit, and as not rushing to physical intimacy. He passes on Morgan's remark that love is too sacred to be trifled with. Henry reads v1 as the believer's wish for closer communion with Christ and v4 as a charge "not to interrupt her communion with her beloved." JFB reads v1 as the church after the ascension, no longer able to hold Jesus physically (John 20:17) and longing for his return, when the embrace of v3 will be renewed.

#### Love Is Strong as Death (vv5–7)
- **"Who is that coming up from the wilderness, leaning on her beloved?" (v5).** In 3:6 the same question was asked of Solomon's wedding procession, glorious with smoke and spices and sixty warriors. Now there is no procession. There is only a woman, walking with her husband and leaning on him. The splendor of the wedding has given way to the closeness of a marriage. The speaker is probably the chorus or her family. Guzik notes that "leaning" here is closeness, not weakness, and passes on Spurgeon's use of the picture for the church's dependence on Christ.
- **"Under the apple tree I awakened you. There your mother was in labor with you" (v5).** In the Hebrew the pronouns are masculine, so she is speaking to him. She recalls where their love first stirred, near the place he was born. The apple tree has been a picture of him (2:3) and of fruitfulness. The mention of his mother giving birth hints that love leads to new life, and that the love they share is part of the line of generations. Henry, reading it spiritually, says of the wilderness: "A sinful state is a wilderness, remote from communion with God, barren and dry."
- **"Set me as a seal upon your heart, as a seal upon your arm" (v6).** A seal was a carved stone, often set in a ring or hung on a cord around the neck, pressed into wax or clay as a signature. It marked ownership and was precious to its owner (Gen 38:18; Jer 22:24; Hag 2:23). She asks to be his seal, worn on his heart and his arm. The Tyndale notes put it this way: privately on his heart, publicly on his arm. It is a plea for a love that is permanent, exclusive and visible. Israel's high priest carried the names of the tribes engraved like seals on his shoulders and over his heart (Exod 28:9–12, 21, 29), and JFB draws that link.
- **"For love is strong as death, jealousy is fierce as the grave" (v6).** Death is the one power no one escapes. Love, she says, is as strong as that, and holds on just as tightly. "Jealousy" (*qin'ah*) here is not petty suspicion. It is the burning, protective zeal that will not share a beloved with a rival, the same word used of God's jealousy for his people (Exod 20:5; Nah 1:2; Zech 1:14). "The grave" is *Sheol*, the realm of the dead, which never lets go of what it takes (Prov 30:15–16). "Fierce" means hard or unyielding.
- **"Its flashes are flashes of fire, the very flame of the LORD" (v6).** "Flashes" (*reshef*) is a word for flames or sparks. Resheph was also the name of a Canaanite god of plague and lightning, and the word is used of fiery bolts (Ps 78:48; Hab 3:5). The last phrase is one Hebrew word, *shalhebetyah*, "flame" plus the ending *-yah*, a short form of the LORD's name. The lexicon reads it as "Yahweh-flame," that is, a most powerful flame. Hebrew often uses God's name this way to mean "mighty" (the Hittites call Abraham "a prince of God," that is, a mighty prince, Gen 23:6). So translators differ. The ESV and ASV give "the very flame of the LORD." The NIV and others give "a mighty flame." If the ESV is right, this is the one place God is named in the Song: love at its truest is a fire that he lit. If it is only a superlative, the Song still says love is the strongest flame there is.
- **"Many waters cannot quench love, neither can floods drown it" (v7).** Fire and water are opposites, and water wins. But not against love. In the Old Testament "many waters" are the chaos and danger that threaten to overwhelm (Ps 18:16; 93:3–4; Isa 43:2). Love outlasts them.
- **"If a man offered for love all the wealth of his house, he would be utterly despised" (v7).** Love cannot be bought. Anyone who tries is not just refused but scorned. This leads straight into the vineyard riddle of vv11–12, where even King Solomon's wealth meets something it cannot purchase.
- **Commentators:** Guzik follows Carr in reading "flame of the LORD" as a superlative, not the divine name, while noting the versions that take it as God's name. He passes on Glickman's line that sex can be bought but love can only be given. Henry: "Love is a violent vigorous passion," and "Many waters, though they will quench fire, cannot quench this love." He sums up with "Love sat king upon the floods," an echo of Psalm 29:10. JFB takes the flame as "the fire-flame of Jehovah," the only mention of God's name in the Song, and reads the waters as persecutions that could not put out the love of the martyrs.

#### We Have a Little Sister (vv8–10)
- **"We have a little sister, and she has no breasts" (v8).** New speakers, labeled "Others" in the ESV. Most readers take them to be her brothers, the same brothers who once put her to work in the vineyards (1:6). They are remembering, or she is quoting them, from when she was a young girl, not yet grown. "On the day when she is spoken for" means when a man asks for her in marriage. In that culture brothers shared responsibility for a sister's protection and her marriage (Gen 24:50–55; 34:13–14).
- **"If she is a wall, we will build on her a battlement of silver, but if she is a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar" (v9).** A wall keeps people out. A door lets them in. If she proves steady and chaste, they will honor her: a silver battlement is decoration and reward. If she is open to every advance, they will board her up and guard her more closely. Guzik passes on Glickman's summary: give responsibility when it can be handled, limit it when it can't.
- **"I was a wall, and my breasts were like towers" (v10).** She answers them, now grown. She kept herself for marriage. "Towers" gently corrects "she has no breasts": she is a woman now, not the little girl they planned for. The Tyndale notes and Guzik both read this as her purity before marriage.
- **"Then I was in his eyes as one who finds peace" (v10).** "Peace" is *shalom*. It is a play on her name, the Shulammite (6:13), and on Solomon's (*shelomoh*). The phrase is like the familiar "find favor in his eyes" (Gen 6:8), but with *shalom* in place of grace. Because she was a wall, she came to her husband whole and found rest with him.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the little sister as the Gentile church, not yet mature in faith and love, and the "what shall we do" as the early church's discussion of how to receive Gentiles (Acts 15). It also prefers "peace" to the KJV's "favour," and links it to the bride of the Prince of Peace. Henry sees the bride interceding for others, "that care might be taken of them." Guzik gives the allegorical readings of Trapp and Poole, but keeps to the plain lesson about families protecting the young.

#### Solomon Had a Vineyard (vv11–12)
- **"Solomon had a vineyard at Baal-hamon… each one was to bring for its fruit a thousand pieces of silver" (v11).** Baal-hamon is not named anywhere else in the Bible, and its location is unknown. The name means "lord of a multitude" or "owner of wealth," which may be the point: it is Solomon's estate of plenty. Like any wealthy owner, he rented it to tenant farmers, who paid a thousand shekels each from the harvest. Isaiah 5:1–7 and Jesus' parable of the tenants (Mark 12:1–9) use the same kind of arrangement. Isaiah speaks of a vineyard with "a thousand vines, worth a thousand pieces of silver" (Isa 7:23).
- **"My vineyard, my very own, is before me" (v12).** In 1:6 she said, "my own vineyard I have not kept." There "vineyard" meant herself. Here it does again. Solomon has a huge estate he can lease out for profit. She has one vineyard, herself, and it is hers to give.
- **"You, O Solomon, may have the thousand, and the keepers of the fruit two hundred" (v12).** Most modern readers hear this as the point of v7 in story form. Solomon can keep his wealth, and his workers their wages. Love is not for sale. She is not one more property in his portfolio, and she gives herself freely. The Tyndale notes give this view, along with a dramatic reading in which she settles a vineyard lease. Some who read the Song as a three-person drama hear her refusing Solomon for her shepherd. Others hear her giving her own vineyard to Solomon as her husband. The "two hundred" is a fifth of the thousand, perhaps the keepers' fair share. The details are hard to pin down across this distance, as Kinlaw, cited by Guzik, admits.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the vineyard as the bride's sense of her own worth: having guarded herself, she was free to give herself wholly. Henry, reading it spiritually, has the church acknowledge herself "his tenant for a vineyard she held of him at Baal-hamon," owing the fruit to Christ. JFB, reading the vineyard as Christ's, says it is "mine" by grace, not by a lease earned through works.

#### Make Haste, My Beloved (vv13–14)
- **"O you who dwell in the gardens, with companions listening for your voice; let me hear it" (v13).** His last words in the Song. He has spoken of her as a garden (4:12–16), and now she lives among gardens with her friends around her. He wants to hear her voice, just as at the start of their love: "let me hear your voice, for your voice is sweet" (2:14).
- **"Make haste, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or a young stag on the mountains of spices" (v14).** Her last words. "Make haste" is literally "flee" or "run." She calls him to come to her swiftly, like the gazelle bounding over the hills in 2:8–9, 17. "Mountains of spices" recalls 4:6, the "mountain of myrrh" that is herself. The Song does not end with "they lived happily ever after." It ends with longing, the lovers still reaching for each other. Married love, it says, is not a finished achievement but a desire that keeps calling.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ charges his spouse that she should often let him hear from her," and in the last verse "she begs of him that he would hasten his return to her." JFB, like Henry, hears the church's prayer for the Second Coming and notes that the Song ends unfinished, waiting for the Savior. Guzik keeps the primary sense of a marriage still full of passion, then passes on Spurgeon's question: does the church long for Christ's return like this?

#### Pointing to Christ
"Set me as a seal upon your heart" (v6). The Song's highest lines describe a love stronger than death, which no flood can drown and no money can buy. One love has proved it. On the cross Christ's love went down into death itself, and death could not hold it (Acts 2:24). Many waters did not quench it. And it cannot be bought, because it was given: "you were bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:20), but the price was paid by him, not by us. Paul's version of v6–7 is Romans 8: neither "death nor life… nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Rom 8:38–39). The Song ends with the bride crying "Make haste, my beloved" (v14). The Bible ends almost the same way. The bride, the church, says "Come," and her Bridegroom answers, "Surely I am coming soon." "Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!" (Rev 22:17, 20).
