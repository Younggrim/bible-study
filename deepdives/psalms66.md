*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Song. A Psalm." For the first time since Psalm 50, no author is named, and no occasion is given. The psalm celebrates a national rescue after a time of severe testing, and it looks back to the exodus as the pattern for every later deliverance. Some scholars connect it with the night the Assyrian army of Sennacherib was struck down outside Jerusalem in Hezekiah's day (2 Kgs 19:35–37), after Hezekiah had spread the Assyrian king's letter before the LORD in the temple and prayed (2 Kgs 19:14–19), but that is only a suggestion. Henry thinks it is meant for general use. Its shape is clear. It begins with all the earth (vv1–4), moves to Israel's history (vv5–7), then to the nation's recent trial (vv8–12), and then, surprisingly, to one person keeping his vows and telling what God has done for his soul (vv13–20). The Tyndale notes see two hymns, one public and one private, that mirror each other. Cresswell, cited by Guzik, notes that the Greek church has long read this psalm at Easter, as a song of resurrection and of the nations coming to God.

#### How Awesome Are Your Deeds! (vv1–4)
- **"Shout for joy to God, all the earth" (v1).** The call goes far beyond Israel. The verb (*rua'*) is the shout of a crowd greeting a king or a battle cry of victory (47:1; 1 Sam 10:24). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says a joyful noise matters more to God than a loud one. Henry sees the verse as "a prediction of the conversion of the Gentiles."
- **"Sing the glory of his name; give to him glorious praise!" (v2).** God's "name" is his revealed character, all that he has shown himself to be (Exod 34:5–7). The Tyndale notes: "the glory of his name represents the perfection of God's character." "Give to him glorious praise" is literally "make glorious his praise" or "set glory as his praise." JFB: let his praise be "such as will glorify Him." Tate, cited by Guzik through Boice, notes that praise centers on God himself, while thanks centers on what we have received.
- **"Say to God, 'How awesome are your deeds!'" (v3).** The psalm even provides the words. "Awesome" (*nora'*) is the same word as in 65:5.
- **"So great is your power that your enemies come cringing to you" (v3).** "Come cringing" is the verb *kachash*, which means to lie, deceive or feign. The King James has "submit themselves," with a margin note "lie." It describes enemies who pretend to submit because they are afraid, not because they love God. Henry: "they shall lie unto thee… Subjection extorted by fear is seldom sincere, and therefore force is no proper means of propagating religion." The same verb appears in 18:44 ("foreigners came cringing to me") and Deuteronomy 33:29. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, makes the link to 18:44. JFB: "a forced subjection… produced by terror." One day every knee will bow, some in joy and some by force (Phil 2:10–11), a passage Guzik connects.
- **"All the earth worships you and sings praises to you… Selah" (v4).** What began as a command (v1) ends as a confident statement of what will be. Henry: "the everlasting gospel shall be preached to every nation," and he points to Revelation 14:6–7.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives four things the call to "all lands" shows: God's worthiness of universal praise, man's duty, the coming conversion of the nations, and the psalmist's own goodwill toward the work. He says we fail to praise God well because "we do not duly and attentively observe" his works. Guzik notes the feigned submission of v3 and contrasts it with real worship that knows God's name.

#### Come and See What God Has Done (vv5–7)
- **"Come and see what God has done" (v5).** An invitation to look at the evidence. Psalm 46:8 uses the same words. "He is awesome in his deeds toward the children of man" means his acts in history are visible to all people, not hidden.
- **"He turned the sea into dry land" (v6).** The crossing of the Red Sea, the great act of rescue that made Israel a nation (Exod 14:21–22). JFB: by this example "let rebels be admonished."
- **"They passed through the river on foot" (v6).** "River" (*nahar*) most naturally means the Jordan, which God held back when Israel entered the land forty years later (Josh 3:14–17). Henry and Guzik both take it that way. The two crossings frame the exodus journey: out of Egypt through the sea, into Canaan through the river. Some take "river" as poetic parallel for the sea in the same line.
- **"There did we rejoice in him" (v6).** "We," as if the singers had been there themselves. Guzik notes that the psalmist speaks as though he stood on the shore. Henry: "our ancestors did, and we in their loins. The joys of our fathers were our joys." For Israel, the exodus was not just past history. Each generation was told to remember it as its own story (Deut 6:20–23). Maclaren, cited by Guzik, says God's works are never out of date.
- **"Who rules by his might forever, whose eyes keep watch on the nations" (v7).** The God who acted then still rules now. The Tyndale notes point out that "might" is the same word used for God's power in creation in 65:6. Henry: God has "a commanding eye" and "a commanding arm."
- **"Let not the rebellious exalt themselves. Selah" (v7).** A warning to the nations invited in v1. The same God who drowned Pharaoh's army watches them. Henry compares Adonijah, who "exalted himself, saying, 'I will be king'" (1 Kgs 1:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry says God's works "are comfortable and beneficial to his people" and "formidable to his enemies." He adds that at the sea "the enemies tremble before them… but there did we rejoice in him" (Exod 15:14–16; Josh 5:1). JFB reads "behold the nations" as God watching their conduct.

#### You Have Tried Us as Silver Is Tried (vv8–12)
- **"Bless our God, O peoples" (v8).** Again the nations are called in. What God has done for Israel is meant to bring the world to worship. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes that Israel's story has worldwide reach.
- **"Who has kept our soul among the living and has not let our feet slip" (v9).** Literally "who has put our soul in life." JFB: "out of danger." The nation had come close to death. Henry: "his providence is a continued creation."
- **"For you, O God, have tested us; you have tried us as silver is tried" (v10).** Silver ore was heated in a crucible until the impurities (dross) rose to the surface and could be skimmed off, leaving pure metal. Testing was God's purpose, not his punishment. The Tyndale notes: as the silversmith heats silver to remove the dross, God's discipline removes sin. Proverbs 17:3 says, "The crucible is for silver… and the LORD tests hearts." Malachi pictures God "as a refiner and purifier of silver" (Mal 3:3), and Peter speaks of faith "more precious than gold that perishes though it is tested by fire" (1 Pet 1:7).
- **"You brought us into the net" (v11).** Caught like a bird or a fish, unable to escape. Notice who is the subject: "You brought us." The psalm does not blame chance or the enemy. Henry: "We are never in the net but God brings us into it."
- **"You laid a crushing burden on our backs" (v11).** Literally "on our loins," the hips and lower back, the seat of strength. JFB: "pressure… laid on the loins… enfeebles the frame." It is the picture of a slave or an animal bent under a load.
- **"You let men ride over our heads" (v12).** Probably the picture of a conquering army driving its horses and chariots over fallen enemies, or of victors using captives as beasts. Poole, cited by Guzik, reads it as being treated like slaves or animals. The Tyndale notes offer another possible reading: "you put a leader over us," a foreign ruler who kept Israel in submission.
- **"We went through fire and through water" (v12).** The two great dangers, every kind of trouble. Isaiah gives God's promise: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you… when you walk through fire you shall not be burned" (Isa 43:2). Henry: "the end of one trouble was the beginning of another."
- **"Yet you have brought us out to a place of abundance" (v12).** "Abundance" (*revayah*) means saturation, overflowing, the word in Psalm 23:5: "my cup overflows." JFB: "literally, 'overflowing,' or, 'irrigated,' and hence fertile." The whole testing had a destination. The God who led them into the net led them out into abundance, just as he led Israel through the sea and wilderness into a land flowing with milk and honey.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives thanks for two things here: "common protection" (v9) and "special deliverance from great distress" (vv10–12). JFB links the trials to Isaiah 48:10 ("I have tried you in the furnace of affliction") and 1 Peter 1:7. Meyer, cited by Guzik, says the purpose of life is to become, not just to do, which is what testing is for. Horne, cited by Guzik, turns it into a prayer that God would try his people so that they come out purer.

#### I Will Perform My Vows to You (vv13–15)
- **"I will come into your house with burnt offerings" (v13).** Suddenly "we" becomes "I." One worshiper steps forward. He may be the king or leader who speaks for the nation, or the psalm may give each worshiper words to make the national story personal. Kidner, cited by Guzik, pictures one man standing at the altar with his gifts. A burnt offering (*'olah*, "that which goes up") was burned entirely on the altar, nothing kept back (Lev 1). Boice, cited by Guzik, says it signified full dedication to God.
- **"I will perform my vows to you, that which my lips uttered and my mouth promised when I was in trouble" (vv13–14).** In trouble, people make promises to God. When the trouble is over, they are tempted to forget them. Ecclesiastes warns, "When you vow a vow to God, do not delay paying it" (Eccl 5:4–5). Jacob vowed at Bethel and was later called back to keep it (Gen 28:20–22; 35:1). Morgan, cited by Guzik, says keeping vows guards character.
- **"Burnt offerings of fattened animals, with the smoke of the sacrifice of rams… bulls and goats. Selah" (v15).** An unusually lavish list: fattened animals, rams, bulls and goats, the best and most costly. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, notes how unusual the generosity is. JFB explains "smoke" (*qetoret*), usually the word for incense, as here meaning the rising fumes of the burnt offering. The worshiper gives freely because he was rescued freely. The Tyndale notes say vows often included thanksgiving sacrifices like these (20:3; 50:8).
- **Commentators:** JFB: "These full and varied offerings constitute the payment of vows" (Lev 22:18–23). JFB also notes that "I will offer" is literally "make to ascend," the smoke going up to God.

#### Come and Hear, All You Who Fear God (vv16–20)
- **"Come and hear, all you who fear God" (v16).** The pattern of v5 ("Come and see") repeats, but now it is "Come and hear," and the audience is narrower: those who fear God. What God did for the nation, the whole world can see. What God did for one soul is told to fellow believers. The Tyndale notes observe that this private testimony matches the public praise of vv1–4.
- **"I will tell what he has done for my soul" (v16).** This is the heart of personal testimony. Not what I did for God, but what God did for me. The man healed of demons was told, "Go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:19).
- **"I cried to him with my mouth, and high praise was on my tongue" (v17).** Literally "exaltation was under my tongue." JFB explains that praise was stored there, ready to come out: "honoring God was habitual." Even while crying for help, he was praising.
- **"If I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened" (v18).** Literally "if I had seen iniquity in my heart," meaning looked on it with approval, held on to it, kept it as a favorite. JFB: "see iniquity with pleasure." The verse does not say God hears only sinless people, or no one would be heard. It says God does not hear the prayers of someone who clings to sin he refuses to give up. Isaiah 59:2: "your iniquities have made a separation between you and your God." Proverbs 28:9: "If one turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination." The Tyndale notes point to 32:5: confession is what restores. Poole, cited by Guzik, thinks especially of idolatry. Horne, cited by Guzik, says it is the penitent whose prayer is heard.
- **"But truly God has listened; he has attended to the voice of my prayer" (v19).** God heard. That is the proof that the psalmist's heart was set right with God.
- **"Blessed be God, because he has not rejected my prayer or removed his steadfast love from me!" (v20).** A beautiful ending. Thomas Fuller, cited by Guzik, notices the logic. Verse 18 and 19 set up a syllogism: if I cherished sin, God would not hear; God did hear; so the expected conclusion is "therefore I am innocent." But the psalmist does not conclude that. He concludes, "Blessed be God… his steadfast love." Fuller said he would rather learn that logic than Aristotle's, and Trapp agreed. The answer to prayer is not a reward for being good. It comes from God's *chesed*, his steadfast love. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes that the prayer and the love go together: an unbroken relationship.
- **Commentators:** JFB sums up vv16–20: the psalmist "vindicates his sincerity, inasmuch as God would not hear hypocrites, but had heard him." Guzik stresses that obedience matters more than sacrifice alone (1 Sam 15:22), and that the generous offerings of vv13–15 would mean nothing from a heart holding on to sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 66 tells the story of a people brought through the sea and the river, tested by fire and water, caught in the net, crushed and ridden over, and finally brought out into abundance. That is the shape of the gospel. Jesus went through fire and water for his people. He was caught in the net of his enemies, crushed under the burden of our sin (Isa 53:5–6), and passed through death itself. On the third day God brought him out, and Christians have sung this psalm at Easter for that reason. He offered the burnt offering that leaves nothing behind, giving his whole self to God (Heb 10:5–10), and because his prayer was heard (Heb 5:7), the prayers of everyone who belongs to him are heard too. And now the invitation goes out to "all the earth": come and see what God has done, and come and hear what he has done for my soul.
