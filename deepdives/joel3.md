*Setting:* Joel has moved from a locust plague (ch 1), to a call to repent before the day of the LORD, to God's promise of restored harvests and of his Spirit poured out on all his people (ch 2). The last verse of chapter 2 promised that "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved," with survivors on Mount Zion. Chapter 3 (chapter 4 in Hebrew Bibles) turns to the other side of the day of the LORD: judgment on the nations that have harmed God's people, and final blessing for Zion. The scene moves from a courtroom, to a battlefield, to a harvest, and ends with water flowing from God's house. Many of its images reappear in the later prophets and in Revelation. Readers differ on how much refers to events soon after Joel's time, such as the fall of Tyre or Edom, and how much to the end of history. Joel himself sets both inside "the day of the LORD," God's decisive action to put the world right.

#### The LORD Judges the Nations (vv1–16)
- **"In those days and at that time, when I restore the fortunes of Judah and Jerusalem" (v1).** "Those days" links back to the outpouring of the Spirit in 2:28–29. "Restore the fortunes" (*shuv shevut*) is an idiom for turning a situation completely around. The KJV renders it "bring again the captivity," and JFB explains it as "reverse it." It is used for the return from exile (Jer 29:14) and for Job's restoration (Job 42:10). Guzik takes "those days" as the whole era of the last days that began at Pentecost, with a final fulfillment at its end.
- **"I will gather all the nations and bring them down to the Valley of Jehoshaphat" (v2).** "Jehoshaphat" means "the LORD judges," and the next words make the pun: "I will enter into judgment (*nishpatti*) with them there." The Tyndale notes say this is a symbolic name, "the valley where the LORD judges," with no connection to King Jehoshaphat. Since at least the fourth century AD, tradition has placed it in the Kidron Valley between the temple mount and the Mount of Olives, and JFB follows that. That is why the slopes of the Mount of Olives are covered with Jewish, Christian and Muslim graves, of people who hoped to rise there on judgment day. Some note that Jehoshaphat's great deliverance took place in the "Valley of Beracah" (2 Chr 20:26), but the name is more likely chosen for its meaning.
- **"On behalf of my people and my heritage Israel" (v2).** God takes what is done to his people personally. "Whoever touches you touches the apple of his eye" (Zech 2:8). Jesus said the same to Saul: "why are you persecuting *me*?" (Acts 9:4), and said nations will be judged by how they treated "the least of these my brothers" (Matt 25:31–46).
- **"They have scattered them among the nations and have divided up my land" (v2).** Three charges follow: exile, theft of the land and the slave trade. "My land" again: the land was God's (Lev 25:23), so dividing it was theft from God.
- **"Cast lots for my people… traded a boy for a prostitute, and have sold a girl for wine" (v3).** Casting lots was how soldiers shared out captives (Obad 11; Nah 3:10). The rest describes contempt: a boy handed over as payment for one night with a prostitute, a girl sold for the price of a drink. Human beings made in God's image were treated as small change. JFB explains that they "gave her a Jewish captive boy as a slave" instead of money.
- **"What are you to me, O Tyre and Sidon, and all the regions of Philistia?" (v4).** Tyre and Sidon were the great Phoenician trading ports on the coast of modern Lebanon. Philistia was the coastal strip to the south, around Gaza. "Are you paying me back for something?" God asks. Do they think they are settling a score with him? If so, "I will return your payment on your own head swiftly and speedily."
- **"You have taken my silver and my gold… into your temples" (v5).** Treasures from Jerusalem, perhaps from the temple, ended up as offerings in pagan shrines. "My silver and my gold" are God's (Hag 2:8).
- **"You have sold the people of Judah and Jerusalem to the Greeks" (v6).** The Hebrew is "sons of Javan," the Ionian Greeks of the Aegean coast (Gen 10:2–4). Phoenicians were the great middlemen of the ancient slave trade (Ezek 27:13; Amos 1:6, 9). The purpose, "to remove them far from their own border," was to make sure they never came home. This verse is one reason some scholars date Joel late, but Greek trading contacts with the Levant go back well before the exile, so it does not settle the question.
- **"I will sell your sons and your daughters into the hand of the people of Judah… to the Sabeans" (vv7–8).** The Sabeans were the people of Sheba, in southwest Arabia (modern Yemen), famous traders (1 Kings 10; Job 1:15). The Greeks were far to the west and the Sabeans far to the southeast: the punishment exactly mirrors the crime. History recorded something like it. In 332 BC Alexander the Great took Tyre after a long siege and sold about 30,000 of its people as slaves, and Gaza suffered the same fate the same year. "For the LORD has spoken" seals it.
- **"Proclaim this among the nations: Consecrate for war" (v9).** Literally "sanctify a war." Ancient armies began campaigns with religious rites (1 Sam 7:9; Jer 6:4). The words echo Joel's own calls to "consecrate a fast" (1:14; 2:15), but now it is the nations who gather, and for battle. God is challenging them to bring everything they have.
- **"Beat your plowshares into swords, and your pruning hooks into spears" (v10).** Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3 give the famous reverse: "they shall beat their swords into plowshares." Joel's version is the call to the final war, and Isaiah's and Micah's describe the peace that follows God's judgment. Both belong to the same story. Farm tools could be reforged as weapons, and in this war every tool is needed. **"Let the weak say, 'I am a warrior.'"** Even the feeble talk themselves into strength. JFB: even the weak "will fancy themselves strong enough." The irony is that no human strength counts on this day.
- **"Bring down your warriors, O LORD" (v11).** In the middle of the summons to the nations, the prophet turns and prays. The nations bring their armies, and Joel asks God to bring his. God's "warriors" are probably his angelic host (Zech 14:5; Matt 25:31; Rev 19:14).
- **"For there I will sit to judge all the surrounding nations" (v12).** A king or judge sat to give judgment (Ps 9:7–8; Dan 7:9–10). The battle turns out to be a courtroom, and God is the Judge, not a combatant.
- **"Put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe… tread, for the winepress is full" (v13).** Two harvest pictures: cutting ripe grain, and treading grapes until the juice overflows. "For their evil is great" explains them. Evil, too, ripens to a harvest (Gen 15:16). Jesus used harvest for the end of the age (Matt 13:39–43). Revelation 14:14–20 draws directly on this verse: one like a son of man with a sharp sickle, and "the great winepress of the wrath of God." Isaiah 63:1–6 pictures the LORD treading the winepress alone.
- **"Multitudes, multitudes, in the valley of decision!" (v14).** The repeated word (*hamonim hamonim*) sounds like the roar of a crowd. "Decision" (*harutz*) means a decision or verdict, from a verb meaning to cut or decide. The same word can also mean a threshing sledge (Isa 41:15), which fits the harvest imagery. Preachers often use "valley of decision" to urge listeners to decide for Christ, and Guzik notes that this is not what Joel means. In Joel, the decision is God's. The valley is where the verdict on the nations is given.
- **"The sun and the moon are darkened, and the stars withdraw their shining" (v15).** The same line as 2:10. Creation itself goes dark before the Judge.
- **"The LORD roars from Zion, and utters his voice from Jerusalem" (v16).** God roars like a lion (Hos 11:10; Jer 25:30). Amos 1:2 opens with exactly these words, so the next book in the Hebrew order of the Twelve picks up where Joel leaves off. "The heavens and the earth quake," as at Sinai (Exod 19:18; Heb 12:26).
- **"But the LORD is a refuge to his people, a stronghold to the people of Israel" (v16).** The same roar that terrifies the nations is shelter for God's people. The lion who judges is the fortress who protects (Ps 46:1).
- **Commentators:** JFB gives the meaning of the name, "the judgment of Jehovah," and the traditional location "between Jerusalem and the Mount of Olives." On v6 it identifies the Greeks as "Javanites, that is, the Ionians," and on v8 places the Sabeans at "the most remote extremity of Arabia Felix." It explains "prepare war" as "sanctify war," and calls the valley of decision the place where the nations meet their "determined doom." Guzik also reads the valley as symbolic, quoting Adam Clarke, and stresses that God takes the persecution of his people personally. He quotes Trapp, who lists persecuting Roman emperors who came to miserable ends, and he warns against using "valley of decision" for evangelistic appeals. Henry says that "None ever hardened his heart against God or his church, and prospered long," and on the day of judgment, "There is no escaping God's judgments." He adds: "To the wicked it will be a terrible day, but to the righteous it will be a joyful day."

#### The Glorious Future of Judah (vv17–21)
- **"So you shall know that I am the LORD your God, who dwells in Zion, my holy mountain" (v17).** This repeats the promise of 2:27, "you shall know that I am in the midst of Israel." The goal of all God's acts, in judgment and in blessing, is that his people know him. **"Strangers shall never again pass through it"** means no more invading armies trampling the holy city. Revelation's new Jerusalem has the same promise: "nothing unclean will ever enter it" (Rev 21:27).
- **"The mountains shall drip sweet wine, and the hills shall flow with milk" (v18).** This reverses chapter 1 completely. There the sweet wine was "cut off" (1:5), the streams were dried up (1:20) and joy had withered (1:12). Now the hills themselves overflow. Amos 9:13 ends with almost the same words, so the two books are framed by the same roar (Joel 3:16; Amos 1:2) and the same harvest. "Milk" recalls the "land flowing with milk and honey" (Exod 3:8).
- **"A fountain shall come forth from the house of the LORD" (v18).** This is one of the great images of the Bible. Ezekiel saw a river flowing from the temple, getting deeper as it went and making the Dead Sea fresh (Ezek 47:1–12). Zechariah saw "living waters" flowing from Jerusalem east and west (Zech 14:8). Revelation shows "the river of the water of life… flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb" (Rev 22:1–2). Jesus stood in the temple courts and said, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink… Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water," and John explains, "this he said about the Spirit" (John 7:37–39).
- **"And water the Valley of Shittim" (v18).** "Shittim" means acacias, a tough tree that grows in dry desert wadis. There was a Shittim on the plains of Moab across the Jordan, Israel's last camp before entering the promised land and the place of its sin at Baal-peor (Num 25:1; Josh 2:1; 3:1). Some think Joel means that place, others a dry valley running from Jerusalem down toward the Dead Sea, possibly the Kidron itself. Either way, the driest ground is watered from God's house. JFB: acacias grow "only in arid regions," so the point is that the desert will be made fruitful.
- **"Egypt shall become a desolation and Edom a desolate wilderness" (v19).** Egypt and Edom stand for Israel's oldest enemies: Egypt from the slavery before the exodus, Edom the brother nation that refused passage and gloated over Jerusalem's fall (Obad 10–14). Their crime is "the violence done to the people of Judah… innocent blood in their land." Egypt also invaded Jerusalem under Shishak (2 Chr 12:2–9), as JFB notes. While Judah's land is restored to fruitfulness, the enemies' land becomes desert, a reversal of chapter 1.
- **"Judah shall be inhabited forever, and Jerusalem to all generations" (v20).** The book began with a question: had anything like this happened "in your days, or in the days of your fathers?" (1:2), and "tell your children" (1:3). It ends with "all generations." The Tyndale notes observe that Joel ends the opposite of how it began. The people whose survival was in doubt will last forever.
- **"I will avenge their blood, blood I have not avenged" (v21).** The Hebrew verb here (*niqqah*) usually means "to clear," "acquit" or "hold innocent." The KJV, with JFB, reads "I will cleanse their blood that I have not cleansed," meaning God will purge Judah's own guilt. The ESV follows a reading supported by the Greek and Syriac that takes it as God avenging the innocent blood of v19. Both readings are possible. One stresses God's justice for his people, the other his forgiveness of them. The ESV's reading connects closely to the previous verse.
- **"For the LORD dwells in Zion" (v21).** The book's last words. Not the abundance, not the defeat of enemies, but God's presence is the final note. Ezekiel ends the same way: the city's name will be "The LORD Is There" (Ezek 48:35). Revelation ends there too: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the fountain as Christ himself: "Christ is this Fountain; his sufferings, merit, and grace, cleanse, refresh, and make fruitful," and adds, "The fountain of this plenty is in the house of God, whence the streams take rise." Guzik ties the fountain to Ezekiel 47 and Zechariah 14:8, and recalls Shittim as both the place of Israel's failure at Baal-peor and the launching point into Canaan. For the last line he quotes Adam Clarke's comparison with Ezekiel 48:35. JFB notes that "Edom was made 'desolate' under the Maccabees," and reads v21 as God purging "the extreme guilt" of Judah that had long gone unpurged. The Tyndale notes connect the fountain with Zechariah 14:8 and Revelation 22:1–2.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joel ends in a valley of judgment and on a mountain of refuge. The nations are judged, evil is harvested, and the LORD who roars from Zion is also his people's stronghold. Jesus spoke of himself as the one who will sit on his glorious throne with all the nations gathered before him (Matt 25:31–32), and Revelation's sickle is in the hand of "one like a son of man" (Rev 14:14). But the same Jesus took the winepress of God's wrath on himself at the cross, so that those who call on his name are sheltered, not condemned (Rom 5:9). And the fountain from God's house is the Spirit he gives to everyone who comes to him thirsty (John 7:37–39). The book's final words, "the LORD dwells in Zion," became flesh when "the Word… dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and they will be fully true when God dwells with his people forever.
