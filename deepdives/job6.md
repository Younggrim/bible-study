*Setting:* Eliphaz has finished his long first speech (chs 4–5). He told Job that the innocent never perish, that trouble comes from sin, that Job should stop his angry complaining, seek God, and accept God's discipline, and that if he did, God would restore everything. Now Job answers. His reply runs through chapter 7. In chapter 6 he speaks mainly to his friends: he defends his outburst in chapter 3 by pointing to the weight of his suffering (vv1–7), repeats his wish that God would end his life (vv8–13), and then turns on his friends for failing him when he needed them most (vv14–30). The chapter is one of the Bible's sharpest pictures of what it feels like when comfort becomes accusation.

#### Job Replies: My Complaint Is Just (vv1–30)
- **"Oh that my vexation were weighed, and all my calamity laid in the balances!" (v2).** "Vexation" is the same Hebrew word (*ka'as*) Eliphaz used in 5:2: "vexation kills the fool." Job picks up the word and throws it back. Eliphaz had judged Job's anguish as foolish resentment. Job asks him to put that anguish on a scale before judging it. The lexicon gives the word's range as vexation, grief or anguish, especially the kind caused by unfair treatment, as with Hannah, provoked by her rival (1 Sam 1:6, 16).
- **"Heavier than the sand of the sea; therefore my words have been rash" (v3).** Sand is a picture of something too heavy to lift and too many to count. Job admits that his words in chapter 3 were "rash," wild and hasty. He doesn't defend every word. He asks that they be heard in the light of what caused them. This is an important line. Job is honest about his own speech, and that honesty is part of why God later says he spoke rightly (42:7).
- **"For the arrows of the Almighty are in me; my spirit drinks their poison" (v4).** Job now says plainly what he only hinted at in 3:23: God is the one attacking him. He feels like a soldier hit by poisoned arrows, and the poison is spreading through his spirit. The Tyndale notes list the many places where arrows stand for disaster or disease sent by God (Deut 32:23; Ps 38:2; Lam 3:12–13). "The terrors of God are arrayed against me": the language is military, troops lined up for battle. Job doesn't know about Satan's role. All he can see is God's hand. And this is the deepest part of his pain. Losing his wealth and health is terrible, but feeling that God has become his enemy is worse.
- **"Does the wild donkey bray when he has grass, or the ox low over his fodder?" (v5).** Animals don't complain when they have what they need. They bray and bellow when something is wrong. Job's point is simple: my cries are not empty noise; there is a real cause behind them.
- **"Can that which is tasteless be eaten without salt, or is there any taste in the juice of the mallow?" (v6).** The mallow (*challamuth*) is a plant with a slimy, bland juice. The word appears only here, and its meaning is uncertain. Older translations, following Jewish tradition, read "the white of an egg." Either way, the picture is food that makes you gag. The Tyndale notes suggest Job may mean both the suffering God has given him and Eliphaz's bland advice. The lexicon leans toward Job's sufferings: his life is now as nauseating as slimy, flavorless food.
- **"My appetite refuses to touch them; they are as food that is loathsome to me" (v7).** Job can't stomach it. Whether "them" means his trials or Eliphaz's words, he's saying he can't swallow it.
- **"That it would please God to crush me, that he would let loose his hand and cut me off!" (v9).** Job repeats his wish from chapter 3, but now he addresses it to God. He wants God to finish what he started. "Cut me off" is a weaver's picture, cutting the finished cloth from the loom (Isa 38:12). Job does not consider taking his own life. He asks God to take it. Moses and Elijah, in their own despair, prayed the same kind of prayer (Num 11:15; 1 Kgs 19:4).
- **"I would even exult in pain unsparing, for I have not denied the words of the Holy One" (v10).** This is the one comfort Job has left. If he died now, even in great pain, he could die knowing he had never denied God's words. "Denied" can also mean "hidden" or "concealed," so it might mean he never covered up what God said. Either way, Job's conscience is clear. He is holding onto his integrity, exactly what Satan said he would let go. "The Holy One" is a title used for God especially in Isaiah. Here it is a sign that Job, even in agony, still thinks of God as holy.
- **"What is my strength, that I should wait? And what is my end, that I should be patient?" (v11).** Eliphaz had told him to wait for God's restoration (5:17–26). Job says he doesn't have the strength. He can see no "end," no goal or future, that would make patient waiting worth it.
- **"Is my strength the strength of stones, or is my flesh bronze?" (v12).** Job is flesh and blood, not a statue. Eliphaz talked about him as though he could take any amount of pain if only he had the right attitude.
- **"Have I any help in me, when resource is driven from me?" (v13).** "Resource" means the ability to succeed or recover. Job has nothing left inside himself to draw on. This is total exhaustion.
- **"He who withholds kindness from a friend forsakes the fear of the Almighty" (v14).** The Hebrew of this verse is difficult, and translations differ. The ESV reads it as a warning to the friends: refusing kindness to a suffering friend is a sign of not fearing God. The KJV and some older readers take it differently: a despairing man should receive kindness from his friend *even if* he has begun to forsake the fear of God. Both readings make the same basic point. "Kindness" is *chesed*, the Bible's great word for loyal, covenant love, the faithful love God shows his people. The Tyndale notes suggest Job and his friends may have had a bond of loyalty like a covenant. Job is saying they owed him *chesed*, and they gave him a lecture instead.
- **"My brothers are treacherous as a torrent-bed" (v15).** Job now uses an image anyone in the desert would understand. A *wadi* is a dry streambed. In winter it rushes with water from melting snow and rain, "dark with ice" (v16). In the hot season, just when travelers need it most, it disappears (v17). Job had counted on his friends like a traveler counts on a stream, and they dried up. Jeremiah uses the same image of God himself in a moment of despair: "Will you be to me like a deceitful brook, like waters that fail?" (Jer 15:18).
- **"The caravans of Tema look, the travelers of Sheba hope" (vv18–20).** Tema was an oasis city in northern Arabia, a meeting point for the great trade roads (Isa 21:14). Sheba was in southern Arabia, famous for its trade in spices and gold (Ps 72:10, 15; 1 Kgs 10). The Tyndale notes point out that Tema is a different place from Teman, Eliphaz's home. Picture the long camel trains turning off the road to find water at a stream they remember, and finding only dry rocks. "They are ashamed because they were confident" (v20). Some of them "perish" in the desert (v18). That is how Job feels about his friends.
- **"For you have now become nothing; you see my calamity and are afraid" (v21).** Job names their real problem. They saw how terrible his suffering was, and it frightened them. If this could happen to Job, it could happen to anyone, including them. Their theory that suffering always comes from sin protected them from that fear. So they held to the theory instead of standing by their friend.
- **"Have I said, 'Make me a gift'?" (vv22–23).** Job never asked them for money, for a bribe to pay off an enemy, or for a ransom from raiders. He asked for nothing costly. All he needed was loyalty and sympathy, and that's what they wouldn't give.
- **"Teach me, and I will be silent; make me understand how I have gone astray" (v24).** This is a fair challenge. If I have sinned, show me where. Eliphaz had only hinted and implied. Job asks him to name the sin. No friend ever does, because there isn't one to name.
- **"How forceful are upright words! But what does reproof from you reprove?" (v25).** Honest, true words have real power. Job would accept correction if it were true. But their arguments prove nothing.
- **"Do you think that you can reprove words, when the speech of a despairing man is wind?" (v26).** Job asks them not to treat his anguished words in chapter 3 like a carefully reasoned position to be refuted. A desperate man's words are like wind, cries of pain, not a theology lecture. To pick them apart is to miss the person.
- **"You would even cast lots over the fatherless, and bargain over your friend" (v27).** This is Job's harshest charge. Casting lots over an orphan probably means gambling over who will get the orphan as a slave, treating a helpless child as property. Job had defended orphans all his life (29:12; 31:17, 21). He says his friends are treating him the same way, as an object to be argued over rather than a person to be loved.
- **"But now, be pleased to look at me, for I will not lie to your face" (v28).** Job asks them to look him in the eye. They have been talking *about* him in general terms. He wants them to see him and know he is telling the truth.
- **"Please turn; let no injustice be done. Turn now; my vindication is at stake" (v29).** "Turn" means "think again," "change your mind." "My vindication is at stake" is literally "my righteousness is still in it." Job's integrity and good name are on trial, and he asks his friends to stop prejudging the case.
- **"Is there any injustice on my tongue? Cannot my palate discern the cause of calamity?" (v30).** Job returns to the picture of taste from v6. His tongue can tell good food from bad; his mind can tell truth from error. He knows what is happening to him is not a just punishment for sin, and he will not say otherwise just to satisfy his friends.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees fault on both sides: Job's complaints are partly just and partly mixed with passion. He writes, "It is seldom that those who are at ease themselves rightly weigh the afflictions of the afflicted," and on v4, "Trouble of mind is the sorest trouble." He reads v14 the KJV way, that pity is owed to a friend in trouble, and on v29 says, "A just cause desires nothing more than a just hearing." JFB explains the streams as "wadies of Arabia, filled in the winter, but dry in the summer," and calls the kindness of v14 "the spirit of Christianity." On v28 JFB paraphrases Job: "my countenance will betray me, if I be the hypocrite." Guzik stresses that Job wants God to end his life, not to end it himself, and points out that God later sides with Job over the friends (42:7). He also passes on F. B. Meyer's contrast between the friends who dried up and Christ, whose love does not fail. On v10, JFB prefers "exult" in pain (as the ESV does), while Henry thinks Job was presumptuous to welcome such pain. On v13, the three read the line differently: Guzik as total helplessness, JFB as "deliverance is driven from me," and Henry as Job insisting his reason is still sound.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job's friends failed him like a dried-up stream, and he longed for one friend who would show him *chesed*, loyal covenant love. Jesus knew what Job felt. In his hour of greatest need, "all the disciples left him and fled" (Matt 26:56). He too was looked at, argued over and bargained for, sold for thirty pieces of silver (Matt 26:15), and soldiers "cast lots" for his clothing (John 19:24). But he is the friend who does not dry up. He promised "living water" that becomes "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14), and he showed the greatest *chesed*: "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends" (John 15:13). Job asked his friends to look him in the face. Jesus looks at the suffering with compassion (Matt 9:36) and does not withdraw.
