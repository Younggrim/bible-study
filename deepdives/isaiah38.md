*Setting:* "In those days" is a loose time marker. Hezekiah reigned 29 years (2 Kings 18:2) and is given fifteen more years here, so his illness fell around 702–701 BC, and v6 shows Jerusalem had not yet been delivered from Assyria. Most scholars think this chapter and the next actually happened *before* the rescue told in chapter 37. Isaiah has arranged them by theme rather than by date. Chapters 36–37 end the Assyrian story with a great victory, and chapters 38–39 turn toward Babylon, which will be the setting of chapters 40–66. The same events appear more briefly in 2 Kings 20:1–11 and 2 Chronicles 32:24, but only Isaiah preserves Hezekiah's own poem about his illness.

#### Hezekiah's Sickness and Recovery (vv1–22)
- **"Hezekiah became sick and was at the point of death" (v1).** Verse 21 tells us the illness centered on a "boil," an inflamed sore or abscess, perhaps from an infection that had spread. Hezekiah was about 39 years old. He had no son yet: Manasseh, who succeeded him, was twelve when he became king (2 Kings 21:1), so he was born during the extra fifteen years. Hezekiah was facing not only death but the end of David's line through him.
- **"Set your house in order, for you shall die, you shall not recover" (v1).** "Set your house in order" means put your affairs and your succession in order. The message sounds final. But like Jonah's "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (Jonah 3:4), God's warnings often carry an unspoken "unless." Jeremiah states the principle: when God announces disaster and people turn to him, he may relent (Jer 18:7–8). Henry: "put both thy affections and thy affairs into the best posture thou canst."
- **"Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and prayed to the LORD" (v2).** He turned away from the people in the room, to be alone with God. JFB: "to hide his emotion and collect his thoughts for prayer." Some suggest the wall faced the temple.
- **"Remember how I have walked before you in faithfulness and with a whole heart" (v3).** This is not a boast of sinlessness. It is the language of covenant loyalty, the same language used of David (1 Kings 9:4). Second Kings 18:5–6 says of Hezekiah: "He trusted in the LORD… there was none like him among all the kings of Judah after him." His faith had wavered in the treaty with Egypt (chs 30–31), but his heart belonged to God. **"And Hezekiah wept bitterly."** The Hebrew is literally "wept with a great weeping."
- **"The God of David your father: I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears" (v5).** God answers before Isaiah has even left the palace courtyard (2 Kings 20:4). He identifies himself as the God of David, the God of the promise of an everlasting dynasty (2 Sam 7:16). The God who sees tears is the God of the psalms: "You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle" (Ps 56:8).
- **"Behold, I will add fifteen years to your life" (v5).** God's answer to prayer is real. Readers have asked whether God "changed his mind." Most Christian traditions answer that God's eternal plan includes his people's prayers. The sentence of death was a true word about where things were heading, and prayer was the means God had planned to use to change the outcome.
- **Was it good that he lived?** Guzik notes that the fifteen years brought both good and bad. Hezekiah saw Jerusalem delivered. But in those years his son Manasseh was born, who became the worst king in Judah's history (2 Kings 21:1–16), and Hezekiah showed Babylon's envoys his treasures (ch 39). Some readers conclude that Hezekiah should have accepted God's first word. Others note that Manasseh later repented (2 Chr 33:12–13), and that Scripture never criticizes Hezekiah for praying. The text simply shows that God answered, and that a longer life is not automatically a holier one.
- **"I will deliver you and this city out of the hand of the king of Assyria" (v6).** This verse ties the king's healing to the city's rescue. Both receive a reprieve, and both reprieves are temporary: Hezekiah will still die, and Jerusalem will still fall, though to Babylon (39:6). The Tyndale notes make this connection.
- **"I will make the shadow cast by the declining sun on the dial of Ahaz turn back ten steps" (v8).** The Hebrew word translated "dial" and "steps" is the same, *ma'alot*, which normally means "steps" or "stairs." Many scholars think the "dial of Ahaz" was a stairway built by Ahaz, perhaps leading to an upper room (2 Kings 23:12 mentions Ahaz's roof chamber), where the shadow of a pillar or wall crept down the steps through the afternoon like a clock. Ahaz may have copied the idea from the Assyrians or Babylonians, who had such devices. In 2 Kings 20:9–10, Hezekiah is offered a choice and asks for the harder sign, the shadow going backward. The fitting point is that God gave more time to the day as he gave more time to the king. How it happened is not explained. Some suggest a local miracle of light, and JFB mentions refraction as a possible means. Chronicles notes that Babylon's envoys came to ask "about the sign that had been done in the land" (2 Chr 32:31). The sign is offered to the son of the king who refused one (7:11–12), and on Ahaz's own stairway.
- **"A writing of Hezekiah king of Judah" (v9).** What follows is a psalm of thanksgiving, written after his recovery. It moves through lament (vv10–14) to praise (vv15–20). Hezekiah was a patron of Scripture: Proverbs 25:1 records that his men copied out proverbs of Solomon.
- **"In the middle of my days I must depart; I am consigned to the gates of Sheol" (v10).** "The middle of my days" is literally "the quiet" or "pause" of my days. JFB suggests it means the noon of life. *Sheol* is the Hebrew word for the realm of the dead, pictured as a city with gates (Ps 9:13; Matt 16:18). Old Testament believers knew God was Lord there too (Ps 139:8), but the full light of resurrection had not yet dawned. Guzik notes that *Sheol* by itself refers to the place of the dead without spelling out torment or blessing.
- **"I shall not see the LORD, the LORD in the land of the living" (v11).** What Hezekiah dreads most is losing fellowship with God in worship. The Hebrew uses the short form *Yah* twice here. He grieves to leave both God and people.
- **"Like a shepherd's tent… like a weaver I have rolled up my life; he cuts me off from the loom" (v12).** Two pictures of how quickly life can end. A nomad's tent is pulled up in minutes and carried away. A weaver finishing a piece of cloth cuts the threads that hold it to the loom and rolls it up. Paul uses the tent picture: "if the tent that is our earthly home is destroyed, we have a building from God" (2 Cor 5:1). JFB notes that a tent moved is not destroyed, a hint that Hezekiah expected some kind of continued existence. **"From day to night you bring me to an end."** In a single day he expected to be gone.
- **"I calmed myself until morning; like a lion he breaks all my bones" (v13).** He tried to steady himself through the night, but the pain felt like a lion crushing him. Hezekiah does not hide that he felt God himself was behind the suffering, and he says so honestly, as Job did.
- **"Like a swallow or a crane I chirp; I moan like a dove" (v14).** The sounds of a sick man, thin chirps and low moans. The mourning dove was a standard picture of grief (59:11; Ezek 7:16). **"O Lord, I am oppressed; be my pledge of safety!"** "Pledge" is the word for a guarantor, someone who stands surety for another's debt (Gen 43:9; Prov 6:1). Hezekiah asks God himself to stand in for him. Job asks the same thing of God (Job 17:3), and Hebrews calls Jesus "the guarantor of a better covenant" (Heb 7:22).
- **"What shall I say? For he has spoken to me, and he himself has done it" (v15).** The turning point. God spoke the word of healing, and God did it. **"I walk slowly all my years because of the bitterness of my soul."** The meaning is debated. It may mean he will live humbly and carefully all his days, remembering what he went through (the Tyndale notes and many versions). Or it may describe his slow, painful walk during the sickness.
- **"Oh restore me to health and make me live!" (v16).** The first half of this verse is one of the hardest lines in Isaiah to translate, but the plea is clear.
- **"It was for my welfare that I had great bitterness" (v17).** "For my welfare" is literally "for peace (*shalom*)." The KJV has "for peace I had great bitterness." Hezekiah sees that God used the suffering for his good, a thought Paul makes central (Rom 8:28; 2 Cor 4:17). Guzik reads the line as an honest admission that Hezekiah's concern was largely for his own peace. Most commentators read it as a statement about God's good purpose in the pain.
- **"In love you have delivered my life from the pit of destruction, for you have cast all my sins behind your back" (v17).** Hezekiah links healing with forgiveness. A person throws something behind his back when he does not want to see it again. God does not forget the way people forget, but he chooses not to hold the sins against him. Compare Micah 7:19, "You will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea," and Psalm 103:12, "as far as the east is from the west." Henry: "When God pardons sin he casts it behind his back."
- **"Sheol does not thank you; death does not praise you" (v18).** This is a common theme in the psalms (Ps 6:5; 30:9; 88:10–12; 115:17). It does not deny life after death. It says that the dead no longer take part in the worship of God's people on earth. The resurrection hope that grows clearer in Isaiah (26:19) and in Daniel (12:2) comes into full light in Christ, who "brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10).
- **"The living, the living, he thanks you, as I do this day; the father makes known to the children your faithfulness" (v19).** The repetition shows his joy. He means to tell the next generation. Sadly, his own son Manasseh grew up to undo his reforms.
- **"We will play my music on stringed instruments all the days of our lives, at the house of the LORD" (v20).** The poem ends in the temple, with music for the rest of his life. Henry: "Thanksgiving is good, but thanksliving is better."
- **"Let them take a cake of figs and apply it to the boil" (v21).** Fig poultices were a known treatment for boils and ulcers in the ancient world, mentioned in texts from Ugarit and by later medical writers. God's promise did not rule out medicine. Isaiah used the ordinary means, and God gave the healing. Henry: "We must not put physicians, or physic, in the place of God." Guzik adds that trusting God is no reason to refuse treatment.
- **"What is the sign that I shall go up to the house of the LORD?" (v22).** Verses 21–22 read like a footnote, because in 2 Kings 20:7–8 they come before the sign of the shadow. Isaiah seems to have placed them at the end so the poem could follow the promise directly. Hezekiah's question shows what he wanted most: to be able to go back to the temple, the place where his poem ends. The Tyndale notes observe that a person with an open sore would not have been allowed in, so going up to the temple was a sign of full recovery.
- **Commentators:** Henry follows the chapter through sickness, prayer, answer and thanksgiving, and notes that Hezekiah "put this thanksgiving in writing, that he might review it himself afterwards," and says of the sign that "he that set that clock a going can set it back when he pleases." Guzik calls the warning to set his house in order a mercy, since not everyone has time to prepare, and he raises the question of whether the fifteen years were wholly a blessing, given Manasseh and the Babylonian envoys. JFB sees in the shepherd's tent of v12 a hint of life beyond death, "He plainly expects to exist, and not cease to be in another state," and suggests refraction as a possible means for the shadow's movement, which is a guess, not the text.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hezekiah turned to the wall and wept, and God heard. Jesus, the greater Son of David, prayed "with loud cries and tears to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard" (Heb 5:7). But where Hezekiah was given fifteen years and then died, Jesus went through death and was raised, never to die again. Hezekiah asked God to be his "pledge," the one who stands surety for him. In Christ, God did exactly that. And where Hezekiah could only say that Sheol does not praise God, the risen Christ holds "the keys of Death and Hades" (Rev 1:18), so that for those who belong to him, even death is not the end of praise.
