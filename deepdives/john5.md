*Setting:* Jesus returns to Jerusalem for an unnamed feast, probably in AD 28 or 29. Chapters 2–4 showed people responding to him: disciples, Nicodemus, a Samaritan town and a royal official. Chapter 5 begins a new stage. From here through chapter 10, Jesus appears at Israel's sacred times (a Sabbath, Passover, the Feast of Booths, Hanukkah) and shows that he fulfills what each one points to. Here, a healing on the Sabbath leads to the first open plot to kill him, and Jesus answers with one of his longest statements about who he is. The Tyndale notes observe that the chapter reads like a trial: an event, a charge, and a defense with witnesses.

#### The Healing at the Pool on the Sabbath (vv1–17)
- **"A feast of the Jews" (v1).** John doesn't name it, which is unusual for him. Some manuscripts read "the feast," which would mean Passover or Booths. Suggestions include Passover, Pentecost, Booths and Purim. It matters mainly for dating Jesus' ministry, and the text leaves it open.
- **"By the Sheep Gate a pool… called Bethesda" (v2).** The Sheep Gate was in the north wall of Jerusalem, near the temple, where animals for sacrifice were probably brought in (Neh 3:1). Manuscripts spell the pool's name several ways: Bethesda ("house of mercy" or "house of flowing"), Bethzatha and Bethsaida. A copper scroll found among the Dead Sea Scrolls mentions a pool with a similar name in this area, which supports "Bethesda."
- **"Five roofed colonnades" (v2).** For a long time critics took these five porches as symbolic or invented. Excavations near the Church of St. Anne in Jerusalem uncovered two large pools separated by a wall, with colonnades on four sides and one across the middle, five in all. John knew the city well. His "there is" (present tense) has led some to suggest he wrote before Rome destroyed the city in AD 70, but a writer can use the present tense for a place he remembers, so it is not strong evidence.
- **Verse 4 is missing (vv3–4).** The ESV jumps from v3 to v5. Later manuscripts add the end of v3 and v4: "waiting for the moving of the water; for an angel of the Lord went down at certain seasons into the pool and stirred the water," and whoever entered first was healed. The KJV includes it. The earliest and best manuscripts lack it, and the copies that include it vary a lot and often mark it as doubtful. Most scholars think a scribe added it to explain v7. The verse does reflect what people believed about the pool. Some think the pool was fed by an intermittent spring that bubbled now and then. Others link the site to a later healing shrine of the Greek god Asclepius. John does not say the belief was true. He simply reports that the sick man held it.
- **"An invalid for thirty-eight years" (v5).** That was longer than many people lived then. He had no one to help him and no hope of getting to the water first. Some note that Israel wandered thirty-eight years in the wilderness (Deut 2:14), but John makes nothing of it.
- **"Do you want to be healed?" (v6).** It seems an odd question for a man who has been lying there for decades. But after so long, a person can lose hope or grow used to a way of life. Begging was his livelihood, and healing would change everything. Jesus wakes his will and invites him to want something.
- **"Sir, I have no one" (v7).** He doesn't answer yes or no. He explains why it's impossible. He sees only the pool and the competition, and he doesn't know who is speaking to him.
- **"Get up, take up your bed, and walk" (v8).** The "bed" (*krabattos*) was a light mat or pallet that could be rolled up and carried. Jesus doesn't touch him, doesn't take him to the water, and doesn't ask for faith first. He speaks, and "at once the man was healed" (v9). Almost the same command appears in the healing of a paralyzed man in Capernaum (Mark 2:11), a different event.
- **"Now that day was the Sabbath" (v9).** John adds this after the miracle, and it changes everything. The Sabbath was God's gift of rest (Gen 2:2–3; Exod 20:8–11). By Jesus' time, the rabbis had defined thirty-nine categories of forbidden work, later recorded in the Mishnah (Shabbat 7:2). The last was carrying an object from one domain to another. The Old Testament forbade carrying loads through the city gates on the Sabbath (Jer 17:21–22), but that was about trade. Carrying a sleeping mat broke the tradition, not the Law of Moses itself.
- **"The man who healed me, that man said to me" (v11).** "The Jews" here means the religious leaders in Jerusalem, as often in John. The man shifts the blame immediately. Notice what the leaders ask: not "who healed you?" but "who told you to carry your bed?" (v12). A man paralyzed for thirty-eight years is walking, and they care about the mat.
- **"Did not know who it was" (v13).** Unlike the Samaritan woman or the official, this man has no idea who Jesus is. Jesus has slipped away into the crowd.
- **"See, you are well! Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you" (v14).** "You are well" is in the perfect tense: he has been made whole and stays that way. Jesus finds him, not the other way round. The warning does not mean every illness comes from a person's own sin. Jesus denies that link in 9:2–3. But sin can bring suffering, and here it may have. The "worse" thing is final judgment, which Jesus speaks of in vv28–29.
- **"The man went away and told the Jews that it was Jesus" (v15).** Readers differ on his motive. Some think he simply wanted to clear himself, or even meant to give Jesus credit. Many think it was a betrayal: he knew the leaders were hostile and gave them a name. Unlike the blind man in chapter 9, who is also healed on the Sabbath and stands up for Jesus, this man never shows faith. The two healings seem to be told as a pair, showing two different responses to the same grace.
- **"This was why the Jews were persecuting Jesus" (v16).** "Persecuting" and "doing" are both continuous in Greek, suggesting a pattern: Jesus kept healing on the Sabbath, and they kept hounding him for it. The other Gospels record several Sabbath conflicts (Mark 2:23–3:6; Luke 13:10–17).
- **"My Father is working until now, and I am working" (v17).** This is Jesus' whole defense in a sentence. Jewish teachers agreed that God "rested" on the seventh day from creating (Gen 2:2), but they also agreed that he kept working every Sabbath: babies were born, people died, rain fell and the world was held together. The rabbis discussed this exact question. Jesus says his work is the Father's work. If God may work on the Sabbath, so may his Son.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws out the name "house of mercy," noting the place showed "much of the mercy of God to the sick and diseased." He writes of the man, "He was lame longer than most live," and on Jesus' question, "Christ is willing to heal, if we be but willing to be healed." Henry treats the angel in v4 as part of the text, as the KJV did in his day. Guzik reports the manuscript evidence and, following Trench, sees it as an early addition, though the belief it describes is clearly behind v7. Guzik notes that carrying the mat broke rabbinic tradition, not God's command, and reads the man's report to the authorities as fear. Henry says the "worse thing" for those who fall back into sin is far worse than "thirty-eight years' lameness." On v17, Henry explains that God's rest was from creating, not from upholding and governing all things.

#### Jesus Is Equal with God (v18)
- **"Seeking all the more to kill him" (v18).** This is the first mention in John of a plan to kill Jesus. It will hang over the rest of the Gospel (7:1, 19, 25; 8:37, 40; 11:53).
- **"Calling God his own Father" (v18).** Jews spoke of God as the Father of Israel (Deut 32:6; Isa 63:16) and occasionally called on him as "our Father." Jesus says "*my* Father" in a unique and personal way. In v17 he claims the same right to work on the Sabbath that only God has.
- **"Making himself equal with God" (v18).** The leaders understood him correctly. They were wrong only in thinking he was a man "making himself" God, a blasphemer, when in fact he is the eternal Son (1:1). Jesus does not correct their understanding. Instead, in the verses that follow, he explains how his equality with the Father works.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Henry both stress that the leaders understood exactly what Jesus was claiming. Henry: Christ "was and is God, equal with the Father," so the charge was true, though it was meant as an accusation. Guzik notes that "breaking" and "calling" are both continuous: Jesus was doing both habitually.

#### The Authority of the Son (vv19–29)
- **"The Son can do nothing of his own accord" (v19).** This sounds like a step back, but it is the opposite. Jesus never acts independently of the Father, not because he is less, but because he and the Father are perfectly united. "Whatever the Father does, that the Son does likewise." No mere man could say that. Some scholars see a small picture from the carpenter's shop here: a son apprenticed to his father, watching and doing what his father does.
- **"The Father loves the Son and shows him all" (v20).** "Loves" here is *phileō*, where 3:35 used *agapaō* for the same truth. John uses the two verbs interchangeably. "Greater works than these" points ahead to giving life and judging (vv21–22), and to raising Lazarus (ch 11).
- **"So also the Son gives life to whom he will" (v21).** In Jewish belief only God could raise the dead (Deut 32:39; 1 Sam 2:6). The Son does it too, and "to whom he will," by his own free choice.
- **"The Father… has given all judgment to the Son" (v22).** The final judgment is another prerogative of God alone (Gen 18:25). The Father has entrusted it to the Son.
- **"That all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father" (v23).** This is one of the clearest claims to deity in the Gospels. In Isaiah God says, "My glory I give to no other" (Isa 42:8). Yet the Son is to receive the same honor. Honoring God while dishonoring the Son is impossible. In the ancient world, an envoy carried the authority of the one who sent him, so an insult to the messenger was an insult to the king. But Jesus is more than an envoy. He shares the Father's honor.
- **"Has passed from death to life" (v24).** "Has" eternal life is present tense, and "has passed" is perfect: the move from death to life has happened and stands. "Does not come into judgment" means condemnation (3:18). Believers will still stand before Christ (2 Cor 5:10), but not to be condemned (Rom 8:1).
- **"An hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice" (v25).** This is spiritual resurrection now. People dead in sin hear Jesus' voice and live (Eph 2:1–5). "Is now here" means it is already happening.
- **"The Father has life in himself… granted the Son also to have life in himself" (v26).** Every creature has life derived from someone else. God has life in himself, owing it to no one. The Son has that same self-existent life. "Granted" describes the eternal relationship between Father and Son, not a moment when the Son received life after lacking it (1:4).
- **"Because he is the Son of Man" (v27).** This is the only place in the Gospels where "Son of Man" has no article in Greek, matching the phrase "one like a son of man" in Daniel 7:13. There God gives that figure everlasting dominion. The judge of humanity is himself human. He knows from the inside what it is to be tempted and to suffer (Heb 4:15).
- **"All who are in the tombs will hear his voice" (vv28–29).** Now Jesus speaks of the future, bodily resurrection. "An hour is coming," without "and is now here." Daniel 12:2 promised that "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." Jesus says it is his voice that will wake them. Lazarus coming out of the tomb at Jesus' voice (11:43–44) will be a preview.
- **"Those who have done good… those who have done evil" (v29).** This does not teach that people earn life by good works. Elsewhere in this same chapter, life comes by hearing and believing (v24). Deeds show what a person truly is: those who believe "do what is true" and come to the light (3:21). Works are the evidence, not the price.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the Son "quickens whom he will" as a free agent, and he distinguishes a present spiritual resurrection from sin ("The hour is coming, and now is") from the future bodily resurrection. JFB likewise reads v25 as spiritual life beginning now, with its full flowering at Pentecost. On v24 JFB exclaims, "What a transition!" On v27 JFB calls it "one of the most beautiful arrangements of divine wisdom in redemption" that the judge shares our human nature. Guzik draws out "life in himself" as the self-existence that belongs to God alone, and warns against two old errors: blurring the Father and the Son into one person, or making the Son less than God. He notes, citing Morris, that works in v29 reveal faith rather than earn salvation. Guzik also treats the *phileō*/*agapaō* switch as making no real difference.

#### Witnesses to Jesus (vv30–47)
- **"I can do nothing on my own" (v30).** This repeats v19 and opens the next section. Jesus' judgment is just because he seeks only the Father's will.
- **"If I alone bear witness about myself, my testimony is not true" (v31).** The Law required two or three witnesses for a case to stand (Deut 17:6; 19:15). Jesus accepts the rule for the sake of argument. He doesn't mean his own words are false (8:14). He means his opponents need not take his word alone, because others testify too. He then calls his witnesses.
- **"There is another who bears witness about me" (v32).** This is most likely the Father (v37), though some take it as John. Jesus then names several witnesses.
- **John the Baptist (vv33–35).** The leaders had sent a delegation to John (1:19). He testified that Jesus was the Lamb of God and the Son of God (1:29–34). Jesus doesn't need John's endorsement, but he mentions it "so that you may be saved" (v34). He calls John "a burning and shining lamp" (v35). The word is *lychnos*, a small oil lamp, not *phōs*, "light." John was a lamp that burned out. Jesus is the light itself (1:8–9; 8:12). "You were willing to rejoice for a while" suggests the leaders enjoyed John's excitement but never took his message to heart.
- **The works (v36).** Jesus' miracles are a greater witness. "The works that the Father has given me to accomplish" includes the signs, and finally the work on the cross (17:4; 19:30).
- **The Father (vv37–38).** The Father has testified, at Jesus' baptism and through the Scriptures. But the leaders have "never heard" his voice or "seen" his form. That is a rebuke: Israel heard God's voice at Sinai (Deut 4:12), but these men, for all their religion, have no real knowledge of God. The proof is that they reject the one he sent.
- **"You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life" (v39).** The Greek verb (*eraunate*) can be either a statement ("you search") or a command ("search!"). The KJV took it as a command: "Search the scriptures." Most modern translations, including the ESV, take it as a statement, which fits the flow: they study the Scriptures carefully, yet miss the one the Scriptures are about. Rabbis prized the Torah as the source of life. Jesus says the Scriptures are not the life itself. They point to him, and he is the life (14:6; Luke 24:27).
- **"Yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (v40).** The problem is not lack of evidence but lack of will. The verb is "you are not willing."
- **"I do not receive glory from people" (v41).** "Glory" (*doxa*) is a key word in this paragraph (vv41, 44). It means honor, praise, reputation. Jesus doesn't need human approval. The leaders crave it.
- **"You do not have the love of God within you" (v42).** This can mean love for God or the love that comes from God. Probably both: they do not love God because his love has never filled them.
- **"If another comes in his own name, you will receive him" (v43).** History bore this out. In the decades after Jesus, several men claimed to be the Messiah and drew followings. The most famous was Simon bar Kosiba, hailed as "Bar Kokhba" ("son of the star") by the great Rabbi Akiva. His revolt against Rome (AD 132–135) ended in disaster for the Jews of Judea.
- **"How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another?" (v44).** This is one of the most searching lines in the Gospel. Caring most about the approval of others makes faith impossible, because faith means accepting God's verdict, including his verdict on one's own sin. "The only God" echoes the Shema (Deut 6:4).
- **"There is one who accuses you: Moses" (v45).** The leaders "set your hope" on Moses as their defender before God. Jesus says the very one they trust will testify against them. Their own Law condemns them.
- **"He wrote of me" (v46).** Moses wrote of the "prophet like me" whom God would raise up (Deut 18:15–18). Moses' books also hold the promised offspring of the woman (Gen 3:15), the blessing to all nations through Abraham (Gen 12:3), the Passover lamb (Exod 12), the sacrifices, and the bronze serpent Jesus has already mentioned (3:14). Jesus read the whole Torah as pointing to himself.
- **"If you do not believe his writings, how will you believe my words?" (v47).** The chapter ends with a question and no answer. It contrasts Moses' "writings" with Jesus' spoken "words." The leaders claimed to honor written Scripture, and Jesus says that if they really believed it, they would believe him.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls John "a burning and a shining light," the burning for his zeal and the shining for his influence. JFB notes that Jesus is never called by this lesser word *lychnos*, being "ever the Light." On v39, Henry and JFB both follow the KJV's command, "Search the scriptures." Henry urges reading with purpose, looking for Christ, and JFB says all "have a right and are bound to search" them. Guzik follows modern translations, reading the verb as a statement: the leaders studied hard and missed the point. The lexicon allows both. Henry says sinners perish because they "will not come," not because they cannot. JFB says the "will not" of v40 and the "cannot" of v44 are "just different features of the same awful state of the human heart." On v43 Guzik and JFB both point to false messiahs in Jewish history. Guzik names Bar Kokhba, and JFB cites Bengel's count of sixty-four. Guzik adds a list of the ways Moses wrote of Christ, from the prophet like Moses to the bronze serpent and the sacrifices.

#### Pointing to Christ
A man waits thirty-eight years by a pool for a cure that never comes, and Jesus heals him with a word. The healing reveals far more than a cure for one man. Jesus claims to do what only God does: work as the Father works, give life to whom he will, judge all people, and receive the same honor as the Father. One day his voice will empty every tomb. The leaders searched the Scriptures for life but would not come to the one they spoke about. The chapter's warning is for religious people above all. It is possible to know the Bible, guard the Sabbath and still miss the Lord of the Sabbath. Moses, the Scriptures, John and the Father all point to Jesus, and "whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life."
