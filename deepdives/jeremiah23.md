*Setting:* This chapter closes the collection of messages to Judah's royal house that began at 21:11, probably gathered during Zedekiah's reign (597–586 BC). Chapter 22 ended with a dynasty seemingly finished: Shallum gone to Egypt, Jehoiakim dumped outside the gates, Coniah written down as childless. Now come two answers. The first is a promise: God himself will shepherd his scattered flock and raise up a righteous King from David's line. The second is a long attack on the prophets. Kings were not the only ones who failed. The prophets who should have warned them kept saying, "It shall be well with you." Much of this section reads like an argument Jeremiah lived through day after day, and it prepares for his face-to-face clash with Hananiah in chapter 28.

#### The Righteous Branch (vv1–8)
- **"Woe to the shepherds who destroy and scatter the sheep of my pasture!" (v1).** Across the ancient Near East kings were called shepherds. Hammurabi of Babylon called himself one, and so does David's line in Scripture (2 Sam 5:2; Ps 78:70–72). The "shepherds" here are the kings of chapter 22, together with the leaders around them. Notice "*my* pasture." The flock never belonged to the kings.
- **"You have not attended to them. Behold, I will attend to you" (v2).** A wordplay on the verb *paqad*, which means to look after, visit or call to account. They did not "attend" to the sheep, so God will "attend" to them, in judgment. Ezekiel 34 is a longer sermon on the same text.
- **"Then I will gather the remnant of my flock" (v3).** The scattering is blamed on the shepherds in v2 but also on God: "where *I* have driven them." Both are true. Exile was God's judgment, and bad leadership brought it on. **"They shall be fruitful and multiply."** The words of creation (Gen 1:28) and of the promise to Abraham (Gen 17:6). Restoration will be a new beginning.
- **"I will set shepherds over them who will care for them" (v4).** **"Neither shall any be missing."** The same verb *paqad* again. No sheep will be unaccounted for (compare John 10:28; 17:12).
- **"I will raise up for David a righteous Branch" (v5).** "Branch" is *tsemach*, a sprout or shoot growing from the ground or from a stump. David's family tree had been cut down (22:30), but a new shoot will rise from its root. Isaiah used the same picture: "a shoot from the stump of Jesse" (Isa 11:1). *Tsemach* became almost a title for the Messiah: "the Branch" (Jer 33:15; Zech 3:8; 6:12).
- **"He shall reign as king and deal wisely" (v5).** "Deal wisely" can also mean "prosper" or "succeed." Isaiah 52:13 uses the same verb of the suffering Servant. **"Shall execute justice and righteousness in the land."** This is exactly what Josiah did (22:15) and his sons did not (22:3, 13).
- **"In his days Judah will be saved, and Israel will dwell securely" (v6).** Both kingdoms are named. The northern kingdom had been gone for over a century, but the promised King will rule a reunited people (3:18; Ezek 37:15–22).
- **"The LORD is our righteousness" (v6).** In Hebrew, *YHWH tsidqenu*. This almost certainly plays on the name of the king on the throne, Zedekiah, *Tsidqiyahu*, "the LORD is (my) righteousness." Zedekiah bore the name and contradicted it. The coming King will be what the name says. Jeremiah 33:16 gives the same name to Jerusalem, so the name describes what God gives to his people as much as what the King is. Christians have long read it in light of the New Testament, where Christ "became to us… righteousness" (1 Cor 1:30), and where God's own righteousness is credited to those who believe (Rom 3:21–22; 2 Cor 5:21). Whether the name itself teaches the Messiah's deity is debated. Similar names were given to people and places (Gen 22:14; Exod 17:15), but in the light of the New Testament many have seen more here.
- **"As the LORD lives who brought up the people of Israel out of the land of Egypt" (v7).** People swore oaths by naming what God had done. The exodus was Israel's defining rescue. **"Out of the north country and out of all the countries" (v8).** A day is coming when the return from exile will be so great that it outshines the exodus. These two verses also appear at 16:14–15.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the shepherds "are not owners of the sheep" and that the people's peace "is not bound up in the pomp of her rulers." JFB reads "our" as pointing to Christ's humanity and "LORD" as pointing to his divinity, since "God alone can justify the ungodly." That is a theological reading more than a grammatical one. Guzik, drawing on Thompson and Ryken, notes the play on Zedekiah's name, and he cites the Targum for an early Jewish messianic reading of the verse. He stresses that righteousness here is a gift given, not only sin taken away. He sees modern Israel as a partial, early fulfilment of vv3–4. Many interpreters would put the weight on the return from Babylon and on the gathering of God's people in Christ.

#### Lying Prophets (vv9–40)
- **"Concerning the prophets" (v9).** This heading introduces everything that follows, up to v40, just as "to the house of the king" introduced the royal oracles (21:11).
- **"My heart is broken within me… I am like a drunken man" (v9).** The words of God against the prophets hit Jeremiah so hard that he staggers. He is not glad to deliver them. He is shaken by their holiness.
- **"The land is full of adulterers" (v10).** This means both literal adultery (5:7–8; 29:23) and spiritual unfaithfulness. **"Because of the curse the land mourns."** The "curse" is the covenant curse (Deut 28:23–24; 29:20). Drought had hit the land (ch 14). **"Their might is not right."** They use their power for wrong ends.
- **"Both prophet and priest are ungodly; even in my house I have found their evil" (v11).** The word means "profane" or "polluted." Manasseh had put idol altars in the temple courts (2 Kgs 21:4–5), and Ezekiel saw idolatry going on inside the temple in Zedekiah's time (Ezek 8:5–16).
- **"Like slippery paths in the darkness" (v12).** A path on a mountainside, wet and in the dark. Falling is certain (Ps 35:6; 73:18).
- **"In the prophets of Samaria I saw an unsavory thing" (v13).** The northern prophets openly served Baal (1 Kgs 18:19). That was bad enough. **"But in the prophets of Jerusalem I have seen a horrible thing" (v14).** Jerusalem's prophets claimed to speak for the LORD, and they lived in adultery and lies. In a sense that was worse, because it used God's name to cover sin.
- **"They strengthen the hands of evildoers, so that no one turns from his evil" (v14).** This is the heart of the charge. By promising peace, they removed every reason to repent. **"Like Sodom… like Gomorrah."** Isaiah had called the leaders of Jerusalem "rulers of Sodom" (Isa 1:10).
- **"Bitter food… poisoned water" (v15).** Literally "wormwood" and "water of gall." This repeats 9:15 word for word, but there it was aimed at the people. Now it falls on the prophets, because "from the prophets of Jerusalem ungodliness has gone out into all the land."
- **"Filling you with vain hopes" (v16).** The verb comes from *hebel*, "vapor" or "breath," the word of Ecclesiastes. They fill people with air. **"Visions of their own minds."** Literally "of their own heart."
- **"It shall be well with you… No disaster shall come upon you" (v17).** Literally "you shall have peace" (*shalom*). This was the false prophets' message from start to finish (6:14; 8:11; 14:13). And note who received it: "those who despise the word of the LORD" and "everyone who stubbornly follows his own heart." They told the unrepentant exactly what they wanted to hear.
- **"Who among them has stood in the council of the LORD?" (v18).** The "council" (*sod*) is the picture of God's throne room, where heaven's court gathers and decisions are made (1 Kgs 22:19–22; Job 1:6; Ps 89:7). A true prophet is one who has been admitted there and sent out with the King's decision (Isa 6:1–8; Amos 3:7). These prophets had never been in the room.
- **"Behold, the storm of the LORD!" (v19).** Verses 19–20 appear again at 30:23–24. **"In the latter days you will understand it clearly" (v20).** After the judgment falls, Judah will see that Jeremiah was right and the optimists were wrong.
- **"I did not send the prophets, yet they ran" (v21).** "Ran" pictures a royal messenger sprinting with news (2 Sam 18:19–27). These men ran with no message. **"If they had stood in my council… they would have turned them from their evil way" (v22).** The test of a true prophet is not only whether predictions come true (Deut 18:22). It is also whether the message calls people back from sin to God (Deut 13:1–5).
- **"Am I a God at hand… and not a God far away?" (v23).** The false prophets seem to have treated God as a local deity who could be handled. The LORD is near, but he is not small. **"Do I not fill heaven and earth?" (v24).** No one can hide from him, and he has heard every word they spoke in his name (v25). Psalm 139:7–12 is the great meditation on this truth.
- **"I have dreamed, I have dreamed!" (v25).** God did speak through dreams (Gen 37; Num 12:6; Dan 2). But a dream was easy to claim and impossible to check. Deuteronomy 13:1–5 had warned about dreamers whose message led people away from the LORD.
- **"Make my people forget my name by their dreams" (v27).** Even in the LORD's name, they were teaching a different god, one who never judges, as their fathers forgot the LORD for Baal.
- **"What has straw in common with wheat?" (v28).** Straw and grain grow on the same stalk and look alike in the field. Only one of them feeds anyone. Psalm 1:4 calls the wicked "chaff that the wind drives away."
- **"Is not my word like fire… and like a hammer that breaks the rock in pieces?" (v29).** God's word burns, as Jeremiah knew from his own bones (20:9; 5:14), and it shatters. Hebrews 4:12 says the same with the image of a sword. The false prophets' dreams soothe. God's word breaks hard hearts so they can be remade.
- **"Who steal my words from one another" (v30).** They borrowed lines from each other, or perhaps from true prophets, and passed them off as fresh revelation. **"Who use their tongues and declare, 'declares the LORD'" (v31).** The phrase "declares the LORD" (*ne'um YHWH*) was the prophets' seal of authority. Here, uniquely, it is turned into a verb: they "declare-the-LORD" with their own tongues. They had the formula without the source. **"Their recklessness" (v32).** The word suggests boasting or careless swagger.
- **"What is the burden of the LORD?" (v33).** The Hebrew *massa* means both "burden," a load someone lifts (from *nasa*, "to lift"), and "oracle," a message a prophet lifts up (Isa 13:1; Nah 1:1). People were mocking Jeremiah: "So, what's today's heavy message?"
- **"You are the burden, and I will cast you off" (v33).** The ESV follows the Greek and Latin versions here, which divide the Hebrew letters a little differently from the traditional Hebrew text. The traditional text reads "What burden?" Either way the answer turns the joke around. The burden God carries is these people, and he is about to put them down.
- **"But 'the burden of the LORD' you shall mention no more" (v36).** The phrase had become a sneer, and also a cover for false prophets to give their own words the weight of God's. So God bans it. Instead people should ask plainly, "What has the LORD answered? What has the LORD spoken?" (vv35, 37). **"The burden is every man's own word."** Each man's own words will be the load that crushes him.
- **"I will surely lift you up and cast you away" (v39).** Another wordplay. "Lift up" is *nasa*, the same root as *massa*. God will pick up the "burden" and throw it away. The traditional Hebrew text reads "I will surely forget you" (*nashah*), which differs by one letter. Several manuscripts and ancient versions read "lift," which the ESV follows.
- **"Everlasting reproach and perpetual shame" (v40).** The prophets who promised peace would be remembered only as the men who were wrong.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "Nothing more effectually debauches a nation than the debauchery of ministers," and that "the mocking of God's messengers was the baffling of his messages." JFB reads "steal my words" as "a twofold plagiarism," and says God's word "burns and breaks the hard-hearted." He notes that the people would be taken at their own word. His point on v21 is useful: a prophet needs more than an outward position, he needs an inner call from God. Guzik, drawing on Kidner, makes the point that the false prophets took the shame out of sin, so no one repented. He applies the passage to the New Testament call to test every prophecy (1 Cor 14:29; 1 Thess 5:20–21; 1 John 4:1), and he explains the double sense of *massa* and the alternative reading "You are the burden," which matches the ESV.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter sets two pictures side by side: shepherds who scatter, and prophets who speak without being sent. Jesus is the answer to both. He is the Good Shepherd who gathers the scattered and loses none (John 10:11, 16, 28). He is the Branch from David's stump who reigns wisely and does justice. And he is the Prophet who truly stood in the council of God, because he came from the Father's side (John 1:18). He said, "I have not spoken on my own authority, but the Father who sent me has himself given me a commandment—what to say" (John 12:49). The false prophets promised peace without repentance. Jesus made peace by the blood of his cross (Col 1:20) and calls sinners to turn. His name is the one this chapter longed for: "The LORD is our righteousness."
