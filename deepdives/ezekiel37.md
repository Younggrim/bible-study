*Setting:* Babylonia, among the Judean exiles, some time after the news of Jerusalem's fall (33:21), probably in 585 BC or a little later. Ezekiel has just promised that God will cleanse his people, give them a new heart and put his Spirit within them (36:25–27). But the people could not believe it. Their city was ash, their king a prisoner, and the northern tribes had been scattered by Assyria more than 130 years earlier. Their own saying sums up their mood: "Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost" (v11). Chapter 37 answers them with two pictures. The first is a vision: a valley of bones brought back to life by God's word and God's breath. The second is a sign that Ezekiel acts out with two sticks: the long-divided kingdoms of Israel and Judah made one again under one king. It is the most famous chapter in Ezekiel, and it turns the promise of 36:27, "I will put my Spirit within you," into a scene no one could forget.

#### The Valley of Dry Bones (vv1–14)
- **"The hand of the LORD was upon me, and he brought me out in the Spirit of the LORD" (v1).** This is a vision, as in 8:3 and 11:24, where the Spirit carried Ezekiel to Jerusalem. **"The valley"** has the definite article in Hebrew, and it may be the same broad plain where God met Ezekiel in 3:22–23 and 8:4. There, God had shown him his glory and his people's sin. Here he shows him his power to give life.
- **"It was full of bones… very many… and behold, they were very dry" (vv1–2).** Twice the text says "behold," and twice "very." This is not a recent battlefield. The bones are scattered on the surface, unburied, bleached by sun and wind. To be left unburied was the final disgrace in the ancient world, a sign of the covenant curse (Deut 28:26; Jer 8:1–2). The scene shows how dead the people felt: not just dead, but long dead, past all hope.
- **"Son of man, can these bones live?" (v3).** Every natural answer is no. Ezekiel, a priest, would also have known that contact with human bones made a person unclean (Num 19:16). **"O Lord GOD, you know."** This is neither doubt nor bold faith. It is humble faith: Ezekiel will not say "impossible" to God, and he will not claim to know God's plan. He leaves the question with the one who can answer it.
- **"Prophesy over these bones, and say to them, O dry bones, hear the word of the LORD" (v4).** Preaching to a field of skeletons looks absurd. That is the point. God's word does not depend on how able the hearers are to respond. The same word that called the world into being (Gen 1:3; Ps 33:6) can call the dead to life.
- **"I will cause breath to enter you, and you shall live" (v5).** The key word of the chapter is *ruach*. It means "breath," "wind" and "spirit," and the chapter plays on all three. It appears ten times in vv1–14: the Spirit who carries Ezekiel (v1), the breath promised to the bones (vv5–6, 8–10), the winds (v9) and God's own Spirit (v14). English must pick one word each time. Hebrew readers heard the same word throughout.
- **Sinews, flesh, skin, breath (v6).** The order runs backward from decay: bones, then the tendons that hold them, then the flesh, then the skin, then breath. It is a reversal of death, step by step. **"And you shall know that I am the LORD"** is Ezekiel's refrain. Life from the dead is a revelation of who God is.
- **"There was a sound, and behold, a rattling, and the bones came together, bone to its bone" (v7).** "Rattling" (*ra'ash*) usually means a quaking or earthquake, the shaking that accompanies God's appearing (3:12–13; 38:19). The valley shakes as the bones snap back into place. **"Bone to its bone"**: each to its own match. Nothing is lost.
- **"But there was no breath in them" (v8).** Now the valley holds a field of complete corpses. This pause is important. Bodies put back together are still dead. Israel could be brought back to the land, rebuilt and reorganized, and still not live. What it needed was the breath of God.
- **"Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe on these slain" (v9).** This recalls the creation of Adam: God formed the man from dust and "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature" (Gen 2:7). In Eden, God breathed into one body. Here the breath comes from the four corners of the earth into a whole people. **"These slain"** shows that the bones are not the result of natural death. They are the victims of judgment.
- **"They lived and stood on their feet, an exceedingly great army" (v10).** A valley of death becomes a mustered army. Ezekiel himself had twice been set "on my feet" by the Spirit (2:2; 3:24). Now a nation is.
- **"These bones are the whole house of Israel" (v11).** God gives the interpretation. **"The whole house"** includes the north as well as Judah, which prepares for the sign of the two sticks. **"Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost; we are indeed cut off."** The vision answers the people's own words. They said they were dry bones, and God says: yes, and I will raise dry bones.
- **"I will open your graves and raise you from your graves, O my people" (v12).** The picture shifts from a battlefield to a cemetery, but the meaning is the same. The "graves" are the exile itself, where Israel lay as good as dead. **"O my people"** is said twice (vv12–13). Even in their graves they are his.
- **"I will put my Spirit within you, and you shall live" (v14).** This is almost word for word 36:27. The vision explains the promise: the Spirit will do for the nation what breath did for the bones. **"I have spoken, and I will do it."**
- **Is this about resurrection?** In its first meaning, the vision is about the nation's return from exile and its spiritual renewal, as v11 says plainly. Still, the picture only works if God can raise the dead. Isaiah 26:19 and Daniel 12:2 speak of real bodily resurrection, and many Jewish and Christian readers have heard that hope here too. Jewish tradition reads this passage at Passover, and early Christians carved it on their tombs and coffins. The national meaning and the larger hope are not rivals. The God who can raise a nation can raise the dead, and in Christ he has begun to.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the vision on several levels: Israel's hopeless state, the spiritual deadness of sinners, and a picture of the general resurrection. He treats the two commands, to prophesy to the bones and then to call on the breath, as the pairing of preaching and prayer. JFB places the valley "probably" by the Chebar, insists the scene is "not literal, but in vision," and on v3 says "faith leaves the question of possibility to rest with God." He reads the graves as Israel's "politically dead state." Guzik sees the same two steps as Henry: the word preached, then the Spirit prayed for. With Wright and Feinberg, he reads the vision as national revival, not individual resurrection, and holds that its full fulfilment for Israel is still future.

#### I Will Be Their God; They Shall Be My People (vv15–28)
- **"Take a stick and write on it, 'For Judah… ' then take another stick and write on it, 'For Joseph (the stick of Ephraim)'" (v16).** Ezekiel acts out another sign, as he had often done (4:1–5:4; 12:3–7). The "stick" (*ets*, wood) may have been a plain piece of wood or a wooden writing board. Israel had split in two after Solomon's death, about 930 BC (1 Kgs 12). The southern kingdom was Judah. The northern kingdom was often called Ephraim after its leading tribe, the descendants of Joseph's younger son, which is why it is called "the stick of Joseph." Numbers 17:2–3 offers a background: Moses wrote each tribe's name on a staff.
- **"Join them one to another into one stick, that they may become one in your hand" (v17).** Some think Ezekiel held the two pieces end to end in his fist so they looked like one. The meaning is clear either way.
- **"Will you not tell us what you mean by these?" (v18).** Ezekiel's signs were designed to make people ask (12:9; 24:19).
- **"That they may be one in my hand" (v19).** Ezekiel's hand becomes God's hand. The reunion will not be the result of diplomacy. God himself will hold them together. The northern tribes had been deported by Assyria in 722 BC and mixed with other peoples. Many assumed they were gone for good. God says they are not forgotten. Some later groups have claimed that these "sticks" mean books of scripture, but the text explains itself: they are the two kingdoms.
- **"One king shall be king over them all, and they shall be no longer two nations" (v22).** For four centuries the two kingdoms had fought, made rival alliances and worshiped at rival altars (1 Kgs 12:26–30). A single king means the end of the old split. Even after the return from Babylon, the rift persisted: in Jesus' day Jews and Samaritans, living in the old northern territory, did not speak to each other (John 4:9).
- **"They shall not defile themselves anymore with their idols" (v23).** Unity comes with holiness, not instead of it. **"I will save them from all the backslidings in which they have sinned, and will cleanse them."** The ESV follows a slight change in the Hebrew here. The standard text reads "from all their dwellings," which many scholars think arose from a copying slip. "Backslidings" fits the context better, and the Greek translation supports it. **"They shall be my people, and I will be their God"** is the covenant formula again (11:20; 36:28).
- **"My servant David shall be king over them, and they shall all have one shepherd" (v24).** This takes up 34:23–24. In chapter 34 Ezekiel called the coming David "prince" (*nasi*). Here he calls him "king" (*melek*) and then "prince" again (v25). The two titles together show a real king who still serves under God. **"They shall walk in my rules"** is the fruit of the new heart and the Spirit (36:27).
- **"They and their children and their children's children shall dwell there forever, and David my servant shall be their prince forever" (v25).** "Forever" is repeated five times in vv25–28. No human king lives or reigns forever. The promise looks beyond any ordinary son of David to the one whose kingdom "will have no end" (Luke 1:32–33; 2 Sam 7:13, 16).
- **"I will make a covenant of peace with them. It shall be an everlasting covenant" (v26).** The "covenant of peace" of 34:25 is now called "everlasting." Jeremiah calls it the "new covenant" (Jer 31:31). Hebrews speaks of Jesus as the great shepherd raised "by the blood of the eternal covenant" (Heb 13:20).
- **"I will set my sanctuary in their midst forevermore" (v26).** Ezekiel had watched God's glory leave the temple (10:18–19; 11:22–23). Now God promises to come back and stay. The "sanctuary" (*miqdash*) is the holy place. **"My dwelling place shall be with them" (v27).** The word is *mishkan*, the "tabernacle," the tent where God lived among Israel in the wilderness. This line prepares for the great temple vision of chapters 40–48.
- **"The LORD who sanctifies Israel" (v28).** This was the title God used when he gave Israel the law (Exod 31:13; Lev 20:8). Holiness is not something Israel achieves. It is something God does, and his presence among them is the proof.
- **What kind of fulfilment?** The return from Babylon brought back mostly Judeans, with some from the northern tribes (1 Chr 9:3), but no son of David took the throne and the glory did not return to the second temple in the way Ezekiel describes. Christians generally agree the promise centers on Jesus, the Son of David. They differ on how. Many read it as fulfilled in Christ's reign over one people of God, Jews and Gentiles together, with God dwelling among them now by the Spirit and fully in the new creation (Eph 2:14–22; Rev 21:3). Others, especially dispensationalists, also expect a future national restoration of Israel under Christ's rule on earth, and a few, Guzik among them, take David to mean David himself raised to rule under the Messiah.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in the two sticks the reunion of Judah and Israel in worship and loyalty to one king, and also a type of Jews and Gentiles made one in Christ. He identifies "David" as Christ. JFB links the sticks to the tribal rods of Numbers 17:2, and notes that the union after the return "embraced but comparatively few of Israel," so a fuller fulfilment is still to come. On the king JFB is blunt: "Messiah is meant." JFB reads the sanctuary as "spiritual in the heart of all true followers of Messiah," and also as literal in a restored Israel. Guzik, citing Feinberg, rejects the idea of lost tribes, since the promise covers all of Jacob's line. He grants that most commentators take "David" as the Messiah but prefers a literal David in a future kingdom, and with Block he reads *shalom* in v26 as harmony, not merely the absence of war.

#### Pointing to Christ
The valley of dry bones is the gospel in picture. Paul says we "were dead in the trespasses and sins," and God "made us alive together with Christ" (Eph 2:1, 5). On the evening of his resurrection Jesus "breathed on" his disciples and said, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22), the same breath that filled Adam and the slain army of Ezekiel's vision. He is the one shepherd and the Son of David who gathers a divided people into one (John 10:16; 11:52). And he is the dwelling of God among us: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), literally "pitched his tent," the same idea as the *mishkan* of v27. One day the voice from the throne will finish what this chapter promises: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people" (Rev 21:3).
