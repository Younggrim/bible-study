*Setting:* About 870–867 BC, in the reign of Ahab over the northern kingdom of Israel. Three years earlier Elijah announced that there would be "neither dew nor rain" except by his word (17:1), and then vanished. God hid him by the brook Cherith and then in the home of a Gentile widow in Zarephath, in Jezebel's home country. Meanwhile Jezebel, the Phoenician queen, has been hunting down and killing the LORD's prophets, and Baal worship has become the royal religion. Baal was the storm god, believed to ride the clouds and send the rain. After three rainless years, the question hanging over Israel is plain: who really controls the sky? Chapter 18 answers it on Mount Carmel, in the most dramatic contest in the Old Testament.

#### Elijah Confronts Ahab (vv1–19)
- **"In the third year" (v1).** Counted from the start of the drought or from Elijah's move to Zarephath, it is the third year. James and Jesus both say the drought lasted "three years and six months" (James 5:17; Luke 4:25). The two fit easily if the drought had already begun before Elijah went to Zarephath, or if the dry season before the first missed rains is counted. JFB dates the "third year" from six months after the first warning.
- **"Go, show yourself to Ahab, and I will send rain" (v1).** The same God who said "hide yourself" (17:3) now says "show yourself." Elijah's movements are set by God's word, not by his own sense of safety. Notice also that the rain is promised *before* the contest. Carmel is not a gamble. God has already said what he will do.
- **"Obadiah, who was over the household" (v3).** He was the palace manager, the king's chief steward (compare 4:6). His name means "servant of the LORD" (*'Ovadyahu*), and the narrator stops the story to say he "feared the LORD greatly." Most scholars do not think he is the prophet who wrote the book of Obadiah, which seems to come from a later time, though some older writers made the link.
- **"Hid them by fifties in a cave" (v4).** Carmel and the hills of Israel are limestone, honeycombed with caves. Splitting the prophets into two groups meant one discovery would not kill them all. The "prophets" here were probably members of the prophetic groups or "sons of the prophets" known since Samuel's day (1 Sam 10:5; 2 Kgs 2:3). Feeding a hundred men in a famine, under Jezebel's nose, was costly and dangerous.
- **"Perhaps we may find grass and save the horses and mules" (v5).** The king's concern in a famine is his animals. That was not only callous. Horses meant chariots, and chariots meant military power. An Assyrian inscription of Shalmaneser III records that Ahab brought 2,000 chariots to the Battle of Qarqar in 853 BC, more than any other king in the coalition. Still, the picture of a king searching for grass for his horses while his people starve says a great deal.
- **"Is it you, my lord Elijah?" (v7).** Obadiah falls on his face. He calls Elijah "my lord" and Ahab "your lord" (v8). Elijah's reply quietly draws the line: Ahab is Obadiah's master, not Elijah's.
- **"How have I sinned...?" (v9).** Obadiah is terrified. Ahab had searched "every nation or kingdom" for Elijah and made their rulers swear they did not have him (v10). Treaties of the time often required kings to hand back fugitives. If Obadiah announced Elijah and then Elijah disappeared again, Obadiah would be the one to die.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD will carry you I know not where" (v12).** Obadiah assumes the Spirit might whisk Elijah away. The sons of the prophets later assume the same about Elijah (2 Kgs 2:16). It shows what people believed about this mysterious prophet, who appeared and disappeared without warning.
- **"I your servant have feared the LORD from my youth" (v12).** Obadiah reminds Elijah of his record (v13). He is not boasting, only pleading that a faithful man should not be thrown away.
- **"As the LORD of hosts lives, before whom I stand" (v15).** Elijah repeats the oath of 17:1, adding "of hosts," the LORD of heaven's armies. He keeps his word: "I will surely show myself to him today."
- **"Is it you, you troubler of Israel?" (v17).** The word for "troubler" (*'oker*) is a heavy one. It is the word Joshua used of Achan, whose sin brought disaster on the whole camp: "Why did you bring trouble on us?" (Josh 7:25). The valley where Achan died was called Achor, "trouble." Ahab blames the drought on the man who announced it.
- **"I have not troubled Israel, but you have" (v18).** Elijah hands the charge straight back. The drought is the covenant curse for idolatry (Deut 11:16–17; 28:23–24). Ahab and "your father's house," the dynasty of Omri, have "abandoned the commandments of the LORD and followed the Baals." The plural "Baals" probably refers to Baal in his many local forms.
- **"450 prophets of Baal and the 400 prophets of Asherah, who eat at Jezebel's table" (v19).** Asherah was the Canaanite mother goddess, often paired with Baal. These prophets were on the royal payroll, supported from the queen's own household. That 850 such men existed shows how far Jezebel had pushed. The prophets of Asherah are not mentioned again at the contest. The Greek translation includes them later, but the Hebrew does not, and JFB thinks they found a way not to come.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads Ahab's "troubler" charge through Ahab's own beliefs: if Baal sends rain and Elijah has offended him, Elijah is to blame. Henry answers: "those who cause God's judgments do the mischief, not he that foretells them." Henry also sees Obadiah as proof of how "God raises up friends for his ministers and people" even in a wicked court. Guzik, drawing on Spurgeon, honors Obadiah's courage and on Meyer notes Ahab's care for his horses over his people. JFB calls Obadiah "a secret and sincere worshipper" who stayed at court because he could do good there.

#### The Prophets of Baal Defeated (vv20–40)
- **Mount Carmel (v20).** Carmel is the ridge that runs about 15 miles from the Mediterranean coast (where Haifa is today) southeast toward the Jezreel Valley. It is green and wooded, and catches the sea rains. It sat near the border with Phoenicia and was a known holy place. An altar of the LORD had once stood here (v30), and Baal was also worshiped on its heights. If Baal could win anywhere, it was here. Tradition places the contest at el-Muhraqa, "the place of burning," on the southeastern end of the ridge, with a view over the Kishon and the plain.
- **"How long will you go limping between two different opinions?" (v21).** The verb is *pasach*, "to limp." It is used of Mephibosheth, who became lame (2 Sam 4:4). The same verb appears in v26, where Baal's prophets "limped around the altar" in their ritual dance. Elijah's question has a sting: Israel's wavering is its own clumsy dance between two gods. "Opinions" is a rare word that probably means "branches" or "divided paths." Older translations said "halt between two opinions," using "halt" in its old sense of "limp." The people wanted the LORD *and* Baal, the God of their fathers and the god of the weather. Elijah insists they cannot have both. **"If the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him."**
- **"The people did not answer him a word" (v21).** Their silence says everything. They are not convinced either way, and perhaps afraid of the king.
- **"I, even I only, am left a prophet of the LORD" (v22).** Elijah knows of Obadiah's hundred (v13), so this is not strictly a census. He means that he alone stands publicly on the LORD's side here, one against 450. The feeling of being the last one left will come back to haunt him in 19:10.
- **"The God who answers by fire, he is God" (v24).** This was Baal's own ground. As the storm god, he was pictured holding a thunderbolt. Lightning was his specialty, so a fire-from-heaven test should have been easy. But fire from heaven was also the LORD's sign of accepting a sacrifice (Lev 9:24; Judg 6:21; 1 Chr 21:26; 2 Chr 7:1). The people agree: "It is well spoken."
- **"Choose... and prepare it first, for you are many" (v25).** Elijah gives them every advantage: their pick of the bull, first turn, the whole morning.
- **"O Baal, answer us!" ... But there was no voice, and no one answered (v26).** The Hebrew word for "answer" ('*anah*) runs through the whole scene (vv24, 26, 29, 37). The contest is about which God answers.
- **"Either he is musing, or he is relieving himself, or he is on a journey, or perhaps he is asleep" (v27).** This is biting sarcasm. Baal stories really did picture him traveling, sleeping and dying. The word the ESV renders "relieving himself" most likely means "gone aside," a polite way to say he is in the toilet. Older versions softened it to "he is busy." Elijah is mocking a god made in man's image, with man's needs.
- **"Cut themselves after their custom with swords and lances" (v28).** Ancient texts from Ugarit describe the god El gashing himself in mourning for dead Baal, and self-wounding was a known way of trying to move a god to pity. The law of Moses forbade it for Israel (Lev 19:28; Deut 14:1). The scene is terrible: sincere, bleeding, frantic men, and nothing.
- **"They raved on" (v29).** The word is the same verb used for prophesying, here in a frenzied, ecstatic sense. **"No one answered; no one paid attention."** The triple silence of the verse is the point. A god who does not exist cannot listen.
- **"Until the time of the offering of the oblation" (v29).** This is the evening offering at the temple in Jerusalem, mid-afternoon (Exod 29:38–41). In the northern kingdom, cut off from the temple, Elijah still keeps time by it. His sacrifice will rise at the hour the true worship of God was being offered in Jerusalem.
- **"He repaired the altar of the LORD that had been thrown down" (v30).** Someone had wrecked it, probably in Jezebel's purge. Before Israel can be restored, the worship of God must be restored.
- **"Twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes" (v31).** The northern kingdom had only ten tribes. Elijah builds as if the nation were still one people, the twelve sons of Jacob, the man to whom God said "Israel shall be your name" (Gen 32:28; 35:10). It echoes the twelve stones Joshua set up after crossing the Jordan (Josh 4) and the twelve pillars Moses set at Sinai (Exod 24:4). The division of the kingdom is not the last word in God's eyes.
- **"A trench... as great as would contain two seahs of seed" (v32).** A seah was a dry measure, perhaps about seven quarts. The phrase may describe the volume of the trench, or the area of ground that much seed would sow. Either way, it was a sizable ditch.
- **"Fill four jars with water... a second time... a third time" (vv33–34).** Twelve jars in all, again matching the tribes. Where did water come from after three years of drought? There is a spring near the traditional site that JFB says never fails, and the sea is in sight of Carmel. The point is that no one could later say there was a hidden fire or a trick. Elijah makes the test harder for himself.
- **"O LORD, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (v36).** Elijah's prayer is short, calm and reasoned, in sharp contrast to the hours of shouting before it. He names God by his covenant with the patriarchs, using "Israel" for Jacob as in v31. He asks for three things to be known: that the LORD is God in Israel, that Elijah is his servant, and that "I have done all these things at your word." The whole show was God's idea, not Elijah's.
- **"That you have turned their hearts back" (v37).** The goal is not Elijah's vindication but Israel's repentance. "Answer me, O LORD, answer me" doubles the cry. The verb is again '*anah*.
- **"Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed... the stones and the dust, and licked up the water" (v38).** The fire eats what fire cannot normally eat: rock, dust and water. This is not a natural lightning strike on a wet altar. It echoes the fire that fell at the first sacrifices at the tabernacle (Lev 9:24) and at Solomon's temple (2 Chr 7:1).
- **"The LORD, he is God; the LORD, he is God" (v39).** In Hebrew, *YHWH hu ha-'Elohim*. It is also a play on Elijah's own name, *Eliyahu*, "my God is the LORD." The people finally answer the question of v21. This confession echoes Moses: "the LORD is God; there is no other besides him" (Deut 4:35, 39).
- **"Seize the prophets of Baal; let not one of them escape" (v40).** Elijah executes them at the brook Kishon, the stream that runs along the foot of Carmel. This is hard for modern readers. The law of Moses required death for prophets who led Israel to other gods (Deut 13:1–5; 18:20), and Israel was a nation under covenant law, with God as its King. These men were also Jezebel's agents in killing the LORD's prophets (v4). The New Testament does not give the church the sword in this way. Jesus rebuked James and John when they wanted to call down fire like Elijah (Luke 9:54–55). The Kishon was also where God defeated Sisera's army long before (Judg 4–5).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is dangerous to halt between the service of God and the service of sin." Guzik links the "limping" of v21 to the prophets' limping dance in v26 and passes on Adam Clarke's picture of a bird hopping from branch to branch. He also notes the many readings of Elijah's taunt as crude humor about bodily functions. JFB calls the attempt to blend the worship of God with Baal "an impracticable union" and describes Carmel as "a bold, bluff promontory." On the water, JFB points to a spring below the altar site, while the Tyndale notes allow either the sea or the Kishon. Guzik answers modern theories of a trick, such as hidden fuel, by pointing out that the fire consumed even the stones and water. He also notes that the people's confession was real but short-lived. No lasting revival followed.

#### The LORD Sends Rain (vv41–46)
- **"Go up, eat and drink, for there is a sound of the rushing of rain" (v41).** Ahab has fasted all day watching the contest. Elijah sends him to eat. Elijah hears rain before there is a cloud. He is hearing God's promise of v1.
- **"He bowed himself down on the earth and put his face between his knees" (v42).** Elijah, who stood tall before 450 prophets, now crouches low before God. The posture is the one of deep, humble prayer, and some have noted it resembles a woman in childbirth in the ancient world: Elijah is laboring in prayer for what God has promised. James makes this the model: "he prayed again, and heaven gave rain" (James 5:18).
- **"Go again,' seven times" (v43).** God promised rain, but Elijah still prays, and keeps praying. Promise does not replace prayer. It invites it. Seven, the number of completeness, shows persistence to the end.
- **"A little cloud like a man's hand is rising from the sea" (v44).** From the top of Carmel there is a wide view west over the Mediterranean, where Israel's rain comes from. A tiny cloud on the horizon is enough for Elijah. "Prepare your chariot and go down, lest the rain stop you." The Kishon plain turns to deep mud in heavy rain. That is how Sisera's chariots were swept away (Judg 5:21).
- **"The heavens grew black with clouds and wind, and there was a great rain" (v45).** The drought ends not because Baal woke up but because the LORD spoke. The God who answered by fire also answers by water.
- **"Ahab rode and went to Jezreel" (v45).** Jezreel was Ahab's second capital, a royal residence in the fertile valley about 17 miles from the traditional contest site. It is where Jezebel was waiting.
- **"He gathered up his garment and ran before Ahab" (v46).** Elijah tucked his long robe into his belt, as men did for running or hard work. Royal runners commonly went ahead of a king's chariot (1 Sam 8:11; 2 Sam 15:1). Elijah outruns the horses to the city gate. The "hand of the LORD" was on him. It may be a gesture of respect to the king, offering Ahab a fresh start, as JFB thinks, or a herald going ahead to announce God's victory. Chapter 19 will show that Jezebel had no intention of being won over.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "we must continue earnest in prayer, and not faint or give over," and "God will strengthen his people for every service to which his commandments and providence call them." Guzik, following Meyer, notes that the man who stood so boldly before Ahab bowed so low before God, and that God's promises call us to pray, not to sit back. He sees the small cloud as a picture of the small first signs of revival and passes on Spurgeon's encouragement to watch for them. JFB explains royal runners as a common sight in the East and reads Elijah's run as loyalty to the king, while noting the reform that followed did not last.

#### Pointing to Christ
Carmel asks the question every age must answer: "If the LORD is God, follow him." Elijah stood alone, rebuilt a broken altar for all twelve tribes, and asked God to turn the people's hearts back. Fire fell on the sacrifice instead of on the people. Jesus is the greater prophet who came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17) and beyond him. On the cross, the fire of God's judgment fell on the true sacrifice, offered at the hour of the afternoon offering (Matt 27:46; Acts 3:1), so that sinners could be turned back to God. He did not call down fire on his enemies (Luke 9:55). He took it himself, and then poured out his Spirit like rain on a dry land (Acts 2:17; Isa 44:3).
