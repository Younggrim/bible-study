*Setting:* Hosea is still preaching to the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim, with its capital at Samaria) in its last decades, probably in the 740s or 730s BC, before the Assyrian conquest of 722 BC. Chapter 8 sounded the trumpet: the enemy was coming because Israel had made kings and calves without God. Chapter 9 seems to be spoken at a harvest festival, most likely the Feast of Booths in the autumn, when the grain was threshed, the grapes trodden and the whole nation celebrated. Into the party Hosea steps with a word no one wanted: stop rejoicing. The land you are thanking Baal for is about to be taken away, and with it your festivals, your sacrifices and even your children.

#### The LORD Will Punish Israel (vv1–17)
- **"Rejoice not, O Israel! Exult not like the peoples" (v1).** The Feast of Booths was God's own festival (Deut 16:13–15), a week of joy for the harvest. But Israel was celebrating it "like the peoples," the Canaanite way, as a fertility feast for Baal. Their joy looked religious, but it had been given to the wrong god.
- **"You have loved a prostitute's wages on all threshing floors" (v1).** The picture goes back to Hosea's marriage (chs 1–3). Israel is the wife who thinks her grain and wine are payment from her lovers (2:5, 12). Threshing floors were open, flat places outside a village where grain was beaten out and sifted, and they were scenes of harvest-night revelry and, often, ritual sex in Baal worship. Israel treated the harvest as Baal's fee for her favours.
- **"Threshing floor and wine vat shall not feed them" (v2).** The very things they credited to Baal will fail. This is what God threatened in 2:9: "I will take back my grain in its time, and my wine in its season." The LORD, not Baal, is the giver of rain and harvest, and he can stop it.
- **"They shall not remain in the land of the LORD" (v3).** The land was never Israel's to keep on any terms. It was "the land of the LORD," lent to them under covenant (Lev 25:23: "the land is mine"). Disobedience meant eviction (Lev 18:28; Deut 28:63–64).
- **"Ephraim shall return to Egypt, and they shall eat unclean food in Assyria" (v3).** Egypt is the land of slavery they were rescued from. Going back there undoes the exodus. Some Israelites did flee to Egypt, but the parallel line shows the main point: Assyria will be a new Egypt. JFB: "Assyria was to be a second Egypt to them." In a pagan land they would be "reduced by necessity to eat meats pronounced unclean by the Mosaic law" (compare Ezek 4:13; Dan 1:8).
- **"Like mourners' bread… it shall not come to the house of the LORD" (v4).** Food in a house where someone had died was ritually unclean, because a corpse defiled everything under its roof (Num 19:14; Deut 26:14). In exile all their food would be like that: it would fill their stomachs ("for their hunger only"), but none of it could be offered to God. Without the land and the altar, worship as they knew it would end.
- **"What will you do on the day of the appointed festival?" (v5).** A pointed question asked in the middle of a festival. In exile there would be no feast days, no harvest joy, no pilgrimage. The thing they were enjoying so loudly would simply be gone (2:11).
- **"Egypt shall gather them; Memphis shall bury them" (v6).** Memphis (Hebrew *Moph*) was the old capital of Lower Egypt, south of modern Cairo, and famous for its vast burial grounds, including the pyramids at Saqqara nearby. Those who fled south to escape Assyria would find a grave, not a refuge. **"Nettles shall possess their precious things of silver; thorns shall be in their tents"**: weeds will grow over their homes and buried treasures. Abandoned land goes back to thorns quickly.
- **"The days of punishment have come… Israel shall know it" (v7).** "Punishment" is literally "visitation" (*pequddah*). When God "visits," he comes to set accounts right, for blessing or, as here, for judgment. Israel had refused to "know" the LORD (4:1, 6; 5:4). Now they will know, the hard way.
- **"The prophet is a fool; the man of the spirit is mad" (v7).** "Mad" is *meshugga*, the word the army officers used of the young prophet who came to anoint Jehu: "Why did this mad fellow come to you?" (2 Kgs 9:11). Two readings are possible. Many take this as the people's sneer: they mocked Hosea as a crazy man, because no one could believe disaster was coming in a bumper harvest. Others, including JFB, take it as God's verdict on the false prophets who promised peace: when judgment arrives, they will be shown up as fools. Either way, the cause is the same: "your great iniquity and great hatred." Hostility to God shows itself in hostility to his messengers.
- **"The prophet is the watchman of Ephraim with my God" (v8).** A watchman stood on the wall to warn of danger (Ezek 3:17; 33:1–7). The Hebrew is compressed and hard, but the ESV reads it as Hosea's own calling: the true prophet stands guard for the nation alongside God. Yet "a fowler's snare is on all his ways": the people lay traps for him, as a bird-catcher sets nets, and there is "hatred in the house of his God," even in the place of worship (compare Amos 7:10–13, where the priest at Bethel orders Amos to leave).
- **"As in the days of Gibeah" (v9).** This reaches back to one of the darkest stories in the Bible (Judges 19–21). Men of Gibeah, in Benjamin, gang-raped a Levite's concubine until she died, and the town defended them. The result was civil war that nearly wiped out a tribe. Hosea says Israel's corruption has sunk that low again (10:9 repeats the charge). **"He will remember their iniquity"** is the opposite of the forgiveness God longs to give (Jer 31:34).
- **"Like grapes in the wilderness, I found Israel" (v10).** The tone suddenly softens into memory. Finding grapes in the desert is an unexpected delight to a thirsty traveller, and the first figs of the season were a prized treat (Isa 28:4; Mic 7:1). This is how God felt when he took Israel as his people in the wilderness (compare Jer 2:2; Ezek 16:6–14). Hosea's book keeps returning to God's love at the start of the relationship (2:14–15; 11:1).
- **"But they came to Baal-peor" (v10).** Even before entering the land, at the edge of Moab, Israelite men joined in sexual rites with Moabite women and "yoked" themselves to Baal of Peor (Num 25:1–9). Twenty-four thousand died in the plague that followed. Hosea's generation is doing the same thing on a national scale.
- **"Consecrated themselves to the thing of shame" (v10).** "Consecrated" is the root of *Nazirite*, someone set apart to God (Num 6). Israel set itself apart to Baal instead. "The thing of shame" is *bosheth*, a word the prophets substituted for Baal's name (Jer 3:24; 11:13), refusing to give the idol its title. Some Israelite names were even rewritten this way: Saul's son Eshbaal appears as Ish-bosheth (2 Sam 2:8; 1 Chr 8:33).
- **"And became detestable like the thing they loved" (v10).** One of the Bible's sharpest statements of a basic truth: people become like what they worship. Psalm 115:8 says of idols, "Those who make them become like them." The word is *shiqquts*, an abomination, used for idols themselves (1 Kgs 11:5).
- **"Ephraim's glory shall fly away like a bird" (v11).** In this chapter Israel's "glory" is its children and its fruitfulness. **"No birth, no pregnancy, no conception!"** runs the process backwards, from delivery to the womb to the first moment. The irony is bitter: Baal worship was meant to secure fertility, and it will end in barrenness. The name Ephraim itself sounds like "fruitful" (Gen 41:52), and Hosea plays on that through the chapter.
- **"Woe to them when I depart from them!" (v12).** This is the worst part of the judgment. Losing the harvest, the feasts and even children is terrible, but behind them all is the loss of God himself. Compare 5:15: "I will return again to my place, until they acknowledge their guilt." Ezekiel later saw God's glory leave the temple (Ezek 10–11).
- **"Like a young palm planted in a meadow" (v13).** The Hebrew of the first line is difficult; some versions read "like Tyre." The ESV follows a reading that pictures Ephraim as well-placed and flourishing, like a tree in a good pasture. That makes the second line all the more grievous: "Ephraim must lead his children out to slaughter." Assyrian warfare was notoriously brutal to the young (13:16; 2 Kgs 8:12).
- **"Give them, O LORD—what will you give?" (v14).** Hosea starts to pray and stops mid-sentence. What can a prophet ask for a people under this sentence? His answer, "a miscarrying womb and dry breasts," sounds harsh, but it is the cry of a man who knows what is coming. Better no children at all than children born only to be slaughtered. Jesus said something similar on the way to the cross: "Blessed are the barren and the wombs that never bore" (Luke 23:29).
- **"Every evil of theirs is in Gilgal; there I began to hate them" (v15).** Gilgal, near Jericho, was a place of beginnings: Israel's first camp in the land, where they were circumcised and kept Passover (Josh 4:19–5:10). It was also where Saul was made king and where he was rejected for disobedience (1 Sam 11:15; 13:8–14; 15:12–23). In Hosea's day it had become a busy shrine of false worship (4:15; 12:11; Amos 4:4). "Hate" here is covenant language for rejection, as in Malachi 1:2–3. It is not a temper but a judicial decision: God sets himself against what they have become.
- **"I will drive them out of my house. I will love them no more" (v15).** The language may echo a husband sending away an unfaithful wife, and "house" can mean God's land as well as his household (8:1). "Love them no more" recalls the name of Hosea's daughter, *Lo-ruhamah*, "No Mercy" (1:6). **"All their princes are rebels"** is a pun in Hebrew: *sarehem sorerim*, their princes are rebels (the same verb as "stubborn" in 4:16 and Deut 21:18).
- **"Ephraim is stricken; their root is dried up; they shall bear no fruit" (v16).** Another play on Ephraim's name: the "fruitful" one bears no fruit. The tree that looked like a palm in a meadow (v13) is blighted at the root.
- **"My God will reject them… they shall be wanderers among the nations" (v17).** Hosea speaks in his own voice: "*my* God." The LORD is still Hosea's God, though Israel has made itself no longer his people (1:9). "Wanderers" fulfils the curse of Deuteronomy 28:64–65, and it reminds the reader of Cain, sentenced to be "a fugitive and a wanderer on the earth" (Gen 4:12). After 722 BC the northern tribes were scattered across the Assyrian empire and largely disappeared from history as a distinct people.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and the Tyndale notes both place the chapter at a harvest festival given over to Baal. Henry sees in v7 the moment when "Israel shall know the difference between true prophets and false," and comments that "the best things, when corrupted, become the worst." JFB reads v7–8 as aimed at false prophets who promised safety, while Guzik takes v7 as the people's mockery of Hosea; the Hebrew allows both. On v14 Guzik pictures Hosea beginning to pray in anger, stopping short, and then asking for barrenness as a kind of mercy, and he warns that zeal for God can curdle into bitterness toward people. JFB agrees on the mercy: "Of two evils he chooses the least." JFB also notes the pun on "princes" and "rebels" in v15 and calls God's hatred holy opposition to sin, not human passion. Guzik closes by setting "he will remember their iniquity" (v9) against the new covenant promise that God will remember sins no more (Heb 8:12).

#### Pointing to Christ
Hosea 9 is about a people who had everything and lost it: land, feasts, altar, children and finally the presence of God. The grief of the chapter is that God once found them like grapes in the desert. The gospel does not cancel that grief, but it answers it. Jesus is the true Israel who did not consecrate himself to shame but to his Father (John 17:19), and on the cross he took the curse of being driven out and forsaken (Mark 15:34) so that wanderers could be brought home. What Israel heard at Gilgal, "I will love them no more," Paul hears reversed in Hosea's own words: "those who were not my people I will call 'my people'" (Rom 9:25). The God who said "he will remember their iniquity" now says, in Christ, "I will remember their sins no more" (Heb 8:12).
