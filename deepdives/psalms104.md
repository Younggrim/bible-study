*Setting:* Psalm 104 has no title in the Hebrew. The Greek and Latin translations attribute it to David, and since it opens and closes with the same words as Psalm 103 ("Bless the LORD, O my soul"), many readers, Henry among them, take the two as a pair by one author. Henry puts the difference well: in Psalm 103 "God is there praised as the God of grace, here as the God of nature." This is the Bible's great creation hymn. It walks roughly through the days of Genesis 1, from light and sky to land and sea, plants, sun and moon, animals and people, but it is not a retelling of creation week. It praises God for a world he made and still keeps running, day by day and breath by breath. Scholars have long noticed resemblances between parts of it (especially vv20–30) and the Egyptian *Great Hymn to the Aten*, written about 1350 BC. If the psalmist knew that kind of poetry, he turned it on its head: the sun is not a god but a creature that "knows its time for setting" (v19). The psalm also quietly answers Canaanite religion, which credited Baal with taming the sea and sending rain. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement through creation.

#### You Are Clothed with Splendor and Majesty (vv1–4)
- **"O LORD my God, you are very great!" (v1).** The psalm begins with God himself before it turns to his works. "LORD" is the covenant name, *Yahweh*, the God of Israel, not a vague divine power. **"Clothed with splendor and majesty"** uses words usually said of kings (Ps 45:3). God's glory is pictured as royal robes.
- **"Covering yourself with light as with a garment" (v2).** Light came first in Genesis 1:3, and here it is God's clothing. Even light, the brightest thing anyone knows, only covers him. Paul says God "dwells in unapproachable light" (1 Tim 6:16), and at the Transfiguration Jesus' clothes "became white as light" (Matt 17:2). JFB calls it "a figurative representation of the glory of the invisible God."
- **"Stretching out the heavens like a tent" (v2).** The sky is spread over the earth as easily as a nomad pitches a tent (Isa 40:22). This matches the second day of creation, when God made the expanse (Gen 1:6–8).
- **"He lays the beams of his chambers on the waters" (v3).** God's upper rooms are built above the waters of the sky, the "waters above the expanse" of Genesis 1:7. Amos uses the same picture: God "builds his upper chambers in the heavens" (Amos 9:6). These are poetic images, not a diagram of the universe.
- **"He makes the clouds his chariot; he rides on the wings of the wind" (v3).** Canaanite texts called Baal "the rider on the clouds." Israel's psalms take that title for the LORD (Ps 68:4; 18:10; Isa 19:1). The storm is not Baal's power. It is the LORD's vehicle. In Daniel 7:13 "one like a son of man" comes "with the clouds of heaven," and Jesus took that image for himself (Mark 14:62).
- **"He makes his messengers winds, his ministers a flaming fire" (v4).** The Hebrew words can be read both ways round, because *mal'akh* means both "messenger" and "angel," and *ruach* means both "wind" and "spirit." The ESV reads it as God using winds and lightning as his messengers. The Greek translation reads "who makes his angels spirits, and his ministers a flame of fire," and Hebrews 1:7 quotes it that way to show that angels are servants, while the Son is enthroned forever (Heb 1:8). Both readings make the same point: everything in the sky, natural or spiritual, serves God.
- **Commentators:** Henry heads this section "the splendour of his majesty in the upper world." JFB reads "walketh" (KJV) in v3 as simply "moveth," and says Hebrews cites v4 "to denote the subordinate position of angels." Guzik passes on Maclaren's line that the universe is God's garment, and uses Hebrews 1:7 to argue for the deity of Christ, since the angels are made while the Son is addressed as God. The Tyndale notes read vv2–4 against the first two days of creation.

#### He Set the Earth on Its Foundations (vv5–9)
- **"He set the earth on its foundations, so that it should never be moved" (v5).** The earth is pictured as a building on firm footings (Job 38:4–6). The point is stability: the world is not at the mercy of chaos. Some later readers took verses like this to mean the earth cannot rotate, and Galileo's critics cited them, but the psalm is poetry about God's dependable rule, not a statement about planetary motion.
- **"You covered it with the deep as with a garment" (v6).** "The deep" (*tehom*) is the word from Genesis 1:2, "darkness was over the face of the deep." In the beginning the earth was wrapped in water, as God is wrapped in light (v2). **"The waters stood above the mountains."**
- **"At your rebuke they fled; at the sound of your thunder they took to flight" (v7).** In Canaanite and Babylonian myths the sea is a rival god who must be fought and defeated. In the Bible the waters are not a rival at all. God only has to speak sharply and they run. Jesus "rebuked the wind" and said to the sea, "Peace! Be still!" (Mark 4:39), the same authority.
- **"The mountains rose, the valleys sank down to the place that you appointed for them" (v8).** The Hebrew can also be read with the waters as the subject ("they went up the mountains, they went down the valleys"), as the KJV does. Either way, land and water go where God assigns.
- **"You set a boundary that they may not pass, so that they might not again cover the earth" (v9).** The sea has limits. God told Job, "Thus far shall you come, and no farther" (Job 38:8–11; see Jer 5:22). "Not again cover the earth" recalls God's promise after the flood (Gen 9:11–15).
- **Creation or flood?** Readers differ on what vv6–9 describe. Most take them as the third day of creation, when God gathered the waters and let dry land appear (Gen 1:9–10). JFB reads them as the flood and its going down ("God's method of arresting the flood"). Guzik sees both events in view, the original separation and the flood that undid it for a time. The language of v9 ("not again") fits the flood, while the order of the psalm fits Genesis 1.
- **Commentators:** Henry heads this section "the creation of the sea and the dry land." JFB notes, "The earth is firmly fixed by His power." The Tyndale notes add that God's rule over sea and land was a direct answer to Baal worship, since Canaanites celebrated Baal's victory over the waters. Guzik connects the rebuke of v7 to Jesus calming the storm.

#### You Make Springs Gush Forth (vv10–18)
- **"You make springs gush forth in the valleys" (v10).** The same waters that once covered the mountains now serve life. JFB makes the contrast: the waters that destroyed now sustain. In a dry land like Israel, a spring was a gift worth singing about.
- **"They give drink to every beast of the field; the wild donkeys quench their thirst" (v11).** The wild donkey was the image of an untamed creature that no one owned or fed (Job 39:5–8). God waters it anyway. His care reaches animals that are of no use to anyone.
- **"Beside them the birds of the heavens dwell; they sing among the branches" (v12).** Trees grow along watercourses, and birds nest there. The psalmist hears their singing as part of creation's praise.
- **"From your lofty abode you water the mountains" (v13).** Springs below, rain from above. Canaanites thanked Baal for rain. The psalmist thanks the LORD, whose "lofty abode" is the upper chambers of v3. **"The earth is satisfied with the fruit of your work."**
- **"Grass to grow for the livestock and plants for man to cultivate" (v14).** God provides for tame animals and for people, but people must work for their food. "Cultivate" is literally "for the service of man," and the Tyndale notes see human beings as managers of creation (Gen 2:15). God gives the growth and people do the farming.
- **"Wine to gladden the heart of man, oil to make his face shine and bread to strengthen man's heart" (v15).** These three were the staples of Israel's farms (Deut 7:13). Bread keeps people alive. Wine and olive oil go beyond survival to joy: oil soothed sun-dried skin and was used at feasts (Ps 23:5). God gives not only what is necessary but what is glad. Scripture also warns against abusing wine (Prov 20:1; 23:29–35), and the Tyndale notes say both can be misused, but here it is a gift.
- **"The trees of the LORD are watered abundantly, the cedars of Lebanon that he planted" (v16).** No one planted the great cedar forests of Lebanon. They were "the LORD's trees." "Watered abundantly" is literally "are satisfied," and the KJV adds "full of sap," a word not in the Hebrew (Guzik notes this).
- **"In them the birds build their nests; the stork has her home in the fir trees" (v17).** Storks migrate through Israel each year in huge numbers. The Tyndale notes say they come down from Europe and winter in the region. Jeremiah notes that "the stork in the heavens knows her times" (Jer 8:7).
- **"The high mountains are for the wild goats; the rocks are a refuge for the rock badgers" (v18).** The wild goat is the Nubian ibex, still seen around En-gedi by the Dead Sea. The "rock badger" (*shaphan*) is the rock hyrax, a rabbit-sized animal that lives among rocks. "Badger" is an old guess and the hyrax is the better identification (Prov 30:26). Every creature has a place made for it, the high cliffs as much as the farmland.
- **Commentators:** Henry groups vv10–18 with vv27–28 as "the provision he makes for the maintenance of all the creatures according to their nature." JFB reads the oil that makes the face shine as something that cheers and invigorates, and sees vv16–19 extending God's care to wild creatures and untilled lands. Guzik, citing Poole, notes that the wild donkey shows God caring even for creatures that will not be tamed, and says the songs of birds should shame humans who do not give thanks.

#### He Made the Moon to Mark the Seasons (vv19–23)
- **"He made the moon to mark the seasons" (v19).** This matches the fourth day of creation (Gen 1:14–18). Israel's calendar was lunar: months began at the new moon, and the great feasts were fixed by it. "Seasons" (*mo'adim*) means appointed times, including the feasts. The moon is named first because the Hebrew day began at sunset (Gen 1:5, "evening and morning").
- **"The sun knows its time for setting" (v19).** In Egypt the sun was the chief god, and the Aten hymn praises the sun disk as the source of all life. In this psalm the sun is a servant that knows its schedule. Genesis 1:16 does not even name it, calling it only "the greater light."
- **"You make darkness, and it is night, when all the beasts of the forest creep about" (v20).** Darkness is not an enemy force. God makes it, and it has a purpose: it is the hunting time for wild animals.
- **"The young lions roar for their prey, seeking their food from God" (v21).** Lions lived in Israel in Bible times (Judg 14:5; 1 Sam 17:34). Their roar sounds like menace, but the psalmist hears it as a prayer. Even predators depend on God for food (Job 38:39–41).
- **"When the sun rises, they steal away and lie down in their dens" (v22).** Night belongs to the animals, day to people, and the two take turns. God has arranged the world so they mostly keep out of each other's way.
- **"Man goes out to his work and to his labor until the evening" (v23).** Human work has a place in God's order. It is not a curse in itself, since Adam was given work before the fall (Gen 2:15). The day ends at evening, and rest is built in too.
- **Commentators:** Henry heads this section "the regular course of the sun and moon." JFB sees God ordering times and seasons to meet human needs. Guzik, citing Caryl through Spurgeon, notes that even the strongest creatures cannot feed themselves, and passes on Maclaren's point that the moon comes first because Israel's day began in the evening.

#### O LORD, How Manifold Are Your Works! (vv24–30)
- **"O LORD, how manifold are your works! In wisdom have you made them all" (v24).** The psalmist stops to wonder. "Manifold" means many and varied. "Wisdom" is the skill of a master craftsman (Prov 3:19; 8:22–31). The world is not random. It fits together, land and sea, night and day, lion and man.
- **"Here is the sea, great and wide, which teems with creatures innumerable" (v25).** This matches the fifth day of creation, when "the waters swarm with swarms of living creatures" (Gen 1:20–21). Israelites were not seafarers by habit and many feared the sea, but here it is full of life rather than chaos.
- **"There go the ships, and Leviathan, which you formed to play in it" (v26).** Ships were a marvel of the ancient world. Leviathan appears elsewhere as a fearsome sea monster, a symbol of chaos that God crushes (Ps 74:14; Isa 27:1; Job 41). Here it is a creature God "formed to play," almost God's pet, as the Tyndale notes put it. Some identify it with a whale or crocodile, and the word may cover any great sea creature. Whatever it is, the thing other nations feared as a god is, to Israel, God's plaything. Some read "play in it" as "play with it," that is, God playing with Leviathan (Job 41:5).
- **"These all look to you, to give them their food in due season" (v27).** All the creatures, land and sea, wait on God like servants at a master's table. Psalm 145:15–16 repeats this almost exactly, and Jesus says the heavenly Father feeds the birds (Matt 6:26).
- **"When you give it to them, they gather it up; when you open your hand, they are filled with good things" (v28).** God gives, and creatures gather. "Gather up" (*laqat*) is the word used for the Israelites gathering manna (Exod 16:4–5). God's provision has to be picked up, but it is God who scatters it.
- **"When you hide your face, they are dismayed; when you take away their breath, they die and return to their dust" (v29).** Life depends on God from moment to moment. "Breath" is *ruach*. When God withdraws it, creatures go back to the dust they came from (Gen 3:19; Eccl 12:7; Job 34:14–15).
- **"When you send forth your Spirit, they are created, and you renew the face of the ground" (v30).** The same word *ruach* comes back, now translated "Spirit." What God takes away in v29 he sends out in v30. English must choose between "breath" and "Spirit," but Hebrew uses one word, and the overlap is the point: the breath of every living creature is God's gift, and God's Spirit gives life (Gen 1:2; 2:7; Ezek 37:9–10). "Created" is *bara*, the verb of Genesis 1:1, used only of God's creating. Every spring, every newborn animal, is God creating again. The church has long sung this verse at Pentecost.
- **Commentators:** Henry groups vv27–28 with the earlier provision section, and heads vv29–32 "God's sovereign power over all the creatures." JFB says that for God, "to kill or make alive is equally easy," and that his breath or word is enough to renew the earth. Guzik passes on Caryl's saying, through Spurgeon, that providence is creation continued, and illustrates v28 with Spurgeon's picture of chickens picking up corn scattered for them. The Tyndale notes take v30 to show the Spirit as the agent of renewal, looking ahead to new creation.

#### May the Glory of the LORD Endure Forever (vv31–35)
- **"May the glory of the LORD endure forever; may the LORD rejoice in his works" (v31).** God looked at what he made and "it was very good" (Gen 1:31). The psalmist prays that God will go on delighting in it. It is a bold thought: God taking pleasure in grass, storks and hyraxes. Isaiah looks ahead to God rejoicing in his new creation (Isa 65:17–19).
- **"Who looks on the earth and it trembles, who touches the mountains and they smoke!" (v32).** The gentle Provider is also the awesome God. A glance makes the earth quake, and a touch sets mountains smoking, as Sinai did when God came down on it (Exod 19:18). The Tyndale notes read this as God coming in judgment and salvation (Ps 18:7–15).
- **"I will sing to the LORD as long as I live" (v33).** The proper response to creation is lifelong praise. The psalmist does not plan to stop until his own breath (v29) is taken.
- **"May my meditation be pleasing to him, for I rejoice in the LORD" (v34).** He hopes that his reflections on creation, this psalm itself, will please God as the creatures do. The psalmist rejoices in God as God rejoices in his works (v31).
- **"Let sinners be consumed from the earth, and let the wicked be no more!" (v35).** This line can feel jarring after so much beauty. It is the one dark thread in the world the psalm describes. Sin is the only thing in creation that does not fit God's good order. The prayer is not personal revenge but a longing for the world to be what God made it to be, cleared of everything that spoils it. Revelation ends with exactly that (Rev 21:1–5, 27).
- **"Bless the LORD, O my soul! Praise the LORD!" (v35).** "Praise the LORD" is *Hallelu-Yah*, and this is its first appearance in the Psalms. It will close the next psalm too, and ring through Psalms 146–150.
- **Commentators:** Henry ends with "a pleasant and firm resolution to continue praising God." JFB says sinners "spoil" God's creation by refusing him his due, and that "Praise ye the LORD" is "peculiar to Psalms composed after the captivity." That dating is JFB's view, not a firm rule. Guzik notes that the psalm praises God as Creator without yet speaking of redemption, and that creation alone gives enough reason to praise for a lifetime. Citing VanGemeren, he says v35 is not vindictive but longs for a world fully ordered by the Lord, and he connects it to Romans 1, where people suppress what creation shows about God.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 104 sings of a world made by God's word and wisdom and kept alive by his breath. The New Testament names that Word and Wisdom: "All things were made through him" (John 1:3), and "in him all things hold together" (Col 1:17). The one who rides the clouds and rebukes the waters walked on the sea and stilled the storm with a word, and his disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41). The God who sends his *ruach* to renew the face of the ground sent his Spirit at Pentecost to make a new people, and the psalm's last prayer, that sin be removed from the earth, is answered at the cross and finished at Christ's return, when creation itself "will be set free from its bondage to corruption" (Rom 8:21).
