*Setting:* Genesis means "beginning," and it is the first of the five books of the Law, the Pentateuch. Jewish and Christian tradition, and Jesus himself (Mark 12:26; Luke 24:44), connect these books with Moses, writing for Israel after the exodus, around the 15th or 13th century BC depending on how the exodus is dated. Many scholars think the books were shaped and edited over a longer period from older sources, and most evangelical scholars hold that Moses stands behind them while allowing for later editorial touches. Either way, Genesis 1 was first heard by a people just out of Egypt, surrounded by nations who worshiped the sun, the moon, the sea, the Nile and the stars, and who told creation stories full of warring gods. Into that world this chapter says something astonishing in its calm: there is one God, he made everything, he made it by his word, and he made it good. The ESV treats 1:1–2:3 as one section, so the seventh day, the climax of the week, belongs with the first verses of chapter 2.

#### How Christians read Genesis 1
Faithful Christians who hold Genesis to be God's true word read the "days" in several ways:
- **Six ordinary days:** God created in six 24-hour days, fairly recently. This is the traditional reading of most of church history and is taught by Guzik.
- **Day-age:** each "day" is a long age, fitting the text with an old earth.
- **Framework:** the six days are a literary structure, two sets of three, telling *that* God made everything and in what order of meaning, not a timetable.
- **Analogical days:** God's workdays are described on the pattern of a human work week, so Israel could copy it (Exod 20:11), without fixing their length.
- **Gap theory:** a long gap lies between vv1 and 2. This was popular in the 19th and early 20th centuries and is allowed by JFB, but most scholars, including Guzik, now reject it on grammatical grounds.

Many scholars also stress that ancient readers cared most about *function* and *order*. On that reading, Genesis 1 is chiefly about God giving each part of the world its place and purpose, and it doesn't answer every modern scientific question. These views disagree about the days, but they agree on what matters most: God alone is Creator, the world is not divine, humans bear his image, and creation was very good.

#### The Creation of the World (vv1–31)
- **"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth" (v1).** The Bible's first sentence names its main character without argument or introduction: God. It doesn't try to prove him. It starts with him. "The heavens and the earth" is a Hebrew way of saying "everything." Most translations take v1 as a headline over the whole account. Some take it as the first act of creation, and a few as a time clause ("When God began to create…"), but the ESV's reading is the oldest and most common.
- **"God" (v1).** The Hebrew is *Elohim*, a plural form that normally takes a singular verb when it means Israel's God, as it does here ("God created," singular). Most Hebrew scholars take it as a plural of majesty or fullness. Many Christian readers, including JFB, have seen it as leaving room for the later revelation of the Trinity, though it doesn't prove it.
- **"Created" (v1).** *Bara* is used in the Old Testament only with God as the subject. No human ever does it. It is used of the universe (1:1), the great sea creatures (1:21) and humanity (1:27, three times). The lexicon shows it can also mean transforming something already there ("create in me a clean heart," Ps 51:10; "I create Jerusalem to be a joy," Isa 65:18). So the word itself doesn't mean "out of nothing," but the context and the rest of Scripture teach that God made everything from nothing (Heb 11:3; John 1:3; Rom 4:17).
- **"Without form and void" (v2).** *Tohu wabohu*, a rhyming phrase like "waste and wild." The earth isn't evil, just unfinished: unshaped and empty. The rest of the chapter fixes both problems. Days 1–3 *form* it and days 4–6 *fill* it. Jeremiah uses the same rare pair to picture the land undone by judgment (Jer 4:23), and Isaiah says God did not create the earth to be *tohu* (Isa 45:18).
- **"Darkness was over the face of the deep" (v2).** "The deep" is *tehom*, the primeval ocean. Some scholars link it to Tiamat, the sea-goddess the Babylonian god Marduk slew to make the world in the epic *Enuma Elish*. If the echo is there, Genesis empties it. The deep is no goddess and puts up no fight. It is just water waiting for God's word.
- **"The Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters" (v2).** "Spirit" is *ruach*, which also means breath or wind, and some translate "a wind from God." "Hovering" (*rachaph*) is used again only in Deuteronomy 32:11, of an eagle hovering over its young. The picture is of the Spirit poised over creation, protective and ready to bring life. Christians have long seen the Spirit at work in creation here (Ps 104:30), just as he would later "overshadow" Mary (Luke 1:35) and give new birth (John 3:5).
- **"And God said" (v3).** This phrase comes ten times in the chapter. God makes the world by speaking. There is no struggle, no raw material wrestled into shape, no rival. "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made" (Ps 33:6). John opens his Gospel on purpose with "In the beginning was the Word… all things were made through him" (John 1:1–3).
- **"Let there be light," and there was light (v3).** Light comes on day one, before the sun on day four. Some say God himself was the light, as he will be at the end (Rev 21:23; 22:5). Others, like JFB, think the sun already existed and its light now broke through to the earth's surface. Either way, the order teaches that light comes from God, not from the sun, which Egypt worshiped as the god Ra. Paul draws on this verse for conversion: "God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts" (2 Cor 4:6).
- **"God saw that the light was good" (v4).** The phrase "it was good" comes six times, and the seventh time it becomes "very good" (v31). God takes pleasure in what he makes. Matter, bodies, food and the physical world are not bad or second-rate.
- **"God called the light Day" (v5).** In the ancient world, naming something showed authority over it. God names day, night, heaven, earth and seas. Later he lets Adam name the animals (2:19).
- **"Evening and… morning, the first day" (v5).** Hebrew days ran from sunset to sunset, which is why Israel's Sabbath still begins at evening. The word *yom* can mean daylight (as earlier in this verse), a 24-hour day, or a longer period ("in the day that the LORD God made," 2:4). The readings above differ on which is meant here.
- **"An expanse in the midst of the waters" (vv6–8).** "Expanse" is *raqia*, from a verb meaning to beat out or spread, as metal is hammered flat. The Greek translation rendered it *stereōma*, the Latin *firmamentum*, hence the KJV's "firmament." The lexicon describes it as the vault of heaven, which ancient Hebrews pictured as solid. Others, like JFB, take it as the atmosphere. Either way, Genesis describes the sky as it looks from the ground, as Scripture usually does (we still say "sunrise"). Day two is the only day without "it was good," probably because the work of separating the waters isn't finished until day three.
- **"Let the dry land appear" (vv9–10).** In the myths of Israel's neighbors the sea was a hostile power. Here God simply sets its limits. Job 38:8–11 and Psalm 104:9 celebrate those boundaries, and the flood (Gen 7:11) is creation briefly run in reverse.
- **"Each according to its kind" (vv11–12).** This phrase comes ten times in the chapter. Life is ordered, and plants and animals reproduce their own kind. The Hebrew *min*, "kind," is not the same as the modern "species," and Christians differ on how much variety it allows.
- **"Lights in the expanse… for signs and for seasons" (vv14–19).** On day four God fills the spaces of day one. The sun and moon aren't even named. They are "the greater light" and "the lesser light," almost certainly on purpose, since the Hebrew words for sun and moon were also names of gods. They are lamps hung by God, not gods to be feared. **"Seasons"** (*mo'adim*) is the word later used for Israel's appointed feasts (Lev 23:2), so the sky becomes Israel's calendar. **"And the stars"** is added almost as an afterthought, in a world where nations read the stars to know the future (Isa 47:13). God made them in half a sentence.
- **"The great sea creatures" (v21).** *Tanninim* are elsewhere sea monsters or dragons (Isa 27:1; Ps 74:13). In other ancient stories these were cosmic enemies. Here they are just creatures, made by God, and *bara* is used for them, as if to make the point. Psalm 104:26 even pictures Leviathan playing in the sea.
- **"God blessed them, saying, 'Be fruitful and multiply'" (v22).** This is the first blessing in the Bible. Fertility comes from God's word, not from the fertility rites of Baal and Asherah that would later tempt Israel.
- **Days 1–3 and 4–6 (vv3–25).** The two sets of days match. Light (day 1) is filled by the sun, moon and stars (day 4). Sky and sea (day 2) are filled with birds and fish (day 5). Land and plants (day 3) are filled with animals and people (day 6). The framework view builds on this pattern, though every reading recognizes it.
- **"Let us make man in our image, after our likeness" (v26).** The rhythm changes. Instead of "Let there be," God speaks as if in deliberation, "Let *us*." Four main explanations are given:
  - **The Trinity:** Father, Son and Spirit speak together. This is the historic Christian reading, held by Henry and Guzik.
  - **The heavenly court:** God speaks to the angels around his throne (1 Kings 22:19–22; Isa 6:8). The Tyndale notes favor this.
  - **A plural of majesty**, like a king's "we."
  - **Self-deliberation**, like "let's see."
  - Against the second view, people are made in "*our* image," and Scripture never says angels share in creating. Against the first, the original hearers would not yet have known the doctrine of the Trinity. Many Christians hold that the verse is fully explained only by later revelation (John 1:3; Col 1:16).
- **"Image" and "likeness" (v26).** *Tselem* means a statue or image, often an idol (2 Kings 11:18). *Demuth* means a likeness or resemblance. The two words largely overlap. In the ancient world, a king set up his image in conquered lands to show who ruled there, and only kings were called the image of a god. Genesis gives that title to every human being. What the image *is* has been debated:
  - our reason, conscience and capacity for relationship with God (Henry, Guzik);
  - our role as God's representatives ruling his world (the next phrase, "and let them have dominion");
  - our relationships, mirroring the relationship within God ("male and female");
  - most likely, all of these together.
- **"Male and female he created them" (v27).** This is the first poetry in the Bible: three lines, and *bara* three times. "Man" here is *adam*, humanity as a whole. The image belongs to both men and women equally. This is why murder is so serious (9:6) and why even cursing a person is wrong (Jas 3:9).
- **"Fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion" (v28).** Humanity is given a job: to spread over the earth, bring order to it and care for it as God's stewards, as royal representatives rather than owners. Dominion is not license to abuse. It is ruling the way God rules, and Psalm 8 marvels at it. Hebrews 2:5–9 says we don't yet see everything under humanity's feet, "but we see… Jesus."
- **"Every plant yielding seed… You shall have them for food" (vv29–30).** Plants are given as food for both people and animals. Meat is first explicitly permitted after the flood (9:3). Many read this as a world without violence or killing, and Isaiah's picture of the wolf and the lamb (Isa 11:6–9) looks back to it. Others think it simply highlights God's generous provision without ruling out that animals died before the fall. Christians who differ on the age of the earth tend to differ here.
- **"Behold, it was very good" (v31).** After people are made, the verdict rises to "very good." The word "behold" invites readers to look with God. Notably, the sixth day alone has "the" in Hebrew: "*the* sixth day," setting it apart as the high point.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv1–2 "the work of creation in its epitome and in its embryo," and writes of the Spirit, "Dead matter would be for ever dead if he did not quicken it." On v26 he says, "the word of command is turned into a word of consultation," and that "the three persons of the Trinity, Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, consult about it and concur in it." He places the image "in knowledge, righteousness, and true holiness," adding that man reflects God "only as the shadow in the glass, or the king's impress upon the coin," and that dominion "is very much diminished and lost by the fall." JFB reads "in the beginning" as "a period of remote and unknown antiquity" and allows that the earth lay waste "for ages perhaps" before the six days, which it takes as natural days. It explains the sun and moon as "unveiled for the first time" on day four, not newly made, and the firmament as the atmosphere. It locates the image chiefly in "original righteousness." Guzik reads the days as ordinary days and rejects both the gap theory and the day-age view. He says *bara* means creating from nothing, though the lexicon shows the word can also describe transforming what already exists, so that point rests more on the context and other Scriptures than on the word. He sees the image of God in personality, moral conscience and spiritual capacity, and draws out its implication: every human life has dignity. The Tyndale notes say that Genesis 1 was first of all teaching Israel that the world was made by the one true God and not by the gods of the nations around them.

#### Pointing to Christ
The New Testament doesn't leave Genesis 1 behind. It goes back to it. John's Gospel starts with "In the beginning" and names the Word through whom "all things were made" (John 1:1–3). Paul calls Christ "the image of the invisible God… by him all things were created" (Col 1:15–16), the true image that humanity was made to be and failed to be. The God who said "Let there be light" shines in the face of Jesus Christ (2 Cor 4:6), and in him anyone is "a new creation" (2 Cor 5:17). The Bible that begins with a very good world made by the Word ends with that world made new by the Lamb (Rev 21:5).
