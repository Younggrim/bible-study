*Setting:* First John is a letter without a greeting, a signature or a named recipient. From the second century on, the church has received it as the work of the apostle John, the son of Zebedee, and its vocabulary is so close to John's Gospel (light and darkness, life, truth, abiding, "from the beginning") that most readers hear the same voice. Tradition places John in Ephesus late in life, and the letter is usually dated around AD 85–95, to churches in the province of Asia (western Turkey today) that he had cared for over many years. Something painful had just happened. A group had left the fellowship (2:19), and they were still trying to win over those who stayed (2:26). They claimed special knowledge of God, played down sin, and denied that "Jesus Christ has come in the flesh" (4:2). John writes to steady those left behind, so that they "may know" they have eternal life (5:13). Chapter 1 begins where the Gospel began, at "the beginning," and then sets out three false claims about sin that the rest of the letter will keep answering.

#### The Word of Life (vv1–4)
- **A sentence that tumbles out (vv1–3).** In Greek, verses 1–3 are one long, breathless sentence. The main verb, "we proclaim," does not arrive until verse 3. John piles up what he and the other eyewitnesses experienced before he can bring himself to say what he is doing with it. The ESV's dashes show the break in the middle where he stops to explain (v2) and then starts over.
- **"That which was from the beginning" (v1).** This echoes the first words of the Gospel, "In the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1), which in turn echo Genesis 1:1. John starts not with a birth in Bethlehem but before time. Oddly, he says "that which," not "he who." He is speaking of the whole reality of the Word of life, the person and everything he revealed, and he holds back the name until verse 3.
- **Heard, seen, looked upon, touched (v1).** The senses come closer step by step. "Heard" could happen in a crowd. "Seen with our eyes" adds personal witness. "Looked upon" (*theaomai*) means to gaze at something long and attentively, the word John used for beholding the Word's glory (John 1:14). "Touched" (*psēlaphaō*) means to feel or handle. It is the very word the risen Jesus used on Easter evening: "Touch me, and see. For a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have" (Luke 24:39). The Jesus the apostles knew was no phantom.
- **Why the senses matter here.** The men who had left were moving toward an idea that later grew into full-blown Gnosticism: that matter is unworthy of God, so the divine Christ could not really have had a body. Some taught that he only *seemed* to be human (Docetism, from the Greek *dokeō*, "to seem"). John answers with eyewitness testimony. He had leaned against this man at supper (John 13:23) and seen his blood and water at the cross (John 19:34–35).
- **"The word of life" (v1).** The ESV footnote offers "Word" with a capital W. Either reading works. It is the message that brings life, and it is also Jesus himself, the Word who "was life" (John 1:4). John may intend both.
- **"The life was made manifest" (v2).** "Made manifest" (*phaneroō*, "to make visible") is one of John's favorite verbs. Eternal life is not first a gift detached from a person. It is a person, "the eternal life, which was with the Father," who stepped into view. "With the Father" mirrors "the Word was with God" (John 1:1).
- **"So that you too may have fellowship with us" (v3).** This is the purpose of the proclamation. "Fellowship" is *koinōnia*, sharing something in common, a partnership. The readers never saw Jesus, but by receiving the apostles' witness they join the same fellowship, and that fellowship is "with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ." There is no route to God that bypasses the apostles' testimony. That was exactly the claim of those who had left.
- **"So that our joy may be complete" (v4).** A footnote notes that some manuscripts read "your joy." The better-attested reading is "our," and it is the more striking one: the apostle's own joy is not full until his readers share what he has. Jesus said much the same in the upper room (John 15:11; 16:24; 17:13).
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the four verbs of the senses as an eyewitness case against the Gnostic idea that Jesus only seemed to have a body, and he observes that people often first find fellowship with God through God's people. Matthew Henry calls Christ "the Word of life, the vital Word," and says of v4 that the gospel "is not properly a dispensation of fear, sorrow, and dread, but of peace and joy." JFB presses a tense point in v1: the Word "was" (*ēn*), existing before he was revealed, not "began to be" (*egeneto*). On v4 JFB notes the manuscript split between "our joy" and "your joy." Guzik adds that full joy can be lost and has to be won back by restored fellowship.

#### Walking in the Light (vv5–10)
- **"This is the message" (v5).** John condenses everything he heard from Jesus into one sentence: "God is light, and in him is no darkness at all." The Greek has a double negative, "no darkness, not any," for emphasis. Light in Scripture means holiness, truth and life together (Ps 27:1; Isa 60:19; John 8:12). Darkness means evil, falsehood and death. God is not a mixture. Nothing in him is shadowy, deceptive or wrong.
- **Three claims, three answers.** The rest of the chapter is built on three "if we say" sentences (vv6, 8, 10). Each is a false claim, most likely slogans of the group that left. Each is followed by the truth that corrects it (vv7, 9; 2:1). The same structure runs on into 2:4, 6 and 9 ("whoever says…").
- **First claim: fellowship with God while living in darkness (v6).** To "walk" is a Hebrew picture for a whole way of life (Ps 1:1; Gen 5:24). Some were claiming closeness to God while their lives showed no change, perhaps on the theory that what the body does cannot touch the spirit. John's verdict is blunt: "we lie and do not practice the truth." In John, truth is something you *do*, not only something you think (John 3:21).
- **"If we walk in the light, as he is in the light" (v7).** Notice John does not say "if we are sinless." Walking in the light means living in the open before God, honest about what the light shows. Two results follow. First, "we have fellowship with one another." One might expect "with him," but John shows that right relationship with God shows up in real relationships with other believers. Those who had walked out of the church had broken both.
- **"The blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (v7).** "Blood" means the death of Jesus offered as a sacrifice, as at the Passover and on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:15–16; 17:11). The verb "cleanses" is present tense: it goes on cleansing. The man named here is "Jesus," with human blood, and he is also "his Son." The two titles together answer the error in a single phrase.
- **Second claim: "We have no sin" (v8).** This may mean "we have no sinful nature," or "sin is no longer a category that applies to us." Either way, the one being fooled is the speaker: "we deceive ourselves." Others usually see it plainly.
- **"If we confess our sins" (v9).** "Confess" is *homologeō*, literally "to say the same thing," to agree. To confess sin is to say about it what God says: to name it as sin, not as a mistake or a weakness. "Sins" is plural. John has in mind specific acts named honestly, not a vague admission that nobody is perfect.
- **"He is faithful and just to forgive" (v9).** "Faithful" is natural: God keeps his promises (Deut 7:9). "Just" is surprising. One might expect "merciful." But forgiveness is a matter of justice because the debt has been paid. The "blood of Jesus" (v7) and the "propitiation" coming in 2:2 are why a holy God can forgive without denying his own justice (Rom 3:25–26). The two verbs match the two needs: "forgive" deals with the guilt, and "cleanse from all unrighteousness" deals with the stain.
- **Third claim: "We have not sinned" (v10).** This goes further than verse 8. It denies ever having sinned at all. The result is worse too. Verse 8 said we deceive ourselves. Verse 10 says "we make him a liar," because God's whole word, from the Law to the cross, assumes that all have sinned (1 Kgs 8:46; Ps 14:3; Rom 3:23). "His word is not in us" means it has not taken root, whatever we claim.
- **Does verse 9 apply to believers?** Christians have read the verse in two main ways. Many, especially in Reformed and Lutheran traditions, see it as describing the ongoing honesty of the Christian life: those already justified keep confessing and keep being cleansed, as children before a Father. Others read it mainly as the first confession of a person coming to faith, set against those who refused to admit any sin. The present-tense "if we confess" and the "we" that includes John himself favor a habit of life more than a single moment. Both readings agree that no one comes to God by claiming innocence.
- **Commentators:** All three treat confession as the believer's ongoing duty, not a single act. Guzik takes *homologeō* as saying the same thing God says about sin, and stresses that confession should be specific and personal. Henry: "Penitent confession and acknowledgment of sin are the believer's business." He also says that denying sin "reflects dishonour upon God." On v9 JFB writes that "not merely the mercy, but the justice or righteousness of God is set forth," and Guzik makes the same point: God is just to forgive because the penalty has been paid. They differ slightly on v7. Henry stresses the blood discharging "from the guilt of all sin." JFB says John is speaking "not of justification… once for all, but of the present sanctification." Guzik keeps both together. JFB also notes that "have" in v8 is present tense, so the claim concerns believers now, not their life before conversion. Guzik frames v6–7 as a matter of fellowship more than salvation.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter opens with hands that touched the Word of life and closes with the blood of Jesus that cleanses from all sin. Both are about the same body. The Son who "was with the Father" became someone who could be heard, watched and handled. He then shed real blood for real sins. That is why fellowship with a God who is pure light is possible for people who are not. The light does not have to be dimmed for sinners to come near. Their sins are confessed, forgiven and washed away by the one who walked among us.
