*Setting:* Joseph's house in Egypt, about twenty-two years after his brothers sold him (37:2; 41:46; 45:6). The silver cup has been "found" in Benjamin's sack (44:12), and the brothers, free to go home without him, have all come back. Judah has just made the speech of his life, offering to stay as a slave in Benjamin's place so their old father will not die of grief (44:18–34). Every test Joseph set has been passed. The brothers have shown they are no longer the men who threw him in a pit. Now the disguise comes off. This is the emotional peak of the whole Joseph story, and its central line, "God sent me," is the key to everything in Genesis 37–50.

#### Joseph Provides for His Brothers and Family (vv1–28)
- **"Joseph could not control himself" (v1).** The same verb was used in 43:31, when he did manage to hold it in. Judah's speech breaks him. He orders every Egyptian out of the room, so that "no one stayed with him when Joseph made himself known to his brothers." JFB suggests he was sparing them from having their old crime exposed in front of strangers. Whatever happens next is family business.
- **"He wept aloud, so that the Egyptians heard it" (v2).** This is not quiet tears in a side room (43:30). It is loud, uncontrollable weeping, heard through the walls and reported all the way to Pharaoh's household. Twenty-two years of grief, loneliness and longing come out at once.
- **"I am Joseph! Is my father still alive?" (v3).** Two short sentences, and the second shows where his heart is. Judah has just spent several minutes describing their father. Joseph has heard that Jacob is alive (43:27–28), but now he asks as a son, not as a governor. Matthew Henry pictures him telling them "very abruptly (as one uneasy till it was out)."
- **"They were dismayed at his presence" (v3).** The word (*bahal*) means terrified, stunned, thrown into panic. These are men facing the brother they sold, who now has the power of life and death over them. They cannot say a word. Guzik connects this with Zechariah 12:10, where Israel will one day look on the one they pierced and mourn.
- **"Come near to me, please… I am your brother, Joseph, whom you sold into Egypt" (v4).** He draws them close, perhaps so the Egyptians outside will not hear, perhaps simply so they can look at his face. He calls himself "your brother" before he names their sin. And he does name it. Joseph does not pretend nothing happened. Forgiveness in this chapter is not denial.
- **"Do not be distressed or angry with yourselves… for God sent me before you to preserve life" (v5).** This is the theological heart of the chapter. The brothers "sold" him; God "sent" him. Both are true at once. Joseph says "God sent me" three times (vv5, 7, 8) and adds, **"So it was not you who sent me here, but God" (v8).** He is not excusing them; he has just said "whom you sold." He is saying that God was at work through their sin, and was bigger than it. Joseph will say it even more plainly after Jacob dies: "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (50:20). The Tyndale notes call this a classic statement of God's sovereignty and point out that it is also what makes Joseph's forgiveness possible. A man who believes God was in charge does not need to settle scores.
- **"The famine has been in the land these two years, and there are yet five years" (v6).** This dates the scene: the second year of the famine, so Joseph is about thirty-nine (41:46, 53). "Neither plowing nor harvest" means farmers would not even bother to sow. JFB explains the old English "earing" in the KJV as plowing, and suggests the Nile's yearly flood failed.
- **"To preserve for you a remnant on earth, and to keep alive for you many survivors" (v7).** "Remnant" (*sheʾerit*) is the word later prophets use for the faithful few God keeps alive through judgment (Isa 10:20–22; Mic 2:12). "Many survivors" can also be read "a great deliverance" (KJV). God's purpose was not only Joseph's career or even Egypt's grain supply. It was the survival of the family through whom God promised to bless all nations (12:3).
- **"He has made me a father to Pharaoh" (v8).** "Father" here means a trusted adviser and protector, as when a king's counselor is called his "father" (compare Isa 22:21). Some scholars connect it with an Egyptian title for senior officials, "father of the god," the king being regarded as divine. Either way Joseph lists three roles: adviser to the king, master of his household, and ruler of the whole land (41:40–43).
- **"Come down to me; do not tarry… You shall dwell in the land of Goshen" (vv9–10).** Goshen was in the eastern part of the Nile delta, the side nearest Canaan, good pastureland a few days' journey from the border. The family can live there as herdsmen, **"near me,"** and Joseph will feed them through the five remaining years. Without this move they would starve. With it, they will grow from seventy people into a nation (46:27; Exod 1:7), just as God told Abraham his descendants would be "sojourners in a land that is not theirs" for four hundred years and come out with great possessions (15:13–14).
- **"Your eyes see, and the eyes of my brother Benjamin see, that it is my mouth that speaks to you" (v12).** Up to now Joseph spoke through an interpreter (42:23). Now, most likely, he speaks to them directly in Hebrew, their own language, and that alone is proof. Benjamin is named as a witness alongside them.
- **"Tell my father of all my honor in Egypt" (v13).** This is not boasting. Jacob will need to hear how great Joseph's position is before he will believe it or dare to make the move. "Hurry and bring my father down here." Joseph says "hurry" twice (vv9, 13).
- **"He fell upon his brother Benjamin's neck and wept, and Benjamin wept upon his neck" (v14).** Rachel's two sons, both motherless, hold each other and cry. Then **"he kissed all his brothers and wept upon them" (v15).** All of them, including Simeon and Levi, including Judah who proposed the sale, including Reuben who failed to stop it.
- **"After that his brothers talked with him" (v15).** This small line answers 37:4: "they hated him and could not speak peacefully to him." For twenty-two years they could not talk with him. Now they can.
- **"It pleased Pharaoh and his servants" (v16).** Pharaoh's whole court is glad. Joseph has saved Egypt, and Pharaoh is happy to repay him through his family. He doubles Joseph's offer: **"I will give you the best of the land of Egypt, and you shall eat the fat of the land" (v18).** "The fat of the land" means its richest produce. God is fulfilling his promise to bless those who bless Abraham's family (12:3) in reverse: Pharaoh is blessed through Joseph, and now he blesses Joseph's people.
- **"Take wagons from the land of Egypt" (v19).** These were Egyptian carts, rare in the hill country of Canaan, as JFB notes, where travel was by foot and donkey. They are meant for the women, the children and an old man. **"Have no concern for your goods" (v20)**, literally "let not your eye pity your belongings": leave the old furniture behind. Better things are waiting.
- **"To each and all of them he gave a change of clothes" (v22).** Clothes have marked every turn in Joseph's life: the robe his brothers stripped off him (37:23), the garment left in the hand of Potiphar's wife (39:12), the prison clothes he changed out of (41:14), the fine linen Pharaoh put on him (41:42). The brothers tore their own clothes in grief (44:13). Now Joseph gives them new ones. To Benjamin he gives **"three hundred shekels of silver and five changes of clothes."** The brothers once sold Joseph for twenty shekels (37:28). Joseph gives Rachel's other son fifteen times that, and this time no one is jealous.
- **"Ten donkeys loaded with the good things of Egypt, and ten female donkeys loaded with grain" (v23).** A caravan for his father, the opposite of the Ishmaelite caravan that carried Joseph down to Egypt. It is a gift and a proof. No one in Canaan could have produced it.
- **"Do not quarrel on the way" (v24).** The verb (*ragaz*) means to be agitated or stirred up. It can describe trembling in fear (Exod 15:14) or being stirred up in anger. Most translations take it as "quarrel": Joseph knows that on the long road home, the brothers might start blaming one another ("It was your idea!" "I told you not to!"), as Reuben once did (42:22). Some read it instead as "don't be afraid on the way," reassurance for men who have always come home from Egypt in fear. Both fit the moment.
- **"Joseph is still alive, and he is ruler over all the land of Egypt" (v26).** Now the brothers must tell their father the truth, and that truth includes what they did. The text does not record that conversation, but they cannot explain Joseph being in Egypt without explaining how he got there. **"His heart became numb, for he did not believe them."** The verb (*pug*) means to grow numb or cold, to go limp. Twenty-two years earlier these same sons told Jacob a lie that he believed instantly (37:33). Now they tell him the truth, and he cannot believe it.
- **"When he saw the wagons… the spirit of their father Jacob revived" (v27).** He hears "all the words of Joseph" and sees the Egyptian carts with his own eyes. The bloody robe once convinced him his son was dead. The wagons convince him his son is alive. "Revived" means came back to life, as if Jacob himself had been half-dead since the day he said, "I shall go down to Sheol to my son, mourning" (37:35).
- **"Israel said, 'It is enough; Joseph my son is still alive. I will go and see him before I die'" (v28).** The name changes from Jacob in v27 to Israel in v28, as the grieving father becomes the head of the covenant family again. "It is enough" is the reply of a man who needs nothing more. Not long ago he said, "All this has come against me" (42:36). He was wrong. All of it had been working for him.
- **Commentators:** All three see God's providence as the key. Henry puts it neatly: the brothers "designed, by selling him into Egypt, to defeat his dreams, but God thereby designed to accomplish them." Guzik insists that both truths stand: the brothers sinned, and God had a purpose, and it is a mistake either to deny the wrong or to miss God's hand in it. He adds that the move to Egypt protected the family from melting into Canaanite culture, as Judah nearly did in chapter 38. JFB says Joseph mentions the sale only to prove who he is and points straight to providence without blaming God for their crime. On v24 JFB and the Tyndale notes both think Joseph foresaw the brothers arguing over who was most to blame. Guzik draws an application from Jacob coming to believe: people come to believe Jesus is alive when they hear his words and see what he has done.

#### Pointing to Christ
The brother who was rejected, sold and counted dead is alive and enthroned, and the men who betrayed him discover that his power over them is the power to save. He calls them near, names their sin, weeps over them and feeds them. Stephen pointed to this scene in his defense before the council: "on the second visit Joseph made himself known to his brothers" (Acts 7:13), and in the same speech he accused Israel's leaders of betraying the Righteous One (Acts 7:52). The cross is the greatest case of what Joseph says here: people did their worst, "but God" was sending salvation through it (Acts 2:23–24). And like Jacob, many find the news too good to believe, until they hear the risen Lord's own words and see what he has sent.
