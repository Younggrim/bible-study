*Setting:* Israel is still camped at the foot of Mount Sinai. God has spoken the Ten Commandments aloud to the whole nation (20:1–17), and the terrified people have asked Moses to hear the rest for them (20:19). Moses has gone into the thick darkness where God is (20:21), and from 20:22 to 23:33 he receives what Exodus later calls "the Book of the Covenant" (24:7). These are case laws: real-life situations, most beginning "When…" or "If…," that show how the Ten Words apply in a farming and herding society. Other law collections from the ancient Near East use the same style, the best known being the Code of Hammurabi from Babylon (about 1750 BC), and some cases here are strikingly similar to his. The differences are just as striking. These laws come from God rather than a king, and they put a higher value on human life, on the weak, and on slaves. Chapter 21 deals mostly with slaves, violence and injury. Many of these laws were for ancient Israel's courts, not rules for Christians today, but they still show what kind of justice God loves.

#### Laws About Slaves (vv1–32)
- **"Now these are the rules that you shall set before them" (v1).** "Rules" is *mishpatim*, "judgments" or rulings, the kind of decisions a judge would make in a court. They were cases to guide Israel's judges, the very leaders appointed on Jethro's advice (18:21–26).
- **Why start with slaves?** It is striking that the first case in Israel's law is about the rights of a slave. Israel had just been slaves in Egypt. The first thing God does after the Ten Commandments is protect the people at the bottom. The Tyndale notes observe that in much of the ancient world slaves believed the gods had abandoned them, but the LORD cares about the helpless, and his people must too.
- **What kind of slavery?** The Hebrew word *ebed* can mean slave or servant. For a fellow Israelite, it usually meant debt slavery: a man too poor to pay what he owed sold his labor for a set time (Lev 25:39–43; 2 Kgs 4:1). It was closer to long-term bonded service than to the chattel slavery of the American South. Kidnapping someone and selling him was a capital crime (v16). Even so, slavery in Israel was a hard reality, and the law did not abolish it. It limited it and pointed past it.
- **"When you buy a Hebrew slave, he shall serve six years, and in the seventh he shall go out free, for nothing" (v2).** The pattern of six and seven echoes the Sabbath (20:9–10). No Hebrew could be owned forever against his will. Deuteronomy adds that the master must send him away with generous gifts from his flock, threshing floor and winepress, "as the LORD your God has blessed you" (Deut 15:12–15), and gives the reason: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt." Hammurabi's code set debt service at three years, so the length here is not unusual, but the motive is.
- **"If he comes in married, then his wife shall go out with him" (v3).** The master could not split up a family that came to him together.
- **"If his master gives him a wife… the wife and her children shall be her master's" (v4).** This is the hard case. The wife here was presumably a slave herself, perhaps a foreigner, with no right to go free in the seventh year. The man could leave, but his family stayed. The law does not call this good. It describes a painful situation and gives the man a way to keep his family.
- **"I love my master, my wife, and my children; I will not go out free" (v5).** The choice to stay is his alone, and the word is "love." A servant would only say this about a master who treated him well.
- **"His master shall bring him to God… to the door or the doorpost" (v6).** "To God" is *ha-elohim*. The ESV footnote offers "to the judges," and older translations (KJV) say "unto the judges." Either way the step was public and solemn, before God and witnesses, so no one could force a slave into lifelong service by claiming he chose it. **"Bore his ear through with an awl."** The ear pierced against the doorpost of the house marked him as belonging to that household for good. The ear is the organ of hearing and obeying. **"He shall be his slave forever."** "Forever" here means for life.
- **"Ears you have dug for me."** Psalm 40:6 says literally, "ears you have dug for me," in the middle of a passage about willing obedience: "I delight to do your will, O my God" (Ps 40:8). Many Christian readers, Henry among them, have connected it with this law. Hebrews 10:5–7 quotes the psalm, following the Greek Old Testament ("a body have you prepared for me"), as the words of Christ coming into the world. The link with the pierced ear is an old Christian reflection rather than something the New Testament states, but the picture fits Jesus well: he "came not to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45), out of love.
- **"When a man sells his daughter as a slave, she shall not go out as the male slaves do" (v7).** This sounds shocking, and it was a mark of desperate poverty. But in context she was being sold into a household as a future wife or concubine (a secondary wife), as vv8–10 show. The Tyndale notes suggest the price was much like a bride-price. Since she was to become part of the family, sending her out after six years would leave her worse off, without husband or home. The law protects her instead.
- **"If she does not please her master… he shall let her be redeemed" (v8).** If the man changes his mind, her family can buy her back. **"He shall have no right to sell her to a foreign people, since he has broken faith with her."** "Broken faith" is the word for betrayal, used of unfaithful husbands (Mal 2:14). He made her a promise, and if he breaks it, she is protected.
- **"If he designates her for his son, he shall deal with her as with a daughter" (v9).** She is to be treated as a free daughter of the house.
- **"He shall not diminish her food, her clothing, or her marital rights" (v10).** Polygamy was common in the ancient world, and the law here does not approve it. It protects the woman who might be pushed aside. "Marital rights" is a rare word (*onah*), and most take it as the right to sexual relations and children, though some think it means lodging or oil. **"She shall go out for nothing" (v11).** If he fails her in these three things, she goes free without any payment. Jewish law later built the rights of all wives on this verse.
- **"Whoever strikes a man so that he dies shall be put to death" (v12).** Here the law turns to violence, applying "You shall not murder" (20:13). Human life is so valuable that taking it costs life, as God said to Noah: "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image" (Gen 9:6).
- **"But if he did not lie in wait for him, but God let him fall into his hand" (v13).** This is how Hebrew describes an accident: something no one planned, which God in his providence allowed. **"I will appoint for you a place to which he may flee."** This is the first mention of what became the cities of refuge (Num 35:9–28; Deut 19:1–13; Josh 20), where someone who killed by accident could be safe from the dead man's relatives until a fair hearing. The ancient world often allowed blood feuds; God's law separates murder from accident.
- **"You shall take him from my altar, that he may die" (v14).** In the ancient world, people fleeing for their lives would grab the horns of an altar and claim sanctuary. God's altar does not shelter a murderer. Solomon applied this when Joab clung to the altar after murdering two men, and was put to death there (1 Kgs 2:28–34).
- **"Whoever strikes his father or his mother shall be put to death" (v15).** Striking here need not mean killing. Even an assault on a parent is treated as a capital crime, because it attacks the family, which God placed at the center of Israel's life (20:12). These are the maximum penalties a court could give, and Deuteronomy 21:18–21 shows that the parents themselves had to bring the case to the elders. There is no record in the Old Testament of the penalty ever being carried out.
- **"Whoever steals a man and sells him, and anyone found in possession of him, shall be put to death" (v16).** Kidnapping for the slave trade was a death-penalty crime, whether the kidnapper had sold the victim or still had him. Joseph's brothers had done exactly this (Gen 37:28). Paul lists "enslavers" (literally "man-stealers") among those the law condemns (1 Tim 1:10). This verse alone condemns the Atlantic slave trade, which was built on kidnapping.
- **"Whoever curses his father or his mother shall be put to death" (v17).** "Curse" is *qalal*, from a root meaning "light," the opposite of the "heavy" honor of the fifth commandment. To curse a parent is to treat them as worthless, even to call down harm on them. Jesus quotes this verse when rebuking the Pharisees for twisting the law to avoid caring for their parents (Mark 7:10).
- **"When men quarrel and one strikes the other… and the man does not die but takes to his bed" (v18).** Now come injuries that do not kill. **"He shall pay for the loss of his time, and shall have him thoroughly healed" (v19).** The attacker pays lost wages and medical costs. This is restitution, not prison. Israel's law had no prisons as punishment; it aimed to put things right between people.
- **"When a man strikes his slave… and the slave dies under his hand, he shall be avenged" (v20).** This was remarkable. In most of the ancient world a master could do what he liked to his slave. Here, killing a slave is treated as a crime that must be "avenged," the word used for justice on a killer. **"But if the slave survives a day or two, he is not to be avenged, for the slave is his money" (v21).** "His money" is literally "his silver." If the slave lived for a day or two, it was harder to prove the master meant to kill him, and the master's own loss counted as a penalty. To modern ears it is a hard verse. The law is meeting a harsh world where it is and restraining it.
- **"When men strive together and hit a pregnant woman, so that her children come out" (v22).** "Her children come out" is literally "her children go out." Readers disagree on what this means. Many modern translations and interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, take it as a premature birth: the baby is born early, and **"there is no harm"** means neither mother nor child is hurt, so a fine is paid. Others take it as a miscarriage, with "harm" referring only to the mother. The Hebrew word for "children" (*yeled*) is the normal word for a born child, and the word "go out" is used for normal birth (Gen 25:25–26), which supports the first view. On that reading, the law treats the unborn child's life as equal to anyone else's. The plural "children" may simply be general, covering whatever child or children she carried.
- **"But if there is harm, then you shall pay life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (vv23–24).** This is the famous "law of retaliation" (Latin *lex talionis*). It was not permission for private revenge. It was a principle for judges: the penalty must fit the injury, no more and no less. It stopped blood feuds where one man's lost eye turned into a family's lost lives (compare Lamech's boast, Gen 4:23–24). It also put rich and poor under the same rule. Hammurabi's code had eye-for-eye laws too, but applied them only among equals; injuring a commoner or a slave cost only money. In practice, Israel seems to have often allowed payment instead of bodily punishment for injuries, except for murder (Num 35:31 forbids a ransom for a murderer, which suggests ransom was allowed in other cases).
- **Jesus and "eye for eye."** Jesus says, "You have heard that it was said, 'An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.' But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil" (Matt 5:38–39). He does not cancel the courts' duty to give fair judgment. He speaks to personal relationships, where people were using this law to justify getting even. The law limited revenge; Jesus goes further and calls his disciples to give it up.
- **"When a man strikes the eye of his slave… he shall let the slave go free because of his eye" (v26).** If a master permanently injured a slave, even knocking out a tooth (v27), the slave went free. Here the "eye for eye" principle works in a slave's favor: the master loses the slave. No other ancient law code is known to have given slaves this protection.
- **"When an ox gores a man or a woman to death, the ox shall be stoned" (v28).** Even an animal that takes a human life is put to death (Gen 9:5). **"Its flesh shall not be eaten."** The owner could not profit from the meat. **"The owner of the ox shall not be liable"** if the attack could not have been foreseen.
- **"If the ox has been accustomed to gore in the past, and its owner has been warned" (v29).** Now it is negligence. The owner knew and did nothing, so he is guilty of the death. **"If a ransom is imposed on him" (v30).** The victim's family could accept a payment instead of the owner's life. JFB notes this is the one case where money is expressly allowed in place of a death sentence. The difference from murder is that the owner did not intend to kill.
- **"If it gores a man's son or daughter" (v31).** In some ancient law codes, if a man's negligence killed another man's son, the guilty man's own son was put to death. Israel's law refuses this: the guilty person alone answers for his guilt (Deut 24:16).
- **"If the ox gores a slave… the owner shall give to their master thirty shekels of silver" (v32).** Thirty shekels was the compensation price for a slave's life. Hammurabi's similar law set it at twenty. The number returns twice more in Scripture. In Zechariah 11:12–13 the shepherd of God's people is paid off with thirty pieces of silver, "the lordly price at which I was priced by them." And Judas agreed to betray Jesus for exactly this sum (Matt 26:15; 27:9–10). The Lord of glory was valued at the price of a gored slave.
- **Commentators:** Henry says these laws relate to the fifth and sixth commandments, and though the penalties are not binding on us, "they are of great use for the explanation of the moral law, and the rules of natural justice." He sees mercy to servants placed first because Israel had been in bondage. He connects the bored ear with Psalm 40 ("My ears hast thou opened") and Christ's willing service, and Guzik draws the same picture of the bondservant who serves out of love. On eye for eye, Henry adds that "our Saviour commands us to forgive injuries, and not to meditate revenge," and on v26–27, "though it was only striking out a tooth, that should be their discharge." JFB says "every Israelite was free-born; but slavery was permitted under certain restrictions," calls the *mishpatim* rules for judges and magistrates, and holds that eye for eye "was given to regulate the procedure of the public magistrate," not to license private revenge. Guzik agrees with both and reads these laws as limiting slavery and moving it toward its end. JFB does not comment on vv12–22.

#### Laws About Restitution (vv33–36)
- **"When a man opens a pit… and does not cover it" (v33).** Pits and cisterns for storing water were dug in the ground all over Israel. An uncovered one was a deadly trap, as Joseph's brothers knew (Gen 37:24). **"The owner of the pit shall make restoration… and the dead beast shall be his" (v34).** He pays the full value of the animal and keeps the carcass. Responsibility is the theme: if your carelessness harms your neighbor, you make it right. Deuteronomy applies the same idea to building a parapet on a flat roof (Deut 22:8).
- **"When one man's ox butts another's, so that it dies, then they shall sell the live ox and share its price" (v35).** When neither owner is at fault, both share the loss equally. The same law appears almost word for word in the earlier Laws of Eshnunna (about 1800 BC), a reminder that God's law used the best of ordinary justice that people already knew. **"If it is known that the ox has been accustomed to gore… he shall repay ox for ox" (v36).** When the owner knew the danger, he bears the whole loss.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the pit law simply: "he that opened the pit must make good the loss," and he draws from these cases that people must take care not to harm others through carelessness. Guzik notes that the owner keeps the dead animal, so the payment is fair to both sides. JFB groups vv28–36 together and has little to add on the details.

#### Pointing to Christ
These laws were written for a hard world, and they bent that world toward mercy: a slave who could go free, a woman who could not be cast off, an accidental killer with a place to run, a limit on revenge. They also show a God who values every human life, even of those the world counted as property. In Jesus the themes come together. He is the willing servant who loves his Master and his family and will not go out free, choosing to serve forever (Mark 10:45; Phil 2:7). He was sold for thirty shekels, the price of a gored slave, so that slaves of sin might go free (John 8:34–36). And he is the refuge to which the guilty can run, not for an accident but for real sin, finding safety the altar could never give a murderer, because his own blood was shed on it (Heb 6:18).
