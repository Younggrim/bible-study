*Setting:* "Of David." No occasion is given, but Henry and most older readers place it in the years when Saul and his court were hunting David. The pain in it is not only danger but betrayal. These are people David had prayed and fasted for when they were sick (v13), and they repaid him with false testimony, mockery and plots on his life. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, notes that the opening "Contend… with those who contend with me" uses the same word David used to Saul at the cave of En-gedi: "May the LORD therefore be judge… and see to it and plead my cause" (1 Sam 24:15). The psalm moves through three rounds, each ending with a promise of praise: a call for God to fight (vv1–10, ending "who is like you?"), a description of betrayal (vv11–18, ending "I will thank you in the great congregation"), and a plea for vindication (vv19–28, ending "my tongue shall tell of your righteousness"). It is one of the "imprecatory" psalms, which ask God to bring judgment on enemies, and it raises honest questions for Christians. Jesus himself quoted from it: "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25; Ps 35:19).

#### Contend, O LORD (vv1–3)
- **"Contend, O LORD, with those who contend with me" (v1).** "Contend" (*riv*) is a legal word: to bring a case, to argue in court. "Fight against those who fight against me" uses a military word. David asks God to be both his lawyer and his soldier. The psalm will keep moving between the courtroom (false witnesses, vindication) and the battlefield (shield, spear, pursuit).
- **"Take hold of shield and buckler and rise for my help!" (v2).** God is pictured as a warrior arming for battle (Exod 15:3: "The LORD is a man of war"; Deut 32:41). The "buckler" was a small round shield, and the "shield" here a large one that covered the whole body. God picks up both, as if one were not enough. It is bold poetry: the God of Israel strapping on armor for one hunted man.
- **"Draw the spear and javelin against my pursuers!" (v3).** "Javelin" (*segor*) is uncertain. The KJV reads it as a verb, "stop the way against them," that is, block the path of the pursuers. Many modern versions take it as the name of a weapon, perhaps a battle-axe. Either way, God stands between David and those chasing him.
- **"Say to my soul, 'I am your salvation!'" (v3).** In the middle of the battle language comes this quiet, personal request. David needs not only rescue but the inner assurance that God is on his side. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, says the armed God facing the enemy "whispers consolation to the trembling man crouching behind his shield." Spurgeon, also cited, says nothing makes a believer stronger than the full assurance that God is his salvation.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "A righteous cause may, with the greatest satisfaction imaginable, be laid before a righteous God." He notes that if a fellow-subject had wronged David he could have appealed to the king, "but, when his prince wronged him, he appealed to his God." JFB reads the opening as God "invoked in the character of a warrior." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line that the accuser of the brethren will be met by the Advocate of the saints.

#### Let Them Be Put to Shame (vv4–10)
- **"Let them be put to shame and dishonor who seek after my life!" (v4).** These are people trying to kill David. He prays that their plans would fail and their faces fall. The words return almost exactly at the end (v26) and in 40:14 and 70:2.
- **"Let them be like chaff before the wind, with the angel of the LORD driving them away!" (v5).** Chaff is the light husk blown away when grain is tossed in the air at harvest. Psalm 1:4 describes the wicked this way. "The angel of the LORD" appeared in the previous psalm encamping around those who fear God (34:7). Here the same figure drives the wicked away. Kidner, cited by Guzik, observes that the angel of the LORD rescues some and destroys others, depending on which side of God they stand (Exod 23:20–22). Boice, also cited, holds that this figure was the Son of God before his incarnation, a view many Christian interpreters share and others leave open. Trapp, cited by Guzik, adds a vivid line: chaff may come to rest against a wall, "but where shall they rest who are chased by an angel?"
- **"Let their way be dark and slippery" (v6).** Fleeing in the dark on a slippery path, with an angel chasing them. JFB calls it "a terrible fate," and compares the angel who struck Israel with plague (2 Sam 24:16) and the one who struck Herod (Acts 12:23).
- **"For without cause they hid their net for me; without cause they dug a pit for my life" (v7).** Hunters dug pits and covered them with nets to trap animals. "Without cause" comes twice here and again in v19. Kidner, cited by Guzik, sees this as the heart of David's hurt. He had done nothing to deserve this. The Hebrew of this verse seems a little out of order ("their net-pit"), and JFB and others think it means "the pit of their net."
- **"Let the net that he hid ensnare him" (v8).** This is not a request for something beyond justice. It is the Bible's common pattern: the trap catches the trapper (7:15–16; 9:15; Prov 26:27). Haman was hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai (Esth 7:10).
- **"Then my soul will rejoice in the LORD, exulting in his salvation" (v9).** David's joy is not in their fall but "in the LORD." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: "We do not triumph in the destruction of others, but in the salvation given to us of God."
- **"All my bones shall say, 'O LORD, who is like you?'" (v10).** The whole person, down to the bones, praises God (contrast 32:3, where the bones wasted away). "Who is like you?" is the cry of Moses' song after the Red Sea (Exod 15:11). The answer is no one, because God rescues "the poor from him who is too strong for him." David, the future king, counts himself among the poor and needy.
- **How should Christians read prayers like this?** Jesus told his followers to love their enemies (Matt 5:44), and Christians have rightly wrestled with these verses. Several things help. David hands the matter to God rather than taking revenge himself, which is exactly what Paul tells believers to do (Rom 12:19). His actual conduct toward Saul shows this: twice he could have killed Saul and refused (1 Sam 24; 26). He asks for justice, that the trap catch the trapper, not for cruelty. And he prays against people who are fighting God's purposes, not people who have merely offended him. The Tyndale notes grant that the language sounds vengeful but trace it to a longing for justice. Henry argues that David speaks "by the spirit of prophecy," foretelling God's judgment rather than voicing private revenge. Christians may pray for God's justice against evil and, at the same time, pray for evildoers to repent, as Stephen did for those stoning him (Acts 7:60).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We find how patiently he bore Shimei's curses," so "we cannot suppose that he who was so meek in his conversation would give vent to any intemperate heat or passion in his devotion." JFB: "Their imprecations on impenitent rebels against God need no vindication; His justice and wrath are for such; His mercy for penitents." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that loving sinners as people is not the same as wishing success to rebels against God.

#### They Repay Me Evil for Good (vv11–16)
- **"Malicious witnesses rise up; they ask me of things that I do not know" (v11).** Literally "witnesses of violence." They accuse him of crimes he knows nothing about. Henry imagines Saul having David declared a traitor, with false witnesses swearing to treasonable words he never said. The same pattern would come in Jesus' trial, when "many bore false witness against him" (Mark 14:56).
- **"They repay me evil for good; my soul is bereft" (v12).** "Bereft" (*shekol*) is the word for losing a child. Betrayal by people he had cared for left David feeling like a bereaved parent. Henry: "Call a man ungrateful and you can call him no worse." He lists David's service to Saul: "witness his harp, witness Goliath's sword."
- **"But I, when they were sick—I wore sackcloth; I afflicted myself with fasting" (v13).** David had grieved for these people as if they were family. Sackcloth and fasting were the signs of deep mourning and earnest prayer (2 Sam 12:16).
- **"I prayed with head bowed on my chest" (v13).** The Hebrew is literally "my prayer returned to my bosom." The ESV takes it as a posture: head bowed low in grief, like Elijah with his face between his knees (1 Kings 18:42). Others take it to mean the prayer came back unanswered for them, but its blessing returned to him. JFB lists both views. Jesus said something similar to his disciples: if a house is not worthy, "let your peace return to you" (Matt 10:13).
- **"As one who laments his mother, I bowed down in mourning" (v14).** He mourned for them like a friend, a brother, and finally like a son losing his mother, the deepest grief of all. JFB: "his grief was that of one for a dearly loved relative."
- **"But at my stumbling they rejoiced and gathered" (v15).** "Stumbling" (*tsela'*) is literally "limping." When David was down and vulnerable, they gathered like a pack.
- **"Wretches whom I did not know tore at me without ceasing" (v15).** "Wretches" is a rare word that may mean "smiters" or people of the lowest sort. JFB notes that "tore at me" is literally "were not silent," showing that the tearing was done with words, by slander.
- **"Like profane mockers at a feast, they gnash at me with their teeth" (v16).** The Hebrew is obscure, possibly "mockers for a cake," people who would mock anyone for a free meal, like hired jesters at a banquet. JFB reads it that way. Gnashing teeth is a sign of rage and contempt (37:12; Lam 2:16; Acts 7:54).
- **Commentators:** Henry: David "was a man of war, and yet a man of tender spirit," who mourned for people "not related to him… purely from a principle of compassion." Guzik passes on Maclaren: the good are often hated for their goodness alone. Kidner, also cited, notes that this ganging up on a man suddenly made weak was played out again at the trial of Jesus.

#### How Long, O Lord, Will You Look On? (vv17–21)
- **"How long, O Lord, will you look on?" (v17).** God sees, but seems to do nothing. "How long?" is one of the most common cries in the Psalms (6:3; 13:1–2; 74:10). It is the prayer of the martyrs in heaven too: "O Sovereign Lord… how long before you will judge?" (Rev 6:10).
- **"My precious life from the lions!" (v17).** "Precious life" (*yechidah*) means "my only one," the one life he has. The same word and the same lions appear in Psalm 22:20–21: "Deliver my soul from the sword, my precious life from the power of the dog! Save me from the mouth of the lion!"
- **"I will thank you in the great congregation" (v18).** Again like Psalm 22:22, 25. Rescue leads to public praise. David will tell everyone what God did.
- **"Let not those… wink the eye who hate me without cause" (v19).** Winking was a sign of sly, malicious agreement among plotters (Prov 6:13; 10:10). Jesus quoted the last words of this verse (or the same words in 69:4) about himself: "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25). The Tyndale notes make the link.
- **"Against those who are quiet in the land they devise words of deceit" (v20).** "The quiet in the land" are peaceful, ordinary believers who want no trouble. David's enemies don't just target him. They target people like him. Meyer, cited by Guzik, describes God's "quiet ones" in every age, withdrawn from the world's noise "because they had entered into the secret of a life hidden in God."
- **"They open wide their mouths against me; they say, 'Aha, Aha! Our eyes have seen it!'" (v21).** "Aha!" (*he'ach*) is a cry of gloating glee. Mouths gaping wide in mockery recall the scene at the cross (22:7, 13).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "My soul is my only one, and therefore the greater is the shame if I neglect it." On v19 he notes the verse "is quoted, with application to Christ, and is said to be fulfilled in him." JFB reads "without a cause" as showing "more malice than having a wrong cause," and links "the quiet in the land" to "the pious lovers of peace."

#### Awake and Rouse Yourself for My Vindication (vv22–28)
- **"You have seen, O LORD; be not silent!" (v22).** The enemies said, "Our eyes have seen it!" (v21). David answers: you, LORD, have seen. Kidner, cited by Guzik, points out how neatly David's words answer the enemies' boast. They saw his fall. God saw everything, including the truth.
- **"Awake and rouse yourself for my vindication" (v23).** God does not sleep (121:4). This is the language of a desperate man pleading with a God who seems inactive. The disciples woke Jesus in the storm with much the same cry: "Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38).
- **"For my cause, my God and my Lord!" (v23).** "My God and my Lord" is the confession Thomas made when he saw the risen Jesus and his wounds: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes that the words are the same except for their order, and that Thomas was giving Jesus the very titles David gave the LORD.
- **"Vindicate me, O LORD, my God, according to your righteousness" (v24).** "Vindicate" is literally "judge me," as in 26:1. David does not ask to be judged by his own goodness but "according to your righteousness," God's own fairness and faithfulness.
- **"Let them not say, 'We have swallowed him up'" (v25).** The enemies imagine their victory complete. David prays that they will never get to say it.
- **"Let them be clothed with shame and dishonor who magnify themselves against me!" (v26).** The psalm returns to the words of v4. To "magnify themselves" is the opposite of 34:3, "magnify the LORD with me." They make themselves great; David makes God great.
- **"Let those who delight in my righteousness shout for joy and be glad" (v27).** There are others on David's side, people who want to see justice done. David prays that they too would celebrate when God acts.
- **"Great is the LORD, who delights in the welfare of his servant!" (v27).** "Welfare" is *shalom*, his peace and wholeness. The enemies delighted in his calamity (v26). God delights in his good. "Great is the LORD" is the line the ESV uses as the psalm's heading.
- **"Then my tongue shall tell of your righteousness and of your praise all the day long" (v28).** The enemies' tongues slandered him all day (v15). Now his tongue will praise God all day. The psalm ends where each of its three rounds ends: in praise.
- **Commentators:** Henry: David "will speak God's praise every day… for it is a subject that will never be exhausted." He also observes that the mercy David "hoped to win by prayer he promises to wear with praise." JFB: "God's righteous government is the hope of the pious and terror of the wicked." Guzik passes on Boice's point that God has seen everything, both David's innocence and the lies told against him.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 35 reads like a description of Jesus' last days. He was hated "without a cause," as he said himself (John 15:25). Malicious witnesses rose up and accused him of things he never said (Mark 14:56–59). He had healed the sick and wept over Jerusalem, and they repaid him evil for good. Mockers gnashed their teeth and gaped at him at the cross. But where David asked God to put his enemies to shame, Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). He did not call down the angel of the LORD against them, though he could have had legions (Matt 26:53). Instead he took the judgment the psalm asks for and bore it himself. God answered his plea, "Awake and rouse yourself for my vindication," by raising him from the dead, so that Thomas could look at his wounds and say, "My Lord and my God!" And because the Judge has become the Advocate, those who were once his enemies can be forgiven, while every unrepented evil still meets the justice this psalm cries for.
