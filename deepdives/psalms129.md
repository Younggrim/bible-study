*Setting:* Psalm 129 is the tenth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. Psalm 128 ended with a blessing, "Peace be upon Israel!" Psalm 129 looks back over the hard side of Israel's story: centuries of enemies who tried and failed to destroy her. It has no author's name and no clear date. It speaks of Israel as one person who has suffered "from my youth," and most readers think of the whole span of the nation's history: slavery in Egypt, the attacks of Canaanites, Philistines, Arameans and Assyrians, and the exile in Babylon. Many scholars place the psalm after the exile, when the returned community could look back on all of it. Like Psalm 124, it opens with a leader's call, "let Israel now say," and the congregation repeating the line. Most nations sing about their victories. This psalm sings about what Israel survived, and who preserved her. It has two halves: testimony (vv1–4) and prayer against Zion's enemies (vv5–8). The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Greatly Have They Afflicted Me (vv1–4)
- **"Greatly have they afflicted me from my youth" (v1).** Israel speaks as one person, a common way in the Old Testament of picturing the nation (Hos 11:1: "When Israel was a child, I loved him"). "From my youth" points back to the nation's beginning, its time in Egypt and the wilderness (Jer 2:2; Ezek 23:3). "Afflicted" (*tsarar*) means to show hostility, to press hard, to oppress. "They" is never named. The enemies were too many to list.
- **"Let Israel now say" (v1).** As in Psalm 124:1, a leader calls the people to repeat the words. The line is said twice (vv1–2), first by the leader and then by everyone together. Remembering suffering is part of worship here, not something to be hidden.
- **"Yet they have not prevailed against me" (v2).** This is the heart of the testimony. The enemies were many and the suffering was real, but Israel was not destroyed. Pharaoh tried to wipe out Israel's sons (Exod 1:22). Haman plotted to destroy every Jew in the Persian Empire (Esth 3:6). Babylon took the people far away. Yet Israel was still there to sing. The burning bush that Moses saw was "burning, yet it was not consumed" (Exod 3:2), and the Church of Scotland later took that line as its motto: *Nec tamen consumebatur*, "yet it was not consumed."
- **"The plowers plowed upon my back; they made long their furrows" (v3).** A shocking picture. Israel's back is a field, and the enemies are plowmen dragging the blade across it, cutting long, deep furrows. It is a vivid image of whipping and scourging, cruelty that went on and on. "Plowed" (*charash*) can also mean to cut or engrave, as on stone or metal (Jer 17:1). Isaiah used a similar picture of oppressors telling Israel, "Bow down, that we may pass over," so that "you have made your back like the ground" (Isa 51:23).
- **"The LORD is righteous" (v4).** The turning point. The psalm does not explain why the suffering came, though Israel's prophets were honest that some of it was the result of Israel's own sin. It simply confesses that God is righteous, both faithful to his covenant and just in his dealings. He did not abandon his people.
- **"He has cut the cords of the wicked" (v4).** The picture continues from v3. A plow was pulled by oxen, harnessed with ropes or cords. Cut the cords, and the plowing stops, however determined the plowman. God did not necessarily destroy every enemy at once. He simply cut the ropes, and their work came to nothing. Jeremiah promised the same: "I will break his yoke from off your neck, and I will burst your bonds" (Jer 30:8).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the church of God, in its several ages, is here spoken of, or, rather, here speaks, as one single person," and describes the enemies as having "tore them, as the husbandman tears the ground with his plough-share." JFB writes that "Israel's youth was the sojourn in Egypt" and that "the ploughing is a figure of scourging." He explains the cords as "the cords... which fasten the plough to the ox," which is the reading this deep dive follows. Guzik passes on Kidner's observation that this psalm, unlike most national songs, celebrates survival rather than achievement, and notes Boice's comment that Israel's survival is itself a witness to the God who kept her. He traces that thread from Egypt to the twentieth century, when Hitler's regime tried to destroy the Jewish people and failed.

#### May All Who Hate Zion Be Put to Shame (vv5–8)
- **"May all who hate Zion be put to shame and turned backward!" (v5).** The psalm now prays about the future. "Zion" is Jerusalem, the city where God chose to dwell, and so a name for God's people as a whole. To hate Zion was to oppose God's purpose. The prayer is that their attacks would fail: "put to shame" means their plans would come to nothing, and "turned backward" is the picture of an army retreating in defeat (Ps 35:4; 40:14).
- **A measured prayer.** Compared with some psalms that call down judgment on enemies (Ps 137:7–9; 109), this one is restrained. It does not ask for them to suffer as Israel suffered. It asks that they fail, wither and receive no blessing. The enemies in view are not people with a private quarrel but those who set themselves against God and his people.
- **"Let them be like the grass on the housetops, which withers before it grows up" (v6).** Houses in Israel had flat roofs made of beams covered with packed mud or clay. After the rains, seeds blown onto the roof would sprout quickly, but with no depth of soil they dried up in the first heat. Isaiah uses the same picture of the Assyrians' victims, "like grass on the housetops, blighted before it is grown" (Isa 37:27). Jesus told of seed on rocky ground that "sprang up, since they had no depth of soil," then withered (Matt 13:5–6).
- **"With which the reaper does not fill his hand nor the binder of sheaves his arms" (v7).** At harvest, a reaper grasped a handful of stalks in one hand and cut them with a sickle in the other. A binder gathered the handfuls into sheaves. Roof grass is not worth the effort. The enemies' work produces nothing anyone can gather. This contrasts sharply with Psalm 126:6, where the faithful sower comes home "bringing his sheaves with him."
- **"Nor do those who pass by say, 'The blessing of the LORD be upon you!'" (v8).** Harvest in Israel was a time for greetings. When Boaz came to his fields, he said to the reapers, "The LORD be with you!" and they answered, "The LORD bless you" (Ruth 2:4). Travelers passing a field at harvest would call out a blessing. Roof grass gets no harvest and so no blessing. The enemies of Zion are pictured as people with nothing to show and no one to bless them.
- **"We bless you in the name of the LORD!" (v8).** The psalm ends, strangely, with words of blessing, though they are the blessing the enemies will *not* receive. The last word belongs to "the name of the LORD." The blessing that the enemies miss is exactly the blessing the next psalms will promise to God's people (Ps 134:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "there are many that hate Zion, that hate Zion's God," and describes roof grass that "withers before it grows up to any maturity, having no root." On v8 he notes that "no wise man will pray God to bless the mowers or reapers" of a crop like that. JFB sees in the grass "the utter uselessness and the rejection of the wicked," and has no separate note on vv7–8. Guzik follows Boice in noting how mild the prayer is. He passes on Spurgeon's distinction between wishing the defeat of those who fight God's cause and holding personal ill will, and links v8 to the harvest greetings of Ruth 2:4.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 129 tells the story of a people afflicted again and again, whose enemies plowed deep furrows on their back, yet who were never destroyed. That story comes to its center in Jesus, the true Israel. Isaiah described the Servant of the LORD: "I gave my back to those who strike" (Isa 50:6). Before his crucifixion "Pilate took Jesus and flogged him" (John 19:1), and the plowers plowed upon his back. The powers of the world did their worst and seemed to prevail. But on the third day God "cut the cords of the wicked," raising him from the dead, "because it was not possible for him to be held by it" (Acts 2:24). Now his church shares the same pattern. Paul wrote, "We are afflicted in every way, but not crushed... struck down, but not destroyed" (2 Cor 4:8–9), and Jesus promised that "the gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (Matt 16:18). Those who hate his people will wither like grass on a roof, but the blessing of the LORD rests on those who belong to him.
