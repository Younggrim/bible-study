*Setting:* Still at Shiloh, before the tabernacle (18:10). Benjamin received the first of the seven remaining lots in chapter 18. Now the other six come up in turn: Simeon in the far south, four tribes in Galilee in the north (Zebulun, Issachar, Asher and Naphtali), and Dan on the western slopes toward the coast. The chapter reads quickly, mostly lists of borders and towns, many of which can no longer be located. But the lists hold surprises: the hometown of Jonah, the hills where Jesus grew up, and the shore where he taught. The chapter, and the whole division of the land, ends with Joshua himself, who takes his own share last.

#### The Inheritance for Simeon (vv1–9)
- **"Their inheritance was in the midst of the inheritance of the people of Judah" (v1).** Simeon gets no separate territory. Its towns lie inside Judah's southern region, the Negeb. This fulfills Jacob's dying words about Simeon and Levi, who had massacred the men of Shechem: "I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel" (Gen 49:5–7). Levi was scattered as the priestly tribe, with towns all over the land. Simeon was scattered by being absorbed into Judah.
- **The towns (vv2–8).** Beersheba heads the list. Most of the names already appeared among Judah's southern towns (15:26–32), including Moladah, Hazar-shual, Hormah and Ziklag. Beth-marcaboth ("house of chariots") and Hazar-susah ("village of horses") sound like military stations. Sharuhen (v6) was a fortress known from Egyptian records. 1 Chronicles 4:28–33 gives almost the same list, and adds that these were Simeon's cities "until David reigned."
- **"Because the portion of the people of Judah was too large for them" (v9).** Judah's first allotment, made from a rough knowledge of the land, turned out to be more than it could fill. Simeon's share was carved out of it. The two tribes fought together from the start (Judg 1:3, 17). Over time Simeon faded into Judah, and Moses' blessing in Deuteronomy 33 does not mention Simeon at all.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Simeon's lot came after Judah, Joseph and Benjamin "because Jacob had put that tribe under disgrace," yet still before the younger tribes, and that the arrangement fulfilled Jacob's word, "I will divide them in Jacob." JFB explains that the land was first divided on "rough survey" and that time showed Judah's portion was too large. The Tyndale notes add that Simeon's land was dry and far from the centers of power.

#### The Inheritance for Zebulun (vv10–16)
- **Zebulun's land (vv10–14).** A small territory in the hills of Lower Galilee, on the northern edge of the Jezreel Valley, between Mount Carmel and the Sea of Galilee. Jacob had said, "Zebulun shall dwell at the shore of the sea" (Gen 49:13), and the tribe was not far from both the Mediterranean and the Sea of Galilee, though its land touched neither.
- **"Gath-hepher" (v13).** The hometown of the prophet Jonah (2 Kings 14:25), a few miles northeast of Nazareth.
- **"Japhia" (v12).** A village very close to the site of Nazareth. Nazareth itself is never mentioned in the Old Testament, but it lay in this tribe's territory. The boy Jesus grew up in the hills that Zebulun received.
- **"Bethlehem" (v15).** A different Bethlehem, seven miles northwest of Nazareth, not the Bethlehem of Judah where David and Jesus were born. The judge Ibzan may have come from here (Judg 12:8–10). The list names more than twelve towns; some probably lay on Zebulun's border rather than in it.
- **Commentators:** Henry points out that Zebulun's land became "much more illustrious in the New Testament," for within it lay Nazareth, Mount Tabor (which tradition, though not the Gospels, names as the mountain of the Transfiguration), and the Galilean shore where Christ "preached so many sermons and wrought so many miracles." JFB notes that many of the border towns cannot be located.

#### The Inheritance for Issachar (vv17–23)
- **Issachar's land (vv17–22).** Most of the fertile Jezreel Valley and the hills to its east, down to the Jordan. Instead of a boundary line, the text gives a list of towns. Jacob had described Issachar as "a strong donkey… he saw that a resting place was good, and that the land was pleasant, so he bowed his shoulder to bear" (Gen 49:14–15). The rich plain lay across the main international road, so the tribe prospered in peace and suffered in war.
- **"Jezreel… Shunem" (v18).** Jezreel later became a royal city of King Ahab, where Naboth's vineyard stood (1 Kings 21:1). Shunem was the home of the woman who hosted Elisha and whose son he raised (2 Kings 4:8–37), and of Abishag, who cared for King David in his old age (1 Kings 1:3).
- **"Tabor" (v22).** Mount Tabor, the round mountain rising alone from the plain, on the border of Issachar, Zebulun and Naphtali. Deborah and Barak gathered their army there (Judg 4:6, 14).
- **"En-gannim" (v21).** "Spring of gardens," probably modern Jenin, at the southern edge of the valley.
- **Commentators:** JFB observes that "instead of describing the boundaries of this tribe," the writer lists its cities, all in the eastern part of the plain. The Tyndale notes add that the Jezreel Valley was a Canaanite stronghold, which made it hard for this small tribe to take control at first.

#### The Inheritance for Asher (vv24–31)
- **Asher's land (vv24–30).** The western coast of Galilee, from Mount Carmel north toward Tyre and Sidon, including the Plain of Acco (later Acre). Moses had blessed Asher with olive oil: "let him dip his foot in oil" (Deut 33:24), and the region is still olive country.
- **"As far as Sidon the Great… the fortified city of Tyre" (vv28–29).** These great Phoenician ports lay at the edge of Asher's allotment, but Israel never took them, and Judges 1:31–32 lists Acco, Sidon and Achzib among the towns Asher did not drive out. Instead, "the Asherites lived among the Canaanites." Later, Hiram king of Tyre became a friend and trade partner of David and Solomon (2 Sam 5:11; 1 Kings 5).
- **"Cabul" (v27).** Solomon later gave twenty towns in this area to Hiram of Tyre in payment for timber and gold. Hiram was not impressed and called them "the land of Cabul," which sounds like "as good as nothing" (1 Kings 9:11–13).
- **Asher in the New Testament.** The prophetess Anna, who saw the infant Jesus in the temple and spoke of him "to all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem," was "of the tribe of Asher" (Luke 2:36–38).
- **Commentators:** JFB says Sidon is called "great" because it was "the flourishing metropolis of the Phoenicians," but "this town was never possessed" by Asher. Henry mentions the guess that many Canaanites took refuge in Sidon and Tyre when Joshua invaded. The Tyndale notes say the twenty-two cities do not include Tyre and Sidon.

#### The Inheritance for Naphtali (vv32–39)
- **Naphtali's land (vv32–34).** Eastern Galilee, the hills west of the Sea of Galilee and the upper Jordan valley, reaching north toward Mount Hermon. It was the land of the great battle at the waters of Merom (ch 11).
- **"Judah on the east at the Jordan" (v34).** A puzzling phrase, since Judah lay far to the south. The Greek Septuagint leaves out "Judah." One older explanation, which JFB gives following Keil, is that the "towns of Jair" east of the Jordan counted as Judah's because Jair descended from Judah through his father's line (1 Chr 2:21–22). Other scholars suspect a copying error. No answer is certain.
- **"Hammath, Rakkath, Chinnereth" (v35).** Towns along the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Hammath means "hot springs," and hot springs still flow just south of Tiberias. Chinnereth gave its name to the lake. In the New Testament the plain nearby is called Gennesaret (Luke 5:1).
- **"Hazor… Kedesh" (vv36–37).** Hazor, burned by Joshua (11:10–13), now falls to Naphtali. Kedesh became a city of refuge (20:7) and the hometown of Barak, who led Naphtali and Zebulun against Sisera (Judg 4:6).
- **Naphtali in the New Testament.** Isaiah promised that the land of Zebulun and Naphtali, the first to suffer under Assyria (2 Kings 15:29), would be the first to see a great light (Isa 9:1–2). Matthew records that Jesus made his home in Capernaum, "in the territory of Zebulun and Naphtali," so that this would be fulfilled (Matt 4:13–16). Much of Jesus' Galilean ministry took place on Naphtali's shore.
- **Commentators:** JFB places the boundary running toward "the sources of the Jordan." Henry notes that Dan's later conquest of Leshem, on Naphtali's northern edge, set the far end of the phrase "from Dan to Beersheba."

#### The Inheritance for Dan (vv40–48)
- **Dan's land (vv40–46).** The last lot, a small territory west of Benjamin, between the hills and the coast, with towns taken from Judah's and Ephraim's edges. Zorah and Eshtaol (v41) appear in Judah's list too (15:33). Dan's territory reached toward Joppa (v46), the port where Jonah would later board a ship and where Peter would receive his vision about the Gentiles (Jonah 1:3; Acts 10).
- **Samson's country.** Zorah, Eshtaol and Timnah are the setting of Samson's life. Samson was a Danite from Zorah (Judg 13:2), and he took a Philistine wife from Timnah (Judg 14:1). By his time the Philistines dominated the lowland.
- **"When the territory of the people of Dan was lost to them" (v47).** The Hebrew is brief, literally "the territory of the people of Dan went out from them." The ESV takes it to mean they lost it. Judges 1:34 explains: "The Amorites pressed the people of Dan back into the hill country, for they did not allow them to come down to the plain." Unable to hold their allotment, part of the tribe went far north.
- **"Fought against Leshem… calling Leshem, Dan" (v47).** Leshem, called Laish in Judges 18, lay near the springs of the Jordan below Mount Hermon. Judges 18 tells the story in full: the Danites sent spies, found a quiet, unprotected town, and took it, along with a stolen idol and a hired priest, setting up their own place of worship. Later King Jeroboam placed one of his golden calves at Dan (1 Kings 12:28–30). The town became Israel's northern limit, "from Dan to Beersheba" (Judg 20:1). Here the note is told without comment, but the later story is a sad one. Dan is also missing from the list of tribes in Revelation 7:5–8, though the reason is not given.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the Danites, "finding their inheritance too small, decided to enlarge its boundaries by the sword." Henry notes that Dan, which had led a whole division of the camp in the wilderness, "was last provided for in Canaan." The Tyndale notes add that Philistine pressure pushed the Danites out of the lowland.

#### The Inheritance for Joshua (vv49–51)
- **"When they had finished… the people of Israel gave an inheritance among them to Joshua" (v49).** Joshua could have taken the best land first. He was the leader, the conqueror, the man who had held the lots. Instead he waited until every tribe was served, and then the people gave him his portion. Caleb's inheritance opened the division of the land (14:6–15), and Joshua's closes it. The two faithful spies from Kadesh frame the whole.
- **"By command of the LORD they gave him the city that he asked" (v50).** Like Caleb, Joshua apparently had a promise from God, though it is not recorded elsewhere. He asked for Timnath-serah, a modest town in the hill country of his own tribe, Ephraim, not far from Shiloh. Judges 2:9 calls it Timnath-heres ("portion of the sun"), the same letters in a different order. Timnath-serah may mean "portion that remains" or "abundant portion."
- **"He rebuilt the city and settled in it" (v50).** Even his reward required work. Joshua had told the Joseph tribes to clear the forest (17:15–18), and he did the same on his own land. He would be buried there (24:30).
- **"At the entrance of the tent of meeting. So they finished dividing the land" (v51).** The same three names as in 14:1, Eleazar, Joshua and the tribal leaders, close the account, and the place is named: Shiloh, before the LORD. The division began and ended in God's presence. JFB calls this verse "the formal close" of the whole section.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "He was last served, though the eldest and greatest man of all Israel," and could have "demanded the first settlement." He adds that Joshua's waiting for the people to give it showed his humility, and that his share was "doubly pleasant" because it came by special promise. JFB notes that the gift came "according to the word of the Lord," probably an unrecorded promise like Caleb's. Guzik sees Joshua's choice to take his portion last as humble service and care for others, and a picture of Jesus Christ.

#### Pointing to Christ
The list of northern tribes reads like a list of places from the Gospels: Nazareth's hills in Zebulun, Gennesaret's shore in Naphtali, the prophetess Anna from Asher. Isaiah promised that the land of Zebulun and Naphtali, darkened by invasion, would see "a great light" (Isa 9:2), and Matthew says that light came when Jesus began to preach there (Matt 4:16). The chapter ends with Joshua, the leader who served everyone else before himself and took a small hill town as his reward. Jesus, whose name is the same as Joshua's, said, "the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Joshua received his portion last. Jesus went further, making himself last of all, so that his people could share his inheritance.
