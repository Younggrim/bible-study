*Setting:* The apostle John, late in the first century, to churches in Asia shaken by teachers who had left them (2:19). These teachers claimed to speak by the Spirit, and they denied that Jesus Christ had truly come in the flesh. Chapter 3 ended with the assurance that God abides in his people "by the Spirit whom he has given us" (3:24). That raises an obvious question: how do we know which spirit is speaking? Chapter 4 answers that first, with a test of doctrine. Then John writes some of the most famous words in the Bible about love: "God is love," "perfect love casts out fear," "we love because he first loved us."

#### Test the Spirits (vv1–6)
- **"Do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits" (v1).** In the early church, people stood up in worship and spoke in the name of the Spirit, and some did so falsely. John's "spirit" means a prophetic voice and the spiritual power behind it. "Test" is *dokimazō*, the word for assaying metal to see if it is genuine (compare 1 Thess 5:20–21). John is not telling believers to be cynical. He is telling them not to be gullible.
- **"Many false prophets have gone out into the world" (v1).** False prophets were nothing new (Deut 13:1–5; 18:20–22; Jer 23:16). Jesus warned that they would come (Matt 7:15; 24:11, 24). "Gone out" echoes 2:19: these may well be the same people who had "gone out from us."
- **"Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (v2).** This is the test. It is not about whether a teacher is impressive, emotional or popular, but what they say about Jesus. The Greek uses a perfect participle, "has come in the flesh," which means he came and remains human, now risen and glorified in a real body. That rules out the Docetists, who said Jesus only seemed to have a body, and teachers like Cerinthus, who separated the man Jesus from a heavenly "Christ."
- **"Every spirit that does not confess Jesus" (v3).** A few early writers knew another reading here: "every spirit that *looses* (or 'divides') Jesus," using *lyō*, the word for "destroy" in 3:8. It appears in the Latin Vulgate and in some church fathers, but no Greek manuscript has it in the text itself (one tenth-century copy mentions it in the margin). Almost all scholars take "does not confess" as original. The other reading probably arose as a comment on what the false teachers were doing: splitting Jesus apart.
- **"This is the spirit of the antichrist" (v3).** This picks up 2:18–22. The antichrist figure is still future, but its spirit is "in the world already."
- **"He who is in you is greater than he who is in the world" (v4).** One of the most loved verses in the letter. "He who is in you" is God, by his Spirit. "He who is in the world" is the devil (5:19; John 12:31). The believers have "overcome them," the false prophets, not by out-arguing them but by holding to the truth. The victory belongs to the one living in them.
- **"The world listens to them" (v5).** False teaching often succeeds because it tells the world what it already wants to hear (2 Tim 4:3–4). Popularity is not a test of truth.
- **"Whoever knows God listens to us" (v6).** "Us" here means the apostles, the eyewitnesses of chapter 1. The second test is whether a teacher agrees with the apostles' teaching. Jesus said the same: "Whoever is of God hears the words of God" (John 8:47), and "My sheep hear my voice" (John 10:27).
- **"The Spirit of truth and the spirit of error" (v6).** "Spirit of truth" is Jesus' name for the Holy Spirit in the upper room (John 14:17; 15:26; 16:13). The Dead Sea Scrolls also speak of a spirit of truth and a spirit of falsehood at war in the world, which shows John was using language Jewish readers would recognize.
- **Commentators:** All three insist that every believer, not only church leaders, must test the spirits. JFB: "All believers are to do so: not merely ecclesiastics." Guzik adds that dramatic experiences and a big reputation prove nothing, and that Scripture is the measure. Henry sums up the test: "The sum of revealed religion is comprehended in the doctrine concerning Christ, his person and office." JFB notes that the Greek perfect "has come" means "not a mere past historical fact." He is also the only one of the three to discuss the v3 variant. He follows the oldest manuscripts in reading "confesseth not" and records the Vulgate's reading, a spirit that "destroys" or "does away with" Jesus. Guzik links the error to Docetism and takes "us" in v6 as the apostles' authoritative witness, now preserved in Scripture.

#### God Is Love (vv7–21)
- **"Let us love one another, for love is from God" (v7).** Love's source is God. Human love at its best is a reflection of his. So "whoever loves has been born of God and knows God." John means the self-giving love of verses 9–10, not mere affection.
- **"God is love" (v8).** The Greek is *ho theos agapē estin*, literally "the God love is." John does not say "love is God," which would make love into a god. He says God's very nature is love. In the same letter he has said "God is light" (1:5), and the two belong together: God's love is holy, and his holiness is loving. Christians have long seen in this verse a hint of the Trinity: love needs someone to love, and the Father has loved the Son "before the foundation of the world" (John 17:24).
- **"God sent his only Son into the world, so that we might live through him" (v9).** "Only" is *monogenēs*, which the lexicon gives as "only," "only begotten" or "unique." It is used of an only child (Luke 7:12; 8:42) and of Isaac, Abraham's one son of promise (Heb 11:17). The echo of John 3:16 is clear. Love is not just declared. It was "made manifest" in a sending.
- **"In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us" (v10).** Love is defined by God's initiative, not ours. He loved first, and he loved people who had not loved him (Rom 5:8). He "sent his Son to be the propitiation (*hilasmos*) for our sins." This is the second and last use of the word in the New Testament (2:2). The sacrifice that satisfies God's justice is itself the gift of God's love.
- **"If God so loved us, we also ought to love one another" (v11).** One would expect "we also ought to love God." John says "one another" instead. God's love does not stop with us. It flows through us.
- **"No one has ever seen God" (v12).** John said the same in his Gospel and then added that the only Son "has made him known" (John 1:18). Here he adds something remarkable: "if we love one another, God abides in us and his love is perfected in us." The invisible God becomes visible in a loving church.
- **"Because he has given us of his Spirit" (v13).** Again the Spirit is the inner assurance (3:24). "Of his Spirit" means a share of the Spirit, not that the Spirit is divided.
- **"We have seen and testify" (v14).** John comes back to the eyewitness language of 1:1–3. "The Savior of the world" appears only here and in John 4:42, where the Samaritans confess it. Roman emperors were sometimes hailed as "savior" in inscriptions across Asia, so believers there would have heard the contrast.
- **"Whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God" (v15).** Faith and love belong together. Verse 12 said God abides in those who love. Verse 15 says God abides in those who confess Jesus.
- **"We have come to know and to believe the love that God has for us" (v16).** Both verbs are perfect tense: knowledge and faith that have taken hold and remain. Then John repeats the great statement, "God is love," and adds the key word of the letter: whoever abides in love abides in God.
- **"So that we may have confidence for the day of judgment" (v17).** *Parrēsia* again (2:28; 3:21; 5:14), boldness before the Judge. "As he is so also are we in this world" probably means that as Jesus is now, the beloved Son accepted by the Father, so are believers, even while still in this world. Others read it as "as he is in love, so are we." Either way, the believer can face the judgment day in the Son.
- **"There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear" (v18).** The fear here is not reverent awe (Prov 1:7) but dread of punishment. "Punishment" (*kolasis*) is used only one other time in the New Testament, of "eternal punishment" (Matt 25:46). Someone who truly receives God's love no longer lives under the threat of that verdict (Rom 8:1, 15). "Perfect" (*teleios*) means complete, brought to its goal, not flawless. As love grows, dread shrinks.
- **"We love because he first loved us" (v19).** Later manuscripts (followed by the KJV) add "him": "We love him." The shorter text is better, and it is broader: all our love, for God and for others, is a response to his love.
- **"He who does not love his brother whom he has seen cannot love God whom he has not seen" (v20).** The final "liar" in the letter's run of claims ("If anyone says…"). The logic is simple: the brother is visible, God is not. If love fails where it can be tested, it is not really there where it cannot be. The ESV footnote notes that some manuscripts have "how can he" instead of "cannot."
- **"Whoever loves God must also love his brother" (v21).** Jesus joined the two great commandments, love for God and love for neighbor (Mark 12:29–31). John says they cannot be separated.
- **Commentators:** On "God is love," Henry says God's "nature and essence are love, his will and works are primarily love." JFB calls God "fundamentally and essentially LOVE." Guzik is more guarded: love colors every one of God's attributes, but it is not his only attribute and is nothing like sentimentality. JFB on v10: "The love was all on God's side, none on ours." Guzik says the Father's love and the Son's love must never be set against each other at the cross. On v18 Henry describes the fear that love drives out as "a disquieting torturing passion," and Guzik distinguishes this tormenting fear from the reverent fear of God that Scripture commands (1 Pet 2:17). On v20 Henry observes, "The eye is wont to affect the heart; things unseen less catch the mind." The JFB text that could be fetched breaks off at v17, so it is not drawn on for vv18–21.

#### Pointing to Christ
John never defines love in the abstract. He points to an event: "God sent his only Son into the world" (v9) "to be the propitiation for our sins" (v10). The test of every spirit is what it says about this Jesus, come in the flesh. The proof of God's love is that this Jesus died for those who had not loved him. And the reason his people can face judgment without dread is that they are in him, so "as he is so also are we in this world" (v17). The invisible God was seen in Jesus, and he is seen again whenever those who belong to Jesus love one another.
