*Setting:* Numbers picks up where Leviticus left off, with Israel still camped at Mount Sinai. It is "the first day of the second month, in the second year" after the exodus (v1), one month after the tabernacle was set up (Exod 40:17) and about nineteen days before the camp breaks and marches north (10:11). The English title "Numbers" comes from the Greek and Latin versions and refers to the two censuses in chapters 1 and 26. The Hebrew title is *Bemidbar*, "in the wilderness," from this first verse, and that describes the book better. Numbers covers nearly forty years, from Sinai to the plains of Moab across from Jericho, and most of it is the story of a generation that did not trust God. This first census counts the men who should have conquered Canaan. Chapter 26 will count their children, who actually did.

#### A Census of Israel's Warriors (vv1–46)
- **"In the tent of meeting" (v1).** In Exodus, God spoke from the mountain. Now he speaks from the tabernacle in the middle of the camp. God has come down to live among his people and lead them, as Leviticus 26:11–12 promised.
- **"Take a census… every male, head by head" (v2).** The Hebrew is literally "lift up the head" of the congregation, an idiom for counting. Each man was counted by name, within his **"clan"** and **"fathers' house"** (extended family), inside his tribe. The census was also a genealogy. It recorded who belonged where, which would matter when the land was divided by tribes and clans (26:52–56).
- **"From twenty years old and upward… able to go to war" (v3).** This was a military count. **"Company by company"** uses a word for army divisions, the same word in "LORD of hosts" (*tsevaot*, "armies"). Israel had left Egypt as a crowd of slaves (Exod 12:41 already calls them "the hosts of the LORD"). Now they are being formed into an army, preparing to take the land God promised. Women, children, older men and the Levites were not counted.
- **Is counting wrong?** Later, when David counted Israel, God judged the nation (2 Sam 24). The difference is that this census was commanded by God (v19), while David's was born of pride in his own strength. Exodus 30:12 required a half-shekel "ransom" from everyone counted, and an earlier count had collected exactly that from 603,550 men (Exod 38:25–26), the same total as here.
- **The twelve leaders (vv4–16).** Each tribe supplied **"the head of the house of his fathers"** to help. These men appear again as tribal leaders bringing gifts for the altar (ch 7) and leading on the march (ch 10). Notice **Nahshon the son of Amminadab** of Judah (v7). His sister Elisheba married Aaron (Exod 6:23), and he is an ancestor of Boaz, David and Jesus (Ruth 4:20–22; Matt 1:4). Many of the names include God's name, such as *Elizur* ("my God is a rock"), *Shelumiel* ("God is my peace") and *Zurishaddai* ("the Almighty is my rock"), small confessions of faith carried through generations of slavery.
- **"The sons of Joseph… Ephraim… Manasseh" (v10).** Levi has been taken out of the list (v47), and Joseph is counted as two tribes, after his two sons. Jacob had adopted them as his own (Gen 48:5), so there are still twelve tribes.
- **"Who registered themselves… as the LORD commanded" (vv18–19).** It all happened on the same day the command came, the first of the second month. The people obeyed at once.
- **The tribal totals (vv20–43).** Each tribe is counted in the same words, a rhythm that sounds monotonous to modern readers but would have meant something to each tribe as they heard their name. Reuben comes first as **"Israel's firstborn"** (v20), but he had lost the firstborn's leadership (Gen 49:3–4; 1 Chr 5:1–2). **Judah**, with 74,600, is the largest. Jacob had said, "The scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10), and Judah leads the march (10:14). **Ephraim** (40,500) outnumbers his older brother **Manasseh** (32,200), just as Jacob crossed his hands to bless the younger first (Gen 48:14–20). The order here puts **Gad** with Reuben and Simeon, the group they will camp with on the south side (2:10–16).
- **"All those listed were 603,550" (v46).** With women, children and older men added, this suggests a population of perhaps two million or more. JFB calls it "an astonishing increase" from the seventy people who went down to Egypt with Jacob (Gen 46:27). That growth is exactly what Exodus records: "the people of Israel were fruitful… so that the land was filled with them" (Exod 1:7).
- **How should the large numbers be read?** This is a well-known question. Some scholars point out that the Hebrew word for "thousand," *eleph*, can also mean a clan or military unit (Judg 6:15, "my clan is the weakest"). On that reading, "46,500" might mean "46 units, 500 men," giving a much smaller army. Others note the difficulty of feeding and moving two million people in the Sinai desert, though the Bible presents that as a miracle (manna, water from the rock). Still others see the numbers as stylized or symbolic. The traditional view, held by many conservative scholars, takes the numbers at face value, and observes that the totals are consistent across Exodus 12:37, Exodus 38:26, Numbers 1 and Numbers 11:21, and that each tribe's sum adds up exactly to the total. The text itself plainly means a very large people, the fulfilment of God's promise to make Abraham's offspring as many as the stars (Gen 15:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry describes Israel being formed into "a commonwealth, or rather a kingdom," where "the Lord was their King," and "next to the institution of good laws was necessary the institution of good order." JFB stresses that Ephraim outnumbered Manasseh "as had been predicted," and contrasts this commanded census with David's sinful one. Guzik notes Nahshon's place in Jesus' genealogy and estimates Israel at two to two and a half million. He says the large numbers have been questioned but trusts the text, allows for possible copying errors in a few places, and points out that all the totals are rounded except Gad's. He draws a lesson from the count: God knows each person by name, and believers do well to take an honest inventory of their own spiritual strength before they set out.

#### Levites Exempted (vv47–54)
- **"Only the tribe of Levi you shall not list" (v49).** The Levites were not counted for war because they had a different calling. They will get their own census in chapter 3, counted from one month old, not twenty years, because their service was not military. The Levites had earned their place by standing with Moses at the golden calf (Exod 32:26–29), and God took them in place of Israel's firstborn sons (3:11–13).
- **"Appoint the Levites over the tabernacle of the testimony" (v50).** The "testimony" is the tablets of the covenant, kept in the ark. The Levites' jobs were to **carry** the tabernacle, **take it down** when the camp moved, **set it up** when it stopped, and **guard** it (vv50–51). Chapters 3–4 give the details, clan by clan.
- **"If any outsider comes near, he shall be put to death" (v51).** An "outsider" here means anyone who was not a Levite, including other Israelites. God's presence was holy and dangerous. Uzzah's death when he touched the ark centuries later (2 Sam 6:6–7) shows this was not an empty warning.
- **"Each man by his own standard" (v52).** Each tribe camped under its own banner. Chapter 2 sets out the layout: three tribes on each side, all facing the tabernacle in the center.
- **"So that there may be no wrath on the congregation" (v53).** The Levites camped in a ring around the tabernacle as a buffer. This was not to keep God away from Israel, but to protect Israel from the consequences of coming near God carelessly. They were like guards at the palace gate, and their care spared the people.
- **"Thus did the people of Israel" (v54).** The chapter ends, like several in this section of Numbers, with full obedience. Sadly, it won't last. By chapter 11, the complaining begins.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the Levites were "discharged from military services," set apart to guard the tabernacle "from being either plundered or profaned," and adds that "The ministry is itself work enough for a whole man." JFB says the Levites camped around the tabernacle "so that there should be no manifestation of the divine displeasure among the people," and notes they replaced the firstborn. Guzik observes that some of the most important things in God's work, like the Levites' service, are not measured in the count.

#### Pointing to Christ
This census counts an army of 603,550 men, and almost every one of them died in the wilderness because they would not trust God to bring them into the land (14:29). Numbers is a sobering book. But the chapter also shows a God who counts his people one by one, by name. Jesus is the Good Shepherd who "calls his own sheep by name" (John 10:3), who knows the number of hairs on our heads (Matt 10:30), and who will lose none of those the Father has given him (John 6:39). Nahshon's name in the list is a quiet pointer to the line of Judah that leads to Jesus. And the tabernacle at the center of the camp, guarded so carefully, points to the one in whom God "tabernacled" among us (John 1:14). Through him, the people who could not come near now "draw near with confidence" (Heb 4:16).
