*Setting:* James, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, is writing to Jewish believers scattered outside Judea, probably in the 40s or 50s. Chapter 1 ended with a challenge: be "doers of the word, and not hearers only," and the test of real religion is how it treats the helpless, "orphans and widows in their affliction" (1:22, 27). Chapter 2 presses that home in two directions. First, a church that fawns over a rich visitor and pushes a poor one aside has broken God's royal law. Second, a "faith" that offers a hungry brother only kind words is not living faith at all. The second half contains the most debated verses in the letter, which seem at first to contradict Paul. Read in context, James and Paul are answering different questions and agree on the answer that matters.

#### The Sin of Partiality (vv1–13)
- **"Show no partiality" (v1).** "Partiality" is *prosōpolēmpsia*, literally "receiving the face," a Greek coinage from a Hebrew idiom meaning to judge by appearance, to favor someone because of who they are. The Old Testament forbids it in judges (Lev 19:15; Deut 1:17), and Paul uses the same word to say God never does it (Rom 2:11; Eph 6:9; Col 3:25). The word is plural in Greek: "acts of favoritism," the many small ways it shows.
- **"As you hold the faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory" (v1).** The Greek reads literally "our Lord Jesus Christ of the glory," and translations take it different ways: "our glorious Lord Jesus Christ" (NIV) or "the Lord of glory" (ESV). Some suggest James is calling Jesus *the* Glory, the presence of God among his people, as the *shekinah* filled the tabernacle (John 1:14). Either way, an early Jewish believer, Jesus' own brother, gives Jesus the honor that belongs to God. Next to that glory, the glitter of a rich man's ring is nothing.
- **"A man wearing a gold ring and fine clothing comes into your assembly" (v2).** "Gold ring" is a single word, *chrysodaktylios*, "gold-fingered," found nowhere earlier in Greek. Wealthy Romans sometimes wore rings on several fingers as a display of status. "Fine clothing" is literally "bright" or "shining." "Assembly" is *synagōgē*, "synagogue," the only time the New Testament uses this word for a Christian gathering. It fits very early Jewish believers who still thought of their meeting in synagogue terms.
- **"A poor man in shabby clothing" (v2).** "Poor" (*ptōchos*) means destitute, a beggar, not just someone with little. "Shabby" is the word for "filthy" used in 1:21 (*rhyparia*): dirty clothes, perhaps the only ones he owns. The scene may be a worship meeting, or, as some suggest, the church gathered to settle a dispute, since vv4 and 6 speak of judges and courts. Most take it as worship.
- **"You sit here in a good place… You stand over there, or, Sit down at my feet" (v3).** Synagogues had benches along the walls and seats of honor near the front (Matt 23:6). The poor man is told to stand at the back or sit on the floor by someone's footstool. The details are vivid, and James may be describing something he had seen.
- **"Have you not then made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?" (v4).** "Made distinctions" is *diakrinomai*, the same verb James used of the "doubting" man in 1:6. Favoritism is a kind of double-mindedness: claiming to honor the Lord of glory while really honoring money. "Judges with evil thoughts" means they have appointed themselves judges, and their standard is corrupt.
- **"Has not God chosen those who are poor in the world to be rich in faith" (v5).** God's choice runs the other way from the world's (1 Cor 1:26–29; Luke 6:20). This does not mean every poor person is saved or every rich person lost. It means God delights to lift the lowly (1:9) and that the poor, with fewer things to trust in, have often been quickest to trust him. **"Heirs of the kingdom, which he has promised to those who love him"** repeats the phrase about the crown of life in 1:12.
- **"But you have dishonored the poor man" (v6).** The church has honored those God despises and despised those God honors.
- **"Are not the rich the ones who oppress you, and… drag you into court?" (v6).** James points out the absurdity. The rich, as a class, were exploiting these believers, taking poor debtors to court and seizing what little they had (compare 5:1–6). Yet the church still flattered them, perhaps hoping for donations or protection.
- **"The honorable name by which you were called" (v7).** Literally "the good name called over you." In the Old Testament, a name "called over" people or things marked them as belonging to the one named (Deut 28:10; Amos 9:12, which James quotes in Acts 15:17). Believers bore the name of Jesus, probably first spoken over them at baptism. The rich mocked that name.
- **"If you really fulfill the royal law… 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself'" (v8).** From Leviticus 19:18, which Jesus called the second great commandment (Mark 12:31). It is "royal" because it is the law of the King and his kingdom (v5), and perhaps because it rules over the other commands, which all hang on it (Matt 22:40). James is drawing on Leviticus 19 throughout: three verses before "love your neighbor" comes "you shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great" (Lev 19:15).
- **"If you show partiality, you are committing sin" (v9).** Favoritism cannot hide behind the love command, as if being nice to the rich man were "loving my neighbor." The poor man is a neighbor too, and favoritism is not a small social failing but sin.
- **"Whoever keeps the whole law but fails in one point has become guilty of all of it" (v10).** The law is not a list of separate rules where a good score in one makes up for a bad score in another. It is the expression of one will, from one Lawgiver (v11). Break one command and the whole is broken, just as one break in a chain lets the whole thing fall. Jewish teachers said something similar. This does not mean every sin is equally serious (Jesus spoke of "the weightier matters," Matt 23:23). It means every sin makes a person a lawbreaker.
- **"He who said, 'Do not commit adultery,' also said, 'Do not murder'" (v11).** From the Ten Commandments (Exod 20:13–14). Adultery comes first here, the order found in some manuscripts of the Greek Old Testament and also in Luke 18:20 and Romans 13:9. The point is that it is the same God speaking both.
- **"So speak and so act as those who are to be judged under the law of liberty" (v12).** "The law of liberty" is God's law as fulfilled by Jesus and written on the heart (1:25). Believers are free from the law as a way of being made right with God, but they are not free from accountability. Words and deeds both matter, a hint of what is coming in chapter 3.
- **"Judgment is without mercy to one who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment" (v13).** This echoes Jesus: "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy" (Matt 5:7), and the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matt 18:23–35). "Triumphs" means boasts against, exults over. The merciful will stand at judgment not because their mercy earns forgiveness but because it shows they have received God's mercy. Mercy is the last word.
- **Commentators:** JFB says believers "derive all their glory from their union with" Christ, so favoritism clashes with the faith itself. It notes that the verb for "made distinctions" in v4 is related to the word for doubting, and calls the royal law "the law that is king of all laws." On v10 JFB compares breaking one point to a man whose body is partly diseased with leprosy: the whole man is a leper. Guzik draws from "Lord of glory" that the earliest Christians regarded Jesus as God. He adds that God's choice of the poor does not mean the church should be prejudiced against the rich. Disputes should still be judged on the facts.

#### Faith Without Works Is Dead (vv14–26)
- **"If someone says he has faith but does not have works" (v14).** Notice the wording: "says he has faith." James is not talking about real faith that somehow failed to produce works, but about a *claim* to faith with nothing behind it. "Works" here are not Jewish rituals but deeds of love and obedience, like feeding the hungry (vv15–16).
- **"Can that faith save him?" (v14).** The Greek has the article: "*the* faith," meaning *that kind* of faith, the faith just described. The question expects the answer no. James is not asking whether faith saves. He is asking whether a faith without works saves.
- **"Go in peace, be warmed and filled" (vv15–16).** "Go in peace" was a common Jewish farewell, a blessing. Here it is a pious way of sending someone off empty-handed, perhaps with the thought that God would provide. "Be warmed and filled" may be read as a command or as a wish: "keep warm and eat well." The speaker means it kindly, but the brother or sister is still cold and hungry. **"What good is that?"** echoes the opening question in v14. John asks the same (1 John 3:17–18).
- **"Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead" (v17).** Not weak, but dead, a corpse. James says it three times (vv17, 20 [as "useless"], 26).
- **"But someone will say, 'You have faith and I have works'" (v18).** Another imagined objector, in the diatribe style James used in 1:13. The objector seems to say faith and works are separate gifts: some people are believers, others are activists, and both are fine. Because ancient Greek had no quotation marks, scholars debate where the objector's words end. The ESV ends them after "works." James's reply is clear either way: **"Show me your faith apart from your works, and I will show you my faith by my works."** Faith is invisible. Works are how it becomes visible.
- **"You believe that God is one; you do well" (v19).** This is the Shema, Deuteronomy 6:4, "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one," which devout Jews recited twice a day. Believing it is correct. "You do well" may be sincere or a little ironic.
- **"Even the demons believe, and shudder!" (v19).** The demons are perfectly orthodox. In the Gospels they knew exactly who Jesus was (Mark 1:24; 5:7). "Shudder" (*phrissō*) means to bristle or have one's hair stand on end in terror. Their belief does not lead to love or trust but to fear. Mere agreement that a doctrine is true is not saving faith.
- **"Faith apart from works is useless" (v20).** There is a pun in the Greek: faith without *erga* ("works") is *argē* ("useless," literally "workless," from *a-ergos*). Faith that does no work does not work.
- **"Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered up his son Isaac?" (v21).** The binding of Isaac (Gen 22) was the supreme test of Abraham's trust in God's promise. "Our father" fits Jewish readers. James chooses the very figure Paul uses in Romans 4 and Galatians 3.
- **"Faith was active along with his works, and faith was completed by his works" (v22).** Faith and works are not rivals. "Active along with" means working together. "Completed" (*teleioō*) means brought to maturity, the same idea as in 1:4. Abraham's faith did not begin at Mount Moriah, but it reached its full expression there.
- **"Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness" (v23).** James quotes Genesis 15:6, the same verse Paul builds on (Rom 4:3; Gal 3:6). Genesis 15 comes before Ishmael's birth, and Genesis 22 is many years later, after Isaac is born and has grown into a boy. James says that later act "fulfilled" the earlier statement, proving it true. Abraham was declared righteous by faith long before the offering, and his obedience showed that faith was real. **"He was called a friend of God"** comes from 2 Chronicles 20:7 and Isaiah 41:8. Faith in James is a loyal relationship, the opposite of being a "friend of the world" (4:4).
- **"A person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (v24).** This is the only place in the Bible where the words "faith alone" occur, and it is a denial. Paul says, "one is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Rom 3:28). Do they contradict each other? Most interpreters, Protestant, Catholic and Orthodox, say no, though they explain it in different ways:
- **How James and Paul fit together.** First, they mean different things by "works." Paul means "works of the law," things like circumcision and law-keeping offered as grounds for acceptance with God. James means deeds of love flowing from faith. Second, they mean different things by "faith." Paul means living trust in Christ, the kind that "works through love" (Gal 5:6). James is attacking an empty claim, a faith like the demons'. Third, many Protestant interpreters say "justified" in James means shown to be righteous, vindicated, as in "wisdom is justified by her deeds" (Matt 11:19). The lexicon allows that sense, though it actually lists James's uses under "declare righteous." So others read James as speaking of God's final verdict at judgment, which will take account of the works that true faith produces (v12; Matt 12:37; Rom 2:6–7). Catholic and Orthodox teaching gives works a real part in final justification, as the fruit of grace. On all these readings, Paul and James agree that real faith works and that works without faith cannot save.
- **Luther's struggle with James.** Martin Luther, in the preface to his 1522 German New Testament, called James "an epistle of straw" compared with books like Romans and Galatians, because he thought it did not preach Christ clearly and seemed to contradict justification by faith. He kept James in his Bible, quoted it often and softened his remark in later editions. Most of the Reformers who followed him read James as Paul's ally against dead faith, not his opponent.
- **"Rahab the prostitute… received the messengers and sent them out by another way" (v25).** Rahab is the opposite of Abraham in almost every way: a woman, a Gentile, a Canaanite and a prostitute (Josh 2). She confessed that "the LORD your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath" (Josh 2:11), then risked her life hiding the spies. Hebrews 11:31 calls this "faith." James calls it "works." It was both at once. She became an ancestor of David and of Jesus (Matt 1:5).
- **"As the body apart from the spirit is dead, so also faith apart from works is dead" (v26).** God breathed life into Adam's body (Gen 2:7). Without breath, a body is a corpse. James does not say works are the cause of faith's life. He says their absence shows there is no life there. A real, living faith breathes.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "that faith" in v14 as "such a faith," an empty profession, and says works "show" faith, since "faith is unseen save by God." It explains Abraham's justification as "evidentially" by works, and says Paul speaks of faith "in its germ," James of faith in its "visible development." Henry: "That faith which does not save will not really profit us," and of the demons, "thus far the devils go." He uses Rahab to show that "faith will save the worst, when evidenced by proper works." Guzik agrees that James is not contradicting Paul, pairing Ephesians 2:8–9 with 2:10: saved by grace through faith, for good works. He notes that Abraham was justified by faith in Genesis 15 long before the test of Genesis 22.

#### Pointing to Christ
"The Lord of glory" stands at the head of this chapter, and he is the one who reverses the church's partiality. He "though he was rich, yet for your sake became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). He was the poor man pushed to the back and finally "outside the gate" (Heb 13:12). He is also the one in whom faith and works are never separate. He trusted his Father perfectly and obeyed him perfectly, all the way to the cross. Abraham offered his son and was spared. God gave his own Son, and it is that mercy which "triumphs over judgment" for everyone who truly trusts him. Faith in him is never only words, because it is joined to a living Lord.
