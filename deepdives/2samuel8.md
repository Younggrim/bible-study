*Setting:* David's reign in Jerusalem, roughly 1000–990 BC. In the previous chapter God promised David a lasting dynasty and said, "I will make for you a great name" and "I will give you rest from all your enemies" (7:9, 11). This chapter shows those promises being kept. It is a summary, not a single story. It gathers David's wars on every side, west, east, north and south, into one report, without strict time order, and ends with a list of his chief officials. Some of these wars are told in more detail later (ch 10 covers the Ammonite and Aramean war), and 1 Chronicles 18 gives a near-parallel account. The chapter closes the first great section of 2 Samuel, the rise and success of David, before the darker chapters that begin with Bathsheba.

#### David's Victories (vv1–14)
- **"After this David defeated the Philistines and subdued them" (v1).** West first. The Philistines, Israel's great enemy since the days of Samson, are finally brought under control. "Subdued" is the word used of Israel's enemies being humbled under the judges (Judg 3:30; 1 Sam 7:13).
- **"David took Metheg-ammah out of the hand of the Philistines" (v1).** The phrase is obscure. It may mean "the bridle of the mother city," that is, control of the chief Philistine town. Chronicles says plainly that David "took Gath and its villages" (1 Chr 18:1). David had once lived at Gath as a refugee and vassal (1 Sam 27). Now he holds it.
- **"He defeated Moab and he measured them with a line" (v2).** East next. Moab lay across the Dead Sea. David's ancestor Ruth was a Moabite (Ruth 4:13–22), and David had once left his parents with the king of Moab for safety (1 Sam 22:3–4). Something must have gone badly wrong since then. A Jewish tradition says the Moabites killed David's parents, but the Bible says nothing about it.
- **"Two lines he measured to be put to death, and one full line to be spared" (v2).** The captured soldiers were made to lie down in rows, and David measured them off with a cord. Two-thirds were executed and one-third spared. This is hard to read. It was a recognized practice in ancient warfare, and the text records it without praise or criticism. Some readers see a measure of mercy in sparing a third, when complete slaughter was common. Others see it as a harsh act from a man who would later be told he had "shed much blood" (1 Chr 22:8). It is honest to admit that the Bible reports many things its heroes did without approving all of them. Balaam's ancient oracle had said a star from Jacob would "crush the forehead of Moab" (Num 24:17).
- **"The Moabites became servants to David and brought tribute" (v2).** "Servants" here means subject peoples, vassals. "Tribute" was yearly payment to the overlord.
- **"Hadadezer the son of Rehob, king of Zobah" (v3).** North now. Zobah was an Aramean (Syrian) kingdom in the Beqaa Valley region of modern Lebanon and Syria. Hadadezer's name means "Hadad is help." Hadad was the Aramean storm god. **"As he went to restore his power at the river Euphrates."** It is not clear whether "he" means Hadadezer or David. Either way, this campaign reached toward the Euphrates, the border God had promised Abraham: "from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates" (Gen 15:18).
- **"David hamstrung all the chariot horses but left enough for 100 chariots" (v4).** To hamstring is to cut the tendon at the back of the leg so a horse can never run or pull a chariot again. Joshua did the same at God's command (Josh 11:6, 9). The law told Israel's king not to "acquire many horses for himself" (Deut 17:16), so that he would trust God, not military power: "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (Ps 20:7). David keeps only a hundred.
- **"The Syrians of Damascus came to help Hadadezer" (v5).** Damascus was another Aramean kingdom. Its army comes to help and is crushed. David puts **garrisons** there, groups of soldiers stationed to hold the territory (v6). Damascus would later become one of Israel's fiercest enemies (1 Kgs 11:23–25; 2 Kgs 8:12).
- **"And the LORD gave victory to David wherever he went" (v6).** This line comes twice (vv6, 14), framing the northern and southern wars. The Hebrew word is *yasha*, "to save" or "deliver," the root of the name Joshua and of Jesus (*Yeshua*). The narrator wants it clear: the victories are God's.
- **"The shields of gold" (v7).** Hadadezer's guards carried gold shields, probably for display. David brings them to Jerusalem. Generations later these same shields come out of the temple to arm the guards who crowned young King Joash (2 Kgs 11:10).
- **"Very much bronze" (v8).** Chronicles adds that Solomon later used this bronze for the great bronze sea, the pillars and the temple vessels (1 Chr 18:8). David's wars are already supplying the temple he is not allowed to build.
- **"Toi king of Hamath" (v9).** Hamath was a kingdom farther north on the Orontes River, in modern Syria. Hadadezer had been Toi's enemy, so Toi is glad to see him beaten. He sends his son to congratulate David and seek friendship, with gifts of silver, gold and bronze. In practice this was a smaller king accepting David as the great power.
- **"Joram" (v10).** The name contains a short form of the LORD's name, "Jo-" for Yahweh, and means "the LORD is exalted." Chronicles calls him Hadoram (1 Chr 18:10), "Hadad is exalted." Perhaps he carried a name to suit each court, or perhaps the name was adjusted in the telling.
- **"These also King David dedicated to the LORD" (v11).** David does not keep the treasure for himself. He sets it apart for God, along with the spoil from Edom, Moab, Ammon, the Philistines and Amalek (v12). This also honors the law that the king must not "acquire for himself excessive silver and gold" (Deut 17:17). Chronicles says David stored up these treasures for the temple (1 Chr 22:14; 26:26–28).
- **"David made a name for himself when he returned from striking down 18,000 Edomites in the Valley of Salt" (v13).** South last. The Valley of Salt was probably near the south end of the Dead Sea, toward Edom. The standard Hebrew text actually reads "Aram" (Syrians) here, but in Hebrew writing "Aram" and "Edom" differ by a single letter that looks very similar, so most translations follow a few Hebrew manuscripts, the Greek and Chronicles in reading "Edom" (1 Chr 18:12). The location and v14 fit Edom. Chronicles credits the victory to Abishai, and the title of Psalm 60 credits Joab with 12,000. Commanders' victories were counted as the king's, and the numbers may count different stages of the campaign.
- **"Made a name for himself" (v13).** God had promised, "I will make for you a great name" (7:9). Here the promise comes true, though the phrase is a little uneasy, since elsewhere making a name for oneself is a mark of pride (Gen 11:4).
- **"All the Edomites became David's servants" (v14).** This fulfilled an old word. Before Esau and Jacob were born, God said, "the older shall serve the younger" (Gen 25:23), and Balaam foresaw "Edom shall be dispossessed" (Num 24:18).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in the Philistines' defeat a picture of Christ overcoming his people's enemies, and he notes the mercy of sparing a third of Moab. JFB, describing the measuring line, calls it a custom "not, perhaps, usually practised by the people of God." Guzik points out the painful history with Moab, given David's Moabite ancestry and his parents' refuge there, and suggests Moab may have betrayed them. JFB explains the hamstringing: "the horses were thus mutilated because they were forbidden to the Hebrews." Guzik ties it to trust in God rather than horses, and sees the reach to the Euphrates as the fulfilment of the promise to Abraham. Henry: "All the precious things David was master of, were dedicated things; they were designed for building the temple." JFB calls this "the first instance of a practice" of storing up spoil for the temple, and on v13 agrees that "Edomites" is "the true reading."

#### David's Officials (vv15–18)
- **"So David reigned over all Israel" (v15).** The wars are a means to an end: a secure, united kingdom.
- **"David administered justice and equity to all his people" (v15).** This is the heart of good kingship in Israel. "Justice" (*mishpat*) means right judgment, and "equity" (*tsedaqah*, usually "righteousness") means doing what is right. The two words often go together in the prophets as a description of what God wants from rulers (Jer 22:3, 15; Isa 9:7). This is David at his best. Later his failure to give justice will open the door to Absalom's rebellion (15:1–6).
- **"Joab the son of Zeruiah was over the army" (v16).** Joab, David's nephew, has been commander since the capture of Jerusalem (1 Chr 11:6).
- **"Jehoshaphat the son of Ahilud was recorder" (v16).** The recorder (literally "the one who causes to remember") kept the royal records and may have served as a kind of chief of staff, announcing matters to the king.
- **"Zadok the son of Ahitub and Ahimelech the son of Abiathar were priests" (v17).** Two priestly families serve side by side. Abiathar escaped Saul's massacre of the priests at Nob and joined David (1 Sam 22:20). The order of the names here seems reversed, since elsewhere Abiathar is the son of Ahimelech (1 Sam 22:20; 2 Sam 20:25). Many think a copyist swapped them, though it is possible Abiathar had a son named Ahimelech after his grandfather (1 Chr 24:6). Zadok's line will become the chief priesthood under Solomon (1 Kgs 2:35), fulfilling the word against Eli's house (1 Sam 2:35).
- **"Seraiah was secretary" (v17).** The secretary or scribe handled official letters and records. His name appears in other forms elsewhere (Shavsha, 1 Chr 18:16; Sheva, 2 Sam 20:25).
- **"Benaiah the son of Jehoiada was over the Cherethites and the Pelethites" (v18).** Benaiah was one of David's mightiest warriors (23:20–23). The Cherethites and Pelethites were the royal bodyguard, foreign soldiers loyal to the king personally. The Cherethites were probably from Crete, and the Pelethites may have been Philistines, who also came from the Aegean area (Amos 9:7; Zeph 2:5). They stay loyal to David through every later crisis (15:18; 20:7; 1 Kgs 1:38).
- **"And David's sons were priests" (v18).** This is surprising, since only Levites descended from Aaron could be priests. The Hebrew word is *kohanim*, the normal word for priests. Chronicles, however, describes the same men as "chief officials in the service of the king" (1 Chr 18:17). There are several views. Some think *kohen* could also mean a high royal official or adviser at this time. Others think David's sons had a ceremonial role at court without offering sacrifices. Others think the Chronicler deliberately clarified a title that later readers could misunderstand. The text does not say they offered sacrifices.
- **Commentators:** Henry praises David's rule: "David neither did wrong, nor denied or delayed right to any," and sees in it a picture of Christ's righteous reign. Guzik calls justice the basic duty of government and connects it to Romans 13:1–7. JFB calls the recorder a "historiographer or daily annalist," and says Joab held his post "by virtue of a special promise." On v18, both Henry and JFB follow the older reading "chief rulers," taking David's sons as high officials rather than priests, and Guzik likewise reads "chief ministers." The Tyndale notes suggest David may have created a royal religious office for his sons, separate from the Levitical priests.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter shows the shape of the promised King's reign: victory on every side, enemies subdued, treasures brought in and dedicated to God, and "justice and equity to all his people." Isaiah takes up these very words for the coming Son of David: "Of the increase of his government and of peace there will be no end, on the throne of David and over his kingdom, to establish it and to uphold it with justice and with righteousness from this time forth and forevermore" (Isa 9:7). David's victories came at great cost in blood, and his justice later failed. Jesus wins his victory by giving his own blood (Col 2:15), and his justice never fails. "The LORD gave victory to David wherever he went." God gives his people the victory "through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 15:57).
