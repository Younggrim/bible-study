*Setting:* Egypt, probably in the fifteenth or thirteenth century BC (scholars debate the date of the exodus). Four plagues have fallen: blood, frogs, gnats and flies. The magicians have given up ("This is the finger of God," 8:19), God has begun to set Goshen apart from the rest of Egypt, and Pharaoh has twice promised to let Israel go and twice broken his word. Chapter 9 brings plagues five, six and seven: a deadly disease on livestock, boils, and a hailstorm. The pressure rises, the warnings grow longer, and for the first time some Egyptians take the LORD's word seriously.

#### The Fifth Plague: Egyptian Livestock Die (vv1–7)
- **"Thus says the LORD, the God of the Hebrews" (v1).** The same demand again: **"Let my people go, that they may serve me."** God keeps calling Israel "my people." Pharaoh is holding someone else's property. A warning still comes first, which is itself mercy: Pharaoh is told what will happen and given time to change his mind.
- **"The hand of the LORD will fall with a very severe plague" (v3).** Earlier signs came through Aaron's staff or Moses' hand. This one is simply "the hand of the LORD." The word for "plague" is *dever*, pestilence, a deadly livestock disease. Notice the list: **horses, donkeys, camels, herds and flocks** "in the field." Egypt's wealth, transport, farming and army all depended on these animals.
- **Egypt's sacred animals.** Egyptians honored several animals as signs of divine power. The Apis bull at Memphis was treated as a living image of the god Ptah, the cow was linked with the goddess Hathor, and the ram with Amun and Khnum. A disease that kills cattle in the field strikes at what Egypt revered.
- **"The LORD will make a distinction" (v4).** The line drawn around Goshen in 8:22 now runs between herds. The Tyndale notes point out that this is what rules out a purely natural explanation: diseases do not check whose animals they are infecting.
- **"Tomorrow the LORD will do this thing" (v5).** God sets the time in advance so that no one can call it chance.
- **"All the livestock of the Egyptians died" (v6).** "All" is the Bible's ordinary way of saying "the whole range of," not a census. Some Egyptian animals are still alive in v19 and get boils in v9. The point is the scale and the contrast: **"not one of the livestock of the people of Israel died."**
- **"Pharaoh sent, and behold" (v7).** Pharaoh checks. He gets the evidence he wanted, and it changes nothing. **"The heart of Pharaoh was hardened"** uses *kaved*, "heavy," the word used for Pharaoh's own stubbornness in 8:15 and 8:32.
- **Commentators:** JFB stresses that this plague came "directly by the hand of the Lord," not through the staff, and that "all" means "not absolutely every beast." Because Egyptians venerated useful animals, it "struck a heavy blow at their superstition." Guzik links the plague with Hathor and sees the warning itself as mercy. Henry reads the whole episode as a contest over whose word will stand, and notes that God keeps a distinction for his people even when judgments are falling all around them.

#### The Sixth Plague: Boils (vv8–12)
- **"Handfuls of soot from the kiln" (v8).** The word is *kivshan*, a kiln or furnace, very likely the brick kilns where Israel's slaves had sweated (1:14; 5:7–19). The ash of Israel's misery becomes Egypt's disease. JFB compares the gesture to Eastern sorcerers who threw ashes into the air to bring a curse on someone. Here the curse is real, and it comes from God.
- **This plague comes without warning.** It is the third plague of the second set, and like the gnats (8:16) it falls without notice.
- **"Boils breaking out in sores on man and beast" (v9).** The word for boils, *shechin*, is used for Job's sores (Job 2:7) and Hezekiah's illness (2 Kgs 20:7). Moses later warns Israel that if they disobey, God will strike them with "the boils of Egypt" (Deut 28:27). These are festering, blistering sores.
- **"The magicians could not stand before Moses" (v11).** For the first time the plague strikes Egypt's experts personally. They had copied two signs, failed at the third, and now they cannot even show up. Notice the wordplay: Moses and Aaron **"stood before Pharaoh"** (v10), while the magicians **"could not stand before Moses."**
- **"But the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh" (v12).** This is the first time the text says outright that the LORD himself did the hardening, though God had said he would (4:21; 7:3). The verb is *chazaq*, "made strong" or "made firm." Up to now the narrator has said Pharaoh hardened his own heart or that his heart "was hardened." The order matters.
- **How to read the hardening.** Readers have long weighed God's sovereignty and Pharaoh's responsibility here. Many see God confirming a choice Pharaoh had already made several times, handing him over to it, as Romans 1:24 describes. Others, especially in the Reformed tradition, stress that God was in control from the start (4:21) without making Pharaoh any less guilty. Both hold to what the text says: Pharaoh really chose, and God really ruled.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees poetic justice in the ashes: "the ashes of the furnace are made as much a terror to them as ever their task-masters had been to the Israelites." JFB agrees that the kiln "was one of the principal instruments of oppression," so the Egyptians "were made to read their sin in their punishment." On v12 Henry writes that "wilful hardness is commonly punished with judicial hardness," and Guzik makes the same point, counting several self-hardenings before this one. Guzik also ties the boils to the healing god Imhotep and says the Hebrew word comes from the idea of burning. The lexicon simply gives "boil" and does not confirm a burning root, so that is best held loosely.

#### The Seventh Plague: Hail (vv13–35)
- **"Rise up early in the morning" (v13).** A third set of plagues begins, and as at the first and fourth, Moses meets Pharaoh in the morning. This warning is the longest yet.
- **"This time I will send all my plagues on you yourself" (v14).** Literally "to your heart." The plagues are aimed at Pharaoh's will, not only his fields. The purpose is stated plainly: **"that you may know that there is none like me in all the earth."**
- **"By now I could have… struck you… and you would have been cut off" (v15).** The word for "pestilence" is *dever*, the same word as the livestock plague in v3. What fell on the cattle could have fallen on Pharaoh. He is alive only because God has held back.
- **"For this purpose I have raised you up" (v16).** The Hebrew means "I have caused you to stand" or "kept you standing," from *amad*, the same verb as the magicians who "could not stand" (v11). Pharaoh stays on his feet only so that God's power will be seen. **"So that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth."** It was: forty years later Rahab in Jericho had heard what the LORD did when Israel came out of Egypt (Josh 2:10), and the Philistines still feared it in Samuel's day (1 Sam 4:8).
- **Paul and v16.** Paul quotes this verse in Romans 9:17, following the Greek wording "I raised you up," to show that God is free to have mercy and free to harden. Paul's point there is God's freedom in salvation history, not a full account of every individual's fate, and readers in different traditions weigh it differently.
- **"You are still exalting yourself against my people" (v17).** The verb pictures someone heaping himself up like a dam or a ramp. Pharaoh sets himself as a barrier between God and his people.
- **"Very heavy hail" (v18).** Egypt gets very little rain, especially in the south, and real hailstorms are rare. A storm "such as never has been in Egypt" would be terrifying. "Heavy" is *kaved* again, the same word for Pharaoh's heavy heart. Pharaoh makes his heart heavy, and God sends heavy hail.
- **"Get your livestock… into safe shelter" (v19).** In the middle of judgment, God gives Egyptians a way of escape. Anyone who believes the warning can act on it.
- **"Whoever feared the word of the LORD among the servants of Pharaoh" (v20).** This is a turning point. Some of Pharaoh's own officials now believe God's word more than their king's, and they act on it. Others **"did not pay attention"** (literally "did not set his heart," v21). The plague divides Egypt itself. Some of these God-fearing Egyptians may be among the "mixed multitude" who leave with Israel (12:38).
- **"Moses stretched out his staff toward heaven" (v23).** **"Thunder and hail, and fire ran down to the earth."** "Thunder" is literally "voices" (*qolot*), the same word for the thunder at Sinai (19:16). The "fire" is lightning, **"flashing continually in the midst of the hail" (v24).** Fire and ice together in a land that hardly sees rain.
- **"Struck down every plant… and broke every tree" (v25).** People, animals, crops and trees in the open are all hit. Egypt honored Nut as goddess of the sky and Osiris as lord of crops and new growth, and neither could hold back this storm. Psalm 78:47–48 and 105:32–33 remember it.
- **"Only in the land of Goshen… was there no hail" (v26).** One storm, with a border around God's people.
- **"This time I have sinned; the LORD is in the right" (v27).** These are the most humble words Pharaoh has spoken. "In the right" is *tsaddiq*, righteous, and "in the wrong" is *rasha'*, guilty, the language of a courtroom. He admits the verdict. But **"there has been enough of God's thunder"** (v28) shows what he really wants: the storm to stop.
- **"So that you may know that the earth is the LORD's" (v29).** Moses agrees to pray, and again names the purpose. Psalm 24:1 uses the same words. **"I know that you do not yet fear the LORD God" (v30).** Moses has learned that words spoken under pressure are not the same as a changed heart.
- **The flax and barley (vv31–32).** A short note in parentheses tells us which crops were ruined. Flax (used for linen, Egypt's main fabric) and barley were nearly ripe, and wheat and emmer (an ancient wheat, the KJV's "rie") had not yet come up. In Egypt this points to about January or February. It also explains how the locusts in the next chapter still find something to eat (10:5, 15).
- **"Moses went out of the city… and stretched out his hands" (v33).** Spreading out the hands was a posture of prayer (1 Kgs 8:22). Moses prays outside, in the storm, and it stops.
- **"He sinned yet again and hardened his heart, he and his servants" (v34).** Pharaoh's confession in v27 is now called sin "yet again." The verb is *kaved* once more, and his officials join him. Verse 35 closes the account with *chazaq*: **"as the LORD had spoken through Moses."**
- **Commentators:** Henry calls v27 a "penitent acknowledgment" on the surface, "I have sinned in standing it out so long," but notes that such words can come from people with no real sorrow, and that afterward "he sinned yet more, and hardened his heart." Guzik makes the same distinction between regret over consequences and repentance over sin, and notes that the God-fearing officials show God's warnings were not wasted. JFB notes that hailstones in Egypt "are small and of little force," which made this storm more appalling, and dates the event by the crops: flax and barley ripen "about a month earlier than wheat," which places the plague around February. JFB has no comment on vv15–16. The Tyndale notes add that pride, faced with evidence like this, "was not merely a failure" but sin, and that Moses' words in v29 sum up the message of all the plagues: the whole earth belongs to the LORD.

#### Pointing to Christ
God tells Pharaoh he is alive only because God has held back, and in the middle of judgment he tells Egyptians how to shelter. That is the shape of the gospel. Judgment is real, but God gives warning and a way of escape, and "whoever feared the word of the LORD" found it. Paul draws on this chapter in Romans 9 to show that salvation rests on God's mercy, not human effort. The same mercy that kept Goshen dry under the storm is offered to all in Christ, who bore the storm of judgment himself (Rom 5:9). Pharaoh's "I have sinned" was regret without turning. The lost son's "I have sinned" (Luke 15:18) was the real thing, and it ended in the Father's arms.
