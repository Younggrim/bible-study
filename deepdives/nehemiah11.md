*Setting:* Judah, soon after the covenant renewal of 445 BC. Back in 7:4 Nehemiah had noticed the problem: "The city was wide and large, but the people within it were few, and no houses had been rebuilt." He took a census using the old register of returnees (7:5–73), and then the story turned to the reading of the Law, the confession and the covenant (chs 8–10). Now the account returns to the empty city. Chapter 11 records how Jerusalem was repopulated and lists the families who lived there, followed by the towns where the rest of Judah and Benjamin settled. Much of the Jerusalem list (vv3–19) parallels 1 Chronicles 9:2–17, which seems to draw on a similar record. As a list, this chapter gets a shorter treatment than a narrative chapter. Its purpose is to show the restored people in place, with Jerusalem at the center and the towns around it.

#### The Leaders in Jerusalem (vv1–24)
- **"The leaders of the people lived in Jerusalem" (v1).** The leaders went first. Living in the capital was a duty, not a privilege. Jerusalem was still half in ruins, surrounded by hostile neighbors and far from the fields most families depended on for food.
- **"The rest of the people cast lots to bring one out of ten" (v1).** A tenth of the population was chosen by lot. Lots were a common way in Israel to let God decide a matter: dividing the land (Josh 14:2), choosing a king (1 Sam 10:20–21) and, later, choosing an apostle (Acts 1:26). "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Prov 16:33). The one in ten recalls the tithe. A tenth of the harvest belonged to God, and now a tenth of the people would live in his city.
- **"Jerusalem the holy city" (v1).** The title appears again in v18. Jerusalem was holy because God had chosen it for his temple and his name (1 Kgs 11:36; Ps 132:13). Isaiah had promised that it would be called "the holy city" again after its ruin (Isa 48:2; 52:1). Matthew uses the same title (Matt 4:5; 27:53), and Revelation ends with "the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God" (Rev 21:2).
- **"The people blessed all the men who willingly offered to live in Jerusalem" (v2).** Some may have volunteered in addition to those chosen by lot, or some of those chosen may have accepted willingly. Either way, moving meant leaving land and family, giving up farm life and living in a target city. "Willingly offered" uses the same verb as the freewill offerings for the tabernacle (Exod 35:29) and the volunteers in Deborah's song (Judg 5:2, 9). Those who moved gave themselves.
- **"In the towns of Judah everyone lived on his property" (v3).** Most of the people lived on their family lands outside the city. The list names "Israel" (lay people), priests, Levites, temple servants and Solomon's servants, the same groups as the register in chapter 7.
- **"Of the sons of Judah… of the sons of Perez" (vv4–6).** Perez was a son of Judah through Tamar (Gen 38:29), and the ancestor of David (Ruth 4:18–22). The descendants of Perez in the city numbered 468 "valiant men," a phrase that suggests they could fight. The city needed defenders.
- **"The son of the Shilonite" (v5).** This probably means a descendant of Shelah, Judah's third son (Num 26:20), not a man from the town of Shiloh.
- **"These are the sons of Benjamin" (vv7–9).** Jerusalem sat on the border between Judah and Benjamin (Josh 15:8; 18:16, 28), so both tribes had a share in it. Benjamin supplied 928 men. "Joel the son of Zichri was their overseer; and Judah the son of Hassenuah was second over the city" (v9). The phrase may mean Judah was deputy over the city, or that he was over the city's "second quarter" (compare "the Second Quarter," 2 Kgs 22:14; Zeph 1:10).
- **"Of the priests" (vv10–14).** Three priestly groups are listed, 1,192 men in all. Seraiah is called "ruler of the house of God" (v11), a high officer of the temple, though not the high priest. His genealogy runs back through Zadok, the high priest of Solomon's day (1 Kgs 2:35). JFB describes him as an assistant to the high priest.
- **"Mighty men of valor… their overseer was Zabdiel the son of Haggedolim" (v14).** Even the priests were counted as strong men. "Haggedolim" means "the great ones." It may be a family name or a way of saying he was from one of the leading families.
- **"Of the Levites" (vv15–18).** Shabbethai and Jozabad "were over the outside work of the house of God" (v16). The Tyndale notes suggest this meant collecting the tithes and running the storerooms (10:37–39). Mattaniah was "the leader of the praise, who gave thanks" (v17). He was the choir director who led the thanksgiving at the daily sacrifices. He descended from Asaph, and Abda from Jeduthun. Both were among the three musicians David set over temple worship (1 Chr 25:1–6). Twelve psalms are headed "of Asaph" (Ps 50; 73–83), and three mention Jeduthun (Ps 39; 62; 77). The singers who came back were heirs of David's music.
- **"All the Levites in the holy city were 284" (v18).** That is a small number compared with the priests, as in the first return (7:43). Levites remained scarce.
- **"The gatekeepers, Akkub, Talmon and their brothers… 172" (v19).** These families had guarded the temple gates since David's day (1 Chr 9:17). Counting the men in vv4–19, about 3,000 heads of households lived in the city, perhaps 10,000 to 15,000 people with their families.
- **"The temple servants lived on Ophel" (v21).** Ophel was the hill just south of the temple, close to their work (3:26).
- **"Uzzi… of the sons of Asaph, the singers, over the work of the house of God" (v22).** The overseer of the Levites came from the family of singers. Worship and work were led by the same people.
- **"For there was a command from the king concerning them" (v23).** This probably refers to the Persian king, who supported the temple and freed its staff from taxes (Ezra 7:24). Some think "the king" means David, who organized the singers (12:24 mentions "the command of David"). A "fixed provision… as every day required" ensured the singers were paid, so they could serve daily rather than farm.
- **"Pethahiah… was at the king's side in all matters concerning the people" (v24).** Pethahiah seems to have been a Jewish official who represented the people's affairs to the Persian king, perhaps at court, perhaps in the province's administration. He was from the line of Zerah, Perez's twin brother (Gen 38:30).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that before this, Jerusalem "was walled round, but it was not as yet fully inhabited, and therefore was weak and despicable." On the lot he writes, "they plainly saw God appointing the bounds of their habitation." He links the tenth to the tithe: "what is given to the holy city he reckons given to himself." On the volunteers he says, "God and man will bless those that are public blessings." He also adds a pointed line: "Those who care not for being holy themselves are shy of dwelling in a holy city." JFB explains the differences from 1 Chronicles 9 as the result of changed names and later movements of the people. He says Mattaniah led the choir at the morning and evening sacrifice and suggests that Ezra or Nehemiah prompted the king's command for the singers. Guzik admires the pioneer courage of those who moved into a rough, exposed city, giving up land and family ties. He applies to them Zechariah's question, "who has despised the day of small things?" (Zech 4:10).

#### Villages Outside Jerusalem (vv25–36)
- **"As for the villages, with their fields" (v25).** The rest of the people lived in towns spread across the old territories of Judah and Benjamin. JFB notes that the whole area "had been completely devastated by the Chaldean invasion," so these towns had to be rebuilt before people could settle.
- **"Kiriath-arba and its villages" (v25).** This is the old name for Hebron (Gen 23:2; Josh 14:15), where Abraham, Isaac and Jacob were buried. Using the ancient name links the returnees to the patriarchs and to the conquest under Joshua.
- **The towns of Judah (vv25–30).** Many of these names come from Joshua's list of Judah's towns (Josh 15). Beersheba marked Israel's old southern border ("from Dan to Beersheba," Judg 20:1). Ziklag was the town the Philistines gave David (1 Sam 27:6). Adullam was where David hid in a cave (1 Sam 22:1). Lachish and Azekah were among the last fortified cities to hold out against Babylon (Jer 34:7). Soldiers' letters found at Lachish in 1935 mention watching for signals from Azekah during that siege.
- **Inside or outside the province?** Historians note that some of these southern towns, including Hebron and Beersheba, seem to have been outside the borders of the Persian province of Judah in this period, in territory controlled by Edomites. So the list may record places where Jewish communities lived scattered among other peoples, or it may describe the old tribal claim based on Joshua. The text does not say which.
- **"So they encamped from Beersheba to the Valley of Hinnom" (v30).** The Valley of Hinnom ran along the south and west side of Jerusalem. The phrase describes the full spread of Judah's settlements, from the far south up to the city. "Encamped" is an unusual word for settling in towns. It is the word used of Israel camping in the wilderness (Num 33), and it may hint that their hold on the land was still unsettled.
- **"The people of Benjamin also lived from Geba onward" (vv31–35).** The Benjamin towns lay north of Jerusalem. Many have long histories: Michmash, where Jonathan attacked the Philistines (1 Sam 14); Bethel, where Jacob dreamed (Gen 28:19); Anathoth, Jeremiah's home (Jer 1:1); Nob, the city of priests destroyed by Saul (1 Sam 22:19); Ramah, Samuel's home (1 Sam 7:17); Lod, later called Lydda, where Peter healed Aeneas (Acts 9:32–35); and Ono, where Nehemiah's enemies tried to lure him (6:2). "The valley of craftsmen" (v35) may have been a place of skilled workers (compare 1 Chr 4:14).
- **"Certain divisions of the Levites in Judah were assigned to Benjamin" (v36).** Some Levite groups were moved to live in Benjamin's towns, so that both tribes had Levites among them to teach and serve. JFB says these "divisions" were Levite settlements outside Jerusalem.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines this section as the other cities and villages of Judah and Benjamin that the rest of the people settled. He sees all of it as God setting each family in its place. JFB has little to say beyond the point that the towns had to be rebuilt after the Babylonian devastation. Guzik passes over the list briefly, noting only that the remaining people spread through the towns of Judah and Benjamin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jerusalem was "the holy city," and yet few wanted to live there. It was dangerous, poor and unfinished, and it needed people willing to give up comfort to fill it. The people blessed those who went. The New Testament points to another city, one that "has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:10), "the holy city, new Jerusalem" (Rev 21:2). No lot is needed to fill it. Christ himself prepares a place there for his people (John 14:2–3), and he went "outside the gate" of the earthly Jerusalem to suffer so that they could belong to "the city that is to come" (Heb 13:12–14).
