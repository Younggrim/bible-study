*Setting:* Around 1050 BC. In chapter 8 the elders of Israel demanded "a king to judge us like all the nations," and the LORD told Samuel, "Obey their voice and make them a king" (8:22). Samuel sent everyone home. Chapter 9 shows how God picked the man. There is no election and no campaign. A young farmer goes looking for his father's lost donkeys and ends up at a dinner where the seat of honor has been saved for him. The story is told with warmth and gentle humor, and it is full of providence: God steering ordinary events toward his purpose. It also drops quiet hints about what kind of king Saul will be. The private anointing follows at the start of chapter 10.

#### Saul Chosen to Be King (vv1–27)
- **"A man of Benjamin whose name was Kish… a man of wealth" (v1).** The family line runs back five generations, a sign of a respected family. "Man of wealth" (*gibbor chayil*) can mean a mighty warrior or a man of substance; here it is probably wealth and standing. Benjamin was the small tribe whose territory lay between Ephraim and Judah. It had been nearly wiped out in the civil war at the end of Judges (Judg 20–21), all because of an atrocity at Gibeah, which is Saul's hometown (10:26). Israel's first king comes from the most compromised town in the book of Judges.
- **"Saul, a handsome young man… From his shoulders upward he was taller than any of the people" (v2).** "Saul" (*Sha'ul*) means "asked." The people "asked" for a king (8:10), and they get a man named "Asked." "Handsome" is literally "good," and "young man" can mean a man in his prime. He looked every inch a king. He was exactly what people would choose. When God later chooses David, he tells Samuel, "Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature… the LORD looks on the heart" (16:7). The text says nothing here about Saul's heart toward God.
- **"The donkeys of Kish… were lost" (v3).** Donkeys were valuable animals, used for riding and carrying. A wealthy family would care about losing several. Kish sends Saul with a servant to find them.
- **The long search (vv4–5).** They wander through the hill country of Ephraim and regions called Shalishah and Shaalim, which cannot now be located, then back through Benjamin. The repeated "but they did not find them… they were not there" builds frustration. The future king cannot find his father's donkeys. In the ancient Near East kings were called shepherds of their people, and this one cannot keep track of a few animals. David, by contrast, will be found tending his father's sheep (16:11).
- **"Come, let us go back, lest my father… become anxious about us" (v5).** Saul is considerate. He thinks of his father. But it is the servant, not Saul, who knows there is a prophet nearby.
- **"There is a man of God in this city… all that he says comes true" (v6).** The land of Zuph was Samuel's home district (1:1), and "this city" is probably Ramah, though it is not named. Samuel is the most famous man in Israel, the judge who has ridden a circuit for years (7:16), yet Saul seems not to know him. Samuel's reputation matches the test of a true prophet in Deuteronomy 18:22: "all that he says comes true" (compare 3:19).
- **"What can we bring the man?" (vv7–8).** It was customary to bring a gift when consulting a prophet (1 Kgs 14:3; 2 Kgs 8:8–9). It was a mark of respect, not a fee. "A quarter of a shekel of silver" was a small weight of silver, roughly a tenth of an ounce. Coins did not exist yet, and silver was weighed. Again it is the servant who has the answer.
- **"Today's 'prophet' was formerly called a seer" (v9).** This is an explanatory note added for later readers. "Seer" (*ro'eh*) means "one who sees," someone given visions. "Prophet" (*navi'*) is someone called to speak for God. By the time the book was written, "prophet" had become the usual word, so the editor explains the older term the characters use in vv11, 18 and 19.
- **"Young women coming out to draw water" (v11).** Towns were built on hills for defense, and the spring was usually at the bottom. Meetings at wells are a familiar scene in Genesis and Exodus, where they often lead to a marriage (Gen 24; 29; Exod 2). Here the meeting leads to a kingdom.
- **"The people have a sacrifice today on the high place" (v12).** A "high place" (*bamah*) was an open-air worship site on a hilltop. With the tabernacle at Shiloh gone and the ark in a private house, worship of the LORD happened at local altars like this. Later, after the temple was built, high places were condemned because they became centers of idolatry (1 Kgs 3:2; 2 Kgs 23). The girls' chatter, "He is; behold, he is just ahead of you. Hurry," has a lively, breathless feel. Everything is falling into place to the minute.
- **"He must bless the sacrifice; afterward those who are invited will eat" (v13).** A peace offering was shared: part burned, part given to the priest, and the rest eaten by the worshipers in a feast before God.
- **"The day before Saul came, the LORD had revealed to Samuel" (v15).** Literally, God "uncovered Samuel's ear," a Hebrew idiom for telling someone a secret, as if whispering into an ear with the hair pushed back. The narrator pulls back the curtain. What looked like chance (lost donkeys, a servant's idea, a timely meeting) had been planned a day ahead.
- **"You shall anoint him to be prince over my people Israel" (v16).** God does not say "king" (*melek*). He says *nagid*, a "leader" or "designated ruler," literally "one in front." It is the word used later for David (13:14; 2 Sam 7:8). It stresses that the ruler stands in front of God's people as God's appointee, under God. "My people" appears twice in this verse. Israel still belongs to God.
- **"He shall save my people from the hand of the Philistines. For I have seen my people, because their cry has come to me" (v16).** The words echo God's call of Moses: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people… and have heard their cry" (Exod 3:7–9). The people asked for a king with mixed motives, but God answers their real need. The Philistines are pressing again, and he is sending a deliverer. Here is grace in the middle of judgment.
- **"He it is who shall restrain my people" (v17).** "Restrain" (*'atsar*) usually means to hold back or shut up. The lexicon notes that here it means "rule over." It may carry a hint of the "ways of the king" (8:11–18): this king will rule by holding the people in check.
- **"Tell me where is the house of the seer?" (v18).** Saul asks Samuel himself, not recognizing him. "I am the seer," Samuel replies.
- **"As for your donkeys… they have been found" (v20).** Samuel answers a question Saul has not asked, which proves he is a true seer. Then he turns from donkeys to the kingdom: "for whom is all that is desirable in Israel? Is it not for you and for all your father's house?" "All that is desirable" (*chemdah*) means the most precious things, the treasures of the nation. Samuel is hinting at the crown.
- **"Am I not a Benjaminite, from the least of the tribes of Israel?" (v21).** Saul's answer sounds like genuine humility, like Gideon's "my clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house" (Judg 6:15). His family was in fact wealthy (v1), so some read it as conventional politeness. But Benjamin really was the smallest tribe after Judges 20–21. And later Samuel reminds him, "Though you are little in your own eyes, are you not the head of the tribes of Israel?" (15:17). His sense of smallness will turn into insecurity rather than dependence on God.
- **"Gave them a place at the head of those who had been invited, who were about thirty persons" (v22).** The leading men of the town are at the feast, and the stranger in from the country is seated at the head. The servant is seated too, a kind touch.
- **"The cook took up the leg and what was on it and set them before Saul" (v24).** The "leg" (*shoq*) is the thigh. In the law the right thigh of a peace offering was the priest's portion (Lev 7:32–34). Samuel, acting as priest, gives his own portion, or a choice cut set aside like it, to Saul. "What was kept is set before you… it was kept for you until the hour appointed." Samuel had planned this before Saul arrived. The honored portion was a public sign that this young man was special, even if no one yet knew why.
- **"A bed was spread for Saul on the roof" (v25).** Houses had flat roofs reached by an outside stair, used for drying crops, sleeping on warm nights, and private talk (Josh 2:6; Acts 10:9). The Greek translation reads "they spread a bed for Saul on the roof, and he lay down," which the ESV follows. The Hebrew says Samuel "spoke with Saul on the roof." Either way, Samuel had time alone with him.
- **"Stop here yourself for a while, that I may make known to you the word of God" (v27).** At dawn, at the edge of town, Samuel sends the servant ahead. What follows (10:1) is private. The anointing of Israel's first king takes place in a quiet street with no witnesses.
- **Commentators:** Henry delights in the providence of the lost donkeys. Saul went out to find them "for he met with the kingdom, which he never dreamed of," and "Samuel knew him before he knew Samuel." He says the gift for the seer was "a token of their respect and the value they put upon his office." He reads the reserved portion as "an implicit resignation of his place to him." JFB takes "man of wealth" as "great wealth and substance," says money was a fitting gift "to a man of rank," and treats Saul's words in v21 as "affected humility," since his family was prominent in Benjamin. Guzik also judges v21 to be more modesty than accuracy. He notes that the chapter says nothing about Saul's walk with God, and that outward image is no substitute for a heart for God. He also warns against two mistakes in reading providence: seeing a hidden message in every event, and ignoring God's guidance through ordinary circumstances. The Tyndale notes add that Samuel, like Moses, combined the roles of prophet and priest.

#### Pointing to Christ
God guided a young man through lost animals, a servant's suggestion and a few girls at a well to a seat of honor kept for him "until the hour appointed." Saul did not know Samuel, but Samuel knew him. The same God works through ordinary, frustrating days in his people's lives. But Saul himself points to Christ mostly by contrast. He was the people's choice, handsome and tall, the image of a king. Isaiah says of the true King, "he had no form or majesty that we should look at him, and no beauty that we should desire him" (Isa 53:2). God saw his people's affliction and heard their cry, and sent Saul to save them from the Philistines (v16). In the fullness of time he heard a greater cry and sent his Son, exalted as "Leader and Savior" (Acts 5:31), to save his people from their sins (Matt 1:21).
