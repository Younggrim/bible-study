*Setting:* Haggai is the first of three prophets who spoke after the exile (with Zechariah and Malachi), and his book is one of the shortest in the Old Testament: four messages over less than four months, each dated to the day. The year is 520 BC, the second year of the Persian king Darius I. About eighteen years earlier, in 538 BC, Cyrus had allowed the Jews to return from Babylon and rebuild the temple (Ezra 1). The returnees rebuilt the altar and laid a foundation (Ezra 3), but opposition from their neighbors and their own discouragement brought the work to a stop (Ezra 4:4–5, 24). Since then, the temple site had sat as a weed-grown foundation among the ruins Nebuchadnezzar left in 586 BC. Ezra 5:1–2 tells, from the outside, how Haggai and Zechariah got the work going again. Haggai 1 gives the inside story: the first sermon, and how the people responded. Nothing is known of Haggai beyond this book and the two mentions in Ezra (5:1; 6:14). Some think he was an old man who had seen the first temple (2:3), but that is only a guess. His name is related to the Hebrew word for "feast" (*chag*), perhaps because he was born on a festival day.

#### The Command to Rebuild the Temple (vv1–11)
- **"In the second year of Darius… in the sixth month, on the first day" (v1).** That is August 29, 520 BC. The sixth month (Elul) falls in late summer, at harvest time for grapes, figs and pomegranates. The first day of each month was the new moon, a holy day when people gathered for worship (Num 28:11; Isa 66:23), so Haggai had an audience. Dating by a Persian king's reign is itself telling. Israel had no king of its own. The book that ends with a promise about David's heir (2:23) begins with the reminder that a foreign king was on the throne.
- **"By the hand of Haggai the prophet" (v1).** The word came "by the hand" of the prophet, a phrase that stresses he was the instrument and God was the speaker. "Thus says the LORD" and "declares the LORD" come again and again in these two short chapters. Haggai keeps his own voice out of the way.
- **"To Zerubbabel… governor of Judah, and to Joshua… the high priest" (v1).** The message goes first to the two leaders responsible for the work. Zerubbabel was of David's royal line, the grandson of King Jehoiachin (1 Chr 3:17–19), and now served Persia as governor (Hebrew *pechah*, a Persian-era title for a provincial official). Joshua (called Jeshua in Ezra) was the high priest. The civil and religious leaders together had let the work lapse, and together they would need to lead it again.
- **"Thus says the LORD of hosts" (v2).** "LORD of hosts" (*YHWH tsebaoth*) means the LORD who commands the armies of heaven. Haggai uses the title fourteen times in 38 verses. For a weak little province under a vast empire, it was a reminder of who really held power.
- **"These people say the time has not yet come" (v2).** Notice "these people," not "my people." God speaks of them with a distance that is itself a rebuke. Their excuse sounded pious: a matter of timing. Hard times, failed harvests, hostile neighbors and a Persian ban that had never been lifted (Ezra 4:21–24) made "not yet" seem reasonable. Some may also have reckoned that Jeremiah's seventy years (Jer 25:11; 29:10) had not yet run out, if counted from the temple's destruction in 586 BC. Whatever the reason, they were not refusing to build. They were postponing.
- **"Is it a time for you yourselves to dwell in your paneled houses?" (v4).** God answers their "time" with his own "time." The word "paneled" (*sephunim*) means covered, either with wood paneling on the walls or with a finished roof. Cedar paneling was a mark of wealth, used in Solomon's temple and palace (1 Kgs 6:9; 7:3) and condemned in King Jehoiakim's luxurious house (Jer 22:14). The point is not that everyone was rich, but that they had found the resources and energy to finish their own houses while the house of God stood roofless. **"This house lies in ruins."** The word is *chareb*, "desolate" or "waste."
- **"Consider your ways" (v5).** Literally "set your heart upon your ways." In Hebrew the heart is the center of thought and will, not just feeling. Haggai repeats the call five times in the book (1:5, 7; 2:15, 18 twice). He does not first tell them what to do. He tells them to look honestly at what has been happening to them.
- **"You have sown much, and harvested little" (v6).** Five short clauses describe a life of effort without satisfaction: sowing without harvest, eating without being filled, drinking without quenching thirst, wearing clothes without warmth, earning wages that vanish. **"A bag with holes"** is the most vivid. Coins were carried in a pouch, and these people's money seemed to leak away as fast as they earned it. These were the very curses the Law warned would follow disobedience (Deut 28:38–40; Lev 26:20). Haggai is asking them to read their circumstances in the light of the covenant.
- **"Go up to the hills and bring wood and build the house" (v8).** The command is simple and practical: go and get timber. The hills around Jerusalem would have grown back with woodland during the decades of the exile. The cedar from Lebanon purchased years before (Ezra 3:7) may have been used elsewhere, or a different kind of wood was needed now. Stone was already lying on the site.
- **"That I may take pleasure in it and that I may be glorified" (v8).** God's goal is not a grand building but his own pleasure and glory. "Be glorified" can also be read "appear in my glory," and the Hebrew is close to the word for the glory that filled the tabernacle and Solomon's temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kgs 8:11). God promises to meet with them in the house they build.
- **"When you brought it home, I blew it away" (v9).** The little harvest they did bring in, God scattered with a breath, as wind carries off chaff. **"Because of my house that lies in ruins, while each of you busies himself with his own house."** The Hebrew is literally "each of you runs to his own house." The contrast is vivid: God's house lies still, while every man hurries about his own business.
- **"The heavens above you have withheld the dew" (v10).** In the dry summer of Judah, dew was the only moisture many crops received for months. Its loss was devastating. In the Law, dew is a sign of blessing (Gen 27:28; Deut 33:13).
- **"I have called for a drought" (v11).** Here is Haggai's wordplay. "Drought" is *choreb*, from the same root as *chareb*, the "ruins" of the temple (vv4, 9). Because God's house was a ruin, the land would be a ruin. The drought touched every part of life: "grain, the new wine, the oil" (the three staples of the land, Deut 7:13), "man and beast, and… all their labors." It was not a natural disaster to be shrugged off. God says, "I have called" for it.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the "not yet" excuse a common habit of putting off good work as "all in good time," and says it is bad to neglect duty but worse to blame Providence for our neglect. On "I did blow upon it" he notes that what God does not bless does not last. JFB explains "consider your ways" as literally "Set your heart," calls the holed bag "proverbial for labor and money spent profitlessly," and links the "wasting drought" to the "waste" house. JFB thinks the excuse may have come from a dispute over when Jeremiah's seventy years began. Guzik makes the same suggestion and adds that the problem was not that they had houses but that their priorities were out of order. He cites an older writer who observed that Solomon built God's house before his own.

#### The People Obey the Lord (vv12–15)
- **"Then Zerubbabel… and Joshua… with all the remnant of the people, obeyed" (v12).** Few prophets in Israel's history saw a response like this. Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel preached for decades to people who would not listen. Haggai preached once, and the leaders and people obeyed. They are called "the remnant," a word the prophets used for the faithful survivors through whom God would continue his purposes (Isa 10:20–22; Zeph 3:12–13).
- **"Obeyed the voice of the LORD their God, and the words of Haggai the prophet" (v12).** The two are joined. The people recognized that Haggai's words were God's words. They also obeyed "as the LORD their God had sent him," so they accepted his authority as God's messenger.
- **"And the people feared the LORD" (v12).** The fear of the LORD in the Old Testament is not panic but reverent awe and submission. It is "the beginning of wisdom" (Prov 9:10). Hearing that their hardships came from God's hand made them take God seriously again.
- **"Haggai, the messenger of the LORD" (v13).** "Messenger" is *mal'ak*, the same word translated "angel" in "the angel of the LORD." It is used of prophets (2 Chr 36:15–16) and priests (Mal 2:7), and of the coming forerunner, "my messenger" (Mal 3:1). It is a rare title for a prophet.
- **"I am with you, declares the LORD" (v13).** Haggai's second message is four Hebrew words long, one of the shortest prophetic messages in the Bible. After rebuke, God gives a promise. This is the promise God made to Isaac (Gen 26:3), Jacob (Gen 28:15), Moses (Exod 3:12), Joshua (Josh 1:5) and Jeremiah (Jer 1:8). It covered every fear the people had: enemies, poverty, a Persian ban. If God was with them, they could build. Haggai will repeat it in 2:4.
- **"The LORD stirred up the spirit of Zerubbabel… Joshua… and… all the remnant" (v14).** The same verb (*ur*, "stir up, rouse") was used when God "stirred up the spirit of Cyrus" to issue his decree and stirred up the spirits of the first returnees (Ezra 1:1, 5). The writer makes the point carefully: the people obeyed (v12), and God stirred them (v14). Both are true. Human obedience and God's inner work are not rivals. "It is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure" (Phil 2:13).
- **"On the twenty-fourth day of the month, in the sixth month" (v15).** Twenty-three days passed between the sermon (v1) and the start of the work. That gap was probably spent clearing the rubble, gathering timber, organizing workers and finishing the late-summer harvest of grapes and figs. Most scholars connect the first part of v15 to this chapter and the second half ("in the second year of Darius the king") to the start of the message in 2:1. Either way, the obedience was not a passing feeling. It turned into work within weeks.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the sermon's "good success," with the people returning to the work and the prophet assuring them that God was with them. JFB notes that "messenger" is also used of priests (Mal 2:7), and that the assurance "I am with you" came after their obedience. He reads v14 as God giving them "alacrity and perseverance in the good work." JFB also thinks the twenty-three days were spent gathering materials before the actual building. Guzik stresses that the leaders obeyed first, and that God's stirring of their spirits led to action, not just inner feeling.

#### Pointing to Christ
Haggai's whole message turns on God's house: the place where God dwells among his people. The people neglected it because they were absorbed in their own houses, and God called them to put his presence first. Jesus took up the same priority when he told his followers, "Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (Matt 6:33), words that could stand over this chapter. He also showed what the temple was always pointing to. He is the place where God dwells with his people (John 1:14), and he is building a new house made of living stones, his church (1 Pet 2:4–5; Eph 2:21–22). The four-word promise "I am with you" finds its fullest form in his name, Immanuel, "God with us" (Matt 1:23), and in his last promise to his disciples as he sent them out to build: "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20).
