*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. This is the last chapter of 1 Chronicles and the last scene of David's life. The assembly of chapter 28 is still gathered. David has handed Solomon the throne and the temple plans. Now he gives his own fortune to the work, calls the leaders to give, and the people respond with a flood of gifts and joy. David answers with one of the great prayers of the Bible, and the chapter ends with Solomon on the throne and David's death. For readers who had given what little they had to rebuild a smaller temple (Ezra 2:68–69), this was a picture of how God's house is built: by a willing people who know that everything they give was his already.

#### Offerings for Building the Temple (vv1–9)
- **"Solomon my son, whom alone God has chosen, is young and inexperienced" (v1).** Solomon was perhaps around twenty. David is not insulting him. He is explaining why he has prepared so much and why everyone must help (22:5). Solomon himself later prays, "I am but a little child. I do not know how to go out or come in" (1 Kings 3:7).
- **"The palace will not be for man but for the LORD God" (v1).** "Palace" here is *birah*, a late word for a fortress or citadel. It is the word used for the Persian citadel of Susa (Neh 1:1; Esth 1:2) and for the fortress by the second temple (Neh 2:8). The Chronicler uses a word from his own day to say the temple would be the great King's royal residence.
- **"So far as I was able" (v2).** David lists everything he had gathered: gold, silver, bronze, iron, wood, onyx, stones for setting, antimony (a dark mineral, possibly for decorative inlay), colored stones and marble. The list echoes the materials given for the tabernacle (Exod 25:3–7; 35:5–9).
- **"A treasure of my own… because of my devotion to the house of my God" (v3).** Beyond the national stores, David gives his personal fortune. "Devotion" is literally "because I take pleasure in" the house of my God. The gift comes from love. Twice in one verse he says "the house of *my* God."
- **"3,000 talents of gold, of the gold of Ophir, and 7,000 talents of refined silver" (v4).** A talent was roughly 75 pounds (about 34 kg), so this is on the order of 100 tons of gold and 230 tons of silver. **Ophir** was famous for the finest gold (Job 28:16; Isa 13:12), though no one knows for sure where it was: southern Arabia, East Africa and India have all been proposed. The figures in Chronicles are enormous, and scholars debate whether they are exact, rounded, or partly affected by copying. The point is unmistakable: David gave more than anyone.
- **"Who then will offer willingly, consecrating himself today to the LORD?" (v5).** "Consecrating himself" is literally "to fill his hand." That is the idiom used for ordaining priests (Exod 28:41; 29:9), when their hands were filled with their first offerings. David invites every leader to act like a priest for a day: to come with full hands and give himself along with his gift.
- **"Freewill offerings" (vv6–7).** Leaders of families and tribes, commanders and officials all give. Their gifts are listed in talents, and also **"10,000 darics of gold."** A daric was a Persian gold coin, first minted under Darius I (522–486 BC), five centuries after David. The Chronicler is converting the value into a unit his readers knew, the way a modern translation might give dollars. It is a small sign of when and for whom the book was written.
- **"In the care of Jehiel the Gershonite" (v8).** Precious stones went to the temple treasury kept by Jehiel's family of Levites (26:21–22). Gifts were not handed over carelessly but put in trusted, accountable hands.
- **"The people rejoiced because they had given willingly, for with a whole heart they had offered freely" (v9).** The joy came from the giving itself. "Willingly" and "freely" are the same Hebrew root (*nadab*), used for freewill offerings, and it runs through the chapter (vv5, 6, 9, 14, 17). "Whole heart" is *lev shalem*, the undivided heart David asked of Solomon (28:9). This is the scene of the tabernacle repeated, when Moses had to tell the people to stop bringing because they had given too much (Exod 36:5–7). Paul has the same spirit in mind: "God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Cor 9:7).
- **Commentators:** All three see David's example as the key. Henry says David "had set his affection on the house of his God," and that his gift came from love rather than show. Guzik links v3 to Jesus' saying that where your treasure is, there your heart will be (Matt 6:21), and he notes, with another writer, that "consecrate" is literally "fill his hand." JFB agrees, calling it a freewill gift in which the giver acts as a kind of priest, and reads the "drams" of the KJV as "darics, a Persian coin," familiar to the Jews from the captivity on. JFB calls the gold of Ophir "then esteemed the purest and finest in the world."

#### David Prays in the Assembly (vv10–22a)
- **"Blessed are you, O LORD, the God of Israel our father" (v10).** "Israel our father" here means Jacob, the ancestor of the nation, whose name was changed to Israel (Gen 32:28). The prayer has three parts: praise (vv10–13), the offering of the gifts (vv14–17), and requests (vv18–19).
- **"Yours, O LORD, is the greatness and the power and the glory and the victory and the majesty" (v11).** Five words pile up, each claiming that every kind of greatness belongs to God, "for all that is in the heavens and in the earth is yours." **"Yours is the kingdom"** reminds a crowd gathered to crown a king that the kingdom is God's first. Many later manuscripts of the Lord's Prayer end with "For yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory, forever. Amen" (Matt 6:13, footnoted in the ESV). That ending is not in the earliest manuscripts, but it was very likely shaped by this verse, and the church has prayed it for centuries.
- **"Both riches and honor come from you" (v12).** David had both, and he knew where they came from. God alone can "make great and… give strength." This guards the king and his people against pride (Deut 8:17–18).
- **"But who am I, and what is my people, that we should be able thus to offer willingly?" (v14).** David asked the same question when God first promised him a dynasty (17:16). Even the ability and the desire to give are gifts.
- **"For all things come from you, and of your own have we given you" (v14).** This is the heart of the chapter, and many churches still say it when the offering is brought forward. Nothing given to God is new to him. Giving is returning what was always his. That removes both pride (we gave so much) and fear (we cannot afford to give).
- **"We are strangers before you and sojourners, as all our fathers were" (v15).** A "sojourner" (*toshav*) was a resident foreigner living on someone else's land. Abraham called himself "a sojourner and foreigner" when he bought a grave (Gen 23:4), and the law said Israel lived in God's land as "strangers and sojourners" with him (Lev 25:23). The richest king in Israel's history admits he owns nothing permanently. Hebrews 11:13 and 1 Peter 2:11 take up the same language for Christians.
- **"Our days on the earth are like a shadow, and there is no abiding" (v15).** A shadow lengthens, fades and disappears (Ps 39:12; 144:4; Job 8:9). "No abiding" can also be translated "no hope" of staying. David speaks as a man close to death.
- **"You test the heart and have pleasure in uprightness" (v17).** God sees motives. David can say his own gift was given "in the uprightness of my heart," and he has watched the people give "freely and joyously." Giving that is forced or for show is not what God delights in.
- **"O LORD, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (v18).** This is the name God gave himself at the burning bush (Exod 3:15), the God of the covenant promises. David asks God to **"keep forever such purposes and thoughts in the hearts of your people, and direct their hearts toward you."** He knows enthusiasm fades. Only God can keep a heart willing.
- **"Grant to Solomon my son a whole heart" (v19).** The Tyndale notes point out what David did *not* ask: not power, victory, wealth or long life for Solomon, but a heart wholly set on God's commands. In 28:9 he told Solomon to have one. Here he asks God to give it. Both are true: a whole heart is commanded, and it is a gift.
- **"Bless the LORD your God" (v20).** The assembly bows and pays homage "to the LORD and to the king." The same verb (*shachah*, bow down) is used for both, but in different senses: worship to God, honor to God's anointed king.
- **The sacrifices and the feast (vv21–22a).** A thousand bulls, rams and lambs were offered as burnt offerings, wholly given to God, and many more "sacrifices" (peace offerings) were shared in a meal. **"They ate and drank before the LORD on that day with great gladness."** Worship ended in a family feast in God's presence (Deut 12:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "All that is in the heaven, and in the earth, is thine," and the people's gift "cometh of thy hand, and is all thy own." He notes that aged, dying saints should have hearts enlarged in praise, and that the feast showed generous giving had not left them poorer. Guzik says that both the ability and the will to give are God's gifts, and that David's praise turns into a prayer that the people's willing spirit would last. Guzik says v10 is the first place in Scripture where God is called Father of his people, but the Hebrew reads "the God of Israel our father," with "our father" describing Israel (Jacob), so the verse does not quite bear that weight. JFB treats vv10–19 together and adds no notes on individual verses there.

#### Solomon Anointed King (vv22b–25)
- **"They made Solomon the son of David king the second time" (v22).** The first time was the hurried anointing at the Gihon spring when Adonijah tried to take the throne (1 Kings 1:32–40). This second, public ceremony confirmed it before the whole nation. Chronicles does not retell the Adonijah crisis, but this phrase quietly assumes it.
- **"Anointed him as prince for the LORD, and Zadok as priest" (v22).** King and priest are anointed side by side. Abiathar had backed Adonijah and was removed (1 Kings 2:26–27, 35), so Zadok's line became the high priests. Ezekiel's vision of the restored temple gives the priesthood to "the sons of Zadok" (Ezek 44:15).
- **"Solomon sat on the throne of the LORD as king in place of David his father" (v23).** The king of Israel sat on God's throne as his deputy (28:5). The Chronicler repeats this to remind readers that the true King of Israel was the LORD, and that his promise to David's line still stood when no king sat in Jerusalem.
- **"All the sons of King David, pledged their allegiance" (v24).** The Hebrew is literally "gave a hand under" Solomon, a gesture of submission. This includes his older brothers, the rivals of 1 Kings 1. Kings does not show that kind of unity at the start (1 Kings 2:13–25). Chronicles chooses to show the settled outcome.
- **"Such royal majesty as had not been on any king before him in Israel" (v25).** There had been only two kings before him, Saul and David, but the point stands. God kept his promise to make David's son great (17:11–14; 2 Chr 1:12).
- **Commentators:** Henry and JFB both explain the second anointing as a confirmation after the first was done in haste. JFB: "done precipitately on Adonijah's conspiracy." Henry notes they "anointed him to the Lord," which he takes to mean that rulers are set apart to serve God. Guzik says Solomon's greatness rested on what David had built, prayed for and prepared.

#### The Death of David (vv26–30)
- **"Forty years… seven years in Hebron and thirty-three years in Jerusalem" (v27).** David reigned over Judah alone in Hebron and then over all Israel from Jerusalem (2 Sam 5:4–5), roughly 1010–970 BC.
- **"He died at a good age, full of days, riches, and honor" (v28).** "Full of days" is said of Abraham and Isaac (Gen 25:8; 35:29). David is buried with the patriarchs' kind of blessing. Chronicles leaves out David's last orders about Joab and Shimei (1 Kings 2:1–9) and ends on God's faithfulness to him.
- **"The Chronicles of Samuel the seer… Nathan the prophet… Gad the seer" (v29).** The Chronicler names his sources, three prophets who knew David: Samuel, who anointed him (1 Sam 16), Nathan, who brought both the promise and the rebuke (ch 17; 2 Sam 12), and Gad, who spoke for God after the census (21:9). David's story was written by prophets, so it was told from God's point of view. These records are probably in large part what we know as 1 and 2 Samuel, along with other sources now lost.
- **"All the kingdoms of the countries" (v30).** The book ends with David's reign set against the wider world. Israel's king mattered beyond Israel, which anticipates the son of David whose rule will reach the nations (Ps 2:8; 72:8–11).
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls David Israel's model king and a pointer to Jesus, the Son of David. He does not hide David's great sin with Bathsheba and Uriah, but stresses his repentance and God's mercy. He passes on Adam Clarke's summary of David's life: "By birth, a peasant; by merit, a prince; in youth, a hero; in manhood, a monarch; and in age, a saint." JFB has no notes on vv27–30.

#### Pointing to Christ
David's last prayer gives God everything: the greatness, the power, the glory, the kingdom. Jesus taught his disciples to pray "your kingdom come," and the church has long closed that prayer with David's own words. David's people were "strangers and sojourners" whose days were a shadow. Christ, the eternal Son, became a sojourner with us, and through him believers are no longer strangers but "members of the household of God" (Eph 2:19). David asked God to give his son a whole heart, and Solomon's heart was divided. Jesus is the Son whose heart was undivided toward the Father, who said, "I always do the things that are pleasing to him" (John 8:29). And where David gave his treasure for a house of stone, Christ "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor" (2 Cor 8:9), giving himself to build a living temple. Every gift his people bring back is still "of your own."
