*Setting:* Jacob's family, seventy strong plus wives and servants, has just arrived in Egypt in Pharaoh's wagons (46:5–27). Jacob and Joseph have wept on each other's necks in Goshen after twenty-two years apart (46:29–30). Joseph has coached his brothers to tell Pharaoh honestly that they are herdsmen, so that the Egyptians, who despised shepherds, will settle them apart in Goshen (46:31–34). The famine is in its second or third year, with five more to come (45:6). This chapter has three parts: the family is presented to Pharaoh, Joseph manages the worst years of the famine, and old Jacob makes Joseph swear to bury him in Canaan. Egypt is where they live now, but it is not home.

#### Jacob's Family Settles in Goshen (vv1–12)
- **"They are now in the land of Goshen" (v1).** Joseph handles his family's arrival carefully. He does not simply settle them where he likes. He tells Pharaoh they are already in Goshen, which nudges the king toward approving it. JFB praises his humility: the second man in Egypt waits for the king's word.
- **"From among his brothers he took five men" (v2).** Why five is not said. JFB guesses the five eldest. The number five keeps turning up in the Joseph story: a fifth of the harvest (41:34; 47:24), Benjamin's five portions (43:34) and five changes of clothes (45:22). Whether that means anything beyond Egyptian custom is hard to say.
- **"Your servants are shepherds, as our fathers were" (v3).** They answer just as Joseph told them to (46:33–34). **"We have come to sojourn in the land" (v4).** "Sojourn" (*gur*) means to live as a resident foreigner, not a citizen. It is the word for how Abraham and Isaac lived in Canaan (17:8; 35:27). They are saying they mean to stay only for a while. JFB sees faith in that: they still think of Canaan as home.
- **"Settle your father and your brothers in the best of the land" (v6).** Pharaoh is generous. He gives them Goshen and offers jobs to the capable ones as overseers of the royal herds. Egypt's kings kept large herds, and a few Hebrews now work for the crown. Pharaoh is blessing Abraham's family, and God promised to bless those who do (12:3).
- **"Jacob blessed Pharaoh" (v7).** The scene is striking: a 130-year-old foreign herdsman, a refugee from famine, stands before the most powerful king on earth, and it is the herdsman who blesses the king. The verb *barak* can be used for a respectful greeting, but here it is said twice, on arrival and departure (vv7, 10), and Hebrews states the principle: "It is beyond dispute that the inferior is blessed by the superior" (Heb 7:7). In God's eyes, Jacob is the greater man. Guzik adds that Egyptians regarded their king as a son of the sun god Ra, which makes the moment even more remarkable.
- **"How many are the days of the years of your life?" (v8).** It is a polite question to an old man. Ancient Egyptians thought 110 years was the ideal lifespan, so a man of 130 was a marvel.
- **"The days of the years of my sojourning are 130 years" (v9).** Jacob describes his whole life as "sojourning," the same word his sons used in v4. He has always been a resident alien, in Canaan, in Haran and now in Egypt. Hebrews picks this up: the patriarchs "acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth," looking for "a better country, that is, a heavenly one" (Heb 11:13–16).
- **"Few and evil have been the days of the years of my life" (v9).** By Pharaoh's standards 130 is not few, but Abraham lived to 175 (25:7) and Isaac to 180 (35:28). Jacob will die at 147 (v28). "Evil" here means hard, full of trouble, and his life was: cheating Esau and running for his life, twenty years under Laban, the rape of Dinah, Rachel's death, Reuben's sin, the loss of Joseph, the famine. Much of the trouble he brought on himself. Guzik reads it as honest reflection, not self-pity. JFB agrees that his life had been marked by almost constant trouble.
- **"The land of Rameses" (v11).** Rameses is the name of a city the Israelites later built as slaves (Exod 1:11), and it was named after Pharaoh Ramesses, who lived long after Joseph. Most scholars think the narrator or a later scribe used the name readers would know, as a modern writer might say "New York" of a story set in New Amsterdam. The Tyndale notes say Goshen appears in later Egyptian writings as "the region of Rameses."
- **"Joseph provided… with food, according to the number of their dependents" (v12).** Literally "according to the little ones." Joseph's family receives what each household needs, down to the children. Joseph's dreams of his family bowing to him come true in a very different shape than his brothers feared. His rule over them means feeding them.

#### Joseph and the Famine (vv13–31)
- **"There was no food in all the land" (v13).** The narrator turns to Egypt as a whole, and the picture is grim. Egypt and Canaan both "languished," a word meaning to faint or be exhausted.
- **Money, then livestock, then land (vv14–19).** Joseph sells grain for money until the money runs out and goes into Pharaoh's treasury (v14). Next the people trade their livestock: horses, flocks, herds and donkeys (vv16–17). JFB thinks this was wise, since the state could feed the animals better than starving farmers could. When that year ends, they come back with nothing left "but our bodies and our land," and they make the offer themselves: **"Buy us and our land for food, and we with our land will be servants to Pharaoh" (v19).** The phrase "that we may live and not die" is the same one Jacob and Judah used (42:2; 43:8). Everyone in this story is trying to stay alive, and Joseph is the one who can make that happen.
- **"The land became Pharaoh's" (v20).** All the farmland of Egypt now belongs to the crown, and the people farm it as tenants. Only the priests' land is excepted, because the priests lived on a royal allowance and never had to sell (v22). Egyptian temples did hold large estates, so this detail fits.
- **"He made servants of them" (v21).** Here there is a textual question. The standard Hebrew text says "he removed them to the cities," and the KJV follows that. The Samaritan Pentateuch and the Greek translation read "he made them servants," and the ESV and most modern translations follow them. In Hebrew the two readings look very much alike. "Servants" fits what the people offered in v19. JFB, following the older reading, thought people were moved to towns near the grain stores.
- **"You shall give a fifth to Pharaoh, and four fifths shall be your own" (vv23–24).** Joseph hands out seed, and from now on a fifth of every harvest goes to Pharaoh. In an age when tenant farmers often paid a third or half of their crop, a fifth was not harsh. It is the same rate Joseph collected in the seven good years (41:34).
- **"You have saved our lives" (v25).** The Egyptians do not feel cheated. They thank Joseph. Matthew Henry: "There is good reason that the Saviour of our lives should be the Master of our lives." He also praises Joseph's honesty: "He did not put the money into his own pocket, nor entail the lands upon his own family."
- **Was Joseph's policy right?** Readers have answered differently. Some modern interpreters are uneasy: Joseph turned free farmers into crown tenants and made Pharaoh enormously powerful, and a later Pharaoh would use that power to enslave Joseph's own people (Exod 1:8–14). Others, including Guzik and Henry, see a wise administrator saving a whole nation from death on fair terms that the people themselves proposed and thanked him for. The narrator does not judge, beyond recording the people's gratitude. Guzik notes the general lesson that a crisis tends to concentrate power in the hands of the state.
- **"Israel… gained possessions in it, and were fruitful and multiplied greatly" (v27).** Here is the contrast. While Egyptians sell their land and become servants, Israel gains property and grows. "Fruitful and multiplied" are the words of God's first blessing on humankind (1:28) and his promise to Jacob (35:11). Exodus opens with the same words (Exod 1:7). The promise of many descendants is coming true in Egypt, even though the promise of the land still waits.
- **"Jacob lived in the land of Egypt seventeen years" (v28).** He had Joseph for seventeen years before losing him (37:2), and now he has him for seventeen more. He dies at 147.
- **"Put your hand under my thigh" (v29).** This is the same solemn oath Abraham asked of his servant when sending for a wife for Isaac (24:2). The thigh may refer to the place of procreation, connecting the oath to the promised descendants. Jacob asks Joseph to "deal kindly and truly" with him. These are *ḥesed* and *ʾemet*, steadfast love and faithfulness, words often used of God's own covenant love (Exod 34:6).
- **"Do not bury me in Egypt… let me lie with my fathers" (vv29–30).** Egypt is comfortable, and Egyptians were famous for elaborate burials. Jacob wants none of it. He wants the cave of Machpelah, which Abraham bought (23), where Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Rebekah and Leah are buried (49:31). It was the one piece of the promised land the family actually owned. His burial request is an act of faith: God said, "I will also bring you up again" (46:4), and Jacob believes it. JFB is right that this is faith in God's promise, not mere homesickness.
- **"Israel bowed himself upon the head of his bed" (v31).** The Hebrew consonants for "bed" (*miṭṭah*) and "staff" (*maṭṭeh*) are the same. Vowels were added to the Hebrew text much later. The Greek translators read "staff," and Hebrews 11:21 follows them: "By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, bowing in worship over the head of his staff." The standard Hebrew text reads "bed," which fits the next chapter where Jacob is sick in bed (48:2). Both readings picture the same thing: an old man worshiping God because his last wish is secured. JFB prefers "staff," arguing that Eastern beds had no head. The Tyndale notes compare King David bowing on his bed in worship (1 Kgs 1:47).
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Henry both defend Joseph's famine policy as fair and life-saving, and Guzik points out that a twenty percent levy compares well with tax rates today. JFB reads the five brothers as the five eldest and the brothers' word "sojourn" as a sign that they still hoped to return home. All three take Jacob's burial oath as faith that God would keep his promise of the land. Henry also draws the warning from the famine: "See how much we lie at God's mercy, and let us keep ourselves always in his love."

#### Pointing to Christ
Two pictures in this chapter point forward. One is Joseph, ruler of the land, with bread in his hands for anyone who comes, so that starving people say, "You have saved our lives," and gladly become his servants. Jesus says, "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger" (John 6:35), and his people serve him not as a tax collector but as the one who saved them. The other is Jacob the pilgrim, who calls his whole life a sojourning and will not be buried in Egypt because he trusts that God will bring his people home. Hebrews says of him and his fathers that "God is not ashamed to be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city" (Heb 11:16). That city is where Christ has gone ahead to prepare a place (John 14:2–3).
