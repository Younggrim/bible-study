*Setting:* Psalm 90 is headed "A Prayer of Moses, the man of God," the only psalm credited to Moses, which would make it the oldest poem in the Psalter. Most traditional readers accept the title, and the psalm's language matches Deuteronomy closely: God as "dwelling place" (Deut 33:27), "the man of God" (Deut 33:1), God's "servants" and his "work" (Deut 32:36, 4). Many link it to the wilderness years, when a whole generation died in the desert under God's judgment after refusing to enter the land (Num 14:26–35). Some scholars think a later writer composed it in Moses' voice. Psalm 90 opens Book Four of the Psalms (Psalms 90–106), a collection that seems to answer the crisis at the end of Book Three, where David's throne has fallen (Ps 89:38–51). The answer begins by going back before David, to Moses, and before Moses, to God himself: "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations." The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanza breaks.

#### Our Dwelling Place (vv1–2)
- **"Lord" (v1).** The word is *Adonai*, "Lord, Master," not the personal name LORD (*Yahweh*), which appears only once, in v13. The psalm begins by addressing God as sovereign.
- **"You have been our dwelling place in all generations" (v1).** "Dwelling place" is *ma'on*, a home or refuge, used of God's holy dwelling in heaven (Deut 26:15) and of a lion's den. Moses had used it in his final blessing: "The eternal God is your dwelling place" (Deut 33:27). Israel spent forty years with no house and no land, living in tents and moving from camp to camp. Moses says they were never homeless. God himself was their home.
- **"In all generations."** Abraham, Isaac and Jacob lived in tents in a land that was not yet theirs, Israel lived as slaves in Egypt, and now a generation is dying in the desert. In every one, God was the home of his people.
- **"Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever you had formed the earth and the world" (v2).** Both verbs are birth words. "Brought forth" is used for a child being born, and "formed" (*chil*) is the word for a woman in labor (Deut 32:18). The mountains seem the oldest and most permanent things in the world. God was there before they were born.
- **"From everlasting to everlasting you are God" (v2).** Literally "from age to age." God has no beginning and no end. Notice the tense: not "you were God," but "you are God." Jesus would later say, "before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Moses "comforts himself and his people with the eternity of God and their interest in him." JFB explains "dwelling place" as a home or refuge and points to Deuteronomy 33:27. Guzik, following Boice, notes the same link, and with Spurgeon draws out that believers are never truly homeless.

#### Man Is Like Grass (vv3–6)
- **"You return man to dust and say, 'Return, O children of man!'" (v3).** The word for "dust" here is *dakka*, "something crushed to powder," not the usual word in Genesis 3:19. But the verb "return" clearly echoes God's sentence on Adam: "to dust you shall return." "Man" is *'enosh*, a word that stresses human weakness. The second line may mean "return to dust," repeating the first, or it may mean God calls the next generation into being as the old one passes away.
- **"For a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past, or as a watch in the night" (v4).** Before the flood people lived almost a thousand years (Methuselah, 969, Gen 5:27). Even such a life is like yesterday to God, a day that already feels short once it is over. **"A watch in the night"** was about four hours (the Hebrews divided the night into three watches, Judg 7:19), most of it slept through. Peter draws on this verse to explain why Christ's return seems slow: "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Pet 3:8).
- **"You sweep them away as with a flood; they are like a dream" (v5).** The Hebrew is literally "you flood them away, they are sleep." Like a flash flood in a desert wadi, or like a dream that is gone the moment one wakes.
- **"Like grass that is renewed in the morning… in the evening it fades and withers" (vv5–6).** In the dry lands of the Middle East, grass springs up after rain and is burned brown by the hot wind within a day. Isaiah uses the same picture: "All flesh is grass… the grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever" (Isa 40:6–8; 1 Pet 1:24–25).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "a thousand years are nothing to God's eternity," and that even the long lives of the patriarchs are brief beside him. He sees this section as Moses humbling "himself and his people with the consideration of the frailty of man." JFB notes that a "watch" may mean a third of the night (Exod 14:24) and links v4 to 2 Peter 3:8. Guzik follows Kidner: the word for dust differs from Genesis 3:19, but the verb "return" still recalls Adam's curse.

#### Under Your Wrath (vv7–11)
- **"For we are brought to an end by your anger" (v7).** The psalm now says why life is so short and hard. It is not only that humans are small and God is great. It is that we are sinners and God is holy. For the wilderness generation this was literally true: their deaths in the desert were God's judgment on their unbelief (Num 14:29; Deut 2:14–16; 1 Cor 10:5).
- **"You have set our iniquities before you, our secret sins in the light of your presence" (v8).** "Secret sins" means hidden ones, even those we have hidden from ourselves (Ps 19:12). The light of God's face, which in the priestly blessing brings grace (Num 6:25), here exposes everything.
- **"We bring our years to an end like a sigh" (v9).** "Sigh" is *hegeh*, a low sound, a murmur or moan. The KJV's "as a tale that is told" is a guess at the same word. A life comes to an end like a breath let out.
- **"The years of our life are seventy, or even by reason of strength eighty" (v10).** This is a description of an ordinary human life, not a promise or a fixed limit. Moses himself lived to 120, Aaron to 123, and Joshua to 110, but they were exceptions. **"Their span is but toil and trouble"**: "span" may mean "pride" or "best part." Even the best years are full of labor and sorrow. **"They are soon gone, and we fly away."** JFB notes the verb "gone" is the one used of the quail driven in on the wind (Num 11:31).
- **"Who considers the power of your anger, and your wrath according to the fear of you?" (v11).** Few people take God's anger seriously. The second line suggests that God's wrath is as great as the reverence due to him. The more one understands who God is, the more one understands how serious sin is.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this section Moses submitting "himself and his people to the righteous sentence of God passed upon them," and connects the psalm with the sentence of Numbers 14, that they "should be wasted away by a series of miseries for thirty-eight years together." Guzik says "seventy years" is a poetic estimate of a lifetime, not a limit, and he points out that Moses lived far longer (Deut 34:7). JFB also treats Moses' long life as an exception, and suggests v11 may be read as a question expecting the answer "no one."

#### Teach Us to Number Our Days (vv12–17)
- **"So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (v12).** The turning point of the psalm. "Number" is *manah*, "count." The prayer is not for a calendar but for the wisdom to treat each day as limited and precious. "Get" is literally "bring in," like a harvest. A heart of wisdom is gathered from facing honestly how short life is. Henry says the psalm offers "meditations and prayers very suitable to the solemnity of a funeral," and it has been read at Christian funerals for centuries.
- **"Return, O LORD! How long?" (v13).** In v3 God said "Return" to dying man. Now Moses says "Return" to God. "How long?" is the cry of many psalms of lament (Ps 6:3; 13:1). **"Have pity on your servants"** uses *nacham*, "relent, change course, be comforted." The KJV reads "let it repent thee." Moses had prayed in the same way after the golden calf: "Turn from your burning anger and relent" (Exod 32:12).
- **"Satisfy us in the morning with your steadfast love" (v14).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. Night has been long, and the prayer is for morning. Like the manna that came each morning in the wilderness (Exod 16:21), God's love is asked for fresh each day. **"That we may rejoice and be glad all our days"**: the same "days" that were numbered in v12. A short life filled with God's love can be glad.
- **"Make us glad for as many days as you have afflicted us" (v15).** A bold request: let the joy be as long as the trouble was. Forty years in the desert, so give forty years of gladness. Paul says even more: "this light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (2 Cor 4:17).
- **"Let your work be shown to your servants, and your glorious power to their children" (v16).** The wilderness generation would not see the conquest of Canaan, but their children would (Num 14:31). Moses prays that the next generation will see what God does.
- **"Let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us" (v17).** "Favor" is *no'am*, "delightfulness, pleasantness," the same word David uses when he longs "to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD" (Ps 27:4). The KJV renders it "beauty."
- **"And establish the work of our hands upon us; yes, establish the work of our hands!" (v17).** The psalm ends with a double prayer. "Establish" is *kun*, "make firm, make last." Life is grass, a sigh, a dream. But work done under God's favor need not vanish. The only thing that lasts from a short life is what God makes last (Ps 127:1; 1 Cor 15:58).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this last section Moses committing "himself and his people to God by prayer for divine mercy and grace." JFB calls the plea "let it repent" in v13 a strong figure, like Exodus 32:12, and explains "early" in v14 as "promptly." On v17 JFB says God's gracious acts should "be illustrated in us, and favor our enterprise." Guzik stresses that numbering our days is a learned wisdom, not automatic, and that satisfaction comes from God's love rather than from success or pleasure. With Spurgeon and Maclaren he adds that God's servants are his tools, and nothing they do lasts unless he establishes it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 90 is honest about death in a way few writings are. It does not pretend life is long or that sin is small. It asks God to "return" and to satisfy his people with steadfast love in the morning. The New Testament answer came on a morning: "very early on the first day of the week, when the sun had risen," the women found the tomb empty (Mark 16:2). The eternal God, who was "from everlasting to everlasting," became a man and shared the short, troubled life of "the children of man." He went down into death so that those who return to dust could rise with him. Because he lives, Paul can end his great resurrection chapter much as Moses ended this psalm: "be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (1 Cor 15:58). In Christ, God answers the last prayer of Moses: he establishes the work of our hands.
