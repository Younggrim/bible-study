*Setting:* "The third year of Cyrus king of Persia" (v1), about 536 BC. This is the last date in the book, and Daniel is probably in his mid-eighties. Two years earlier Cyrus had issued his decree letting the Jews return home (Ezra 1:1–4), the answer to Daniel's prayer in chapter 9. About fifty thousand exiles had gone back under Zerubbabel and laid the foundation of a new temple (Ezra 2:64–65; 3:8–13). Daniel did not go, perhaps because of his age, perhaps because he could do more good where he was. News from Jerusalem was not encouraging. The local peoples were already opposing the rebuilding, and soon the work would stop for about sixteen years (Ezra 4:1–5, 24). Chapters 10–12 are one long final vision. Chapter 10 is its introduction: Daniel's mourning, an overwhelming heavenly messenger, and a rare look behind the curtain at the unseen battles behind the history of nations. The message itself follows in chapters 11 and 12, and the angel's speech runs on into 11:1.

#### Daniel's Terrifying Vision of a Man (vv1–21)
- **"Daniel, who was named Belteshazzar" (v1).** The narrator reminds us of his Babylonian name (1:7) one last time. Babylon is gone. The man who carried its name is still serving God.
- **"The word was true, and it was a great conflict" (v1).** The Hebrew is compact. "Great conflict" may mean the vision foretold great warfare, which chapter 11 describes. It may mean the hard struggle of receiving it, or the conflict in the heavens about to be explained (vv13, 20). The KJV's "the time appointed was long" takes a less likely sense of the same word. **"He understood the word"** contrasts with 8:27, where he did not understand. This time the explanation is given in plain words, not symbols.
- **"Mourning for three weeks" (v2).** The text does not say why. The likeliest reason is the state of things in Jerusalem: few exiles had returned, and those who had were being blocked. It may also be the troubling visions of chapters 8–9. Either way, it is grief for God's people.
- **"I ate no delicacies, no meat or wine… nor did I anoint myself" (v3).** This was not a total fast but a partial one: plain food only and no comforts. Anointing the skin with oil was normal daily grooming in a hot, dry climate, and people in mourning stopped (2 Sam 14:2; Matt 6:17). Some Christians today keep a "Daniel fast" based on this verse and 1:12. "Delicacies" is literally "bread of desirable things," from the same root as the "greatly loved" of v11.
- **"The twenty-fourth day of the first month" (v4).** The first month is Nisan (March–April). Passover fell on the 14th and the feast of Unleavened Bread ran from the 15th to the 21st (Exod 12:1–20). So Daniel's three weeks of mourning covered the time of Israel's great festival of deliverance, kept far from Jerusalem.
- **"The bank of the great river (that is, the Tigris)" (v4).** The Hebrew name is *Hiddekel*, one of the four rivers of Eden (Gen 2:14). Usually "the great river" means the Euphrates (Gen 15:18), which is probably why the narrator adds the explanation. The Tigris ran about twenty miles east of Babylon. Daniel was not alone (v7), so he may have been travelling on government business.
- **"A man clothed in linen" (v5).** Linen was the clothing of priests (Lev 16:4) and of the heavenly scribe in Ezekiel 9:2–3. **"A belt of fine gold from Uphaz"**: Uphaz is a place known only as a source of gold (Jer 10:9). Its location is unknown.
- **"His body was like beryl, his face like the appearance of lightning, his eyes like flaming torches" (v6).** "Beryl" translates *tarshish*, a precious stone of uncertain identity, perhaps a yellow topaz or chrysolite. It is also in the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:20) and in the wheels of Ezekiel's vision (Ezek 1:16). **"Arms and legs like… burnished bronze"** and **"the sound of his words like the sound of a multitude"** echo Ezekiel 1 closely.
- **Who is the man in linen?** The description is very close to John's vision of the risen Christ: eyes like flame, feet like burnished bronze, a voice like many waters, a golden sash (Rev 1:13–16). So many readers see a vision of the pre-incarnate Son of God here. Others note that the same figure says he needed Michael's help (v13) and conclude he is a high angel, perhaps Gabriel. A third view holds that the glorious man of vv5–6 is the Lord, and that the one who touches and speaks to Daniel from v10 on is an angel attending him. The text does not settle it. Whoever he is, his glory reflects God's.
- **"The men who were with me did not see the vision" (v7).** Compare Paul on the Damascus road, whose companions heard a sound but did not see anyone or understand (Acts 9:7; 22:9). God's revelation can be present to one person and hidden from others standing next to him. The men sensed enough to run.
- **"My radiant appearance was fearfully changed" (v8).** Literally "my splendour was turned to ruin." The colour drained out of him. This is how holy people respond to the holy God (Isa 6:5; Luke 5:8; Rev 1:17).
- **"I fell on my face in deep sleep" (v9).** Not ordinary sleep, but the stunned unconsciousness of 8:18.
- **"A hand touched me and set me trembling on my hands and knees" (v10).** This is the first of three touches (vv10, 16, 18), each bringing Daniel a step further: from flat on the ground to his hands and knees, then able to speak, then strong enough to listen. God does not overwhelm his servant and leave him there.
- **"O Daniel, man greatly loved" (v11).** Gabriel used the same words in 9:23, and they come once more in v19. Daniel's age, rank and faithfulness are never mentioned. What heaven says about him is that he is loved.
- **"From the first day that you set your heart to understand and humbled yourself" (v12).** Daniel's mourning had two sides: a mind seeking to understand and a heart bowed in humility. **"Your words have been heard, and I have come because of your words."** The prayer was heard on day one. The answer took three weeks to arrive.
- **"The prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days" (v13).** This is one of the most striking verses in the Old Testament. The "prince" is not King Cyrus, since no human king could hold off an angel. He is a spiritual power standing behind the Persian empire. Deuteronomy 32:8 (in the reading preserved in the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek Old Testament) speaks of God dividing the nations "according to the number of the sons of God," and Paul speaks of "rulers… authorities… cosmic powers over this present darkness" (Eph 6:12). Daniel's prayer and the angel's struggle are linked: the delay was due to opposition in the heavens, not to God's indifference.
- **"Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me" (v13).** Michael, whose name means "Who is like God?", appears here for the first time in Scripture. He is called **"your prince"** (v21) and later "the great prince who has charge of your people" (12:1). In the New Testament he is "the archangel" (Jude 9) and leads the angels against the dragon (Rev 12:7). **"I was left there with the kings of Persia"** is difficult Hebrew. It may mean Michael's help let the angel get away, or that the angel was left to keep up the struggle with Persia's powers.
- **"What is to happen to your people in the latter days" (v14).** The coming message (11:2–12:4) concerns Daniel's own people. "The latter days" can mean the future in general, but in the prophets it often points to the time when God finally brings his purposes to completion (Isa 2:2; Hos 3:5).
- **"One in the likeness of the children of man touched my lips" (v16).** Compare Isaiah, whose lips were touched with a coal (Isa 6:6–7), and Jeremiah, whose mouth God touched (Jer 1:9). Daniel is enabled to speak, and his first words are an admission of weakness: **"pains have come upon me,"** the word used of a woman in labour (1 Sam 4:19; Isa 13:8). **"No breath is left in me"** (v17).
- **"Fear not, peace be with you; be strong and of good courage" (v19).** "Be strong" is said twice in Hebrew. The words recall God's charge to Joshua (Josh 1:6–9). Daniel is strengthened by being spoken to.
- **"Now I will return to fight against the prince of Persia; and when I go out, behold, the prince of Greece will come" (v20).** The angel describes history from heaven's side. Persia will fall and Greece will rise, as chapters 2, 7 and 8 foretold, and behind each empire stands a spiritual power that opposes God's people.
- **"The book of truth" (v21).** Literally "the writing of truth," God's record of what will be, the source of what the angel now reveals (compare 7:10; 12:1; Ps 139:16). History is not random. It is written.
- **"None who contends by my side against these except Michael, your prince" (v21).** Two angels against the powers of empires. Israel was small and powerless on earth, but in heaven its cause was in strong hands. The thought runs on into 11:1: the angel had stood by Darius the Mede "to confirm and strengthen him" in the year Babylon fell, the year of Daniel's prayer in chapter 9.
- **Readings of the spiritual warfare.** Some Christians build detailed teaching on "territorial spirits" from this chapter, naming and binding the spirits over places. Most interpreters are more cautious. The chapter shows that unseen powers are real and that prayer is bound up with their conflict, but Daniel never addresses the prince of Persia, never commands him, and never learns his name. He prays to God, and God sends his messengers.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the vision "a glorious appearance of the Son of God" and notes that "Daniel prayed daily, but had a vision only now and then." JFB calls the man in linen "an angel of the highest order," and the prince of Persia "the angel of darkness that represented the Persian world power." He calls the book of truth "the secret book of God's decrees." Guzik also leans toward a high angel rather than Christ, reasoning that the Son of God would not need Michael's help. He takes the prince of Persia as a high-ranking demon. He draws a pastoral point about persistent prayer: the angel came "because of your words," and the delay was meant to make Daniel a person who keeps praying, not to wear down God's reluctance. Guzik and JFB both connect Daniel's mourning to the setbacks in Jerusalem.

#### Pointing to Christ
Daniel, worn out and grieving for his people, was met by a glorious figure who touched him, called him greatly loved, and told him not to fear. John had the same experience on Patmos. He saw a man with eyes like fire and feet like bronze and fell "as though dead." The risen Jesus "laid his right hand on me, saying, 'Fear not'" (Rev 1:17). Whether or not the man by the Tigris was the Son himself, Daniel was shown in advance the kind of Lord his people have: glorious beyond bearing, yet gentle with weak servants. The struggles of the powers behind the empires are real. But Christ has "disarmed the rulers and authorities" by the cross (Col 2:15), and the angel's words "Fear not… be strong" (v19) still hold for his people.
