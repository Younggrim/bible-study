*Setting:* Babylonia. The first message (vv1–16) is dated the twelfth day of the tenth month of the tenth year of the exile, about January 587 BC. Jerusalem had been under siege for a year, and the siege had been briefly lifted when an Egyptian army marched north to help Judah (Jer 37:5–11). Many in Jerusalem and among the exiles still hoped Pharaoh would save them. The Pharaoh was Hophra (Greek Apries), who reigned about 589–570 BC. Chapters 29–32 are the seventh and longest of Ezekiel's oracles against the nations: seven messages against Egypt, six of them dated. Egypt had been Israel's house of slavery, and then, again and again, the ally Israel trusted instead of the LORD. The second message in this chapter (vv17–21) is dated seventeen years later, in 571 BC, and is the latest dated word in the whole book. It was placed here because it is about Egypt too.

#### Prophecy Against Egypt (vv1–21)
- **"Set your face against Pharaoh king of Egypt" (v2).** Ezekiel, an exile with no power, is sent to address the ruler of the oldest kingdom in the world. "Pharaoh" was a title, not a name, from an Egyptian phrase meaning "great house," the palace.
- **"The great dragon that lies in the midst of his streams" (v3).** "Dragon" is *tannim*, a large creature of the water. In some passages it is the sea monster of ancient poetry, a symbol of chaos that God defeats (Isa 27:1; 51:9; Ps 74:13). Here it is plainly also the Nile crocodile, lying in the channels of the delta. Egyptians feared and honored the crocodile, and the crocodile god Sobek was widely worshiped. The Tyndale notes point out that Genesis had already made such creatures part of God's good creation (Gen 1:21). The monster is no rival to God. Pharaoh is pictured as the great beast of the Nile, at ease in his own waters.
- **"That says, 'My Nile is my own; I made it for myself'" (v3).** Egypt's whole life depended on the Nile. Each year its flood watered the fields and laid down fresh soil in a land that otherwise is desert. Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the river." Pharaoh, regarded as divine, was thought to guarantee its flow. Here he claims to have made it. Feinberg, quoted by Guzik, puts it well: the river made him, not he the river. Henry: "Self is the great idol that all the world worships."
- **"I will put hooks in your jaws" (v4).** The Greek historian Herodotus describes crocodiles being caught with a baited hook and dragged ashore. Assyrian kings were shown leading captives with hooks through their lips, and God used the same picture for Sennacherib (2 Kings 19:28). The crocodile who thought himself lord of the river is caught like any other.
- **"Make the fish of your streams stick to your scales" (v4).** The fish are Pharaoh's people, his officials and soldiers. They cling to him and are dragged out with him.
- **"I will cast you out into the wilderness… you shall fall on the open field, and not be brought together or gathered" (v5).** A crocodile thrown onto dry land dies. "Not… gathered" means not buried. The pharaohs spent fortunes on their tombs, the pyramids and the burial valleys near Thebes. This one will be left for birds and wild animals. Hophra was in fact killed around 570 BC in a struggle with his general Amasis, who took the throne.
- **"Because you have been a staff of reed to the house of Israel" (v6).** The giant reeds along the Nile looked strong but split when leaned on. A century earlier the Assyrian field commander mocked Hezekiah for relying on Egypt, "that broken reed of a staff, which will pierce the hand of any man who leans on it" (2 Kings 18:21; Isa 36:6). Isaiah warned, "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help" (Isa 31:1).
- **"When they grasped you with the hand, you broke and tore all their shoulders" (v7).** The picture is of a man leaning his full weight on a staff that snaps, the splinters cutting him and his body falling. Hophra's army had come out, and the Babylonians had withdrawn from Jerusalem for a while. Then the Egyptians went home, and the siege resumed (Jer 37:5–8). Judah's last hope broke in its hand. The Tyndale notes observe that relying on Egypt was Judah's sin, but encouraging rebellion and then failing to help was Egypt's.
- **"I will bring a sword upon you, and will cut off from you man and beast" (v8).** The sword is Babylon's (v19; 30:10). **"Then they will know that I am the LORD" (v9).** The words answer Pharaoh's boast. He said, "I made it." God says, "I am the LORD."
- **"From Migdol to Syene, as far as the border of Cush" (v10).** Migdol was a fortress in the northeast delta, near the route from Sinai (Exod 14:2). Syene is modern Aswan, at the first cataract of the Nile, where Egypt met Cush (Nubia, in modern Sudan). The phrase means the whole country, north to south, like "from Dan to Beersheba" for Israel.
- **"It shall be uninhabited forty years" (vv11–12).** Forty years is a generation and a set time of judgment, the same span as Israel's wandering in the wilderness (Num 14:33–34), and the same number of years Ezekiel lay on his side for Judah (4:6). Interpreters handle this differently. No known period of total desolation in Egypt lasted forty years. Some, as Guzik notes, still expect a literal fulfilment. Some see it fulfilled in the Babylonian invasion and the exile of Egyptian captives until Persian times, roughly forty years. Henry suggests the forty years ended around the first year of Cyrus, when Judah's own captivity ended. Others read "forty years" as a round number for a full generation of judgment, like the same number in 4:6.
- **"I will scatter the Egyptians among the nations" (v12).** The Babylonian historian Berossus, quoted by Josephus, says Nebuchadnezzar took Egyptian captives to Babylon. Egypt would share the fate it had failed to prevent for Judah.
- **"At the end of forty years I will gather the Egyptians" (v13).** This is remarkable. It is the only place in Ezekiel where a foreign nation is promised restoration. Jeremiah says the same: "Afterward Egypt shall be inhabited as in the days of old" (Jer 46:26). Isaiah goes further, foreseeing a day when "the LORD will make himself known to the Egyptians" and say, "Blessed be Egypt my people" (Isa 19:21–25).
- **"Bring them back to the land of Pathros, the land of their origin" (v14).** Pathros is Upper Egypt, the southern Nile valley, where Egyptian civilization and kingship began. **"There they shall be a lowly kingdom."**
- **"It shall be the most lowly of the kingdoms, and never again exalt itself above the nations" (v15).** History bears this out. After Babylon came Persia, which conquered Egypt in 525 BC. Then Alexander the Great, the Greek Ptolemies, Rome, Byzantium and the Arab caliphs. For more than two thousand years Egypt was ruled by foreigners and never again was an empire ruling other nations.
- **"It shall never again be the reliance of the house of Israel, recalling their iniquity" (v16).** The real point is Israel's heart. Egypt will be humbled so that Israel stops trusting it. Even its weakness will remind Israel of its old sin of looking to Egypt. Henry: "The creatures we confide in are often therefore ruined, because there is no other way effectually to cure us of our confidence in them."
- **"In the twenty-seventh year, in the first month, on the first day of the month" (v17).** About April 571 BC. Jerusalem has been in ruins for fifteen years. This is Ezekiel's last dated message, later even than the temple vision of chapters 40–48 (40:1). Its placement here, out of date order, keeps the Egypt material together.
- **"Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon made his army labor hard against Tyre" (v18).** The thirteen-year siege of Tyre (chs 26–28) had just ended, around 573 BC. **"Every head was made bald, and every shoulder was rubbed bare"** from years of carrying earth and stones for siege works, wearing helmets and hauling loads. Tyre had submitted, but its island city was not plundered. Perhaps its people shipped their wealth away by sea. **"Yet neither he nor his army got anything from Tyre to pay for the labor."**
- **The honesty of this verse.** Ezekiel had said Nebuchadnezzar would "plunder your riches and loot your merchandise" (26:12). Here, years later, he records plainly that Babylon got almost nothing from Tyre. The book does not hide this. Interpreters explain it in different ways. Many note that chapter 26 itself moved from "he" (Nebuchadnezzar) to "they" (many nations), so the full plundering was always going to come later, under Alexander. Others see a prophet adjusting nothing, simply reporting what God said next: Tyre was humbled, as promised, but Babylon was to be paid elsewhere. Either way, the verse shows prophecy recorded and preserved by people who were not afraid of its details.
- **"I will give the land of Egypt to Nebuchadnezzar… it shall be the wages for his army" (v19).** God pays his workers. Nebuchadnezzar did not know he was serving the God of Israel, but he was (Jer 25:9; 27:6, where God calls him "my servant"). **"Because they worked for me" (v20).** A broken Babylonian tablet records a campaign by Nebuchadnezzar against Amasis of Egypt in his thirty-seventh year, 568–567 BC. How far it went is not known, but it fits this prophecy. Henry: "Even great men and bad men are tools that God makes use of, and are working for him even when they are pursuing their own covetous and ambitious designs."
- **"On that day I will cause a horn to spring up for the house of Israel" (v21).** A horn is the Bible's picture of strength, from the horns of a wild ox (Deut 33:17; 1 Sam 2:1). "To make a horn spring up" (*tsamach*, "to sprout") is the language of Psalm 132:17, "There I will make a horn to sprout for David," and the word "sprout" is related to the "Branch" (*tsemach*), a title of the coming Messiah (Jer 23:5; Zech 3:8). As Egypt sinks, Israel will rise. Many commentators see a messianic hope here.
- **"I will open your lips among them" (v21).** Ezekiel's restricted speech ended when news of Jerusalem's fall came (24:27; 33:22). This may mean that when the promises come true, Ezekiel's word will be vindicated and he will speak with full authority. The Tyndale notes suggest that the delay at Tyre may have made some doubt him. God promises that events will prove him a true prophet.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the main lesson from v3: "Worldly carnal minds please themselves with, and pride themselves in, their property, forgetting that whatever we have we have only the use of it, the property is in God." He also notes, "Though God will find out a way to humble the proud, yet he will not contend for ever," seeing the promise of Egypt's partial restoration as mercy. JFB identifies the Pharaoh as Hophra and notes that his reign began prosperously, with victories in Phoenicia, before he was overthrown. He links the forty years to Israel's own wilderness years and explains the bald heads and bare shoulders as the result of carrying baskets of earth and stones for the siege works. He reads "no wages" as meaning not in proportion to the effort, and notes that Tyre did receive a king appointed from Babylon. Guzik, citing Feinberg, notes that 29:1 falls a year after the siege began and seven months before the city fell, when hope in Egypt was at its height. He cites Wright that Egypt's later restoration came under Greek rule, when Alexandria became a major center of Judaism and then of Christianity.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is about a broken reed. Judah leaned on Egypt, and it snapped and wounded them. Isaiah described the coming Servant of the LORD in the opposite way: "a bruised reed he will not break" (Isa 42:3), and Matthew applies those words to Jesus (Matt 12:20). Pharaoh was a reed that broke the people who leaned on him. Jesus is the strong one who does not break the weak who lean on him. The "horn" that springs up for Israel (v21) is fulfilled in him as well. Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, praised God because he "has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" (Luke 1:69). And the surprising mercy promised to Egypt, a humbled people gathered again, comes to its full shape in the gospel. Jesus was carried to Egypt as a child and called out of it (Matt 2:15), and on the day of Pentecost visitors from "Egypt and the parts of Libya" heard the mighty works of God in their own language (Acts 2:10–11).
