*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapters 21–25 gather a wide range of laws that can seem to jump from topic to topic, from lost donkeys to bird nests to marriage. Many scholars see them loosely following the order of the Ten Commandments: chapters 19–21 dealing with life (the sixth), chapter 22 moving into sexual faithfulness (the seventh), and chapters 23–24 into property and fair dealing. Whether or not that pattern was intended, the aim is clear. These laws carry the covenant into ordinary life: walking down a road, building a house, planting a field, getting dressed, getting married. Chapter 22 has two halves: everyday kindness and order (vv1–12), and laws protecting marriage and the women most at risk within it (vv13–30).

#### Various Laws (vv1–12)
- **"You shall not see your brother's ox or his sheep going astray and ignore them" (v1).** "Ignore" is literally "hide yourself from them." It names something everyone does: look away and walk on. The law forbids it. Exodus 23:4–5 says the same even of an *enemy's* ox. In Deuteronomy the owner is "your brother," which pushes the point further: a neighbor's loss is a family matter.
- **"It shall stay with you until your brother seeks it" (v2).** If the owner lived far away or was unknown, the finder had to take the animal home and feed it, at his own cost, until it was claimed. "Finders keepers" was ruled out. Leviticus 6:3–5 treats lying about lost property as a sin needing a guilt offering.
- **"His donkey or… his garment, or with any lost thing" (v3).** The law starts with animals and widens to everything. **"You may not ignore it"** repeats the point.
- **"Fallen down by the way… You shall help him to lift them up again" (v4).** A loaded donkey that collapsed on the road could not be raised by one man. In Jesus' parable, the priest and the Levite saw a man fallen on the road and "passed by on the other side" (Luke 10:31–32), the very thing this law forbade.
- **"A woman shall not wear a man's garment, nor shall a man put on a woman's cloak" (v5).** The word for the man's item, *keli*, is broader than clothing. It can mean any article or equipment, including weapons, and some ancient Jewish readers took the verse to include a woman taking up a soldier's gear. **"An abomination to the LORD"** is strong language, used elsewhere for idolatry. Some scholars think cross-dressing was part of certain Canaanite rituals, though the evidence is limited. The Tyndale notes take the principle to be the distinction God made between man and woman at creation (Gen 1:27), which is not to be deliberately blurred. Styles of clothing differ between cultures, and the verse does not fix a particular style. It forbids presenting oneself as the other sex.
- **"You shall not take the mother with the young" (v6).** A traveler finding a nest could take eggs or chicks for food, but had to let the mother go. Leviticus 22:28 and Exodus 23:19 show the same restraint: do not kill a mother and its young on the same day, and do not boil a kid in its mother's milk. It is a law about mercy and about not destroying the source of future life.
- **"That it may go well with you, and that you may live long" (v7).** This is the same promise attached to honoring father and mother (5:16). The rabbis noticed that one of the smallest commands and one of the weightiest carry the same reward. Jesus said God sees every sparrow (Matt 10:29).
- **"You shall make a parapet for your roof" (v8).** Houses had flat roofs, used for drying crops, sleeping in summer, and praying (Josh 2:6; 1 Sam 9:25–26; Acts 10:9). A low wall around the edge kept people from falling. **"That you may not bring the guilt of blood upon your house"**: if someone fell from a roof without a parapet, the owner was responsible. This is a building code with a theology behind it. Life matters, and carelessness that costs a life is guilt (compare the goring ox in Exod 21:28–29).
- **"You shall not sow your vineyard with two kinds of seed, lest the whole yield be forfeited" (v9).** "Forfeited" is literally "become holy," that is, it would be taken as sanctuary property. Leviticus 19:19 gives the wider rule against mixing kinds of seed, animals and cloth.
- **"You shall not plow with an ox and a donkey together" (v10).** The two animals differ in size, strength and pace, so the yoke would hurt both, and an ox was a clean animal while a donkey was unclean. Paul seems to echo this when he writes, "Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers" (2 Cor 6:14).
- **"You shall not wear cloth of wool and linen mixed together" (v11).** The word *sha'atnez*, "mixed stuff," appears only here and in Leviticus 19:19 and is probably borrowed from Egyptian. Interestingly, the high priest's garments and the tabernacle curtains did combine wool and linen (Exod 26:1; 28:6; 39:29). Many scholars think that is the point: what belonged to the holy place was not for ordinary wear.
- **Why these laws about mixtures?** The text gives no reason, and faithful readers have suggested several. They may have kept Israel from pagan practices that mixed kinds for magic, as some older commentators thought. They may have pictured Israel as a people set apart from the nations, as the Tyndale notes say. Or they may have respected the order of creation, where God made each thing "according to its kind" (Gen 1:11–12, 21, 24). The New Testament does not bind Christians to them (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15; Col 2:16–17), but the call to belong wholly to God remains.
- **"You shall make yourself tassels on the four corners of the garment" (v12).** Numbers 15:38–40 explains why: "to look at and remember all the commandments of the LORD." The word here is *gedilim*, "twisted threads." Jesus wore them. The woman with the bleeding touched "the fringe of his garment" (Matt 9:20), and others begged to touch it (Matt 14:36). He also rebuked the Pharisees who made theirs long to be seen (Matt 23:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry on vv1–4: "We can have no property in any thing that we find," and "Religion teaches us to be neighbourly, and to be ready to do all good offices to all men." On vv5–12: "God's providence extends itself to the smallest affairs," and "Our eye should be single, our heart simple, and our behaviour all of a piece." JFB says "brother" in v1 covers anyone, even a foreigner or enemy, and calls the parapet "a wise and prudent precaution." It also suggests that sparing the mother bird preserved species that kept down pests. Guzik stresses that vv1–4 condemn doing nothing, and applies the parapet to Philippians 2:4, looking to the interests of others. He follows older writers who saw pagan superstition behind the mixtures.

#### Laws Concerning Sexual Immorality (vv13–30)
- **Reading these laws today.** These laws are hard for modern readers, and some of their penalties are severe. They come from a culture where a woman's father arranged her marriage, virginity was expected, and a ruined reputation could leave a woman with no future. Read in that setting, much of this section works to *protect* women from men who would use or slander them.
- **"If any man takes a wife… and then hates her and accuses her of misconduct" (vv13–14).** "Hates" here means he has turned against her. To get out of the marriage without returning the bride price, and perhaps to get money back, he claims she was not a virgin when they married.
- **"The father… and her mother shall take and bring out the evidence of her virginity" (v15).** Families commonly kept the cloth or garment from the wedding night, stained with blood when the marriage was first consummated. The Tyndale notes say the word for "cloak" in v17 could also mean a sheet or nightgown. Both parents stand with their daughter at the gate. She is not left to defend herself alone.
- **"The elders… shall take the man and whip him… fine him a hundred shekels of silver" (vv18–19).** The slanderer is beaten, and pays twice the bride price set in v29, a very large sum. **"He may not divorce her all his days."** He loses the right to send her away. This was not a reward to him but protection for her: he could not later throw her out and leave her with nothing. Exodus 23:1 and Deuteronomy 19:16–19 lie in the background: false witness is punished.
- **"But if the thing is true… the men of her city shall stone her" (vv20–21).** If she had concealed sex before marriage and been given as a virgin, the penalty was death. **"An outrageous thing in Israel"** is *nevalah*, "folly" or "disgrace," the word used for the rape of Dinah (Gen 34:7) and of Tamar (2 Sam 13:12). **"Whoring in her father's house"** means she had sex while still under her father's care and then deceived both him and her husband.
- **"If a man is found lying with the wife of another man, both of them shall die" (v22).** Adultery was a capital crime for both, with no double standard (Lev 20:10). "Found" implies they were caught in the act, with witnesses. When the scribes brought Jesus a woman "caught in the act of adultery," they did not bring the man (John 8:3–5), which already broke this law.
- **"A betrothed virgin… in the city… because she did not cry for help" (vv23–24).** Betrothal was legally binding, so she is called "his neighbor's wife." If it happened in a town where a cry would be heard and she made none, the law presumed she consented. Many interpreters note that the real point is the presumption: where she could have called for help and did not, the act was treated as consensual.
- **"But if in the open country… the man seizes her… only the man… shall die" (v25).** Here the verb is *chazaq*, "overpower, seize by force." The woman is presumed innocent: **"though the betrothed young woman cried for help there was no one to rescue her"** (v27). The law gives her the benefit of the doubt, since no one could have heard. **"This case is like that of a man attacking and murdering his neighbor"** (v26). Rape is put alongside murder, and the victim bears no blame.
- **"If a man meets a virgin who is not betrothed, and seizes her and lies with her" (v28).** The verb here is *taphas*, "take hold of," which is weaker than the forcing in v25. Exodus 22:16–17 covers a similar case with "seduces," and many think this verse describes the same kind of case. Others read it as rape. **"He shall give to the father… fifty shekels of silver, and she shall be his wife… He may not divorce her all his days"** (v29). In a world where a woman who had lost her virginity might never marry, this forced the man to take full responsibility for her for life. Exodus 22:17 adds that her father could refuse the marriage, and the man still paid. The law bound the man. It did not bind her to him against her family's will.
- **"A man shall not take his father's wife" (v30).** This means a stepmother, his father's other wife or widow. "Uncover his father's nakedness" is literally "uncover his father's skirt," the corner of the garment a man spread over his wife (Ruth 3:9; Ezek 16:8). Reuben lost his birthright for sleeping with his father's concubine (Gen 35:22; 49:4), and Absalom did it publicly to claim David's throne (2 Sam 16:21–22). Paul was shocked to find a man in Corinth "has his father's wife," a sin "not tolerated even among pagans" (1 Cor 5:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The laws relate to the seventh commandment, laying a restraint upon fleshly lusts which war against the soul." He and JFB both handle the details briefly, and JFB says readers need not probe them too curiously. Guzik notes that the two-witness rule made executions for adultery rare, and that the Pharisees in John 8 brought only the woman. He reads vv25–27 as clear protection for the victim: the woman who could not be heard is presumed innocent. The Tyndale notes explain that the fifty shekels in v29 stood in for the bride price her father would have expected (Gen 34:12), and that the marriage assumed she and her father agreed (Exod 22:17).

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus summed up the law in two commands: love God, and love your neighbor as yourself (Matt 22:37–40). This chapter shows what loving a neighbor looks like at ground level: returning his donkey, building a parapet, refusing to slander his wife. When Jesus told of the Samaritan who did not "hide himself" from a man fallen by the road, he was asking who really kept this law. Jesus himself is that neighbor who came looking for strays: "the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10). And to a woman whom the law would have condemned, brought to him by men who were not keeping it themselves, he said, "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (John 8:11). He did not lower God's standard of faithfulness. He bore its penalty for those who had broken it, and he calls his church to be a bride kept pure for him (Eph 5:25–27).
