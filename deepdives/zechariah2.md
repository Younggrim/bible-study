*Setting:* On one night in February 519 BC, Zechariah received eight visions about God's plans for Jerusalem (1:7–6:8). The returned exiles had been rebuilding the temple for five months, prompted by Haggai and Zechariah (Hag 1:14–15). Jerusalem was still mostly rubble. Its walls would not be rebuilt for another seventy-five years, until Nehemiah came in 445 BC, and its population was small enough that Nehemiah later had to draw lots to move people into the city (Neh 7:4; 11:1). Chapter 1 gave the first two visions: God's scouts report that the nations are at ease while Jerusalem lies in ruins, God promises to return to Jerusalem with mercy, and four craftsmen come to break the horns of the nations that scattered Judah. Chapter 2 gives the third vision, a man with a measuring line, and then a poem calling the exiles still in Babylon to come home and Zion to rejoice because the LORD is coming to dwell in her. In Hebrew Bibles this chapter begins four verses earlier, with 1:18–21, so 2:1 in English is 2:5 in Hebrew.

#### A Vision of a Man with a Measuring Line (vv1–5)
- **"A man with a measuring line in his hand!" (v1).** A measuring line was a cord of flax used by builders and surveyors (Ezek 40:3). The vision follows from God's promise in 1:16: "the measuring line shall be stretched out over Jerusalem." It also recalls Ezekiel's long vision of a man measuring the future temple (Ezek 40–42), and in Revelation John is told to measure the temple and later sees the heavenly city measured (Rev 11:1; 21:15–17). The man is not explained. Some take him to be an angel, others the angel of the LORD from 1:11, and JFB identifies him with the Messiah.
- **"To measure Jerusalem, to see what is its width and what is its length" (v2).** The man seems to mean to mark out the city's limits, the line where its walls would go. That is a sensible plan for a ruined city in a hostile region.
- **"Run, say to that young man" (v4).** Another angel comes with an urgent message: stop the measuring. "That young man" may be the surveyor, eager but thinking too small. Or it may be Zechariah himself, who could then pass the message on. The Hebrew word (*na'ar*) can mean a youth or a servant. Either way, the point is that Jerusalem cannot be measured.
- **"Jerusalem shall be inhabited as villages without walls" (v4).** The Hebrew word (*perazot*) means open country towns, unwalled settlements (Ezek 38:11; Esth 9:19). Jerusalem will be so full "of people and livestock" that no wall could hold them. This promise looks far beyond Zechariah's day. Even after Nehemiah's walls went up, Jerusalem stayed small. Interpreters see a fulfilment in the growth of God's people in the gospel age, when the people of God spread far beyond any city's walls, and a final fulfilment in the new Jerusalem, whose gates are never shut (Rev 21:25). Isaiah foresaw the same overcrowding: "The place is too narrow for me; make room for me to dwell in" (Isa 49:19–20; 54:2–3).
- **"I will be to her a wall of fire all around" (v5).** Ancient cities depended on walls for safety. Jerusalem's had been torn down by Babylon in 586 BC. God promises to be her wall himself. Fire recalls the pillar of fire that guarded Israel in the wilderness (Exod 13:21; 14:19–20) and the horses and chariots of fire around Elisha (2 Kgs 6:17). "I will be" uses the emphatic form of the verb, which may echo God's name "I AM" (Exod 3:14).
- **"And I will be the glory in her midst" (v5).** Fire around, glory within. The glory that left the temple in Ezekiel's day (Ezek 10:18; 11:23) will return to dwell in the middle of the city. Protection is not enough. God promises his own presence.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the vision was given "not for his own entertainment, but for his satisfaction and the edification of those to whom he was sent." He reads it as promising that Jerusalem would be repopulated and enjoy safety and honor, and that God's church will always have room for whoever is brought in. JFB identifies the measuring man as the Messiah and the "young man" as Zechariah, still a youth. JFB sees a spiritual parallel in the church spreading beyond the boundaries of the old ceremonial law, and describes the wall of fire as one "which none durst approach." Guzik takes the measuring man to be an angel, possibly the angel of the LORD, and sees a near fulfilment under Ezra and Nehemiah and a final fulfilment under the Messiah's rule.

#### Up! Flee from the North (vv6–9)
- **"Up! Up! Flee from the land of the north" (v6).** The Hebrew begins *hoy, hoy*, an urgent "Ho!" or "Hey!" Babylon lay east of Judah, but the road from Babylon came down from the north, following the Euphrates and around the Arabian desert. So the prophets called Babylon "the north" (Jer 1:14; 6:22). Most Jews were still there. Only about 50,000 had come home with Zerubbabel (Ezra 2:64–65). Many had built houses and businesses in Babylon, as Jeremiah told them to (Jer 29:5–7), and they were comfortable. Zechariah urges them to come home.
- **"For I have spread you abroad as the four winds of the heavens" (v6).** God takes responsibility for scattering them. It was his judgment, not only Babylon's policy. The scattering went in every direction, as far as Egypt and Persia.
- **"Escape to Zion, you who dwell with the daughter of Babylon" (v7).** "Daughter of Babylon" is a poetic name for the city and its people (Ps 137:8; Isa 47:1). The call to escape repeats Isaiah 48:20 and Jeremiah 51:6: "Flee from the midst of Babylon." Babylon was not yet destroyed, but its time was short. It revolted against Darius twice in 522–521 BC and was harshly punished, and Xerxes crushed another revolt about 482 BC. In Revelation the same call, "Come out of her, my people" (Rev 18:4), is addressed to believers living in the world system that "Babylon" came to stand for.
- **"After his glory sent me to the nations who plundered you" (v8).** This is one of the hardest phrases in the book. The Hebrew is literally "after glory he sent me." There are three main readings. It may mean God sent the speaker "for the sake of glory," that is, to win glory for God by judging the nations. It may mean "with glory," with authority. Or it may mean "after the glory," following the glory that had appeared in the vision. Who is "me"? The speaker is sent by "the LORD of hosts" and yet speaks with God's own voice ("I will shake my hand," v9). Some think it is the prophet. Many Christian interpreters, including Henry and JFB, hear the angel of the LORD, the Son, sent by the Father.
- **"He who touches you touches the apple of his eye" (v8).** "The apple of the eye" is the pupil, the most sensitive and instinctively protected part of the body. The Hebrew word here (*babah*) is used only in this verse, and it may mean "the little gate" or "the little one" of the eye. The idea is familiar from Deuteronomy 32:10, where God kept Israel "as the apple of his eye," and Psalm 17:8. To strike God's people is to poke God in the eye. Jewish scribal tradition lists this verse among a few places where early scribes are said to have softened the wording out of reverence, changing "my eye" to "his eye." Either way the meaning is the same.
- **"I will shake my hand over them" (v9).** A wave of God's hand is enough. Isaiah uses the same gesture for God's judgment on Egypt and Assyria (Isa 11:15; 19:16). **"They shall become plunder for those who served them."** The tables will turn. Those who enslaved God's people will be stripped by their former slaves, as Egypt was "plundered" by the departing Israelites (Exod 12:35–36).
- **"Then you will know that the LORD of hosts has sent me" (v9).** The fulfilment of the word will prove the messenger true. The phrase recurs in v11, 4:9 and 6:15.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the summons to leave Babylon a sign of God's willingness to be reconciled, after the scattering that was his wrath. He says the speaker is Christ, sent by the Father "after the glorious beginning of their deliverance… to perfect it," and that the "apple of his eye" image shows how deeply God cares for his church. JFB identifies "the north" as Babylon, reads the speaker as "God the Son" commissioned by the Father, and calls the apple of the eye "the tenderest part of the eye." Guzik notes that most exiles were too comfortable in Babylon to take up the hard work of rebuilding, and that Babylon stands both for the literal city and for the world system. He explains the "apple of his eye" as something tender and easily hurt, which demands protection.

#### Sing and Rejoice, O Daughter of Zion (vv10–13)
- **"Sing and rejoice, O daughter of Zion, for behold, I come and I will dwell in your midst" (v10).** "Daughter of Zion" is a tender name for Jerusalem and its people (Isa 62:11; Zeph 3:14). The verb "dwell" (*shakan*) is the root of *mishkan*, "tabernacle," and the later Jewish word *Shekinah* for God's dwelling presence. God promises to come and live among his people, as he did in the tabernacle (Exod 25:8; 29:45). Zechariah will use almost the same call again when he foretells Jerusalem's king: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion!… Behold, your king is coming to you" (Zech 9:9).
- **"Many nations shall join themselves to the LORD in that day, and shall be my people" (v11).** This is one of the widest promises in the Old Testament. "My people" was the covenant name for Israel alone (Exod 6:7; Hos 1:9–10). Now it is given to "many nations." God's plan from the beginning was that all nations would be blessed through Abraham (Gen 12:3), and Isaiah saw foreigners "who join themselves to the LORD" welcomed into God's house (Isa 56:6–7). The verb "join" (*lavah*) is the same one in Isaiah 56. The returned exiles had just refused a mixed partnership with neighbors who worshiped other gods (Ezra 4:3). This promise is different: these nations come to the LORD himself and become his.
- **"And you shall know that the LORD of hosts has sent me to you" (v11).** The same puzzle as v8: the speaker is the LORD who dwells among them, and he is sent by the LORD of hosts. Christian readers have long seen here a glimpse of the Father sending the Son.
- **"The LORD will inherit Judah as his portion in the holy land" (v12).** God's people are his "portion" and inheritance (Deut 32:9). **"The holy land"** is a phrase found nowhere else in the Bible. The land is holy because the Holy One dwells there. **"And will again choose Jerusalem"** repeats 1:17. God's choice of Jerusalem, interrupted by the exile, is renewed.
- **"Be silent, all flesh, before the LORD" (v13).** The Hebrew word *has* is like "Hush!" Habakkuk (2:20) and Zephaniah (1:7) use it for the silence that falls when God appears. "All flesh" means all humanity, especially the proud nations who had been loud with their own plans.
- **"For he has roused himself from his holy dwelling" (v13).** God had seemed asleep or absent during the long years of exile (Ps 44:23). Now he has "roused himself" (the verb *ur*, "stir up," used of Cyrus and the returnees in Ezra 1:1, 5 and of the leaders in Hag 1:14). His "holy dwelling" is heaven (Deut 26:15). When God rises to act, the right response is silence.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v10 as having two fulfilments: God's presence in the rebuilt temple, and Christ's coming to dwell among the Jewish nation. He takes v13 as a caution not to dictate terms to God or place limits on him, but to wait patiently. JFB says the sending in vv9–11 shows that "Jehovah the Sender and Jehovah the Sent must be One," and notes that many Gentiles became proselytes in the centuries that followed, though he sees the full conversion of the nations as still future. Guzik notes that "holy land" appears only here, and that Israel was always meant to be a channel of blessing to the nations.

#### Pointing to Christ
Zechariah 2 promises a city too full for walls, guarded by God's fire and filled with his glory. It promises that the LORD will come to dwell among his people, that many nations will become his people, and that the one who speaks is sent by the LORD of hosts. Each strand meets in Jesus. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and the Greek verb (*skēnoō*, "to pitch a tent") recalls the tabernacle, God's dwelling place. He is the one sent by the Father (John 5:36–37; 17:3), who is also one with the Father (John 10:30), which is exactly the mystery v11 points to. Through him, Gentiles who were "not my people" have become "my people" (1 Pet 2:10; Rom 9:24–26), joined to the LORD in a city no surveyor can measure. And the final picture, the new Jerusalem, needs no sun, "for the glory of God gives it light" (Rev 21:23), the glory in her midst that Zechariah saw.
