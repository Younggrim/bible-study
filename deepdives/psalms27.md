*Setting:* "Of David." No occasion is named, and the guesses vary. Henry reports that some placed it in David's years as a fugitive before he was king, while Jewish tradition tied it to his old age, when a Philistine giant nearly killed him and his men swore he would never again go out to battle, "lest you quench the lamp of Israel" (2 Sam 21:16–17). Henry adds, wisely, that "perhaps it was not penned upon any particular occasion." The psalm falls into two halves so different in mood that some scholars think two psalms were joined. Verses 1–6 are bold confidence: no army can frighten a man whose God is his light. Verses 7–14 are urgent prayer: hear me, don't hide your face, don't hand me over. The Tyndale notes point out what holds the halves together, a longing for God's presence. Psalm 26 ended with David's love for "the habitation of your house" (26:8). Psalm 27 makes that love the "one thing" he asks for.

#### The LORD Is My Light and My Salvation (vv1–3)
- **"The LORD is my light and my salvation" (v1).** Light in the Old Testament is the picture of life, joy, guidance and God's favor (4:6; 36:9; 43:3). Boice, whom Guzik cites, notes that this is the only place in the Old Testament where "light" is used directly as a name for God, though the idea is everywhere. Isaiah later promises the Servant as "a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth" (Isa 49:6), and John opens his Gospel with the true light coming into the world (John 1:4–9). "Salvation" (*yesha*) means rescue, being brought out of danger into safety. It is the root of the name Jesus.
- **"Whom shall I fear?" (v1).** The questions are not doubts. They are a way of saying "no one" with more force. JFB: "The interrogations give greater vividness to the negation implied." Paul asks the same kind of question: "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31).
- **"The LORD is the stronghold of my life" (v1).** "Stronghold" (*ma'oz*) is a fortress, a place of refuge you can run into. The KJV's "strength" is possible, but most modern versions read "stronghold." Light, salvation, stronghold: each name implies the opposite. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, observes that the very names suggest darkness, danger and enemies all around. David is not describing a quiet life. He is describing who God is in a hard one.
- **"When evildoers assail me to eat up my flesh" (v2).** Enemies are pictured as wild animals tearing at a body (Job 19:22; Ps 14:4). Guzik recalls Goliath's threat to give David's flesh "to the birds of the air and to the beasts of the field" (1 Sam 17:44). Whether or not David has that day in mind, he has been here before.
- **"It is they who stumble and fall" (v2).** The Hebrew stresses "they." JFB: "not I, but they were destroyed." Henry notices that David does not say "I struck them down," only that they stumbled. He compares the soldiers in Gethsemane, who "drew back and fell to the ground" at a word from Jesus (John 18:6).
- **"Though an army encamp against me, my heart shall not fear" (v3).** From a few attackers to a whole army, from a raid to a war. The danger grows, and the confidence does not shrink. "Yet I will be confident" is the verb *batach*, "trust," the word Psalms 25:2 and 26:1 have just used.
- **Commentators:** Henry has a lovely note on v1. David's people called him "the light of Israel" (2 Sam 21:17), but David "owns that he shone, as the moon does, with a borrowed light." He adds: "If God be his light, he fears no shades." Guzik stresses that this is battle-tested faith, not the calm of someone who has never been in danger, and passes on Spurgeon's comment linking v2 to the arresting party falling back in Gethsemane. JFB reads "light" as "a common figure for comfort" and "strength" as "stronghold."

#### One Thing Have I Asked of the LORD (vv4–6)
- **"One thing have I asked of the LORD, that will I seek after" (v4).** A man surrounded by enemies might be expected to ask for a bigger army or a safer fortress. Henry makes exactly this point: David asks instead to be near God. Jesus said the same of Mary at his feet: "one thing is necessary" (Luke 10:42). Paul's version is "one thing I do" (Phil 3:13). Notice the two verbs: he has *asked* in prayer, and he will *seek after* it with his life.
- **"That I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life" (v4).** In David's time this was the tabernacle, the tent that housed the ark. David was not a priest and could not literally live there. He means to belong there, to be a regular, settled worshipper, not an occasional visitor. Psalm 23:6 ended with the same hope: "I shall dwell in the house of the LORD forever."
- **"To gaze upon the beauty of the LORD" (v4).** "Beauty" is *no'am*, delightfulness or pleasantness. The lexicon (BDB) gives this verse as "delightfulness of the LORD contemplated in temple." The same word is "favor" in 90:17 ("let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us"), and Proverbs 3:17 uses it of wisdom's "ways of pleasantness." David wants to look at God and enjoy him. Worship here is not first a duty. It is delight.
- **"And to inquire in his temple" (v4).** "Inquire" (*baqar*) is a rare verb, used seven times. The lexicon gives "seek," "consider" and, for this verse, "contemplate." Elsewhere a priest uses it of examining carefully (Lev 13:36), and God uses it of a shepherd searching out his sheep (Ezek 34:11–12). It may mean asking God for guidance, or it may mean reflecting long and carefully on him. Both fit. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, sees the two verbs as a pair: first perceiving God's beauty, then reflecting on it.
- **"Temple" for a tent?** "Temple" (*hekal*) means a palace or great house. It was already used of the tabernacle at Shiloh (1 Sam 1:9; 3:3). Its use here does not by itself prove the psalm is later than David.
- **"He will hide me in his shelter in the day of trouble" (v5).** "Shelter" is literally a booth or covert (*sok*, related to *sukkah*), the shelter of leafy branches used at the Feast of Booths. "The cover of his tent" is its most hidden part. In the ancient Near East, a guest who came under a host's tent was under his protection. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, calls it the privilege of asylum. Guzik adds that this is not a promise of no trouble, but of safety in it.
- **"He will lift me high upon a rock" (v5).** The picture changes from tent to rock, but the idea is the same: out of reach. "Lead me to the rock that is higher than I" (61:2).
- **"My head shall be lifted up above my enemies" (v6).** A raised head is the posture of a winner and of someone no longer ashamed (3:3). The bowed head of fear and shame is gone.
- **"Sacrifices with shouts of joy" (v6).** Literally "sacrifices of *teru'ah*," the word for a trumpet blast or a battle shout. These are thank offerings brought with loud celebration (Num 10:10). The man hidden in God's tent in v5 becomes the worshipper singing in that tent in v6.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls David "one of the greatest and best of kings that ever was," who "would gladly have taken his lodging" among the priests. He adds: "Do we hope that praising God will be the blessedness of our eternity? Surely then we ought to make it the business of our time." He reminds readers that the young king Joash was hidden in the house of the LORD for six years (2 Kings 11:3), and then adds that the safety of believers is "not in the walls of the temple, but in the God of the temple." JFB says the secret of David's confidence is "his delight in communion with God," beholding "the harmony of His perfections." Guzik passes on Boice's observation that in a few lines David reaches for four different words for God's house, and Spurgeon's reply to the poet Pope: the proper study of a Christian is God himself.

#### Hear, O LORD, When I Cry Aloud (vv7–10)
- **The mood turns (v7).** After six verses of confidence, David is suddenly pleading. "Hear… be gracious to me and answer me." This is not a loss of faith. Confident faith still has to pray, and the psalm is honest that the confident man of v1 is the same man crying aloud in v7. JFB says that "pressing need extorts prayer," and adds that people who are in earnest often pray out loud even when alone.
- **"You have said, 'Seek my face'" (v8).** The Hebrew is compressed and hard to translate. Literally it runs something like "To you my heart said, 'Seek my face.'" Most translations, the ESV included, take it as David's heart recalling God's invitation and answering it. JFB: "The meaning is clear, though the construction in a literal translation is obscure." To seek someone's face meant to come into their presence and seek their favor, like a subject before a king (105:4; 2 Chr 7:14).
- **"Your face, LORD, do I seek" (v8).** David's prayer starts from God's word, not his own feelings. God said "seek," so he seeks. Henry calls this "the best preaching": hearing "twice what God speaks once."
- **"Hide not your face from me" (v9).** In the priestly blessing, God's shining face means grace and peace (Num 6:25–26). A hidden face means God feels absent (13:1; 22:24). David pleads on the strength of the past: "O you who have been my help." Four negatives pile up: hide not, turn not, cast not, forsake not.
- **"Your servant" (v9).** David calls himself God's servant, the title of a loyal official to his king, and of Moses, and later of the Messiah in Isaiah.
- **"For my father and my mother have forsaken me, but the LORD will take me in" (v10).** The Hebrew can be read as a fact ("have forsaken") or a supposition ("though my father and mother forsake me"), and versions differ. We have no record of David's parents abandoning him. He did send them to Moab for safety while Saul hunted him (1 Sam 22:3–4), and Guzik wonders whether that separation lies behind the line. Either way the point is the same: the closest human love can fail, and God's will not (Isa 49:15). "Take me in" is *asaph*, "gather." It is the same verb as "sweep away" in 26:9: "Do not sweep my soul away with sinners." There David asked not to be gathered with the wicked. Here God gathers him in like an orphan brought home.
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures David reverently touching the golden scepter held out to him, like Esther before the king (Esth 5:2): it would be presumption to come uninvited, but God has invited. On v10 he writes that God's time to help "is when all other helpers fail," and that "God is a surer and better friend than our earthly parents are or can be." JFB links v10 to the poor and forsaken whom Christ takes in (Matt 25:35). Guzik notes that David does not turn against God when God seems to be hiding. He seeks him harder. He also passes on Boice's point that what we long for from a parent, acceptance, a hearing, guidance and protection, God gives, even to people who never received it at home.

#### Teach Me Your Way, O LORD (vv11–12)
- **"Teach me your way, O LORD" (v11).** The same prayer as 25:4: "Make me to know your ways, O LORD; teach me your paths." David does not only want rescue. He wants to be shown how to walk.
- **"Lead me on a level path because of my enemies" (v11).** "Level path" is the word *mishor*, level ground, the same word as the closing line of the previous psalm ("my foot stands on level ground," 26:12). It is not a request for an easy life but for a road without hidden pits. "Enemies" here is a rare word that JFB and Morgan (cited by Guzik) explain as "watchers," people lying in wait for him to slip (5:8). The more people are watching for a fall, the more a man needs a straight road.
- **"Give me not up to the will of my adversaries" (v12).** "Will" is literally *nephesh*, "soul," here meaning appetite or desire. His enemies hunger for him, which echoes the "eat up my flesh" of v2.
- **"False witnesses have risen against me" (v12).** In Israel's courts, two or three witnesses could condemn a man (Deut 19:15). The law forbade false witness (Exod 20:16) and ordered that a lying witness receive the punishment he tried to bring on another (Deut 19:18–19). David's enemies use lies as a weapon.
- **"They breathe out violence" (v12).** As if violence were the air they exhale, so full of it that it comes out with every breath. JFB compares Saul of Tarsus, "still breathing threats and murder" (Acts 9:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads "teach me thy way" as a prayer both to understand God's dealings and to know one's duty "in every doubtful case." JFB takes "will" as "soul" or "desire" and "enemies" as "oppressors," noting that "falsehood aids cruelty." Guzik stresses that the "level path" is a stable place to stand in the storm, not an escape from it.

#### Wait for the LORD (vv13–14)
- **"I believe that I shall look upon the goodness of the LORD in the land of the living!" (v13).** The Hebrew begins with a word, *lulē'*, that means "if not" or "unless," and the sentence breaks off. The KJV supplies the missing thought: "I had fainted, unless I had believed." In the standard Hebrew text the Masoretes, the scribes who copied and pointed it, marked this word with special dots above and below, a sign that they had doubts about it. Some ancient versions leave it out, and the ESV does too, giving a simple statement of faith. Either way the sense is close: faith in God's goodness is what keeps David from giving up. JFB takes the broken sentence as a sign of "strong emotion."
- **"The goodness of the LORD" (v13).** This echoes 23:6, "goodness and mercy shall follow me," and 25:7, "for the sake of your goodness, O LORD!" The Tyndale notes point out that "light" in v1 and "goodness" here belong together: God's light is his goodness shining on his people.
- **"In the land of the living" (v13).** Most naturally, in this life, before he dies (116:9; Isa 38:11). David expects to see God's goodness now, not only after death. Many Christian readers have also heard in it a hint of the life to come. Guzik passes on a Puritan reflection, quoted by Spurgeon, that this world is really the land of the dying, and that the true land of the living is where death has been swallowed up in victory. Both readings hold: God's goodness is seen in this life and finally in the next.
- **"Wait for the LORD" (v14).** The verb *qavah* probably first meant to twist or stretch, like the strands of a rope, and from there to wait with tension and expectation. It is the word of Isaiah 40:31: "they who wait for the LORD shall renew their strength." Waiting here is not passive. It is leaning forward, looking eagerly for God to act.
- **"Be strong, and let your heart take courage" (v14).** These are the words God and Moses spoke to Joshua before the conquest: "Be strong and courageous" (Deut 31:7; Josh 1:6–9). The man who began by saying "whom shall I fear?" now turns to others, or to his own soul, and says the same.
- **"Wait for the LORD!" (v14).** The psalm ends by repeating its command. The first "wait" is a call; the second drives it home.
- **Commentators:** Henry: even the best believers "are subject to faint when their troubles become grievous and tedious," but "faith is a sovereign cordial." He reads "the land of the living" first as this life, that David would "outlive his troubles." JFB says to "see" means to experience, and notes that the last clause is literally "and wait," as if expecting new help still to come. Guzik passes on Spurgeon: "Wait at his door with prayer; wait at his foot with humility; wait at his table with service; wait at his window with expectancy."

#### Pointing to Christ
John wrote that "in him was life, and the life was the light of men" (John 1:4), and Jesus said, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12). The God who was David's light and salvation came near in a person whose name means "the LORD saves." David longed to gaze on God's beauty in the tabernacle. In Jesus, "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us," literally "tabernacled," "and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). The rest of the psalm reads like a description of his last night. False witnesses rose up against him (Matt 26:59–61). The arresting soldiers drew back and fell to the ground (John 18:6). His friends abandoned him (Mark 14:50). And on the cross he knew what it was for the Father's face to be hidden: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). He waited on the LORD through death and saw God's goodness in the land of the living on the third day. Because he was forsaken, those who seek God's face in him are taken in, and will one day "see his face" (Rev 22:4).
