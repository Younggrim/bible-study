*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A Maskil of David, when the Ziphites went and told Saul, 'Is not David hiding among us?'" Like Psalm 52, the title takes us to David's years as a fugitive from King Saul, about 1010 BC. After the massacre at Nob, David moved with his small band of men into the wilderness of Judah, the dry, rocky hill country between Hebron and the Dead Sea. Ziph was a town in that region, a few miles southeast of Hebron (Josh 15:24, 55). The Ziphites were men of Judah, David's own tribe. Yet twice they went to Saul at Gibeah and offered to hand David over (1 Sam 23:19–20; 26:1). Saul blessed them for their "compassion" toward him and set out to hunt David down (23:21). The first time, Saul's army closed in on one side of a mountain while David's men fled down the other. At the last moment a messenger arrived: "Hurry and come, for the Philistines have made a raid against the land," and Saul turned back. The place was named "the Rock of Escape" (23:26–28). Just before this, Jonathan had come to David at Horesh and "strengthened his hand in God" (23:16). Guzik thinks the psalm fits the first betrayal best, written before the rescue came. It is one of the shortest laments in the Psalter, seven verses, with a clear turn in the middle: a cry (vv1–3), "Behold" (v4), and thanks (vv6–7).

#### O God, Save Me by Your Name (vv1–3)
- **"O God, save me by your name" (v1).** The prayer is short and urgent. "Your name" is not a magic word. It is who God has shown himself to be: his character, reputation and promises (Exod 34:5–7). David appeals to everything God is. Guzik passes on Maclaren's point that nothing less than the whole fullness of God revealed is enough for the needs of one man in trouble. JFB takes the name as standing for God's power.
- **"And vindicate me by your might" (v1).** "Vindicate" (*din*) is a court word: judge my case, plead my cause. David was being treated as a traitor and an outlaw (1 Sam 22:8). He does not take matters into his own hands. He asks God to rule in his favor and so show that he is innocent. David later said exactly this to Saul's face: "May the LORD therefore be judge and give sentence between me and you, and see to it and plead my cause" (1 Sam 24:15).
- **"O God, hear my prayer; give ear to the words of my mouth" (v2).** A double plea, both lines saying the same thing, as Hebrew poetry likes to do. Nothing is left but prayer. Boice, cited by Guzik, notes that at this point David had nowhere safe left to turn.
- **"For strangers have risen against me" (v3).** "Strangers" (*zarim*) usually means foreigners or outsiders. The Ziphites were not foreigners. They were fellow members of Judah. Their betrayal made them act like outsiders. The Tyndale notes say they had put themselves outside the covenant community. Henry: "They have used me more basely and barbarously than the Philistines themselves would have done."
- **A textual note.** Psalm 86:14 has an almost identical line: "insolent men (*zedim*) have risen up against me; a band of ruthless men seeks my life, and they do not set you before them." Some Hebrew manuscripts and the Aramaic paraphrase (Targum) read "insolent men" here too. In Hebrew script the two words differ only in one letter, *d* or *r*, which look very much alike. Both make sense, and either way they are arrogant men who have broken faith.
- **"Ruthless men seek my life" (v3).** "Ruthless" (*'aritsim*) means violent, terrifying, tyrants. JFB: "terrible ones," Saul and his army. "Seek my life" is not a figure of speech. Saul went out with three thousand men to find him (1 Sam 24:2; 26:2).
- **"They do not set God before themselves. Selah" (v3).** Here is the root of it all. Contrast David in 16:8: "I have set the LORD always before me." These men think only of Saul's favor and his reward. JFB: they act as practical atheists, "without fear of God." It is the fool of 53:1 again.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls out the Ziphites' eagerness: "How treacherous, how officious, were these Ziphim!" ("Officious" meant over-eager to serve.) Guzik, citing Spurgeon, says David sensed that godlessness lay behind their hostility, and, citing Horne, that their betrayal turned brothers into strangers. JFB reads "by thy name" as God's power and ties "judge me" to 7:8 and 26:1.

#### Behold, God Is My Helper (vv4–5)
- **"Behold, God is my helper" (v4).** This is the hinge of the psalm. "Behold" means look! Nothing in David's situation has changed yet. Saul is still coming. But David has stopped looking at the men around the mountain and looked at God. "Helper" (*'ozer*) is the word used of God helping Israel against its enemies (33:20; Deut 33:29), and it is also used of the woman God made for Adam (Gen 2:18). It does not mean someone lesser who assists. It means the one whose strength supplies what is lacking.
- **"The Lord is the upholder of my life" (v4).** The Hebrew is literally "the Lord is among those who uphold my life," which the King James renders "the Lord is with them that uphold my soul." It can mean God is the chief of David's supporters, or (as the ESV takes it) that God himself is his upholder. JFB: "on their side, and for me." Kidner, cited by Guzik, reads it as giving God the credit for every human help. David had real human helpers. His men stood by him. Just before this Jonathan came to him in the wilderness and "strengthened his hand in God" (1 Sam 23:16). Behind every friend, God was the one holding him up.
- **"He will return the evil to my enemies" (v5).** There is a small difference between the written Hebrew text and the way Jewish scribes read it aloud. The written text says "the evil will return to my enemies," and the scribes' reading says "he will return." Either way, the harm they planned comes back on their own heads, a frequent theme in the Psalms (7:15–16; 57:6). "Enemies" here is an unusual word meaning "those who watch me," people lying in wait to ambush (JFB: "watchers"). The Ziphites spied on David and reported his every move (1 Sam 23:22–23).
- **"In your faithfulness put an end to them" (v5).** "Faithfulness" (*'emet*) is God's truth, his reliability in keeping his word. JFB: God's "verified promise." God had promised David the throne (1 Sam 16:1, 13). If God is faithful, the men trying to kill David cannot win. "Put an end to them" means silence them, destroy them.
- **Praying against enemies.** Christians sometimes struggle with prayers like this, since Jesus said, "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matt 5:44). Guzik notes that David's prayer is for God to act, not for permission to act himself. And when David had the chance to kill Saul, twice, the second time right after the Ziphites' second betrayal, he refused (1 Sam 24:6; 26:9–11). Asking God to bring justice is not the same as taking revenge. JFB adds that David's satisfaction is not selfish, since these men are God's enemies too.
- **Commentators:** Henry on v4: "He that by his providence upholds all things by his grace upholds the souls of his people." Guzik, citing Kidner, honors the way the verse sees God's hand behind human help, and, through Spurgeon, Venema's sense that "cut them off" means a decisive blow. JFB renders the start of v5 "Evil shall return on" his enemies.

#### I Will Give Thanks to Your Name (vv6–7)
- **"With a freewill offering I will sacrifice to you" (v6).** A "freewill offering" (*nedavah*) was a sacrifice not required by any vow or law, given gladly, out of thanks (Lev 7:16; Num 15:3). It is the same root as the "willing spirit" of 51:12. David is not bargaining with God. He is promising an overflow of gratitude. The Tyndale notes connect it with Psalm 50:14: a sacrifice of praise is what God most desires. Henry: "All our spiritual sacrifices must, in this sense, be free-will-offerings; for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Cor 9:7).
- **"I will give thanks to your name, O LORD, for it is good" (v6).** The psalm began with "save me by your name" (v1) and ends with "give thanks to your name." The name he called on in trouble is the name he will praise in deliverance. Here, for the only time in the psalm, God's covenant name *YHWH* (LORD) appears, though this book of the Psalms mostly prefers "God." It is as though David reaches for God's most personal name in his thanks. "For it is good" echoes 52:9 word for word.
- **"For he has delivered me from every trouble" (v7).** David speaks as though the rescue had already happened. Some think he wrote this part after the escape at the Rock of Escape (1 Sam 23:28). Others, like Trapp in Guzik, call it the language of faith, speaking of what God will do as if it were already done (compare 52:9, "because you have done it"). The rescue, when it came, was not of David's making. God used a Philistine raid to call Saul away at the very moment the trap was closing.
- **"And my eye has looked in triumph on my enemies" (v7).** "In triumph" is added by the ESV to bring out the Hebrew idiom; literally, "my eye has looked upon my enemies." It means to see their defeat (92:11; 118:7). It is not gloating but relief at seeing that the danger has passed and God has kept his word. JFB again says this is not unholy, since David's enemies were God's enemies.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "A thankful heart, and the calves of our lips giving thanks to his name, are the sacrifices God will accept" ("the calves of our lips" is Hosea 14:2 in older translations: praise offered in place of bulls). Guzik notes that David praises God before the answer comes, and ties the deliverance to the Philistine raid of 1 Samuel 23:27–28. Guzik also sees David here as a picture of Christ, betrayed by some of his own people.

#### Pointing to Christ
David was betrayed by his own people, the men of his own tribe, who went to the king with the news of where he could be found. The Son of David knew the same thing. "He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him" (John 1:11). One of his own followers went to the authorities and told them where to find him (John 18:2–3), and the leaders of his own nation handed him over to the Romans. Like David, Jesus did not fight back but "continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23), praying "save me by your name, and vindicate me." And God did vindicate him. Jesus "was declared to be the Son of God in power… by his resurrection from the dead" (Rom 1:4), and he was "vindicated by the Spirit" (1 Tim 3:16). For his followers, v4 has become a promise to rely on: "So we can confidently say, 'The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me?'" (Heb 13:6).
