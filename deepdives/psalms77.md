*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Jeduthun. A Psalm of Asaph." Jeduthun was one of the three chief musicians David appointed to lead worship, alongside Asaph and Heman (1 Chr 16:41; 25:1–3), and "according to Jeduthun" probably names a tune or style linked to his guild (Pss 39 and 62 carry the same note). "Asaph" here likely means one of the singers in Asaph's line rather than David's musician himself. The psalm gives no date. Its sorrow sounds personal at first, but the answer it finds is national, the exodus, and many readers, Henry among them, have heard in it a believer grieving over the troubles of God's people, perhaps after Jerusalem's fall (Henry passes on a rabbi's remark that it is "spoken in the dialect of the captives"). Psalm 77 is one of the clearest pictures in Scripture of a believer climbing out of despair, not by feeling better, but by deliberately remembering what God has done. The first half is full of "I" and "my"; the second half is full of "you" and "your." Two *Selahs* and the turn at v10 mark the movement.

#### I Cry Aloud to God (vv1–3)
- **"I cry aloud to God, aloud to God, and he will hear me" (v1).** The repetition is a cry, not polished prose. Yet even here the psalmist expects to be heard. His trouble is not that he has stopped praying. It is that he keeps praying and the darkness stays.
- **"In the night my hand is stretched out without wearying" (v2).** Raised or outstretched hands were the posture of prayer (28:2; 143:6). The Hebrew says his hand "was poured out" or "flowed," an unusual picture of hands held out all night. "Without wearying" means it did not go numb or drop. JFB calls it "his importunacy." Night prayer is a theme in the Psalms (6:6; 63:6; 119:62), and here the night is the hardest time.
- **"My soul refuses to be comforted" (v2).** The same words describe Jacob mourning Joseph (Gen 37:35), and Rachel weeping for her children (Jer 31:15; Matt 2:18). Some grief is too deep for easy answers, and kind words bounce off. Henry: "he had no mind to hearken to those that would be his comforters."
- **"When I remember God, I moan" (v3).** A startling line. Thinking of God, which should bring comfort, only makes the pain worse, because the God he remembers seems far away or against him. "My spirit faints" means it grows weak and feeble, the word used of Jonah's life ebbing away in the deep (Jonah 2:7). **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that the psalmist knew God heard and was still troubled, and he compares the experience to Paul praying three times about his thorn (2 Cor 12:7–9). He adds that refusing shallow comfort is not always wrong, since some comfort is too thin for real grief. Henry: "When he remembered God his thoughts fastened only upon his justice, and wrath, and dreadful majesty." Henry adds that "days of trouble must be days of prayer."

#### The Search in the Night (vv4–9)
- **"You hold my eyelids open" (v4).** He blames his sleeplessness on God, honestly. He is so shaken that he "cannot speak," the point where prayer runs out of words (Rom 8:26).
- **"I consider the days of old, the years long ago" (v5).** He starts looking backward, but at first memory only hurts. **"Let me remember my song in the night" (v6)** recalls nights when he sang to God with joy (42:8; Job 35:10). Now the contrast makes things worse. Henry: "he was out of tune for them."
- **"My spirit made a diligent search" (v6).** The verb means to search carefully, as one would hunt through a house for something lost. He is doing theology in the dark, asking whether everything he believed still holds.
- **Six questions (vv7–9).** "Will the Lord spurn forever… never again be favorable? Has his steadfast love forever ceased? Are his promises at an end…? Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has he in anger shut up his compassion?" Each question tests one of God's revealed attributes, and several use the very words of God's self-description to Moses: "merciful and gracious… abounding in steadfast love" (Exod 34:6). "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. "Compassion" (*rachamim*) is related to the word for womb, a mother's tender love. To "shut up" compassion pictures a door closed or a spring stopped. The questions are honest, but they also answer themselves. To ask "Has God forgotten to be gracious?" is already to admit that being gracious is who he is. **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** Guzik treats these questions not as sinful unbelief but as the honest wrestling many saints have known. He cites Boice on Spurgeon's long battles with depression, and Spurgeon's remark that the footprints of sufferers like Asaph in the mud are a comfort to those who come after. Henry hears the psalmist reasoning with himself: "Come, my soul, what will be the issue of these things?" JFB sees v4 leading him "to express his anxious feelings in several earnest questions."

#### The Turn (v10)
- **"Then I said, 'I will appeal to this, to the years of the right hand of the Most High'" (v10).** This is the hinge of the psalm, and its Hebrew is famously hard. The word the ESV renders "I will appeal to this" can also be read "This is my grief" (or "my sickness"), and the word for "years" can be read as a form of "change." So the ESV footnote gives another reading: "This is my grief: that the right hand of the Most High has changed." The KJV has "This is my infirmity: but I will remember the years of the right hand of the most High."
- **Both readings lead the same way.** On the footnote reading, the psalmist names the deepest wound, that God's right hand, the hand that saved Israel (Exod 15:6, 12), seems to have changed sides, and then in v11 turns from that to remembering. On the ESV's main reading, he has already made his decision: he will lay his case before God by appealing to the years when God's right hand acted. Either way, the turning point is a choice to look at God's record instead of his own feelings.
- **Commentators:** Henry follows the KJV: "Despondency of spirit, and distrust of God, under affliction, are too often the infirmities of good people," and he says the psalmist checks himself by calling his fear a weakness, not the truth. Guzik reads the verse as the gap between what Asaph believed and what he felt, then underlines the "but I will remember" that follows. He cites Spurgeon comparing memory to Joseph opening the granaries in famine. JFB, more cautiously, takes "years" as parallel to the affliction, so that even the trial comes from the Most High's hand.

#### I Will Remember the Deeds of the LORD (vv11–15)
- **"I will remember… I will remember… I will ponder… and meditate" (vv11–12).** Four verbs of deliberate thought. Notice what he remembers now. Earlier he remembered God and his own past joy, and it hurt (vv3, 6). Now he remembers "the deeds of the LORD," the hard facts of what God has done. Faith is fed not by mood but by God's works. The words for "remember" and "ponder" can also mean to speak of them aloud, and Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes that remembering here includes telling.
- **The pronouns change.** From v13 on, the psalmist stops talking about God and starts talking to him: "your way… you are the God… you with your arm." Prayer moves from self-examination to worship.
- **"Your way, O God, is holy" (v13).** The Hebrew can be read "in holiness" or "in the sanctuary" (KJV: "Thy way, O God, is in the sanctuary"). Either way God's dealings are set apart, pure, beyond question. **"What god is great like our God?"** echoes the Song of the Sea: "Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?… majestic in holiness" (Exod 15:11).
- **"You are the God who works wonders" (v14).** Again Exodus 15:11, "doing wonders." The God whose questions filled vv7–9 is now addressed by his record.
- **"You with your arm redeemed your people" (v15).** God's arm is his strength in action. At the exodus he promised, "I will redeem you with an outstretched arm" (Exod 6:6). "Redeemed" (*ga'al*) is the word for a kinsman buying back a family member from slavery. **"The children of Jacob and Joseph"** names the whole nation. Joseph may be singled out because he preserved the family in Egypt, and because his sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, were the heart of the northern tribes, which fits an Asaphite psalm (Asaph's psalms often speak of Joseph, 80:1; 81:5). **Selah.**
- **Commentators:** Guzik lays out a three-step pattern for the discouraged: remember God's works, meditate on them, and speak of them to others. Henry on v13: "He has holy ends in all he does, and will be sanctified in every dispensation of his providence." JFB: "Jacob and Joseph—representing all." Guzik, citing Kidner, adds that both Jacob and Joseph insisted on being buried in the Promised Land, not Egypt (Gen 47:29–30; 50:24–25), so their names point to a hope beyond the house of slavery.

#### The Waters Saw You (vv16–20)
- **The rhythm changes.** Verses 16–19 move from two-line to three-line verses, and the poem rises to a thunderstorm. Many scholars think this section may draw on an older hymn of the exodus. It is close to Habakkuk 3:10–15 and to Psalm 18:7–15.
- **"When the waters saw you, O God… they were afraid; indeed, the deep trembled" (v16).** The sea is personified as a creature that sees God and shudders. In the ancient world the sea stood for chaos and threat, and the Canaanites made the sea a god (Yam). The Tyndale notes point out that the psalm puts it in its place: the sea is God's creature, and it trembles before him (114:3–5).
- **"The clouds poured out water; the skies gave forth thunder; your arrows flashed on every side" (v17).** The "arrows" are lightning bolts (18:14; Hab 3:11). Exodus says that in the night watch "the LORD in the pillar of fire and of cloud looked down… and threw the Egyptian forces into a panic" (Exod 14:24). The psalm fills in a storm that the narrative only hints at. The Jewish historian Josephus, cited by Guzik, also describes rain, thunder and lightning at the crossing. Whether this preserves a memory or is poetic coloring, the point is the same: the whole creation moved when God came to save.
- **"The crash of your thunder was in the whirlwind" (v18).** "Whirlwind" (*galgal*) means a wheel or a rolling thing, perhaps the rumble of thunder rolling, or the swirl of the storm. "The earth trembled and shook," like Sinai (Exod 19:18).
- **"Your way was through the sea… yet your footprints were unseen" (v19).** One of the most beautiful lines in the Psalms. God walked a road through the sea that no one else could walk, and when the waters closed again, there was no trace of his steps. The verse answers the night of vv1–9. God's ways are real even when they leave no marks we can follow. He is working when he cannot be seen (Rom 11:33; Isa 55:8–9). JFB: the footsteps "may refer to His actual leading the people through the sea," and also express "the mysteries of providence."
- **"You led your people like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron" (v20).** After the thunder, a shepherd. The God who shook the earth also led his sheep gently, and he did it through ordinary men. The psalm ends here, abruptly, without returning to the psalmist's own trouble. The ending is the answer. The one who led Israel through the sea has not changed (v10), and he still shepherds his people (Isa 63:11–14, which also remembers the shepherd who led them through the depths).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes there "was no mark set upon the place, as there was, afterwards, in Jordan" (Josh 4), and says of v20, "Moses was their governor, Aaron their high priest." He observes that the psalm "concludes abruptly, and does not apply those ancient instances of God's power" to the present, leaving the reader to draw the conclusion. Guzik, quoting Spurgeon, notes that the one who struck Egypt was the same one who shepherded Israel. His closing application is that God works both by great wonders and by ordinary human leaders, and one does not rule out the other.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 77 does not end with the psalmist's problem solved. It ends with a God who walks through deep waters where no footprints show, and who leads his flock like a shepherd. Christians read that line in the light of a greater exodus. Luke says Moses and Elijah spoke with Jesus about "his departure" (literally his *exodus*) that he would accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31). Jesus walked on the sea and calmed its storm (Mark 4:39–41; 6:48–51), doing what this psalm says only God does. On the cross he went down into the deepest night, crying aloud to God (Matt 27:46), and on the third day God's right hand raised him. Because of him the answer to every question of vv7–9 is no: his steadfast love has not ceased, his promises are not at an end, and the Good Shepherd still leads his flock (John 10:11).
