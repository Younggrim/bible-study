*Setting:* Isaiah 36–39 changes from poetry to prose. These four chapters tell the story of King Hezekiah in the crisis Isaiah has been prophesying about: Sennacherib's invasion of 701 BC (chs 36–37), Hezekiah's illness and recovery (ch 38), and the visit of envoys from Babylon (ch 39). The same account appears almost word for word in 2 Kings 18:13–20:19, with a shorter version in 2 Chronicles 32. The background is this. When Sennacherib became king of Assyria in 705 BC, Hezekiah joined a revolt, stopped paying tribute and looked to Egypt for support, against Isaiah's warnings (chs 30–31). In 701 Sennacherib marched west, crushed the coastal cities, beat an Egyptian army, and swept through Judah. Second Kings 18:14–16 adds what Isaiah leaves out: Hezekiah tried to buy him off with 300 talents of silver and 30 of gold, stripping the gold from the temple doors, and Sennacherib took it and came for Jerusalem anyway.

#### Sennacherib Invades Judah (vv1–22)
- **"In the fourteenth year of King Hezekiah" (v1).** That is 701 BC. The number raises a known puzzle, since 2 Kings 18:10 puts the fall of Samaria (722 BC) in Hezekiah's sixth year, which would make 701 his twenty-seventh. Scholars suggest several solutions: that Hezekiah served as co-regent with his father Ahaz before ruling alone, that the years are counted from different starting points, or that a number was miscopied. Faithful scholars disagree on the details, and none of them affects the story itself.
- **"Came up against all the fortified cities of Judah and took them" (v1).** Sennacherib's own record, written on clay prisms found at Nineveh (the best known is the Taylor Prism in the British Museum), boasts that he besieged and took forty-six of Hezekiah's walled cities and countless villages, carried off 200,150 people, and shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." Notice what he does *not* claim: that he took Jerusalem. The Bible and the Assyrian record agree on everything except the ending, and Assyrian kings did not record defeats.
- **"The king of Assyria sent the Rabshakeh from Lachish" (v2).** Lachish was Judah's second city, a heavily fortified town about 30 miles southwest of Jerusalem guarding the road from the coast. Sennacherib was so proud of taking it that he had the siege carved in stone panels along a whole room of his palace at Nineveh. Those reliefs, now in the British Museum, show siege ramps, battering rams, archers, and Judean families being led away while others are impaled. Excavations at Lachish have found the Assyrian siege ramp and a mass grave. **"The Rabshakeh"** is a title, not a name. It comes from Assyrian *rab shaqe*, literally "chief cupbearer," by this period a senior official. Second Kings 18:17 shows he came with two other top officers, the Tartan (commander in chief) and the Rab-saris.
- **"He stood by the conduit of the upper pool on the highway to the Washer's Field" (v2).** This is an intentional echo. Thirty-odd years earlier, Isaiah met King Ahaz at exactly this spot, "at the end of the conduit of the upper pool on the highway to the Washer's Field" (7:3), and called him to trust God instead of Assyria. Ahaz refused and bought Assyria's help instead (2 Kings 16:7–9). Now, at the very same place, the power Ahaz trusted stands at the gate. The Tyndale notes point out that the call to trust comes back, this time from mocking foreign lips. The location also mattered militarily: it was near the city's water supply.
- **Eliakim, Shebna and Joah (v3).** These are the king's three top officials. **Eliakim** is "over the household," the palace administrator, the office Isaiah said would be taken from Shebna and given to him (22:15–22). **Shebna** has been demoted to "secretary," or royal scribe, just as Isaiah warned. **Joah the son of Asaph** is "the recorder," probably the royal herald or spokesman.
- **"Thus says the great king, the king of Assyria" (v4).** "Great king" was the standard title of an emperor over lesser kings. The Rabshakeh deliberately never gives Hezekiah the title "king" (vv4, 14, 15, 16). He uses the prophets' formula, "Thus says," as if Sennacherib were a god speaking through his messenger.
- **"On what do you rest this trust of yours?" (v4).** This is the key word of the chapter. Forms of *batach*, "trust," come up seven times in vv4–15. The Rabshakeh's question is exactly Isaiah's question to Judah all along. He is right about some things and wrong about the one that matters.
- **"Do you think that mere words are strategy and power for war?" (v5).** Literally "a word of lips." He mocks Judah's confidence as talk.
- **"Egypt, that broken reed of a staff, which will pierce the hand of any man who leans on it" (v6).** Reeds grew thick along the Nile, and a dry one would split if leaned on and stab the hand. Here the enemy is completely right, and he is saying what Isaiah had said (30:1–7; 31:1–3). Ezekiel later uses the same picture of Egypt (Ezek 29:6–7).
- **"Is it not he whose high places and altars Hezekiah has removed?" (v7).** This is clever but wrong. Hezekiah had torn down the local shrines where Israel had worshiped, often mixing in pagan practices, and centered worship at the one temple in Jerusalem, as the law required (Deut 12:5–14; 2 Kings 18:4, 22). To a polytheist, closing a god's shrines was an insult to him. Some people in Judah may have felt the same. The Rabshakeh tries to persuade them that their reform had angered the LORD. In fact it was obedience.
- **"I will give you two thousand horses, if you are able on your part to set riders on them" (v8).** A taunt: Judah does not have enough trained cavalry to use 2,000 horses even if they were given to them. Assyria had the most advanced cavalry of the age. Isaiah's answer is already on record: "Woe to those who… rely on horses" (31:1).
- **"The LORD said to me, 'Go up against this land and destroy it'" (v10).** This is the most unsettling claim. And there is a grain of truth in it, because Isaiah himself had said God would use Assyria as "the rod of my anger" (10:5–6). But Isaiah also said Assyria did not intend to serve God, only "to destroy" (10:7), and that God would punish its arrogance (10:12). Being used by God is not the same as being approved by God. Henry: "It is an easy thing, and very common, for those that persecute the church and people of God to pretend a commission from him."
- **"Please speak to your servants in Aramaic… Do not speak to us in the language of Judah" (v11).** Aramaic was the international language of diplomacy and trade across the Assyrian Empire, understood by officials but not by ordinary Judeans. "The language of Judah" is Hebrew, the people's everyday speech. The officials want to keep the negotiation away from the soldiers and citizens listening on the wall.
- **"Doomed with you to eat their own dung and drink their own urine" (v12).** The Rabshakeh refuses. His real audience is the people, and he paints in the crudest terms what a long siege means: starvation so severe that people eat filth. It was not an exaggeration (2 Kings 6:25). This is psychological warfare, meant to split the people from their king.
- **"Do not let Hezekiah deceive you… Do not let Hezekiah make you trust in the LORD" (vv14–15).** He attacks the people's trust in both their king and their God. Strikingly, he quotes what Hezekiah had been telling them: "The LORD will surely deliver us" (2 Chr 32:7–8 records Hezekiah's speech to the commanders: "With him is an arm of flesh, but with us is the LORD our God"). The Tyndale notes contrast Hezekiah's faith with Ahaz's unbelief in chapter 7.
- **"Each one of you will eat of his own vine, and each one of his own fig tree" (v16).** This is a phrase from Israel's own promises of peace under God's blessing (1 Kings 4:25; Mic 4:4). The enemy offers God's blessing without God. **"Make your peace with me"** is literally "make a blessing with me."
- **"Until I come and take you away to a land like your own land" (v17).** He cannot hide the catch. Assyria's policy was mass deportation, moving conquered peoples across the empire to break their national identity. Samaria's people had suffered exactly this twenty years earlier (2 Kings 17:6, 24). He dresses exile up as a relocation package. The Tyndale notes call it a counterfeit of what the LORD promised his obedient people (1:19).
- **"Where are the gods of Hamath and Arpad? Where are the gods of Sepharvaim? Have they delivered Samaria out of my hand?" (v19).** Hamath and Arpad were Syrian cities Assyria had crushed (10:9). People from Hamath and Sepharvaim had been resettled in Samaria after its fall (2 Kings 17:24). His logic is simple: no god has stopped Assyria, so why would Judah's? But now he has gone too far. He has put the LORD on the same level as the idols, and that is where the story turns. In the next chapter Hezekiah's prayer and Isaiah's answer both focus on this blasphemy (37:4, 17, 23).
- **"They were silent and answered him not a word, for the king's command was, 'Do not answer him'" (v21).** The wisest response to the speech was silence. There was nothing to gain from a shouting match, and the battle was not theirs to win with words. Henry: "It is sometimes prudent not to answer a fool according to his folly" (Prov 26:4).
- **"With their clothes torn" (v22).** Tearing clothes was the sign of grief and horror, especially at blasphemy (Matt 26:65). The three officials return to the king, and the next chapter begins with Hezekiah himself tearing his clothes and going to the temple.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the event "was so memorable an event that it was well worthy to be twice recorded," and he sees the Rabshakeh's whole method as an attempt to "conquer them by frightening them." Guzik draws a parallel between the Rabshakeh's tactics and the devil's: mixing truth with lies, using real facts (Egypt was unreliable) to push people toward despair rather than faith, and making surrender look attractive. JFB notes the Rabshakeh's self-contradiction. First he claims to come with the LORD's command (v10), then he lumps the LORD in with the idols (v20): "Liars need good memories." JFB also explains Sepharvaim as possibly "the two scribes," but that is an old guess, and the city's identity and the meaning of its name are uncertain.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Rabshakeh's voice is the voice of every temptation to give up on God: your help is weak, your God may be against you, you're surrounded, and surrender looks comfortable. Jesus faced the same voice in the wilderness and on the cross, where onlookers mocked, "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (Matt 27:43). Like Hezekiah's men, Jesus did not answer his accusers (Isa 53:7; 1 Pet 2:23). He entrusted himself to the Father, and the Father did not fail him. The question "On what do you rest this trust of yours?" has its answer in the one who was raised from the dead.
