*Setting:* Jerusalem, 622–609 BC. In Josiah's eighteenth year, while the temple was being repaired, Hilkiah the high priest found "the Book of the Law" (22:8). When it was read to him, Josiah tore his clothes, because Judah had ignored it for generations. The prophetess Huldah confirmed that the curses written in it would fall on Jerusalem, but promised that Josiah himself would die before the disaster came (22:15–20). Assyria, which had dominated the region for a century, was collapsing: Babylon would take Nineveh in 612 BC. That left Josiah free to act, even in the old northern kingdom. This chapter tells what he did with the book, and then how quickly everything fell apart after him. 2 Chronicles 34–35 tells the same story with more detail on the Passover and the battle.

#### Josiah's Reforms (vv1–20)
- **"All the elders… all the people, both small and great" (vv1–2).** Josiah does not keep the book to his court. He gathers the whole nation, including "the priests and the prophets." Jeremiah, who began preaching five years earlier (Jer 1:2), may well have been in the crowd. Deuteronomy told Israel to read the law aloud to everyone, men, women, children and foreigners (Deut 31:10–13), and Joshua did exactly that (Josh 8:34–35). The Tyndale notes point to both.
- **"He read in their hearing" (v2).** The king himself reads. Deuteronomy pictured the king as a man with his own copy of the law, reading it "all the days of his life" (Deut 17:18–19). "The Book of the Covenant" here is the same scroll found in 22:8, called by what it is: the terms of the relationship between God and his people.
- **"The king stood by the pillar and made a covenant" (v3).** The pillar was the king's place in the temple court. Joash was crowned standing there (11:14). Covenant renewals mark turning points all through Israel's history: Joshua at Shechem (Josh 24), Solomon at the dedication (1 Kings 8), Jehoiada with the boy Joash (11:17). "With all his heart and all his soul" echoes the great command of Deuteronomy 6:5.
- **"All the people joined in the covenant" (v3).** Literally "stood to the covenant." They took their stand with the king. Whether their hearts went as far as their feet is the question the rest of the chapter, and Jeremiah, will answer.
- **Cleaning the temple first (v4).** The cleansing starts at the center. "The vessels made for Baal, for Asherah, and for all the host of heaven" had been sitting in the LORD's own house. Josiah burns them in the Kidron Valley, east of the city, and carries the ashes to Bethel. Bethel was the site of Jeroboam's rival altar (1 Kings 12:28–29). Dumping pagan ashes there defiled it for good.
- **"He deposed the priests" (v5).** The word is *kemarim*, which the lexicon gives as "idol-priests." In the Old Testament it is used only for priests of false gods (also Hos 10:5; Zeph 1:4, where Zephaniah, preaching in Josiah's day, names them too). These were not Levites but men the kings of Judah had appointed. "The constellations" is probably the signs of the zodiac. Star worship came in with Assyrian influence under Manasseh (21:3–5).
- **The Asherah burned and its dust thrown "upon the graves of the common people" (v6).** Manasseh had set up a carved Asherah pole, the symbol of a Canaanite mother goddess, inside the temple (21:7). Josiah burns it, grinds it to powder, and scatters it on a common burial ground. Graves were unclean under the law (Num 19:16), so the idol ends as defilement on top of defilement.
- **"The houses of the male cult prostitutes… in the house of the LORD" (v7).** The Tyndale notes call it what it was: shocking. Canaanite fertility religion used ritual sex, and it had been happening in rooms inside the temple precincts, where women also wove "hangings" for the Asherah. JFB thinks the "houses" were tents. Earlier kings had tried to clear these men out (1 Kings 15:12; 22:46). They kept coming back.
- **"From Geba to Beersheba" (v8).** That is Judah from its northern border to its southern edge. Josiah brings the country priests into Jerusalem and defiles their local shrines. "The gate of Joshua the governor of the city" appears only here. It was probably a city official's gate, not the famous Joshua.
- **"The priests of the high places did not come up to the altar" (v9).** These priests were probably Levites who had served at local shrines. Deuteronomy allowed a country Levite to serve in Jerusalem (Deut 18:6–8), but these men had worshiped wrongly, so they could not serve at the altar. Josiah still let them eat the priests' share of unleavened bread among their brothers. He removed them from office without throwing them out of the family.
- **"He defiled Topheth… that no one might burn his son or his daughter as an offering to Molech" (v10).** Topheth was in the Valley of Hinnom, on Jerusalem's southwest side, where children had been burned as sacrifices. Ahaz and Manasseh both did this to their own sons (16:3; 21:6). Jeremiah later says the valley will be renamed "the Valley of Slaughter" (Jer 7:31–32). In Hebrew the valley is *Ge Hinnom*, which came into Greek as *Gehenna*, the word Jesus used for hell (Mark 9:43). JFB passes on the old rabbinic idea that the name came from *toph*, "drum," beaten to drown out the children's cries. That is a tradition, not something the text says.
- **"The horses that the kings of Judah had dedicated to the sun… the chariots of the sun" (v11).** Across the ancient Near East the sun god was pictured driving a chariot across the sky, and the Tyndale notes say archaeology points to sun worship in Israel well before this. Ezekiel, a generation later, still sees men in the temple court with their backs to the sanctuary, bowing to the sun in the east (Ezek 8:16). The ESV footnotes that the word for "precincts" is uncertain.
- **Rooftop altars and Manasseh's altars (v12).** Flat roofs were used for offerings to the stars (Jer 19:13; Zeph 1:5). Even Manasseh's late repentance had only gone so far: Chronicles says he removed these altars (2 Chr 33:15), but here they are again, and Josiah smashes them for good.
- **"The mount of corruption" (v13).** This is the southern end of the Mount of Olives, facing the temple. The lexicon notes that *har hammashchit*, "mount of destruction," is probably a deliberate wordplay on *har hammishchah*, "mount of anointing" (from the olive oil). For more than three hundred years Solomon's shrines to Ashtoreth, Chemosh and Milcom had stood there (1 Kings 11:5–7). No king, not even Hezekiah, had removed them. Josiah does.
- **"Filled their places with the bones of men" (v14).** Human bones made a place permanently unclean. No one would rebuild a shrine on a graveyard.
- **Bethel (v15).** Josiah now crosses into the old northern kingdom, which Assyria had held since 722 BC. With Assyria failing, no one stopped him. Jeroboam's altar, "who made Israel to sin," the refrain of all of Kings, is pulled down and burned. The ESV notes that the Greek translation reads "broke in pieces its stones."
- **The bones on the altar (v16).** Here a prophecy made three centuries earlier comes true to the letter. A man of God from Judah stood at this altar while Jeroboam offered incense and cried: "a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name, and he shall sacrifice on you the priests of the high places… and human bones shall be burned on you" (1 Kings 13:2). JFB calls it "one of the most remarkable prophecies in the Bible." The narrator says it plainly: "according to the word of the LORD."
- **"Let him be; let no man move his bones" (vv17–18).** The men of Bethel still remember whose tomb it is. That prophet himself had asked to be buried beside the man of God (1 Kings 13:31–32), and now the bones of both men are spared. The word of God honors the man who spoke it, even though he died for disobeying it (1 Kings 13:24).
- **"He sacrificed all the priests of the high places who were there" (v20).** This is harsher than what Josiah did in Judah (v9). These northern priests ran a rival cult that had never been part of the LORD's worship, and Deuteronomy commands death for those who lead the people to other gods (Deut 13:5; 17:2–5). It also fulfills 1 Kings 13:2 word for word. Modern readers recoil at it, and it is right to see it as the old covenant's severe judgment, not a model for Christian practice.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as Josiah "rooting out idols and idolatry, with all the relics thereof, in all places" as far as his power reached. Guzik notes that the cleansing began at the temple and worked outward, and that Josiah dealt with the priests who promoted the idols, not only the idols. He also credits the people's response in v3 to God's Spirit moving on a crowd. JFB says the ashes were taken to Bethel "to associate ideas of horror" with the place, and that the men of Bethel in v17 were Israelites who remembered the story, "not the Assyrian colonists" brought in after the exile of 722 BC (17:24).

#### Josiah Restores the Passover (vv21–27)
- **"Keep the Passover… as it is written in this Book of the Covenant" (v21).** The reform ends with a feast, not just a demolition. Passover remembered the night God brought Israel out of Egypt (Exod 12), and Deuteronomy said it must be kept "at the place that the LORD will choose" (Deut 16:1–8), which is why Josiah holds it in Jerusalem.
- **"No such Passover had been kept since the days of the judges" (v22).** Hezekiah held a great Passover about a century earlier (2 Chr 30), but it was a month late and many who ate it were ceremonially unclean (2 Chr 30:2–3, 18). Josiah's kept the book exactly. Chronicles gives the details: thousands of animals, the Levites organized by family, the singers in their places (2 Chr 35:1–19). The Tyndale notes draw the same contrast.
- **"Mediums and the necromancers and the household gods" (v24).** Josiah goes after private, household religion as well as public shrines. Manasseh had practiced all of this himself (21:6). The "household gods" (*teraphim*) were small figures kept at home, like the ones Rachel stole from Laban (Gen 31:19).
- **"Before him there was no king like him… nor did any like him arise after him" (v25).** This is the highest praise in Kings. Hezekiah was praised for trust (18:5); Josiah is praised for obedience. The words "with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might" are the great command of Deuteronomy 6:5, and Josiah is the only person in the Bible of whom they are said.
- **"Still the LORD did not turn from the burning of his great wrath" (v26).** This is the hardest verse in the chapter. The best king Judah ever had, after the most thorough reform ever carried out, and judgment is still coming. The reason given is "all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him." Manasseh's sin was not only his own. The nation followed him gladly for fifty-five years, and Jeremiah shows the change under Josiah did not go deep. God said of Judah in Josiah's day that she "did not return to me with her whole heart, but in pretense" (Jer 3:10). One king's heart could not stand in for a nation's.
- **"I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel" (v27).** God does not deny that he chose Jerusalem and put his name there (1 Kings 8:29). He says the choice does not protect a people who will not keep the covenant. Jeremiah preached the same thing at the temple gate: do not trust in the words "the temple of the LORD" (Jer 7:4).
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls Josiah's reform an Old Testament picture of putting Scripture above everything, but stresses that it stayed largely outward, and cites Jeremiah 3:10 for the people's pretense. He also notes that a king can command the feast but not the heart. Henry agrees: the people "were turned by force" and kept their love for idols. JFB simply repeats the verdict that "the Lord turned not from the fierceness of his wrath," and offers no separate notes on vv24–25 or v27.

#### Josiah's Death in Battle (vv28–30)
- **"Pharaoh Neco king of Egypt went up to the king of Assyria" (v29).** The year is 609 BC. Nineveh had fallen, and the last Assyrian king was holding out at Haran, in northern Mesopotamia. Egypt now wanted Assyria alive as a buffer against Babylon, so Neco marched north to help. The KJV reads "against the king of Assyria," and older commentators followed that. The Babylonian Chronicle has since shown that Egypt was Assyria's ally, and the Hebrew preposition can mean "to" or "toward." Modern translations follow the evidence.
- **"King Josiah went to meet him" (v29).** Kings gives no reason. Josiah may have wanted to keep Assyria from recovering, or to stop Egypt controlling the road through his land. Chronicles adds that Neco warned him, "God has commanded me to hurry… Cease opposing God," that Josiah "did not listen to the words of Neco from the mouth of God," and that he disguised himself and was hit by archers (2 Chr 35:21–23).
- **"At Megiddo" (v29).** Megiddo guarded the pass where the coastal road crossed into the Jezreel Valley, one of the great battlefields of the ancient world. Its Hebrew name lies behind "Armageddon," *Har-Megiddo* (Rev 16:16). Zechariah later uses the mourning for Josiah, "the mourning for Hadad-rimmon in the plain of Megiddo," as a picture of Israel mourning "him whom they have pierced" (Zech 12:10–11).
- **"Buried him in his own tomb" (v30).** Huldah had said he would be gathered to his grave "in peace" and not see the disaster (22:20). He died in battle, but he was buried in Jerusalem with honor before any foreign army entered the city. Chronicles adds that Jeremiah wrote laments for him and singers still sang them years later (2 Chr 35:25).
- **"The people of the land took Jehoahaz" (v30).** "The people of the land," the landowning men of Judah, had put Josiah himself on the throne (21:24). Now they skip his older son and choose Jehoahaz, probably because he would continue his father's anti-Egyptian line.
- **Commentators:** Henry is blunt: "We cannot justify Josiah's conduct," since he had no call to fight and did not seek God. He also sees mercy in it, since Josiah was spared the sight of what came next, and judgment, since the people learned his worth only by losing him. Guzik is just as critical, drawing on Chronicles: the warning came from God through Neco, and Josiah ignored it. He locates the mourning of Zechariah 12:11 at this battle.

#### Jehoahaz's Reign and Captivity (vv31–35)
- **"Twenty-three years old… three months" (v31).** His personal name was Shallum (1 Chr 3:15; Jer 22:11). Jehoahaz, "the LORD has grasped," was the name he took as king. His mother Hamutal was from Libnah, a town in the lowlands. Her father "Jeremiah" is not the prophet, who came from Anathoth and never married (Jer 16:2).
- **"He did what was evil" (v32).** Three months is long enough for the verdict. Josiah's reform did not pass to his sons. The Tyndale notes observe that the same verdict falls on every one of them (vv32, 37; 24:9, 19).
- **"Pharaoh Neco put him in bonds at Riblah" (v33).** Riblah was on the Orontes River in Syria, a military base where great powers summoned their vassals. Nebuchadnezzar later uses the same place to pass sentence on Zedekiah (25:6). The tribute was a hundred talents of silver and one of gold. The ESV footnote puts a talent at about 75 pounds, so about three and three-quarter tons of silver.
- **"He came to Egypt and died there" (v34).** Jeremiah wrote of him: "Weep bitterly for him who goes away, for he shall return no more" (Jer 22:10–12). He is the first king of Judah to die in exile.
- **Eliakim renamed Jehoiakim (v34).** Renaming a vassal king showed who was in charge, and the Tyndale notes say this followed Assyrian practice. Neco does not even change the god in the name: Eliakim, "God raises up," becomes Jehoiakim, "the LORD raises up." The point is that Pharaoh did the naming.
- **"He taxed the land… from everyone according to his assessment" (v35).** The king pays Egypt by squeezing his people. Jeremiah adds that while they paid, Jehoiakim built himself a fine palace with unpaid forced labor (Jer 22:13–17).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Shallum was a throne-named younger son chosen over the custom, and that Jehoahaz is missing from Matthew's genealogy of Jesus. Henry passes quickly over "the bad reigns of his two sons." JFB has no notes on vv30–37 at all.

#### Jehoiakim Reigns in Judah (vv36–37)
- **"Twenty-five years old… eleven years" (v36).** About 609–598 BC. He was older than Jehoahaz, so the people had passed him over the first time. His mother Zebidah was from Rumah, a town that may have been in Galilee; if so, Josiah had married into the north.
- **"He did what was evil" (v37).** Kings gives one line. Jeremiah fills it in: Jehoiakim had the prophet Uriah hunted down in Egypt and killed (Jer 26:20–23). When Jeremiah's scroll was read to him, he cut it up column by column and threw it into the fire (Jer 36:23). His father had torn his clothes when he heard God's word (22:11). The son tore up the word itself.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws the same contrast from Jeremiah 22 and 36: forced labor for his palace, and a scroll burned in the brazier. Henry gives no separate comment on these verses.

#### Pointing to Christ
Josiah is the best of the kings, the only one who loved the LORD with all his heart, soul and might, and it was not enough. He cleaned the temple, kept the Passover and fulfilled an ancient prophecy, but he could not change the hearts of his people, and he died at Megiddo with judgment still coming. His sons undid his work in months. The chapter shows the limit of every good king and every outward reform. What Judah needed was a King who would keep the great command perfectly and also write God's law on his people's hearts (Jer 31:33). Jesus kept the Passover in Jerusalem and then became the Passover lamb (1 Cor 5:7). Zechariah took the mourning for Josiah and turned it into a picture of a nation mourning "him whom they have pierced." That mourning leads to a fountain opened "to cleanse them from sin" (Zech 13:1).
