*Setting:* The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to Jewish Christians worn down by pressure and tempted to drift back to the old ways, has spent chapter 7 on Melchizedek. Jesus is a priest of a different order from Aaron's: appointed by God's oath, holding his priesthood "permanently, because he continues forever" (7:24), able "to save to the uttermost" (7:25), and the "guarantor of a better covenant" (7:22). Chapter 8 pauses to say what all of that adds up to, then opens a new stage of the argument that runs to 10:18: where this high priest serves, what he offers, and under what covenant. Its centre is the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament, Jeremiah 31:31–34, the promise of a new covenant. If the temple in Jerusalem was still standing when the sermon was written, as most scholars think, its priests were still offering sacrifices every day, and that gives the chapter's last line its edge.

#### Jesus, High Priest of a Better Covenant (vv1–13)
- **One section, two movements.** The ESV keeps the whole chapter under one heading. Verses 1–6 say where Christ serves and why his ministry is better. Verses 7–13 prove from Scripture that God himself promised to replace the first covenant.
- **"Now the point in what we are saying is this" (v1).** "Point" is *kephalaion*, from *kephalē*, "head." The lexicon gives "the chief point" for this verse; the same word means a sum of money in Acts 22:28, which is why the KJV has "sum." The writer is not just summing up chapter 7. He is putting his finger on the heart of the matter.
- **"We have such a high priest" (v1).** Not "we used to have priests and lost them." The hearers may have felt that leaving the temple meant losing their priests, their altar and their access to God. The writer's answer is that they have a better high priest than the temple ever had.
- **"Seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven" (v1).** This picks up the opening of the sermon (1:3) and Psalm 110:1, the psalm that also gave the Melchizedek promise (Ps 110:4). No Levitical priest ever sat down in the tabernacle; there was no chair in it. Their work was never finished (10:11). Christ sits, because his offering is done (10:12), and he sits on a throne, so he is king and priest at once, as Melchizedek was.
- **"A minister in the holy places" (v2).** "Minister" is *leitourgos*, a public servant. In classical Greek it described a citizen who carried out a public duty at his own expense; in the Greek Old Testament it is used for those who serve in God's house. "Liturgy" comes from the same family. Christ is still serving on his people's behalf (7:25).
- **"The true tent that the Lord set up, not man" (v2).** "True" here means real as opposed to a copy, not true as opposed to false. Moses' tent was not a lie, but it was a model. The real place where God dwells is heaven itself (9:24). The contrast "the Lord, not man" echoes the way Jesus spoke of a temple "not made with hands" (Mark 14:58).
- **"It is necessary for this priest also to have something to offer" (v3).** This repeats 5:1, where every high priest offers "gifts and sacrifices for sins." A priest with nothing to offer is no priest. The writer does not say here what Christ offered. He has already said it once (7:27, "he offered up himself") and will unfold it in chapters 9–10. The tense of "offer" in v3 points to a single act, not an ongoing series.
- **"If he were on earth, he would not be a priest at all" (v4).** Jesus was from Judah, not Levi (7:13–14), so under the Law he could never have served at the altar. The present tense, "there are priests who offer gifts," suggests the temple service was still going on as the writer spoke. His priesthood belongs to a different place as well as a different order.
- **"A copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (v5).** "Copy" is *hypodeigma*, a sketch or figure; "shadow" is *skia*, the outline a thing casts, real enough to show its shape but without its substance. Colossians 2:17 uses the same picture: the Law's rituals are "a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ." A shadow is not bad; it means something real is near.
- **"According to the pattern that was shown you on the mountain" (v5).** The quotation is Exodus 25:40, said to Moses on Sinai as he received the plans for the tabernacle (compare Exod 25:9; 26:30; Acts 7:44). "Pattern" in the Greek Old Testament is *typos*, the root of "type." The tabernacle was built from a heavenly blueprint, which is why its details mattered so much and why it could point beyond itself.
- **Reading "copy and shadow."** Some readers hear Plato here, with heaven as the world of perfect forms and earth as its shadow. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish thinker of the same century, read the tabernacle that way. Most scholars think Hebrews is closer to the Old Testament and Jewish hope than to Plato. Its "shadow" is mainly about time: the earthly sanctuary looked ahead to what Christ would do (10:1, "a shadow of the good things to come"). Jewish writers of the period, and Revelation (Rev 11:19; 15:5), also speak of a temple in heaven. Christians differ on how literally to picture a heavenly sanctuary, but all agree the point is the presence of God himself.
- **"A ministry that is as much more excellent… as the covenant he mediates is better" (v6).** The priest and the covenant rise and fall together. A better priest serves a better covenant, and a better covenant needs a better priest. "Better" is one of the sermon's key words (1:4; 7:19, 22; 9:23; 11:40).
- **"The covenant he mediates" (v6).** A mediator (*mesitēs*) stands between two parties to bring them together. Moses stood between God and Israel at Sinai (Gal 3:19). Jesus is "the one mediator between God and men" (1 Tim 2:5), and he can be, because he is both. In 7:22 he was the "guarantor," the one who stands surety that the covenant will be kept. Here he is the go-between who establishes it.
- **"Enacted on better promises" (v6).** "Enacted" is a legal word, used of laws being passed. The better promises are spelled out in vv10–12: an inward change, a personal knowledge of God and full forgiveness. The old covenant promised much, including land and blessing, but it could not change the heart of the people who received it.
- **"If that first covenant had been faultless" (v7).** "Faultless" (*amemptos*) means blameless, without defect. The writer's argument is simple and very Jewish: God does not plan a replacement for something that is already complete. The same logic appeared in 7:11 ("if perfection had been attainable through the Levitical priesthood…") and in 4:8 (if Joshua had given rest, God would not have spoken of another day).
- **"For he finds fault with them" (v8).** The fault lies with the people, not chiefly with the covenant. "They did not continue in my covenant" (v9). Paul says much the same: the Law is "holy and righteous and good" (Rom 7:12), but it was "weakened by the flesh" (Rom 8:3). Greek manuscripts are split here between "finding fault with *them*" and "finding fault, he says *to them*," but either way the next verse makes plain that Israel's unfaithfulness is in view. Still, the covenant had limits of its own: it gave commands on stone, not new hearts, and its sacrifices could not clear the conscience (9:9).
- **Jeremiah's setting.** Jeremiah prophesied as Judah collapsed, around 627–580 BC, and saw the temple burned by Babylon in 586 BC. He spent his life warning that the Sinai covenant had been broken past repair. Chapters 30–33 of his book, often called the "Book of Consolation," are the bright spot: beyond the ruin God would do something new. Hebrews quotes the passage from the Greek Old Testament almost word for word.
- **"Behold, the days are coming" (v8).** The promise is to "the house of Israel and… the house of Judah," the northern and southern kingdoms split since about 930 BC and both by then scattered. The new covenant reunites God's people. In Jesus' hands it reaches further still: at the Last Supper he takes the cup and says, "This cup… is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25), and he says it to the twelve who will carry it to the nations. Christians read the promise as fulfilled in the church of Jews and Gentiles together. Many dispensational readers also expect a further, national fulfilment for Israel at the end (Rom 11:26–27). Both read Jesus as the one who brings it in.
- **"New" (v8).** The word is *kainos*. Older commentators often said *kainos* means new in quality while *neos* means new in time. The lexicon agrees that is the main sense of *kainos*, but notes the line between the two words had blurred by the New Testament era, and Hebrews 12:24 calls the same covenant *neos*. The new covenant is fresh in kind, but the point should not rest on that word alone.
- **"Not like the covenant that I made with their fathers" (v9).** The Sinai covenant is pictured tenderly: God "took them by the hand," like a father leading a small child out of danger (Hos 11:1–4). The failure was all the sadder because it came after so much kindness. Hebrews 3–4 has already retold that failure in the wilderness.
- **"And so I showed no concern for them" (v9).** Here the Greek Old Testament, which Hebrews quotes, and the Hebrew of Jeremiah 31:32 differ. The Hebrew reads "though I was their husband," using *ba'al*, "to be master" or "to marry." The lexicon notes that some scholars think the Greek translators read a very similar Hebrew word meaning "to loathe" or "reject," which would explain the different sense. The two readings are two sides of one truth: God was a faithful husband, and a broken covenant brought its own judgment (Deut 28).
- **"I will put my laws into their minds, and write them on their hearts" (v10).** At Sinai God wrote with his own finger on stone (Exod 31:18). Now he writes on the inside. The Law is not abolished; it is moved from outside to inside, so that God's people want what he wants. Ezekiel saw the same promise as a heart transplant and the gift of the Spirit (Ezek 36:26–27), and Paul calls believers a letter "written… with the Spirit of the living God… on tablets of human hearts" (2 Cor 3:3). Moses had looked forward to it too: "the LORD your God will circumcise your heart" (Deut 30:6).
- **"I will be their God, and they shall be my people" (v10).** This is the covenant formula repeated through the whole Bible, from Exodus 6:7 to its final fulfilment, "they will be his people, and God himself will be with them" (Rev 21:3). The goal of every covenant is the same. What is new is that God himself guarantees the people's side of it.
- **"They shall not teach, each one his neighbor… for they shall all know me" (v11).** "Know" in the Bible means more than know about. It means a personal relationship, the way a husband knows his wife. Under the old covenant knowledge of God came through priests and prophets, and most people stood at a distance. Under the new covenant every member knows the Lord, "from the least of them to the greatest." This does not mean teachers are no longer needed. The New Testament is full of them (Eph 4:11; Heb 5:12). It means no believer is a second-class member who knows God only second-hand (1 John 2:20, 27).
- **"I will be merciful toward their iniquities, and I will remember their sins no more" (v12).** The verse begins with "for." Forgiveness is the foundation of everything else in the promise. God does not suffer memory loss. To "remember" sin in the Bible means to act on it in judgment (1 Kings 17:18), so not to remember is to treat it as settled for good. The old sacrifices were themselves "a reminder of sins every year" (10:3). Hebrews will come back to this verse at the very end of the argument (10:17–18): where sins are forgiven, "there is no longer any offering for sin." The KJV adds "and their iniquities" at the end of the verse, following later manuscripts; the ESV follows the older ones.
- **"In speaking of a new covenant, he makes the first one obsolete" (v13).** The verb *palaioō* means to make or declare old, and it can carry the sense of cancelling. The writer's logic is in the word itself: the moment God said "new," centuries before Christ, he called the Sinai covenant "old." The same verb appeared in 1:11 for the heavens that "will all wear out like a garment." Only God's Son remains.
- **"Ready to vanish away" (v13).** "Vanish" is *aphanismos*, disappearance, used only here in the New Testament. In the Greek Old Testament the word family is used for ruin and desolation. The writer says the old order is "growing old" and *ready* to vanish, not that it has already gone. If the letter was written before AD 70, the Romans burned the temple and ended its sacrifices only a few years later. Many readers see that as the quiet fulfilment of this verse, though the writer's point is theological, not a prediction of the date.
- **Commentators:** All three agree that the fault in v8 lies chiefly with the people and not with the Law itself. JFB says the law was blameless morally, but "in saving us it was defective," and on v13 it puts the argument plainly: "Both covenants cannot exist side by side." Henry calls the old covenant "a dispensation of darkness and dread," fit for its purpose but never the gospel's equal, and says the new rests on promises "more clear and express, more spiritual, more absolute." He likens the old order in gospel times to candles after sunrise: "It is antiquated, canceled, out of date." Guzik stresses that no priest ever sat in the tabernacle because the work was never done, rejects reading the "true tent" as the church or Christ's body (JFB is open to linking it with Christ's glorified body), and contrasts the Sinai covenant's "if you" (Exod 19:5) with the new covenant's repeated "I will." Guzik also leans on the *kainos*/*neos* distinction, which the lexicon supports only loosely. On v9 JFB notes that the KJV of Jeremiah reads "although I was an husband unto them," while Hebrews follows the Greek translation. On v11 Henry hopes for a day of such full gospel preaching and outpouring of the Spirit that private instruction would be less needed, and JFB stresses that under the new covenant "God Himself teaches." The Tyndale notes add that God's taking Israel "by the hand" pictures a parent guiding a child who cannot yet look after itself.

#### Pointing to Christ
Everything in the chapter depends on who is sitting at the right hand of the Majesty. He is the priest the tabernacle sketched, the sacrifice the altar waited for, and the mediator Jeremiah could only promise. On the night he was betrayed Jesus lifted the cup and called it "the new covenant in my blood." The promise of hearts rewritten, God known by the least, and sins remembered no more is not a hope for some later age. It is the covenant he sealed and now keeps from his throne. The shadows were good, but no one who has found the substance needs to go back to them.
