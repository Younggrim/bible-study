*Setting:* About 850 BC. Ahab is dead, his son Ahaziah has died after a short reign (ch 1), and Elijah has been taken to heaven (ch 2). Ahab's second son Jehoram (also called Joram) now rules Israel. One line in 1:1 said Moab had rebelled after Ahab's death. This chapter tells what Israel did about it. Moab lay east of the Dead Sea, on a high, grassy plateau ideal for sheep. It had been under Israel's control since Omri, Ahab's father. Unusually, we have Moab's side of the story too. The Moabite Stone (Mesha Stele), found in 1868 and now in the Louvre, is King Mesha's own inscription. It boasts that Omri "humbled Moab many days" because the Moabite god Chemosh was angry with his land, and that Mesha later threw off Israel's rule. This chapter shows Elisha for the first time as a prophet to kings, and it ends with one of the darkest and most puzzling verses in Kings.

#### Moab Rebels Against Israel (vv1–27)
- **"In the eighteenth year of Jehoshaphat king of Judah, Jehoram the son of Ahab became king" (v1).** Jehoram reigned about 852–841 BC. The date here seems to clash with 1:17, which dates his accession to "the second year of Jehoram the son of Jehoshaphat." The usual explanation is that Jehoshaphat's son ruled alongside his father for some years, so the two notes count from different starting points.
- **"He did what was evil... though not like his father and mother, for he put away the pillar of Baal" (v2).** A "pillar" (*matstsebah*) was a standing stone set up as a sacred object, here for Baal. Ahab had built a temple to Baal in Samaria (1 Kgs 16:32). Jehoram removed this one object, but not the Baal temple or its priests, which Jehu would destroy later (10:26–27). Jezebel, still alive, kept her influence. Jehoram's motives are not given. It may have been a nod to Jehoshaphat, or fear after his brother's death. Henry: "Jehoram took warning by God's judgment, and put away the image of Baal, yet he maintained the worship of the calves."
- **"He clung to the sin of Jeroboam" (v3).** The golden calves at Bethel and Dan, set up as Israel's official religion (1 Kgs 12:28–30), stayed. The verb "clung" (*dabaq*) is the word used for a man holding fast to his wife (Gen 2:24) and for Israel holding fast to the LORD (Deut 10:20). Jehoram held fast to the wrong thing. Partial reform is not repentance.
- **"Mesha king of Moab was a sheep breeder" (v4).** The Hebrew word (*noqed*) is used elsewhere only of Amos (Amos 1:1). It may mean a breeder of a special kind of sheep, prized for its wool, or simply a wealthy owner of flocks. **"100,000 lambs and the wool of 100,000 rams."** This was the tribute Moab paid each year, or perhaps once, at a new king's accession. Either way it was a crushing burden. Mesha's own inscription confirms Israel's rule over northern Moab and names "Omri king of Israel" and "his son."
- **"When Ahab died, the king of Moab rebelled" (v5).** Vassal states often rebelled when an overlord died, testing the new king. Ahaziah's short reign and injury (ch 1) gave Mesha his chance.
- **"Will you go with me to battle against Moab?" (v7).** Jehoram asks Jehoshaphat of Judah, and Jehoshaphat answers in the very words he used to Ahab before the disaster at Ramoth-gilead: **"I am as you are, my people as your people, my horses as your horses"** (1 Kgs 22:4). He has not learned. A prophet had rebuked him for that earlier alliance (2 Chr 19:2), and his shipping venture with Ahaziah had been wrecked (2 Chr 20:37). Now he joins another of Ahab's sons.
- **"By the way of the wilderness of Edom" (v8).** The direct route into Moab was from the north, across the Arnon gorge, where Mesha had fortified his towns. Mesha's inscription says he rebuilt and fortified several cities in the north. So the allies take the long route south around the bottom of the Dead Sea, through Edom's territory, and attack Moab from its weaker southern border. JFB calls it "a long and circuitous route, by the southern bend of the Dead Sea." Edom was under Judah's control at this time, with a deputy ruling (1 Kgs 22:47), so the "king of Edom" comes along too (v9).
- **"A circuitous march of seven days, there was no water" (v9).** The southern desert is dry country. The allies counted on finding water in the wadis and found none. Animals in an ancient army included pack animals, horses and herds brought for food. Without water, all of them would die within days.
- **"Alas! The LORD has called these three kings to give them into the hand of Moab" (v10).** Jehoram's first thought in trouble is that God is out to destroy him. He has not asked God for anything, but he is quick to blame him. A guilty conscience reads every hardship as judgment.
- **"Is there no prophet of the LORD here?" (v11).** Jehoshaphat asks the same question he asked before Ramoth-gilead (1 Kgs 22:7), again only after committing himself. One of Jehoram's servants knows: **"Elisha the son of Shaphat is here, who poured water on the hands of Elijah."** Pouring water over a master's hands for washing was a servant's job. The phrase means Elisha was Elijah's personal attendant, and JFB says it simply means "was his servant." That was Elisha's credential: he had served the great prophet. Somehow Elisha was traveling with or near the army, which none of the kings seems to have known.
- **"The word of the LORD is with him" (v12).** Jehoshaphat recognizes a true prophet. And the three kings go **down to him**. The kings come to the prophet, not the prophet to the kings.
- **"What have I to do with you? Go to the prophets of your father and to the prophets of your mother" (v13).** "What have I to do with you?" is a sharp idiom: "What do we have in common?" The prophets of Ahab and Jezebel were the 400 who lied to Ahab (1 Kgs 22:6) and the prophets of Baal. Elisha says, in effect: you worship their gods, go ask them. Jehoram's reply repeats his complaint: it is the LORD who has brought us here to die. Even in his panic he knows only the LORD can help.
- **"As the LORD of hosts lives, before whom I stand" (v14).** Elisha uses Elijah's own oath (1 Kgs 17:1; 18:15). **"Were it not that I have regard for Jehoshaphat... I would neither look at you nor see you."** For the sake of one faithful king, the whole army, including the wicked one, will be saved. It is the principle of Abraham's prayer for Sodom (Gen 18:23–32).
- **"Bring me a musician" (v15).** The word means someone playing a stringed instrument, probably a harp or lyre. Music and prophecy were linked from early on: the band of prophets Saul met came with harp, tambourine, flute and lyre (1 Sam 10:5), and David appointed temple musicians "who prophesied with lyres" (1 Chr 25:1). David's playing calmed Saul (1 Sam 16:23). Elisha may have needed to quiet his own anger after facing Jehoram, so he could listen to God. **"The hand of the LORD came upon him."** The music did not produce the prophecy. It prepared him to receive it. JFB stresses that prophecy was a gift from God, not something natural to the person.
- **"I will make this dry streambed full of pools" (v16).** The Hebrew can be read as a command, "Make this valley full of ditches," as the KJV has it, or as God's promise, "I will make it full of pools." Either way, the next verses show the people were to dig (Henry: "To try their faith and obedience, Elisha bids them make the valley full of pits"). The "streambed" (*nachal*) is a wadi, a desert ravine dry most of the year.
- **"You shall not see wind or rain, but that streambed shall be filled with water" (v17).** Water without a storm. JFB notes the Eastern expression of "seeing wind," as in dust devils whirling before a storm. There will be none of the usual signs. **"This is a light thing in the sight of the LORD"** (v18). Water is easy for God. He will also give Moab into their hand.
- **"Fell every good tree and stop up all springs of water and ruin every good piece of land with stones" (v19).** This was standard ancient warfare, meant to cripple an enemy's economy for years. It sits uneasily with the law of Moses, which forbade cutting down fruit trees when besieging a city (Deut 20:19–20). Interpreters handle this in different ways. Some read v19 as a prediction of what the allies would do rather than a command, and others as a specific judgment on Moab for this war rather than a general rule for Israel's sieges. Adam Clarke, cited by Guzik, raises the tension with Deuteronomy.
- **"About the time of offering the sacrifice, behold, water came from the direction of Edom" (v20).** The morning offering at the temple in Jerusalem (Exod 29:38–39). As at Carmel (1 Kgs 18:36), God answers at the hour of true worship. The water came from Edom, the highlands to the south and east. Most likely rain fell in the mountains, far out of sight, and the flash flood ran down the wadi in the night. Flash floods like this still happen in that region. God's provision came by a natural means, at exactly the time and place he promised.
- **"All who were able to put on armor, from the youngest to the oldest, were called out" (v21).** Moab mobilizes everyone. They gather at the southern border, facing the allies across the wadi.
- **"The Moabites saw the water opposite them as red as blood" (v22).** The morning sun rising behind them in the east shone on the water, perhaps already reddish from the red sandstone soil of Edom. "Edom" itself means "red" (Gen 25:30), so the wordplay is close at hand. The Moabites had no idea water had come. They saw red pools in a dry wadi and drew the obvious conclusion: **"This is blood; the kings have surely fought together"** (v23). An alliance of three old enemies, Israel, Judah and Edom, could easily have fallen out. **"Now then, Moab, to the spoil!"**
- **"The Israelites rose and struck the Moabites" (v24).** The Moabites rushed in disorder to loot a camp they thought was full of dead men and found an army waiting. God used the same water both to save the allies and to defeat Moab.
- **"On every good piece of land every man threw a stone until it was covered" (v25).** Fields covered in stones could not be plowed for years. The allies did exactly as Elisha said (v19). **"Till only its stones were left in Kir-hareseth."** Kir-hareseth ("city of potsherds" or "wall of potsherds") was Moab's chief fortress, almost certainly modern Kerak in Jordan, on a steep hill with a Crusader castle on top today. The Hebrew is hard here. It probably means that only Kir-hareseth was left standing, its stone walls intact, surrounded by slingers.
- **"He took with him 700 swordsmen to break through, opposite the king of Edom" (v26).** Mesha tries to break out at what he thinks is the weakest point in the siege, the Edomite lines. Perhaps he hoped the Edomites, unwilling allies, would let him through. It fails.
- **"He took his oldest son who was to reign in his place and offered him for a burnt offering on the wall" (v27).** In full view of the besieging armies, Mesha sacrifices his heir to the god Chemosh. Child sacrifice was practiced by Israel's neighbors, and the law of Moses condemned it in the strongest terms (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31). JFB calls it "a deed of horror." Some ancient Jewish interpreters thought the victim was the son of the king of Edom, captured in the breakout, linking this to Amos 2:1, where Moab is condemned for burning the bones of Edom's king. But "his oldest son who was to reign in his place" most naturally means Mesha's own son.
- **"And there came great wrath against Israel. And they withdrew" (v27).** This is the chapter's great puzzle. The word for wrath (*qetseph*) is almost always used of God's anger (Num 1:53; Josh 9:20; Jer 21:5). Whose wrath was it? There are several main views. One is that it was God's anger against Israel, whether for driving Mesha to such an act, or for going beyond what God allowed, or because the campaign itself sprang from Jehoram's unfaithfulness. JFB takes this view: "a divine judgment." A second is that it was the Moabites' fury, roused by their king's sacrifice to fight with desperate courage, so the allies could not take the city. A third is that it was horror and outrage among the allies themselves, perhaps the Edomites turning on Israel, which broke up the coalition. A fourth is that the writer reports how it appeared from Moab's side, as if Chemosh's wrath had come out against Israel, without endorsing that Chemosh was real. The text does not say, and faithful interpreters still disagree. What is clear is the result: the campaign ended without full victory. Mesha's inscription, as might be expected, records only his triumphs.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees Jehoram as less wicked than his parents but still unfaithful, passing on Knapp's description of him as spiritually neutral and Poole's suggestion that his reform was motivated by fear or a wish to please Jehoshaphat. On the musician, Guzik cites Poole and Trapp on music quieting Elisha's spirit to hear God. On the ditches, Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that God's people should prepare for a blessing before they see any sign of it, and Henry makes the same point in his own words: "Those who expect God's blessings, must dig pools for the rain to fill." JFB reads "poured water on the hands" as servant language and describes the water from Edom as a cloudburst in the hills. On v27 Guzik quotes Patterson and Austel's view that the allies were sickened by the sight and withdrew, while JFB sees "a divine judgment." The Tyndale notes observe that the Hebrew word for "wrath" is the one used for the LORD's fury against covenant-breaking Israel, which supports JFB's reading.

#### Pointing to Christ
Three kings in a dry wilderness, facing death, are saved for the sake of one king who sought the LORD. That is a small picture of the gospel: God shows mercy to many for the sake of one, and the one is finally Christ, the true King, through whom God saves even those who do not deserve it (Rom 5:18–19). The water came "at the time of offering the sacrifice," with no wind and no rain, as a gift that was "a light thing" to the LORD. Jesus promised living water to the thirsty (John 7:37–39) and gave it through his own sacrifice. The chapter ends at the opposite pole: a desperate king offering his firstborn son on a wall to buy his god's favor, and it achieves nothing but horror. The gospel turns this upside down. No one needs to sacrifice a child to move God. God himself "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32), offered outside the city wall, so that sinners could be saved.
