*Setting:* Jesus is still in the temple at Jerusalem during or just after the Feast of Booths, in the autumn about six months before the cross. Chapter 7 ended with the leaders' plan to arrest him collapsing and the crowds divided. Chapter 8 opens with a story that most early manuscripts do not contain, the woman caught in adultery (7:53–8:11), and then returns to the long temple debate. That debate is the sharpest in John's Gospel. It moves from Jesus as the light of the world, to freedom and slavery, to who the true children of Abraham are, and finally to Jesus' claim that "before Abraham was, I am," at which his hearers pick up stones to kill him.

#### The Woman Caught in Adultery (vv1–11)
- **Is this passage part of John?** The ESV brackets 7:53–8:11 with the note that the earliest manuscripts do not include it. That is the scholarly consensus across traditions. It is missing from the oldest Greek papyri and the great fourth-century codices. No Greek church writer comments on it for about a thousand years. Many of the manuscripts that do include it mark it as doubtful. And where it does appear, it moves around: usually after John 7:52, but in some copies after 7:36, at the end of John, or after Luke 21:38. Its vocabulary is more like Luke's than John's. Yet many scholars also think it is a true story from Jesus' life, passed down orally and later written into the Gospels. The early church historian Eusebius reports that Papias, early in the second century, told a story of a woman accused of many sins before the Lord, and a third-century church manual (the *Didascalia*) refers to it. The story fits Jesus' character exactly. Christians therefore read it with respect, though most avoid building doctrine on it alone.
- **"Jesus went to the Mount of Olives" (v1).** The Mount of Olives rises across the Kidron Valley east of the temple. Jesus often spent nights there during visits to Jerusalem (Luke 21:37). "Early in the morning" (v2) he is back in the temple courts, seated as a rabbi with people gathered around.
- **"A woman who had been caught in adultery" (v3).** "The scribes and the Pharisees" is a pairing common in the other Gospels but found nowhere else in John. The scribes were experts in the Law. "Caught in the act" (v4) means there were witnesses, which the Law required (Deut 17:6). That raises a question: where is the man? The Law called for both to die (Lev 20:10; Deut 22:22). Bringing only the woman suggests that justice was not really the point.
- **"Moses commanded us to stone such women. So what do you say?" (v5).** Stoning is specified in Deuteronomy 22:23–24 for a betrothed woman. It was a trap with two jaws. If Jesus said "let her go," he would contradict Moses. If he said "stone her," he would lose his reputation for mercy toward sinners, and he would also defy Rome, which did not let the Jews carry out death sentences on their own authority (18:31).
- **"Jesus bent down and wrote with his finger on the ground" (v6).** This is the only time the Gospels say Jesus wrote anything, and no one knows what he wrote. Suggestions include the accusers' sins, the text of Exodus 23:1 ("You shall not… be a malicious witness"), or Jeremiah 17:13 ("those who turn away from you shall be written in the earth"). It may simply have been a pause, a refusal to be hurried, turning attention away from the shamed woman. The "finger" of God wrote the Ten Commandments on stone (Exod 31:18), and some readers see that echoed here.
- **"Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her" (v7).** Under the Law, the witnesses threw the first stones (Deut 17:7). Jesus doesn't overturn the Law, and he doesn't excuse adultery. He turns the spotlight on the accusers' own hearts. "Without sin" probably means not only sinless in general, but free of the same kind of sin and of the malice that drove this setup.
- **"They went away one by one, beginning with the older ones" (v9).** The older men perhaps had more years of sin to remember, or more wisdom to recognize it. Jesus is "left alone with the woman," which Augustine famously summed up as *misera et misericordia*: misery and mercy, face to face.
- **"Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (v11).** "Woman" (v10) is the same respectful address Jesus used for his mother (2:4). The only one present who *was* without sin, and therefore could have thrown the stone, refuses to condemn her. But he doesn't call her sin nothing. Mercy comes first, and with it a call to a new life. The order matters: he does not say "sin no more, and then I won't condemn you." Readers differ on whether this is a full declaration of forgiveness or a release from the legal penalty, leaving her to seek God's forgiveness. The parallel with John 5:14 ("Sin no more") and Romans 8:1 has led many to read it as grace that changes the person.
- **Commentators:** Guzik acknowledges the manuscript evidence and still treats the account as historical. JFB grants that it is "wanting in some of the earliest and most valuable manuscripts," but finds that "the internal evidence in its favor is almost overpowering." Henry comments on it as Scripture without discussing the question, as the KJV text of his day included it. On what Jesus wrote, Henry says "It is impossible to tell, and therefore needless to ask." Guzik sees Jesus' bending down as a humble act that drew attention away from the woman's shame. On v7, JFB reads "without sin" as someone whose conscience clears him of such sin. Henry says "They who heard it were convicted by their own consciences." On v11 they differ. JFB says Jesus "pronounces no pardon upon the woman" and does not act as her judge. Henry reads it as release from the penalty and a sign of forgiveness, and Guzik links it to Romans 8:1: no condemnation, and a call to holiness.

#### I Am the Light of the World (vv12–30)
- **"I am the light of the world" (v12).** This picks up the scene from 7:52. The Mishnah describes a ceremony at the Feast of Booths in the Court of the Women: four giant golden lampstands, each with four bowls of oil, were lit, and their light was said to shine over every courtyard in Jerusalem. Men danced through the night with torches, and Levites sang the Psalms on the fifteen steps. Jesus speaks in the same court (v20), probably as the feast ends. He doesn't just claim to be a light like the lamps. He claims to be the light of the whole world, like the pillar of fire that led Israel through the wilderness (Exod 13:21), the "light to the nations" (Isa 49:6). "The LORD is my light" (Ps 27:1).
- **"Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness" (v12).** Following the light was exactly what Israel did in the wilderness, moving when the pillar moved. "The light of life" means light that gives life (1:4).
- **"Even if I do bear witness about myself, my testimony is true" (v14).** The Pharisees object that a lone witness proves nothing (v13; Deut 19:15). In 5:31 Jesus granted that rule for argument's sake. Here he explains why his witness is valid anyway: he alone knows where he came from and where he is going. Only someone who has seen heaven can testify about it.
- **"Where is your Father?" (v19).** They may be looking for a human father, perhaps with a sneer about Jesus' birth (see v41). Jesus answers that knowing him is the only way to know the Father (14:7–9).
- **"In the treasury" (v20).** The treasury was in the Court of the Women, where thirteen trumpet-shaped offering boxes stood (Mark 12:41–44). It was near the hall where the Sanhedrin met, and yet "no one arrested him, because his hour had not yet come."
- **"You will die in your sin" (v21).** In v21 "sin" is singular, the root sin of unbelief. In v24 it is plural, "sins," all that follows from it. It is one of the gravest warnings in the Gospels.
- **"Unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins" (v24).** In Greek, "he" is not there. Jesus says "unless you believe that I am." In context it can mean "that I am the one I claim to be," but it echoes God's words in Isaiah: "that you may know and believe me and understand that I am he" (Isa 43:10), where the Greek Old Testament uses *egō eimi*. Jesus is staking eternal life on faith in who he is.
- **"Who are you?" (v25).** Jesus' answer is one of the hardest sentences in John's Greek. It can be read "What I have been telling you from the beginning" (ESV), or as an exclamation, "Why do I speak to you at all?" Either way, the point is that he has already told them.
- **"When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he" (v28).** This is the second "lifted up" saying (3:14; 12:32). "You" will lift him up: Jesus names his enemies as the ones who will crucify him. And the cross will reveal who he is. At the moment they think they are getting rid of him, his identity will be made plain (Mark 15:39).
- **"He has not left me alone, for I always do the things that are pleasing to him" (v29).** No one else in Scripture could say "always." The Father's presence never leaves him (though on the cross he will cry out as forsaken, Mark 15:34, a mystery John does not record).
- **Commentators:** Guzik ties v12 to the lamp-lighting at the feast and to the pillar of fire in the exodus. JFB stresses that Jesus calls himself the light "in the most absolute sense," unlike the lesser lights of John the Baptist (5:35) or the disciples. JFB locates the treasury in "part of the court of the women." On v24, JFB says the hearers "knew well enough what He meant" and links "I am he" to God's self-declarations in Deuteronomy and Isaiah. Guzik also notes that "he" is supplied by translators and that the bare "I am" claims deity, and he draws out the shift from "sin" to "sins." JFB calls v28 "the plainest intimation He had yet given in public of the manner and the authors of His death."

#### The Truth Will Set You Free (vv31–38)
- **"The Jews who had believed him" (v31).** The verb is perfect, describing people who had come to some kind of faith. But within a few verses they are claiming never to have been slaves, and by v59 they are picking up stones. This is John's sharpest example of the shallow faith he warned about in 2:23–25.
- **"If you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples" (v31).** "Abide" (*menō*) means to stay, remain, make one's home. A true disciple is not someone who starts well but someone who stays. "Truly" (*alēthōs*) is from the same root as "truth" (v32): genuine disciples know the genuine truth.
- **"You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (v32).** This verse is carved over libraries and universities, often meaning academic knowledge. In context, "the truth" is not knowledge in general but the truth Jesus speaks, the truth that is Jesus himself (14:6). The freedom is freedom from sin (v34).
- **"We are offspring of Abraham and have never been enslaved to anyone" (v33).** An astonishing claim from a nation enslaved in Egypt, exiled to Babylon, ruled by Persia, Greece and Syria, and now occupied by Rome, with Roman soldiers garrisoned beside the temple. They probably mean they had never accepted slavery inwardly, that as Abraham's sons they were free in spirit. A later rabbinic saying claimed that all Israelites are sons of kings.
- **"Everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin" (v34).** "Practices" (present tense) describes ongoing sin as a way of life. Sin is not only something people do. It is a master that rules them (Rom 6:16–17).
- **"The slave does not remain in the house forever; the son remains forever" (v35).** A slave has no permanent place in the family and can be sold. A son belongs. This may echo Abraham's own household, where Ishmael, the son of the slave woman, was sent away and Isaac, the son of promise, remained (Gen 21:10; compare Gal 4:21–31).
- **"If the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (v36).** In Roman society a son could free slaves in his father's house with full legal effect. Jesus is the Son with authority in the Father's house. "Indeed" (*ontōs*) means really, actually.
- **"You seek to kill me because my word finds no place in you" (v37).** Physical descent from Abraham is real ("I know that you are offspring of Abraham"), but it doesn't make them Abraham's true children. Jesus' word has no room in them, so violence fills the space.
- **Commentators:** Guzik defines "abide" as being at home in Jesus' word and says the freedom it brings comes from living in the truth, not from study alone. JFB calls the claim of v33 "bluster" born of "the pride of the Jewish nation," and thinks it came from other hearers rather than the new believers of v31. Guzik takes it as the same group and quotes J. C. Ryle on how deep self-deception runs in an unconverted heart. On v35 JFB says the Son's relation to the Father is "an indefeasible tie." Guzik notes that "practices" is a present tense describing habitual sin.

#### You Are of Your Father the Devil (vv39–47)
- **"Abraham is our father" (v39).** Jesus answers that real children resemble their father. Abraham believed God and welcomed God's messengers (Gen 15:6; 18:1–8). These men want to kill the messenger who told them God's truth. "This is not what Abraham did" (v40).
- **"We were not born of sexual immorality" (v41).** "We" is emphatic in Greek: *we* weren't born that way, implying "you were." Rumors about Jesus' birth evidently circulated, since Mary was pregnant before her marriage (Matt 1:18). Later Jewish writings repeat such slanders. The irony is that Jesus has no human father. The prophets also used "sexual immorality" for idolatry (Hos 2:4), so the men may also be claiming religious purity: "we are not a mixed race of idolaters."
- **"You are of your father the devil" (v44).** This is one of the harshest things Jesus says, and it must be read carefully. It is spoken to a particular group of opponents who are trying to kill him, not to the Jewish people as a whole. Jesus and all his disciples were Jews, and "salvation is from the Jews" (4:22). Sadly, this verse has been twisted to justify hatred of Jews, which is a terrible misuse of it. Jesus is saying that their actions, murder and lies, show whose family they really belong to.
- **"He was a murderer from the beginning" (v44).** This refers to Eden. Satan's lie brought death to the human race (Gen 3:1–5; Rom 5:12). Some connect it to Cain's murder of Abel, which John links to the devil in 1 John 3:12. "He is a liar and the father of lies." His first recorded words in Scripture question what God said ("Did God actually say…?" Gen 3:1).
- **"Which one of you convicts me of sin?" (v46).** It is an extraordinary challenge. No other teacher in history could ask it of his enemies with confidence, and no one answers. "Convicts" (*elenchō*) means to prove guilty. The New Testament consistently affirms that Jesus was without sin (2 Cor 5:21; Heb 4:15; 1 Pet 2:22).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls v44 "one of the most decisive testimonies to the objective (outward) personality of the devil," and Guzik agrees that the passage strongly supports a real, personal Satan. JFB says "a murderer from the beginning" refers to the fall of Adam, not Cain's murder. On v41, JFB reads the claim as meaning they were "not an illegitimate race in point of religion," while the Tyndale notes hear a jab at the circumstances of Jesus' birth. Both may be in play.

#### Before Abraham Was, I Am (vv48–59)
- **"You are a Samaritan and have a demon" (v48).** Out of arguments, they turn to insult. To call a Jew a "Samaritan" was to call him a heretic and half-breed, perhaps because Jesus had questioned their descent from Abraham as Samaritans did, or because he had spent time in Samaria (ch 4).
- **"If anyone keeps my word, he will never see death" (v51).** "Never" is the emphatic double negative. Believers will die physically, but death as final ruin, separation from God, will never be their experience (11:25–26). They "taste" it (v52) only as a passage.
- **"Your father Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day. He saw it and was glad" (v56).** When did Abraham see Christ's day? Several answers have been given. He received the promise that through his offspring all nations would be blessed (Gen 12:3; 22:18). He laughed for joy at the promise of Isaac (Gen 17:17), which Jewish tradition interpreted as joy. On Mount Moriah he told Isaac "God will provide for himself the lamb" (Gen 22:8, 14). Some Jewish writings taught that God showed Abraham the future ages, including the days of the Messiah. Hebrews says Abraham and the patriarchs saw the promises "from afar" and greeted them (Heb 11:13).
- **"You are not yet fifty years old" (v57).** This is a round number, not an estimate of Jesus' age. Luke says he was about thirty when he began (Luke 3:23). Fifty was also the age when Levites retired from service (Num 8:25). Some manuscripts read "has Abraham seen you?", but the ESV's reading is better supported.
- **"Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am" (v58).** Grammar carries the claim. "Abraham was" uses *genesthai*, "came into being," a verb for something that began at a point in time. "I am" is *egō eimi*, present tense, with no beginning. Jesus doesn't say "I was before Abraham," which would only claim he was older. He says "I am," an unbroken present, the name God gave Moses at the burning bush: "I AM WHO I AM… Say this to the people of Israel: I AM has sent me to you" (Exod 3:14). It echoes Isaiah's "I am he; before me no god was formed" (Isa 43:10–13).
- **"So they picked up stones to throw at him" (v59).** They understood exactly what he meant. Blasphemy was punished by stoning (Lev 24:16). Building work was still going on in the temple courts, so stones may have been close at hand. Their reaction shows that Jesus was claiming to be God. The same crowd will try again in 10:31–33 for the same reason.
- **"But Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple" (v59).** John doesn't say whether this was a miracle or a quiet escape in the crowd. Some later manuscripts add "going through the midst of them," echoing Luke 4:30. The point is the same as all through these chapters: his hour has not yet come. He will die when and how the Father has appointed, not by a mob's stones but lifted up on a cross. And Jesus leaving the temple is a sad picture. The true temple walks out of the house that rejected him.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the "Samaritan" insult as the opponents running out of arguments. JFB says the term implies they thought Jesus' claim to be among Abraham's true sons was false. On v56, JFB says Abraham "actually beheld it, to his joy," perhaps in his close fellowship with God. Guzik suggests Abraham's words on Mount Moriah (Gen 22:8) as one possible moment. On v57, JFB says "No inference can be drawn from this as to the age of our Lord," and Guzik also calls fifty a round number. On v58 JFB puts the difference simply: "'Abraham was brought into being'; the other, 'I exist.'" Guzik connects the words directly with Exodus 3:14 and notes that the stoning shows the hearers took it as a claim to deity. Guzik doesn't call the escape a miracle. He says simply that the hour had not yet come.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter moves from a woman about to be stoned to Jesus about to be stoned. In the first scene, the only sinless person present refuses to condemn the guilty. In the last, the guilty try to execute the sinless one. In between, Jesus claims to be the light the feast lamps pointed to, the Son who sets slaves free, the one no one could convict of sin, and the eternal "I am" who was before Abraham. He tells the woman "neither do I condemn you" knowing that the condemnation will fall on him when he is "lifted up" (v28). The same Jesus who offers freedom and life to anyone who abides in his word warns that those who refuse him will die in their sins. Mercy and truth stand together in him, and both come to their fullest at the cross.
