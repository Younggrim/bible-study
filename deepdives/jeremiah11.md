*Setting:* A new collection of Jeremiah's messages begins here, running through chapter 20. Chapter 11 opens with a formal speech about "this covenant," the agreement God made with Israel at Sinai and restated in Deuteronomy. Many scholars connect it with King Josiah's reform, which began in 622 BC when the Book of the Law was found in the temple and the nation publicly renewed the covenant (2 Kgs 22–23). Jeremiah had been prophesying about five years by then. Others, including the Tyndale notes, place the speech later, in the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), when the reform had collapsed and the people had gone back to their idols. Either way, the message is the same: Judah has broken the covenant, and its curses are now coming due. The chapter then turns personal. The men of Jeremiah's own hometown are plotting to kill him, and this begins a series of raw prayers often called Jeremiah's "confessions" (11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18).

#### The Broken Covenant (vv1–23)
- **"Hear the words of this covenant" (v2).** In Hebrew the command "hear" and "speak" are plural, as if Jeremiah is one of several voices calling the people back to the covenant. The covenant was not new. Every prophet stood on the ground Moses had laid. Jeremiah's job is to read Judah's life back against the terms it had agreed to.
- **"Cursed be the man who does not hear the words of this covenant" (v3).** This echoes Deuteronomy 27:15–26, where the Levites pronounced twelve curses on covenant-breakers and all the people answered "Amen" to each. The last of them, "Cursed be anyone who does not confirm the words of this law by doing them" (Deut 27:26), is quoted by Paul in Galatians 3:10 to show that no one is put right with God by keeping the law.
- **"From the iron furnace" (v4).** Egypt is called an iron furnace also in Deuteronomy 4:20 and 1 Kings 8:51. An iron-smelting furnace burned hot enough to melt metal, a picture of the crushing ordeal of slavery. God brought them out of that fire to make them his own.
- **"So shall you be my people, and I will be your God" (v4).** This is the heart of the covenant, repeated through the Old Testament (Exod 6:7; Lev 26:12). The relationship was a gift of grace, since God rescued them before he gave them a single law. But it was meant to be lived out in listening and obeying: "Listen to my voice."
- **"A land flowing with milk and honey, as at this day" (v5).** God had kept his side. They were living in the land he had sworn to give. Milk points to flocks and herds grazing on good pasture, and honey to flowering plants and fruit trees.
- **"So be it, LORD" (v5).** In Hebrew it is "Amen, LORD," the same answer the people gave to the curses in Deuteronomy 27. Jeremiah agrees with God's terms, curses included. He does not soften the covenant. He signs his name to it.
- **"Proclaim all these words in the cities of Judah" (v6).** Jeremiah is sent on what sounds like a preaching tour. If this belongs to Josiah's time, he may have been carrying the message of the newly found law from town to town, as Josiah's officials did (2 Chr 34:29–33). **"Hear… and do them."** James says the same: "be doers of the word, and not hearers only" (Jas 1:22).
- **"Warning them persistently, even to this day" (v7).** Literally "rising early and warning," Jeremiah's repeated picture of God as one who got up early to call his people (7:13, 25). For centuries God had not stopped calling.
- **"Everyone walked in the stubbornness of his evil heart" (v8).** "Stubbornness" is one of Jeremiah's key words (3:17; 7:24; 9:14). It pictures a heart that has set hard, like clay left out in the sun. **"Therefore I brought upon them all the words of this covenant."** The curses were not God changing his mind. They were part of the covenant all along (Lev 26; Deut 28).
- **"A conspiracy exists among the men of Judah" (v9).** The word *qesher* normally means a political plot or treason, as when servants conspired against a king (2 Kgs 11:14; 12:20). No one needed to hold a secret meeting. The people moved together against God as though they had planned it. If this is after Josiah's reform, the picture is sharper still: a nation that had sworn loyalty to the covenant quietly went back on its oath.
- **"They have turned back to the iniquities of their forefathers" (v10).** The reform had not reached their hearts. Josiah cleaned out the temple, but the next generation simply returned to the old gods. **"The house of Israel and the house of Judah"** together: the northern kingdom had already been destroyed for the same sin in 722 BC, and Judah has walked the same road (3:6–11).
- **"Though they cry to me, I will not listen to them" (v11).** A hard verse. God is not deaf to genuine repentance (Jer 18:7–8; 2 Chr 7:14). But a cry for rescue that has no intention of giving up other gods is not repentance. They want relief, not God. Proverbs 1:28 and Micah 3:4 say the same.
- **"They will go and cry to the gods to whom they make offerings, but they cannot save them" (v12).** The idols will be tested exactly when they are needed most, and they will fail. Deuteronomy 32:37–38 had predicted this taunt: "Where are their gods…? Let them rise up and help you."
- **"Your gods have become as many as your cities, O Judah" (v13).** This repeats 2:28 nearly word for word. Every town had its god, every street its altar. **"Altars… to shame."** The word *boshet*, "shame," is used here as a mocking substitute for Baal's name (3:24; Hos 9:10). Some Israelite names containing "Baal" were later rewritten with *bosheth*, so that Saul's son Eshbaal is called Ish-bosheth, "man of shame" (1 Chr 8:33; 2 Sam 2:8).
- **"Do not pray for this people" (v14).** God has said this before (7:16) and will say it again (14:11). Moses and Samuel were famous intercessors, but even their prayers would not change the outcome now (15:1). The command shows that Judah had passed a point where judgment was certain. It does not mean God stopped caring. Jeremiah kept praying anyway (14:7–9, 19–22), and God kept calling the people to turn.
- **"What right has my beloved in my house?" (v15).** God still calls Judah "my beloved." That makes the sin worse, not better. The Hebrew of this verse is difficult and the ancient translations differ, but the ESV's sense is clear: Judah keeps coming to the temple with sacrifices while doing "many vile deeds" elsewhere, and imagines that "sacrificial flesh" (literally "holy flesh") can turn away the coming doom. Jeremiah's temple sermon said exactly this (7:8–11). Haggai later explains that holy meat cannot make the unclean clean (Hag 2:12–14).
- **"A green olive tree, beautiful with good fruit" (v16).** Olive trees can live for centuries, giving oil for food, light and anointing. The psalmist pictured himself "like a green olive tree in the house of God" (Ps 52:8; also Hos 14:6). God gave Judah that name. Now, "with the roar of a great tempest," the tree will burn. The picture is probably a lightning strike in a storm, splitting branches and setting the tree alight in a moment. Paul uses the olive tree in Romans 11:17–24: natural branches broken off for unbelief, and wild branches grafted in.
- **"The LORD of hosts, who planted you" (v17).** The one who planted the tree has the right to cut it down (2:21; Isa 5:1–7). The disaster comes "because of the evil… they have done," and the Hebrew adds that they did it to themselves (compare 7:19). Sin is self-harm.
- **"The LORD made it known to me and I knew" (v18).** The poem moves without warning from the nation to the prophet's own danger. Jeremiah had no idea he was in danger until God told him. The sudden switch from "the LORD" to "you" shows a man shaken.
- **"I was like a gentle lamb led to the slaughter" (v19).** The phrase can mean a pet lamb, the kind of household lamb a poor man raised with his children (2 Sam 12:3). It trusts the hand that feeds it and follows its owner anywhere, even to the butcher's block. Jeremiah had trusted his neighbors in the same way. The words are close to Isaiah 53:7, "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter."
- **"Let us destroy the tree with its fruit" (v19).** The plotters use God's own picture of the olive tree (v16) against the prophet: cut him down, fruit and all. The Hebrew literally reads "the tree with its bread," and some scholars repoint the word to read "the tree in its sap," meaning while it is still green and growing. The sense is the same. **"That his name be remembered no more."** With no wife or children (16:2), Jeremiah's name could only live through his work. They want him wiped out completely.
- **"Who tests the heart and the mind" (v20).** Literally "the kidneys and the heart." In Hebrew thought the kidneys stood for the deepest emotions and the heart for the thinking will. God sees all of it (17:10; Ps 7:9).
- **"Let me see your vengeance upon them, for to you have I committed my cause" (v20).** Jeremiah does not take revenge into his own hands. He hands his case to the righteous Judge, as the psalmists do (Ps 7; 35). His prayer for vengeance is honest, and it is a prayer for justice, not a plan of his own. The New Testament shows a further step, praying for enemies (Matt 5:44; Luke 23:34), but even there vengeance is left with God (Rom 12:19).
- **"The men of Anathoth, who seek your life" (v21).** Anathoth was Jeremiah's hometown, a town of priests about three miles northeast of Jerusalem (1:1). It was where Solomon had banished the priest Abiathar (1 Kgs 2:26–27). Why would they want Jeremiah dead? Perhaps because he supported the reform that closed local shrines and centered worship in Jerusalem, which may have cost country priests their livelihood (2 Kgs 23:8–9). Perhaps simply because his preaching shamed them. Their demand, "Do not prophesy in the name of the LORD," is the oldest way to silence a prophet (Amos 2:12; 7:12–13). Chapter 12:6 adds that even his own brothers and his father's house were in on it.
- **"None of them shall be left" (vv22–23).** The judgment falls on the plotters and their families. It does not mean the town itself vanished forever. After the exile 128 men of Anathoth returned (Ezra 2:23; Neh 7:27), and Jeremiah himself later bought a field there as a sign of hope (32:6–15). "The year of their punishment" is the coming invasion, when Anathoth, lying on the northern approach to Jerusalem, would be among the first towns hit (Isa 10:30).
- **Commentators:** JFB links "this covenant" to "the book of the law… found in the temple by Hilkiah" under Josiah, while the Tyndale notes date the message to Jehoiakim. Both settings are possible. On v5 Henry reads Jeremiah's "Amen" as his own consent and his longing for the people to keep the land, and Guzik, quoting Morgan, contrasts an empty liturgical amen with one that comes from real agreement with God's purposes. Guzik also cites Thompson that the conspiracy of v9 is a metaphor: no secret plot was needed, because the people's drift looked exactly like one. JFB reads the "shame" of v13 as the idol itself, "the essence of all that is shameful." On v19 JFB explains the "gentle lamb" as "a 'pet lamb'… for their children to play with" and notes that the language is the same as that applied to the Messiah in Isaiah 53. Guzik draws the same line to Jesus, rejected by his own people (John 1:11).

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah could not keep the people inside the covenant, and the covenant's curses fell. Paul says Christ "redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13), and Jesus calls his own blood "the new covenant" (Luke 22:20), the covenant Jeremiah will announce in 31:31–34. Jeremiah was a trusting lamb led toward slaughter by his own townspeople, but God rescued him. Jesus was "led to the slaughter" by his own people as well (Isa 53:7; Acts 8:32), and this time the Lamb was not spared. "When he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23), the same trust Jeremiah expresses in v20, carried all the way to the cross and answered by resurrection.
