*Setting:* Some years have passed since Esther became queen. The decree in this chapter is dated to "the twelfth year" of Ahasuerus (v7), about 474 BC, five years after her coronation (2:16). Mordecai has saved the king's life and gone unrewarded (2:21–23), and Esther's Jewish identity is still a secret (2:20). Now the villain appears. Chapter 3 is where the story turns dark: a proud man's wounded pride becomes a royal order to wipe out an entire people, and the king signs it without asking who they are.

#### Haman Plots Against the Jews (vv1–15)
- **"After these things King Ahasuerus promoted Haman" (v1).** The man who saved the king is forgotten; another man is lifted to the top. The narrator lets the contrast sit without comment. "Set his throne above all the officials" means Haman became the king's chief minister, the second most powerful man in the empire. His chair literally sat higher than the others.
- **"Haman the Agagite, the son of Hammedatha" (v1).** "Agagite" most naturally points back to Agag, king of the Amalekites, whom Saul spared against God's command and Samuel put to death (1 Sam 15:8–33). Amalek was Israel's oldest enemy: the people who attacked the weak stragglers in the wilderness (Exod 17:8–16; Deut 25:17–19). Mordecai is introduced as a descendant of Kish, Saul's family (2:5). So the narrator sets a Benjaminite from Saul's line against a descendant of Agag, and an old unfinished war resumes. A few scholars suggest "Agagite" names a place or is simply a Persian family name, but most readers, ancient and modern, hear the echo of 1 Samuel 15, and the book seems to intend it. Josephus calls him an Amalekite outright, while the later Greek additions to Esther call him a Macedonian.
- **"Bowed down and paid homage… But Mordecai did not" (v2).** The two verbs mean to kneel and to bow down to the ground. Bowing to officials was normal in the ancient world, and Israelites did it without sin (Gen 23:7; 2 Sam 14:4; 1 Kings 1:16). So why does Mordecai refuse? The text gives only one clue: "he had told them that he was a Jew" (v4). There are three main views. Some think the Persian homage, called *proskynesis* by the Greeks, came close to worship. Others think Mordecai would not honor an Amalekite, given God's command about Amalek. Still others see personal pride or integrity. The book does not settle it, and the narrator does not criticize Mordecai.
- **"Why do you transgress the king's command?" (v3).** The other servants press him "day after day" (v4). The phrase echoes Joseph, whom Potiphar's wife pressed "day after day" (Gen 39:10). Mordecai, like Joseph, simply will not.
- **"To see whether Mordecai's words would stand" (v4).** The servants report him, partly to test whether being a Jew would excuse him. Mordecai's identity, which Esther keeps hidden, is out in the open in his case.
- **"Haman was filled with fury" (v5).** Haman did not notice the refusal until it was pointed out. Then one man's lack of a bow is enough to ruin his day. Pride of this kind cannot rest while anyone withholds honor (compare 5:13).
- **"He disdained to lay hands on Mordecai alone" (v6).** Killing one man was beneath him. "So… Haman sought to destroy all the Jews, the people of Mordecai, throughout the whole kingdom." That would include the Jews who had returned to Judah, which was part of the empire. The leap from one insult to genocide shows the hatred was there already, waiting for an excuse.
- **"They cast Pur (that is, they cast lots)" (v7).** *Pur* is a Babylonian (Akkadian) word, *pūru*, meaning a lot or die. The narrator explains it for Hebrew readers, because the festival of Purim will be named after it (9:24–26). Archaeologists have found an inscribed clay die of this kind from ancient Assyria. Persian advisers used lots and astrology to find lucky days. Haman's diviners began "in the first month, Nisan," the New Year, when Babylonians believed the gods fixed the destinies of the year. The lot fell on **"the twelfth month, Adar"**, eleven months away.
- **The lot and God.** Haman thinks chance or the gods have chosen a lucky day. The reader of Proverbs knows better: "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Prov 16:33). The long delay is exactly what gives Esther and Mordecai time to act.
- **"There is a certain people" (v8).** Haman never names them. He is a skilled liar. "Scattered abroad and dispersed" is true. "Their laws are different" is true: the law of Moses set Israel apart in food, Sabbath and worship (Lev 20:26). "They do not keep the king's laws" is false, apart from one man refusing a bow. "It is not to the king's profit to tolerate them" turns it into a matter of money and security. It is the oldest pattern of antisemitism: a half-truth about difference, a lie about disloyalty, and a claim that the nation would be better off without them.
- **"I will pay 10,000 talents of silver" (v9).** A talent weighed about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this was over 300 tons of silver. Herodotus puts the whole yearly tax income of the Persian Empire at about 14,500 talents, so Haman is offering roughly two-thirds of a year's revenue. He presumably expected to recover it from Jewish property, which the decree allows the killers to plunder (v13). Some think he was exaggerating to sound generous.
- **The signet ring (v10).** The king's ring carried his seal, used to stamp clay or wax on official documents. Handing it over meant handing over his authority to sign laws. Pharaoh gave Joseph his signet when he made him second in Egypt (Gen 41:42); here the same gesture puts power in the hands of a would-be mass murderer. The narrator now gives Haman his full title: **"the enemy of the Jews,"** a label that follows him to the end (8:1; 9:10, 24).
- **"The money is given to you, the people also" (v11).** This may mean the king refused the money, or, as in Near Eastern bargaining (compare Abraham and Ephron, Gen 23:10–16), it may be a polite way of accepting it. Mordecai later speaks of "the exact sum of money that Haman had promised to pay" (4:7), and Esther says "we have been sold" (7:4), so the money was at least on the table. Either way the king never asks who the people are, how many they are, or whether the charge is true.
- **"The thirteenth day of the first month" (v12).** The decree was written on Nisan 13, the eve of Passover (Exod 12:6). As Jews gathered to remember how God delivered them from Pharaoh, the news that they were to be destroyed would arrive. The reader is meant to hear the irony: the God of the exodus is not named, but his calendar is.
- **"To destroy, to kill, and to annihilate" (v13).** Three verbs piled on top of each other, the language of total destruction. **"Young and old, women and children, in one day."** The same three verbs come back word for word when the decree is reversed (8:11; 9:5). The Amalekite war in 1 Samuel 15 was also a command to destroy, and now an Agagite turns that language on Israel.
- **"To plunder their goods" (v13).** The killers could keep what they took. Haman bought his army with the victims' own property. When the Jews later defend themselves, the narrator stresses three times that "they laid no hand on the plunder" (9:10, 15, 16), perhaps a deliberate contrast with Saul, who did (1 Sam 15:9, 19).
- **Couriers (vv13–15).** The Persian royal road had relay stations a day's ride apart, so a message could cross the empire in about a week. Herodotus said of these riders that neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night stopped them, the words later carved on the New York post office.
- **"The king and Haman sat down to drink, but the city of Susa was thrown into confusion" (v15).** One of the coldest lines in the Bible. The two men who have just sentenced a people to death relax over wine. Meanwhile ordinary people in the city are bewildered. The word means confused, in turmoil. Even non-Jews in Susa could see something was badly wrong.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry and JFB both think Mordecai refused because the honor demanded went beyond civil respect toward something like worship, and because Haman was an Amalekite. JFB says Persian kings "demanded a sort of adoration, which… even the Greeks reckoned it degradation to express." Henry adds the Saul-and-Agag reading: "Saul the son of Kish, a Benjamite, spared Agag, but Mordecai the son of Kish… shall find no mercy with this Agagite." Guzik disagrees on the first point. He argues the Bible does not forbid bowing to rulers and that Mordecai acted from personal integrity, likely because of who Haman was. He calls Haman's charge a dangerous half-truth and suggests the king probably thought he was approving the execution of a few troublemakers. On v7 Guzik cites Proverbs 16:33. Henry's summary is vivid: "The head must come off that will not bow to Haman; if he cannot have his honours, he will have his blood."

#### Pointing to Christ
Haman's plan was not only against a people but against a promise. God had said the Messiah would come from Abraham, from Judah, from David's line. Had the Jews been wiped out, that promise would have died with them. The same pattern runs through Scripture: Pharaoh, Haman and Herod each tried to destroy the people or the child through whom God's salvation would come, and each failed (Exod 1; Matt 2; Rev 12:1–5). In the end Jesus himself was condemned by a ruler who did not care whether the charge was true, handed over for silver, and put to death by those who sat back satisfied. But the lot, and the cross, were in God's hand all along (Acts 2:23).
