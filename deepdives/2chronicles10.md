*Setting:* About 931–930 BC. Solomon has died after forty years on the throne (9:30–31), and his son Rehoboam, forty-one years old (12:13), expects to inherit the whole kingdom of Israel. Chronicles was written much later, after the exile, probably in the 400s BC, for a small community back in Judah that needed to know whether God's promises to David still stood. So the Chronicler tells Israel's story with his eye on David's line and the temple. He has just portrayed Solomon's reign at its most glorious and left out the dark ending that Kings records: Solomon's many foreign wives, his idolatry, God's anger, and the prophet Ahijah's announcement that ten tribes would be torn away and given to Jeroboam (1 Kgs 11:1–40). This chapter is copied almost word for word from 1 Kings 12:1–19, and it assumes the reader knows that earlier story, because verse 15 refers straight back to it. From here on, the Chronicler follows only the kings of Judah. The northern kingdom appears only when it touches David's line.

#### The Revolt Against Rehoboam (vv1–19)
- **"Rehoboam went to Shechem, for all Israel had come to Shechem to make him king" (v1).** Rehoboam does not summon the tribes to Jerusalem. He goes to them. Shechem lay in the hill country of Ephraim, between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, about 40 miles north of Jerusalem. It was full of memories: God's promise to Abraham (Gen 12:6–7), Joseph's burial (Josh 24:32), and Joshua's covenant renewal with all the tribes (Josh 24:1, 25). It was also where Abimelech once tried to make himself king (Judg 9). Going there shows that the northern tribes' loyalty to David's house could not be taken for granted. David himself had been made king of the north by a separate covenant (2 Sam 5:1–3), and those tribes had rebelled against him more than once (2 Sam 20:1–2).
- **"Jeroboam the son of Nebat… was in Egypt, where he had fled from King Solomon" (v2).** Jeroboam was an Ephraimite official whom Solomon put in charge of forced labor from the house of Joseph. The prophet Ahijah tore his new cloak into twelve pieces and gave Jeroboam ten, a sign that God would give him ten tribes because of Solomon's idolatry. Solomon tried to kill him, and he fled to Shishak king of Egypt (1 Kgs 11:26–40). The Chronicler mentions none of that here. He expects his readers to know it.
- **"They sent and called him. And Jeroboam and all Israel came" (v3).** The northern tribes want Jeroboam as their spokesman. He knew the labor system from the inside, and he had a prophet's word behind him.
- **"Your father made our yoke heavy" (v4).** A "yoke" is the wooden beam laid across an ox's neck, a standard picture for forced service. Solomon's building projects, his temple, his palace and his fortified cities, ran on heavy taxes and labor drafts (1 Kgs 4:7, 22–23; 5:13–14; 9:15). The Chronicler has presented Solomon at his best, but he does not hide this complaint. Notice what the people ask for. They do not complain about Solomon's idolatry. They want lighter taxes and shorter labor. **"Lighten… and we will serve you."** It is a reasonable offer: a contract between king and people, like the covenant David made with them.
- **"Come to me again in three days" (v5).** Taking time to think is wise. The question is whom he will listen to.
- **"The old men, who had stood before Solomon his father" (v6).** These were Solomon's senior advisers, men with long experience in government. "Stood before" means served in his presence.
- **"If you will be good to this people and please them and speak good words to them, then they will be your servants forever" (v7).** The parallel in Kings reads, "If you will be a servant to this people today… they will be your servants forever" (1 Kgs 12:7). The Chronicler softens "be a servant" into "be good," but the point is the same: serve them for a day, and they will serve you for life. Leadership by kindness wins lasting loyalty. Jesus turns this into a principle of his kingdom: "whoever would be great among you must be your servant" (Mark 10:43).
- **"He abandoned the counsel that the old men gave him" (v8).** "Abandoned" is the word the Chronicler often uses for forsaking the LORD (7:19, 22; 12:1, 5). Rehoboam had already decided what he wanted to hear.
- **"The young men who had grown up with him and stood before him" (v8).** They were not teenagers. Rehoboam was forty-one, so they were men about his age who had grown up in Solomon's luxurious court, possibly some of his many half-brothers. "Young" here is about outlook more than years: privileged men with no experience of hardship or of the people outside the palace.
- **"My little finger is thicker than my father's thighs" (v10).** The Hebrew is literally "my littleness" or "my little one." It is the language of a crude boast. Many scholars think it was a coarse joke about the king's manhood, and the translations politely say "little finger." Either way, it means: whatever you thought my father was, I am far worse.
- **"My father disciplined you with whips, but I will discipline you with scorpions" (v11).** "Scorpions" may be a word for a whip with barbed or weighted tips that tore the skin, or it may be a vivid picture: you complained about whips; wait until you feel stinging scorpions. Either way, it is a threat of harsher rule, from a king who has not yet even been confirmed.
- **"The king answered them harshly" (v13).** He repeats the young men's words almost exactly, though without the crude boast about his finger.
- **"It was a turn of affairs brought about by God that the LORD might fulfill his word, which he spoke by Ahijah the Shilonite" (v15).** This is the key verse. The phrase means "a turn of events from God." Rehoboam's folly was entirely his own. No one forced him. Yet behind it God was fulfilling the word he had spoken years earlier, judgment on Solomon's idolatry (1 Kgs 11:11–13, 31–33). Scripture holds both truths together without embarrassment: human beings freely choose and are responsible, and God rules over their choices to accomplish his purposes (Gen 50:20; Prov 21:1; Acts 2:23). The next chapter makes this explicit: "This thing is from me" (11:4).
- **"What portion have we in David? We have no inheritance in the son of Jesse" (v16).** This is almost the same cry Sheba raised in his revolt against David (2 Sam 20:1). The Chronicler has deliberately set it against an earlier scene in his own book, where a group of warriors from Benjamin and Judah came to David saying, "We are yours, O David, and with you, O son of Jesse!" (1 Chr 12:18). That loyalty is now reversed. **"Each of you to your tents, O Israel!"** was a call to go home and break up the assembly, a phrase dating from Israel's tent-dwelling days. **"Look now to your own house, David"** means "you are on your own now."
- **"Rehoboam reigned over the people of Israel who lived in the cities of Judah" (v17).** A few northerners living in Judah's towns stayed with him. Chronicles notes later that Benjamin went with Judah (11:1, 12), and priests, Levites and faithful worshipers from all the tribes moved south to Jerusalem (11:13–17). God's promise that one tribe would remain for David's sake (1 Kgs 11:13, 32, 36) held.
- **"Rehoboam sent Hadoram, who was taskmaster over the forced labor" (v18).** He is called Adoram in Kings (1 Kgs 12:18). An official with nearly the same name, Adoniram, oversaw forced labor under Solomon (1 Kgs 4:6; 5:14), and Adoram had held that job under David (2 Sam 20:24). He may be the same man, by now very old, or a relative. Sending him was a terrible mistake. It is like sending the tax collector to calm a tax revolt. **"The people of Israel stoned him to death."** Rehoboam jumps into his chariot and races back to Jerusalem, about 40 miles. He came to Shechem to be crowned and barely escaped with his life.
- **"So Israel has been in rebellion against the house of David to this day" (v19).** "To this day" is copied from Kings, written while the northern kingdom still existed. By the Chronicler's time the northern kingdom had been gone for three centuries, deported by Assyria in 722 BC, but the split between north and south was still a wound, and the Samaritans in the old northern territory were still hostile to Jerusalem (Ezra 4; Neh 4). The word for "rebellion" (*pasha'*) is often used for rebellion against God. The Chronicler sees the north's break from David's house as a break from God's chosen king and his temple. He makes that point openly in Abijah's speech a few chapters later (13:4–12).
- **What the Chronicler leaves out.** Kings goes on to describe Jeroboam's golden calves at Bethel and Dan and his new priesthood (1 Kgs 12:25–33). Chronicles saves that for later and mentions it only in passing (11:14–15; 13:8–9). Its focus stays on Judah, Jerusalem and the line of David, the line through which God's promise would continue.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "The wisest and best cannot give every body content," and suggests Solomon's heavy taxes may have paid for the luxury and idolatry of his later years. On Rehoboam: "Gentleness will do what violence will not do," and harsh answers "do but stir up anger and bring oil to the flames." On v15 he writes, "No more needs to be done to ruin men than to leave them to themselves, and their own pride and passion." JFB notes the chapter is the same as 1 Kings 12:1–19 "with a few verbal alterations," and says the people and the king acted as free agents while God intended the split as a punishment of David's house for Solomon's apostasy, divine predictions "accomplished by the operation of human passions." Guzik calls Rehoboam's search for agreeable advisers "advice shopping," seeking approval instead of truth. Like Henry, he insists God did not cause Rehoboam's sin but let him follow it, and he quotes F. B. Meyer that God makes the wrath of man praise him. Guzik also points out, through Knapp, that Rehoboam should have been grateful for the two tribes he kept.

#### Pointing to Christ
The people asked for a lighter yoke, and the son of David answered, "I will add to your yoke." A thousand years later another Son of David stood before weary people and said, "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you… for my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matt 11:28–30). Rehoboam refused to serve his people for one day. Jesus "came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). The split of the kingdom was a judgment, but the prophets promised it would not be the last word: God would one day make the two into one nation again under one king, "my servant David" (Ezek 37:15–24), a promise fulfilled in the Shepherd-King who gathers one flock (John 10:16).
