*Setting:* Directly after David's escape from Gath, where he pretended to be mad before King Achish (21:10–15). He has no home, no army and no standing at court. He lied to the priest Ahimelech at Nob to get bread and Goliath's sword, and Saul's servant Doeg the Edomite saw it all (21:7). This chapter sets two pictures side by side. In the first, David becomes a leader of outcasts in the wilderness, and a prophet and soon a priest join him, so God's guidance is now with David. In the second, Saul sits at home with his spear, sure that everyone is plotting against him, and orders the slaughter of the priests of the LORD. The contrast between the rejected king and the chosen one could hardly be sharper.

#### David at the Cave of Adullam (vv1–5)
- **"The cave of Adullam" (v1).** Adullam was an old Canaanite town in the low hills of Judah, about 16 miles southwest of Jerusalem and 10 or so from Bethlehem, near the Philistine border (Josh 15:35; Gen 38:1). The limestone hills there are full of caves. Some think "cave" should be read "stronghold," since the Hebrew words are close and the place is called "the stronghold" in vv4–5. Either way, it was a hiding place David knew from his boyhood.
- **"His brothers and all his father's house… went down there to him" (v1).** These are the same brothers who once mocked David on the battlefield (17:28). Saul's hatred now threatens the whole family, since in the ancient world a king often wiped out an enemy's relatives too, so they had good reason to leave Bethlehem.
- **Psalms from the cave.** The titles of Psalm 57 and Psalm 142 place them "when he fled from Saul, in the cave" and "when he was in the cave." That may be Adullam or the cave at En-gedi in chapter 24. Psalm 142 sounds like this moment: "no one cares for my soul… You are my refuge" (142:4–5).
- **"Everyone who was in distress, and everyone who was in debt, and everyone who was bitter in soul" (v2).** Saul's kingdom has produced a crowd of people with nowhere to go: those under pressure, those owing money they could not repay (who could be sold into slavery for it, 2 Kings 4:1), and those "bitter in soul," angry and hurt. David does not get the cream of Israel. He gets its leftovers, and he makes them into an army. Some of them later appear among his "mighty men," famous for their courage (2 Sam 23:8–39; 1 Chr 12:8). The same cave is where three of them broke through Philistine lines to bring David water from Bethlehem's well (2 Sam 23:13–17).
- **"About four hundred men" (v2).** Like Jephthah in Judges 11:3, David now leads a band of outsiders. The number will grow to six hundred (23:13).
- **"Mizpeh of Moab" (v3).** "Mizpeh" means "watchtower." This was a fortress somewhere in Moab, east of the Dead Sea. David's great-grandmother Ruth was a Moabite (Ruth 4:17–22), which may explain why the king of Moab took in his parents. Saul had also fought Moab (14:47), so its king had reasons to welcome Saul's enemy.
- **"Till I know what God will do for me" (v3).** David does not know the plan. He only knows that God has one. He cares for his elderly parents while his own life is in danger, a quiet act of obeying "honor your father and your mother."
- **"The prophet Gad said to David" (v5).** This is Gad's first appearance. He will serve David for decades, bringing God's word on the census (2 Sam 24:11–19) and helping arrange the temple music (2 Chr 29:25). Saul has lost Samuel and God no longer answers him (28:6). David now has a prophet.
- **"Go into the land of Judah" (v5).** The advice is counterintuitive. Moab was safer, but David belongs in his own tribe's land, where the people would come to know him as their protector and, in time, as their king. He obeys and goes to "the forest of Hereth," a wooded area in Judah whose exact location is unknown.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees a picture of Christ: "The Son of David is ready to receive distressed souls, who will be commanded by him," and draws a lesson from David's care for his parents: "Let children learn to honour their parents." Guzik links the name Adullam with "refuge" and points out that the LORD, not the cave, is David's real refuge. He sees in the 400 the way God gathers unlikely people and shapes them, and observes that God rarely calls a leader to work alone. JFB says Gad's advice "came from a higher source than Gad's own sagacity." The meaning of the name Adullam is actually uncertain, so the "refuge" idea is best kept as a devotional picture, not a word fact.

#### Saul Kills the Priests at Nob (vv6–23)
- **"Saul was sitting at Gibeah under the tamarisk tree on the height with his spear in his hand" (v6).** Kings often held court in the open air under a notable tree (compare Deborah under her palm, Judg 4:5). The spear has been Saul's symbol all along (18:10; 19:9–10; 20:33). He sits with it ready, and he will use it to the end.
- **"Hear now, people of Benjamin" (v7).** Saul's officials are men of his own tribe. He plays on tribal loyalty: will the son of Jesse, a man of Judah, give Benjaminites fields and vineyards and officer posts? This is exactly what Samuel warned that a king would do, taking the best fields and giving them to his servants (8:14). Saul has built a court on favors and now fears losing it.
- **"The son of Jesse" (vv7–9, 13).** Saul refuses to call David by name. The phrase is meant to belittle him as a nobody's son.
- **"None of you is sorry for me" (v8).** The self-pity is striking. Saul accuses his own son of stirring up David "to lie in wait," which was never true. David has been running, not plotting. Saul imagines a conspiracy everywhere.
- **"Then answered Doeg the Edomite" (vv9–10).** Doeg sees his chance. He tells the truth, but only part of it. He says Ahimelech "inquired of the LORD" for David, which Ahimelech seems to admit (v15) though 21:1–9 does not mention it. He leaves out that David claimed to be on the king's business. Psalm 52, titled "when Doeg, the Edomite, came and told Saul," describes him: "You love evil more than good, and lying more than speaking what is right" (52:3).
- **"All his father's house, the priests who were at Nob" (v11).** Saul summons the entire priestly family. The trial is decided before it starts.
- **"Who among all your servants is so faithful as David…?" (v14).** Ahimelech's defense is honest and brave. David is the king's son-in-law, captain of the bodyguard, honored in the palace. Of course a priest would help him. "Is today the first time that I have inquired of God for him?" (v15) suggests David had sought God's guidance through the priests before, while serving Saul. Ahimelech says he "has known nothing of all this, much or little," which was true, because David had lied to him.
- **"You shall surely die, Ahimelech, you and all your father's house" (v16).** Saul does not weigh the defense. He passes sentence.
- **"The servants of the king would not put out their hand" (v17).** Saul's own guards refuse. Once before, the army refused to let Saul kill Jonathan (14:45). Again his own people see what Saul will not. Like the Egyptian midwives who would not kill Hebrew babies (Exod 1:17), they fear God more than the king.
- **"Doeg the Edomite turned and struck down the priests" (v18).** A foreigner from Esau's people does what no Israelite would. He kills eighty-five men "who wore the linen ephod," the plain linen garment of serving priests (2:18). The Greek Septuagint gives the number as 305, but the Hebrew reads 85.
- **"Both man and woman, child and infant, ox, donkey and sheep" (v19).** The list is the very wording of the ban Saul was ordered to carry out on the Amalekites (15:3). There he spared the king and the best animals. Here he wipes out a whole town of Israel's own priests. His zeal is real, but it is aimed at the wrong target.
- **The word against Eli's house.** Years earlier a man of God told Eli that his family would be cut off and that no old man would be left in his house, except one kept to weep (2:31–33). Ahimelech was Eli's descendant. Saul is responsible for his own crime, yet the prophecy is being fulfilled through it. Its final stage comes when Solomon removes Abiathar from the priesthood (1 Kings 2:27).
- **"One of the sons of Ahimelech… named Abiathar, escaped and fled after David" (v20).** Abiathar carries the ephod used to seek God's will (23:6). From now on David can ask the LORD for direction (23:2, 9–12; 30:7–8), and Saul cannot (28:6). Abiathar serves David for the rest of his reign.
- **"I have occasioned the death of all the persons of your father's house" (v22).** David does not blame Doeg or Saul alone. He admits his own part. He had seen Doeg at Nob and guessed what would happen, and his lie had left the priests defenseless. It is a costly confession, and it shows the kind of heart that will later say "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Sam 12:13).
- **"Stay with me… With me you shall be in safekeeping" (v23).** The man who brought disaster on Abiathar's family now binds himself to protect the survivor. They share the same enemy and the same danger.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of Saul, "Nothing so vile but those may do it, who have provoked God to give them up to their hearts' lusts," and of the massacre's link to Eli, "No word of God shall fall to the ground." He also notes, "It is great trouble to a good man to find himself any way the cause of evil to others." JFB praises the guards, who "chose rather to disobey the king than to offend God," and calls Ahimelech "the poor simple-minded high priest" who knew nothing of the feud. Guzik contrasts Saul's reluctance to obey God against Amalek with his eagerness to kill priests, and says David's confession was real but the bad fruit of his lie remained. The Tyndale notes draw the same Amalek contrast.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two kings are on display. One sits with his spear, demanding loyalty and killing the innocent to keep his throne. The other, hunted and in a cave, gathers the distressed, the indebted and the bitter, and makes them his people. Jesus is the greater Son of David who says, "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden" (Matt 11:28). He too was rejected by the rulers of his day, and his followers were not the impressive ones (1 Cor 1:26–28). David could promise Abiathar safety only as long as David himself survived. Christ offers lasting safety because he died and rose, and no one can snatch his people out of his hand (John 10:28).
