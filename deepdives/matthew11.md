*Setting:* Galilee, around AD 28–29. Chapter 10 sent the Twelve out to preach and heal in the towns of Israel, and chapter 11 opens with Jesus going out to preach as well. John the Baptist, who announced Jesus in chapter 3, has been in prison since 4:12. The Jewish historian Josephus says Herod Antipas held him at Machaerus, a fortress east of the Dead Sea. This chapter begins a new stage in Matthew. From here to chapter 12, the main theme is how people respond to Jesus, and the answer is increasingly mixed. John wonders, the crowds cannot be pleased, and the towns that saw the most miracles do not repent. Then, at the end, comes one of the warmest invitations in the Bible: "Come to me… and I will give you rest."

#### Messengers from John the Baptist (vv1–19)
- **"When Jesus had finished instructing his twelve disciples" (v1).** This is the formula that closes each of Matthew's five teaching blocks (7:28; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). The mission speech is over.
- **"When John heard in prison about the deeds of the Christ" (v2).** Matthew uses "the Christ" here on purpose: the deeds John hears about are messianic deeds. Note the word "deeds" (*erga*). It comes back at the end of this section (v19).
- **"Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?" (v3).** "The one who is to come" was John's own phrase for the Messiah (3:11). Why would John ask? He had preached a Messiah with an axe and a winnowing fork, who would burn the chaff (3:10–12). Jesus was healing, eating with sinners, and leaving the wicked in power, while John sat in Herod's prison. Most readers think John's question was real, a faithful man's struggle under suffering and disappointed expectations. Some, including many older interpreters, think John asked for his disciples' sake. The text gives no hint of rebuke from Jesus, only an answer.
- **"Go and tell John what you hear and see" (v4).** Jesus does not say "yes." He points to what is happening and lets the evidence speak in the words of Isaiah. "The blind receive their sight… the lame walk… the deaf hear" (Isa 35:5–6), "the dead… shall rise" (Isa 26:19), "good news to the poor" (Isa 61:1). Every item has happened in chapters 8–9. John knew these prophecies well enough to draw the conclusion.
- **What Jesus leaves out.** Isaiah 61:1–2 also speaks of "liberty to the captives" and "the day of vengeance of our God." Jesus does not mention those to the prisoner. The judgment John expected is real, but it comes later. The kingdom comes first in mercy.
- **"Blessed is the one who is not offended by me" (v6).** "Offended" (*skandalizō*) means to stumble or fall away. It is a gentle warning and a blessing: happy is the one who does not trip over the way Jesus fulfils God's plan, even when it is not what was expected.
- **"A reed shaken by the wind?" (v7).** Reeds grew thick along the Jordan where John preached, and swayed with every breeze. Some scholars note that Herod Antipas put a reed on his coins, so there may be a sly contrast. John was no reed. He told a king to his face that his marriage was wrong, and paid for it.
- **"A man dressed in soft clothing?" (v8).** John wore camel's hair (3:4). Soft clothing belonged in palaces, where John now was, but as a prisoner, not a courtier.
- **"More than a prophet" (v9).** John was a prophet, and more: he was the prophet who was himself the subject of prophecy. Jesus quotes Malachi 3:1, with an echo of Exodus 23:20: "I send my messenger before your face." In Malachi, God sends a messenger to prepare *his own* way. Here, the messenger prepares the way "before *you*," meaning before Jesus.
- **"No one greater than John… Yet the one who is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he" (v11).** John stands at the summit of the old order, the last and greatest of the prophets. But he stands at the border of the kingdom, not inside its full blessing. The least disciple who lives after the cross and resurrection, with the Spirit given, sees more than John saw. This is about privilege, not about personal holiness.
- **"The kingdom of heaven has suffered violence, and the violent take it by force" (v12).** This is one of the hardest verses in the Gospels. The verb (*biazō*) can be passive, "suffers violence," or middle, "advances forcefully," and the lexicon lists both. Main readings: (1) the kingdom is being attacked by violent men, like Herod who imprisoned John; (2) the kingdom is forcefully advancing, and determined people press into it; (3) a mix, where the kingdom breaks in with power and also meets violent opposition. Luke's similar saying, "everyone forces his way into it" (Luke 16:16), supports a positive sense for the second half. John's chains support the negative sense for the first.
- **"All the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John" (v13).** Everything before pointed forward. With John, the waiting ends.
- **"He is Elijah who is to come" (v14).** Malachi's last prophecy promised that God would send "Elijah the prophet" before the great day of the LORD (Mal 4:5–6). John dressed like Elijah (2 Kings 1:8) and came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17). When asked, John denied being Elijah in person (John 1:21). Jesus' "if you are willing to accept it" shows that John fulfils the role, not that Elijah came back to life. Jesus says it again in 17:10–13.
- **"Children sitting in the marketplaces" (vv16–17).** Village children played at weddings and funerals. One group pipes a dance tune and the others will not dance. They switch to a funeral lament and the others will not mourn. "This generation" is like the sulky children who will not play any game.
- **"John came neither eating nor drinking… The Son of Man came eating and drinking" (vv18–19).** John fasted and lived in the desert, and they said he was demon-possessed. Jesus feasted with tax collectors, and they called him "a glutton and a drunkard." That phrase comes from Deuteronomy 21:20, which describes a rebellious son who was to be put to death. It was a serious charge. People who do not want God's message find a reason to dismiss every messenger.
- **"A friend of tax collectors and sinners" (v19).** Meant as an insult, it has become one of the most loved titles of Jesus.
- **"Wisdom is justified by her deeds" (v19).** God's wisdom, seen in both John's way and Jesus' way, is proven right by what it produces. Luke has "by all her children" (Luke 7:35), and some manuscripts of Matthew read "children" too. But "deeds" fits Matthew, since it repeats the "deeds of the Christ" from v2. The section is bracketed: the deeds that raised John's question are the deeds that answer it.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks John asked partly for his own assurance and partly for his disciples, adding "it is good to be sure." He reads v12 as "a holy violence; self must be denied." Guzik thinks John's doubt came from prison and from expecting a political Messiah, and on v12 he follows D.A. Carson's reading that the kingdom advances forcefully and demands a determined response. Guzik also notes that the insult about being a friend of sinners has become a title of honor. JFB does not comment on vv3–19, referring readers to Luke 7.

#### Woe to Unrepentant Cities (vv20–24)
- **"The cities where most of his mighty works had been done" (v20).** Jesus now speaks judgment, and the reason is stated plainly: "because they did not repent." They saw and were impressed, but did not turn.
- **Chorazin and Bethsaida (v21).** Chorazin was a town about two miles north of Capernaum. Its ruins, including a black basalt synagogue from a later century, still stand. The Gospels mention it only here and in Luke's parallel, and they record no miracle there. Bethsaida, "house of fishing," was the hometown of Peter, Andrew and Philip (John 1:44), on the north shore. Many of Jesus' works went unrecorded (John 21:25).
- **"Tyre and Sidon" (v21).** These were wealthy Phoenician ports on the Mediterranean coast, whose pride and idolatry the prophets condemned (Isa 23; Ezek 26–28). Jesus says that if they had seen what these Jewish towns saw, they would have repented "in sackcloth and ashes," the rough cloth and dust of mourning and repentance (Jonah 3:6).
- **"More bearable on the day of judgment" (v22).** Judgment will take into account how much light people had. Greater privilege means greater responsibility (Luke 12:47–48). Scripture speaks of degrees in judgment as well as in reward.
- **"And you, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven? You will be brought down to Hades" (v23).** Jesus echoes Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon, who said "I will ascend to heaven" and was "brought down to Sheol" (Isa 14:13–15). "Hades" is the Greek equivalent of *Sheol*, the realm of the dead. Capernaum was Jesus' own town and had seen more than anywhere else. Its ruins are a reminder that privilege is not protection.
- **"If the mighty works done in you had been done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day" (v23).** Sodom is the Bible's byword for wickedness (Gen 19). Jesus says even Sodom would have repented. The verse also shows that God knows what would have happened under other circumstances.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "Sodom will have many things to answer for, but not the sin of neglecting Christ." JFB calls Capernaum "the most favored spot upon earth," since Jesus made it his base, and calls Bethsaida "a fishing station." Guzik draws the principle that greater light brings greater accountability, and applies it as a warning to places with easy access to the gospel. He passes on William Barclay's point that simply ignoring Jesus can be as deadly as opposing him.

#### Come to Me, and I Will Give You Rest (vv25–30)
- **"At that time Jesus declared" (v25).** Right after the woes, Jesus prays. The rejection does not leave him bitter. He thanks his Father.
- **"Lord of heaven and earth" (v25).** He addresses God as Father and as sovereign Creator, the one who decides what is revealed and to whom.
- **"Hidden these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to little children" (v25).** "The wise" here are those who think themselves wise: the scribes and Pharisees, experts who saw Jesus' works and rejected him. "Little children" (*nēpioi*) are the simple and humble: fishermen, tax collectors, the sick and the poor. God is not against learning. He is against pride, and he gives understanding to those who know they need it (1 Cor 1:26–29).
- **"Yes, Father, for such was your gracious will" (v26).** "Gracious will" (*eudokia*) is the word for God's good pleasure, the same family as "with whom I am well pleased" at his baptism (3:17). Jesus delights in the Father's way of doing things.
- **"All things have been handed over to me by my Father" (v27).** This verse sounds like John's Gospel (John 3:35; 10:15; 17:2), which shows that the claims there are not a later invention. The Father has given the Son everything, including the right to reveal God. "No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him." Jesus claims a relationship with God that is unique and mutual, and says that only through him can anyone know God truly (John 14:6).
- **"Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden" (v28).** The one who alone can reveal the Father now invites everyone. "Labor" means to toil until worn out. "Heavy laden" means loaded down like a pack animal. In context, the burden includes the endless rules the Pharisees laid on people (23:4), but also the weight of sin, guilt and life itself. The call is not to a system or institution, but to a person: "to *me*."
- **"I will give you rest" (v28).** Rest was promised to Israel in the land (Deut 12:9), and Hebrews says that rest pointed beyond itself to the rest God gives in Christ (Heb 4:8–10). Notably, the very next scenes are two Sabbath controversies (12:1–14), where Jesus claims to be Lord of the Sabbath, the day of rest.
- **"Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me" (v29).** A yoke was the wooden frame fitted across the necks of oxen so they could pull together. Jews spoke of "the yoke of the Torah" or "the yoke of the kingdom" for the duty of obeying God. A Jewish wisdom book written about two centuries earlier pictures Wisdom calling: "Put your neck under her yoke… she is close to those who seek her" (Sirach 51:26). Jesus speaks as Wisdom herself, and invites people to his own yoke. Rest does not come by having no yoke, but by taking the right one.
- **"For I am gentle and lowly in heart" (v29).** "Gentle" (*praus*) is the same word as "meek" in "Blessed are the meek" (5:5). "Lowly" (*tapeinos*) means humble. The one to whom all things are given describes his own heart this way. This is the only place in the Gospels where Jesus describes his own heart.
- **"You will find rest for your souls" (v29).** This quotes Jeremiah 6:16, where God tells Israel to walk in the old good way "and find rest for your souls," and they answer, "We will not walk in it." Jesus is that good way.
- **"My yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (v30).** "Easy" (*chrēstos*) means good, kind, pleasant or serviceable. The lexicon notes that the same word describes God's kindness elsewhere (Luke 6:35; Rom 2:4). A good yoke was well made and did not chafe. Jesus' demands are not small (5:48; 10:38), but his yoke fits, because he carries it with the one who wears it, and his commands are not burdensome (1 John 5:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry says God "is not the Author of their ignorance and error": the proud are left in their own blindness. He reads the invitation as open to those who feel sin's burden, and calls Christ's yoke "a yoke that is lined with love." JFB calls v28 "Incomparable, ravishing sounds these" and says that rest in Christ "makes all yokes easy, all burdens light." Guzik explains *chrēstos* as "well-fitting," like a carpenter's ox yoke shaped so it would not rub. The lexicon gives "good," "kind" and "serviceable," which allows the idea but does not spell it out. Guzik's picture of Jesus as the strong ox pulling alongside the young one is a fair illustration rather than something the word itself says.

#### Pointing to Christ
John asked whether Jesus was the one, and the answer was in his deeds: the blind seeing and the poor hearing good news. The towns that saw those deeds missed him. The children grasped what the wise could not. And then the Son, who alone knows the Father, opens the door to everyone who is worn out: "Come to me." He is not a harsh master adding to the load. He is gentle and lowly in heart. Not long after, he took up the heaviest burden of all, the sin of the world, so that those who come to him could find rest for their souls.
