*Setting:* After the "The LORD reigns" psalms (93–100), which celebrated God as King, Psalm 101 gives the vow of an earthly king who wants to rule under him. The title is "A Psalm of David," and it reads like a pledge made at the start of a reign. Many think it fits David's early years as king, after Saul's troubled rule, perhaps around the time he brought the ark to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6). Others think it was used at the coronation of later kings of David's line. David makes a set of promises: first about his own heart and home (vv1–4), then about the people he will keep near him and the people he will remove (vv5–8). In later centuries it was known in Europe as "the prince's psalm," a mirror for rulers. Martin Luther wrote a long exposition of it, and a story is told of Ernest the Pious, a 17th-century German duke, who would send a copy of Psalm 101 to any official who misbehaved. The standards are high, and David himself fell badly short of them (2 Sam 11). That is part of what makes the psalm point beyond him. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow its two halves.

#### I Will Walk with Integrity of Heart (vv1–4)
- **"I will sing of steadfast love and justice" (v1).** "Steadfast love" (*hesed*) is God's loyal covenant love, and "justice" (*mishpat*) is right judgment. Some read these as God's love and justice toward David, which he praises. Others read them as the qualities David vows to show as king. Both fit, and they belong together: a king can only rule with love and justice if he has first received them from God. The same pair appears in Micah 6:8: "to do justice, and to love kindness (*hesed*)."
- **"To you, O LORD, I will make music" (v1).** The vow is set to music and sung to God. Henry suggests David put it in a song so he could remember it and so his family would learn it.
- **"I will ponder the way that is blameless" (v2).** "Ponder" (*askilah*) means to give careful attention, to act wisely. The KJV has "I will behave myself wisely," and 1 Samuel 18:14 uses the same verb of the young David: "David had success (*maskil*) in all his undertakings, for the LORD was with him." "Blameless" (*tamim*) means whole, complete, with integrity. It does not mean sinless, but a life that is all of one piece before God (Gen 17:1).
- **"Oh when will you come to me?" (v2).** The cry breaks into the vow. David longs for God's presence. Some connect it with the ark: when Uzzah died, David asked, "How can the ark of the LORD come to me?" (2 Sam 6:9), and he waited three months before bringing it up. The psalm may echo that longing. In any case, David knows his vows are empty unless God comes to him.
- **"I will walk with integrity of heart within my house" (v2).** "Within my house" may mean his family life, his palace and court, or both. Either way, he begins where he is least watched. A king could be careful in public and do as he pleased at home. David resolves that integrity starts in private. Paul says the same of church leaders: "he must manage his own household well" (1 Tim 3:4–5).
- **"I will not set before my eyes anything that is worthless" (v3).** "Worthless" is literally "a thing of *beliyya'al*," a word for what is good-for-nothing, base, destructive, later used as a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15). The eyes are the gateway to desire. Job said, "I have made a covenant with my eyes" (Job 31:1). Sadly, it was through his eyes, on a palace roof, that David later fell into adultery and murder (2 Sam 11:2).
- **"I hate the work of those who fall away; it shall not cling to me" (v3).** "Those who fall away" (*setim*) are those who swerve or turn aside from God's way. Their "work" is like something sticky that clings once touched. David will keep clear of it.
- **"A perverse heart shall be far from me; I will know nothing of evil" (v4).** "Perverse" (*iqqesh*) means twisted, crooked, the opposite of the straight path (Prov 11:20; 17:20). "Know" here means to be intimate with, to be involved in. He will not make evil his familiar companion.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this psalm "an excellent plan or model for the good government of a court," but also "the householder's psalm." He says "it is not enough to put on our religion when we go abroad," and notes from 1 Samuel 18:14 that "David, as he purposed, behaved himself wisely in all his ways; and, as he prayed, the Lord was with him." On "mercy and judgment" he observes that God's providences are "commonly mixed," like "April-days, showers and sunshine." JFB reads v3 as "no wicked thing—literally, 'word,' plan or purpose of Belial," and "turn aside" as "apostates." Guzik notes that power tends to expose flaws of character, and that falling short of a high standard is not hypocrisy. Hypocrisy, he says, is holding others to a standard higher than the one held for oneself. He also points to David's later failures through the eyes (2 Sam 11:2).

#### I Will Look with Favor on the Faithful (vv5–8)
- **"Whoever slanders his neighbor secretly I will destroy" (v5).** Now David turns from his own life to his court. A royal court was full of whisperers trying to gain favor by running others down. David himself had suffered from slander at Saul's court (1 Sam 24:9; 26:19). "Slanders" (*melawshni*) comes from the word for "tongue." "Destroy" (*atsmit*) means to put an end to, silence. As a king he had real power to remove such people.
- **"Whoever has a haughty look and an arrogant heart I will not endure" (v5).** "Haughty look" is literally "high of eyes," and "arrogant heart" is "broad of heart," puffed up. Proverbs lists "haughty eyes" first among the things the LORD hates (Prov 6:16–17). The king will not put up with pride in those around him.
- **"I will look with favor on the faithful in the land, that they may dwell with me" (v6).** David's eyes will not rest on worthless things (v3), but on faithful people. "Faithful" (*ne'emanim*) are the trustworthy, reliable ones. He wants them near him, at his table and in his service. JFB paraphrases: "I will select reliable and honest men for my servants."
- **"He who walks in the way that is blameless shall minister to me" (v6).** The same phrase from v2 returns. What David vowed for himself he looks for in his officials. "Minister" (*sharat*) means to serve, the word for officials at court and for priests at the altar.
- **"No one who practices deceit shall dwell in my house" (v7).** Deceit in the king's house poisons everything. Later David's own court was torn apart by deceit, when his son Absalom stole the people's hearts at the city gate (2 Sam 15:1–6) and his adviser Ahithophel joined the revolt (2 Sam 15:31). **"No one who utters lies shall continue before my eyes."** JFB says "continue" is literally "not sit" or "be established."
- **"Morning by morning I will destroy all the wicked in the land" (v8).** Kings in Israel heard cases in the morning at the city gate (2 Sam 15:2; Jer 21:12: "Execute justice in the morning"). "Morning by morning" means every day, steadily, as a regular duty. It does not mean a daily massacre. It means that each day the king will judge cases and not let wrongdoers off.
- **"Cutting off all the evildoers from the city of the LORD" (v8).** The psalm ends with its reason. Jerusalem is not just David's capital. It is "the city of the LORD," where God's name and ark were. Evil does not belong there. The king's justice serves God's holiness.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that David "does not mention drunkards, adulterers, murderers or blasphemers," since he was in little danger of employing such men, but names the subtler sins of court life: slander, pride and deceit. He says David will not employ the vicious, "lest they should spread the infection of sin in his family." JFB reads "early" as "diligently," and "city of the Lord" as the "holy place" (Ps 48:2), "where wicked men shall not be tolerated." Guzik notes that a ruler who wants to live rightly must choose carefully whom he keeps close, recalling Paul's warning that "bad company ruins good morals" (1 Cor 15:33).

#### Pointing to Christ
David did not keep this vow. He set a worthless thing before his eyes, his house was torn by deceit and violence, and the king who promised to destroy the wicked became a murderer himself. The Tyndale notes say plainly that Jesus is the one who meets this ideal. He is the Son of David who always walked "in the way that is blameless" (Heb 4:15; 7:26), whose eyes were never set on anything worthless, and in whose mouth "no deceit" was found (1 Pet 2:22). He loves righteousness and hates wickedness (Heb 1:9). He chooses the faithful to dwell with him, and promises that no one who "practices abomination or falsehood" will enter the city of God (Rev 21:27). But he also does what David's psalm could not: he makes sinners faithful, cleansing them so that they can dwell with him. The king who will one day cut off evildoers from the city of the LORD first died for evildoers so that they could be welcomed into it.
