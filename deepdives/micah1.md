*Setting:* Judah, roughly 740–700 BC. Micah preached in the same years as Isaiah, through the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah (2 Chr 27–32). Isaiah moved among kings and priests in Jerusalem. Micah was a country man from Moresheth, a small town in the foothills about 25 miles southwest of the capital, near the Philistine border, and he spoke for farmers losing their land to the rich (2:1–2). His years saw the Assyrian empire destroy the northern kingdom and its capital Samaria (722 BC), and then sweep through Judah's towns under Sennacherib (701 BC), stopping only at the gates of Jerusalem. The book falls into three rounds, each opening with the call "Hear" (1:2; 3:1; 6:1), and each moving from judgment to hope. A century later, elders in Jerusalem quoted Micah by name and remembered that King Hezekiah had listened and repented (Jer 26:17–19). Chapter 1 opens with God coming down to judge, first Samaria and then Judah, and closes with a funeral song full of puns on the names of Micah's own neighbouring towns.

#### The Coming Destruction (vv1–16)
- **"The word of the LORD that came to Micah of Moresheth" (v1).** Micah is a short form of Micaiah, "Who is like the LORD?" The book ends by turning his name into praise: "Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity?" (7:18). Moresheth (called Moresheth-gath in v14) lay in the Shephelah, the low hills between Judah's highlands and the coastal plain, the very region the Assyrians would overrun.
- **"In the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah" (v1).** About sixty years spanning a good king, one of the worst, and one of the best. Micah's harshest charges fit the years of Ahaz and before Hezekiah's reforms (2 Chr 28–29). Unlike Isaiah and Hosea, he leaves out Uzziah and the northern kings.
- **"Which he saw concerning Samaria and Jerusalem" (v1).** The prophets "saw" the word, meaning God gave it in visions as well as words. The two capitals stand for the two kingdoms. Micah speaks to both, though most of the book is aimed at Judah.
- **"Hear, you peoples, all of you" (v2).** These exact words were the last recorded words of an earlier prophet with nearly the same name, Micaiah son of Imlah, when he told King Ahab he would die in battle (1 Kgs 22:28). Micah may be consciously picking up where his namesake left off. The verb is *shama'*, the word that begins Israel's creed, "Hear, O Israel" (Deut 6:4): listen, and obey.
- **"Let the Lord GOD be a witness against you, the Lord from his holy temple" (v2).** The scene is a courtroom, and the whole earth is summoned to watch. The "holy temple" here is God's dwelling in heaven, as the next verse makes clear, not the corrupted temple in Jerusalem.
- **"The LORD is coming out of his place, and will come down and tread upon the high places of the earth" (v3).** This is the language of a theophany, God appearing in power (Amos 4:13; Judg 5:4–5). "High places" plays on two senses: the mountain heights, and the hilltop shrines where Israel worshipped idols. The Tyndale notes point out that Canaanites said much the same of Baal, the storm god who strode over the mountains. Micah says the LORD alone does this.
- **"The mountains will melt under him… like wax before the fire" (v4).** The picture is of earthquake and fire, the most solid things in creation dissolving like candle wax or water rushing down a slope (Ps 97:5; Nah 1:5). If the mountains cannot stand before him, neither can a sinful city.
- **"All this is for the transgression of Jacob" (v5).** The cosmic upheaval has a moral cause. Jacob and Israel here mean the whole covenant people. Judah might have expected God to come down against the pagan nations. Instead the trial begins with his own household (compare 1 Pet 4:17).
- **"What is the transgression of Jacob? Is it not Samaria? And what is the high place of Judah? Is it not Jerusalem?" (v5).** This is the shock of the chapter. The capitals, which should have been centres of justice and true worship, are the sources of the rot. Samaria was built by the wicked king Omri (1 Kgs 16:24–25). But Micah puts Jerusalem, with its temple, in the same sentence and calls it a pagan "high place." The Hebrew text has the plural "high places"; the Greek translation reads "sin of the house of Judah." The point is the same in either reading.
- **"I will make Samaria a heap in the open country, a place for planting vineyards" (v6).** Samaria stood on a hill. Micah pictures its walls pushed down the slopes and its foundations laid bare, the site turned back into farmland. Assyria took the city after a three-year siege in 722 BC (2 Kgs 17:5–6). Archaeologists have found the Israelite royal quarter on the hilltop, and the slopes below are still farmed today.
- **"For from the fee of a prostitute she gathered them, and to the fee of a prostitute they shall return" (v7).** The word is *'etnan*, a prostitute's pay (Deut 23:18; Hos 9:1). Idolatry is spiritual adultery, and Canaanite fertility worship often involved actual temple prostitution. The gold and silver offered at those shrines paid for Samaria's idols. Now conquering soldiers will carry the idols off, melt them down and spend them on prostitutes in their own temples. What was gained by unfaithfulness is lost the same way.
- **"For this I will lament and wail; I will go stripped and naked" (v8).** The prophet becomes the chief mourner. "Stripped and naked" probably means barefoot and wearing only a loincloth, the dress of a mourner or a prisoner of war. Isaiah did the same thing in these years as a sign of coming captivity (Isa 20:2–4). Micah does not announce judgment with satisfaction. It breaks his heart.
- **"Like the jackals… like the ostriches" (v8).** Both live in deserted places and make eerie, wailing cries at night. The old KJV had "dragons" and "owls," but jackals and ostriches are what the Hebrew words most likely mean.
- **"For her wound is incurable, and it has come to Judah; it has reached to the gate of my people, to Jerusalem" (v9).** Samaria's disease is spreading south. "Incurable" means the people would not repent, and so the judgment could not be turned aside. "To the gate" is exact: in 701 BC Sennacherib reached Jerusalem and surrounded it, but the city did not fall (2 Kgs 18–19). Hezekiah turned to God, partly under Micah's preaching (Jer 26:19), and God struck the Assyrian camp.
- **A lament made of puns (vv10–16).** Micah now names about a dozen towns, most of them in his own home region, and in almost every line he plays on the sound or meaning of a town's name. The effect is something like saying "Dustbury rolls in the dust" or "Bittersfield waits for good." The Hebrew of these verses is among the hardest in the prophets, and translations differ, but the main puns are clear. The towns lie roughly along the path an army would take from the coast toward Jerusalem, and the Tyndale notes connect them with Sennacherib's march in 701 BC. His own records boast that he took forty-six walled towns of Judah.
- **"Tell it not in Gath; weep not at all" (v10).** Micah borrows David's lament over Saul: "Tell it not in Gath… lest the daughters of the Philistines rejoice" (2 Sam 1:20). Gath sounds like the Hebrew for "tell." Do not let the enemy gloat. **"In Beth-le-aphrah roll yourselves in the dust."** The name means "house of dust" (*'aphar*), so the town of dust is told to roll in it, a mourning gesture.
- **"Inhabitants of Shaphir, in nakedness and shame" (v11).** Shaphir means "beautiful" or "pleasant." Beauty-town will go into exile stripped and shamed. **"The inhabitants of Zaanan do not come out."** Zaanan sounds like the verb *yatsa'*, "go out." Go-out-town will not dare go out, hiding behind its walls. **"The lamentation of Beth-ezel."** The name probably means "house beside" or "nearby house." The neighbour town will be no help to anyone; its "standing place" (support) is taken away.
- **"The inhabitants of Maroth wait anxiously for good, because disaster has come down from the LORD" (v12).** Maroth means "bitterness." They hope for good and get bitterness. Notice the source: disaster "from the LORD." Assyria is only the instrument. And again, it reaches "to the gate of Jerusalem."
- **"Harness the steeds to the chariots, inhabitants of Lachish" (v13).** Lachish sounds like *rekesh*, "steeds." Lachish was Judah's second city, a great fortress guarding the road from the coast. Sennacherib besieged and took it, and he was so proud of it that he lined a room of his palace at Nineveh with carved panels of the siege. Those reliefs are now in the British Museum, along with an Assyrian siege ramp still visible at the site. The order to harness horses may mean "flee," or it may mock the city's trust in chariots.
- **"It was the beginning of sin to the daughter of Zion, for in you were found the transgressions of Israel" (v13).** What sin began at Lachish is not said. Many think it was trust in horses and military might, which the law warned kings against (Deut 17:16) and which Micah later says God will cut off (5:10). Others think northern-style idolatry first entered Judah through this border city. Either way, a city that brought sin in now sees judgment come in.
- **"You shall give parting gifts to Moresheth-gath" (v14).** Micah's own hometown. The word for "parting gifts" (*shilluchim*) is used of a dowry given to a daughter leaving home to marry (1 Kgs 9:16), and Moresheth sounds like *me'orasah*, "betrothed." Judah must give up its town like a bride given away to a new master.
- **"The houses of Achzib shall be a deceitful thing to the kings of Israel" (v14).** The neatest pun of all: Achzib becomes *'akzab*, "a deceitful thing." The word describes a stream that dries up in summer just when a traveller needs water (Jer 15:18). The town Judah's kings counted on will fail them. "Kings of Israel" here means the kings of Judah, God's Israel.
- **"I will again bring a conqueror to you, inhabitants of Mareshah" (v15).** Mareshah sounds like *yoresh*, "one who takes possession" or "heir." Possession-town will be possessed. **"The glory of Israel shall come to Adullam."** Adullam was the cave where David hid as a fugitive from Saul (1 Sam 22:1). "The glory of Israel" probably means Judah's nobles and leaders. They will end up hiding in caves, back where the royal line began, as though the whole history of David's house were being rolled back.
- **"Make yourselves bald… as bald as the eagle, for they shall go from you into exile" (v16).** Shaving the head was a sign of deep mourning (Isa 22:12). The bird (*nesher*) is probably the griffon vulture, whose head and neck are bare. The address turns to Zion as a mother, grieving for "the children of your delight." Assyria deported thousands from these towns, and a century later Babylon would take Jerusalem's own children.
- **Commentators:** Henry says these prophecies "might well be called his lamentations." On v5 he writes, "Sin has done all the mischief; sin has laid all waste," and adds that sin spreads fastest from the most visible places: "Spiritual distempers are most contagious in persons and places that are most conspicuous." On Lachish he warns, "Those that help to bring sin into a country do but thereby prepare for the throwing of themselves out of it." On v8 he says we "ought to lament the punishments of sinners as well as the sufferings of saints." JFB notes that Micah deliberately opens as Micaiah son of Imlah did. He reads "dragons" and "owls" as jackals and ostriches and works through the puns name by name, with "Achzib" meaning "a lie" and Mareshah "an inheritance." He reads v9 as the enemy coming to Jerusalem but not entering. Guzik, drawing on Waltke, traces the cycle of idols bought with prostitutes' wages and melted down for the same use. With Boice he stresses that a prophet must weep over those he warns. He also sets out the town-name puns in a list, and adds that a wound is only incurable when people refuse to bring it to the God for whom nothing is impossible.

#### Pointing to Christ
Micah weeps over cities that will not repent, going stripped and barefoot like a captive in their place. Seven centuries later Jesus came near the same Jerusalem, "saw the city, he wept over it," and spoke of its coming destruction (Luke 19:41–44). But he did more than mourn. The judgment that "came down from the LORD to the gate of Jerusalem" fell at last on him, led out through that gate to be crucified outside the city (Heb 13:12), stripped of his clothes and shamed like a prisoner. The wound Micah called incurable is the very wound Isaiah, his fellow prophet, said would be healed: "with his wounds we are healed" (Isa 53:5). And the answer to the question in Micah's name, "Who is like the LORD?", is a God who pardons (7:18).
