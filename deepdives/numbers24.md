*Setting:* The hills above the plains of Moab, at the end of the wilderness years. Balak, king of Moab, has twice paid for seven altars and fourteen animals, and twice Balaam, the diviner he hired from the Euphrates, has blessed Israel instead of cursing them (ch 23). Now Balak has brought him to a third summit, the top of Peor, near a shrine of Baal, and the altars are smoking again (23:28–30). This chapter holds Balaam's last two great oracles. The first is the richest blessing of the series. The second, which Balaam gives unasked as he leaves, looks far into the future and contains one of the Old Testament's best-known prophecies of a coming king: "a star shall come out of Jacob."

#### Balaam's Third Oracle (vv1–14)
- **"He did not go, as at other times, to look for omens" (v1).** Before, Balaam went off alone to watch for signs (23:3, 15). The word (*nachash*) means divination by omens, forbidden in Israel (Lev 19:26; Deut 18:10). Now he gives up his trade. He has seen enough to know **"it pleased the LORD to bless Israel,"** and no omen will change that. **"Set his face toward the wilderness"**, toward the camp spread out below.
- **"Saw Israel camping tribe by tribe" (v2).** He sees the ordered camp of chapter 2: twelve tribes, each under its banner, with the tabernacle at the center. God's people arranged around God's presence.
- **"The Spirit of God came upon him" (v2).** The same phrase is used of Saul (1 Sam 10:10) and others whom God used for a moment without their hearts being changed. God's Spirit can speak through a person without that person belonging to God. Jesus warned of people who prophesy in his name but whom he never knew (Matt 7:22–23).
- **"The man whose eye is opened" (v3).** The Hebrew word (*shetum*) occurs only here and in v15, and its meaning is uncertain. The lexicon allows "opened," as the ESV has it, but some scholars read "closed," which would describe a seer whose physical eyes are shut in a trance while his inner eye sees. Either way, the irony of chapter 22 hangs over it. This is the man who could not see the angel his donkey saw, until God opened his eyes (22:31).
- **"Who sees the vision of the Almighty, falling down with his eyes uncovered" (v4).** "Almighty" is *Shaddai*, the name by which God revealed himself to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Exod 6:3). Balaam falls down overwhelmed, as he did before the angel.
- **"How lovely are your tents, O Jacob" (v5).** In Jewish worship these words (*Mah tovu*) are still said on entering the synagogue, a striking case of a pagan diviner's words becoming a prayer of God's people.
- **"Like palm groves… gardens beside a river… aloes… cedar trees beside the waters" (v6).** A wilderness camp described as an oasis. "Gardens beside a river" recalls Eden (Gen 2:10), and a tree beside the waters is the picture of the blessed person in Psalm 1:3 and Jeremiah 17:8. **"Aloes that the LORD has planted"** were fragrant trees, not the medicinal aloe plant. Cedars don't actually grow beside water, but poetry is free to gather every image of beauty and strength into one picture.
- **"Water shall flow from his buckets, and his seed shall be in many waters" (v7).** For people who had just spent forty years in the desert, often thirsty, abundant water was the picture of blessing. "Seed" means both crops well watered and many descendants.
- **"His king shall be higher than Agag" (v7).** Israel has no king yet. The Amalekites were Israel's first enemy after the exodus (Exod 17:8–16). "Agag" may have been a royal title for their kings, as "Pharaoh" was for Egypt's. Centuries later Saul defeated an Amalekite king named Agag (1 Sam 15:8). The prophecy already sees Israel as a kingdom.
- **"Like the horns of the wild ox… shall break their bones" (v8).** The second oracle's picture of strength (23:22) returns, now pressed to victory over enemies.
- **"He crouched, he lay down like a lion… who will rouse him up?" (v9).** Almost word for word Jacob's blessing on Judah (Gen 49:9). Israel's enemies had better let this lion sleep.
- **"Blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you" (v9).** God's promise to Abraham (Gen 12:3), spoken by the man hired to curse Abraham's children. Without saying so, Balaam has just pronounced Balak cursed.
- **"He struck his hands together" (v10).** A gesture of scorn and anger (Job 27:23; Lam 2:15). **"Blessed them these three times."** Balak paid for one curse and got three blessings.
- **"Flee to your own place… the LORD has held you back from honor" (v11).** Balak sneers that Balaam's God has cost him his fee. The truth is that God has kept him from earning a reward for evil, though Balaam will find another way to earn one (31:16).
- **"I would not be able to go beyond the word of the LORD" (v13).** Balaam repeats what he said at the start (22:18). Here at least he has kept it.
- **"What this people will do to your people in the latter days" (v14).** "In the latter days" points to the future, sometimes far future. Balak sought harm for Israel; Balaam will tell him what Israel will do to Moab. Balak gets a prophecy he didn't pay for.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains that Balaam had learned these "superstitious accompaniments" were useless, and calls v5 "a fine burst of admiration, expressed in highly poetical strains." JFB takes "Agag" as "a title common to their kings." Guzik counts twenty-one animals sacrificed by now, Balak's heavy investment in a curse he never got, and notes that each oracle has gone worse for Balak, ending in a curse on those who curse Israel. The Tyndale notes add that the fast-growing population is about to be shown in the second census (ch 26).

#### Balaam's Final Oracle (vv15–25)
- **"Knows the knowledge of the Most High" (v16).** This introduction adds one line to the last: "Most High" (*Elyon*), the title Melchizedek used for the God of Abraham (Gen 14:19–20).
- **"I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near" (v17).** Balaam sees a person, far in the future. The "him" is not Israel the nation but a single ruler.
- **"A star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel" (v17).** In the ancient Near East a star was a common image for a king or a god, and a scepter is a ruler's rod (Gen 49:10: "the scepter shall not depart from Judah"). The star and scepter are one figure: a coming king from Israel.
- **"It shall crush the forehead of Moab" (v17).** The forehead, or temples, is the head. The very nation that hired Balaam will be struck by Israel's king. King David conquered Moab (2 Sam 8:2) and Edom (2 Sam 8:14), so most interpreters see David as the first fulfilment. Jeremiah reuses these lines against Moab centuries later (Jer 48:45), showing the prophecy was still read as pointing forward.
- **"All the sons of Sheth" (v17).** The meaning is uncertain. It may name a people called the Shutu, nomads known from Egyptian and Mesopotamian texts, or it may be another name for Moab. Jeremiah 48:45 has "sons of tumult" in its version, reading a similar word. It is not Seth the son of Adam, which would mean all humanity.
- **Who is the star?** Jewish interpreters before Christ read this as the Messiah: the Dead Sea Scrolls apply it to a messianic leader, and the Aramaic paraphrase (Targum) renders the star as "a king" and the scepter as "the Messiah." In AD 132 the Jewish rebel Simon ben Kosiba was hailed as the star of this verse and nicknamed Bar Kokhba, "son of the star," before Rome crushed his revolt. Christians have read it of Jesus, the son of David, whose birth was marked by a star (Matt 2:2) and who calls himself "the bright morning star" (Rev 22:16; also 2 Pet 1:19). Many think the magi from the east, perhaps heirs of the same eastern tradition of seers as Balaam, knew this oracle. That is an old guess, not something Matthew says. The best reading sees both: David first, and in David the pattern of the greater King to come.
- **"Edom shall be dispossessed; Seir also" (v18).** Seir is the mountain country of Edom, south of the Dead Sea. Edom had just refused Israel passage (20:14–21). David subdued them (2 Sam 8:14), and Obadiah prophesies their final end (Obad 18–21).
- **"Israel is doing valiantly… one from Jacob shall exercise dominion" (vv18–19).** Again a single ruler from Jacob. The Hebrew of v19 is difficult, and "the survivors of cities" may mean the remnants of enemy towns.
- **"Amalek was the first among the nations, but its end is utter destruction" (v20).** "First" probably means first to attack Israel (Exod 17:8), or first in rank among desert peoples. God had sworn war on Amalek "from generation to generation" (Exod 17:16). Saul fought them (1 Sam 15), David fought them (1 Sam 30), and the last remnant was destroyed in Hezekiah's day (1 Chr 4:42–43).
- **"The Kenite… your nest is set in the rock" (v21).** There is a pun: "Kenite" (*qeni*) sounds like "nest" (*qen*). The Kenites were a desert people related to Moses' father-in-law (Judg 1:16; 4:11), mostly friendly to Israel (1 Sam 15:6). Their homes in rocky heights looked secure. **"Kain shall be burned when Asshur takes you away captive" (v22).** Kain is another name for the Kenites. Asshur is Assyria, which centuries later swept through the region and carried peoples into exile, including the northern tribes of Israel (2 Kgs 17:6).
- **"Alas, who shall live when God does this?" (v23).** Balaam is overwhelmed by what he sees: empire after empire rising and falling.
- **"Ships shall come from Kittim and shall afflict Asshur and Eber" (v24).** Kittim was originally Kition on Cyprus, and the name came to mean the peoples of the Mediterranean coasts and islands (Gen 10:4; Jer 2:10). Daniel uses "ships of Kittim" for Rome (Dan 11:30), and the Dead Sea Scrolls use "Kittim" for the Romans too. **Eber** is the name of the ancestor of the Hebrews (Gen 10:21), but here it may mean the peoples "beyond" the Euphrates. **"He too shall come to utter destruction"**: even the conqueror from the sea falls. Only God's King lasts.
- **"Balaam rose and went back to his place" (v25).** It sounds like the end of his story. It isn't. He soon turns up among the Midianites, and chapter 31 reveals that he advised them how to bring Israel down through sexual immorality and idolatry, the disaster of chapter 25 (31:16; Rev 2:14). He was killed in Israel's war on Midian (31:8).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v17 as Balaam seeing the Messiah "at a very great distance," with the star signifying glory and the scepter authority, and he suggests this prophecy may have set the magi looking for the star. JFB holds the oracle points "primarily David; but secondarily and pre-eminently, the Messiah," which matches the Tyndale notes, which also see David first and Jesus beyond him. Guzik reads the star and scepter directly as Jesus. On the Kenites, Henry warns that "even a nest in a rock will be no perpetual security." JFB takes Kittim as "the countries lying on the Mediterranean, particularly Greece and Italy," and Eber as "the posterity of the Hebrews." JFB has no notes on vv16 and 22.

#### Pointing to Christ
A pagan seer, hired to curse, looks over the hills of Moab and sees a King rising from Jacob, glorious as a star and ruling with a scepter, who will overcome every enemy of God's people while empires rise and fall around him. David fulfilled it in part. Jesus, the son of David, fulfils it completely. At his birth a star brought Gentile seekers from the east to worship him (Matt 2:1–11), and he will come again as "the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star" (Rev 22:16). Balaam saw him "not now… not near." Those who know Jesus see him near, and can say, "Blessed are those who bless you."
