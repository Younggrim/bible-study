*Setting:* David has been living among the Philistines for over a year, based at Ziklag and lying to King Achish about his raids (27:8–12). Achish trusts him completely. Now the Philistines gather for a major war against Israel, and the story cuts between the two camps. Saul, rejected by God years earlier (15:23–28), faces the Philistine army with no word from the LORD, no Samuel and no David. In desperation he does what he had himself banned: he goes to a woman who claims to call up the dead. It is one of the darkest and strangest scenes in the Old Testament, and it is Saul's last night before his death.

#### Saul and the Medium of En-dor (vv1–25)
- **"You and your men are to go out with me in the army" (v1).** David's lies have trapped him. Achish expects his loyal servant to fight Israel.
- **"Very well, you shall know what your servant can do" (v2).** David's answer is cleverly vague. Achish hears loyalty. The reader wonders what David will actually do. "I will make you my bodyguard for life" is literally "keeper of my head." The future king of Israel is about to be the personal guard of a Philistine king marching against Israel. The story leaves David there and picks him up again in chapter 29.
- **"Now Samuel had died" (v3).** The narrator repeats what was said in 25:1, to set the scene. Saul has lost the prophet who once spoke God's word to him.
- **"Saul had put the mediums and the necromancers out of the land" (v3).** The Law forbade consulting the dead and prescribed death for those who practiced it (Lev 19:31; 20:6, 27; Deut 18:10–12). Saul had obeyed that, at least. The Hebrew words are *'ob* and *yidde'oni*. The second comes from the verb "to know," a "knowing one" who claims secret knowledge. The first is less clear. In Job 32:19 it means a "wineskin," and Isaiah describes such people as those "who chirp and mutter" (Isa 8:19; 29:4). The Greek Septuagint translated it "one who speaks from the belly," which has led some to think of a ventriloquist's hollow voice. The lexicon notes these ideas but holds them loosely.
- **"Encamped at Shunem… they encamped at Gilboa" (v4).** The Philistines have marched far north, into the Jezreel Valley, the broad plain that cuts across northern Israel. Shunem was on the slope of the Hill of Moreh on the north side of the valley. Saul's army is on Mount Gilboa, a ridge on the south side. The two armies face each other across the plain.
- **"He was afraid, and his heart trembled greatly" (v5).** This is the man who once rallied Israel to rescue Jabesh-gilead (ch 11). Compare Israel before Goliath, "dismayed and greatly afraid" (17:11), when Saul had no answer and David did.
- **"The LORD did not answer him, either by dreams, or by Urim, or by prophets" (v6).** These were the three normal ways God gave guidance: dreams (Num 12:6), the Urim, the sacred lot carried by the high priest (Exod 28:30), and the word of prophets. Saul had killed the priests of Nob, and the ephod with the Urim had gone to David (22:20; 23:6). Samuel was dead, and Gad was with David. God had stopped answering Saul before (14:37). The silence is a judgment. 1 Chronicles 10:13–14 says Saul died "because he did not keep the command of the LORD, and also consulted a medium… He did not seek guidance from the LORD." Saul inquired, but he did not seek God himself, repenting and obeying.
- **"There is a medium at En-dor" (v7).** The servants know where to find one, so the ban was not complete. En-dor was on the north side of the Hill of Moreh, beyond the Philistine camp at Shunem. To get there, Saul had to slip around the enemy army at night.
- **"Saul disguised himself and put on other garments" (v8).** The king takes off his royal robes. The image is sad and fitting. The man whose robe was torn when the kingdom was torn from him (15:27–28), and whose robe David cut in the cave (24:4), now hides who he is.
- **"Bring up for me whomever I shall name to you" (v8).** "Bring up" reflects the ancient belief that the dead were below, in Sheol, the shadowy place of the dead (2:6).
- **"Why then are you laying a trap for my life?" (v9).** The woman fears a sting operation, and with reason.
- **"As the LORD lives, no punishment shall come upon you" (v10).** Saul swears by the LORD to protect someone breaking the LORD's law. It is the last time Saul uses God's name in the story.
- **"Bring up Samuel for me" (v11).** Saul asks for the prophet who had rebuked him and announced his rejection. Even after all that, Samuel is the one voice Saul still trusts to tell him the truth.
- **"When the woman saw Samuel, she cried out with a loud voice" (v12).** Something happens that she did not expect. Her scream suggests that this is not her usual performance. Somehow, in that moment, she also realizes who her customer is: "You are Saul."
- **"I see a god coming up out of the earth" (v13).** "A god" is *elohim*, which usually means God or gods, but can also mean a mighty spiritual being (compare Ps 82:6). She sees a figure of supernatural majesty.
- **"An old man is coming up, and he is wrapped in a robe" (v14).** The robe is a *me'il*, the same word used for the robe Samuel's mother made for him every year as a boy (2:19) and the robe Saul tore (15:27). Saul recognizes Samuel by it and bows to the ground.
- **Did Samuel really appear?** Faithful Christians have answered differently. There are three main views.
- **View one: a real appearance by God's permission.** The text says plainly "Samuel said" several times (vv15–16, 20). The woman is shocked. The message is a true prophecy that comes to pass the next day. Many, including Henry and Guzik, think God himself sent Samuel, overriding the medium, much as Moses and Elijah appeared at the Transfiguration (Matt 17:3). The Jewish book of Sirach (46:20) also says Samuel prophesied after his death.
- **View two: a demonic impersonation.** Some early church writers and many Reformers believed an evil spirit took Samuel's form, since God would not work through a forbidden practice and the dead cannot be summoned.
- **View three: a trick by the woman.** A few think she staged it all, but her surprise and the accuracy of the prophecy make this hard to hold.
- **What every view agrees on.** In every view, the passage does not endorse what Saul did. It condemns it (1 Chr 10:13). If Samuel came, it was because God sent him, not because the medium had any power over him.
- **"Why have you disturbed me by bringing me up?" (v15).** Saul pours out his distress: the Philistines, God's silence, no dreams, no prophets. He asks Samuel "what I shall do." He wants instructions. He does not ask for forgiveness.
- **"Why then do you ask me, since the LORD has turned from you and become your enemy?" (v16).** If God himself will not answer, his prophet will not overrule him. Saul has spent years treating David as his enemy. Samuel names his real opponent, the LORD.
- **"The LORD has torn the kingdom out of your hand and given it to your neighbor, David" (v17).** Samuel repeats almost word for word what he said in 15:28, and now he names the "neighbor." God's word has not changed with time.
- **"Because you did not… carry out his fierce wrath against Amalek" (v18).** The root of it all goes back to chapter 15. Saul's disobedience then is the reason for his judgment now. And in the very next chapters, it is David who fights the Amalekites (ch 30).
- **"Tomorrow you and your sons shall be with me" (v19).** This is the sentence of death. "With me" means in the place of the dead, not necessarily in the same condition. The Old Testament speaks of all the dead as going down to Sheol, with a fuller picture of the righteous and the wicked revealed later (Luke 16:22–26). Israel's army will be defeated too.
- **"Saul fell at once full length on the ground" (v20).** The tallest man in Israel (9:2) lies flat on the floor, drained by fear and fasting. He had eaten nothing "all day and all night."
- **"Let me set a morsel of bread before you" (vv21–22).** The medium, of all people, shows Saul kindness. She asks him to "obey" her, as she obeyed him, and eat.
- **"A fattened calf… unleavened bread" (v24).** She prepares a real meal, killing her own calf and baking bread in a hurry, like Abraham's hospitality (Gen 18:6–8). The Tyndale notes point to a striking contrast. Saul's reign began with a feast at Samuel's table, when he was honored as Israel's future king (9:22–24). It ends with a meal in a medium's house, the night before his death.
- **"Then they rose and went away that night" (v25).** The chapter ends in darkness. Saul walks back to his army knowing he will die.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks Samuel truly appeared by God's permission: "The whole shows that it was no human fraud or trick," and "The woman's surprise and terror proved that it was an unusual and unexpected appearance." He adds that the medium "could not cause Samuel's being sent," though Saul's inquiry "might be the occasion of it." JFB lays out both the deception view and the real-appearance view and does not decide. It reads David's reply to Achish as "a wary and politic generality." Guzik weighs four explanations and concludes that God sent Samuel. He notes that it is bitterly ironic that Saul's last use of the LORD's name is to shield a medium. He links God's silence to judgment on a man who had already ignored the guidance he had, and quotes G. Campbell Morgan that God never leaves a person until that person has left him. Guzik explains *'ob* as a hollow, muttering voice. The lexicon allows that idea from Isaiah 8:19 but does not settle it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Saul, cut off from God, tries to reach past the grave for a word of hope, and hears only the confirmation of judgment. Scripture forbids seeking the dead because God has given a better way to hear him: "should not a people inquire of their God? Should they inquire of the dead on behalf of the living? To the teaching and to the testimony!" (Isa 8:19–20). In Christ, God has spoken his final word (Heb 1:1–2). And the one who truly came back from the dead did not come to announce doom but life: "I died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades" (Rev 1:18). Saul ate a last meal before his death in a medium's house. Jesus shared a last meal before his death with his friends and gave it as a sign of the new covenant, so that those who eat with him will never be cut off from God.
