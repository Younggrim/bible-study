*Setting:* Amos, the shepherd and fig-tree farmer from Tekoa in Judah, is preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760 BC, in the prosperous reign of Jeroboam II. Chapter 7 gave the first three of his five visions: locusts and fire, which Amos prayed away, and then the plumb line, after which God said he would no longer pass by. Between the third and fourth visions came the clash at Bethel, where the king's priest Amaziah ordered Amos to go home and stop prophesying. Chapter 8 picks the visions back up as if Amaziah had never spoken. The fourth vision, a basket of ripe summer fruit, announces that Israel's end has come. The rest of the chapter explains why, returning to the merchants who cheat the poor, and then describes the day itself: darkness at noon, funerals instead of feasts, and worst of all, a famine of the word of God. The ESV prints the whole chapter as one section.

#### The Coming Day of Bitter Mourning (vv1–14)
- **"This is what the Lord GOD showed me" (v1).** The same opening as the first three visions (7:1, 4, 7). Amaziah tried to send Amos away, and the next thing on the page is the Lord showing him more. The Tyndale notes read the chapter as the continuation of the sermon that the confrontation interrupted.
- **"A basket of summer fruit" (vv1–2).** Summer fruit means the late crops picked at the end of the dry season, especially figs, the fruit Amos himself tended (7:14). Like the plumb-line vision, it comes with a question, "Amos, what do you see?", and the meaning hangs on a pun. "Summer fruit" is *qayits*; "the end" is *qets*. In the spoken Hebrew of the north the two words may have sounded almost the same. Amos sees *qayits*, and God says *qets*: "The end has come upon my people Israel." Ripe fruit is at its best and closest to rotting at the same moment, and Israel under Jeroboam II looked its best just before its fall. Ezekiel later uses the same word, "An end has come, the end has come" (Ezek 7:2, 6), over Judah.
- **"My people Israel" (v2).** Even as he pronounces the end, God still calls them "my people." The judgment is not on strangers but on a family that has broken covenant with him.
- **"I will never again pass by them" (v2).** Word for word the sentence from the plumb-line vision (7:8). To "pass by" here means to overlook or let go. The patience shown in the first two visions, when Amos prayed and God relented, is finished. Amos does not pray this time.
- **"The songs of the temple shall become wailings" (v3).** The word translated "temple" can also mean "palace," and translations divide; the KJV has "temple," others "palace." Either way the place of celebration becomes a place of howling. This temple would be Bethel, "the king's sanctuary" (7:13), not Jerusalem. Amos has already said God hates the noise of their songs (5:23). Now those songs are replaced.
- **"So many dead bodies!… Silence!" (v3).** The Hebrew is clipped and broken, a few gasped words, and the ESV keeps that feel with short exclamations. The last word is *has*, "Hush!" It appeared in 6:10, where survivors burying the dead are afraid even to mention the LORD's name. JFB reads it as an adverb, bodies thrown out "silently," because "there shall be such great slaughter as even to prevent the bodies being buried." The ESV takes it as a cry. Either reading pictures a city past the point of grief.
- **"Hear this, you who trample on the needy" (v4).** Amos now returns to the charges of 2:6–7 and 5:11–12. "Trample" is the verb used in 2:7, where the rich "trample the head of the poor into the dust." They do not merely neglect the poor; they "bring the poor of the land to an end." The word for "end" is related to the one in v2. The rich are making an end of the poor, so God is making an end of the rich.
- **"When will the new moon be over, that we may sell grain?" (v5).** The new moon was a monthly holy day with sacrifices and rest (Num 28:11; 2 Kings 4:23), and the Sabbath forbade buying and selling (Exod 20:8–10; later Neh 13:15–22). These merchants keep the holy days, but only outwardly, drumming their fingers until the shops can open. JFB puts it well: they "love market days better than sabbath days." Their worship has become a pause in business, not a meeting with God.
- **"Make the ephah small and the shekel great" (v5).** This describes a double fraud. The ephah was the dry measure for grain, roughly three-fifths of a bushel. Sell with a short ephah and the buyer takes home less grain. The shekel was a weight of silver, not yet a coin, measured on a balance. Use a heavy shekel weight and the buyer has to hand over more silver. So the merchant cheats on what he gives and on what he gets, and the balance itself is rigged ("false balances"). The law was plain: "You shall have just balances, just weights, a just ephah" (Lev 19:35–36; Deut 25:13–16), and Proverbs says "a false balance is an abomination to the LORD" (Prov 11:1). Archaeologists have found stone shekel weights from this period that vary noticeably in mass, which shows how easy the trick was.
- **"Buy the poor for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals" (v6).** This repeats 2:6 almost exactly, but with a twist. There the poor were *sold* for silver and sandals; here the merchants *buy* them. A family that falls into debt through cheating at the market ends up selling itself into service. The law allowed a poor Israelite to work off a debt but forbade treating him as a slave (Lev 25:39–43). For "a pair of sandals" a human being changes hands.
- **"And sell the chaff of the wheat" (v6).** The last insult: the sweepings off the threshing floor, mixed into the grain or sold on their own to people too poor to buy better. JFB calls it what "the poor eat at a low price." Every step of the trade is crooked, from the measure to the merchandise.
- **"The LORD has sworn by the pride of Jacob" (v7).** God swears by himself (Heb 6:13), and here the oath is "by the pride of Jacob." The phrase is hard. In 6:8 the same words mean Israel's arrogance, which God says he abhors. Most interpreters think that here it is a title for God himself, the one who should have been Jacob's pride and glory (compare 1 Sam 15:29, "the Glory of Israel"). The Tyndale notes allow another reading: elsewhere the phrase means the promised land (Ps 47:4). Some see bitter irony: God swears by Israel's pride, as if to say their arrogance is as fixed as he is, and so is his judgment. JFB takes it as Israel's spiritual privileges. The main point is clear in every reading: this is sworn and will not be revoked.
- **"Surely I will never forget any of their deeds" (v7).** Those who cheated thought no one was watching the scales. God was. Psalm 10:11 describes the wicked saying, "God has forgotten." Amos answers that he has not and will not. Hebrews 6:10 gives the other side of the same truth: God is not unjust to forget the love his people show.
- **"Shall not the land tremble… rise like the Nile" (v8).** The Nile rose every summer and flooded its valley, then sank back. Israelites knew of it, though few had seen it. Amos pictures the whole land heaving up and dropping like that river, a vivid way to describe an earthquake. The book opened by dating Amos's preaching "two years before the earthquake" (1:1), and its hearers would remember that quake vividly. This verse repeats almost word for word in 9:5.
- **"I will make the sun go down at noon" (v9).** "On that day" points to the day of the LORD, which Amos has already said is "darkness, and not light" (5:18–20). Creation itself seems to come undone. Joel (2:10, 31), Isaiah (13:10) and Jeremiah (4:23) use similar language. Many scholars also note that a total solar eclipse passed over the Near East on 15 June 763 BC; Assyrian records list it, and it falls within Amos's lifetime. There may have been another partial eclipse in 784 BC. If so, Amos's hearers may have remembered noon darkness, and he turns that memory into a warning. The text does not name an eclipse, so this is background, not a claim of the text.
- **"I will turn your feasts into mourning" (v10).** Every sign of joy is reversed. Sackcloth was rough goat-hair cloth worn in grief, and shaving the head was a mourning custom (Isa 15:2; Jer 47:5), though Israel was told not to imitate pagan forms of it (Deut 14:1). Religious festivals, the very feasts God said he despised (5:21), become funerals.
- **"Like the mourning for an only son" (v10).** In the ancient world, losing an only son meant losing a family's future, its name and its support in old age. There was no deeper grief (Jer 6:26). Zechariah later uses the same picture for Israel's mourning over "him whom they have pierced… as one mourns for an only child" (Zech 12:10). **"The end of it like a bitter day"**: some sorrows end in comfort; this one ends only in bitterness.
- **"A famine… of hearing the words of the LORD" (v11).** This is the climax of the chapter. Israel had known real famines, and Amos listed one among the warnings they ignored (4:6–8). But this famine is worse. They had silenced God's messengers: "You commanded the prophets, saying, 'You shall not prophesy'" (2:12), and Amaziah had just done exactly that (7:12–13). God now gives them what they asked for. Notice the wording: a famine not of the word but "of hearing" it. Scripture would still exist, but no living prophet would bring God's word to them, and their hearts would no longer be able to receive it. Saul is the sad picture of this: when he sought the LORD in his crisis, "the LORD did not answer him, either by dreams, or by Urim, or by prophets" (1 Sam 28:6).
- **"They shall wander from sea to sea, and from north to east" (v12).** "Sea to sea" likely means from the Dead Sea to the Mediterranean, the whole land from east to west. The odd pairing "north to east" leaves out the south. JFB suggests this is because the south meant Judah and Jerusalem, where God's true worship stood, the one direction they refused to go. **"Run to and fro"** is the picture of people rushing about in a panic. They seek a word only when disaster has come, and it is too late. Proverbs warns of those who call when calamity strikes, "but I will not answer; they will seek me diligently but will not find me" (Prov 1:28).
- **"The lovely virgins and the young men shall faint for thirst" (v13).** Young men and women in the prime of life were the nation's strength and hope. If even they collapse, no one is left standing. The thirst is both physical and spiritual: the drought of the land and the drought of God's word.
- **"Those who swear by the Guilt of Samaria" (v14).** To swear by a god was to acknowledge it as real and binding. "Guilt" is *ashmah*, "guilt" or "wrongdoing," a word the lexicon gives for Samaria's idols in this verse. Many scholars think it is a deliberate pun on *Ashimah*, a god later worshiped in Samaria (2 Kings 17:30), so that Amos renames the idol "Guilt." Others think it simply refers to the golden calf at Bethel, which JFB calls "the sin of Samaria."
- **"As your god lives, O Dan" (v14).** Dan, at the far north, held the other golden calf that Jeroboam I set up (1 Kings 12:28–30). The oath "As the LORD lives" belonged to the true God (Jer 4:2). Here it is spoken to a statue.
- **"As the Way of Beersheba lives" (v14).** Beersheba was at the far south of Judah. "From Dan to Beersheba" was the old phrase for the whole land (Judg 20:1). Northerners made pilgrimages to it (5:5). "The Way" may mean the pilgrim road itself, or the cult practiced there; JFB takes it as "the mode of worship." Either way, people are swearing by the journey and the shrine, not by God. Archaeologists found a large horned altar at Beersheba, which some connect to this worship.
- **"They shall fall, and never rise again" (v14).** The same words close Amos's lament for "the virgin Israel" in 5:2. The northern kingdom fell to Assyria in 722 BC and never returned as a nation.
- **Commentators:** All three hear the *qayits / qets* pun at the heart of the vision. JFB adds that summer is "the end of the year and the time of the ripeness of fruits," so Israel is ripe for its final reaping. Henry sums up the chapter as "sinful times are here attended with sorrowful times, so necessary is the connexion between them." Guzik presses the merchants' hypocrisy into an application: dishonesty in business is never a small sin, because God keeps the account. He also notes, following Hubbard, the eclipses some scholars link to v9, and joins the only-son mourning to Zechariah 12:10. On v11, Guzik and Henry agree that the hearing was the problem, not a lack of revelation. Henry calls "a famine of the word of God… the sorest famine, the heaviest judgment," and notes that they still do not mourn over this one. JFB, unlike the Tyndale notes, takes "pride of Jacob" as Israel's privileges, not a title for God; both readings are held by careful scholars.

#### Pointing to Christ
Amos foretold a day when the sun would go down at noon and a land would mourn as for an only son. On the day Jesus was crucified, "there was darkness over all the land" from noon until three (Matt 27:45), while God's only Son hung on a cross. The judgment Amos announced on a people who cheated and silenced God's word was gathered up and carried by the one who never cheated anyone and who was himself the Word made flesh (John 1:14). And the famine of the word ended not with a new prophet but with the Son: "Long ago, at many times… God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:1–2). Jesus said, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matt 4:4), and those who come to him do not wander from sea to sea for it.
