*Setting:* Jerusalem, summer of 445 BC. Under Nehemiah's leadership the people of Judah have begun rebuilding the city walls, each crew working its own section around the whole circuit (ch 3). The local officials who wanted Jerusalem weak, Sanballat of Samaria, Tobiah from the east and Geshem the Arab, had already mocked the plan and hinted at rebellion against the king (2:19). Now that the walls are actually rising, the opposition moves from scorn to threats of violence. The chapter shows how Nehemiah answered: with prayer and with practical action, always both. In the Hebrew Bible, vv1–6 are numbered 3:33–38, so 4:7 in English is 4:1 in Hebrew.

#### Opposition to the Work (vv1–14)
- **"He was angry and greatly enraged, and he jeered at the Jews" (v1).** Sanballat's first weapon is ridicule. He has no legal right to stop the work, since Nehemiah carries the king's letters, so he tries to make the builders feel foolish.
- **"In the presence of his brothers and of the army of Samaria" (v2).** He speaks before his colleagues and the local troops, perhaps at a public gathering or military review. The mockery is a show of power, and it carries a threat: these are soldiers.
- **Five mocking questions (v2).** "What are these feeble Jews doing?" "Feeble" means withered, without strength. "Will they restore it for themselves?" means "Do they really think they can do this?" "Will they sacrifice?" may mean "Do they think prayers and offerings will build a wall?" "Will they finish up in a day?" mocks their eagerness. "Will they revive the stones out of the heaps of rubbish, and burned ones at that?" The city's walls were largely limestone, which cracks and crumbles when burned. The Babylonian fire of 586 BC had left much of the old stone weakened. Sanballat's questions had some truth in them. What they missed was God.
- **"If a fox goes up on it he will break down their stone wall!" (v3).** Tobiah adds his own joke: the wall is so flimsy that a fox's weight would knock it over. Foxes, or jackals, prowled ruined places (Lam 5:18: "Mount Zion which lies desolate; jackals prowl over it"). Archaeologists have found a section of wall on the eastern slope that many date to Nehemiah's time, and it is about eight feet thick. It held well enough.
- **"Hear, O our God, for we are despised" (v4).** Nehemiah does not answer them. He turns to God. This is one of his short "arrow prayers," prayed in the middle of the action (2:4; 5:19; 6:9, 14; 13:14, 22, 29, 31).
- **"Turn back their taunt on their own heads and give them up to be plundered in a land where they are captives" (v4).** He asks God to do to them what they wanted for Judah: let them know shame and exile. The prayer matches the principle of justice in the Law, where the punishment fits the wrong (Deut 19:19).
- **"Do not cover their guilt, and let not their sin be blotted out from your sight" (v5).** These are almost the words of Jeremiah 18:23, where the prophet prayed against those plotting to kill him. Prayers like this, called imprecatory prayers, appear in many Psalms (Ps 69:27–28; 79:12). They hand vengeance to God instead of taking it, which is what Paul later commands (Rom 12:19). Nehemiah's reason is that the enemies "have provoked you to anger in the presence of the builders." Their mockery was aimed at God's work, so it was aimed at God. Christians reading this also hear Jesus, who taught his followers to pray for those who persecute them (Matt 5:44) and prayed for his own killers (Luke 23:34). Both are true: evil must be judged, and the believer leaves that judgment to God while seeking the good of enemies.
- **"So we built the wall… for the people had a mind to work" (v6).** Literally "the people had a heart to work." The best answer to mockery is to keep working. "Joined together to half its height" means the whole circuit was now continuous, though only halfway up. The halfway point is often the hardest place in any long task.
- **Enemies on every side (v7).** Sanballat to the north (Samaria), "the Arabs" with Geshem to the south, "the Ammonites" with Tobiah to the east, and "the Ashdodites" to the west, from the old Philistine city of Ashdod, now the center of a Persian province on the coast. Jerusalem was surrounded. They grew "very angry" because the plan was working: "the breaches were beginning to be closed."
- **"Plotted together to come and fight against Jerusalem and to cause confusion in it" (v8).** Now the threat is violence. Openly attacking a project the king had approved would be risky, so the plan seems to have been raids and confusion, the kind of trouble that could be blamed on the Jews themselves.
- **"And we prayed to our God and set a guard" (v9).** This verse sums up Nehemiah's way. Prayer did not replace a guard, and a guard did not replace prayer. "Day and night" they kept watch.
- **"The strength of those who bear the burdens is failing. There is too much rubble" (v10).** Now the trouble comes from inside. In Hebrew this verse has a rhythm and rhyme, and some scholars think it was a work song or a complaint that spread among the workers. The people of Judah, worn out from hauling rubble, began to say "we will not be able." Discouragement can stop a work as surely as an enemy can. Clearing old ruins was the hardest and least visible part of the job.
- **"They will not know or see till we come among them and kill them" (v11).** The enemies plan a surprise attack. Their goal is clear: "stop the work."
- **"The Jews who lived near them came… and said to us ten times" (v12).** "Ten times" means over and over. Jews from towns near the enemy borders heard the threats and kept bringing them. The Hebrew of their words is hard to read. The ESV takes it as "You must return to us," meaning that families wanted their men to come home from Jerusalem to protect their villages. Others read it as a warning: "From every place where you turn, they will come up against us." Either way, fear was spreading.
- **"In the lowest parts of the space behind the wall, in open places, I stationed the people by their clans" (v13).** Nehemiah placed armed families where the wall was lowest and most exposed, in plain sight. The show of force was itself a deterrent. Grouping them "by their clans" meant men stood beside their own relatives.
- **"Do not be afraid of them. Remember the Lord, who is great and awesome" (v14).** Nehemiah uses the words of his first prayer (1:5) and the language Moses used before battle (Deut 7:21; 20:3–4). Fear is answered first by remembering who God is.
- **"Fight for your brothers, your sons, your daughters, your wives, and your homes" (v14).** Then he reminds them what is at stake. The wall was not an abstract project. It was protection for the people they loved.
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that "Those who disagree in almost every thing, will unite in persecution," and that Nehemiah "did not answer these fools according to their folly, but looked up to God by prayer." His line on v9 is often quoted: "If we think to secure ourselves by prayer, without watchfulness, we are slothful, and tempt God; if by watchfulness, without prayer, we are proud, and slight God." JFB explains the fox jibe from Lamentations 5:18. He defends the imprecation as coming from "zeal for the glory of God," not personal revenge. Guzik notes that Tobiah called it *their* wall when it was really God's, and that the halfway point, where fatigue and rubble meet, is where discouragement usually strikes.

#### The Work Resumes (vv15–23)
- **"God had frustrated their plan" (v15).** Once the enemies knew their surprise was no surprise, they backed off. Nehemiah gives the credit to God, though the means were human: informants, guards and a show of force. Then "we all returned to the wall, each to his work." Defending the work was not the goal. Finishing it was.
- **"Half of my servants worked on construction, and half held the spears, shields, bows, and coats of mail" (v16).** Nehemiah's own household guard was split in two. "The leaders stood behind" the builders, backing them up. "Coats of mail" were armor of small overlapping metal scales.
- **"Each labored on the work with one hand and held his weapon with the other" (v17).** Those carrying loads kept one hand free for a weapon. Builders, who needed both hands, wore a sword at their side (v18). The image of the trowel and the sword has become a picture of the Christian life: building and guarding together.
- **"The man who sounded the trumpet was beside me" (v18).** The trumpet was the *shofar*, the ram's horn, used to call people to worship and to war (Num 10:9; Judg 3:27). With the crews spread around a circuit of perhaps a mile and a half, an alarm was the only way to concentrate a defense quickly.
- **"Our God will fight for us" (v20).** This is the old language of holy war: "The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent" (Exod 14:14); "the LORD your God is he who goes with you to fight for you" (Deut 20:4). Nehemiah has the people armed, organized and alert, and he still says the battle belongs to God.
- **"From the break of dawn until the stars came out" (v21).** Long days, with half the force always on guard.
- **"Let every man and his servant pass the night within Jerusalem" (v22).** Workers from outlying towns were told not to go home at night. They would guard by night and work by day, and no one would be exposed on the roads.
- **"None of us took off our clothes; each kept his weapon at his right hand" (v23).** Nehemiah and his men slept in their clothes, ready at every moment. The last words of the verse are hard in Hebrew. Literally they read something like "each his weapon, the water." It may mean that even when they went to get water they took their weapons. The ESV's "at his right hand" is a slight correction of the text. Either way, the point is constant readiness, and the leader set the example.
- **Commentators:** Henry turns the sword and trowel into a picture of Christian life: "Every true Christian is both a labourer and a soldier, working with one hand, and fighting with the other," and "The word of God is the sword of the Spirit, which we ought to have always at hand." He adds that we must not put off our armor "till our work and warfare are ended." JFB says that Nehemiah, "night and day on the spot," animated the people "by his pious exhortations and example." Guzik makes the point that victory was not merely surviving the threat but going back to the work.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's people build in the open, mocked, threatened and tired, with a trowel in one hand and a sword in the other. The New Testament describes the church in the same way. Christ is building his church, and "the gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (Matt 16:18). His people work and watch, "praying at all times" and taking up "the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God" (Eph 6:17–18). Jesus himself was mocked as one who could not finish what he began (Matt 27:40–42), yet he finished it: "It is finished" (John 19:30). And his answer to those who scorned him was not Nehemiah's prayer for judgment but "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). The God who fought for Nehemiah fights for his people still, and the trumpet that gathered builders on the wall points to the trumpet that will gather all his people at the end (1 Thess 4:16).
