*Setting:* This is the last chapter of Ezekiel and the end of the great temple vision that began in chapter 40, in the spring of 573 BC. Chapter 47 set the borders of the land and promised a share even to foreigners who had settled in Israel. Chapter 48 now hands out the land itself, tribe by tribe, in thirteen east–west strips: seven tribes north of the holy district and five south, with the temple, the priests, the Levites, the city and the prince's estate in between. It closes with the city's twelve gates and its new name. Ezekiel began his book among the exiles by the Chebar canal, where "the heavens were opened" and he saw the glory of God far from home (1:1). He watched that glory leave Jerusalem (chs 10–11) and heard of the city's fall (33:21). The book ends with a city whose very name is the promise that God will never leave again.

#### Division of the Land (vv1–29)
- **"These are the names of the tribes: Beginning at the northern extreme" (v1).** The list starts at the far north border set in 47:15–17 and runs south. Each tribe gets **"one portion"** stretching **"from the east side to the west,"** from the Jordan to the Mediterranean. Under Joshua the tribes' territories were irregular and of different sizes, some east of the Jordan and some west. Here they are equal bands across the land (47:14), all lined up on the same east–west axis as the temple, as the Tyndale notes point out.
- **Dan, first and farthest (v1).** Dan's original land was in the south near the Philistines, but the tribe failed to take it and moved to the far north, where it set up a stolen idol and its own priesthood (Judg 18). Later Jeroboam put one of his golden calves at Dan (1 Kings 12:29–30). Dan is missing from the list of tribes in Revelation 7:4–8, which many link to that history. Yet here Dan is named first and given an equal share. Guzik sees this as grace: the tribe that led the way into idolatry is not left out.
- **Asher, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim (vv2–5).** The other northern tribes follow. Manasseh, which had been split by the Jordan into two half-tribes (Num 32:33; Josh 17), now holds a single strip west of the river. Ephraim, once the leading tribe of the northern kingdom, so dominant that "Ephraim" could mean the whole north (Hos 5:3), is just one tribe among the rest.
- **Reuben (v6).** Reuben, Jacob's firstborn, lost his birthright for sleeping with his father's concubine (Gen 35:22; 49:3–4), and his tribe had settled east of the Jordan. Here Reuben is brought inside the promised land, next to Judah.
- **Judah, north of the holy district (v7).** Historically Judah lay south of Jerusalem and Benjamin north of it. Ezekiel reverses them: Judah is now directly north of the holy district, and Benjamin directly south (v23). The Tyndale notes suggest the point is to erase the old split of north and south, Israel and Judah. Judah, the royal tribe, now stands among the northern tribes, and the divided kingdom is over (37:15–22).
- **"The portion which you shall set apart… with the sanctuary in the midst of it" (v8).** This "portion" (*terumah*, the "offering" of 45:1) is a strip 25,000 cubits wide, about 8 miles. It is not at the exact center of the land: there are seven tribes to the north and five to the south. The Tyndale notes observe that the spiritual center has shifted north from where old Jerusalem stood. What matters is that the sanctuary is at the heart of the people.
- **"25,000 cubits in length, and 20,000 in breadth" (v9).** As in 45:1, the traditional Hebrew text reads 10,000 for the breadth, and the ESV follows the Greek translation, which agrees with the total in v13.
- **"The priests shall have an allotment… with the sanctuary of the LORD in the midst of it" (v10).** This repeats 45:3–4, with the four sides spelled out. The temple sits inside the priests' land, so anyone approaching it passes through holy ground first.
- **"The sons of Zadok, who kept my charge, who did not go astray when the people of Israel went astray, as the Levites did" (v11).** This recalls 44:10–16. Even in the land settlement, faithfulness in the past is remembered. **"A most holy place"** (v12) is the strongest term for holiness, normally used for the inner room of the temple.
- **"The Levites shall have an allotment 25,000 cubits in length and 10,000 in breadth" (v13).** The Levites' strip lies next to the priests', on the north side, between the priests and Judah (45:5).
- **"They shall not sell or exchange any of it… for it is holy to the LORD" (v14).** Most family land in Israel could be sold and bought back, and returned to its family in the jubilee (Lev 25:23–28). Levitical pastureland could never be sold at all (Lev 25:34). Here the whole holy portion is God's, and the people who live on it are its trustees, not its owners. **"This choice portion"** is literally "the first," the language of firstfruits. The holy district is the land's firstfruits offered back to God.
- **"The remainder… shall be for common use for the city" (v15).** "Common use" is the word translated "common" in 42:20 and 44:23, the opposite of "holy." It does not mean unclean. It means ordinary, for daily life. The city, with its houses and fields, is deliberately set apart from the sacred zone. In old Jerusalem the palace, the city and the temple had crowded together on the same hills (43:7–8).
- **"The north side 4,500 cubits, the south side 4,500…" (v16).** The city is a perfect square, about 1.5 miles (2.3 km) on each side, with a belt of open land 250 cubits wide all around it (v17). Like the temple area (42:20) and the holy district (v20), it is square, a shape of completeness.
- **"Its produce shall be food for the workers of the city" (v18).** Farmland on either side of the city feeds those who live and work there.
- **"The workers of the city, from all the tribes of Israel, shall till it" (v19).** The city does not belong to Judah or Benjamin, as old Jerusalem had stood on their border. It is the capital of all twelve tribes, worked by people from all of them. Henry sees it as the common good of the whole nation, served by "the most eminent men" chosen "out of all the tribes of Israel."
- **"The whole portion… shall be 25,000 cubits square" (v20).** Priests, Levites and city together make a square about 8 miles each way.
- **"What remains on both sides… shall belong to the prince" (v21).** The prince's land fills in east and west of the square, as in 45:7. **"The holy portion with the sanctuary of the temple shall be in its midst."** Even the ruler's land is arranged around God's house.
- **"The portion of the prince shall lie between the territory of Judah and the territory of Benjamin" (v22).** Judah was David's tribe, and Benjamin was Saul's and the tribe that stayed with Judah when the kingdom split (1 Kings 12:21). The royal land lies between the two tribes that had stood by the house of David.
- **Benjamin, Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun, Gad (vv23–27).** The southern tribes follow. Simeon had been absorbed into Judah's territory long before (Josh 19:1, 9) and had nearly disappeared as a separate tribe. Issachar and Zebulun, once in Galilee in the north, now live in the south. Gad, which had settled east of the Jordan, now has the southernmost strip within the promised land.
- **The maidservants' sons on the edges.** The Tyndale notes point out a pattern. The four tribes farthest from the center, Dan, Asher and Naphtali in the north and Gad in the south, descend from Jacob's sons by the servant women Bilhah and Zilpah (Gen 30:3–13). The sons of Leah and Rachel are nearer the sanctuary. Yet every tribe receives an equal share. Nearness reflects honor, but no one is left out.
- **"From Tamar to the waters of Meribah-kadesh… along the Brook of Egypt to the Great Sea" (v28).** This repeats the southern border of 47:19.
- **"This is the land that you shall allot as an inheritance… declares the Lord GOD" (v29).** The land is God's gift by lot. No one could seize it, earn it or buy it.
- **What is this division?** Those who expect a literal millennial kingdom, including Guzik (who cites Feinberg that the detailed measurements should be taken as literally as the tabernacle's), see a real future map of Israel. Others, like Henry, read it as a picture of the church's ordered unity, every member given a portion by God's appointment. Many modern readers, including the Tyndale notes, see theology expressed through geography: God at the center, the tribes reunited, holiness arranged in rings, and justice built into the map.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes "that every tribe in this visionary distribution had its particular lot assigned it by a divine appointment," and stresses that the priests' land is "a holy oblation to the Lord" that may not be sold. JFB gives a short note on many of the tribes. He says Judah and Benjamin get the places beside the center because of their loyalty to David's house, and Dan "the least honorable place, at the extreme north." He calls Asher "a tribe of which no one of note is mentioned in the Old Testament," which overlooks the aged prophetess Anna, "of the tribe of Asher" (Luke 2:36). JFB explains the "profane" land of v15 as "not strictly sacred… but applied to secular uses," and has no comment on vv8–14 or vv18–23. Guzik reads Dan's place at the head of the list as grace, and stresses that no tribe is forgotten before God.

#### The Gates of the City (vv30–35)
- **"These shall be the exits of the city" (v30).** The temple vision has been full of gates, entrances and exits (43:11; 44:5). The book closes on the same theme: who goes in and out, and where. City gates were where elders sat, cases were judged and business was done (Ruth 4:1; Prov 31:23). Most ancient cities had few gates, for defense. This city has twelve, as if it has nothing to fear.
- **"The gate of Reuben, the gate of Judah, and the gate of Levi" (v31).** The north side faces the temple, so the most honored names are here: Reuben the firstborn, Judah the royal tribe, and Levi the priestly tribe. Levi had no tribal land, but it has a gate. **"The gates of the city being named after the tribes of Israel."** Every tribe has a name on the city.
- **"The gate of Joseph, the gate of Benjamin, and the gate of Dan" (v32).** On the east are Rachel's sons, Joseph and Benjamin, and Dan, the son of Rachel's maid Bilhah. Because Levi now has a gate, Ephraim and Manasseh share one under their father Joseph's name, which keeps the number at twelve.
- **"The gate of Simeon, the gate of Issachar, and the gate of Zebulun" (v33).** On the south are three of Leah's sons, whose lands lie south of the city.
- **"The gate of Gad, the gate of Asher, and the gate of Naphtali" (v34).** On the west are the remaining sons of the servant women. The arrangement of the gates mirrors the arrangement of the land.
- **John's vision of the new Jerusalem.** The city John sees also has "twelve gates… and on the gates the names of the twelve tribes of the sons of Israel were inscribed," three on each side (Rev 21:12–13). He adds what Ezekiel's vision does not: on its foundations are the names of "the twelve apostles of the Lamb" (Rev 21:14). Israel's tribes and Jesus' apostles together form one people.
- **"The circumference of the city shall be 18,000 cubits" (v35).** Four sides of 4,500 cubits make about six miles around. That is a modest size for a capital, much smaller than Babylon, but several times the size of the Jerusalem Ezekiel knew.
- **"And the name of the city from that time on shall be, The LORD Is There" (v35).** In Hebrew, *YHWH shammah*. Through all of chapters 40–48 the city has never been called Jerusalem. Jerusalem had been called "the bloody city" (22:2; 24:6) and compared to an unfaithful wife (ch 16). Now it gets a new name. Other prophets also promised the city new names: "The City of the LORD" (Isa 60:14), "My Delight Is in Her" (Isa 62:4), "The LORD is our righteousness" (Jer 33:16), "the faithful city" (Zech 8:3). Many readers also hear a play on sound between *Yerushalayim*, Jerusalem, and *YHWH shammah*.
- **The last word of the book.** The final Hebrew word of Ezekiel is *shammah*, "there." The glory that left the city in judgment (11:23) and returned to the temple (43:1–5) now gives the city its identity. What makes the place holy is not its walls, its size or its gates, but who lives in it. This is the promise of 37:27–28 fulfilled: "My dwelling place shall be with them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people."
- **Commentators:** Henry notes "these twelve gates were inscribed to the twelve tribes." Of the city that God fills with his presence he writes, "it may truly be said, The Lord is there," and he closes his commentary on the book with an appeal: "Let us therefore give all diligence to make sure to ourselves a place in that city." JFB says the title Jehovah-Shammah is not just a name but describes the city's reality: God present in it. Guzik, citing Feinberg, sees in the twelve gates a picture of unity after centuries of division, compares them with the gates of the new Jerusalem in Revelation 21, and links the name with the voice John hears: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3).

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel's book ends with a name: "The LORD Is There." The whole Bible moves toward the same place. God walked with Adam in Eden, filled the tabernacle and the temple, left them in judgment, and came back in a way Ezekiel could not have imagined: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and his name is Immanuel, "God with us" (Matt 1:23). Jesus promised that "where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them" (Matt 18:20), and "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20). The city with twelve gates open to every tribe appears again at the end of Revelation, where its glory is that "the throne of God and of the Lamb will be in it… They will see his face" (Rev 22:3–4). That is the final meaning of *YHWH shammah*: God is there, and his people are with him.
