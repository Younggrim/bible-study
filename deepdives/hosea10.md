*Setting:* Hosea is preaching to the northern kingdom of Israel in its last years, probably in the 730s or early 720s BC. Kings were being murdered and replaced in quick succession, Assyria was demanding tribute, and the golden calf at Bethel still stood as the national shrine (1 Kgs 12:28–29). Chapter 9 ended with Israel sentenced to wander among the nations. Chapter 10 has no new ESV heading: it continues the same message, "The LORD Will Punish Israel." It works through a series of pictures from the farm and the vineyard, a vine, a heifer, a ploughed field, and in the middle of them one of Hosea's great calls to repentance: "Sow for yourselves righteousness… it is the time to seek the LORD."

#### The LORD Will Punish Israel, continued (vv1–15)
- **"Israel is a luxuriant vine that yields its fruit" (v1).** The vine is a classic picture of Israel (Ps 80:8–16; Isa 5:1–7; Jer 2:21). The Hebrew word *boqeq* is unusual. BDB gives two roots with the same spelling, one meaning "be luxuriant" and one meaning "empty" or "lay waste." The ESV and most modern versions read "luxuriant," while the KJV has "an empty vine." The first fits the next lines better: Israel was prospering. The trouble was what it did with the prosperity.
- **"The more his fruit increased, the more altars he built" (v1).** Prosperity did not lead to gratitude but to more religion of the wrong kind. Every good harvest was credited to Baal and celebrated with new altars and better "pillars," standing stones used in Canaanite worship (Exod 23:24; Deut 16:22). The reign of Jeroboam II (about 793–753 BC) was Israel's richest period since Solomon, and the Assyrians were still busy elsewhere. It was also the time when Amos and Hosea found the worship most corrupt (Amos 4:4–5; 5:21–23).
- **"Their heart is false" (v2).** The Hebrew verb *chalaq* has two senses: "divide" and "be smooth." Older versions take it as "divided" (KJV), a heart split between the LORD and Baal. The ESV reads "false," a heart that is smooth and slippery, flattering God with words while serving idols. Both fit Hosea's picture of Gomer. **"The LORD will break down their altars"** uses a verb for breaking an animal's neck (Exod 13:13), a vivid word for smashing altars.
- **"We have no king, for we do not fear the LORD" (v3).** Two readings are possible, and the Tyndale notes give both. It may be what the people will say after the monarchy falls, an admission that they lost their king because they lost their fear of God. Or it may be their cynical shrug in the chaos of Hosea's day, when kings came and went by assassination (2 Kgs 15): "A king—what could he do for us?" Either way, a nation that does not fear the LORD finds that no human king can save it.
- **"With empty oaths they make covenants" (v4).** This may mean broken promises in public life and courts, or treaties sworn with Assyria and Egypt and then broken (2 Kgs 17:4). **"Judgment springs up like poisonous weeds in the furrows of the field"**: what should be justice has become poison, like a bitter weed choking a ploughed field. Amos uses the same picture: "you have turned justice into poison" (Amos 6:12).
- **"The calf of Beth-aven" (v5).** Beth-aven means "house of wickedness." It is Hosea's scornful nickname for Bethel, "house of God," where Jeroboam I set up a golden calf about two hundred years earlier (4:15; 5:8). The people of Samaria will "tremble" for their idol, a god so weak its worshippers have to worry about it.
- **"Its idolatrous priests" (v5).** The word is *kemarim*, a term the Bible uses only for priests of false gods (2 Kgs 23:5; Zeph 1:4). These priests "rejoiced over it and over its glory." **"For it has departed from them"**: the verb *galah* is the word for going into exile. It also echoes the cry at the loss of the ark: "The glory has departed from Israel" (1 Sam 4:21–22). Here the "glory" is a gold-plated statue, and it is the idol that goes into exile.
- **"The thing itself shall be carried to Assyria as tribute to the great king" (v6).** "The great king" was a title of the Assyrian emperor (5:13 uses a similar phrase). Assyrian records and carvings show soldiers carrying off the statues of conquered peoples' gods as trophies. Israel's god-in-a-box will end up as one more piece of plunder on a cart, and Israel will be "ashamed of his idol."
- **"Samaria's king shall perish like a twig on the face of the waters" (v7).** "Twig" may also be translated "foam" or "chip." Either way, it is something light and helpless carried off by a current. Israel's last king, Hoshea, was arrested by Assyria and disappears from the record (2 Kgs 17:4).
- **"The high places of Aven, the sin of Israel" (v8).** The hilltop shrines will be destroyed, and **"thorn and thistle shall grow up on their altars."** The phrase recalls the curse on the ground after the fall (Gen 3:18). Once-busy altars will be so deserted that weeds grow on them.
- **"They shall say to the mountains, 'Cover us,' and to the hills, 'Fall on us'" (v8).** The terror will be so great that people will prefer to be crushed than to face what is coming. Jesus quoted these words to the women weeping as he carried his cross, warning of Jerusalem's coming destruction (Luke 23:30), and Revelation uses them of people hiding from the wrath of the Lamb (Rev 6:16).
- **"From the days of Gibeah, you have sinned" (v9).** Hosea returns to the horror of Judges 19–21 (see 9:9). There the other tribes went to war against the wickedness of Gibeah. Now Israel as a whole has become Gibeah, and **"the war against the unjust"** will overtake them as it overtook Benjamin. JFB notes the shift from "you" to "they" in the verse, which he takes as a sign of God's turning away from them.
- **"Bound up for their double iniquity" (v10).** The Hebrew is difficult. "Double iniquity" may mean their two calves at Dan and Bethel, or two great sins, rejecting God as king and rejecting him in worship. The written Hebrew text can also be read "their two furrows," which fits the farming pictures around it. Nations will be gathered against them like oxen yoked for work.
- **"Ephraim was a trained calf that loved to thresh" (v11).** A heifer treading out grain had an easy job. She walked in circles over the sheaves, and the law said she was not to be muzzled, so she could eat as she worked (Deut 25:4). Ephraim liked the easy, well-fed life. **"I spared her fair neck"**: God had not put a hard yoke on her. **"But I will put Ephraim to the yoke"**: now she must do the heavy work of ploughing, a picture of servitude under Assyria. Even **"Judah must plow"**: the southern kingdom is not exempt.
- **"Sow for yourselves righteousness; reap steadfast love" (v12).** In the middle of the threats comes an invitation. Sowing comes before reaping. If they sow righteousness, right living in line with the covenant, they will reap *hesed*, God's steadfast, loyal love. This is the same word God said he desired "and not sacrifice" (6:6). It is the opposite of 8:7: "they sow the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind."
- **"Break up your fallow ground" (v12).** Fallow ground is land left unploughed, which becomes hard, packed and overgrown. Before anything can grow it has to be broken open. Jeremiah repeats the call to Judah (Jer 4:3). The picture is of a heart that has hardened and must be broken open by repentance before God's word can take root (compare the parable of the soils, Matt 13:3–23).
- **"For it is the time to seek the LORD, that he may come and rain righteousness upon you" (v12).** There is still time. "Rain" is the key word: Israel looked to Baal, the storm god, for rain. Hosea says the LORD is the one who sends rain, and the rain he wants to send is righteousness itself (compare 6:3; Isa 45:8; Joel 2:23). The verb "rain" can also mean "teach," and some older interpreters read "teach righteousness," but "rain" fits the farming context.
- **"You have plowed iniquity; you have reaped injustice" (v13).** This is what they actually did. They ploughed wickedness, harvested injustice and **"eaten the fruit of lies."** **"Because you have trusted in your own way and in the multitude of your warriors"**: their sin was self-reliance, trusting their own plans and their army instead of God (compare Ps 20:7; Isa 31:1).
- **"As Shalman destroyed Beth-arbel on the day of battle" (v14).** Hosea's hearers knew this atrocity, but we do not. No other text records it. Shalman may be Shalmaneser V of Assyria, who besieged Samaria (2 Kgs 17:3), or Salamanu, a king of Moab named in Assyrian tribute lists around 730 BC. Beth-arbel is often identified with Irbid in northern Jordan, in Gilead. **"Mothers were dashed in pieces with their children"** describes the cruelty of ancient warfare (13:16; Ps 137:9; 2 Kgs 8:12).
- **"Thus it shall be done to you, O Bethel" (v15).** Hosea now uses Bethel's real name, "house of God," which makes the judgment sharper. The place that should have been God's house is the source of Israel's ruin. **"At dawn the king of Israel shall be utterly cut off"**: suddenly, as the dawn comes, the monarchy will end. It did. After Hoshea there was never another king in the northern kingdom.
- **Commentators:** All three read v1 as a fruitful vine whose fruit went to Israel itself and its idols rather than to God. Henry: "A vine is only valuable for its fruit," and "God is the Sovereign of the heart; he will have all, or none." Guzik notes both meanings of *chalaq* in v2 (divided or smooth) and links it to Gomer's unfaithfulness. JFB calls the calves Jeroboam's political device to keep Israel from going to Jerusalem, "not politic, but fatal to them." On the heifer, JFB says threshing was "a far easier and more self-indulgent work than ploughing." On v12 Guzik pictures the hard, unworked heart that resists God's word until it is broken up, and urges patience while the harvest grows. Henry adds, "Seeking the Lord is to be every day's work, but there are special occasions when to seek him." JFB stresses that the reward is "not of debt, but of grace." JFB identifies Shalman with Shalmaneser, while the Tyndale notes say only that the event is otherwise unknown.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel was a vine that bore fruit for itself, and the vineyard was given over to thorns. Jesus took up the picture and said, "I am the true vine" (John 15:1). He is the Israel who bore fruit for God, and those joined to him bear fruit that lasts. Hosea's call, "it is the time to seek the LORD, that he may come and rain righteousness upon you," finds its answer in him: God sent righteousness down from heaven in the person of his Son (Jer 23:6; 1 Cor 1:30). And the cry "Fall on us" was quoted by Jesus on the road to the cross, where he took on himself the judgment people would rather be crushed than face.
