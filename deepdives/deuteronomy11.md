*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Chapter 11 closes the first half of Moses' second sermon. Since chapter 5 he has been laying the foundation: the Ten Commandments, the Shema ("love the LORD your God with all your heart," 6:5), warnings against forgetting God in plenty (ch 8) and against self-righteousness (ch 9), and the call to "circumcise… your heart" (10:16). Starting in chapter 12, he will turn to detailed laws for life in the land. Here he gathers up everything so far into one final appeal: remember what you have seen, love God, teach your children, and choose. The chapter ends by pointing ahead to a ceremony on two mountains in the middle of Canaan, where Israel will hear the blessing and the curse read out once they have crossed the Jordan.

#### Love and Serve the LORD (vv1–32)
- **"You shall therefore love the LORD your God and keep his charge" (v1).** "Therefore" ties this to everything God did in chapter 10, where he renewed the covenant and showed himself the God who loves the outsider. Love comes first, then keeping his "charge" (*mishmeret*), a word for a duty one is posted to guard, like a watchman's post.
- **"I am not speaking to your children who have not known or seen it" (v2).** Moses speaks to people who were children or teenagers at the Exodus. The death sentence on the first generation fell on those twenty and older (Num 14:29), so many of those listening now had seen the Red Sea with their own eyes. Seeing brings responsibility. They are the living link to their own children, who were born in the wilderness.
- **"Consider the discipline of the LORD your God" (v2).** "Discipline" (*musar*) is the same family of words as in 8:5: God's training of his son. It includes the plagues on Egypt, the judgments in the wilderness and his care along the way.
- **"How he made the water of the Red Sea flow over them" (v4).** The Hebrew is *yam suph*, usually understood as "sea of reeds." The Greek Old Testament translated it "Red Sea," which is where the English name comes from. **"Destroyed them to this day"** means Egypt's army had not recovered. Pharaoh's chariots never came after them again.
- **"What he did to Dathan and Abiram" (v6).** These two Reubenites joined Korah's revolt against Moses and Aaron (Num 16). They refused to come when summoned and said Moses had brought them "out of a land flowing with milk and honey" to kill them in the wilderness, calling Egypt the land of milk and honey (Num 16:13). The ground split open and swallowed them with their households. Korah isn't named here, perhaps because his sons survived and later became temple singers (Num 26:11; Ps 42–49 titles). The lesson for Israel: rebellion against God's appointed order comes from inside the camp, and God judges it.
- **"For your eyes have seen all the great work of the LORD" (v7).** Moses is not asking them to believe a rumor. They were there.
- **"Keep the whole commandment… that you may be strong" (v8).** Strength comes from obedience, not the other way around. Joshua will be told the same thing: "Only be strong and very courageous, being careful to do according to all the law" (Josh 1:7).
- **"Not like the land of Egypt… where you sowed your seed and irrigated it, like a garden of vegetables" (v10).** The Hebrew says literally "watered it with your foot," as the ESV footnote notes. Egypt almost never sees rain. Farms depended on the Nile's yearly flood, channels and constant irrigation. Farmers may have opened and closed little mud dams with their feet, or worked water-lifting devices with their feet, or carried water on foot. In any case, Egypt's water came by human labor, from a river that seemed always there.
- **"A land of hills and valleys, which drinks water by the rain from heaven" (v11).** Canaan has no great river to farm from. The Jordan runs low in a deep valley, too far below most farmland to be used. Everything depends on rain, so everything depends on God. That could feel like insecurity. Moses presents it as a relationship: **"a land that the LORD your God cares for. The eyes of the LORD your God are always upon it, from the beginning of the year to the end of the year" (v12).**
- **"If you will indeed obey my commandments… he will give the rain for your land in its season" (vv13–14).** This begins the second paragraph of the Shema as Jews pray it (vv13–21 are recited with 6:4–9). Notice that God now speaks in the first person: "*my* commandments" (v13). The standard Hebrew text carries this on into "I will give the rain" (vv14–15), as if God himself breaks into Moses' speech. The ESV follows the Samaritan and Greek texts, which read "he," and gives the Hebrew in a footnote. The meaning is the same either way.
- **"The early rain and the later rain" (v14).** The **early rain** (*yoreh*) came in October and November, softening the hard summer ground so it could be ploughed and sown. The **later rain** (*malqosh*) came in March and April and filled out the grain before harvest. Between them were the winter rains. If either failed, the harvest failed. The prophets use these rains as a picture of God's renewing blessing (Joel 2:23; Hos 6:3), and James uses them to encourage patience (Jas 5:7).
- **Why rain was the battleground.** The chief god of Canaan was Baal, worshipped as the storm god who rode the clouds and sent the rain. To farmers in Canaan, worshipping Baal seemed simple common sense. Moses heads off the temptation before they even arrive: the LORD, not Baal, gives the rain. Centuries later Elijah's drought (1 Kgs 17:1) and the contest on Mount Carmel (1 Kgs 18) were a direct challenge to Baal on his own ground.
- **"Take care lest your heart be deceived" (v16).** Idolatry rarely begins with a decision to rebel. It begins with a heart that is talked into something. The verb (*patah*) means to be enticed or naive, easily persuaded.
- **"He will shut up the heavens, so that there will be no rain" (v17).** The God who opens the sky can close it. Solomon later prayed about exactly this (1 Kgs 8:35–36), and it happened in Elijah's day.
- **"Lay up these words of mine in your heart and in your soul" (vv18–20).** These verses repeat 6:6–9 almost word for word: bind them on the hand, set them between the eyes, teach the children, talk of them at home and on the road, write them on the doorposts. The repetition is deliberate. The section from 6:4 to 11:21 is framed by the same instruction at both ends, like bookends. One difference: in chapter 6 the words come straight after "love the LORD your God." Here they come after the warning about rain and drought, so the words of God are a guard against being "deceived" (v16).
- **"As long as the heavens are above the earth" (v21).** A Hebrew way of saying "forever." The promise to the fathers stands as long as the sky does.
- **"Loving the LORD your God, walking in all his ways, and holding fast to him" (v22).** Three phrases that sum up the whole commandment. "Holding fast" (*davaq*) is the clinging word used of marriage (10:20; Gen 2:24).
- **"Every place on which the sole of your foot treads shall be yours" (v24).** Walking a piece of land was a way of claiming it. God told Abraham, "walk through the length and the breadth of the land" (Gen 13:17), and Joshua hears this exact promise (Josh 1:3).
- **"From the wilderness to the Lebanon, and from the River, the river Euphrates, to the western sea" (v24).** The southern desert, the Lebanon mountains in the north, the Euphrates in the far northeast and the Mediterranean in the west. These are the widest borders promised to Abraham (Gen 15:18). Israel's control came closest to them under David and Solomon, whose rule reached the Euphrates (1 Kgs 4:21), though even then much of that area was held by subject kings rather than settled by Israel.
- **"See, I am setting before you today a blessing and a curse" (v26).** "See" is singular in Hebrew, as if speaking to each person, and the choice is stark. In ancient covenants, blessings and curses were the standard closing section. Deuteronomy will spell them out at length in chapters 27–28. Here they are set out in two lines: blessing "if you obey," curse "if you do not obey… to go after other gods that you have not known" (v28). Israel had known the LORD by experience. The other gods were strangers who had done nothing for them.
- **"You shall set the blessing on Mount Gerizim and the curse on Mount Ebal" (v29).** These two mountains face each other across a narrow valley in the heart of the land, with the town of Shechem in the pass between them. Gerizim is to the south, Ebal to the north, both rising nearly 3,000 feet above sea level. The valley forms a natural amphitheater where voices carry. Chapter 27 gives the full instructions, and Joshua carried them out (Josh 8:30–35). Some travelers have described Gerizim as greener and Ebal as barer, but that has been overstated, and the text doesn't make anything of it.
- **"Beside the oak of Moreh" (v30).** This is where Abraham built his first altar in Canaan when God promised, "To your offspring I will give this land" (Gen 12:6–7). Jacob buried his household's idols under an oak near Shechem (Gen 35:4). Israel's renewal of the covenant would take place where the promise first came to Abraham. Later, Jesus met the Samaritan woman nearby, and she pointed to Gerizim: "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain" (John 4:20).
- **"Opposite Gilgal" (v30).** Some take this as the Gilgal near Jericho where Israel first camped after crossing the Jordan (Josh 4:19), seen from the road that runs up toward Shechem. Others think it is a different Gilgal near Shechem, since the Jericho site is more than twenty miles away. "Gilgal" means "circle," perhaps of standing stones, and several places bore the name.
- **"You shall be careful to do all the statutes and the rules that I am setting before you today" (v32).** This sentence closes the general sermon and opens the door to the specific "statutes and rules" in chapters 12–26.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry contrasts the flat, river-fed land of Egypt with Canaan, which "drank water of the rain of heaven," and draws a lesson: "the less we have to do for our bodies the more we should do for God and our souls." He says the blessing and curse were proclaimed first thing in Canaan so that Israel "might know upon what terms they stood." JFB describes Egypt's sluices and channels "worked by the foot" and dates the early rain to September and October and the latter rain to March and April. It reads v24 as describing the promised land's borders, fulfilled under Solomon, not a promise of world rule. JFB has nothing on Dathan and Abiram, vv18–23 or vv26–32, referring the reader back to 6:1 and forward to 27:11. Guzik explains the irrigation as a foot-operated device and links the rain promise to Baal, the Canaanite weather god. He presses the warning that prosperity deceives, and notes that constant dependence on rain kept Israel dependent on God, which is a gift, not a hardship. On the blessing and curse, he is careful to add that believers in Christ stand under a different arrangement: the curse fell on Christ (Gal 3:13), so blessing rests on his work, though God's discipline still applies. He passes on an older suggestion that the names Gerizim and Ebal suggest fruitfulness and barrenness, which is not well supported.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel stood between two mountains, one of blessing and one of curse, and over the centuries chose the curse again and again. The New Testament says Christ came under that curse in his people's place: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us… so that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles" (Gal 3:13–14). He is also the one who "loved the LORD with all his heart" and so inherits the land and the promise, and who shares that inheritance with those joined to him. Near Mount Gerizim he told a Samaritan woman that the time had come when true worshipers would worship "in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24), not on one mountain or another, but through him.
