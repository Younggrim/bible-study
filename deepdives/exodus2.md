*Setting:* Pharaoh has just ordered every Hebrew boy thrown into the Nile (1:22). Chapter 2 moves from a whole nation to one family, and from Pharaoh's plans to God's quiet ones. Eighty years pass in twenty-five verses. Stephen's speech in Acts 7:20–30 divides Moses' life into three forties: forty years in Pharaoh's court, forty in Midian, and forty leading Israel in the wilderness. This chapter covers the first two. God is barely mentioned until the last three verses, but his hand is behind every coincidence.

#### The Birth of Moses (vv1–10)
- **"A man from the house of Levi… a Levite woman" (v1).** Their names, Amram and Jochebed, come later (6:20). They are left unnamed here, and so is the sister, because the focus is on the child. Both parents being Levites matters later: the deliverer comes from the tribe that will serve at God's tabernacle. Moses already has an older sister and a brother, Aaron, three years older (7:7).
- **"She saw that he was a fine child" (v2).** The Hebrew is simply *tov*, "good," the word God spoke over creation: "God saw that it was good" (Gen 1:4). Stephen calls him "beautiful in God's sight" (Acts 7:20). Hebrews 11:23 credits the parents' hiding of him to faith: "they were not afraid of the king's edict."
- **"A basket made of bulrushes" (v3).** The word for "basket" is *tevah*, used elsewhere in the Bible only for Noah's ark (Gen 6:14). It was probably an Egyptian loanword for a chest. Both arks are sealed against the water and carry the one through whom God will save his people. The "bulrushes" are papyrus reeds, light and strong, which Egyptians also used to make boats (Isa 18:2). "Bitumen and pitch" waterproofed it.
- **"Among the reeds by the river bank" (v3).** "Reeds" is *suph*. Moses is saved among the *suph* of the Nile, and he will later lead Israel through the *Yam Suph*, the "Sea of Reeds," the Red Sea (15:4). She obeyed Pharaoh's order in a strange way: her son was put in the Nile, but in an ark.
- **"His sister stood at a distance" (v4).** This is almost certainly Miriam (15:20; Num 26:59). She watches "to know what would be done to him," a quiet echo of God, who "knew" at the end of the chapter (v25).
- **"The daughter of Pharaoh came down to bathe" (v5).** The Egyptians revered the Nile as a life-giving god, so this bathing may have been partly religious. Her name isn't given. Josephus calls her Thermuthis, and Jewish tradition links her with "Bithiah, the daughter of Pharaoh" in 1 Chronicles 4:18. Those who hold the earlier date sometimes suggest Hatshepsut, who later ruled as Pharaoh herself, but that is speculation.
- **"The baby was crying. She took pity on him" (v6).** She knows at once he is a Hebrew, and she knows her father's law. Her compassion is the first crack in Pharaoh's plan, and it comes from inside his own house.
- **"Shall I go and call you a nurse from the Hebrew women?" (v7).** The sister's quick thinking is perfect. The result is a beautiful irony: Moses' mother gets her own son back and is paid by Pharaoh's daughter to nurse him (v9). Children were often nursed for two or three years, long enough for a mother to shape a child's sense of who his people and his God were.
- **"He became her son. She named him Moses" (v10).** *Mose* is an Egyptian word meaning "born of" or "son," found in royal names like Ahmose and Thutmose. The narrator hears a Hebrew pun in it: *mashah*, "to draw out." Strictly, the Hebrew form *Mosheh* means "one who draws out," not "one drawn out." The baby drawn from the water will be the one who draws Israel out through the water. Stephen adds that Moses "was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (Acts 7:22): reading and writing, law and administration, all useful for the man who would write the Law.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in vv1–10 "the perils of his birth and infancy" and then "his preservation through those perils." JFB says papyrus is "a thick, strong, and tough reed," that the basket was laid at the bank rather than set adrift, and that the princess's bathing was probably "a religious solemnity." Guzik notes that *tevah* is used elsewhere only for Noah's ark (the lexicon agrees) and reads the mother's act as an act of trust, letting go of her son into God's care. He cites Josephus's claims that Moses was heir to the throne and led Egypt's armies, but treats them as tradition, not Scripture.

#### Moses Flees to Midian (vv11–22)
- **"When Moses had grown up, he went out to his people" (v11).** Stephen says he was "forty years old" (Acts 7:23). "His people" appears twice in this verse. Raised as an Egyptian prince, he still knows whom he belongs to. Hebrews 11:24–26 says he "refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter, choosing rather to be mistreated with the people of God," counting "the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt."
- **"He looked this way and that… he struck down the Egyptian" (v12).** The same verb "strike" (*nakah*) is used for what the Egyptian was doing to the Hebrew. Moses checks that no one is watching but never looks up to ask God. He acts on his own, and he hides the body in the sand. Stephen says "he supposed that his brothers would understand that God was giving them salvation by his hand, but they did not understand" (Acts 7:25). The right goal pursued the wrong way. Bible readers have long debated whether this was justified defense of the helpless or plain murder, and the text itself doesn't say. What it shows is that it failed.
- **"Why do you strike your companion?" (v13).** Moses now tries to make peace between two Hebrews. The man in the wrong turns on him: **"Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (v14).** It's a question the book will answer: God made him one (3:10). Stephen applies the line to Israel's pattern of rejecting the deliverers God sends (Acts 7:35), and ultimately to the rejection of Jesus (Acts 7:51–52).
- **"Moses was afraid… Pharaoh… sought to kill Moses" (vv14–15).** Hebrews 11:27 says Moses left Egypt "not being afraid of the anger of the king." Most interpreters think Hebrews refers to the final exodus, not this flight, or that it describes Moses' deeper motive, faith choosing God's people over Egypt.
- **"Stayed in the land of Midian. And he sat down by a well" (v15).** The Midianites were descendants of Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:1–2), desert herders and traders living east of the Gulf of Aqaba and in parts of Sinai. A meeting at a well is a familiar Bible scene: Abraham's servant found Rebekah at a well (Gen 24), and Jacob met Rachel at one (Gen 29). Readers would expect a marriage to follow, and it does.
- **"Moses stood up and saved them" (v17).** "Saved" is *yasha*, the root of the names Joshua and Jesus. The would-be rescuer of Israel is still a rescuer by nature, defending seven women against bullying shepherds and then doing their work for them.
- **"An Egyptian delivered us" (v19).** The daughters take him for an Egyptian by his clothes and speech. "Delivered" is *natsal*, the word God will use when he says, "I have come down to deliver them" (3:8).
- **"Their father Reuel" (v18).** His name means "friend of God." He is later called Jethro (3:1; 18:1). People in the region often had more than one name or title, and Numbers 10:29 adds another layer, so scholars debate how the names fit. As "priest of Midian" he seems to have worshipped the true God in some form, since he later offers sacrifice to the LORD (18:12).
- **"Why have you left the man? Call him, that he may eat bread" (v20).** Desert hospitality was a sacred duty, and Reuel is also not about to lose a capable son-in-law.
- **Zipporah and Gershom (vv21–22).** Zipporah means "bird." Gershom sounds like *ger sham*, "a sojourner there." The prince of Egypt now feels like a foreigner everywhere. Abraham called himself the same (Gen 23:4), and so did Israel in Egypt (Gen 15:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Moses "looked on their burdens as one that not only pitied them" but was ready to help, and reckoned it "much more his honour and advantage to be a son of Abraham" than a prince of Egypt. On the Hebrew's retort: "It is a sign of guilt to be impatient of reproof." JFB calls the killing, "in plain terms, a deed of assassination," but warns against judging it by modern standards, since custom bound a kinsman to defend his brother's blood. Guzik is harder on Moses: first a killer, then a meddler, a man too full of his own strength for God to use yet, and Midian is where God humbles him. The Tyndale notes agree that his heart for his people was right while his timing and method were not.

#### God Hears Israel's Groaning (vv23–25)
- **"During those many days the king of Egypt died" (v23).** Forty years have passed. A new king brings no relief. **"Groaned… cried out… their cry… came up to God."** The groaning isn't said to be a prayer. They cry out in pain, and God hears it anyway.
- **"God heard… God remembered… God saw… God knew" (vv24–25).** "God" is named five times in three verses and is the subject of four strong verbs, after being almost silent through the chapter. "Remembered" doesn't mean God had forgotten. In Hebrew it means to act on a commitment, as when "God remembered Noah" and the waters went down (Gen 8:1). The covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Gen 15; 26:3; 28:13–15) is the reason he acts. The last phrase is literally just "and God knew," with no object. It's personal, caring knowledge, and it means he is about to act.
- **Commentators:** Henry and Guzik both stress that God acted for his covenant, "not to any merit of theirs" (Henry). Henry adds that God "remembered his covenant, which he seemed to have forgotten, but of which he is ever mindful." JFB thinks Israel may have had some relief under Moses' royal patroness, but that the persecution "was renewed with increased severity" under the next king, and has no note on vv24–25.

#### Pointing to Christ
A baby is born under a king's death sentence, hidden and saved by God's providence, and grows up to be rejected by his own people with the words "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" Stephen draws the line straight to Jesus (Acts 7:35–52). Jesus too escaped a murderous king as a baby (Matt 2:13–16) and "came to his own, and his own people did not receive him" (John 1:11). But where Moses tried to save by striking down an enemy and failed, Jesus saved by being struck down himself. And the God who "heard… remembered… saw… knew" is the same God who, in Jesus, came down to share his people's suffering (Heb 2:14–18; 4:15).
