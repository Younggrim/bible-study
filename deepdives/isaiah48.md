*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapters 40–47 have argued one case from many angles: the LORD alone is God, he alone foretells the future, and he will free his people through Cyrus and bring down Babylon and its gods. Chapter 48 closes that part of the book by turning the spotlight on Israel itself. The tone is blunt. God's people are religious in name but not in heart, and stubborn from birth. Yet the chapter ends with the command to leave Babylon singing. Its last line, "There is no peace… for the wicked," returns word for word at 57:21. Many readers see it as a refrain that divides chapters 40–66 into three sections of nine chapters each.

#### Israel Refined for God's Glory (vv1–11)
- **"Hear this, O house of Jacob, who are called by the name of Israel" (v1).** "Called by the name of" can mean simply bearing a name without living up to it. Jacob was the grasping heel-holder (Gen 25:26; 27:36). Israel was the name God gave him after he wrestled at the Jabbok (Gen 32:28). The people carry the honored name, but the chapter will show they are still Jacob at heart.
- **"Who came from the waters of Judah" (v1).** It is a poetic way of saying "descended from Judah," as a stream comes from a spring (Ps 68:26). Judah was the tribe of the kings and the line from which the Messiah would come (Gen 49:10). JFB suggests the image of a fountain from which Messiah would spring. Guzik takes it less kindly, recalling Judah's own failings (Gen 37–38). The text itself simply names their ancestry.
- **"Who swear by the name of the LORD and confess the God of Israel, but not in truth or right" (v1).** They used God's name in oaths and spoke of him in worship, but it was empty. Jeremiah says the same: "Though they say, 'As the LORD lives,' yet they swear falsely" (Jer 5:2). Jesus quotes Isaiah on the point: "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (Matt 15:8; Isa 29:13).
- **"For they call themselves after the holy city, and stay themselves on the God of Israel" (v2).** They claimed citizenship in Jerusalem, "the holy city," and said they leaned on God. It sounds like faith. But leaning on God's name while ignoring his word was a false security, the same mistake Jeremiah exposed: "This is the temple of the LORD" (Jer 7:4).
- **"The former things I declared of old… then suddenly I did them" (v3).** God reminds them of prophecies already fulfilled, such as the fall of Samaria and the defeat of Sennacherib (chs 36–37). He announced them long before and then acted suddenly.
- **"Because I know that you are obstinate, and your neck is an iron sinew and your forehead brass" (v4).** A stiff neck is the Bible's picture of an ox that will not bend to the yoke (Exod 32:9; Acts 7:51). A forehead of brass is a face that cannot blush, shameless and hard (Jer 3:3; Ezek 3:7).
- **"Lest you should say, 'My idol did them'" (v5).** God announced events ahead of time *because* he knew Israel would otherwise give the credit to idols. Prophecy was not only proof to the nations. It was a defense against Israel's own idolatry.
- **"From this time forth I announce to you new things, hidden things that you have not known" (v6).** The "new things" are the fall of Babylon, the release under Cyrus, and beyond that the work of the Servant in chapters 49–53. **"They are created now"** (v7). "Created" is *bara'*, God's creation word. These events are not the natural outcome of what came before. They are fresh acts of God.
- **"Lest you should say, 'Behold, I knew them'" (v7).** God kept these things hidden until now so that Israel could not claim it saw them coming by its own wisdom.
- **"For I knew that you would surely deal treacherously, and that from before birth you were called a rebel" (v8).** "From before birth" probably means from the very start of Israel's history as a nation. They rebelled at the Red Sea, at Sinai with the golden calf, and in the wilderness (Ps 106:7; Ezek 20:5–8). It is also true of Jacob the ancestor, who was grasping his brother's heel at birth. Many readers also hear in it the wider truth of Psalm 51:5: sin runs deeper than single acts, to the roots of human nature.
- **"For my name's sake I defer my anger; for the sake of my praise I restrain it" (v9).** Here is the turning point. Why would God not destroy such a people? Not because they deserved mercy, but because of who he is. "Restrain" may mean "muzzle," as JFB puts it. "For my name's sake" means for the sake of his reputation and character, his faithfulness to his promises (Ps 23:3; Ezek 36:22–23).
- **"Behold, I have refined you, but not as silver; I have tried you in the furnace of affliction" (v10).** A silversmith melted ore in a furnace until the impurities rose and could be skimmed off. God put Israel through the furnace of exile, but "not as silver." That may mean he did not refine them as long or as hard as silver needs, or he would have found nothing left (1:22, 25; Jer 6:29–30). The exile was discipline, not destruction. Moses called Egypt "the iron furnace" from which God took Israel (Deut 4:20). Here Babylon is the new furnace.
- **A difference in the manuscripts.** The traditional Hebrew text reads "I have *chosen* you in the furnace of affliction" (KJV). The great Isaiah scroll from the Dead Sea Scrolls, about a thousand years older than most Hebrew manuscripts, reads "I have *tested* you," which fits the parallel with "refined." The ESV follows the scroll, with a footnote. The two Hebrew words differ by one letter. Both are true: God chose Israel and tested it, and the tested people are still his chosen.
- **"For my own sake, for my own sake, I do it, for how should my name be profaned? My glory I will not give to another" (v11).** The double "for my own sake" is emphatic. If God destroyed Israel, the nations would say Israel's God had failed (Ezek 36:20–23). Israel's sin had already dragged his name through the mud. God acts to protect his name, which means he acts to save. "My glory I will not give to another" repeats 42:8.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God took pains to predict events "because he knew they were obstinate," so that they would be "inexcusable." On v9 he stresses that God's mercy came "not for their sake… but for his name's sake." Guzik draws a pointed lesson: God, not ourselves, is at the center of the universe, so his refining fires serve his glory, and they serve our good too. JFB notes the alternative reading of v10, "tried… proved," and links Zechariah 13:9 and 1 Peter 1:7, where faith is tested like gold in fire. Guzik reads "Jacob" as "deceiver, cheater." The name is tied to grasping the heel (Gen 25:26) and to "he has cheated me" in Esau's bitter wordplay (Gen 27:36). "Deceiver" is a popular summary rather than the name's literal meaning.

#### The LORD's Call to Israel (vv12–22)
- **"Listen to me, O Jacob, and Israel, whom I called! I am he; I am the first, and I am the last" (v12).** The trial speeches of chapters 41–48 come to a close with God's full name for himself (41:4; 44:6). The risen Jesus uses the same words in Revelation 1:17 and 22:13.
- **"My hand laid the foundation of the earth… when I call to them, they stand forth together" (v13).** Heaven and earth stand at attention when God calls, like soldiers answering a roll call (40:26). If creation answers his call, his people should too.
- **"Who among them has declared these things?" (v14).** The challenge to the idols one final time. None of them predicted Cyrus.
- **"The LORD loves him; he shall perform his purpose on Babylon" (v14).** "Him" is Cyrus. It is startling that God says he "loves" a pagan king. In covenant language, "love" can mean choosing someone for a purpose, as in "Jacob I loved" (Mal 1:2). Cyrus is God's chosen instrument against Babylon. "His arm shall be against the Chaldeans" means Cyrus's power, given by God.
- **"I, even I, have spoken and called him… he will prosper in his way" (v15).** The repeated "I" stresses that this is God's doing from start to finish.
- **"From the beginning I have not spoken in secret" (v16).** God's prophecies were given openly, not in riddles like pagan oracles (45:19).
- **"And now the Lord GOD has sent me, and his Spirit" (v16).** Suddenly a new voice speaks. Who is "me"? There are three main views. Some say the prophet, claiming his authority as sent by God and the Spirit. Some say Cyrus, though nothing suggests Cyrus has the Spirit. Many Christian interpreters, Guzik and JFB among them, hear the Servant, who speaks in the first person in the very next verses (49:1). The verse also reads naturally in the light of the Trinity: the Lord GOD and his Spirit send the speaker. Henry notes that the Spirit is spoken of "as a person distinct from the Father and the Son." Grammatically it is also possible to read "the Lord GOD has sent me *with* his Spirit." Either way, Jesus later says, "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me" (Luke 4:18, quoting Isa 61:1).
- **"I am the LORD your God, who teaches you to profit, who leads you in the way you should go" (v17).** God is a teacher and a guide, not only a judge. "To profit" means for their real good. The idols "do not profit" (44:9–10), but God's teaching does.
- **"Oh that you had paid attention to my commandments!" (v18).** This is one of the most tender cries in the Old Testament. God grieves over what might have been. Compare Deuteronomy 5:29, "Oh that they had such a mind as this always," and Psalm 81:13. Jesus wept over Jerusalem the same way: "Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace!" (Luke 19:42).
- **"Then your peace would have been like a river, and your righteousness like the waves of the sea" (v18).** In a dry land, a river that never runs dry was a picture of endless blessing. "Peace" (*shalom*) means wholeness and well-being. Waves keep coming and never stop. Amos uses a similar picture: "let righteousness roll down like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24).
- **"Your offspring would have been like the sand" (v19).** This echoes God's promise to Abraham (Gen 22:17). Instead, the exile had cut Israel down to a remnant. Their "name would never be cut off," which is what disobedience nearly brought about.
- **"Go out from Babylon, flee from Chaldea" (v20).** The command is urgent. When the door opens, leave. Not all exiles did. Many had settled down in Babylon (Jer 29:5–7), and a large Jewish community stayed there for centuries. Jeremiah gives the same command (Jer 50:8; 51:6, 45). Revelation echoes it for the last Babylon: "Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins" (Rev 18:4).
- **"Declare this with a shout of joy… send it out to the end of the earth; say, 'The LORD has redeemed his servant Jacob!'" (v20).** The return is good news for the whole world, not only for Israel. It is an early picture of the gospel going "to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8).
- **"They did not thirst when he led them through the deserts; he made water flow for them from the rock" (v21).** This recalls the first Exodus, when Moses struck the rock and water came out (Exod 17:6; Num 20:11). The return from Babylon is a new Exodus. Ezra does not record water from a rock on the way home, and the language is probably poetic, drawn from the Exodus, to say God would provide. Paul says of the first rock, "the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4).
- **"'There is no peace,' says the LORD, 'for the wicked'" (v22).** After the call to joy comes a sober warning. Leaving Babylon does not automatically bring peace. Those who stay wicked, in Babylon or in Jerusalem, will not know the river of peace of v18. The same line ends chapter 57.
- **Commentators:** JFB hears in v18 "the desire expressed by the same Messiah" in Matthew 23:37 and Luke 19:42. Henry links it to Deuteronomy 5:29 and says God "did not afflict them willingly." Guzik calls v18–19 a lament over unfulfilled potential: peace like a river, righteousness like waves, children like sand, all missed through disobedience. On v16, Guzik and JFB both hear the Messiah speaking, and Henry takes it as the prophet speaking as a type of the great Prophet. The Tyndale notes leave the speaker open. JFB notes on v21 that Ezra does not mention water from the rock, and reads the language as blending the two deliverances. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both note that the "no peace" refrain closes a section at 48:22 and again at 57:21.

#### Pointing to Christ
God would not destroy his stubborn people, "for my own sake," and that is the ground of every sinner's hope. God's name is not protected by sweeping sinners away but by saving them in a way that shows he is both just and merciful. The voice "sent" by "the Lord GOD and his Spirit" is heard fully in Jesus, anointed with the Spirit and sent by the Father (Luke 4:18; John 20:21). He wept, as God does in v18, over a city that would not listen. He is the rock from which living water flows (John 7:37–39; 1 Cor 10:4). And he alone gives the peace the wicked cannot have: "Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you" (John 14:27).
