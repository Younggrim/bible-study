*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Eight chapters of genealogies have traced Israel from Adam to the tribes in their land. Now the Chronicler jumps straight to his own era. In one sentence he passes over the exile itself, and then he lists the people who came back to live in Jerusalem: lay families, priests, Levites, gatekeepers and temple workers. Many of the same names appear in Nehemiah 11, which lists Jerusalem's settlers in Nehemiah's day (about 445 BC), so this list probably comes from the first generations after the return that began in 538 BC. The chapter is the bridge from the family records to the story. Its last section repeats Saul's family tree from chapter 8, because the narrative is about to start with Saul's death. For the first readers, the message was that the line had not been broken. The people who rebuilt Jerusalem and served in its temple were the same Israel, doing the same work, in the same order David had set up.

#### A Genealogy of the Returned Exiles (vv1–34)
- **"So all Israel was recorded in genealogies" (v1).** This closes the genealogies that began in chapter 1. "All Israel" is one of the Chronicler's favorite phrases. Even though most of the northern tribes had been scattered, Israel is still twelve tribes in his eyes, and the returned community stands for the whole.
- **"These are written in the Book of the Kings of Israel" (v1).** Not the biblical books of Kings, but an official record now lost, which the Chronicler cites as a source (2 Chr 20:34). JFB thinks the public registers survived the exiles and were carefully preserved, which is why the returned community could prove its families (Ezra 2:62).
- **"And Judah was taken into exile in Babylon because of their breach of faith" (v1).** Seventy years of history in one clause, with the reason stated plainly. "Breach of faith" is *maal* again, the unfaithfulness that sent the eastern tribes into exile (5:25) and that will end Saul's reign (10:13). The Chronicler does not blame Babylon's army or politics. Judah went into exile because it was unfaithful to God.
- **"The first to dwell again in their possessions in their cities" (v2).** And then, at once, the return. The exile gets one line, and the homecoming gets a chapter. Guzik, following Martin Selman, puts it this way: the winter is over and spring has come. **"In their possessions"** recalls the land God gave each family under Joshua. They came back not to strange land but to their inheritance.
- **"Israel, the priests, the Levites, and the temple servants" (v2).** The four groups of the returned community, as in Ezra 2:70. "Israel" means the lay families. The **temple servants** are the *Nethinim*, literally "the given ones," people given to help the Levites in the temple's heavy work (Ezra 8:20). Many think they descended from foreigners such as the Gibeonites, who were made woodcutters and water carriers for God's house (Josh 9:27). Outsiders had become a fixed part of Israel's worship.
- **"Judah, Benjamin, Ephraim, and Manasseh lived in Jerusalem" (v3).** Two northern tribes are named alongside Judah and Benjamin. Some of the northern families had come south long before, when Jeroboam set up idolatrous worship and when faithful northerners came to Judah under kings like Asa and Hezekiah (2 Chr 11:16; 15:9; 30:11). The Tyndale notes take this verse as evidence that members of the northern tribes were part of the return. The returned community was not Judah alone.
- **Perez, Shelah and Zerah (vv4–6).** The three clans of Judah from Judah's sons (2:3–4). "The Shilonites" are probably descendants of Shelah. **690** of Zerah's family settled in the city.
- **"Of the Benjaminites… 956" (vv7–9).** Benjamin was well represented, as the previous chapter led us to expect.
- **"Azariah the son of Hilkiah… the chief officer of the house of God" (v11).** The priests' list includes a descendant of Hilkiah and Zadok (6:12–13), the high-priestly line. Jedaiah and Jehoiarib (v10) were the heads of two of the priestly divisions David set up (24:7). Jehoiarib's division later produced the Maccabees, and the division of Abijah, from the same list, produced Zechariah, father of John the Baptist (Luke 1:5).
- **"Mighty men for the work of the service of the house of God" (v13).** The phrase is the same one used of warriors, "men of valor." Guzik picks up G. Campbell Morgan's point that it is striking to describe priests this way. Temple service took strength, skill and courage as much as war did.
- **The Levites (vv14–16).** A Merarite, then descendants of **Asaph** and **Jeduthun**, two of the three singers' families David appointed (6:39, 44; 25:1). The music David organized was still being sung by the same families centuries later. **"The villages of the Netophathites"** (v16) were near Bethlehem, where singers lived after the return (Neh 12:28).
- **"The gatekeepers were Shallum, Akkub, Talmon, Ahiman" (v17).** The gatekeepers get more space than anyone else in the chapter. Their job was guarding the temple's entrances, keeping out the unclean, managing the storerooms and opening the doors each morning.
- **"In the king's gate on the east side" (v18).** The east gate faced the Mount of Olives and was the main entrance. JFB suggests it was the king's private entrance and that the gatekeepers kept up their post with no king on the throne, "probably in the hope" that David's line would be restored. That is a guess, but it fits the hope that runs through Chronicles. Ezekiel saw the glory of the LORD return to the temple through the east gate (Ezek 43:1–4).
- **"Shallum the son of Kore, son of Ebiasaph, son of Korah… the Korahites" (v19).** The gatekeepers were Korah's descendants, the family of the rebel who died in the wilderness (Num 16). One of the psalms of the sons of Korah says, "I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness" (Ps 84:10). Here are the sons of Korah doing exactly that.
- **"As their fathers had been in charge of the camp of the LORD, keepers of the entrance" (v19).** The Chronicler links the gatekeepers of his day to the wilderness camp under Moses, when the Levites guarded the tabernacle (Num 3:5–10, 38). The work was the same across a thousand years.
- **"Phinehas the son of Eleazar was the chief officer over them in time past; the LORD was with him" (v20).** Phinehas was Aaron's grandson, who stopped a plague by acting with zeal for God's holiness when an Israelite brought a Midianite woman into the camp (Num 25:7–13). God gave him "a covenant of peace." "The LORD was with him" is a short blessing, and Guzik, following Selman, notes that it implies the same presence for the faithful gatekeepers who followed.
- **"David and Samuel the seer established them in their office of trust" (v22).** The arrangement went back to David, with Samuel's involvement, even before the temple was built. "Office of trust" uses a word related to *emunah*, faithfulness. These were posts given to people who could be relied on. **212** gatekeepers served at the thresholds.
- **"Obligated to come in every seven days, in turn" (v25).** The gatekeepers lived in villages around Jerusalem and came in by turns for a week of duty, as the priests did (Luke 1:8–9). The four chief gatekeepers lived on site.
- **"They lodged around the house of God, for on them lay the duty of watching, and they had charge of opening it every morning" (v27).** Men who slept by the doors and unlocked God's house at dawn. It was ordinary, faithful work, and it was written down.
- **Utensils, flour, wine, oil, spices and flat cakes (vv28–32).** Every task had a name attached. Some counted the sacred vessels in and out (v28). Some kept the supplies (v29). Only priests mixed the holy incense, since that blend was reserved for God's use (Exod 30:34–38). **Mattithiah** baked the flat cakes for the grain offerings (Lev 2:4–5; 6:20–21). Kohathites prepared **the showbread** every Sabbath, the twelve loaves set before the LORD, one for each tribe (Lev 24:5–9). Twelve loaves were still being set out for twelve tribes, even though most of the twelve had been scattered.
- **"The singers… free from other service, for they were on duty day and night" (v33).** Praise went on around the clock. The singers were released from other duties so the music never stopped. Compare the night praise of Psalm 134:1, "who stand by night in the house of the LORD." **"These lived in Jerusalem"** (v34) echoes v3 and closes the list.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the genealogies served to guide the returned Jews to their families and homes, and that the returnees "began the rebuilding of it upon the old foundation." On the gatekeepers he writes, "This seemed a mean office; and yet David would rather have it than dwell in the tents of wickedness." On the clear division of duties: "God is the God of order: but that which is every body's work will be nobody's work." And on the singers: "Thus devout people might, at any hour, have assistance in their devotion." Guzik, drawing on F. B. Meyer, makes a similar point: the whole work depended on each person being faithful in his own part. JFB notes how many of these names reappear in Nehemiah 11 and that the fourfold division of priests, Levites, laypeople and temple servants continued after the exile.

#### Saul's Genealogy Repeated (vv35–44)
- **"In Gibeon lived the father of Gibeon, Jeiel" (v35).** These ten verses repeat 8:29–38 almost word for word, with small spelling differences (Tarea and Tahrea, Jehoaddah and Jarah) and with **Ner** included in v36, where 8:30 left him out. The Tyndale notes explain the purpose: Saul's family has already been placed within Benjamin, and repeating it here makes the turn from the lists to the story, which opens with Saul's last battle.
- **"These also lived opposite their kinsmen in Jerusalem" (v38).** Saul's relatives lived in Jerusalem alongside everyone else. Guzik, following Selman, sees hope in that: the family that lost the throne still had a place among God's people.
- **"Ner fathered Kish, Kish fathered Saul" (v39).** The list is laid out once more, through Jonathan and Merib-baal (Mephibosheth) to Azel's six sons. It stops short of the archers of 8:39–40. The Chronicler has what he needs. Saul is about to die, and the kingdom will be turned over to David (10:14).

#### Pointing to Christ
Everything in this chapter turns on a building and the people who served in it. Gatekeepers kept its doors, priests offered its sacrifices, Levites baked its bread, and singers filled it with praise day and night. The returned exiles kept that worship going in a small, poor city, waiting for God to finish what he had begun. Jesus said of himself, "something greater than the temple is here" (Matt 12:6). He is the true bread set before the Father, the gate through whom the sheep come in (John 10:9), and the priest who has passed through the heavens (Heb 4:14). The night-and-day praise of the singers has its fulfilment where the living creatures "never cease to say, 'Holy, holy, holy'" (Rev 4:8), and every redeemed person, from every tribe, has a place in that service.
