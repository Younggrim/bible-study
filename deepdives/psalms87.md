*Setting:* "A Psalm of the Sons of Korah. A Song." The Sons of Korah were a family of Levite singers and temple gatekeepers (1 Chr 9:19; 26:1), descendants of the Korah who rebelled against Moses and died under God's judgment (Num 16). His sons did not die with him (Num 26:11), and their line became temple musicians. That history gives this short psalm an edge: a family that once stood outside God's favor now sings of outsiders being written in as citizens. No date or event is named. JFB thinks it was prompted by the same deliverance as Psalm 46, perhaps the rescue of Jerusalem from Assyria in Hezekiah's day, and Henry allows that it may come either from a time when Zion flourished or from the ruins after the exile, as a song of hope. Psalm 87 is one of the "Songs of Zion" (46; 48; 76; 84; 122), but it goes further than any of them. Its subject is not only the city's walls and temple but its citizen list, and the names on it are astonishing. The Hebrew is compact and abrupt, and some scholars think the lines have been disturbed in copying. The ESV reads it as it stands, and the sense is clear enough.

#### The City He Founded (vv1–3)
- **"On the holy mount stands the city he founded" (v1).** The psalm opens without naming God, as if everyone already knows whose city this is. Literally "his foundation is on the holy mountains" (the Hebrew is plural). Jerusalem sits on a group of hills, with Zion and the temple mount among them. The city's strength is not its height or its walls but its founder. Isaiah says the same: "The LORD has founded Zion, and in her the afflicted of his people find refuge" (Isa 14:32).
- **"The LORD loves the gates of Zion more than all the dwelling places of Jacob" (v2).** "Gates" stand for the whole city, the place where people came in and where public life happened. "The dwelling places of Jacob" are the towns and villages of Israel. God loved all his people, but he chose one place to put his name (Deut 12:5; Ps 78:68; 132:13–14). The love is God's choice, not Zion's merit. Mount Zion was a Jebusite fortress until David took it (2 Sam 5:6–9).
- **"Glorious things of you are spoken, O city of God" (v3).** The words are addressed to the city itself. "Spoken" suggests things said by God through his prophets: that he dwells there, that he will defend it, that the nations will stream to it (Isa 2:2–3). The line became the first line of John Newton's hymn "Glorious Things of Thee Are Spoken," and Augustine took the title of his great work *The City of God* from this psalm's phrase. **Selah** is probably a musical or liturgical marker, a pause.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads Zion as "a type and figure of the gospel-church," and on v1 says, "Holiness is the strength and stability of the church." JFB notes that "spoken of thee" may also be rendered "in thee," things said within the city. Guzik insists that the city's glory comes from God's presence and choice, and he lists the great moments of redemption tied to Jerusalem, from Abraham offering Isaac on Moriah to the cross, the empty tomb and Pentecost.

#### Born in Zion (vv4–6)
- **"Among those who know me I mention Rahab and Babylon" (v4).** Now God himself speaks. "Rahab" here is not the woman of Jericho. It is a different Hebrew word, *rahab*, meaning "storm" or "arrogance," a name for the sea monster of chaos (Job 26:12; Ps 89:10) and a poetic nickname for Egypt (Isa 30:7). The two names are Israel's two great oppressors: Egypt, which enslaved them at the beginning, and Babylon, which would destroy the city and carry them away. God puts them on the list of "those who know me." To "know" God in the Old Testament is not mere awareness but relationship, the word used of covenant faithfulness (Jer 31:34; Hos 6:6).
- **"Behold, Philistia and Tyre, with Cush" (v4).** The list keeps going: the Philistines, Israel's old enemies on the coast; Tyre, the proud, wealthy trading city to the north (Ezek 27–28); and Cush, the kingdom south of Egypt in what is now Sudan, the far edge of the known world. North, south, east, west, enemy and stranger, they are all named.
- **"'This one was born there,' they say" (v4).** This is the shock of the psalm. People from these nations will be counted as native-born citizens of Zion, not tolerated foreigners or second-class guests. In the ancient world, birthplace set your rights. Paul was proud that he was "born a citizen" of Rome (Acts 22:28). Here the birthplace that counts is God's city, and he grants it to people born somewhere else entirely.
- **"Of Zion it shall be said, 'This one and that one were born in her'" (v5).** Literally "a man and a man," that is, one after another, many. JFB compares Isaiah 44:5, where people say "I am the LORD's" and write his name on their hands. Zion pictured as a mother of many children fits Isaiah 54:1–3 and 66:7–11. The Greek translation of the Old Testament reads "Mother Zion" here, and Paul writes, "the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother" (Gal 4:26).
- **"For the Most High himself will establish her" (v5).** The growth of the city is God's work, not the result of politics or conquest. "Most High" (*Elyon*) is the title first used of the God whom Melchizedek served at Salem (Gen 14:18–20), the earliest mention of Jerusalem in the Bible.
- **"The LORD records as he registers the peoples, 'This one was born there'" (v6).** The picture is a census roll, a city register of citizens. Ancient cities kept such lists (Neh 7:5; Ezek 13:9), and Israel's prophets spoke of God's own book of the living (Exod 32:32; Isa 4:3; Dan 12:1). In the New Testament it becomes "the Lamb's book of life" (Rev 21:27) and "the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven" (Heb 12:22–23). God himself writes the names, so no one can challenge a citizen's right to be there.
- **One family, not many.** The Old Testament promised this from the start: in Abraham "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). Isaiah 19:23–25 even speaks of a day when God says, "Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance." Psalm 87 says the same thing in the language of citizenship. Paul's words to Gentile believers are almost a commentary on it: "you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Eph 2:19).
- **Readings differ, a little.** A few interpreters read v4 more narrowly, as Jews born or living abroad in those nations being counted as Zion's children. Henry mentions a reading in which Zion simply produces greater men than those lands. Most Christian readers, and most modern scholars, take it as the conversion of the nations themselves, which fits the prophets and the New Testament best.
- **Commentators:** All three read vv4–6 as Gentiles brought in. Henry: God says, "I will reckon Egypt and Babylon with those that know me," and the nations that fought the church will be numbered in it on equal terms. He links v6 with the new birth, being "born again of his Spirit," and warns that this privilege brings responsibility. JFB calls the nations examples and reads "born there" as becoming a spiritual native of Zion, with God's register as in Isaiah 4:3. Guzik adds that God's register is not like a human census that ranks people by status, and he hears in "born there" a foreshadowing of the new birth Jesus spoke of. The Tyndale notes say God keeps a record of all who confess him as Lord.

#### All My Springs (v7)
- **"Singers and dancers alike say" (v7).** The newly enrolled citizens form a procession of worship, singing and dancing (or, as some translate, playing pipes) as they come to Zion. JFB pictures "a great procession" of the registered, coming up as Isaiah 2:3 foresaw.
- **"All my springs are in you" (v7).** A spring (*ma'yan*) is a source of flowing water, life itself in a dry land, and used in the Old Testament for a source of joy and blessing (Isa 12:3: "With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation"). "You" is most naturally Zion, or the God who lives there. Zion had no great river, only the Gihon spring, but the psalms speak of "a river whose streams make glad the city of God" (46:4), and the prophets saw water flowing out from the temple to heal the land (Ezek 47:1–12; Zech 14:8). Everything the worshipers need flows from God's presence among his people. The Hebrew is very brief here, and a few scholars suggest a slightly different reading ("all my dwellings are in you"), but the ESV's "springs" is the standard text.
- **Commentators:** Henry finds the springs in the word, prayer and the fellowship of believers, and then goes deeper: "Christ is the true temple; all our springs are in him, and from him all our streams flow." JFB: "All my sources of spiritual joy are in Thee." Guzik leans toward reading "you" as God rather than the city, noting that the grammar allows either, and calls God's goodness a hidden spring that refreshes life.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 87 is one of the Old Testament's widest windows onto the gospel. The nations that enslaved, raided and destroyed God's people are named one by one and written into his city as native-born children. That happens in Jesus. He died outside the gates of Zion and rose again, and his Spirit was poured out in Jerusalem on people "from every nation under heaven" (Acts 2:5), Egypt and Cyrenians and Arabs among them (Acts 2:10–11). Soon after, an Ethiopian, a man from Cush, was baptized on the road down from Jerusalem (Acts 8:27–39). Jesus told a teacher of Israel that no one enters God's kingdom unless he is "born again" (John 3:3), and that new birth is the only citizenship that counts. Believers have come "to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem" (Heb 12:22), and Jesus himself is the spring: "whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again" (John 4:14).
