*Setting:* The third round of speeches has begun, and it is already coming apart. Eliphaz opened it (ch 22) by finally accusing Job of specific sins he could not have known about: stripping the poor of their clothing and sending widows away empty (22:6–9). Job did not answer him directly. In chapters 23–24 he longed to find God and lay his case before him, "Oh, that I knew where I might find him" (23:3), and then pointed again to the injustice in the world that God seems to leave alone (24:1–12). Now Bildad the Shuhite speaks for the third and last time. In the first round he spoke for 22 verses (ch 8), in the second for 21 (ch 18). This time he manages six, the shortest speech in the book. He does not answer anything Job has said about the wicked prospering. He simply repeats, in a few lines of poetry, the one point all three friends have made since the beginning: God is too great and too pure for any human being to be right before him. And after Job replies, the third friend, Zophar, who should speak next, never speaks again. The pattern of the debate (Eliphaz, Job, Bildad, Job, Zophar, Job) breaks down here, and the book signals that the friends have run out of things to say.

#### Bildad Speaks: Man Cannot Be Righteous (vv1–6)
- **The shortest speech (v1).** "Then Bildad the Shuhite answered" is the same formula used for every speech in the book, but what follows is barely a speech. Readers have long noticed how the third cycle thins out. Bildad's turn is cut to a handful of lines, and Zophar has no third turn at all. Guzik passes on Mike Mason's description of the cycle as broken, with speeches that should be there simply missing. G. Campbell Morgan, also through Guzik, takes the brevity as a sign that Job has silenced his friends. Matthew Henry says Bildad speaks "as one that began to be tired of the cause." Most traditional readers take the breakdown as part of the story: the friends' theology has nothing more to give.
- **How scholars explain it (vv1–6).** Because the third cycle is so uneven, many modern scholars think the text may have been rearranged in copying. A common proposal gives 26:5–14 (the great hymn about God's power) to Bildad, so that his speech would be longer and his theme of God's greatness would continue. Another gives 27:13–23, which describes the wicked man's ruin much as the friends did, to Zophar as his missing third speech. Some scholars move parts of chapter 24 as well. These proposals are reasonable guesses, but no ancient manuscript or translation arranges the chapters differently. The traditional reading takes the text as it stands: Bildad sputters out, Zophar falls silent, and Job keeps speaking. Notably, 27:1 and 29:1 do not say "Job answered" but "Job again took up his discourse," as though Job paused for a reply that never came and then went on. Guzik passes on Elmer Smick's caution that there is no clear reason to move 26:5–14 to Bildad, since the hymn is similar to Bildad's theme but not the same. Both readings agree on what matters: the friends' case has collapsed.
- **"Dominion and fear are with God" (v2).** Bildad begins with God's rule. "Dominion" is the right to govern, and "fear" here means the dread God's power inspires. JFB takes the two together as "terror-inspiring power." Henry: "God is the sovereign Lord of all." None of this is in dispute. Job has said as much himself, at greater length (9:4–13; 12:13–25).
- **"He makes peace in his high heaven" (v2).** God keeps order even in the heavens. Some take this as a hint that God has subdued rebellion among heavenly powers (see 4:18; 15:15). JFB sees the line pointing forward to the gospel, where God makes "peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20). Bildad's implication is that if God brings the heavens into line, one complaining man on the earth should submit too.
- **"Is there any number to his armies?" (v3).** God's "armies" or hosts are the angels and the stars, as in the title "LORD of hosts." JFB reads them as "angels and stars" and points to God calling the stars out by number (Isa 40:26). Guzik passes on Matthew Poole's reading that these are all the creatures wholly at God's command. Henry draws a lovely contrast from the line: "He has numberless armies, and yet makes peace."
- **"Upon whom does his light not arise?" (v3).** God's light reaches everyone, like the sun. Henry: "All partake of the light of his goodness." Jesus says something similar: the Father "makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good" (Matt 5:45). For Bildad the point is that nothing escapes God's notice. Job would agree, but his complaint is the opposite: God sees him and still does not answer (23:8–9).
- **"How then can man be in the right before God?" (v4).** Here is the heart of the speech, and it is borrowed. Job himself asked almost the same words in his first reply to Bildad: "how can a man be in the right before God?" (9:2). The cross-references also link it to Eliphaz's vision, "Can mortal man be in the right before God?" (4:17), and to Job's "Man who is born of a woman is few of days and full of trouble" (14:1). The Tyndale notes show how each friend has handled this question. Eliphaz said purity before God is nearly impossible (4:17–19; 15:14–16). Bildad earlier said only the fully blameless can expect God's favor (8:20). Now he says no one can be pure at all. But Job has never claimed to be sinless (7:21; 13:26). His claim is narrower: he has not done the kind of wickedness that would explain what has happened to him. Guzik says Bildad is trying to get Job to admit he is a sinner like everyone else, which Job never denied, and that Bildad misreads Job as trying to prove God wrong.
- **"He who is born of woman" (v4).** A common phrase in Job for human frailty (14:1; 15:14). Guzik passes on John Trapp's view that it recalls the law's teaching on ritual uncleanness. More simply it means anyone who comes into the world the ordinary way, weak and mortal.
- **"Even the moon is not bright, and the stars are not pure in his eyes" (v5).** Compared with God, even the brightest things in the night sky look dim. Isaiah uses the same picture: when the LORD reigns, "the moon will be confounded and the sun ashamed" (Isa 24:23). Eliphaz had said God "puts no trust in his holy ones, and the heavens are not pure in his sight" (15:15). JFB connects the stars here to the angels of that verse and explains: "Theirs is the light and purity but of creatures; His of the Creator." Guzik notes Adam Clarke's hesitant suggestion that the stars might stand for fallen angels.
- **"How much less man, who is a maggot, and the son of man, who is a worm!" (v6).** Bildad's last words. JFB notes that the two words are different. The first, *rimmah*, the lexicon defines as a worm that is both the cause and sign of decay. It is the word Job used for the worms covering his own diseased flesh (7:5) and the dead in the grave (17:14; 21:26). JFB calls it "a worm bred in putridity." The second, *tola'at*, is the ordinary crawling worm or grub, which the lexicon says is used as a "symbol of insignificance." Henry: man is "a worm, hatched in putrefaction." For a man sitting in the ashes with worms in his sores, Bildad's choice of words could hardly be more painful. Andersen, through Guzik, calls it a hopeless ending for the friends' contribution, and Guzik passes on Mason's irony that these "maggots" have spent the whole debate judging Job.
- **True words, wrong use (vv4–6).** Everything Bildad says is true. Scripture agrees that no one is righteous before God by his own record: "no one living is righteous before you" (Ps 143:2). But Bildad uses the truth to shut Job's mouth, not to bring him to God. He says nothing of mercy or forgiveness. Guzik passes on Smick's observation that Bildad leaves no room for grace, unlike Paul, who proves everyone guilty (Rom 3:10–20) only in order to announce how God justifies the ungodly (Rom 3:21–26; 4:5). Bildad gets as far as Romans 3:20 and stops.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees two lessons here, to think highly of God (vv2–3, 5) and humbly of ourselves (vv4, 6), and calls them "two good lessons for us all to learn," even though Bildad applies them unfairly to Job. He adds that Bildad drops the question of why the wicked prosper because he could not answer Job's proofs. "What little reason has man to be proud, and what great reason to be humble!" Guzik agrees that nothing in the speech is new or contested, since Job said the same in 9:2. His sources read the brevity as exhaustion (Morgan, Mason), and he passes on Oswald Chambers' description of Bildad as weighty in manner and thin in substance. JFB is brief and mostly explains words: the "armies" (v3), the light of creatures and Creator (v5), and the two worms (v6). JFB has no comment on v1 and only cross-references on v4. The lexicon supports JFB's distinction between the two words for worm.

#### Pointing to Christ
Bildad ends the friends' speeches with man as a worm before a God too high and holy to reach. He is right about the distance and wrong about God. Through Isaiah, God himself calls his people "you worm Jacob," using the same word, *tola'at*, and then says, "Fear not... I am the one who helps you, declares the LORD; your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel" (Isa 41:14). God names the smallness of his people in order to help them, not to crush them. And in Psalm 22, the psalm Jesus prayed from the cross (Ps 22:1; Mark 15:34), the sufferer says, "I am a worm and not a man" (Ps 22:6). The Son of God came down to the place Bildad assigned to all humanity, mocked and despised, so that people who could never be "in the right before God" on their own might be declared right by him. Bildad's question, "How then can man be in the right before God?", is a fair one, and Paul answers it: we are "justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Rom 3:24).
