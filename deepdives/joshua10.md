*Setting:* Soon after the treaty with Gibeon (ch 9). Israel's camp is still at Gilgal in the Jordan valley. Gibeon and its towns sit on the high plateau just north of Jerusalem, and their peace with Israel gave Israel a wedge in the middle of the hill country, splitting the south of Canaan from the north. The kings of the south could not allow it. This chapter tells how their alliance was crushed in a single day of battle and how Joshua then swept through the southern cities. Letters found at Amarna in Egypt, written in the 1300s BC by Canaanite kings to Pharaoh, show a land of small rival city-states, including a king of Jerusalem pleading for help against raiders. Joshua 10 is a story of that same kind of world.

#### The Sun Stands Still (vv1–15)
- **"Adoni-zedek, king of Jerusalem" (v1).** His name means "my lord is righteous" (or "lord of righteousness"). Centuries before, an earlier king of the same city, Melchizedek ("my king is righteous"), had blessed Abraham as "priest of God Most High" (Gen 14:18–20). The names are alike, but the men are opposites: one welcomed Abraham's family, the other leads the war against them. This is the first time "Jerusalem" appears in the Bible by that name.
- **"He feared greatly, because Gibeon was a great city" (v2).** Gibeon was bigger than Ai and full of warriors. Its change of sides was a military disaster for the south. Adoni-zedek decides to make an example of it, so no other city would make peace with Israel.
- **The five kings (vv3–5).** Jerusalem, Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish and Eglon: the hill-country cities and the cities of the western foothills (the *Shephelah*). They are called "the five kings of the Amorites," the general name for the hill peoples. Instead of attacking Israel directly, they "encamped against Gibeon."
- **"Do not relax your hand from your servants" (v6).** The Gibeonites call on the treaty. Their message is urgent: "Come up to us quickly and save us and help us." Israel could easily have let them fall. The treaty had been obtained by fraud, and losing Gibeon would have removed the embarrassment. Joshua keeps faith.
- **"Do not fear them, for I have given them into your hands" (v8).** God's promise comes again (1:9; 8:1). Notice that God now backs the defense of the Gibeonites. Israel's mistake in chapter 9 does not stop God from working through the result.
- **"Having marched up all night from Gilgal" (v9).** Gilgal was near the Jordan, below sea level, and Gibeon was on the plateau some 20 miles away and well over 3,000 feet higher, up steep, rough valleys. Israel marched through the night and attacked at dawn, catching the coalition by surprise. Coming from the east, they may have had the rising sun behind them.
- **"The LORD threw them into a panic" (v10).** The verb (*hamam*) is used for the confusion God sends on Israel's enemies, as at the Red Sea (Exod 14:24) and against the Philistines (1 Sam 7:10). The enemy broke and fled west down "the ascent of Beth-horon," the main road from the plateau down to the coastal lowlands, then south toward Azekah and Makkedah.
- **"The LORD threw down large stones from heaven" (v11).** Large hailstones, which can be deadly, fell on the fleeing army on the slopes. The writer adds that the hail killed more than Israel's swords did. Israel was in pursuit and somehow spared. The plague of hail in Egypt was the last time God sent hail as judgment (Exod 9:22–26).
- **"Joshua spoke to the LORD… and he said in the sight of Israel" (v12).** Joshua prays to God, and then speaks to the sun and moon in front of the whole army. The command is in poetry: "Sun, stand still at Gibeon, and moon, in the Valley of Aijalon." Aijalon lay to the west, below Beth-horon, so the sun was over Gibeon in the east and the moon over Aijalon in the west, which suggests early morning.
- **"Stand still" (v12).** The verb *damam* means to be silent, be still, or cease. It is used of Aaron keeping silent (Lev 10:3) and of people stopping in place (1 Sam 14:9). The parallel line uses *'amad*, "stood" or "stopped." So "stand still" is a fair translation, but the root also allows "be silent," that is, stop shining.
- **"Is this not written in the Book of Jashar?" (v13).** *Jashar* means "the upright." It was an ancient collection of poems about Israel's heroes, now lost. 2 Samuel 1:18 quotes David's lament for Saul and Jonathan from the same book. The writer of Joshua quotes his source, as a historian would.
- **"The sun stopped in the midst of heaven and did not hurry to set for about a whole day" (v13).** What happened? Bible-believing Christians have held several views.
- **View 1: a longer day.** God halted or slowed the earth's rotation, or extended the daylight by some other means, so the day lasted much longer than normal. This is the traditional reading. Its supporters note that the God who made the heavens can certainly do this, and that the text says the sun "did not hurry to set for about a whole day."
- **View 2: prolonged light.** God extended the light by refraction or a local miracle, without stopping the earth. JFB, following earlier commentators, takes this view.
- **View 3: relief from the sun.** Reading *damam* as "be silent," some suggest Joshua asked for the sun to stop beating down, and God answered with the cloud and hailstorm that made the day cool enough for the long pursuit.
- **View 4: a sign in the sky.** Some scholars note that in the ancient Near East the positions of sun and moon were read as omens. On this view Joshua prayed that the sun and moon would stand in a position that was a sign of victory for Israel and defeat for the Canaanites.
- **What is clear on any reading (vv13–14).** The text's main point is plain: God answered Joshua's prayer in a way that changed the course of the battle, and the Canaanites' sun and moon, which they worshiped as gods, obeyed the LORD.
- **"There has been no day like it before or since, when the LORD heeded the voice of a man" (v14).** The unique thing is not only the sign in the sky, but that God listened to a man and acted on his word. "For the LORD fought for Israel" is the sentence that sums up the whole chapter (v42).
- **"So Joshua returned… to the camp at Gilgal" (v15).** This verse is repeated exactly in v43, and it seems out of place, since Joshua is still at Makkedah in v21. Perhaps it closes the quotation from the Book of Jashar, or summarizes ahead of time. The Greek Septuagint leaves it out.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It was generous and just in Joshua to help his new allies," and "Israel did what they could, and yet God did all." On the command to the sun: "It was bold indeed to say so before Israel, and argues a very strong assurance of faith." JFB reads v14 as showing that Joshua's words were really a prayer, and explains the miracle as light prolonged by refraction, following Keil. Guzik lists several possible explanations without choosing one, and dismisses speculative theories like Velikovsky's comet. He sees Joshua's all-night march as faith working together with God's promise, not instead of it. The Tyndale notes say plainly that not every miracle can be fully understood, and observe that the sun and moon were gods in Canaan, helpless before Israel's God.

#### Five Amorite Kings Executed (vv16–28)
- **"The cave at Makkedah" (v16).** The Hebrew says "the cave," as though it was well known. The western foothills are full of limestone caves. The kings who had come out to crush Gibeon hid underground.
- **"Roll large stones against the mouth of the cave" (v18).** Joshua does not stop to deal with the kings. He seals them in and sends the army after the fleeing troops, to keep them from reaching their walled cities (v19). Victory first, judgment later.
- **"Not a man moved his tongue against any of the people of Israel" (v21).** Literally "no one sharpened his tongue," an idiom also used when Israel left Egypt: "not a dog shall growl" (Exod 11:7). Nobody dared even speak against Israel. The army returned "safe," apparently without loss.
- **"Put your feet on the necks of these kings" (v24).** In the ancient Near East, conquerors were often pictured standing on defeated enemies. Egyptian and Assyrian art shows kings with their feet on captives' necks. The gesture was a public sign of total victory. Psalm 110:1 uses the image of God's anointed king: "until I make your enemies your footstool."
- **"Do not be afraid or dismayed; be strong and courageous" (v25).** Joshua passes on to his officers the very words God gave him at the start (1:9). What happened to these five kings is a promise of what God will do to every enemy.
- **"He hanged them on five trees… until evening" (vv26–27).** As with the king of Ai, they were executed first and their bodies displayed, then taken down at sunset as the law required (Deut 21:22–23). They were thrown into the cave where they had hidden, and the cave became their tomb, marked with stones "to this very day." The place they ran to for safety became their grave.
- **Makkedah (v28).** The town near the cave falls the same day, and its king is treated like the king of Jericho. This begins a formula repeated through the rest of the chapter.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the feet on the kings' necks was a symbol of complete victory, not an insult for its own sake. Guzik contrasts Joshua's full obedience here with Saul, who later spared the Amalekite king Agag against God's command (1 Sam 15). He also notes that Joshua did not let the capture of the kings distract him from finishing the pursuit. The Tyndale notes add that sealing the cave freed more men for the chase.

#### Conquest of Southern Canaan (vv29–43)
- **A pattern of seven cities (vv28–39).** Makkedah, Libnah, Lachish, Eglon, Hebron and Debir fall one after another, with Gezer's army defeated in the field. The account is told with repeated formulas: "passed on from… to…," "fought against it," "the LORD gave it," "struck it with the edge of the sword," "he left none remaining," "as he had done to…" The repetition is deliberate and gives the sense of an unstoppable advance. Ancient conquest accounts from Egypt and Assyria use the same kind of summary style.
- **Lachish (vv31–32).** Lachish was one of the strongest cities in Judah's foothills, and it took two days to capture. Its king Japhia was one of the five already executed. Centuries later the Assyrian king Sennacherib's siege of Lachish was carved in stone reliefs that hang today in the British Museum (compare 2 Kings 18:14).
- **"Horam king of Gezer came up to help Lachish" (v33).** Gezer was an important city about 20 miles north. Its army was destroyed, but the city itself was not taken. Israel never captured Gezer in this era (16:10). It finally came to Israel centuries later, as a wedding gift from Pharaoh to Solomon's Egyptian wife (1 Kings 9:16).
- **Hebron and Debir (vv36–39).** Hebron was where Abraham, Sarah, Isaac and Jacob were buried (Gen 23; 49:29–31). Hebron's king had been killed at Makkedah, so this attack probably fell on a new king or the remaining garrison. Later chapters show that Caleb and Othniel had to take Hebron and Debir again from the Anakim who returned (14:12–15; 15:13–17; Judg 1:10–13). The conquest here was a sweeping military campaign, not a full occupation. Israel broke the armies and the ruling cities, but settling the land took far longer.
- **"The hill country and the Negeb and the lowland and the slopes" (v40).** The four regions of the south: the Judean hills, the dry Negev to the south, the *Shephelah* foothills, and the slopes running down to the Dead Sea.
- **"Devoted to destruction all that breathed, just as the LORD God of Israel commanded" (v40).** This follows Moses' command for the cities of Canaan (Deut 20:16–17). As with Jericho, this is the hardest kind of passage in Joshua. The book presents it as God's judgment on long-ripened evil (Gen 15:16; Lev 18:24–25), not as Israel's own ambition. Many scholars note that ancient conquest reports used sweeping language ("none remaining," "all that breathed") as standard rhetoric for a decisive victory. The book itself shows that many Canaanites remained afterward (13:1–6; 15:63; Judg 1). Both points are true to the text: the judgment was real, and the language is the language of a war report.
- **"From Kadesh-barnea as far as Gaza, and all the country of Goshen, as far as Gibeon" (v41).** The boundaries of the southern campaign. Kadesh-barnea, in the far south, is the very place where the first generation refused to enter the land forty years earlier (Num 13:26; 14). The campaign's reach now runs back to the scene of Israel's great failure. "Goshen" here is a region in southern Judah (15:51), not the Goshen in Egypt.
- **"At one time" (v42).** In a single campaign. Joshua 11:18 says that, overall, Joshua "made war a long time with all those kings." This chapter is the decisive blow in the south.
- **"Because the LORD God of Israel fought for Israel" (v42).** The chapter's key sentence, repeated from v14. Moses had said, "The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent" (Exod 14:14). At the Red Sea Israel stood still. Here Israel marched all night and fought all day, and the LORD still fought for them.
- **"Joshua returned… to the camp at Gilgal" (v43).** Back to the base camp by the Jordan, where the stones from the riverbed still stood as a reminder of how they got there.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the run of victories as a series of tests that Israel passed, and repeats the chapter's own point: the LORD fought for Israel. He applies it to believers, who win spiritual battles by trusting Christ's finished victory, not by their own strength. JFB groups vv28–42 together as a summary of the conquest of the south, pointing to the phrase "at one time." The Tyndale notes observe that the southern cities were captured but not burned, in keeping with the promise that Israel would live in cities it had not built (Deut 6:10), and that the campaign opened the way for the southern tribes to settle later.

#### Pointing to Christ
Gibeon was a city that had come to Israel under false pretenses, and when it called for help, Joshua marched all night to save it because he had given his word. Our place with God rests on something far firmer: not a treaty obtained by a lie, but promises God made freely and sealed with Christ's own blood. And when we call, he does not relax his hand from his servants. Joshua's officers put their feet on the necks of the kings as a sign of what God would do to every enemy. The New Testament applies Psalm 110 to Jesus: he reigns "until he has put all his enemies under his feet," and "the last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Cor 15:25–26). Paul even tells a struggling church that "the God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Rom 16:20). The LORD who fought for Israel fights for his people still.
