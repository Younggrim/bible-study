*Setting:* This is the third part of the "Isaiah Apocalypse" (chs 24–27). Chapter 24 showed the world's proud city collapsing, and chapter 25 celebrated God's feast on Zion and the swallowing up of death. Chapter 26 is a song "in that day" for the people of God's own city, followed by a long prayer from those still waiting for it. It moves from confident praise (vv1–6), to longing and honest struggle (vv7–18), to one of the Old Testament's clearest promises of bodily resurrection (v19), and ends with a call to shelter until God's judgment passes (vv20–21). The Tyndale notes connect some of its pain with the Assyrian invasion of 701 BC, when Judah writhed like a woman in labor (37:3), but the chapter reaches far beyond that crisis. Verse 3 is among the best-loved verses in Isaiah.

#### You Keep Him in Perfect Peace (vv1–21)
- **"In that day this song will be sung in the land of Judah" (v1).** "That day" is the day of God's victory described in chapters 24–25. Like chapter 12, Isaiah puts a song in the mouths of the redeemed before the day arrives, so his hearers can learn it in advance.
- **"We have a strong city; he sets up salvation as walls and bulwarks" (v1).** This is the answer to the "lofty city" of man that has fallen (24:10; 25:2; 26:5). God's city is not strong because of stone but because God himself makes "salvation" its wall. Isaiah later says, "You shall call your walls Salvation, and your gates Praise" (60:18). Henry: "The church is a strong city, for it has walls and bulwarks… of God's own appointing." Hebrews and Revelation pick up the same hope: "the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:10; Rev 21).
- **"Open the gates, that the righteous nation that keeps faith may enter in" (v2).** This echoes the psalms sung by pilgrims at the temple gates: "Open to me the gates of righteousness, that I may enter through them" (Ps 118:19–20; 24:3–7). "Keeps faith" (*shomer 'emunim*) means guarding faithfulness, staying loyal. Entry is not by birth but by faithful trust. Guzik stresses that the city is for those whom God has made righteous.
- **"You keep him in perfect peace whose mind is stayed on you, because he trusts in you" (v3).** "Perfect peace" is in Hebrew simply *shalom shalom*, "peace, peace." Doubling a word is Hebrew's way of saying "complete" or "the utmost." "Mind" is *yetser*, the inner bent or frame of a person, what shapes their thoughts and intentions (Gen 8:21). "Stayed" is *samuk*, from *samakh*, "lean, rest on, be supported." BDB lists this verse under "sustained, firm." The same verb describes a priest leaning his hand on a sacrifice (Lev 1:4). The picture is a person whose whole inner life leans its weight on God, and God guards that person with peace. "Keep" is *natsar*, "guard, watch over." JFB compares it to a city kept by a garrison inside the walls, which Paul echoes: "the peace of God… will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus" (Phil 4:7).
- **"Trust in the LORD forever, for the LORD GOD is an everlasting rock" (v4).** The Hebrew is literally "in Yah, the LORD, is a rock of ages" (*tsur 'olamim*). The KJV translated it "everlasting strength," but the margin of older Bibles gave "rock of ages," and that phrase gave its name to Augustus Toplady's hymn "Rock of Ages, cleft for me." A rock was a refuge in war and a symbol of what does not move (Deut 32:4; Ps 18:2). Henry: "Those that trust in God shall not only find in him, but receive from him, everlasting strength."
- **"He has humbled the inhabitants of the height, the lofty city… The foot tramples it, the feet of the poor" (vv5–6).** The city that looked down on everyone is laid flat, and the people it trampled now walk over its ruins. This is the great reversal Mary sings about: "he has brought down the mighty… and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52).
- **"The path of the righteous is level; you make level the way of the righteous" (v7).** The song now turns to prayer. A level path is one cleared of obstacles. "Level" (*yashar*) also means "upright." God makes the road of the upright straight, though not necessarily easy. The Tyndale notes point out that the godly may still suffer for a time (24:16).
- **"In the path of your judgments, O LORD, we wait for you" (v8).** "Judgments" here means God's rulings, his just ways of acting. Waiting means trusting him to act in his time. **"Your name and remembrance are the desire of our soul"**: God's name is his revealed character, and "remembrance" is the memorial of what he has done. Moses was told, "This is my name forever, and thus I am to be remembered" (Exod 3:15).
- **"My soul yearns for you in the night; my spirit within me earnestly seeks you" (v9).** Night and morning: "earnestly seeks" comes from a root related to "dawn," and some translate "seek you early." The longing is like that of Psalm 63:1, "my soul thirsts for you."
- **"When your judgments are in the earth, the inhabitants of the world learn righteousness" (v9).** God's judgments are not only punishment. They teach. **"If favor is shown to the wicked, he does not learn righteousness" (v10).** This is a sober truth about human nature. Kindness alone does not change a hardened heart. The wicked take God's patience as permission (Eccl 8:11). Paul warns that God's kindness is meant to lead to repentance (Rom 2:4), and the Tyndale notes add that God's patience will not last forever (2 Pet 3:9). **"In the land of uprightness he deals corruptly"**: even surrounded by good, the wicked do evil.
- **"O LORD, your hand is lifted up, but they do not see it" (v11).** God's raised hand is a sign that he is about to act. The wicked are blind to it. The prayer asks that they see God's zeal for his people "and be ashamed." **"Let the fire for your adversaries consume them"** may also be read "the fire reserved for your adversaries."
- **"O LORD, you will ordain peace for us, for you have indeed done for us all our works" (v12).** "Ordain" means set or establish, as a meal is set on a table. Peace is God's gift. Even the good works of his people are his doing in them. Paul says the same: "it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure" (Phil 2:13).
- **"Other lords besides you have ruled over us, but your name alone we bring to remembrance" (v13).** Israel had served foreign kings, Egyptian, Assyrian and later Babylonian, and had also bowed to false gods such as Baal, whose name means "lord" or "master." JFB reads it of both earthly tyrants and idols. Guzik connects it with Baal worship. Whatever masters they once served, God's people now confess him alone.
- **"They are dead, they will not live; they are shades, they will not arise" (v14).** "Shades" is *repha'im*, the term for the faint, shadowy dead in Sheol (14:9). The tyrants who ruled them are gone and will not come back to power. Compare v19, where the same words are reversed for God's people: "Your dead shall live."
- **"You have increased the nation, O LORD… you have enlarged all the borders of the land" (v15).** The people look back on God's past blessing, perhaps on the growth promised to Abraham, and forward to a greater Israel (54:2–3). The Tyndale notes connect it with Hezekiah's expansion. The point is that God is "glorified" in what he does for them.
- **"O LORD, in distress they sought you; they poured out a whispered prayer" (v16).** The tone darkens again. A "whispered prayer" is literally a "charm" or "incantation," a murmured prayer. Under God's discipline, they could only manage a whisper.
- **"Like a pregnant woman who writhes… we have given birth to wind" (vv17–18).** Labor pains are a common biblical image for distress before deliverance (13:8; 37:3; John 16:21). But here all the pain produces nothing. "Given birth to wind" means all that effort and agony came to nothing. **"We have accomplished no deliverance in the earth."** God's people could not save themselves or the world. Guzik and JFB both see here the futility of human effort without God, the true reason for the honesty in v12.
- **"Your dead shall live; their bodies shall rise" (v19).** After "we have given birth to wind," God answers with life. "Your dead" are the LORD's people, set against "they are dead" in v14. The tyrants stay in the grave, but God's people will rise. "Their bodies" is literally "my corpse" or "my dead body," a striking shift as if the prophet, or God, claims these bodies as his own. **"You who dwell in the dust, awake and sing for joy!"** Death is pictured as sleep in the dust, and the call is to wake up (Dan 12:2; John 11:11).
- **"Your dew is a dew of light, and the earth will give birth to the dead" (v19).** In Israel's dry summers, dew was what kept plants alive (Hos 14:5). "Light" is *'orot*. BDB reads it as "light of life," the light that revives dead bodies the way dew revives plants, but notes that some scholars read "herbs," the same word used in 2 Kings 4:39. Either way, God's life-giving power falls on the dead like morning dew on the ground. **"The earth will give birth to the dead."** The labor that failed in v18 now succeeds, and the earth gives up its dead.
- **Is v19 about resurrection?** Some interpret it as a picture of the nation revived after exile, the way Ezekiel's valley of dry bones is (Ezek 37:11–14). But the language is concrete: bodies, dust, the earth giving birth to the dead. Most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, Guzik and JFB, see a real hope of bodily resurrection here, building on 25:8 and pointing forward to Daniel 12:2 and the New Testament. JFB reads it as national restoration first and literal resurrection in its fullest sense.
- **"Come, my people, enter your chambers, and shut your doors behind you" (v20).** This echoes two great rescues. Noah's family entered the ark "and the LORD shut him in" (Gen 7:16), and on Passover night Israel stayed inside behind blood-marked doors while the destroyer passed by (Exod 12:22–23). JFB names both. **"Hide yourselves for a little while until the fury has passed by."** God's people are not exempt from troubled times, but they are kept safe in him. Guzik suggests a possible link to Christ gathering his people before the final judgment, though other faithful readers see God's protection of his people through judgment rather than removal from it.
- **"The LORD is coming out from his place to punish the inhabitants of the earth" (v21).** God's "place" is heaven (Mic 1:3). He comes out to judge. **"The earth will disclose the blood shed on it, and will no more cover its slain."** Since Abel, whose blood cried out from the ground (Gen 4:10), the earth has hidden countless murders. On that day it will hide them no longer. Every injustice comes to light. This flows straight into the opening of chapter 27, where God slays the great serpent.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the strong city as the church and v3 as the promise that whoever leans on God is put into peace and kept in it. Guzik notes that the doubled *shalom* signals intensity and that "stayed" means to lean on or be upheld by, which the lexicon supports. JFB explains "keep" as guarding a besieged city from within and reads v19 as both national revival and literal resurrection, tied to Christ's own rising. All three see the hiding in v20 as God's protection in judgment, though they differ on how it will happen.

#### Pointing to Christ
"We have given birth to wind." That is an honest verdict on every human attempt to save the world. The answer comes from outside: "Your dead shall live." Jesus fulfilled this first in his own body, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep (1 Cor 15:20), and he promises that "all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out" (John 5:28–29). He is the gate of the strong city (John 10:9), the rock of ages to lean on, and the one who gives a peace the world cannot give (John 14:27). Those who hide themselves in him are safe until the fury passes by.
