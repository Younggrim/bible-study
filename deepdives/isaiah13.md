*Setting:* With chapter 13 the book turns outward. Chapters 13–23 are a series of "oracles" against the nations around Judah: Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Cush, Egypt, Edom, Arabia, Tyre, and, pointedly, Jerusalem itself (ch 22). The Tyndale notes observe that putting Jerusalem in the middle of the list makes a point: being God's people is no shield when they sin as the nations do. The series opens with Babylon, which is surprising. In Isaiah's day (about 740–685 BC) Assyria was the superpower, and Babylon was a restless province under Assyria's thumb that kept rebelling. Its ambitious king Merodach-baladan would soon court Hezekiah (ch 39). Isaiah looks past Assyria to the empire that would one day burn Jerusalem (586 BC) and announces its fall, which came in 539 BC when the Medes and Persians under Cyrus took the city. Because the language swells to the whole world and the heavens, Babylon here also stands for every proud, God-defying power, as "Babylon the great" does in Revelation 17–18.

#### The Judgment of Babylon (vv1–22)
- **"The oracle concerning Babylon" (v1).** "Oracle" is *massa'*, which older versions translate "burden." The word comes from a verb meaning "to lift up." The lexicon lists two related nouns: a load that is carried, and an utterance, a message "lifted up" by the prophet's voice. The heading for each of these chapters uses it (15:1; 17:1; 19:1; 21:1). Many preachers draw out the idea of a heavy, burdensome message. That fits the content, but the lexicon treats "oracle" as its own sense, so the "heavy" idea should not be pressed.
- **"Which Isaiah the son of Amoz saw" (v1).** The heading repeats Isaiah's full name, as in 1:1 and 2:1, marking a new major section. Many modern critical scholars think chapters 13–14 were written during the exile, since they speak of Babylon as the great oppressor. The heading itself claims Isaiah, and traditional readers take it as genuine prediction a century and a half before the event. Isaiah already foresaw Judah's treasures carried to Babylon (39:6–7), so Babylon was not outside his horizon.
- **Why Babylon first?** Babylon was more than a city. It sat on the site of Babel, where humanity first united to "make a name for ourselves" against God (Gen 11:4). Throughout Scripture it is the great symbol of human pride organized against God, ending in Revelation's "Babylon the great… fallen, fallen" (Rev 18:2). Starting the oracles with Babylon signals that God will judge pride at its source.
- **"On a bare hill raise a signal" (v2).** A banner on a treeless height could be seen for miles and was a standard call to muster troops. In 5:26 and 11:10–12 God raised a signal to call nations and to gather his people. Here the same signal summons an army against Babylon.
- **"The gates of the nobles" (v2).** Some scholars hear a wordplay here. Babylon's own name for itself was *Bab-ili*, "gate of god," and the city was famous for its gates. The proud "gate of god" will have its gates thrown open to the enemy. The wordplay is a suggestion, not certain.
- **"My consecrated ones… my mighty men… my proudly exulting ones" (v3).** "Consecrated" means set apart for a task. These are pagan soldiers, not holy men. JFB explains they are not inwardly holy but "designated to fulfil God's holy purpose." Just as God used Assyria as "the rod of my anger" (10:5), he will use the Medes and Persians. Ancient armies often dedicated themselves to their gods before battle, so the language of holy war fits.
- **"The LORD of hosts is mustering a host for battle" (v4).** "Hosts" (*tseva'ot*) means armies. The title "LORD of hosts" usually pictures God commanding the armies of heaven. Here he commands earthly armies too. The verse is all sound: a roar on the mountains like a great crowd, "kingdoms… nations gathering together." The Persian empire was a coalition of many peoples.
- **"From a distant land, from the end of the heavens" (v5).** Media and Persia lay in the mountains of what is now western Iran, far to the east from Judah's point of view. The phrase "the end of the heavens" means the far horizon.
- **"Wail, for the day of the LORD is near" (v6).** "The day of the LORD" is the prophets' phrase for the time God steps in to judge and to put things right. Amos had already warned Israel it would be "darkness, and not light" for them (Amos 5:18). It can describe a particular historical judgment, as here, and also the final judgment toward which every such day points.
- **"As destruction from the Almighty it will come" (v6).** In Hebrew it is a pun: *keshod mi-Shaddai*, "like *shod* (destruction) from *Shaddai* (the Almighty)." Joel uses the identical line (Joel 1:15). The name *Shaddai* goes back to the patriarchs (Gen 17:1). The God who made promises to Abraham is the one who acts.
- **"Every human heart will melt… like a woman in labor" (vv7–8).** Courage drains away. Labor pain is the prophets' standard picture for sudden, unavoidable agony (Jer 30:6; Mic 4:9). Paul uses the same image for the day of the Lord: "sudden destruction will come upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman" (1 Thess 5:3). "Their faces will be aflame" pictures faces burning red with fear and shame.
- **"Cruel, with wrath and fierce anger" (v9).** "Cruel" is a startling word for God's day. It means unsparing and unrelenting. This is not cruelty for its own sake. It is the day when God's patience ends and sin receives its full weight. The purpose is stated: "to destroy its sinners from it."
- **"The stars of the heavens and their constellations will not give their light" (v10).** "Constellations" is literally *kesilim*, the plural of the Hebrew name for Orion (Job 9:9; Amos 5:8), a word that also means "fool." Babylon was famous for star-watching and astrology, and its religion honored the sun, moon and stars as gods. God switches off the lights the Babylonians worshiped. Darkened heavens are standard prophetic language for the collapse of a world order (Ezek 32:7–8; Joel 2:31). Jesus took up this very verse when he described his own coming: "the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will fall from heaven" (Matt 24:29).
- **"I will punish the world for its evil" (v11).** The scope widens from Babylon to "the world." The target is pride: "the pomp of the arrogant… the pompous pride of the ruthless." This picks up Isaiah's great theme from chapter 2: "the haughty looks of man shall be brought low" (2:11).
- **"More rare than fine gold… the gold of Ophir" (v12).** Ophir was a distant land, perhaps in southern Arabia, east Africa or India, famous for the finest gold, which Solomon imported (1 Kgs 9:28). So many will die that a living man will be harder to find than the best gold.
- **"The heavens tremble, and the earth will be shaken out of its place" (v13).** Cosmic shaking accompanies God's coming in judgment. Hebrews quotes Haggai's similar promise: "Yet once more I will shake not only the earth but also the heavens," so that only what cannot be shaken remains (Heb 12:26–27).
- **"Like a hunted gazelle, or like sheep with none to gather them" (v14).** Babylon was a cosmopolitan city full of foreign merchants, mercenaries and deportees. When it falls, "each will turn to his own people." The gazelle, quick to startle, and the shepherdless sheep both picture panic with no protector (compare Matt 9:36).
- **"Their infants will be dashed in pieces" (v16).** This is the horror of ancient warfare, and the Bible does not hide it. It is not commanded; it is described. Babylon had done exactly this to others, and Psalm 137, written by exiles in Babylon, cries out for Babylon to receive what it gave (Ps 137:8–9). The Tyndale notes point out that these acts would fall on a cruel conquering power. Scripture often shows God's judgment as letting violent people reap what they sowed (Gal 6:7).
- **"I am stirring up the Medes against them" (v17).** This is the first time Isaiah names the agent. The Medes lived in northwest Iran. In Isaiah's day they were scattered tribes that Assyria raided. By the 600s they had become a power, helped Babylon destroy Nineveh in 612 BC, and then were absorbed by Cyrus the Persian around 550 BC. The Persian empire that took Babylon was a Medo-Persian empire, which is why Daniel speaks of "the law of the Medes and the Persians" and of "Darius the Mede" receiving the kingdom (Dan 5:28–31; 6:8). Later Isaiah names Cyrus himself (44:28; 45:1).
- **"Who have no regard for silver and do not delight in gold" (v17).** Babylon could not buy them off. The usual way to stop an invader was to pay tribute (2 Kgs 18:14). These attackers want conquest, not cash. JFB points to the Greek writer Xenophon, whose account of Cyrus has him tell the Medes they did not fight for money, as "a curious confirmation of this prophecy."
- **"Their bows will slaughter the young men" (v18).** The Medes and Persians were famous archers. Persian royal art at Persepolis and Susa shows rows of the king's bowmen.
- **"Babylon, the glory of kingdoms" (v19).** At its height under Nebuchadnezzar (605–562 BC), Babylon was the greatest city in the world: massive double walls, the blue-glazed Ishtar Gate, the great ziggurat that many link with the tower of Babel, and the famous hanging gardens of tradition. Nebuchadnezzar boasted, "Is not this great Babylon, which I have built… for the glory of my majesty?" (Dan 4:30). "The Chaldeans" were the dynasty and people who ruled it.
- **"Like Sodom and Gomorrah when God overthrew them" (v19).** Sodom is the Bible's picture of total, permanent overthrow (Gen 19:24–25; Deut 29:23). Isaiah earlier said Jerusalem would have become like Sodom except for God's mercy (1:9).
- **How the prophecy unfolded.** Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC, and according to his own inscription, the Cyrus Cylinder, the city opened to him with little fighting. The Greek historian Herodotus says the Persians lowered the Euphrates and entered along the riverbed during a festival, the night of Belshazzar's feast in Daniel 5. The city was not leveled that night. It was damaged after revolts against later Persian kings, Alexander the Great planned to make it his capital and died there in 323 BC, and then its people were moved to the new city of Seleucia nearby. By the early centuries AD it was largely deserted, and it has remained a ruin mound ever since. In the 1980s Iraq's Saddam Hussein began rebuilding parts of it as a showpiece, but it was never again a living city. The prophecy describes the end point, not every step along the way, which is common in the prophets.
- **"No Arab will pitch his tent there" (v20).** "Arab" here means a desert nomad. Even wandering herders, who will camp anywhere, will avoid it. The city that once drew the whole world will not even draw a shepherd.
- **Wild animals in the palaces (vv21–22).** The exact animals are hard to identify. The ESV has ostriches, wild goats, hyenas and jackals. The word for "wild goats" (*se'irim*, "hairy ones") is also used for goat-demons people sacrificed to (Lev 17:7), so older versions say "satyrs." The point is the same either way: a place of splendor becomes a haunt of desert creatures and dark things. Revelation echoes this exactly: Babylon becomes "a dwelling place for demons, a haunt for every unclean spirit" (Rev 18:2). Isaiah uses the same picture for Edom (34:11–15).
- **"Its time is close at hand" (v22).** From Isaiah's day the fall was over a century away. Prophets often speak of coming judgment as near, because it is certain and because God's time is not ours (2 Pet 3:8–9). The Tyndale notes add that speaking of future judgment as already arriving underlines the urgency of turning back to God.
- **One day or two?** Christians read the cosmic language in vv9–13 in two main ways. Many see a single event, Babylon's fall, described in "end of the world" language because for Babylon it *was* the end of its world (Jeremiah uses similar language for Judah's fall, Jer 4:23–28). Others see a double horizon: the near fall of Babylon in 539 BC and the final day of the Lord, which Jesus and Revelation describe with Isaiah's words. Both agree the historical fall of Babylon is a real preview of the last judgment.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the scene "a little day of judgment, when God will act as a just avenger of his own and his people's injured cause," and he draws the comfort: "he that is King of saints is also King of nations." On v17 he says bluntly, "the Medes shall not regard silver; it is blood they thirst for, not gold." Guzik takes the double-fulfillment view: the near fall of Babylon felt to its people like the world ending, while the ultimate fulfillment is Christ's return (Matt 24:29). He also traces Babylon's slow decline, noting that Cyrus did not destroy the city and that it emptied over centuries. JFB dates the oracle to early in Hezekiah's reign, about 174 years before the fall, and explains the "constellations" as Orion, pictured in ancient myth as a giant chained to the sky. JFB also suggests the wild goats are the "satyrs" or goat-spirits locals believed haunted ruins. All three agree the prophecy was given long before Babylon became a world power, and they read it as proof that God rules the rise and fall of empires.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah 13 is hard reading, and it is meant to be. It shows that God's patience with proud and cruel powers has an end, and that the day of the Lord is no fairy tale. But the same prophet says this judgment clears the way for the people of God to go home (14:1–2). The terror of the day of the Lord is the reason the gospel is good news. At the cross the sun was darkened at midday (Luke 23:44–45), as Jesus bore that day's wrath for his people. And the final fall of Babylon in Revelation is followed by the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:1–9). Those who belong to Christ do not need to fear that day; they long for it, because it ends every Babylon for good.
