*Setting:* The last years of Asa, king of Judah, who reigned about 911–870 BC. For most of his reign Asa sought the LORD. He tore down idols, won a great victory over Zerah's Cushite army by crying out and relying on God (14:9–15), and led all Judah in a joyful covenant to seek the LORD with all their heart (15:12–15). He even removed his own grandmother as queen mother for her idol (15:16). This chapter is the sad other half. Faced with a far smaller threat, Asa reaches for silver and a pagan ally instead of prayer, then jails the prophet who rebukes him, and dies still refusing to seek God. Verses 1–6 and 11–14 follow 1 Kings 15:16–24. Hanani's rebuke, Asa's anger and the details of his illness are found only in Chronicles.

#### Asa's Last Years (vv1–14)
- **"In the thirty-sixth year of the reign of Asa, Baasha king of Israel went up against Judah" (v1).** A well-known puzzle. Kings says Baasha died in Asa's twenty-sixth year (1 Kgs 15:33; 16:8), ten years before this. There are two main solutions. Many scholars, following JFB and the Tyndale notes, think the Chronicler's source counted the thirty-fifth and thirty-sixth years from the division of the kingdom, the years of "the kingdom of Asa," that is, of Judah. That would put this war around 895 BC, about Asa's sixteenth year, right after the covenant of chapter 15. Others think a copyist misread the numbers, perhaps fifteen and sixteen. Either way, the Chronicler's point is plain: this crisis came after many years of faithfulness and blessing.
- **Baasha.** He seized Israel's throne by killing Jeroboam's son Nadab and wiping out Jeroboam's family (1 Kgs 15:27–30), and continued Jeroboam's calf worship (1 Kgs 15:34).
- **"Built Ramah, that he might permit no one to go out or come in to Asa" (v1).** Ramah, modern er-Ram, was about five miles north of Jerusalem on the main north–south road. Fortifying it put an Israelite garrison on Jerusalem's doorstep and blocked the road. Chapter 15 explains the motive: "great numbers had deserted to him from Israel when they saw that the LORD his God was with him" (15:9). Baasha wanted to stop the flow of northerners to Jerusalem, the same fear that drove Jeroboam to set up the calves (1 Kgs 12:26–27).
- **"Asa took silver and gold from the treasures of the house of the LORD and the king's house" (v2).** Only a few verses earlier Asa brought "sacred gifts… silver, and gold" into the temple (15:18). Now he takes them out again to pay a pagan king. What was given to God is used to buy help from man.
- **"Ben-hadad king of Syria, who lived in Damascus" (v2).** "Syria" is Aram, the Aramean kingdom centered on Damascus, northeast of Israel. Ben-hadad, meaning "son of Hadad" (the storm god), was a title several Aramean kings bore. This is probably Ben-hadad I, son of Tabrimmon (1 Kgs 15:18).
- **"There is a covenant between me and you… Go, break your covenant with Baasha" (v3).** Asa's appeal is morally twisted. He asks Ben-hadad to honor one treaty by breaking another, and the treaty he invokes is with a pagan king, when Judah had just sworn a covenant with the LORD (15:12). The word "covenant" appears in both chapters. The question is which covenant Asa will trust.
- **"They conquered Ijon, Dan, Abel-maim, and all the store cities of Naphtali" (v4).** Ben-hadad struck Israel's far north, the towns around the headwaters of the Jordan and the region of Galilee. Abel-maim, "meadow of waters," is called Abel-beth-maacah in Kings (1 Kgs 15:20). Dan was the site of Jeroboam's second golden calf. Kings adds "all Chinneroth," the land around the Sea of Galilee.
- **"He stopped building Ramah" (v5).** The plan worked. Baasha pulled back to defend his northern border.
- **"They carried away the stones of Ramah and its timber… and with them he built Geba and Mizpah" (v6).** Asa turned Baasha's building materials into his own border forts, a few miles north. Mizpah's walls were huge. Jeremiah mentions a great cistern there that "King Asa had made for defense against Baasha" (Jer 41:9). By worldly measures it was a triumph: no battle, no losses, a secure border.
- **"At that time Hanani the seer came to Asa" (v7).** "Seer" (*ro'eh*) is an older word for a prophet (1 Sam 9:9). Hanani's son Jehu would later rebuke Baasha (1 Kgs 16:1–7) and King Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 19:2). A family of prophets served across two kingdoms.
- **"Because you relied on the king of Syria, and did not rely on the LORD your God" (v7).** The key word of chapters 13–16 again: *sha'an*, to lean on. Abijah's army won because they "relied on the LORD" (13:18). Asa prayed, "we rely on you" (14:11). Now he leans on Damascus.
- **"The army of the king of Syria has escaped you" (v7).** A surprising statement. Asa thought Israel was the problem. Hanani says that if Asa had trusted God, he would have defeated not only Baasha but Syria too, perhaps because Syria was allied with Baasha at the time. Instead he made Damascus stronger. A century of Aramean wars against Israel and Judah followed (1 Kgs 20; 2 Kgs 6–13; 2 Chr 24:23–24; 28:5).
- **"Were not the Ethiopians and the Libyans a huge army…? Yet because you relied on the LORD, he gave them into your hand" (v8).** Hanani reminds Asa of his own experience. The God who beat a million men could have handled Baasha. The odd thing is that Asa trusted God in the bigger crisis and failed in the smaller one. Perhaps the smaller threat felt manageable, something he could solve himself.
- **"For the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to give strong support to those whose heart is blameless toward him" (v9).** One of the best-known verses in Chronicles. God is not passive. He is searching, actively looking for people to strengthen. Zechariah uses the same picture of God's seven eyes "which range through the whole earth" (Zech 4:10). "Give strong support" is the verb *chazaq*, to make strong, to hold fast. "Blameless" is *shalem*, the same word used of Asa's heart in 15:17: whole, undivided. The tragedy is that this was once true of Asa.
- **"You have done foolishly in this, for from now on you will have wars" (v9).** "Done foolishly" is the same verb Samuel used to Saul when he disobeyed (1 Sam 13:13). Asa's reign had been marked by rest and peace (14:1, 5–7; 15:15, 19). Now it will be marked by war.
- **"Asa was angry with the seer and put him in the stocks in prison" (v10).** Literally "the house of the stocks," a cell where a prisoner's body was clamped in a frame. Jeremiah suffered the same (Jer 20:2). This is the first time in Chronicles that a king of Judah persecutes a prophet. It will not be the last (18:25–26; 24:20–22; 36:16).
- **"And Asa inflicted cruelties upon some of the people at the same time" (v10).** "Inflicted cruelties" means crushed or oppressed. Probably those who sided with Hanani. A king who will not accept God's word soon turns on those who speak it or agree with it.
- **"The Book of the Kings of Judah and Israel" (v11).** The Chronicler's main written source for the kings, probably a version or expansion of our books of Kings.
- **"In the thirty-ninth year of his reign Asa was diseased in his feet, and his disease became severe" (v12).** About two years before his death. Older writers, including JFB and Matthew Henry, guessed gout. Some modern writers suggest a circulation disease ending in gangrene. The text does not say. Some scholars note that "feet" is sometimes a polite Hebrew way of referring to other parts of the body (Exod 4:25; Isa 7:20), but most take it literally here.
- **"Yet even in his disease he did not seek the LORD, but sought help from physicians" (v12).** The Chronicler's verdict uses his key word: Asa, who once commanded all Judah "to seek the LORD" (14:4) and swore to seek him with all his heart (15:12), now "did not seek the LORD." The problem is not seeing a doctor. The Bible does not condemn medicine (Isa 38:21; Col 4:14; 1 Tim 5:23). The problem is the word "but": he sought doctors *instead of* God. Some think "physicians" here may mean healers who used magic, which would make it worse. There is also a wordplay. "Physicians" is *rophe'im*, while one of God's names is "the LORD, your healer," *YHWH rophe'ka* (Exod 15:26). Asa turned from the Healer to healers.
- **"Asa slept with his fathers, dying in the forty-first year of his reign" (v13).** A long reign, the longest of any king of Judah up to that time.
- **"The tomb that he had cut for himself in the city of David" (v14).** Rock-cut tombs were carved out of the hillside. He had prepared his own grave in advance.
- **"A bier that had been filled with various kinds of spices… and they made a very great fire in his honor" (v14).** The spices were for honor and to mask decay. The "very great fire" was a royal funeral custom, burning spices or incense, not cremating the body, which Israel did not practice for honored dead. Jeremiah promised King Zedekiah, "as spices were burned for your fathers, the former kings… so people shall burn spices for you" (Jer 34:5). Jehoram, by contrast, was denied this honor (21:19). For all his failures, Judah still honored Asa.
- **The verdict as a whole.** Chronicles never cancels Asa's earlier faithfulness. It records both "the heart of Asa was wholly true all his days" (15:17) and these last years. His life is a warning that a good beginning does not guarantee a good finish, and that the greatest danger can come after the greatest victories.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry calls it "a foolish thing to lean on a broken reed, when we have the rock of ages to rely upon." On the illness he writes, "He had put the prophet in the stocks, and now God put him in the stocks," and on the physicians, that using them was his duty, but trusting them and expecting from them what only God could give was "his sin and folly." On v9 he notes that Providence is "never out of the way, never to seek, never at a loss." Guzik notes that Asa's aim of keeping the road to Jerusalem open was good, but his method was wrong, and quotes Spurgeon that God was robbed so an unbelieving king could lean on an arm of flesh. He cites Morgan that what looks most successful can be most disastrous to faith, and stresses that age and time do not make anyone better automatically. Like Henry, Guzik insists the sin was not medicine but replacing God with it. JFB thinks the physicians were "most probably Egyptian physicians, who were anciently in high repute," and reckons the dates of v1 from the division of the kingdom. JFB also allows that the "great burning" may have been cremation, a view most later scholars reject.

#### Pointing to Christ
"The eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to give strong support to those whose heart is blameless toward him." No human heart has been perfectly whole toward God except one. Jesus never leaned on silver or human power. He refused the kingdoms of the world when Satan offered them (Matt 4:8–10) and trusted his Father all the way to the cross. And where Asa jailed the prophet who brought God's word and turned from the Healer, Jesus is the Word made flesh and the great Physician who came "not to call the righteous, but sinners" (Mark 2:17). Asa's story warns against finishing badly. The gospel offers grace for those who have: the Son of David who never failed now gives strength to those who rely on him (Heb 4:15–16).
