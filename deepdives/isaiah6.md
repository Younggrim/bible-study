*Setting:* Jerusalem, "in the year that King Uzziah died," around 740 BC. Uzziah (also called Azariah) had reigned for about fifty-two years, one of the longest and most successful reigns in Judah's history. He built up the army, fortified Jerusalem, and expanded trade (2 Chr 26:1–15). But at the height of his power he grew proud, walked into the temple to burn incense, a duty reserved for priests, and was struck with leprosy. He spent the rest of his life in isolation (2 Chr 26:16–21). Now the strong king is dead, Assyria is on the rise under Tiglath-pileser III, and Judah's future looks uncertain. Into that moment Isaiah sees the true King. Scholars debate whether this is Isaiah's first call, placed here rather than at the start of the book, or a later commission for a harder phase of ministry. Either way, the chapter sits at the hinge between Isaiah's early messages (chs 1–5) and the crisis under Ahaz (chs 7–12).

#### Isaiah's Vision of the Lord (vv1–7)
- **"In the year that King Uzziah died I saw the Lord" (v1).** The contrast is deliberate. An earthly king who tried to push into God's holy place has died a leper. Isaiah is shown the King who truly sits enthroned in that holy place. Human thrones change. God's throne does not.
- **"Sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up" (v1).** "Lord" here is *Adonai*, "Master" or "Sovereign." "High and lifted up" (*ram venissa'*) is the very pair of words Isaiah used for the proud things God will bring low (2:12–14). Only God truly holds this height. Isaiah uses the same pair for the Servant who will be "high and lifted up" (52:13) and for God, "the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity" (57:15).
- **"The train of his robe filled the temple" (v1).** The "train" is the hem or skirts of a robe. In the ancient world, the longer a king's robe, the greater his rank. Here the mere edge of God's robe fills the whole sanctuary. Isaiah never describes God himself. He sees only the throne, the robe and the attendants. Whether he was standing in the earthly temple or saw the heavenly temple in a vision is not said, and the two may merge, since the earthly temple was a copy of the heavenly one (Heb 8:5).
- **"Above him stood the seraphim" (v2).** "Seraphim" means "burning ones," from *saraph*, "to burn." This is the only place in Scripture where they are called by this name. The same word is used for the "fiery serpents" in the wilderness (Num 21:6) and for a "flying fiery serpent" (Isa 14:29; 30:6), so some think of fiery, winged beings, but the text only gives their wings, faces, feet, hands and voices. The living creatures around God's throne in Revelation 4:8 also have six wings and cry "holy, holy, holy," and they seem closely related.
- **"With two he covered his face, and with two he covered his feet, and with two he flew" (v2).** Even sinless heavenly beings hide their faces from the full glory of God. "Feet" may be modesty, or it may be a polite way of referring to the whole lower body. Four wings for reverence and two for service.
- **"Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts" (v3).** Hebrew uses repetition for emphasis. Saying something twice means "very." Saying it three times is the strongest form possible. Holiness is the one quality of God that Scripture repeats like this, here and in Revelation 4:8. "Holy" (*qadosh*) means set apart, utterly other, and it includes moral purity: God is completely unlike his creatures and completely free of evil. From this moment on, Isaiah's favorite name for God is "the Holy One of Israel." Many Christians, from the early church onward, have heard a hint of the Trinity in the threefold "holy," though the main point of the repetition is intensity.
- **"The whole earth is full of his glory!" (v3).** "Glory" (*kavod*) comes from a root meaning "heavy," the weight and splendor of who God is. The Hebrew has no verb, so it could mean the earth *is* full of his glory now, in creation, or that it *will be*. Habakkuk promises that "the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD as the waters cover the sea" (Hab 2:14; compare Isa 11:9).
- **"The foundations of the thresholds shook… and the house was filled with smoke" (v4).** The doorframes of the temple trembled at the angel's voice. Smoke recalls Sinai, where the mountain smoked and shook when God came down (Exod 19:18), and the cloud that filled the tabernacle and temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kgs 8:10–11). It both reveals and hides God's presence.
- **"Woe is me! For I am lost" (v5).** Isaiah has just pronounced six woes on others (5:8–23). Now he says "woe" over himself. "Lost" is *nidmeti*, from a root meaning "to be cut off," "silenced" or "destroyed." He expects to die. Scripture says no one can see God and live (Exod 33:20), and others who glimpsed God's glory reacted the same way (Judg 13:22; Luke 5:8; Rev 1:17).
- **"A man of unclean lips… a people of unclean lips" (v5).** Why lips? The seraphim are using their lips to praise God perfectly, and Isaiah realizes his own mouth is unfit to join them, let alone to speak for God as a prophet. The mouth shows what is in the heart (Matt 12:34). He does not separate himself from his people. He confesses that he shares their sin.
- **"For my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!" (v5).** Judah's king had just died. Isaiah has seen the real King. That is what undoes him.
- **"A burning coal that he had taken with tongs from the altar" (v6).** The altar may be the altar of incense inside the temple, where coals burned before God, or the bronze altar of sacrifice outside, where atonement was made by blood. The coal is too hot even for the seraph to hold with his hand. On the Day of Atonement, the high priest carried coals from the altar into the Most Holy Place (Lev 16:12). The point is that cleansing comes from the place of sacrifice.
- **"Your guilt is taken away, and your sin atoned for" (v7).** "Atoned for" is *kuppar*, from *kipper*, the main Old Testament word for atonement, used again and again in Leviticus for sacrifices that cover or remove sin. The burning coal must have hurt. Cleansing is not painless, but it is complete. Isaiah did nothing to earn it. God took the initiative, and the order matters: first conviction, then cleansing, then the call.
- **Commentators:** All three identify the Lord Isaiah saw with Christ, on the basis of John 12:41, where John says Isaiah "saw his glory and spoke of him." JFB adds that what Isaiah saw was not God's essence, which no one has seen (John 1:18), but a visible sign of his presence, and JFB calls holiness "the keynote of Isaiah's whole prophecies." Guzik contrasts Uzziah, who began well and ended in pride and leprosy, with the King who never changes. He notes the seraphim's priority of reverence before activity. He sees the coal as a picture of the cross, where judgment fell so that sin could be removed. JFB says it is "only by sacrifice" that sin can be pardoned. Matthew Henry says Isaiah "was thus humbled, to prepare him for the honour he was now to be called to as a prophet."

#### Isaiah's Commission from the Lord (vv8–13)
- **"Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" (v8).** God speaks as a king in his council, as in 1 Kings 22:19–22. The shift from "I" to "us" has been read in several ways. Many Christians, from early on, have seen a hint of the Trinity. Others take it as God speaking with his heavenly court. The text does not settle it, but Genesis 1:26 and 11:7 use the same plural.
- **"Here I am! Send me" (v8).** Only a moment earlier Isaiah was "lost." Now, cleansed, he volunteers before he even knows the assignment. In Hebrew it is two words, *hinneni shelacheni*. *Hinneni*, "here I am," was also the answer of Abraham, Moses and Samuel when God called them (Gen 22:1; Exod 3:4; 1 Sam 3:4). Forgiveness produces willingness.
- **"Keep on hearing, but do not understand" (v9).** The assignment is shocking. Isaiah is not promised success but failure. His preaching will make the people more hardened, not less. Note that God says "this people," not "my people," a distancing phrase Isaiah uses again (8:6, 11–12).
- **"Make the heart of this people dull, and their ears heavy, and blind their eyes" (v10).** The heart in Hebrew is the center of thinking and choosing, not just feeling. "Dull" is literally "fat," the heart insulated against God's word. How can God command this? The prophet does not cause the hardness so much as reveal it and confirm it. These people had already refused to listen (1:3; 5:12, 24). Hearing truth and rejecting it again and again makes the heart harder. God's judgment is to give them what they chose, as Pharaoh hardened his own heart and then God hardened it (Exod 8:15; 9:12). Paul describes the same pattern: God "gave them up" (Rom 1:24, 26, 28).
- **One of Isaiah's most-quoted passages in the New Testament.** Jesus quotes vv9–10 to explain why he taught in parables (Matt 13:14–15; Mark 4:12; Luke 8:10). John quotes them to explain why many did not believe in Jesus despite his signs (John 12:39–41). Paul quotes them in Rome, the last words of Paul in Acts, as Jewish leaders reject his message and the gospel goes to the Gentiles (Acts 28:25–28). Matthew follows the Greek Septuagint, which turns the command into a description: "this people's heart has grown dull." Both are true. The hardening was their own act and God's judgment.
- **"Lest they… turn and be healed" (v10).** "Turn" (*shuv*) is the great prophetic word for repentance. The verse shows what God really wants: to heal. The tragedy is that these people will not turn.
- **"How long, O Lord?" (v11).** This is the cry of the psalms of lament (Ps 6:3; 13:1; 90:13). Isaiah does not argue with the assignment. He asks how long it will last.
- **"Until cities lie waste without inhabitant" (vv11–12).** The answer is: until judgment has run its full course. The Assyrian invasions devastated the land in Isaiah's own lifetime, and Sennacherib boasted of taking forty-six walled cities of Judah in 701 BC. Babylon completed it with the exile, when "the LORD removes people far away" (586 BC).
- **"Though a tenth remain in it, it will be burned again" (v13).** Even the remnant will go through further judgment, like a tree cut down and then its stump burned.
- **"Like a terebinth or an oak, whose stump remains when it is felled" (v13).** Terebinths and oaks are known for sprouting again from a stump. Job says, "there is hope for a tree, if it be cut down, that it will sprout again" (Job 14:7). The last line is the hope: **"The holy seed is its stump."** The nation will be cut down to the ground, but God will keep a remnant alive in it, and new life will grow from it. Isaiah picks up the image five chapters later: "There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse" (11:1).
- **A textual note.** The last line, "the holy seed is its stump," is missing from the main Greek Septuagint manuscripts, but it is in the Hebrew text and in the great Isaiah scroll from the Dead Sea (1QIsa^a), the oldest complete copy of the book, from about 125 BC. Most scholars accept it as original.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says that until now Isaiah had prophesied "as a candidate," with only a tacit commission, and that this vision gave him his full one. Henry reads the hard commission as preaching that would "harden the impenitent in sin and ripen them for ruin," "yet with a reservation of mercy for a remnant." JFB cautions that the "us" of v8 points to the Trinity but is "not a sure argument for the doctrine," and notes that Matthew 13:15 quotes v10 as a statement, not a command. JFB sees v11 fulfilled at the Babylonian exile and more fully when Titus destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70. Guzik emphasizes that God calls but does not draft: he wants willing servants. He says Isaiah's ministry would seal the guilt of those who refused to hear, while the same word brings healing to those who receive it.

#### Pointing to Christ
John says plainly that Isaiah saw Jesus' glory (John 12:41). The one on the throne, "high and lifted up," is the same Lord who would be lifted up on a cross (John 12:32–34), where the burning coal of judgment fell on him so that the guilt of people with unclean lips could be taken away. The commission Isaiah received became the pattern of Jesus' own ministry: he spoke, and many heard but did not understand. But Isaiah's chapter does not end at the hardening. It ends with a stump and a holy seed. Out of the felled tree of Israel came the shoot of Jesse, and from him a new people, cleansed and sent out with the same answer Isaiah gave: "Here I am! Send me."
