*Setting:* This chapter is the heart of Daniel's last vision (chapters 10–12), given "in the third year of Cyrus" (10:1), about 536 BC. The angelic messenger of chapter 10 now reads from "the book of truth" (10:21). What follows is the most detailed prophecy in the Bible: about 375 years of history, from the Persian kings to the persecution of the Jews under Antiochus IV in the 160s BC, and then, many believe, beyond it to the end. Most of it concerns two dynasties that came out of Alexander the Great's empire: the Ptolemies of Egypt, "the king of the south," and the Seleucids of Syria, "the king of the north." The kings are not named, and the titles pass from father to son, so the chapter reads like a chess game seen from above. Judea, "the glorious land," lay between the two and was fought over again and again. The detail is so exact that since the third century AD (the pagan critic Porphyry) some have argued it must have been written after the events, around 165 BC. The traditional view, held by Jews and Christians through most of history, is that this is real prophecy from Daniel's time, and Jesus himself spoke of "Daniel the prophet" (Matt 24:15). This study follows the history either way. The main names are given below as they come.

#### The Kings of the South and the North (vv1–45)
- **"In the first year of Darius the Mede, I stood up to confirm and strengthen him" (v1).** This finishes the angel's words from chapter 10: he had backed Darius in the year Babylon fell. The chapter break here is unfortunate.
- **"Three more kings shall arise in Persia, and a fourth shall be far richer" (v2).** After Cyrus came Cambyses (530–522 BC), a short-lived usurper (Gaumata or "Smerdis," 522), and Darius I (522–486). The fourth is almost certainly Xerxes I (486–465), the Ahasuerus of Esther, famous for his wealth. **"He shall stir up all against the kingdom of Greece"**: in 480 BC Xerxes led a huge army against Greece and was beaten at Salamis and Plataea. Persia had more kings after him, but the vision skips them. From here the story is about Greece.
- **"A mighty king shall arise… and do as he wills" (v3).** This is Alexander the Great (336–323 BC), the goat's great horn of 8:5–8, 21.
- **"His kingdom shall be broken… but not to his posterity" (v4).** When Alexander died at thirty-two, his half-brother and his infant son were both murdered within a few years. His generals fought over the empire, and by 301 BC it had split into four main parts. As in 8:8, they go "toward the four winds of heaven."
- **"The king of the south… one of his princes shall be stronger" (v5).** From here "south" means Egypt and "north" means Syria, each in relation to Judea. The first king of the south was Ptolemy I (323–285 BC). One of his commanders, Seleucus I, set up his own kingdom from Babylon to Syria and became stronger than his old master.
- **"The daughter of the king of the south shall come to the king of the north" (v6).** About 250 BC, Ptolemy II sent his daughter Berenice to marry the Seleucid king Antiochus II, who divorced his wife Laodice to seal a peace treaty. It did not last. Antiochus went back to Laodice, who had him poisoned and had Berenice, her baby son and her attendants killed (246 BC). **"She shall be given up."**
- **"From a branch from her roots one shall arise" (vv7–8).** Berenice's brother, Ptolemy III (246–221), invaded Syria to avenge her, took the Seleucid port of Seleucia, and **"carried off to Egypt their gods."** Jerome, drawing on older histories, says he brought back Egyptian idols that Persia had taken centuries before. Egyptians honoured him as *Euergetes*, "Benefactor."
- **"The latter shall come into the realm of the king of the south but shall return" (v9).** Seleucus II tried a counter-invasion about 242 BC and failed.
- **"His sons shall wage war" (v10).** Seleucus II's sons, Seleucus III (226–223) and Antiochus III "the Great" (223–187), rebuilt the northern army. **"Overflow and pass through"** is flood language, used often in this chapter (vv22, 26, 40).
- **"The king of the south, moved with rage" (vv11–12).** Ptolemy IV beat Antiochus III at the battle of Raphia, near Gaza, in 217 BC, one of the largest battles of the age, with elephants on both sides. **"He shall not prevail"**: Ptolemy did not follow up his victory, and Egypt began to weaken.
- **"The king of the north shall again raise a multitude" (v13).** About fifteen years later, Antiochus III came back with a larger army after campaigns in the east.
- **"The violent among your own people shall lift themselves up in order to fulfill the vision, but they shall fail" (v14).** This is the first direct mention of Daniel's people. Some Jews sided with Antiochus against Egypt, perhaps hoping to bring in a new age. Whatever their motive, their attempt came to nothing.
- **"Take a well-fortified city" (v15).** Antiochus III beat the Egyptian general Scopas at Paneas near the springs of the Jordan (later Caesarea Philippi) about 200 BC, then besieged him in the port of Sidon until he surrendered. From then on Judea was Seleucid territory. **"He shall stand in the glorious land"** (v16). Josephus says the Jerusalem Jews welcomed him, and he granted them privileges. **"With destruction in his hand"** may refer to the damage of the war or to the power he now held over the land.
- **"He shall give him the daughter of women" (v17).** In 194/193 BC Antiochus III married his daughter Cleopatra I to the young Ptolemy V, hoping she would bring Egypt under his influence. Instead she took her husband's side, and the scheme **"shall not stand."** She was an ancestor of the famous Cleopatra VII.
- **"He shall turn his face to the coastlands… but a commander shall put an end to his insolence" (v18).** Antiochus III pushed into Asia Minor and Greece, where he ran into Rome. Roman armies beat him at Thermopylae (191 BC) and Magnesia (190). The Roman commander was Lucius Cornelius Scipio. The peace of Apamea (188) stripped Antiochus of his lands west of the Taurus mountains and loaded him with a huge war debt.
- **"He shall stumble and fall, and shall not be found" (v19).** Needing money to pay Rome, Antiochus III tried to rob a temple of Bel in Elymais (in what is now Iran) in 187 BC and was killed by the local people.
- **"One who shall send an exactor of tribute" (v20).** His son Seleucus IV (187–175) sent his minister Heliodorus to raise money, and 2 Maccabees 3 tells how Heliodorus tried to take the treasury of the Jerusalem temple and was struck down. **"Broken, neither in anger nor in battle"**: Seleucus died not in war but, it seems, murdered by Heliodorus.
- **"A contemptible person to whom royal majesty has not been given" (v21).** This is Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175–164 BC), the "little horn" of 8:9–12, 23–25. He was not the heir. He had been a hostage in Rome, and when his brother died he took the throne ahead of his nephew, **"by flatteries."** The rest of the chapter, at least up to v35 and probably v39, follows his career closely. It gets more space than all the kings before him together because he did more harm to God's people than any of them.
- **"Even the prince of the covenant" (v22).** Most take this to be the high priest Onias III, whom Antiochus removed in 175 BC in favour of his brother Jason, who paid for the office. Onias was murdered in 171 BC (2 Macc 4:7–10, 30–38). Some, including JFB, take it instead as Ptolemy VI, with whom Antiochus had a treaty.
- **"He shall act deceitfully… with a small people" (vv23–24).** Antiochus won ground through alliances and bribery more than through large armies. **"Scattering among them plunder, spoil, and goods"**: ancient historians tell how he handed out money lavishly to win support. "Epiphanes" means "God made manifest," and some of his subjects called him "Epimanes," "madman."
- **"Against the king of the south with a great army" (vv25–27).** In 170–169 BC Antiochus invaded Egypt and captured his nephew Ptolemy VI. **"Those who eat his food shall break him"**: Ptolemy's own advisers betrayed him. **"They shall speak lies at the same table"**: uncle and nephew negotiated over dinner, each trying to deceive the other. **"For the end is yet to be at the time appointed."** God has fixed the times, and no one's schemes succeed beyond them.
- **"His heart shall be set against the holy covenant" (v28).** On the way home in 169 BC, Antiochus entered Jerusalem and stripped the temple of its gold altar, lampstand and other treasures (1 Macc 1:20–24). "The holy covenant" stands for the Jewish people and their faith.
- **"Ships of Kittim shall come against him" (vv29–30).** Kittim was originally Cyprus (Gen 10:4) and came to mean the western lands across the sea. In 168 BC Antiochus invaded Egypt again. Near Alexandria he was met by a Roman envoy, Gaius Popilius Laenas, who handed him the Senate's order to leave Egypt. When Antiochus asked for time to think, Popilius drew a circle in the sand around him and told him to answer before stepping out of it (Polybius 29.27). Antiochus gave in. Humiliated, **"he shall… be enraged and take action against the holy covenant."** He took his anger out on Judea and rewarded **"those who forsake the holy covenant,"** the Jews who supported his Greek reforms.
- **"Profane the temple… take away the regular burnt offering… set up the abomination that makes desolate" (v31).** In 167 BC his troops occupied Jerusalem. He banned the Sabbath, circumcision and the food laws, ordered the Scriptures destroyed, and on 15 Kislev (December) set up a pagan altar or image on the altar of burnt offering (1 Macc 1:41–61). This fulfils 8:11–13 and is one fulfilment of 9:27.
- **"He shall seduce with flattery those who violate the covenant, but the people who know their God shall stand firm and take action" (v32).** This is the turning point of the chapter, and one of its best-known verses. Some Jews gave in for favour and safety. Others refused, whatever it cost. Some fought: the priest Mattathias and his sons, led by Judas "Maccabeus," "the hammer," began a revolt in 167 BC. Others died rather than disobey, like the old scribe Eleazar and the mother with seven sons whose martyrdoms are told in 2 Maccabees 6–7. Hebrews 11:35–38 may remember them.
- **"The wise among the people shall make many understand" (v33).** The "wise" (*maskilim*, "those who have insight") are a key group in Daniel's last chapters (11:35; 12:3, 10). Their work is teaching. They help others understand God's word and his ways. **"Though for some days they shall stumble by sword and flame."** Faithfulness does not protect them from suffering.
- **"They shall receive a little help" (v34).** Most think this is the Maccabean revolt. It was real help, but "little": it brought back the temple worship in 164 BC (the origin of Hanukkah, John 10:22), but it was not the kingdom of God. **"Many shall join themselves to them with flattery"**: not everyone who joined the winning side did so sincerely.
- **"So that they may be refined, purified, and made white" (v35).** Suffering is not wasted. It refines God's people like metal in a fire (Zech 13:9; Mal 3:2–3; 1 Pet 1:6–7). **"Until the time of the end, for it still awaits the appointed time."** The phrase "time of the end" signals that the vision is about to reach beyond the second century BC.
- **"The king shall do as he wills. He shall exalt himself… above every god" (v36).** Is this still Antiochus? The words fit his arrogance: he put "God manifest" on his coins and blasphemed the God of Israel. But Paul echoes this verse when he describes the "man of lawlessness," who "exalts himself against every so-called god" and "takes his seat in the temple of God" (2 Thess 2:4), which suggests a figure still to come. **"He shall prosper till the indignation is accomplished; for what is decreed shall be done."** He has a limit.
- **Three main readings of vv36–45.** (1) *Antiochus all the way through.* Most critical scholars hold this view, and some see vv40–45 as a prediction about his end that did not come true in detail. (2) *A shift to the final Antichrist,* either at v36 or at v40. This is common among conservative and dispensational interpreters. (3) *Antiochus as a type.* Antiochus is in view, but he is described in language that reaches past him to the last great enemy of God's people, so the passage has a near and a far fulfilment. Many Reformed and evangelical interpreters hold this view, and the Tyndale notes lean toward it.
- **"The one beloved by women" (v37).** Uncertain. Many think it means a god popular with women, such as Tammuz or Adonis (compare Ezek 8:14). Antiochus did neglect the Seleucid family god, Apollo, in favour of Olympian Zeus. Others think it means the Messiah, whom every Jewish woman hoped to bear. Some take it as natural affection for women. The text is too brief to be sure.
- **"He shall honor the god of fortresses" (v38).** A god his fathers did not know, honoured with gold and silver. The Roman historian Livy (41.20) says Antiochus began a magnificent temple to Jupiter Capitolinus, Rome's chief god, at Antioch. Others take "god of fortresses" as the worship of military power itself.
- **"At the time of the end… the king of the north shall rush upon him like a whirlwind" (vv40–43).** A final war: the north overruns many lands, enters **"the glorious land,"** and takes the wealth of Egypt, with **"the Libyans and the Cushites"** (peoples west and south of Egypt) in his train. **"Edom and Moab and the main part of the Ammonites"**, Israel's old neighbours, escape. No known history matches these verses as a description of Antiochus. He did not conquer Egypt a third time, and he died in Persia.
- **"He shall pitch his palatial tents between the sea and the glorious holy mountain. Yet he shall come to his end, with none to help him" (v45).** The setting is the land of Israel, between the Mediterranean and Mount Zion. The tyrant camps at the very doors of God's dwelling, and there he falls. As in 8:25, he is broken by no human hand. Antiochus died of illness in Persia in 164 BC, far from Jerusalem. Those who read these verses as the final Antichrist compare Revelation 19:19–20, where the beast gathers the kings of the earth and is captured.
- **Commentators:** JFB identifies each king in turn, calls the fourth Persian "Xerxes, whose riches were proverbial," and the people who know their God "the Maccabees and their followers." On v36 he says the king is "primarily Antiochus," but "antitypically and mainly Antichrist," and suggests the "god of forces" was "probably Jupiter Capitolinus." In vv40–45 he follows older Protestant readers who saw the Turks and Saracens, a reading few hold today. Henry outlines the chapter as Persia, then the "Grecian monarchy," then the quarrels of Egypt and Syria, and treats vv21–45 as the story of Antiochus "and his fall and ruin at last, when he is in the heat of his pursuit." Guzik argues the chapter's precision shows God's foreknowledge and rejects the late date. He moves to the Antichrist at v36, citing 10:14, Jesus' words in Matthew 24:15 and 2 Thessalonians 2. He calls Antiochus a preview of the final figure, and takes the "desire of women" as the Messiah, an uncommon reading. He admits the identities in vv40–45 are uncertain. The Tyndale notes take the "prince of the covenant" as Onias III where JFB has Ptolemy VI.

#### Pointing to Christ
Kings rise, marry, poison, invade and die, and their names fill this chapter. Not one of them is the hero. The hope of the chapter rests in "the people who know their God" and "the wise" who "make many understand," refined and made white through suffering. Antiochus called himself "God made manifest." The true God made manifest did not exalt himself above every god. He "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death" and was therefore exalted (Phil 2:8–9). In the very temple Antiochus defiled, at the feast that remembered its cleansing, Jesus said, "My sheep hear my voice… and no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:22–28). Every tyrant in this chapter "shall come to his end, with none to help him." The one who came to his end on a cross, abandoned by all, rose again, and his kingdom is the one that does not end.
