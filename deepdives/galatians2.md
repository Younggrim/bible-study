*Setting:* Paul is writing to churches in the Roman province of Galatia (central Turkey today), most likely the towns he and Barnabas reached on the first missionary journey: Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra and Derbe (Acts 13–14). Since he left, teachers have arrived insisting that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be fully God's people. They also seem to have called Paul a second-hand apostle who got his message from Jerusalem. Chapter 1 showed he received it by revelation from Jesus Christ. Chapter 2 finishes the story. When Paul did lay his gospel before Jerusalem, the leaders added nothing to it. And when Peter himself later wavered at Antioch, Paul corrected him in public. That clash leads straight into the heart of the letter: a person is justified not by works of the law but through faith in Christ. The letter dates from about AD 48 to the mid-50s, depending on how this chapter matches Acts.

#### Paul Accepted by the Apostles (vv1–10)
- **"After fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem" (v1).** Most count the fourteen years from Paul's conversion (1:15–16) rather than from the visit in 1:18, though both are possible. Ancient counting usually included the first and last year, so this could be twelve or thirteen years by modern reckoning.
- **Which visit is this?** Acts records two possible visits, and faithful scholars divide. 1) *The famine visit of Acts 11:27–30.* That would explain "because of a revelation" (v2), perhaps given through Agabus, as well as the private meeting and the request to "remember the poor" (v10). It also explains why Paul never cites the Jerusalem Council's letter (Acts 15:23–29), which would have settled the Galatian dispute in one line: the council had not happened yet. And it keeps Paul's careful, almost sworn list of visits (1:20) complete. The Tyndale notes and Guzik both favour this view. 2) *The Jerusalem Council of Acts 15.* Same people, same city, same question of circumcision, and "false brothers" (v4) sound like the believing Pharisees of Acts 15:5. This is the older traditional view, taken by JFB. Either way the substance is the same: Jerusalem's leaders recognized Paul's gospel.
- **"With Barnabas, taking Titus along with me" (v1).** Titus was a Gentile convert who became one of Paul's most trusted helpers (2 Cor 7:6; Titus 1:5). Bringing an uncircumcised Greek believer to Jerusalem was a test case walking on two legs. And Paul went "because of a revelation" (v2), not because Jerusalem summoned him.
- **"Privately before those who seemed influential" (v2).** The phrase *hoi dokountes* means "those of repute," people regarded as leaders. Paul uses it four times here (vv2, 6, 9). It is not an insult, but the repetition creates distance. The false teachers were waving these names around, and Paul refuses to treat reputation as the final court.
- **"Running or had run in vain" (v2).** Paul was not unsure whether his gospel was true (1:8–12). He feared that a split between Jewish and Gentile churches would wreck the work he had done.
- **"Even Titus… was not forced to be circumcised" (v3).** The Jerusalem leaders did not require it. That was the point Paul needed the Galatians to hear. Later Paul did circumcise Timothy (Acts 16:3), whose mother was Jewish, to open doors among Jews. The difference is the reason: circumcision as a missionary courtesy is one thing, circumcision as a requirement for salvation is another.
- **"False brothers secretly brought in" (v4).** The adjective *pareisaktos*, used only here in the New Testament, means "brought in secretly," as spies or traitors are smuggled in. They came to "spy out" (*kataskopeō*, also only here) "our freedom." The Greek Old Testament uses that verb for scouting out a city before an attack (2 Sam 10:3).
- **"Into slavery… not… even for a moment" (vv4–5).** Freedom and slavery become the letter's great contrast (4:21–5:1). Paul did not yield, literally "not even for an hour," so that "the truth of the gospel might be preserved for you." The Galatians themselves benefited from his stubbornness in Jerusalem.
- **"God shows no partiality" (v6).** Literally "God does not receive the face of a man," a Hebrew idiom for judging by outward appearance or status (Deut 10:17; Lev 19:15). The pillars had walked with Jesus, and that mattered, but it did not put them above God's call on Paul. They "added nothing" to his message. They simply recognized it.
- **Peter to the circumcised, Paul to the Gentiles (vv7–8).** The same God "worked through" both. The division was about focus, not territory. Peter had preached to Cornelius (Acts 10), and Paul always went "to the Jew first" (Rom 1:16). These two verses are the only places Paul calls him "Peter"; everywhere else he uses the Aramaic "Cephas," meaning "rock."
- **"James and Cephas and John, who seemed to be pillars" (v9).** James is the Lord's brother and leader of the Jerusalem church (1:19; Acts 15:13), listed first, which reflects his standing there. John is the son of Zebedee. *Stylos*, "pillar," is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for pillars in the temple. The church was already being thought of as God's new temple, with leaders as its supports (compare 1 Tim 3:15; Rev 3:12).
- **"The right hand of fellowship" (v9).** They "perceived the grace" already given to Paul; they did not create his ministry. A handshake in the ancient world sealed an agreement between equals. *Koinōnia*, fellowship, means partnership, a shared stake in one work.
- **"Remember the poor" (v10).** The Jerusalem believers were poor, hit by famine in the 40s (Acts 11:28). Paul's great collection from the Gentile churches (Rom 15:25–27; 1 Cor 16:1–4; 2 Cor 8–9) grew from this request. It showed Gentile believers belonged to the same family.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Paul "would not give place by subjection, no, not for an hour" because yielding on Titus would have betrayed Gentile freedom. On v6: "God does not accept the persons of men on the account of any such outward advantages." Yet Henry also grants the three were "pillars of the church" "(and justly too)," so Paul's distance is not contempt. He leans, "as many think," toward the Acts 15 identification. JFB takes Acts 15, counts "in the fourteenth year from his conversion," and reads "seemed" as "were reputed as persons of some consequence," which matches the lexicon. Guzik prefers Acts 11 and draws out the tone of the passage: Paul took care to meet privately and protect relationships, while refusing to budge on the substance. He also distinguishes the false brothers' sincerity from their standing: they may have thought themselves believers, but a gospel that adds requirements is, by 1:6–9, another gospel.

#### Paul Opposes Peter (vv11–14)
- **"When Cephas came to Antioch" (v11).** This is Antioch in Syria, the empire's third-largest city and the church that sent Paul and Barnabas out (Acts 13:1–3). Jews and Gentiles worshipped together there in large numbers, and believers were first called Christians there (Acts 11:26). Acts does not record this incident. Many place it after the first journey (Acts 14:26–28) and before the Jerusalem Council.
- **"I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned" (v11).** *Kata prosōpon*, face to face: Paul did not complain behind Peter's back. "Stood condemned" (*kataginōskō*, "to blame, condemn") does not mean condemned to hell. Peter was plainly in the wrong, and everyone could see it.
- **"He was eating with the Gentiles" (v12).** The verb tense points to a habit: he *used to* eat with them. Table fellowship mattered deeply. For a law-observant Jew, eating with Gentiles risked unclean food and contact. Peter had learned in Acts 10 that "God shows no partiality" (Acts 10:34), the same truth Paul just cited (v6). If these meals included the Lord's Supper, as is likely, separating meant two tables at the Lord's Table.
- **"Certain men came from James" (v12).** Paul does not say James sent them with this demand. Later, James's own letter after the Council disowns men who went out from Jerusalem "though we gave them no instructions" (Acts 15:24). Whatever their errand, their arrival changed Peter's behaviour.
- **"He drew back and separated himself" (v12).** The verb *hypostellō* can mean to draw in or furl, as a sail is taken in, and then to draw back or shrink. Hebrews 10:38 uses it for the one who "shrinks back" from faith. The tense again suggests a gradual pulling away, one meal at a time. "Separated himself" uses the root that Paul used for God setting him apart (1:15). Peter was setting himself apart from the wrong people.
- **"Fearing the circumcision party" (v12).** Peter, who had once denied Jesus out of fear, gives way to fear again. He may also have feared for Judean believers under nationalist pressure, but a motive is not an excuse.
- **"The rest of the Jews acted hypocritically along with him" (v13).** *Synypokrinomai*, "to play a part with, dissemble with." The root (*hypokrisis*) originally meant playing a role on stage, then pretence. They knew better and acted otherwise.
- **"Even Barnabas was led astray" (v13).** "Even" carries the hurt. Barnabas was Paul's partner, the man who first vouched for him in Jerusalem (Acts 9:27) and who had seen Gentiles saved with his own eyes.
- **"Not in step with the truth of the gospel" (v14).** *Orthopodeō*, used only here in the New Testament, means "to walk straight." Peter's doctrine was sound. His feet were not following it. The offence was public and was leading the whole church astray, so Paul spoke "before them all."
- **"How can you force the Gentiles to live like Jews?" (v14).** *Ioudaizō*, "to live like a Jew" or "to Judaize," appears only here in the New Testament. Its only use in the Greek Old Testament is Esther 8:17, where Persians "declared themselves Jews." Peter never told anyone to be circumcised, but his withdrawal said Gentile believers were second-class until they adopted Jewish ways. A practice can preach a false gospel without a word.
- **Where does the quotation end?** The ESV closes Paul's words to Peter at v14. Greek has no quotation marks, and many think the speech runs on through v16 or even to v21, turning gradually from Peter to the Galatians. The Tyndale notes say the boundary cannot be fixed. JFB thinks Paul moves from Peter to his general argument around v19.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Peter's conduct a plain fault ("he was to be blamed") and says Barnabas "was carried away with their dissimulation," which shows how powerful a bad example can be. JFB renders "stood condemned" as "(self)-condemned," "his act at one time condemning his contrary acting at another time." The lexicon gives the verb as simply "blame, condemn," so "self-condemned" is JFB's interpretation, a fair one, but not a meaning the word itself requires. JFB on v14: "not walking with straightforward steps." Guzik reads Peter's withdrawal as a regular habit that was then reversed, and stresses how hard Paul's stand was: Peter was the leading apostle, Paul still had a past as a persecutor, and the whole Jewish group was against him.
#### Justified by Faith (vv15–21)
- **"Jews by birth and not Gentile sinners" (v15).** "Gentile sinners" was a standard Jewish phrase. Gentiles lived outside the law, so they were "sinners" by definition. Paul uses it with irony. He is about to show that Jews by birth are in exactly the same position.
- **"A person is not justified by works of the law" (v16).** This is the first time Paul uses "justify" (*dikaioō*) in the letter. The lexicon gives its main New Testament sense as "to declare, pronounce righteous," a courtroom verdict. Justification is God the judge declaring a person in the right with him.
- **What are "works of the law"?** Two main readings. 1) The traditional Protestant reading: any attempt to gain God's approval by obeying his commands, so that the law becomes a ladder of merit. 2) The "New Perspective" associated with scholars such as James Dunn and N. T. Wright: especially the practices that marked Jews off from Gentiles, such as circumcision, food laws and Sabbath. The context here, meals and circumcision, shows why that reading arose. But Paul's conclusion is universal: "by works of the law no one will be justified," echoing Psalm 143:2, "no one living is righteous before you." Many readers hold both together: boundary markers are the presenting issue, and the deeper problem is that no one keeps the law well enough to be declared righteous by it (3:10).
- **"Through faith in Jesus Christ" (v16).** The Greek is *pistis Iēsou Christou*, literally "faith of Jesus Christ." The same phrase appears again in this verse and in v20. Readers take it two ways. 1) "Faith *in* Christ," Christ as the object of our trust. This is the reading of the ESV, NIV and most translations, of Luther and Calvin and most Reformed and evangelical scholars. The Abbott-Smith lexicon lists Galatians 2:16 among examples of faith with Christ as its object. 2) "The faithfulness *of* Christ," his faithful obedience unto death as the ground of our justification. The KJV's "faith of Jesus Christ" leaves the door open, and scholars such as Richard Hays have argued for it. In this verse the middle clause settles the human side beyond dispute: "we also have believed *in* Christ Jesus." Either way, justification rests on Christ, not on law-keeping, and it is received by trusting him.
- **"Is Christ then a servant of sin? Certainly not!" (v17).** The objection: if Jews like Peter drop the law to be justified in Christ, they end up "sinners" just like Gentiles, so Christ has made them sinners. "Servant" is *diakonos*, an agent or minister. "Certainly not" (*mē genoito*) is Paul's strongest denial. Coming to Christ does not make anyone a sinner. It admits what the law already showed.
- **"If I rebuild what I tore down" (v18).** Paul turns the charge around. The real transgression is not leaving the law for Christ but going back to it after leaving. That is what Peter did at Antioch: he tore down the wall at the table and then put it back up.- **"Through the law I died to the law" (v19).** The law did not die. Paul did. The law itself exposed his sin and passed its sentence (Rom 7:9–11). In Christ that sentence was carried out, so the law has no further claim on him, as death ends a person's obligations (Rom 7:1–4). The purpose is not lawlessness but "that I might live to God."
- **"I have been crucified with Christ" (v20).** *Systauroō*, "to crucify together with." It is the word the Gospels use for the two robbers crucified beside Jesus (Matt 27:44; John 19:32), and Paul uses it again in Romans 6:6. The perfect tense means a past event with lasting effect. The believer is united to Christ, so Christ's death counts as the believer's death.
- **"It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me" (v20).** This is not the loss of personality. Paul still says "the life I now live in the flesh," meaning ordinary bodily life. It is a change of centre. The old self that tried to stand before God on its own record is gone, and Christ, by his Spirit, is now the source of life (Rom 8:9–10; Col 1:27).
- **"Who loved me and gave himself for me" (v20).** Paul makes the cross intensely personal. The same phrase about Christ giving himself opened the letter (1:4). Love comes first; the cross is its proof.
- **"I do not nullify the grace of God" (v21).** *Atheteō* means "to set aside, make void." Accused of making light of God's law, Paul answers that his opponents make void God's grace.
- **"Christ died for no purpose" (v21).** The word is *dōrean*, "freely, as a gift." It can also mean "without cause," as in "they hated me without a cause" (John 15:25). If righteousness came by the law, Christ died for nothing. But Paul uses the same word in Romans 3:24: we are "justified *freely* by his grace." Either Christ died for nothing, or we are justified for nothing. There is no third option.
- **Commentators:** Henry summarizes the section as teaching "that justification is only by faith in Christ, and not by the works of the law." On v19: "He was thus dead to the law, through the law itself." On v20 he loves the paradoxes: "He is crucified, and yet he lives," and "He lives in the flesh, and yet lives by faith." JFB reads "faith of Jesus Christ" as Christ "the object of faith, as the ground of our justification," that is, the objective reading. It takes the "I" of v18 as Peter's case put in the first person, and on v20 says, "His eternal gratuitous love is the link that unites me to the Son of God." On v21 it turns back to Peter: "I do not make it void, as thou, Peter, art doing by Judaizing." Guzik calls "justify" a legal verdict, ties v16 to Psalm 143:2, and sees in v20 a great exchange: the old self put to death and Christ's life given in its place. He stresses that "loved" is past tense, so assurance rests on what Christ has already done on the cross, not on how strong a believer feels.

#### Pointing to Christ
Galatians 2 starts with a handshake and ends at a cross. The handshake in Jerusalem showed that Jewish and Gentile believers stand on the same ground, and the clash at Antioch showed how easily even an apostle can quietly rebuild the wall. Paul's answer is not better manners at the table but the death of Jesus. Everyone at that table was declared righteous the same way, through faith in the Son of God "who loved me and gave himself for me." If any other way existed, "Christ died for no purpose." Because none does, the cross is enough, and it is the only ground on which the church can eat together. Peter seems to have taken the lesson to heart. Years later he called Paul "our beloved brother" (2 Pet 3:15).
