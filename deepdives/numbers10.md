*Setting:* This is the hinge of the book of Numbers. Israel arrived at Mount Sinai in the third month after the exodus (Exod 19:1) and has camped there for almost a year, receiving the law, building the tabernacle and, in Numbers 1–9, being counted, arranged, cleansed and consecrated. One last instrument is made, two silver trumpets to signal the camp. Then, on the twentieth day of the second month of the second year, the cloud lifts, and for the first time the whole ordered nation marches out. Deuteronomy 1:2 notes it was only "eleven days' journey from Horeb… to Kadesh-barnea," the doorway into Canaan. It would take thirty-eight more years. The chapter ends with Moses' prayer at the ark, a high point of faith just before the complaints of chapter 11 begin.

#### The Silver Trumpets (vv1–10)
- **"Make two silver trumpets. Of hammered work" (v2).** These are not the ram's-horn *shofar* blown at Sinai (Exod 19:16) or at Jericho (Josh 6:4). The word is *chatsotsrah*, a straight metal trumpet, long and narrow with a flared end. Like the lampstand (8:4), they were beaten out of solid metal. Two silver trumpets appear on the Arch of Titus in Rome among the treasures carried off from the Jerusalem temple in AD 70.
- **"For summoning the congregation and for breaking camp" (v2).** With perhaps two million people spread across miles of desert, voices could not carry. The trumpets gave clear signals that everyone could learn.
- **Different calls (vv3–7).** **Both trumpets** called the whole congregation to the tabernacle entrance (v3). **One trumpet** called only the tribal leaders (v4). An **"alarm"** (*teruah*, a series of short or wavering blasts) set the camps marching: once for the east, twice for the south, and so on (vv5–6; the Greek translation adds the west and north). A **"long blast"** (v7) gathered the assembly without setting them moving. Paul uses this very picture: "if the bugle gives an indistinct sound, who will get ready for battle?" (1 Cor 14:8).
- **"The sons of Aaron, the priests, shall blow the trumpets" (v8).** The signals came from God's sanctuary, through God's priests. They were not a general's commands but the LORD's. Chapter 31 sends Phinehas into battle "with the vessels of the sanctuary and the trumpets for the alarm in his hand" (31:6).
- **"When you go to war in your land… sound an alarm… that you may be remembered before the LORD" (v9).** God does not need reminding, but the trumpet was a cry for help, an appeal to his promise. When King Abijah of Judah was surrounded by Jeroboam's larger army, the priests blew the trumpets, Judah cried out, and "God defeated Jeroboam and all Israel" (2 Chr 13:12–16). Notice **"in your land"**: the law looks ahead with confidence to life in Canaan.
- **"On the day of your gladness… your appointed feasts… the beginnings of your months" (v10).** The same trumpets that called for help in war also sounded over the sacrifices at festivals. The Feast of Trumpets on the first day of the seventh month (Lev 23:24; Num 29:1) is literally "a day of *teruah*." The trumpets sounded at the dedication of Solomon's temple (2 Chr 5:12–13) and at the laying of the second temple's foundation (Ezra 3:10). **"A reminder of you before your God"**: in sorrow and in joy, Israel's sounds rose before God.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes the trumpets were solid silver, giving "a shrill, distinct sound," and suggests there were two because Aaron had two surviving sons. It calls the battle alarm an act of "devout confidence in God." Guzik likens the trumpets to preaching: it takes effort to make, must be clear and loud enough, gathers and directs God's people, and comes from those set apart for it. He also notes that the trumpets worked together with the cloud. The trumpets announced; God's presence led. The Tyndale notes observe that the silver probably came from Egypt.

#### Israel Leaves Sinai (vv11–36)
- **"In the second year, in the second month, on the twentieth day" (v11).** Nineteen days after the census began (1:1) and about eleven months after Israel arrived at Sinai. The book of Numbers covers roughly thirty-nine years, and nearly a quarter of it (1:1–10:10) happens in these few weeks at the mountain.
- **"The cloud lifted… and the people of Israel set out by stages" (vv11–12).** "By stages" is literally "according to their journeys," the stopping-and-starting of a long march. **"The wilderness of Paran"** was the large dry region in the north of the Sinai Peninsula, south of the Negev, where Hagar and Ishmael had lived (Gen 21:21). The journey to get there takes several stops, and the next chapters name them: Taberah, Kibroth-hattaavah and Hazeroth (11:3, 34–35; 12:16). Kadesh in the wilderness of Paran is where the spies will be sent out (13:26).
- **"They set out for the first time at the command of the LORD by Moses" (v13).** It all happened as chapter 9 described. The ordered camp of chapter 2 now moves.
- **The order of march (vv14–28).** Judah goes first under Nahshon, with Issachar and Zebulun (vv14–16). Then the **Gershonites and Merarites** with the tabernacle's frame and curtains (v17), then Reuben's division (vv18–20), then the **Kohathites "carrying the holy things"** (v21). This is a little more detailed than 2:17, which simply put the Levites in the middle. The reason is given: **"the tabernacle was set up before their arrival"** (v21). The structure arrived first, so the holy furniture could go straight inside. Ephraim's division follows (vv22–24), and Dan comes last, **"acting as the rear guard of all the camps"** (v25), the job of collecting stragglers and protecting the column from attack from behind (compare Josh 6:9). Amalek had attacked exactly that weak tail of the column before (Deut 25:17–18).
- **A sobering list.** Every leader named here had already helped in the census and brought gifts for the altar. Except for Caleb and Joshua, who appear later, all the men of this generation over twenty would die in the wilderness (14:29–30). The marching order is perfect; the hearts would not be.
- **"Hobab the son of Reuel the Midianite, Moses' father-in-law" (v29).** Moses' father-in-law is called Reuel in Exodus 2:18 and Jethro in Exodus 3:1 and 18:1, probably a personal name and a title, or two names for one man. Jethro had visited Moses at Sinai and then gone home (Exod 18:27). The phrase "Moses' father-in-law" here could refer either to Reuel or to Hobab. Judges 4:11 calls Hobab Moses' father-in-law, but the Hebrew word can mean any relative by marriage, so most take Hobab to be Moses' brother-in-law, Reuel's son.
- **"Come with us, and we will do good to you, for the LORD has promised good to Israel" (v29).** Moses' first appeal is an invitation to share in God's blessing. The word "good" appears five times in vv29–32. Moses is not offering wages. He is offering a place among God's people.
- **"I will not go. I will depart to my own land and to my kindred" (v30).** His words echo, in reverse, God's call to Abraham: "Go from your country and your kindred" (Gen 12:1). Hobab chooses the opposite.
- **"You will serve as eyes for us" (v31).** Moses' second appeal is to Hobab's usefulness. Hobab knew the desert: where water lay, where there was grazing and shade. Does this show a lack of faith, since the cloud was guiding them? Most commentators say no. The cloud decided *when* to move and roughly *where*. A local guide could find water, pasture and a safe campsite within that. Trusting God does not rule out using wise human help.
- **Did Hobab come?** The text does not say. Judges 1:16 says "the descendants of the Kenite, Moses' father-in-law, went up with the people of Judah," and Judges 4:11 places Hobab's descendants in the land. That suggests he did. Later, King Saul spared the Kenites because "you showed kindness to all the people of Israel when they came up out of Egypt" (1 Sam 15:6). Jael, who killed Sisera, was a Kenite (Judg 4:17–21), and the faithful Rechabites of Jeremiah 35 came from the same family (1 Chr 2:55).
- **"The ark of the covenant of the LORD went before them three days' journey" (v33).** This is puzzling, because 10:21 places the ark with the Kohathites in the middle of the column. Some think that on this first march the ark went at the front as a special sign. Others read it as "went before them during the three days' journey," leading in the sense that the whole column followed its lead. JFB says the ark went before them with the cloud above it. Either way, the point is that God himself was looking out **"a resting place for them."** Moses asked Hobab to be their eyes, but the LORD was already scouting ahead.
- **"And the cloud of the LORD was over them by day" (v34).** Not only before them but over them, as a covering.
- **"Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered" (v35).** Every morning the ark set out, Moses prayed this. It treats the LORD as the warrior-king marching out at the head of his army (Exod 15:3). David took up these words in Psalm 68:1 ("God shall arise, his enemies shall be scattered"), and Psalm 132:8 echoes them when the ark is brought to Zion: "Arise, O LORD, and go to your resting place."
- **"Return, O LORD, to the ten thousand thousands of Israel" (v36).** Every evening, when the ark came to rest, Moses asked God to settle among his people again. "Ten thousand thousands" is the largest number Hebrew could easily express, a way of saying "countless."
- **Set apart in the Hebrew text.** In Hebrew Bibles, vv35–36 are bracketed by two inverted letters *nun*, an ancient scribal mark found nowhere else in the Torah. The rabbis treated these two verses as if they were a book of their own, which made the Torah seven books instead of five. Scholars still debate what the marks originally meant, but the old scribes plainly saw these two prayers as special.
- **Commentators:** Henry faults Hobab for preferring his homeland to God's promise, but thinks his silence after Moses' second appeal may mean he agreed, and notes that his family later benefited. On the prayer at the ark, Henry says the church's safety lies in God's presence rather than its numbers, and reads "let your enemies be scattered" as a prophecy that all God's enemies will finally fall. JFB, unlike Henry and the Tyndale notes, thinks Hobab went home. It explains "eyes" by saying the cloud showed the general route but not hidden water, pasture and shade, and ties Moses' prayer to his words in Exodus 33:15, "If your presence will not go with me, do not bring us up from here." Guzik estimates three days' journey at roughly forty to forty-five miles, and connects "Arise, O LORD" with the resurrection of Christ, when his enemies were scattered. He also notes the sad irony that, right after this prayer, the people begin to complain.

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel left Sinai with the ark going ahead to find them rest, and with a prayer every morning that the LORD would arise and scatter his enemies. That prayer is answered most fully in the morning God raised Jesus from the dead and "disarmed the rulers and authorities" (Col 2:15). He is the one who goes ahead of his people "to prepare a place" (John 14:2), the one who offers the "rest" Israel's wilderness generation never entered (Heb 4:1–11). And the trumpet that gathered Israel and sent them out will sound again: "the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command… and with the sound of the trumpet of God" (1 Thess 4:16), to gather his people home.
