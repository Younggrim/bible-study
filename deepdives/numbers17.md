*Setting:* This follows straight on from Korah's rebellion (ch 16). In two days Israel had seen the ground swallow Korah, Dathan and Abiram, fire consume 250 men who offered incense, and a plague kill 14,700 more until Aaron stood between the dead and the living. The question behind it all was still open in many minds: who has the right to come near to God as priest? Chapter 16 answered it with judgment. Chapter 17 answers it with life, a dead stick that blossoms overnight. In Hebrew Bibles this chapter is numbered 17:16–28, because the end of chapter 16 is counted as the start of 17.

#### Aaron's Staff Buds (vv1–13)
- **"Get from them staffs, one for each fathers' house… twelve staffs" (v2).** A staff was a walking stick, a shepherd's rod, and a sign of a leader's authority, something like a scepter (Gen 38:18; Ps 110:2). There is a wordplay at the heart of this test. The Hebrew *matteh* means both "staff" and "tribe." Twelve tribes, twelve staffs. God will show which "tribe" he has chosen by what happens to its "staff."
- **"Write each man's name on his staff" (v2).** Each tribal chief marks his own staff. There can be no swapping or confusion afterward, and no one can say Moses switched them.
- **"Write Aaron's name on the staff of Levi" (v3).** Not "Levi," but "Aaron." The question was not only whether Levi was chosen, but whether Aaron's family within Levi was. Korah was a Levite too. Putting Aaron's name on the staff settles both disputes at once.
- **Twelve or thirteen?** Levi was not counted among the twelve land-holding tribes, since Joseph's descendants formed two tribes, Ephraim and Manasseh. So either Joseph's two tribes shared one staff here, making twelve with Levi, or Aaron's was a thirteenth staff "among their staffs" (v6). The text does not say. The point is the same either way.
- **"Deposit them in the tent of meeting before the testimony, where I meet with you" (v4).** "The testimony" is the stone tablets of the covenant kept in the ark, so the staffs were laid before the ark in the most holy place. They would be in God's presence overnight, out of anyone's reach.
- **"The staff of the man whom I choose shall sprout" (v5).** Moses does not choose. God does. The aim is pastoral as well as judicial: **"Thus I will make to cease from me the grumblings."** After so many deaths, God wants a sign that will end the argument without more judgment.
- **"All their chiefs gave him staffs" (v6).** These are the same kind of leaders who had joined Korah (16:2). This time they submit to the test.
- **"Sprouted and put forth buds and produced blossoms, and it bore ripe almonds" (v8).** One night, and a dead, dried stick goes through the whole life of a branch: buds, flowers and ripe fruit, all at once on the same piece of wood. God did not just make it twitch with life. He gave more than enough. The Tyndale notes add that almond wood is only hard enough to make a staff after it has been cut and dried for a long time, so there was no natural way for this one to come back to life.
- **Why almonds?** The almond tree is the first to blossom in the land, as early as January or February, while the rest of the land is still bare. Its Hebrew name *shaqed* is linked to the verb *shaqad*, "to watch" or "be awake," as if the tree wakes first from winter. Jeremiah's vision of an almond branch plays on exactly this: "I am watching (*shoqed*) over my word to perform it" (Jer 1:11–12). The golden lampstand in the holy place also had cups "made like almond blossoms" (Exod 25:33–34). Aaron's staff was blossoming in the same room where the almond-blossom lamp burned, a sign that his family belonged to that sanctuary.
- **"Each man took his staff" (v9).** Moses brings them all out. The eleven staffs are still dry sticks. Every chief takes his own back and can see for himself. No one needs to be told what it means.
- **"Put back the staff of Aaron before the testimony, to be kept as a sign for the rebels" (v10).** The blossoming staff is not thrown away or forgotten. It is kept before the ark, as the jar of manna was (Exod 16:33–34). Hebrews 9:4 lists "Aaron's staff that budded" with the manna and the tablets in or beside the ark. By Solomon's day only the tablets were inside the ark (1 Kgs 8:9), so the staff and the manna seem to have been lost, or kept nearby, at some point.
- **"That you may make an end of their grumblings against me, lest they die" (v10).** God's aim in the sign is mercy. He gives proof so that people will stop rebelling before they bring more judgment on themselves. Note that the grumbling against Aaron is called grumbling "against me."
- **"As the LORD commanded him, so he did" (v11).** A small sentence of obedience. Moses had obeyed throughout, while everyone around him was arguing.
- **"Behold, we perish, we are undone, we are all undone" (v12).** At last the people stop arguing and start trembling. Three times they say, in effect, "we're dead." After the fire, the earthquake, the plague and now the staff, they finally see how dangerous it is to come near a holy God on their own terms.
- **"Everyone who comes near… to the tabernacle of the LORD, shall die. Are we all to perish?" (v13).** This is partly true and partly an overreaction. God's presence really is dangerous to sinners who approach it wrongly, as Korah's company found. But God never said no one could come near. He had appointed priests so that the people could be served safely. The question in v13 is answered by chapter 18, which spells out how the priests and Levites guard the sanctuary "so that there may never again be wrath on the people of Israel" (18:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the dead stick "became a living branch, budded, and blossomed, and yielded almonds," which left no room for any suspicion of a trick. He concludes that "fruitfulness is the best evidence of a divine call," and that Aaron's priesthood "was designed, not only for an honour to Aaron, but for a blessing to Israel." He sees Christ in the branch, "the man whose name is the Branch" (Zech 6:12), and the "root out of dry ground" (Isa 53:2). Henry reads the people's cry two ways, either as a complaint against a harsh God or as finally submitting to God's boundary, and notes the first is a "very wicked thing." Guzik points out that a tiny green shoot would have proved the point, but God gave buds, flowers and fruit. He stresses that the choice did not make Aaron the holiest man in Israel. It made him God's appointed priest, which the nation had to accept. Guzik also notes that Jesus' priesthood is not from Aaron's line but after the order of Melchizedek (Heb 7). JFB explains that writing Aaron's name rather than Levi's avoided "a door of controversy among the Levites," cites Josephus describing a staff bearing fruit "in three different stages at once," and reads the people's cry as "an exclamation of fear." JFB closes with a line worth keeping: "Let us fear and sin not."

#### Pointing to Christ
God answered the question "Who may come near?" by bringing life out of a dead stick. That is the same way he answered it for good. Jesus was cut off, laid in a tomb, and on the third day came out alive. The resurrection is the Father's sign that this is the priest he has chosen, "appointed a priest… by the power of an indestructible life" (Heb 7:16). Henry's picture fits: the "tender plant" and "root out of dry ground" (Isa 53:2) blossomed from death. And the people's fear in v13, "everyone who comes near… shall die," finds its answer in him. Because of the priest God chose, sinners can "draw near with confidence to the throne of grace" (Heb 4:16) without dying.
