*Setting:* Moses has given the people God's call for materials, and they have brought them with willing hearts (35:4–29). Bezalel and Oholiab, filled with God's Spirit, have been named as the master craftsmen (35:30–35). Now the work begins. Chapter 36 opens with one of the happiest scenes in the Old Testament, a people who have to be told to stop giving, and then reports the making of the tabernacle's tent: its curtains, coverings, frames, bars, veil and entrance screen. These were described in God's instructions in chapter 26, and the account here follows them closely. The aim is not to tell readers something new but to show that the builders did exactly what God had said.

#### Construction of the Tabernacle (vv1–38)
- **"Every craftsman in whom the LORD has put skill and intelligence" (v1).** This verse finishes Moses' speech from 35:30. The skill is God's gift, and the standard is God's: **"in accordance with all that the LORD has commanded."** The phrase, or something like it, will be repeated again and again through chapter 40.
- **"Everyone whose heart stirred him up to come to do the work" (v2).** Literally "whose heart lifted him up." Those who gave materials gave from willing hearts (35:21), and the same is true of those who gave their labor. Nobody was drafted.
- **"They still kept bringing him freewill offerings every morning" (v3).** The work had started, the storerooms were full, and the gifts kept coming. Morning by morning, more arrived.
- **"The people bring much more than enough" (v5).** The craftsmen stopped their work to report it. They could have quietly kept the extra. Instead they told Moses honestly that no more was needed.
- **"Let no man or woman do anything more for the contribution" (v6).** **"So the people were restrained from bringing."** The word "restrained" is the same verb used of the rain being "restrained" from heaven after the flood (Gen 8:2). It is rare in Scripture for God's people to have to be held back from giving. A few weeks earlier these same people had stripped off their gold for an idol (32:2–3). Now they cannot give enough to the living God. It was a sign of real change of heart. The church in Macedonia showed the same spirit, begging "earnestly for the favor of taking part" in giving (2 Cor 8:3–4).
- **"Sufficient to do all the work, and more" (v7).** Moses did not stockpile what was not needed. He knew what the work required and stopped when it was met.
- **The order of the work.** In God's instructions, the description began from the inside out: the ark first (25:10), then the table and lampstand, and only then the tent (ch 26). That was the order of importance, starting with the place of God's presence. Here the order is the builders' order: first the tent that would shelter everything (36:8–38), then the furniture (ch 37), then the courtyard (ch 38). Same plan, seen from a workshop rather than from the throne room.
- **"Ten curtains… of fine twined linen and blue and purple and scarlet yarns, with cherubim skillfully worked" (v8).** These were the inner curtains, the actual "tabernacle," that formed the ceiling and walls seen from inside (26:1–6). Each was **twenty-eight cubits long and four cubits wide** (v9), about 42 by 6 feet (13 by 2 meters). Cherubim, the guardian angels of God's throne, were woven into them. Priests inside the tent would see cherubim all around and above them, as if in the throne room of heaven. Cherubim guarded the way to Eden (Gen 3:24), and here they line the way back to God's presence.
- **"Fifty loops… fifty clasps of gold… So the tabernacle was a single whole" (vv12–13).** The ten curtains were made in two sets of five, joined by blue loops and gold clasps. "A single whole" is literally "one." God's dwelling, made of many pieces, was one tent.
- **"Curtains of goats' hair for a tent over the tabernacle… eleven curtains" (v14).** Over the linen went a tent of goats' hair, the material Bedouin tents are still made from, dark, tough and weatherproof. These curtains were slightly longer (thirty cubits, v15) so they covered the linen ones fully, and there were eleven, one extra to fold over at the front (26:9). They were joined with **clasps of bronze** (v18), not gold. The less precious material is on the outside.
- **"A covering of tanned rams' skins and goatskins" (v19).** Two more layers on top. "Goatskins" translates a word whose meaning is uncertain (the KJV has "badgers' skins"). Many think it means the hide of a sea animal such as a dugong, or a fine leather. From outside, the tabernacle looked plain and weatherworn. The glory was inside.
- **"Upright frames for the tabernacle of acacia wood" (v20).** Each frame was **ten cubits** long and **a cubit and a half** wide (v21), about 15 feet by 27 inches (4.5 m by 68 cm). The KJV calls them "boards," but many scholars think they were open frames, like ladders, which would have been far lighter to carry and would let the cherubim curtains show through from inside. Each had **two tenons**, projecting pegs at the bottom, that fitted into sockets (v22).
- **"Twenty frames for the south side… forty bases of silver" (vv23–24).** Twenty frames on each long side, six at the back, and two corner frames (vv23–28): forty-eight in all. Each stood in two silver bases, making 96, and four more held up the veil's pillars (v36), for 100 in all. Chapter 38 explains that these bases were made from the silver of the census ransom, one talent of silver for each base (38:25–27). The whole house of God stood on silver paid as "a ransom for his life" (30:12).
- **"Separate beneath but joined at the top" (v29).** The corner frames were built to brace the back corners, though the exact design is not clear from the text.
- **"Bars of acacia wood, five for the frames of the one side" (v31).** Bars slid through gold rings on the frames to hold them together. **"The middle bar to run from end to end halfway up the frames" (v33)**, a single long bar that tied a whole wall together. **"He overlaid the frames with gold"** (v34). Wood from the desert, covered in gold.
- **"He made the veil… with cherubim skillfully worked into it" (v35).** This was the curtain between the Holy Place and the Most Holy Place, where the ark would stand (26:31–33). It hung on **four pillars** with gold hooks and **four bases of silver** (v36). Only the high priest passed through it, once a year, with blood (Lev 16; Heb 9:7). When Jesus died, "the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (Matt 27:51). Hebrews says believers now enter the holy places "by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20).
- **"A screen for the entrance of the tent… embroidered with needlework" (v37).** The outer doorway of the tent had a colorful curtain too, but without cherubim, hung on **five pillars** whose bases **were of bronze** (v38), not silver. The materials grow more precious as one moves inward: bronze at the entrance, silver under the veil, gold inside. Everything in the design says that drawing near to God means moving step by step into greater holiness.
- **"He made."** Through most of chapters 36–39 the verb is singular, "he made." Chapter 37 begins by naming Bezalel (37:1). Many hands did the work (36:8), but the master builder is credited with it, as a building today carries its architect's name.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the people kept bringing freewill offerings every morning, some stirred by others' zeal. He praises the honesty of the workmen who would not profit from the surplus, and observes that the people "looked upon it as a restraint upon them not to be allowed to do more for the tabernacle." He laments that such generosity had grown cold in his own day. JFB also commends the craftsmen as men of integrity who reported that the supply "already far exceeded the demand," and describes the veil as "embroidered with cherubim and of great size and thickness." JFB compares the entrance curtain to hangings used in Eastern tents and palaces, preferred to wooden doors as cooler and more elegant. Guzik takes the scene as proof that giving is most generous when it is free from pressure. He also sees in Moses a leader of integrity who knew exactly what was needed and stopped collecting once it was met. Guzik passes on Clarke's note that the craftsmen's hearts were literally "lifted up," which fits the Hebrew.

#### Pointing to Christ
From the outside, the tabernacle was a dark tent of goats' hair and skins, with nothing much to look at. From the inside, it was gold and blue and purple and scarlet, with cherubim all around. Isaiah says of the coming servant, "he had no form or majesty that we should look at him" (Isa 53:2), and yet those who came near saw "his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father" (John 1:14). The Word "became flesh and dwelt among us," literally pitched his tent among us. And the veil that kept everyone but the high priest out of God's presence was torn open by his death, so that all who belong to him can "draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (Heb 10:22).
