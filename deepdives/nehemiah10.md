*Setting:* Jerusalem, late in the seventh month of 445 BC. The wall is finished (6:15), the Law has been read and explained (ch 8) and the people have confessed their sins and their fathers' sins in a long prayer that ended, "we are slaves this day… and we are in great distress" (9:36–37). The last line of that prayer announced a decision: "we make a firm covenant in writing; on the sealed document are the names of our princes, our Levites, and our priests" (9:38, which is 10:1 in Hebrew Bibles, so the verse numbers in this chapter run one behind the Hebrew). Chapter 10 gives the names on that document and then the terms. It is not a new covenant. It is a pledge to keep the covenant God had already made at Sinai, with specific promises on the points where Israel had been failing: marriage, the Sabbath, the seventh year and the support of the temple. The chapter ends with the line that sums it all up: "We will not neglect the house of our God."

#### The People Who Sealed the Covenant (vv1–27)
- **"On the seals are the names" (v1).** Important documents in the Persian world were sealed with clay impressions of personal seals, small engraved stones pressed into wet clay. Archaeologists have found hundreds of such seal impressions (bullae) from this period, some from the province of Judah. Putting a seal on a document was like signing it.
- **"Nehemiah the governor, the son of Hacaliah" (v1).** The governor signs first, as his office required. JFB says this was "on account of his high official rank." He was asking nothing of the people that he would not bind himself to.
- **"Zedekiah" (v1).** He is named after Nehemiah but before the priests. He may have been Nehemiah's secretary or deputy. Some connect him with "Zadok the scribe" (13:13), but that is a guess.
- **"These are the priests" (vv2–8).** Twenty-one priestly names follow. Most of them match the heads of priestly houses who came back with Zerubbabel (12:1–7), so they seem to be family names, signed by the current head of each house. Ezra himself is not named. He belonged to the house of Seraiah (Ezra 7:1), the first name listed, which may be why. The names Jeremiah, Daniel and Baruch here are not the famous men of those names.
- **"And the Levites" (vv9–13).** Seventeen Levites follow, several of them the same men who led the confession in 9:4–5 and helped explain the Law in 8:7. The men who taught the Law now commit themselves to keep it.
- **"The chiefs of the people" (vv14–27).** Forty-four lay leaders sign. The first names, Parosh, Pahath-moab, Elam, Zattu and Bani, are the same clan names that head the list of returnees (7:8–15). Other names, such as Meshullam, Hasshub and Hananiah, appear among the builders of the wall (3:4, 8, 11, 23). Those who had built with their hands now pledge with their names. Altogether, 84 names appear on the document, counting Nehemiah and Zedekiah.
- **Why the list?** A list of names can seem dull, but it made the commitment concrete. A crowd can feel moved without anyone actually promising anything. Each of these leaders put his own name on record, so the promise could not be lost in a general mood.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the people were represented by their elders, since everyone could not sign. Guzik points out that the Hebrew idiom for making a covenant is literally "cutting" one, since covenants were sealed with sacrifice and always cost something (compare Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18). He places this pledge in the line of God's covenants with Abraham, Moses and David, all looking toward the new covenant. He stresses that a nation's feelings achieve nothing unless individuals commit themselves, and that these 84 put their names where everyone could see them. Henry observes that "they struck while the iron was hot," putting their good resolve into action at once, "lest, if it should be delayed, it might be dropped."

#### The Obligations of the Covenant (vv28–39)
- **"The rest of the people… their wives, their sons, their daughters, all who have knowledge and understanding" (v28).** Beyond the leaders, everyone old enough to understand joined. That echoes the reading of the Law, which was given to "all who could understand what they heard" (8:2). The list includes gatekeepers, singers and temple servants, the humbler staff of the temple. This was a whole-community commitment, not a leaders' agreement.
- **"All who have separated themselves from the peoples of the lands to the Law of God" (v28).** The separation is described positively. It is separation *to* the Law of God, not merely away from others. This seems to include converts, people of foreign background who had joined Israel and committed themselves to the LORD (compare Ezra 6:21, which uses similar words of those who joined the Passover).
- **"Enter into a curse and an oath" (v29).** An oath (*shevu'ah*) was a solemn promise. A curse (*'alah*) was a penalty called down on oneself if one broke it. The covenant at Sinai had its blessings and curses (Lev 26; Deut 27–28), and this pledge accepts them again. "The LORD our Lord" combines God's personal name with the title of master. They bind themselves to "all the commandments," then name specific ones.
- **"We will not give our daughters to the peoples of the land" (v30).** The first specific pledge concerns marriage. The Law forbade Israel to marry the peoples of Canaan, "for they would turn away your sons from following me, to serve other gods" (Deut 7:3–4; Exod 34:16). The concern was religious, not racial: Ruth the Moabite and Rahab the Canaanite married into Israel after turning to the LORD. Ezra had already faced a crisis over mixed marriages (Ezra 9–10). Parents arranged marriages, so the vow is phrased about "our daughters" and "our sons." Paul's later teaching for Christians runs along the same line: marry "only in the Lord" (1 Cor 7:39; 2 Cor 6:14).
- **"We will not buy from them on the Sabbath or on a holy day" (v31).** The Law said not to work on the Sabbath (Exod 20:8–11), but it did not say outright that one could not buy from foreigners who came to sell. The people close that gap. Amos had condemned merchants who could not wait for the Sabbath to end so they could start selling again (Amos 8:5). Jeremiah warned Jerusalem not to carry loads through its gates on the Sabbath (Jer 17:21–27). This promise is the one Nehemiah later found broken, with Tyrian fish merchants selling in Jerusalem on the Sabbath (13:15–22).
- **"We will forgo the crops of the seventh year" (v31).** Every seventh year the land was to lie fallow, with whatever grew left for the poor and wild animals (Exod 23:10–11; Lev 25:1–7). This took real faith for a poor farming community. God had promised enough in the sixth year to carry them through (Lev 25:20–22). Ignoring these Sabbath years had been one reason for the exile: the land would "enjoy its Sabbaths" during the seventy years (2 Chr 36:21; Lev 26:34–35).
- **"And the exaction of every debt" (v31).** Every seventh year, debts between Israelites were to be released (Deut 15:1–2). This pledge connects directly to the debt crisis of chapter 5, when the poor were losing their fields, vineyards and children to wealthy creditors (5:1–5). The people now commit themselves to the law that would prevent such a crisis from happening again.
- **"A third part of a shekel for the service of the house of our God" (v32).** The Law had required a half-shekel from every man counted in a census, "for the service of the tent of meeting" (Exod 30:11–16). Here the people set a yearly temple tax of a third of a shekel. JFB thinks the amount was reduced because of the people's poverty. Others suggest that the shekel in use in the Persian period was heavier than the old one, so a third of it was close to the earlier half. By Jesus' day the tax was half a shekel again, the "two-drachma tax" Peter was asked about (Matt 17:24–27).
- **"For the showbread, the regular grain offering, the regular burnt offering" (v33).** The tax paid for the daily round of worship: the twelve loaves set before God each week (Lev 24:5–9), the morning and evening offerings (Num 28:3–8), the extra offerings for Sabbaths, new moons and feasts (Num 28–29) and the sin offerings "to make atonement for Israel." Persian kings had promised help with temple costs (Ezra 6:9–10; 7:21–23), but the people now take responsibility themselves.
- **"Cast lots for the wood offering" (v34).** The fire on the altar was never to go out (Lev 6:12–13), and that required a steady supply of firewood, a scarce resource in the hill country of Judah. In Joshua's day the Gibeonites cut the wood (Josh 9:27), but few of the temple servants had returned. So the families took turns, with lots deciding when each would bring its share. "As it is written in the Law" refers to the command that the fire keep burning, not to a specific law about a wood offering. The Jewish historian Josephus later mentions a yearly festival of wood-bringing, and the Mishnah lists the families' appointed days. The custom started here.
- **"The firstfruits of our ground and the firstfruits of all fruit of every tree" (v35).** The first and best of every harvest belonged to God (Exod 23:19; Deut 26:1–11). Giving it first, before the rest of the harvest was in, was an act of trust that more would come.
- **"The firstborn of our sons and of our cattle" (v36).** Every firstborn belonged to God because he had spared Israel's firstborn at the Passover (Exod 13:2, 11–15). Firstborn sons were not sacrificed. They were redeemed with five shekels of silver (Num 18:15–16). Clean animals were offered, and unclean ones redeemed. Jesus himself was presented at the temple under this law (Luke 2:22–24).
- **"The first of our dough… to the priests" (v37).** A portion of the first dough of each batch was set aside for God (Num 15:20–21). This brought the principle of firstfruits into the kitchen.
- **"To bring to the Levites the tithes from our ground" (v37).** The tithe, a tenth of the harvest, supported the Levites, who had no land of their own (Num 18:21–24). They collected it in the farming towns where people lived.
- **"The priest… shall be with the Levites when the Levites receive the tithes" (v38).** A priest was present at collection. JFB calls it "a prudential arrangement" to keep everything fair. The Levites then gave a tenth of what they received, "the tithe of the tithes," to the priests (Num 18:25–32). The system supported everyone who served at the temple, all the way up.
- **"To the chambers of the storehouse" (v38).** The temple had storerooms for grain, wine, oil and offerings. Malachi, a prophet of the same period, speaks of bringing "the full tithe into the storehouse" (Mal 3:10). Nehemiah would later find these same rooms emptied of grain and given to Tobiah (13:4–9).
- **"We will not neglect the house of our God" (v39).** The last line sums up the covenant. "Neglect" (*'azav*) means "abandon" or "forsake." In 13:11 Nehemiah uses the same verb when he finds the Levites gone home because the tithes had not been paid: "Why is the house of God forsaken?" Within about a dozen years, the people had broken nearly every specific promise in this chapter: the marriages (13:23–27), the Sabbath (13:15–22), the tithes (13:10–13) and the temple chambers (13:4–9). The covenant was sincere, but it was not strong enough to hold them.
- **Commentators:** Henry lists the specific pledges: they "would not marry with the heathen, nor profane the sabbath, nor be rigorous with their debtors," and "they would carefully pay their church-dues, for the maintenance of the temple service." JFB explains that those who could not sign ratified the covenant "by lifting up their hands" and calling a curse on themselves if they broke it. He links the wood offering to the later festival described by Josephus, and he sees the repeated final pledge as a sign of "the intense zeal" of a people moved by repentance and gratitude. Guzik sees the curse as accepting God's discipline for the purpose of being brought back, like a believer praying "whatever it takes." He applies the Sabbath promise to the temptation to compromise for money a little at a time, and he notes that giving the firstfruits and the firstborn took faith, since the rest of the harvest and herd was not yet secure (compare Prov 3:9–10).

#### Pointing to Christ
This is a sincere covenant, signed by leaders and sworn by the whole community, and within a few years it lay in pieces (ch 13). The fault was not in the Law, which was good, but in the human heart, which could promise but not perform. Jeremiah had foreseen exactly this. God would make "a new covenant… not like the covenant that I made with their fathers," writing his law on their hearts and remembering their sin no more (Jer 31:31–34). On the night he was betrayed, Jesus took the cup and said, "This cup… is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20). The house of God that Israel promised not to neglect points to the one who said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19). He is the perfect offering the daily sacrifices pictured, and the new covenant rests on his faithfulness, not on the strength of the people's promises.
