*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Saul has died on Mount Gilboa, and "the LORD… turned the kingdom over to David" (10:14). The Chronicler now tells David's reign, and he tells it selectively. He leaves out the seven and a half years David ruled Judah alone from Hebron and the civil war with Saul's son Ish-bosheth (2 Sam 2–4). He also leaves out, later, the story of Bathsheba and the rebellion of Absalom. This is not because he is hiding them, since his readers had 2 Samuel. He has a purpose: to show David as the king God chose, the founder of Jerusalem's worship and the father of the temple, and to hold him up as the pattern of the coming king that the returned exiles were still waiting for. This chapter does three things quickly. All Israel makes David king at Hebron (about 1003 BC), David takes Jerusalem and makes it his capital, and the Chronicler lists the warriors who stood with him. In 2 Samuel that list comes near the end of David's life (2 Sam 23). Here it comes first, as if to say that the whole nation, its best fighters included, rallied to God's king from the start.

#### David Anointed King (vv1–3)
- **"Then all Israel gathered together to David at Hebron" (v1).** "Then" jumps straight from Saul's death. In the history it was seven years later, after Saul's general Abner and his son Ish-bosheth had both been murdered (2 Sam 3–4). The Chronicler's "all Israel" is the point. This is the whole nation, north and south together.
- **"Behold, we are your bone and flesh" (v1).** A phrase of kinship, "we are your own family." Laban said it to Jacob (Gen 29:14), and it echoes Adam's words over Eve (Gen 2:23). The tribes are saying David is not a foreign ruler. He is one of them.
- **"It was you who led out and brought in Israel" (v2).** "Led out and brought in" is the language of a military leader taking the army out to battle and bringing it home (Num 27:17; 1 Sam 18:13, 16). Even while Saul reigned, David was the one actually winning Israel's battles.
- **"You shall be shepherd of my people Israel, and you shall be prince over my people Israel" (v2).** Two titles. A king in the ancient Near East was often called a shepherd, but in Israel the true Shepherd was God (Ps 23:1; 80:1). David was a shepherd under the Shepherd, caring for a flock that was "my people," God's, not his own. "Prince" is *nagid*, leader appointed by God, the title used when God first chose him (1 Sam 13:14; 2 Sam 7:8). Guzik, following Selman, notes that the shepherd image made the king answerable to God for how he treated the people.
- **"David made a covenant with them at Hebron before the LORD" (v3).** The king bound himself by solemn agreement to the people, "before the LORD." Israel's king was not above God's law (Deut 17:18–20). Henry: "Between prince and people there is an original contract, which both ought religiously to observe."
- **"They anointed David king over Israel, according to the word of the LORD by Samuel" (v3).** This was David's third anointing: privately by Samuel as a boy (1 Sam 16:13), by the men of Judah at Hebron (2 Sam 2:4), and now by all Israel. God's word through Samuel, spoken perhaps twenty years earlier, came true at last. Henry: "God's counsels will be fulfilled at last, whatever difficulties lie in the way."
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws three marks of a God-given leader from the elders' words: he belongs to God's people, he has proved he can lead, and he has a clear call from God. He also observes that Israel came to David only when every other option had run out, and many come to Jesus the same way. JFB notes that a public gathering and fresh anointing were needed for the whole nation to recognize David, as Saul had been confirmed at Gilgal (1 Sam 11:15).

#### David Takes Jerusalem (vv4–9)
- **"Jerusalem, that is, Jebus, where the Jebusites were" (v4).** Jerusalem was an old Canaanite city still held by the Jebusites, four centuries after Joshua. It sat on the border of Judah and Benjamin, belonging fully to neither. That made it an ideal capital for a king of all Israel. It was neutral ground, and it had strong natural defenses, with deep valleys on three sides.
- **"You will not come in here" (v5).** 2 Samuel 5:6–8 records the full taunt: the Jebusites boasted that even the blind and lame could hold off David. It also mentions a "water shaft" by which the city was entered. The Chronicler leaves those details out.
- **"Nevertheless, David took the stronghold of Zion, that is, the city of David" (v5).** This is the first mention of **Zion** in Chronicles. It was the fortified ridge on the southeast side of the later city. The name came to stand for Jerusalem, God's dwelling place and the hope of his people (Ps 2:6; 48:2). The "city of David" was a small area, perhaps twelve acres, and archaeologists have excavated parts of it.
- **"Whoever strikes the Jebusites first shall be chief and commander" (v6).** Only Chronicles tells how **Joab** won his command, by leading the assault. Joab, David's nephew (2:16), was brave and able, and also ruthless. He murdered two rival commanders and later killed Absalom against David's orders (2 Sam 3:27; 18:14; 20:10). Henry notes that the reward offered "quickens and engages resolution in great undertakings."
- **"He built the city all around from the Millo" (v8).** "Millo" means "filling." It was probably a system of terraces and fill that supported buildings on the steep slope. Archaeologists have uncovered a massive stepped stone structure on the eastern side of the City of David that many identify with it. JFB suggests Joab's work meant repairing the old Jebusite town after the siege.
- **"And David became greater and greater, for the LORD of hosts was with him" (v9).** The reason for David's rise is given in one phrase. "LORD of hosts" means the LORD of armies, both Israel's armies and the armies of heaven. This is the same note struck of Joseph (Gen 39:2) and of the young David (1 Sam 18:14). Guzik notes that David's greatness came after long years of hiding and waiting, the hidden price God's servants often pay first.

#### David's Mighty Men (vv10–47)
- **"Who gave him strong support in his kingdom, together with all Israel, to make him king, according to the word of the LORD" (v10).** The Chronicler adds this introduction to the list he takes from 2 Samuel 23:8–39. JFB points out the addition: these warriors helped make David king. The Tyndale notes add that the people did not choose David. God did, and the warriors and people simply rallied to his choice. Many of these men had joined David years earlier when he was a fugitive at the cave of Adullam, a band of men who were "in distress… in debt… bitter in soul" (1 Sam 22:1–2). Guzik draws attention to that: David's mighty men were not mighty when they came to him.
- **"Jashobeam, a Hachmonite, was chief of the three. He wielded his spear against 300" (v11).** 2 Samuel 23:8 gives the figure as 800 and a slightly different name. Numbers were easily miscopied in Hebrew manuscripts, and Guzik and JFB both treat this as a copying difference. **"The three"** were the top rank of David's heroes, above **"the thirty,"** a larger group of honored warriors.
- **"Eleazar the son of Dodo… at Pas-dammim" (vv12–13).** Pas-dammim is almost certainly Ephes-dammim, the place where the Philistines camped when Goliath came out to challenge Israel (1 Sam 17:1). David's career began on that battlefield.
- **"There was a plot of ground full of barley, and the men fled… But he took his stand in the midst of the plot and defended it" (vv13–14).** In 2 Samuel 23:9–12 there are two separate stories: Eleazar fought at Pas-dammim "till his hand clung to the sword," and then a third hero, **Shammah**, defended a field of lentils. Chronicles seems to have lost a line in copying, when a scribe's eye jumped from one mention of the Philistines gathering to the next, joining the two stories and dropping Shammah's name. That explains why "the three mighty men" (v12) has only two named. JFB notes that Shammah is missing. **"And the LORD saved them by a great victory"** gives the credit where the Chronicler always puts it.
- **"Three of the thirty chief men went down to the rock to David at the cave of Adullam" (v15).** This scene goes back to the war with the Philistines early in David's reign or just before it. Adullam was a hill fortress about twelve miles southwest of Bethlehem. The **Valley of Rephaim** ran southwest of Jerusalem toward Bethlehem, a route the Philistines used to invade the hill country (14:9).
- **"Oh that someone would give me water to drink from the well of Bethlehem that is by the gate!" (v17).** David was homesick. Bethlehem was his hometown, now occupied by an enemy garrison, and he longed out loud for the taste of water he had drunk as a boy. It was a wish, not an order.
- **"The three mighty men broke through the camp of the Philistines and drew water" (v18).** Three men heard the wish and fought their way through enemy lines to grant it. That is the kind of devotion David inspired.
- **"But David would not drink it. He poured it out to the LORD" (v18).** He poured it on the ground as a drink offering, the same act priests performed with wine at the altar (Num 28:7). Water that had cost so much was too precious for him to drink, so he gave it to God.
- **"Shall I drink the lifeblood of these men? For at the risk of their lives they brought it" (v19).** The Law taught that blood is life, and that blood belongs to God and must be poured out, never consumed (Lev 17:10–14; Deut 12:23–24). David treats the water as if it were the men's blood. Henry puts it well: in David's eyes "it turns the water into blood." He also notes the self-denial: "It is an honour to a man to have the command of himself; but he that will command himself must sometimes cross himself."
- **"Abishai, the brother of Joab, was chief of the thirty" (v20).** Abishai was Joab's brother and David's nephew, fiercely loyal and quick to the sword. He offered to kill the sleeping Saul (1 Sam 26:8) and to behead Shimei for cursing David (2 Sam 16:9). In one battle he saved David's life from a Philistine giant (2 Sam 21:17). The Hebrew of vv20–21 has "three" in some places where the sense needs "thirty," and the ESV follows the Syriac translation. The point is clear: he was the most honored of the second rank, but "he did not attain to the three."
- **"Benaiah the son of Jehoiada… struck down two heroes of Moab" (v22).** The Hebrew for "heroes" is *ariel*, which can mean "lion of God," a title for a champion warrior. It may also be a personal name: "the two sons of Ariel of Moab." JFB takes it as "lions of God," great champions.
- **"He also went down and struck down a lion in a pit on a day when snow had fallen" (v22).** Snow in Judah's hills is rare. A lion trapped in a pit is at its most dangerous, and snow makes the footing treacherous. Benaiah went down anyway.
- **"An Egyptian, a man of great stature, five cubits tall… a spear like a weaver's beam" (v23).** About seven and a half feet tall, armed with a spear "like a weaver's beam," the same description used of Goliath's spear (1 Sam 17:7). Benaiah fought him with a club, took his spear and killed him with it, just as David killed Goliath with Goliath's own sword (1 Sam 17:51). Only Chronicles gives the Egyptian's height. JFB makes the same comparison with Goliath.
- **"David set him over his bodyguard" (v25).** Benaiah commanded the Cherethites and Pelethites, David's foreign guard troops (18:17). Under Solomon he became commander of the whole army, replacing Joab (1 Kings 2:35).
- **"Asahel the brother of Joab" (v26).** The list of the thirty begins with Asahel, "swift of foot as a wild gazelle," who was killed by Abner during the civil war (2 Sam 2:18–23). He was dead before David reigned over all Israel. The list honors those who fought for David across his whole career, not only those living at one moment.
- **"Elhanan the son of Dodo of Bethlehem" (v26).** David's own town. Several men in the list come from Bethlehem, Tekoa, Netophah and Anathoth, villages of Judah and Benjamin near his home.
- **"Uriah the Hittite" (v41).** In 2 Samuel the list ends with his name (2 Sam 23:39), a quiet, painful finish. Uriah was one of David's most loyal soldiers, and David took his wife and arranged his death (2 Sam 11). The Chronicler never tells that story, but he keeps Uriah's name on the honor roll. Guzik notes that David should have remembered whose wife Bathsheba was when he heard her name (2 Sam 11:3). JFB says Uriah's place among the heroes makes David's crime worse.
- **"Zabad the son of Ahlai… Jaasiel the Mezobaite" (vv41–47).** Here Chronicles goes beyond the list in Samuel, adding sixteen more names. The Tyndale notes observe that many of these came from east of the Jordan, from Reuben (v42), Ashtaroth (v44) and Aroer (v44). Chronicles wants us to see that David's support reached across the river too. Some were foreigners: **Zelek the Ammonite** (v39), **Ithmah the Moabite** (v46), and Uriah the Hittite. David himself had a Moabite great-grandmother, Ruth (Ruth 4:17).
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "David is great because he has great men about him; take these away, and he is where he was." He adds, "The way to be great is to do good," since these men won honor by costly service. Guzik, quoting G. Campbell Morgan, argues that the men David shaped are a greater testimony to him than his victories over enemies. On the water from Bethlehem he cites F. B. Meyer: do not live in wistful longing for the past, but look to the Lord, who gives living water. JFB notes that **Joab** himself is not in the list, probably because as commander-in-chief he stood above it (v39 names his armor-bearer).

#### Pointing to Christ
The tribes came to David saying, "We are your bone and flesh," and the Son of God took on our flesh so that he could be our King and our brother (Heb 2:14–17). David was shepherd and prince over a people who belonged to God; Jesus is "the good shepherd" who "lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). David's men came to him in distress and debt and were made into heroes. Christ gathers the same kind of people and makes them new. The water from Bethlehem points further still. David refused to drink what had cost his men their blood. The Son of David, born in that same Bethlehem, did the opposite: he gave his own blood so that others could drink. "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" (1 Cor 11:25). What David poured out to God because it was too costly, Jesus pours out for us because he loves us, and he offers "living water" to anyone who is thirsty (John 7:37).
