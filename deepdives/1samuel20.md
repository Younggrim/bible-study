*Setting:* Directly after the scene at Naioth, where Saul's messengers, and then Saul himself, were overcome by the Spirit and prophesied while trying to capture David (19:18–24). David has escaped Saul's spear three times (18:11; 19:10) and climbed out of a window to avoid being killed in his bed (19:11–17). He slips back from Ramah to Gibeah, a few miles away, to find Jonathan. The two friends have already made a covenant (18:3). This chapter puts it to the test. It is the longest scene of their friendship, and it ends with their parting. Jonathan has to choose between his father's kingdom, which would one day be his, and his loyalty to the man God has chosen. He chooses David, and in doing so he chooses the LORD's will.

#### Jonathan Warns David (vv1–42)
- **"What have I done? What is my guilt? And what is my sin before your father?" (v1).** Three questions in a row show David's distress. He asks the same thing later when he confronts Saul directly (24:11; 26:18). David is genuinely bewildered. He has done nothing but serve the king.
- **"Far from it! You shall not die" (v2).** Jonathan cannot believe his father would kill David after swearing an oath not to (19:6). He thinks Saul tells him everything. He has not yet seen what David sees.
- **"There is but a step between me and death" (v3).** David answers with an oath of his own, "as the LORD lives and as your soul lives." He suggests why Jonathan has not heard: Saul knows how close they are and is hiding his plans from his son. "A step" is a vivid phrase. Death is one stride away.
- **"Tomorrow is the new moon, and I should not fail to sit at table with the king" (v5).** Israel's calendar followed the moon, and each new month began with special sacrifices and trumpets (Num 10:10; 28:11–15). It was also a feast day, apparently over two days here, when the king's inner circle ate together. As a commander and the king's son-in-law, David was expected to be there.
- **"A yearly sacrifice there for all the clan" (v6).** Families and clans held yearly sacrifices in their hometowns (1:3, 21; 2:19). David proposes a test. If Saul accepts the excuse, all is well. If he is angry, his intent to kill is plain. Interpreters have discussed whether David's excuse was a lie. The text does not say whether there was such a sacrifice in Bethlehem that month. What is clear is that David is in mortal danger and is trying to find out the truth of a king who has already broken his word.
- **"Deal kindly with your servant, for you have brought your servant into a covenant of the LORD with you" (v8).** "Deal kindly" is from *chesed*, the great Hebrew word for loyal, committed love, the kind of faithfulness a covenant creates. It runs through this chapter (vv8, 14, 15) and becomes a key word of David's whole story. David calls it "a covenant of the LORD," a promise made before God, with God as witness. "If there is guilt in me, kill me yourself" is the cry of a man with nothing to hide.
- **"Come, let us go out into the field" (v11).** The two men go where they cannot be overheard.
- **"The LORD, the God of Israel, be witness!" (v12).** Jonathan now takes the lead and speaks a solemn oath. He promises to warn David either way, and he binds himself with the oath formula Saul used against him: "the LORD do so to Jonathan and more also" (v13; compare 14:44).
- **"May the LORD be with you, as he has been with my father" (v13).** This is an astonishing sentence from a crown prince. Jonathan sees that the LORD's presence, which once rested on Saul (10:7), now rests on David. He is praying a blessing on the man who will take the throne he should have inherited. There is no bitterness in it.
- **"Show me the steadfast love of the LORD, that I may not die" (v14).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed* again, and here it is "the *chesed* of the LORD," a loyal love that reflects God's own. In the ancient world a new king often wiped out the family of the old one to remove rivals. (Later kings in Israel did exactly this, 1 Kgs 15:29; 2 Kgs 10:6–7.) Jonathan asks David to promise that, when he becomes king, he will not do it to Jonathan or "my house forever" (v15).
- **"When the LORD cuts off every one of the enemies of David from the face of the earth" (v15).** Jonathan expects David's victory. He even counts his own father among David's enemies, though he does not say so directly.
- **"Jonathan made a covenant with the house of David" (v16).** The covenant now reaches beyond the two men to both families. Years later, David asks, "Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may show him kindness for Jonathan's sake?" (2 Sam 9:1). He finds Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth and gives him a place at the king's table for life. And when the Gibeonites demand vengeance on Saul's descendants, David spares Mephibosheth "because of the oath of the LORD that was between them, between David and Jonathan" (2 Sam 21:7).
- **"He loved him as he loved his own soul" (v17).** The phrase from 18:1, 3 returns. Jonathan's love is the reason for the covenant, not a political calculation.
- **The arrow signal (vv18–22).** Jonathan works out a code in case they cannot speak face to face. David will hide by "the stone heap." (The Hebrew reads "the stone Ezel," a name found nowhere else; the ESV follows the Greek, which reads "this mound.") Jonathan will shoot three arrows as if practicing, then call out to a boy where the arrows are. "On this side of you" means safety. "Beyond you" means "go, for the LORD has sent you away." Even the bad news is framed in faith: if David must flee, it is the LORD sending him.
- **"The LORD is between you and me forever" (v23).** The LORD is the witness and guardian of the covenant. Laban and Jacob used similar words when they made a covenant: "The LORD watch between you and me" (Gen 31:49).
- **The seating at the feast (v25).** The king sits by the wall, the safest seat, with no one behind him. Abner, the army commander and Saul's cousin, sits beside him. Jonathan sits opposite (following the Greek; the Hebrew reads "Jonathan rose," perhaps giving his seat to Abner). The detail sets the scene: an intimate royal table with one empty place.
- **"He is not clean; surely he is not clean" (v26).** Under the law, someone who was ritually unclean, for example after contact with a dead body or certain bodily discharges, could not eat a sacred meal (Lev 7:20–21; 15:16–18). Most such uncleanness lasted until evening. On the first day Saul assumes this. When David's seat is empty a second day, the excuse no longer works.
- **"Why has not the son of Jesse come?" (v27).** Saul refuses to use David's name. "Son of Jesse" is a way of putting David down, reminding everyone of his ordinary family (compare 22:7–9; 25:10).
- **"You son of a perverse, rebellious woman" (v30).** Saul's fury explodes at his own son. The insult is aimed at Jonathan, not really at his mother, much as crude insults about someone's mother are today. "The shame of your mother's nakedness" means Jonathan is disgracing the mother who bore him. Saul sees Jonathan's friendship as treason against his own family.
- **"As long as the son of Jesse lives on the earth, neither you nor your kingdom shall be established" (v31).** Here Saul says the quiet part out loud. He knows David is a threat to his dynasty, which means he knows, at some level, what Samuel has said (13:14; 15:28). He is fighting God's decision. "He shall surely die" is literally "he is a son of death," a man condemned.
- **"Why should he be put to death? What has he done?" (v32).** Jonathan asks the question David asked at the start (v1). He is not defying his father's authority. He is asking for justice.
- **"Saul hurled his spear at him to strike him" (v33).** Saul turns the spear he has thrown at David on his own son. Now Jonathan knows. The test David proposed has given its answer, more violently than anyone expected.
- **"Jonathan rose from the table in fierce anger and ate no food… for he was grieved for David, because his father had disgraced him" (v34).** "Him" probably means David: Saul had shamed David by sentencing an innocent man to death. Jonathan's anger is righteous anger, and his grief is for his friend more than for himself.
- **"Is not the arrow beyond you?" (v37).** The next morning, Jonathan goes out with a young boy and gives the signal. "Hurry! Be quick! Do not stay!" (v38) is addressed to the boy, but it is meant for David's ears. "The boy knew nothing" (v39). The secret stays between the two friends.
- **"David… fell on his face to the ground and bowed three times" (v41).** Once the boy is gone, David comes out of hiding and bows deeply, honoring Jonathan as the king's son and his rescuer. "They kissed one another and wept with one another, David weeping the most." A kiss was the ordinary greeting and farewell between men in that culture (Gen 33:4; 2 Sam 14:33). Their tears say what words cannot. They seem to sense that their friendship, from now on, will be lived mostly at a distance. They will meet only once more, briefly, in the wilderness of Ziph (23:16–18).
- **"Go in peace, because we have sworn both of us in the name of the LORD" (v42).** Jonathan's last words to David here are a blessing grounded in the covenant, "between my offspring and your offspring, forever." David leaves as a fugitive. Jonathan goes back to the city, to his father's court, where he will stay until he dies beside Saul in battle (31:2).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "True friendship rests on a firm basis, and is able to silence ambition, self-love, and undue regard for others," and on the farewell, "Christians need not sorrow, as men without hope." He also says the trials David met "prepared him for future advancement." On Saul's outburst: "What savage beasts, and worse, does anger make men!" JFB explains that David put his fears to Jonathan "in terms delicately chosen," notes that the seat by the wall was a place of honor, and calls Saul's spear-throw "a sad proof of the maniacal frenzy into which the unhappy monarch was transported." JFB reads the Hebrew "Ezel" as "the stone of the way," a kind of milestone. Guzik notes that Jonathan answered a discouraged friend with practical help and encouragement rather than a lecture, and that Saul's oath in 19:6 shows some rulers treat oaths as binding only on others. He also observes how much rode on something as small as where an arrow landed. The Tyndale notes explain *chesed* as covenant faithfulness and point out that in the ancient world a new king often killed the old king's family, which is why Jonathan's request mattered.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jonathan, heir to the throne, gladly gives way to the king God has chosen, asks only to be shown the LORD's steadfast love, and binds his family to that king by covenant forever. John the Baptist would say much the same about Jesus: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30). But the deeper picture is the covenant itself. David's *chesed* to Jonathan's lame son, seating him at the king's table "for Jonathan's sake," is a picture of the gospel. People who could never earn a place at God's table are welcomed there for the sake of another, through a covenant sealed in the LORD's name. In Christ, God shows "the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us" (Eph 2:7), and his steadfast love is never cut off from his house.
