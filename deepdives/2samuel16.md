*Setting:* The same day as the end of chapter 15, around 976 BC. David has fled Jerusalem on foot with his household, his bodyguard and a few loyal friends. He has crossed the Kidron, climbed the Mount of Olives weeping, sent the ark back with the priests and sent Hushai back as a spy. Now he heads east down the steep road through the wilderness of Judah toward the Jordan, about 20 miles away and some 3,500 feet lower. On the way he meets two men from the house of Saul, one with gifts and lies, the other with stones and curses. Meanwhile Absalom rides into the city his father has left. The chapter cuts back and forth between the two kings, and it ends on the palace roof where David's troubles began.

#### David and Ziba (vv1–4)
- **"Ziba the servant of Mephibosheth met him" (v1).** Ziba had been Saul's steward. David put him in charge of Saul's estate to farm it for Mephibosheth, Jonathan's disabled son, whom David had taken into his own household (9:9–13).
- **Two hundred loaves, raisins, summer fruit and wine (v1).** It is a generous and timely gift, and the donkeys are a thoughtful touch for "the king's household to ride on" (v2). Abigail once met David with almost the same list of supplies when he was a fugitive (1 Sam 25:18). Whatever Ziba's motives, the food was real help.
- **"Where is your master's son?" (v3).** David asks about the man he had shown *hesed*, kindness, for Jonathan's sake.
- **"Today the house of Israel will give me back the kingdom of my father" (v3).** Ziba claims Mephibosheth stayed behind hoping to be made king. The story is unlikely. A disabled man with no army had no hope of the throne, least of all if Absalom won. In 19:24–30 Mephibosheth says Ziba deceived him and left him behind, and his unkempt state when David returns supports his account. But in this moment of betrayal, the story sounds plausible to David.
- **"Behold, all that belonged to Mephibosheth is now yours" (v4).** David gives judgment on one man's word, without hearing the accused. The king who failed to judge Amnon now judges too fast. Proverbs 18:17 puts it well: "The one who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry calls it "a rash judgment, and which afterwards he was ashamed of." He adds that God's providence used Ziba's gift for David's good, whatever Ziba intended. JFB says David "pronounced a rash and unrighteous judgment" on "a devoted friend," and thinks Ziba was betting on Absalom's failure. Guzik calls Ziba a man who used a crisis for his own gain. The Tyndale notes add the political background: as Saul's grandson, Mephibosheth was a possible rival, which made the lie easy to believe.

#### Shimei Curses David (vv5–14)
- **"When King David came to Bahurim" (v5).** Bahurim was a village of Benjamin just east of the Mount of Olives. It is where Paltiel walked weeping behind his wife Michal when Abner took her back to David (3:16). Benjamin was Saul's tribe, and resentment against David still ran deep there.
- **"Shimei, the son of Gera… cursed continually. And he threw stones" (vv5–6).** Shimei was of Saul's clan. Walking on the ridge above the road, he hurls stones and dust down at the king, though David is surrounded by his "mighty men." It is a picture of how far David has fallen.
- **"Get out, get out, you man of blood, you worthless man!" (v7).** "Worthless man" is "man of *beliyyaʿal*," a phrase for scoundrels and troublemakers (1 Sam 2:12; 25:25). "Man of blood" means a murderer.
- **"The LORD has avenged on you all the blood of the house of Saul" (v8).** Shimei blames David for the deaths in Saul's family: Saul and Jonathan, Abner, Ish-bosheth, and perhaps the seven grandsons of Saul handed over to the Gibeonites (21:1–9), if that happened before this. But David had no hand in Saul's death, mourned Abner and executed Ish-bosheth's killers (1:14–16; 3:31–37; 4:9–12). Shimei's charge is false. And yet David *is* a man of blood, because of Uriah. Shimei hits the right man with the wrong accusation.
- **"Why should this dead dog curse my lord the king? Let me go over and take off his head" (v9).** Abishai, Joab's brother, is a man of action (1 Sam 26:8). The law said, "You shall not… curse a ruler of your people" (Exod 22:28), so he had a case. "Dead dog" is the lowest insult: worthless and contemptible (1 Sam 24:14; 9:8).
- **"What have I to do with you, you sons of Zeruiah?" (v10).** This is an idiom meaning "this is none of your business" or "we are not of one mind." David uses it again in 19:22. He had already complained that "these men, the sons of Zeruiah, are more severe than I" (3:39).
- **"If he is cursing because the LORD has said to him, 'Curse David,' who then shall say, 'Why have you done so?'" (v10).** David does not say Shimei is right. He says God may be using him. Behind the curses he sees the hand of God disciplining him, as Nathan had promised. He will not fight God's discipline.
- **"My own son seeks my life; how much more now may this Benjaminite!" (v11).** After Absalom's betrayal, a stranger's curses seem small. David also understands why a man of Saul's house might hate him.
- **"It may be that the LORD will look on the wrong done to me, and… repay me with good for his cursing today" (v12).** David entrusts himself to God. Psalm 7 is headed "concerning the words of Cush, a Benjaminite," and some have connected it with this scene, though that is only a guess. The spirit is the same: "If I have repaid my friend with evil… let the enemy pursue my soul" (Ps 7:4–5).
- **"Shimei went along on the hillside opposite him and cursed as he went and threw stones at him and flung dust" (v13).** He keeps it up the whole way. Throwing dust was a gesture of contempt and rage (compare Acts 22:23).
- **"The king, and all the people who were with him, arrived weary… And there he refreshed himself" (v14).** The ESV footnote says the Hebrew does not have the words "at the Jordan." The Greek Old Testament adds them, and the ESV follows it. Some think the Hebrew originally named a place, perhaps "Ayephim," a word that looks like the Hebrew for "weary." Either way, the king rests at last, catching his breath, as Psalm 3 puts it: "I lay down and slept; I woke again, for the LORD sustained me" (Ps 3:5).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says David "bore Shimei's curses much better than he had borne Ziba's flatteries," a sharp observation, since the flattery led him into a wrong judgment. Henry also notes that Shimei had hated David all along but only dared to say so now that David was down. JFB says David "was guiltless of the crime of which Shimei accused him; but his conscience reminded him of other flagrant iniquities." Guzik observes that if David really were the bloodthirsty man Shimei called him, Shimei would already be dead. He sees David's willingness to let go of the throne as a faint picture of Christ, who did not cling to his rights (Phil 2:6–8). The Tyndale notes add that David's restraint here contrasts with his deathbed instruction to Solomon about Shimei (1 Kings 2:8–9).

#### Absalom Enters Jerusalem (vv15–23)
- **"Absalom and all the people, the men of Israel, came to Jerusalem, and Ahithophel with him" (v15).** The scene moves back to the capital. Absalom takes the city without a fight.
- **"Long live the king! Long live the king!" (v16).** Hushai greets Absalom with a double cry, but notice what he does not say. He never names Absalom. In his own mind the king who should live is David. The Tyndale notes point out how carefully ambiguous his words are throughout.
- **"Is this your loyalty to your friend?" (v17).** "Loyalty" is *hesed*. Absalom mocks Hushai for deserting David. The rebel son lectures a supposed turncoat on faithfulness.
- **"Whom the LORD and this people and all the men of Israel have chosen, his I will be" (v18).** Again, it is true as Hushai means it. The LORD had chosen David (1 Sam 16:12–13). Absalom hears only flattery.
- **"As I have served your father, so I will serve you" (v19).** Literally "as I served before your father, so I will be before you." He can mean that he will serve David just as faithfully while standing before Absalom. Absalom, vain and eager for support, does not see the double meaning.
- **"Go in to your father's concubines… and all Israel will hear that you have made yourself a stench to your father" (v21).** "Stench" is the same word used when the Ammonites insulted David's envoys (10:6). Ahithophel's advice is cold and clever. Taking a king's women was a public claim to his throne (3:7; 1 Kings 2:22). It would also make reconciliation impossible, so Absalom's followers would know he could not make peace and leave them exposed. It broke the law forbidding a son to take his father's wife (Lev 18:8; Deut 22:30). Reuben did something like it and lost his birthright (Gen 35:22; 49:3–4).
- **"They pitched a tent for Absalom on the roof… in the sight of all Israel" (v22).** This is the roof of the palace, the same roof from which David watched Bathsheba bathing (11:2). Nathan had said: "he shall lie with your wives in the sight of this sun. For you did it secretly, but I will do this thing before all Israel" (12:11–12). The prophecy is fulfilled word for word. Ahithophel, if he was Bathsheba's grandfather, may have seen it as a fitting revenge. The text does not say.
- **"The counsel that Ahithophel gave was as if one consulted the word of God" (v23).** His advice was treated almost like an oracle, a word from God through the priests. That makes David's prayer in 15:31 bold, and the next chapter will show God answer it.
- **Commentators:** On Hushai's words, Henry says Absalom was "pleasing himself with this thought, that all would be his." JFB thinks Hushai convinced him by arguing that God and the nation had seemingly decided for Absalom. Henry says Ahithophel, by giving wicked counsel, "did as effectually betray him as he did who was designedly false to him," a striking point: the loyal adviser ruined Absalom as surely as the spy did. Guzik sees bitterness behind Ahithophel's advice. JFB calls it "atrocious advice," meant to make reconciliation impossible. The Tyndale notes and Guzik both tie the rooftop tent to David's sin on that same roof.

#### Pointing to Christ
David, the anointed king, goes out of his city humbled and cursed, and he will not let his men strike back. "Leave him alone… It may be that the LORD will… repay me with good." His restraint points ahead to the Son of David who, "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return… but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). When James and John wanted to call down fire on a village that rejected Jesus, he rebuked them as David rebuked the sons of Zeruiah (Luke 9:54–55). The difference is that David deserved some of what he suffered. Jesus was cursed though innocent, and he became "a curse for us" (Gal 3:13) so that those who deserved the curse could receive the blessing.
