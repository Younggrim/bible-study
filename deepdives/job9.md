*Setting:* Bildad the Shuhite has just finished his first speech (ch 8). Shorter and blunter than Eliphaz, he called Job's words "a great wind" (8:2), insisted that God never perverts justice (8:3), suggested that Job's children died for their own sins (8:4), and appealed to the wisdom of past generations: plants without water wither, and so do people who forget God (8:8–19). His closing promise sounded kind but carried a sting: "God will not reject a blameless man" (8:20). If Job is suffering, the logic runs, he must not be blameless. Now Job answers, and his reply runs through chapter 10. In chapter 9 he does not argue with Bildad's theology so much as follow it to a frightening place. If God is perfectly just and all-powerful, how could anyone ever win a case against him? The chapter is full of courtroom words, and it ends with one of the most famous longings in the Old Testament: the wish for someone to stand between God and man.

#### Job Replies: There Is No Arbiter (vv1–35)
- **"Truly I know that it is so" (v2).** Job begins by agreeing. Yes, God does not pervert justice, and yes, the wicked do not finally prosper. He has never denied that. His problem is not with the principle but with how it could ever help him.
- **"But how can a man be in the right before God?" (v2).** "Be in the right" is the Hebrew verb *tsadaq*, to be just, to have a just cause, to be acquitted in court. The lexicon lists this very verse under the sense "be justified, in one's plea," a man's standing with God. Job is picking up Eliphaz's words from 4:17, which use the same verb: "Can mortal man be in the right before God?" Eliphaz meant it as a rebuke. Job turns it into a real question, and a desperate one. The same question returns in Bildad's last speech (25:4) and in the psalmist's cry: "If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?" (Ps 130:3). The whole Bible eventually answers it in Romans 3–5.
- **"If one wished to contend with him" (v3).** "Contend" is *riv*, a word for quarreling that often means bringing a lawsuit. The lexicon lists this verse under contending with God, and the word's legal sense, conducting a case, is well attested (Isa 3:13; Mic 6:1). The Tyndale notes trace the legal pattern through the whole chapter: a summons and a response, the danger of incriminating oneself, a question of accusation, a sentence, a declaration of guilt, and finally a mediator. Job imagines taking God to court, and in the same breath sees that he would lose: he "could not answer him once in a thousand times."- **"Wise in heart and mighty in strength" (v4).** These are the two things a person needs to win a dispute: brains and power. God has both, without limit. "Who has hardened himself against him, and succeeded?" Here Job agrees with his friends. Defying God never works.
- **The God who shakes creation (vv5–7).** Job now praises God's power in a hymn that could sit in the Psalms. God moves mountains before they know it, shakes the earth so that "its pillars tremble" (the earth pictured as a building on foundations), and can command the sun not to rise and "seal up" the stars like a document sealed shut. The Tyndale notes point out that in Scripture, mountains shaking and the earth trembling mark God's coming as lawgiver (Exod 19:18), as judge (Jer 4:24) and as rescuer (Ps 18:7). But Job's examples are all overturning, shaking and darkening: power he has felt as terror.
- **"Who alone stretched out the heavens and trampled the waves of the sea" (v8).** The sky is pictured like a tent stretched out (Isa 40:22; Ps 104:2). "Trampled the waves" is literally "treads on the high places (or backs) of the sea." In the ancient Near East the sea was a picture of chaos, the untamed force that threatened order. The true God simply walks on it. The Greek translation of Job renders this line as God "walking on the sea as on dry ground," which makes Jesus walking on the water (Mark 6:48) all the more striking.
- **"The Bear and Orion, the Pleiades and the chambers of the south" (v9).** These are the best-known star groups of the ancient sky, and the same names return when God himself speaks to Job (38:31–32; see also Amos 5:8). The identifications are not certain. The lexicon gives *'ash* as "perhaps Great Bear" (the KJV's "Arcturus" is an older guess), *kesil* as Orion, and *kimah* as "perhaps Pleiades," noting that some scholars swap the two and read *'ash* as the Pleiades. "The chambers of the south" probably means stars of the southern sky, or the hidden regions they come from. The point is simple: every light in the night sky is God's handiwork.
- **"Great things beyond searching out" (v10).** This line repeats almost word for word what Eliphaz said in 5:9. Eliphaz used God's greatness to say, "So seek him and he will restore you." Job uses the same truth to say, "So who could ever understand him?" Same theology, opposite feeling.
- **"He passes by me, and I see him not" (v11).** This may be the saddest line in the hymn. The God who commands the stars is close enough to pass by Job, yet Job cannot see or perceive him. "Pass by" is the language used when God showed his glory to Moses (Exod 33:22) and to Elijah (1 Kgs 19:11), and Eliphaz used it of the spirit that "glided past" his face (4:15). For Job there is no vision, only absence.- **"Who will say to him, 'What are you doing?'" (v12).** God "snatches away," as he took Job's children, wealth and health, and no one can call him to account. What Job cannot see is the conversation in heaven (1:6–12) behind it.
- **"Beneath him bowed the helpers of Rahab" (v13).** Rahab here is not the woman of Jericho (a different Hebrew word). The lexicon gives the root meaning as "storm, arrogance," and says the name is used only for a mythical sea monster, as here and in 26:12, Psalm 89:10 and Isaiah 51:9, or as a nickname for Egypt (Ps 87:4; Isa 30:7). The KJV translates the phrase "the proud helpers." In the Babylonian creation story the sea monster Tiamat had allies who were defeated with her, and many scholars hear a deliberate echo. The Tyndale notes take Rahab and her helpers as a picture of hostile powers of chaos, even demonic powers. Job is not saying such a monster is real. He is borrowing well-known imagery to say that even the mightiest forces in the universe have bowed to God. So how could he stand?
- **"Though I am in the right, I cannot answer him; I must appeal for mercy to my accuser" (v15).** "In the right" is *tsadaq* again. Job is sure of his innocence, and the narrator agrees (1:1, 8; 2:3). But with God, being in the right is not enough to win a debate. The word translated "accuser" can also mean "judge" (Henry's KJV has "my judge"). Either way, Job says that all he can do is plead for mercy.
- **"I would not believe that he was listening to my voice" (v16).** Even if God agreed to hear the case, Job does not think he would really listen. This is pain talking, not settled belief. He keeps praying throughout the book.
- **"He crushes me with a tempest and multiplies my wounds without cause" (v17).** "Without cause" is *chinnam*, the same word Satan used in 1:9 ("Does Job fear God for no reason?") and God used in 2:3 ("to destroy him without reason"). Job does not know it, but he is describing exactly what happened. The Tyndale notes mention a possible alternate reading of "with a tempest": with a small change in vowels the Hebrew could mean "for a hair," that is, over the smallest thing, which would match "without cause."
- **"If it is a contest of strength... if it is a matter of justice" (v19).** With no time even to "get my breath" (v18), Job runs through his options. A fight? God is mighty. A trial? "Who can summon him?" There is no higher court to bring God before.
- **"Though I am in the right, my own mouth would condemn me" (v20).** This is the heart of Job's fear. Faced with God, he imagines himself so overwhelmed that his own words would trip him up and make him look guilty, "perverse," crooked. Some readers hear more here: before a perfectly holy God, any claim to be righteous in oneself becomes a kind of pride that condemns the one making it.
- **"I am blameless; I regard not myself; I loathe my life" (v21).** "Blameless" is *tam*, the word the narrator used of Job in 1:1. Job will not give up this claim, and he is right not to (27:5–6). The Tyndale notes trace how consistently he maintains his integrity through the whole debate. "I regard not myself" is literally "I do not know my soul." It probably means "I no longer care what happens to me." Job is holding on to his integrity and letting go of everything else.
- **"He destroys both the blameless and the wicked" (v22).** This is Job's boldest statement so far. "It is all one": the same disasters fall on good and bad alike. On one level this is simply true, and it demolishes the friends' theory that suffering always proves guilt. Jesus says something similar: God "sends rain on the just and on the unjust" (Matt 5:45), and those killed by a falling tower were not worse sinners than others (Luke 13:4–5). But Job pushes it further, as if God doesn't care about justice at all.
- **"He mocks at the calamity of the innocent" (v23).** "Disaster" here is literally "the scourge," a whip, perhaps a sudden epidemic or invasion. These are some of the hardest words in the book, not offered as true teaching about God but recorded faithfully as the cry of a man in agony. God later rebukes Job for darkening counsel "by words without knowledge" (38:2), yet he also says Job spoke of him "what is right" (42:7), more honestly than the friends with their tidy answers.
- **"The earth is given into the hand of the wicked... if it is not he, who then is it?" (v24).** Corrupt rulers prosper, and judges are blindfolded so that they cannot see justice. Job assumes God must be behind it all. The irony is sharp. "The wicked" is singular in Hebrew, "the wicked one," and the reader knows that God has in fact handed Job over, for a time, into the hand of the accuser (1:12; 2:6). Job asks "who then is it?" without knowing the answer is in chapters 1 and 2.
- **"My days are swifter than a runner" (vv25–26).** Job now turns from God's power to his own short life. He uses three pictures of speed: a runner (probably a royal courier carrying messages), "skiffs of reed," light boats made of papyrus that skimmed along the Nile, and "an eagle swooping on the prey." Earlier he said his days were "swifter than a weaver's shuttle" (7:6). Life is racing past, and "they see no good."
- **"If I say, 'I will forget my complaint'" (vv27–28).** Job tries the advice his friends would give: put on a brave face, cheer up. It doesn't work. Fear comes flooding back, because "I know you will not hold me innocent." Notice the switch to "you." The Tyndale notes point out that this is now a prayer. Job talks *about* God for most of the chapter, then suddenly turns and talks *to* him.
- **"If I wash myself with snow and cleanse my hands with lye" (vv30–31).** Snow water was thought to be especially pure, and "lye" (*bor*) was an alkali made from plant ashes, the strongest cleaning agent known. Washing the hands was a public sign of innocence (Ps 26:6; Deut 21:6–7; Pilate in Matt 27:24). Job says even the most thorough washing would be useless, because God would "plunge me into a pit," a ditch of filth or the grave (the word can mean both), until "my own clothes will abhor me." Dirty clothes are a biblical picture of guilt, and clean clothes of righteousness given by God (Zech 3:3–5; Isa 61:10; 64:6). Job means his innocence counts for nothing. But read in light of the whole Bible, the verse says something deeper: no human washing can make anyone clean before God (Jer 2:22). Only God can do that (Ps 51:7; Isa 1:18).
- **"For he is not a man, as I am, that I might answer him" (v32).** Here is the root of the problem. A trial needs two parties on a level footing. God is not a man, so there is no common ground on which Job can meet him.
- **"There is no arbiter between us, who might lay his hand on us both" (v33).** "Arbiter" is *mokiach*, a form of the verb *yakach*. The lexicon gives its senses as decide, judge, prove, reprove, and lists this verse under "decide between" two parties. The closest parallel is Jacob and Laban, who agree to let their kinsmen "decide between us two" (Gen 31:37). An arbiter in this sense is an umpire whom both sides accept. The Tyndale notes add that he does not judge God and man so much as bring them together. Laying a hand on both was a sign of authority over both parties, like an umpire placing a hand on each fighter's shoulder. Older English Bibles call him a "daysman," someone who sets a day for the hearing. The Hebrew says "there is no arbiter," but the ancient Greek translation reads it as a wish, "Would that there were a mediator," with the same word Paul uses of Christ in 1 Timothy 2:5. Either way, the longing is the same. Job will push this hope further: a witness in heaven who argues his case (16:19–21), and a Redeemer who lives (19:25).
- **"Let him take his rod away from me" (v34).** The rod (*shebet*) can be a rod of discipline or a ruler's scepter. Job asks two things: that God lift the crushing pressure of his suffering, and that God's overwhelming terror stop pressing on him. Then, he says, "I would speak without fear of him" (v35). He longs for an honest conversation with God without being flattened by fear. "I am not so in myself" probably means "I am not the guilty man my fear makes me feel like." Job's terror, not his conscience, is what silences him.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry outlines the chapter as Job asserting God's justice and power (vv1–13), applying it to himself (vv14–21), denying that outward condition shows a person's character (vv22–24), and then complaining in a more "querulous" tone (vv25–35). On v20 Henry writes, "my defence will be my offence," and on v22 he counters Job's gloom: "The wicked have the earth given them, but the righteous have heaven given them." Guzik separates two kinds of righteousness: the real but relative goodness Job had among people (1:1), and the legal standing before God that only God can give (Rom 5:19). He calls v2's question the most important in the world. Following Andersen, Guzik takes "contend" in v3 as a technical term for a lawsuit, and the lexicon supports that sense of *riv*. On v9, JFB reads Orion (*kesil*) as "the fool," identifies him with Nimrod, and calls the Pleiades "literally, 'the heap of stars.'" The lexicon is more cautious: *kesil* is spelled like the word for "fool," but the lexicon calls the link obscure, and it gives only "perhaps Pleiades" for *kimah*, with no "heap" meaning, so those details are best held loosely. JFB explains the reed boats as "papyrus skiffs, used on the Nile," and says the rod of v34 is "not here the symbol of punishment, but of power." JFB has no comment at all on vv18 and 31. All three commentators see Christ as the answer to v33. Henry writes, "Our Lord Jesus is the blessed days-man." JFB says he is the Mediator who stands on a level with both parties. Guzik lists what a true mediator needs: both sides must accept him, he must have authority to settle the case, he must be able to relate to both, and he must want a just outcome. Christ alone meets all four. On v24, Henry notes that the Hebrew can read "the wicked one," and Guzik stresses that Job could not see the heavenly scene of chapters 1–2. Where Guzik sees Job's wish to argue with God (v3) as the fault he finally repents of (42:1–6), Henry is a little gentler: he admits the querulous tone, yet says even the dark words of vv30–31 "are capable of a good construction," as a confession that God could charge anyone with secret sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job asked the question every human heart eventually asks: "How can a man be in the right before God?" (v2). He knew no amount of washing could make him clean (v30), and he knew God "is not a man, as I am" (v32). What he longed for was someone who could stand between, lay a hand on both God and man, and bring them together. The New Testament announces that this person has come: "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Tim 2:5). Jesus is fully God and fully man, so he can lay his hand on both. Job's question about being in the right finds its answer in the cross, where God shows himself "just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:26). Job saw God tread the waves and pass by unseen (vv8, 11). The disciples saw Jesus walk on the sea and heard him say, "Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid" (Mark 6:50). The terror Job wanted lifted (v34) is removed in Christ, so that believers "draw near to the throne of grace" with confidence (Heb 4:16).
