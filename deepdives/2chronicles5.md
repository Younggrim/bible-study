*Setting:* Autumn of about 959 BC, in Jerusalem. The temple building was finished in Solomon's eleventh year (1 Kgs 6:38), and chapters 3–4 described the house and its furnishings. Now comes the moment the whole account has been building toward: the ark of the covenant, which David had brought to Jerusalem and kept in a tent (1 Chr 15–16), is carried into the Most Holy Place, and God's glory fills the house. This chapter follows 1 Kings 8:1–11 almost word for word, with one major addition: the Levitical singers and 120 priestly trumpeters praising God at the very moment the cloud comes (vv11–13). That addition is typical of Chronicles, which takes great interest in the Levites and in music for worship (1 Chr 15–16; 25). For the Chronicler's readers, who had sung this same refrain when the second temple's foundation was laid (Ezra 3:10–11), it showed what true worship looks like and how God answers it.

#### The Temple's Furnishings (v1)
- **"Thus all the work that Solomon did for the house of the LORD was finished" (v1).** This verse closes the section that began at 4:1. The word "finished" (*shalam*) sounds like Solomon's own name, *Shlomo*, and his name is tied to peace (*shalom*). The man of peace completes the house of peace (1 Chr 22:9–10).
- **"The things that David his father had dedicated" (v1).** David had set aside silver, gold and bronze taken from defeated nations (1 Chr 18:7–11) and his own personal treasure (1 Chr 29:3–5). Some of it went into the building, and the rest was stored in the temple treasuries, which the Levites guarded (1 Chr 26:20–28). The plunder of Israel's enemies ended up serving Israel's God, as Egypt's gold once built the tabernacle (Exod 12:35–36; 35:22).

#### The Ark Brought to the Temple (vv2–14)
- **"Solomon assembled the elders of Israel and all the heads of the tribes" (v2).** The whole leadership of the nation was summoned to carry the ark a short distance, from the City of David, the old Zion ridge, up the hill to the temple just north of it.
- **"At the feast that is in the seventh month" (v3).** The Feast of Booths (Tabernacles), the autumn harvest festival, when Israel lived in shelters to remember the wilderness (Lev 23:33–43). The temple was finished in the eighth month (1 Kgs 6:38), so either Solomon waited eleven months for this feast or it took that long to finish the furnishings. Celebrating God's permanent house at the feast of temporary shelters was fitting. Kings names the month Ethanim (1 Kgs 8:2). Chronicles, written in the Persian era, simply calls it the seventh month.
- **"The Levites took up the ark" (v4).** Kings says "the priests took up the ark" (1 Kgs 8:3). The law assigned the carrying of the ark to the Levites of Kohath (Num 4:15; Deut 10:8), while only priests could enter the Most Holy Place. David learned that lesson the hard way, when Uzzah died after the ark was carried on a cart (1 Chr 13:9–10; 15:2, 13–15). Chronicles stresses that this time everything was done by the book. Priests were themselves Levites, so both accounts are true.
- **"The Levitical priests brought them up" (v5).** Literally "the priests, the Levites," a phrase Deuteronomy uses for the priests as members of Levi's tribe (Deut 17:9; 18:1). Kings has "the priests and the Levites." **"The tent of meeting"** is Moses' tabernacle, brought from Gibeon (1:3) and probably stored in the temple's rooms. The old was not discarded but gathered into the new.
- **"Sacrificing so many sheep and oxen that they could not be counted" (v6).** The procession moved to the sound of sacrifice, as when David first brought the ark to Jerusalem, sacrificing after every six steps (2 Sam 6:13).
- **"Underneath the wings of the cherubim" (vv7–8).** The ark, with its own small gold cherubim on the lid, was set under the great olive-wood cherubim whose wings stretched across the room (3:10–13). The picture is God enthroned above the cherubim, the ark his footstool (1 Chr 28:2; Ps 99:1, 5).
- **"The ends of the poles were seen from the Holy Place… And they are there to this day" (v9).** The law said the poles must never be removed from the ark (Exod 25:15). They were pulled forward so the ends pressed against the veil. **"To this day"** is copied from Kings (1 Kgs 8:8). By the Chronicler's own time the ark had disappeared. It was probably destroyed or taken when Babylon burned the temple in 586 BC, and the second temple's Most Holy Place stood empty. Jeremiah had foretold a day when people would no longer even miss it: "They shall no more say, 'The ark of the covenant of the LORD'… it shall not be made again" (Jer 3:16). Faithful copying of the old source shows the Chronicler's respect for it.
- **"There was nothing in the ark except the two tablets" (v10).** The Ten Commandments written on stone at Horeb (Sinai) (Deut 10:1–5). The jar of manna and Aaron's staff, once kept "before the testimony" (Exod 16:33–34; Num 17:10; Heb 9:4), are not mentioned. They may have been kept beside the ark or lost. At the center of the temple, under God's throne, lay God's covenant word.
- **"All the priests who were present had consecrated themselves, without regard to their divisions" (v11).** David had organized the priests into 24 divisions that took turns serving a week at a time (1 Chr 24). On this day every priest in Jerusalem came. "Consecrated themselves" means they had gone through the washings and offerings that made them ritually ready to serve (Exod 29; 2 Chr 29:34).
- **"Asaph, Heman, and Jeduthun, their sons and kinsmen" (v12).** These were the three chief musicians David appointed (1 Chr 16:4–7, 41–42; 25:1–7). Asaph's name stands over twelve psalms (Ps 50; 73–83), Heman's over Psalm 88, and Jeduthun's over Psalms 39, 62 and 77. **"Arrayed in fine linen"** (*buts*, a costly white linen) was the dress of those serving before God (1 Chr 15:27). They stood **"east of the altar,"** between the altar and the people, facing the house.
- **"120 priests who were trumpeters" (v12).** The silver trumpets (*chatsotserot*) were a priestly instrument from Moses' day (Num 10:1–10), used to call the assembly and to sound "before the LORD your God" at feasts and sacrifices. The Tyndale notes suggest the number came from five priests from each of the 24 divisions.
- **"To make themselves heard in unison" (v13).** Literally "as one," *keʾechad*. Many voices and instruments, one sound. Unity in praise is the setting in which God's glory comes. The early church was "together in one place" when the Spirit fell (Acts 2:1).
- **"For he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever" (v13).** This is Israel's great refrain of worship. It was sung when David brought up the ark (1 Chr 16:34, 41), it will be sung again when fire falls on the altar (7:3, 6), and it forms the chorus of Psalm 136, repeated twenty-six times. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. Above all, it was sung when the returned exiles laid the second temple's foundation: "they sang responsively, praising and giving thanks to the LORD, 'For he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever toward Israel'" (Ezra 3:11). The Chronicler's readers knew these words as their own.
- **"The house, the house of the LORD, was filled with a cloud" (v13).** The repetition, "the house, the house," marks the climax. Kings places the cloud right after the priests leave the Holy Place (1 Kgs 8:10). Chronicles places it at the moment the song is raised. The cloud recalls the pillar of cloud in the wilderness (Exod 13:21), the cloud on Sinai (Exod 24:15–18), and above all the day the tabernacle was finished: "the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle. And Moses was not able to enter" (Exod 40:34–35).
- **"The priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud" (v14).** No one could carry on with the service. God's presence was too weighty. "Glory" is *kavod*, from a root meaning "heavy" or "weighty." The glory of the LORD is the felt weight of his presence. The temple was built for this, and when it came, all human activity stopped.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God took possession of the temple; he filled it with a cloud," and "Would we have God dwell in our hearts, we must leave room for him; every thing else must give way." JFB explains that the priests' weekly courses were for ordinary duty, while "on solemn occasions" they "attended in a body." He notes that by the time of writing "there is no trace of either ark or staves" after the exile. Guzik stresses that the glory came in a setting of unified praise, quoting Spurgeon that we cannot expect God's presence among people who do not praise him together. Guzik also passes on Spurgeon's remark that where God is, man is forgotten, and the scholar Martin Selman's point that the temple was for God's glory, not anyone else's. Guzik notes, as the Tyndale notes do, that the manna and Aaron's rod were no longer in the ark.

#### Pointing to Christ
The ark was carried in, the people sang "for he is good," and God's glory filled the house so fully that the priests could not stand. John writes of a greater filling: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). The ark that vanished at the exile pointed to a presence no army could carry off. Christ is that presence, and he promised to be with his people wherever two or three gather in his name (Matt 18:20). At Pentecost, when the disciples were together in one place, the Spirit filled not a building but a people (Acts 2:1–4; 1 Cor 3:16).
