*Setting:* Israel is camped at Gilgal on the west bank of the Jordan, a few miles from Jericho, in the days just after the crossing (4:19). The kings of Canaan are terrified, and from a military point of view this is the moment to strike. Instead, God has Israel stop. Before a single battle is fought, the nation renews the two great signs of its covenant with God, circumcision and the Passover, which had both lapsed in the wilderness. The manna stops, and Joshua meets the commander of the LORD's army. The chapter is about who Israel is, and whose war this is, before it is about winning.

#### The New Generation Circumcised (vv1–9)
- **"Their hearts melted" (v1).** The same phrase Rahab used (2:11). "The kings of the Amorites… to the west" were the hill-country rulers. "The kings of the Canaanites who were by the sea" were the coastal city-states. The flood had been their natural defense, and it had failed. Now no one has the nerve to attack Israel, which is the only reason what follows was survivable.
- **"Make flint knives and circumcise the sons of Israel a second time" (v2).** Circumcision was the sign of God's covenant with Abraham and his descendants (Gen 17:9–14), and no uncircumcised male could eat the Passover (Exod 12:48). "A second time" does not mean the same men were circumcised twice. It means the nation, as a whole, received the sign again. Flint knives were used long after metal tools were common, perhaps out of tradition or because a fresh-struck flint edge is very sharp and clean. Zipporah used one to circumcise Moses' son (Exod 4:25).
- **"Gibeath-haaraloth" (v3).** It means "Hill of the Foreskins." Biblical place names are often blunt about what happened there.
- **"This is the reason" (vv4–7).** The writer explains at length. The generation that left Egypt had been circumcised. Their sons born in the wilderness had not. Why the rite was neglected is not said directly. Some think the forty years under judgment (Num 14:33–34) were a time when the covenant was in suspense for that rebellious generation. Others suggest the hardships of constant travel made it impractical. The writer's emphasis is on the cause of the forty years: "because they did not obey the voice of the LORD." The men who refused to enter died outside; their children, "whom he raised up in their place," now receive the covenant sign at the border.
- **"A land flowing with milk and honey" (v6).** The phrase God used at the burning bush (Exod 3:8). Milk from flocks and herds, and honey, probably wild bees' honey as well as syrup from dates or figs: the picture is a land rich in pasture and fruit, a sharp contrast with the wilderness.
- **Courage of a different kind.** Circumcising the fighting men of a whole army in enemy territory left them helpless for days. In Genesis 34:24–25, Jacob's sons used exactly this weakness to slaughter the men of Shechem. Israel was obeying God at the cost of its own military strength, trusting the Lord to guard the camp while it could not guard itself.
- **"They remained in their places in the camp until they were healed" (v8).** "Healed" literally means "until they lived" or "revived." Recovery from adult circumcision takes several days.
- **"Today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from you" (v9).** "Reproach" (*cherpah*) is shame or disgrace, often the taunt of an enemy. BDB lists uncircumcision as one kind of reproach (Gen 34:14). What exactly was "the reproach of Egypt"? There are three main readings, and they overlap. It may be Egypt's taunt that Israel's God had brought them out only to kill them in the desert, a fear Moses raised more than once (Exod 32:12; Num 14:13–16; Deut 9:28). It may be the shame of having been slaves. Or it may be the disgrace of the wilderness years, when a whole generation lived under God's judgment and outside the covenant sign. On any reading, the point is the same: that chapter of Israel's story is closed.
- **"Gilgal" (v9).** The name sounds like *galal*, "to roll." Place names in the Bible are often explained by a wordplay like this rather than a formal derivation. The name also suggests a circle (*gilgal*), perhaps the ring of twelve stones (4:20).
- **Commentators:** JFB gives what it calls the most common view of the lapse: "a temporary suspension of the covenant with the unbelieving race," and reads the renewal as "a practical announcement of the restoration of the covenant." It notes the kings' hearts melted, "unhappily not into faith and penitent submission," as Rahab's had. JFB takes the reproach as Egypt's taunt that Israel had been rejected by God. Guzik reads it as the identity of slaves, which the new generation leaves behind through obedience. He also stresses the faith it took to be disabled within sight of Jericho, and compares it with Shechem in Genesis 34. The Tyndale notes also treat the circumcision as a covenant renewal, God preparing his people before the battle.

#### First Passover in Canaan (vv10–12)
- **"They kept the Passover on the fourteenth day of the month" (v10).** This was the date set in Exodus 12:6. Numbers records a Passover kept at Sinai a year after the Exodus (Num 9:1–5), and no other is recorded in the wilderness. For most of the people this was the first Passover of their lives. Forty years earlier the Passover marked the night God brought Israel *out* of Egypt. Now it marks the week he brought them *in*.
- **"The day after the Passover… they ate of the produce of the land" (v11).** "Unleavened cakes and parched grain" fits the Feast of Unleavened Bread, which began the next day (Lev 23:5–6). Parched grain was freshly harvested barley roasted over a fire, a simple harvest food (Ruth 2:14). "On that very day" is a solemn phrase used of key dates (Exod 12:17, 41).
- **"The produce of the land" (v11).** Jericho's fields had been harvested or were ready for harvest, and the people of the region had fled into the walled city. Israel was eating crops it had not planted. Moses had promised exactly this: "cities that you did not build… vineyards and olive trees that you did not plant" (Deut 6:10–11).
- **"And the manna ceased" (v12).** For forty years, every morning except the Sabbath, God had given bread from heaven (Exod 16:35). It stopped the day after Israel ate the food of Canaan, not a day early and not a day late. Manna was God's provision for the road. Now God would provide through the land, by sowing and reaping. Both were his gifts, but the manner changed.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls this Passover a "solemn act of religious dedication," and says the manna's timely cessation is "a striking proof of its miraculous origin." Guzik makes a similar point: God changed the way he provided, not his faithfulness, and the people now had to trust him through their own work. The Tyndale notes add that for the older people this was their first Passover since childhood.

#### The Commander of the Lord's Army (vv13–15)
- **"When Joshua was by Jericho" (v13).** Joshua is probably out looking over the city's defenses alone, wondering how to take a walled town with an army that had no siege machines.
- **"A man was standing before him with his drawn sword in his hand" (v13).** A drawn sword signals readiness for war. Balaam met the angel of the LORD with a drawn sword (Num 22:23, 31), and David saw the same at Jerusalem (1 Chr 21:16). Joshua walks straight up to the stranger. That is courage.
- **"Are you for us, or for our adversaries?" (v13).** The natural question for a soldier: friend or enemy?
- **"No" (v14).** He refuses to be placed on either side. He is not there to join Israel's army. Israel's army is there to join his. A few ancient versions read "to him" instead of "No" (in Hebrew the two words sound alike and differ by one letter), but the standard Hebrew text's "No" is well supported and makes good sense.
- **"I am the commander of the army of the LORD" (v14).** "Army" (*tsaba'*) is the word behind the title "LORD of hosts." The LORD's army includes the angels (1 Kings 22:19; Ps 103:20–21), and Israel's tribes could also be called "the hosts of the LORD" (Exod 12:41). The commander leads both. "Now I have come" sounds unfinished, as if Joshua interrupts him by falling down.
- **"Joshua fell on his face to the earth and worshiped" (v14).** The verb can mean simply to bow low before a superior, as people bowed to kings. But the next verse settles that this is more than courtesy.
- **"Take off your sandals from your feet, for the place where you are standing is holy" (v15).** These are almost the very words spoken to Moses at the burning bush (Exod 3:5). There, the speaker was "the angel of the LORD," who then speaks as God himself (Exod 3:2–6). Joshua's commission began with God saying, "Just as I was with Moses, so I will be with you" (1:5). Now he receives his own burning-bush moment.
- **Who is he?** Christians have read this figure in three ways. Many, beginning with early writers such as Justin Martyr, have seen the Son of God before his incarnation, as with "the angel of the LORD" elsewhere. The main reason is that he accepts worship and makes the ground holy, while angels in Scripture refuse worship (Rev 19:10; 22:8–9). Others see a high angel, perhaps Michael, who is called a "prince" of God's people (Dan 10:13, 21; 12:1), noting that he calls himself the LORD's commander, not the LORD. Still others take him as the angel of the LORD and leave his exact identity open. The Tyndale notes lay out all three views. The text itself identifies him in the next verse, where it is "the LORD" who speaks to Joshua about Jericho (6:2), and most readers take that as the commander continuing his message.
- **"And Joshua did so" (v15).** The chapter ends abruptly, with no more words, only obedience. The battle plan follows in chapter 6.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We have reason to think that this man was the Son of God, the eternal Word," and "those cannot but be victorious that have such a captain." On the sword, "If the sovereign draw the sword, this proclaims war, and authorizes the subject to do so too." JFB calls him "the angel of the covenant," says this was "not a mere vision, but an actual appearance," and argues that his acceptance of worship shows who he truly is. Guzik agrees, and his often-repeated point about "No" is that the real question is not whether God is on our side, but whether we are on his.

#### Pointing to Christ
Before Israel fought for the land, God marked his people again with the covenant sign and gathered them at the Passover lamb. Paul calls Christ "our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7) and speaks of a "circumcision made without hands" in him, the cutting away of the old self (Col 2:11). Jesus is also the true bread from heaven, of which the manna was only a sign (John 6:31–35). And many readers see the commander with the drawn sword as the Son himself, who will not be recruited to anyone's side but calls all his people to stand on his. At the end of the Bible he rides out again at the head of heaven's armies, "Faithful and True," judging and making war in righteousness (Rev 19:11–16).
