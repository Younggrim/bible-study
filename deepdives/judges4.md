*Setting:* Ehud's victory brought eighty years of rest (3:30), and Shamgar held off the Philistines (3:31). Now the cycle turns again, and this time the threat comes from the north and from inside the land itself: a Canaanite king in Hazor, the great city Joshua had burned a century or more earlier (Josh 11:10–13). The story is told twice: in prose here, and in poetry in chapter 5, an old victory song that fills in details. It is a story full of surprises. The judge is a woman, the general will not go without her, and the enemy commander dies at the hands of a woman who was not even an Israelite.

#### Deborah and Barak (vv1–24)
- **"Again did what was evil… after Ehud died" (v1).** The pattern of 2:19. JFB says Ehud's death "left his infatuated countrymen without the restraint of religion."
- **"Jabin king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor" (v2).** Joshua killed a Jabin of Hazor and burned the city (Josh 11:1–11). This is a later king with the same name, which was probably a dynastic name, like "Pharaoh." Letters from the ancient city of Mari, centuries earlier, name a king of Hazor "Ibni-Addu," and many scholars think "Jabin" is the same name. Hazor, north of the Sea of Galilee, was the largest city in Canaan, covering some 200 acres, and controlled the main trade routes. Archaeologists have found that it was destroyed by fire in the 13th century BC, though they debate which destruction was Joshua's and how quickly it was rebuilt.
- **"Sisera, who lived in Harosheth-hagoyim" (v2).** Sisera's name does not sound Semitic. Many scholars think he came from the Sea Peoples, the same wave that brought the Philistines. Harosheth-hagoyim means something like "the woodland (or farmland) of the nations," probably in the western Jezreel Valley. Its exact site is unknown.
- **"900 chariots of iron… oppressed the people of Israel cruelly for twenty years" (v3).** Chariots were the most advanced weapons of the day. On the flat ground of the Jezreel Valley they were unstoppable against foot soldiers, and the Canaanites used them to keep Israel's tribes cut off and cowed. Deborah's song says that in those days "the highways were abandoned" (5:6–7). Twenty years is the longest oppression yet.
- **"Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth" (v4).** Deborah means "bee." Lappidoth means "torches," and some have read the phrase as "a woman of torches," a fiery woman, though "wife of Lappidoth" is the natural reading. She is the only judge in this book also called a prophet, and other women prophets in the Old Testament include Miriam (Exod 15:20), Huldah (2 Kings 22:14) and Isaiah's wife (Isa 8:3). JFB calls her "instructed in divine knowledge by the Spirit and accustomed to interpret His will."
- **"Was judging Israel at that time" (v4).** Deborah is the only judge in the book shown actually deciding cases. "The people of Israel came up to her for judgment" (v5). She held court "under the palm of Deborah" in the hill country of Ephraim, between Ramah and Bethel. JFB notes "it is common still in the East to administer justice in the open air." Her work looks ahead to Samuel, who later traveled a circuit around Bethel and Ramah (1 Sam 7:15–17).
- **A woman leading Israel.** Readers have long asked what Deborah's leadership means. Some see it as a sign that men had failed to lead, pointing to Barak's hesitation and the prophecy of v9. Others note that the text simply presents her as raised up by God, with no hint of disapproval. Guzik holds that God's use of Deborah shows women can be greatly used and given prophetic gifts, while he still reads the New Testament as giving men the leadership of church and home. Readers who hold other views also take Deborah as a key example. The text itself is clear that she was a prophet and judge, and that God spoke through her.
- **"Barak the son of Abinoam from Kedesh-naphtali" (v6).** Barak means "lightning." He came from Naphtali in the far north, one of the tribes most oppressed by Hazor. Deborah does not suggest a plan. She delivers a command already given by God: "Has not the LORD, the God of Israel, commanded you…?"
- **"Gather your men at Mount Tabor" (v6).** Mount Tabor rises alone from the north side of the Jezreel Valley, a rounded hill about 1,800 feet high, wooded and steep. Chariots could not climb it. "I will draw out Sisera… to meet you by the river Kishon" (v7). The Kishon drains the Jezreel Valley north-west toward the sea near Mount Carmel. It is usually a small stream, but in a heavy rain it floods.
- **"If you will go with me, I will go" (v8).** Barak hesitates, like Moses (Exod 4:13) and Gideon after him (6:15). Readers differ on why. Some see weak faith. JFB says his request "was not altogether the result of weakness": he wanted God's prophet, and so God's word and presence, with the army. Hebrews 11:32 still lists Barak among the heroes of faith.
- **"The LORD will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman" (v9).** Barak will win, but the glory will go to a woman. Everyone, including Barak, would assume she meant Deborah. JFB: "This was a prediction which Barak could not understand at the time."
- **"Heber the Kenite had separated from the Kenites" (v11).** An aside that matters later. The Kenites, related to Moses by marriage, had settled in the far south of Judah (1:16). Heber's clan moved north and camped near Kedesh. "Hobab the father-in-law of Moses" raises a known question: Exodus calls Moses' father-in-law Jethro or Reuel (Exod 2:18; 3:1), and Numbers 10:29 calls Hobab Reuel's son. The Hebrew word can also mean "brother-in-law," and most think it means that here. JFB says the KJV's "plain of Zaanaim" should be "the oaks of the wanderers."
- **"Sisera called out all his chariots" (vv12–13).** Sisera takes the bait. He brings his chariots to the valley floor along the Kishon, which is exactly where God had said he would "draw him out."
- **"Up! For this is the day… Does not the LORD go out before you?" (v14).** Deborah gives the word. The picture is of God marching ahead of his army as a king (Deut 20:4; 2 Sam 5:24). Barak charges down Tabor with ten thousand men, giving up the safety of the hill. JFB notes that "they relinquished their advantageous position on the hill" because they trusted the promise.
- **"The LORD routed Sisera and all his chariots" (v15).** "Routed" (*hamam*) means to throw into panic and confusion. It is the word used of God at the Red Sea (Exod 14:24) and at Gibeon (Josh 10:10). The prose does not say how. The song does: "the stars fought from heaven," and "the torrent Kishon swept them away" (5:20–21). A sudden storm flooded the valley, and the heavy chariots stuck in the mud. JFB: "the disorder was produced by a supernatural panic."
- **"Sisera got down from his chariot and fled away on foot" (v15).** The man of 900 chariots is reduced to running. "Not a man was left" (v16).
- **"For there was peace between Jabin… and the house of Heber" (v17).** Heber had a treaty with Hazor. Sisera runs to what he thinks is allied territory.
- **Jael's welcome (vv18–20).** Jael means "mountain goat." "Turn aside, my lord; turn aside to me; do not be afraid." She covers him with a rug and, when he asks for water, gives him milk, more than he asked for. The song calls it "curds in a noble's bowl" (5:25), something like yogurt, and many think it made him drowsy. In that culture, guest-friendship was sacred, and a man entering a woman's tent was unthinkable, so Sisera's pursuers would not look there. JFB: "the privacy of the harem, even in a tent, cannot be intruded on without express permission."
- **"Took a tent peg, and took a hammer in her hand" (v21).** Among nomads, women pitched the tents, so Jael knew exactly how to use a mallet and a peg. "He was lying fast asleep from weariness." The general who came with iron chariots dies by a wooden peg in a woman's hand.
- **"Come, and I will show you the man whom you are seeking" (v22).** Barak arrives too late. Deborah's prophecy comes true in a way no one expected. The "woman" was Jael.
- **Was Jael right?** Faithful readers disagree. Deborah's song calls her "most blessed of women" (5:24), and she sided with Israel against its oppressor at great personal risk, breaking with her husband's treaty. Others note that she broke the sacred duty of hospitality and lied by implication, and that the text praises her courage without approving every act. JFB is blunt: the killing "was murder," and Deborah's prophecy reflected "divine foreknowledge only," not approval. Henry thinks Jael at first "really intended kindness to Sisera," and was then moved by God to see him as God's enemy. The narrator doesn't settle it. He reports that God used her.
- **"So on that day God subdued Jabin… until they destroyed Jabin" (vv23–24).** The battle broke Jabin's power, and Israel kept pressing until the kingdom fell. The credit goes to God: "God subdued." The song ends, "the land had rest for forty years" (5:31).
- **Commentators:** Henry says of Deborah, "she would not send him where she would not go herself," and says Barak "values the satisfaction of his mind, and the good success of his enterprise, more than mere honour." On Jael he says "all our connexions with God's enemies must be broken off, if we would have the Lord for our God." Guzik, following Meyer and Trapp, reads Barak's faith as real but partly borrowed, which cost him the honor of the victory. He calls Jael's act a betrayal of hospitality and argues that God can use even treacherous acts to accomplish his purposes without excusing them, as with Judas. JFB is the harshest of the three on Jael. All three agree that the victory was God's, won by storm and panic rather than by Israel's strength.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jabin's 900 chariots of iron looked unbeatable, and God brought them down with rain, mud and a tent peg. This is the God who "has chosen what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Cor 1:27). The story also has a strange echo of the very first promise of the gospel, when God told the serpent that the woman's offspring "shall bruise your head" (Gen 3:15). The enemy of God's people falls with a crushed head at a woman's hand. That promise found its fulfilment in Christ, born of a woman (Gal 4:4), who through his death and resurrection crushed the head of the true enemy, and God "will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Rom 16:20).
