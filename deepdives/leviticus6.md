*Setting:* God is still speaking to Moses at Sinai. Chapters 1:1–6:7 have explained the five main offerings from the worshiper's side: what to bring and why. The first seven verses here finish the guilt offering, applying it to wrongs done to a neighbor. Then, at 6:8, the audience changes. "Command Aaron and his sons" (v9) opens a section for the priests (6:8–7:38), going back over the same offerings from the altar's side: how to keep the fire, who eats what and where. Hebrew Bibles start chapter 6 at the ESV's 6:8, so their verse numbers run seven behind here.

#### Laws for Guilt Offerings (vv1–7)
- **"A breach of faith against the LORD by deceiving his neighbor" (v2).** The wrong is against a neighbor, but the law calls it unfaithfulness (*ma'al*) against the LORD. The Tyndale notes put it plainly: for God's people there is no such thing as a purely "secular" sin. David, after wronging Uriah, still prays, "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (Ps 51:4).
- **The cases (vv2–3).** All involve property and dishonesty: lying about a **deposit** left in one's keeping (there were no banks, so valuables were left with neighbors, Exod 22:7–13), a **security** or pledge, **robbery**, **oppression** (extortion, using power to cheat), and keeping **something lost** while lying about it. Each case ends with **"swearing falsely,"** lying under oath in God's name. That turned a wrong against a neighbor into a direct offense against God.
- **"He shall restore it in full and shall add a fifth… on the day he realizes his guilt" (v5).** First the victim is repaid, plus 20 percent, and then the guilty person comes to the altar (v6). The order matters. Jesus says the same: leave your gift at the altar, "first be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift" (Matt 5:23–24). The Tyndale notes point out that Exodus sets much heavier penalties, double or more, for a thief who was caught (Exod 22:1–9). The lower 20 percent rewarded the one whose conscience made him come forward on his own.
- **"He shall be forgiven for any of the things that one may do" (v7).** Unlike the cases in 5:14–19, these are deliberate sins: lying, theft, perjury. Yet confession, restitution and sacrifice bring forgiveness. Leviticus is more generous than a quick reading of "unintentional" sins might suggest.
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that the restitution and the offering happened on the same day, showing that a person cannot be right with God while refusing to make things right with people. He ties v7 to 1 John 1:9. Henry: "the injury reflects upon that God who has made the command of loving our neighbour next to that of loving himself." JFB notes that even restitution plus a fifth was not enough without the ram.

#### The Priests and the Offerings (vv8–30)
- **"This is the law of the burnt offering" (v9).** "Law" is *torah*, which means "instruction." Each offering now gets its own *torah* for the priests (6:9, 14, 25; 7:1, 11). The burnt offering was to stay **"on the hearth on the altar all night until the morning."** The evening lamb of the daily offering (Exod 29:38–42) burned slowly through the night.
- **"His linen garment… his linen undergarment" (v10).** Even cleaning out ashes was holy work, done in priestly dress. Then the priest **"shall take off his garments and put on other garments"** (v11) before carrying the ashes **"outside the camp to a clean place."** The holy clothes stayed at the sanctuary. The ashes were still the remains of what had been given to God and were treated with respect.
- **"Fire shall be kept burning on the altar continually; it shall not go out" (v13).** The command is given three times (vv9, 12, 13). "Continually" is *tamid*, the word for the "regular" daily offering. In chapter 9 fire comes "out from before the LORD" to consume the first offerings (9:24), and many interpreters, Jewish and Christian, think this fire was then kept alive. The Tyndale notes suggest the warning looks ahead to Nadab and Abihu, who offered "unauthorized fire" from somewhere else (10:1). Spiritually, the never-dying fire pictures worship that never stops.
- **"This is the law of the grain offering" (vv14–18).** The priests burned a handful and ate the rest (compare 2:2–3), **"unleavened in a holy place… in the court of the tent of meeting"** (v16). "A holy place" here means the sanctuary courtyard, not the Holy Place inside the tent. **"I have given it as their portion of my food offerings"** (v17). The priests received no land (Num 18:20), and God fed them from his own table.
- **"Whatever touches them shall become holy" (v18; also v27).** This is debated. Some read it as "must be holy," meaning only those already consecrated could touch these things. Others think holiness passed by touch, so whatever touched them now belonged to the sanctuary. Haggai 2:11–13 later teaches that uncleanness spreads by touch more readily than holiness does. Either way, the point is that these things were not to be handled casually.
- **The priest's own grain offering (vv19–23).** **"On the day when he is anointed"** may mean the ordination day, or it may mean "from the day," starting a regular daily offering. The words **"regular grain offering, half of it in the morning and half in the evening"** (v20) suggest the second, and Jewish tradition held that the high priest offered it every day. Unlike the people's grain offering, a priest's was **"wholly burned. It shall not be eaten"** (v23). A priest could not offer a gift for himself and then eat it himself.
- **"This is the law of the sin offering" (vv24–30).** The sin offering was killed in the same place as the burnt offering, on the north side (1:11). For an ordinary person's sin, **"the priest who offers it for sin shall eat it"** (v26), again in the courtyard. Eating it was part of his priestly duty, not just his wages. Leviticus 10:17 says the priests were given it "to bear the iniquity of the congregation."
- **Splashed garments and broken pots (vv27–28).** Blood on a garment had to be washed out at the sanctuary. A clay pot used for boiling the meat had to be **broken**, since porous clay soaked up what was cooked in it and could not be fully cleaned. A **bronze** pot could be scoured. Holy things could not afterward be mixed with ordinary use.
- **"No sin offering shall be eaten from which any blood is brought into the tent of meeting" (v30).** This links back to 4:1–21. When the blood went inside for the high priest or the whole nation, the body was burned outside the camp, not eaten. The priests could not eat an offering made for their own sin.
- **Commentators:** All three treat the perpetual fire as a picture of continual devotion. Henry: "The priest must take care of the fire upon the altar," which he applies to keeping faith, love and prayer alive. Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's question whether that fire still burns on the altar of the heart, and he adds that the never-ending fire also showed that the work of sacrifice was never finished until Christ. JFB says the grain offering was split morning and evening, and that the clay pots had become too sacred for ordinary use. Henry on vv27–28: the blood of Christ "thoroughly cleanses from all sin." Henry also uses vv16–18 to say God means those who serve him to be provided for (1 Cor 9:13–14).

#### Pointing to Christ
The fire never went out because the sacrifices never ended. Morning and evening, year after year, the priests stood "daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down" (Heb 10:11–12). The sin offering whose blood went inside was not eaten but burned outside the camp, which Hebrews applies to Jesus, who "suffered outside the gate" (Heb 13:11–12). And the opening verses keep the gospel honest: forgiveness from God and making things right with a neighbor go together, as Zacchaeus showed (Luke 19:8–9).
