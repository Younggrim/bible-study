*Setting:* Jerusalem, autumn of 445 BC. The wall has just been finished in fifty-two days, and the enemies who mocked it have been humbled (6:15–16), though Tobiah's letters are still arriving. Nehemiah, the Persian-appointed governor, now turns from building to keeping. A wall with no guards and a city with no people are not much use, so he sets watchmen at the gates and then plans to fill the empty city. To do that he needs to know who belongs to Israel and where each family lives. He finds the old register of the first returnees, the people who came back with Zerubbabel nearly a century earlier, and copies it into his memoir. That list is almost the same as Ezra 2, with small differences in names and numbers. The ESV's first section, "The Wall Is Finished," began at 6:15 and runs through 7:4. As a list, most of this chapter gets a shorter treatment than a narrative chapter, and the plan to repopulate the city is not carried out until chapter 11.

#### The Wall Is Finished (vv1–4)
- **"I had set up the doors" (v1).** In 6:1 the doors were the one thing still missing. Now they hang in place, and the wall is complete in every part.
- **"The gatekeepers, the singers, and the Levites had been appointed" (v1).** Gatekeepers normally guarded the temple gates, and the singers and Levites served in worship. Nehemiah seems to have put temple staff on the city gates too, at least for now, because they were organized, trusted and available. The Tyndale notes point out that Levites regularly guarded the temple area. Worship and watchfulness stand side by side at the start of the city's new life.
- **"My brother Hanani and Hananiah the governor of the castle" (v2).** Hanani is the brother who first brought Nehemiah the news of Jerusalem's broken walls (1:2). Hananiah commanded the *birah*, the fortress beside the temple, the same "fortress of the temple" whose gates Nehemiah had asked the king to supply with timber (2:8). The word is a late one, possibly borrowed from Akkadian, and is also used of the citadel at Susa where Nehemiah once served (1:1). Nehemiah was planning to return to the Persian court (13:6), so he needed trustworthy men in charge.
- **"For he was a more faithful and God-fearing man than many" (v2).** The reason given is character, not skill or family. "Faithful" means reliable, a man whose word could be trusted. The fear of God was the same quality that set Nehemiah apart from earlier governors who abused the people (5:15). A leader who fears God can be left alone with power.
- **"Let not the gates of Jerusalem be opened until the sun is hot" (v3).** Gates in the ancient Near East usually opened at dawn. Nehemiah wanted them shut until the day was well under way and people were awake and about. Some read the Hebrew differently, as an order to keep the gates closed *during* the heat of midday, when guards and townspeople rested, and the Tyndale notes take it that way. Either way the point is the same: no gate stands open at the hour when no one is watching.
- **"Some at their guard posts and some in front of their own homes" (v3).** Ordinary residents become the guard. A man watching the wall in front of his own house has every reason to stay awake. This echoes the building in chapter 3, where families repaired the section opposite their houses (3:10, 23, 28–30).
- **"The city was wide and large, but the people within it were few, and no houses had been rebuilt" (v4).** The wall enclosed far more ground than the population could fill. "No houses" is probably a way of saying very few, since some people clearly lived there (3:20–30). Most families had settled on their farms in the villages around Judah. A walled city with empty streets was still weak.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls worship a city's real defense: "God's worship is the defence of a place, and his ministers, when they mind their duty, are watchmen on the walls." On Hananiah he writes, "Those that fear God must evidence it by their being faithful to all men." He adds that good governors, "when and where they cannot act themselves, must be very careful whom they depute," and that watchmen wake in vain unless the Lord keeps the city (Ps 127:1). JFB calls Hananiah "the ruler of the palace" and reads the late opening of the gates as a precaution against the enemies' schemes. He explains the large, empty city by houses standing apart with gardens between them. Guzik notes that Nehemiah did not hold on to power for himself, and that a finished victory still has to be guarded.

#### Lists of Returned Exiles (vv5–65)
- **"Then my God put it into my heart" (v5).** Nehemiah used the same words when he first planned the rebuilding: "what my God had put into my heart to do for Jerusalem" (2:12). Taking a census was a sensible administrative step, yet Nehemiah credits God with the idea. The plan was to move some families into the city, which happens in 11:1–2, when one family in ten is chosen by lot.
- **"The book of the genealogy of those who came up at the first" (v5).** Nehemiah does not draw up a new list here. He finds the record of the first return, which began around 538 BC under Cyrus (Ezra 1–2), and copies it. Verses 6–73a match Ezra 2:1–70 closely. The repetition is deliberate. A family's place in this list proved it belonged to the restored people and had a claim to its ancestral town.
- **"They came with Zerubbabel, Jeshua, Nehemiah…" (v7).** Zerubbabel, heir of David's royal line, and Jeshua the high priest led the first return. The Nehemiah and Mordecai in this verse are earlier men, not the governor writing the book or Esther's cousin. Here twelve leaders are named, where Ezra 2:2 has eleven. Nahamani is missing from Ezra's copy, and a few other names are spelled differently (Azariah for Seraiah, Raamiah for Reelaiah, Nehum for Rehum). Twelve leaders probably stand for the twelve tribes: the returnees saw themselves as all Israel, not just the tribe of Judah.
- **Families, then towns (vv8–38).** The list first counts clans named after an ancestor (vv8–25), then people known by their hometown (vv26–38). Most of the towns, such as Bethlehem, Anathoth, Ramah, Michmash and Bethel, lie within a day's walk of Jerusalem, chiefly to the north in the old territory of Benjamin.
- **Ezra and Nehemiah differ.** About twenty numbers in this list differ from Ezra 2. The sons of Arah are 652 here and 775 in Ezra (v10; Ezra 2:5). Adonikam has 667 here and 666 there (v18; Ezra 2:13). Bethel and Ai have 123 here and 223 there (v32; Ezra 2:28). Senaah has 3,930 here and 3,630 there (v38; Ezra 2:35). Several explanations are offered. Hebrew numbers were easy to miscopy. One list may record those who signed up to go and the other those who actually arrived. One may be an updated copy. None of the differences touches any teaching of Scripture.
- **The priests (vv39–42).** Four priestly families returned, with 4,289 men in all, about a tenth of the whole company. David had organized twenty-four priestly divisions (1 Chr 24), but only four came home. JFB notes that these four were later divided again into twenty-four courses that kept the old names. The division of Abijah, to which Zechariah the father of John the Baptist belonged (Luke 1:5), was one of the names revived.
- **The Levites, singers and gatekeepers (vv43–45).** Only 74 Levites returned, against more than 4,000 priests. Levites did the heavier, humbler work of the temple, and few were willing to leave Babylon for it. Ezra later had the same trouble finding Levites for his journey (Ezra 8:15–20). The singers here number 148, where Ezra 2:41 has 128.
- **"The temple servants" (vv46–56).** The Hebrew is *Nethinim*, "given ones," people given to the temple to help the Levites with menial tasks. Some of their names suggest foreign origins: the Meunim recall the Meunites, an Arabian people (2 Chr 26:7), and one family is "the sons of Sisera" (v55), the name of the Canaanite general Deborah's army defeated (Judg 4). Many may have descended from war captives or from the Gibeonites, whom Joshua made "cutters of wood and drawers of water for the house of my God" (Josh 9:23). Outsiders who once served Israel's God by compulsion now return from exile by choice to serve him.
- **"The sons of Solomon's servants" (vv57–60).** Another group of temple workers, probably descended from the forced labor Solomon used (1 Kgs 9:20–21). With the temple servants they total 392, exactly the figure in Ezra 2:58.
- **"They could not prove their fathers' houses" (vv61–62).** Some families came from Babylonian towns such as Tel-melah and Tel-harsha but had lost their family records. They were not turned away. They returned with everyone else, but their claim to Israelite descent could not be confirmed. The 642 here is 652 in Ezra 2:60.
- **"The sons of Barzillai… was called by their name" (v63).** Barzillai the Gileadite was the old man who fed David during Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 17:27–29; 19:31–39). A priest had married into that famous family and taken its name, and so his descendants could no longer prove their priestly line.
- **"Excluded from the priesthood as unclean" (v64).** Only descendants of Aaron could serve as priests (Num 3:10; 16:40). These families were not called sinners. They simply could not prove their right to serve at the altar. The concern was not snobbery. It was obedience to God's rules for who could come near.
- **"Until a priest with Urim and Thummim should arise" (v65).** Urim and Thummim were objects kept in the high priest's breastpiece and used to learn God's will in matters people could not settle (Exod 28:30; Num 27:21). There is no clear record of them being used after the early monarchy, and there is no record they were ever used again after the exile. "The governor" (*Tirshatha*, a Persian title, perhaps "His Excellency") is part of the old record, so it originally referred to Zerubbabel or Sheshbazzar, not Nehemiah. The ruling was left open, a humble admission that some questions must wait for God to answer.
- **Commentators:** Henry explains the census by saying a city's strength lies in its people more than its walls: "the more the stronger, the more the merrier." On "my God put it into my heart" he writes, "Whatever good motion is in our minds, either prudent or pious, we must acknowledge it to come from God." On the repeated names he says God "knows those that are his, knows them all, knows them by name." He puts the number differences down to copyists or different copies, and concludes, "Blessed be God that our faith and hope are not built upon the niceties of names and numbers." JFB says the register "differs in some few particulars" from Ezra's because the two were compiled at different times and places, some people died, and some went by two names, such as Hariph and Jorah (v24; Ezra 2:18). Guzik notes that only a small minority of the exiles ever returned, and he sees these pioneers recorded twice in Scripture as a sign that God remembers those who answer his call (compare Mal 3:16).

#### Totals of People and Gifts (vv66–73)
- **"The whole assembly together was 42,360" (v66).** The total matches Ezra 2:64 exactly, yet the numbers listed above add up to only about 31,000. The Tyndale notes suggest the families listed are incomplete, or the total includes people not counted family by family, perhaps women, or members of other tribes. The difference was noticed in ancient times, and no single answer is certain.
- **"7,337 male and female servants… 245 singers, male and female" (v67).** About one in seven of the company were servants, a sign that some returnees were well-off. These singers seem to be paid entertainers for feasts and funerals, not the temple choir of v44. Ezra 2:65 gives 200 of them.
- **"Their horses were 736, their mules 245" (v68).** Many Hebrew manuscripts lack this verse, probably because a copyist's eye jumped between two lines ending with the same number. The ESV restores it from Ezra 2:66 and some Hebrew manuscripts, which is why the Hebrew verse numbers run one behind English Bibles from here on. Camels and donkeys (v69) carried the loads. Most of the people walked the roughly 900 miles from Babylon.
- **"The governor gave to the treasury 1,000 darics of gold" (v70).** A daric was a Persian gold coin, probably named after Darius I and weighing about a quarter ounce. A mina was a weight of silver of about a pound and a quarter. The ESV's "50 basins, 30 priests' garments and 500 minas of silver" follows a corrected reading. The Hebrew as it stands reads "530 priests' garments," which looks like two lines run together. Gifts are listed in three groups: the governor, the family heads and the rest of the people (vv70–72).
- **Different totals again.** Ezra 2:69 gives a single total of 61,000 darics, 5,000 minas and 100 garments. Nehemiah's three gifts add up to 41,000 darics, 4,700 minas and 97 garments. Ezra may round the figures or count gifts given at different times. Either way, giving to God's house went back to the tabernacle, when the people brought so much that Moses had to tell them to stop (Exod 36:5–7).
- **"All Israel, lived in their towns" (v73).** The old list ends as Ezra 2:70 does, with everyone settled, each family on its inheritance.
- **"And when the seventh month had come, the people of Israel were in their towns" (v73).** In Ezra 3:1 these same words introduce the first returnees gathering in the seventh month to rebuild the altar. Here they introduce chapter 8, where the people gather in the seventh month of 445 BC to hear Ezra read the Law. The writer lets the old record and the new story meet in one line. The first generation began with the altar, and this one begins with the Book. The seventh month, Tishri (September–October), held the Feast of Trumpets, the Day of Atonement and the Feast of Booths, so it was the high point of Israel's year of worship.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the totals agree with Ezra except for the singing men and women, and he treats the difference as small. On the gifts he writes, "Whatever is given to the work of God, he is not unrighteous to forget it" (compare Heb 6:10). JFB identifies the *Tirshatha* of v70 with Nehemiah, though most scholars think the copied register meant the governor of the first return. JFB adds that a mina was sixty shekels. Guzik treats the recorded gifts as worth noting and sees the people settled in their towns as the real goal of the wall. The stones were never the point. A secure people, free to worship, was.

#### Pointing to Christ
Long lists of names show that God's people are real families with real histories, and that belonging to Israel mattered. Some could not prove their place and had to wait for a priest who could give God's answer. That priest has come. Jesus, a high priest "after the order of Melchizedek" (Heb 7:11–17), does not depend on a register of descent. He speaks with God's full authority and brings in people who could never prove they belonged: foreigners, outsiders, the sons of Sisera. Behind the old register stands a better one, "the book of life" (Phil 4:3; Rev 21:27), where the Lord writes the names of those who are his.
