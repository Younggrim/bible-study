*Setting:* The temple vision continues (chapters 40–48, dated 573 BC). In chapter 43 the glory of the LORD came back through the east gate and filled the temple, and God declared it his throne "forever." That raises the next question at once: who may come near a God like this, and how? Chapter 44 answers with rules about gates and access. One gate closes for good. Foreigners who had served in the sanctuary are barred. Levites who had led Israel into idolatry are demoted to the outer work, and the faithful priests of Zadok's family are given the inner service, with strict rules on clothing, hair, wine, marriage, mourning and food. Ezekiel was himself a priest (1:3), so these were matters he knew from the inside. Throughout, the old principle of 42:20 is applied to people: a separation between the holy and the common.

#### The Gate for the Prince (vv1–14)
- **"He brought me back to the outer gate of the sanctuary, which faces east. And it was shut" (v1).** Ezekiel had stood at this gate when the glory entered (43:1–4). Now its doors are closed.
- **"No one shall enter by it, for the LORD, the God of Israel, has entered by it" (v2).** In the ancient world a gate a king had used for a triumphal entry might be closed afterward in his honor. The reason here goes further. The Tyndale notes take the shut gate as a promise: God has come back and will never leave again. The glory once left by the east (11:23). That exit is now sealed.
- **The Golden Gate today.** The east gate of Jerusalem's Temple Mount, often called the Golden Gate, has been walled up for centuries, sealed in its present form under the Ottomans in the 1500s. Many visitors connect it with this verse. But Ezekiel's gate belongs to the visionary temple, not to the walls of the later city, and the bricked-up gate is at most an interesting echo.
- **A reading from church history.** Several church fathers, including Ambrose and Jerome, read the shut gate as a picture of Mary, through whom the Lord entered the world, and used it to support the belief that she remained a virgin. This is an allegorical use of the verse, not its plain meaning, and Protestant interpreters have generally not followed it.
- **"Only the prince may sit in it to eat bread before the LORD" (v3).** The "prince" (*nasi'*) appears often in chapters 44–48. Ezekiel deliberately avoids the word "king" (*melek*) for him in these chapters, after the long record of kings who abused power (34:1–10; 45:8–9). "Eat bread" means eating his share of the peace offerings, a fellowship meal in God's presence (Lev 7:15).
- **"He shall enter by way of the vestibule of the gate" (v3).** He may sit in the gate building, but he comes in from the outer court through the porch at the inner end, not through the outer doors the LORD used. The Tyndale notes draw the point: the prince is honored, but he is a man under God, and the temple is not his private chapel (compare 43:8).
- **Who is the prince?** Ezekiel has called the coming David God's "prince" (34:24; 37:25), so some readers identify him with the Messiah. But this prince offers a sin offering for himself (45:22) and has sons who inherit land (46:16), which fits a human ruler better. JFB and Guzik both reject the identification with Christ. Guzik thinks of a raised David ruling under Christ, while JFB calls him the civil ruler under the Messiah. Many others see him simply as the ideal leader of a restored people, a picture of godly government kept in its place.
- **"Behold, the glory of the LORD filled the temple of the LORD. And I fell on my face" (v4).** Before the rules come, Ezekiel sees the glory again. The rules of access only make sense with the glory in view.
- **"Mark well, see with your eyes, and hear with your ears" (v5).** "Mark well" is literally "set your heart." The guide said the same at the start of the vision (40:4). Ezekiel is told to give special attention to "the entrance to the temple and all the exits" because who goes in, and how, is the subject of the chapter.
- **"Say to the rebellious house… enough of all your abominations" (v6).** "Rebellious house" is the name God gave Israel at Ezekiel's call (2:5–8; 3:9). Even in the vision of restoration, the past is named honestly.
- **"Admitting foreigners, uncircumcised in heart and flesh, to be in my sanctuary" (v7).** The phrase joins outward and inward circumcision: these were outsiders who had neither the covenant sign nor a heart turned to God (Deut 10:16; Jer 9:25–26). The Tyndale notes suggest Israel had hired them as temple guards and gatekeepers. Foreign mercenaries called Carites guarded the temple during Joash's coronation (2 Kings 11:4–8), and some think of the Gibeonites, who had served at the sanctuary since Joshua's time (Josh 9:23–27).
- **"You have set others to keep my charge for you" (v8).** The sin was not only who served, but that Israel had handed off a sacred duty God gave to the Levites (Num 3:5–10; 18:1–7) to hired hands.
- **"No foreigner, uncircumcised in heart and flesh… shall enter my sanctuary" (v9).** This is not a ban on every non-Israelite. The same vision gives land to foreigners who live among Israel as full members (47:22–23), and Isaiah welcomes the foreigner who holds fast to God's covenant to his altar (Isa 56:6–7). What is barred is the one who belongs neither outwardly nor inwardly.
- **"The Levites who went far from me… after their idols… shall bear their punishment" (v10).** All priests were Levites, but not all Levites were priests. Only Aaron's descendants offered sacrifices. Many Levitical families had served at the high places, the local shrines that mixed the worship of the LORD with idolatry. When Josiah shut those shrines about fifty years before this vision, their priests "did not come up to the altar of the LORD in Jerusalem," though they ate bread among their brothers (2 Kings 23:8–9). Ezekiel's vision makes that demotion permanent.
- **"They shall be ministers in my sanctuary, having oversight at the gates" (v11).** The punishment is real, but so is the mercy. They keep a place in God's house. They guard the gates (the job foreigners had wrongly done), and they slaughter the people's sacrifices, which under Moses the worshiper did himself (Lev 1:5, 11). The Tyndale notes observe that the people too are now one step further back from the altar.
- **"They ministered to them before their idols and became a stumbling block of iniquity" (v12).** A "stumbling block" (*mikshol*) is a key word in Ezekiel (7:19; 14:3–7). Leaders who serve idols trip up the people they lead. **"I have sworn concerning them"** is literally "I have lifted my hand," the gesture of an oath.
- **"They shall not come near to me, to serve me as priest" (v13).** "Come near" (*qarab*) is the word for priestly approach to God. Those who led others away from him lose the right to come close.
- **"Yet I will appoint them to keep charge of the temple" (v14).** The section ends on grace. Failure has consequences, but God does not throw the Levites out. They will "do all its service," the humbler but needed work of the house.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes on v2, "God will have a way by himself," and on vv7–9 applies the passage to the church: admitting the openly wicked to the Lord's ordinances pollutes his sanctuary. On the Levites he is direct: "those who have robbed God of his honour will justly be deprived of their honour," though their being kept in service shows mercy. JFB says the prince "must mean the civil ruler under Messiah," because he offers sacrifice for himself, and observes that the sins of priests and people "acted and reacted on one another." JFB compares the Levites to John Mark, who stayed in service after failing (Acts 15:38). Guzik weighs the main identifications of the prince and leans toward David raised to rule under Christ. He calls the closed gate encouraging rather than threatening, and mentions Adam Clarke's report of the Catholic use of v2 for Mary, which he judges a shaky argument.

#### Rules for Levitical Priests (vv15–31)
- **"The Levitical priests, the sons of Zadok, who kept the charge of my sanctuary when the people of Israel went astray" (v15).** Zadok descended from Aaron's son Eleazar (1 Chr 6:3–8). He stayed loyal to David through Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 15:24–29) and anointed Solomon when Abiathar sided with the rival Adonijah (1 Kings 1:7–8, 39). Abiathar, of Eli's family, was then removed, fulfilling the word against Eli's house (1 Kings 2:27; 1 Sam 2:35). Zadok's family led the Jerusalem priesthood from then on. Not every Zadokite was faithful, as chapter 8 shows, but as a line they kept the central sanctuary when the high places drifted. The principle the Tyndale notes draw out runs through these chapters: those faithful in the past are brought nearer.
- **"They shall come near to me to minister to me… offer me the fat and the blood" (v15).** The fat and the blood were God's portion of every sacrifice (Lev 3:16–17; 17:11). The priests' first service is toward God, not the crowd.
- **"They shall approach my table" (v16).** This likely means the altar (compare Mal 1:7, 12), or possibly the wooden table before the holy place (41:22). Either way, priests serve at the table of the King.
- **"They shall wear linen garments. They shall have nothing of wool on them" (v17).** Linen was the material of priestly dress (Exod 28:39–43; Lev 16:4). Angels in Ezekiel and Daniel wear linen too (9:2; Dan 10:5), and in Revelation "fine linen… is the righteous deeds of the saints" (Rev 19:8).
- **"Anything that causes sweat" (v18).** The Hebrew word for sweat (*yeza'*) appears only here. In Israel's law bodily discharges made a person unclean, and sweat soaked into wool in a hot climate would not suit holy service. Many readers also recall Genesis 3:19: "by the sweat of your face you shall eat bread." Sweat belongs to the curse of toil, and serving God is not supposed to be grinding labor.
- **"Lest they transmit holiness to the people with their garments" (v19).** This repeats 42:14. "Transmit holiness" is one word, *qadash*, "make holy." In the law, contact with the holiest things made a person or thing holy (Exod 29:37; Lev 6:27), which meant it now belonged to God and fell under his strict rules. Ordinary people were not ready for that. Uzzah's death beside the ark shows how seriously Israel took it (2 Sam 6:6–7).
- **"They shall not shave their heads or let their locks grow long" (v20).** Shaving the head and letting hair go wild were both signs of mourning (Lev 10:6; 21:5), and pagan priests in Egypt shaved their heads. A trimmed head was a sign of a priest in order, neither grieving nor careless.
- **"No priest shall drink wine when he enters the inner court" (v21).** This repeats Leviticus 10:9, given right after Nadab and Abihu died for offering "unauthorized fire." A priest needed a clear head to tell holy from common (Lev 10:10).
- **"They shall not marry a widow or a divorced woman, but only virgins… or a widow who is the widow of a priest" (v22).** Under Moses, ordinary priests could not marry a divorced woman, but only the high priest was restricted to a virgin (Lev 21:7, 13–14). Ezekiel extends the stricter rule to all priests, with one exception for a priest's widow. Notably, no high priest appears in Ezekiel's temple.
- **Ezekiel and the law of Moses.** These differences from the Torah troubled Jewish teachers. The Talmud tells of a rabbi, Hananiah son of Hezekiah, who spent long nights and three hundred jars of lamp oil working to reconcile Ezekiel with the Five Books, so that Ezekiel would not be set aside from the Scriptures (Shabbat 13b). The differences are one reason many readers think the vision is describing something new, not simply restoring the old.
- **"They shall teach my people the difference between the holy and the common" (v23).** This is the priests' great task, and exactly the one Ezekiel charged them with failing (22:26). Priests were teachers of the law, not only performers of rituals (Deut 33:10; Mal 2:7).
- **"In a dispute, they shall act as judges" (v24).** Difficult cases went to the priests and the judge (Deut 17:8–9). They are to judge "according to my judgments," not their own ideas, and keep God's feasts and Sabbaths, which Israel had profaned (20:12–13; 22:8).
- **"They shall not defile themselves by going near to a dead person" (v25).** Death was the strongest source of uncleanness, the opposite of the living God. Priests could make an exception only for close family (Lev 21:1–3). Ezekiel himself was forbidden even to mourn his wife (24:15–18).
- **"After he has become clean, they shall count seven days for him" (v26).** The normal cleansing after touching a dead body took seven days (Num 19:11–19). Ezekiel adds another seven days and a sin offering before the priest may serve again (v27). The bar is higher for those who come closest.
- **"I am their inheritance… I am their possession" (v28).** Priests received no tribal territory (Num 18:20; Deut 18:1–2; Josh 13:33). This could look like a loss. God presents it as the best share of all. The psalmist uses the same language: "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup" (Ps 16:5).
- **"They shall eat the grain offering, the sin offering, and the guilt offering, and every devoted thing" (v29).** The priests lived on their share of the offerings (Lev 6:16–18, 26; 7:6–7). A "devoted thing" (*cherem*) was something given wholly and permanently to the LORD (Num 18:14; Lev 27:28).
- **"The first of your dough, that a blessing may rest on your house" (v30).** The firstfruits of bread dough belonged to God (Num 15:20–21). Giving to support those who serve brings blessing back to the giver's home (Prov 3:9–10; Mal 3:10).
- **"Anything… that has died of itself or is torn by wild animals" (v31).** This was forbidden to priests (Lev 22:8), and Ezekiel himself had protested he had never eaten it (4:14). Such meat had not been properly drained of blood, and it carried the touch of death.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the Zadokites' reward: "If we keep close to God, God will keep us close to him." On the linen and sweat he writes, "Sweat came in with sin and was part of the curse." JFB explains that wool "soon induces perspiration" in the hot East and notes that Egyptian idol priests shaved their heads, which is part of why Israel's priests were not to. On v21 JFB adds that priests should never let their zeal be confused with drunkenness, recalling the charge against the apostles at Pentecost (Acts 2:13). JFB is silent on vv22–27, and Henry covers them only briefly. Guzik reads the Zadokites' coming near "to minister to me" as the first calling of every believer, who is now a priest (1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 1:6). He sees in the linen a picture of service done in rest rather than strain, and applies the priests' support from the offerings to supporting those in full-time ministry today.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel's chapter is about who may come near, and the answer is narrow: a gate closed, outsiders barred, unfaithful Levites kept back, and only faithful priests in linen allowed to the altar. Hebrews announces the reversal. Jesus is the priest who is "holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners" (Heb 7:26), and through his blood believers "have confidence to enter the holy places… by the new and living way that he opened for us" (Heb 10:19–20). In him outsiders far from God "have been brought near" (Eph 2:13), and his people become "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9). The priests' best gift, "I am their inheritance," becomes the inheritance of all who belong to him.
