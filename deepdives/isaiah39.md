*Setting:* This short chapter closes the Hezekiah story and the whole first half of Isaiah. It probably took place around 703 BC, soon after Hezekiah's recovery (ch 38) and before Sennacherib's invasion of 701 (chs 36–37), but Isaiah has placed it last on purpose. Chapter 37 ended with Assyria defeated. Chapter 39 ends with the first clear announcement that Judah will be carried off to Babylon, and that is where chapter 40 picks up, speaking comfort to exiles in Babylon. The account is paralleled in 2 Kings 20:12–19, and 2 Chronicles 32:25–31 adds that Hezekiah's heart had grown proud.

#### Envoys from Babylon (vv1–8)
- **"Merodach-baladan the son of Baladan, king of Babylon" (v1).** His Babylonian name was Marduk-apla-iddina, "Marduk has given an heir." He was a tough Chaldean chieftain from the marshes of southern Mesopotamia who twice seized the throne of Babylon in defiance of Assyria, first from about 721 to 710 BC, when Sargon II drove him out, and again briefly in 703, when Sennacherib drove him out a second time. Assyrian records mention him often as a troublemaker. At this time Babylon was not yet a great empire. It was a restless city under Assyria's thumb, looking for allies.
- **"Sent envoys with letters and a present to Hezekiah, for he heard that he had been sick and had recovered" (v1).** The get-well message was the polite reason. The real reason was almost certainly politics: Merodach-baladan was organizing a revolt against Assyria and wanted Judah on his side, with Babylon in the east and Judah and Egypt in the west. Second Chronicles 32:31 gives another reason: they came "to inquire about the sign that had been done in the land," the shadow going back on the steps. Babylonians were famous astronomers, and a sign involving the sun would have interested them greatly. JFB says Merodach-baladan "would naturally court the alliance of Hezekiah."
- **"Hezekiah welcomed them gladly" (v2).** Literally "Hezekiah was glad over them." A king of a small hill country being courted by envoys from famous Babylon was flattered.
- **"He showed them his treasure house, the silver, the gold, the spices, the precious oil, his whole armory" (v2).** Hezekiah was very wealthy (2 Chr 32:27–29). Spices and fine oils were luxury goods, almost as valuable as metal. The "armory" was perhaps the "House of the Forest of Lebanon," Solomon's great hall where weapons were kept (22:8; 1 Kings 10:17). Showing it all was saying, in effect, "I would be a strong ally." The Tyndale notes put it this way. **"There was nothing in his house or in all his realm that Hezekiah did not show them."** The repetition of "nothing" and "all" in vv2–6 is deliberate. Everything he showed, Babylon will take.
- **What was wrong with it?** On the surface, nothing more than hospitality and diplomacy. But Isaiah had spent years warning against alliances with foreign powers in place of trust in God (30:1–2; 31:1). Looking to Babylon was the same sin as looking to Egypt. And 2 Chronicles 32:25 says, "Hezekiah did not make return according to the benefit done to him, for his heart was proud." Verse 31 adds, "God left him to himself, in order to test him and to know all that was in his heart." The king who had just prayed so humbly (37:14–20; 38:2–3) took the glory for himself. JFB: "It was not the mere act, but the spirit of it, which provoked God."
- **"Then Isaiah the prophet came to King Hezekiah" (v3).** Hezekiah had not sent for him this time. The prophet came uninvited, as he had come to Ahaz (7:3). His questions are like God's questions to Adam ("Where are you?") or to Elijah ("What are you doing here?"), not because he needed information, but to make Hezekiah face what he had done.
- **"They have come to me from a far country, from Babylon" (v3).** Hezekiah answers the second question and skips the first ("What did these men say?"). He seems proud that envoys came so far. The phrase "a far country" was exactly what the Gibeonites used to trick Joshua into a treaty (Josh 9:6, 9), a quiet warning about foreign alliances.
- **"They have seen all that is in my house. There is nothing in my storehouses that I did not show them" (v4).** It is an honest answer, and a sad one.
- **"Hear the word of the LORD of hosts" (v5).** The title "LORD of hosts," commander of heaven's armies, reminds Hezekiah who really controls the nations, not Babylon or Assyria.
- **"Behold, the days are coming, when all that is in your house… shall be carried to Babylon. Nothing shall be left" (v6).** This is the first time in Isaiah that Babylon is clearly named as the place of exile, about a century before it happened. In Hezekiah's day it looked impossible. Assyria was the superpower, and Babylon was its rebellious subject. But Babylon defeated Assyria and destroyed Nineveh in 612 BC, and Nebuchadnezzar carried off the palace and temple treasures of Jerusalem in 605, 597 and 586 BC (2 Kings 24:13; 25:13–17). JFB calls it "an exact retribution in kind": the people he showed his treasures to would take them.
- **"Some of your own sons, who will come from you, whom you will father, shall be taken away" (v7).** "Sons" here means descendants. King Jehoiachin, five generations after Hezekiah, was taken to Babylon in 597 BC (2 Kings 24:15), and he was released from prison there decades later (2 Kings 25:27–30).
- **"They shall be eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon" (v7).** The word *saris* can mean a castrated court official or more generally a high palace officer, and scholars debate which is meant in particular cases. Daniel 1:3–7 describes young men "of the royal family and of the nobility" taken to serve in Nebuchadnezzar's palace under "the chief of the eunuchs." Daniel and his three friends were among them. Guzik and JFB both link the prophecy to Daniel. God later speaks tenderly to eunuchs who hold fast to his covenant: "I will give them… a name better than sons and daughters" (56:3–5).
- **"The word of the LORD that you have spoken is good" (v8).** Hezekiah accepts the word. That much is right, as Eli accepted God's judgment: "It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him" (1 Sam 3:18). And 2 Chronicles 32:26 says that Hezekiah "humbled himself for the pride of his heart," so that the judgment did not come in his days.
- **"For he thought, 'There will be peace and security in my days'" (v8).** This is the troubling line. "Security" is *'emet*, truth or stability. Readers differ on how to take it. Some, like JFB, see thankfulness that God in mercy delayed the judgment, which is a proper response to grace. Others, like Guzik, see a self-centered relief that the disaster would fall on his children and not on him. The text lets the line stand without comment, and it is hard to read it as fully admirable. The man who wept for fifteen more years seems not to weep for his grandchildren.
- **Why end the first half of Isaiah here?** The first 39 chapters have been building up hope for a son of David who will reign in righteousness (9:6–7; 11:1–5; 32:1). Hezekiah is the best king Judah had for generations, and for a moment he seems to fit. Chapters 38–39 show that he is not the one: he is mortal, he becomes proud, and his reign ends with a sentence of exile. The Tyndale notes say these chapters prepare the reader for the true Messiah of chapters 40–66. The next words of the book are "Comfort, comfort my people" (40:1), spoken to the very exiles this chapter foresees.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as the embassy, "the pride and folly of Hezekiah, in showing his treasures," Isaiah's examination of him "in God's name," the sentence, and Hezekiah's submission. Guzik sees people-pleasing at the root of Hezekiah's display (Gal 1:10), and concludes that the extra years did not make him more godly, since time alone does not make a person better. JFB says relying on Babylon "was a similar sin to the previous reliance on Egypt," and reads v8 more kindly as true repentance that "acquiesces in all God's ways."

#### Pointing to Christ
Hezekiah showed Babylon all his treasures and lost them. The best of David's sons, after a great rescue, still fell to pride and left his descendants to exile. Isaiah's story needs a better King, and the next chapter begins to announce him: a voice in the wilderness, the glory of the LORD revealed, and a Shepherd who carries the lambs (40:3–11). Jesus is the Son of David who did not grasp for his own glory, but "emptied himself" (Phil 2:7), and whose kingdom will never be carried off. The exile Hezekiah shrugged off became the place where God's comfort was spoken.
