*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A Psalm. A Song." No author or occasion is named. The psalm is short, seven verses, and it sits in the middle of a cluster of harvest and thanksgiving songs (Pss 65–68). Verse 6 ("The earth has yielded its increase") suggests it was sung at a harvest festival, perhaps the Feast of Weeks (Pentecost) or the Feast of Booths, when Israel brought the first or the last of the crops to God. But the psalmist looks past the barley and wheat to a much larger harvest: the nations of the world coming to know and praise Israel's God. It takes the priestly blessing of Numbers 6, which Aaron pronounced over Israel, and asks God to answer it in a way the whole world can see. Its shape is carefully built: the same refrain (vv3, 5) frames verse 4, the center of the psalm, where the nations rejoice in God's just rule. Jewish tradition counts 49 Hebrew words in the body of the psalm, and it has long been recited during the 49-day "counting of the Omer" between Passover and Pentecost, sometimes printed in the shape of a lampstand. In the Anglican Book of Common Prayer it is the *Deus misereatur*, sung at Evening Prayer. Guzik notes, as an odd fact, that Luther passed over it in his large commentary on the Psalms. Guzik, Henry and JFB all read it as a missionary psalm.

#### May God Be Gracious to Us and Bless Us (vv1–3)
- **"May God be gracious to us and bless us and make his face to shine upon us" (v1).** This is the priestly blessing almost word for word: "The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you" (Num 6:24–25). Two changes stand out. The priest said "you," and the psalmist says "us": the people now pray the blessing back to God. And the covenant name "the LORD" (*YHWH*) becomes "God" (*Elohim*). Psalms 42–83 are often called the "Elohistic Psalter" because they mostly use *Elohim*. But here the choice also suits a psalm about all nations, since *Elohim* is the general word for God that any people would recognize.
- **"Gracious" first.** The verb (*chanan*) means to show favor to someone who has no claim on it. Mercy comes before blessing. Henry: "all our happiness comes from God's mercy and takes rise in that," and he links it to the tax collector's prayer, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (Luke 18:13).
- **"Make his face to shine upon us."** A shining face is a smiling face. In the ancient Near East a king's expression decided a petitioner's fate: a bright face meant favor, a dark face meant trouble (Prov 16:15). Asaph's repeated prayer, "let your face shine, that we may be saved!" (80:3, 7, 19), uses the same picture. JFB: "show us favor." Guzik, drawing on VanGemeren and Boice, makes the same point about a king's face signaling his favor. **Selah** marks a pause, probably musical, after the first request.
- **"That your way may be known on earth" (v2).** Here is the reason for the prayer. Israel does not ask to be blessed for its own sake. It asks to be blessed so that the world will see what God is like. This was the point of God's promise to Abraham from the start: "I will bless you… and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:2–3). God's "way" is his manner of dealing with people and the path he calls them to walk. JFB: his "way… of gracious dealing" (Isa 55:8).
- **"Your saving power among all nations" (v2).** The word is *yeshu'ah*, "salvation," the root of the name Jesus (*Yeshua*). The KJV's "saving health" is old English for the same thing. JFB: "literally, 'salvation.'" The Tyndale notes observe that God's goodness to Israel affects his reputation among the nations (96:1–3). When God visibly blesses his people, others ask why.
- **"Let the peoples praise you, O God; let all the peoples praise you!" (v3).** The refrain. It is a wish and a prayer, and it grows as it goes: first "the peoples," then "*all* the peoples." The word (*'ammim*) is plural. This is not a hope that the nations will become Israelites, but that people of every nation will praise Israel's God. Revelation shows the answer: "a great multitude… from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" (Rev 7:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry notices that the psalmist does not say "God be merciful to *me*" but "to *us*," and he ties it to the Lord's Prayer: "Our Saviour, in teaching us to say, Our Father, has intimated that we ought to pray with and for others." He calls "God bless us" "a comprehensive prayer; it is a pity such excellent words should ever be used slightly and carelessly." Guzik stresses that the purpose of blessing is outward. A church that looks lifeless does nothing to draw others, and a "bless me" faith misses why God blesses at all. The Tyndale notes compare the whole psalm to the opening of the Lord's Prayer: may God's name be honored and his kingdom come (Matt 6:9–10).

#### Let the Nations Be Glad (vv4–5)
- **"Let the nations be glad and sing for joy" (v4).** This is the center of the psalm. Elsewhere in the Psalms the nations rage against God (2:1) or tremble before him (99:1). Here they sing. Henry calls it "holy joy… joy in God's dominion."
- **"For you judge the peoples with equity" (v4).** Why would the nations be *glad* to be judged? Because in the Bible "judge" usually means to rule and to set things right, not only to condemn. For the oppressed, a just judge is good news (96:10–13; 98:9). "Equity" (*mishor*) is the word for level ground, a plain or tableland without obstacles. BDB gives it here the sense of "uprightness in government." God's rule is level: no favorites, no rigged scales. The Tyndale notes: God brings the ultimate justice that human beings long for.
- **"And guide the nations upon earth" (v4).** "Guide" (*nachah*) is the shepherd's word, the one in "He leads me in paths of righteousness" (23:3). It is also the word for God leading Israel through the wilderness with the pillar of cloud (Exod 13:21) and in the Song of Moses, "You have led in your steadfast love the people whom you have redeemed" (Exod 15:13). The psalmist prays that the God who shepherded Israel out of Egypt would shepherd the nations too. **Selah** pauses again.
- **"Let the peoples praise you, O God; let all the peoples praise you!" (v5).** The refrain returns word for word. The repetition is deliberate. It closes the frame around verse 4 and lets the congregation sing it again.
- **Commentators:** JFB sums up vv3–5: thanks will be rendered "for the blessings of His wise and holy government," and points to Isaiah 2:3–4, where the nations stream to Zion to learn God's ways and he judges between them. Henry reads the verses as both prayer and prophecy: "Many scripture-prophecies and promises are wrapped up in prayers, to intimate that the answer of the church's prayer is as sure as the performance of God's promises." He contrasts the Old Testament saints, who longed for the Gentiles to come in, with those in the apostles' day who were "enraged at the preaching of the gospel to the Gentiles." Guzik argues that the hope of Christ's coming as judge should fire, not dampen, the urge to reach the nations, and cites Spurgeon and Trapp that the repeated refrain is not empty: "thee" is emphatic, God alone and no idol.

#### God Shall Bless Us (vv6–7)
- **"The earth has yielded its increase" (v6).** The ESV reads this as something that has already happened: the harvest is in. The KJV reads it as future: "Then shall the earth yield her increase." The Hebrew verb form can be read either way, and both translations are defensible. The ESV's reading suits a harvest festival. The harvest is the first answer to the blessing of verse 1. "Increase" (*yevul*) is the produce of the land, the word in Leviticus 26:4: "the land shall yield its increase" if Israel walks in God's statutes. The Tyndale notes point to Genesis 26:12 and Isaiah 30:23, where a rich harvest is a sign of God's favor.
- **"God, our God, shall bless us" (v6).** "Our God" is personal and covenantal. The God of all nations is still Israel's own God. Henry: "It is here spoken with an air of triumph. God, even our own God."
- **"God shall bless us; let all the ends of the earth fear him!" (v7).** The word "bless" appears for the third time (vv1, 6, 7), and "God" appears three times in vv6–7 alone. The psalm ends where it began, with blessing, but now the blessing reaches outward to "all the ends of the earth." "Fear" here is not terror but reverent worship, the awe that leads people to trust and obey (Prov 1:7). It is how the Old Testament describes Gentiles coming to the true God: Jonah's sailors "feared the LORD exceedingly" and made vows to him (Jonah 1:16).
- **A harvest that points past itself.** The earthly harvest becomes a picture of a greater one. Jesus used the same picture: "Lift up your eyes, and see that the fields are white for harvest" (John 4:35). And it is striking that, if this psalm was sung at the Feast of Weeks, that was the very feast on which the Spirit was poured out in Jerusalem and the first harvest of the nations began, with Jews and converts "from every nation under heaven" hearing God's works in their own languages (Acts 2:1–11).
- **Commentators:** JFB: "The blessings of a fruitful harvest are mentioned as types of greater and spiritual blessings, under which all nations shall fear and love God." Henry: "The lower world shall smile upon them… The upper world shall smile upon them… which is much better." He adds, "The leaven hidden in the meal shall diffuse itself, till the whole be leavened" (Matt 13:33). Guzik sees a circle in the closing verses: God blesses his people, they carry his name outward, and that brings more blessing. He passes on a Puritan image (Manton, via Spurgeon) of a pump that gives back more water the more is poured in, and, with Kidner, links the whole psalm to God's promise to Abraham.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 67 asks God to answer Aaron's blessing so publicly that every nation will see his salvation. That prayer is answered in Jesus. He is the true priest who lifted his hands and blessed his people as he ascended (Luke 24:50–51), and in his face "the light of the knowledge of the glory of God" shines (2 Cor 4:6). The salvation (*yeshu'ah*) the psalmist longed to see known among all nations has a name, Yeshua, and old Simeon held him and called him "your salvation that you have prepared in the presence of all peoples, a light for revelation to the Gentiles" (Luke 2:30–32). The psalm's harvest began at Pentecost and still goes on, until the refrain comes true and "all the peoples" praise him.
