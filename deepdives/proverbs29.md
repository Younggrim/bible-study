*Setting:* This is the last chapter of the collection copied by "the men of Hezekiah" (25:1–29:27), set in Judah around 700 BC. Like chapter 28 it is mostly two-line contrasts, and the same concerns carry over: rulers and the people under them, the poor and their rights, the law, and the danger of a hard heart. Two new threads stand out. One is the home: a father glad or grieved over his son (vv3, 15, 17) and servants raised well or badly (vv19, 21). The other is the inner life: anger let loose or held back (vv11, 22), pride and humility (v23), and the fear of man against trust in the LORD (v25). The chapter opens with a warning about a hardened neck (v1) and ends with the deep divide between the just and the wicked (v27), which closes the whole Hezekiah collection. The ESV has no headings inside the collection, so the headings below follow its groupings.

#### A Hardened Neck and a Glad Father (vv1–7)
- **"He who is often reproved, yet stiffens his neck, will suddenly be broken beyond healing" (v1).** A "stiff neck" is the picture of an ox that won't bow to the yoke. It is God's word for Israel at the golden calf: "a stiff-necked people" (Exod 32:9). The person here has been warned again and again, not once. "Broken beyond healing" repeats word for word the end of the troublemaker in 6:15. God is patient, but warnings are not unlimited. Pharaoh is the classic case, and Eli's sons ignored their father's rebuke until judgment fell (1 Sam 2:25; 4:11). Henry: "If God wounds, who can heal?"
- **"When the righteous increase, the people rejoice, but when the wicked rule, the people groan" (v2).** This is the third of the four markers in chapters 28–29 (28:12, 28; 29:16). "Increase" can mean grow in number or grow in power, and JFB takes it as "increased in power." A nation's mood follows its leaders' character. Henry: "The people have cause to rejoice or mourn, as their rulers are righteous or wicked." Israel groaned under Pharaoh (Exod 2:23) and rejoiced when Hezekiah restored the temple (2 Chr 29:36).
- **"He who loves wisdom makes his father glad, but a companion of prostitutes squanders his wealth" (v3).** The book's first collection opened with this theme (10:1), and the warnings against the adulteress in chapters 5–7 come back in one line. The prodigal son "devoured your property with prostitutes" (Luke 15:30). The Tyndale notes: spending money on prostitutes wastes a man's wealth quickly. Henry: "Divine wisdom best keeps us from ruinous lusts."
- **"By justice a king builds up the land, but he who exacts gifts tears it down" (v4).** "Builds up" is literally "makes it stand." "Exacts gifts" may mean bribes or heavy taxes, and the Hebrew word is often used for offerings, so a "man of offerings" is one who is always on the take. Either way, a ruler who uses power for his own gain destroys his country. Solomon's heavy taxes and forced labor led to the kingdom's split (1 Kgs 12:4). The Tyndale notes: a fair king protects others' rights, a bribed one serves himself.
- **"A man who flatters his neighbor spreads a net for his feet" (v5).** Flattery is a trap. It is not clear in Hebrew whether the net catches the neighbor or the flatterer himself, and both happen. The person flattered drops his guard and walks into folly. The Tyndale notes: flattery is often a trick to exploit someone (7:21–22). Henry: flatterers put people off their guard.
- **"An evil man is ensnared in his transgression, but a righteous man sings and rejoices" (v6).** Sin sets its own trap (5:22). The righteous have a lighter step. Paul and Silas, beaten and in stocks, were "praying and singing hymns to God" at midnight (Acts 16:25). Henry: "Transgressions always end in vexations."
- **"A righteous man knows the rights of the poor; a wicked man does not understand such knowledge" (v7).** "Knows" means more than being aware: he takes their case to heart, especially in court. "Rights" is the word for a legal cause. God's law repeatedly protects the poor, the widow and the orphan (Exod 23:6; Deut 24:17). Job could say, "I searched out the cause of him whom I did not know" (Job 29:16). The wicked man doesn't care, and JFB calls it "voluntary neglect." Guzik, with Waltke, says this blindness is moral, not intellectual.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, with Waltke, takes v1 to mean the window for repentance is real but not endless. On v4 he passes on Trapp's boast that this one verse is better political advice than the famous political writers of his day. Kidner, quoted by Guzik, has innocence going on its way singing (v6).

#### Scoffers, Fools and Self-Control (vv8–11)
- **"Scoffers set a city aflame, but the wise turn away wrath" (v8).** "Set aflame" is literally "blow on" a city, fanning a fire. Mockers who sneer at authority and at God stir up a whole town. A wise word calms it. When Sheba's rebellion brought Joab's army to the town of Abel, a wise woman negotiated and saved the city (2 Sam 20:16–22), an example Guzik passes on from Ross. JFB defines scorners as those who "contemptuously disregard God's law."
- **"If a wise man has an argument with a fool, the fool only rages and laughs, and there is no quiet" (v9).** The setting may be a court case. Whatever the wise man does, the fool either blows up or laughs it off, and nothing gets settled. JFB: the fool, "whether angry or good-humored, is unsettled." Compare 26:4–5 on when to answer a fool and when not to. Henry: arguing with a conceited quarreler brings anger or ridicule, "and no good is done."
- **"Bloodthirsty men hate one who is blameless and seek the life of the upright" (v10).** Goodness provokes hatred in evil people. Cain killed Abel "because his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous" (1 John 3:12). Jesus warned his followers they would be hated (John 15:18). The second line is ambiguous in Hebrew. The ESV reads it as the bloodthirsty seeking the upright person's life to kill him. JFB reads "the upright seek his life" as the upright seeking to *protect* the blameless man's life, which would make the verse a contrast. Both are possible.
- **"A fool gives full vent to his spirit, but a wise man quietly holds it back" (v11).** "Spirit" here means anger or strong feeling. The fool lets it all out. The wise man "holds it back," a word used of calming a storm (Ps 89:9). The Tyndale notes: wise people control their emotions and stay calm under stress. Self-control is not pretending to feel nothing. It is not being ruled by what you feel (16:32; Jas 1:19–20). Henry reads the verse also of speech: a fool "tells every thing he knows, and can keep no counsel."
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Kidner's note that the Hebrew of v11 suggests anger quieted, not just bottled up for later. On v8 he passes on Trapp's line that scoffing spreads like a plague. Henry on v10 links the hatred of the blameless to Christ's warning that his disciples would be "hated of all men."

#### Rulers, the Poor and the Rod (vv12–17)
- **"If a ruler listens to falsehood, all his officials will be wicked" (v12).** Corruption flows downhill. If a king rewards flattery and gossip, his officials learn quickly that lies get ahead. JFB: "His servants imitate him." Saul listened to slander about David (1 Sam 24:9). Ahab surrounded himself with prophets who told him what he wanted (1 Kgs 22:6–8). Henry: such a ruler causes his servants "to become liars and false accusers."
- **"The poor man and the oppressor meet together; the LORD gives light to the eyes of both" (v13).** This echoes 22:2, "The rich and the poor meet together; the LORD is the Maker of them all." "Gives light to the eyes" means gives life and sight (Ps 13:3; Ezra 9:8). Oppressor and oppressed both breathe God's air and see by his light, and both will answer to him. The Tyndale notes: since God created both, the oppressor should treat the poor kindly. Guzik extends it to the light God gives every person through creation and conscience (Rom 1:19–21). Jesus: God "makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good" (Matt 5:45).
- **"If a king faithfully judges the poor, his throne will be established forever" (v14).** "Faithfully" is literally "in truth." The promise of a throne "forever" recalls God's covenant with David (2 Sam 7:16). Psalm 72, a prayer for the king, makes justice for the poor the center of a lasting reign: "he delivers the needy when he calls" (Ps 72:12). Isaiah's coming king will "judge the poor" with righteousness (Isa 11:4). JFB: "Such is the character of the King of kings."
- **"The rod and reproof give wisdom, but a child left to himself brings shame to his mother" (v15).** Correction, by consequence and by word, produces wisdom. "Left to himself" is literally "let loose," like an animal sent off untended. The Tyndale notes: wisdom does not come naturally; it must be learned. Mentioning the mother is natural in Hebrew parallelism, and Guzik passes on Poole's guess that mothers were thought more likely to indulge. In the Bible the most famous example is a father: David never crossed his son Adonijah, "never at any time displeased him by asking, 'Why have you done thus and so?'" (1 Kgs 1:6). The rod in Proverbs (13:24; 22:15; 23:13–14) has been read different ways. Many Christians take it as permitting careful physical discipline. Others take it as a picture for discipline of any kind. All agree the point is loving, consistent correction, never rage (Eph 6:4).
- **"When the wicked increase, transgression increases, but the righteous will look upon their downfall" (v16).** The fourth of the marker sayings. Sin spreads when sinners multiply and get power. But the end is sure. "Look upon" means see it happen, not gloat (24:17). Henry: the righteous should not let growing sin shake their faith, but "wait with patience" (Ps 37:34–36).
- **"Discipline your son, and he will give you rest; he will give delight to your heart" (v17).** "Rest" means peace of mind. A parent who corrects a child early sleeps better later. "Delight" is literally "dainties," the choice foods of a feast. Guzik notes the verse appeals to the parent's own interest: discipline blesses the one who gives it too. Hebrews: God disciplines us "for our good, that we may share his holiness" (Heb 12:10).
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Bridges' point that discipline is the order of God's own government, and he notes even Jesus "learned obedience through what he suffered" (Heb 5:8). Trapp, cited by Guzik, sees v14 pointing beyond any human king to Messiah's throne. Henry on v13: God gives both sorts life's comforts, and "to some of both sorts he gives his grace."

#### No Vision, No Restraint (vv18–21)
- **"Where there is no prophetic vision the people cast off restraint" (v18).** The KJV's "Where there is no vision, the people perish" is often quoted as being about leaders having goals for the future. That is not what it means. "Vision" is *chazon*, the word for a prophet's revelation from God, the same word in "the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision" (1 Sam 3:1) and the title of Isaiah's book (Isa 1:1). "Cast off restraint" is *para'*, "let loose," the word used when Aaron "had let them break loose" at the golden calf (Exod 32:25). Without God's word, people run wild. The period of the judges is the picture: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 21:25). **"But blessed is he who keeps the law."** Having God's word is not enough. It has to be kept. The Tyndale notes: wise people follow God's law, which shows his will. Henry: "How bare does a place look without Bibles and ministers!"
- **"By mere words a servant is not disciplined, for though he understands, he will not respond" (v19).** Some people understand perfectly well what is asked and still don't do it. Words alone don't move them, and there need to be consequences. The Tyndale notes say much the same. Guzik passes on Ross's reminder that Joseph and Daniel served faithfully as servants without any such pressure. Henry draws a lesson for the heart: a servant who serves only from fear, "not from conscience, or love," is of little use.
- **"Do you see a man who is hasty in his words? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (v20).** This echoes 26:12, where the man "wise in his own eyes" is the one worse than a fool. Speaking before thinking comes from the same root: thinking you already know. JFB: it implies "self-conceit." James: "let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak" (Jas 1:19).
- **"Whoever pampers his servant from childhood will in the end find him his heir" (v21).** The last word (*manon*) appears only here in the Old Testament and its meaning is uncertain. The ESV guesses "heir," and the KJV "son": the spoiled servant ends up claiming the place of a son. Others guess "grief" or "insolence." The point is clear either way: indulging someone without boundaries doesn't produce gratitude but presumption. JFB: he may "assume the place and privileges" of a son. Henry adds: kindness to a servant "does not mean indulgence."
- **Commentators:** Guzik is emphatic that v18 is about God's revealed word, not "visionary leadership," and Kidner, Waltke and Ross, all cited by him, agree. JFB says the same: the vision is "instruction in God's truth, which was by prophets." On v21 Guzik passes on Morgan's observation that kindness makes a bad servant arrogant and a good one devoted.

#### Anger, Pride and the Fear of Man (vv22–27)
- **"A man of wrath stirs up strife, and one given to anger causes much transgression" (v22).** "Given to anger" is literally "master of fury," a man whose temper runs him. Anger multiplies sin: harsh words, broken relationships, rash acts (15:18; Eph 4:26–27). Guzik passes on Waltke's description of the two Hebrew words: one for anger snorting out through the nostrils, the other for heat boiling inside.
- **"One's pride will bring him low, but he who is lowly in spirit will obtain honor" (v23).** A wordplay: pride (literally "height") makes a man "low," and the "low" in spirit are raised. The Tyndale notes list the many places Proverbs says this (11:2; 16:18; 18:12). Jesus said it outright: "whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted" (Luke 14:11). James and Peter both quote Proverbs 3:34: "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (Jas 4:6; 1 Pet 5:5). Henry: "Only those who humble themselves shall be exalted and established."
- **"The partner of a thief hates his own life; he hears the curse, but discloses nothing" (v24).** The background is Leviticus 5:1. When a theft was reported, a public curse was pronounced on anyone who knew something and kept quiet. The thief's partner hears the curse and stays silent, so he brings it on himself. He is guilty twice: of the theft and of perjury by silence. JFB: he "risks the punishment, rather than reveal truth." The Tyndale notes point to Leviticus 5:1.
- **"The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is safe" (v25).** "Fear" here is a word for trembling, panic. Worrying about what people think, or what they might do, catches people in a trap: they compromise, keep quiet, or lie. Abraham lied about Sarah out of fear (Gen 12:12–13). Aaron gave in to the crowd and made the calf (Exod 32:22–24). Saul admitted, "I feared the people and obeyed their voice" (1 Sam 15:24). Peter denied Jesus to a servant girl (Mark 14:66–72). Pilate handed Jesus over "wishing to satisfy the crowd" (Mark 15:15). "Safe" is literally "set on high," out of reach, like a fortress (18:10). The cure for fearing people is not courage in general but trust in the LORD. Jesus: "do not fear those who kill the body… fear him who… has authority to cast into hell" (Luke 12:4–5), which the Tyndale notes cite. The apostles: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
- **"Many seek the face of a ruler, but it is from the LORD that a man gets justice" (v26).** "Seek the face" means seek an audience, look for a favor. People line up to get the ruler's ear. But the ruler is not the last word. "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD" (21:1). JFB: "God alone will and can do exact justice." Guzik passes on Garrett's clarification that this doesn't forbid going to court, only trusting in human power above God.
- **"An unjust man is an abomination to the righteous, but one whose way is straight is an abomination to the wicked" (v27).** The Hezekiah collection ends on the great divide. Each side finds the other repulsive. This is not personal hatred but a clash of whole ways of life, as old as God's word to the serpent, "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring" (Gen 3:15). Jesus: "If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you" (John 15:18). Henry: righteous people abhor sin and avoid wicked company.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lists Saul, Aaron and Peter as men caught by the fear of man, and passes on Spurgeon's point about Pilate and his reassurance that a believer may not always feel safe, but is. On v27 Guzik passes on Trapp's distinction: the righteous hate sin, not the sinner, as a doctor hates the disease, not the patient. Bridges, cited by Guzik, traces the enmity back to Genesis 3:15. Henry on v25: "Many are ashamed to own Christ now."

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 29 longs for a king who builds up the land by justice (v4), judges the poor in truth, and has a throne established forever (v14). Psalm 72 and Isaiah 11 turned that longing into a prophecy, and Jesus fulfills it, the Son of David who preached good news to the poor and whose kingdom has no end (Luke 1:32–33). He is the "vision" that keeps God's people from running wild (v18): "In many and various ways God spoke… by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:1–2). He was the blameless one the bloodthirsty hated (v10). He was "lowly in heart" (Matt 11:29) and was given "the name that is above every name" (v23; Phil 2:8–9). And he freed his people from the snare of the fear of man (v25), so that the same Peter who trembled before a servant girl could tell the council, "We must obey God rather than men."
