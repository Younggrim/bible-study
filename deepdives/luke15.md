*Setting:* About AD 29–30, still on the long journey to Jerusalem that began in 9:51, probably in Perea, east of the Jordan. Chapter 14 ended with Jesus telling the crowds what it costs to follow him: "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (14:35). The people who seem to hear best are the ones respectable society had written off. Chapter 15 opens with a complaint about the company Jesus keeps, and he answers with three stories about something lost, a sheep, a coin and a son, each one ending in a party. The third story adds a second son who will not come to the party, and it ends without telling us whether he ever does.

#### The Parable of the Lost Sheep (vv1–7)
- **"The tax collectors and sinners were all drawing near to hear him" (v1).** Tax collectors bought the right to collect tolls and customs for Rome or for Herod's family, and many made their living by charging more than was owed. Their fellow Jews counted them traitors and cheats. "Sinners" means people known for openly breaking the law, or for not keeping the Pharisees' standards of purity. Luke says "all," and they come "to hear," which is exactly what 14:35 asked for.
- **"This man receives sinners and eats with them" (v2).** "Grumbled" is a strengthened form of the verb used for Israel grumbling against Moses in the wilderness (Exod 16:2 in the Greek Old Testament). Eating together meant acceptance and friendship, so sharing a table with sinners looked to the Pharisees like approving of them. Their complaint is meant as an insult, but it is one of the truest sentences in the Gospel. Levi's banquet drew the same complaint (5:30), and Zacchaeus's house will draw it again (19:7).
- **"So he told them this parable" (v3).** The word is singular. The three stories are one answer to one complaint, told to the grumblers, not to the tax collectors.
- **"What man of you, having a hundred sheep" (v4).** Jesus asks his hearers to picture themselves as shepherds, a trade many Pharisees looked down on as dirty and unreliable. A hundred sheep was a modest flock for a family. Leaving the ninety-nine "in the open country" would not mean abandoning them, since flocks were often shared and other shepherds could watch them. The point is the shepherd's single-minded search "until he finds it." Matthew gives the same parable in a different setting, about caring for believers who stray (Matt 18:12–14). Jesus seems to have used the story more than once.
- **The shepherd in Israel's Scriptures.** God called himself Israel's shepherd (Ps 23:1), and through Ezekiel he condemned the leaders who fed themselves and did not "seek the lost," then promised, "I myself will search for my sheep" (Ezek 34:4, 11, 16). The Pharisees would have known that passage. Jesus is doing what God promised to do, while the shepherds of Israel stand and complain.
- **"He lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing" (v5).** A lost sheep often lies down and will not move, so it must be carried, usually across the shoulders with its legs held in front. The sheep does nothing to get home. The shepherd carries the weight, and he does it gladly, not with irritation at the trouble.
- **"Rejoice with me" (v6).** Joy is the word that ties the chapter together (vv5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 23, 24, 32). Joy this big has to be shared.
- **"Ninety-nine righteous persons who need no repentance" (v7).** This is gentle irony. No one in Luke truly needs no repentance (5:31–32; 18:9–14). Jesus takes the Pharisees at their own estimate of themselves and says that even so, heaven is happier over one sinner coming home. Some readers take "righteous" more plainly, of those already in God's flock, but in context the sharper edge is hard to miss.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that a sheep has little sense of direction and is helpless to find its own way back, and that the rabbis taught God would receive a sinner who came to him properly, while this parable has God going out to search, an idea foreign to the Pharisees. Henry says the lost sheep stands for the sinner "departed from God... yet not desirous to return," and that "Christ is earnest in bringing sinners home." JFB takes the ninety-nine as those who have walked with God from childhood, not angels, and says the shepherd's joy is "almost too much for himself to bear alone."

#### The Parable of the Lost Coin (vv8–10)
- **"What woman, having ten silver coins" (v8).** The coin is a drachma, a Greek silver coin worth about a day's wage, close to the Roman denarius. Ten of them might have been her savings or part of her dowry. Some suggest they were strung together as a headdress worn by married women, though the evidence for that custom is late. Either way, one coin out of ten was a real loss for a poor household.
- **A man and then a woman.** Luke often pairs stories of a man and a woman: Simeon and Anna (2:25–38), the widow's son and the centurion's servant (7:1–17), the mustard seed and the leaven (13:18–21). Jesus has just asked the Pharisees to picture themselves as shepherds. Now he pictures God's seeking love through a woman sweeping her floor, which would have surprised them.
- **"Light a lamp and sweep the house and seek diligently" (v8).** A village house had small windows or none and a floor of packed earth or rough stone. A silver coin could hide in a crack or in the straw. The search takes light, effort and patience, and once again it lasts "until she finds it."
- **The loss grows.** The sheep was one in a hundred. The coin is one in ten. The son will be one in two. The stories move closer to home each time.
- **"Joy before the angels of God" (v10).** This is a reverent way of saying God himself rejoices, with the angels around him sharing it. Compare Zephaniah 3:17: the LORD "will rejoice over you with gladness." Heaven celebrates the very thing the Pharisees are grumbling about.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the three stories as one parable in three scenes, and he sees three ways of being lost: the sheep by wandering off, the coin by no fault of its own, and the son by deliberate rebellion. He also notes the shrinking proportions: one in a hundred, one in ten, one in two. JFB sees the lamp and sweeping as a picture of the effort and means God uses to recover sinners, and on v10 says the joy is God's own, which the angels share.

#### The Parable of the Prodigal Son (vv11–32)
- **"There was a man who had two sons" (v11).** The first line tells us the story is about two sons, not one. The traditional title, "the prodigal son," fits only half of it. Many readers prefer "the two lost sons" or "the waiting father." Scripture is full of older and younger brothers, and God often chooses the younger: Cain and Abel, Ishmael and Isaac, Esau and Jacob.
- **"Father, give me the share of property that is coming to me" (v12).** Under the law the firstborn received a double share (Deut 21:17), so the younger of two sons could expect a third. A father could divide property before his death, but the son normally would not ask, and the father kept the use of it while he lived. To demand it now was to treat his father as already dead, an insult in that culture. The word translated "property" in "divided his property" is *bios*, "life" or "livelihood." The father gives away part of his living.
- **"He divided his property between them" (v12).** "Between them" means the older son receives his share too, and the father does nothing to stop the younger one. He does not argue or chase him. He lets him go.
- **"Not many days later... a far country" (v13).** The son turns his share into cash, which means selling family land, a deep disgrace in Israel, where land was kept in the family (Lev 25:23; 1 Kgs 21:3). A far country means Gentile territory, out of reach of home.
- **"He squandered his property in reckless living" (v13).** "Squandered" is *diaskorpizō*, "scattered," the word for winnowing grain into the wind. "Reckless living" is *asōtōs*, from a word meaning "wasteful" or "past saving," used only here in the New Testament. The lexicon notes that it means "wastefully" and does not by itself mean sexual sin. The charge of prostitutes comes later, from the older brother (v30), who has no way of knowing. The text leaves it to him.
- **"A severe famine... he began to be in need" (v14).** Money and famine run out together. The friends that money buys do not last.
- **"Sent him into his fields to feed pigs" (v15).** Pigs were unclean animals under the law (Lev 11:7). A later rabbinic saying puts a curse on the man who raises pigs. A Jewish young man feeding a Gentile's pigs has fallen as low as he can go, outside the land, outside the law and outside his family.
- **"The pods that the pigs ate" (v16).** These are carob pods (*keration*, "little horn," from their curved shape). They were cheap fodder that poor people ate only in hard times. The carob seed was later used as a jeweler's weight, which gives English the word "carat." "No one gave him anything" means even pig food is out of his reach.
- **"But when he came to himself" (v17).** This was a common phrase for coming to one's senses, as if sin had made him a stranger to himself. His thinking is still about bread more than his father, and his motives are mixed. But he turns, and the turning is real.
- **"I have sinned against heaven and before you" (v18).** "Heaven" is a reverent Jewish way to say God, as in "the kingdom of heaven." His confession names both sins: against God and against his father. Pharaoh used almost the same words to Moses without meaning them (Exod 10:16). This son means them.
- **"Treat me as one of your hired servants" (v19).** A hired worker was a day laborer, lower than a household slave, who at least belonged to the family. He hopes to earn his keep and perhaps, in time, repay what he took. He plans to live near his father, not as his son.
- **"While he was still a long way off, his father saw him" (v20).** The father sees him first, which means he has been watching the road.
- **"Felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (v20).** "Felt compassion" is *splanchnizomai*, a gut-level pity, used in Luke for the Samaritan (10:33) and for Jesus at Nain (7:13). Older men in that culture did not run. They would have to hitch up their robes, which was undignified. Kenneth Bailey and others suggest the father also ran to reach his son before the village could shame him, though that is a reconstruction, not in the text. "Embraced him" is literally "fell on his neck," the same picture as Esau welcoming Jacob (Gen 33:4) and Joseph weeping on Jacob's neck (Gen 46:29). "Kissed him" can carry the sense of kissing again and again.
- **The rehearsed speech cut short (v21).** The son starts his speech, but he never gets to "treat me as one of your hired servants." Some manuscripts add that line here, and the ESV notes it. It looks like a scribe copying it in from v19. Without it, the father simply interrupts. He has already decided what his son will be.
- **"The best robe... a ring... shoes" (v22).** The best robe was likely the father's own, a mark of honor (compare Joseph's robe from Pharaoh, Gen 41:42). A ring could be a signet ring, a sign of family authority. Shoes marked a free man. Slaves went barefoot. Each gift answers the son's request to be a servant with a plain no. "Bring quickly" means it happens before anyone else can treat him as a disgrace.
- **"The fattened calf" (v23).** A calf kept and fed for a special feast would feed far more than a household. The whole village is being invited to celebrate.
- **"This my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found" (v24).** The two pairs gather up the whole chapter. "Lost" recalls the sheep and the coin. "Dead... alive" goes deeper. Paul uses the same language for salvation: "you were dead in... trespasses and sins" and God "made us alive together with Christ" (Eph 2:1, 5).
- **"Now his older son was in the field" (v25).** He has been working, as a dutiful son should. He hears music and dancing and has to ask a servant what is going on (v26). No one came to fetch him, or he did not wait to be fetched.
- **"Safe and sound" (v27).** The servant's word means "healthy," whole. The servant tells it plainly. The older son hears it as an outrage.
- **"He was angry and refused to go in. His father came out" (v28).** For a son to refuse to join his father's feast in front of the guests was its own public insult. The father answers it the same way he answered the first one: he goes out. He leaves his own party to plead with this son as well. "Entreated" is *parakaleō*, to urge or appeal.
- **"These many years I have served you" (v29).** "Served" is the verb for working as a slave (*douleuō*). He thinks of himself as a slave in his father's house, the very thing the younger son offered to become. He calls up his record ("I never disobeyed your command") and feels cheated ("you never gave me a young goat"). A goat was worth far less than a fattened calf. He does not call his father "Father."
- **"This son of yours" (v30).** He will not say "my brother." He also adds the prostitutes, a detail the story has not told us.
- **"Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours" (v31).** "Son" here is *teknon*, a tender word, "my child." The father reminds him that the inheritance is already his (v12). He has had his father's company and his father's goods all along, but he lived like a hired man waiting for wages.
- **"This your brother" (v32).** The father gently corrects "this son of yours." The joy was "fitting," literally "necessary." There was no other right response.
- **The story stops.** We never hear whether the older brother goes in. Jesus leaves the door open, and the Pharisees and scribes of v2 are standing outside it. The ending is theirs to write.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "It is bad, and the beginning of worse, when men look upon God's gifts as debts due to them." He traces the son's fall as a picture of sin: "A sinful state is a spending state," a wanting state and a slavish one, and the devil's service "is no better than feeding swine." On the older brother he says God's grace shines "almost as bright in his tender and gentle bearing with peevish saints" as in receiving prodigals. JFB describes the younger son as "weary of restraint, panting for independence," and on v20 notes the father does not say, "Let him come to Me and sue for pardon first." JFB calls the robe, ring and shoes "a threefold symbol of freedom and honor." Guzik observes that the father lets the son leave without forcing him, and that if someone had funded the son's wild living, he might never have come to himself. He contrasts the son's two requests: the demand to be given sent him away, while the humble request to be made a servant brought him home. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both stress that the older brother refuses to call the prodigal his brother, and the Tyndale notes point out that the parable is left open-ended on purpose.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus is not only telling stories about God's seeking love. He is the shepherd of Ezekiel 34, come "to seek and to save the lost" (19:10), and the table the Pharisees grumble about is the father's feast already begun. Some readers see a hint of Christ in the older brother too, by contrast: the true elder Son did not stay home and resent the lost. He went out to the far country himself, and paid the cost of bringing his brothers home.
