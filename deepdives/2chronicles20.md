*Setting:* Late in the reign of Jehoshaphat, king of Judah, probably around 853–850 BC. He has come home safely from the disastrous battle at Ramoth-gilead, where Ahab died (ch 18), and the prophet Jehu has rebuked him for his alliance with Ahab: "wrath has gone out against you from the LORD," yet "some good is found in you" (19:2–3). Since then he has traveled his kingdom calling people back to God and set up courts in every district (19:4–11). Now an alliance of three peoples from east and south of the Dead Sea invades. Almost everything in vv1–30 is found only in Chronicles, and it is the Chronicler's great showcase of the promise in Solomon's temple prayer and God's answer: "if my people… humble themselves, and pray and seek my face… then I will hear from heaven" (7:14). It is one of the best-loved chapters in the Old Testament for anyone who has faced something too big for them.

#### Jehoshaphat's Prayer (vv1–12)
- **"The Moabites and Ammonites, and with them some of the Meunites" (v1).** Moab and Ammon lay east of the Dead Sea and the Jordan, in modern Jordan. Both descended from Lot, Abraham's nephew (Gen 19:36–38). The Hebrew text actually says "some of the Ammonites" a second time, which makes little sense after "Ammonites." The ESV follows the ancient Greek translation in reading "Meunites," a desert people from the region of Edom, south of the Dead Sea, mentioned again in 26:7 and 1 Chronicles 4:41. Verses 10 and 22–23 call the third group "Mount Seir," another name for Edom (Gen 32:3; 36:8).
- **"A great multitude is coming against you from Edom, from beyond the sea" (v2).** "The sea" is the Dead Sea. Most Hebrew manuscripts read "from Aram" (Syria), but Aram was far to the north. In Hebrew, Aram and Edom differ by one letter, and the letters *resh* and *dalet* look almost identical. The ESV, with one Hebrew manuscript and most modern translations, reads "Edom," which fits the geography and the rest of the chapter.
- **"They are in Hazazon-tamar (that is, Engedi)" (v2).** Engedi, "spring of the young goat," is an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea, about 25 miles southeast of Jerusalem. David hid from Saul in its caves (1 Sam 24:1). Hazazon-tamar, "gravel of palms," was its older name (Gen 14:7). The invaders had come around the southern end of the Dead Sea, or across a shallow ford, and were climbing into Judah from the east. Jerusalem was only two days' march away.
- **"Then Jehoshaphat was afraid and set his face to seek the LORD" (v3).** His fear is honest. The question is what he does with it. Fear drives him to God, not away from him. "Set his face" means fixed his whole attention, like Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51).
- **"Proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah" (v3).** Fasting in the Old Testament goes with humbling oneself, mourning, repentance and urgent prayer (Judg 20:26; Joel 2:12–15; Ezra 8:21–23). Going without food says: this matters more than eating.
- **"From all the cities of Judah they came to seek the LORD" (v4).** "Seek" appears twice in one verse, and the whole nation responds. The teaching mission and judicial reforms of chapters 17 and 19 had prepared a people who knew where to turn.
- **"Jehoshaphat stood in the assembly… in the house of the LORD, before the new court" (v5).** He stands where Solomon stood to pray at the dedication (6:12–13). "The new court" was probably the temple's outer court, perhaps recently renovated. The king himself leads the prayer.
- **"O LORD, God of our fathers, are you not God in heaven? You rule over all the kingdoms of the nations" (v6).** He starts not with the problem but with who God is. Moab's god Chemosh and Ammon's god Milcom were thought to rule their own territories. Jehoshaphat confesses that the LORD rules them all. **"In your hand are power and might"** echoes David's great prayer (1 Chr 29:11–12).
- **"Did you not… give it forever to the descendants of Abraham your friend?" (v7).** Abraham is called God's "friend" only here, in Isaiah 41:8 and in James 2:23. The land was a gift sworn to Abraham (Gen 15:18–21; 17:8), and Jehoshaphat reminds God of his own promise.
- **"If disaster comes upon us… we will stand before this house… and cry out to you in our affliction, and you will hear and save" (v9).** A summary of Solomon's dedication prayer (6:28–30, 34–35) and of God's answer that he would hear prayer made toward this house (7:12–15). Jehoshaphat is doing exactly what that prayer described.
- **"The men of Ammon and Moab and Mount Seir, whom you would not let Israel invade when they came from the land of Egypt" (v10).** In the wilderness, God told Israel not to attack Edom, Moab or Ammon, because he had given those lands to Esau's and Lot's descendants (Deut 2:4–9, 19). Israel obeyed and went around them (Num 20:14–21). **"They reward us by coming to drive us out of your possession" (v11).** Jehoshaphat appeals to fairness and to God's own word: Israel spared them at your command, and now they repay it with invasion.
- **"O our God, will you not execute judgment on them?" (v12).** Literally "will you not judge them?" The king whose name means "the LORD judges," who has just appointed judges throughout the land (19:5–11), now asks the supreme Judge to act.
- **"For we are powerless against this great horde… We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you" (v12).** One of the most honest prayers in Scripture. Jehoshaphat had over a million soldiers on paper (17:14–18). Yet he admits weakness and ignorance and simply looks to God. Psalm 123:2 has the same image: "as the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master… so our eyes look to the LORD our God."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says, "Consciousness of guilt made him fear," but "Holy fear is a spur to prayer and preparation." He notes that the king "was the mouth of the congregation to God, and did not devolve the work upon his chaplains," and calls "our eyes are upon thee" "a sovereign remedy… an eye of acknowledgment and humble submission." Guzik, through Selman, explains "seek" as both worship and asking for God's guidance, and points out that Jehoshaphat argued from God's own words, as Spurgeon urged believers to do. He cites Adam Clarke's view that this is one of the most sensible and devout prayers in the Old Testament. JFB says the prayer "embraces every topic and argument" that could be urged, and identifies Hazazon-tamar as En-gedi from Genesis 14:7.

#### The Battle Is Not Yours (vv13–19)
- **"All Judah stood before the LORD, with their little ones, their wives, and their children" (v13).** Whole families stand in silence, waiting. Everyone has something at stake, and everyone looks to God together.
- **"The Spirit of the LORD came upon Jahaziel… a Levite of the sons of Asaph" (v14).** An unknown Levite, otherwise unmentioned, from the family of temple musicians founded by Asaph in David's time (1 Chr 25:1–2). Chronicles says these musicians "prophesied" with their instruments (1 Chr 25:1). His long family tree, five generations back, is given to show he belonged to that line. God's answer comes not through the king but through a worship leader in the crowd.
- **"Do not be afraid and do not be dismayed at this great horde, for the battle is not yours but God's" (v15).** Almost the words of Moses' charge for the priest to speak before battle: "do not fear or panic… for the LORD your God is he who goes with you to fight for you" (Deut 20:3–4). David said the same to Goliath: "the battle is the LORD's" (1 Sam 17:47).
- **"Tomorrow go down against them… by the ascent of Ziz… at the end of the valley, east of the wilderness of Jeruel" (v16).** God's word is very specific. The ascent of Ziz was probably one of the steep passes climbing from Engedi into the Judean wilderness. The places are not certainly known today. The precision mattered: it would prove the word came from God.
- **"You will not need to fight in this battle. Stand firm, hold your position, and see the salvation of the LORD on your behalf" (v17).** Almost exactly what Moses told Israel at the Red Sea: "Fear not, stand firm, and see the salvation of the LORD… The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent" (Exod 14:13–14). Notice that they still have to go out (v16). Faith is not staying home. It is going where God sends and letting him fight.
- **"Jehoshaphat bowed his head with his face to the ground… worshiping the LORD" (v18).** They worship before anything has happened. The promise is enough.
- **"The Levites, of the Kohathites and the Korahites, stood up to praise the LORD… with a very loud voice" (v19).** The Korahites were a branch of the Kohathite Levites, temple musicians and gatekeepers, credited with eleven psalms (Pss 42–49, 84–85, 87–88). Some scholars link Psalms 47 and 48, which celebrate God's victory over gathered kings, with this event. That is a guess, but a natural one.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that "the spirit of prophecy came upon a Levite that was present," and stresses that "the battle is God's" and Judah would "only stand still and see it." Guzik argues that standing still was not passive: Judah had to go out, take its position and trust, a partnership of faith. Through Spurgeon he observes that they worshiped before the victory because they believed God's word. JFB notes Jahaziel is "not elsewhere mentioned, but his claim to the inspiration of a prophetic spirit was verified," and places the ascent of Ziz on the pass "from En-gedi to Jerusalem."

#### God Delivers Judah (vv20–30)
- **"They rose early in the morning and went out into the wilderness of Tekoa" (v20).** Tekoa was about ten miles south of Jerusalem, on the edge of the barren wilderness sloping down to the Dead Sea. It was later the home of the prophet Amos (Amos 1:1).
- **"Believe in the LORD your God, and you will be established; believe his prophets, and you will succeed" (v20).** A deliberate echo of Isaiah's word to King Ahaz, though Isaiah came later: "If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all" (Isa 7:9). Both play on the Hebrew root *'aman*, from which we get "amen." "Believe" (*ha'aminu*) and "be established" (*te'amenu*) are the same verb: trust firmly and you will stand firm. Jehoshaphat's second line reflects his own lesson. Ahab refused to believe Micaiah and died (ch 18).
- **"When he had taken counsel with the people" (v21).** The king consults, but the decision is a remarkable one.
- **"He appointed those who were to sing to the LORD and praise him in holy attire, as they went before the army" (v21).** The choir goes out in front of the soldiers. If God did not act, they would be the first to die. "In holy attire" can also be translated "in the splendor of holiness." It may describe the singers' priestly robes, or the majesty of God they praised (1 Chr 16:29; Ps 29:2). Either way, they march as worshipers, not warriors.
- **"Give thanks to the LORD, for his steadfast love endures forever" (v21).** The great refrain of Israel's worship, sung when the ark came to Jerusalem (1 Chr 16:34, 41), when the glory filled the temple (5:13; 7:3, 6), and in every verse of Psalm 136. The returned exiles sang it when they laid the second temple's foundation (Ezra 3:11). "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal, covenant love.
- **"When they began to sing and praise, the LORD set an ambush" (v22).** The timing is the point. The Hebrew says "ambushers" without explaining who they were. Some think God stirred up one group of invaders to ambush another. Others think of angelic forces, like the angel who struck Sennacherib's army (2 Kgs 19:35). The text simply says the LORD set them.
- **"The men of Ammon and Moab rose against the inhabitants of Mount Seir… and when they had made an end of the inhabitants of Seir, they all helped to destroy one another" (v23).** The alliance turned on itself. The same thing happened in Gideon's battle, when "the LORD set every man's sword against his comrade" (Judg 7:22), and in Jonathan's attack (1 Sam 14:20). "Devoting them to destruction" (*charam*) is the word for total destruction, as in the conquest of Canaan.
- **"When Judah came to the watchtower of the wilderness… there were dead bodies lying on the ground; none had escaped" (v24).** From a lookout point over the wilderness, they see the battle is already over. They never drew a sword.
- **"They were three days in taking the spoil, it was so much" (v25).** The invaders had come prepared for a long campaign and loaded with goods. Some Hebrew manuscripts read "garments" and others "corpses" in this verse. The ESV's "clothing" follows the first.
- **"On the fourth day they assembled in the Valley of Beracah, for there they blessed the LORD" (v26).** *Berakah* means "blessing." The name has survived in Wadi Berekut, between Tekoa and Hebron. The place where the enemy fell became a place of praise.
- **"Returning to Jerusalem with joy… with harps and lyres and trumpets, to the house of the LORD" (vv27–28).** The story ends where it began, at the temple. They went out singing and came home singing.
- **"The fear of God came on all the kingdoms of the countries when they heard that the LORD had fought against the enemies of Israel" (v29).** As in 17:10, God's reputation, not Judah's army, kept the peace.
- **"So the realm of Jehoshaphat was quiet, for his God gave him rest all around" (v30).** The Chronicler's word "rest" again, the gift God gave to Solomon (1 Chr 22:9) and Asa (14:6–7; 15:15).
- **Is this a battle method?** Some Christians take "praise goes before the army" as a strategy for spiritual warfare. The text records a specific command for a specific day. Jahaziel's instructions were given for this battle, not as a standing rule. The lasting lessons are wider and deeper: the battle belongs to God, faith shows itself in obedience and praise before the answer comes, and God's steadfast love really does endure forever.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry on v20: "The heart is fixed that thus trusteth in the Lord, and is kept in perfect peace." On the enemies' self-destruction: "Those are wretchedly infatuated, to their ruin, that fall foul upon their friends as if they were enemies." Guzik stresses how much faith it took to put the singers in front, and warns, like the note above, against turning this into a formula. Through Selman, he suggests the "ambushers" may have been supernatural, and Trapp thought angels. JFB also reports that "some think that this was done by angels in human form," and locates Berachah ("benediction") at "Wady Bereikut."

#### The End of Jehoshaphat's Reign (vv31–37)
- **"He was thirty-five years old when he began to reign, and he reigned twenty-five years" (v31).** About 872–848 BC, counting a co-regency with Asa. His mother, Azubah daughter of Shilhi, is known only from this verse and 1 Kings 22:42.
- **"He walked in the way of Asa his father and did not turn aside from it" (v32).** Copied from Kings (1 Kgs 22:43). Asa's way at its best is meant.
- **"The high places, however, were not taken away" (v33).** Compare 17:6, where he "took the high places… out of Judah." As with Asa, the shrines to foreign gods were removed but some local altars stayed, or came back. The Chronicler adds his own explanation: **"the people had not yet set their hearts upon the God of their fathers."** A king can teach, reform and lead, but he cannot set other people's hearts. Jehoshaphat had set his own (19:3). Many of his people had not.
- **"The chronicles of Jehu the son of Hanani" (v34).** The prophet who rebuked Jehoshaphat (19:2) also wrote his history. The Chronicler trusted a source that was honest about the king's faults.
- **"Jehoshaphat… joined with Ahaziah king of Israel, who acted wickedly" (v35).** Ahaziah was Ahab's son and successor, who reigned only about two years and "served Baal and worshiped him" (1 Kgs 22:51–53). Jehoshaphat did not learn the whole lesson of chapters 18–19. The word "joined" (*chabar*) appears three times in vv35–37.
- **"Building ships to go to Tarshish… in Ezion-geber" (v36).** Ezion-geber was at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba on the Red Sea, where Solomon built his fleet (8:17–18). Tarshish was far west, probably in Spain, and could not be reached from the Red Sea. Kings calls them "ships of Tarshish to go to Ophir for gold" (1 Kgs 22:48). "Ships of Tarshish" was a name for large, ocean-going merchant ships, like "East Indiaman" in later English, so Chronicles' phrase probably describes the type of ship rather than the destination. Jehoshaphat was trying to revive Solomon's gold trade.
- **"Eliezer the son of Dodavahu of Mareshah prophesied against Jehoshaphat" (v37).** Another prophet known only here, from Mareshah in the western foothills (11:8; 14:9). **"Because you have joined with Ahaziah, the LORD will destroy what you have made."** God broke the ships, likely in a storm, before they ever sailed.
- **Kings and Chronicles together.** Kings says that after the ships were wrecked, Ahaziah offered to send his sailors with Jehoshaphat's, and "Jehoshaphat was not willing" (1 Kgs 22:49). Kings does not mention the earlier partnership, and Chronicles does not mention the refusal. Read together, Jehoshaphat joined Ahaziah in building the fleet, the prophet condemned it, the ships were destroyed, and this time Jehoshaphat learned and refused a second venture.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says the people "had not prepared their hearts to serve the God of their fathers" and "were not hearty in it," and notes that Jehoshaphat "saw and knew that Ahaziah, the son of Ahab, did very wickedly." His closing lesson: "A man may much better keep himself from being taken in the snare than recover himself out of it." Guzik, following Poole, lays the blame for the high places on the people more than the king, and sees God's wrecking of the ships as mercy that kept Jehoshaphat from going further into a bad alliance. JFB says the fleet was meant "to voyage to Tartessus," the Spanish Tarshish, and that Eliezer "denounced this unholy alliance." The Tyndale notes, like Guzik, see God destroying the fleet to stop the partnership from succeeding.

#### Pointing to Christ
"We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you." Judah stood helpless, heard that "the battle is not yours but God's," and went out singing of God's steadfast love before the victory came. At the cross, Jesus fought the decisive battle alone. His people did not need to fight; they were scattered, and he won without them. Through his death he disarmed the powers arrayed against his people "and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Col 2:15). Believers now go out not to win the victory but to collect its spoil, praising the God whose steadfast love endures forever. "Thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 15:57).
