*Setting:* Jerusalem, about 734 BC, a few years after the vision of chapter 6. Uzziah's grandson Ahaz is now king of Judah, a young man in his early twenties who "did not do what was right in the eyes of the LORD" and even burned his own son as an offering (2 Kgs 16:2–3). The great threat on the horizon is Assyria under Tiglath-pileser III, who was conquering his way west. The small kingdoms nearby, led by Rezin king of Syria (Aram, capital Damascus) and Pekah king of Israel (the northern kingdom, also called Ephraim, capital Samaria), formed an alliance to resist him. Ahaz refused to join, so they marched on Jerusalem to remove him and put a puppet king on his throne. Historians call this the Syro-Ephraimite war (2 Kgs 15:37; 16:5–9; 2 Chr 28:5–21). Chapters 7–12 are sometimes called "the book of Immanuel," and this chapter is where that name first appears.

#### Isaiah Sent to King Ahaz (vv1–9)
- **"Rezin the king of Syria and Pekah the son of Remaliah… came up to Jerusalem… but could not yet mount an attack" (v1).** Isaiah gives the outcome first: the siege failed. 2 Kings 16:5 says the same. But before that, Syria and Israel had already inflicted terrible losses on Judah, killing many thousands and taking captives (2 Chr 28:5–8). Ahaz had every reason to be afraid.
- **"Syria is in league with Ephraim" (v2).** "Ephraim" was the largest tribe of the northern kingdom and often stands for the whole of it. The phrase may mean "Syria has camped in Ephraim," that is, the Syrian army is already in Israel's territory, a few days' march away.
- **"The heart of Ahaz and the heart of his people shook as the trees of the forest shake before the wind" (v2).** A vivid image of panic. The word "shook" (*nua'*) is used of trees swaying in a storm. Isaiah calls the royal family "the house of David" on purpose. God had promised David a lasting throne (2 Sam 7:16). The question of this chapter is whether David's heir will trust that promise.
- **"You and Shear-jashub your son" (v3).** Isaiah's son's name means "a remnant shall return." The name is a sermon in itself, both warning and hope: judgment will come, and only a remnant will be left, but a remnant *will* return (10:20–22). Isaiah and his children were "signs and portents in Israel" (8:18).
- **"At the end of the conduit of the upper pool on the highway to the Washer's Field" (v3).** The "Washer's Field" (older translations "Fuller's Field") was where cloth was washed and bleached, which needed plenty of water. Ahaz was probably inspecting the city's water supply to get ready for a siege, the most practical thing a king could do. God sends Isaiah to meet him right there. About thirty years later, the Assyrian field commander would stand at the very same spot to demand Hezekiah's surrender (36:2). The father failed the test there, and the son passed it.
- **"Be careful, be quiet, do not fear" (v4).** "Be quiet" means stay calm and do not do anything rash, in other words, do not run to Assyria for help. Isaiah says the same later: "In quietness and in trust shall be your strength" (30:15).
- **"These two smoldering stumps of firebrands" (v4).** A firebrand is a burning stick. These two are nearly burned out, just smoking ends. Rezin and Pekah look terrifying, but they are almost finished. Within two years both would be dead: Tiglath-pileser took Damascus and killed Rezin in 732 BC (2 Kgs 16:9), and Pekah was assassinated about the same time (2 Kgs 15:30). Isaiah calls Pekah only "the son of Remaliah," a contemptuous way of not even giving his name. He was a usurper, not a king of a royal line.
- **"Set up the son of Tabeel as king" (v6).** This man is otherwise unknown. The name sounds Aramean. The plan was to end David's dynasty in Jerusalem, a direct attack on God's promise to David.
- **"It shall not stand, and it shall not come to pass" (v7).** God's verdict on their plan is short and final. People plan; the LORD decides (Prov 19:21).
- **"The head of Syria is Damascus, and the head of Damascus is Rezin" (vv8–9).** The point is in what is left unsaid. These nations are headed by mere men. The implied third line is: and the head of Judah is Jerusalem, and the head of Jerusalem is the LORD.
- **"Within sixty-five years Ephraim will be shattered from being a people" (v8).** Samaria fell in 722 BC, twelve years later. But the northern tribes were finally ended as a people when the Assyrian kings Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal resettled the land with foreigners, about 670 BC (2 Kgs 17:24; Ezra 4:2, 10), roughly sixty-five years after this prophecy. Some scholars think the number was added later; others see it as a precise prediction.
- **"If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all" (v9).** The key verse of the chapter, and a wordplay. Both verbs come from the Hebrew root *'aman*, the root of "amen," meaning firm, steady, reliable. *Im lo ta'aminu, ki lo te'amenu*: "If you will not stand firm (in faith), you will not stand firm (at all)." An old English rendering keeps the rhyme: "If you will not confide, you shall not abide." The "you" is plural, addressed to the whole house of David. Faith is not just an inner feeling. It means leaning your full weight on God's word, and the alternative is collapse.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read the firebrands as all smoke and no fire. Guzik quotes the Puritan John Trapp: the two kings had "more pride than power." JFB explains that Pekah is named only as "son of Remaliah" in contempt of a usurper. JFB ties the sixty-five years to the later Assyrian resettlement of the north. Guzik notes that Judah's deliverance was certain, but Ahaz's own security depended on whether he would believe. Matthew Henry explains Shear-jashub's name as a sign that the captives taken by the two kings would come home (2 Chr 28:8–15).

#### The Sign of Immanuel (vv10–25)
- **"Ask a sign of the LORD your God; let it be deep as Sheol or high as heaven" (v11).** An astonishing offer. God invites a faithless king to name any sign he likes, from the depths of the earth to the heights of heaven, as proof. "The LORD *your* God": God still claims Ahaz as his own.
- **"I will not ask, and I will not put the LORD to the test" (v12).** It sounds pious. Deuteronomy 6:16 does say, "You shall not put the LORD your God to the test," and Jesus quoted it (Matt 4:7). But testing God means demanding proof he has not offered. Refusing a sign God *has* offered is unbelief dressed as reverence. Ahaz had already decided to trust Assyria. 2 Kings 16:7–8 records his message to Tiglath-pileser: "I am your servant and your son. Come up and rescue me," with silver and gold from the temple as payment. He did not want a sign that would oblige him to trust God instead.
- **"Is it too little for you to weary men, that you weary my God also?" (v13).** Isaiah's tone changes. In v11 it was "your God." Now it is "my God." Ahaz has distanced himself from the LORD. "O house of David" is plural again: the sign that follows is for the whole royal line, not only for Ahaz.
- **"Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign" (v14).** "You" is plural. Ahaz refused to choose a sign, so God chooses one himself.
- **"Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son" (v14).** The Hebrew word is *'almah*. It means a young woman of marriageable age, a maiden. It appears only seven times in the Old Testament (for example Rebekah before her marriage, Gen 24:43, and Moses' sister, Exod 2:8), and it is never clearly used of a married woman. It is not the most technical word for a virgin, which is *betulah*. But in that culture a maiden was assumed to be a virgin. About two centuries before Christ, the Jewish translators of the Septuagint rendered it *parthenos*, the normal Greek word for "virgin," and Matthew quotes that translation (Matt 1:23). The Hebrew has the definite article, "*the* maiden," a particular young woman.
- **How is it fulfilled?** Faithful Christians read this in a few ways. (1) *A single, direct prophecy of Christ.* The sign is the virgin birth of Jesus, and the timing words in vv15–16 mean "before a child conceived now could grow up." (2) *A near sign with a greater fulfilment.* A young woman in Isaiah's day, perhaps a royal bride, perhaps Isaiah's own wife (8:3), would bear a son, and before he was old enough to know right from wrong, Syria and Israel would be gone. That birth was a real sign to Ahaz. But the name "God with us," and the way Isaiah develops this child in 8:8, 9:6–7 and 11:1–5 as a divine king on David's throne, points beyond any ordinary baby. Matthew sees the full meaning in Jesus, conceived by the Holy Spirit in a virgin's womb. Many evangelical scholars hold this second view, and the Tyndale notes favor it. Either way, Matthew's reading is not an afterthought. Isaiah himself pushes the child beyond his own time.
- **"And shall call his name Immanuel" (v14).** *Immanu-El* means "with us is God." It is not a name anyone in the Gospels calls Jesus. It describes who he is. In Isaiah's day the name was both comfort and rebuke: God is with us, so why run to Assyria? In Matthew it frames the whole Gospel, from "God with us" (1:23) to the last line, "I am with you always" (28:20).
- **"He shall eat curds and honey when he knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good" (v15).** Curds are thick soured milk, like yogurt. This sounds like rich food, but vv21–22 explain it: the land will be so emptied of people and farms that the few left will live on what grazing animals and wild bees provide. The child will grow up in the aftermath of invasion.
- **"Before the boy knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good, the land whose two kings you dread will be deserted" (v16).** Within a few years, a child's lifetime of learning right from wrong, Syria and Israel will be devastated. Tiglath-pileser destroyed Damascus and stripped Israel of its northern territories by 732 BC (2 Kgs 15:29; 16:9), about two years later.
- **"The LORD will bring upon you… the king of Assyria!" (v17).** The great twist. Ahaz has hired Assyria as his rescuer, and God says Assyria will become his worst disaster, days worse than anything "since the day that Ephraim departed from Judah," when the kingdom split two centuries earlier (1 Kgs 12). The words "the king of Assyria" come at the end of the sentence like a thunderclap. Ahaz did get his rescue, but 2 Chronicles 28:20–21 says Tiglath-pileser "afflicted him instead of strengthening him."
- **"The LORD will whistle for the fly… and for the bee" (vv18–19).** "Whistle" again (5:26). The fly comes from Egypt, the land of the Nile's many streams, where flies swarmed. The bee comes from Assyria. Judah will be caught between the two great powers, and the invaders will settle everywhere, in ravines, caves and thornbushes, with no hiding place left (2:19).
- **"The Lord will shave with a razor that is hired beyond the River" (v20).** Bitter irony: Ahaz *hired* Assyria with temple gold, and God says that hired razor will shave Judah. "The River" is the Euphrates. Shaving a man's head and beard was the ultimate humiliation, done to prisoners and slaves (2 Sam 10:4–5). "The hair of the feet" is a polite expression for the hair of the lower body. Judah will be stripped of everything.
- **"A man will keep alive a young cow and two sheep" (vv21–22).** A household reduced to a single cow and a couple of sheep. With so few people left and so much empty pasture, the animals will give more milk than the survivors can use, so "everyone who is left in the land will eat curds and honey." It sounds like plenty but is a sign of emptiness, the same diet as the child of v15.
- **"Every place where there used to be a thousand vines, worth a thousand shekels of silver, will become briers and thorns" (vv23–25).** A shekel per vine shows how valuable a mature vineyard was. Now it is wilderness, good only for hunting with bow and arrows and for letting cattle roam. The vineyard of 5:1–7 has become exactly what God warned: "briers and thorns shall grow up" (5:6).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads the sign as pointing to Christ "born of a virgin," and he links the timing of v16 to Isaiah's son Shear-jashub, the boy standing there with them. Before *he* could tell good from evil, Syria and Israel "shall be forsaken of both their kings." JFB calls Ahaz's refusal a "hypocritical pretext of keeping the law," and observes that "men often excuse their distrust in God" by professed reverence. JFB holds a double fulfilment, a temporary one in the prophet's day and the "fullest, most appropriate, and exhaustive accomplishment" in Christ, but insists that the name Immanuel "cannot in a strict sense apply to Isaiah's son." JFB also notes that the "hired" razor alludes to the payment Ahaz sent Tiglath-pileser. Guzik also sees a near and a far fulfilment, and points out that the Septuagint translators, long before Christ, chose the word "virgin." He argues that Ahaz refused the sign because a fulfilled sign would have left him no excuse not to believe.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ahaz was the heir of David who would not trust God. Faced with danger, he leaned on the empire of his day and lost his kingdom's freedom. In his place God gave a sign that Ahaz did not ask for and did not deserve: a son, born of a maiden, whose name was "God with us." Seven centuries later Joseph, another "son of David" (Matt 1:20), was told the same thing in a dream, and he believed where Ahaz had not. The child born in Bethlehem is the true heir of David's house, the one who did not just bring God's presence but was God present in a human body. In him the promise of v9 is turned around: the one who stands firm in faith does stand firm, because God is with us.
