*Setting:* Still at Sinai, just before the march. Chapters 1–4 put the camp in order: tribes counted and placed, Levites assigned around the tabernacle. Chapters 5–6 turn from the camp's shape to its purity. If the holy God lives in the middle of Israel, then what happens inside the camp matters: uncleanness, wrongs done between neighbors, and secret unfaithfulness in a marriage. The three laws in this chapter look unrelated, but one Hebrew word ties the last two together. The same verb, "to break faith," is used both for wronging a neighbor (v6) and for a wife's unfaithfulness (v12). Sin against people is a breach of trust with God, and the camp cannot be holy while it festers.

#### Unclean People (vv1–4)
- **"Everyone who is leprous or has a discharge… unclean through contact with the dead" (v2).** These are the three major kinds of ritual uncleanness already set out in Leviticus. "Leprous" covers a range of contagious-looking skin diseases, not only what we call leprosy today (Lev 13–14). "Discharge" refers to bodily fluids from the sexual organs (Lev 15), the condition of the woman Jesus healed in Luke 8:43–48. Contact with a corpse is treated fully in chapter 19.
- **Uncleanness is not the same as sin.** Touching a dead body was often a duty of love, such as burying a parent. A person with a skin disease had done nothing wrong to get it. The point is not that these people were wicked, but that death and decay, the effects of sin in the world, could not sit side by side with the living God. The Tyndale notes stress that the main concern was not hygiene, though the rules also protected health, but holiness.
- **"Both male and female" (v3).** The rule fell on everyone equally.
- **"That they may not defile their camp, in the midst of which I dwell" (v3).** This is the reason for everything in the chapter. God is not a distant ruler. He lives in the camp. Leviticus had already applied this rule to the sanctuary. Now that Israel is about to march, it is extended to the whole camp. Being "outside the camp" was usually temporary, until the person was healed or purified, but it was lonely.
- **"As the LORD said to Moses, so the people of Israel did" (v4).** Obedience again, as at the end of chapters 1 and 2.
- **Commentators:** Guzik is careful to say these conditions did not mark someone as a notorious sinner, though people often assumed so. He compares them to the sin nature inherited from Adam: no one chooses it, and God deals with it, not only with individual sins. JFB treats the rule as partly sanitary but chiefly about keeping the camp worthy of its King. Henry calls it "an order, pursuant to the laws already made, for the removing of the unclean out of the camp."

#### Confession and Restitution (vv5–10)
- **"Breaking faith with the LORD" (v6).** The Hebrew is literally "to act unfaithfully an unfaithfulness" (*ma'al*), the word for a breach of trust. The case in view is a wrong done to another person, such as theft, fraud or a false oath about property (Lev 6:1–7). The law calls it treachery against God. David says the same in Psalm 51:4: "Against you, you only, have I sinned." Wronging a neighbor is never merely a private matter.
- **"And that person realizes his guilt" (v6).** Literally "and that person is guilty." The ESV reads it as the moment the conscience wakes up. This law is about the sinner who comes forward, not one caught in court.
- **"He shall confess his sin" (v7).** Leviticus 6 gave the restitution law. Numbers adds confession in so many words. Repentance starts by naming the wrong.
- **"Make full restitution… adding a fifth to it" (v7).** Pay back everything, plus twenty percent. Sorrow is not enough. The wronged person must actually be made whole, and the extra fifth both compensates for the loss and makes wrongdoing costly. Zacchaeus went far beyond this when he offered to repay fourfold (Luke 19:8), the rate for a stolen sheep (Exod 22:1).
- **"If the man has no next of kin" (v8).** The word is *go'el*, the "redeemer" or kinsman responsible to stand up for a family member, like Boaz in Ruth. If the wronged man has died and has no *go'el* to receive the payment, the debt does not disappear. It **"shall go to the LORD for the priest."** There is no escaping restitution because the victim is gone.
- **"In addition to the ram of atonement" (v8).** Making things right with the neighbor and making things right with God both had to happen. The guilt offering (Lev 5:14–6:7) dealt with the offense against God.
- **"Whatever anyone gives to the priest shall be his" (vv9–10).** These verses close the section by making clear that gifts brought to a particular priest belonged to that priest. The priests had no land and lived on what was given (18:8–20).
- **Commentators:** All three see the double direction of sin. JFB calls a wrong against a neighbor also "a trespass against the Lord," and the added fifth both "compensation" and a deterrent. Guzik notes that the law's answer to theft was restitution, not prison, and that making restitution mattered for the sinner's own standing with God, not only for the victim. The Tyndale notes put it simply: being right with God leads to being right with others.

#### A Test for Adultery (vv11–31)
- **The situation (vv12–14).** A husband suspects his wife of adultery, but there is no witness and she was not caught. Under the law, adultery proved by witnesses was punished by death for both partners (Lev 20:10; Deut 22:22), so it was not a small charge. Notice v14: the "spirit of jealousy" may come over a man whose wife is guilty, **"or… though she has not defiled herself."** The law openly allows that the husband may be wrong.
- **Why such a law?** In much of the ancient world, a suspicious husband could simply divorce his wife or take matters into his own hands. This law takes the case out of his hands and puts it before the LORD. He cannot punish her himself. He must bring her to the priest, and God decides. The Tyndale notes call it a trial by ordeal, the only one in the Old Testament, though such ordeals were common in the region. Hammurabi's laws, for example, told a wife accused without proof to "leap into the river" for her husband; if she sank she was guilty. In that kind of ordeal the innocent could easily die. Here the reverse is true. Holy water with a little dust in it harms no one. Only a direct act of God could bring the curse. The test was weighted toward clearing the innocent.
- **"A tenth of an ephah of barley flour… no oil… no frankincense" (v15).** About two quarts of the cheapest grain, barley, the food of the poor and of animals, not the fine wheat flour of most grain offerings. No oil, a sign of joy, and no frankincense, a sign of pleasing fragrance. This offering is not a celebration. It is **"a grain offering of remembrance, bringing iniquity to remembrance,"** calling on God to bring to light whatever is hidden.
- **"Holy water in an earthenware vessel… dust that is on the floor of the tabernacle" (v17).** The water was probably from the bronze basin (JFB says from the laver). The dust from the sanctuary floor carried the holiness of the place. Dust is also the sign of the curse on sin (Gen 3:14, 19). The cheap clay pot fits the humbling of the scene.
- **"Unbind the hair of the woman's head" (v18).** Loosened hair was a sign of mourning or disgrace (Lev 10:6; 13:45). She stands before God exposed.
- **"The water of bitterness that brings the curse" (v18).** The water is not bitter because of anything in it. It is "bitter" in its possible effect: it brings the curse, if she is guilty.
- **The oath (vv19–22).** The priest first states the innocent outcome: **"be free from this water"** (v19). Only then does he state the curse (vv20–21). The woman answers **"Amen, Amen"** (v22), "so be it." She accepts God's verdict either way.
- **"Your thigh fall away and your body swell" (vv21–22, 27).** "Thigh" is a polite word for the reproductive organs (Gen 24:2; 47:29). Exactly what was meant is uncertain. Suggestions include a miscarriage, a prolapsed womb, or lifelong infertility. The Tyndale notes take it as infertility, which fits v28, where the innocent woman's reward is that she **"shall conceive children."** The punishment matches the sin.
- **"Write these curses in a book and wash them off into the water" (v23).** The priest wrote the curses on a scroll and rinsed the ink into the water, so that she literally drank the words. The Tyndale notes compare Ezekiel and John eating a scroll (Ezek 2:8–3:3; Rev 10:8–11).
- **The memorial portion (vv25–26).** The barley offering was waved before the LORD and a handful burned on the altar, the same "memorial portion" as other grain offerings (Lev 2:2). The case is placed, formally, in God's hands.
- **"She shall be free and shall conceive children" (v28).** For an innocent woman, the test ended the suspicion publicly and for good. Her husband could no longer accuse her, and her name was cleared before the whole community.
- **"The man shall be free from iniquity" (v31).** A husband who brought an honest suspicion to God was not guilty for doing so, even if he turned out to be wrong. Some readers see a double standard, since no matching procedure is given for a wife who suspects her husband. That is a fair observation. The Tyndale notes answer that the rest of Israel's law punished both partners in adultery equally (Lev 20:10), and Guzik suggests that the law, given case by case, would presumably apply the same principle in reverse. Either way, the law put real limits on male jealousy in a world that rarely did.
- **What happened to this law?** There is no example of it in the Old Testament. Jewish tradition (the Mishnah's tractate *Sotah*) discusses it at length and says it was stopped in the first century, when adultery had become so common that the test was considered pointless.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the law was given so that an innocent wife "might not continue under the reproach and uneasiness of her husband's jealousy," while a guilty one would be exposed so that others "might hear, and fear, and take warning." He adds that "none need fear the curse of the law if they have not broken the commands of the law," and warns husbands against "causeless or unjust suspicions." Guzik stresses that the law protected women in an honor-and-shame culture from being killed or thrown out on suspicion, and that judgment, if it came, came from God alone. JFB calls the dust "an emblem of vileness and misery," and sees in this law God giving Israel a corrected form of a widespread Eastern practice. JFB also reads the double "Amen" as her willingness to accept the consequences if guilty.

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole chapter is about a camp where God lives and where sin cannot stay hidden. Uncleanness had to go outside the camp; wrongs had to be named and repaid; secret unfaithfulness was brought before God's judgment in a cup. Israel itself is pictured again and again as God's unfaithful wife (Hos 2; Ezek 16), and every one of us stands where the guilty woman stood. Jesus took all three on himself. He touched lepers and the dead and the woman with a discharge, and instead of becoming unclean he made them clean (Mark 1:41; 5:27–41). He "suffered outside the gate" (Heb 13:12), outside the camp, to sanctify his people. And in Gethsemane he took the cup of God's judgment and drank it for the unfaithful (Matt 26:39), so that the bride could be presented "without spot or wrinkle" (Eph 5:27).
