*Setting:* About AD 46–48. Barnabas and Saul have just come back to Antioch in Syria from delivering famine relief to Jerusalem, bringing young John Mark with them (12:25). Antioch is now the second center of the church: a mixed congregation of Jews and Gentiles, the place where believers were first called Christians (11:26). Up to now the gospel has spread mostly as believers were scattered by persecution. In this chapter a church deliberately sends missionaries for the first time. It is the start of what is usually called Paul's first missionary journey, which runs through chapter 14: across to Cyprus, then up onto the high plateau of central Asia Minor (modern Turkey). From here on Acts is mostly Paul's story.

#### Barnabas and Saul Sent Off (vv1–3)
- **"Prophets and teachers" (v1).** Prophets brought messages from God, and teachers explained the Scriptures and the apostles' teaching. Luke lists five men, and the list is a picture of the church's mix. **Barnabas** was a Levite from Cyprus (4:36). **Simeon who was called Niger** has a Jewish name and a Latin nickname meaning "black," so he was probably African. Some have wondered whether he is Simon of Cyrene, who carried Jesus' cross (Luke 23:26), but Luke spells the names differently and does not connect them. **Lucius of Cyrene** came from North Africa and was probably one of the founders of the Antioch church (11:20). **Manaen** (the Hebrew name Menahem) was "a lifelong friend of Herod the tetrarch," literally "brought up with," a childhood companion at court of the Herod Antipas who beheaded John the Baptist. The Tyndale notes suggest he may have been one of Luke's sources for Herod's thoughts in the Gospel (Luke 9:7–9; 23:8). Last is **Saul**, the former persecutor.
- **"While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting" (v2).** "Worshiping" is *leitourgeō*, the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the service of priests and Levites, from which English gets "liturgy." The lexicon notes that in the New Testament it describes ministry carried out in an office, here service offered directly to the Lord. These leaders were not planning a campaign. They were worshiping, and the direction came in the middle of it.
- **"Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them" (v2).** The Holy Spirit speaks as a person, with "me" and "I." The call had already been given ("I have called"). Saul had been told at his conversion that he would carry Christ's name to the Gentiles (9:15), and Paul later says God "set me apart before I was born" (Gal 1:15) and that he was "set apart for the gospel of God" (Rom 1:1). The church does not create the call. It recognizes it. Notice too who is sent: the church's two best teachers. Antioch gave away its strongest men.
- **"After fasting and praying they laid their hands on them and sent them off" (v3).** The laying on of hands was not ordination to a higher rank. It identified the church with them and commissioned them to a task, as the seven were commissioned in 6:6. The last word is literally "released them." When they come home, Luke says they returned to the place "where they had been commended to the grace of God for the work that they had fulfilled" (14:26).
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks Barnabas is named "first… probably because he was the eldest, and Saul last, probably because he was the youngest," and observes that "religious fasting is of use in our ministering to the Lord." He reads the laying on of hands as their "discharge from the present service" in Antioch so they could take up a new one. Guzik notes that *leitourgeō* is the priestly word for temple service, and draws from it that worship is the first work of anyone who serves God, with service flowing from it. JFB is more cautious, saying the word "denotes the performance of official duties of any kind." The lexicon supports both: priestly in the Greek Old Testament, broader in ordinary Greek. Guzik also contrasts the two boys raised together at Herod's court: one became a leader in the church, the other killed John the Baptist.

#### Barnabas and Saul on Cyprus (vv4–12)
- **"Being sent out by the Holy Spirit" (v4).** The church sent them (v3), and Luke says the Spirit sent them. Both are true. They walked about sixteen miles to **Seleucia**, Antioch's port at the mouth of the Orontes, and sailed southwest to **Cyprus**, Barnabas's home island, about 60 miles offshore.
- **"At Salamis, they proclaimed the word of God in the synagogues of the Jews" (v5).** Salamis was the main harbor on the east coast and had several synagogues. Going to the synagogue first became Paul's pattern in every new town (13:14; 14:1; 17:1–2). There he found people who knew the Scriptures and were waiting for the Messiah, along with Gentile "God-fearers" already drawn to Israel's God. **"They had John to assist them"** uses *hypēretēs*, an assistant or attendant. Mark probably handled practical arrangements, and possibly helped teach the stories of Jesus.
- **"As far as Paphos" (v6).** They crossed about 90 miles, the length of the island, to Paphos on the west coast, the seat of the Roman governor. Nearby Old Paphos had a famous shrine to Aphrodite, the goddess of love. Here they meet **"a Jewish false prophet named Bar-Jesus,"** a magician attached to the governor's household. Roman officials often kept astrologers and diviners close. "Bar-Jesus" means "son of Jesus" (or "son of Joshua"), a common Jewish name.
- **"The proconsul, Sergius Paulus, a man of intelligence" (v7).** Luke's title is exactly right. Cyprus was a senatorial province, governed by a *proconsul*, and had been since 22 BC. Inscriptions mention a Lucius Sergius Paullus as a Roman official under Claudius, and others show that the Sergii Paulli family owned land around Pisidian Antioch, which some scholars think explains why Paul headed there next. That is a reasonable guess, not a fact in the text. "Intelligence" means a thoughtful, discerning man, and he "sought to hear the word of God."
- **"Elymas the magician (for that is the meaning of his name)" (v8).** Luke means that Elymas is a title meaning "magician" or "sorcerer." It is probably from a Semitic root, perhaps the Arabic *'alim*, "wise man," or an Aramaic word for a dream-interpreter. Scholars are not sure. What is clear is his motive: if the governor believed, the magician lost his place.
- **"But Saul, who was also called Paul" (v9).** Saul is the Hebrew name, after Israel's first king from his own tribe of Benjamin (Phil 3:5). Paul, Latin *Paullus*, was his Roman name. He did not get it at his conversion: as a Roman citizen from birth (22:28) he likely carried both all his life, using whichever suited the setting. Luke switches here, in front of a Roman official who happens to share the name, and never switches back except when Paul retells his conversion. From now on Paul leads. In verse 13 it is "Paul and his companions," and the pair is usually "Paul and Barnabas."
- **"Filled with the Holy Spirit, looked intently at him" (v9).** This is not a temper flaring. Luke stresses the Spirit, as he did with Peter facing Simon the magician (8:20–23). **"You son of the devil"** (v10) is a sharp play on "Bar-Jesus": this "son of Jesus" is really a son of the devil. **"Making crooked the straight paths of the Lord"** reverses Isaiah 40:3, the verse John the Baptist lived out: "make straight in the desert a highway for our God." Elymas is doing the opposite of preparing the Lord's way. Hosea 14:9 has the same picture: "the ways of the LORD are right… but transgressors stumble in them."
- **"You will be blind and unable to see the sun for a time" (v11).** The judgment fits the sin: a man who blinded others to the truth is blinded. It is also strikingly like Paul's own story. Paul too was struck blind and had to be "led by the hand" (9:8), and a related rare word appears for Elymas "seeking people to lead him by the hand." **"For a time"** leaves room for mercy, as Paul's own blindness had ended in sight and faith. Luke does not tell us whether Elymas ever repented.
- **"Then the proconsul believed… for he was astonished at the teaching of the Lord" (v12).** The miracle got his attention, but Luke says what amazed him was the *teaching*. Some have questioned whether he truly believed, since Luke mentions no baptism, but Luke uses the same plain "believed" for every convert in Acts. The first named convert of Paul's journeys is a Roman governor.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Sergius Paulus "a very sensible man" and notes that Elymas "was only struck blind, when he might most justly have been struck dead." He thinks Paul took the name Paul as "a very common name among them," that is, among the Romans. JFB similarly calls the name "a softening of his former name, in accommodation to Roman ears." Guzik notes that "Paulus" means "little" and passes on Ramsay's view, reported in a later writer, that inscriptions suggest the proconsul's family became Christians. That is possible but debated, and the inscriptions themselves do not say so. Guzik stresses that leading someone else away from God is among the gravest sins in Scripture.

#### Paul and Barnabas at Antioch in Pisidia (vv13–52)
- **"John left them and returned to Jerusalem" (v13).** They sailed northwest to Perga, near the south coast of Asia Minor, and there Mark went home. Luke gives no reason. Suggestions include homesickness, fear of the dangerous mountain road ahead, unease at the turn to Gentiles, or resentment that his cousin Barnabas was no longer the leader. Paul took it hard and later refused to take Mark again (15:37–39), but the story ends in restoration: years later Paul calls Mark "very useful to me for ministry" (2 Tim 4:11).
- **"Antioch in Pisidia" (v14).** Not the Syrian Antioch they left from, but a Roman colony high on the plateau beyond the Taurus Mountains, over 3,500 feet up and about 100 miles inland by a hard road through bandit country (compare "danger from robbers," 2 Cor 11:26). Paul tells the Galatians he first preached to them "because of a bodily ailment" (Gal 4:13), and some scholars, following Ramsay, suggest he caught malaria on the coast and climbed to the highlands to recover. That is an educated guess.
- **"After the reading from the Law and the Prophets" (v15).** A synagogue service had prayers, the Shema ("Hear, O Israel," Deut 6:4), a reading from the five books of Moses and one from the Prophets, then an explanation. Visiting teachers could be invited to give a "word of encouragement," literally "word of exhortation," the same phrase that describes the letter to the Hebrews (Heb 13:22). Paul's sermon is the longest of his recorded speeches to a Jewish audience, and Luke gives it as a sample of how Paul preached in synagogues.
- **"Men of Israel and you who fear God" (v16).** The sermon has three movements, each opened by an address: "Men of Israel" (v16), "Brothers" (v26), and "brothers" again (v38). The audience is Jews and the Gentile God-fearers who worshiped with them.
- **Israel's story, from Egypt to David (vv17–22).** Paul starts on common ground, the history every hearer knew. **"With uplifted arm he led them out"** (v17) is the language of Exodus 6:6. **"For about forty years he put up with them in the wilderness"** (v18) has a famous one-letter variant. Many manuscripts read *etropophorēsen*, "put up with their ways," and others read *etrophophorēsen*, "carried them like a nurse," the word the Greek Old Testament uses in Deuteronomy 1:31. JFB prefers the second, many modern editors the first, and both are true of God in the wilderness. **"Seven nations"** (v19) are those listed in Deuteronomy 7:1.
- **"All this took about 450 years" (v20).** In the oldest manuscripts, followed by the ESV, the 450 years cover the whole stretch from Egypt to the conquest: about 400 years in Egypt (Gen 15:13), forty in the wilderness, and several years of conquest. The King James Version, following later manuscripts, attaches the figure to the period of the judges, which clashes with the 480 years from the exodus to Solomon's temple in 1 Kings 6:1. The older reading removes the difficulty.
- **"Saul the son of Kish, a man of the tribe of Benjamin, for forty years" (v21).** The Old Testament text of 1 Samuel 13:1 is damaged at this point, and the figure of forty years appears also in Josephus. Paul, a Benjamite named after this king, passes over him quickly. **"When he had removed him, he raised up David"** (v22) leads to a quotation stitched from three places: "I have found David" (Ps 89:20), "a man after his own heart" (1 Sam 13:14), and "he shall fulfill all my purpose" (Isa 44:28). The Tyndale notes add that David was a man after God's heart despite serious sin (2 Sam 11–12).
- **"Of this man's offspring God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as he promised" (v23).** The whole history has been heading here: God's promise to David of a son whose kingdom would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16). Paul then points to **John the Baptist** (vv24–25), whom many Jews of the dispersion had heard of (compare the followers of John in Ephesus, 19:1–7), quoting John's own disclaimer: he was not even worthy to untie the coming one's sandals, a slave's job.
- **"To us has been sent the message of this salvation" (v26).** The second movement. "To us" includes the God-fearing Gentiles in the room.
- **"They… fulfilled them by condemning him" (v27).** This is the sermon's sharpest irony. The leaders in Jerusalem heard the prophets read every Sabbath, did not understand them, and then fulfilled them by condemning the very one they spoke of. **"They found in him no guilt worthy of death"** (v28) matches Luke's Gospel, where Pilate three times finds Jesus innocent (Luke 23:4, 14, 22). **"They took him down from the tree"** (v29) uses the word from Deuteronomy 21:23, "a hanged man is cursed by God," which Paul later explains in Galatians 3:13: Christ became a curse for us.
- **"But God raised him from the dead" (v30).** This is the center of everything. Paul appeals to **witnesses** (v31), the Galilean disciples who saw Jesus "for many days" (1:3; 1 Cor 15:5–8). Paul does not mention his own meeting with the risen Jesus here, perhaps because he is building on those who walked with Jesus from the beginning.
- **"You are my Son, today I have begotten you" (v33).** Psalm 2 was a coronation psalm for the king of David's line. Paul applies its "today" to the resurrection, the day God publicly declared and enthroned Jesus as his Son. Paul says the same in Romans 1:4: Jesus "was declared to be the Son of God in power… by his resurrection." This does not mean Jesus *became* God's Son at Easter. JFB puts it carefully: the resurrection was "the manifestation of a prior Sonship."
- **"The holy and sure blessings of David" (v34) and "You will not let your Holy One see corruption" (v35).** Paul links two texts by a shared word, a standard Jewish way of reading Scripture. Isaiah 55:3 in Greek promises the *hosia*, "holy things" or "sure mercies," of David, and Psalm 16:10 speaks of God's *hosios*, his "Holy One," who will not decay in the grave. The promise to David was for a king who would live forever. **David "fell asleep… and saw corruption"** (v36), so the psalm cannot be about David himself. It must be about the one God raised, who "did not see corruption" (v37). Peter made the same argument at Pentecost (2:25–31). The phrase "after he had served the purpose of God in his own generation" is a fine epitaph for any life.
- **"Through this man forgiveness of sins is proclaimed to you" (v38).** The third movement is the application. **"Everyone who believes is freed from everything from which you could not be freed by the law of Moses"** (v39). "Freed" is the verb *dikaioō*, "justified," declared righteous. This is the first time in Acts that Paul's great word appears, years before Galatians and Romans. Some have asked whether the sentence means the law freed people from *some* things and Christ from the rest. Most readers take it as Paul does elsewhere: the law could not justify anyone from anything (Gal 2:16; Rom 3:20), and Christ justifies from everything. JFB: "By Christ the believer is justified from all things, whereas the law justifies from nothing."
- **"Look, you scoffers, be astounded and perish" (vv40–41).** Paul ends with a warning from Habakkuk 1:5, quoted from the Greek version. In Habakkuk, God's astonishing "work" was raising up the Babylonians to judge his unbelieving people. Now the astonishing work is the gospel itself, and the warning is the same: do not refuse to believe it when it is told.
- **"Devout converts to Judaism" (v43).** These are proselytes, Gentiles who had been circumcised and taken on the whole law. Many of them, along with Jews, followed Paul and Barnabas, who **"urged them to continue in the grace of God."** "Grace" is the word for what they had just received: God's undeserved favor in Christ.
- **"Almost the whole city gathered" (v44).** A week later the synagogue was overwhelmed by Gentiles. **"The Jews… were filled with jealousy"** (v45). The word is *zēlos*, zeal turned sour. A synagogue that had worked for years to win a few God-fearers saw these visitors draw the whole town with a message that welcomed Gentiles without circumcision. Their reaction is contradiction and abuse.
- **"It was necessary that the word of God be spoken first to you" (v46).** "Necessary" is the language of God's plan. The gospel was "to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Rom 1:16). **"You… judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life"** is pointed: they thought the Gentiles unworthy, and by rejecting the message they passed that verdict on themselves. **"We are turning to the Gentiles"** applies to this town, not to all Jews everywhere. In the very next town Paul goes to the synagogue first again (14:1).
- **"I have made you a light for the Gentiles" (v47).** The quotation is from Isaiah 49:6, one of the Servant Songs. In Luke's Gospel old Simeon applies it to the baby Jesus, "a light for revelation to the Gentiles" (Luke 2:32). Here Paul and Barnabas take it as their own command. The Servant is Christ, and those sent by him carry his light to "the ends of the earth," the phrase from Jesus' commission in 1:8.
- **"As many as were appointed to eternal life believed" (v48).** "Appointed" is *tassō*, a word first used of soldiers drawn up in their ranks, and so "arranged, assigned, appointed." Calvinist readers see here God's choosing of those who will believe, and JFB says plainly that "a divine ordination to eternal life is the cause, not the effect, of any man's believing." Arminian readers note that the lexicon allows the form to be read as middle voice, "as many as had set themselves toward eternal life," or take it of God's appointing in response to foreknown faith. The plainest reading of the passive is that God's appointing comes first. Luke holds it beside human responsibility in the same paragraph: the Jews "thrust it aside" and judged themselves unworthy (v46). Scripture keeps both truths without explaining how they fit.
- **"The devout women of high standing and the leading men of the city" (v50).** Wealthy Gentile women were often attracted to the synagogue, and their husbands held power in the colony. The opponents used them to get Paul and Barnabas expelled.
- **"They shook off the dust from their feet against them" (v51).** Jesus had told the Twelve and the seventy-two to do exactly this in a town that rejected them (Luke 9:5; 10:11). Pious Jews were said to shake off Gentile dust on returning to the land of Israel. Here the gesture means "we are not responsible for you any longer." They went on to **Iconium**, about 90 miles east.
- **"The disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit" (v52).** The missionaries leave, but a church remains, and it is full of joy under persecution. The same pattern shows up later at Thessalonica, where believers received the word "in much affliction, with the joy of the Holy Spirit" (1 Thess 1:6).
- **Commentators:** On Mark, Henry is blunt: "It was his fault, and we shall hear of it again." Guzik lays out the possible reasons for his leaving without choosing one. Guzik also explains verses 38–39 as two gifts: forgiveness clears the debt of sin, while justification goes further and credits righteousness. On the jealousy of verse 45, Henry says the opponents "grudged the interest the apostles had in the people," and on verse 46: "If one will not, another will." On verse 48 Henry agrees with JFB: "Those believed to whom God gave grace to believe." On verse 51 Henry writes, "Dust they are, and let them keep their dust to themselves." JFB takes the 450 years as an approximate total counted from God's promise to Abraham, while the ESV's reading points to the stay in Egypt, the wilderness and the conquest.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul's sermon is the chapter's Christ-centered heart. All of Israel's story, from the exodus to the throne of David, was leading to "a Savior, Jesus, as he promised" (v23). The rulers fulfilled the prophets by condemning him, and God answered by raising him, so that the one who hung on the "tree" now lives forever and never sees decay. Through him "everyone who believes" is justified from everything the law could never touch. The light Isaiah promised for the nations is Christ himself, and in this chapter it begins to shine across the Roman world, carried by a former persecutor who knew what it was to be blind and then to see.
