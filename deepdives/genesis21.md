*Setting:* Abraham is now 100 and Sarah 90, probably sometime around 2000 BC, though dates for the patriarchs are approximate. It has been twenty-five years since God first promised Abraham offspring when he left Haran at 75 (12:4). Chapter 18 gave the promise a date, "about this time next year" (18:10), and Sarah laughed. Since then Sodom has burned (ch 19), and Abraham has moved south into the Negev and the region of Gerar. There, for the second time, he passed Sarah off as his sister, and God protected her from King Abimelech (ch 20). The family that carries the promise has just nearly lost it again through Abraham's own fear. Chapter 21 is where the long wait ends. Isaac is born, the household splits as Hagar and Ishmael are sent away, and Abraham settles at Beersheba with a sworn right to a well. Through it all God keeps every word he has spoken, to Sarah, to Hagar, and to Abraham.

#### The Birth of Isaac (vv1–7)
- **"The LORD visited Sarah as he had said" (v1).** "Visited" is *paqad*, a word for paying close attention to someone and acting on it. The lexicon lists this verse under "visit graciously," the same sense as Joseph's dying words, "God will surely visit you" (50:25), and God's visiting his people in Egypt (Exod 4:31). The verse says the same thing twice: "as he had said… as he had promised." The narrator wants no doubt about who did this.
- **"At the time of which God had spoken" (v2).** The phrase picks up "the appointed time" of 17:21 and 18:14. The birth happens on God's schedule, not a moment early or late. Hebrews draws the lesson: "she considered him faithful who promised" (Heb 11:11).
- **"Abraham called the name of his son… Isaac" (v3).** God had chosen the name back in 17:19. Abraham's naming is an act of obedience, not invention. *Yitschaq* means "he laughs," from the verb *tsachaq*, "laugh," the same verb used when Abraham laughed (17:17) and Sarah laughed (18:12). The lexicon confirms that v6 uses this verb with the sense "laugh at" or "laugh over."
- **"Circumcised… when he was eight days old, as God had commanded" (v4).** Isaac is the first child to receive the covenant sign on the eighth day, exactly as 17:12 required. Ishmael was circumcised at 13 (17:25). Isaac is marked as a covenant child from the start.
- **"A hundred years old" (v5).** The narrator stops to give the number. Paul says Abraham faced the fact that his body "was as good as dead" and still "grew strong in his faith" (Rom 4:19–20).
- **"God has made laughter for me" (v6).** Sarah's laugh in 18:12 was tired and doubtful. Now it has turned to joy. "Everyone who hears will laugh over me" can be read two ways. Most take it as laughing *with* her in delight, and the Tyndale notes read it that way. A few think she expects some to laugh *at* an old mother. The context of joy favors the first.
- **"Who would have said to Abraham that Sarah would nurse children?" (v7).** Her words sound like a short poem. The point is that no human being would ever have predicted this. Only God had said it.
- **Commentators:** Henry's introduction sums up the section: "Long-looked-for comes at last," and on v1 he writes, "No word of God shall fall to the ground; for he is faithful that has promised." Guzik stresses that the promise was kept because of God's faithfulness, not Abraham's record, which is pointed after chapter 20. He also lists parallels between Isaac and Jesus: a promised son, a long wait, a birth only God could bring about, at an appointed time, and great joy. JFB treats v1 as God's grace at work and reads Isaac's name and circumcision (vv3–4) as acts done by divine command. JFB has no separate notes on vv2, 5, 6 or 7.

#### God Protects Hagar and Ishmael (vv8–21)
- **"The child grew and was weaned" (v8).** In the ancient world children were often nursed until two or three, and the apocryphal book of 2 Maccabees mentions a mother nursing for three years (2 Macc 7:27). The "great feast" marked Isaac publicly as Abraham's heir. Ishmael was born when Abraham was 86 (16:16), so at Isaac's weaning he was about 16 or 17.
- **"Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian… laughing" (v9).** Notice that Sarah does not use Ishmael's name, and neither does the narrator through the rest of this section. The word is *metsacheq*, from the same verb as Isaac's name. The lexicon gives its meaning here as "sport, play," the same verb used of Israel's revelry around the golden calf (Exod 32:6) and of Isaac caressing Rebekah (26:8). The ESV footnote offers "mocking." The old Greek translation and the Latin Vulgate add "with Isaac her son." The text does not spell out what Ishmael was doing, and readers have taken it in several ways:
- **Readings of v9.** Many Christian readers, following Paul, take it as mockery or bullying. Paul says Ishmael "persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit" (Gal 4:29). Rabbinic tradition went further, reading the word as something seriously wicked. Some modern scholars think he was simply playing, and that Sarah saw in the older boy laughing alongside Isaac, the "laughing one," a rival heir. The wordplay is the point either way: on the day of Isaac's feast, the other son is "Isaac-ing," and Sarah cannot bear it.
- **"Cast out this slave woman with her son" (v10).** This is harsh language. Sarah had first given Hagar to Abraham (16:2–3) and then mistreated her until she fled (16:6). Now Sarah wants both gone for good, "for the son of this slave woman shall not be heir with my son." Some scholars point to old Mesopotamian law codes where a slave wife's children could be set free and released from any claim on the estate. If something like that lies behind Sarah's demand, sending them away would also mean Ishmael's freedom. That is a scholarly reconstruction, not the text.
- **"Very displeasing to Abraham on account of his son" (v11).** Abraham loves Ishmael. Years earlier he had pleaded, "Oh that Ishmael might live before you!" (17:18). The narrator lets the reader feel his grief.
- **"Whatever Sarah says to you, do as she tells you" (v12).** In chapter 16 Abraham listened to Sarah and things went wrong (16:2). This time God tells him to listen, but God gives the reason, not Sarah: "through Isaac shall your offspring be named." The line of promise runs through Isaac alone. Paul quotes this sentence to show that God's promise rests on his choice, not on natural descent (Rom 9:7). Hebrews quotes it in the story of Abraham's offering of Isaac (Heb 11:18).
- **"I will make a nation of the son of the slave woman also, because he is your offspring" (v13).** God does not abandon Ishmael. The promise of 16:10 and 17:20 still stands. Ishmael is not the son of the covenant, but he is still Abraham's son and still under God's care.
- **"Abraham rose early in the morning" (v14).** He obeys at once, the same phrase used when he sets out to offer Isaac (22:3). In both chapters Abraham is asked to give up a son, and in both God provides. He gives Hagar bread and "a skin of water," a goatskin bag, and puts it on her shoulder. The Hebrew word order is awkward, and the old Greek translation seems to read it as putting *the child* on her shoulder. The ESV keeps the Hebrew order and takes "along with the child" as part of what Abraham gave her. A boy in his teens would not be carried, and the Hebrew word for "child" here is used of young men too.
- **"Wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba" (v14).** This is the dry northern Negev, about 25 miles southwest of Hebron. "Wandered" suggests she was lost. When the water runs out, she leaves the boy in the shade of a bush (v15).
- **"About the distance of a bowshot" (v16).** She sits close enough to watch but far enough not to hear him die. The Tyndale notes point out that "bowshot" anticipates what Ishmael will become, "an expert with the bow" (v20). "She lifted up her voice and wept." The ESV footnote says the old Greek translation reads "the child lifted up his voice." The Hebrew has Hagar weeping. The next verse says God heard the boy, so both were crying.
- **"God heard the voice of the boy" (v17).** This is the heart of the scene. Ishmael's name means "God hears" (16:11). The angel had told Hagar that years before, and now the name comes true. "God has heard the voice of the boy *where he is*." Jewish tradition read this to mean God judged the boy as he was then, not by what his descendants would later do. The plain sense is simpler: God meets him right there, under that bush.
- **"The angel of God called to Hagar from heaven" (v17).** In chapter 16 "the angel of the LORD" found her by a spring on the road to Shur, and she called God "a God of seeing" (16:13). Now the angel speaks "from heaven," the same way the angel calls to Abraham in 22:11. The parallel is deliberate. Both sons of Abraham come near death, and both are rescued by a voice from heaven.
- **"Up! Lift up the boy, and hold him fast" (v18).** God gives Hagar a task and a future. She is to take hold of the son she could not bear to watch die, "for I will make him into a great nation."
- **"God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water" (v19).** The well was there all along. What changed was her sight. Wells run through these chapters as signs of God's provision: Beer-lahai-roi in 16:14, this well, and the disputed well at Beersheba in v25.
- **"God was with the boy" (v20).** This is the same phrase Abimelech uses of Abraham two verses later (v22). Ishmael's life is shaped by God's presence even outside the line of promise.
- **"The wilderness of Paran… a wife for him from the land of Egypt" (v21).** Paran is the wilderness of the central Sinai and northern Arabia, south of Canaan, where Israel later wandered (Num 10:12). Hagar, an Egyptian herself, arranges his marriage, as parents did. His twelve sons become tribal leaders (25:12–18), as God promised (17:20). Many trace Arab peoples to Ishmael, though the Bible itself names only his twelve sons and their territories.
- **Galatians 4.** Paul uses this story as an illustration. Hagar and her son stand for the covenant of Sinai and slavery under law. Sarah and Isaac stand for the promise and freedom. He even quotes Sarah's words, "Cast out the slave woman and her son" (Gal 4:30). Paul says openly that he is reading it "allegorically" (Gal 4:24), using the shape of the story to make a point about grace, not passing judgment on Hagar as a person.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns that "Mocking is a great sin, and very provoking to God," and that "The children of promise must expect to be mocked." He also says Ishmael "might be Isaac's play-fellow and school-fellow, yet not his fellow-heir." Guzik agrees the conflict came from Ishmael, follows Paul in applying the story to the conflict between flesh and Spirit, and suggests Abraham may have kept Ishmael in reserve as a backup plan, which God would not allow. JFB reads the mocking as resentment at Ishmael's lost prospects. Henry praises Abraham's "speedy" obedience, and he pictures Hagar "like one under the power of the spirit of bondage" who "despairs of relief." The Tyndale notes are more careful about Sarah, noting that she drove Hagar away once before (16:6), and they stress that God met Hagar and Ishmael in their despair. Guzik notes that God answered the boy's voice, not only Hagar's weeping. JFB has no separate notes on vv11 or 16–18.

#### A Treaty with Abimelech (vv22–34)
- **"Abimelech and Phicol the commander of his army" (v22).** Abimelech is the king of Gerar from chapter 20. Bringing his general along shows this is a matter of state. Most readers take him as the same man as in chapter 20, since the text says "at that time." A king with the same name and a general named Phicol also meet Isaac in 26:26. Some think "Abimelech" (meaning "my father is king") was a royal title passed down, like "Pharaoh."
- **"God is with you in all that you do" (v22).** A Canaanite king sees what Abraham's own failures could not hide. God has blessed him. This is a small sign of the promise in 12:3, that the nations will be blessed through Abraham, and that others will recognize it.
- **"Swear to me here by God that you will not deal falsely with me" (v23).** The Tyndale notes observe the irony. Abimelech asks for an oath because he has already caught Abraham in a lie (20:9). "As I have dealt kindly with you" uses *chesed*, the word for steadfast, loyal love. Abimelech is calling on the kindness he showed in 20:14–15.
- **"I will swear" (v24).** The verb is *shaba*, "swear," which becomes the key to the place's name in v31.
- **"A well of water that Abimelech's servants had seized" (v25).** Before he swears, Abraham raises the issue. In the dry Negev a well meant life for a herding family. "Reproved" means he made a formal complaint.
- **"Sheep and oxen… and the two men made a covenant" (v27).** Gifts sealed the agreement. The word *berit*, "covenant," is the same word used of God's covenants with Noah and Abraham. Here it is a treaty between equals.
- **"Seven ewe lambs" (vv28–30).** Abraham sets these apart as a separate gift. If Abimelech accepts them, he admits in public that Abraham dug the well. The lambs serve as living witnesses, a kind of receipt.
- **"Therefore that place was called Beersheba" (v31).** *Be'er* means "well." The Hebrew for "seven" is *sheva*, and the verb "swear" is *shaba*, built from the same letters. The lexicon gives Beersheba as "well of seven," explained here as the place of swearing by seven lambs, or "well of oath." The text plays on both meanings at once. Isaac later renews the name after his own oath with Abimelech (26:33). Beersheba became the southern edge of Israel, as in the phrase "from Dan to Beersheba" (Judg 20:1).
- **"Returned to the land of the Philistines" (v32).** The Philistines best known from Judges and Samuel arrived by sea from the Aegean around 1200 BC, long after Abraham. The Tyndale notes suggest the later name is used here simply for the region and its earlier inhabitants. Abimelech's name is Semitic, not Aegean, which fits an earlier people in the same area. Some scholars instead think there were earlier Aegean traders in the region. Either way, the people Abraham deals with are peaceful, unlike the later Philistines.
- **"Abraham planted a tamarisk tree" (v33).** The tamarisk (*eshel*) is a hardy desert tree with deep roots that gives good shade. It grows slowly, so planting one is a sign of settling in for the long term. The Tyndale notes see it as a statement of confidence that Abraham's right to the land and its water was secure. The lexicon notes that the word occurs only three times, and the other two both involve Saul (1 Sam 22:6; 31:13). Older English versions read "grove."
- **"Called there on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God" (v33).** The divine name here is *El Olam*. *Olam* means a long duration, stretching back into the distant past or forward without end. This is the only place in Genesis where God is called by this name. Isaiah later uses the same title: "The LORD is the everlasting God… He does not faint or grow weary" (Isa 40:28). Calling on the LORD's name is what Abraham did at his altars (12:8; 13:4).
- **"Abraham sojourned many days" (v34).** "Sojourn" means living as a resident foreigner. Abraham has a well, a tree and a treaty, but he still doesn't own the land. Hebrews says he lived "in the land of promise, as in a foreign land, living in tents" (Heb 11:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "Abimelech felt sure that the promises of God would be fulfilled to Abraham," and notes that "Wells of water are scarce and valuable in eastern countries," so Abraham took care to have his title "allowed, to prevent disputes in future." On v33 Henry writes, "There he called on the name of the Lord, as the everlasting God." Guzik holds that this Abimelech was probably a different king, taking the name as a title, while the Tyndale notes and most commentators assume the king of chapter 20. Guzik glosses Beersheba as a watering place, along with "well of seven" or "well of the oath." The lexicon supports the last two, which the text itself explains. JFB says the grove was "of tamarisks, in which sacrificial worship was offered, as in a roofless temple," and calls Abraham's long stay in the land "a picture of pastoral and an emblem of Christian life." JFB has no notes on vv23–24 or 32.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaac is the child God promised, born when no one could make it happen, at the time God set, and named for laughter that became joy. In all of that he points ahead to another promised son born at "the fullness of time" (Gal 4:4), when an angel said "nothing will be impossible with God" (Luke 1:37) and announced "good news of great joy" (Luke 2:10). The chapter also shows that God's grace reaches beyond the chosen line. The God who heard a dying boy under a desert bush is the same God who sends his Son to bring the nations in. Paul uses this story to say that believers, "like Isaac, are children of promise" (Gal 4:28), not because of who they are but because of what God has promised. Abraham worshiped the Everlasting God while he still lived in a tent. The church does the same, looking for "the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:10).
