*Setting:* The title reads "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A Psalm of David." "To the choirmaster" (*lamnatseach*) appears in 55 psalm titles and probably means the psalm was handed to the leader of temple music for public worship. "With stringed instruments" (*binginot*) tells how it was to be accompanied, by lyres and harps rather than horns or cymbals. No occasion is named. Some, including JFB, think it comes from the same time as Psalm 3, Absalom's rebellion. Others, including Guzik, think the attack here is on David's reputation rather than his life and point to some other time of slander. Psalm 3 is a morning prayer ("I woke again," 3:5) and Psalm 4 an evening one ("In peace I will both lie down and sleep," 4:8), and the two are placed side by side for that reason. For centuries the Western church has sung Psalm 4 at Compline, the last prayer service before sleep. The psalm moves from a cry to God, to a rebuke of those who oppose the psalmist, to counsel for the troubled, and ends in joy and rest.

#### Answer Me When I Call (v1)
- **"Answer me when I call, O God of my righteousness!" (v1).** The prayer opens with urgency. "God of my righteousness" can mean "the God who is the source of my righteousness" or "the God who will vindicate me, who will show I am in the right." Both fit. David is being falsely accused (v2), and he appeals to the God who knows the truth and will defend it. He is not claiming to be sinless (see Ps 51), but that God is his judge and his advocate.
- **"You have given me relief when I was in distress" (v1).** The Hebrew picture is vivid. "Distress" (*tsar*) means a narrow, tight place, being hemmed in. "Given me relief" (*hirchavta*) means "you made room for me," "you set me in a broad place" (compare 18:19; 118:5). God has taken him out of tight corners before. That past mercy becomes the ground for asking again.
- **"Be gracious to me and hear my prayer!" (v1).** He asks for grace (*chanan*), undeserved favor, not a reward. Even the man God has vindicated before still comes as a beggar.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that David pleads God's mercy and his own past experience, since God once "enlarged me when I was in distress." JFB: "Past favor is a ground of hope for the future," and it explains "enlarged" as relief from straits or pressure. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's remark that this is "David's common habit of pleading past mercies as a ground for present favour," and he stresses that David knew his righteousness came from God rather than himself.

#### How Long? (vv2–3)
- **"O men" (v2).** The Hebrew is *bene ish*, "sons of man." It can simply mean people, but where it stands beside the more ordinary phrase *bene adam* (49:2; 62:9) it seems to mean people of rank or standing. JFB takes it that way: David is addressing powerful men who are spreading lies about him.
- **"How long shall my honor be turned into shame?" (v2).** "Honor" is *kavod*, the same word as "my glory" in 3:3. In Psalm 3 David says God is his glory. Here men are trying to drag that honor into the mud. A king's reputation was part of his authority; destroy it and his rule collapses. Slander is a weapon.
- **"How long will you love vain words and seek after lies?" (v2).** "Vain words" (*riq*) are empty, worthless things. "Lies" (*kazav*) are deceptions. They do not merely tell lies; they *love* them and *seek* them out. The Tyndale notes suggest that the words may also point to false gods, since idols are called "lies" and "vanities" elsewhere (40:4; Jer 16:19). Either way, they have chosen emptiness over truth.
- **"Selah" (v2).** The pause lets the question sink in. Where does a life devoted to empty words lead?
- **"But know that the LORD has set apart the godly for himself" (v3).** "Set apart" (*palah*) is the verb used when God made a distinction between Israel and Egypt during the plagues: flies, hail and death fell on Egypt, but not on Goshen (Exod 8:22; 9:4; 11:7). God marks out his own and treats them differently. The **"godly"** (*chasid*) is someone who lives by *chesed*, God's steadfast covenant love, someone who has received that love and returns it in faithfulness. The enemies may slander him, but they cannot change whose he is.
- **"The LORD hears when I call to him" (v3).** Verse 1 asked, "Answer me when I call." Verse 3 states the answer as a settled fact. The prayer has already begun to change the one praying.
- **Commentators:** Henry urges the "sons of men" to live up to their dignity as human beings and warns that loving vanity and lies only deceives and ruins them. He takes v3 to mean that "the Lord has set apart him that is godly" for himself, so God hears and guards his people. JFB reads "sons of men" as "men of note or prominence." Guzik agrees with an older writer that "it is the psalmist's reputation rather than his person that is being attacked," and observes that Jesus faced the same attempt to turn his glory into shame.

#### Be Angry, and Do Not Sin (vv4–5)
- **"Be angry, and do not sin" (v4).** The verb *ragaz* means to tremble or be stirred up, whether with fear, awe or rage. The KJV took it as fear: "Stand in awe, and sin not." The Greek Old Testament took it as anger, and Paul quotes that Greek wording in Ephesians 4:26: "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger." The ESV follows Paul. Read this way, the verse speaks to anyone stirred up by injustice, the psalmist's friends, perhaps, or the psalmist himself. Anger is not wrong in itself. Revenge, bitterness and rash words are.
- **Who is being addressed?** The verse can be read as still speaking to the enemies of v2 (tremble before God and stop sinning) or turning to the psalmist's own supporters (do not let your anger at these men lead you into sin). Both readings have good support, and Paul's use suggests the second.
- **"Ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent" (v4).** Literally "speak in your hearts on your beds." Night, when the house is quiet, is the time to think things through before God instead of lashing out. "Be silent" (*damam*) is the stillness of someone who has stopped arguing and waits (37:7: "Be still before the LORD and wait patiently for him"). A furious heart, laid down on a bed and quieted before God, is the first step to sleeping in peace (v8).
- **"Offer right sacrifices" (v5).** Literally "sacrifices of righteousness" (*zivche tsedeq*). These are sacrifices offered rightly, from a right heart, and not as a cover for sin. David says elsewhere that God delights in right sacrifices once the heart is broken and contrite (51:17, 19). In Moses' blessing the same phrase is used of worship that pleases God (Deut 33:19).
- **"And put your trust in the LORD" (v5).** Worship and trust go together. Instead of fighting slander with slander, the godly hand the matter to God.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that Paul quotes v4 in Ephesians 4:26 following the Greek, which reads "be angry," and that "not" governs both clauses. Henry, following the KJV, says to "stand in awe and sin not" and "commune with your own heart" in quiet, especially at night, and to trust in the Lord rather than one's own wealth or understanding. Guzik says David had reason to be angry at ungodliness but no reason to sin, and that outward worship cannot replace trust in the Lord, though together they draw us near to God.

#### The Light of Your Face (vv6–8)
- **"There are many who say, 'Who will show us some good?'" (v6).** This is the voice of discouragement, perhaps cynicism. Some think it comes from a time of drought or failed harvest, given the mention of grain and wine in v7. Others hear the grumbling of David's own followers in hard times. Either way, people are asking where anything good will come from.
- **"Lift up the light of your face upon us, O LORD!" (v6).** David answers the question with a prayer, and his prayer is a quotation. It blends two lines of the priestly blessing that Aaron spoke over Israel: "The LORD make his face to shine upon you… The LORD lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace" (Num 6:25–26). Good does not come first from circumstances but from God's face turned toward us in favor. Note that he says "upon *us*." He includes the discouraged in his prayer.
- **"You have put more joy in my heart than they have when their grain and wine abound" (v7).** Harvest was the happiest season in Israel (Isa 9:3: "they are glad before you as with joy at the harvest"). David says the joy God has given him is greater than a bumper crop. That joy is "in my heart," placed there by God, so a poor harvest or a ruined reputation cannot take it away.
- **"In peace I will both lie down and sleep" (v8).** He does not lie awake turning things over. He lies down and sleeps at once, in peace (*shalom*), which is the last word of the priestly blessing he quoted in v6. Psalm 3 said, "I lay down and slept" (3:5). Here the same trust becomes a settled habit.
- **"For you alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety" (v8).** The word "alone" (*levadad*) can attach to God, "you alone make me safe," or to the psalmist, "you make me dwell alone, in safety." In Deuteronomy 33:28 the same word describes Israel dwelling "alone," secure and undisturbed. Either way, his safety rests on God and nothing else.
- **Commentators:** Henry faults the "many" of v6 for seeking only outward, visible good and not turning to God, who alone gives lasting good. He describes the joy of God's favor as greater than "the joy of harvest, of a plentiful harvest, when the corn and wine increase," while of worldly mirth he says, "even in laughter their heart is sorrowful." On v8 he gives the believer's resolve: "I will lay myself down (having the assurance of thy favour) in peace." Guzik ties v6 to the priestly blessing and says David slept safely because his security came from the Lord, not from circumstances or feelings. He quotes G. Campbell Morgan on "alone," describing the tired child of God "pavilioned in His peace." JFB links v8 back to 3:5.

#### Pointing to Christ
The priestly blessing David prays in v6 found its fullest answer when "God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6). The face of God turned toward us in favor is seen in Jesus. He too had his honor turned into shame by men who loved lies, and he did not answer slander with sin: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return… but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). Because he bore the shame, those who belong to him are "set apart" for God. They can lie down at night, angry hearts stilled and fears handed over, in a peace the world cannot give or take away (John 14:27).
