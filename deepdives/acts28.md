*Setting:* Winter of AD 59–60 and the following spring. All 276 people from the wreck are ashore on Malta, soaked and freezing. Three months later another ship carries Paul to Italy, and the last leg is on foot to Rome. The promise of 19:21 and 23:11 is fulfilled. The book Luke opened with "you will be my witnesses… to the end of the earth" (1:8) closes with the gospel preached openly in the capital of the world.

#### Paul on Malta (vv1–10)
- **"The island was called Malta" (v1).** Malta lies about 60 miles south of Sicily and was under Roman rule. Its people spoke a Punic (Phoenician) dialect. That is why Luke calls them *barbaroi* (v2, "native people"), which simply means people who didn't speak Greek. It wasn't an insult.
- **"Unusual kindness… they kindled a fire and welcomed us all" (v2).** "Kindness" is *philanthrōpia*, love for people. It is the root of "philanthropy," and Paul uses the same word of God's kindness in Titus 3:4. Two hundred seventy-six wet strangers, including prisoners, are welcomed by the islanders.
- **"Paul had gathered a bundle of sticks" (v3).** The apostle who had just saved the whole ship is collecting firewood like everyone else. Leadership in Acts looks like service.
- **"A viper came out because of the heat and fastened on his hand" (v3).** It was cold and torpid in the brushwood, and the fire roused it. Malta has no venomous snakes today, which some cite against the story. But the island was far more wooded in the first century, and species have disappeared from many islands. The locals clearly expected the bite to be deadly (v6).
- **"No doubt this man is a murderer… Justice has not allowed him to live" (v4).** "Justice" is *Dikē*, the Greek goddess of justice and revenge. Their reasoning is common in every culture: disaster must be punishment. Jesus rejected that reasoning (Luke 13:1–5; John 9:2–3).
- **"He… shook off the creature into the fire and suffered no harm" (v5).** This fulfills Jesus' promise in Luke 10:19 and the longer ending of Mark (16:18), a passage most scholars think was added later but which records what early believers saw. It is not a pattern for handling snakes. It is God protecting his messenger and his promise: Paul "must stand before Caesar" (27:24).
- **"They changed their minds and said that he was a god" (v6).** They go from "murderer" to "god" in one afternoon, just as Lystra went from worshiping Paul to stoning him (14:11–19). Luke doesn't record Paul's response, but Paul's habit was to point away from himself (14:15).
- **Publius, "the chief man of the island" (v7).** "Chief man" (*prōtos*) was the actual official title of Malta's Roman governor, and it has been found on inscriptions from the island. That is another of Luke's precise local details. Publius hosts the whole company for three days.
- **"Fever and dysentery" (v8).** Luke the physician gives the medical terms, both plural in Greek, suggesting recurring fevers. This may be "Malta fever" (brucellosis), long common on the island and spread through goat's milk. Paul **prayed and put his hands on him** (compare Jesus with Peter's mother-in-law, Luke 4:38–40). Prayer comes first. The power is God's.
- **"The rest of the people on the island who had diseases also came and were cured" (v9).** The verb tense suggests they kept coming over the three months. Some suggest Luke treated patients too, since the word for "cured" (*therapeuō*) can include medical care. "Honored us greatly" (v10) may include gifts or payment (the word is used of money in 4:34; 7:16).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Paul "a planet, and not a fixed star," always moving, always useful. Of the storm that brought him there he says, "an ill wind indeed it is that blows nobody any good; this ill wind blew good to the island." He notices Paul "as busy as any of them in gathering sticks," and connects the hidden viper to Amos 5:19, where a man leans on a wall and a serpent bites him. JFB notes the islanders' belief in "a Supreme, Resistless, Avenging Eye and Hand, however vague their notions." It also sees two promises of Mark 16:18 fulfilled here, the serpents and the healing by laying on of hands. Guzik draws a simple lesson from the crowd's flip from "murderer" to "god": other people's opinions are a poor thing to stand on. He suggests Luke may have helped as a physician.

#### Paul Arrives at Rome (vv11–16)
- **"A ship of Alexandria, with the twin gods as a figurehead" (v11).** The twin gods were Castor and Pollux (the *Dioskouroi*), patron gods of sailors. The figurehead may have been the ship's name, *Twin Brothers*. Luke notes it without comment. Paul sails to Rome under a pagan sign, carried by the true God's hand. They sail in early spring, around February, as soon as the seas were judged safe enough.
- **Syracuse, Rhegium, Puteoli (vv12–13).** Syracuse was the main city of Sicily. Rhegium sits on the toe of Italy at the Strait of Messina. A south wind brings them 200 miles north to **Puteoli** (modern Pozzuoli) on the Bay of Naples, where the Egyptian grain fleet unloaded.
- **"There we found brothers and were invited to stay with them for seven days" (v14).** There are already Christians in Puteoli. The gospel had reached Italy before Paul, probably through Jewish believers returning from Pentecost (2:10) or traveling merchants. The centurion's willingness to allow a week's stay shows how much Julius had come to trust Paul.
- **"And so we came to Rome" (v14).** Luke says it twice (vv14, 16). After twenty chapters of moving toward Rome, Paul arrives.
- **"The Forum of Appius and Three Taverns" (v15).** These were stops on the Appian Way, about 43 and 33 miles from Rome. Two groups of believers walk out to meet him, a day's march or more. Three years earlier Paul had written to them, "I long to see you" (Rom 1:11). Now they come to see him. **"Paul thanked God and took courage."** Even Paul needed encouragement. The word for "meet" (*apantēsis*) was used for a delegation going out to welcome an arriving official, the same word Paul uses for believers meeting the Lord at his coming (1 Thess 4:17).
- **"Allowed to stay by himself, with the soldier who guarded him" (v16).** This was light custody (*custodia militaris*). He lived in rented quarters, chained by the wrist to a soldier on rotating shifts. Over two years, many of the emperor's elite guard would have spent hours chained to Paul, and "it has become known throughout the whole imperial guard… that my imprisonment is for Christ" (Phil 1:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry quotes Virgil's *Aeneid* on Aeneas's long journey to Latium, then adds, "We make for heaven." He notes the two Alexandrian ships, both bound for Italy and both on the same island, "but one is wrecked there and the other is saved." He also notes the crew had learned not to sail in winter: "their learning did them good when they had paid dearly for it." Guzik points out the gospel reached Rome before Paul did, since believers were already waiting there. JFB pictures crowds on the shore recognizing the Alexandrian grain ships as they came in, relying on Howson.

#### Paul in Rome (vv17–31)
- **"He called together the local leaders of the Jews" (v17).** Only three days after arriving, Paul starts with the Jews, as he always did (13:46; Rom 1:16). Rome had a large Jewish community, perhaps 40,000–50,000, with many synagogues. Claudius had expelled Jews about AD 49 (18:2), but they had since returned.
- **"I had done nothing against our people… I was compelled to appeal to Caesar—though I had no charge to bring against my nation" (vv17–19).** Paul calls them "brothers." He came to Rome as a defendant, not to accuse Israel.
- **"It is because of the hope of Israel that I am wearing this chain" (v20).** He holds up the chain. "The hope of Israel" is the Messiah and the resurrection, the thread through all his defenses (23:6; 24:15; 26:6–7).
- **"We have received no letters from Judea about you" (v21).** The Jerusalem leaders apparently never followed up. Perhaps the shipwreck delayed any messengers, or they knew their case wouldn't hold up before Caesar. Under Roman law prosecutors who failed to appear could lose their case. That may be why Paul's two years ended without a recorded trial.
- **"With regard to this sect we know that everywhere it is spoken against" (v22).** By AD 60 the Way had a reputation across the empire. Within four years, Nero would blame Christians for the great fire of Rome.
- **"From morning till evening he expounded… from the Law of Moses and from the Prophets" (v23).** It was an all-day Bible study, like Jesus on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:27, 44). "Testifying to the kingdom of God… about Jesus" is the same message Jesus preached (Luke 4:43) and that Acts opened with (1:3).
- **"Some were convinced… others disbelieved" (v24).** That was the familiar split, as in synagogue after synagogue.
- **"The Holy Spirit was right in saying to your fathers through Isaiah" (vv25–27).** Paul quotes Isaiah 6:9–10, God's warning to Isaiah that his own generation would hear without understanding. Jesus applied the same words to his hearers (Matt 13:14–15; John 12:39–41). It is the Old Testament passage the New Testament quotes most often to explain unbelief. Notice "the Holy Spirit… said": Paul treats Isaiah's words as the Spirit's own.
- **"This salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen" (v28).** This is the third such announcement in Acts (13:46; 18:6). It does not mean God has given up on Israel. Paul had just written that Israel's hardening is "partial" and temporary, "until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in" (Rom 11:25–26). Paul still welcomed "all who came" (v30), Jews included. Verse 29, in the KJV ("the Jews departed, and had great reasoning among themselves"), is missing from the earliest manuscripts, so the ESV leaves it out.
- **"He lived there two whole years at his own expense" (v30).** Paul rented his lodging, perhaps supported by gifts like the one from Philippi (Phil 4:10–18). From here he likely wrote **Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians and Philemon**, the "prison letters." Visitors included Onesimus the runaway slave (Philemon 10), Epaphroditus (Phil 2:25), Tychicus, Mark, Luke and Aristarchus (Col 4:7–14).
- **"Proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance" (v31).** The last word of Acts in Greek is *akōlytōs*, "unhindered." The messenger is chained, but the message is not. As Paul later wrote, "the word of God is not bound" (2 Tim 2:9).
- **Why does Acts end here?** Luke doesn't tell us how Paul's trial ended. The main explanations:
  - Luke wrote at this very point, about AD 62, and recorded events as far as they had gone.
  - The ending is deliberate. The goal of the book, the gospel reaching Rome "unhindered," is reached, and Paul's personal fate is not the point.
  - Luke planned a third volume.
  - Early church tradition, consistent with the Pastoral Letters, holds that Paul was released about AD 62, traveled again (perhaps even to Spain, Rom 15:24, as Clement of Rome suggests), and was later rearrested. He was beheaded under Nero about AD 64–67. Second Timothy is his last letter: "I have finished the race" (2 Tim 4:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry admits his sorrow at parting from Paul "in bonds for Christ," and compares his long wait to Joseph forgotten by the cupbearer (Gen 40:23). He sees the bright side: "now his prison was his castle," a refuge from the plots he had always faced. He adds a line from Samson's riddle: "out of the eater came forth meat." On the meeting with the Jews, Henry's verdict is that "God was glorified, many were edified, the rest left inexcusable." Guzik reads Paul's use of Isaiah 6 as a warning of judgment on unbelieving Jerusalem, fulfilled in AD 70. He doesn't see the ending as abrupt. He suggests the two-year delay may have been a court backlog, and that Luke may have written Acts partly as a brief for Paul's defense. He holds, with early tradition, that Paul was released and later martyred under Nero. He reads "unhindered" as the book's real conclusion: the story of the church continues. The Tyndale notes say the same: *akōlytōs*, one word, is a key to the meaning of the whole book.

#### Pointing to Christ
Acts began with the risen Jesus teaching about "the kingdom of God" (1:3) and ends with Paul doing the same (28:31). The book never ends because the work hasn't ended. The Spirit who carried the word from Jerusalem through Judea and Samaria to Rome is still carrying it "to the end of the earth." The last picture is a prisoner in a rented room, chained to a guard, welcoming everyone who comes and telling them about Jesus. It shows that the King's word goes on unhindered, whatever happens to his servants.
