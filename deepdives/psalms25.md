*Setting:* The title says simply "Of David." No event is named. The psalm is an *acrostic*: each verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from *aleph* to *tav*, like an A-to-Z poem. The pattern is a little irregular (a couple of letters are missing and one is doubled), and a final verse (v22) stands outside the alphabet, beginning with the word "redeem." Psalm 34, also David's, has exactly the same extra closing verse, also beginning with "redeem." Acrostics helped people memorize a psalm, and they gave a sense of completeness: the whole of a subject, A to Z. This one is a prayer of someone with enemies outside and guilt inside. He asks to be taught God's ways, confesses his sins, and holds on to God's steadfast love. Psalm 24 ended with the King of glory entering his gates and asked who may come near: the one "who does not lift up his soul to what is false" (24:4). Psalm 25 opens, "To you, O LORD, I lift up my soul."

#### To You I Lift Up My Soul (vv1–3)
- **"To you, O LORD, I lift up my soul" (v1).** To "lift up the soul" to something means to set one's longing and trust on it. Psalm 24:4 used the same phrase for idols. David lifts his to God alone. This is the heart of prayer: not first asking for things, but handing oneself to God.
- **"O my God, in you I trust; let me not be put to shame" (v2).** "Put to shame" (*bosh*) runs through the psalm (vv2, 3, 20). It means more than embarrassment. It is the disgrace of having trusted something that let you down, and being publicly seen to have been wrong. If God failed him, his enemies would "exult," crow over him.
- **"None who wait for you shall be put to shame" (v3).** "Wait" (*qavah*) means to look eagerly toward, to hope in. The root is related to a word for a cord or rope stretched taut. The psalm uses it three times (vv3, 5, 21). Isaiah 40:31 says, "they who wait for the LORD shall renew their strength." Paul quotes the same idea from Isaiah: "Everyone who believes in him will not be put to shame" (Rom 10:11).
- **"They shall be ashamed who are wantonly treacherous" (v3).** Literally "the treacherous without cause." David's enemies attack him for no reason. Jesus applied similar words from the Psalms to himself: "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25; Ps 35:19; 69:4).
- **Commentators:** JFB: "lift up my soul—set my affections (compare Col 3:2)," and "not be ashamed" means not disappointed in "hopes of relief." Henry says the psalm teaches "what it is to pray," and that its first act is lifting up the soul. Guzik passes on Boice's point that biblical shame is being let down by something unworthy of trust, and Morgan's that v3 is not a request but a statement of confidence.

#### Make Me to Know Your Ways (vv4–7)
- **"Make me to know your ways, O LORD; teach me your paths" (v4).** Four requests for guidance pile up: make me know, teach me, lead me, teach me (vv4–5). Moses prayed the same at Sinai: "please show me now your ways, that I may know you" (Exod 33:13). David is in danger, but his first request is not "get me out." It is "show me how to walk."
- **"Lead me in your truth" (v5).** "Truth" (*emet*) means firmness, faithfulness, reliability, what can be leaned on. God's truth is both what he says and his faithfulness to it.
- **"For you are the God of my salvation; for you I wait all the day long" (v5).** Here is the reason he asks God to teach him: God is the one who saves him. A rescued person wants to know the rescuer's ways.
- **"Remember your mercy, O LORD, and your steadfast love" (v6).** "Mercy" (*rachamim*) is tender compassion, from the word for womb, the feeling of a mother for her child. "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) is God's loyal covenant love. Both are plural in Hebrew, mercies and loves, many acts of kindness. "From of old" means God has always been like this. David is reminding God of his own character.
- **"Remember not the sins of my youth or my transgressions" (v7).** David asks God to remember three times (vv6, 7) and to forget once. Remember mercy, forget sin, remember *me*. "Sins of my youth" are the careless, foolish wrongs of early years. "Transgressions" (*pesha*) are acts of rebellion. Old sins can come back to haunt a person, especially in trouble (Job 13:26). David does not excuse them. He asks God to look at them through his love.
- **"According to your steadfast love remember me, for the sake of your goodness" (v7).** He gives God no reason from himself. Every reason is in God: his love, his goodness.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Lord, remember thy mercies, which speak for me, and not my sins, which speak against me." He adds that "time does not wear out the guilt of sin," so youthful faults should still be repented of. JFB says David pleads for mercy "not on palliations of sin, but on God's well-known benevolence." Guzik notes that the plurals in v6 suggest a love too great to fit in one word. He passes on Horne's line that when God remembers his mercy, he forgets our sins.

#### Good and Upright Is the LORD (vv8–15)
- **"Good and upright is the LORD; therefore he instructs sinners in the way" (v8).** The psalm stops asking and starts describing God. This is surprising logic: because God is upright, he teaches *sinners*. A holy God might be expected to reject sinners. Instead, his goodness moves him to teach them. Jesus came "not to call the righteous, but sinners" (Mark 2:17).
- **"He leads the humble in what is right" (v9).** "Humble" (*anav*) means lowly, meek, teachable, the opposite of proud. The word is related to "afflicted" in v16. Only the humble can be taught. The proud think they already know.
- **"All the paths of the LORD are steadfast love and faithfulness" (v10).** Not some paths, but all. Even the hard roads God leads his people on are made of his love and truth. This is a promise "for those who keep his covenant and his testimonies," not for those who walk away from him.
- **"For your name's sake, O LORD, pardon my guilt, for it is great" (v11).** This verse sits at the exact center of the acrostic. It is a startling prayer. David does not say "forgive me because my sin is small." He says "forgive me because it is great." The bigger the sin, the more God's name, his reputation for mercy, is shown in forgiving it. God revealed his name to Moses as "the LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious… forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exod 34:6–7).
- **"Who is the man who fears the LORD? Him will he instruct" (v12).** The question form recalls Psalms 15 and 24. The answer is not someone who never sinned, but someone who fears God, who holds him in reverent awe. "The way that he should choose" can mean the way the man should choose, or the way God chooses. Both are true.
- **"His soul shall abide in well-being, and his offspring shall inherit the land" (v13).** "Well-being" is literally "goodness." "Inherit the land" was the promise to Abraham. Jesus widened it: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5; Ps 37:11).
- **"The friendship of the LORD is for those who fear him" (v14).** "Friendship" (*sod*) means a council, an intimate circle, the confidential conversation of close friends. Job remembered when "the friendship of God was upon my tent" (Job 29:4). God said of Abraham, "Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?" (Gen 18:17). Jesus told his disciples, "I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you" (John 15:15). Fearing God does not keep him distant. It is the door into his friendship.
- **"My eyes are ever toward the LORD, for he will pluck my feet out of the net" (v15).** A picture of someone whose feet are caught in a hunter's trap, but who keeps his eyes on God rather than on the snare.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the greatness of the sin is a reason for pardon: "the greater it is the more will divine mercy be magnified in the forgiveness of it. It is the glory of a great God to forgive great sins." On v14 he cites Abraham: "He call them not servants, but friends." JFB says the "sinners" of v8 are "limited by the meek—who are penitent," and on v13 that "inherit the earth" includes "all the blessings… temporal as well as spiritual" of the promise of Canaan. Guzik notes that "paths" in v10 can suggest the deep ruts of wheel tracks, and passes on Spurgeon's comment that God's ways may cut deep but are all of them mercy. On v14 Guzik passes on VanGemeren's description of the word as an intimate circle, and compares this knowledge to trying to explain color to someone born blind (1 Cor 2:14).

#### Turn to Me and Be Gracious (vv16–21)
- **"Turn to me and be gracious to me, for I am lonely and afflicted" (v16).** "Lonely" (*yachid*) means alone, solitary, the only one. It is the same word as "my precious life," literally "my only one," in 22:20. David feels as if no one else is on his side.
- **"The troubles of my heart are enlarged; bring me out of my distresses" (v17).** "Distresses" (*metsuqot*) are tight, narrow places. His troubles are growing wide while his room to move is shrinking. Compare 4:1 and 18:19, where God brings him into "a broad place."
- **"Consider my affliction and my trouble, and forgive all my sins" (v18).** The third request for pardon (vv7, 11, 18). The psalm does not say his troubles are caused by his sins, but he cannot think of one without the other. He asks God to look at both: the pain and the guilt.
- **"Consider how many are my foes, and with what violent hatred they hate me" (v19).** "Violent hatred" is literally "hatred of violence" (*chamas*), the word used for the violence that filled the earth before the flood (Gen 6:11).
- **"Oh, guard my soul, and deliver me! Let me not be put to shame" (v20).** He returns to where he began (v2). "For I take refuge in you" uses the psalms' familiar word *chasah* (2:12; 16:1).
- **"May integrity and uprightness preserve me, for I wait for you" (v21).** "Integrity" (*tom*) and "uprightness" are pictured almost as bodyguards. This is not a claim of sinlessness, since he has just confessed his sins three times. It is a prayer that a whole and honest heart before God will keep him safe. The last word of the acrostic is "I wait for you."
- **Commentators:** JFB on v11 notes that God's perfections are "manifested… in pardoning sin, and the greatness of sin renders pardon more needed." Guzik compares this section to Paul's "afflicted in every way, but not crushed" (2 Cor 4:8–10), and observes that David's sins troubled him more than his enemies did.

#### Redeem Israel (v22)
- **"Redeem Israel, O God, out of all his troubles" (v22).** This extra verse stands outside the alphabet. Psalm 34:22 does the same, also starting with "redeem" (*padah*, to ransom, to buy free). Some think it was added for use in public worship, turning one man's prayer into the whole nation's. Either way, it widens the prayer from "me" to "us." "Troubles" (*tsarot*) is the same word as in v17. What David asked for himself, he asks for God's people.
- **Commentators:** Henry includes this verse among the psalm's requests as prayer for "the salvation of the church of God." Guzik notes that even in his own pain David did not turn inward but prayed for others, and passes on Poole's paraphrase: if you will not pity me, still spare your people.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every prayer in this psalm finds its answer in Jesus. David asked to be taught God's ways, and Jesus said, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). David asked God not to remember the sins of his youth, and in the new covenant God promises, "I will remember their sins no more" (Heb 8:12). David asked God to pardon his guilt "for your name's sake," and God's name was revealed most fully at the cross, where he was both just and the one who justifies sinners (Rom 3:26). David felt lonely and hated without cause. Jesus was truly left alone, and truly hated without a cause (John 15:25). David's last line asked God to redeem Israel out of all its troubles. Jesus came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45), and those who wait for him will not be put to shame.
