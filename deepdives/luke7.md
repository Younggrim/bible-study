*Setting:* About AD 28–29, in Galilee. Jesus has just finished the Sermon on the Plain (6:20–49), with its call to love enemies, show mercy and build on his words. Chapter 7 shows that teaching lived out and shows who Jesus is. A Gentile officer believes him, a widow's dead son is given back, John the Baptist in prison asks whether Jesus really is the one, and a notorious woman is forgiven at a Pharisee's dinner. The chapter keeps circling one question, spoken aloud at the end: "Who is this?" (v49). Two of its stories echo the prophets Elijah and Elisha, whom Jesus named in his first sermon at Nazareth (4:25–27).

#### Jesus Heals a Centurion's Servant (vv1–10)
- **"He entered Capernaum" (v1).** Capernaum was Jesus' base in Galilee (4:31), a lakeside town on a main road, with a customs post (5:27) and, it seems, a small garrison. The remains of a fourth-century limestone synagogue stand there today, built over the basalt foundations of an earlier one that may be the very building of v5.
- **"A centurion" (v2).** He commanded a "century," nominally a hundred soldiers. In Galilee he probably served Herod Antipas, whose troops were organized on the Roman model, rather than a Roman legion. Either way he was a Gentile and a soldier of the occupying order. Centurions in the New Testament come off well (23:47; Acts 10:1–2; 27:43).
- **"A servant… highly valued by him" (v2).** The word is "slave" (*doulos*). Under Roman law a slave was property. This officer cares about him enough to seek help from a Jewish teacher.
- **"He sent to him elders of the Jews" (v3).** Matthew 8:5–13 tells the story more briefly, as if the centurion came himself. Luke shows that he spoke through messengers. Both are true in the way the ancient world understood it: what a man's agents said, he said.
- **"He is worthy… for he loves our nation, and he is the one who built us our synagogue" (vv4–5).** Wealthy Gentiles who admired Judaism sometimes paid for synagogues. Inscriptions from elsewhere in the empire record such gifts. He was probably a "God-fearer," like Cornelius (Acts 10:2), who worshiped Israel's God without becoming a full convert.
- **"I am not worthy to have you come under my roof" (v6).** The elders say "he is worthy." He says "I am not worthy." The Greek word is the same. He knows a pious Jew would become ritually unclean by entering a Gentile home (Acts 10:28), and he also senses that Jesus is greater than he is. The church's liturgy has long put his words in the mouth of worshipers coming to the Lord's Table.
- **"But say the word" (v7).** He believes distance does not matter. Jesus' word is enough, the way God's word is enough (Ps 107:20, "He sent out his word and healed them").
- **"For I too am a man set under authority" (v8).** He reasons from his own job. He takes orders from above and gives orders below, and they are obeyed because of the authority behind them. He sees that Jesus' word carries authority over sickness in the same way.
- **"He marveled at him" (v9).** Jesus is said to marvel only twice in the Gospels: here at a Gentile's faith, and in Mark 6:6 at his hometown's unbelief.
- **"Not even in Israel have I found such faith" (v9).** This points ahead to Acts, where Gentiles welcome the gospel. Matthew includes Jesus' warning that many will come from east and west to the kingdom while "sons of the kingdom" are thrown out (Matt 8:11–12). Luke keeps that saying for 13:28–29.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the elders' praise "a testimony most precious," and links the centurion's love for Israel to the truth that "salvation is from the Jews" (John 4:22). Henry draws from v2 that "servants should study to endear themselves to their masters," and from v9 that Christ wants his followers to notice great examples of faith. Guzik notes how unusual it was for a master to care so much for a sick slave, and stresses that the centurion's strong faith and deep humility go together.

#### Jesus Raises a Widow's Son (vv11–17)
- **"A town called Nain" (v11).** It appears only here in the Bible. It is usually identified with the modern village of Nein, on the north slope of the Hill of Moreh, about six miles southeast of Nazareth. Just around the hill lay Shunem, where Elisha raised a woman's only son (2 Kings 4:8–37). The echo would not be lost on people who knew the land.
- **"As he drew near to the gate" (v12).** Two crowds meet at the gate: one following the Lord of life, and one following a dead body out of town. The dead were buried outside the walls, usually on the day of death, carried on an open stretcher with the women mourning in front.
- **"The only son of his mother, and she was a widow" (v12).** Luke piles up the grief. With no husband and no son, she had lost not only her child but her support, her place in the community and her future. The Old Testament speaks of mourning for an only son as the deepest grief (Jer 6:26; Amos 8:10; Zech 12:10). God repeatedly commands care for widows (Exod 22:22; Deut 10:18).
- **"When the Lord saw her" (v13).** Here for the first time the narrator himself calls Jesus "the Lord" (*ho kyrios*), the title the Greek Old Testament uses for God. Luke does it often from here on.
- **"He had compassion on her" (v13).** The verb *splanchnizomai* comes from the word for the inner organs, a compassion felt in the gut. Luke uses it again for the Good Samaritan (10:33) and the father of the prodigal (15:20). No one asked Jesus for anything. His mercy moved first.
- **"Do not weep" (v13).** From anyone else, the words would ring hollow. From him, they are a promise about to be kept.
- **"He came up and touched the bier, and the bearers stood still" (v14).** Touching anything connected with a corpse made a person unclean (Num 19:11–16). Jesus touches it, as he touched the leper (5:13), and instead of being defiled he brings life.
- **"Young man, I say to you, arise" (v14).** Elijah and Elisha prayed and stretched themselves over the dead child, sometimes more than once (1 Kings 17:21; 2 Kings 4:33–35). Jesus simply speaks, on his own authority: "I say to you."
- **"Jesus gave him to his mother" (v15).** These are the exact words of the Greek Old Testament in 1 Kings 17:23, where Elijah raised the son of the widow of Zarephath and "delivered him to his mother." Luke means the reader to hear it.
- **"A great prophet has arisen among us!" and "God has visited his people!" (v16).** The crowd reaches for Elijah and Elisha. That is true but not enough. "Visited" recalls Zechariah's song: God "has visited and redeemed his people" (1:68, 78). It is more literally true than they know.
- **Raised to die again.** This young man came back to ordinary life and would die again later. Jesus' own resurrection is different, a rising to a life that death can no longer touch (Rom 6:9). The raisings in the Gospels are signs pointing to that.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the details "affecting particulars, told with delightful simplicity," and says v13 has comforted the bereaved for generations. It sees here "Incarnate Compassion" using divine power to dry a widow's tears, a miracle beyond Elijah's and Elisha's. Henry: "Christ's dominion over death was evidenced by the immediate effect of his word." Guzik notes that Jesus spoke to the dead as if he were alive (Rom 4:17) and treated death as an enemy. He calls this a restoration of mortal life rather than resurrection in the full sense.

#### Messengers from John the Baptist (vv18–35)
- **"The disciples of John reported all these things to him" (v18).** John was in prison at Machaerus, Herod Antipas' fortress east of the Dead Sea (3:19–20; the location comes from Josephus). His followers kept visiting and bringing news.
- **"Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?" (v19).** "The one who is to come" was a way of speaking of the Messiah (Mal 3:1; Ps 118:26). John had announced one who would bring a winnowing fork and unquenchable fire (3:16–17). Instead he hears of healings and meals with sinners, while he sits in Herod's dungeon. Some think John was asking for his disciples' sake. The plainer reading is that the prophet himself was struggling. Faithful people can have honest questions, and John brought his to Jesus.
- **"In that hour he healed many" (v21).** Jesus does not give a speech. He lets the messengers watch.
- **"The blind receive their sight, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, the poor have good news preached to them" (v22).** This is a weave of Isaiah's promises for the age of salvation (Isa 29:18–19; 35:5–6; 26:19; 61:1). Every item has just happened in Luke, from the leper (5:12–13) to the widow's son (7:15). The list ends not with the most spectacular item but with "the poor have good news preached to them," the heart of Jesus' Nazareth sermon (4:18).
- **What Jesus leaves out.** Isaiah 61:1–2 also speaks of "liberty to the captives" and "the day of vengeance." John is a captive, and the judgment has not yet fallen. Jesus answers with what the kingdom is doing now and leaves the rest in God's timing.
- **"Blessed is the one who is not offended by me" (v23).** "Offended" is *skandalizō*, from *skandalon*, the trigger-stick of a trap, and so anything that trips a person up. Jesus gently warns John not to stumble over a Messiah who does not match his expectations.
- **"A reed shaken by the wind? … A man dressed in soft clothing?" (vv24–25).** Reeds grew thick along the Jordan where John preached, bending with every breeze. John was nothing like that. Nor was he a courtier; Herod Antipas was the one in fine clothes, and John was in prison for telling him the truth. Some scholars note that Antipas put a reed on his early coins, which may add a sharp edge to the picture.
- **"More than a prophet… 'Behold, I send my messenger before your face'" (vv26–27).** The quote is Malachi 3:1, with an echo of Exodus 23:20. In Malachi the messenger prepares the way for the Lord himself, who then comes to his temple. Malachi 4:5 calls this messenger "Elijah." John was the herald, and Jesus is the one heralded.
- **"Among those born of women none is greater than John. Yet the one who is least in the kingdom of God is greater than he" (v28).** John stood at the edge of the new age and pointed into it. The point is not that every believer is more devoted than John. It is that the least person who shares in the kingdom Jesus brings, with the cross, the resurrection and the Spirit, stands in a greater position than the greatest prophet who only announced it.
- **"They declared God just… but the Pharisees and the lawyers rejected the purpose of God for themselves" (vv29–30).** The ESV puts these verses in parentheses as Luke's own comment. "Declared God just" means they agreed God was right in calling them to repent, and they showed it by being baptized. The religious experts refused, and in doing so turned down God's plan for them. "Lawyers" means experts in the law of Moses, not courtroom attorneys.
- **"Children sitting in the marketplace" (vv31–32).** The picture is of children playing make-believe, wedding one minute and funeral the next. One group complains that the others will not join in. John came with a call to mourn and repent, and they would not weep. Jesus came with the joy of the wedding feast, and they would not dance.
- **"He has a demon… A glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!" (vv33–34).** Critics found a reason to dismiss each messenger, the austere one and the feasting one. "Glutton and drunkard" echoes the charge against a rebellious son in Deuteronomy 21:20, a serious accusation. The insult "friend of… sinners" became, in Christian memory, one of his dearest titles.
- **"Wisdom is justified by all her children" (v35).** In Proverbs, Wisdom is pictured as a woman calling people to life (Prov 8–9). Her "children" are the people who respond: the tax collectors of v29, and, in the next story, a sinful woman. Their changed lives prove God's wisdom right. Matthew 11:19 reads "by her deeds."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls John "a man of unshaken self-consistence," and on the children's game says "this is the ruin of multitudes, they can never persuade themselves to be serious in the concerns of their souls." JFB pictures them "as cross, capricious children," and reads "justified God" as the people approving John's baptism. Guzik suggests John's question grew out of prison discouragement and expectations of a political deliverer, and explains *skandalon* as the trigger of a bird trap. He also lists what made John great, while stressing that the least believer after the cross has a greater privilege.

#### A Sinful Woman Forgiven (vv36–50)
- **"One of the Pharisees asked him to eat with him" (v36).** Luke is the only Gospel that shows Jesus dining with Pharisees, and he does it three times (11:37; 14:1). Simon is curious, perhaps testing, but not openly hostile. "Reclined at table" means Jesus lay on a couch, propped on his left elbow, with his feet stretched away from the table. That is how a woman could stand "behind him at his feet."
- **"A woman of the city, who was a sinner" (v37).** Her sin was known in town, and the wording suggests sexual immorality, perhaps prostitution. Banquets in that culture were semi-public, and people could come in and watch from the edges. Her presence is shocking, but not impossible.
- **Who was she?** Luke does not name her. Since the sixth century, Western tradition has often identified her with Mary Magdalene, but nothing in the text supports that. Mary Magdalene is introduced in the very next paragraph (8:2) as someone delivered from demons, not as this woman. The anointing at Bethany by Mary the sister of Lazarus, days before the crucifixion (Matt 26:6–13; Mark 14:3–9; John 12:1–8), is a different event, in Judea, near the end of the ministry, with a different meaning. The host there is also a Simon, but Simon was one of the most common names in Israel.
- **"An alabaster flask of ointment" (v37).** Alabaster was a soft, translucent stone used for expensive perfume jars. This was costly, perhaps her most valuable possession.
- **"Weeping… wiped them with the hair of her head and kissed his feet" (v38).** Her tears fall on his feet, and she has no towel, so she lets down her hair. A respectable woman did not unbind her hair in public. Kissing the feet was an act of deep humility and gratitude. She does not speak a word in the whole story.
- **"If this man were a prophet, he would have known who and what sort of woman this is" (v39).** Simon thinks it but does not say it. His logic: a prophet would know and would not let her touch him. Jesus is about to show that he knows exactly who she is, and exactly what Simon is thinking.
- **"Simon, I have something to say to you" (v40).** The Pharisee who doubted Jesus was a prophet is about to have his thoughts read aloud.
- **Two debtors (vv41–42).** A denarius was a day's wage for a laborer. Five hundred denarii was well over a year's earnings, and fifty was nearly two months'. Neither could pay. The verb for "cancelled" is *charizomai*, from *charis*, "grace": he graciously forgave both.
- **"The one, I suppose" (v43).** Simon's "I suppose" sounds reluctant. He sees where this is going.
- **"Do you see this woman?" (v44).** Simon had seen her only as a "sinner." Jesus asks him to really look.
- **"You gave me no water… no kiss… did not anoint my head" (vv44–46).** These were ordinary courtesies to an honored guest: water for dusty feet, a kiss of greeting, a little olive oil on the head. Simon skipped all three. She did all three, more than over: tears instead of water, feet kissed instead of a cheek, costly perfume instead of common oil. The contrast is built in exact pairs.
- **"Her sins, which are many, are forgiven—for she loved much" (v47).** Does she love in order to be forgiven, or because she has been forgiven? The parable answers it. The debtor loves because the debt was cancelled. So "for she loved much" gives the evidence, not the cause: her great love shows she has been forgiven much. The Greek perfect tense, "have been forgiven," also points to a forgiveness already received, likely through some earlier encounter with Jesus or his message. Roman Catholic interpreters have sometimes read love here as part of what disposes a sinner to receive forgiveness. Most Protestant interpreters, and many Catholic ones, stress the order the parable gives: forgiveness first, love in response. Both agree that faith and love go together.
- **"He who is forgiven little, loves little" (v47).** This is aimed at Simon. The problem is not that Simon has few sins. It is that he thinks he has few.
- **"Your sins are forgiven" (v48).** Jesus now says it to her directly, in front of everyone. Her standing in the town might not change overnight, but her standing before God has been declared out loud.
- **"Who is this, who even forgives sins?" (v49).** The question of 5:21 comes back. The answer has been building all chapter: a Lord whose word heals at a distance, who raises the dead, who fulfills Isaiah, and who forgives sins.
- **"Your faith has saved you; go in peace" (v50).** Jesus says she was saved by her faith, not her love, which is the response. "Go in peace" is the Hebrew blessing *shalom*: wholeness and rightness with God.
- **Commentators:** JFB says there is "no ground whatever" for making her Mary Magdalene, compares the parable to Nathan trapping David into judging himself, and reads "for she loved much" as meaning "in proof of which." It concludes that Jesus is either a blasphemer or God manifest in the flesh. Henry writes that it "well becomes penitents… to renew their godly sorrow and shame for sin," and on v50, "they who know that their faith hath saved them may go in peace." Guzik agrees with JFB and the Tyndale notes that her love is the evidence of forgiveness, not its cause. He notes that Simon saw only her past while Jesus saw what she had become, and calls this forgiveness the greatest work in the chapter, because a healed body dies but forgiven sin stays forgiven.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke 7 answers the question "Who is this?" piece by piece. He is the Lord whose word reaches across distance to heal a Gentile's slave. He is the one who sees a widow's tears, touches the bier and gives her son back, greater than Elijah. He is the one John was sent to announce, whose works fulfill Isaiah's promises. He is the friend of tax collectors and sinners, mocked for it and glad to bear the name. And he forgives a debt no one could pay, the debt he would cancel by his own death, so that a woman the town called "a sinner" could hear "Your faith has saved you; go in peace."
