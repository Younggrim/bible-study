*Setting:* Psalm 102 has an unusual title. It names no author but describes the person praying: "A Prayer of one afflicted, when he is faint and pours out his complaint before the LORD." It is the only psalm titled this way, and the title invites anyone in that condition to use it. The psalm combines a personal lament with prayer for the city of Zion, which appears to be in ruins (vv13–14). So many scholars place it during or near the end of the Babylonian exile (586–538 BC), when Jerusalem's walls and temple lay in rubble and the faithful waited for the seventy years Jeremiah had foretold (Jer 25:11–12; 29:10). Henry mentions older views that David wrote it during Absalom's revolt, or that Daniel or Nehemiah did. The text does not say. The church has long counted it among the seven "penitential psalms" (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143), though it is less a confession of sin than a cry of suffering. It follows Psalm 101's royal vow and Psalms 93–100's songs of God's kingship. Here someone is living through what looks like the collapse of everything those psalms celebrated, and holds on to the King anyway. Hebrews 1:10–12 quotes vv25–27 as words spoken to the Son. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's main movements.

#### Hear My Prayer, O LORD (vv1–11)
- **"Hear my prayer, O LORD; let my cry come to you!" (v1).** The first two verses are woven almost entirely from phrases found in other psalms (Ps 18:6; 27:9; 31:2; 39:12; 69:17). That is not a lack of feeling. A suffering person reaches for words already given, the prayers of God's people, when their own words run out.
- **"Do not hide your face from me in the day of my distress!" (v2).** To have God's face turned toward someone is to have his favor (Num 6:25–26). To have it hidden is the deepest kind of trouble (Ps 13:1; 27:9). The Tyndale notes observe that Israel experienced God's face as hidden during the exile (Deut 31:17; Isa 54:8). **"Answer me speedily"** shows how close to the end he feels.
- **"My days pass away like smoke, and my bones burn like a furnace" (v3).** Smoke rises and vanishes (Ps 37:20; James 4:14). "Bones" stand for the whole person. He is burning up from the inside, perhaps with fever, perhaps with grief.
- **"My heart is struck down like grass and has withered; I forget to eat my bread" (v4).** In Israel grass sprang up after the rains and was scorched within weeks by the sun and the hot east wind. Grief has taken away his appetite, as it did for David when his child was dying (2 Sam 12:16–17).
- **"Because of my loud groaning my bones cling to my flesh" (v5).** He has wasted away to skin and bones. Job said the same: "My bones stick to my skin and to my flesh" (Job 19:20). Much of this psalm's language recalls Job.
- **"I am like a desert owl of the wilderness, like an owl of the waste places" (v6).** The exact birds are uncertain. The first (*qa'at*) has been translated "pelican" (KJV), "vulture" or "desert owl," and the second (*kos*) is probably the little owl. Both are listed among the birds that live in ruins: Isaiah says the *qa'at* will possess ruined Edom (Isa 34:11), and Zephaniah says it will lodge in the ruins of Nineveh (Zeph 2:14). The picture is loneliness among rubble, which fits a man mourning a ruined city.
- **"I lie awake; I am like a lonely sparrow on the housetop" (v7).** "Sparrow" (*tsippor*) can mean any small bird. Flat roofs were where people slept on hot nights, so a lone bird there, awake while the household sleeps, is the picture of a sleepless, solitary person.
- **"All the day my enemies taunt me; those who deride me use my name for a curse" (v8).** His suffering has become a byword. People curse by saying "may you end up like him," as in Jeremiah 29:22, where the names of executed false prophets became a curse formula. JFB explains, "wishing others as miserable as I am" (Num 5:21).
- **"For I eat ashes like bread and mingle tears with my drink" (v9).** Mourners sat in ashes and put dust on their heads (2 Sam 13:19; Job 2:8). The ashes are so constant they might as well be his food. Psalm 80:5 speaks of God feeding his people "with the bread of tears."
- **"Because of your indignation and anger; for you have taken me up and thrown me down" (v10).** The deepest pain is not the illness or the enemies. It is the sense that God is behind it. The image may be of a wrestler who lifts his opponent high only to slam him down harder, or of a wind that picks up chaff and flings it away (Isa 64:6). The psalmist does not explain why God is angry. Whether for his own sin or the nation's (as the exile was), he takes his trouble to God honestly.
- **"My days are like an evening shadow; I wither away like grass" (v11).** An evening shadow grows longer and longer and then vanishes into night. This verse ends the first section where v3 and v4 began: days fading, life withering.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes how carefully the title is chosen: "here, as often elsewhere, the Holy Ghost has drawn up our petition for us, has put words into our mouths." He says the writer was "afflicted with the church and for it; and on those that are of a public spirit afflictions of that kind lie heavier than any other." JFB says "the figures express extreme loneliness," and that "voice… groaning" is "effect put for cause, my agony emaciates me." He reads the "shadow that declineth" as one "soon to vanish in the darkness of night." Guzik observes that the affliction was made far worse by the sense that God did not see or care, and that the psalmist's tone and phrasing borrow from Job, the Bible's great example of suffering.

#### But You, O LORD, Are Enthroned Forever (vv12–17)
- **"But you, O LORD, are enthroned forever" (v12).** Everything turns on this line. Verses 1–11 were full of "I," "me" and "my." Now the focus lifts to God. The psalmist's days are smoke, shadow and grass. God sits on his throne forever. Psalm 90:1–2 makes the same contrast, and this verse echoes the "The LORD reigns" psalms just before (93:2). **"You are remembered throughout all generations"** means God's name is called on in every age. JFB reads it as "that by which Thou art remembered, Thy promise."
- **"You will arise and have pity on Zion" (v13).** His own trouble and Zion's are tied together. "Have pity" (*racham*) is tender compassion, the love of a mother for her child (the word is related to "womb"). **"It is the time to favor her; the appointed time has come."** "Appointed time" (*mo'ed*) is a fixed, set time. If the psalm comes from the exile, the psalmist may be thinking of the seventy years Jeremiah foretold. Daniel did the same thing: he read in Jeremiah that the seventy years were nearly up and began to pray (Dan 9:2–3).
- **"For your servants hold her stones dear and have pity on her dust" (v14).** God's people love the city even in ruins. They treasure the broken stones and grieve over the dust. Nehemiah wept when he heard that Jerusalem's walls lay broken down (Neh 1:3–4). JFB sees this love as itself a sign that the set time has come: when God's people care deeply about Zion, it shows God is about to act.
- **"Nations will fear the name of the LORD, and all the kings of the earth will fear your glory" (v15).** The rebuilding of Zion will not be a private matter. When God restores his people, the nations will see his hand (Isa 60:1–3; Ezek 36:23).
- **"For the LORD builds up Zion; he appears in his glory" (v16).** The verbs may be read as prophetic past tense, describing a future event as though already done ("the LORD has built up Zion"), which shows how certain the psalmist is.
- **"He regards the prayer of the destitute and does not despise their prayer" (v17).** "Destitute" (*ar'ar*) means stripped bare. Jeremiah uses a related word for a shrub in the desert (Jer 17:6). The God who rebuilds cities also listens to people who have nothing. The psalmist is one of them.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v16 as "Because the Lord hath built," and says that in building Zion "and hearing the humble, He is most glorious." Henry says the psalmist "comforts himself" in his trouble "with the consideration of God's eternity, and the church's prosperity and perpetuity." Guzik points out that although overwhelmed by his own ruin, the psalmist did not turn completely inward but cared about the community. He applies the love for Zion's stones to love for every believer, each one a living stone in God's house (1 Pet 2:5).

#### Let This Be Recorded for a Generation to Come (vv18–22)
- **"Let this be recorded for a generation to come" (v18).** The psalmist wants God's answer written down. People remember injuries far better than kindnesses, and Israel was told again and again to record and retell what God had done (Exod 17:14; Ps 78:4–6). The psalm itself is that record.
- **"So that a people yet to be created may praise the LORD" (v18).** "Created" (*bara*) is the word used for God's creating in Genesis 1:1. It suggests a people God will make new, a restored nation, or even a people not yet born. Isaiah speaks of God creating "Jerusalem to be a joy" (Isa 65:18). The psalmist is praying for people he will never meet.
- **"He looked down from his holy height; from heaven the LORD looked at the earth" (v19).** God is high above, but not distant. He looks down, as he did when he "saw the affliction" of his people in Egypt (Exod 3:7).
- **"To hear the groans of the prisoners, to set free those who were doomed to die" (v20).** "Doomed to die" is literally "sons of death," people under sentence of death. The exiles in Babylon were like prisoners on death row (Ps 79:11 uses almost the same words). God looks down to release them.
- **"That they may declare in Zion the name of the LORD, and in Jerusalem his praise" (v21).** The freed prisoners will come home and praise God in the very city that lay in ruins.
- **"When peoples gather together, and kingdoms, to worship the LORD" (v22).** The vision widens beyond Israel. Restored Zion will become the place where the nations gather to worship (Isa 2:2–4; Zech 8:20–23). The Tyndale notes see Jews and Gentiles worshiping together here.
- **Commentators:** JFB takes "people… created" to mean "an organized body, as a Church," and v21 to mean "God's name may be celebrated in the assemblies of His Church, gathered from all nations (Zec 8:20-23)." Henry says of the whole psalm that it "is intended for the use of others that may be in the like distress; for whatsoever things were written aforetime were written designedly for our use." Guzik notes that God has people in mind before they exist, and that registers of God's kindness deserve to be kept as carefully as records of disasters.

#### Of Old You Laid the Foundation of the Earth (vv23–28)
- **"He has broken my strength in midcourse; he has shortened my days" (v23).** The psalmist returns to his own pain. "In midcourse" is literally "in the way," on the journey, before reaching its end. He feels he is dying before his time.
- **"'O my God,' I say, 'take me not away in the midst of my days'" (v24).** He asks not to be cut off halfway through life. Hezekiah prayed similarly when told he would die (Isa 38:10). **"You whose years endure throughout all generations!"** He sets his short years against God's endless ones. Surely the God who has all the years in the world can spare him a few.
- **A textual note on vv23–24.** The Greek translation reads the consonants of the first word in v23 differently: as "he answered" (*'anah*) rather than "he afflicted" (*'innah*). In that reading, God answers the one who prays, and the words that follow, including vv25–27, become God speaking to him. This helps explain how Hebrews, which quotes the Greek, can present vv25–27 as God addressing his Son.
- **"Of old you laid the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are the work of your hands" (v25).** God is older than the earth. He laid its foundations like a builder (Job 38:4; Isa 48:13).
- **"They will perish, but you will remain; they will all wear out like a garment" (v26).** Even the heavens and earth, the most permanent things anyone knows, are not permanent. They grow old like clothes. Isaiah says the same: "the heavens vanish like smoke, the earth will wear out like a garment" (Isa 51:6).
- **"You will change them like a robe, and they will pass away" (v26).** A person changes worn-out clothes for new ones. God will one day do this with creation. The New Testament speaks of new heavens and a new earth (Isa 65:17; 2 Pet 3:10–13; Rev 21:1).
- **"But you are the same, and your years have no end" (v27).** "You are the same" is literally "you are he" (*attah hu*), a phrase Isaiah uses as almost a divine name: "I am he; I am the first, and I am the last" (Isa 41:4; 48:12). Everything changes. God does not (Mal 3:6; James 1:17).
- **"The children of your servants shall dwell secure; their offspring shall be established before you" (v28).** The psalm ends not with the psalmist's recovery but with the future of God's people. He may be cut off in midcourse, but because God's years have no end, the next generation will live secure in God's presence. That is the answer to v18's "generation to come."
- **Hebrews 1:10–12.** The writer of Hebrews quotes vv25–27 as one of several Old Testament passages showing that the Son is greater than the angels. He introduces it as words said "of the Son": "You, Lord, laid the foundation of the earth in the beginning." Elsewhere the New Testament says all things were made through the Son (John 1:3; Col 1:16). So the eternal, unchanging Creator of this psalm is identified with Jesus. Hebrews 13:8 echoes v27: "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v24 as the church's prayer during the exile not to be "cut off till the Messiah shall come," but also as a prayer an individual may pray, asking to be spared "to do him further service." On Hebrews 1 he writes, "He saith this to the Son, and in that exposition we must acquiesce," and draws comfort that "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, to-day, and for ever." JFB says "God's eternal existence is a pledge of faithfulness to His promises," and that Paul (as JFB takes the author of Hebrews to be) applies these verses "to Christ in His divine nature." Most scholars today do not think Paul wrote Hebrews. Guzik notes that the psalmist was thinking of God without any clear sense of the Trinity, but that Hebrews rightly sees these as words of the Father to the Son, drawing on the Greek translation. He adds that, like Paul, the psalmist recognized God's hand even in his weakness (2 Cor 12:9–10).

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 102 is the prayer of a man cut off in the middle of his days, mocked by enemies, feeling thrown down by God's wrath, and still trusting the God whose years have no end. Jesus prayed this way. He was cut off in midcourse in his early thirties, mocked by those who passed by, and on the cross he felt the full weight of God's judgment against sin, crying "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34). Yet Hebrews tells us that the same Jesus is the Lord who laid the foundations of the earth, who remains when the heavens wear out, and who is always "the same" (Heb 1:10–12). The afflicted one and the eternal Creator meet in him. Because he was raised and lives forever, "the children of your servants shall dwell secure" (v28), and those who were "doomed to die" (v20) are set free (Heb 2:14–15).
