*Setting:* Thirty-eight years pass in silence between chapters 19 and 20. Numbers 33:36–39 shows that Israel is back at Kadesh in the fortieth year after the exodus, the very place where their parents had refused to enter the land (chs 13–14). Most of that first generation is now buried in the desert. The people in this chapter are mostly their children. Moses is nearly 120 and Aaron about 123. This is the year of transition, and it begins with three endings: Miriam dies, Moses and Aaron lose the right to lead Israel into Canaan, and Aaron dies on Mount Hor. In between, Edom, Israel's brother nation, shuts the road. The new generation will have to find another way.

#### The Death of Miriam (v1)
- **"Came into the wilderness of Zin in the first month" (v1).** The year is not given here, but comparing 33:38 (Aaron died in the fortieth year, fifth month) makes it most likely the first month of the fortieth year. The wilderness of Zin lies around Kadesh, on Canaan's southern edge.
- **"Miriam died there and was buried there" (v1).** One plain sentence for the woman who watched over baby Moses in the Nile reeds (Exod 2:4–8), led Israel's women in song at the sea (Exod 15:20–21), and later challenged her brother (Num 12). No mourning is recorded, which is striking next to the thirty days for Aaron (v29). The sentence of chapter 14 spared no one, not even the prophet's own sister. Micah 6:4 still remembers her alongside Moses and Aaron as one of the three God sent to lead his people.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes Miriam's mixed record, brave as a girl, a leader in praise, yet once in rebellion, and sees her death as proof that God made no exceptions to his sentence, even for Moses' family. Henry calls her a prophetess who did real good for Israel, yet, having murmured, she "must not enter Canaan." JFB places her death "four months before Aaron."

#### The Waters of Meribah (vv2–9)
- **"There was no water for the congregation" (v2).** Kadesh had springs, but the dry season, or the needs of a huge camp, could exhaust them. This is almost a replay of Exodus 17:1–7, the first time the people quarreled with Moses over water, at Rephidim, near the start of the journey. Forty years later, the children sound exactly like their parents.
- **"Would that we had perished when our brothers perished before the LORD!" (v3).** They mean those who died in Korah's rebellion and the plagues (16:35, 49). "Quarreled" (*riv*) is the verb behind the name Meribah (v13), a word for a legal dispute or an argument. They are not only thirsty. They are picking a fight.
- **"Why have you made us come up out of Egypt to bring us to this evil place?" (v5).** The same old complaint, now a generation old. **"No place for grain or figs or vines or pomegranates"**: these are exactly the fruits the spies had carried back from Canaan (13:23). They are blaming Moses for not giving them the land their parents refused.
- **Moses and Aaron "fell on their faces" (v6).** Their usual response (14:5; 16:4, 22, 45). **"The glory of the LORD appeared to them."** This time there is no threat of destruction. God simply gives instructions.
- **"Take the staff… and tell the rock before their eyes to yield its water" (v8).** The key instruction: take the staff, but *speak* to the rock. At Rephidim God had told Moses to strike the rock (Exod 17:6). Here he is to speak, in front of everyone. Notice also that God does not rebuke the people for their complaint. He just provides.
- **"Moses took the staff from before the LORD" (v9).** "From before the LORD" suggests the staff kept in the tabernacle. Some think this was Aaron's budded staff, laid up "before the testimony" as "a sign for the rebels" (17:10). If so, it was meant to be held up as a reminder of God's chosen priesthood, not used as a club. Others think it was Moses' own staff, the "staff of God" used in the plagues (Exod 4:20; 17:9), since v11 calls it "his staff." The text allows either.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees ingratitude in the people's wish that they had died, and in their choosing "the house of bondage before the land of promise." Guzik cites Clarke: "As the fathers murmured, so also did the children." He stresses that God told Moses only to take the staff and speak to the rock, not to lecture anyone. JFB calls the tabernacle Moses and Aaron's "usual refuge in seasons of perplexity and danger," and identifies the staff as "the wonder-working rod" kept in the tabernacle.

#### Moses Strikes the Rock (vv10–13)
- **"Hear now, you rebels" (v10).** "Rebels" (*morim*) is a harsh word. It is true, but God had not told Moses to say it. Psalm 106:32–33 says the people "made his spirit bitter, and he spoke rashly with his lips."
- **"Shall we bring water for you out of this rock?" (v10).** "We." For a moment Moses and Aaron stand where only God should stand, as if the miracle were theirs. It can also be heard as exasperation: "Must we do this for you again?"
- **"Struck the rock with his staff twice" (v11).** God said speak. Moses struck, and struck twice. After forty years of grumbling, the meekest man on earth (12:3) finally boils over.
- **"Water came out abundantly" (v11).** God still provides. His people's need is met even though his servant has failed. God's blessing does not mean God approves of everything his servant did.
- **"Because you did not believe in me, to uphold me as holy in the eyes of the people of Israel" (v12).** God names the sin. It was not simply losing his temper. It was unbelief, not trusting that God's word alone would be enough, and it was failing to show God as holy, the one who gives water by his word. Other passages describe it as rebellion against God's command (20:24; 27:14) and breaking faith (Deut 32:51). And it happened publicly, "in the eyes of the people," at a moment meant to display God's glory.
- **"Therefore you shall not bring this assembly into the land" (v12).** The punishment seems severe for one outburst after forty faithful years. But leaders who stand before God's people are held to a stricter account (Jas 3:1), and what Moses did in public taught the whole nation something false about God. Moses later begged God to let him cross over, and God refused (Deut 3:23–27). Yet God showed him the land from Mount Nebo (Deut 34:1–4), and centuries later Moses stood with Jesus on a mountain in the promised land (Luke 9:30–31).
- **Why striking was so serious: the rock and Christ.** Paul writes that Israel "drank from the spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4). Many interpreters see a deeper reason here. At Rephidim the rock was struck once, and water flowed. Christ was struck once for all (Heb 9:28; 10:10). After that, the life-giving water comes by asking, by speaking (John 4:10; 7:37). Striking the rock again spoiled the picture. That is a reading of the passage in the light of the New Testament, not something the text of Numbers states, but it fits well.
- **"These are the waters of Meribah… and through them he showed himself holy" (v13).** Meribah means "quarreling." The Rephidim site had the same name (Exod 17:7), so this one is sometimes called Meribah-kadesh (Deut 32:51). The last line has a wordplay: Kadesh means "holy," and "showed himself holy" is *yiqqadesh*. Moses failed to show God as holy, so God showed himself holy, by keeping his word in judgment even on his greatest servant.
- **Commentators:** Henry lists the faults: they struck when told to speak, they took credit for the miracle and so "did not sanctify God," and the root was unbelief, "You believed me not." He notes that their punishment was "the disgrace and mortification of dying" short of Canaan. Guzik sees the root sin as unbelief together with failure to honor God before the people, and develops the picture of Christ the rock, struck once. He cites Morgan: Moses' failure was natural, and that was its danger. JFB says the striking "betrayed a doubt, not of the power, but of the will of God," and suggests there may have been other unrecorded factors behind so severe a sentence.

#### Edom Refuses Passage (vv14–21)
- **"Thus says your brother Israel" (v14).** Edom was descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Gen 25:30; 36:1). Their land lay south and east of the Dead Sea, in the rugged hills of Seir. Israel had been told not to fight Edom: "you shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deut 23:7; 2:4–6).
- **The message (vv14–16).** It follows the formal diplomatic style of the time, the way kings wrote to each other: a greeting, a review of history, then the request. It tells Israel's story of slavery and deliverance by God's **"angel"** (v16), the angel of God's presence who went before them (Exod 14:19; 23:20).
- **"We will go along the King's Highway" (v17).** This was the main north–south trade route east of the Jordan, running from the Gulf of Aqaba up to Damascus along the high plateau. Israel promises to stay on the road, not touch their fields or vineyards, and even **"pay for"** any water (v19). In the dry East, water from wells was often sold.
- **"You shall not pass through, lest I come out with the sword" (v18).** Edom refuses twice, and backs up the refusal with **"a large army and with a strong force"** (v20).
- **"So Israel turned away from him" (v21).** Israel does not fight. They obey God's instruction not to attack their brother (Deut 2:4–5), and take the long way around. The old tension between Jacob and Esau (Gen 27:41) lived on. Later prophets condemned Edom for its hostility to Israel, especially when Jerusalem fell (Obadiah 10–14; Ps 137:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Israel's appeal rested on kinship and that Edom still refused. Guzik draws attention to Israel's restraint: they left vengeance to the LORD. He cites Wenham on the diplomatic letter form, and Clarke's view that the refusal was unjust. JFB calls Edom's refusal "churlish" and notes that "the practice of levying a tax for the use of the wells is universal" in the dry East.

#### The Death of Aaron (vv22–29)
- **"Mount Hor, on the border of the land of Edom" (vv22–23).** Its exact location is unknown. Tradition, from Josephus on, places it at Jebel Harun ("Aaron's mountain") near Petra, where a small shrine still stands on the summit. Many modern scholars think it was nearer Kadesh, since Israel had not yet entered Edom's territory.
- **"Let Aaron be gathered to his people" (v24).** A gentle Old Testament phrase for death (Gen 25:8; 35:29), implying joining those who had gone before. God tells Aaron ahead of time, so he can die with dignity, in full view of what is happening.
- **"Because you rebelled against my command at the waters of Meribah" (v24).** Aaron shared in the failure, so he shares in the sentence.
- **"Strip Aaron of his garments and put them on Eleazar his son" (v26).** The high priest's holy garments (Exod 28) are passed on while Aaron is still alive. Moses, who had once dressed Aaron in them at his ordination (Lev 8:7–9), now takes them off him. The office continues even when the man dies.
- **"They went up Mount Hor in the sight of all the congregation" (v27).** Three men climb, and two come down (v28). The whole people watch them go.
- **"Aaron died there on the top of the mountain" (v28).** Numbers 33:38–39 records the date, the first day of the fifth month of the fortieth year, and his age, 123. He had been Moses' spokesman since the burning bush, the first high priest, the man who once made a golden calf and later stood between the dead and the living.
- **"All the house of Israel wept for Aaron thirty days" (v29).** Thirty days was a long, solemn mourning, the same honor later given to Moses (Deut 34:8). Normal mourning was seven days (Gen 50:10). The people who had once grumbled against him mourned him deeply.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees God's notice as a kindness that let Aaron prepare, and stresses that the office mattered more than the man. He points to Jesus as the high priest who never dies (Heb 7:23–25), and observes that Moses, Aaron and Miriam all fell short of Canaan, but Joshua, whose name is the Hebrew form of "Jesus," would lead Israel in. JFB says "the manner of his death was arranged in tenderness of love," explains the removal of the robes as "in token of his resignation" and Eleazar's investiture as "the inauguration into his high office," and sees Aaron's death as a sign of "the imperfection of the Levitical priesthood."

#### Pointing to Christ
"The Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4). God's people were dying of thirst, and he gave them water from a rock, even when his servant failed. Jesus stood up at the feast and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37). He was struck once, and now the water flows to all who ask. Moses, the greatest prophet, could not bring Israel into the land. Aaron, the first high priest, died on a mountain and had to hand on his robes. Hebrews draws the contrast: "the former priests were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing in office, but he holds his priesthood permanently, because he continues forever" (Heb 7:23–24).
