*Setting:* This is the climax of God's second speech from the whirlwind (40:6–41:34). God has dared Job to put on royal majesty and bring down every proud and wicked person (40:10–14), and then shown him Behemoth, a huge beast only its Maker can approach (40:15–24). Now comes Leviathan, the most terrifying creature in the whole tour, and the only one God describes at such length. Chapter 41 is entirely about him. It is the last thing God says before Job's final reply (42:1–6). In Hebrew Bibles the chapter numbering is slightly different: English 41:1–8 is Hebrew 40:25–32, so the Hebrew chapter 41 has 26 verses.

#### The LORD Challenges Job (vv1–11)
- **"Can you draw out Leviathan with a fishhook?" (v1).** The name *Livyathan* probably comes from a root meaning "to twist" or "coil." It appears six times in the Bible. In Psalm 104:26 Leviathan is a sea creature God made "to play in" the sea. In Psalm 74:14 God "crushed the heads of Leviathan." Isaiah 27:1 promises that God will punish "Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent... the dragon that is in the sea." Job himself mentioned those "ready to rouse up Leviathan" when he cursed his birthday (3:8). So by the time God names him, the reader already knows Leviathan is something more than a big fish.
- **"Or press down his tongue with a cord?" (v1).** Can Job land him like an ordinary catch? JFB notes that a crocodile's tongue is tiny and fixed to its lower jaw, which makes the question even more absurd.
- **"Can you put a rope in his nose or pierce his jaw with a hook?" (v2).** Captives and animals were led by hooks and rings through the nose or jaw. Assyrian kings boasted of leading conquered rulers that way, and God says he will do the same to Assyria (2 Kings 19:28) and to Gog (Ezek 38:4).
- **"Will he make many pleas to you? Will he speak to you soft words?" (v3).** The picture turns comic. Will Leviathan beg Job for mercy?
- **"Will he make a covenant with you to take him for your servant forever?" (v4).** "Covenant" and "servant forever" are the language of treaties and slavery (Exod 21:5–6; Deut 15:17). Will this monster sign a contract to work for Job? Job had complained that God would not come to terms with him (9:32–33). Here the creature will not come to terms with Job.
- **"Will you play with him as with a bird, or will you put him on a leash for your girls?" (v5).** A pet for the children? God made Leviathan to "play" in the sea (Ps 104:26), and God can play with him. Job cannot.
- **"Will traders bargain over him?" (v6).** Will merchants cut him up and sell the pieces at market? "Can you fill his skin with harpoons?" (v7). No weapon gets through.
- **"Lay your hands on him; remember the battle—you will not do it again!" (v8).** Anyone who tries will not try twice.
- **"The hope of a man is false; he is laid low even at the sight of him" (v9).** Just seeing him is enough to make a person collapse.
- **"No one is so fierce that he dares to stir him up. Who then is he who can stand before me?" (v10).** This is the turning point of the chapter. If no one dares to rouse Leviathan, who could stand up to the God who made him? Job had wanted to stand before God and argue his case. A few Hebrew manuscripts and ancient versions read "before him," keeping the focus on Leviathan, but most texts and the ESV read "before me," and that fits the argument: the beast is a lesson about its Maker.
- **"Who has first given to me, that I should repay him? Whatever is under the whole heaven is mine" (v11).** This is the deepest line in the chapter. God owes no one anything. Job had spoken as if his righteous life gave him a claim on God, and he wanted God to answer for not paying what was owed. God says no creature has ever given him something first, so as to put him in debt. Everything already belongs to him (Ps 24:1; 50:10–12). Paul quotes this verse at the end of his great hymn about God's mercy: "Or who has given a gift to him that he might be repaid?" (Rom 11:35). Paul's point is the same: salvation is all of grace, not repayment.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that since Job cannot subdue Leviathan, he must admit he is "utterly unable to stand before the great God." On v11 he says the creature's power is "no more than what God has given him, nor more than he has under his check." JFB glosses "who has first given" as "done Me a favor first: anticipated Me with service," and stresses that God is under no "prior obligation" to any creature. Guzik says the point of vv1–11 is Job's helplessness, and he extends it: Job is as powerless against the spiritual enemy behind his trial as he is against Leviathan, and only God can overcome that enemy.

#### The LORD Challenges Job (vv12–24)
- **"I will not keep silence concerning his limbs" (v12).** God now describes Leviathan in loving detail. He is proud of this creature: "his mighty strength, or his goodly frame."
- **"Who can strip off his outer garment? Who would come near him with a bridle?" (v13).** The outer garment is his armor-like hide. "Bridle" may also be read "double coat of mail," the layered scales.
- **"Who can open the doors of his face? Around his teeth is terror" (v14).** The jaws are doors, and no one wants to open them. A Nile crocodile can have more than sixty teeth.
- **"His back is made of rows of shields, shut up closely as with a seal" (vv15–17).** The scales fit so tightly that "no air can come between them." Crocodile skin has rows of bony plates called scutes, which do act like armor. Henry lists the scales as "close." JFB has no comment on vv16–17.
- **"His sneezings flash forth light, and his eyes are like the eyelids of the dawn" (v18).** Now the description goes beyond any ordinary animal. A crocodile surfacing and blowing out spray in the sunlight could look like a flash. Its eyes are often the first thing seen above the water, glowing reddish, like dawn breaking on the horizon. Horapollo, a late ancient writer on Egyptian hieroglyphs, says the Egyptians used a crocodile's eyes as their sign for dawn, which older commentators often cite here.
- **"Out of his mouth go flaming torches; sparks of fire leap forth" (vv19–21).** Smoke pours from his nostrils "as from a boiling pot," and "his breath kindles coals." This is the language of a fire-breathing dragon. The same imagery is used of God himself in a storm: "smoke went up from his nostrils, and devouring fire from his mouth; glowing coals flamed forth from him" (Ps 18:8). Interpreters read these verses differently. Many take them as vivid poetry for a crocodile's spray and steam in the morning sun. Others see the description deliberately moving from a real animal to the mythic chaos-dragon of the ancient world, the creature everyone feared most. Some, especially in young-earth creationist circles, think it describes a real fire-breathing creature now extinct.
- **"In his neck abides strength, and terror dances before him" (v22).** Terror runs ahead of him like a herald.
- **"The folds of his flesh stick together, firmly cast on him and immovable" (v23).** Even the soft parts are hard.
- **"His heart is hard as a stone, hard as the lower millstone" (v24).** The lower millstone was the harder of the pair, the one that stayed fixed while the upper one turned on it. Leviathan's heart is fearless and pitiless.
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline moves through the face, the scales "close," the breath and sneezes "sparkling," and the flesh "firm." JFB reads v18 as the sneeze making light shine as breath is expelled in the sun, the eyes seeming to "flash fire," and calls the fire of v21 "poetical imagery." Guzik thinks the armor and teeth suggest a mighty crocodile, but he says the fire and smoke go beyond any known crocodile and sound like a dragon. He wonders whether the dragon stories found in so many cultures might preserve a memory of some real creature. He also turns the tightly joined scales into a picture of believers knit closely together.

#### The LORD Challenges Job (vv25–34)
- **"When he raises himself up, the mighty are afraid" (v25).** "The mighty" can also be translated "the gods" (*elim*). If so, even the gods of the nations are afraid of him, which would fit the ancient myths where gods battled the sea monster. Either way, the strongest beings cower. "At the crashing they are beside themselves": they lose their nerve and run.
- **"Though the sword reaches him, it does not avail" (v26).** Sword, spear, dart, javelin, iron, bronze, arrows, sling stones, clubs: God lists every weapon of the ancient world, and none work (vv26–29). "He counts iron as straw, and bronze as rotten wood" (v27). "He laughs at the rattle of javelins" (v29). The war horse laughed at fear (39:22). Leviathan laughs at weapons.
- **"His underparts are like sharp potsherds; he spreads himself like a threshing sledge on the mire" (v30).** A threshing sledge was a heavy board studded underneath with sharp stones or iron, dragged over grain to break it (Isa 41:15). When Leviathan crawls out on the mud bank, he leaves a mark like one.
- **"He makes the deep boil like a pot; he makes the sea like a pot of ointment" (v31).** When he thrashes, the water churns and foams like a pot of spiced ointment being stirred.
- **"Behind him he leaves a shining wake; one would think the deep to be white-haired" (v32).** His wake is so white with foam that the sea looks like an old man's white hair. It is a beautiful, almost playful image.
- **"On earth there is not his like, a creature without fear" (v33).** He was made fearless. This is the opposite of Job, whose whole trial has been full of fear (3:25).
- **"He sees everything that is high; he is king over all the sons of pride" (v34).** God's speech ends on these words. "Sons of pride" (*bene shachats*) appears only here and in 28:8, where the ESV translates it "proud beasts." It refers to the great, majestic wild animals. Leviathan looks down on all of them. He is the king of the proud. And God has just said he is under God's rule. Back in 40:11–12 God dared Job to bring down "everyone who is proud." Job cannot. God has made, and masters, the very king of the proud.
- **What is Leviathan?** The Tyndale notes say most scholars think he was like a crocodile, with powerful jaws, tough skin and a twisting body in the water. But they also note the fire-breathing details and point to the background: ancient Near Eastern stories of a seven-headed sea monster representing chaos. Texts from Ugarit describe a sea serpent called Lotan using almost the same words as Isaiah 27:1: "the fleeing serpent... the twisting serpent." The lexicon lists the crocodile here, a whale in Psalm 104:26, and the mythic dragon in Job 3:8. In the Bible, this monster becomes a picture of chaos and evil, the creature whose heads God crushes (Ps 74:14; Isa 27:1; 51:9). Revelation calls Satan "the great dragon... that ancient serpent" (Rev 12:9). So Christians read Leviathan in several ways: a real crocodile described in grand poetry; a real animal that also stands for the forces of chaos and evil; or chiefly a picture of Satan. These readings are not as far apart as they look. What matters is that whatever Leviathan is, he is God's creature, made by God and held on God's leash.
- **Commentators:** Henry concludes that Leviathan is "a very terrible creature, and man is no match for him," and that the description was given "to convince Job of his own impotency, and of God's omnipotence." He calls Leviathan "a very large, strong, formidable fish, or water-animal." JFB calls Leviathan a "poetical generalization" for sea, serpent and reptile monsters, fitting "especially" the crocodile, though "all the description applies to no one animal." It reads v25 as a picture of "the awe which the Creator inspires when He rises in wrath" and v34's "children of pride" as the proud and fierce beasts, and it says the aim of the whole speech is "to humble the pride of man and to teach implicit submission." Guzik goes furthest in reading Leviathan as a picture of Satan. He thinks "king over all the sons of pride" (v34) is the clearest link, pointing to the unseen adversary of chapters 1–2 whom Job never saw. He notes that God never tells Job about that heavenly scene, and that this is what makes Job's story useful to sufferers who also never get an explanation.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's last word to Job is about a monster no human can tame, "king over all the sons of pride." Job never learned about the accuser in chapters 1–2, but readers do, and the Bible goes on to name the ancient serpent behind human suffering. Isaiah promised that "in that day the LORD with his hard and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent" (Isa 27:1). That promise goes back to the first promise in Scripture, that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head (Gen 3:15). Jesus did what no harpoon could do. Through death he destroyed "the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil" (Heb 2:14), and Revelation shows the dragon thrown down and finally destroyed (Rev 12:9; 20:10). And v11, "Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?", is the reason that victory is pure gift. No one put God in debt. He gave his Son anyway. As Paul says after quoting this very verse, "For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be glory forever" (Rom 11:36).
