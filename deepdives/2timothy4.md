*Setting:* This is the last chapter of the last letter Paul wrote. He is a prisoner in Rome again, most likely around AD 64–67 under Nero, and this time he does not expect release. Unlike his first Roman imprisonment, when he rented his own house and welcomed visitors (Acts 28:30–31), he is now chained like a criminal (2:9), hard to find (1:17), and mostly alone. Timothy is probably still in Ephesus, leading a church troubled by false teachers. Chapter 3 warned that the "last days" would bring people who love themselves and money more than God, and pointed Timothy back to what holds when everything else shifts: "All Scripture is breathed out by God" (3:16). Chapter 4 turns that into a final charge, then lets us hear Paul's own words about his coming death, and ends with a crowded, very human list of friends, deserters, errands and greetings.

#### Preach the Word (vv1–8)
- **"I charge you in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus" (v1).** "Charge" is *diamartyromai*, to testify solemnly, calling witnesses. It is the language of an oath, and Paul has used it twice before in these letters (1 Tim 5:21; 2 Tim 2:14). The witnesses are not a church board. They are God himself and Christ, "who is to judge the living and the dead" (Acts 10:42; 2 Cor 5:10). Timothy's ministry is carried out under the eye of the final Judge.
- **"By his appearing and his kingdom" (v1).** "Appearing" is *epiphaneia*, a word Greeks used for a god showing himself or an emperor's state visit. Paul uses it of both comings of Christ (1:10 of the first; 4:1, 8 of the second). The charge looks forward: the King is coming, and the work is done in light of that day.
- **"Preach the word" (v2).** "Preach" is the herald's verb (*kēryssō*): announce publicly what the king has said. "The word" is the message just described as God-breathed Scripture (3:15–17). The herald's job is not to invent the message but to deliver it faithfully.
- **"Be ready in season and out of season" (v2).** That is, when it is welcome and when it is not, when it is convenient and when it is not. There is no off-season for the word.
- **"Reprove, rebuke, and exhort" (v2).** Three verbs that cover the whole range: convince someone of what is wrong, correct it firmly, and urge and encourage them onward. They echo 3:16, where Scripture is profitable "for reproof, for correction." Then the brakes: **"with complete patience and teaching."** Correction without patience turns harsh, and rebuke without teaching leaves people knowing they are wrong but not what is right.
- **"Having itching ears" (v3).** The verb *knēthō* means to scratch or tickle, and in the passive to itch. The picture is of hearers who want their ears tickled, so they go shopping for **teachers "to suit their own passions,"** and "accumulate" them, piling them up, one after another. The problem Paul names is not a shortage of teachers but a surplus of the wrong kind, chosen by the audience to say what it already wants.
- **"Wander off into myths" (v4).** "Myths" (*mythoi*) has come up before in these letters (1 Tim 1:4; 4:7; Titus 1:14). The Ephesian false teaching seems to have mixed Jewish legends, genealogies and speculation. Note the sad sequence: first they "will not endure" sound teaching, then they "turn away" from the truth, then they "wander off." Leaving truth never leaves a vacuum.
- **"But as for you" (v5).** The same turn as 3:10 and 3:14. Whatever others do, Timothy has his own orders, four of them: **"be sober-minded"** (clear-headed and steady, not intoxicated by fashion), **"endure suffering"** (a word Paul has pressed all through the letter, 1:8; 2:3, 9), **"do the work of an evangelist"** (keep telling the good news to those outside, even while managing a troubled church), and **"fulfill your ministry,"** carry it all the way through. Paul said the same to Archippus (Col 4:17).
- **"For I am already being poured out as a drink offering" (v6).** The "for" ties Timothy's charge to Paul's death: the older man is leaving, so the younger must take up the work. The verb *spendomai* means to be poured out as a libation. In Israel's worship a drink offering of wine was poured out alongside the daily burnt offerings (Num 28:7; Exod 29:40–41). Paul used the same picture years earlier when he faced possible death in his first imprisonment (Phil 2:17). Then it was "even if." Now it is "already." He sees his coming death not as a defeat but as an act of worship, the last of his life poured out for God.
- **"The time of my departure has come" (v6).** "Departure" is *analysis*, a loosing, used of a ship slipping its moorings or a soldier striking his tent. Paul speaks of death as setting sail. He used the related verb in Philippians 1:23: "My desire is to depart and be with Christ."
- **"I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith" (v7).** Three perfect-tense verbs, each describing something completed with lasting result. The "fight" (*agōn*) is the word for any athletic contest, not only a battle, so the first two lines may be one sports picture: the contest and the race. Paul told the Ephesian elders years before that his one aim was "that I may finish my course" (Acts 20:24). "Kept the faith" can mean he stayed faithful, or that he guarded the body of truth entrusted to him, as he told Timothy to do (1:14; 1 Tim 6:20). Both are true here.
- **"The crown of righteousness" (v8).** "Crown" is *stephanos*, the wreath given to a winner at the games, not a royal diadem. It may mean a crown that *is* righteousness (the final verdict of "righteous" spoken over him), or a crown *for* a righteous life. Either way it comes from **"the Lord, the righteous judge,"** a quiet contrast with the Roman court about to condemn him. The verdict that counts has not yet been given.
- **"Not only to me but also to all who have loved his appearing" (v8).** Paul refuses to keep the prize to himself. The mark of those who receive it is not fame or ministry size but love for Christ's return. Hebrews 9:28 says the same: he will appear "to save those who are eagerly waiting for him."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the word "the pure plain word of God," and on "in season and out of season" urges Timothy to "let slip no opportunity." He reads Paul calling death his "departure" as a release to another world, not an ending. JFB glosses "charge" as "adjure," which the lexicon supports, and on itching ears passes on the old preacher Robert South's line that an itch in the ear is as bad as one anywhere else in the body, "and perhaps worse." JFB also notes that *analysis* literally means "loosing anchor," and that "it is a righteous retribution" when those who turn from truth are turned over to fables. Guzik makes the same oath point about *diamartyromai*, and stresses that preaching means proclaiming God's word, not the preacher's opinions. He also links the libation to the Roman custom of pouring out wine at the end of a meal, which Paul's Gentile readers would have known, though the Old Testament drink offering is the closer background. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both treat the crown as the athlete's wreath.

#### Personal Instructions (vv9–18)
- **"Do your best to come to me soon" (v9).** The great apostle is lonely, and he says so. He repeats the request in v21. He wanted Timothy near him at the end (1:4).
- **"Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me" (v10).** Demas was once a "fellow worker" (Philem 24) and sent greetings to Colossae (Col 4:14). Now he has gone to Thessalonica. "Deserted" (*enkataleipō*) means to leave someone behind in trouble, the same verb in "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34). "This present world" is literally "the now age." Paul has just spoken of those who love Christ's *appearing* (v8). Demas loved the *now*. The contrast is deliberate: two loves, two directions.
- **"Crescens has gone to Galatia, Titus to Dalmatia" (v10).** Unlike Demas, these two seem to have gone on assignment. Crescens appears only here. A few early manuscripts read "Gaul" instead of "Galatia," and Greek writers sometimes called Gaul "Galatia" anyway, so he may have gone to central Turkey or to France. Titus, whom Paul had left on Crete (Titus 1:5), is now in Dalmatia on the Adriatic coast (roughly modern Croatia), not far from Nicopolis, where Paul had asked Titus to meet him (Titus 3:12).
- **"Luke alone is with me" (v11).** The doctor and historian (Col 4:14) stayed to the end. The "we" passages of Acts show him with Paul on the voyage to Rome (Acts 27–28), and now he is the only companion left.
- **"Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry" (v11).** This is one of the most moving lines in the letter. Mark had left Paul and Barnabas mid-journey (Acts 13:13), and Paul refused to take him again, splitting with Barnabas over it (Acts 15:37–40). Now Paul asks for him by name. "Useful" (*euchrēstos*) is the same word Paul used of Onesimus (Philem 11). The failure was not final.
- **"Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus" (v12).** Paul's trusted courier (Acts 20:4; Eph 6:21; Col 4:7). If Timothy is in Ephesus, Tychicus probably carried this letter and could take over Timothy's work while Timothy traveled.
- **"Bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas" (v13).** The *phailonēs* was a heavy, circular outer cloak, a cape with a hole for the head. Winter is coming (v21), and stone prisons were cold. Paul may have been arrested suddenly at Troas and had no chance to collect his things. Carpus appears only here, perhaps the man who had hosted him.
- **"Also the books, and above all the parchments" (v13).** "Books" are papyrus scrolls. "Parchments" (*membranai*, a Latin loanword) were sheets of prepared animal skin, more durable and more expensive, sometimes bound as notebooks. What was on them is not said. Most think they included copies of the Old Testament Scriptures, perhaps also Paul's own notes or copies of his letters. A dying man still wanted to read.
- **"Alexander the coppersmith did me great harm" (v14).** "Coppersmith" means a metalworker generally. He may be the Alexander whom Paul had put out of the church for blasphemy (1 Tim 1:20), though Alexander was a common name. "Did me great harm" literally means "showed me many evils," and the Tyndale notes suggest he may have had a hand in Paul's arrest, perhaps as an informer. **"The Lord will repay him according to his deeds"** echoes Psalm 62:12. Paul hands the matter to God rather than taking revenge (Rom 12:19).
- **A small textual note (v14).** The later manuscripts behind the King James read a wish, "the Lord reward him," which can sound like a curse. The older manuscripts, which the ESV follows, read a statement about the future: "the Lord *will* repay." The difference is one letter in Greek, and the older reading fits Paul's spirit in v16.
- **"Beware of him yourself, for he strongly opposed our message" (v15).** Timothy may cross paths with Alexander, so Paul gives a plain warning. Forgiving someone does not mean trusting them blindly.
- **"At my first defense no one came to stand by me" (v16).** "Defense" is *apologia*, a legal answer in court. Roman trials often began with a preliminary hearing. Most scholars think Paul means that first hearing in his current trial. No friend or patron appeared to speak for him. **"May it not be charged against them!"** This is the prayer of Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:34) and of Stephen as he died (Acts 7:60), a death Paul once stood by and approved (Acts 8:1). Now he prays as Stephen prayed.
- **"But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me" (v17).** When every human supporter vanished, the Lord was present. He had stood by Paul before, in the Jerusalem barracks (Acts 23:11) and on the sinking ship (Acts 27:23). "Strengthened" (*endynamoō*) is the word Paul used of Christ's strength in his weakness (1 Tim 1:12; Phil 4:13).
- **"So that through me the message might be fully proclaimed and all the Gentiles might hear it" (v17).** The courtroom became a pulpit. Paul's defense was a gospel witness before Roman officials, at the heart of the empire. Jesus had promised that his followers would be brought before governors and kings "to bear witness before them and the Gentiles" (Matt 10:18; compare Acts 9:15).
- **"So I was rescued from the lion's mouth" (v17).** Probably a figure for deadly danger, as in Psalm 22:21, "Save me from the mouth of the lion!", the psalm that begins with the cry of the forsaken. Some think of literal lions in the arena, though a Roman citizen was not normally sentenced to the beasts. Others think of Nero, or of Satan, the "roaring lion" (1 Pet 5:8). The psalm background seems most likely: Paul, deserted like the psalmist, was rescued like him.
- **"The Lord will rescue me from every evil deed and bring me safely into his heavenly kingdom" (v18).** Paul does not expect rescue from death. He has just said his departure has come (v6). He expects rescue *through* death, kept from every evil that could make him deny Christ, and brought safely home. The word "rescue" echoes the Lord's Prayer, "deliver us from evil." **"To him be the glory forever and ever. Amen."** In the Greek "him" refers back to "the Lord," which here is Christ. Paul gives Jesus the doxology that belongs to God.
- **Commentators:** On Demas, Henry says love of this world often draws people from Christ, and JFB thinks his motive "seems to have been love of worldly ease, safety, and comforts at home." Guzik notes "this present world" is literally "the now age," which the Greek supports. On Mark, JFB says Paul "wipes out the past censure by high praise of Mark," and Henry draws the lesson of a forgiving spirit toward those who have failed. On the books, Henry: "As long as we live, we must be still learning." JFB suggests the parchments might have held Paul's own letters, and Guzik takes them as Old Testament Scriptures, both guesses. JFB also says "personal revenge certainly did not influence the apostle" on Alexander. On v16 Henry calls the desertion a sin of weakness, still blameworthy, which is why Paul could pray for them while handing Alexander, who opposed the message itself, to God. JFB takes the lion as "Satan, the roaring, devouring lion," Henry allows either Nero or a proverb for great danger, and Guzik calls Paul's forgiving prayer a mark of real grace.

#### Final Greetings (vv19–22)
- **"Greet Prisca and Aquila" (v19).** The tentmaking couple who worked with Paul in Corinth, taught Apollos in Ephesus and risked their lives for Paul (Acts 18:2–3, 26; Rom 16:3–4). They moved between Rome and Ephesus over the years. Paul usually names Prisca (Priscilla) first, perhaps because she was the more prominent in the church's work.
- **"The household of Onesiphorus" (v19).** Onesiphorus had searched Rome to find Paul and was "not ashamed of my chains" (1:16–18). Paul greets his household, not him, and in 1:18 prays that he will find mercy "on that Day." Many think he had died. Some use 1:18 to support praying for the dead, but the text does not say he was dead, and most Protestant readers see a prayer for his family and a hope for him, not a doctrine.
- **"Erastus remained at Corinth" (v20).** An Erastus was city treasurer of Corinth (Rom 16:23), and a first-century paving stone found in Corinth names an Erastus who paid for it as a city official. Whether he is the same man as this Erastus, or as Paul's helper in Acts 19:22, is likely but not certain.
- **"I left Trophimus, who was ill, at Miletus" (v20).** Trophimus, an Ephesian, was the Gentile whose presence with Paul in Jerusalem sparked the riot that led to Paul's arrest years before (Acts 21:29). The apostle through whom God did "extraordinary miracles" (Acts 19:11) left a friend sick. Healing was God's gift to give, not a power Paul could use at will. Sickness here is not blamed on weak faith.
- **"Do your best to come before winter" (v21).** Sea travel across the Mediterranean shut down roughly from November to March (Acts 27:9–12). If Timothy missed the autumn sailings, he might not reach Rome until spring, and Paul might be dead by then. Whether Timothy made it, Scripture does not say. Hebrews 13:23 mentions that Timothy had been released from prison, which some think means he did come and was arrested too.
- **"Eubulus… Pudens and Linus and Claudia and all the brothers" (v21).** Members of the Roman church. Eubulus and Claudia appear only here. Pudens was a name used in upper-class families. Early church writers (Irenaeus, about AD 180, and later Eusebius) say Linus became the first leader of the Roman church after the apostles. A legend makes Pudens and Claudia husband and wife, even linking Claudia to a British princess, but that is guesswork. Paul says "all deserted me" (v16), yet here are friends sending greetings. Perhaps they were afraid to stand with him in court, or arrived only later.
- **"The Lord be with your spirit. Grace be with you" (v22).** The first blessing is singular, to Timothy himself. The second "you" is plural in Greek: the whole church in Ephesus was meant to hear this letter. These are probably the last recorded words of the apostle Paul, and they are a prayer for others. Christian tradition holds that he was beheaded on the road outside Rome, as a citizen would be, not long after.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks Paul's leaving Trophimus sick shows the apostles did not use their healing gift at will, even for their friends. Guzik makes the same point and mentions a Spurgeon sermon on the verse. JFB thinks Prisca and Aquila were "most likely to be at Ephesus," which agrees with the Tyndale notes. JFB repeats the old tradition that Pudens and Claudia "afterwards" married, and adds that Linus is named third, so was not yet a bishop. Those are traditions, not the text. JFB also notes that "Grace be with you" is plural in the oldest manuscripts. Henry reads "before winter" as both longing to see Timothy and the danger of a winter journey.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul's last chapter follows his Lord's path more closely than he may have meant it to. He is deserted by friends, as Jesus was (Mark 14:50). He prays that the deserters be forgiven, as Jesus did. He hears the words of Psalm 22, the psalm of the forsaken one, and finds that the Lord "stood by me" even when no one else did. He goes to death calling it an offering poured out and a ship setting sail. All of this rests on what Christ has already done: the one who "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light" (1:10) is the "righteous judge" who will crown him, and the crown is not only for apostles but for everyone who has "loved his appearing."
