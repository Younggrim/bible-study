*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Leviticus has dealt with holy offerings, holy priests and a holy people. Chapter 23 turns to holy *time*. It sets out Israel's sacred calendar: the weekly Sabbath and seven yearly occasions, from Passover in the spring to the Feast of Booths in the autumn. Some of these were given before (Exod 12; 23:14–17; 34:18–26), and Numbers 28–29 later lists the sacrifices for each in detail. Here they are gathered into one year. Most assume a settled life in the land, built around the barley, wheat and fruit harvests. The calendar teaches Israel to remember what God has done, to thank him for what he gives, and to look ahead to what he will do.

#### Feasts of the LORD (vv1–2)
- **"The appointed feasts of the LORD" (v2).** The word is *moed*, "appointed time" or "meeting," the same word as in "tent of *meeting*." It is not mainly about parties. These are set times when God meets with his people. Some of them, like the Day of Atonement, are anything but festive.
- **"Holy convocations" (v2).** A *miqra* is a sacred assembly, a day "called out" for worship, when ordinary work stopped. **"They are my appointed feasts."** The calendar belongs to God. Israel does not invent these days. It keeps them.

#### The Sabbath (v3)
- **"On the seventh day is a Sabbath of solemn rest" (v3).** The Sabbath comes first, and it comes every week. The other feasts happen once a year, but the rhythm of six days and one is the base of the whole calendar (Gen 2:2–3; Exod 20:8–11). **"In all your dwelling places"** means it was kept at home, not only at the sanctuary. Jesus later said that "the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath," and that he is its Lord (Mark 2:27–28).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this chapter "the institution of holy times," following laws about holy persons, things and places. JFB notes the Sabbath was kept by families "in their dwellings," and later also in synagogues. Guzik observes that the Sabbath was not strictly one of the annual feasts but heads the list as the pattern for all holy time.

#### The Passover (vv4–8)
- **"In the first month, on the fourteenth day… at twilight" (v5).** The first month was called Abib ("fresh ears of grain"), later Nisan, falling in March–April. The Passover lamb was killed at twilight and eaten that night with bitter herbs and unleavened bread, recalling the night the LORD passed over the houses marked with blood (Exod 12:1–14).
- **"The Feast of Unleavened Bread… seven days" (vv6–8).** Passover ran straight into a week without leaven (yeast). Israel had left Egypt in such a hurry that the dough had no time to rise (Exod 12:34, 39). Leaven also became a picture of sin spreading quietly through a whole batch (1 Cor 5:6–8). The first and last days were holy convocations.
- **The fulfilment.** Jesus was crucified at Passover. He ate the Passover meal with his disciples and gave it new meaning: "This is my body… this is my blood of the covenant" (Matt 26:26–28). Paul says plainly, "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed. Let us therefore celebrate the festival, not with the old leaven… but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (1 Cor 5:7–8). The Gospels differ in how they time the Last Supper and the crucifixion against the Passover meal, and scholars offer several ways to fit them together, but all four place Jesus' death at Passover.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls these "mercies never to be forgotten." JFB says the Passover commemorated Israel's redemption and "typified a greater redemption." Guzik links Unleavened Bread to Jesus' sinless body in the tomb, whose burial Paul counts as part of the gospel (1 Cor 15:3–4).

#### The Feast of Firstfruits (vv9–14)
- **"When you come into the land" (v10).** This feast assumed fields Israel did not yet own. It is a promise that they will have a harvest to bring.
- **"The sheaf of the firstfruits" (vv10–11).** Barley ripened first, in early spring. Before anyone ate from the new crop, a sheaf was brought and **waved** before the LORD, lifted and moved as an offering presented to God. The first part of the harvest stood for the whole: it was all God's gift.
- **"On the day after the Sabbath" (v11).** Which Sabbath? In Jesus' day the Pharisees took it as the first day of Unleavened Bread, so the sheaf was waved on the 16th of the month. The Sadducees took it as the weekly Sabbath during the feast, so the sheaf was waved on a Sunday. The text can be read either way.
- **"Neither bread nor grain… until… you have brought the offering" (v14).** God was to be thanked before the people fed themselves.
- **The fulfilment.** Jesus rose from the dead "on the first day of the week" (Mark 16:2) during Passover week. Paul writes, "Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Cor 15:20). Like the sheaf, his resurrection is the first part of a harvest that guarantees the rest.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "our Lord Jesus rose from the dead on the very day that the first-fruits were offered." JFB says the sheaf "sanctified the whole harvest" and "pre-intimated the resurrection of Christ." Guzik makes the same connection to 1 Corinthians 15:20 and Colossians 1:18. The Tyndale notes add that the first part of the harvest stood for the whole harvest, and even the land itself, as belonging to God.

#### The Feast of Weeks (vv15–22)
- **"You shall count seven full weeks" (vv15–16).** Seven weeks after the sheaf, on the fiftieth day, came the Feast of Weeks (Exod 34:22), at the end of the wheat harvest in late spring. Greek-speaking Jews called it **Pentecost**, from the Greek word for "fiftieth." Jewish tradition later also linked it with the giving of the law at Sinai, about seven weeks after the exodus (Exod 19:1).
- **"Two loaves… baked with leaven" (v17).** This is striking. Leaven was banned from almost every offering burned on the altar (2:11), and the Passover week was strictly unleavened. These loaves were not burned. They were waved and given to the priests (v20). They were everyday bread, an offering of the ordinary food God provided.
- **The offerings (vv18–20).** Seven lambs, a bull and two rams for burnt offerings, a goat for a sin offering, and two lambs for peace offerings. Thanksgiving still required atonement.
- **The fulfilment.** On "the day of Pentecost," fifty days after Jesus' resurrection, the Holy Spirit was poured out on the disciples and about three thousand people were added to the church (Acts 2:1–41). It was the first great harvest of the gospel. Many readers see the two leavened loaves as a picture of the church, Jew and Gentile, not yet sinless but accepted by God. That is a reading, not something the text states.
- **"You shall not reap your field right up to its edge" (v22).** The gleaning law from 19:9–10 is repeated right in the middle of the calendar, at harvest time. Worship and care for the poor belong together.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes the two loaves were leavened, unlike the Passover bread, and that only the first day was kept as a Sabbath. On v22 it says the law was repeated so the people would "unite piety to God with charity to the poor." Guzik connects Pentecost to the birth of the church in Acts 2 and the inclusion of the Gentiles, though in one place he calls the loaves unleavened, when the text says they were baked with leaven.

#### The Feast of Trumpets (vv23–25)
- **"In the seventh month, on the first day" (v24).** The seventh month (Tishri, September–October) was the high point of the religious year, with three occasions in it. Every new moon was marked with trumpets (Num 10:10), but this one was special: a day of rest **"proclaimed with blast of trumpets."** The trumpet here was probably the *shofar*, a ram's horn.
- **"A memorial" (v24).** The text does not say what the trumpet calls to mind. It may call Israel to prepare for the Day of Atonement ten days later, or call on God to remember his people. After the exile, this day came to be kept as the Jewish New Year, *Rosh Hashanah*, because the civil year began in the autumn.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls it "the first day of the ancient civil year." Guzik reads the trumpets as pointing to the trumpet of God at Christ's return (1 Thess 4:16) and the future gathering of Israel. Many interpreters see that link, though the chapter itself does not make it.

#### The Day of Atonement (vv26–32)
- **"The Day of Atonement" (v27).** Chapter 16 described what the high priest did on this day. This section tells the people what *they* must do. **"You shall afflict yourselves"** is generally understood as fasting (Ps 35:13; Isa 58:3), with humble self-examination. It was the only commanded fast in the law.
- **"Whoever is not afflicted… whoever does any work" (vv29–30).** These are the strongest warnings in the chapter: anyone who refuses will be cut off, and God himself will **"destroy"** whoever works. The day was not optional. Everyone's sins were being dealt with, and everyone had to be part of it.
- **"From evening to evening" (v32).** The day ran from sunset on the ninth to sunset on the tenth, the Bible's way of counting days (Gen 1:5).
- **The fulfilment.** Hebrews explains that Christ entered "once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption" (Heb 9:12). What the yearly fast could only repeat, he finished.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls it "an unusual festival, at which the sins of the whole year were expiated," and says the severest penalty attached to breaking it. Guzik sees it pointing to Jesus' atonement, and he also connects it with a future day when Israel mourns and turns to the Messiah (Zech 12:10), a reading that depends on his view of end-time events.

#### The Feast of Booths (vv33–44)
- **"On the fifteenth day of this seventh month and for seven days" (v34).** Five days after the Day of Atonement came the most joyful feast of the year. It was also called the Feast of Ingathering (Exod 23:16), since it came after the grapes, olives and other fruit were brought in. Many Bibles call it "Tabernacles." The Hebrew name is *Sukkot*, "booths."
- **"The eighth day… a solemn assembly" (vv36, 39).** The feast closed with an extra day of assembly. John 7:37 likely refers to it: "On the last day of the feast, the great day, Jesus stood up and cried out, 'If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink.'"
- **"Besides the LORD's Sabbaths" (vv37–38).** The feast offerings were extra. They did not replace the regular Sabbath offerings or anyone's own vows and freewill gifts.
- **"The fruit of splendid trees, branches of palm trees… willows of the brook" (v40).** Later Jewish practice used a citron fruit and a bundle of palm, myrtle and willow branches, waved in joy. Nehemiah's generation went out and cut branches to build booths (Neh 8:14–17). **"You shall rejoice before the LORD your God seven days."** God commanded his people to celebrate.
- **"You shall dwell in booths for seven days… that your generations may know" (vv42–43).** Families lived in rough shelters of branches for a week, on rooftops and in courtyards, so that each generation would remember that Israel once lived in tents in the wilderness, and that God provided for them there. Settled, prosperous people were reminded every year that they had once been homeless travelers, and that everything they had was a gift.
- **The fulfilment.** John writes that the Word "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). The verb literally means "tabernacled" or "pitched his tent." Zechariah foresees all nations going up to keep the Feast of Booths (Zech 14:16), and the Bible ends with the promise, "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3). The feasts that began with Passover end by looking ahead to God dwelling with his people forever.
- **"Thus Moses declared to the people of Israel the appointed feasts of the LORD" (v44).** The chapter closes as it opened (v2). These are the LORD's times, given to all his people.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the feast was "instituted in grateful commemoration of the Israelites having securely dwelt in booths," and calls it "a season of great rejoicing." It names the later Jewish tradition of the four plants as "the citron, the palm, the myrtle, and the willow." Guzik sees the feast pointing to God's people at rest in the age to come (Zech 14:16–19). He arranges the calendar in two groups: the four spring feasts fulfilled at Christ's first coming, and the three autumn feasts awaiting his return, with the long summer harvest between them as a picture of the church age. That scheme is popular, though it goes beyond what this chapter says.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul calls the festivals "a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ" (Col 2:16–17). The shadow fits him closely. He died at Passover as the Lamb. He lay in the tomb during Unleavened Bread. He rose on the day of the firstfruits, the first of the great harvest. Fifty days later the Spirit fell at Pentecost, and the harvest began. Christians differ on whether the autumn feasts map onto future events in the same way, but the New Testament clearly ties them to Christ too: the trumpet that will sound at his coming, the atonement he made once for all, and the God who "tabernacled" among us and will one day dwell with his people forever. Israel's year was built to remember, to give thanks and to hope, and all three lead to Jesus.
