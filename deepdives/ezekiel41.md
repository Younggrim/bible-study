*Setting:* The vision continues, still dated to the spring of 573 BC (40:1). Ezekiel's heavenly guide has led him through the outer and inner courts of the new temple, measuring every wall, gate, step and room, and has brought him to the porch of the temple building itself (40:48–49). Chapter 41 goes inside. It measures the nave, the inner room, the side rooms around the building, a large building behind it, and the carved decoration that covers every wall. The plan closely follows Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 6–7): the same three rooms in a line, the same inner dimensions, the same side chambers in three stories, the same cherubim and palm trees. That continuity matters. The God who walked out of the old temple (chs 10–11) is preparing to come back to a house built on the same pattern, only purer. As with chapter 40, some of the building terms are rare and their exact meaning debated, so the notes below follow the main features rather than every number.

#### The Inner Temple (vv1–26)
- **"Then he brought me to the nave" (v1).** The temple building had three parts in a line from east to west: the vestibule or porch (40:48–49), the nave or main hall (called "the Holy Place" in the tabernacle), and the inner room, the Most Holy Place. This was the plan of the tabernacle (Exod 26:33) and of Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 6:16–17). Each step inward was holier.
- **"The breadth of the entrance was ten cubits… the length of the nave, forty cubits, and its breadth, twenty cubits" (v2).** The nave was about 70 by 35 feet, exactly the size of the main hall in Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 6:2, 17).
- **Narrowing doorways (vv1–3).** The entrance to the porch was fourteen cubits wide (40:48), the entrance to the nave ten cubits (41:2), and the entrance to the inner room only six cubits (41:3). The Tyndale notes point out that the way grows narrower as it nears God's presence. Access to the Holy One is real, but it is not casual.
- **"Then he went into the inner room" (v3).** Notice who goes in. Ezekiel was a priest, so he could enter the nave. Only the high priest could enter the Most Holy Place, and only once a year (Lev 16:2, 34; Heb 9:7). So the guide goes in alone and measures it while Ezekiel waits outside.
- **"Twenty cubits, and its breadth, twenty cubits… 'This is the Most Holy Place'" (v4).** About 35 feet square, the same as in Solomon's temple, where it was a perfect cube (1 Kgs 6:20). This is the one time in the tour that the guide speaks to name a room. The Most Holy Place in Solomon's temple held the ark of the covenant with the cherubim over it. Ezekiel mentions no ark at all. It had probably been lost when Babylon burned the temple. Jeremiah said a day would come when no one would miss the ark or make another, because Jerusalem itself would be called "the throne of the LORD" (Jer 3:16–17). In Ezekiel's vision, God's own glory will fill the house (43:5).
- **"The wall of the temple, six cubits thick" (v5).** About 10 feet. Solomon's temple walls are not measured in Kings, but walls this thick suggest strength and permanence.
- **"The side chambers were in three stories, one over another, thirty in each story" (v6).** Solomon's temple also had storerooms in three stories around its sides and back (1 Kgs 6:5–10). They probably held temple equipment, priestly clothing, and the tithes and gifts brought by the people (Neh 13:4–9).
- **"So that they should not be supported by the wall of the temple" (v6).** The temple wall stepped inward at each story, so the beams of the side rooms could rest on ledges without being cut into the temple wall itself. Solomon's builders did the same (1 Kgs 6:6). The holy building was not to be pierced for the sake of the rooms around it.
- **"It became broader as it wound upward" (v7).** Because the temple wall grew thinner as it rose, the rooms grew wider on each higher floor. "Wound upward" may refer to a staircase, perhaps a winding one, as in Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 6:8).
- **"A raised platform all around" (v8).** The temple stood on a platform a full reed high, about 10 feet, the top of the stairway of ten steps (40:49). It was the highest point in the whole complex.
- **"The free space… a breadth of twenty cubits all around" (vv9–11).** Between the side rooms and the priests' chambers in the inner court lay an open strip. Even the buildings near the temple were kept at a distance.
- **"The building that was facing the separate yard on the west side was seventy cubits broad… and its length ninety cubits" (v12).** Behind the temple, filling the western end, stood a large building, about 120 by 155 feet. Its use is not given. It may have been for storage, or for disposing of what was left from the sacrifices. Its main effect was to close off the back of the temple, so that no one could approach the Most Holy Place from behind. The "separate yard" (*gizrah*, "cut-off place") was a zone kept apart. It may also be a deliberate contrast with Solomon's temple, where the royal palace and even royal tombs had crowded right up to the house of God (43:7–8).
- **"A hundred cubits long… a hundred cubits" (vv13–15).** The temple measures a hundred cubits each way, about 170 feet, and so does the yard with the western building. The whole temple area forms a set of squares. Ezekiel's temple is a world of perfect proportions.
- **"Paneled with wood all around, from the floor up to the windows" (v16).** Solomon lined his temple with cedar and cypress so that "no stone was seen" (1 Kgs 6:15–18). Ezekiel mentions wood paneling but no gold. Solomon overlaid everything with gold (1 Kgs 6:21–22). Ezekiel says nothing about it. The new temple's glory is not its gold but the presence of God.
- **"Cherubim and palm trees, a palm tree between cherub and cherub" (v18).** The same decoration covered Solomon's temple walls and doors (1 Kgs 6:29, 32, 35). Cherubim were the guardian creatures of God's presence: they guarded the way to the tree of life (Gen 3:24), spread their wings over the ark (Exod 25:18–22), and carried the throne in Ezekiel's own visions (1:5–14; 10:1–22). Palm trees suggest life and fruitfulness. Together they make the temple a picture of the garden of Eden restored, guarded by cherubim and full of trees.
- **"Every cherub had two faces: a human face… and the face of a young lion" (vv18–19).** The living cherubim of Ezekiel's visions had four faces: human, lion, ox and eagle (1:10; 10:14). On a flat carved wall, only two can be shown, one turned to the palm tree on each side. The human face and the lion's face are the highest of the faces: the man, head of creation, and the lion, king of the beasts.
- **"Something resembling an altar of wood… 'This is the table that is before the LORD'" (vv21–22).** This is the only piece of furniture described inside the temple, and it is not clear which piece it is. It looks like an altar, but the guide calls it a table. In the tabernacle, two wooden pieces stood in the Holy Place: the table for the bread of the Presence (Exod 25:23–30) and the golden altar of incense (Exod 30:1–10). Some take this as the table, where twelve loaves were set out every Sabbath "before the LORD." Others think it is the incense altar, which stood "before the LORD" in front of the veil (Lev 16:12). It may combine the two. Its size, about 5 feet high and 3½ feet square, is larger than either piece in the tabernacle. There is no mention of the golden lampstand, and no gold at all.
- **"The nave and the Holy Place had each a double door" (v23).** Each doorway had two doors, and each door had two folding leaves, like Solomon's doors of cypress (1 Kgs 6:31–35). Notice what is missing: Ezekiel mentions no veil. In the tabernacle and in Herod's temple, a curtain separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place. At Jesus' death, that curtain was torn in two from top to bottom (Matt 27:51).
- **"A canopy of wood in front of the vestibule" (v25).** The word is rare. It may mean a wooden porch roof or a threshold of thick planks. **"Narrow windows and palm trees on either side" (v26)** finish the description as it began at the gates (40:16): windows and palm trees.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the temple's description creates "much difficulty to the critical expositors" because building terms differ from age to age. He notes that "the most holy place was an exact square," explains how "the side-chambers that were on the height of the house… were six cubits, whereas the lowest were but four," and of v22 remarks, "Here, as before, we find the altar turned into a table." He reads that as a sign that "the incense to be offered in the gospel-temple shall be purely spiritual." JFB says the dimensions match Solomon's because, "being fixed originally by God, they are regarded as finally determined." He takes the wooden piece as "the altar of incense," called a table "as being that at which the Lord will take delight." JFB has no notes on the cherubim or the doors. Guzik stresses that the guide entered the Most Holy Place alone, since Ezekiel as an ordinary priest could not. He follows Feinberg in seeing the wooden piece as an altar-like table, cites Morgan's suggestion that the two faces stand for created life and its royal rule, and treats John Trapp's reading of the doors as baptism and the Lord's Supper as speculation without support in the text.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel stood outside the Most Holy Place while his guide went in alone. That was the rule of every earthly temple: the way into God's presence was narrow, and ordinary worshipers could not enter it. Hebrews says the Holy Spirit was teaching by this "that the way into the holy places is not yet opened as long as the first section is still standing" (Heb 9:8). Then Christ entered "once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12), and the curtain was torn. Now believers "have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Heb 10:19–20). The table "before the LORD," with its bread set out in God's presence, finds its fulfilment in the one who said, "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35).
