*Setting:* Babylonia, about 591–588 BC. In 597 BC Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon had taken Jerusalem, deported the young King Jehoiachin with his court and his best craftsmen (Ezekiel among them), and set Jehoiachin's uncle Mattaniah on the throne under the new name Zedekiah (2 Kings 24:10–17). Zedekiah swore loyalty to Babylon in the LORD's name (2 Chr 36:13). But Egypt kept tempting Judah with promises of help, and around 589 BC, with a new and ambitious pharaoh, Hophra, on the Egyptian throne, Zedekiah broke his oath and rebelled (2 Kings 24:20). This chapter may come shortly before or shortly after that decision. It tells the story as a fable of two eagles and a vine, explains it plainly, and then adds a surprising promise about a new shoot from David's line.

#### Parable of Two Eagles and a Vine (vv1–24)
- **"Propound a riddle, and speak a parable" (v2).** A riddle (*ḥidah*) is a puzzle, a saying with a hidden meaning, like Samson's riddle (Judg 14:12) or the "hard questions" the Queen of Sheba put to Solomon (1 Kings 10:1). A parable (*mashal*) is a comparison or story that carries a lesson. The fable form uses birds and plants to tell a story about kings. It makes the hearers listen before they realize the story is about them, the way Nathan's story caught David (2 Sam 12:1–7).
- **"A great eagle with great wings and long pinions, rich in plumage of many colors" (v3).** The Hebrew *nesher* can mean eagle or griffon vulture, the great bird of prey. Mighty kings were often pictured this way (Jer 48:40; 49:22; Dan 7:4). Babylon's royal art used winged figures as symbols of power. The rich, many-colored plumage suggests the many peoples and languages of the empire.
- **"Came to Lebanon and took the top of the cedar" (v3).** Lebanon's cedars were the noblest trees of the ancient world, used for Solomon's temple and palace. His palace was even called "the House of the Forest of Lebanon" (1 Kings 7:2). Here Lebanon stands for Jerusalem and the house of David, and the cedar's top is the king. **"The topmost of its young twigs" (v4)** is Jehoiachin, who was eighteen when he was taken (2 Kings 24:8). He was carried **"to a land of trade… a city of merchants"**, Babylon, famous for its commerce (16:29).
- **"He took of the seed of the land and planted it in fertile soil" (v5).** This is Zedekiah, a native prince of David's line, not a foreign governor. Nebuchadnezzar treated Judah fairly well. He left it a king, a temple and a land beside **"abundant waters."** **"He set it like a willow twig"**, a plant that grows easily near water.
- **"It… became a low spreading vine, and its branches turned toward him" (v6).** The vine was meant to stay low, a humble vassal kingdom that would not rise up again, but it could still flourish. Its branches turned toward the eagle in loyalty. That was the arrangement: modest, safe and alive.
- **"There was another great eagle… and behold, this vine bent its roots toward him" (v7).** The second eagle is Egypt, impressive but less glorious than the first (no "long pinions" or "many colors"). Pharaoh Psammetichus II toured the region in 591 BC, and Hophra (Apries), who took the throne in 589 BC, encouraged revolt across the area. Jeremiah calls him by name (Jer 44:30). The vine had everything it needed where it was planted, **"on good soil by abundant waters"** (v8), yet it stretched its roots toward someone else for water. The folly is the point.
- **"Will it thrive? Will he not pull up its roots…?" (v9).** The questions answer themselves. The first eagle will rip out the disloyal vine, and **"it will not take a strong arm or many people."** Judah's rebellion was hopeless from the start.
- **"Will it not utterly wither when the east wind strikes it?" (v10).** In Palestine, the east wind blows off the desert, hot and dry, and can wither crops in a day (Gen 41:6; Hos 13:15). Babylon lay to the east. But behind Babylon stands the LORD, who sends the east wind (Jonah 4:8).
- **"Do you not know what these things mean?" (v12).** Ezekiel now explains the riddle in plain prose. The **"rebellious house"** cannot complain that it did not understand. **"The king of Babylon came to Jerusalem, and took her king and her princes."** That was 597 BC.
- **"He took one of the royal offspring and made a covenant with him, putting him under oath" (v13).** This was a vassal treaty, a common form in the ancient Near East. The great king promised protection, and the lesser king swore loyalty and paid tribute, with curses invoked on anyone who broke the oath. Treaties like this survive from Assyria and the Hittites. Nebuchadnezzar took **"the chief men of the land"** as hostages and **"that the kingdom might be humble and not lift itself up"** (v14). It was a hard settlement, but a merciful one. Jeremiah urged Zedekiah to accept it, because submitting to Babylon was God's will for that time (Jer 27:12–15).
- **"He rebelled against him by sending his ambassadors to Egypt, that they might give him horses and a large army" (v15).** This broke more than a treaty. The law warned Israel's kings not to "acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses" (Deut 17:16). Isaiah had condemned the same policy a century earlier: "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses" (Isa 31:1). **"Can he break the covenant and yet escape?"**
- **"In Babylon he shall die" (v16).** This is a sworn oath of God himself: "As I live." Zedekiah was taken to Babylon and died there in prison (Jer 52:11). This is the same fate foretold in 12:13.
- **"Pharaoh with his mighty army… will not help him" (v17).** That is exactly what happened. When the Babylonians besieged Jerusalem, an Egyptian army marched out, and the siege was briefly lifted. The people rejoiced, but Jeremiah warned that Pharaoh's army would go home and Babylon would return (Jer 37:5–10). It did. **"Mounds are cast up and siege walls built"** describes the siege works the Babylonians built against the city.
- **"He gave his hand and did all these things" (v18).** To give the hand was to make a pledge, like a handshake on an oath. Zedekiah gave his hand and then broke his word.
- **"Surely it is my oath that he despised, and my covenant that he broke" (v19).** This is the key verse. The oath was sworn in the LORD's name (2 Chr 36:13), so breaking it was treachery against God, not only against Nebuchadnezzar. Oaths in God's name were never to be taken lightly (Exod 20:7; Lev 19:12). Joshua kept his oath to the Gibeonites even though he had been tricked into it (Josh 9:19–20), and God later punished Saul's house for breaking it (2 Sam 21:1–2). Faithfulness to one's word, even to a pagan king, is part of faithfulness to God (Ps 15:4).
- **"I will spread my net over him… I will bring him to Babylon and enter into judgment with him there" (v20).** These are almost the words of 12:13. Nebuchadnezzar was the hunter, but God owned the net. At Riblah, Zedekiah was sentenced, saw his sons killed, and was blinded (2 Kings 25:6–7).
- **"All the pick of his troops shall fall by the sword, and the survivors shall be scattered" (v21).** When Zedekiah fled, his army scattered from him on the plains of Jericho (2 Kings 25:5).
- **"I myself will take a sprig from the lofty top of the cedar" (v22).** Now the fable turns. The emphatic "I myself" (repeated twice) answers the first eagle. Nebuchadnezzar broke off a twig from David's cedar and carried it away, but God will break off a twig of his own. **"A tender one"** (*rak*) suggests something small and young, which recalls Isaiah's shoot from the stump of Jesse (Isa 11:1) and the servant who "grew up before him like a young plant" (Isa 53:2). Jeremiah and Zechariah call the coming king "the Branch" (Jer 23:5; Zech 3:8; 6:12).
- **"I myself will plant it on a high and lofty mountain… the mountain height of Israel" (vv22–23).** This is Zion, the mountain of the LORD, which the prophets said would be lifted above all hills, so that the nations would stream to it (Isa 2:2; Mic 4:1). Ezekiel's later temple vision is set on "a very high mountain" (40:2).
- **"Under it will dwell every kind of bird" (v23).** In the ancient world a great tree sheltering birds was a picture of a great empire, as with Egypt in 31:6 and Nebuchadnezzar's dream-tree in Daniel 4:12, 21. This tree is planted by God himself, and every kind of bird finds shelter in it, which points to the nations coming under the rule of David's heir. Jesus echoed the image in the parable of the mustard seed, the smallest seed that becomes a tree "so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches" (Matt 13:31–32).
- **"I bring low the high tree, and make high the low tree" (v24).** This is a song of reversal, like Hannah's (1 Sam 2:7–8) and Mary's (Luke 1:52): "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate." Babylon, the high green tree, will be brought low. David's dried-up line, which looked dead after Zedekiah, will flourish again. **"I am the LORD; I have spoken, and I will do it."** The chapter ends with the certainty of God's word.
- **The fulfillment.** Some readers have seen a partial fulfillment in Zerubbabel, Jehoiachin's grandson, who led exiles home and rebuilt the temple (Hag 2:21–23). But Zerubbabel never ruled as king, and no tree sheltered the nations in his day. Most Christian interpreters, along with many Jewish readers who took this as messianic, see the full fulfillment in the Messiah. Matthew's genealogy traces Jesus' descent through Jehoiachin (Jeconiah) and Zerubbabel (Matt 1:11–13), right through the very branch Babylon broke off.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "mighty conquerors are aptly likened to birds or beasts of prey," and on the broken oath, "In every solemn oath, God is appealed to as a witness of the sincerity of him that swears." Of the twig he says, "It appears only applicable to Jesus, the Son of David, the Messiah of God." JFB reads the eagle's "divers colours" as the varied languages and customs of the peoples Babylon ruled, and it notes that Pharaoh "did not act in concert with" Zedekiah but pulled back to Egypt. JFB argues that the tender twig cannot be Zerubbabel, who never reigned, and links the sheltering tree to the gospel's mustard seed. Guzik agrees that the twig is the Messiah, and he presses the point about the oath: since it was sworn in God's name, breaking it was rebellion against God, and believers' promises still carry that weight. The Tyndale notes add an insight of their own. The first eagle's care for the vine reads more like God's care for Israel than like Nebuchadnezzar's, so Zedekiah's foolish rebellion against Babylon mirrors Israel's far greater folly in rebelling against God.

#### Pointing to Christ
Babylon broke off the top of David's cedar and carried it away, and after Zedekiah it looked as if the royal line of David was finished. But God said, "I myself will take a sprig… a tender one, and I myself will plant it." Jesus came from that broken line. He was born in obscurity, grew up "like a young plant" in Nazareth, and was cut down on a tree outside Jerusalem. God raised him and set him on his holy hill (Ps 2:6), and now birds "of every sort," people from every nation, find shelter under his branches. He is the King who never breaks his oath. Zedekiah despised the covenant he swore in God's name. Jesus kept every covenant promise, and "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20). The God who brings low the high tree and makes the dry tree flourish did it most fully at the empty tomb.
