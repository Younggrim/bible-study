*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah." Psalm 85 looks back on a great act of restoration and asks God to finish what he began. Most interpreters, and the Tyndale notes, place it after the return from exile in Babylon, which began in 538 BC. The people are home, the sin that caused the exile has been forgiven, but life is still hard: the land yields little, the walls are down, and God's anger seems to linger. Haggai, preaching in 520 BC, describes exactly this: "You have sown much, and harvested little… the heavens above you have withheld the dew" (Hag 1:6, 10). JFB takes "restored the fortunes" more broadly, of any great rescue, and Guzik notes that it could also fit earlier times of trouble. The psalm has three movements: thanks for past restoration (vv1–3), prayer for restoration now (vv4–7), and God's answer of peace, ending in one of the most beautiful pictures of salvation in Scripture: "steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other" (vv8–13). The key word is "turn" or "restore" (*shuv*), which appears five times in the Hebrew (vv1, 3, 4, 6, 8).

#### You Were Favorable to Your Land (vv1–3)
- **"LORD, you were favorable to your land" (v1).** "Favorable" means God took pleasure in it and accepted it. It is still "your land," God's own.
- **"You restored the fortunes of Jacob" (v1).** The phrase *shavta shevut* is literally "you turned the turning" or "returned the captivity." The KJV has "brought back the captivity of Jacob." It can mean bringing exiles home, or more broadly restoring someone's former condition (Job 42:10). Psalm 126:1 uses the same words: "When the LORD restored the fortunes of Zion, we were like those who dream." JFB: "not necessarily the Babylonian, but any great evil."
- **"You forgave the iniquity of your people; you covered all their sin" (v2).** Two pictures of pardon. "Forgave" (*nasa*) means lifted, carried away, like a heavy load removed, the word used of the scapegoat carrying Israel's sins into the wilderness (Lev 16:22). "Covered" (*kasah*) means hidden from view. Psalm 32:1 uses the same pair: "Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered." The return from exile was not only a change of address. It was God's sign that he had forgiven them (Isa 40:1–2, "her iniquity is pardoned"). **Selah.**
- **"You withdrew all your wrath; you turned from your hot anger" (v3).** "Hot anger" is literally "the burning of your nose," a Hebrew picture of anger flaring. God's anger at Israel's long idolatry was real, and it had been turned away. JFB: it "implies that He was reconcilable."
- **Commentators:** Guzik, following Maclaren, draws out the two pictures in v2, sin lifted off like a weight and sin covered like a stain, and Clarke's link to the scapegoat. He sees in v3 a pointer to the cross, where God's wrath against sin was fully satisfied, and uses Boice's terms "atonement" and "propitiation." The Tyndale notes add that forgiveness opened the way to fellowship with God again.

#### Will You Not Revive Us Again? (vv4–7)
- **"Restore us again, O God of our salvation" (v4).** The same verb as v1, now a prayer. "You turned the fortunes of Jacob; now turn us." Past mercy becomes the ground for present asking. The restoration had begun but was not complete. Psalm 126 has the same shape: "Restore our fortunes, O LORD, like streams in the Negeb!" (126:4).
- **"Will you be angry with us forever? Will you prolong your anger to all generations?" (v5).** The returned exiles still felt under a cloud. Poor harvests, enemies and slow rebuilding (Ezra 4; Neh 1:3) felt like God's anger had not fully lifted. JFB says the people still had "occasion for the anger which is deprecated," meaning they still had sins for which they needed forgiveness.
- **"Will you not revive us again, that your people may rejoice in you?" (v6).** "Revive" means give life again, bring back to life (80:18; 71:20). "Again" is the word *shuv* once more. The aim of revival is joy, and specifically joy "in you." A revived people rejoice not mainly in better crops but in God himself (Hab 3:17–18).
- **"Show us your steadfast love, O LORD, and grant us your salvation" (v7).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. "Salvation" here is broad: rescue, deliverance, well-being. The prayer asks God to make his love visible.
- **Commentators:** Guzik treats v6 as a model prayer for revival. He says revival is God's work, but his people should ask for it, especially after remembering his past faithfulness and when they feel spiritually dry. He tells of a congregation whose prayers came before a great revival, and cites Boice's point that a church needing revival has become spiritually dead and needs new life. The Tyndale notes say the prayer rests on God's unfailing love, not on the people's merit.

#### He Will Speak Peace (vv8–9)
- **"Let me hear what God the LORD will speak" (v8).** The psalmist stops praying and starts listening, like a prophet waiting for God's word (Hab 2:1, "I will take my stand at my watchpost… and look out to see what he will say to me"). Henry: "Compose thyself, O my soul! in a humble silence to attend upon God and wait his motions."
- **"For he will speak peace to his people, to his saints" (v8).** "Peace" (*shalom*) is far more than the absence of war. It means wholeness, well-being, everything as it should be. "Saints" (*chasidim*) means his loyal, faithful ones. JFB: "He is confident God will favor His penitent people."
- **"But let them not turn back to folly" (v8).** The fifth *shuv*. God has turned his people back from exile. They must not turn back to the folly that sent them there. In the Bible "folly" is not lack of intelligence but moral and spiritual stupidity, living as if God did not matter (14:1). Henry: "All sin is folly, but especially backsliding." Jesus says the same to a man he healed: "Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you" (John 5:14).
- **"Surely his salvation is near to those who fear him, that glory may dwell in our land" (v9).** "Glory" here means God's own glorious presence, the *kavod* that filled the tabernacle and Solomon's temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kgs 8:11) and that Ezekiel saw leave the temple before the exile (Ezek 10:18; 11:23). The returned exiles longed for that glory to come back. Haggai promised it would: "I will fill this house with glory… The latter glory of this house shall be greater than the former" (Hag 2:7, 9).
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that hearing God's word means surrendering to it, and, following Horne, links "peace" here to the gospel, "preaching good news of peace through Jesus Christ" (Acts 10:36). He quotes Spurgeon's warning that those who will not hear God's word cannot know its peace. The Tyndale notes say God will not tolerate foolish ways like those that led to the exile.

#### Righteousness and Peace Kiss (vv10–13)
- **"Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other" (v10).** Four of God's qualities, pictured as people. "Steadfast love" (*chesed*) and "faithfulness" (*'emet*, truth, reliability) are the pair from God's self-revelation to Moses: "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exod 34:6). "Righteousness" (*tsedeq*) is God doing what is right, and "peace" (*shalom*) is the wholeness that results. They meet like old friends on the road and embrace. In a world of sin, these qualities can seem to pull against each other: mercy wants to forgive, truth and righteousness demand that sin be dealt with. In the salvation the psalm pictures, they are no longer at odds. JFB: the "work of righteousness" in God's holy government shall be "peace" (Isa 32:17).
- **How the church has read v10.** Christians from the early church onward have seen this verse fulfilled at the cross, where God's mercy and his justice met. God is "just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:26). Medieval writers even turned it into a story, the "Four Daughters of God," in which Mercy and Truth, Righteousness and Peace argue over fallen humanity until they are reconciled in Christ. Henry: "Christ, as Mediator, brings heaven and earth together again, which sin had set at variance."
- **"Faithfulness springs up from the ground, and righteousness looks down from the sky" (v11).** Heaven and earth answer each other. Faithfulness grows like a plant from the soil, righteousness leans down from above like the sun looking over the land. Isaiah uses similar language: "Shower, O heavens, from above, and let the clouds rain down righteousness; let the earth open, that salvation and righteousness may bear fruit" (Isa 45:8). Henry: "the great affair of our salvation is so well contrived, so well concerted."
- **"Yes, the LORD will give what is good, and our land will yield its increase" (v12).** The answer includes the very thing the struggling community needed: harvests. Spiritual restoration and physical blessing go together in the covenant promises (Lev 26:4; Hag 2:19, "From this day on I will bless you"). It echoes 67:6: "The earth has yielded its increase."
- **"Righteousness will go before him and make his footsteps a way" (v13).** Righteousness is pictured as a herald running ahead of God the King to prepare his road, the way a messenger went before a royal procession (89:14). God's people can then walk in his footsteps. The Tyndale notes describe righteousness as a messenger preparing the world for God's coming. Henry: "Righteousness is a sure guide both in meeting God and in following him." JFB says men are thereby "set," or guided, in God's holy ways.
- **Commentators:** Guzik follows Clarke in placing the meeting of v10 at Calvary, and links it to John 1:14, 17, where grace and truth come together in Jesus. He sees a future fulfillment in v11 when creation is set free (Rom 8:21). On v13 he cites Spurgeon's picture of God's path as a track that his people can follow with joy.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 85 asks God to finish the restoration he began, to speak peace, to bring his glory back, and to make righteousness and peace embrace. All of this meets in Jesus. In him "glory" came to dwell in the land: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory… full of grace and truth" (John 1:14), the same pair as v10. At the cross, God's love and his justice met, and "righteousness and peace" kissed, because "since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 5:1). He is the one who came "preaching peace" to those far off and those near (Eph 2:17). John the Baptist went before him to prepare his way (Mark 1:2–3), and now those who follow him walk in his steps (1 Pet 2:21). The prayer "Will you not revive us again?" is still the church's prayer, and its answer is the risen Christ, who gives life.
