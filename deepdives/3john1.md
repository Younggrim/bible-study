*Setting:* This is the shortest book in the Bible, about 220 words in Greek, short enough to fit on one sheet of papyrus. Like 2 John, it comes from "the elder," whom the early church identified as the apostle John. He was probably writing from Ephesus late in the first century, near the end of his long life. 2 John warns a church not to welcome false teachers. 3 John is its mirror image: it urges a friend to keep welcoming true ones. The letter is built around three men: Gaius, who is commended; Diotrephes, who is rebuked; and Demetrius, who is recommended. It is a rare, personal glimpse into a real church dispute.

#### Greeting to Gaius (vv1–4)
- **"The elder to the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth" (v1).** "Elder" (*presbyteros*) can mean an older man, a church office, or both. John, perhaps the last apostle still living, chooses the humble title (compare 1 Pet 5:1). Gaius was one of the most common names in the Roman world. This Gaius is probably not the Gaius of Corinth (Rom 16:23) or Macedonia (Acts 19:29). He was a respected believer in a church near Ephesus.
- **"I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul" (v2).** This was a standard letter greeting, given real warmth. John assumes Gaius's soul is flourishing and prays that his body would do as well. Some have used this verse to teach that God promises financial prosperity and health to all believers, but that reads far too much into a friend's greeting. The verse does show God cares about the whole person, body as well as soul. For most believers the prayer would need to run the other way: that their souls would prosper as much as their bodies.
- **"Testified to your truth, as indeed you are walking in the truth" (v3).** Traveling believers had reported back to John. "Walking" (*peripateō*) means a way of life, not just correct belief (2 John 4).
- **"I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth" (v4).** "My children" suggests John led Gaius to faith or discipled him. It is every parent's and every pastor's deepest joy.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that John, though an apostle, takes the humble title "elder." He says "it becomes not Christ's ministers to affect swelling pompous titles," and suggests that with most apostles gone, John was honoring the ordinary ministry by sharing its name. His concise commentary says "soul prosperity is the greatest blessing on this side heaven," and adds that "a rich soul may be lodged in a weak body." JFB suggests John may have heard that Gaius was in poor health, which explains the prayer. It reads "be in health" as the particular case of the general "prosper."

#### Gaius Commended for His Hospitality (vv5–8)
- **"It is a faithful thing you do in all your efforts for these brothers, strangers as they are" (v5).** Gaius took in traveling preachers and teachers he had never met. In a world of dangerous inns, Christian hospitality was the lifeline of the early mission (Rom 12:13; Heb 13:2; 1 Pet 4:9).
- **"Send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God" (v6).** "Send on" (*propempō*) was a technical term for supplying missionaries with food, money and provisions for the next stage of their trip (Acts 15:3; Rom 15:24; Titus 3:13). "Worthy of God" sets the standard: treat God's messengers as you would treat the One who sent them.
- **"They have gone out for the sake of the name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles" (v7).** "The name" is Jesus' name (Acts 5:41; Phil 2:9–10). The missionaries refused support from unbelievers, unlike the wandering philosophers and religious peddlers of the day, so no one could say they preached for profit (2 Cor 2:17; 1 Thess 2:9).
- **"Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth" (v8).** This is the principle of the whole letter. Those who can't go can still share in the work by sending and supporting (Matt 10:40–42; Phil 4:14–18). "Fellow workers" (*synergoi*) is the word Paul uses for his ministry partners.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes Gaius as having "a catholic spirit." He "could overlook the petty differences among serious Christians, and be communicative to all who bore the image and did the work of Christ." His concise commentary adds that those who can't preach "may yet receive, help, and countenance those who do so." JFB reads "in a manner worthy of God" as: "He who honours God's missionaries… honours God."

#### Diotrephes and Demetrius (vv9–12)
- **"I have written something to the church" (v9).** This may mean 1 John, 2 John, or a letter now lost.
- **"Diotrephes, who likes to put himself first" (v9).** "Likes to put himself first" is one Greek word, *philoprōteuō*, "love of being first." It appears nowhere else in Greek before this. Diotrephes was probably a leader in the local church who wanted control. Notice that John says nothing about false doctrine. The problem was pride and power. Jesus had warned the disciples, John among them, about exactly this (Mark 10:35–45). John himself once asked for the top seat.
- **"Does not acknowledge our authority" (v9).** He rejected John's letter and leadership.
- **"Talking wicked nonsense against us" (v10).** "Talking nonsense" (*phlyareō*) means gossiping and babbling: empty words with a nasty intent.
- **"He refuses to welcome the brothers, and also stops those who want to and puts them out of the church" (v10).** Diotrephes did four things: slandered the apostle, refused hospitality, blocked others from giving it, and expelled those who did. He used church discipline as a weapon to protect his own position.
- **"If I come, I will bring up what he is doing" (v10).** John doesn't ignore it, and he doesn't attack from a distance either. He plans to deal with it in person, openly (Matt 18:15–17; 1 Tim 5:19–20).
- **"Do not imitate evil but imitate good" (v11).** "Imitate" is *mimeomai*, the root of "mimic." Believers learn by copying examples, so choose them carefully. **"Whoever does good is from God; whoever does evil has not seen God."** This is John's familiar test (1 John 3:6, 10). Conduct shows whether someone truly knows God.
- **"Demetrius has received a good testimony from everyone, and from the truth itself" (v12).** Demetrius was probably the one carrying this letter, and perhaps one of the traveling preachers Gaius was asked to welcome. He has three witnesses: everyone who knows him, the truth itself (his life matches the gospel), and John. Some suggest he's the Demetrius the silversmith of Acts 19:24 after his conversion, but that is only a guess.
- **Commentators:** Guzik's summary contrasts the three men. Diotrephes's real problem was pride, a craving for preeminence that led him to silence and expel others. John's public naming of him follows the apostolic command to mark those who cause divisions (Rom 16:17). It isn't a failure of love. Henry's concise commentary warns against following "that which is proud, selfish, and of bad design, though the example may be given by persons of rank and power." He adds that "few are well spoken of by all; and sometimes it is ill to be so." JFB reads v11's "evil" as Diotrephes and "good" as Demetrius. It takes "hath not seen God" spiritually.

#### Final Greetings (vv13–15)
- **"I would rather not write with pen and ink… we will talk face to face" (vv13–14).** "Pen" is literally "reed." "Face to face" is literally "mouth to mouth" in Greek. It is almost word for word the ending of 2 John. Some things are better handled in person than in writing, especially church conflict.
- **"Peace be to you" (v15).** This is the Hebrew *shalom*, which Jesus gave his disciples after the resurrection (John 20:19–21).
- **"The friends greet you. Greet the friends, each by name" (v15).** Calling believers "friends" (*philoi*) echoes Jesus: "I have called you friends" (John 15:14–15). "Each by name" is personal care, the opposite of Diotrephes's power games. A shepherd knows his sheep by name (John 10:3).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "face to face" is literally "mouth to mouth." On "by name" it quotes Bengel: "not less than if their names were written." Henry's concise commentary closes: "Those may well salute and greet one another on earth, who hope to live together in heaven."

#### Pointing to Christ
3 John is a small window onto Jesus' teaching about greatness: "whoever would be first among you must be slave of all" (Mark 10:44). Diotrephes loved to be first. Gaius and Demetrius served. The traveling preachers went out "for the sake of the name," and supporting them made ordinary believers "fellow workers for the truth." The letter's last word, friends greeted "each by name," reflects the Good Shepherd, who knows his own and calls them by name.
