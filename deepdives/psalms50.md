*Setting:* "A Psalm of Asaph." This is the first psalm in the Psalter credited to Asaph. The rest of his psalms come together later (73–83). Asaph was a Levite whom David appointed as a chief singer when the ark came to Jerusalem (1 Chr 15:17–19; 16:4–7). He played the cymbals, and his family led temple music for centuries afterward (Ezra 2:41). Chronicles calls him a "seer," a prophet who spoke through song (1 Chr 25:2; 2 Chr 29:30), and this psalm is a prophecy set to music. Some Asaph psalms come from later times, written by "the sons of Asaph" who carried on his name. Psalm 50 pictures a courtroom. God comes as judge, calls heaven and earth as witnesses, and puts his own people on trial. Prophets often used this form of covenant lawsuit (Isa 1:2–20; Mic 6:1–8). The charges come in two parts: against those who think ritual is enough (vv7–15), and against those who recite God's law while breaking it (vv16–21). Matthew Henry: "This psalm, as the former, is a psalm of instruction, not of prayer or praise." Psalm 49 warned against trusting money. Psalm 50 warns against trusting religion.

#### The Mighty One Speaks (vv1–6)
- **"The Mighty One, God the LORD" (v1).** Three names in a row: *El*, *Elohim*, *YHWH*. *El* is the basic word for God, stressing might. *Elohim* is the usual word for God, plural in form, the fullness of deity. *YHWH* (LORD) is his covenant name, the name he gave Moses (Exod 3:14–15). The only other place all three are stacked like this is in Joshua 22:22, also a solemn oath before a trial. The opening is meant to make the whole earth stand up.
- **"Speaks and summons the earth from the rising of the sun to its setting" (v1).** From east to west, the whole world is called to court. But not to be judged: as witnesses (v4).
- **"Out of Zion, the perfection of beauty, God shines forth" (v2).** "Perfection of beauty" echoes 48:2 and is the phrase Lamentations quotes over the ruined city (Lam 2:15). "Shines forth" is the language of God appearing in glory, like the sun rising (Deut 33:2: "he shone forth from Mount Paran"). At Sinai God came from the south; now he shines from Zion.
- **"Our God comes; he does not keep silence" (v3).** This line sets up v21: "These things you have done, and I have been silent." God's patience had looked like silence. Now the silence is over.
- **"Before him is a devouring fire, around him a mighty tempest" (v3).** This is Sinai again: fire, thunder, storm and smoke (Exod 19:16–18; Deut 4:24, "the LORD your God is a consuming fire"). The God who gave the law comes to ask whether it has been kept. Spurgeon, quoted by Guzik, calls fire the emblem of justice in action.
- **"He calls to the heavens above and to the earth, that he may judge his people" (v4).** In ancient treaties, the gods were called on as witnesses. In the Bible, God calls heaven and earth themselves. Moses did it first: "I call heaven and earth to witness against you today" (Deut 30:19; 32:1), and Isaiah began his book the same way (Isa 1:2). The shock is that the defendant is "his people." Israel might have expected God to judge the nations. Instead, as Peter later wrote, "it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God" (1 Pet 4:17).
- **"Gather to me my faithful ones, who made a covenant with me by sacrifice!" (v5).** "Faithful ones" (*chasidim*) comes from *chesed*, steadfast covenant love. They are the people who belong to God by covenant, though the trial will show that not all live up to the name. "Made a covenant" is literally "cut a covenant," because covenants were sealed by cutting up a sacrifice (Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18). At Sinai, Moses sprinkled the blood of the sacrifice on the people and said, "Behold the blood of the covenant" (Exod 24:5–8). The covenant began with a sacrifice. The question is whether the sacrifices since then have meant anything.
- **"The heavens declare his righteousness, for God himself is judge! Selah" (v6).** God does not need a human judge. And he will judge rightly. Heaven itself vouches for that.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees here Christ as judge, since he who gave the law will judge by it: "Christ is fitly represented speaking as a Judge, then when he speaks as a Lawgiver." Guzik, following Morgan, explains the three divine names in v1, and with Kidner stresses the shock that God's own people, who expected to sit with the judge, find themselves in the dock. JFB notes that "made" is literally "cut," referring to the dividing of the victim when a covenant was made, and that the scene recalls the giving of the law.

#### Not for Your Sacrifices (vv7–15)
- **"Hear, O my people, and I will speak; O Israel, I will testify against you" (v7).** "Hear, O Israel" is the opening of the Shema, the creed Israel recited daily (Deut 6:4). "Testify against" means bring formal charges.
- **"I am God, your God" (v7).** An echo of the opening of the Ten Commandments: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (Exod 20:2). The judge is not a stranger. He is their own God, which is exactly why their failure matters. JFB: he has a right to pure worship, by covenant and by creation.
- **"Not for your sacrifices do I rebuke you" (v8).** This is important. God is not saying sacrifice is wrong. He commanded it. "Your burnt offerings are continually before me." They were doing the rituals. Morning and evening, the offerings kept coming. The problem lay elsewhere.
- **"I will not accept a bull from your house" (v9).** Israel's neighbors believed their gods needed sacrifices as food. Temples fed their idols daily, and in the Babylonian flood story the gods gather "like flies" around the sacrifice because they are hungry. The LORD wants none of that thinking.
- **"For every beast of the forest is mine, the cattle on a thousand hills" (v10).** A famous line. God already owns every animal that might be offered to him. A gift of a bull to God is like a child buying a parent a present with the parent's own money. It can still be a loving gift, but it cannot be a payment.
- **"I know all the birds of the hills" (v11).** "Know" here means know as an owner knows his property, and also know personally. Jesus said not one sparrow "will fall to the ground apart from your Father" (Matt 10:29).
- **"If I were hungry, I would not tell you" (v12).** A touch of holy irony. Even if God were hungry, he would not need to ask Israel for food. "The world and its fullness are mine" echoes 24:1, and Paul quotes it in 1 Corinthians 10:26.
- **"Do I eat the flesh of bulls or drink the blood of goats?" (v13).** The question answers itself. God is spirit (John 4:24). Paul told the Athenians the same: God is not "served by human hands, as though he needed anything" (Acts 17:25).
- **"Offer to God a sacrifice of thanksgiving" (v14).** Now God says what he does want. "Thanksgiving" is *todah*, which was also the name of a real sacrifice, the thank offering (Lev 7:12–15). So this is not throwing out sacrifice. It is calling for the sacrifice to mean what it says: a grateful heart. Hebrews uses the same idea for Christians: "let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15).
- **"And perform your vows to the Most High" (v14).** Vows were promises made to God, often in trouble ("if you rescue me, I will…"), and were paid with an offering afterward (66:13–14). Keep the promises you made.
- **"Call upon me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me" (v15).** This may be the most surprising line in the psalm. God does not want Israel's food. He wants their need. What honors him is not what they give him, but their asking him for help, his giving it, and their thanks. The circle is complete: trouble, prayer, rescue, praise. In Daniel Defoe's novel, a shipwrecked Robinson Crusoe opens a Bible to this verse and it begins his turn to God. Spurgeon preached a sermon on it called "Robinson Crusoe's Text."
- **Commentators:** Henry asks, "What occasion had he for their bullocks and goats," when he "has the command of all the beasts of the forest," and says thanks pleases the Lord "if it come from a humble thankful heart, full of love to him and joy in him." Guzik, quoting Clarke, says the sacrifices were God's own appointment, but once the people trusted in them, God rejected them. He also passes on a story told of Harry Ironside praying for Dallas Seminary in a financial crisis, recalling that God owns the cattle on a thousand hills, and a gift arriving that same day from a man who had sold some cattle. JFB reads vv8–15 as aimed at external worship offered as though it put God in debt.

#### What Right Have You? (vv16–23)
- **"But to the wicked God says" (v16).** The second charge is more serious. These are people within the covenant who know the law and talk about it, but live in plain sin. JFB: "formalists" leading vicious lives.
- **"What right have you to recite my statutes or take my covenant on your lips?" (v16).** They could quote the Bible. They may even have taught it. But words about God's covenant are out of place in a mouth that ignores it. Paul makes the same charge: "you then who teach others, do you not teach yourself? While you preach against stealing, do you steal?" (Rom 2:21–23).
- **"For you hate discipline, and you cast my words behind you" (v17).** "Discipline" (*musar*) means instruction and correction, the key word of Proverbs (Prov 1:2–3). To "cast behind you" is to throw something over your shoulder, out of sight and mind (1 Kgs 14:9; Neh 9:26).
- **"If you see a thief, you are pleased with him, and you keep company with adulterers" (v18).** Three of the Ten Commandments are now named in order: stealing, adultery (v18), and false witness (vv19–20). These people may not steal themselves, but they enjoy those who do and cheerfully join in. Paul says the same of those who "not only do them but give approval to those who practice them" (Rom 1:32). Guzik cites VanGemeren: consenting to sin is a form of sin.
- **"You give your mouth free rein for evil, and your tongue frames deceit" (v19).** "Frames" means weaves or stitches together, like a cloth. Lies are made with care.
- **"You sit and speak against your brother; you slander your own mother's son" (v20).** "Sit" suggests a settled habit, sitting in a circle of gossip (1:1, "nor sits in the seat of scoffers"). A "mother's son" was the closest of brothers in a world with many half-brothers. Nothing is sacred to the slanderer.
- **"These things you have done, and I have been silent" (v21).** God's patience is not approval. Peter: "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish" (2 Pet 3:9). Ecclesiastes says sinners grow bold because the sentence is not quickly carried out (Eccl 8:11).
- **"You thought that I was one like yourself" (v21).** This is the root of the whole problem. They made God in their own image: a God who does not mind a little theft, a little adultery, a little slander, as long as the sacrifices keep coming. Every idol begins this way.
- **"But now I rebuke you and lay the charge before you" (v21).** Literally "set it in order before your eyes," like a prosecutor laying out the evidence item by item.
- **"Mark this, then, you who forget God" (v22).** "Forget" in the Bible means not just failing to remember but living as if God did not matter (Deut 8:11–14). JFB: "unmindfulness of His true character."
- **"Lest I tear you apart, and there be none to deliver!" (v22).** "Tear" (*taraf*) is the word for a lion tearing its prey (Hos 5:14; 7:2). It is a fearful contrast to v15: "I will deliver you." Those who call on God are delivered. Those who forget him will find no one to deliver them.
- **"The one who offers thanksgiving as his sacrifice glorifies me" (v23).** The psalm ends with the two themes together. Thanksgiving answers the first charge (v14). Right living answers the second.
- **"To one who orders his way rightly I will show the salvation of God!" (v23).** "Orders his way" is literally "sets a way," prepares a road. The Hebrew is a little uncertain, and some scholars read a near-identical word meaning "blameless in his way." The sense is the same: the one who makes his life straight before God will see God's salvation. The last word of the psalm is not "tear" but "salvation." Even this hard psalm ends with an open door.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this a word "for those that declare God's statutes to others" while living "in disobedience to them themselves," and gives the reason they dare to: "Sinners take God's silence for consent and his patience for connivance." On v23 he writes: "The right ordering of the conversation is the only way, and it is a sure way, to obtain the great salvation" ("conversation" in 1710 meant one's conduct). Guzik, citing Maclaren, says God's silence is patient tolerance meant to lead to repentance, which sinners abuse, and stresses that thanksgiving and right living do not earn salvation but put a person where they can receive it. JFB reads v23 as making outward worship "a true index of the heart."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 50 says God does not need bulls and goats and is not fed by sacrifice. Hebrews agrees: "it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins," and then shows what God did want instead, quoting Psalm 40: "a body have you prepared for me… I have come to do your will, O God" (Heb 10:4–7). Jesus is the one whose sacrifice did not need the cattle on a thousand hills, because he gave himself. Through him the covenant made "by sacrifice" (v5) became the new covenant in his blood (Luke 22:20). Jesus also took up this psalm's warning. He quoted Hosea against the religious leaders of his day: "Go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice'" (Matt 9:13). He condemned those who tithe mint and dill but neglect "justice and mercy and faithfulness" (Matt 23:23). And the God who "comes" and "does not keep silence" (v3) will come again as judge, for "the Father… has given all judgment to the Son" (John 5:22). For those who have called on him in the day of trouble, the last word of the psalm is the last word for them too: "I will show the salvation of God."
