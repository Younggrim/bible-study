*Setting:* Psalm 126 is the seventh of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. It is the middle psalm of the fifteen, and one of the best loved. It has no author's name. Most readers, ancient and modern, connect it with the return from exile in Babylon. In 539 BC the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon and the next year issued a decree allowing the Jews to go home and rebuild the temple (Ezra 1:1–4). After seventy years of exile it seemed too good to be true. But the return was not the glorious restoration the prophets had described. The first exiles came back to ruins, poor harvests, hostile neighbors and a small, struggling community (Ezra 4; Hag 1:5–11). That explains the psalm's shape. The first half remembers a great joy (vv1–3). The second half prays for God to do it again, because the restoration is not yet complete (vv4–6). Some, including Guzik, allow that it could come from an earlier deliverance, such as David's return to Jerusalem after Absalom's revolt, and the key phrase "restored the fortunes" can be used for any great reversal (Job 42:10). The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's two halves.

#### When the LORD Restored the Fortunes of Zion (vv1–3)
- **"When the LORD restored the fortunes of Zion" (v1).** The Hebrew idiom is *shub shebut*, literally "turn the turning" or "return the captivity." The lexicon lists both senses: bringing back captives, and more generally restoring someone's fortunes. It is used of Israel's return from exile (Deut 30:3; Jer 29:14) and also of Job, when God "restored the fortunes of Job" (Job 42:10). The ESV's "restored the fortunes" covers both. The verb is the LORD's. The exiles did not engineer their own return. Cyrus issued the decree, but Scripture says "the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus" (Ezra 1:1).
- **"We were like those who dream" (v1).** The news seemed unreal. When Jacob heard that Joseph was still alive, "his heart became numb, for he did not believe them" (Gen 45:26). When Peter was freed from prison by an angel, "he thought he was seeing a vision" (Acts 12:9). The disciples first thought the women's news of the resurrection was "an idle tale" (Luke 24:11). Grace sometimes arrives so suddenly that it is hard to believe it is real.
- **The tense question.** Some translators read v1 as a future, a hope rather than a memory ("When the LORD restores... we shall be like those who dream"). Most English versions, including the ESV, read it as past, which fits the prayer of v4 well: God did it once, so the people ask him to do it again.
- **"Then our mouth was filled with laughter, and our tongue with shouts of joy" (v2).** The joy was physical and loud. The word for "laughter" (*sechoq*) recalls Sarah, who laughed when Isaac was born: "God has made laughter for me" (Gen 21:6). "Shouts of joy" (*rinnah*) is a ringing cry, the sound of a harvest celebration or a festival. The same word appears three times in the psalm (vv2, 5, 6).
- **"Then they said among the nations, 'The LORD has done great things for them'" (v2).** The surrounding peoples saw it too. Israel's restoration was a testimony to the nations, as the prophets said it would be: "the nations will know that I am the LORD" (Ezek 36:23). Few small peoples deported by an empire ever went home. This one did, and its God got the credit.
- **"The LORD has done great things for us; we are glad" (v3).** Israel takes the nations' words and makes them its own, changing only "them" to "us." The sentence ends simply: "we are glad." After the laughter and shouting of v2, it is a quieter, steadier joy. Mary sings nearly the same words in her song: "he who is mighty has done great things for me" (Luke 1:49).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the psalm as Ezra's time and says, "It is the repenting reforming people that are, and shall be, the rejoicing people." JFB calls the returnees' joy "ecstatic" and passes on Hengstenberg's alternate reading of v1, that "God returns to His people when they return to Him." Guzik notes how often believers have used the words "we were like those who dream" to describe times of revival, and passes on Spurgeon's point that the nations who said "the LORD has done great things" were not sharing the blessing themselves, yet still gave God the credit. He contrasts Clarke, who hears wild joy in v3, with Maclaren, who hears something calmer.

#### Restore Our Fortunes, O LORD (vv4–6)
- **"Restore our fortunes, O LORD" (v4).** The same phrase as v1, now as a prayer. Remembering what God did leads to asking him to do it again, or to finish it. The study notes point out that the prophets had promised a restoration like Eden (Isa 51:3; Ezek 47), and what the returned exiles actually found fell far short of that. They had been brought back, but the land and the people still needed life.
- **"Like streams in the Negeb!" (v4).** The Negeb (Negev) is the dry land in the far south of Judah, toward the Sinai desert. Its streambeds, called wadis, sit empty and cracked for most of the year. When the winter rains fall in the hills, those wadis can fill within hours, and the desert along their banks turns green almost overnight. The prayer asks for that kind of change: sudden, overflowing, and wholly God's doing. Nobody can make it rain.
- **"Those who sow in tears shall reap with shouts of joy!" (v5).** The picture changes from a flash flood to a farmer's year. Sowing seed in the ancient world was risky. Seed grain was food that could have been eaten. In a hungry year, throwing it on the ground was an act of faith, and if the rains failed, it was lost. Haggai describes exactly this in the returned community: they "have sown much, and harvested little" (Hag 1:6). The psalm promises that tears now will be followed by a harvest of joy.
- **"He who goes out weeping, bearing the seed for sowing" (v6).** The Hebrew doubles both verbs for emphasis: literally "going, he goes, weeping... coming, he will come with shouts of joy." The repetition stresses both the length of the weeping and the certainty of the return. "Seed for sowing" is literally "a trail (or bag) of seed," the seed he carries and scatters as he walks.
- **"Shall come home with shouts of joy, bringing his sheaves with him" (v6).** Sheaves were the bundles of cut grain carried in from the field. The man who went out with a small bag of seed comes back with his arms full. The two pictures in vv4–6 belong together. The Negeb streams are pure gift, something only God can send. The sowing is human labor, done faithfully while waiting. Both end in the same joy.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the beginnings of mercy are encouragements to us to pray for the completing of it," and applies the harvest to the soul: "Those that sow in the tears of godly sorrow shall reap in the joy of a sealed pardon and a settled peace." He adds that the saints' troubles "will not last always." JFB explains the prayer of v4 historically: "All did not return at once; hence the prayer for repeated favors." He also notes the doubled verbs of v6. Guzik, following Kidner and Boice, sets the sudden gift of v4 beside the slow work of vv5–6, and passes on Meyer's encouragement that no prayer or tear prompted by the Spirit is wasted.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 126 holds together a great restoration that has already happened and a fuller one still being waited for. Christians live in exactly that place. In Jesus, God has done the greatest of "great things for us": the true return from exile, the forgiveness of sins and the resurrection from the dead. When the disciples saw the risen Lord, they "disbelieved for joy" (Luke 24:41), like those who dream. Yet the full harvest has not yet come. Jesus himself is the one who "goes out weeping, bearing the seed." He wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41), offered "loud cries and tears" in Gethsemane (Heb 5:7), and said, "unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). His death was the sowing, and the church gathered from every nation is the harvest. His people now sow in tears too, trusting his promise: "your sorrow will turn into joy" (John 16:20), until the day God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Rev 21:4).
