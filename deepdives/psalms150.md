*Setting:* Psalm 150 is the last psalm of the Psalter and the last of its five "Hallelujah psalms" (146–150). The book of Psalms is arranged in five books, and each of the first four ends with a short doxology, a burst of praise: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting! Amen and Amen" (41:13; also 72:18–19; 89:52; 106:48). Psalm 150 is the doxology for Book Five and for the whole collection. JFB calls it "a suitable doxology for the whole book," which recites the "place, theme, mode, and extent of God's high praise." It has no heading, no author, no story, no request, and no lament. It does not argue or teach. It simply calls for praise. The word "praise" (*hallel*) appears thirteen times in six verses. The four questions JFB names are answered in order: where to praise God (v1), why (v2), how (vv3–5) and who should do it (v6). The whole Psalter has traveled a long way to get here. It opened with "Blessed is the man" (Ps 1:1) and has passed through laments, confessions, cries from caves and songs of exile. It ends with every breathing thing praising the LORD. The name of the book in Hebrew, *Tehillim*, means "Praises," and this is where it was always heading. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's two stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### Praise God in His Sanctuary (vv1–2)
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v1).** *Hallelu Yah*, as in each of the last five psalms. This one is the climax of them all.
- **"Praise God in his sanctuary" (v1).** "God" here is *'El*, the short, ancient word for God, and the call "praise God" (*halelu-'El*) in this form is unusual. "Sanctuary" (*qodsho*) is literally "his holiness" or "his holy place." It may mean the temple in Jerusalem, where Israel gathered to worship. JFB takes it as "on earth." It may also mean God's heavenly dwelling, since the next line moves to the heavens. The Hebrew allows either, and perhaps both are meant: praise on earth and in heaven together.
- **"Praise him in his mighty heavens!" (v1).** "Mighty heavens" is literally "the expanse of his strength." "Expanse" (*raqia'*) is the word of Genesis 1:6–8, where God made "an expanse in the midst of the waters" and called it Heaven. The Tyndale notes say the phrase alludes to Genesis 1 and God's work of filling what was empty. The sky itself shows his strength (Ps 19:1). So the place of praise reaches from the temple courts to the sky: everywhere God is, and that is everywhere.
- **"Praise him for his mighty deeds" (v2).** This is the first reason: what God has done. "Mighty deeds" (*gevurot*) are his acts of power, especially his saving acts in Israel's history: the exodus, the sea, the wilderness, the land, the return from exile (Ps 145:4, 12). JFB cross-references 145:4. For Christians, the mightiest deeds are the death and resurrection of Jesus, when God worked "the immeasurable greatness of his power" in raising Christ from the dead (Eph 1:19–20).
- **"Praise him according to his excellent greatness!" (v2).** This is the second reason: who God is. "Excellent greatness" (*rov gudlo*) is literally "the abundance of his greatness." JFB gives "abundance of greatness." "According to" sets the measure: praise should match his greatness. It never can fully, since his greatness is "unsearchable" (145:3), but the aim is to praise him as fully as possible. So praise has two foundations: God's works and God's character. Both are needed. Praise for what God does without love for who he is would be thin, and praise for who he is would be abstract without what he has done.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "The holiness and the love of our God are more displayed in man's redemption" than in any other of his works, so the mighty deeds point above all to salvation. Guzik traces the theme of the sanctuary through the New Testament: Christ serves in the true heavenly sanctuary (Heb 8:1–2), the church is God's temple (2 Cor 6:16), each believer is a temple of the Spirit (1 Cor 3:16), and in the new creation the Lord himself is the temple (Rev 21:22). Kidner, as Guzik reports, points out that since God's glory fills the universe, his praise must fill it too.

#### Let Everything That Has Breath Praise the LORD (vv3–6)
- **The orchestra (vv3–5).** Now the psalm lists how God should be praised, and it reads like a roll call of every instrument Israel had: wind, strings and percussion, with dancing too. Several of these instruments are named in the accounts of David bringing the ark to Jerusalem (1 Chr 15:16, 28) and of the dedication of Nehemiah's walls (Neh 12:27). Exactly what each instrument looked like is not always certain, and translations differ. The point is clear enough: nothing is held back. Henry: "We need not care to know what instruments of music are mentioned." What matters is that God is praised with everything available.
- **"Praise him with trumpet sound" (v3).** The "trumpet" here is the *shofar*, a ram's horn, not the metal trumpets the priests also used (*chatsotsrot*, Num 10:2). The shofar sounded at Sinai (Exod 19:16), at the fall of Jericho (Josh 6:20), at the coronation of kings (1 Kings 1:34) and to announce the Day of Atonement and the Year of Jubilee (Lev 25:9). JFB says it was "used to call religious assemblies." It still sounds in synagogues on the Jewish New Year. In the New Testament, "the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised" (1 Cor 15:52; 1 Thess 4:16). Spurgeon, as Guzik reports, thinks the trumpet comes first because it belongs to the grandest and most solemn occasions.
- **"Praise him with lute and harp!" (v3).** The "lute" (*nevel*) and the "harp" (*kinnor*) were the two main stringed instruments of temple music. The *nevel* was probably larger, perhaps a kind of standing harp, and the *kinnor* a smaller, portable lyre, the one David played for Saul (1 Sam 16:23). The Levites played both (1 Chr 15:16).
- **"Praise him with tambourine and dance" (v4).** The "tambourine" (*tof*) was a hand drum, and the "dance" (*machol*) a joyful, often circling dance. They are paired in Miriam's song at the sea (Exod 15:20) and in Psalm 149:3. Tambourine and dance were often led by women. Adam Clarke, as Guzik reports, argued the word means a pipe, but most scholars and translations keep "dance," as in Psalm 30:11.
- **"Praise him with strings and pipe!" (v4).** "Strings" (*minnim*) is a general word for stringed instruments (Ps 45:8). The "pipe" (*'ugav*) was a wind instrument, perhaps a flute or reed pipe. It is one of the first instruments named in the Bible: Jubal "was the father of all those who play the lyre and pipe" (Gen 4:21). The Tyndale notes observe that the pipe is not mentioned as being used in temple worship. Here, in the last psalm, it is welcomed too.
- **"Praise him with sounding cymbals; praise him with loud clashing cymbals!" (v5).** The cymbals (*tseltselim*) were bronze plates or bowls clashed together, played by Levites like Asaph (1 Chr 15:19; 16:5). The Tyndale notes describe temple musicians striking two bronze bowls together. "Sounding" is literally "of hearing," cymbals that can be heard. "Loud clashing" is literally "of shouting" (*teru'ah*), the word for a victory shout or the blast of an alarm. Some think two kinds of cymbals are meant, smaller ones with a clear ring and larger ones with a crash. JFB calls them "suited to loud praise." Praise in Israel was not timid.
- **What the instruments mean.** Christians have differed on whether instruments belong in church worship. Some traditions, including some Reformed and Church of Christ congregations, have sung without instruments, holding that temple music was part of the old covenant's ceremonies. Most churches have taken this psalm as warrant for using music of every kind. Either way, the psalm's point is wider than any one practice: everything people have, every skill and sound, can be turned to praising God. Boice, as Guzik reports, says the list is not meant to be complete but to show that all we have can be used in worship.
- **"Let everything that has breath praise the LORD!" (v6).** The last call widens to include all living things. "Everything that has breath" (*kol ha-neshamah*) is literally "all breath." The word *neshamah* is the "breath of life" God breathed into Adam (Gen 2:7). Every breath is God's gift, so every breath owes him praise. The psalm began in the sanctuary and the heavens. It ends with every creature that breathes. VanGemeren, as Guzik reports, notes that the word covers all living things, always distinct from their Creator. Instruments fall silent, but breath goes on. JFB pictures it: "Living voices shall take up the failing sounds of dead instruments," and the praise of redeemed spirits and angels will continue forever.
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v6).** The final *Hallelujah*, the last word of the book of Psalms. After 150 psalms, many of them filled with tears, fear, confession and complaint, the last word is praise. That is not because the troubles did not matter. It is because God is greater than all of them.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Since we must shortly breathe our last, while we have breath let us praise the Lord." He lists the forms praise takes in a life, among them strong faith, holy love, "entire confidence in Christ," obedience and cheerful submission. He closes his commentary on the Psalms by calling it a book "which has supplied the songs of the church for more than three thousand years." JFB ends with Revelation's song: "Alleluia! for the Lord God omnipotent reigneth" (Rev 19:6). Guzik passes on Meyer's observation that the Psalter opens with "Blessed" and closes with "Hallelujah," so a life with many ups and downs can still end in praise. He also cites Morgan: the only requirement for praising God is having breath.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Psalter ends with every breath praising the LORD, and the Bible ends the same way. In Revelation, John hears "every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea" praising God and the Lamb (Rev 5:13), and a great multitude crying "Hallelujah!" because "the Lord our God the Almighty reigns" (Rev 19:6). Jesus himself sang the psalms. On the night he was betrayed, he and his disciples "sung a hymn" before going out to the Mount of Olives (Matt 26:30), most likely the Hallel psalms sung at Passover. He prayed Psalm 22 on the cross and committed his spirit to the Father in the words of Psalm 31. The one who breathed his last for his people rose again and breathed on his disciples, giving them the Holy Spirit (John 20:22). By that breath, people from every nation now join the song. Every lament in the Psalter finds its answer in him, and that is why the last word is praise.
