*Setting:* Isaac has just died, and his two sons have buried him together at Hebron (35:29). Before Genesis follows Jacob's family into the long story of Joseph, it does what it always does with a son who is not the line of promise: it gives his family tree and then lets him step off the stage. Ishmael's line was listed after Abraham's death (25:12–18). Now Esau's line is listed after Isaac's. The chapter is mostly names: Esau's wives and sons, the chiefs descended from them, the older Horite clans of the land they moved into, a list of early kings, and a closing list of chiefs. It reads like a set of family and tribal records gathered into one place. It is not padding. Edom will be Israel's neighbor, rival and enemy for a thousand years, and this chapter shows where that nation came from and how fully God kept his word to Esau too.

#### Esau's Descendants (vv1–43)
- **"These are the generations of Esau (that is, Edom)" (v1).** "Generations" (*toledot*) is the word that marks each new section of Genesis (2:4; 5:1; 6:9; 11:27; 25:12, 19). It appears twice here (vv1, 9), which suggests two records joined together: Esau's family in Canaan, then his people in Seir. "Edom" means "red." It recalls Esau's ruddy color at birth (25:25) and the "red stuff" he traded his birthright for (25:30). The name sticks to his nation. The chapter repeats it like a refrain: "Esau is Edom" (vv1, 8, 19, 43).
- **"Esau took his wives from the Canaanites" (v2).** This is the point the earlier chapters made with pain: Esau's Hittite wives "made life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah" (26:35), and it was one reason Jacob was sent to Paddan-aram for a wife (27:46–28:2). Esau then added a daughter of Ishmael, as if to please his parents after the fact (28:8–9).
- **The wives' names don't match the earlier lists (vv2–3).** In 26:34 and 28:9 the wives are Judith daughter of Beeri, Basemath daughter of Elon, and Mahalath daughter of Ishmael. Here they are Adah daughter of Elon, Oholibamah daughter of Anah, and Basemath daughter of Ishmael. The Tyndale notes list the options: some wives may have died and others been married later, Esau may have had more wives than three, or women may have had two names, which was common. The text does not give enough to decide. The ancient Samaritan version of the Pentateuch smooths it over by reading "Mahalath" here in place of Basemath, which shows that scribes noticed the problem long ago. Also in v2, "Anah the daughter of Zibeon" may be a copying slip for "son," since v24 makes Anah Zibeon's son. The Greek and Samaritan texts read "son."
- **"Born to him in the land of Canaan" (v5).** The Tyndale notes point out a quiet contrast. Esau's sons were born in Canaan and then left it. Jacob's sons, except Benjamin, were born outside Canaan and came into it. The land was meant for Jacob, and God made room for him.
- **"He went into a land away from his brother Jacob" (vv6–8).** The wording echoes Abram and Lot: "the land could not support both of them dwelling together" because "their possessions were so great" (13:6). As with Lot, the one who leaves chooses the visible goods, and the one who stays holds the promise. Esau was already living in Seir when Jacob came home (32:3; 33:14–16), so this passage probably sums up his final, complete move. **"The hill country of Seir"** is the rugged red-sandstone range south and southeast of the Dead Sea, in today's southern Jordan. God gave it to Esau as his own: "I have given Mount Seir to Esau as a possession," and Israel was forbidden to take any of it (Deut 2:5).
- **Esau's sons and grandsons (vv9–14).** Several names here meet us again in the Bible. **Teman** (v11) became a region of Edom famous for its wise men (Jer 49:7). Job's friend "Eliphaz the Temanite" (Job 2:11) bears both the region's name and the name of Esau's son, which suggests Job's story is set among Edomite clans. **Kenaz** (v11) gave its name to the Kenizzites. Caleb, the faithful spy, was "the son of Jephunneh the Kenizzite" (Num 32:12), and many think his family was an Edomite clan absorbed into Judah, though the link is not certain.
- **"Timna… bore Amalek to Eliphaz" (v12).** This short parenthesis carries a lot of weight. Amalek became the father of the Amalekites, the first nation to attack Israel after the exodus (Exod 17:8–16). Moses told Israel to "remember what Amalek did to you" (Deut 25:17–19). Saul lost his kingdom partly for sparing Agag, Amalek's king (1 Sam 15), and in Esther the villain Haman is "the Agagite" (Esth 3:1). Timna was a Horite, "Lotan's sister" (v22), so Amalek came from the mixing of Esau's line with the people of Seir.
- **"The chiefs of the sons of Esau" (vv15–19).** "Chief" is *alluph*. The lexicon connects it with *eleph*, "thousand" or "clan," so the word means the head of a clan. It is used almost only of Edom's leaders, and Moses' song remembers "the chiefs of Edom" trembling when Israel crossed the sea (Exod 15:15). The older English "dukes" sounds like European nobility. These were tribal sheikhs. Korah appears twice, among Eliphaz's chiefs (v16) and Oholibamah's (v18). The Samaritan text lacks the first, so it may be a copying error, or two men may have had the name. He is not the Levite Korah of Numbers 16.
- **"The sons of Seir the Horite, the inhabitants of the land" (vv20–30).** Before Esau came, Seir belonged to the Horites. Deuteronomy says Esau's descendants "dispossessed them and destroyed them… and settled in their place, as Israel did to the land of their possession" (Deut 2:12, 22). But this list and the marriages in it (vv2, 12, 25) show that much of the Horite population was also absorbed by marriage. Scholars debate whether these Horites are the Hurrians known from ancient records of northern Mesopotamia or simply a local people. The name may mean "cave dwellers," which would suit the rock country of Seir.
- **"The Anah who found the hot springs in the wilderness" (v24).** The Hebrew word, *yemim*, appears only here, and nobody is quite sure what it means. Jerome's Latin version read "hot waters," and the ESV follows that. The King James Version, following some Jewish interpretation, has "mules," as if Anah discovered mule breeding while grazing his father's donkeys. "Hot springs" fits the region, which has well-known hot springs east of the Dead Sea. It is a small detail of family memory, the kind of thing a clan would tell about its ancestor.
- **"Uz and Aran" (v28).** Uz is the land of Job (Job 1:1), and Lamentations links Uz with Edom: "Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom, you who dwell in the land of Uz" (Lam 4:21).
- **"The kings who reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the Israelites" (v31).** This line is striking. It reads naturally as a note from someone writing after Israel had kings, a later editor updating the record, as with "to this day" in 35:20. Others read it as Moses writing with the promise in view, since God had just said "kings shall come from your own body" (35:11). Either way the point stands: Edom had kings first. Jacob was promised that "the older shall serve the younger" (25:23), yet Esau's line reached royal power long before Jacob's did. Henry's comment fits: "the children of the covenant are often cast behind" in outward success, but God's promises, "though they are slow, are sure and lasting."
- **Eight kings and no dynasty (vv32–39).** None of these kings is the son of the one before, and each comes from a different town. Kingship in early Edom seems to have passed by choice, strength or conquest rather than by birth. **Bela the son of Beor** (v32) shares his father's name with Balaam son of Beor (Num 22:5), and some ancient interpreters linked them, though most think it a coincidence. **Jobab** (v33) was identified with Job by an ancient addition to the Greek book of Job, which most scholars doubt. **Bozrah** (v33) became Edom's great fortress city, and Isaiah pictures the divine warrior coming from there: "Who is this who comes from Edom, in crimsoned garments from Bozrah?" (Isa 63:1). **Hadad… who defeated Midian in the country of Moab** (v35) is the only king given an achievement. **Baal-hanan**, "Baal is gracious" (v38), shows whom Edom worshiped. The list stops with **Hadar** (v39), the only king whose wife is named, as if the record ends in the writer's own time.
- **"The chiefs of Esau… according to their dwelling places" (vv40–43).** The final list may name clans by their territory or main town rather than as individuals. **"Esau, the father of Edom"** closes the chapter. The man who once said, "I am about to die; of what use is a birthright to me?" (25:32) became a nation with chiefs and kings.
- **Edom in the rest of the Bible.** Israel was told, "You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deut 23:7). But Edom refused Israel passage on the way to Canaan (Num 20:14–21), was conquered by David (2 Sam 8:14), broke free under Judah's king Joram (2 Kgs 8:20–22), and cheered when Jerusalem fell to Babylon (Ps 137:7). The short book of Obadiah is entirely about Edom's pride and judgment. By New Testament times Edomites lived in southern Judea, called Idumea, and Herod the Great, who tried to kill the infant Jesus, came from an Idumean family. Yet Mark records that people "from Idumea" came to hear Jesus (Mark 3:8).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Moses keeps a fuller record of the chosen family than of Esau's, which gets only names, and draws from v31 that "God's time is the best time." Guzik argues that Esau's worry, "Have you but one blessing, my father?" (27:38), proved unfounded: God blessed Esau richly in material things, though he was not chosen to carry the line of redemption (Mal 1:2–3; Rom 9:13). Guzik also observes that some names, like Baal-hanan, show the family's drift from the true God. JFB describes the chiefs as being like sheikhs or Highland clan chieftains, not British peers, and says Esau's wealth "was the occasion of his leaving Canaan," which made way for Jacob. On v24 JFB notes that several ancient versions read "water springs" rather than "mules." On whether Eliphaz or Jobab is Job's Eliphaz or Job himself, Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's judgment that there is no real proof. JFB has no notes on vv4–5 or vv9–14.

#### Pointing to Christ
Esau got what he wanted: wealth, land, chiefs and kings, and he got it quickly. Jacob's family got a promise and a long wait, and the next chapter opens with his sons selling their brother into slavery. Yet the line of promise runs through Jacob, because God's choice rests on grace, not on who looks strongest (Rom 9:10–13). Edom's story ends in the prophets with judgment, but even there the picture turns toward a Savior: Isaiah's warrior comes from Bozrah in garments red with judgment, mighty to save (Isa 63:1). Hebrews warns believers not to be like Esau, who sold his birthright "for a single meal" (Heb 12:16). And while an Idumean, Herod, sat on the throne in Jerusalem, the true King was born in Bethlehem, and crowds from Idumea itself later walked out to hear him.
