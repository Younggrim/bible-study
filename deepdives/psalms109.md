*Setting:* Psalm 109 is titled "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David." It was meant for public worship, which is surprising, because it contains the longest and fiercest curse in the Psalms (vv6–19). The occasion is not named. David is being slandered and accused by people he had loved and prayed for, and the charges seem to threaten his life (v31). Possible settings include Saul's persecution, the betrayal of a trusted friend such as Ahithophel during Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 15–17; Ps 41:9), or the cursing of Shimei (2 Sam 16:5–8), but the text does not say. The New Testament gives this psalm a startling second life. Peter quotes v8 in Acts 1:20 about Judas: "Let another take his office." Psalm 109 is one of the "imprecatory" psalms, prayers that call down God's judgment on enemies (compare Pss 35, 69, 137). Christians have long wrestled with how such prayers fit alongside Jesus' command to love enemies, and the main answers are set out below. The psalm is built in five parts: a complaint about the accusers, the long curse, a plea for help, a prayer for vindication, and a closing vow of praise. Henry calls it a complaint lodged "in the court of heaven." The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Be Not Silent, O God of My Praise! (vv1–5)
- **"Be not silent, O God of my praise!" (v1).** David's enemies are talking. God, it seems, is not. "O God of my praise" means the God whom David praises. He has praised God before, and he trusts he will again. The psalm begins and ends with praise (v30).
- **"Wicked and deceitful mouths are opened against me, speaking against me with lying tongues" (v2).** The weapon is words: lies, slander, false testimony. In Israel a false witness could bring a death sentence (Deut 19:16–19; 1 Kgs 21:10–13). At Jesus' trial "many bore false witness against him" (Mark 14:56).
- **"They encircle me with words of hate, and attack me without cause" (v3).** "Without cause" (*chinnam*) means freely, for nothing. David has done nothing to deserve this. Jesus quoted the same phrase from another psalm: "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25, quoting Ps 35:19 or 69:4).
- **"In return for my love they accuse me" (v4).** "Accuse" (*satan*, as a verb) means to act as an adversary or prosecutor. The noun *satan* returns in v6, "accuser," and the verb again in vv20 and 29. The word is where the name Satan comes from, "the accuser of our brothers" (Rev 12:10; Zech 3:1). These men take the role of a prosecutor against someone who loved them.
- **"But I give myself to prayer" (v4).** The Hebrew is just three words: "but I, prayer." It is as if David were nothing but prayer. He does not answer slander with slander. He takes it to God.
- **"So they reward me evil for good, and hatred for my love" (v5).** Repaying evil for good is the deepest kind of betrayal (Prov 17:13). Psalm 35:12–14 describes David fasting and mourning for these same kinds of people when they were sick.
- **Commentators:** Henry says David "lodges a complaint in the court of heaven of the malice and base ingratitude of his enemies." JFB reads v1 as "recognizing God as a certain helper" and v4 as prayer being David's comfort in distress. Guzik notes the link between the Hebrew word for "accuse" and the name Satan, and passes on Boice's point that the Hebrew of v4 reads abruptly, "I prayer," as if prayer were all David was. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says the devil has no worse weapons than deceitful tongues.

#### Appoint a Wicked Man Against Him (vv6–15)
- **A sudden shift (v6).** In vv1–5 the enemies are plural: "they." From v6 to v19 the target is singular: "him." This has led to two main readings, and Bible translations differ.
- **View one: David's own prayer.** Most commentators through history, including Henry, JFB and Guzik, take vv6–19 as David's own words against a leading enemy, perhaps the ringleader. The singular fits a single chief accuser, and Peter's use of v8 for Judas (Acts 1:20) fits a guilty man, not an innocent one.
- **View two: the enemies' curse quoted.** Some modern scholars and translations (the NLT, for example, adds "They say") read vv6–19 as the curse David's enemies are hurling at *him*, which he quotes to God. That would explain the change from plural to singular, and v20 would then mean "may this be what my accusers get" (compare v17, "He loved to curse"). Morgan held this view. The Tyndale notes observe that the Hebrew has no "they say," so the speaker is unclear.
- **"Appoint a wicked man against him; let an accuser stand at his right hand" (v6).** The scene is a courtroom. In Israel the accuser stood at the defendant's right hand (Zech 3:1, where Satan stands at the right hand of Joshua the high priest "to accuse him"). The prayer asks that the slanderer be tried by someone as unjust as himself and face a prosecutor like the one he has been. "Accuser" here is *satan*, and the KJV translates "let Satan stand at his right hand."
- **"When he is tried, let him come forth guilty; let his prayer be counted as sin!" (v7).** "His prayer" may be his plea to the judge for mercy. Let even his plea count against him. JFB: "The condemnation is aggravated when prayer for relief is treated as a sin." Compare Proverbs 28:9: "If one turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination."
- **"May his days be few; may another take his office!" (v8).** "Office" (*pequddah*) means oversight, a position of responsibility. The man was apparently a leader. The Greek translation renders it *episkopē*, "overseership," the word from which "bishop" comes (1 Tim 3:1). In Acts 1:20, before the believers chose Matthias to replace Judas, Peter quoted this line together with Psalm 69:25. The early church saw in Psalm 109 a pattern fulfilled in the friend who betrayed the Son of David.
- **"May his children be fatherless and his wife a widow!" (v9).** The curse spreads to the family. In the ancient world a family was seen as one unit, sharing honor and shame. Achan's household died with him (Josh 7:24–25), and Naboth's property was seized after his execution (1 Kgs 21:13–16). The law itself forbade punishing children for their father's crime by human courts (Deut 24:16), so this is a prayer for God to act, not a request for anyone to harm them.
- **"May his children wander about and beg" (v10).** The family is driven from their ruined home. Psalm 37:25 says the righteous' children are not seen begging, so this is a picture of a household under God's curse.
- **"May the creditor seize all that he has; may strangers plunder the fruits of his toil!" (v11).** The man who perhaps took others' goods (v16) loses his own. "Seize" is the image of a creditor laying a snare.
- **"Let there be none to extend kindness to him" (v12).** "Kindness" is *chesed*, the loyal love the man himself refused to show (v16). The punishment matches the crime.
- **"May his posterity be cut off; may his name be blotted out in the second generation!" (v13).** For an Israelite, having no descendants to carry on the family name was a kind of second death (Deut 25:6; Prov 10:7). JFB notes the word for "posterity" can also mean "end."
- **"May the iniquity of his fathers be remembered before the LORD" (v14).** This draws on Exodus 20:5, where God visits "the iniquity of the fathers on the children… of those who hate me." The point is that this man continues his family's sin. Jesus said something similar to the religious leaders who plotted his death: "fill up, then, the measure of your fathers" (Matt 23:32–36).
- **"That he may cut off the memory of them from the earth!" (v15).** The family line and its memory vanish.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the curses "the expressions, not of passion, but of the Spirit of prophecy," and notes "that imprecation (Ps. 109:8) is applied to Judas." He says David "had an eye to Christ, his sufferings and his persecutors." JFB remarks that "this Psalm is remarkable for the number and severity of its imprecations," but says they are aimed at impenitent enemies of God and his people, and he notes that the Greek word and Peter's use both mean "oversight." Guzik weighs the quotation view, which Morgan held, and comes down with Kidner on David's own words: real, righteous anger, recorded for our learning, not for imitation. He adds Boice's point that David twice refused to kill Saul when he could have (1 Sam 24; 26), so these are not the words of a vindictive man.

#### He Loved to Curse (vv16–20)
- **"For he did not remember to show kindness, but pursued the poor and needy and the brokenhearted, to put them to death" (v16).** Now comes the reason. The man refused *chesed* (kindness) and hunted down the vulnerable. "The brokenhearted" are those God is closest to (Ps 34:18; 51:17). The man is not merely David's personal enemy. He is an enemy of the poor and of God's justice. Poole, cited by Guzik, thinks the "poor and needy" man is David himself (compare v22).
- **"He loved to curse; let curses come upon him! He did not delight in blessing; may it be far from him!" (v17).** This is justice measure for measure. He chose cursing, so let him have it. He refused blessing, so let it stay away. God's law often works this way: people reap what they sow (Gal 6:7; Prov 26:27).
- **"He clothed himself with cursing as his coat" (v18).** Cursing was not an occasional outburst but his whole outfit, his way of life. **"May it soak into his body like water, like oil into his bones!"** JFB compares the bitter water a woman suspected of adultery had to drink, which would "go into" her body if she were guilty (Num 5:22–27).
- **"May it be like a garment that he wraps around him, like a belt that he puts on every day!" (v19).** He put on cursing, so let it cling to him. The clothing picture returns in v29.
- **"May this be the reward of my accusers from the LORD" (v20).** The plural "accusers" returns. "From the LORD" is the key phrase. David does not take revenge himself. He hands the case to God, which is exactly what Paul later urges: "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Rom 12:19, quoting Deut 32:35).
- **How should believers read these curses?** Christians have answered in several ways, and they are not exclusive. (1) They are prophetic, announcing God's judgment on those who finally reject him, as Henry says, and Acts 1:20 treats them that way. (2) They are honest prayers of a wronged person who hands vengeance to God instead of taking it, which is itself a kind of restraint. (3) They express God's own hatred of evil and concern for the poor and broken (v16), which believers rightly share. (4) They belong to a stage before the full revelation of Christ, who taught "pray for those who persecute you" (Matt 5:44) and prayed for his killers (Luke 23:34). Most readers combine several of these. None of them makes the psalm a model for cursing personal enemies.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v20 as God's judicial "wages" for the adversaries, a legal act rather than private revenge, and connects vv17–19 to the ordeal of bitter water. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, calls this measure-for-measure justice. Guzik notes that David leaves the reward to the LORD, and the Tyndale notes see v20 as an appeal to the principle of retribution (Ps 94:23).

#### But You, O GOD My Lord (vv21–25)
- **"But you, O GOD my Lord, deal on my behalf for your name's sake" (v21).** The tone changes completely. "GOD" in small capitals stands for *Yahweh*, and "my Lord" is *Adonai*. David does not ask for help because he deserves it but "for your name's sake," for the sake of God's own reputation, and "because your steadfast love is good." His appeal rests on God's character, not his own record.
- **"For I am poor and needy, and my heart is stricken within me" (v22).** David, a king, calls himself poor and needy. These are the very people the enemy persecuted (v16). His heart is "stricken," wounded, pierced.
- **"I am gone like a shadow at evening; I am shaken off like a locust" (v23).** An evening shadow lengthens and then vanishes (Ps 102:11). A locust on one's clothing is flicked away with a shake. He feels that insubstantial, that easily disposed of.
- **"My knees are weak through fasting; my body has become gaunt, with no fat" (v24).** Fasting was a sign of grief and urgent prayer (Ps 35:13; 69:10). He has fasted until his body is wasting away.
- **"I am an object of scorn to my accusers; when they see me, they wag their heads" (v25).** Wagging the head was a gesture of mockery (Ps 22:7; Lam 2:15). At the cross, "those who passed by derided him, wagging their heads" (Matt 27:39), fulfilling exactly this picture.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes this section as a prayer "that God would help and succour him in his low condition." JFB notes the pleas rest on God's name and mercy, not David's merit. Guzik draws the same point: David asks for God's name's sake and because his mercy is good. The Tyndale notes say the accusations have crushed both his spirit and his physical strength.

#### Let Them Curse, but You Will Bless! (vv26–31)
- **"Help me, O LORD my God! Save me according to your steadfast love!" (v26).** The appeal is again to *chesed*, God's steadfast love, the very thing the enemy refused to show (v16).
- **"Let them know that this is your hand; you, O LORD, have done it!" (v27).** David wants his rescue to be so clearly God's work that even his enemies have to admit it. He does not want to be vindicated by his own cleverness but by God.
- **"Let them curse, but you will bless!" (v28).** This may be the key line of the whole psalm. Curses from people cannot stand against blessing from God. Balaam was hired to curse Israel and could only bless (Num 23:8; Neh 13:2). **"They arise and are put to shame, but your servant will be glad!"**
- **"May my accusers be clothed with dishonor; may they be wrapped in their own shame as in a cloak!" (v29).** The clothing image from vv18–19 returns. They dressed in cursing, so let them wear their own shame.
- **"With my mouth I will give great thanks to the LORD; I will praise him in the midst of the throng" (v30).** The mouths of the wicked were opened against David (v2). Now his own mouth opens in thanksgiving, in public, before the crowd at worship.
- **"For he stands at the right hand of the needy one, to save him from those who condemn his soul to death" (v31).** The last verse answers v6. There an accuser stood at the wicked man's right hand to condemn him. Here God himself stands at the needy one's right hand, as his defender in court. Whoever has God at his right hand cannot finally be condemned.
- **Commentators:** Henry says David "concludes with a joyful expectation that God would appear for him." JFB says on v28 that David no longer cares about their curses, since God's blessing outweighs them. Guzik, citing Kidner, calls v31's picture of God at the needy man's right hand "the complete answer" to the accuser of v6, and passes on Maclaren's observation that the psalm opens with God as David's praise and closes with God as his defender.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 109 is the cry of a man who loved and prayed for people who repaid him with lies and hatred "without cause." Jesus lived this fully. He was betrayed by a friend he had loved, surrounded by false witnesses, mocked by people wagging their heads, and condemned to death. Peter saw Judas in v8. But where David prayed for judgment on his accusers, Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34), and took the curse on himself: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). The psalm's final picture is the gospel's: the accuser stands against God's people (Rev 12:10), but Christ stands at their right hand. "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect?… Christ Jesus is the one who died… who is at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us" (Rom 8:33–34).
