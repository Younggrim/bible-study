*Setting:* "A Psalm of Asaph." Psalm 82 is short, dramatic and one of the most debated psalms. It opens in a courtroom. God stands up "in the divine council… in the midst of the gods" and puts the judges themselves on trial for failing the weak. The psalm gives no date or event. It belongs to the group of Asaph psalms (73–83) that wrestle with injustice and God's judgment, and it follows Psalm 81's call to listen to God with a picture of what happens when those who rule do not. The great question is who the "gods" are. There are three main views, and the notes below give each. Whatever the answer, the psalm's message is plain: those entrusted with authority answer to God, and their failure to protect the weak shakes the foundations of the world. Jesus quoted v6 in an argument with his opponents (John 10:34–36), which makes this psalm important for understanding his claim to be the Son of God.

#### God Holds Judgment (vv1–4)
- **"God has taken his place in the divine council" (v1).** "Has taken his place" means he stands up, as a judge rises to give a verdict or an accuser to bring a charge (Isa 3:13, "The LORD has taken his place to contend; he stands to judge peoples"). "Divine council" translates *'adat-'el*, literally "the assembly of God" (or "of El"). Other passages show God in the midst of a heavenly court: the "sons of God" presenting themselves before him (Job 1:6), "the host of heaven standing beside him" (1 Kgs 22:19), "the council of the holy ones" (89:5–7).
- **"In the midst of the gods he holds judgment" (v1).** "Gods" is *'elohim*, the ordinary word for God or gods. It can also be used of beings who represent God or act with divine authority. Who is meant here? The three main views:
- **View 1: Human judges and rulers.** This is the view of most older Jewish and Christian interpreters, including Henry, JFB and Guzik. *'Elohim* is used in the law for the judges before whom cases were brought: "his master shall bring him to God" (Exod 21:6; 22:8–9, where many translations have "judges"). Judges stood in God's place, deciding life and death, and Moses told Israel's judges, "the judgment is God's" (Deut 1:17; 2 Chr 19:6). The charges in vv2–4 (partiality, neglect of orphans and the poor) are what Old Testament prophets charge human judges with. JFB: "or, 'judges'… God's representatives."
- **View 2: Spiritual beings set over the nations.** Many modern scholars read the "gods" as heavenly beings, the "sons of God" whom, according to one reading of Deuteronomy 32:8 (found in the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek Old Testament), God set over the nations when he divided them. These powers ruled their nations unjustly and are now sentenced to die. Daniel shows such spiritual "princes" of Persia and Greece (Dan 10:13, 20), and Paul speaks of "rulers… authorities… cosmic powers" (Eph 6:12). This view fits the language of a heavenly council and the verdict "you shall die like men" (v7), which would be shocking only for beings who are not men.
- **View 3: Israel at Sinai.** Some ancient rabbis read "you are gods" as spoken to Israel when they received the law, raised to near-immortality, and then condemned to die like Adam after the golden calf. Few follow this today, though it is relevant to how Jesus' hearers may have understood the verse.
- **"How long will you judge unjustly and show partiality to the wicked?" (v2).** "Show partiality" is literally "lift up the faces of," a Hebrew idiom for favoring someone, especially by taking a bribe (Lev 19:15; Deut 10:17). God does not lift faces. His judges should not either. JFB adds that the phrase is "literally, 'lift up the faces,' that is, from dejection," of the wicked. **Selah.**
- **"Give justice to the weak and the fatherless; maintain the right of the afflicted and the destitute" (v3).** The weak, the orphan, the afflicted and the poor were the people with no one to stand up for them. God calls himself "father of the fatherless and protector of widows" (68:5), and he expects his representatives to act the same way (Deut 10:18; Isa 1:17, 23; Jer 22:3).
- **"Rescue the weak and the needy; deliver them from the hand of the wicked" (v4).** Justice is not only fair rulings but active rescue. A judge who stays neutral while the strong crush the weak has already sided with the wicked.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the psalm as teaching "the dignity of magistracy and its dependence upon God," and "the duty of magistrates." He says it was written "to instruct the judges of the earth." Guzik weighs four possible identities for the "gods" (the true God, false gods, angels, human judges) and concludes human judges is best, pointing to Exodus 21–22. JFB on vv3–4: "So must good judges act."

#### All the Foundations Are Shaken (v5)
- **"They have neither knowledge nor understanding, they walk about in darkness" (v5).** Who is speaking, and about whom? It may be God describing the judges, or the psalmist commenting on the scene. "They" may be the corrupt judges, who do not know God's ways, or the oppressed, who cannot see a way out. Most take it of the judges: they wander blind, willfully ignorant of right (Isa 59:9–10; John 3:19–20).
- **"All the foundations of the earth are shaken" (v5).** When those who should uphold justice abandon it, the whole moral order of the world totters. Psalm 11:3 asks, "if the foundations are destroyed, what can the righteous do?" Justice is not a minor social matter. It holds up the world (75:3; 89:14, "Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne"). The Tyndale notes say the oppressors are unmoved even as the world order falls apart around them.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "By the wilful ignorance and negligence of judges, anarchy ensues." Guzik, citing Boice, traces a chain from ignorance, to blind conduct, to a shaken society.

#### You Shall Die Like Men (vv6–7)
- **"I said, 'You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you'" (v6).** God himself gave them this title and this rank. Whether judges or heavenly powers, they held their position by his appointment. Henry: "They have been honoured with the name and title of gods."
- **"Nevertheless, like men you shall die, and fall like any prince" (v7).** "Like men" is *ke'adam*, which can also mean "like Adam." Their high title will not save them. Death is the great leveler. "Fall like any prince" may mean they will fall like ordinary human rulers, or, on the heavenly-beings view, like one of the fallen "princes" (Isa 14:12–15). Henry: they "shall die like men, and all their honour shall be laid in the dust." JFB: "Though God admitted their official dignity (Joh 10:34), He reminds them of their mortality."
- **How Jesus used v6 (John 10:34–36).** When Jesus said, "I and the Father are one," his opponents picked up stones, saying, "you, being a man, make yourself God." Jesus answered: "Is it not written in your Law, 'I said, you are gods'? If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be broken—do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, 'You are blaspheming,' because I said, 'I am the Son of God'?" His argument moves from lesser to greater. If Scripture could call those who received God's word "gods," then it cannot be blasphemy for the one whom the Father set apart and sent to call himself God's Son. Jesus is not saying he is a god only in the way those judges were. He is showing his opponents that their charge does not hold even on their own Scripture's terms, and then pointing to his works as proof of who he really is (John 10:37–38). "To whom the word of God came" fits the human-judges view well (judges received God's law to administer it) and also the Sinai view. Note that Jesus calls the Psalms "your Law," since "Law" could mean all of Scripture.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Maclaren, takes Jesus' use of the verse as strong support for the human-judges reading. Henry says the rulers' titles were real but did not exempt them from judgment, and their high place only made their fall more marked.

#### Arise, O God, Judge the Earth (v8)
- **"Arise, O God, judge the earth" (v8).** The psalmist turns from the courtroom to prayer. If the appointed judges have failed, the only hope is that the Judge of all the earth will act himself (Gen 18:25). "Arise" was Israel's battle cry when the ark set out (Num 10:35; 68:1). Henry: "Arise, O God! judge the earth, appear against those that judge unjustly."
- **"For you shall inherit all the nations!" (v8).** All nations belong to God (24:1; 2:8). On the heavenly-beings reading, this reverses Deuteronomy 32:8–9: God had allotted the nations to lesser powers and kept Israel as his own portion, but now he will take back every nation as his inheritance. The Tyndale notes read it this way. On any reading, the psalm ends with the hope that God's just rule will one day reach the whole world. Henry: "Thou, O Christ! shalt inherit all nations, and be the governor over them."
- **Commentators:** JFB: "As rightful sovereign of earth, God is invoked personally to correct the evils of His representatives." Guzik reads the final line as pointing to Jesus Christ, who will rule the nations in righteousness. Henry calls v8 "the desire and prayer of all good people that the kingdom of God may be set up more and more."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 82 ends with a plea for God himself to rise up and judge the earth, because those he appointed have failed the weak. Jesus is God's answer. He used this psalm to show that his claim to be God's Son is not blasphemy, and he backed it up with works that only the Father's Son could do (John 10:36–38). He is the judge who does not lift up faces, who rescues the weak and the needy, and to whom the Father has given "all judgment" (John 5:22). On the cross he "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame" (Col 2:15), and after his resurrection he said, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" (Matt 28:18–19). The nations God promised to inherit are being gathered to Christ, and at his return he will "judge the world in righteousness" (Acts 17:31).
