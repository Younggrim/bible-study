*Setting:* The feast in Joseph's house is over (43:31–34). The brothers have brought Benjamin, Simeon has been released, the returned silver has been explained, and the governor has sent them away fed and honored. They have even watched him give Benjamin five times as much food as anyone else without a flicker of the old jealousy. But Joseph still has one question he cannot answer from the outside. More than twenty years ago these men got rid of their father's favorite son, Rachel's son, and came home without him. If they are handed an excuse to do it again with Rachel's other son, will they? This chapter is the last and hardest test, and it ends with the speech that changes everything.

#### Joseph Tests His Brothers (vv1–17)
- **"Fill the men's sacks… put my cup, the silver cup, in the mouth of the sack of the youngest" (vv1–2).** Once again each man's silver goes back into his sack, and this time Joseph's personal cup goes into Benjamin's. JFB notes that the word suggests a large goblet, and returning everyone's money makes Benjamin look no more guilty than the rest. The trap is set so that only Benjamin will be caught.
- **"As soon as the morning was light, the men were sent away" (v3).** This time they leave Egypt with everything: grain, silver, Simeon and Benjamin. JFB pictures them setting out in high spirits. They do not get far.
- **"Why have you repaid evil for good?" (v4).** The steward's first words are an accusation the brothers deserve, though not for this. Joseph had done them good, and they had once repaid a brother's good errand (37:14) with a pit and a sale. The charge lands on a guilty conscience.
- **"By this… he practices divination" (v5).** The verb is *naḥash*, to divine or read omens. Ancient Near Eastern diviners poured water, oil or wine into a cup and read the patterns, and the Tyndale notes name these practices. The law later forbids divination outright (Lev 19:26; Deut 18:10). Did Joseph actually use the cup this way? The text never says he did. Every time Joseph knows secret things in Genesis he credits God: "Do not interpretations belong to God?" (40:8; 41:16). Most interpreters think he is playing his Egyptian role, letting the brothers believe this man can see hidden things. That is exactly what will frighten them in v15.
- **"Far be it from your servants to do such a thing!" (vv7–8).** Their defense is reasonable: they brought back silver they could have kept, so why would they steal more? Then they overreach: **"Whichever of your servants is found with it shall die, and we also will be my lord's servants" (v9).** Rash oaths like this run in the family. Jacob once said to Laban, "Anyone with whom you find your gods shall not live," not knowing Rachel had them (31:32). Now Jacob's sons stake a life on a search, not knowing what is in Benjamin's sack.
- **"He who is found with it shall be my servant, and the rest of you shall be innocent" (v10).** The steward quietly softens their terms. No one dies, and only the guilty man stays. This is the heart of the test: the other ten are offered a clean way home.
- **"Beginning with the eldest and ending with the youngest" (vv11–12).** The steward knows exactly where the cup is, but he searches in birth order, the same order as the seating at the feast (43:33). Ten sacks are opened and found clean, and with each one the tension rises, until the last: **"And the cup was found in Benjamin's sack."** The word "found" runs through the chapter (vv8, 9, 10, 12, 16, 17). The cup is found, and in v16 Judah will say that God has "found out" something else.
- **"They tore their clothes" (v13).** Tearing clothes was the sign of grief (37:29, 34). When Jacob tore his clothes over Joseph's bloody robe, his sons stood by dry-eyed. Now they tear theirs. And here is the real answer to the test: **"every man loaded his donkey, and they returned to the city."** They were free to go. Not one of them went home. Twenty years ago they let Rachel's son be carried off to Egypt. Now all of them walk back into Egypt with him.
- **"Judah and his brothers came to Joseph's house" (v14).** The narrator now names Judah as the leader. Joseph "was still there," waiting for them. "They fell before him to the ground." Earlier they bowed politely (43:26, 28). This is collapse, men flat on the ground.
- **"Do you not know that a man like me can indeed practice divination?" (v15).** Joseph keeps up the role: you cannot hide anything from a man like me. It is truer than they know. He does know what they have done, though not by any cup.
- **"God has found out the guilt of your servants" (v16).** This is the confession the whole story has been waiting for. On this charge they are innocent, and Judah surely suspects as much. But he does not argue. "Guilt" is *ʿawon*, iniquity and the guilt it carries. He is not talking about the cup. He is talking about the brother they sold, the sin they hid from their father for twenty years. They first felt it in 42:21 ("we are guilty concerning our brother"). Now Judah says it before the man himself: God has caught up with us. He also refuses to separate Benjamin from the rest: "we are my lord's servants, both we and he also."
- **"Go up in peace to your father" (v17).** Joseph refuses: only the one with the cup will be a slave. The rest may go home "in peace," *shalom*. It is the exact situation of chapter 37 offered to them again: go back to your father without Rachel's son, and give him whatever explanation you like. The cruelty of the offer is the point. What will they do with it?
- **Commentators:** All three see Joseph testing his brothers, not taking revenge. Guzik lists the signs of changed hearts: no envy at Benjamin's portion, no accusing one another, staying together, confessing their guilt, and refusing to abandon Benjamin. He adds that time does not wash away guilt. Twenty years later their sin was still on their consciences, and only Christ's blood takes it away. On the divining cup, Guzik thinks it possible but unlikely that Joseph used it, and JFB agrees: "It is not likely that Joseph, a pious believer in the true God," practiced it, but he may have used the popular belief for his plan. Henry says that if Benjamin had really stolen it, it would have been "the basest piece of dishonesty and ingratitude that could be," and he finds God's purpose in the trial: "Our God thus humbles those whom he loves and loads with benefits." JFB says the steward's charge "must have come upon them like a thunderbolt."

#### Judah Intercedes for Benjamin (vv18–34)
- **"Judah went up to him" (v18).** The verb means to come near, approaching someone with a request. Here it is a man stepping toward the most powerful official in the land, "like Pharaoh himself," with his life in his hands. It is one of the longest speeches in Genesis, and it is entirely about other people.
- **Judah tells the story again (vv19–23).** He retells the first journey, carefully and respectfully, and adds something the earlier account did not record: the brothers said, "The boy cannot leave his father, for if he should leave his father, his father would die" (v22). JFB observes how the speech starts in short, broken sentences and grows freer as Judah warms to his plea.
- **"His brother is dead" (v20).** Judah does not know he is speaking to that brother. He says it as the family has told it for twenty years. In v28 he is more careful, quoting his father: "Surely he has been torn to pieces," and "I have never seen him since." Guzik notes that Judah avoids claiming outright that Joseph is dead. He says only what his father believes and what he himself has seen.
- **"His father loves him" (v20).** This is a striking line from Judah. The brothers once hated Joseph because "their father loved him more than all his brothers" (37:4). Now Judah states the same favoritism toward Benjamin plainly, with no bitterness, as a reason to protect the boy, not to resent him.
- **"You know that my wife bore me two sons" (v27).** This is the most painful sentence in the speech. Jacob had four wives and twelve sons, but in his grief he speaks as if only Rachel counted as his wife and only her two boys as his sons. Judah is a son of Leah, the unloved wife (29:31). He has every reason to resent that sentence. Instead he quotes it to the Egyptian governor without complaint, to explain why his father must not lose Benjamin. Judah has accepted that his father loves another more than him, and he loves his father anyway.
- **"You will bring down my gray hairs in evil to Sheol" (v29).** *Sheol* is the Old Testament's word for the realm of the dead, the grave. Jacob said the same when he first refused to send Benjamin (42:38). Judah takes it seriously: the old man's "life is bound up in the boy's life" (v30). The Hebrew is literally "his soul is bound to his soul," the same expression used for Jonathan's love for David (1 Sam 18:1). The word "father" comes up more than a dozen times in this speech. Judah, who once handed his father a bloody robe and let him mourn, now cannot bear to see him grieve.
- **"For your servant became a pledge of safety for the boy" (v32).** Judah keeps the promise he made in 43:9, using the same word for standing surety (*ʿarab*). He made himself liable, and now the bill has come due.
- **"Please let your servant remain instead of the boy as a servant to my lord" (v33).** For the first time in the story, a brother offers himself in a brother's place. "Instead of" is *taḥat*, the same word used when Abraham offered the ram "instead of his son" (22:13). The brother who said, "Come, let us sell him" (37:27), now asks to be the slave so that Rachel's son can go free. The man who once traded a brother for silver gives himself for a brother for nothing.
- **"How can I go back to my father if the boy is not with me? I fear to see the evil that would find my father" (v34).** Twenty years ago the brothers did go back to their father without the boy, and they watched the evil that found him (37:31–35). Judah will not do that again. He would rather spend his life as a slave in Egypt. And with that, the test is over. Joseph cannot hold back any longer (45:1).
- **Commentators:** The Tyndale notes call Judah's plea one of the most moving acts of intercession in Scripture, and they connect his self-giving to John 15:13 and to the kingship promised to Judah in 49:10. Guzik compares Judah's offer to Moses asking to be blotted out of God's book for Israel's sake (Exod 32:31–32) and to Paul wishing himself cut off for his people (Rom 9:1–3), and he lists Judah's concern for Jacob as clear evidence of a changed heart. Matthew Henry notes the irony that the plea was not needed: "Neither Jacob nor Benjamin needed an intercessor with Joseph; for he himself loved them." He also sees in Judah's care for his old father that "the love that descended first must again ascend." JFB calls the governor's whole conduct "genuine, brotherly kindness," however harsh it looked.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah, of whose tribe Jesus would be born, steps forward as the guarantor for his brother, takes on guilt that is not his, and asks to be bound so that the guilty-looking one can go home free to his father. It is only a shadow. Judah offers himself partly to pay his own old debt, and his offer is never taken. But the shape is unmistakable. The greater Son of Judah did not only offer: he went into slavery and death "instead of" us, so that we who were found with guilt in our hands could go up in peace to the Father. And the one who sat in judgment over the brothers, whose questions dug up their hidden sin, turns out to be the brother who loved them all along.
