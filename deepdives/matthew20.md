*Setting:* Spring of AD 30, the last weeks before Passover. Jesus is traveling south through Perea, east of the Jordan, and will cross the river at Jericho for the final climb to Jerusalem. Chapter 19 ended with Peter asking, "We have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?" (19:27), and with Jesus' warning that "many who are first will be last, and the last first" (19:30). Chapter 20 opens with a parable that explains that saying, and it closes the same way: the kingdom reverses the world's ranking. In between come the third and fullest prediction of the cross, two brothers asking for the best seats, and Jesus' own statement of why he came.

#### Laborers in the Vineyard (vv1–16)
- **"For the kingdom of heaven is like" (v1).** "For" ties the parable to 19:30. This story appears only in Matthew. It is bracketed by the same saying at both ends, reversed: "first… last" in 19:30 and "last… first" in 20:16.
- **"A master of a house who went out early in the morning to hire laborers" (v1).** Day laborers gathered in the marketplace at dawn hoping to be hired. They were among the poorest people in the land, below even slaves in security, since a slave at least had a master who fed him. A day without work meant a family without bread. Grape harvest needed many hands quickly before the autumn rains.
- **"A denarius a day" (v2).** The denarius was a Roman silver coin, the normal wage for a day's labor, enough to feed a family for a day.
- **The hours (vv3–6).** The day was counted from sunrise, about 6 a.m. The third hour is 9 a.m., the sixth is noon, the ninth is 3 p.m., and the eleventh is 5 p.m., one hour before sunset when work ended. To the 9 a.m. workers the master promises only "whatever is right" (v4). The later groups get no promise at all.
- **"Why do you stand here idle all day?" "Because no one has hired us" (vv6–7).** These are not lazy men. They waited all day for work that never came. The master takes them on with an hour left.
- **"When evening came… pay them their wages" (v8).** The law required paying a laborer before sunset on the day he worked, "for he is poor and counts on it" (Deut 24:14–15; Lev 19:13). **"Beginning with the last, up to the first."** The order of payment is the point of the story. If the first workers had been paid first, they would have gone home content. The master makes sure they see what the last receive.
- **"Each of them received a denarius" (v9).** One hour's work, a full day's pay. To a poor man with a family, it was the difference between eating and going hungry that night.
- **"They thought they would receive more" (v10).** No one had promised them more. They received exactly what they had agreed to. It was the comparison that soured it.
- **"You have made them equal to us" (v12).** That is the complaint: not that they were cheated, but that others were treated as their equals. They "have borne the burden of the day and the scorching heat," which may refer to the hot east wind, the *sirocco*.
- **"Friend, I am doing you no wrong" (v13).** "Friend" (*hetairos*) is a polite address to someone whose name is not used, with a hint of reproach. Matthew uses it as a form of address only three times: here, to the guest without a wedding garment (22:12), and to Judas in Gethsemane (26:50). Each time the person addressed is in the wrong.
- **"Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me? Or do you begrudge my generosity?" (v15).** The second question is literally "Is your eye evil because I am good?" The "evil eye" was a Hebrew idiom for envy or stinginess (Deut 15:9; Prov 28:22; Matt 6:23). The grumblers' eye is jealous because the master's hand is open.
- **"So the last will be first, and the first last" (v16).** The KJV adds, "for many be called, but few chosen." Most early manuscripts lack those words here, and scholars think they were copied in from 22:14, where they are secure.
- **What the parable means.** The parable is not about wages for work. It is about grace, which can never be calculated. Some read the groups of workers as Israel (called first) and the Gentiles (called late), others as lifelong believers and late converts, like the thief on the cross. Both fit, and Jesus may intend both. In its setting, though, it is first a warning to Peter and the disciples, who have just asked what they will get for leaving everything. They will be rewarded generously (19:28–29). But if they start comparing themselves to others, they risk becoming the grumblers. The Tyndale notes compare the elder brother in the parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15:25–32), who also stood near the father's generosity and resented it.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the main lesson, "That God is debtor to no man," and on late conversion adds, "not to discourage any, but to awaken all, be it remembered, that now is the accepted time." On v15 he writes, "It is an evil eye, which is displeased at the good of others." JFB calls the eleventh hour "a most unusual hour both for offering and engaging," and reads the complaint as "unworthy envy of your neighbor, and discontent with the goodness that engaged and rewarded you." JFB comments on "many called, few chosen" because its text includes the line, and takes "called" as invited, not effectually called. Guzik says God is never unfair but is free to be more generous than fairness requires, and that even the ability and opportunity to serve are gifts of grace. He urges believers to rejoice in others' blessings rather than measure them.

#### Jesus Foretells His Death a Third Time (vv17–19)
- **"As Jesus was going up to Jerusalem" (v17).** Jerusalem sits about 2,500 feet above sea level, and Jericho about 800 feet below, so the road climbs some 3,300 feet in about fifteen miles. Pilgrims always spoke of "going up" to Jerusalem (Ps 122:4). Mark adds a vivid detail: Jesus walked ahead of them, and they were amazed and afraid (Mark 10:32).
- **"He took the twelve disciples aside" (v17).** Pilgrim crowds were heading to Passover. Jesus tells the twelve privately.
- **The fullest prediction (vv18–19).** Compare 16:21 and 17:22–23. This time Jesus names every step: "delivered over to the chief priests and scribes," "condemn him to death," "deliver him over to the Gentiles," "mocked and flogged and crucified," and "raised on the third day." For the first time he says he will be handed to the Gentiles, and for the first time Matthew records the word "crucified." The Jewish council could not carry out a death sentence under Roman rule (John 18:31), so a Roman execution was the only way he would die. Each step is fulfilled exactly in chapters 26–28.
- **Commentators:** Guzik argues that such detailed knowledge of events Jesus could not control shows he was more than a man, and that he walked into suffering deliberately, out of obedience to the Father. He thinks the disciples could not take it in because it clashed with their hopes of a political kingdom, as the very next scene shows. JFB defers these verses to its notes on Mark 10.

#### A Mother's Request (vv20–28)
- **"The mother of the sons of Zebedee came up to him with her sons" (v20).** James and John were fishermen from Galilee and part of Jesus' inner circle (4:21; 17:1). Their mother appears again at the cross (27:56). Comparing that verse with Mark 15:40 suggests her name was Salome. Some go further and suggest from John 19:25 that she was the sister of Jesus' mother, Mary, which would make James and John Jesus' cousins. That is possible but not certain.
- **"Say that these two sons of mine are to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left" (v21).** These were the places of highest honor beside a king. Jesus had just promised the twelve thrones (19:28), and they want the two best. Mark has James and John ask for themselves (Mark 10:35–37). Matthew's version shows the mother speaking, but Jesus answers the sons ("You do not know what you are asking" is plural), so the request was theirs as much as hers. The timing is painful. Jesus has just described being flogged and crucified, and they are thinking about seating arrangements.
- **"Are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?" (v22).** In the Old Testament "the cup" often stands for God's judgment poured out on sin, "the cup of his wrath" (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17, 22; Jer 25:15). In Gethsemane Jesus prays, "let this cup pass from me" (26:39). The KJV adds "and to be baptized with the baptism that I am baptized with," which belongs securely in Mark 10:38 but is missing from the earliest manuscripts of Matthew.
- **"We are able" (v22).** They answer quickly and confidently, and have no idea what they are saying. Within days they will flee (26:56).
- **"You will drink my cup" (v23).** Jesus does not mean they will bear God's wrath for sin. That cup is his alone. He means they will share in suffering for him. James was the first of the twelve to be martyred, executed by Herod Agrippa I around AD 44 (Acts 12:2). John, by tradition, lived longest of the twelve, but suffered imprisonment, beating and exile to Patmos (Acts 4:3; 5:40; Rev 1:9).
- **"Not mine to grant… for those for whom it has been prepared by my Father" (v23).** The Son defers to the Father. Matthew's readers would know who ended up at Jesus' right and left: two crucified robbers (27:38). The places beside the King in his hour of glory were on crosses.
- **"The ten… were indignant at the two brothers" (v24).** Probably not because the request was wrong, but because the brothers got there first. The same ambition was in all of them (18:1).
- **"The rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them" (v25).** "Lord it over" (*katakyrieuō*) means to rule down on people, to dominate. Peter later uses the same word warning elders not to be "domineering over those in your charge" (1 Pet 5:3). Everyone in Jesus' world knew how Roman rulers and Herod's family used power.
- **"It shall not be so among you" (v26).** Jesus does not abolish greatness. He redefines it. **"Servant"** (v26) is *diakonos*, one who waits on others, the word from which "deacon" comes. **"Slave"** (v27) is *doulos*, the lowest rung of all, someone with no rights of his own. The greater the ambition, the lower the place to take.
- **"Even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve" (v28).** The Son of Man in Daniel's vision receives a kingdom where "all peoples… should serve him" (Dan 7:13–14). Jesus is that Son of Man, and he will receive that kingdom, but first he comes to serve. "Came" also hints that he existed before he arrived.
- **"And to give his life as a ransom for many" (v28).** A ransom (*lytron*) was the price paid to free a slave or a prisoner of war. "For" is *anti*, which normally means "in place of" or "instead of." The picture is substitution: his life given in exchange for theirs. "Many" echoes Isaiah 53, where the Servant "bore the sin of many" and "makes many to be accounted righteous" (Isa 53:11–12). "Many" does not mean "not all." It is a Hebrew way of contrasting the one who dies with the great number who benefit. Early Christian writers sometimes asked to whom the ransom was paid, and some said to the devil. Most Christians since have thought that presses the image too far. Scripture's point is the cost and the freedom, not a transaction with Satan.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the brothers "abused what Christ said to comfort the disciples," turning the promise of thrones into an excuse for rivalry, and on v28 writes that his death "was a ransom for many, enough for all, working upon many." Guzik notes, following R. T. France, that *lytron* with *anti* points plainly to substitution, which the lexicon supports, and he also warns against stretching the ransom picture into theories about who received payment. He applies the passage to the church, which should not copy the world's chase after status and should measure leaders by service. JFB defers this section to Mark 10.

#### Jesus Heals Two Blind Men (vv29–34)
- **"As they went out of Jericho" (v29).** Jericho was the last town before the climb to Jerusalem. In Jesus' day there were two Jerichos: the ancient city mound, largely abandoned, and the new Roman town about a mile to the south, where Herod the Great had built a winter palace. Pilgrim crowds from Galilee who took the Jordan route passed through here.
- **"Two blind men sitting by the roadside" (v30).** Mark names one, Bartimaeus son of Timaeus, and says Jesus was leaving Jericho (Mark 10:46). Luke mentions one man and says Jesus was drawing near Jericho (Luke 18:35). The accounts are compressed differently. Mark and Luke may focus on the better-known of two men. On the place, a common suggestion is that the healing happened between the old and new towns, so it was both "leaving" one Jericho and "approaching" the other. Matthew often has two where others have one (8:28; 9:27), which fits the law's need for two witnesses.
- **"Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!" (v30).** "Son of David" is a messianic title. God promised David a descendant whose throne would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16), and Isaiah promised that in the Messiah's day "the eyes of the blind shall be opened" (Isa 35:5). These blind men see what the crowds do not.
- **"The crowd rebuked them… but they cried out all the more" (v31).** The crowd sees two beggars making a nuisance of themselves on a holy pilgrimage. Like the disciples with the children (19:13), the people closest to Jesus try to keep the needy away. The men refuse to be silenced.
- **"What do you want me to do for you?" (v32).** These are the same words Jesus asked the mother of James and John: "What do you want?" (v21). The two requests sit side by side. The brothers asked for power. The blind men ask for mercy. Jesus gives the blind men exactly what they ask.
- **"Lord, let our eyes be opened" (v33).** The disciples, who can see, have been blind to what Jesus just told them. The blind men ask for sight and receive it.
- **"Jesus in pity touched their eyes" (v34).** "Pity" is *splanchnizomai*, deep inward compassion (9:36; 14:14). Even with the cross days away, he stops for two beggars. **"And followed him."** They follow him up the road to Jerusalem, where he is about to be welcomed as the Son of David (21:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "They cried out as men in earnest. Cold desires beg denials." Guzik sees in their persistence a picture of seeking mercy with urgency, and notes that Jesus' question invites people to name their need to him in trust. He stresses that healing led straight to following. JFB defers these verses to Luke 18. None of the three works through the differences between the Gospel accounts here.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter turns on v28. The master who pays the last as much as the first is generous because the kingdom runs on grace, not wages, and the reason that grace can be given freely is that the Son of Man paid for it with his life. He gives what no one can earn because he himself is the price. The brothers wanted to sit at his right and left in glory, but his glory came on a cross between two criminals, and he calls everyone who follows him to the same road of service. The blind men by the road understood it better than the disciples: they asked the Son of David for mercy, received their sight, and followed him to Jerusalem.
