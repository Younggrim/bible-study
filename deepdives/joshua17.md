*Setting:* The second half of Joseph's allotment. Chapter 16 gave Ephraim's share in the central hills; chapter 17 gives the share of the other half of Manasseh, west of the Jordan, in the northern part of those hills, around Shechem, reaching to the edge of the great Jezreel Valley. (The first half of Manasseh had already settled east of the Jordan, 13:29–31.) The chapter has three parts: Manasseh's clans and the five daughters of Zelophehad, the boundaries along with another list of cities not taken, and a complaint from the Joseph tribes that their land is too small, with Joshua's sharp and hopeful reply.

#### The Allotment for Ephraim and Manasseh, continued (vv1–18)
- **"For he was the firstborn of Joseph" (v1).** Ephraim received the greater blessing (Gen 48:19), but Manasseh kept the firstborn's standing and receives a full share of his own. Two lots for Joseph, one blessed more highly, one born first.
- **"Machir the firstborn of Manasseh, the father of Gilead… because he was a man of war" (v1).** Machir's clan had captured Gilead and Bashan east of the Jordan (Num 32:39–40), and Moses gave it to them (13:29–31). "Father of Gilead" can mean he was the ancestor of a man named Gilead (Num 26:29), and JFB suggests it also means "lord or possessor" of the region. The clan's reputation as fighters survived for centuries: Deborah's song mentions "commanders" coming down from Machir (Judg 5:14).
- **"Abiezer, Helek, Asriel, Shechem, Hepher, and Shemida" (v2).** The six clans of Manasseh west of the Jordan (Num 26:29–33). Gideon, one of the great judges, would come from the clan of Abiezer (Judg 6:11). Several of these clan names, along with the names of two of Zelophehad's daughters, Hoglah and Noah, appear as district names in the Samaria Ostraca, tax receipts written on pottery in the 700s BC and found in the ruins of Samaria. Many scholars see this as a sign that these family lands kept their names for centuries.
- **"Zelophehad… had no sons, but only daughters" (v3).** Normally land passed from father to sons. A man with no sons would see his family's name and land disappear. About seven years earlier, Zelophehad's five daughters, Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah and Tirzah, had gone to Moses and asked, "Why should the name of our father be taken away from his clan because he had no son?" (Num 27:4). Moses took the case to the LORD, and God answered, "The daughters of Zelophehad are right" (Num 27:7). The ruling became law for all Israel: if a man had no sons, his daughters would inherit. A later ruling added that they must marry within their father's tribe, so the land would stay in the tribe (Num 36:1–12), and they did, marrying cousins (Num 36:10–11).
- **"They approached Eleazar the priest and Joshua" (v4).** The daughters do not wait to see if anyone will remember. They come to the committee in charge of the land and quote God's word: "The LORD commanded Moses to give us an inheritance." Like Caleb (14:6–12) and Achsah (15:18–19), they ask boldly for what God has promised. Joshua grants it "according to the mouth of the LORD," literally, "at the LORD's command."
- **"There fell to Manasseh ten portions" (v5).** Five shares went to the five clans with sons, and five to Zelophehad's five daughters in place of their grandfather Hepher's clan. That makes ten. JFB explains it the same way. Matthew Henry suggests the five daughters' portions together might have been equal to one clan's, but the text simply says ten portions. The tribe's shares were divided down to families, and women held land among their brothers.
- **The boundaries (vv7–10).** Manasseh's southern border with Ephraim runs from Michmethath, east of Shechem, past Tappuah and along the brook Kanah to the sea. Shechem, the town where Abraham first built an altar in Canaan (Gen 12:6–7) and where Jacob bought land (Gen 33:18–19), lay in Manasseh. The arrangement was tangled: "The land of Tappuah belonged to Manasseh, but the town of Tappuah… belonged to the people of Ephraim" (v8). Some towns south of the brook were Ephraim's, though within Manasseh's area (v9; compare 16:9). The two brother tribes were meant to live closely together.
- **"In Issachar and in Asher Manasseh had Beth-shean… Ibleam… Dor… En-dor… Taanach… Megiddo" (v11).** Manasseh was also given a chain of cities that lay within the neighboring tribes' land, along the Jezreel Valley and the coast. These were strategic: Beth-shean guarded the eastern end of the Jezreel Valley where it meets the Jordan valley, and Egypt kept a garrison there in this period. Taanach and Megiddo guarded the passes through the Carmel ridge. Dor was a harbor. En-dor is where Saul later consulted a medium (1 Sam 28:7). The phrase "the third is Naphath" is obscure. It may mean "the three heights" or refer to the third city in the list, Dor, which is elsewhere called Naphath-dor (12:23).
- **"Yet the people of Manasseh could not take possession of those cities" (v12).** The wording is like Judah's at Jerusalem (15:63). "The Canaanites persisted in dwelling in that land": they were determined to stay. Judges 1:27–28 repeats the list almost word for word. Beth-shean was still in enemy hands generations later, when the Philistines hung Saul's body on its wall (1 Sam 31:10).
- **"When the people of Israel grew strong, they put the Canaanites to forced labor, but did not utterly drive them out" (v13).** The verse admits that Israel later had the strength to act, but chose to profit from the Canaanites instead of removing them, as Ephraim did at Gezer (16:10). JFB suggests the cause was "indolence, a love of ease," a mistaken kindness, and "a decreasing principle of faith and zeal." The Tyndale notes point out that over time the remaining Canaanites blended into Israel and drew it into idolatry, which finally cost Israel the land.
- **"Why have you given me but one lot and one portion?" (v14).** The Joseph tribes, speaking as one, complain to Joshua. They are "a numerous people," and they say the LORD has blessed them. The complaint is odd, since they had received the central hill country and two shares, more land than most. Matthew Henry suspects they expected favor because Joshua was from Ephraim, and notes that he "had no more regard to his own tribe than to any other."
- **"Go up by yourselves to the forest, and there clear ground" (v15).** Joshua turns their boast against them. If they are so many and so strong, let them work. The central hills were then thickly wooded with oak and pine, and lightly settled. Clearing forest for farmland, with terraces cut into the hillsides, was hard, slow labor, but it was land already theirs. Archaeologists have found that in this period hundreds of small new villages appeared in these very hills, which many take as the footprint of Israel's settlement. "The land of the Perizzites and the Rephaim" refers to peoples still living in the hills, including the remnants of the giant clans.
- **"The hill country is not enough for us. Yet all the Canaanites who dwell in the plain have chariots of iron" (v16).** Now the real problem comes out. The tribes want the open, cleared land of the valleys, but they are afraid. "Chariots of iron" were probably chariots reinforced with iron fittings. In this early period iron was scarce and valuable, and Israel had little of it (compare 1 Sam 13:19–22). Chariots were deadly on flat ground like the Jezreel Valley, but nearly useless in wooded hills.
- **"You are a numerous people and have great power" (v17).** Joshua repeats their own words back to them and agrees: you are great, so you shall not have one lot only. He gives them both things they wanted, more land and the valleys, but on his terms.
- **"The hill country shall be yours, for though it is a forest, you shall clear it… For you shall drive out the Canaanites, though they have chariots of iron" (v18).** Two promises, each one beginning with a hard fact ("though it is a forest," "though they have chariots of iron") and ending with God's certainty ("you shall"). The answer to fear is not a bigger allotment but faith and work. Compare Caleb, who at eighty-five asked for the hardest land in Judah (14:12). These tribes, young and numerous, asked for easier land and were afraid of chariots. Judges 4–5 later shows that God could indeed defeat iron chariots in the Jezreel Valley, when he routed Sisera's 900 chariots (Judg 4:3, 15).
- **Commentators:** Henry says Joshua "would administer impartially, without favour or affection," leaving "an excellent example to all in public trusts." On v15 he writes: "Most people's poverty is the effect of their idleness; would they dig, they need not beg." He has Joshua say, in effect, "Never fear them… thou hast God on thy side." JFB says Joshua "sarcastically turned all their arguments against themselves" and so "rebuked their unbelief and cowardice." JFB adds that the iron chariots were "unusually strengthened with that metal, and perhaps armed with projecting scythes," though there is little evidence for scythed chariots this early. Guzik notes that the Canaanites' determination was to be expected. What was missing was Israel's determination, as they settled for forced labor. He contrasts the Joseph tribes with Caleb, and draws the lesson that the way to receive more from God is to be faithful with what he has already given. The Tyndale notes suggest that iron tools coming into use at this time made the clearing of forests possible, and that Joshua himself settled in a forested area of Ephraim (19:50).

#### Pointing to Christ
Two requests stand side by side in this chapter. Zelophehad's daughters had no standing under the usual rules, but they came with God's own word in their hands and received an inheritance among their brothers. The Joseph tribes had every advantage and came with complaints, and were sent back to work in faith. The gospel answers both. Those who have no claim of their own receive a share by God's promise: "in Christ Jesus you are all sons of God, through faith… If you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" (Gal 3:26, 29). And those who have received much are called to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you" (Phil 2:12–13). The forest still has to be cleared, but the land is already given, and "though they have chariots of iron," the outcome is not in doubt.
