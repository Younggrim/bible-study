*Setting:* The day after Mount Carmel, around 867 BC. Fire fell from heaven, the people cried "The LORD, he is God," the prophets of Baal were put to death, and the three-year drought broke in a great storm. Elijah ran ahead of Ahab's chariot to the royal city of Jezreel, perhaps expecting the king and the nation to turn back to God. Instead, the real power in the palace, Queen Jezebel, answers with a death threat. Chapter 19 is the story of a great prophet's collapse into fear and despair, and of how God meets him: with sleep and food, with a long journey back to Mount Sinai where Israel's covenant began, with a quiet voice instead of a show of power, and with new work and a successor.

#### Elijah Flees Jezebel (vv1–8)
- **"Ahab told Jezebel all that Elijah had done" (v1).** Ahab reports, but he does not decide. He witnessed the fire from heaven, and yet he goes home and tells his wife about the dead prophets. Notice what he leaves out: the LORD. In his telling it is all what "Elijah had done."
- **"So may the gods do to me and more also" (v2).** This was a standard curse formula, used by Solomon against Adonijah (2:23) and later by the king of Syria against Elisha (2 Kgs 6:31). Jezebel swears by "the gods," plural. **"By this time tomorrow."** Oddly, she sends a messenger instead of soldiers. If she simply wanted Elijah dead, warning him made no sense. Many think she wanted him to run, because a prophet who fled in fear would be discredited, and that would undo Carmel. If so, it worked.
- **"Then he was afraid" (v3).** The standard Hebrew text reads "he saw" (*wayyar'*), while many Hebrew manuscripts and the ancient Greek and Syriac read "he was afraid" (*wayyira'*). The two words are spelled with the same consonants. The ESV and most translations go with "afraid." Either way, Elijah saw the danger, and the man who faced 450 prophets alone ran from one woman's message.
- **"Ran for his life and came to Beersheba, which belongs to Judah" (v3).** Beersheba was the southern edge of the land ("from Dan to Beersheba"), about 100 miles from Jezreel, and outside Ahab's kingdom. But Judah's king Jehoshaphat was allied with Ahab (2 Chr 18:1), so even Judah was not safe. **He left his servant there.** Elijah goes on alone.
- **"A day's journey into the wilderness... under a broom tree" (v4).** This is the Negev, the dry wilderness south of Beersheba. The broom tree (*rotem*), which the KJV called a "juniper," is a desert shrub that can grow to about ten feet. It gives thin but welcome shade. JFB corrects the old translation: "not juniper, but broom shrubs."
- **"It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life" (v4).** The great prophet asks to die. Notice he asks *God* to do it. He ran from Jezebel to save his life, and now wants to lose it. That contradiction is typical of exhaustion and despair. Moses (Num 11:15), Jonah (Jonah 4:3, 8) and Job (Job 6:8–9) prayed similar prayers. The Bible does not hide that faithful people can reach this place.
- **"I am no better than my fathers" (v4).** He may mean he had hoped to succeed where earlier prophets failed, and has not. Or simply that he is just another mortal man. Either way, Carmel did not change Israel, and he feels he has failed.
- **"He lay down and slept" (v5).** God's first answer is not a sermon. Elijah is physically spent after Carmel, the run to Jezreel and a flight of over a hundred miles. He needs sleep.
- **"An angel touched him and said to him, 'Arise and eat'" (v5).** At his head is "a cake baked on hot stones and a jar of water" (v6). This is simple desert bread, baked on stones heated in a fire. The God who fed Elijah by ravens and through a widow's jar (ch 17) feeds him again. Then Elijah sleeps again.
- **"The angel of the LORD came again a second time" (v7).** "The angel of the LORD" in the Old Testament is sometimes a messenger and sometimes seems to be the LORD himself appearing (Gen 16:7–13; Exod 3:2–6). **"For the journey is too great for you."** God knows where Elijah is going before Elijah does, and he does not scold him for it. He prepares him for it.
- **"In the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mount of God" (v8).** Horeb is another name for Mount Sinai, where God gave Moses the law. Its traditional site, Jebel Musa in the southern Sinai Peninsula, is about 200 to 250 miles from Beersheba. That trip did not need forty days. The number is a deliberate echo: Moses was on the mountain forty days and nights without food (Exod 34:28), and Israel wandered forty years in the wilderness. Elijah is going back to where the covenant began, as if starting over with Israel's story. Jesus later fasted forty days in the wilderness (Matt 4:2), and Moses and Elijah, the two men who met God on this mountain, appeared with Jesus on the Mount of Transfiguration (Matt 17:3).
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's suggestion that Elijah expected Carmel to convert the court and the nation, and that its failure crushed him. Spurgeon, quoted by Guzik, notes that Elijah failed exactly where he had seemed strongest, in courage. Henry simply notes that "Jezebel sent Elijah a threatening message," and that the threat shows how hardened she was: she saw the same evidence and only grew angrier. All three also stress God's gentleness here. Guzik passes on Morgan, Meyer and Spurgeon on the tenderness of God's care: no rebuke, just rest and food, because the body and the soul both need feeding. JFB links Elijah's collapse to overconfidence after Carmel, and says God fed him so he could finish the journey "in a better but not wholly right frame of mind." The Tyndale notes compare Jonah, who also finished his task and then sank in disappointment at the result.

#### The LORD Speaks to Elijah (vv9–18)
- **"There he came to a cave" (v9).** The Hebrew says "*the* cave." Many think this is the "cleft of the rock" where God placed Moses and passed by in his glory (Exod 33:21–23). Elijah is standing where Moses stood.
- **"What are you doing here, Elijah?" (v9).** God asks questions, not because he lacks information, but to draw people out (Gen 3:9). Henry: "It concerns us often to enquire whether we be in our place and in the way of our duty." The question may also hint: you have come to the mountain of God. Do you understand where you are?
- **"I have been very jealous for the LORD, the God of hosts" (v10).** The Hebrew doubles the word for emphasis: "zealously zealous." The word (*qana'*) is used of God's own jealousy for his people (Exod 20:5). Elijah's list of complaints is all true: Israel has forsaken the covenant, thrown down altars, killed prophets. But one claim is not: **"I, even I only, am left."** Obadiah's hundred prophets (18:13) say otherwise. Elijah's grief has narrowed his vision until he sees only himself. Paul quotes this verse and God's answer in Romans 11:2–4.
- **"Go out and stand on the mount before the LORD" (v11).** Then "the LORD passed by." These are the same words used when God passed before Moses on this mountain (Exod 33:22; 34:6).
- **Wind, earthquake, fire (vv11–12).** These were the signs of God's presence at Sinai when he gave the law: thunder, fire, smoke and a quaking mountain (Exod 19:16–18). They were also the kind of power Elijah had just seen on Carmel. **"But the LORD was not in the wind... not in the earthquake... not in the fire."** God can speak through spectacular power, and has. But he is not limited to it, and he chooses not to use it here.
- **"The sound of a low whisper" (v12).** The Hebrew, *qol demamah daqqah*, is famously hard to put into English. *Qol* is "voice" or "sound." *Demamah* means "silence" or "a whisper." It is used in Psalm 107:29 of God calming a storm to a whisper. *Daqqah* means "thin" or "fine." Literally it is something like "a sound of thin silence." The KJV's "still small voice" has become a proverb. The ESV's "low whisper" gives the sense. The point is the contrast. Elijah wanted God to act in fire. God came to him in quiet.
- **"He wrapped his face in his cloak" (v13).** As Moses hid his face at the burning bush (Exod 3:6), Elijah covers his face. He knows he is in God's presence. Then the same question comes again: "What are you doing here, Elijah?"
- **The same answer, word for word (v14).** Elijah repeats his complaint exactly. The quiet voice has humbled him, but it has not changed his view of things. God does not argue with him further. He gives him work.
- **"Go, return on your way to the wilderness of Damascus" (v15).** God sends Elijah back the way he came, and further north. The cure for his despair is a commission.
- **"Anoint Hazael to be king over Syria" (v15).** Syria (Aram), with its capital at Damascus, was Israel's chief enemy. God rules foreign kingdoms too. Hazael will be his instrument of judgment on Israel (2 Kgs 8:7–15; 10:32). In the end Elisha carries this out, by announcing to Hazael that he would be king.
- **"Jehu the son of Nimshi you shall anoint to be king over Israel" (v16).** Jehu will wipe out Ahab's dynasty and Baal worship (2 Kgs 9–10). Strictly, Jehu was Nimshi's grandson (2 Kgs 9:2), since "son" in Hebrew can mean descendant. A young prophet sent by Elisha anoints him (2 Kgs 9:1–6). JFB notes that "anointing" here really means appointment, and that only Jehu had oil poured on him.
- **"Elisha the son of Shaphat of Abel-meholah... prophet in your place" (v16).** Abel-meholah, "meadow of dancing," was in the Jordan Valley south of Beth-shan. Elijah, who thought he was the last, is told to train his successor. This is the one anointing Elijah does himself.
- **"The one who escapes from the sword of Hazael shall Jehu put to death" (v17).** God's judgment on Baal worship will come, not by fire on a mountain, but through history: a foreign king, a ruthless general and a prophet's word. Elisha's "sword" was likely the word of judgment he spoke (compare Hos 6:5). Elijah wanted God to act. God was going to, more thoroughly than Elijah imagined, but on God's timetable.
- **"Yet I will leave seven thousand in Israel" (v18).** God's answer to "I only am left." Seven thousand is a round, full number (seven times a thousand), and the point is that the faithful remnant was far larger than Elijah thought. They are described as the knees that have not bowed to Baal and the mouths that have not kissed him. Kissing an idol, or one's hand toward it, was an act of worship (Hos 13:2; Job 31:26–27). Paul uses this verse to show that "at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace" (Rom 11:5).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Elijah's claim to be alone was not true but was how he felt, and that his reasons for fear were really reasons to keep going. Henry on the wind, quake and fire: "Those struck an awe upon him, awakened his attention, and inspired humility and reverence." And on the whisper: "Gracious souls are more affected by the tender mercies of the Lord than by his terrors." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that God's quiet work had been going on in Israel all along, in 7,000 hearts Elijah never knew about. On v18, Henry: "In times of the greatest degeneracy and apostasy God has always had, and will have, a remnant faithful to him." JFB reads "kissed" as kissing the hand toward the idol, a gesture of worship.

#### The Call of Elisha (vv19–21)
- **"Plowing with twelve yoke of oxen... and he was with the twelfth" (v19).** Twelve pairs of oxen working together, probably with other plowmen, meant a wealthy farming family. Elisha was at the back, the last in line. Twelve may also quietly recall the twelve tribes and the twelve stones of Carmel. Henry notes that Elijah found him "not in the schools of the prophets, but in the field," at ordinary work.
- **"Elijah passed by him and cast his cloak upon him" (v19).** The cloak (*'adderet*) was the prophet's distinctive garment, probably of hair (2 Kgs 1:8; Zech 13:4). Throwing it on Elisha was a sign of claiming him, of passing the office to him. Elisha understands immediately. The same cloak will fall to him when Elijah is taken up (2 Kgs 2:13–14).
- **"Let me kiss my father and my mother, and then I will follow you" (v20).** A reasonable request. Elijah's answer, **"Go back again, for what have I done to you?"**, is unclear. It may mean "Go, but remember what I have just done to you," a reminder that this is a call from God, not from Elijah. Or it may be a softer "Go on, I am not stopping you." Either way Elijah does not pressure him. The decision is Elisha's.
- **Jesus and this verse.** When a would-be disciple said, "Let me first say farewell to those at my home," Jesus answered, "No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God" (Luke 9:61–62). Jesus' call is even more urgent than Elijah's.
- **"He... took the yoke of oxen and sacrificed them and boiled their flesh with the yokes" (v21).** Elisha burns his bridges, or rather his plow. He uses the wooden yokes and plowing gear as firewood and slaughters the oxen. There is no going back to farming. He gives a feast for the people, probably a celebration meal of thanksgiving. Then **"he arose and went after Elijah and assisted him."** The word for "assisted" (*sharat*) is the word for Joshua serving Moses (Exod 24:13; Num 11:28). The great prophet's successor begins as his servant.
- **Commentators:** JFB: the twelve yoke show "he was a man of substance," and the cloak "was an investiture with the prophetic office." JFB reads Elijah's reply as "Go, but keep in mind the solemn ceremony," and warns not to let "any earthly affection" hold back obedience. Guzik notes Clarke's point on the hairy prophet's mantle and passes on Wiseman's two readings of "What have I done to you?" Guzik also observes that Elijah's part is to call, and Elisha's part is to respond freely.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elijah's story bends toward Jesus at several points. Moses and Elijah, the two men who met God on Horeb, stood with Jesus on another mountain, talking with him about the "departure" he would accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:30–31). Like Elijah, Jesus spent forty days in the wilderness, was ministered to by angels (Mark 1:13), and later lay in agony and was strengthened by an angel (Luke 22:43). But where Elijah fled from death and asked God to end his life in despair, Jesus set his face toward Jerusalem and laid down his life on purpose. Elijah thought he was the only faithful one left. Jesus truly was left alone, deserted by all his friends (Mark 14:50), so that the remnant "chosen by grace" (Rom 11:5) would never be alone. And the God who did not come in fire but in a quiet voice came finally as a man, gentle and lowly in heart (Matt 11:29).
