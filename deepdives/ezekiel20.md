*Setting:* Babylonia, "in the seventh year, in the fifth month, on the tenth day" of King Jehoiachin's exile (v1), which works out to mid-August 591 BC. It is almost a year after the temple vision of chapter 8 (dated 8:1) and about five years before Jerusalem fell. For the third time the elders of the exiles come to sit before Ezekiel (8:1; 14:1). Their question is not recorded, but v32 hints at it. They seem to have been wondering whether, far from the temple and the land, they might as well settle down and worship as the nations did. God refuses to be consulted. Instead he gives them a history lesson, the whole story of Israel from Egypt to Ezekiel's day, told as one long rebellion met each time by undeserved mercy. Psalms 78 and 106 and Stephen's speech in Acts 7 tell the same story in similar ways. Then the chapter turns to the future, a new exodus in which God will again be king over his people, purify them, and bring them home. In Hebrew Bibles, vv45–49 begin chapter 21.

#### Israel's Continuing Rebellion (vv1–32)
- **"Is it to inquire of me that you come? As I live… I will not be inquired of by you" (v3).** As in 14:3, the elders come in the proper way to "inquire" (*darash*) of the LORD, and he refuses. The oath "as I live" shows how firm the refusal is. People who have already decided what they want cannot be given guidance, only a mirror.
- **"Will you judge them, son of man, will you judge them?" (v4).** Ezekiel is told to act as prosecutor, setting out the evidence. **"Let them know the abominations of their fathers."** The history that follows is not meant to excuse the present generation by blaming the past, as the proverb of 18:2 did. It shows them that they are just like their fathers (v30).
- **"On the day when I chose Israel, I swore… making myself known to them in the land of Egypt" (v5).** The story begins with God's choice, not Israel's. "I swore" is literally "I lifted my hand," the gesture of an oath. It is repeated through the chapter (vv5, 6, 15, 23, 28, 42). **"I am the LORD your God"** recalls Exodus 6:2–8, where God revealed his name to Moses and promised to bring Israel out.
- **"Cast away the detestable things your eyes feast on… do not defile yourselves with the idols of Egypt" (v7).** Exodus does not record a command like this in Egypt, but Joshua later told Israel to "put away the gods that your fathers served beyond the River and in Egypt" (Josh 24:14). The golden calf, built only weeks after the exodus, may have copied Egypt's bull cults (Exod 32:4). Ezekiel adds a detail the earlier books only hint at. Israel was already worshiping idols in Egypt.
- **The pattern (vv8–9, 13–14, 21–22).** The history falls into three rounds: Egypt (vv5–9), the first wilderness generation (vv10–17), and their children in the wilderness (vv18–26). Each follows the same sequence. God acts in grace, Israel rebels, God says he will pour out his wrath, and then **"I acted for the sake of my name."** The repetition is the message. Israel survived not because it was better than it seemed but because God guarded his own name.
- **"That it should not be profaned in the sight of the nations" (v9).** In the ancient world a god's reputation rose and fell with his people. If the LORD had destroyed Israel in Egypt or in the desert, the nations would say he could not finish what he started. Moses used exactly this argument when he pleaded for Israel after the golden calf and at Kadesh (Exod 32:12; Num 14:13–16). God's concern for his name is not vanity. His name is his revealed character, and his glory is bound up with the salvation of his people.
- **"I gave them my statutes… by which, if a person does them, he shall live" (v11).** This quotes Leviticus 18:5 and repeats it three times (vv11, 13, 21). God's law was a gift meant for life, the way to flourish in his presence. Paul quotes the same verse when he contrasts the way of law with the way of faith (Rom 10:5; Gal 3:12). Israel's tragedy was that it rejected the very commands that would have given it life.
- **"I gave them my Sabbaths, as a sign between me and them, that they might know that I am the LORD who sanctifies them" (v12).** This echoes Exodus 31:13 almost word for word. The Sabbath was the sign of the Sinai covenant, as circumcision was of Abraham's and the rainbow of Noah's. Keeping it marked Israel as God's people, set apart by him. Ezekiel mentions the profaning of the Sabbaths again and again in this chapter (vv13, 16, 20, 21, 24) and in 22:8, 26. In exile, far from the temple, the Sabbath became one of the main ways Jews kept their identity.
- **"I swore to them in the wilderness that I would not bring them into the land" (v15).** This is the judgment at Kadesh, when the people refused to enter Canaan and God swore that that generation would die in the wilderness (Num 14:28–35). **"Nevertheless, my eye spared them" (v17).** He did not wipe out the whole nation. Their children lived to enter.
- **"Do not walk in the statutes of your fathers" (v18).** The second generation was given a fresh start, the same choice as the righteous grandson of 18:14. But they too rebelled, at Baal-peor among other places (Num 25:1–3).
- **"I swore to them in the wilderness that I would scatter them among the nations" (v23).** Moses had warned of exile before Israel ever entered the land (Lev 26:33; Deut 28:64). The exile Ezekiel's hearers were living through had been on the table from the beginning.
- **"I gave them statutes that were not good and rules by which they could not have life" (v25).** This is the hardest verse in the chapter. It cannot mean that God's own law was bad, since the chapter has just called his statutes the way of life three times. Most interpreters read it as judicial. Because Israel rejected God's good statutes, he handed them over to the bad ones they preferred, the rules and rites of pagan religion, which led to death. Psalm 81:12 says "I gave them over to their stubborn hearts, to follow their own counsels," and Paul says three times that "God gave them up" to the sins they chose (Rom 1:24, 26, 28). Some scholars think Ezekiel means that God allowed Israel to twist his own law of the firstborn (Exod 13:2; 22:29) into an excuse for child sacrifice. Either way, God is not the author of their sin, but he is the judge who lets sin become its own punishment.
- **"I defiled them through their very gifts in their offering up all their firstborn" (v26).** The law said every firstborn belonged to the LORD, but firstborn sons were to be redeemed, bought back with an offering, never killed (Exod 13:13; 34:20). Some Israelites turned this into literal child sacrifice, "passing them through the fire" to Molech (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6; Jer 7:31). God had rescued Israel by sparing its firstborn in Egypt (Exod 12:12–13), and now Israel was killing its own firstborn. **"That I might devastate them."** The word means to appall or make desolate. The horror itself was meant to shock them into knowing the LORD.
- **"Wherever they saw any high hill or any leafy tree, there they offered their sacrifices" (v28).** This was the third stage, Israel in the land. Canaanite worship was carried out at hilltop shrines and under green trees, and Israel copied it (Deut 12:2; 1 Kings 14:23; Jer 2:20). The things they offered there, sacrifices, aromas and drink offerings, were the very offerings God had prescribed for himself.
- **"What is the high place to which you go? So its name is called Bamah to this day" (v29).** This is a pun. "High place" is *bamah*. God's question in Hebrew is *mah habamah*, "what is the high place?" and "you go" is *ha-ba'im*. The sounds play on *ba* ("go") and *mah* ("what"). It is as if God says, "What is this 'go-what' place you keep going to?" The name itself becomes a mockery.
- **"Will you defile yourselves after the manner of your fathers?" (v30).** Now the history reaches the present. The elders sitting before Ezekiel were doing the same things. **"When you present your gifts and offer up your children in fire… to this day" (v31).** Child sacrifice was still being practiced in Jerusalem (16:20–21; Jer 32:35). Whether some exiles practiced it in Babylon is less clear, but the guilt was shared. **"Shall I be inquired of by you?"** The opening refusal of v3 is repeated.
- **"What is in your mind shall never happen—the thought, 'Let us be like the nations… and worship wood and stone'" (v32).** This seems to be what the elders had really come to ask about. In exile it was tempting to blend in, to take on Babylon's gods and become one more people among many. Israel had always wanted to be "like all the nations" (1 Sam 8:5, 20). God says flatly that it will not happen. He will not let his people go, even if they want to leave him.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "Those hearts are wretchedly hardened which ask God leave to go on in sin, and that even when suffering for it." On the Sabbath he writes, "Sabbaths are privileges; they are signs of our being his people." JFB calls the Sabbath "a kind of sacramental pledge of the covenant of adoption," and calls the "name's sake" refrain "His gratuitous mercy abounding above their sins." On v25 JFB reads the statutes as "spiritually corrupting, and, finally, as the consequence, destroying them." Henry, Guzik and the Tyndale notes take the same judicial view, that God let sin become its own punishment, and the Tyndale notes compare Romans 1. JFB says the Bamah name "convicts you of sinning, not from ignorance but perverse rebellion." Guzik follows Feinberg and Alexander in reading "shall live" (v11) as the life of the covenant relationship, not salvation earned by works.

#### The Lord Will Restore Israel (vv33–49)
- **"With a mighty hand and an outstretched arm and with wrath poured out I will be king over you" (v33).** "Mighty hand and outstretched arm" is the language of the first exodus (Deut 4:34; 5:15; 26:8). Then God used it against Egypt. Now it is turned toward Israel. **"I will be king over you"** answers v32. They wanted to be like the nations, but God insists on ruling them. It is a frightening promise, but it is a promise. He will not give them up.
- **"I will bring you out from the peoples and gather you" (v34).** This is a new exodus, not from Egypt but from all the lands where they are scattered. Isaiah 40–55 develops the same theme at length (Isa 43:16–19; 51:9–11).
- **"I will bring you into the wilderness of the peoples, and there I will enter into judgment with you face to face" (v35).** As the first exodus led through the Sinai wilderness, the new one will pass through a wilderness among the nations. This is probably not a place on a map but a period of testing. **"Face to face"** recalls how God spoke with Moses (Exod 33:11; Deut 5:4), but here it is face to face in judgment.
- **"I will make you pass under the rod" (v37).** Shepherds counted their sheep by making them pass one at a time under the shepherd's staff (Lev 27:32; Jer 33:13). Each animal was checked, and the tithe animals were set apart. God will examine his people one by one. **"I will bring you into the bond of the covenant."** The Hebrew phrase is unusual, and some translations follow the Greek, "into the covenant by number." Either way, the sheep that pass the rod are brought back under the covenant.
- **"I will purge out the rebels… they shall not enter the land of Israel" (v38).** As the rebellious generation died in the wilderness and never saw Canaan, so the rebels of the new exodus will be left outside. God's people will be purified. Not everyone who leaves Babylon will make it home.
- **"Go serve every one of you his idols… but my holy name you shall no more profane with your gifts and your idols" (v39).** This is bitter irony. If they will not listen, let them worship idols openly and stop mixing idols with the worship of the LORD. Elijah said much the same on Mount Carmel: "If the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him" (1 Kings 18:21). Jesus' rebuke of lukewarm Laodicea strikes the same note (Rev 3:15–16).
- **"On my holy mountain… all the house of Israel, all of them, shall serve me in the land" (v40).** In contrast to the many high places, there will be one holy mountain, Zion. **"All the house of Israel"** includes the northern tribes as well, a reunited people (37:15–22). **"There I will accept them."** The offerings God rejected on every hill he will receive on his own mountain.
- **"As a pleasing aroma I will accept you" (v41).** The phrase was used for sacrifices (v28). Now the people themselves are the pleasing aroma. Paul uses the same image for believers (2 Cor 2:15; Eph 5:2). **"I will manifest my holiness among you in the sight of the nations."** The name that Israel profaned before the nations will be honored before them.
- **"There you shall remember your ways… and you shall loathe yourselves" (v43).** As in 16:61–63, shame comes after grace, not before. Restoration in the land produces a deep sorrow for sin, not pride. Self-loathing here is not despair. It is the honest disgust at sin that marks true repentance.
- **"When I deal with you for my name's sake, not according to your evil ways" (v44).** The phrase that ran through the history of judgment (vv9, 14, 22) now becomes the ground of salvation. God spared Israel for his name's sake in the past, and he will save them for his name's sake in the future. This is grace in its purest form: not according to their deeds.
- **"Set your face toward the southland… prophesy against the forest land in the Negeb" (v46).** Three different words for "south" are used here (*teman*, *darom*, *negev*). From Babylon, armies came into Judah from the north, so Judah and Jerusalem lay to the "south" of their route. The "forest" stands for the people, as JFB says. The Negeb itself was dry country, not forest, which makes the image more pointed. Chapter 21 explains it plainly: the south is Jerusalem and the land of Israel (21:2).
- **"It shall devour every green tree in you and every dry tree" (v47).** Fire normally passes over green wood and catches dry. This one will burn both, righteous and wicked alike (21:3–4). **"All faces from south to north shall be scorched by it."** No one will be untouched.
- **"Is he not a maker of parables?" (v49).** Ezekiel's hearers brushed him off as a storyteller with riddles they did not need to take seriously. The complaint is ironic. In the very next chapter God strips away the picture and says it plainly. Jesus faced the same dullness and explained that parables both reveal truth and hide it from those unwilling to see (Matt 13:10–15).
- **Commentators:** JFB says that in v33 God will "assert My right over you in spite of your resistance," and reads the wilderness of the peoples as "their spiritual wilderness period of trial, discipline, and purification." All three take the rod of v37 as a shepherd counting his sheep. JFB calls it "a metaphor from a shepherd who makes his sheep pass under his rod in counting them." Henry draws the lesson that "the more we know God's holiness, the more we see the hatefulness of sin," and on v49 notes that "the plainest truths were as parables to the people." Guzik sees a partial fulfillment in the return from Babylon and a fuller one still ahead, and he compares the open idolatry of v39 to the lukewarm church of Revelation 3. The Tyndale notes compare v40 with Jesus' words about worship in spirit and truth (John 4:21–24).

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole history of Ezekiel 20 is one long refrain: Israel rebelled, and God acted "for the sake of my name." Israel never kept the statutes "by which, if a person does them, he shall live." Jesus did. He lived the obedient life that every generation in this chapter refused, and then he took on himself the wrath God kept saying he would pour out. Through him God does exactly what v44 promises: he deals with his people "not according to your evil ways, nor according to your corrupt deeds." He is the Good Shepherd who calls his sheep by name and knows each one that passes under his rod (John 10:3, 14). He leads his people on a new exodus out of slavery to sin (Luke 9:31, where his coming death is literally called his "exodus"). And in him believers become what Israel was meant to be, "a fragrant offering" accepted by God (Eph 5:2).
