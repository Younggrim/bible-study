*Setting:* Nearly two years have passed since the night visions of chapters 1–6. The date is December 518 BC, "the fourth year of King Darius" (v1). The temple has been under construction for about two and a half years and is roughly half built. It will be finished in 515 BC (Ezra 6:15). For seventy years, ever since Jerusalem fell to Babylon, the Jews had kept days of fasting and mourning to remember the disaster. Now a delegation arrives with a practical question: with the temple rising again, should those fasts continue? Chapters 7 and 8 belong together as God's answer. Zechariah does not reply to the question at once. First he asks what their fasting was really for, and reminds them why the disaster came. The direct answer comes only in 8:19, and it is better news than they expected. The ESV treats the whole of chapter 7 as one section.

#### A Call for Justice and Mercy (vv1–14)
- **"On the fourth day of the ninth month, which is Chislev" (v1).** Chislev falls in November–December. The Babylonian month names (Chislev, Nisan, Adar) came home with the exiles and are still used in the Jewish calendar today. Hanukkah begins later in this same month.
- **"The people of Bethel had sent Sharezer and Regem-melech" (v2).** The Hebrew is compressed and can be read two ways. The ESV takes "Bethel" as the sender: the town of Bethel, twelve miles north of Jerusalem, sent these men. The KJV reads "Bethel" in its literal sense, "house of God," and has the men sent "unto the house of God." Bethel had once been the site of Jeroboam's rival golden calf shrine (1 Kgs 12:28–29), and now its people come to Jerusalem's temple to ask the LORD. That would be a quiet sign of healing. Both names are Babylonian in form. Sharezer means something like "protect the king," and Regem-melech may be a court title. These were men born in exile who had come home.
- **"To entreat the favor of the LORD" (v2).** The Hebrew idiom is literally "to soften the face of the LORD," to seek his kindness, usually with prayer and an offering. The same phrase returns at the end of chapter 8, where the nations come "to entreat the favor of the LORD" (8:21–22). The book shows the small delegation from Bethel as a preview of the whole world coming.
- **"Should I weep and abstain in the fifth month, as I have done for so many years?" (v3).** The fast of the fifth month (Av, July–August) mourned the burning of the temple and Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar's commander in 586 BC (2 Kgs 25:8–9; Jer 52:12–13). The Law commanded only one yearly fast, the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29–31). These fasts were customs the exiles had started themselves. The question is sincere and reasonable. If the temple is being rebuilt, is mourning its loss still fitting? Jews still keep this fast today as Tisha B'Av, the ninth of Av.
- **"When you fasted and mourned in the fifth month and in the seventh, for these seventy years" (v5).** God addresses not only Bethel but "all the people of the land and the priests." The fast of the seventh month most likely remembered the murder of Gedaliah, the Jewish governor Babylon appointed after Jerusalem fell. His assassination drove the remaining Jews to flee to Egypt and ended any hope of staying in the land (2 Kgs 25:22–26; Jer 41:1–3). The "seventy years" counts from 586 BC and recalls Jeremiah's prophecy of seventy years of exile (Jer 25:11–12; 29:10; Zech 1:12).
- **"Was it for me that you fasted?" (v5).** The Hebrew repeats the pronoun for emphasis: "Was it for me, me?" God does not condemn fasting. He asks about its aim. Were they mourning their sin against God, or only their lost city and their own misfortune? Grief over consequences is not the same as repentance.
- **"Do you not eat for yourselves and drink for yourselves?" (v6).** Eating and drinking were done for their own enjoyment. If the feasts were for themselves, the fasts may have been too. Paul says the opposite should be true: "whether you eat or drink… do all to the glory of God" (1 Cor 10:31).
- **"Were not these the words that the LORD proclaimed by the former prophets?" (v7).** Zechariah opened his book with the same appeal: "Do not be like your fathers, to whom the former prophets cried out" (1:4). The message is not new. Isaiah had said nearly the same thing about fasting two centuries before: "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness… to share your bread with the hungry?" (Isa 58:5–7). And the warning was given while Jerusalem was still "inhabited and prosperous," when the South (the *Negev*, the dry land toward Egypt) and the lowland (the *Shephelah*, the foothills toward the coast) were full of towns. Most of those towns were still empty in Zechariah's day.
- **"Render true judgments, show kindness and mercy to one another" (v9).** Here is the heart of what the former prophets said, in four commands. "True judgments" means honest courts that cannot be bribed. "Kindness" is *chesed*, the steadfast, loyal love God shows to his covenant people and expects them to show each other. "Mercy" is *rachamim*, from the word for womb, a mother's tender compassion. Micah summed it up the same way: "to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God" (Mic 6:8).
- **"Do not oppress the widow, the fatherless, the sojourner, or the poor" (v10).** These four groups had no one to defend them in court and no land or family to fall back on. The Law repeatedly singles them out (Exod 22:21–24; Deut 24:17–22), and the prophets measured Israel's faithfulness by how it treated them (Isa 1:17; Jer 7:5–7; 22:3). "Let none of you devise evil against another in your heart" goes deeper than action, to the plans formed in private.
- **"They refused to pay attention and turned a stubborn shoulder and stopped their ears" (v11).** The picture is an ox that jerks its shoulder away from the yoke (Neh 9:29; Hos 4:16), and a person putting fingers in their ears. The refusal grows step by step: they would not listen, then they pulled away, then they blocked out the sound, then they hardened their hearts.
- **"They made their hearts diamond-hard" (v12).** The Hebrew *shamir* is an extremely hard stone, probably emery or flint, used to cut and engrave other stones. Jeremiah said Judah's sin was written "with a point of diamond" on the tablet of their heart (Jer 17:1, same word). God gave Ezekiel a forehead "like emery harder than flint" to stand against such people (Ezek 3:9). Note "they made." The hardness was their own choice.
- **"The law and the words that the LORD of hosts had sent by his Spirit through the former prophets" (v12).** A clear Old Testament statement of how prophecy worked: the words came from the LORD, by his Spirit, through human prophets. Peter says the same: "men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Pet 1:21). To ignore the prophets was to ignore God.
- **"As I called, and they would not hear, so they called, and I would not hear" (v13).** A punishment that fits the sin exactly. Proverbs warns that those who refuse wisdom's call will one day call and not be answered (Prov 1:24–28). The verse also explains the people's original question. Their years of weeping and fasting had not been heard because the deeper call, to justice and mercy, had been refused.
- **"I scattered them with a whirlwind among all the nations that they had not known" (v14).** The exile came as Moses warned it would: "the LORD will scatter you among all peoples" (Deut 28:64). The whirlwind (*sa'ar*) is a violent storm that tears things apart and scatters them suddenly. **"The pleasant land was made desolate"**: the land God had given as a delight (Jer 3:19; Ps 106:24) was emptied, and the fault was not the land's or even Babylon's, but Judah's.
- **The point for the returned exiles.** God has not yet said whether to keep the fast. He has said something more important: the fasts were remembering the effects of a disaster whose cause was disobedience. If the new community repeats the sin, the new temple will not protect them. The answer to "Should we fast?" begins with "Will you obey?"
- **Commentators:** All three see the rebuke aimed at empty ritual, not at fasting itself. Henry: "Did you at all fast unto me, even to me?" He says reform of life "would be the best way of fasting," and pictures the people "like a bullock unaccustomed to the yoke," who "pulled away the shoulder." He insists the people's own wickedness, more than the Chaldeans, ruined the land. Guzik lists the four exile fasts (fourth, fifth, seventh and tenth months) and points out that they were human traditions that repetition had made seem sacred. He presses the application that dwelling on past tragedy can become a kind of self-pity rather than seeking God, and that hardness of heart comes gradually. JFB reads "hear" in v7 as meaning "do," and notes the fitting punishment of v13, prayers unheard as warnings went unheeded. The Tyndale notes allow that the seventh-month fast could be the Day of Atonement rather than Gedaliah's death. Most commentators prefer Gedaliah, since the fasts in 8:19 are all tied to the fall of Jerusalem.

#### Pointing to Christ
When people asked Jesus why his disciples did not fast, he said, "Can the wedding guests mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them?" (Matt 9:15). Fasting is for the time of waiting. Zechariah's hearers were told to look past their rituals to the heart God wanted. Jesus did exactly that: he warned against fasting to be seen (Matt 6:16–18), and he summed up the Law and the Prophets as love for God and love for neighbor (Matt 22:37–40). He lived the four commands of v9 perfectly, defending widows, welcoming outsiders and lifting the poor. And where Judah's hearts were made "diamond-hard," God promised through Ezekiel to "remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh" (Ezek 36:26). That new heart is what Jesus gives by his Spirit. Chapter 8 will turn the mourning into feasting, and the gospel explains how.
