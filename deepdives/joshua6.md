*Setting:* A week or so after the Jordan crossing, in spring. Israel is camped at Gilgal, newly circumcised, fresh from its first Passover in the land, and Joshua has just met the commander of the LORD's army with a drawn sword (5:13–15). Ahead stands Jericho, an oasis city by a strong spring, guarding the roads up into the hill country. By modern standards it was small, the walled area only around six to nine acres, but it sat on a high mound behind a massive stone revetment wall topped by mud-brick walls. Israel had no siege engines, no ladders, no experience taking cities. The chapter break hides the fact that 6:2 continues the meeting with the commander: the one who told Joshua to take off his sandals now gives the battle plan.

#### The Fall of Jericho (vv1–27)
- **"Jericho was shut up inside and outside" (v1).** The Hebrew doubles the verb: tightly shut and shut tight. "None went out, and none came in." The city had chosen a siege. It was not seeking peace (compare 11:19–20).
- **"See, I have given Jericho into your hand" (v2).** The verb is in the completed tense, as in 1:3. God speaks of the victory as already done before a step is taken.
- **The plan (vv3–5).** March around once a day for six days, then seven times on the seventh day, then a long trumpet blast, a shout, and the wall would fall. There was nothing military about it. It was more like a religious procession than an assault, and it forced Israel to win in a way that left no doubt who had won.
- **"Seven priests… seven trumpets… seventh day… seven times" (v4).** Seven runs through the chapter, more than a dozen times in all. In Scripture seven speaks of completeness, and it recalls the seven days of creation and the Sabbath. The work belongs to God, finished on his schedule.
- **"Trumpets of rams' horns" (v4).** The *shofar*, a curved ram's horn, was not a musical instrument but a signal horn, used to call assemblies, sound alarms and announce God's presence. It sounded at Sinai when God came down (Exod 19:13, 16, 19). Here the phrase is literally "shofars of the *yobel*." *Yobel* means a ram, and from the ram's-horn blast that opened it, the fiftieth year was called the *yobel*, which became our word "jubilee" (Lev 25:9–10). The same horn that announced liberty for Israel announced judgment on Jericho.
- **The ark at the center (vv6–9).** The order of march was: armed men, seven priests blowing horns, the ark, then the rear guard. The ark is mentioned again and again, usually as "the ark of the LORD." The point is that the LORD himself is circling the city. In v8 the priests go "before the LORD," as if the ark and the LORD are spoken of as one.
- **"You shall not shout… neither shall any word go out of your mouth" (v10).** For six days, thirteen circuits in all, the army said nothing. Only the horns sounded. Silence kept the people from boasting, grumbling, or arguing about the plan, the very sins of their parents' generation. It also would have been unnerving to the people on the walls.
- **"They came into the camp and spent the night" (v11).** Each day ended with nothing to show for it. Patience was part of the obedience.
- **"Joshua rose early" (v12).** Again the phrase from 3:1. Six days of the same routine, done with the same care.
- **"On the seventh day… seven times" (v15).** Walking a circuit around a town of this size might take twenty or thirty minutes, so seven circuits was a long morning. Since the march lasted seven days, one of them must have fallen on a Sabbath. Jewish tradition held it was the seventh, the day of the fall. The text does not say which day it was.
- **"Shout, for the LORD has given you the city" (v16).** The shout was not a magic trick to crack walls. It was a battle cry of faith, shouted before anything happened, praising God for a victory he had promised. In Israel the shout (*teru'ah*) was also a cry of worship and joy before the LORD (Ps 47:5).
- **"Devoted to the LORD for destruction" (v17).** The Hebrew word is *cherem*, "the ban." Something under the ban was given over wholly to God and taken out of human use, usually by destruction. Other peoples of the region practiced it too: the Moabite king Mesha, about 840 BC, boasted on a stone monument that he "devoted" an Israelite town to his god Chemosh. In Israel the ban meant the soldiers could not keep any plunder. Jericho was the firstfruits of the land, and firstfruits belong to God (Exod 23:19).
- **"Only Rahab the prostitute… shall live" (v17).** In the middle of the ban, a promise is kept. Joshua names her before the walls fall.
- **"Keep yourselves from the things devoted" (v18).** Anyone who took what belonged to God would bring the ban on himself and make "the camp of Israel a thing for destruction." "Bring trouble" is *'akar*, the word that will name the valley of Achor in the next chapter (7:25–26). The warning is setting up what comes next.
- **"All silver and gold… are holy to the LORD" (v19).** Metals that could survive fire were not destroyed but given to the LORD's treasury, for the tabernacle.
- **"The wall fell down flat" (v20).** Literally "fell in its place" or "fell beneath itself." The people went up "every man straight before him," with no need to search for a breach. Hebrews 11:30 gives the cause: "By faith the walls of Jericho fell down after they had been encircled for seven days."
- **What does archaeology show?** Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) has been excavated many times. In the 1930s John Garstang found collapsed walls and a burned city and dated them to about 1400 BC, which fits the traditional date. In the 1950s Kathleen Kenyon redated that destruction to about 1550 BC and concluded there was little or no walled city in Joshua's time. In 1990 Bryant Wood re-examined the pottery and argued again for about 1400 BC. The debate is not settled, and the site was badly eroded over the centuries. Some findings fit the story strikingly: mud-brick walls that seem to have tumbled outward to the base of the mound, a city destroyed by fire, and storage jars still full of grain, which suggests a short siege just after harvest and a city not plundered for food (compare v24 and 6:18). Believers hold the text as true while the archaeologists keep arguing.
- **"Both men and women, young and old" (v21).** This is the hardest verse in the chapter, and it should not be softened. Several things in Scripture frame it. God had waited more than four hundred years, "for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Gen 15:16). Canaanite religion included child sacrifice and ritual prostitution (Lev 18:21–28; Deut 12:31), and the land itself is pictured as vomiting out its people. The command was limited to these cities in this time, not a standing license for war (Deut 20:10–18). Israel itself fell under the same judgment when it sinned, as Achan's family discovers in the next chapter, and the nation later went into exile for the same sins. And Rahab shows the door was open: anyone in Jericho who believed what she believed could have done what she did. Faithful Christians still wrestle with this passage, and some read the sweeping language ("all," "men and women") as the standard war rhetoric of the ancient world, which often described victories in total terms. All agree the text presents it as God's judgment, not Israel's cruelty.
- **"Go into the prostitute's house and bring out… the woman" (v22).** If the wall fell, how did her house in the wall survive? The text does not say. Perhaps only part of the wall collapsed, or her section stood. Some excavators have pointed to a stretch of the northern wall at Jericho that remained standing with houses against it, but this is disputed. The spies who swore the oath are the ones who keep it.
- **"Put them outside the camp of Israel" (v23).** For now they were outsiders, not yet cleansed or joined to the covenant people. In the law, people who were ceremonially unclean, such as soldiers returning from battle, stayed outside the camp for a time (Num 31:19). That would change.
- **"They burned the city with fire" (v24).** Like the destruction of Sodom, a whole city given up to fire. Only the metals were kept for "the house of the LORD," meaning the tabernacle treasury.
- **"She has lived in Israel to this day" (v25).** Rahab moved from outside the camp to inside Israel. "To this day" means she was still alive when this was first written, or her family still lived among Israel. She married Salmon and became the mother of Boaz, the great-grandmother of David, and an ancestor of Jesus (Matt 1:5).
- **"Cursed before the LORD be the man who… rebuilds this city" (v26).** The curse is in poetic form. It does not forbid living at the site; it forbids rebuilding it as a fortified city with foundations and gates. It came true about five hundred years later, in the days of Ahab, when "Hiel of Bethel built Jericho. He laid its foundation at the cost of Abiram his firstborn, and set up its gates at the cost of his youngest son Segub" (1 Kings 16:34). Whether Hiel's sons died by sudden judgment or by child sacrifice in the foundations, a known pagan practice, the text does not say.
- **"So the LORD was with Joshua, and his fame was in all the land" (v27).** The promise of 1:5 and 3:7 is kept. Joshua's fame is God's doing.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the shout "a shout of faith; they believed that the walls of Jericho would fall," and on the silence writes, "it does not become us to speak when God is speaking." On the six days with no result: "As promised deliverances must be expected in God's way, so they must be expected in his time." JFB notes that the Hebrew calls the instruments "horns of jubilee," and the lexicon agrees: *yobel* means ram, and the jubilee year took its name from the horn. JFB records the Jewish tradition that the city fell on a Sabbath and says the curse on Jericho's rebuilder was fulfilled about 550 years later. Guzik counts the sevens in the chapter and sees in the daily, silent marching a test of patient faith, and perhaps a last chance for Jericho to repent. He faces the destruction directly, arguing from Genesis 15:16 that God waited centuries before judging, and that the Judge of all the earth has the right to judge.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jericho fell to faith, not force. The people walked where God told them, kept silent when told to keep silent, and shouted before the walls moved, because God had said, "I have given." Hebrews 11:30–31 sets the two halves of the chapter side by side: "By faith the walls of Jericho fell down… By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient." The same day brought judgment to a city and rescue to a household. That is the shape of the gospel. The great trumpet will sound once more, at the end (1 Thess 4:16; Rev 11:15), and those found sheltering under the sign God has given will be brought out safe, as Rahab was, through the one who was born of her line.
