*Setting:* Israel is still camped at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Chapters 17–26 are often called the "Holiness Code," and chapter 19 is its heart. Chapter 18 drew the lines around family and sexual life, and chapter 20 will give the penalties. Between them comes this chapter, a wide-ranging picture of what a holy people looks like in the field, the market, the courtroom and the home. It reads like a fresh walk through the Ten Commandments, applied to ordinary days, and it contains the verse Jesus called the second greatest commandment.

#### The LORD Is Holy (vv1–8)
- **"Speak to all the congregation" (v2).** Most of Leviticus so far was for priests. This chapter is addressed to everyone. Holiness is not a job for specialists.
- **"You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (v2).** The Hebrew word *qadosh* carries the idea of being set apart, different, belonging to God. God's holiness is his utter otherness and moral purity. Israel's holiness is to reflect his, and the rest of the chapter shows what that means in practice. Peter quotes this verse to Christians: "You shall be holy, for I am holy" (1 Pet 1:15–16).
- **"I am the LORD" as a refrain.** The phrase, sometimes with "your God," closes section after section, about fifteen times. Each law rests not on custom or convenience but on who God is. The chapter's last verse ends the same way.
- **Mother and father, and Sabbaths (v3).** This picks up the fifth and fourth commandments (Exod 20:8–12), in reverse order. Unusually, the mother is named first. "Revere" is the word usually used for fearing God, which shows how seriously parents were to be honored.
- **"Do not turn to idols" (v4).** The word for idols, *elilim*, sounds like the word for God (*Elohim*) but comes from a root meaning weak or worthless. BDB gives "worthless gods." The prophets use it with scorn (Isa 2:8; Ps 96:5): next to the living God, idols are nothings.
- **Peace offerings eaten on time (vv5–8).** The peace offering (ch 3; 7:11–18) was a shared meal with God. The meat had to be eaten within two days, and anything left until the third day was **"tainted"** (*piggul*, something foul or abhorrent). The point was not only hygiene in a hot climate. A sacred meal was to be eaten as a sacred meal, not hoarded as leftovers, and eating it late "profaned what is holy."
- **Commentators:** Guzik paraphrases holiness as separation, being like God and apart from what is unlike him, and says honoring parents is a training ground for honoring God. His point that "idols" means "nothings" fits BDB's "worthlessness," though the root is uncertain. He reads the peace-offering rule as God refusing stale fellowship. JFB thinks the third-day rule also kept anyone from attaching "mysterious virtue" to meat from the altar. Henry sees in the whole chapter an exposition of "most of the ten commandments."

#### Love Your Neighbor as Yourself (vv9–18)
- **Leave the edges and the gleanings (vv9–10).** Farmers were not to harvest to the very edge of the field, pick up dropped stalks, strip the vines bare or gather fallen grapes. What remained was **"for the poor and for the sojourner."** This was not a handout. The poor worked for their food, with dignity. The book of Ruth shows the law in action: a widowed foreigner gleaning in Boaz's field, which leads her into the line of David and of Christ (Ruth 2; Matt 1:5).
- **Stealing, lying and false oaths (vv11–12).** The eighth, ninth and third commandments are woven together. To swear falsely by God's name is to **"profane the name of your God,"** using him as cover for a lie.
- **"The wages of a hired worker shall not remain with you all night" (v13).** A day laborer owned no land and lived hand to mouth. Holding back his pay until morning meant his family went without supper. Deuteronomy 24:15 says the same, and James later warns rich landowners that the withheld wages "are crying out" to the Lord (Jas 5:4).
- **The deaf and the blind (v14).** Cursing a deaf man is safe because he cannot hear it. Tripping a blind man is safe because he cannot see who did it. **"But you shall fear your God."** God hears and sees what the victim cannot. Deuteronomy 27:18 puts a curse on anyone who misleads the blind.
- **"Not be partial to the poor or defer to the great" (v15).** Courts must not tilt toward the rich, but neither may they tilt toward the poor out of pity. Justice is blind in both directions (Exod 23:3, 6).
- **"Not go around as a slanderer" (v16).** The word pictures a peddler going from house to house, but trading in gossip. **"Stand up against the life of your neighbor"** probably means testifying falsely when a life is at stake, or standing idle while his life is in danger. Both readings are found.
- **"You shall not hate your brother in your heart, but you shall reason frankly" (v17).** The law reaches inside. Hatred nursed in silence is forbidden, and the remedy is honest, direct rebuke (*yakach*, to correct or reason with). **"Lest you incur sin because of him"** means either "so you don't share in his guilt" or "so you don't sin by your own resentment." Jesus gives the same pattern: go and tell him his fault between you and him alone (Matt 18:15).
- **"Love your neighbor as yourself" (v18).** This is the summit of the chapter. In its setting, "neighbor" means a fellow Israelite, "the sons of your own people." But verse 34 extends the same love to the foreigner. When a lawyer asked Jesus "who is my neighbor?" Jesus answered with a Samaritan, a hated outsider, who showed the love this verse requires (Luke 10:25–37). Jesus named this the second commandment, like the first (Matt 22:39), and Paul says it fulfills the whole law (Rom 13:9; Gal 5:14). "As yourself" is not a command to love yourself first. It assumes people already look after their own interests and tells them to give others the same care.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the gleaning law "the earliest law for the benefit of the poor that we read of in the code of any people," and reads "neighbour" as "synonymous with 'fellow creature.'" Henry on wages: to defer them to a worker's damage is "a sin that cries to heaven for vengeance." On v14: "God sees and hears, though they do not." On v17: "Love covers sin from others, but not from the sinner himself." Guzik adds that the ban on vengeance is about personal relationships and does not remove the courts' role (Rom 12:19; 13:1–7), and that a grudge mostly harms the one who carries it.

#### You Shall Keep My Statutes (vv19–37)
- **Two kinds (v19).** No crossbreeding animals, sowing two seeds in one field, or wearing cloth woven of two materials. Deuteronomy 22:11 names wool and linen. The reason is not stated. The Tyndale notes connect it to the order of creation, where God made each thing "according to its kind" (Gen 1), and to Israel's own call to stay distinct from the nations. Some point out that a mixed wool-and-linen fabric was used in the tabernacle and in the priests' garments (Exod 26:1; 39:29), which would make it a holy fabric not for common use. These laws were living lessons in separation, and the New Testament does not carry them over as rules for Christians.
- **The betrothed slave woman (vv20–22).** A man sleeps with a slave woman promised to another man. Had she been free, this would be adultery with a betrothed woman, a capital crime (Deut 22:23–24). Because she was not free and may have had little power to refuse, **"a distinction shall be made."** The Hebrew word is rare, and some translate it "an inquiry" or "compensation." Either way, no one is executed, but the man is still guilty before God and must bring a guilt offering. The law takes the weaker party's situation into account without treating the sin as nothing.
- **Fruit trees for three years (vv23–25).** The ESV's "regard its fruit as forbidden" is literally "count its fruit as uncircumcised." For three years the fruit was off limits. In the fourth year it was **"holy, an offering of praise"** given to God as firstfruits, and in the fifth year the family could eat. Pinching off early fruit actually strengthens a young tree, so the law was good farming. It also taught patience and that the first of everything belongs to God, with a promise attached: **"to increase its yield for you."**
- **Blood, omens and fortune-telling (v26).** Eating blood was forbidden because "the life of the flesh is in the blood" (17:11). Divination tried to read the future through signs, outside God's word. God had given his own ways of guidance (the Urim and Thummim, Exod 28:30, and later his prophets), and Israel was to trust them.
- **Hair, beard, cuts and tattoos (vv27–28).** Shaving the sides of the head, cutting the edges of the beard and gashing the body were mourning rites among Israel's neighbors, often linked to the cult of the dead. The prophets of Baal cut themselves to get their god's attention (1 Kings 18:28). **"Tattoo"** translates *qaaqa*, a word found only here. BDB gives "incision, imprintment, tattoo." In context it is probably marks made for the dead or for a god. Christians differ on how far this applies to modern tattoos. Most read it as aimed at pagan mourning and religious marks, while some take it more broadly. The principle is clear: God's people were not to carry the signs of other gods on their bodies.
- **"Do not profane your daughter by making her a prostitute" (v29).** Poverty or pagan religion could drive a father to this. Canaanite fertility cults used temple prostitutes. A father who did it would corrupt not only his daughter but the whole land.
- **Sabbaths and sanctuary (v30).** This repeats v3 and joins holy time with holy place. Both belong to God.
- **Mediums and necromancers (v31).** "Necromancers" (*yiddeoni*) comes from the root "to know": people claiming secret knowledge, often from the dead. King Saul, who had banned them, later went to the medium of Endor in despair (1 Sam 28). Isaiah asks the right question: "Should they inquire of the dead on behalf of the living?" (Isa 8:19).
- **"Stand up before the gray head" (v32).** Rising when an elderly person entered was a sign of respect. Once again it is joined to **"fear your God."** Proverbs calls gray hair "a crown of glory" (Prov 16:31).
- **"Love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt" (vv33–34).** The "sojourner" was a foreigner living in Israel without land or family support, among the most vulnerable people. The same words used for the neighbor in v18 are now used for him. Israel's own past as oppressed foreigners was to shape how they treated foreigners (Exod 23:9; Deut 10:19).
- **Just balances and weights (vv35–36).** Merchants could cheat with two sets of weights, one heavy for buying and one light for selling. An **ephah** was a dry measure of about two-thirds of a bushel, and a **hin** a liquid measure of about a gallon. The chapter's last law ties honesty at the market to the exodus: **"I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt."** The God who rescued them from injustice will not let them practice it (Prov 11:1; Amos 8:5).
- **Commentators:** JFB offers several explanations for the mixed-kinds law, including opposition to pagan practice, and quotes the scholar Michaelis on the fruit-tree law: "The wisdom of this law is very striking." It calls the mourning cuts acts meant to placate the gods of the dead and the marks "signs of apostasy." JFB has no note on vv20, 32 or 36. Guzik reads the mixtures and the hair and beard rules as tied to pagan magic and mourning, says the specific rules no longer bind Christians, and draws the principle that believers should not take on marks that identify them with pagan religion. He also suggests the man in vv20–22 would be expected to make things right with the woman. Henry groups the chapter around the two tables of the law, holiness toward God (v2) and love toward neighbor (v18), with vv33–34 answering the question, "Who is my neighbour?"

#### Pointing to Christ
"You shall be holy, for I am holy" and "love your neighbor as yourself" sit at the center of this chapter, and Jesus put them at the center of everything (Matt 22:37–40). He is the only one who lived both completely: holy toward his Father, and loving every neighbor, the poor gleaner, the deaf and blind, the foreigner, even his enemies. When a lawyer tried to narrow "neighbor," Jesus told of a Samaritan who stopped on the road, and he himself is the true Good Samaritan, who came to people who were strangers and enemies and paid for their healing at his own cost. Those who belong to him are given his Spirit, whose fruit is the love that "is the fulfilling of the law" (Rom 13:10).
