*Setting:* The temple courts in Jerusalem, Tuesday of Passover week, spring of AD 30, three days before the crucifixion. On Sunday Jesus entered the city as king; on Monday he drove the traders from the temple. Now the leaders have challenged his authority (21:23–27), and he has answered with two parables aimed at them: the two sons and the wicked tenants (21:28–46). They want to arrest him but fear the crowds. Chapter 22 continues the same day. Jesus tells a third parable of judgment, and then each party in turn tries to trap him with a question: the Pharisees with the Herodians, the Sadducees, and a Pharisee expert in the law. He answers every one, and ends by asking a question of his own that no one can answer.

#### The Parable of the Wedding Feast (vv1–14)
- **"A king who gave a wedding feast for his son" (v2).** Jesus has just told a parable about a son killed by tenants. Now the son is honored at a feast. The great banquet was a familiar Jewish picture of the age to come (Isa 25:6–8; Matt 8:11), and the wedding of God with his people runs through the prophets (Isa 54:5; Hos 2:19–20). Here the King is God, the Son is Jesus, and the feast is the kingdom. Luke has a similar parable of a great supper told at a different meal (Luke 14:15–24), but Matthew's version is sharper and ends in judgment.
- **"Sent his servants to call those who were invited" (v3).** In that culture guests were invited ahead of time and then, when the meal was ready, called again by servants (Esth 5:8; 6:14). These guests had already accepted. To refuse at the second call was a public insult. "Those who were invited" stand for Israel, invited through the prophets and now called by John, Jesus and the apostles.
- **"My oxen and my fat calves have been slaughtered" (v4).** Meat was rare in an ordinary diet, so this was a lavish feast. "Everything is ready." The king himself has paid for it all.
- **"One to his farm, another to his business" (v5).** Most of the guests are not hostile, only uninterested. Their excuses are ordinary, which is the warning: ordinary concerns can keep people from God's kingdom as surely as open hatred.
- **"Seized his servants, treated them shamefully, and killed them" (v6).** This goes beyond what a guest would do, and it shows the story pointing to real events, the treatment of God's messengers (21:35–36; 23:34–37).
- **"He sent his troops and destroyed those murderers and burned their city" (v7).** The scene breaks the frame of a dinner story. A meal is waiting while an army goes out to war. Most interpreters see here a prophecy of the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, when the city and the temple were burned (24:2). Jesus said these words some forty years before it happened.
- **"Go therefore to the main roads" (v9).** The phrase is literally "the partings of the ways," where a road out of town branches into the countryside, a place where travelers of every kind could be found. The call now goes to everyone, a picture of the gospel going to the nations (28:19).
- **"Both bad and good" (v10).** The church gathers a mixed company, as in the parable of the net that brings in good and bad fish (13:47–50). The invitation does not depend on the guests being good.
- **"A man who had no wedding garment" (v11).** There is little solid evidence that kings in Jesus' day handed out festive robes at the door, though some suggest it. The point is plain anyway: the man came in on his own terms, unprepared and unchanged. His clothes showed he did not take the king or his son seriously. In Scripture clothing is often a picture of the righteousness God gives: "he has clothed me with the garments of salvation; he has covered me with the robe of righteousness" (Isa 61:10; Zech 3:3–5; Rev 19:8).
- **What the garment means.** Christians read the garment in two main ways. Many in the Reformed tradition see it as the righteousness of Christ given by faith, which no one can supply for himself. Others see it as the righteous life that true faith produces (Rev 19:8 calls fine linen "the righteous deeds of the saints"). The two belong together: the garment is a gift, but it is meant to be worn.
- **"Friend, how did you get in here…?" And he was speechless (v12).** "Friend" is the same cool address as in 20:13 and to Judas in 26:50. "Speechless" is *phimoō*, literally "muzzled." He has no excuse. The same verb is used in v34 when Jesus "silenced" the Sadducees.
- **"Cast him into the outer darkness… weeping and gnashing of teeth" (v13).** A palace feast at night would be lit by lamps. Outside was black darkness. Matthew uses "weeping and gnashing of teeth" six times for final judgment (8:12; 13:42, 50; 24:51; 25:30).
- **"For many are called, but few are chosen" (v14).** "Called" here means invited, as throughout the parable, not the effective call Paul describes in Romans 8:30. Many hear the invitation; few truly respond and belong to God. The verse holds together human responsibility (the guests refused) and God's choosing, without explaining how they fit.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that God "has not only provided food, but a royal feast, for the perishing souls of his rebellious creatures," and on the garment, "No man has the wedding-garment by nature, or can form it for himself." JFB agrees that the garment is "something which they bring not of their own," and ties it to Zephaniah 1:7–8 and Isaiah 61:10. JFB identifies the troops of v7 as the Romans and the city as Jerusalem. Guzik notes that there is little evidence for a custom of handing out robes, and that indifference, hostility and an unchanged heart all end in the same place, outside. He draws out that the gospel is a finished feast to receive, not a meal to cook.

#### Paying Taxes to Caesar (vv15–22)
- **"The Pharisees went and plotted how to entangle him in his words" (v15).** "Entangle" is a hunting word for catching in a snare.
- **"Their disciples… along with the Herodians" (v16).** The Herodians supported Herod's family and so, indirectly, Roman rule. The Pharisees resented Rome and its taxes. They were natural enemies, joined here against Jesus (Mark 3:6). The flattery that follows is true, but they mean none of it.
- **"Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?" (v17).** The tax was the Roman poll tax (*kēnsos*, from Latin *census*), a yearly head tax paid directly to Rome since AD 6, when Judea came under a Roman governor. That year Judas the Galilean led a revolt against it, saying that paying it meant accepting a pagan master in place of God (Acts 5:37; Josephus). The question was a trap either way. If Jesus said "pay," he would lose the crowds, who hated the tax. If he said "don't pay," the Herodians could report him to Pilate as a rebel. In fact the leaders later accused him of exactly that (Luke 23:2).
- **"Why put me to the test, you hypocrites?" (v18).** "Hypocrites" (*hypokritai*) were stage actors wearing masks. Their praise was a mask over malice.
- **"Show me the coin for the tax." And they brought him a denarius (v19).** Jesus does not seem to have one. They do, in the temple courts. The silver denarius of Tiberius, the emperor at the time, showed his head with the inscription "Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus, Augustus," and on the back "high priest." To many Jews it was an offensive coin, an image of a man with claims of divinity (Exod 20:4). By producing it they show they already use Caesar's money.
- **"Whose likeness and inscription is this?" (v20).** "Likeness" is *eikōn*, "image," the word used in the Greek Old Testament for humanity made "in the image of God" (Gen 1:26–27).
- **"Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (v21).** "Render" (*apodidōmi*) means to give back what is owed, to pay a debt. The coin bears Caesar's image, so give it back to him. Government has a real, God-given place (Rom 13:1–7; 1 Pet 2:13–17), and Jesus did not join the rebels. But the second half is the greater claim. If the coin bears Caesar's image, what bears God's? People do. Caesar may have the coin, but God claims the whole person, Caesar included. When Caesar demands what belongs only to God, such as worship, the apostles' answer stands: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
- **"When they heard it, they marveled. And they left him" (v22).** They are amazed and walk away unchanged.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus' disciples "were taught that the Christian religion is no enemy to civil government." Guzik stresses that "render" carries the sense of paying back what is owed, which the lexicon confirms, and draws the point that since people bear God's image, they owe him themselves. The Tyndale notes add that the coin's image was offensive to many Jews, and that some Roman governors in Judea minted local coins without human faces out of respect for that. JFB defers this section to its notes on Mark 12.

#### Sadducees Ask About the Resurrection (vv23–33)
- **"Sadducees… who say that there is no resurrection" (v23).** The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy who controlled the temple. They accepted the five books of Moses as authoritative and rejected the oral traditions the Pharisees added. They denied the resurrection, angels and spirits (Acts 23:8), and life after death in any real sense. Earlier they joined the Pharisees in demanding a sign (16:1). This is the only time in Matthew they come with a question of their own.
- **"His brother must marry the widow and raise up offspring for his brother" (v24).** This is levirate marriage, from Latin *levir*, "brother-in-law" (Deut 25:5–10; compare Gen 38 and Ruth 4). It kept a dead man's name and land in his family.
- **Seven brothers and one wife (vv25–28).** The story is meant to make resurrection look absurd. If all seven rise, whose wife will she be? There may be an echo of the book of Tobit, in which a woman named Sarah lost seven husbands (Tob 3:8), a story popular at the time. The Sadducees likely used this riddle on the Pharisees often.
- **"You know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God" (v29).** Two errors. They do not understand what Scripture teaches, and they cannot imagine that God can make a life different from this one. They assume the age to come must be a copy of this age.
- **"In the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (v30).** Marriage belongs to this age, where death means families need children to continue. In the resurrection, there is no more death (Luke 20:36), so the purpose that levirate marriage served falls away. Jesus does not say that people become angels, or that they lose their identity or their love for one another. He says they are "like angels" in this one respect: they do not marry. Then he slips in a second correction: the Sadducees did not believe in angels either.
- **"I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (v32).** Jesus answers from Exodus 3:6, from the books of Moses the Sadducees accepted. God spoke these words to Moses at the burning bush, centuries after the patriarchs died. He did not say "I *was* their God," but "I *am*." The argument rests on the covenant: God bound himself to these men, and God's faithfulness does not end at a grave. If he is still their God, they are still alive to him, and he will raise them. **"He is not God of the dead, but of the living."**
- **"They were astonished at his teaching" (v33).** Even the crowd sees that the Sadducees' clever case has collapsed.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "All errors arise from not knowing the Scriptures and the power of God." Guzik notes that Jesus deliberately argued from the five books of Moses, the only part of Scripture the Sadducees fully accepted, and draws the lesson that knowing Scripture as a whole, not just isolated verses, guards against error. He adds that heaven's closeness with God will surpass any earthly relationship. The Tyndale notes stress that Jesus was not teaching that people will be without gender in the resurrection, only that marriage as part of this age will not continue.

#### The Great Commandment (vv34–40)
- **"When the Pharisees heard that he had silenced the Sadducees" (v34).** "Silenced" is *phimoō*, "muzzled," the same verb as v12. The Pharisees may have been glad to see their rivals beaten, but now they try again.
- **"A lawyer, asked him a question to test him" (v35).** A "lawyer" was an expert in the law of Moses, not a courtroom lawyer. Mark tells this story more warmly: the scribe agreed with Jesus, and Jesus told him, "You are not far from the kingdom of God" (Mark 12:28–34).
- **"Which is the great commandment in the Law?" (v36).** The rabbis counted 613 commandments in the law (248 positive and 365 negative) and debated which were "heavy" and which "light." Some said all were equal. Answering might draw Jesus into an argument either way.
- **"You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind" (v37).** Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:5, part of the *Shema* ("Hear, O Israel," Deut 6:4–9), which devout Jews recited twice a day. Deuteronomy has "heart… soul… might." Matthew has "heart… soul… mind." Mark has all four (Mark 12:30). The words are not meant to divide a person into parts. Together they mean the whole self.
- **"A second is like it: You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (v39).** Jesus adds Leviticus 19:18, which was not part of the *Shema*. Some Jewish writings of the period put love of God and love of neighbor side by side, but no one before Jesus is known to have made them the two great commandments in this way. "Like it" means of the same kind and weight. Love for God cannot be separated from love for neighbor (1 John 4:20–21). Jesus does not command self-love as a third duty. He assumes people already look after themselves, and calls them to give the same care to others.
- **"On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets" (v40).** "Depend" is literally "hang," as a door hangs on its hinges. All the other commands are ways of working out these two. Paul says the same: "love is the fulfilling of the law" (Rom 13:8–10; Gal 5:14).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls love for God "the first and great commandment, and the sum of all the commands of the first table" (the commands about God in the Ten Commandments). Guzik notes the rabbis' 613 commandments and their ranking, and reads "as yourself" as caring for others' interests as naturally as one's own, not a call to love oneself first. He sees these two loves as the clearest sign that God's life is truly at work in someone. JFB defers to Mark 12.

#### Whose Son Is the Christ? (vv41–46)
- **"What do you think about the Christ? Whose son is he?" (v42).** Now Jesus asks the question. "The son of David" was the standard answer, based on God's promise to David of a descendant whose throne would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16; Isa 11:1; Jer 23:5). It was correct, but incomplete.
- **"David, in the Spirit, calls him Lord" (v43).** Jesus takes for granted that David wrote Psalm 110 (its title says so) and that he wrote it by the Holy Spirit, which his hearers also accepted.
- **"The Lord said to my Lord" (v44).** In Hebrew the first "Lord" is *YHWH*, God's personal name, and the second is *adoni*, "my lord" or "my master." God speaks to David's Lord and invites him to sit at his right hand, the place of supreme authority, until every enemy is defeated. Psalm 110 is the Old Testament passage most often quoted or echoed in the New Testament (Acts 2:34–35; 1 Cor 15:25; Heb 1:13; 10:12–13).
- **"If then David calls him Lord, how is he his son?" (v45).** In that culture a father would never call his son "lord." So the Messiah must be more than David's descendant. He is also David's Lord. The answer Jesus leaves unspoken is that the Messiah is both human and divine, son of David by birth (1:1) and Son of God (3:17; 16:16; Rom 1:3–4). Some Jewish interpreters, then and later, read Psalm 110 of Abraham or David himself, but Jesus' reading assumes the messianic view his opponents also held.
- **"No one was able to answer him a word, nor from that day did anyone dare to ask him any more questions" (v46).** The debates are over. The leaders have tried every trap and been silenced. From here they turn from argument to arrest. In chapter 23 Jesus speaks against them, and in chapter 26 Psalm 110 returns, when he tells the high priest, "you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power" (26:64).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that in Psalm 110 David "allows the Messiah to be the Son of God, and David's Lord equally with the Father." Guzik notes that Psalm 110 is the most quoted Old Testament passage in the New, and stresses that salvation depends on Jesus being both fully human and fully God. The Tyndale notes suggest no one could answer because no one had imagined the Messiah would be God come in the flesh. JFB defers these verses to Mark 12.

#### Pointing to Christ
On the day his enemies tried hardest to trap him, Jesus answered each of them and then showed them who he was. He is the Son for whom the King has prepared the feast, the one who calls people from every road and clothes them for his table. He gives Caesar what is his and claims for God the people who bear God's image. He is the living God's answer to death, since the God of Abraham does not leave his people in the grave. He perfectly kept the two great commands, loving his Father and loving his neighbors all the way to the cross. And he is David's son and David's Lord, about to be enthroned at God's right hand, though first he must be rejected by the builders.
