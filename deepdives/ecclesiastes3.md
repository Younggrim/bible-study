*Setting:* The Preacher has tested pleasure, great works, wisdom and toil, and each one proved *hebel*, a breath that cannot be held (ch 2). Chapter 2 ended with the first glimpse of something better: ordinary joys received "from the hand of God" (2:24–26). Chapter 3 widens the view from one man's life to the pattern of all life. It opens with the most famous poem in the book, fourteen pairs of opposites, each with its "time" (vv1–8). Then the Preacher asks what that means for us: God has made everything beautiful in its time and put eternity into our hearts, yet we cannot see the whole (vv9–15). Finally he faces injustice and death and asks whether a person really has any advantage over an animal (vv16–22). For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### A Time for Everything (vv1–8)
- **"For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven" (v1).** "Season" (*zeman*) is an appointed time, a set date. "Time" (*'et*) is the right moment, the occasion. The word *'et* appears twenty-nine times in these eight verses. "Matter" (*chephets*) is literally "delight" or "desire," so it can also mean "every purpose" or "every thing a person wants to do." The Tyndale notes read the poem as wisdom: the wise person knows that each thing has its time and learns to recognize it (8:5). Opportunities come and go (Eph 5:16).
- **The shape of the poem.** Fourteen pairs, twenty-eight items, arranged in seven lines of two pairs each. Seven and its double are the Bible's numbers of completeness. The poem begins with birth and death, the two times no one chooses (v2), and ends with war and peace (v8). It moves from the whole of life, to work, to the heart, to the nation. Sometimes the welcome thing comes first and sometimes the painful one, with no fixed order, as if to say both will come.
- **"A time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up what is planted" (v2).** No one picks the day of birth, and no one controls the day of death (8:8). Between them, a farmer must plant in spring and harvest in season. Plant out of season and nothing grows. JFB makes that point.
- **"A time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to break down, and a time to build up" (v3).** "Kill" here (*harag*) is not the word used in the commandment "You shall not murder" (*ratsach*, Exod 20:13). The Tyndale notes mention war and capital punishment, as in Genesis 9:6. Wright, cited by Guzik, makes the same distinction. JFB says such killing is "never" from malice. Breaking down and building up may refer to buildings, cities or even nations (Jer 1:10).
- **"A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance" (v4).** Funerals and weddings. In Israel mourning had its customs: torn clothes, sackcloth, wailing, fasting. Dancing marked feasts and victories (Exod 15:20; 2 Sam 6:14). Jesus used the same pairs: "We played the flute for you, and you did not dance; we sang a dirge, and you did not mourn" (Matt 11:17).
- **"A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together" (v5).** The meaning is uncertain. Several readings exist. A farmer clears stones from a field before planting (Isa 5:2), and a builder gathers them to build. Armies threw stones on enemy fields to ruin them (2 Kgs 3:19, 25). An old Jewish interpretation (the Midrash) took it as a polite way of speaking about marital relations, which would fit the next line: "a time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing." Paul speaks of times for married couples to refrain (1 Cor 7:5), and JFB cites that verse. Embracing may also simply mean greeting and parting.
- **"A time to seek, and a time to lose; a time to keep, and a time to cast away" (v6).** There is a time to go looking for what is lost, and a time to give up and let it go. There is a time to save, and a time to throw out or give away, as sailors threw cargo overboard to save the ship (Jonah 1:5; Acts 27:18–19).
- **"A time to tear, and a time to sew; a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (v7).** Tearing one's clothes was the sign of grief (Gen 37:34; 2 Sam 13:31). Sewing them back marked the end of mourning. Silence fits grief too: Job's friends sat with him seven days without a word (Job 2:13), and it was when they started talking that they went wrong. Proverbs is full of the right time to speak (Prov 15:23; 25:11).
- **"A time to love, and a time to hate; a time for war, and a time for peace" (v8).** The poem ends with a chiasm, an A-B-B-A pattern: love, hate, war, peace. Hate in the Bible is not always wrong. God hates evil (Ps 5:5; Prov 6:16–19), and his people are told to "abhor what is evil" (Rom 12:9). The Tyndale notes mention God's hatred of evil.
- **Is it comforting or troubling?** Readers take the poem both ways. Many hear comfort: God has ordered life, and nothing comes outside his timing. Others, reading it in light of chapters 1–2, hear the Preacher describing something we cannot control: the seasons come whether we like them or not. Guzik leans this way, passing on Morgan's remark that the repetition of "a time" stresses the monotony of all things and Kidner's that the seasons, not our skill, are our masters. Both are true to the book. The next section shows the Preacher himself holding both together.
- **Commentators:** JFB stresses that all these things are lawful in their time: "Abuse, not use," is what is condemned. Henry gives the theme of the whole section in one phrase: "The mutability of all human affairs." Guzik takes v1 as a call to use well the time God gives (Eph 5:16; Col 4:5).

#### The God-Given Task (vv9–15)
- **"What gain has the worker from his toil?" (v9).** The book's question again (1:3). If everything has its time and we cannot control the times, what does our work add?
- **"I have seen the business that God has given to the children of man to be busy with" (v10).** Almost word for word from 1:13. There it was an "unhappy business." Here the adjective is dropped, and what follows is more hopeful.
- **"He has made everything beautiful in its time" (v11).** "Beautiful" (*yapheh*) means fitting, lovely, right. Each of the "times" of vv2–8 has its place in God's design, even the ones that hurt. This echoes Genesis 1, where God sees what he has made and calls it good. The Tyndale notes add that we sometimes see this beauty and often do not. God keeps the full picture to himself, as in Romans 8:28.
- **"Also, he has put eternity into man's heart" (v11).** This is one of the most quoted lines in Ecclesiastes. The word is *ha'olam*. The lexicon gives "long duration, antiquity, futurity," and in the Bible *'olam* usually means "forever" or "the distant past" (as in 1:4, 10). In later Hebrew it came to mean "the world," which is why the KJV has "he hath set the world in their heart." JFB follows that and notes another rendering, "obscurity." Most modern translations take "eternity." Humans, unlike animals, have a sense of a reality beyond the present, a longing for the lasting and the whole. The Tyndale notes call it an awareness of the infinite. Guzik passes on Augustine's famous line, through Eaton, that our hearts are restless until they rest in God.
- **"Yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end" (v11).** Here is the ache. We long to see the whole story, and we cannot. We see a frame of film, not the movie. That tension, between eternity in the heart and the limits of the mind, is the human condition the book explores.
- **"There is nothing better for them than to be joyful and to do good as long as they live… this is God's gift to man" (vv12–13).** The second "enjoyment" passage (2:24). What is added here is "to do good": the right response to life includes moral goodness, not only enjoyment. Food, drink and satisfying work are "God's gift" (*mattat*), not a reward we earn.
- **"Whatever God does endures forever; nothing can be added to it, nor anything taken from it" (v14).** Our works fade (2:18–19). God's do not. Eaton, cited by Guzik, finds three things here: God's work is permanent, complete and secure. "Nothing can be added… nor taken" uses the same language as the warning about God's word (Deut 4:2; 12:32; Rev 22:18–19).
- **"God has done it, so that people fear before him" (v14).** This is the first mention of fearing God, the book's final word (12:13). The fear of God is not terror but reverence, the right response of a creature who sees how small he is beside his Maker. This line is a turning point. The Preacher has lifted his eyes, for a moment, above the sun.
- **"That which is, already has been… and God seeks what has been driven away" (v15).** The first part echoes 1:9: history repeats. The last line is difficult. Literally "God seeks the pursued." It may mean God calls the past back to account, so nothing is lost (the NIV: "God will call the past to account"). Kidner, cited by Guzik, says that with God there are no forgotten men. Or it may mean God seeks out the persecuted to defend them, as the ancient Greek translation took it and JFB mentions. Either way, time does not swallow things beyond God's reach.
- **Commentators:** Henry sets this section against vv1–8: human affairs change, but this is "The immutability of the divine counsels." JFB says the fact that we cannot foresee outcomes is meant to keep us from thinking ourselves independent of God. Guzik sees vv14–15 as the Preacher's brief escape from under-the-sun thinking.

#### From Dust to Dust (vv16–22)
- **"In the place of justice, even there was wickedness" (v16).** The courts, which should defend the innocent, have become corrupt. In Israel, justice was done at the city gate, where elders sat to hear cases (Ruth 4:1–12; Amos 5:12, 15). The Tyndale notes explain the setting. The prophets railed against exactly this (Isa 1:21–23; Mic 3:9–11).
- **"God will judge the righteous and the wicked, for there is a time for every matter" (v17).** The poem of vv1–8 comes back. If there is a time for everything, there is a time for judgment. The book ends on the same note: "God will bring every deed into judgment" (12:14). The Tyndale notes say injustice is temporary and God will correct it. Guzik observes that the Preacher says it in his heart, as a hope he holds, not yet as something he sees.
- **"God is testing them that they may see that they themselves are but beasts" (v18).** "Testing" means sifting or showing up what is really there. Deane, cited by Guzik, notes the emphatic "they themselves." The point is humbling. Human beings, who abuse power as if they were gods, are shown that they die like animals. JFB applies it especially to oppressors (Ps 49:12, 20: "man in his pomp… is like the beasts that perish").
- **"What happens to the children of man and what happens to the beasts is the same… They all have the same breath" (v19).** "What happens" is *miqreh*, "fate" or "what befalls," as in 2:14. "Breath" is *ruach*, which can also mean "spirit." Both breathe, and both stop breathing. "Man has no advantage over the beasts, for all is vanity," *hebel*, a breath. Under the sun, looked at from the outside, death treats them alike.
- **"All go to one place. All are from the dust, and to dust all return" (v20).** This is the curse of Genesis 3:19, "you are dust, and to dust you shall return," which the Tyndale notes point out. The "one place" is the grave.
- **"Who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward and the spirit of the beast goes down into the earth?" (v21).** This is a question about what can be seen. Under the sun, there is no way to observe a difference. The Hebrew vowels, which were added much later, read it as a statement: "Who knows the spirit of man, which goes upward, and the spirit of the beast, which goes down?" The ESV footnote gives that reading, and JFB follows it, taking the verse to assert a real difference between man and beast. The ESV follows the ancient translations in making it a question. Either way, the Preacher later answers it himself: "the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it" (12:7). The Tyndale notes point to that verse.
- **"There is nothing better than that a man should rejoice in his work, for that is his lot" (v22).** The third enjoyment passage. "Lot" (*cheleq*) is "portion," a share of land or of goods. Since no one can "see what will be after him," the sensible thing is to receive his present work with joy. The Tyndale notes observe that here work itself is named as a source of joy, apart from food and drink.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads vv18–21 as the Preacher reasoning under the sun, and warns against taking it as a doctrine that the soul ceases to exist. He points out that it took Christ to bring "life and immortality to light" (2 Tim 1:10). JFB thinks v19 may voice the skeptic's view, perhaps Solomon's own during his years of drifting from God, and finds the answer in v21. Henry draws a practical point from the chapter: since power is short-lived and judgment sure, people should use what power they have for good and not to oppress others, for oppressors "will be called to account for it in the other world."

#### Pointing to Christ
"He has made everything beautiful in its time" (v11). The New Testament speaks of one time above all others: "when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son" (Gal 4:4). In Jesus, the eternity God set in human hearts took on flesh (John 1:14). Every line of the poem of vv1–8 touches his life. He had a time to be born and a time to die, and he spoke of "my hour" (John 2:4; 12:23; 17:1). He wept at a grave (John 11:35). He kept silent before his accusers (Isa 53:7; Mark 14:61). He made peace by the blood of his cross (Col 1:20). And the question of v21, "Who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward?", has an answer the Preacher could only hope for. The risen Christ went upward and promised, "Because I live, you also will live" (John 14:19). Dust does not get the last word.
