*Setting:* Moses is on Mount Sinai during his forty days in the cloud (24:18), receiving God's plans for the tabernacle. The instructions have been moving from the inside out: first the ark and the furniture of the inner rooms (ch 25), then the tent itself with its coverings, frames and veil (ch 26). Chapter 27 steps outside the tent into the open courtyard. It describes the great bronze altar where sacrifices were burned, the linen fence that marked off the holy ground, and the oil for the lamp that burned inside through the night. The courtyard was as far as an ordinary Israelite could come. Whatever else the tabernacle meant, the first thing any worshiper met on entering it was an altar.

#### The Bronze Altar (vv1–8)
- **"You shall make the altar of acacia wood, five cubits long and five cubits broad… and its height shall be three cubits" (v1).** About 7½ feet square and 4½ feet high (2.3 by 1.4 meters), by far the largest object in the tabernacle. It stood in the courtyard between the gate and the tent (40:6, 29), so no one could reach God's dwelling without passing it. It is called the "altar of burnt offering" (30:28) and the "bronze altar" (38:30), to distinguish it from the small golden incense altar inside (30:1–10). "Altar" (*mizbeach*) comes from the verb for slaughtering a sacrifice (*zabach*): it was the place of sacrifice.
- **"You shall make horns for it on its four corners… of one piece with it" (v2).** Archaeologists have found horned altars from ancient Israel, such as the large stone altar from Beersheba, with the horns as raised points at the corners. Horns in the Bible stand for strength (Ps 18:2). The blood of the sin offering was put on them (29:12; Lev 4:7). A person in danger might grab the horns of the altar to plead for mercy, as Adonijah and Joab did (1 Kgs 1:50; 2:28). Psalm 118:27 speaks of binding the festal sacrifice "to the horns of the altar." **"You shall overlay it with bronze."** Gold was for inside the tent; bronze for the courtyard. Bronze could also stand up to the heat of the fire, which burned continually (Lev 6:12–13).
- **"Pots for it to receive its ashes, and shovels and basins and forks and fire pans" (v3).** The tools of daily sacrifice: pots for carrying out ashes, shovels for clearing them, basins for catching and throwing blood, forks for handling the meat, and fire pans for carrying coals. All were bronze.
- **"A grating, a network of bronze… set it under the ledge of the altar so that the net extends halfway down" (vv4–5).** The exact design is debated. The grate may have held the fire and let ashes and air pass through, or it may have been an outer frame around the lower half. The "ledge" may have been a step or rim around the altar on which the priests stood, since steps were forbidden (20:26).
- **"Poles of acacia wood… so that the poles are on the two sides of the altar when it is carried" (vv6–7).** Like the ark and table, the altar traveled with Israel. Numbers 4:13–14 describes the Levites carrying it, covered with a purple cloth.
- **"You shall make it hollow, with boards" (v8).** A hollow box of bronze-plated wood would have burned or warped with a fire on top. Most think it was filled with earth or stones when Israel camped, in keeping with the earlier command for an altar of earth or uncut stone (20:24–25), and the bronze and grate held the fire. **"As it has been shown you on the mountain."** Once again, God's design, not Moses'.
- **A later addition.** After Korah's rebellion, the bronze censers of the rebels were hammered into plates and attached to this altar as a warning "that no outsider… should draw near to burn incense before the LORD" (Num 16:38–40).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the altar "a type of Christ dying to make atonement for our sins," and notes that "the wood had been consumed by the fire from heaven, if it had not been secured by the brass." JFB describes the horns as "angular projections, perpendicular or oblique, in the form of horns" and the grating as "sunk latticework to support the fire," and notes that "the inside was hollow." Guzik points out that the word for altar means a place of slaughter, and connects it with the cross. He takes the horns as a sign of strength and passes on John Trapp's view that the sacrifices were tied to them. He also notes the bronze censers of Korah's rebels added later.

#### The Court of the Tabernacle (vv9–19)
- **"You shall make the court of the tabernacle" (v9).** The court was an open-air enclosure around the tent. **"A hundred cubits long for one side."** The whole court was 100 by 50 cubits (v18), about 150 by 75 feet (46 by 23 meters). The tent stood in the western half, and the altar and basin in the eastern half.
- **"Hangings of fine twined linen" (v9).** The fence was white linen, not colored, hung on pillars. White linen often stands for purity and righteousness in Scripture (Rev 19:8). To the camp, the tabernacle presented a clean white wall around God's house.
- **"Its twenty pillars and their twenty bases shall be of bronze, but the hooks of the pillars and their fillets shall be of silver" (v10).** "Fillets" were silver bands or rods connecting the tops of the pillars, from which the curtains hung. Exodus 38:17 adds that the pillar tops were overlaid with silver. Altogether there were sixty pillars around the court.
- **"For the breadth of the court on the west side… fifty cubits, with ten pillars" (v12).** The back (west) was a solid wall of linen. **"The breadth of the court on the front to the east shall be fifty cubits" (v13).** The entrance always faced east.
- **"The hangings for the one side of the gate shall be fifteen cubits" (vv14–15).** On each side of the gate was a 15-cubit wall of linen, leaving a 20-cubit opening, about 30 feet wide, in the middle.
- **"For the gate of the court there shall be a screen twenty cubits long, of blue and purple and scarlet yarns and fine twined linen, embroidered with needlework" (v16).** The gate stood out from the white walls in the royal colors used inside. There was only one way in. Jesus' words come to mind: "I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved" (John 10:9), and "No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6).
- **"The height five cubits" (v18).** About 7½ feet. An adult standing outside could not see over the fence, but could see the top of the tent rising above it and the smoke of the altar going up. The wall both hid and announced God's presence.
- **"All its pegs and all the pegs of the court, shall be of bronze" (v19).** The pegs anchored the tent and the courtyard pillars with ropes (35:18). Even the humblest pieces are in God's plan.
- **Who could enter?** Ordinary Israelites could come into the court to bring their sacrifices, laying their hands on the animal's head at the altar (Lev 1:3–5). Only priests went into the tent. The Psalms long for these courts: "How lovely is your dwelling place, O LORD of hosts! My soul longs, yes, faints for the courts of the LORD" (Ps 84:1–2), and "Enter his gates with thanksgiving, and his courts with praise!" (Ps 100:4).
- **Commentators:** JFB describes the court as "a rectangular court" enclosed by "a connected series of curtains, made of fine twined linen yarn," hung on "sixty brazen pillars." Henry says that "within this enclosure the priests and Levites offered the sacrifices," and reads the distinction between court and sanctuary as the difference "between the visible nominal church, and the true spiritual church," a spiritual application, not the text's own point. Guzik sees the fence as a barrier and a protection at once, marking off holy from common and leaving only one way in. He also calls God a "fellow-pilgrim" with Israel, since the whole court could be packed up and carried.

#### Oil for the Lamp (vv20–21)
- **"You shall command the people of Israel that they bring to you pure beaten olive oil for the light" (v20).** "Beaten" oil was made by crushing olives gently in a mortar rather than pressing them in a mill. The first oil to run off was the clearest and burned with the least smoke. It was the finest grade. It came from the people, not just the priests. Every Israelite had a share in keeping God's light burning.
- **"That a lamp may regularly be set up to burn" (v20).** "Regularly" (*tamid*) means continually, without fail. The same word is used of the bread on the table (25:30) and the daily burnt offering (29:42). The lamp was not allowed to go out. Leviticus 24:1–4 repeats this law.
- **"In the tent of meeting, outside the veil that is before the testimony" (v21).** This is the first use in Exodus of the name "tent of meeting" for the tabernacle, the place where God meets his people (25:22; 29:42–43). The lamp burned in the Holy Place, in front of the veil that hid the ark.
- **"Aaron and his sons shall tend it from evening to morning before the LORD" (v21).** This is the first duty of the priests mentioned in Exodus, coming even before their garments and ordination (chs 28–29). The lamps were filled and lit at evening and tended until morning (30:7–8). Through every night of Israel's history, a light was burning in God's house. In 1 Samuel 3:3, the boy Samuel heard God's call "before the lamp of God had gone out" in the temple at night.
- **"It shall be a statute forever… throughout their generations."** This command connects the furniture just described with the priests who will serve there. The next chapter turns to them.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "The pure oil signified the gifts and graces of the Spirit, which all believers receive from Christ," and applies the lamp to the work of ministers in "preaching and expounding the Scriptures, which are as a lamp" (Ps 119:105). JFB simply notes that Aaron and his sons "were to take charge of lighting it in all time coming." Guzik also sees the oil as a picture of the Holy Spirit and urges constant dependence on him, and he applies "beaten" oil to the way God refines his people through pressure. He passes on Robert Murray M'Cheyne's call for "beaten oil" in preaching, meaning preaching that has been carefully prepared.

#### Pointing to Christ
The first thing every worshiper met inside the gate was an altar of fire and blood. No one could reach God's house any other way. That is the shape of the gospel: before anyone can draw near, there must be a sacrifice. Jesus is the one door into the court (John 10:9), and he is also the sacrifice on the altar, "offered once to bear the sins of many" (Heb 9:28). Hebrews says, "We have an altar from which those who serve the tent have no right to eat" (Heb 13:10), meaning the cross, outside the camp, where Jesus "suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (Heb 13:12). And the lamp that never went out points to the one who said, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12), and who now walks among the lampstands of his churches, keeping their light burning (Rev 1:12–13, 20).
