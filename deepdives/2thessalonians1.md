*Setting:* Paul writes again to Thessalonica, most likely from Corinth, a few months after 1 Thessalonians, around AD 51. The same three men are still together, which fits Paul's eighteen months in Corinth (Acts 18:5, 11). Three things had happened since the first letter. Persecution had grown worse. Someone was teaching, possibly through a letter falsely claiming to be from Paul, that "the day of the Lord has come" (2:2), and the church was shaken. And the idle members Paul had gently corrected (1 Thess 4:11; 5:14) had not changed. This short letter takes those in order: comfort for the persecuted (ch 1), correction about the day of the Lord (ch 2), and discipline for the idle (ch 3). Most scholars accept Paul as the author, as the church always has. Some modern scholars doubt it because of the letter's different tone and its picture of the end, but the closeness to 1 Thessalonians in names, wording and situation fits best with Paul writing soon after the first letter. Chapter 1 is one long thanksgiving and prayer that turns into a picture of Jesus' return in judgment and glory.

#### Greeting (vv1–2)
- **"Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy" (v1).** This is the same opening as 1 Thessalonians 1:1, word for word. Silvanus is the Latin form of Silas, Paul's partner since the second missionary journey began (Acts 15:40), a Roman citizen like Paul (Acts 16:37). Timothy, from Lystra, had been sent back to Thessalonica and returned with the news that prompted the first letter (1 Thess 3:6). All three had planted the church together, and all three had been forced to leave.
- **"To the church of the Thessalonians in God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" (v1).** "Church" (*ekklēsia*) was the word for a city's citizen assembly. The difference is where this assembly lives: "in God." The first letter said "in God *the* Father." Here it is "*our* Father," a small, warm change.
- **"Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" (v2).** Greek letters opened with "greetings" (*chairein*). Paul turns it into "grace" (*charis*) and adds the Hebrew blessing of peace (*shalom*). Notice that grace and peace come from the Father and Jesus together, joined by a single preposition. Within twenty years of the cross, Paul names Jesus beside God as the one source of blessing. No Jew would say that of any mere man.

#### Thanksgiving (vv3–4)
- **"We ought always to give thanks to God for you, brothers, as is right" (v3).** "Ought" is the language of debt: Paul owes God thanks. Some think the Thessalonians had protested Paul's praise in the first letter, saying they were not worthy of it, and Paul answers that thanks is simply what is due. That is a guess, but it would explain the unusual wording. Either way, the thanks goes to God, not to them. Their growth is his work.
- **"Your faith is growing abundantly" (v3).** The verb (*hyperauxanō*) appears only here in the New Testament: "to increase beyond measure." Paul had prayed to "supply what is lacking in your faith" (1 Thess 3:10). God answered more than abundantly.
- **"The love of every one of you for one another is increasing" (v3).** This also answers a prayer: "may the Lord make you increase and abound in love for one another" (1 Thess 3:12). "Every one of you" is striking in a church where Paul is about to correct some members (3:6–15). The love had not broken down.
- **"Therefore we ourselves boast about you in the churches of God" (v4).** "We ourselves" is emphatic. Paul usually boasts only in the Lord (Gal 6:14), but he is not ashamed to brag about what God is doing in this church. In Corinth and beyond, the Thessalonians had become an example (1 Thess 1:7–8).
- **"Your steadfastness and faith in all your persecutions and in the afflictions that you are enduring" (v4).** "Steadfastness" (*hypomonē*) is endurance under weight, staying put when pressure says to give way. "Faith" here may lean toward faithfulness. The present tense "are enduring" shows the persecution was still going on. It began when Paul was driven out (Acts 17:5–10) and had continued from their own countrymen (1 Thess 2:14).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the thanks "a small but a bounden return" for answered prayer. Henry: "Where faith grows, love will abound, for faith works by love." Guzik notes that the verb for faith's growth is unusual and pictures vigorous, healthy growth, which the lexicon supports.

#### The Judgment at Christ's Coming (vv5–12)
- **"This is evidence of the righteous judgment of God" (v5).** "Evidence" (*endeigma*) means a plain sign or proof. What is the evidence? Most likely the whole situation of v4: their faithful endurance under suffering. Believers often take persecution as a sign that God is absent or unfair. Paul reads it the other way. The fact that God's people hold firm under pressure shows God at work in them now, and it points to the day when he will set everything right.
- **"That you may be considered worthy of the kingdom of God, for which you are also suffering" (v5).** Suffering does not earn the kingdom. It is the road to it, as Paul told new believers on his first journey: "through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God" (Acts 14:22). Those who suffer for the kingdom show that they belong to it. "Considered worthy" means "counted" or "declared" worthy, as God counts righteous those who trust him.
- **"God considers it just to repay with affliction those who afflict you" (v6).** The "since indeed" is not doubtful. It means "if, as is certainly the case." Justice means fitting repayment, the principle of Obadiah 15: "as you have done, it shall be done to you." Believers are told not to take revenge (Rom 12:19; 1 Thess 5:15) precisely because God will deal with it justly.
- **"And to grant relief to you who are afflicted as well as to us" (v7).** "Relief" (*anesis*) means a loosening, like slackening a bow or letting off a tight rope. Paul uses it as the opposite of affliction, the pressure lifting. "As well as to us" puts the apostles among the afflicted. They do not write from comfort.
- **"When the Lord Jesus is revealed from heaven" (v7).** "Revealed" is *apokalypsis*, an unveiling. Jesus is reigning now but hidden from sight. On that day he will be seen as he is (1 Pet 1:7, 13). In the first letter Paul spoke of his "coming" (*parousia*). This word stresses that what is now hidden will be openly shown.
- **"With his mighty angels in flaming fire" (vv7–8).** Jesus spoke of coming "in his glory, and all the angels with him" (Matt 25:31). Fire marks God's presence in the burning bush (Exod 3:2) and at Sinai (Exod 19:18), and his coming in judgment: "the LORD will come in fire… to render his anger in fury" (Isa 66:15). Paul calmly gives Jesus the role the Old Testament gives to the LORD.
- **"Inflicting vengeance on those who do not know God and on those who do not obey the gospel" (v8).** "Vengeance" (*ekdikēsis*) shares a root with "just" (*dikaios*). It is not spite but the full carrying out of justice. "Those who do not know God" echoes Psalm 79:6 and Jeremiah 10:25, which ask God to pour out his wrath on "the nations that do not know you." Some take the two phrases as two groups, pagan Gentiles and Jews who rejected the gospel. More likely they describe one group twice. Not knowing God is not innocent ignorance. It shows itself in refusing the gospel when it comes.
- **"The punishment of eternal destruction" (v9).** "Destruction" (*olethros*) means ruin. It is the same word for the sudden destruction in 1 Thessalonians 5:3. Christians differ on what "eternal destruction" means. The historic view held by most of the church is conscious, unending punishment, since "eternal" here matches the "eternal life" of the righteous (Matt 25:46). A minority of evangelicals hold annihilationism or conditional immortality: the wicked are destroyed, and the destruction is eternal in that it is final and irreversible. Both views take God's judgment with full seriousness.
- **"Away from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might" (v9).** This phrase is taken almost word for word from the Greek of Isaiah 2:10, 19 and 21, where people hide in caves "from before the terror of the LORD, and from the splendor of his majesty." The heart of the punishment is separation from the Lord, shut out from all the goodness of his presence. Some read "away from" as "coming from" the Lord's presence, meaning the judgment flashes out from his face. Either way, the Lord himself is the center of the scene.
- **"When he comes on that day to be glorified in his saints, and to be marveled at among all who have believed" (v10).** This verse turns the picture around. The same day that brings ruin to the persecutors brings glory to the persecuted. Christ will be glorified *in* his people. They are like a mirror showing what his grace has done. Even believers will marvel at him. This also echoes the Greek of Psalm 89:7 and Isaiah 49:3.
- **"Because our testimony to you was believed" (v10).** This short line puts the Thessalonians right inside the great day. They heard Paul's preaching a year or so earlier and believed it. That is enough. They will be among the marveling crowd.
- **"To this end we always pray for you" (v11).** Paul does not stop at teaching. He prays that God will make them "worthy of his calling." The God who called them is the one who makes them worthy of it, and so prayer is the right response.
- **"Fulfill every resolve for good and every work of faith by his power" (v11).** "Resolve for good" could be the believers' good desires or God's own good pleasure. The ESV takes it as the believers'. Either way, God's power brings it to completion. "Work of faith" is the phrase Paul used in 1 Thessalonians 1:3: faith that acts.
- **"That the name of our Lord Jesus may be glorified in you, and you in him" (v12).** The glory runs both ways. Jesus is honored by what he does in his people, and they are honored by being joined to him. Isaiah 66:5 told God's people that their persecutors mocked, "Let the LORD be glorified." Paul answers: he will be, in you.
- **"According to the grace of our God and the Lord Jesus Christ" (v12).** The chapter ends where it began, with grace. In Greek one article governs both "God" and "Lord," and some scholars translate it "our God and Lord, Jesus Christ," a direct statement of Jesus' deity. Most translations, the ESV included, keep two persons here because "Lord Jesus Christ" was nearly a proper name. Either way, the grace comes from both together.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God will trouble those that trouble his people," and "We cannot by all our sufferings, any more than by our services, merit heaven." He adds that "Religion, if worth anything, is worth every thing." Guzik reads v5 like the Tyndale notes: suffering does not show God's absence but his righteous work, refining believers for the kingdom. On v8 he argues that "vengeance" carries no spite, only steady justice, and he insists that the heart of hell is being cut off from the Lord's presence and goodness, not fire alone. On v9, JFB reads the phrase as banishment from God's presence and also gives Alford's reading of the judgment coming *from* his presence. JFB on v10: "glory is holiness manifested." On v12 it sees the single Greek article as "implying the inseparable unity" of the Father and the Son.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul writes this chapter to suffering people, and he points them to Jesus in two ways. Jesus will come as Judge, revealed in fire with his angels, with the authority the prophets gave to the LORD himself. And Jesus will come as the one glorified in his people, whose grace made them worthy. The same Lord who will set every wrong right is the Lord who "was marveled at among all who have believed," and the Thessalonians, who simply believed what they were told, will be there.
