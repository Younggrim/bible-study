*Setting:* Psalm 95 has no title in the Hebrew text. The Greek translation attributes it to David, and Hebrews 4:7 quotes it as spoken "through David," which may name him as author or may simply refer to the Psalter as David's book. It belongs to the group of "The LORD reigns" psalms (93–100) and reads like a call to worship sung as God's people entered the temple courts. For centuries the church has used it the same way: as the *Venite* (Latin for its first words, "O come"), it opens daily morning prayer in many traditions. The psalm has two halves that sound very different. Verses 1–7a are a glad invitation to sing and kneel before the King and Shepherd. Verses 7b–11 suddenly change voice: God himself speaks, warning his people not to repeat the stubbornness of the wilderness generation. The two belong together. Worship that sings God's praise but will not listen to his voice is not real worship. Hebrews 3:7–4:13 builds a whole sermon on this second half. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movements.

#### Let Us Sing to the LORD (vv1–5)
- **"Oh come, let us sing to the LORD" (v1).** "Oh come" (*lekhu*) is literally "go" or "walk," an urging word, like "come on." The verbs are all plural, "let us." This is the worship of a gathered people, not a private devotion.
- **"Let us make a joyful noise to the rock of our salvation!" (v1).** "Make a joyful noise" (*nari'a*) is the shout raised to greet a king (1 Sam 10:24) or at a victory, and the blast of trumpets (Num 10:9). The worship pictured is loud and glad. "Rock of our salvation" recalls Moses' song, where God is "the Rock" and Israel "scoffed at the Rock of his salvation" (Deut 32:4, 15). It also quietly prepares for the second half: in the wilderness, water came from the rock at Massah and Meribah (Exod 17:6). The same Rock they tested is the one they now praise.
- **"Let us come into his presence with thanksgiving" (v2).** Literally "let us meet his face." Coming before a king meant bringing something, and the gift here is thanks (*todah*, which can also mean a thank offering). JFB notes the verb means to "approach" or "meet Him."
- **"For the LORD is a great God, and a great King above all gods" (v3).** The reason for the praise. The nations around Israel thought of gods as local powers, each ruling its own land or force of nature, and a small nation's god might seem minor. The psalm says the LORD is King over all of them. This does not mean the psalmist thought other gods were real rivals. Psalm 96:5 says plainly, "all the gods of the peoples are worthless idols." The point is that whatever people worship, the LORD is above it.
- **"In his hand are the depths of the earth; the heights of the mountains are his also" (v4).** Pagan religion often gave the underworld, the mountains and the sea to different gods. The psalm claims every part of creation for one God, from the lowest depths to the highest peaks. The phrase "in his hand" pictures God holding it all like something small enough to carry.
- **"The sea is his, for he made it, and his hands formed the dry land" (v5).** In Canaanite myth the sea was a monster the gods had to fight. Here it is simply something God made and owns, as in Genesis 1:9–10. Ownership comes from making. That logic will apply to the people in v6.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the praise must be "a joyful noise," because "spiritual joy is the heart and soul of thankful praise," and also "in concert, in the solemn assemblies… not others without me, nor I alone, but others with me." He notes that "the deep places of the earth… are in his hand" and that "his being the Creator of all makes him, without dispute, the owner of all." JFB reads "gods" as those "esteemed such by men, though really nothing" (Jer 10:10–15), and "rock" as "a firm basis, giving certainty of salvation." Guzik draws attention to the community "let us," and observes that joy rather than gloom should mark the main tone of worship. He also notes that the sea, which pagans feared as older than the gods, is here simply God's because he made it.

#### Let Us Worship and Bow Down (vv6–7a)
- **"Oh come, let us worship and bow down; let us kneel before the LORD, our Maker!" (v6).** The second invitation changes the posture. Verses 1–2 were standing, shouting, singing. Now come three words for getting low: "worship" (*hishtachaveh*, to bow down to the ground), "bow down" (*kara*, to bend or crouch), and "kneel" (*barakh*, to go down on the knees). Joy and reverence belong together. Worship that has only one loses something.
- **"The LORD, our Maker" (v6).** God made the sea and the land (v5). He also made Israel, not only as human beings but as a people. Isaiah uses the same language: "the LORD, your Maker… who formed you from the womb" (Isa 44:2; 51:13). He made them, so they belong to him.
- **"For he is our God, and we are the people of his pasture, and the sheep of his hand" (v7).** The great King of vv3–5 is also a Shepherd. "People of his pasture" means the flock he grazes and feeds. "Sheep of his hand" may mean sheep he leads, or sheep he holds and protects. Psalm 100:3 repeats the thought almost word for word. The words prepare for what follows: sheep know their shepherd's voice (John 10:3–4, 27), and the next line asks whether this flock will listen.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that though "bodily exercise, alone, profits little, yet certainly it is our duty to glorify God with our bodies." He contrasts idolaters who "kneel before gods which they themselves made" with believers who "kneel before a God who made us." He calls this "a gospel psalm" and applies the Shepherd to Christ, "the great and good Shepherd." JFB says the shepherd image "illustrates our entire dependence" (Ps 23:3; 74:1), and on v6 that "come" means "enter," with outward forms as well as hearts. Guzik explains the three Hebrew verbs of v6 as rising degrees of humility, and adds that believers owe God worship twice over, as both Maker and Redeemer.

#### Today, If You Hear His Voice (vv7b–11)
- **"Today, if you hear his voice" (v7).** The psalm turns in the middle of a verse. The speaker is now God, speaking through a prophet or Levite in the temple. "If" here may be a wish: "Oh, that today you would listen to his voice!" (as in Ps 81:8, 13). "Today" is the key word. The invitation is real, but it does not stay open forever. Hebrews seizes on it: "exhort one another every day, as long as it is called 'today'" (Heb 3:13).
- **"Do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness" (v8).** Massah means "testing" and Meribah means "quarreling." Soon after leaving Egypt, at Rephidim, the people had no water and accused Moses of bringing them out to die. God gave water from the rock, and Moses named the place for what they had done, because "they tested the LORD by saying, 'Is the LORD among us or not?'" (Exod 17:1–7). A similar quarrel happened forty years later at Kadesh (Num 20:1–13), where the place was also called Meribah. The names became shorthand for the whole wilderness pattern of grumbling and doubt.
- **"Harden your hearts."** A hard heart is not a feeling that happens to someone. It is a choice to resist God until resistance becomes a habit. The warning implies that hearers can do something about it. Pharaoh is the standing example (Exod 8:15, 32).
- **"When your fathers put me to the test and put me to the proof, though they had seen my work" (v9).** To "test" God is to demand proof he has already given. The wilderness generation saw the plagues, the Red Sea opening, manna each morning and water from rock, and still asked whether he was among them. Henry says, "With them even seeing was not believing."
- **"For forty years I loathed that generation" (v10).** "Loathed" (*qut*) means to feel disgust, to be sick of something. It is strong language for God's grief at forty years of unbelief. The heart of the problem is named: **"They are a people who go astray in their heart, and they have not known my ways."** Their wandering in the desert matched the wandering of their hearts. Hebrews 3:9–10, following the Greek, attaches "forty years" to "saw my works": they watched God work for forty years and still did not trust him.
- **"Therefore I swore in my wrath, 'They shall not enter my rest'" (v11).** This recalls Kadesh, where the people refused to enter Canaan after the spies' report, and God swore that none of that generation except Caleb and Joshua would go in (Num 14:21–35). The Hebrew oath is literally "if they shall enter my rest," a form that leaves "may I be cursed" unspoken. JFB explains this as "part of the form of swearing." "My rest" (*menuchati*) meant the land of Canaan, where God would give Israel "rest from all your enemies" (Deut 12:9–10). But it is "*my* rest," God's own rest, and later Scripture uses it of his resting place in the temple (Ps 132:8, 14).
- **The psalm stops there.** There is no happy closing line. It ends on the warning, which leaves the singers facing the choice the psalm sets out.
- **How Hebrews reads it.** Hebrews 3–4 argues that since David was still saying "Today… do not harden your hearts" long after Joshua brought Israel into Canaan, the land could not have been the final rest. "For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on. So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Heb 4:8–9). The rest is God's own seventh-day rest (Gen 2:2), entered by faith in Christ, and the warning applies to every generation that hears the gospel.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this half "an exhortation to those who sing gospel psalms to live gospel lives." He notes that the whole wilderness time "might be called a day of temptation," that "the more experience we have had of the power and goodness of God the greater is our sin if we distrust him," and that "hardness of heart is at the bottom of all our distrusts of God." JFB says "their wanderings in the desert were but types of their innate ignorance and perverseness," and links "Meribah and Massah" to Exodus 17:7 and Psalm 78. Guzik notes that Hebrews 3:7 introduces the words "as the Holy Spirit says," and that the warning implies a hard heart is partly a matter of the will. He sees Kadesh as the moment that came to Israel when faith or unbelief decided the way forward.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews hears the voice of Psalm 95 as God speaking now "by his Son" (Heb 1:2). Jesus is the Shepherd whose sheep hear his voice (John 10:27), and the Rock from which the water of life flows: "the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4). Where the wilderness generation looked at God's works and would not trust him, Jesus calls, "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28). The rest Canaan only pictured is found in him, and Hebrews urges every hearer to "strive to enter that rest" while it is still called "today" (Heb 4:11). The psalm's call to joy and its call to listen are answered together in him.
