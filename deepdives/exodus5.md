*Setting:* Moses and Aaron have come back from the wilderness with God's message and signs. The elders of Israel believed, and the people "bowed their heads and worshiped" (4:29–31). Everything seems ready. God, though, had warned that Pharaoh "will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand" (3:19) and that he would harden Pharaoh's heart (4:21). Chapter 5 is the first meeting with Pharaoh. It goes badly, and for the Israelites things get worse before they get better. The whole chapter falls under one ESV heading.

#### Making Bricks Without Straw (vv1–23)
- **"Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel" (v1).** This is the standard opening of a royal messenger: "Thus says the king." Moses and Aaron come as ambassadors of a greater King. "Let my people go" is heard here for the first time, and it becomes the refrain of the plague chapters (7:16; 8:1, 20; 9:1, 13; 10:3).
- **"That they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness" (v1).** "Feast" is *chag*, a pilgrimage festival, the word later used for Passover, Weeks and Booths (23:14–17). The aim of the exodus is worship from the first word.
- **"Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?" (v2).** Pharaoh was treated as a god himself, the son of the sun god Ra, the keeper of cosmic order. He has never heard of Israel's God and has no reason to obey a slave people's deity. **"I do not know the LORD."** This is the question the plagues answer. Again and again God says he acts so that Pharaoh, Egypt and Israel "shall know that I am the LORD" (7:5, 17; 8:10, 22; 9:14, 29; 14:4, 18). By chapter 14, Egypt knows.
- **"The God of the Hebrews has met with us" (v3).** Moses and Aaron use the name Pharaoh might recognize, "the God of the Hebrews," exactly as God told them to (3:18). **"Lest he fall upon us with pestilence or with the sword."** God had not said this. Perhaps they were stressing how serious the request was. Either way, it ironically describes what is about to fall on Egypt.
- **"Why do you take the people away from their work?" (v4).** Pharaoh treats worship as laziness. To him, Israel exists only for labor, the same view as 1:9–10. **"The people of the land are now many" (v5)** echoes his predecessor's fear about Israel's numbers (1:9).
- **"Taskmasters… and their foremen" (v6).** Two layers of command. The taskmasters were Egyptian slave drivers. The foremen (*shoterim*) were Israelites set over their own people, perhaps scribes who kept the tallies. The same word is later used for Israel's officers in the wilderness and under Joshua (Num 11:16; Josh 1:10). They are caught in the middle.
- **"You shall no longer give the people straw" (v7).** Egyptian mud bricks were made of Nile clay mixed with chopped straw, which helped the bricks hold together as they dried and kept them from cracking. Egyptian records from the New Kingdom set daily brick quotas for work gangs, and one scribe complains of having neither workers nor straw for his bricks. Pharaoh's order means the same quota with an extra job added.
- **"For they are idle" (v8).** The word is *rapheh*, "slack" or "lazy." Pharaoh repeats it in v17, "You are idle, you are idle." He decides that only idle people would want to worship, so he crushes the desire with work. **"Pay no regard to lying words" (v9).** He calls God's word lies.
- **"Thus says Pharaoh" (v10).** The taskmasters use the same messenger formula as Moses in v1. Two kings are now speaking, and the people must decide whose word to trust.
- **"Scattered throughout all the land of Egypt to gather stubble" (v12).** Egyptian harvesters often cut grain high, leaving the stalks standing in the fields. The Israelites now have to roam the countryside collecting this stubble, which made poorer bricks.
- **"The foremen… were beaten" (v14).** The Israelite foremen are held responsible for the shortfall and punished. Egyptian tomb paintings show overseers with sticks standing over brickmakers.
- **"Then the foremen… cried to Pharaoh" (v15).** "Cried" (*tsa'aq*) is the word for crying out to God (3:7, 9), but they bring their cry to Pharaoh instead. Three times in two verses they call themselves "your servants" (vv15–16). Without realizing it, they confess whom they think they serve. **"The fault is in your own people" (v16)** is a brave but useless protest.
- **"Go now and work" (v18).** Pharaoh's answer is final. There will be no straw and no reduction.
- **"The LORD look on you and judge" (v21).** The foremen use God's name to curse God's messengers. **"You have made us stink in the sight of Pharaoh."** Literally "you have made our smell stink," the same idiom Jacob used when his sons' violence made him hated by the Canaanites (Gen 34:30). They also echo 2:14, "Who made you… a judge over us?" Again Moses comes to help and is rejected by his own people. **"Put a sword in their hand to kill us"** answers the "sword" of v3.
- **"Then Moses turned to the LORD" (v22).** This time Moses does what the foremen didn't: he takes his pain to God. **"O Lord, why have you done evil to this people? Why did you ever send me?"** It is blunt, the same honest lament as the Psalms (Ps 13; 44:23–24). God does not rebuke him for it. Moses had forgotten God's warning about Pharaoh's resistance (3:19; 4:21), but his instinct to bring the complaint to God rather than to Pharaoh was right.
- **"You have not delivered your people at all" (v23).** The Hebrew doubles the verb for emphasis: "delivering, you have not delivered." It is the same verb *natsal* God used when he said, "I have come down to deliver them" (3:8). Moses throws God's promise back at him, and God answers in the very next verse (6:1).
- **Commentators:** On v2, Henry hears Pharaoh saying, "I neither know him nor care for him," and draws the lesson that "ignorance and contempt of God are at the bottom of all the wickedness that is in the world." JFB adds that Pharaoh judged Israel's God "by the abject and miserable condition of the worshippers." On the straw, Guzik notes that archaeology confirms chopped straw in Egyptian bricks and that brick quotas are well documented. JFB explains that harvesters cut off only the ears, leaving stalks in the fields. JFB distinguishes the Egyptian taskmasters from the Hebrew officers "placed over their brethren." Henry says the foremen "fly in the face of their best friends" and "quarrel with the instruments of their deliverance," while Moses rightly "returned to the Lord." JFB, on vv20–21: "the greatest darkness is immediately before the dawn." Guzik sees God using the trial to turn a slave people into a free one and to strip away Moses' remaining self-reliance.

#### Pointing to Christ
The deliverer's first visit makes things worse, and his own people say God should judge him. Jesus came to his own and was rejected too, accused of making trouble with the rulers (John 11:47–50). The way to rescue ran through deeper suffering, first for Israel, finally for Christ himself. Moses' lament, "Why did you ever send me?", is answered by the God who sent his own Son into the hardest place, and the Son prayed, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34), and three days later God raised him. The darkest hour in Egypt came just before the dawn of deliverance, and the cross came before the empty tomb.
