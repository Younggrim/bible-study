*Setting:* The book of Joshua picks up exactly where Deuteronomy ends. Moses has died on Mount Nebo, in sight of the land he was not allowed to enter, and Israel has mourned him for thirty days on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan River opposite Jericho (Deut 34). Forty years have passed since the Exodus. The generation that refused to enter Canaan at Kadesh has died in the wilderness (Num 14), and a new generation stands at the river. Most traditional dating puts this around 1400 BC; many scholars prefer a date in the 1200s BC, and the text itself does not settle it. The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition credited Joshua with most of it, and notes such as "to this day" (4:9; 5:9) show that at least some of it was written down later. Chapter 1 is a hinge: the Lord commissions Joshua, Joshua gives his first orders, and the eastern tribes promise to follow him as they followed Moses.

#### God Commissions Joshua (vv1–9)
- **"After the death of Moses the servant of the LORD" (v1).** The book opens with the same words used to open Judges ("after the death of Joshua") and 2 Samuel ("after the death of Saul"). Each marks the end of an era. "Servant of the LORD" was Moses' honored title (Deut 34:5), and the book uses it of him about a dozen times. Joshua himself does not receive the title until his own death notice (24:29).
- **"Joshua the son of Nun, Moses' assistant" (v1).** Joshua had served Moses since his youth (Num 11:28). He led Israel's army against Amalek (Exod 17:8–13), went partway up Sinai with Moses (Exod 24:13), stayed in the tent of meeting (Exod 33:11), and was one of only two spies who urged Israel to trust God at Kadesh (Num 14:6–9). Moses had already laid hands on him before the people (Num 27:18–23; Deut 31:7–8). He did not appear from nowhere. He had been prepared for forty years by serving.
- **His name.** He was first called Hoshea, "salvation," and Moses renamed him *Yehoshua*, "the LORD saves" (Num 13:16). In Greek the name is *Iēsous*, the same name as Jesus. The KJV twice calls this Joshua "Jesus" in the New Testament (Acts 7:45; Heb 4:8).
- **"Moses my servant is dead. Now therefore arise" (v2).** God states the loss plainly and moves straight to the next command. Moses was the greatest prophet Israel had known (Deut 34:10), but the promise never rested on him. "This Jordan" was in spring flood (3:15), and Israel had no boats and no bridge.
- **"The land that I am giving to them" (v2).** The verb points both ways. The land is a gift already decided ("I have given," v3), and it is still to be received. The promise reaches back to Abraham (Gen 12:7; 15:18–21).
- **"Every place that the sole of your foot will tread upon" (v3).** Almost word for word from Deuteronomy 11:24. The land was Israel's by promise, but they had to walk into it. Gift and effort sit side by side throughout the book.
- **The boundaries (v4).** South to north, from "the wilderness" (the Negev and Sinai) to "this Lebanon"; east to the "great river," the Euphrates; west to "the Great Sea," the Mediterranean, "toward the going down of the sun." "The land of the Hittites" probably means the region of Syria, which Near Eastern records of the time called "Hatti." Israel came closest to these borders under David and Solomon (1 Kings 4:21), and even then not as fully settled land.
- **"I will not leave you or forsake you" (v5).** Moses had passed this promise to Joshua and to the people (Deut 31:6, 8). Now God says it to him directly. Hebrews 13:5 applies it to every believer: "I will never leave you nor forsake you." The center of the promise is not success but presence: "Just as I was with Moses, so I will be with you."
- **"Be strong and courageous" (vv6, 7, 9).** God says it three times, and the people say it a fourth time (v18). Moses had said it to Joshua twice (Deut 31:7, 23). The repetition suggests Joshua needed it. Courage here is not a personality trait. It rests on a reason: "for you shall cause this people to inherit" (v6) and "for the LORD your God is with you" (v9).
- **"Only be strong and very courageous, being careful to do according to all the law" (v7).** The hardest courage God asks for is not on the battlefield but in obedience. "Do not turn from it to the right hand or to the left" is Moses' language for staying on the road (Deut 5:32; 17:20, the law for kings).
- **"This Book of the Law" (v8).** A written book already exists, most likely Deuteronomy or the Mosaic law that Moses wrote and handed to the priests (Deut 31:9, 24–26). Israel's leader is placed under a written text from day one.
- **"You shall meditate on it day and night" (v8).** The Hebrew *hagah* has a root sense of a low sound: the growl of a lion over its prey (Isa 31:4), the moan of a dove (Isa 38:14), and to utter or murmur. Ancient people usually read aloud, so to "meditate" was to mutter the words over and over. That is why the verse begins "shall not depart from your *mouth*." Psalm 1:2 uses the same phrase for the blessed man.
- **"Make your way prosperous… have good success" (v8).** Two different verbs. "Prosperous" is *tsalach*, to go forward and succeed, and in the Old Testament it is almost always about a venture or journey turning out well, not about money. "Good success" (vv7, 8) is *sakal*, whose basic meaning is to act wisely or with insight. The promise is that a leader shaped by God's word will act wisely, and his undertakings will go forward. It is not a promise of wealth.
- **"Have I not commanded you?" (v9).** The command rests on God's authority, not Joshua's feelings. "Do not be frightened… dismayed" echoes Deuteronomy 31:8. Forty years earlier the spies' report had melted Israel's heart (Num 13:31–33). Now God speaks to fear before it can take hold.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Moses the servant is dead, but God the Master is not: he lives for ever." Guzik makes the same point in his own way: no human leader is indispensable, and God changes his workers without changing his work. Guzik also contrasts Moses (representing the law), with Aaron and Miriam, none of whom could bring Israel into the land; only Joshua, whose name is Jesus' name, could lead them in. JFB stresses that the promise covered only the land within these borders, "not universal dominion," and ties Joshua's courage to "his firm and inflexible adherence to the law of God." Guzik, following other scholars, says *tsalach* rarely if ever refers to finances, and the lexicon supports that. Henry: "Those that make the word of God their rule… shall both do well and speed well."

#### Joshua Assumes Command (vv10–18)
- **"Joshua commanded the officers" (v10).** These officers (*shoterim*) were administrators and record keepers who passed orders through the tribes (Deut 1:15; 20:5–9). Joshua's first act as leader is not a speech to the nation but an order passed down the line. God commanded Joshua (v9), and Joshua commands others (v10).
- **"Prepare your provisions" (v11).** Manna was still falling and would not stop until after Israel ate the produce of Canaan (5:11–12). Manna spoiled overnight, so the "provisions" were probably grain and food from the conquered lands east of the Jordan, packed for the move.
- **"Within three days" (v11).** Joshua 2 seems to take place alongside or just before this, since the spies hide for three days (2:22) and the crossing follows (3:1–2). The book arranges its material by theme as well as by strict sequence.
- **The two and a half tribes (vv12–15).** Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh had asked Moses for the rich grazing land east of the Jordan, which Israel had taken from Sihon and Og (Num 32). Moses was angry at first: it sounded like Kadesh again, Israelites refusing to cross. They answered by promising that their fighting men would go over and fight until the rest of Israel had its land (Num 32:16–27). Joshua now holds them to it.
- **"The LORD your God is providing you a place of rest" (v13).** "Rest" is a key idea in Joshua: a settled land, free from enemies, where Israel could live in safety with God (21:44; 22:4; 23:1). Hebrews 3–4 picks up this word and says Joshua's rest was real but not final: "If Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on" (Heb 4:8). A deeper Sabbath rest remains for God's people.
- **"All the men of valor among you shall pass over armed" (v14).** "Armed" (*chamushim*) is a rare word meaning "in battle array," used of Israel leaving Egypt (Exod 13:18). The eastern tribes listed about 110,000 fighting men in the second census (Num 26), and about 40,000 crossed (4:13), so a sizeable force stayed behind to guard the families. "Before your brothers" means in the front line.
- **"Until the LORD gives rest to your brothers as he has to you" (v15).** Those already settled were to fight for those not yet settled. Their own comfort did not release them from their brothers' need. They kept the promise for about seven years, and at the end Joshua sent them home with praise (22:1–6).
- **"All that you have commanded us we will do" (v16).** The eastern tribes speak for the nation. Their answer echoes Israel's promise at Sinai (Exod 24:7).
- **"Just as we obeyed Moses in all things, so we will obey you" (v17).** Given Israel's history of complaining against Moses, this is generous, perhaps more generous than accurate. The real condition follows: "Only may the LORD your God be with you, as he was with Moses!" They will follow Joshua as long as God is with him.
- **"Shall be put to death" (v18).** In the ancient Near East, loyalty oaths to a new ruler often included a death penalty for rebellion. In Israel, rebelling against God's appointed leader in a time of holy war was rebellion against God himself (compare Deut 17:12).
- **"Only be strong and courageous" (v18).** The chapter ends with the people saying to Joshua what God has said to him three times. God's word is now coming back to Joshua through his people.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws the lesson that believers should never refuse to help a brother in need because their own situation is settled, citing 1 Corinthians 12:26. He also sees in the people's last line a picture of believers encouraging one another with God's own words. JFB reads "within three days" as the third day, and says the provisions were "not manna, which, though it still fell, would not keep." JFB also thinks "all the mighty men of valour" should not be pressed to mean the whole army, which fits the numbers in 4:13. JFB links *chamushim* to an army marshaled in five divisions. BDB lists only "in battle array" and calls its root uncertain, so the "five" idea is a guess.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joshua's name is Jesus' name, and Hebrews draws the line between them: Joshua led Israel into a real land and a real rest, but not the final one (Heb 4:8–10). Moses, the giver of the law, could see the land but not bring the people in. The one who leads God's people into their inheritance is the Lord's chosen captain, armed with God's promise "I will not leave you or forsake you," and that same promise is spoken to every believer in Christ (Heb 13:5–6). Jesus, the greater Joshua, keeps the Book of the Law perfectly, never turning to right or left, and goes ahead of his people into a rest he has won for them.
