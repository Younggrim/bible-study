*Setting:* This is the fifth of the six "woes" in chapters 28–33, and it returns to the subject of chapter 30: Judah's treaty with Egypt in the years around 705–701 BC, as the Assyrian king Sennacherib prepared to crush Hezekiah's revolt. Chapter 30 described the envoys and their donkeys loaded with treasure. Chapter 31 is shorter and sharper. It sets two kinds of help side by side: Egypt's horses and chariots, which are only flesh, and the LORD, who will defend Jerusalem like a lion over its prey and like a bird over its nest. It ends with a promise that came true almost word for word: Assyria would fall "by a sword, not of man" (37:36).

#### Woe to Those Who Go Down to Egypt (vv1–9)
- **"Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses" (v1).** Egypt was famous for its horses and chariots (1 Kings 10:28–29), and in the hill country of Judah, cavalry was what an army lacked. The law had warned Israel's kings not to "acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses" (Deut 17:16). Henry: "Their kings were forbidden to multiply horses and chariots, and were told of the folly of trusting to them."
- **"Who trust in chariots because they are many… but do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the LORD!" (v1).** The problem is not that they trusted nothing. Guzik makes the point that everyone trusts something. They trusted the wrong thing, and they never asked God. "Look to" and "consult" are the language of worship and seeking God's guidance (Deut 4:29). Psalm 20:7 says it plainly: "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God."
- **"And yet he is wise and brings disaster" (v2).** This is dry irony. Judah's advisers thought they were the clever ones (29:14–15), and Egypt was famous for its wise men (19:11–12). "He too is wise," God says, and his wisdom is not to be ignored. JFB contrasts God's wisdom with that of Egypt's priests. Henry: "They do not seek the Lord, nor make their application to him, yet he also is wise."
- **"He does not call back his words, but will arise against the house of the evildoers and against the helpers of those who work iniquity" (v2).** God's word, once spoken, stands (55:11). Both those who do wrong and those who help them will face him, which here means Judah and Egypt alike.
- **"The Egyptians are man, and not God, and their horses are flesh, and not spirit" (v3).** This is one of Isaiah's clearest lines. "Man… God" is *'adam… 'el*, a creature of dust set against the Mighty One. "Flesh… spirit" is *basar… ruach*, what is weak and dies set against what is powerful and lives. In the Old Testament "flesh" often means human frailty (40:6, "all flesh is grass"). Jeremiah says, "Cursed is the man who trusts in man and makes flesh his strength" (Jer 17:5).
- **"When the LORD stretches out his hand, the helper will stumble, and he who is helped will fall" (v3).** God's outstretched hand is the Exodus image again (Exod 14:26–27). The helper (Egypt) and the helped (Judah) go down together. In 701 BC, the Egyptian force sent to relieve Judah was beaten on the coastal plain, and Judah's towns were taken.
- **"As a lion or a young lion growls over his prey… he is not terrified by their shouting" (v4).** Shepherds in Israel would gather and shout to scare off a lion that had taken a sheep (1 Sam 17:34–35; Amos 3:12). A lion standing over its kill doesn't flinch. Henry: "When the lion comes out to seize his prey a multitude of shepherds come out against him," but their noise changes nothing. **"So the LORD of hosts will come down to fight on Mount Zion."** God will not be scared off Zion by the Assyrian army's noise.
- **A debate about v4.** The Hebrew preposition *'al* can mean "on" or "against." Some scholars think God is pictured as a lion fighting *against* Zion, with Jerusalem as the prey, which would match 29:1–4, where God himself besieges the city. Most readers, including the ESV, the Tyndale notes, Henry, Guzik and JFB, take it as God fighting *for* Zion, his prey in the sense of what belongs to him and will not be surrendered. Verse 5 supports this protective reading.
- **"Like birds hovering, so the LORD of hosts will protect Jerusalem" (v5).** The image shifts from the fierce to the tender. A mother bird hovers over her nest, flapping and swooping to drive off a threat. Moses used the same picture: "like an eagle that stirs up its nest, that flutters over its young" (Deut 32:11). Jesus used it of himself as he wept over this same city: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" (Matt 23:37). JFB sums up the two images: the lion shows God's "fearless might," the birds his "solicitous affection."
- **"He will protect and deliver it; he will spare and rescue it" (v5).** "Spare" is *pasoach*, from *pasach*, the Passover verb. BDB gives "pass or spring over," and lists Exodus 12:13, 23 and 27, where God "passed over" Israel's houses, and then this verse. Isaiah is saying that God will do for Jerusalem what he did on Passover night in Egypt: his judgment will strike the enemy and pass over his people. JFB makes the Passover connection, and Guzik notes it too. It is pointed: Judah ran to Egypt for help, and God answers by recalling the night he rescued them *from* Egypt.
- **"Turn to him from whom people have deeply revolted, O children of Israel" (v6).** Here is the response God wants. "Turn" is *shuv*, repentance (30:15). "Deeply revolted" is literally "made deep the revolt," their rebellion has gone a long way down. Notice the call comes *after* the promise of rescue. God's grace is what makes repentance possible. JFB observes that the speech moves from "people" (third person) to "O children of Israel" (direct address), as if God draws closer when he invites them home.
- **"In that day everyone shall cast away his idols of silver and his idols of gold" (v7).** Hezekiah's reforms did remove many idols (2 Kings 18:4), but Isaiah looks to a day when the idols are thrown out by everyone. The same promise appeared in 2:20 and 30:22. **"Which your hands have sinfully made for you."** The irony is that they trusted things they themselves had made.
- **"The Assyrian shall fall by a sword, not of man; and a sword, not of man, shall devour him" (v8).** The repetition is deliberate. Egypt's swords could not save Judah, and no human sword would bring down Assyria. In 701 BC, "the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians" (37:36; 2 Kings 19:35). The Greek historian Herodotus preserves a garbled Egyptian version of the same event, in which mice overran Sennacherib's camp and chewed up the bowstrings and shield straps. Some scholars think this hints at a plague, but Scripture attributes it to God's angel. JFB calls it "the unseen sword of God." **"His young men shall be put to forced labor"** may mean the survivors become captives. JFB notes it could also mean they are made to pay tribute.
- **"His rock shall pass away in terror, and his officers desert the standard in panic" (v9).** "His rock" is probably Assyria's stronghold or its king, the one the army leaned on. Sennacherib went home to Nineveh (37:37), and his officers abandoned their battle standards. The Tyndale notes connect this directly with his retreat.
- **"Declares the LORD, whose fire is in Zion, and whose furnace is in Jerusalem" (v9).** God's fire burns on the temple altar in Jerusalem (Lev 6:12–13), the sign of his presence among his people. To his people that fire means worship and forgiveness through sacrifice. To his enemies it is a furnace. This is the same double picture as "Ariel," the altar hearth (29:1–2), and the burning place of Topheth (30:33). JFB gives both meanings: the altar fire at Jerusalem, or a furnace awaiting the attackers.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as a warning against Egypt, assurance of God's protection, a call to repentance, and the fall of Assyria's army and the terror of its king. He insists that God "also is wise," though Judah never consulted him. Guzik notes that Judah was not faithless so much as trusting in the wrong place, and he presses the tenderness of the bird image alongside the strength of the lion. JFB is the fullest on the Passover link in v5, God destroying the enemy "and to spare His people," and on the shift to direct address in v6 as an invitation home.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah went down to Egypt for horses and found only flesh. God offered himself: a lion who will not give up what is his, and a mother bird who hovers over her young. Jesus took up that very picture over the same city, longing to gather Jerusalem's children under his wings. And the Passover word of v5, "he will spare," points to the true Passover Lamb, whose blood turns away judgment from those who shelter under it (1 Cor 5:7). Assyria fell "by a sword, not of man." Sin and death fell the same way, not by human strength but by the cross and the empty tomb. The call of v6 is still open: "Turn to him."
