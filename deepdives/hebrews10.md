*Setting:* The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to weary Jewish Christians tempted to drift back to the old ways, is finishing its longest and deepest argument. Since 4:14 the writer has shown Jesus as the great high priest: a priest "after the order of Melchizedek" (ch 7), the mediator of a better covenant (ch 8), who entered the true holy place in heaven "by means of his own blood" (ch 9). Chapter 9 ended with Christ "offered once to bear the sins of many," and coming again to save those waiting for him (9:28). Chapter 10 draws the whole case together. The old sacrifices could never finish the job, and Christ's one offering did. Then the sermon turns, as it always does, from teaching to urging: draw near, hold fast, meet together. That is followed by one more severe warning and a warm reminder of how bravely these believers once stood.

#### Christ's Sacrifice Once for All (vv1–18)
- **"A shadow of the good things to come" (v1).** A shadow proves something real is there, and it shows the rough outline, but nobody mistakes a shadow for the person casting it. "The true form" is *eikōn*, "image," the thing itself in full detail. Paul uses the same picture: the festivals and sabbaths "are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ" (Col 2:17). The writer has already called the tabernacle "a copy and shadow" of the heavenly one (8:5). The law was not wrong. It was a preview.
- **"The same sacrifices that are continually offered every year" (v1).** He is thinking above all of the Day of Atonement (Lev 16), when the high priest entered the Most Holy Place once a year. The key word is "same." Year after year the same blood, the same goats, the same ritual. "Make perfect" means bring to the goal, here a clean conscience and real access to God (9:9).
- **"Would they not have ceased to be offered?" (v2).** This is the argument from repetition. A medicine that has to be taken every day forever has not cured the disease. If the sacrifices had truly cleansed, the worshipers would no longer carry a "consciousness of sins," that is, a guilty conscience.
- **"A reminder of sins every year" (v3).** The Day of Atonement was meant to bring relief, and it did bring a measure of it, but it also reminded everyone that the problem was still there. Jesus used the same word family at the Last Supper: "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). Under the old covenant, worship remembered sins. Under the new, it remembers the one who took them away.
- **"Impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (v4).** Animal blood did bring outward, ceremonial cleansing (9:13), and God truly forgave repentant Israelites. But an animal cannot stand in for a human being, and it cannot choose to. The prophets already knew this (Ps 51:16–17; Mic 6:6–8). The sacrifices pointed past themselves.
- **"When Christ came into the world, he said" (v5).** The writer quotes Psalm 40:6–8, a psalm of David, and hears the Son speaking it at his incarnation. The ESV footnote notes the Greek says simply "he." David gave himself to God's will in a small way. The Son did it completely.
- **"A body have you prepared for me" (v5).** The Hebrew of Psalm 40:6 reads "you have dug out ears for me," which probably means "you have opened my ears" to listen and obey. The writer follows the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint), which reads "a body you have prepared." The two readings agree on the point: God wants a whole life of obedience, not animals. Ears that hear belong to a body that obeys. For Hebrews the Greek wording fits perfectly, because Jesus' obedience was carried out in a real human body, and that body became the sacrifice (v10).
- **"He does away with the first in order to establish the second" (vv8–9).** The writer reads the psalm carefully, in order. First comes God's lack of pleasure in sacrifices "offered according to the law." Then comes "Behold, I have come to do your will." The old system is set aside, and the will of God done by the Son takes its place. Jesus prayed it in Gethsemane: "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42).
- **"By that will we have been sanctified... once for all" (v10).** "Sanctified" means set apart as God's own, made fit for his presence. The tense is perfect: it is done and it stands. "Once for all" (*ephapax*) is one of the sermon's key words (7:27; 9:12). The ground of a believer's holiness is not his or her effort but the Son's obedience and the offering of his body.
- **"Every priest stands daily" (v11).** There was no chair in the tabernacle. The furniture was a lampstand, a table, an altar of incense, the ark, but nowhere to sit, because a priest's work was never finished. "Stands" and "repeatedly the same sacrifices" sum up the old order.
- **"He sat down at the right hand of God" (vv12–13).** This is the fourth time Hebrews has pictured Jesus seated (1:3, 13; 8:1). Sitting means the work is done. Psalm 110:1, "Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool," is the Old Testament verse the New Testament quotes and echoes more than any other. The sacrifice is finished, though the victory over every enemy, death last of all, is still to be displayed (1 Cor 15:25–26). The ESV footnote notes that the Greek says "this one," setting him in sharp contrast with "every priest."
- **"He has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (v14).** Two tenses side by side. "Has perfected" is complete, a finished standing before God. "Are being sanctified" is ongoing, the present growth of believers. A Christian is already fully accepted and is still being made holy in practice. Both rest on the same "single offering."
- **"The Holy Spirit also bears witness to us" (v15).** The writer quotes Jeremiah 31:33–34 again, which he quoted at length in 8:8–12, and he says the Spirit is the one speaking in Jeremiah. Scripture is not only what God once said. It is what the Spirit still says "to us."
- **"I will put my laws on their hearts... I will remember their sins... no more" (vv16–17).** The new covenant changes people from the inside, which stone tablets could never do (Ezek 36:26–27). And God does not forget the way a person forgets. He chooses not to hold the sins against his people ever again. That is exactly what the annual "reminder of sins" (v3) could not provide.
- **"There is no longer any offering for sin" (v18).** This is the conclusion of the argument that began in 4:14. "Forgiveness" (*aphesis*) means release, sending away. If sins are truly forgiven, any further sacrifice is pointless. For readers tempted to go back to the temple, the point is plain: the temple has nothing left to offer that Christ has not already given.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and Matthew Henry both lean hard on the contrast between a shadow and the real thing. Henry compares the law to a man's shadow on a wall next to his reflection in a mirror, and says "the law had a shadow, and but a shadow, of good things to come." He adds that a true atoning sacrifice "must be one capable of consenting," which no animal is. Guzik says the Hebrew word for atonement means "to cover," so animal blood covered sin without removing it. That is an old explanation, but scholars debate it: the verb *kipper* may mean "wipe clean" or "ransom" instead. The point in Hebrews does not depend on it. JFB discusses the "ears" and "body" readings of Psalm 40 and argues that preparing the ear implies preparing the whole body for obedience. On v12 JFB argues that "one sacrifice" for ever rules out any repeated offering. Guzik adds that because God remembers their sins no more, the forgiven are not on probation. Henry calls it "a perfect and a perfecting sacrifice."

#### The Full Assurance of Faith (vv19–39)
- **"Therefore, brothers" (v19).** The ESV footnote gives "brothers and sisters." The long teaching section (4:14–10:18) now turns into exhortation, and these verses repeat the language of 4:14–16 like bookends: a great priest, confidence, drawing near.
- **"Confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus" (v19).** "Confidence" is *parrhēsia*, bold, free speech, the opposite of fearful hesitation (3:6; 4:16). Under the old covenant one man entered the Most Holy Place once a year, with blood, and with fear. Now every believer may come into God's presence, because of better blood.
- **"The new and living way" (v20).** "New" is *prosphatos*, a rare word. The lexicon says it originally meant "freshly slain" before coming to mean simply "fresh" or "recent." Most scholars think the writer simply means "new," but the old sense is a striking echo in a verse about sacrifice. It is also "living," because it leads to the living God through a living Savior (7:25).
- **"Through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (v20).** When Jesus died, the temple curtain was torn in two from top to bottom (Matt 27:51; Mark 15:38). His body broken on the cross was the curtain opened. Whether "his flesh" explains "the curtain" or "the way" is debated, but either way access to God is through Jesus' death.
- **Three "let us" calls (vv22–24).** Draw near, hold fast, consider one another. They line up with faith (v22), hope (v23) and love (v24), the same three Paul often joins (1 Cor 13:13; 1 Thess 1:3).
- **"Hearts sprinkled clean... bodies washed with pure water" (v22).** This is priestly language. Aaron and his sons were washed with water and sprinkled with blood when they were ordained (Exod 29:4, 21; Lev 8:6, 30). Ezekiel promised, "I will sprinkle clean water on you" (Ezek 36:25). The writer pictures all believers as priests fit to enter. Most interpreters, JFB among them, hear an allusion to Christian baptism in "bodies washed," the outward sign of the inward cleansing of the conscience, though some take the whole phrase as a picture of inner cleansing only.
- **"Hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering" (v23).** The "confession" is what they said aloud when they first believed, likely at their baptism (4:14). The ground for holding on is not their own strength: "he who promised is faithful."
- **"Consider how to stir up one another" (v24).** "Stir up" is a strong word (*paroxysmos*). Elsewhere it means a sharp quarrel, like the "sharp disagreement" between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:39). Here the same intensity is aimed in a good direction: provoke one another to love.
- **"Not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some" (v25).** The word for meeting together (*episynagōgē*) is rare. Its only other New Testament use is our "being gathered together to him" at Christ's coming (2 Thess 2:1). Why some were staying away is not said. It may have been fear of persecution, embarrassment at being linked with an unpopular group, or a quiet slide back to the synagogue. Whatever the reason, isolation was the first step toward falling away. Believers need one another to keep going.
- **"As you see the Day drawing near" (v25).** "The Day" is the day of the Lord, Christ's return (9:28). Some interpreters also see a nearer fulfilment in the destruction of Jerusalem and its temple in AD 70, which may have been only a few years away when this was written.
- **"If we go on sinning deliberately" (v26).** This is the fourth and sternest of the sermon's warnings (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 6:4–8). "Go on sinning" is a present participle, a continuing course, not a single failure. "Deliberately" (*hekousiōs*) means willingly, of one's own free choice. The Law of Moses distinguished unintentional sins, which sacrifices covered, from sinning "with a high hand," open defiance, for which there was no sacrifice (Num 15:27–31). In context the sin is turning away from Christ himself after "receiving the knowledge of the truth." It is not the ordinary sins every believer struggles with.
- **"There no longer remains a sacrifice for sins" (v26).** This is the dark side of v18. If Christ's sacrifice is the only one that works, a person who rejects it has nowhere else to go. The temple's altars cannot help.
- **"On the evidence of two or three witnesses" (v28).** The law required this for the death penalty, especially for someone who led Israel into idolatry (Deut 17:2–7). The argument runs from lesser to greater, as in 2:2–3: if rejecting Moses was that serious, rejecting the Son is far more serious.
- **Three charges (v29).** Such a person has "trampled underfoot the Son of God," treating him like dirt. He has "profaned the blood of the covenant," literally counted it "common," ordinary, like any other death. And he has "outraged the Spirit of grace." That verb (*enybrizō*) means to insult or treat with contempt. It is the opposite of receiving grace, and it recalls Jesus' warning about blasphemy against the Spirit (Mark 3:28–30).
- **"By which he was sanctified" (v29).** This phrase is the heart of a long debate. Faithful Christians read the warning in three main ways. Many Wesleyan and Arminian readers believe it describes real believers who can turn away and be lost. Many Reformed readers believe it describes people who were part of the covenant community and outwardly set apart, like Israel in the wilderness, yet never truly born again (see v39 and 3:14). Some others take it as a real warning to real believers about severe discipline or loss of reward rather than eternal judgment. All agree on what the writer wants: no one in the congregation should treat this lightly, and no one should turn back. As in 6:9, he immediately says he expects better things of his hearers.
- **"Vengeance is mine... The Lord will judge his people" (v30).** Both quotations come from the Song of Moses (Deut 32:35–36). Paul quotes the first to tell believers not to take revenge (Rom 12:19). "His people" is the sobering part: judgment begins with those who claim to belong to God (1 Pet 4:17).
- **"A fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (v31).** David once chose to "fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is great" rather than into human hands (2 Sam 24:14). For the repentant, God's hands are the safest place there is. For someone who has spurned his mercy, there is nothing more fearful.
- **"Recall the former days" (v32).** The tone changes completely. "After you were enlightened" uses the same word as 6:4. At their conversion these believers had faced "a hard struggle," a word (*athlēsis*) borrowed from athletic contests.
- **"Publicly exposed to reproach and affliction" (v33).** "Publicly exposed" is *theatrizō*, from the word for theater: to be made a spectacle. Paul says the apostles were "a spectacle to the world" (1 Cor 4:9). Some of them suffered directly, and others stood beside those who did, which in that culture meant sharing the shame.
- **"You had compassion on those in prison" (v34).** Roman prisons gave prisoners little or nothing, so visitors had to bring food and clothing, and visiting a prisoner marked a person as an associate (13:3; Matt 25:36). The King James reads "of me in my bonds," based on later manuscripts, and that reading helped the old tradition that Paul wrote Hebrews. The older manuscripts read "on those in prison," which the ESV follows.
- **"You joyfully accepted the plundering of your property" (v34).** Their goods were seized, by mobs or by authorities. If the readers lived in or near Rome (13:24), many scholars connect this with the trouble that led Emperor Claudius to expel Jews from Rome around AD 49 (Acts 18:2). That is a reasonable guess, not something the text states. They had not yet faced martyrdom (12:4). They could let go of their property because they knew they had "a better possession and an abiding one."
- **"Do not throw away your confidence" (v35).** The same word as v19, *parrhēsia*. Their bold confidence before God is worth keeping, because it "has a great reward."
- **"You have need of endurance" (v36).** This word (*hypomonē*) sets up the next two chapters: the heroes of faith (ch 11) and "let us run with endurance" (12:1). Doing God's will comes first, and receiving the promise follows.
- **"Yet a little while" (v37).** The writer blends two passages from the Greek Old Testament. "A little while" comes from Isaiah 26:20. The rest is Habakkuk 2:3–4. In Habakkuk the subject is the vision that "will surely come." The Greek version makes it a person, "the coming one," and Hebrews reads it as Christ returning (Matt 11:3). He also swaps the order of the two lines, so that the warning about shrinking back comes last and leads into v39.
- **"My righteous one shall live by faith" (v38).** Habakkuk 2:4 is quoted three times in the New Testament (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11). Paul uses it to show that a person is made right with God by faith. Hebrews uses it to show that the righteous keep living by faith, holding on while they wait. The two uses fit together: faith that begins the Christian life is the same faith that keeps going.
- **"We are not of those who shrink back" (v39).** "Shrink back" is *hypostolē*, a drawing back in fear or timidity. The writer includes himself ("we") and states his confidence in them. "Preserve their souls" (*peripoiēsis*) means keep safe or gain possession of. This verse is the doorway into chapter 11, which shows what living by faith looks like.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the veil as Christ's body and says "it is a living way," leading to life through a living Savior. He treats "not to forsake the assembling of ourselves together" as one of the means God uses to keep his people faithful. Guzik notes the old explanation that "new" pictures fresh blood, still warm, and thinks the readers' deepest problem was not hardship but drifting from closeness with God. On v26 all three agree this is a settled turning away from Christ, not ordinary sin. Henry calls it a final, deliberate rejection after knowing the truth. JFB notes the present participle describes a state, not isolated acts. Guzik, following Adam Clarke, says the warning is not aimed at believers who stumble and come back. On the manuscripts, JFB notes that the oldest ones lack "me" in v34. On v38, JFB holds that those who "draw back" had only an apparent faith, while Henry says "drawing back from God is drawing on to perdition." Guzik observes that each New Testament use of Habakkuk 2:4 leans on a different word: Romans on "faith," Galatians on "the righteous" (KJV "the just"), Hebrews on "live."

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole chapter turns on a contrast between priests who stand and a priest who sits. The old priests stood all day because their work could never end. Jesus offered one sacrifice, in a body prepared for that purpose, and sat down because nothing was left to do. That finished work is why sinners can draw near boldly, why the faithful God's promises hold, and why turning away from Christ is so serious: there is nowhere else to turn. It also explains how frightened, plundered believers could hold on with joy. "The coming one will come and will not delay," and he is the same Jesus who opened the way through his own flesh.
