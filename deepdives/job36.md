*Setting:* Elihu has given three speeches (chs 32–35). In the last one he told Job that God does not answer empty cries and that Job "multiplies words without knowledge" (35:16). Job still has not replied. Now Elihu begins his fourth and final speech, which runs through chapter 37. It is different in tone from what came before. He returns to his best idea, that God uses suffering to teach and rescue, and then turns from arguing to praising. By the end of the chapter he is describing rain, clouds and thunder, the greatness of a God "we know... not" (v26). Many readers think a real storm was gathering as he spoke. If so, it is the storm out of which God himself will speak in chapter 38.

#### Elihu Extols God's Greatness (vv1–4)
- **"And Elihu continued" (v1).** The other speeches open with "Elihu answered." This one does not, perhaps because no one had said anything for him to answer.
- **"Bear with me a little" (v2).** He asks for a little more patience. He then speaks for two more chapters. "I have yet something to say on God's behalf." Elihu sees himself as God's advocate. Job had warned the friends against exactly this: "Will you speak falsely for God?" (13:7).
- **"I will get my knowledge from afar" (v3).** Elihu means he will draw on the wide sweep of God's works, not on narrow traditions or proverbs. "And ascribe righteousness to my Maker": his goal is to show God is just. That is a worthy aim.
- **"One who is perfect in knowledge is with you" (v4).** This sounds breathtakingly arrogant if Elihu means himself. The same phrase is used of God in 37:16: "the wondrous works of him who is perfect in knowledge." Two readings: some say Elihu means only that his knowledge is sound and sincere, not false like the friends' (the word for "perfect" can mean "whole" or "honest"). Others think he does mean God: the perfectly knowing God is present with Job. The Tyndale notes take it as a claim of sincerity or sound reasoning.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv2–4 simply Elihu's "preface." JFB reads v4 as Elihu meaning himself, "a man of integrity in sentiments," in contrast to the friends' slanted reasoning. Guzik leans the other way. He thinks the phrase most likely refers to God, citing 37:16, though he passes on Poole's view that Elihu means his own God-given understanding. Guzik also enjoys the irony of "bear with me a little," and passes on one scholar's description of Elihu's self-introduction as a "certificate of genius."

#### Elihu Extols God's Greatness (vv5–12)
- **"God is mighty, and does not despise any" (v5).** This is one of Elihu's finest lines. Power in human hands often looks down on the weak. God's power does not. He is great enough to notice everyone. "Mighty in strength of understanding": his strength is joined to wisdom. Job had feared the opposite, that God "mocks at the calamity of the innocent" (9:23).
- **"He does not keep the wicked alive, but gives the afflicted their right" (v6).** "The afflicted" (*aniyyim*) means the poor and humbled. God takes up their cause (Ps 140:12).
- **"He does not withdraw his eyes from the righteous" (v7).** God watches over the righteous for good (Ps 33:18; 34:15). Job had experienced God's watching as a burden (7:19–20). Elihu turns it into a promise: those eyes are for protection and honor. "With kings on the throne he sets them forever." Joseph rose from prison to Pharaoh's side, and David from the sheepfold to the throne.
- **"If they are bound in chains and caught in the cords of affliction" (v8).** Now Elihu returns to his main idea from 33:14–30. When the righteous suffer, it can be God's way of showing them something. "He declares to them their work and their transgressions, that they are behaving arrogantly" (v9). The word for "behaving arrogantly" suggests making oneself great, overconfident.
- **"He opens their ears to instruction and commands that they return from iniquity" (v10).** "Opens their ears" is the same phrase as in 33:16. "Instruction" (*musar*) is the word for discipline and correction, the word in Proverbs 3:11: "do not despise the LORD's discipline."
- **"If they listen and serve him, they complete their days in prosperity" (v11).** This is the turning point. The same suffering can lead to two outcomes. Those who listen are restored. "But if they do not listen, they perish by the sword and die without knowledge" (v12). The phrase "by the sword" may also be read "cross the river" of death, as in 33:18.
- **Is this true of Job?** In general, yes: God does use suffering to correct, and Hebrews 12:5–11 says so plainly. But the book's opening chapters show Job's suffering did not begin because of hidden arrogance. Elihu has the right principle and the wrong patient. Yet in the end Job *will* listen, and his days will end "in prosperity" (42:10–17), not as reward for repenting of hidden sin but as God's grace after the trial.
- **Commentators:** JFB stresses that affliction does not prove a man a hypocrite. It is God's discipline of the godly, showing them their sins so that they humble themselves, and humble submission brings blessing. It reads "behaving arrogantly" as acting presumptuously or self-confidently. Guzik draws out v5: God's greatness means he despises no one, and he passes on Meyer and Spurgeon developing that comfort. But he also notes the barb: Elihu implies that since God's eyes seem to have left Job, Job must be among the wicked. Henry's outline calls this section "the methods of God's providence towards the children of men."

#### Elihu Extols God's Greatness (vv13–16)
- **"The godless in heart cherish anger; they do not cry for help when he binds them" (v13).** The difference between those restored and those destroyed is the heart. The godless "cherish" anger, literally "set" or "store up" anger, nursing resentment against God instead of calling on him. Paul uses a similar picture: "you are storing up wrath for yourself" (Rom 2:5).
- **"They die in youth, and their life ends among the cult prostitutes" (v14).** The word is *qedeshim*, men attached to pagan shrines (Deut 23:17; 1 Kings 14:24). The point is that they die young and in disgrace, their life ending in shame. Some take it to mean their death is as worthless as the lives of such men.
- **"He delivers the afflicted by their affliction and opens their ear by adversity" (v15).** This is the key verse of Elihu's whole contribution, and there is wordplay in the Hebrew: God rescues the *ani* (afflicted) by means of his *oni* (affliction). The suffering itself becomes the tool of rescue. "Adversity" is the word for being squeezed or pressed. Pressure opens the ear. The psalmist learned it: "Before I was afflicted I went astray, but now I keep your word... It is good for me that I was afflicted, that I might learn your statutes" (Ps 119:67, 71).
- **"He also allured you out of distress into a broad place" (v16).** Elihu turns to Job. God was drawing Job out, or wanted to, from a tight place into wide space, the Bible's picture for freedom and relief ("He brought me out into a broad place," Ps 18:19). "What was set on your table was full of fatness": a feast. The verb "allured" means to entice or draw gently, and the same verb returns as a warning in v18. God has been luring Job toward relief, and Elihu warns him not to be lured the other way. The Hebrew of vv16–20 is among the hardest in the book, and translations differ a good deal.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v15's "afflicted" as the afflicted godly, whom God instructs through oppression, and v16 as God leading them into "a broad place" of liberty with a well-spread table. It takes the "unclean" of v14 as a premature, dishonorable end. Guzik takes vv13–14 as Elihu painting Job as a hypocrite with a bleak future, and passes on Adam Clarke's explanation that those in v14 were men consecrated to idol worship. Henry, in his outline, sees here God's methods of providence: affliction as a call to return.

#### Elihu Extols God's Greatness (vv17–23)
- **"But you are full of the judgment on the wicked" (v17).** Job is preoccupied with the question of judgment on the wicked, why they prosper and why he suffers like one of them. Elihu warns that dwelling on it has gripped him: "judgment and justice seize you."
- **"Beware lest wrath entice you into scoffing" (v18).** "Entice" is the same verb as "allured" in v16. Anger at God can lure a sufferer into mocking him. "Let not the greatness of the ransom turn you aside." "Ransom" (*kopher*) is the word from 33:24. The sense is debated. It may mean: do not let the heavy price of your suffering (the "ransom" you are paying) drive you away from God. Or it may warn against thinking wealth could buy a way out (as the next verse suggests). JFB gives both readings.
- **"Will your cry for help avail... or all the force of your strength?" (v19).** Neither shouting nor resources will rescue Job from trouble. Only God can.
- **"Do not long for the night, when peoples vanish in their place" (v20).** Job had longed for death, the "night" (3:3–13; 7:15–16). Elihu warns him not to wish for it. Death is not a way out from God.
- **"Do not turn to iniquity, for this you have chosen rather than affliction" (v21).** Elihu's warning is that Job is close to choosing sin, rebellion against God, rather than accepting affliction. This is a fair warning to any sufferer. But Job had not chosen sin. God later says Job spoke rightly of him (42:7).
- **"Behold, God is exalted in his power; who is a teacher like him?" (v22).** Here the tone shifts. Elihu's main idea has been God as teacher, through dreams, pain and messengers. Now he simply marvels. "Who has prescribed for him his way, or who can say, 'You have done wrong'?" (v23). No one gives God directions, and no one can correct him. Isaiah asks the same: "Who has measured the Spirit of the LORD, or what man shows him his counsel?" (Isa 40:13; quoted in Rom 11:34).
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline calls vv16–21 "the fair warning and good counsel he gives to Job." JFB reads v20 as Job panting for death as an escape, when it is really darkness, and v21 as sin being worse than suffering: "complaining against God" adds sin to sorrow. On v18 it lists two readings: a great ransom will not save willful despisers, or do not be seduced by riches. Guzik insists that chapters 1–2 show Elihu is plainly wrong about why Job suffers, and that "you have chosen this rather than affliction" would have maddened Job, because it asked him to repent of something he could not honestly confess. Guzik admits Job had faults he would later repent of (42:1–6), but credits him with keeping his integrity. He notes the change of tone from v22 on.

#### Elihu Extols God's Greatness (vv24–33)
- **"Remember to extol his work, of which men have sung" (v24).** Instead of accusing God, Job should praise his works. People in every age have sung of them (Ps 104; Ps 19:1). "Man beholds it from afar" (v25): we see God's work from a distance, only the edges.
- **"Behold, God is great, and we know him not; the number of his years is unsearchable" (v26).** This is the heart of the closing hymn, and perhaps Elihu's truest sentence. God is beyond human measuring, eternal: "from everlasting to everlasting you are God" (Ps 90:2). Job had said much the same (9:10; 26:14), and Zophar too (11:7). Hebrews 1:12 applies to Christ the words "your years will have no end" (Ps 102:27).
- **"He draws up the drops of water; they distill his mist in rain" (v27).** Elihu describes the water cycle: drops drawn up, gathered as mist, poured down as rain. Some scholars have noticed how accurate this is, since evaporation cannot be seen. Whatever Elihu understood of the process, his point is that rain is God's work, "poured down... on mankind abundantly" (v28). The book has already said God "gives rain on the earth" (5:10), and Jesus says the Father "sends rain on the just and on the unjust" (Matt 5:45).
- **"Can anyone understand the spreading of the clouds, the thunderings of his pavilion?" (v29).** The storm clouds are God's tent or canopy. "He made darkness his covering, his canopy around him, thick clouds dark with water" (Ps 18:11). Thunder is the sound from within his tent.
- **"He scatters his lightning about him and covers the roots of the sea" (v30).** The word for lightning is literally "his light." Light spreads across the sky while the depths of the sea are covered in darkness.
- **"By these he judges peoples; he gives food in abundance" (v31).** Rain and storm do two things. They bring judgment, as with the flood (Gen 7) and the hail on Egypt (Exod 9:23–25), and they bring harvest. The Tyndale notes mention that some propose a slightly different Hebrew word meaning "he nourishes," but the usual reading is "he judges."
- **"He covers his hands with the lightning and commands it to strike the mark" (v32).** God holds lightning like a warrior's weapon, aiming it where he wills. Nothing in the storm is random.
- **"Its crashing declares his presence; the cattle also declare that he rises" (v33).** The thunder announces God's coming. Animals sense a storm before people do and grow restless. This verse is one of the hardest in Job, and translations vary widely. The ESV's reading fits the picture: all creation, even the cattle, feels God approaching. The storm continues into chapter 37, and then God speaks "out of the whirlwind" (38:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline calls vv22–33 "his demonstration of God's sovereignty and omnipotence," a reason for all to submit to him. JFB applies "his years" in v26 to Christ (Heb 1:12), reads v31 as rain and lightning both chastising and providing food, and v32 as lightning sent against God's enemies while his friends receive gentle light. It takes v33 as the thunder announcing the storm, sensed by cattle and plants. Guzik notes many commentators feel Elihu becomes truly inspired here, and some, like G. Campbell Morgan, think he is describing an approaching storm as he speaks. He contrasts Job and Elihu when God draws near: Job will fall silent, while Elihu keeps talking. He also passes on Poole's observation that cattle sense a storm before people do.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elihu's best sentence is that God "delivers the afflicted by their affliction" (v15). The fullest proof of that is the cross. God did not rescue the world in spite of suffering but through it. Jesus "learned obedience through what he suffered" (Heb 5:8), and "by his wounds we are healed" (Isa 53:5). The one who is truly "perfect in knowledge" did not stand above the storm lecturing those caught in it. He entered it. On the Sea of Galilee he slept in the storm and then spoke to it, and the disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41). Elihu said, "God is great, and we know him not." In Christ the great God has made himself known: "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (John 1:18).
