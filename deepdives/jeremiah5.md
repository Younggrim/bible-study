*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably in the last years of Josiah or the first years of Jehoiakim (about 615–605 BC). Chapter 4 sounded the alarm: a lion from the north, a scorching wind, and a vision of the land undone. Chapter 5 explains why the judgment cannot be turned aside. It opens with a search through the city for one honest person, and it ends with a nation whose prophets lie, whose priests rule by those lies, and whose people "love to have it so." Twice the same question frames the chapter: "Shall I not punish them for these things?" (vv9, 29). Yet twice in the middle God promises, "I will not make a full end" (vv10, 18).

#### Jerusalem Refused to Repent (vv1–13)
- **"Run to and fro through the streets of Jerusalem" (v1).** The command is plural, as if God sends a search party through the city. They are to check the "squares," the open spaces by the gates where people met to trade, talk and settle disputes. That was where honesty or dishonesty showed.
- **"If you can find a man, one who does justice and seeks truth, that I may pardon her" (v1).** This recalls Abraham bargaining for Sodom (Gen 18:23–32). There God would have spared the city for ten righteous people. Here he would spare Jerusalem for one. It is a hyperbole. There were faithful people in Jerusalem: Jeremiah himself, Baruch, Ebed-melech, the family of Shaphan. The point is that the public life of the city, its courts, markets and leaders, has no honesty left (Ps 14:2–3; Ezek 22:30). "Truth" (*emunah*) means faithfulness, the reliability that makes a person's word worth trusting.
- **"Though they say, 'As the LORD lives,' yet they swear falsely" (v2).** 4:2 called for exactly this oath "in truth." Here they swear by God's name and lie anyway. Using God's name to back a lie broke the third commandment (Exod 20:7).
- **"O LORD, do not your eyes look for truth?" (v3).** Jeremiah answers his own search. God wants faithfulness, and he has disciplined them to get it. **"You have struck them down, but they felt no anguish… they refused to take correction."** Amos 4:6–11 lists the same pattern: famine, drought, plague, war, "yet you did not return to me." **"They have made their faces harder than rock."** The same toughness as the "forehead of a whore" in 3:3.
- **"These are only the poor; they have no sense" (v4).** Jeremiah makes an excuse for them. The poor, he thinks, are uneducated and struggling to survive, so they never learned God's ways. **"I will go to the great"** (v5): surely the leaders, who were taught the law, will be different.
- **"But they all alike had broken the yoke; they had burst the bonds" (v5).** The rich and powerful are no better. The yoke of God's law was meant to bind them to him, as an ox is yoked to its master (2:20; Matt 11:29). Psalm 2:3 puts the same words in the mouths of rebel kings: "Let us burst their bonds apart."
- **"A lion from the forest… a wolf from the desert… a leopard is watching their cities" (v6).** Three predators picture the invaders. The lion is strong. The wolf "from the desert" could also be read "of the evenings," a wolf that hunts at dusk (Hab 1:8; Zeph 3:3), since the Hebrew words look alike. The leopard lies in wait outside the gates for anyone who goes out. Hosea 13:7–8 and Habakkuk 1:8 use the same animals. Leviticus 26:22 warned that wild beasts would be among the covenant curses.
- **"How can I pardon you?" (v7).** God answers v1. He wants to pardon. The question shows he is not looking for an excuse to judge.
- **"When I fed them to the full, they committed adultery" (v7).** Prosperity did not make them grateful. It made them proud and lustful, as Moses warned (Deut 8:10–14; 32:15: "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked"). **"Trooped to the houses of whores"** may mean ordinary prostitution, the ritual sex at pagan shrines, or both. Idolatry and sexual sin went together.
- **"Well-fed, lusty stallions, each neighing for his neighbor's wife" (v8).** A crude picture of men who have lost all restraint. The tenth commandment forbade even coveting a neighbor's wife (Exod 20:17). These men openly chase after one.
- **"Shall I not punish them for these things?" (v9).** The refrain that will return in v29 and 9:9. "Avenge myself" means God will see justice done for the covenant that has been broken. It is not personal spite.
- **"Go up through her vine rows and destroy, but make not a full end" (v10).** Judah is God's vineyard (2:21; Isa 5:1–7). The invaders are told to strip the branches but not dig up the root. **"For they are not the LORD's"**: the branches that turned wild no longer belong to him (John 15:6).
- **"The house of Israel and the house of Judah have been utterly treacherous" (v11).** Both kingdoms, the one already fallen and the one still standing, share the same guilt (3:6–11).
- **"They have spoken falsely of the LORD and have said, 'He will do nothing'" (v12).** In Hebrew it is just two words, *lo hu*: "Not he!" or "He is not." It is not a philosophical denial of God. It is practical unbelief: God will not act, so nothing will happen to us (Ps 10:4, 11; Zeph 1:12). **"No disaster will come upon us, nor shall we see sword or famine."**
- **"The prophets will become wind; the word is not in them" (v13).** These are the people speaking about the true prophets, mocking them as windbags. "Wind" is *ruach*, which also means "spirit." The insult may be that the prophets claim the Spirit but have nothing but air. **"Thus shall it be done to them!"**: let their threats fall on their own heads. Some interpreters, as the Tyndale notes mention, read v13 as God's words about the false prophets instead.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls v1 "a challenge to produce any one right honest man." JFB sees in it the mass of the people, "the king, his counsellors, the false prophets, and the priests," not the godly few who still existed. JFB also reads v2 as a criticism of Josiah's reform, which was "merely superficial" for most. Guzik notes that the search for one man points ahead to the one Man, Jesus, for whose sake God does pardon. On v5 Henry writes, "The rich were insolent and haughty, and therefore they were wicked." JFB calls the three beasts of v6 "the strongest, the most ravenous, and the swiftest." JFB also suggests that the KJV's "battlements" in v10 is better rendered "tendrils," which is the ESV's picture of vine branches. On v12 Henry says they "belied" God and contradicted "the dictates even of natural light concerning him."

#### The LORD Proclaims Judgment (vv14–31)
- **"Because you have spoken this word" (v14).** That is, because they said, "He will do nothing," and called the prophets wind.
- **"I am making my words in your mouth a fire, and this people wood" (v14).** God speaks to Jeremiah. The prophet's words are not wind. They are fire, and the people are dry wood. Later Jeremiah feels this fire inside him: "a burning fire shut up in my bones" (20:9; 23:29).
- **"A nation from afar… an enduring nation… an ancient nation" (v15).** Babylon's civilization went back more than a thousand years, to Hammurabi and beyond. "Enduring" is literally "perennial," like a stream that never runs dry. **"A nation whose language you do not know."** The Babylonians spoke Akkadian. It is a Semitic language, a distant cousin of Hebrew, but ordinary people in Judah could not understand it. Moses had warned of exactly this curse (Deut 28:49), and Isaiah (28:11). Paul quotes the Isaiah line in 1 Corinthians 14:21.
- **"Their quiver is like an open tomb" (v16).** Every arrow in it means a death. Babylon's archers were famous.
- **"They shall eat up… they shall eat up… they shall eat up… they shall eat up" (v17).** Four times. Harvest, children, flocks, vines and figs: everything Judah lived on and loved. Deuteronomy 28:31–33, 51 lists the same losses. **"Your fortified cities in which you trust"** will fall. Archaeologists have found letters from Lachish, one of those fortified cities, written as Babylon closed in (compare 34:7).
- **"But even in those days… I will not make a full end of you" (v18).** The third time this promise appears (4:27; 5:10). Judgment will be severe, but there will be survivors.
- **"Why has the LORD our God done all these things to us?" (v19).** God gives Jeremiah the answer ahead of time, and it fits the crime: **"As you have forsaken me and served foreign gods in your land, so you shall serve foreigners in a land that is not yours."** They wanted foreign gods at home, so they will get foreign masters abroad (Deut 28:47–48).
- **"O foolish and senseless people, who have eyes, but see not" (v21).** "Senseless" is literally "without heart," since the heart in Hebrew was the seat of understanding. Isaiah 6:9–10 and Psalm 115:5–6 (of idols) use the same language. People become like the idols they serve. Jesus applies the words to his disciples' dullness (Mark 8:18).
- **"I placed the sand as the boundary for the sea" (v22).** To ancient peoples the sea was the great picture of chaos and danger. Yet a strip of sand holds it back, because God set the limit (Job 38:8–11; Ps 104:9). **"Though the waves toss, they cannot prevail."** The roaring sea obeys God. His people do not.
- **"A stubborn and rebellious heart" (v23).** The exact phrase used for the son a family could bring to the elders in Deuteronomy 21:18. Judah is that son.
- **"Who gives the rain in its season, the autumn rain and the spring rain" (v24).** Israel depended on two rainy seasons. The autumn (early) rain in October–November softened the ground for plowing and sowing. The spring (latter) rain in March–April filled out the grain. **"The weeks appointed for the harvest"** were the seven weeks from Passover to Pentecost (the Feast of Weeks), when the barley and wheat were reaped (Deut 16:9–10). The people thanked Baal for rain. They should have feared the God who actually sends it (Deut 11:13–17).
- **"Your iniquities have turned these away" (v25).** The drought of 3:3 and 14:1–6 was not an accident. Sin had shut the sky.
- **"They lurk like fowlers lying in wait. They set a trap; they catch men" (v26).** The ESV footnote says the Hebrew is uncertain, but the picture is clear. A fowler caught birds with nets and snares. These men use legal tricks, debt traps and bribed courts to catch people.
- **"Like a cage full of birds, their houses are full of deceit" (v27).** A fowler's wicker cage stuffed with captured birds. Their homes are packed with goods taken by fraud. **"They have become great and rich; they have grown fat and sleek"** (vv27–28).
- **"They judge not with justice the cause of the fatherless… they do not defend the rights of the needy" (v28).** The law repeatedly charged judges to protect the orphan, the widow and the foreigner, who had no one to speak for them (Exod 22:22–24; Deut 10:18; Isa 1:17). The rich have done the opposite.
- **"Shall I not punish them for these things?" (v29).** The refrain from v9 again. The first time it followed sexual sin and idolatry. Now it follows injustice to the poor. God counts both.
- **"An appalling and horrible thing has happened in the land" (v30).** The worst thing is saved for last.
- **"The prophets prophesy falsely, and the priests rule at their direction" (v31).** Literally the priests rule "by their hands," that is, alongside them or under their authority. Prophets and priests were meant to keep each other honest. Instead they worked together in a lie.
- **"My people love to have it so" (v31).** The saddest line in the chapter. Corrupt leaders could not survive without willing followers. The people wanted soothing messages and preferred them to the truth (Isa 30:10; Mic 2:11; 2 Tim 4:3–4: "they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions").
- **"But what will you do when the end comes?" (v31).** The chapter closes with a question with no answer. The lies will hold until the day they fail.
- **Commentators:** Henry puts v14 in eight words: "The word shall be fire and the people wood." On v22 he writes, "A wall of sand shall be as effectual as a wall of brass to check the flowing waves." JFB adds that the sea obeys God more readily than his people. JFB identifies "the weeks appointed" in v24 as the seven weeks between Passover and Pentecost. JFB says the fowlers of v26 "crouch" to catch men "to destroy men," and that the rich of v28 "exceed even the Gentiles in wickedness." On v31 JFB explains "rule by their means" as literally "according to their hands." Guzik names three horrors in v31, false prophets, priests who rule by their own power, and people who love it, and presses the final question. The Tyndale notes add that Babylonian was a Semitic language like Hebrew, yet ordinary Judeans could not understand it.

#### Pointing to Christ
God sent searchers through Jerusalem for one person who did justice and sought truth, and found none. Centuries later that one Man walked those same streets. Jesus is the faithful one, "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14), for whose sake God pardons. The rich of Jeremiah's day trapped the poor and ignored the fatherless. Jesus came "to proclaim good news to the poor" (Luke 4:18). The people loved prophets who told them that all was well. Jesus told the truth even when it cost him his life. Jeremiah asked, "What will you do when the end comes?" The gospel answers: run to the one Man whose righteousness covers a whole city of sinners.
