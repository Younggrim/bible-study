*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Miktam of David, when Saul sent men to watch his house in order to kill him." This is the earliest occasion of any psalm title. The story is in 1 Samuel 19. David was still living in Gibeah, married to Saul's daughter Michal, a hero in the army. One evening, as David played the lyre for him, Saul threw a spear at him, and David fled to his house (19:9–10). "Saul sent messengers to David's house to watch him, that he might kill him in the morning" (19:11). Michal warned him, let him down through a window, and put a household idol in his bed with goat's hair on its head to buy time (19:12–17). That night began roughly ten years of life on the run. The psalm has a clear shape built on two refrains. Twice the enemies come back at evening "howling like dogs" (vv6, 14), and twice David answers with "O my Strength… you, O God, are my fortress" (vv9–10, 17). The first half moves from prayer to waiting. The second moves from waiting to song.

#### Deliver Me from My Enemies (vv1–5)
- **"Deliver me from my enemies, O my God" (v1).** "My God" is the first of several personal names David gives God in this psalm: my God, my Strength, my fortress, the God who shows me steadfast love. Guzik lists them. Each one is a handhold.
- **"Protect me from those who rise up against me" (v1).** The verb is *sagav*, "to set on high," out of reach. The Tyndale notes give "place me on high." It is related to *misgav*, "fortress" or "high stronghold," the word David uses three times later (vv9, 16, 17). He asks to be lifted beyond the reach of men who are coming up the street.
- **"Those who work evil… bloodthirsty men" (v2).** Literally "men of bloods." They are not just rivals. They are murderers on assignment. JFB points back to 5:6, where the LORD "abhors the bloodthirsty and deceitful man."
- **"They lie in wait for my life; fierce men stir up strife against me" (v3).** "Lie in wait" is the language of an ambush. "Fierce men" can also mean "strong ones." These are Saul's soldiers outside the house in the dark.
- **"For no transgression or sin of mine, O LORD, for no fault of mine" (vv3–4).** David claims innocence three times over. He is not claiming to be sinless before God (see 51:3–5). He is saying he has done nothing to deserve this from Saul. That was true. Jonathan said the same to his father a few verses earlier: "he has not sinned against you… why then will you sin against innocent blood by killing David without cause?" (1 Sam 19:4–5). Henry: "The innocency of the godly will not secure them from the malignity of the wicked," but it "will greatly support and comfort us."
- **"They run and make ready" (v4).** JFB: "set themselves as in array," like soldiers forming ranks. Plumer, cited by Guzik, notes how hard the wicked work at evil and how that should shame lazy believers.
- **"Awake, come to meet me, and see!" (v4).** Bold language. God does not sleep (121:4), but David prays as though God has not yet noticed (compare 7:6; 44:23). It is the cry of the disciples in the boat: "Save us, Lord; we are perishing" (Matt 8:25), a link Henry makes.
- **"You, LORD God of hosts, are God of Israel" (v5).** A pile of names: *YHWH* (the covenant name), *'Elohim tseva'ot* (God of armies, commander of angels and stars), and God of Israel (bound to his people). Maclaren, cited by Guzik, says each name is a reason for hope. Henry: when Saul's armies hunted David, he turned to "the Lord of all hosts."
- **"Rouse yourself to punish all the nations" (v5).** This is a surprise. Saul's men were Israelites, not foreign nations. There are three main explanations. Henry thinks David is saying that men who behave this way are no better than pagans, "strangers to the commonwealth of Israel." Many scholars think the psalm, written in a personal crisis, was later used in Israel's worship when foreign enemies threatened, so the prayer was widened. Others note that David's enemies did include foreigners at times, like Doeg the Edomite (52:title). What is clear is that David appeals his case to the Judge of the whole world, not just of Israel.
- **"Spare none of those who treacherously plot evil. Selah" (v5).** "Treacherously" points to betrayal. These were men David had served alongside. Henry: "Let not those expect to find mercy who never showed mercy."
- **Commentators:** Henry says David pleads four things: the evil character of his enemies, their malice, his own innocence, and their contempt for God. He adds that God gives us leave "to fill our mouth with arguments, not to move him, but to move ourselves." Guzik notes that "defend" means to be set up high, in a safe place. JFB reads "awake" as an appeal to God "in His covenant relation to His people."

#### Each Evening They Come Back (vv6–10)
- **"Each evening they come back, howling like dogs and prowling about the city" (v6).** In the ancient Near East, dogs were not pets. They ran in half-wild packs, scavenged rubbish and corpses, and roamed towns at night (1 Kgs 14:11; 21:23). To call someone a dog was a deep insult (1 Sam 17:43; 24:14). Saul's assassins circle the house like a pack waiting for its meal. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, has a memorable line about this "cursing company of curs."
- **"Bellowing with their mouths with swords in their lips" (v7).** "Bellowing" is literally "pouring out" or "gushing," the word for a spring bubbling up (Prov 18:4). The King James has "belch out." Maclaren and Kidner, cited by Guzik, picture slavering, foaming mouths. JFB takes it as "impudent barkings," slanders. Their words are blades. Henry: "The sword perhaps would not have been in Saul's hand if it had not been first in their lips."
- **"For 'Who,' they think, 'will hear us?'" (v7).** The core of their sin is practical atheism: no one is listening, no one will hold us to account (10:11; 94:7). Henry: "It is not strange if those regard not what they say who have made themselves believe that God regards not what they say."
- **"But you, O LORD, laugh at them" (v8).** The answer to "Who will hear?" God hears, and he laughs. This is the laugh of Psalm 2:4: "He who sits in the heavens laughs; the Lord holds them in derision." It is not cruelty. It is the absurdity of creatures plotting against their Creator. JFB points to both 2:4 and 37:13.
- **"You hold all the nations in derision" (v8).** "Nations" again, as in v5. If God can laugh at all the nations, a squad of Saul's men is nothing.
- **"O my Strength, I will watch for you" (v9).** There is a wordplay here. The title says Saul sent men "to watch" (*shamar*) David's house. Now David says, "I will watch (*shamar*) for you." They are watching him to kill him. He is watching for God to save him. In the Hebrew text as it stands, v9 reads "his strength," which the King James renders "Because of his strength will I wait upon thee." Several Hebrew manuscripts and the ancient versions read "my Strength," matching v17, and JFB says "on good grounds" this is the better reading. The ESV follows it. The difference is one letter.
- **"For you, O God, are my fortress" (v9).** *Misgav*, the high stronghold, from the same root as "protect me" in v1. Morgan and Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, picture a tower too high for the dogs to reach.
- **"My God in his steadfast love will meet me" (v10).** "Meet" is *qadam*, to come before, to go out ahead of. The King James has "The God of my mercy shall prevent me," using "prevent" in its old sense, "go before." Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the word can mean going ahead to lead. God's love does not wait for David to reach it. It comes out to meet him (compare 21:3; 79:8). The Hebrew text has a small difference between what is written and what is read aloud here, and the King James "God of my mercy" follows one, the ESV the other, but both say the same thing: God's *chesed* is on David's side.
- **"God will let me look in triumph on my enemies" (v10).** Literally "God will let me look on my watchers," the same idea as 54:7. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes that "desire" (King James) is not in the Hebrew. David expects to look at his enemies calmly, safe at last.
- **Commentators:** Henry takes "his strength" two ways: the enemies' strength, "the fear of which drove him to God," or God's strength, "the hope of which drew him to God." He loves the phrase "God of my mercy": "Whatever mercy there is in God, it is laid up for us." JFB takes "my Strength" as the better reading. Guzik's application: the hunted man can watch for God as eagerly as the hunters watch for him.

#### Kill Them Not, Lest My People Forget (vv11–13)
- **"Kill them not, lest my people forget" (v11).** An unusual prayer. David does not want his enemies wiped out at once, because a quick judgment is quickly forgotten. A slow, visible one teaches a nation for years. JFB points to God leaving the Canaanite nations in the land to test Israel (Judg 2:21–23) and to Cain, who was not killed but made a wanderer (Gen 4:12). Henry: "Swift destructions startle men for the present, but they are soon forgotten."
- **"Make them totter by your power and bring them down, O Lord, our shield!" (v11).** "Totter" means to wander or stagger, as Cain wandered. "Our shield" brings in the whole people, not just David. He prays as Israel's future king.
- **"For the sin of their mouths, the words of their lips, let them be trapped in their pride" (v12).** The tongue is the chief weapon again: cursing and lies. Henry notes that Saul himself was given to rash curses (1 Sam 14:24, 44). The prayer is that they be caught by their own pride, the trap of their own making.
- **"Consume them in wrath; consume them till they are no more" (v13).** This seems to contradict v11. Guzik admits the tension and resolves it this way: David cares more that God acts than how he acts. JFB reads it as order in time: punishment may be "delayed for wise reasons," but "the utter destruction of the wicked must come at last." The slow humbling of v11 and the final end of v13 are stages of one judgment.
- **"That they may know that God rules over Jacob to the ends of the earth. Selah" (v13).** The purpose of judgment is knowledge of God, spread worldwide. Guzik and JFB both hear an echo of young David before Goliath: "that all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel" (1 Sam 17:46).
- **Commentators:** Poole, cited by Guzik, argues David's motive is God's honor and the good of his people, not personal revenge. Henry says the psalmist's prayer here "amounts to a prophecy," and calls the imprecations "the language of nature… of a prophetical spirit, looking forward to Christ and the enemies of his kingdom, and therefore not to be drawn into a precedent." That last point is a helpful caution: these prayers are not a model for cursing personal enemies.

#### I Will Sing of Your Strength (vv14–17)
- **"Each evening they come back, howling like dogs" (v14).** The refrain returns word for word. Nothing outside has changed. The dogs are still in the street.
- **"They wander about for food and growl if they do not get their fill" (v15).** But now the picture turns against them. They prowl all night and go away hungry. They did. When Saul's men burst in at dawn, they found an idol in the bed (1 Sam 19:15–16). JFB says "growl" can be read "they shall stay all night," that is, they get nothing for their trouble. Kidner, cited by Guzik, connects the verb to Israel's grumbling in the wilderness.
- **"But I will sing of your strength; I will sing aloud of your steadfast love in the morning" (v16).** The contrast is deliberate. The dogs howl at evening. David sings in the morning. They spend the night frustrated. He greets the dawn with praise. "In the morning" was exactly when Saul planned to kill him (1 Sam 19:11). The morning of death became a morning of song. JFB notes the two possible senses: when the enemies slink off hungry, or simply the eagerness of early praise (30:5).
- **"For you have been to me a fortress and a refuge in the day of my distress" (v16).** Past tense, already proven.
- **"O my Strength, I will sing praises to you, for you, O God, are my fortress, the God who shows me steadfast love" (v17).** The second refrain answers the first one in v9 with one change: "I will watch for you" becomes "I will sing praises to you." Waiting has turned into worship. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes three different verbs of praise in these last two verses. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, sums up the contrast: the wicked howl, the servant of God sings.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "Contrast the lot of God's servant, who employs his time in God's praise." Guzik connects the psalm's beginning, at the start of David's fugitive years, with the end of those years, when David lamented Saul and Jonathan (2 Sam 1:17–27) rather than gloating. Henry closes his comments by noting that the God who is our strength is also the God of our mercy, and both are reasons to sing.

#### Pointing to Christ
David, innocent, surrounded at night by armed men sent by a jealous ruler, prays "for no fault of mine." Jesus lived this psalm on the night of his arrest. Armed men came to the garden in the dark "with swords and clubs" (Matt 26:47), sent by rulers who had decided to kill him without cause (John 15:25). His accusers had "swords in their lips" at his trial. Psalm 22 even uses the same picture: "dogs encompass me; a company of evildoers encircles me" (22:16). Henry draws the link: "the persecutors of our Lord Jesus, who are compared to dogs… ran him down with noise." David escaped through a window. Jesus did not escape. He went to the cross. But his "morning" came too. Early on the first day of the week (Mark 16:2), the God of steadfast love met him, and the men who had watched his tomb found it empty, just as Saul's men found David's bed.
