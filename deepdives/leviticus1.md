*Setting:* Leviticus picks up the moment Exodus ends. The tabernacle is standing at Mount Sinai, the cloud has settled on it, and the glory of the LORD fills it so completely that Moses cannot go in (Exod 40:34–35). The question hanging over the camp is obvious: how can sinful people live next door to a holy God? Leviticus is God's answer, given over about a month (compare Exod 40:17 and Num 1:1). Its first seven chapters explain the five main offerings, starting with the oldest and most basic, the burnt offering. The book's name comes from the Greek and Latin "Leviticus," "about the Levites," but its Hebrew title is its first word, *wayyiqra*, "And he called."

#### Laws for Burnt Offerings (vv1–17)
- **"The LORD called Moses and spoke to him from the tent of meeting" (v1).** At Sinai God spoke from the mountain. Now he speaks from the tent in the middle of the camp. Moses could not walk in uninvited (Exod 40:35), so God calls him. The Tyndale notes describe the tent as God's royal palace on earth, with the ark as his throne. Jewish scribal tradition writes the last letter of *wayyiqra* small, and the rabbis read it as a sign of Moses' humility. That is a tradition about the text, not part of its meaning.
- **"When any one of you brings an offering to the LORD" (v2).** "Any one" is *'adam*, "a person," the same word as Adam. These laws are for every Israelite, not just priests. The word for "offering" is *qorban*, from the verb "to bring near." An offering was something brought near to God so that the worshiper could come near. Jesus mentions the word in Mark 7:11.
- **"Of livestock from the herd or from the flock" (v2).** Only domestic animals were allowed, not wild game. A hunter's catch cost him a day. An animal from his own herd was part of his livelihood. As David later said, "I will not offer burnt offerings to the LORD my God that cost me nothing" (2 Sam 24:24).
- **"A burnt offering… a male without blemish" (v3).** "Burnt offering" is *'olah*, "that which goes up," because the whole animal went up in smoke. It is the oldest sacrifice in the Bible: Noah offered it after the flood (Gen 8:20), and God asked Abraham for Isaac as an *'olah* (Gen 22:2). "Without blemish" meant whole and healthy. Malachi later rebukes priests who offered lame and sick animals (Mal 1:8).
- **"That he may be accepted before the LORD" (v3).** The aim is acceptance. "Before the LORD" occurs dozens of times in Leviticus, more than in any other book. Worship happens in God's presence, at the door of his house.
- **"He shall lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering" (v4).** The worshiper pressed his hand on the animal's head. The Tyndale notes say this marked the animal as standing in for him, so its death represented his. On the Day of Atonement the high priest lays both hands on the goat and confesses Israel's sins over it (16:21), which shows what the gesture could carry.
- **"It shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him" (v4).** "Make atonement" is *kipper*, a key word of Leviticus. BDB gives "cover over, pacify, propitiate," and the related noun *kopher* means a ransom price paid in place of a life (Exod 30:12). Many recent scholars stress the idea of wiping clean or purging, as the Tyndale notes do. The ideas fit together: sin is dealt with, God's anger is turned away, and the sinner is restored. Even this offering, which is usually thought of as worship and dedication, begins with atonement.
- **"Then he shall kill the bull before the LORD" (v5).** The worshiper himself killed the animal, not the priest. He could not watch from a distance. The priests then took over with **the blood**, throwing it against the altar's sides. Leviticus will explain why: "the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood that makes atonement by the life" (17:11).
- **Flaying, cutting and washing (vv6–9).** The hide went to the priest (7:8). The rest was cut up and arranged on the fire, with the entrails and legs washed first, since nothing dirty belonged on God's altar. Then **"the priest shall burn all of it."** Nothing was kept back for the worshiper or the priest to eat. That is what set the burnt offering apart.
- **"A food offering with a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (v9).** "Food offering" (*'isheh*) is usually connected with *'esh*, "fire," but the Tyndale notes say recent research favors the sense "gift." "Pleasing aroma" (*reah nihoah*) is literally "a soothing, restful smell." The same phrase is used of Noah's offering, after which God promised never again to curse the ground (Gen 8:21). Pagan neighbors thought their gods needed feeding. Israel's God says plainly, "If I were hungry, I would not tell you" (Ps 50:12–13). The phrase means the offering pleased him and he accepted it.
- **"From the flock, from the sheep or goats" (vv10–13).** A sheep or goat cost less than a bull, and the procedure is the same. It was killed **"on the north side of the altar"** (v11), since the ashes went to the east (v16) and the basin stood to the west between altar and tent.
- **"Turtledoves or pigeons" (vv14–17).** A poor family could bring a bird. Here the priest does the killing, because a bird was too small to handle in the same way. The **crop**, the bird's food pouch with its contents, was thrown on the ash heap. The bird was torn open **"by its wings, but shall not sever it completely"** (v17). This is the offering Mary and Joseph brought at Jesus' birth, the poor family's offering (Luke 2:24; Lev 12:8).
- **One pattern, three price levels.** The chapter runs from bull to sheep to bird, and each one ends with the same words: "a food offering with a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (vv9, 13, 17). The poor man's dove was as pleasing as the rich man's bull. God made a way for everyone to come.
- **Commentators:** All three see the poor man's bird as just as acceptable as the bull. JFB says "the disposition of the offerer was the thing looked to," and Henry adds that someone to whom God has given much should not bring the least. Guzik and Henry both read the hand on the head as identification, and Guzik presses that it is not enough to know Christ died; a person must lean on him personally. Guzik explains *kipper* as "to cover," which BDB supports. He contrasts Old Testament atonement, which covered sin, with Christ's work, which takes it away. JFB reads the washing of entrails and legs as a picture of inner purity and a holy walk. That is a symbolic reading the text does not state. Henry says the gentle animals required, lambs and doves, picture the meekness of Christ.

#### Pointing to Christ
The burnt offering was whole, unblemished, killed in the worshiper's place, its blood given for atonement, and all of it given to God. Christ "gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2), the same "pleasing aroma" language in Greek. He was the lamb God provided when Isaac was spared (Gen 22:8, 13; John 1:29). And because nothing of the burnt offering was held back, Paul uses it as the pattern for the believer's response: "present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God" (Rom 12:1).
