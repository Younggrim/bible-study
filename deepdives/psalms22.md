*Setting:* "A Psalm of David," set "according to The Doe of the Dawn," probably the name of a familiar tune. JFB suggests the title may also hint at the subject: a gentle deer hunted in the half-light before morning, with dawn and relief still to come. No event in David's life is named, and nothing we know of David matches all of it. He was hunted, mocked and surrounded many times, but never pierced, stripped and gambled over while dying of thirst. Psalm 22 is the darkest lament in the Psalter, and it turns into one of its widest songs of praise. It falls in a deliberate sequence: Psalms 20 and 21 prayed for and celebrated God's king, Psalm 22 shows the king forsaken and suffering, and Psalm 23 follows with the shepherd's quiet care. The New Testament quotes or alludes to this psalm more than a dozen times in the story of Jesus' death. Jesus spoke its first line from the cross, and the Gospel writers saw its details unfold around him. Henry: "of him, no doubt, David here speaks." Most Christian readers see a real experience of David's, poetically heightened, which the Spirit shaped into a picture fulfilled only in Christ (1 Pet 1:10–11).

#### Why Have You Forsaken Me? (vv1–5)
- **"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (v1).** Jesus cried these words from the cross in his own Aramaic, "Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" (Matt 27:46; Mark 15:34). "Forsaken" (*azav*) means left behind, abandoned. Notice the sufferer still says "*my* God," twice. The cry of abandonment is still a cry of faith, spoken to the God he will not let go of. A psalm could be called to mind by its opening words, so Jesus may also have been pointing his hearers to the whole psalm, including how it ends. But that does not soften the cry itself. He was truly bearing sin, and the one who "knew no sin" was made "to be sin" for us (2 Cor 5:21).
- **"Why are you so far from saving me, from the words of my groaning?" (v1).** "Groaning" is literally "roaring," the word used for a lion's roar (v13). This is no polite prayer. It is the cry of a wounded animal.
- **"I cry by day, but you do not answer, and by night" (v2).** Day and night without an answer. Jesus was crucified in the morning, and from noon until three in the afternoon darkness covered the land (Mark 15:25, 33).
- **"Yet you are holy, enthroned on the praises of Israel" (v3).** The first "yet." Even in the dark, he will not accuse God of wrong. God is holy, and his throne is the praise of his people. In the tabernacle and temple he was "enthroned on the cherubim" over the ark (80:1). Here the throne is Israel's worship itself.
- **"In you our fathers trusted… and you delivered them" (vv4–5).** "Trusted" three times. Israel's history was a record of cries answered: in Egypt, at the Red Sea, under the judges. "Not put to shame" means their trust was not left embarrassed. That makes the sufferer's present silence even harder. Why is he the exception?
- **Commentators:** JFB calls desertion by God "the climax of the sufferer's misery," and notes that the sufferer "not only refrains from charging God foolishly" but appeals to him. Guzik holds that Jesus bore both the loss of the Father's felt presence and the outpouring of his wrath as our substitute, while the union of Father and Son was never broken (2 Cor 5:19). He passes on Spurgeon's comment that we may not question God's holiness, but we may argue from it.

#### A Worm and Not a Man (vv6–11)
- **"But I am a worm and not a man" (v6).** The second contrast: the fathers trusted and were saved, "but I." A worm is the lowest, most helpless, most trampled thing. The word (*tolaat*) is also the name of the scarlet worm from which red dye was made, the "scarlet" of the tabernacle curtains (Exod 25:4). Some, Henry among them, see an allusion to blood. That may be reading too much into it, but the main point is plain. Isaiah said of the Servant that "his appearance was so marred, beyond human semblance" (Isa 52:14; 53:3).
- **"All who see me mock me… they wag their heads" (v7).** "Make mouths" is literally "open wide the lip," a sneer. The Gospels use these words for the crowd at the cross: "those who passed by derided him, wagging their heads" (Matt 27:39; Mark 15:29).
- **"He trusts in the LORD; let him deliver him… for he delights in him!" (v8).** The mockers use the sufferer's own faith against him. "Trusts" is literally "rolls," as in "roll your way onto the LORD" (37:5). The chief priests, scribes and elders said almost exactly this at the cross: "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now, if he desires him" (Matt 27:43). Without realizing it, they were quoting this psalm, and they cast themselves as its villains.
- **"Yet you are he who took me from the womb" (v9).** The second "yet." He answers the mockers' sneer about his trust by tracing that trust back to his birth. God was his midwife. Even as a nursing baby he was safe in God's care.
- **"On you was I cast from my birth" (v10).** "Cast" means thrown on, entrusted, like a newborn placed in someone's arms. For Jesus this is literally true. Born into poverty, hunted as an infant by Herod, he was in his Father's keeping from the first day (Matt 2:13–15).
- **"Be not far from me, for trouble is near, and there is none to help" (v11).** God feels far, and trouble feels near. The disciples had fled (Mark 14:50). The plea "be not far" is repeated in v19 and frames the next section.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that Christ, having already stooped to become man, went lower still, "he becomes a worm, and no man," and adds that "if he had not made himself a worm, he could not have been trampled upon as he was." He mentions the scarlet-dye worm too. JFB on v8: "trusted on the Lord—literally, 'rolled,'" and calls this "the language of enemies sporting with his faith in the hour of his desertion." JFB notes the worm language expresses "not His real, but esteemed, value," what men thought he was worth. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's comment that "faith finds weapons everywhere," even in memories of infancy.

#### Many Bulls Encompass Me (vv12–18)
- **"Strong bulls of Bashan surround me" (v12).** Bashan, east of the Sea of Galilee (today's Golan Heights), had rich pasture and famously large, well-fed cattle (Deut 32:14; Amos 4:1). The enemies are pictured as huge, snorting bulls crowding in. Then as lions, mouths wide open (v13). Then as dogs (v16). The sufferer is ringed by beasts.
- **"I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint" (v14).** The sufferer's own body is failing. Water poured on the ground cannot be gathered up again (2 Sam 14:14). The weight of a body hanging from outstretched arms would pull the joints apart.
- **"My heart is like wax; it is melted within my breast" (v14).** Courage and strength drain away (Josh 7:5).
- **"My strength is dried up like a potsherd, and my tongue sticks to my jaws" (v15).** A potsherd is a broken piece of baked clay, the driest thing imaginable. Crucifixion brought terrible thirst. John tells us that "knowing that all was now finished, said (to fulfill the Scripture), 'I thirst'" (John 19:28).
- **"You lay me in the dust of death" (v15).** Now the sufferer says that God himself is the one laying him in the dust. Behind all the bulls, lions and dogs is the hand of God. Isaiah said the same of the Servant: "it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (Isa 53:10; Acts 2:23).
- **"For dogs encompass me; a company of evildoers encircles me" (v16).** In the ancient Near East dogs were not pets but half-wild scavengers that roamed in packs. Jews sometimes used "dogs" of Gentiles. At the cross it was Gentile soldiers who drove in the nails.
- **"They have pierced my hands and feet" (v16).** This line has a real textual question, stated calmly here. The standard medieval Hebrew text (the Masoretic Text) reads *ka'ari*, "like a lion," which leaves the line without a verb: "like a lion my hands and feet." The ancient Greek translation, made by Jewish scholars two centuries before Christ, reads "they dug" or "pierced," and so do the Syriac and other early versions. A Psalms scroll from the time of Jesus, found in the Judean desert at Nahal Hever, appears to read the Hebrew word as a verb rather than "like a lion," though scholars still debate exactly what that verb means. The ESV, KJV, NIV and most English translations follow "pierced." The New Testament never quotes this line, but Jesus showed the disciples "his hands and his feet" after the resurrection (Luke 24:39–40; John 20:25–27).
- **"I can count all my bones—they stare and gloat over me" (v17).** The body is stretched and exposed, every rib visible. Crucified men were hung naked, on display. "Stare" (*ra'ah*) here means to look with satisfaction, to gloat. Luke says, "the people stood by, watching" (Luke 23:35).
- **"They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots" (v18).** Executioners took a condemned man's clothes as their perk. John is the Gospel writer who notices that both lines came true: the soldiers divided Jesus' outer garments into four shares, and then cast lots for the seamless tunic rather than tear it (John 19:23–24). "This was to fulfill the Scripture," he says, quoting this verse. The details fit a Roman crucifixion, a method of execution invented centuries after David.
- **Commentators:** JFB says of v16 that the meaning of the Hebrew word "has been a subject of much discussion," but that "the remarkable aptness of the description to the facts of the Saviour's history, together with difficulties attending any other mode" of explaining it, "justify an adherence to the terms of our version." He also cautions, "We need not try to find the exact counterpart of each item of the description in the particulars of our Saviour's sufferings," because poetic pictures give "substantial truth, under illustrations." He calls v18 a "literally fulfilled prediction." Guzik matches the details to crucifixion one by one: the joints out of place, the thirst, the unbroken bones (John 19:36), the divided clothing. He passes on Boice's suggestion that the Hebrew vowel marks in v16 may have been added deliberately to avoid the Christian reading. That is a disputed claim, and many scholars think an honest copying confusion between two very similar Hebrew letters is the more likely explanation.

#### You Have Answered Me (vv19–21)
- **"But you, O LORD, do not be far off!" (v19).** The third "but you." He prays again the prayer of v11. "My help" (*eyaluti*) is a word found only here in the Bible, meaning "my strength."
- **"Deliver my soul from the sword, my precious life from the power of the dog!" (v20).** "Precious life" is literally "my only one" (*yechidah*), meaning his one and only life. The same root names Isaac as Abraham's "only son" (Gen 22:2), a scene Christians have long linked with the cross.
- **"Save me from the mouth of the lion!" (v21).** The beasts return in reverse order: sword, dog, lion, wild oxen. The great horned wild ox (the KJV's "unicorn") was the most powerful animal known in Israel (Num 23:22; Job 39:9–10).
- **"You have rescued me from the horns of the wild oxen!" (v21).** The Hebrew is just one word here, *aniytani*, "you have answered me." It is the turning point of the whole psalm, the answer to v2, "you do not answer." Mid-sentence the prayer stops being a plea and becomes a report. Many translations, like the ESV, smooth it into "rescued," but the abrupt shift is the point. God has answered.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "my darling—literally, 'my only one,' or, 'solitary one,' as desolate and afflicted" (25:16). Guzik passes on Maclaren's picture of this sudden turn as a burst of sunshine at the end of a day of storms.

#### I Will Tell of Your Name to My Brothers (vv22–26)
- **"I will tell of your name to my brothers" (v22).** The sufferer, now rescued, stands up in the congregation to tell what God has done. Hebrews 2:11–12 quotes this verse as Jesus' own words: "he is not ashamed to call them brothers, saying, 'I will tell of your name to my brothers.'" On the morning of the resurrection Jesus said to Mary, "go to my brothers" (John 20:17). The one who died alone rises to lead the family in worship.
- **"You who fear the LORD, praise him!… stand in awe of him" (v23).** The circle of praise widens: from "my brothers" to all Israel. The "offspring of Jacob" are invited to fear God, not in terror, but with awe at what he has done.
- **"He has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted" (v24).** This answers the mockery of vv6–8. People despised him, but God did not despise his suffering. "He has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him." Verse 1 asked why God was far. The answer: he was never as far as he seemed.
- **"From you comes my praise in the great congregation" (v25).** Even the praise is God's gift. "My vows I will perform" refers to the promises made in trouble, usually paid by offering a thank offering in the temple.
- **"The afflicted shall eat and be satisfied" (v26).** A vow offering ended in a feast. The worshipper shared the meat with family, servants and the poor (Lev 7:16; Deut 12:18; 16:11). The rescued sufferer throws a feast, and the poor are the first guests. "May your hearts live forever!" is his toast to them. Christians naturally think of the Lord's Supper, where Jesus feeds his people from his own sacrifice.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains v26 as the payment of vows "in celebrating the usual festival, as provided in the law (De 12:18; 16:11)," and suggests "my praise shall be of thee" may be better "from thee, that is, God gives grace to praise Him." Guzik links the joy here to "the joy that was set before him" (Heb 12:2).

#### All the Ends of the Earth Shall Turn (vv27–31)
- **"All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the LORD" (v27).** The circle widens again, from Israel to every nation. "All the families of the nations shall worship" echoes God's promise to Abraham that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). How can one man's rescue bring the whole world to worship? No rescue of David ever did. The psalm only makes sense if this sufferer is more than an ordinary man.
- **"For kingship belongs to the LORD, and he rules over the nations" (v28).** The forsaken one's rescue shows who really reigns. Psalm 2 said the nations would be the inheritance of God's Son.
- **"All the prosperous of the earth eat and worship" (v29).** The rich and powerful, literally "the fat of the earth," come to the same table as "the afflicted" of v26. Then the dying come too: "all who go down to the dust… even the one who could not keep himself alive." The Hebrew of this verse is hard, but the sense is that no one is left out, rich or poor, living or dying. Even death does not shut anyone out of this worship.
- **"Posterity shall serve him; it shall be told of the Lord to the coming generation" (v30).** The circle reaches forward in time. The story will be handed down.
- **"They shall come and proclaim his righteousness to a people yet unborn, that he has done it" (v31).** The psalm ends with a single Hebrew verb, *asah*, "he has done," with no object stated. God has done it. Many readers hear in this the cry of Jesus from the cross, "It is finished" (John 19:30). The words are different (John's Greek is *tetelestai*, "it is completed"), so this is an echo of meaning, not a quotation. But the psalm that opened with Jesus' cry of abandonment closes with a sentence very close to his last word: the work is done.
- **Commentators:** JFB sees "the fat ones, or the rich as well as the poor, the helpless who cannot keep themselves alive" uniting in praise, and that they will "transmit to unborn people the records of His grace." Henry summarizes the psalm as testifying "the sufferings of Christ and the glory that should follow" (1 Pet 1:11), and says "much of it is expressly applied to Christ in the New Testament, all of it may be applied to him, and some of it must be understood of him only." Guzik passes on Kidner's comment that the psalm's last words are close to "It is finished," and draws the lesson that none of the sufferer's pain was wasted.

#### Pointing to Christ
No psalm is more directly about Christ's cross than this one. A thousand years before Jesus, it described a righteous sufferer forsaken by God, mocked for his trust, surrounded, stripped, pierced, gambled over and laid in the dust of death. Then, with one word, "you have answered me," it turns into a feast for the poor and a song that spreads to every nation and every generation. That is exactly the shape of the gospel. Jesus took the first line on his lips at his darkest hour. He was forsaken so that those who trust him never will be (Heb 13:5). Now the risen Lord calls them his brothers, sets a table for the afflicted, and sends his people to tell the ends of the earth and a people yet unborn: he has done it.
