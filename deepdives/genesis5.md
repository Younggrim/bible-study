*Setting:* Chapter 4 ended with two families heading in opposite directions: Cain's line building cities and boasting of revenge, and Seth's line, where "people began to call upon the name of the LORD" (4:26). Chapter 5 now follows Seth's line from Adam to Noah in ten generations. It opens a new section of Genesis, "the book of the generations of Adam," and it carries the story across the long years between the garden and the flood. On the surface it is a list of names and numbers. Read slowly, it says three things: death now reigns, as God warned it would; God's image and blessing still pass from parent to child; and the promise of 3:15 is being carried forward, one birth at a time, toward a son whose name means "rest."

#### Adam's Descendants to Noah (vv1–32)
- **"This is the book of the generations of Adam" (v1).** "Generations" is *toledot*, the word that marks the main sections of Genesis ("these are the generations of…," 2:4; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10, 27 and on). It means roughly "what came of" someone: the family and story that followed. "Book" (*sepher*) is simply a written record. Some, including Guzik, think Adam's own family record lies behind this chapter, handed down and later edited by Moses. The text doesn't say where its sources came from.
- **"He made him in the likeness of God… he blessed them and named them Man" (vv1–2).** The chapter starts by repeating 1:26–28 on purpose. After the fall, after Cain's murder, the first thing said about humanity is still that God made it in his likeness and blessed it. The name "Man" is *adam*, given by God himself to the whole race, male and female together. In the ancient world only kings were called the "image" of a god. Genesis gives that dignity to every human being.
- **"He fathered a son in his own likeness, after his image" (v3).** The same two words, "likeness" and "image," now pass from Adam to Seth. Seth bears God's image, because Adam does (9:6 and James 3:9 assume all people still do). But he also bears the likeness of his father as he now is: mortal and fallen. Paul draws the line from here: "in Adam all die" (1 Cor 15:22; Rom 5:12). Cain and Abel are not mentioned. The line of promise is counted through Seth, the "appointed" son (4:25).
- **"And he died" (v5).** This is the refrain of the chapter, eight times: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Methuselah, Lamech. God said "in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (2:17), and the serpent said "you will not surely die" (3:4). Chapter 5 settles who was right. Each life is long and full ("other sons and daughters"), and each paragraph ends at a grave.
- **The pattern (vv6–20).** Every entry runs the same way: age at the son's birth, years lived after, other children, total years, death. The sameness is the point. Generation after generation, the blessing of 1:28 ("be fruitful and multiply") goes on, and so does the curse. The names in this stretch, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel and Jared, are otherwise almost unknown. Some resemble names in Cain's line (Enoch, Irad/Jared, Methushael/Methuselah, Lamech in 4:17–18). Scholars debate whether the two lists come from a shared tradition. Either way, Genesis sets them side by side so the reader compares the two Enochs and the two Lamechs.
- **The long lifespans.** These ages, 900 years and more, are the most striking thing in the chapter. Faithful Christians read them in different ways:
  - **Literal years.** Most traditional readers, including Henry, JFB and Guzik, take them at face value. JFB says the cause can't be known and is best left to "the sovereign will of God." Guzik suggests the effects of the fall had not yet built up in the human body and that the world before the flood was different. Lifespans fall steadily after the flood (11:10–32) to the "seventy years" of Psalm 90:10. Guzik rejects the idea that the numbers count months, since Enoch would then have fathered a child at about five.
  - **Numbers with a literary purpose.** Many scholars note that the Sumerian King List gives pre-flood kings reigns of tens of thousands of years. Next to those, Genesis's numbers look restrained. On this view the ages follow ancient conventions and mark these men as belonging to a distant, different age, and their exact sizes may carry meaning that is now hard to recover.
  - Both views agree on what the chapter teaches: these were real ancestors, life was long, and death came anyway.
- **"Fathered" and gaps.** The Hebrew verb can mean "became the ancestor of," and the Tyndale notes point out that Hebrew genealogies don't always list every generation. Matthew 1:8, for example, skips three kings. If there are no gaps here, the years add up to 1,656 from Adam's creation to the flood. If there are, the total is longer. Guzik holds that the list can't yield an exact date, though he doubts the gaps are large.
- **Different numbers in other ancient copies.** The ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) and the Samaritan Pentateuch give several different ages. The Greek mostly adds 100 years to the age at which each man fathers his son, so its total to the flood is over 2,200 years. The ESV follows the Hebrew text, and most scholars think the Greek and Samaritan figures are later adjustments, but the differences are real and worth knowing about before building a precise timeline.
- **"Enoch walked with God" (vv22, 24).** The formula breaks for Enoch, seventh from Adam (Jude 14), in deliberate contrast to Lamech, seventh from Adam in Cain's line, who boasted of killing (4:23–24). "Walked" is *halak* in a reflexive form meaning to walk about, to live one's life. The same form is used of God "walking in the garden" (3:8) and of Noah, who also "walked with God" (6:9). It pictures daily companionship, not a single moment. Amos 3:3 asks, "Do two walk together, unless they have agreed to meet?" Micah 6:8 calls God's people "to walk humbly with your God."
- **"After he fathered Methuselah 300 years" (v22).** The text ties Enoch's walk to the years after his son's birth. Guzik suggests the birth was a turning point. The text doesn't say why, only that he walked with God while raising a family. Holiness here is not a monk's life; it is an ordinary household life lived with God.
- **"He was not, for God took him" (v24).** Every other entry ends "and he died." Enoch's ends with a gap. "Took" is *laqach*, the ordinary word for taking, used again when Elijah is "taken" up (2 Kings 2:10). Hebrews 11:5 explains: "By faith Enoch was taken up so that he should not see death… he was commended as having pleased God." Living 365 years, a number that matches the days of a year, he had the shortest life in the list, yet he alone did not die. In a chapter of graves, Enoch is a sign that death will not have the last word.
- **Enoch the prophet.** Jude 14–15 quotes a prophecy of Enoch about the Lord coming with his holy ones to judge the ungodly. The words come from *1 Enoch*, a Jewish book written centuries before Christ and not part of the Bible. Jude quotes a true saying from it, as Paul quotes Greek poets (Acts 17:28), without endorsing the whole book.
- **Methuselah, 969 years (vv25–27).** He is the oldest man in the Bible. If the numbers are added (187 + 182 + 600, see 7:6), he died in the very year of the flood, as the Tyndale notes observe. A popular explanation says his name means "when he dies, it shall come," a prophecy of the flood. Guzik and JFB both take it that way. The lexicon doesn't support it: BDB gives the meaning as uncertain, perhaps "man of the dart" or "man of the spear," and notes other guesses. The timing is striking, and many see God's patience in the long delay before judgment (1 Pet 3:20), but the name itself shouldn't be pressed.
- **"Out of the ground that the LORD has cursed, this one shall bring us relief" (vv28–29).** The formula breaks again, for Lamech's words at Noah's birth. "Noah" (*Noach*) sounds like *nuach*, "rest," and the verb Lamech uses, *nacham*, "comfort" or "relief." BDB calls this the traditional explanation of the name. Lamech remembers the curse on the ground (3:17) and hopes this son will lift it. He speaks of "the LORD," God's covenant name, which shows that Seth's line still called on him (4:26). His hope is partly fulfilled. After the flood God promises never again to curse the ground (8:21), and Noah plants a vineyard (9:20). The full rest Lamech longed for waits for someone greater.
- **Lamech, 777 years (v31).** The other Lamech boasted of "seventy-sevenfold" revenge (4:24). This Lamech lives 777 years and speaks only of hope. Whether the number is meant to echo the other is debated, but the contrast between the two men is clear.
- **"Noah fathered Shem, Ham, and Japheth" (v32).** The list ends with a man and three sons, the family through whom the whole human race will start again (9:19). The chapter's ten generations close an era, and the next list in 11:10–26 runs another ten from Shem to Abram.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the chapter "the only authentic history extant of the first age of the world." On v3 he writes, "A sinner begets a sinner, but a saint does not beget a saint," and "Grace does not run in the blood, but corruption does." Of Adam's death he says his whole life after the fall "was but a reprieve." On Enoch: "To walk with God is to set God always before us, and to act as those that are always under his eye," and "God often takes those soonest whom he loves best." JFB calls walking with God "a common phrase in Eastern countries denoting constant and familiar intercourse," sees Enoch's translation as God's confirmation of his preaching in an unbelieving age, and suggests the list follows the line of promise rather than each man's firstborn. JFB comments only on vv1, 5, 21, 24, 26 and 32 and passes over the rest. Guzik stresses that each of these men died and none of them was the promised deliverer. He reads Methuselah's long life as God's mercy, giving the world more time to repent. All three accept the Methuselah name meaning that the lexicon treats as uncertain.

#### Pointing to Christ
Luke traces Jesus' family back through this very list, "the son of Methuselah, the son of Enoch… the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:37–38). Every name in it ends "and he died," and the curse on the ground Lamech hoped to see lifted was still in place when Jesus was born. Jesus entered that line to break its refrain. He is the true image of God (Col 1:15), the last Adam who gives life (1 Cor 15:45), and the one who says, "Come to me… and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28). Enoch was taken without dying. Christ went through death and came out the other side, so that his people, whether they die or are caught up at his coming (1 Thess 4:17), will walk with God forever.
