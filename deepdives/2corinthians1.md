*Setting:* Paul writes from Macedonia (northern Greece) in about AD 55–56, a year or so after 1 Corinthians. A lot has happened in between, and most of it has to be pieced together from hints in this letter. In 1 Corinthians 16:5 Paul planned to come through Macedonia and then on to Corinth. Instead he made a sudden "painful visit" (2:1) that went badly, and someone in the church publicly wronged him. He went back to Ephesus and wrote a "tearful" letter of rebuke, now lost (2:4; 7:8), and sent it with Titus. Then he faced a crisis in Asia so severe he expected to die (vv8–9). Finally he met Titus in Macedonia and heard that most of the church had repented (7:5–7). This is the most personal of Paul's letters. His critics in Corinth said he was fickle, weak and unimpressive. Paul answers by laying his heart open, and he starts not with a defense but with praise.

#### Greeting (vv1–2)
- **"Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God" (v1).** In this letter Paul's apostleship is under attack (chs 10–13). He grounds it at the start where it belongs: God called him, not human approval.
- **"Timothy our brother" (v1).** Timothy had been to Corinth (1 Cor 16:10) and is now back with Paul. To Timothy himself Paul writes "my son" (1 Tim 1:2). To the church he presents him as a brother and equal.
- **"With all the saints who are in the whole of Achaia" (v1).** Achaia was the Roman province of southern Greece, with Corinth as its capital. The letter was meant to be passed around to believers in nearby towns such as Cenchreae (Rom 16:1).
- **"Grace to you and peace" (v2).** The ordinary Greek letter opened with *chairein*, "greetings." Paul turns it into *charis*, "grace," and adds the Hebrew blessing of peace (*shalom*). Every one of his letters begins this way.

#### God of All Comfort (vv3–11)
- **"Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v3).** Paul's other letters usually open with thanks for the readers. Here he praises God for what God has done for Paul himself. This was a Jewish form of prayer, "Blessed are you, O Lord," now prayed to the Father of Jesus.
- **"The Father of mercies and God of all comfort" (v3).** "Father of mercies" is a Hebrew way of saying the source of all mercies, the one mercy comes from. **Comfort** (*paraklēsis*) and the verb "to comfort" (*parakaleō*) appear about ten times in five verses. The word means to call someone to one's side, and so to encourage, urge or console. It is related to *Paraklētos*, the name Jesus gave the Holy Spirit, "the Helper" (John 14:16). God's comfort is not only soothing but strengthening, and it comes alongside.
- **"Who comforts us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction" (v4).** Comfort is not meant to stop with the one who receives it. It flows on. What God gives in one person's trouble becomes something to give in another's. Isaiah's "Comfort, comfort my people" (Isa 40:1) is the background.
- **"Affliction" (v4).** *Thlipsis* means pressure, being squeezed or crushed. It is the main word for trouble in this letter (vv4, 8; 4:17; 7:4).
- **"As we share abundantly in Christ's sufferings" (v5).** Paul does not mean he adds anything to Christ's atoning death. He means that those who belong to Christ share in the opposition and hardship he met in the world (Phil 3:10; Col 1:24). Christ counts his people's sufferings as his own (Acts 9:4: "why are you persecuting me?"). And as the suffering overflows, so does the comfort.
- **"It is for your comfort and salvation" (v6).** Even Paul's suffering is for the church. When he suffers, they gain. When he is comforted, they gain. **"Which you experience when you patiently endure"**: "endure" (*hypomonē*) is the strength to remain under a load without giving way.
- **"Our hope for you is unshaken" (v7).** After a painful year, Paul says he is sure of them. Those who share suffering will share comfort too.
- **"The affliction we experienced in Asia" (v8).** Asia was the Roman province around Ephesus. We don't know what happened. Suggestions include the riot in Ephesus (Acts 19:23–41), though Paul seems to have escaped that easily; an arrest and trial that could have ended in execution; the "beasts at Ephesus" of 1 Corinthians 15:32; or a severe illness. The Tyndale notes think illness less likely. Paul doesn't say, because the point is not the details but what God did.
- **"So utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself" (v8).** A great apostle admits he hit the end of himself. "Despaired" means he saw no way out.
- **"We felt that we had received the sentence of death" (v9).** The word is *apokrima*, an official verdict. In his own mind the verdict was in, and it was death. **"But that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead."** Here is the reason. God let Paul come to the end of his resources so that he would lean his whole weight on God. The "God who raises the dead" is the resurrection God of 1 Corinthians 15, and Abraham's God too (Rom 4:17; Heb 11:19).
- **"He delivered us… and he will deliver us… he will deliver us again" (v10).** Past, future, and further future. What God has done becomes the ground for trusting him with what is coming. Some later manuscripts read "and does deliver," as the King James has it. The oldest read "will deliver."
- **"You also must help us by prayer" (v11).** Paul believed the Corinthians' prayers had a real part in his rescue. **"So that many will give thanks"**: the Greek says "from many faces," perhaps picturing the upturned faces of a praying congregation. Prayer from many leads to thanks from many.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "All comforts come from God, and our sweetest comforts are in him." On v4: "The favours God bestows on us, are not only to make us cheerful, but also that we may be useful to others." On v10: "Past experiences encourage faith and hope." JFB says "Father of mercies" means "the SOURCE of all mercies," and that Christ "calls His people's sufferings His own." It pictures Paul's and the Corinthians' hearts as "mirrors reflecting the likenesses of each other." Guzik takes comfort as strengthening, not just sympathy. Part of his support is the Latin root of the English word "comfort," "with strength," which tells us about English rather than Greek, though the Greek word does include encouragement. On the trouble in Asia, Guzik leans toward a lasting illness, while JFB points to danger in Ephesus. Guzik adds that comfort for suffering "for Christ" is not a promise of comfort in trouble we bring on ourselves by sin (1 Pet 4:15–16).

#### Paul's Change of Plans (vv12–24)
- **"For our boast is this, the testimony of our conscience" (v12).** Paul's critics said he was sly and self-serving. His answer is a clear conscience: he has dealt with them "**with simplicity and godly sincerity**." *Haplotēs* means openness, singleness of heart, nothing hidden. *Eilikrineia* means unmixed, pure, like metal with no alloy. Some manuscripts read "holiness" instead of "simplicity." In Greek the two words differ by only a few letters, and the ESV notes the other reading.
- **"Not by earthly wisdom but by the grace of God" (v12).** "Earthly wisdom" is the clever, calculating worldly wisdom of 1 Corinthians 1–2, the very thing Corinth admired.
- **"We are not writing to you anything other than what you read and understand" (v13).** No hidden meanings, no reading between the lines. **"On the day of our Lord Jesus you will boast of us as we will boast of you" (v14).** On the day of Christ's return, Paul and the Corinthians will be each other's joy (1 Thess 2:19–20). That future day puts today's quarrel in perspective.
- **"I wanted to come to you first, so that you might have a second experience of grace" (v15).** Paul had changed from the plan in 1 Corinthians 16:5–7 to a better one, visiting them twice: on the way north to Macedonia and again on the way back (v16). It would have been a double blessing. Then the painful visit happened, and he dropped the second visit.
- **"Was I vacillating?" (v17).** *Elaphria* means lightness, fickleness, treating promises as if they don't weigh anything. **"Do I make my plans according to the flesh, ready to say 'Yes, yes' and 'No, no' at the same time?"** That is, saying whatever suits me at the moment. The charge hurt, because if Paul's word could not be trusted, why trust his gospel?
- **"As surely as God is faithful, our word to you has not been Yes and No" (v18).** Paul moves straight from his travel plans to God's faithfulness. His message about Jesus was not unreliable, and neither is he.
- **"Silvanus and Timothy and I" (v19).** Silvanus is the Silas of Acts (15:40; 18:5). These three founded the church in Corinth (Acts 18). **"In him it is always Yes."** Jesus is not a maybe.
- **"All the promises of God find their Yes in him" (v20).** One of the great verses on how the Old Testament fits together. Every promise, to Abraham, to David, through the prophets, is kept in Christ. **"Through him that we utter our Amen."** God says Yes in Christ, and the church answers Amen, "so be it, it is true," in its worship. *Amen* is Hebrew for something firm and reliable. Revelation 3:14 even calls Jesus "the Amen."
- **"It is God who establishes us with you in Christ" (v21).** Four business and legal words follow. **Establishes** (*bebaioō*) was a legal term for guaranteeing a sale. **Anointed** (*chriō*) is the root of *Christos*, "Christ," the Anointed One. Paul makes a wordplay: God establishes us "in Christ" and has "christed" us, set us apart and equipped us with the Spirit as kings, priests and prophets once were. This is the only place the New Testament uses this verb for believers.
- **"Put his seal on us" (v22).** A seal marked ownership and protected the contents (Eph 1:13; 4:30). **"Given us his Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee."** *Arrabōn* was a merchant's word for a down payment, the first installment that commits the buyer to pay the rest (5:5; Eph 1:14). In modern Greek the same word means an engagement ring. The Spirit now is the first part of the life to come, and God's pledge that the rest is coming.
- **Father, Son and Spirit (vv21–22).** Notice the pattern: God establishes us in Christ and gives us his Spirit. Paul is not writing a doctrine of the Trinity here, but it is woven into how he speaks.
- **"I call God to witness against me" (v23).** A solemn oath, the kind found often in the Old Testament (1 Sam 12:5). Jesus' teaching against oaths (Matt 5:33–37) targets oaths used to dodge the truth, and Paul uses one here to tell it. **"It was to spare you that I refrained from coming again."** The real reason for the change: another visit would have meant another painful confrontation (2:1). He stayed away out of love, not fickleness.
- **"Not that we lord it over your faith, but we work with you for your joy" (v24).** Even as an apostle, Paul refuses to act like a master over their consciences. He is a fellow worker (*synergos*) for their joy. **"For you stand firm in your faith."** Their faith rests on Christ, not on Paul.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Conscience witnesses concerning the steady course and tenor of the life," meaning a whole pattern, not one act. On v20: "Nothing can render God's promises more certain." On v24: "our comfort and joy must flow from faith." JFB glosses v17 as "Was I guilty of levity?" and v19 as "The Son of God, though preached by different preachers, was one and the same, unchangeable." It connects "anointed" to Christ's name, and calls the Spirit "a first instalment." Guzik says the Corinthians, used to slick and self-serving teachers, had grown cynical and assumed Paul was the same. He likens the down payment to a store's layaway plan: God has paid too much to walk away from the purchase. He also stresses that leaders are helpers of a church's joy, not lords over its faith.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter's center is v20: "All the promises of God find their Yes in him." Paul's plans changed, and people doubted him. But God's word in Christ has never been Yes and No. The God who raised Jesus from the dead is the same God who raises people out of despair, keeps every promise in his Son, and gives his Spirit now as the down payment on everything still to come.
