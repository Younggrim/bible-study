*Setting:* Directly after the near-capture in the wilderness of Maon, where a Philistine raid called Saul away just as his men were closing in (23:26–28). David has moved east to the strongholds of En-gedi, an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea, with fresh springs below and limestone cliffs full of caves above (23:29). He has about six hundred men. Saul has his army and an obsession. This chapter is the first of two scenes in which God puts Saul helplessly into David's hands and David refuses to kill him. The second comes in chapter 26. Together they show what kind of king David will be: one who waits for God to give him the throne rather than grabbing it.

#### David Spares Saul's Life (vv1–22)
- **"When Saul returned from following the Philistines" (v1).** The Philistine raid bought David time, nothing more. As soon as it is over, Saul is back on the hunt.
- **"Three thousand chosen men out of all Israel" (v2).** This is the size of Saul's standing army from the start of his reign (13:2). David's men are outnumbered five to one. Saul is using the nation's best soldiers against its most loyal servant.
- **"In front of the Wildgoats' Rocks" (v2).** The cliffs near En-gedi are home to Nubian ibex, wild mountain goats that still climb there today. The name "En-gedi" itself means "spring of the young goat."
- **"The sheepfolds by the way, where there was a cave" (v3).** Shepherds often built a low stone wall across the mouth of a cave to make a fold for their flocks. Some of the caves near En-gedi are deep and branching, with room for many men in the dark recesses.
- **"Saul went in to relieve himself" (v3).** The Hebrew is literally "to cover his feet," a polite expression, used also of King Eglon in Judges 3:24. The king of Israel walks alone, unarmed and unguarded, into the very cave where his enemy is hiding. The narrator lets the reader feel the irony.
- **"Here is the day of which the LORD said to you" (v4).** David's men read the moment as God's answer. They may be quoting a promise David had received, or loosely applying God's word that David would be king (16:1–13; 23:17). The Bible records no such promise to give Saul into his hand. Their logic is the same as Saul's at Keilah: the circumstances are right, so God must be in it (23:7). David has to decide whether an open door is the same thing as God's will.
- **"David arose and stealthily cut off a corner of Saul's robe" (v4).** The word for "corner" is *kanaph*, the hem or "wing" of the garment. The Law told Israelites to wear tassels on the corners of their garments to remember God's commandments (Num 15:38–39). Ancient Near Eastern texts show that a person's hem could stand for his identity and authority. Years earlier, when Samuel turned to leave, Saul grabbed his robe and tore it, and Samuel said, "The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you this day" (15:27–28). Now David holds a piece of Saul's royal robe. The message is unmistakable.
- **"Afterward David's heart struck him" (v5).** This is the language of conscience. The same phrase appears when David's heart strikes him after he counts the fighting men (2 Sam 24:10). He did not harm Saul's body, but he had dishonored the office God gave him. David is tender enough to feel that.
- **"The LORD forbid that I should do this thing to my lord, the LORD's anointed" (v6).** "Anointed" is *mashiach*, the word behind "Messiah." Saul was anointed by Samuel at God's command (10:1). David was anointed too (16:13), but he will not take what God has not yet given. Saul's removal is God's business. David says "the LORD's anointed" twice in one sentence, so there is no mistaking his reason.
- **"So David persuaded his men… and did not permit them to attack Saul" (v7).** The verb for "persuaded" is strong. It can mean "tore apart" or "rebuked," so David had to restrain his men firmly. It is one thing to refuse revenge yourself. It is harder to stop loyal men who want it for you.

- **"My lord the king!" (v8).** David steps out of the cave and calls after Saul. This is a huge risk. Saul has three thousand soldiers nearby. David bows "with his face to the earth," the full gesture of honor to a king. He gives up the advantage of hiding to make his loyalty plain.
- **"Why do you listen to the words of men…?" (v9).** David blames Saul's advisers rather than Saul himself. That gives the king a way to back down without losing face. Advisers like Doeg had indeed been feeding Saul's suspicions (22:9–10).
- **"The LORD gave you today into my hand… but I spared you" (v10).** David agrees with his men on one thing: God did put Saul in his hand. He disagrees about what that meant. God gave him the chance to show mercy, not the right to kill.
- **"See, my father, see the corner of your robe in my hand" (v11).** David calls Saul "my father." Saul was his father-in-law (18:27), and the word recalls a closer relationship before the jealousy began. The cloth in his hand is proof. He was close enough to kill, and he did not. "There is no wrong or treason in my hands."
- **"May the LORD judge between me and you, may the LORD avenge me against you, but my hand shall not be against you" (v12).** David does not pretend Saul is innocent. He hands the case to God, the only judge above a king (Gen 16:5; Judg 11:27). The line "my hand shall not be against you" comes twice (vv12–13). David's restraint is not weakness. It is faith that God will set things right. Paul later writes, "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Rom 12:19).
- **"Out of the wicked comes wickedness" (v13).** The old proverb works like "a tree is known by its fruit." If David were wicked, he would have acted wickedly in the cave. He did not, so he is not the rebel Saul thinks.
- **"After a dead dog! After a flea!" (v14).** Dogs in Israel were scavengers, not pets, and "dead dog" was a phrase of utter worthlessness (2 Sam 9:8; 16:9). A flea is tiny and hard to catch. David is saying: why is the king of Israel spending his army hunting someone so small? He will use the flea image again (26:20).
- **"May the LORD… plead my cause and deliver me from your hand" (v15).** "Plead my cause" is courtroom language. God is both judge and defense lawyer. Psalm 57, titled "when he fled from Saul, in the cave," may come from this setting or from Adullam (22:1). It says, "In the shadow of your wings I will take refuge" (57:1).

- **"Is this your voice, my son David?" (v16).** At a distance Saul may not have been able to see clearly, but he knows the voice. He calls David "my son" for the first time since he started calling him "the son of Jesse." Then "Saul lifted up his voice and wept."
- **"You are more righteous than I" (v17).** This is the same thing Judah said about Tamar (Gen 38:26). Saul admits he has repaid good with evil. He even blesses David: "May the LORD reward you with good" (v19).
- **"I know that you shall surely be king" (v20).** Jonathan had said Saul knew this (23:17). Now Saul says it himself, out loud, for the first time.
- **"Swear to me… that you will not cut off my offspring" (v21).** In the ancient world, a new dynasty often killed off the old king's family to remove rivals. Saul asks for the same kind of promise David made to Jonathan (20:14–17). David swears. He later keeps faith with Jonathan's son Mephibosheth (2 Sam 9), though seven of Saul's descendants are executed in a later famine because of Saul's crime against the Gibeonites, and David spares Mephibosheth because of the oath (2 Sam 21:1–9).
- **"Saul went home, but David and his men went up to the stronghold" (v22).** One short verse speaks volumes. Saul's tears were real, but they did not change him, and David knew it. David does not follow Saul home to the palace. He goes back up to the hills. By chapter 26 Saul is hunting him again.
- **Two accounts.** Chapter 26 tells a similar story: Saul in David's power, David's men urging him to strike, David refusing because Saul is the LORD's anointed, a token taken, a speech, and Saul's confession. Some scholars think these are two versions of one event. The details differ throughout (cave and robe here, camp and spear there, a different companion, a different place), and most readers take them as two real events. The repetition underlines the point. David's restraint was not a one-time impulse but a settled conviction.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the cave as "an opportunity given to David to exercise faith and patience," and notes that David "not only would not do this bad thing himself, but he would not suffer those about him to do it." He says David "declares his fixed resolution never to be his own avenger," because "if men wrong us, God will right us, at farthest, in the judgment of the great day." His verdict on Saul has become famous: "Saul went home convinced, but not converted," for "malice often seems dead when it is only asleep." JFB states plainly that God "had never made any promise of delivering Saul into David's hand," so the men's claim was their own reading, and it calls Saul's change "transient." JFB's comment stops at v15. Guzik compares David's refusal with Jesus refusing Satan's offer of the kingdoms of the world (Luke 4:5–8). He warns that "touch not the Lord's anointed" is often misused to shield leaders from accountability, since David openly named Saul's sin and only refused to take his life. And he notes that emotion which does not lead to action leaves a person worse off: the direction of a life proves repentance, not tears in one moment.

#### Pointing to Christ
David, the anointed king-in-waiting, is offered the throne by a shortcut and refuses it, trusting God to give it in his own time and way. Jesus was offered "all the kingdoms of the world" if he would bow to Satan (Luke 4:5–8), and refused, choosing the path of obedience through the cross. David spared his enemy and left judgment to God. Jesus went further: he prayed for those who were killing him (Luke 23:34), and "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return… but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). The word David used for Saul, *mashiach*, anointed, becomes the title of the one true King, who received his kingdom not by seizing it but by giving himself.
