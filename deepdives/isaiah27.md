*Setting:* This is the last of the four chapters called the "Isaiah Apocalypse" (chs 24–27). Chapter 26 ended with God coming out of his place to judge the earth, and chapter 27 opens with him slaying the great sea monster. The rest of the chapter returns to Israel: a new song of God's vineyard that reverses the sad song of chapter 5, a reflection on why God disciplined his people and what that discipline was for, and a closing picture of the scattered people gathered one by one at the sound of a great trumpet. The chapter is hard in places, especially vv7–11, and readers take some details differently. Henry, typically, sees a first fulfillment in God's rescue of Jerusalem from Sennacherib, and a fuller one in the gospel and the church.

#### The Redemption of Israel (vv1–13)
- **"The LORD with his hard and great and strong sword will punish Leviathan" (v1).** Leviathan was a monster of the deep known across the ancient world as a symbol of chaos. Clay tablets from Ugarit, a city on the Syrian coast, describe Baal defeating "Lotan, the fleeing serpent… the twisting serpent, the mighty one with seven heads." Isaiah uses almost the same words: "Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent." He is not endorsing the myth. He is saying that what the pagans imagined their gods did, the LORD will really do: he will destroy evil at its root. The Bible uses Leviathan for real creatures too (Job 41; Ps 104:26), and for God's victory over Egypt at the Red Sea (Ps 74:13–14; Isa 51:9).
- **Who is Leviathan here?** Some see three monsters standing for three empires, perhaps Assyria, Babylon and Egypt. Henry thinks of oppressive powers like Pharaoh, who is called a dragon in Scripture (Ezek 29:3). Most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, Guzik and JFB, see the ultimate enemy behind all such powers: the serpent of Genesis 3, "that ancient serpent, who is the devil and Satan" (Rev 12:9; 20:2). Revelation's seven-headed dragon draws on exactly this imagery (Rev 12:3).
- **"A pleasant vineyard, sing of it!" (v2).** This answers the "Song of the Vineyard" in 5:1–7, where God's vineyard produced only wild grapes and he let it be trampled. Here God sings over a vineyard he delights in. The Hebrew manuscripts differ by one letter: "a vineyard of wine" (*chemer*, the reading behind the KJV's "red wine") or "a pleasant vineyard" (*chemed*). Many Hebrew manuscripts and the Greek translation read "pleasant," and the ESV follows them.
- **"I, the LORD, am its keeper; every moment I water it… I keep it night and day" (v3).** In chapter 5, God commanded the clouds to give no rain (5:6). Now he waters it every moment and guards it day and night, the same care as "he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (Ps 121:4).
- **"I have no wrath" (v4).** God's anger toward his people is spent. "Would that I had thorns and briers to battle!" In chapter 5 thorns and briers overran the vineyard (5:6). Now, if any appear, meaning enemies who threaten it, God would march against them and burn them up. JFB takes the briers as "the wicked foe." Henry: "Fury is not in him towards his vineyard."
- **"Or let them lay hold of my protection, let them make peace with me" (v5).** Here is a remarkable offer. Even the enemies, the thorns and briers, may escape the fire by grabbing hold of God's "protection" (*ma'oz*, "stronghold, refuge"). JFB compares it to a fugitive grabbing the horns of the altar for asylum (1 Kings 1:50) and applies it to sinners taking refuge in Christ. "Let them make peace with me" is repeated for emphasis. Paul speaks the same way: "be reconciled to God" (2 Cor 5:20). The God who has no fury toward his vineyard invites even his foes to peace.
- **"In days to come Jacob shall take root… and fill the whole world with fruit" (v6).** The vineyard that once failed will grow until its fruit covers the earth. Isaiah has already promised a root and a shoot from Jesse (11:1, 10). Jesus calls himself "the true vine" and says his disciples bear much fruit by abiding in him (John 15:1–8). The Tyndale notes see this fulfilled in all God's people, Jew and Gentile together. JFB cites Romans 11:12, where Israel's restoration means "riches for the Gentiles."
- **"Has he struck them as he struck those who struck them?" (v7).** The question is about the difference between how God treated Israel and how he treated Israel's enemies. Assyria and Babylon were destroyed completely. Israel was struck, but not wiped out. God's discipline of his people has a limit and a purpose.
- **"Measure by measure, by exile you contended with them" (v8).** "Measure by measure" may be a pun on the *seah*, a dry measure, meaning God weighed out exactly as much discipline as was needed. Jeremiah says the same: "I will discipline you in just measure" (Jer 30:11). Henry: "He measures out their afflictions by a little at a time, that they may not be pressed above measure." **"The day of the east wind"** is the scorching wind off the desert (Jer 4:11; Hos 13:15). Exile was that wind, blowing Israel out of its land.
- **"By this the guilt of Jacob will be atoned for, and this will be the full fruit of the removal of his sin" (v9).** The discipline is meant to remove sin. "Atoned for" is *kaphar*, the sacrifice word that was denied to Jerusalem's revelers in 22:14. Paul quotes this verse, combined with 59:20–21 and following the Greek translation, in his promise that "all Israel will be saved": "this will be my covenant with them when I take away their sins" (Rom 11:26–27).
- **"When he makes all the stones of the altars like chalkstones crushed to pieces" (v9).** Chalkstones are limestone, which was crushed and burned to make lime. The proof that Israel has truly turned will be the smashing of its pagan altars. **"No Asherim or incense altars will remain standing."** Asherim were wooden poles or carved trees honoring the Canaanite goddess Asherah, often set beside altars (Deut 7:5). Repentance is not only feeling sorry. It means breaking the idols. Henry: "The design of affliction is to separate between us and sin."
- **"The fortified city is solitary, a habitation deserted and forsaken" (v10).** Which city? It is not named, and views differ. JFB takes it as Jerusalem, abandoned in the exile. Others think of Samaria, the capital of the northern kingdom, which fell in 722 BC. Still others, including Guzik, see the same proud city of man that appears throughout chapters 24–26 (24:10; 25:2; 26:5). The picture is the same in any case: calves grazing in the ruins, stripping the bushes.
- **"Women come and make a fire of them. For this is a people without discernment" (v11).** The dry branches are only fit for kindling. Isaiah began his book by saying "Israel does not know, my people do not understand" (1:3). **"He who made them will not have compassion on them."** These are hard words. God made and formed his people, and yet he will not spare those who persist in rejecting him. The Tyndale notes take vv7–11 together as Isaiah explaining the exile. The words do not cancel the promise of v9. They explain why discipline had to come first.
- **"From the river Euphrates to the Brook of Egypt the LORD will thresh out the grain" (v12).** These are the borders God promised Abraham (Gen 15:18) and reached under Solomon (1 Kings 4:21). The Brook of Egypt is the Wadi el-Arish, a dry streambed in the northern Sinai. "Thresh out" (*chabat*) is the word for beating olives off a tree (Deut 24:20) or beating out grain with a stick (Ruth 2:17). It was a gentle way of threshing, so that every grain would be saved.
- **"You will be gleaned one by one, O people of Israel" (v12).** God does not gather his people as a crowd but individually, each one known by name. JFB stresses that the gathering is "one by one."
- **"A great trumpet will be blown" (v13).** The *shofar*, a ram's horn, called Israel to worship, to war and to the Year of Jubilee, when slaves were freed and land returned (Lev 25:9). **"Those who were lost in the land of Assyria and those who were driven out to the land of Egypt"** are the scattered of both north and south, all Israel's exiles. **"Will come and worship the LORD on the holy mountain at Jerusalem."** The chapter, and the whole "Isaiah Apocalypse," ends where it was heading: worship on God's mountain (24:23; 25:6). Jesus said that at his return "he will send out his angels with a loud trumpet call, and they will gather his elect from the four winds" (Matt 24:31), and Paul speaks of "the trumpet of God" at the resurrection (1 Thess 4:16; 1 Cor 15:52).
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as God's care for his church: the oppressors judged, the vineyard guarded, the quarrel laid aside, afflictions moderated and sanctified, and the scattered gathered, all applied to "the grace of the gospel." On v13 he writes that "the great trumpet of all will gather the saints together." Guzik reads Leviathan as Satan and links v9 to Romans 11:26, where "all Israel will be saved." He also contrasts this fruitful vineyard with the failed one of chapter 5 and says fruit comes only by taking hold of God's strength. JFB notes that "leviathan" means "twisted animal" and sees Satan as the ultimate meaning. He calls v5 a picture of grasping the altar horns for refuge, applied to Christ, and reads the great trumpet as the gospel's call in the last days.

#### Pointing to Christ
Leviathan, the twisting serpent, is the enemy behind every enemy, and Jesus came to crush his head (Gen 3:15; 1 John 3:8). The vineyard God sings over is the people joined to Jesus, the true vine, and fruit fills the world through him. The offer of v5 stands open to anyone: "let them lay hold of my protection, let them make peace with me." That peace was made "by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20). And one day the great trumpet will sound, and the Lord who gleans his people one by one will gather every one of them home.
