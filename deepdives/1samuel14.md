*Setting:* Still early in Saul's reign, around 1045 BC, in the hills of Benjamin a few miles north of Jerusalem. Chapter 13 left Israel in a desperate position. The Philistines are camped at Michmash with a huge army and are sending raiding parties through the land. Saul has only about six hundred men, most of them armed with farm tools, because the Philistines control all the blacksmiths (13:19–22). Samuel has just told Saul that his dynasty will not last because he would not wait for the LORD's word (13:13–14). The two armies face each other across a deep gorge, the Philistines on the north side at Michmash and Israel on the south at Geba. Chapter 14 sets two men side by side. Jonathan acts in faith and God gives a great victory. Saul acts in anxious, self-centered religion, and he nearly kills his own son.

#### Jonathan Defeats the Philistines (vv1–23)
- **"Come, let us go over to the Philistine garrison on the other side" (v1).** Jonathan acts while his father sits. "But he did not tell his father," perhaps because Saul would have forbidden it, or because the plan was between Jonathan and the LORD.
- **"Saul was staying in the outskirts of Gibeah in the pomegranate cave at Migron" (v2).** The Hebrew can be read "under the pomegranate tree" (most translations) or as a place name, "the cave of Rimmon" (the ESV's choice, since "Rimmon" means "pomegranate" and there is a well-known Rimmon rock in Benjamin, Judg 20:45–47). Either way, the picture is of a king waiting under cover while his son goes to fight.
- **"Ahijah the son of Ahitub, Ichabod's brother, son of Phinehas, son of Eli" (v3).** This long family tree is not filler. It reminds us that Saul's priest comes from Eli's house, the family God had already rejected (2:30–36). Ichabod's name meant "the glory has departed" (4:21). The rejected king keeps company with the rejected priesthood. Ahijah wears the **ephod**, the priestly vest that held the Urim and Thummim, used to ask God for guidance (Exod 28:30). Many think this Ahijah is the same man as Ahimelech, the priest at Nob in chapter 21, since the names are similar ("brother of the LORD" and "brother of the king").
- **Bozez and Seneh (vv4–5).** The pass between Michmash and Geba runs through a steep ravine, the Wadi Suwenit, with rocky cliffs on both sides. Bozez probably means "shining" or "slippery," and Seneh "thorny," the same word as the burning bush (Exod 3:2). The British officer Vivian Gilbert later told how, in 1918, British troops used this same story to find a path up the cliffs and take the Turkish position at Michmash by surprise.
- **"Let us go over to the garrison of these uncircumcised" (v6).** Circumcision was the sign of God's covenant with Abraham (Gen 17:10–14). Calling the Philistines "uncircumcised" was a way of saying they were outside God's covenant, and so without God's promise. David uses the same words of Goliath (17:26).
- **"It may be that the LORD will work for us, for nothing can hinder the LORD from saving by many or by few" (v6).** Jonathan does not claim to know God's plan. "It may be" is humble. But he knows God's power. This is the lesson of Gideon, whose army God cut down to three hundred so that Israel could not boast (Judg 7:2–7). Later Asa will pray, "there is none like you to help, between the mighty and the weak" (2 Chr 14:11).
- **"Behold, I am with you heart and soul" (v7).** The unnamed armor-bearer is one of the quiet heroes of Scripture. His loyalty is total: literally, "I am with you as your heart."
- **The sign (vv8–10).** Jonathan sets up a test. If the Philistines say "Wait," he and his armor-bearer will stay put. If they say "Come up," the LORD has handed them over. This is not testing God's promise, as Gideon did with the fleece after God had already spoken (Judg 6:36–40). It is a man of faith asking God to guide his steps. And notice which answer counts as "go": the harder one, which means climbing a cliff into an enemy camp.
- **"Look, Hebrews are coming out of the holes where they have hidden themselves" (v11).** The Philistines mock. They know Israelites have been hiding in caves (13:6). "Come up to us, and we will show you a thing" (v12) is a taunt, something like "Come up here and we'll teach you a lesson." Jonathan hears it as God's answer: "the LORD has given them into the hand of Israel." He says "Israel," not "us." He is fighting for the nation.
- **"Jonathan climbed up on his hands and feet" (v13).** He comes up where no one expects an attack, perhaps over the rocks rather than by the path. "They fell before Jonathan, and his armor-bearer killed them after him." Jonathan struck the men down, and his armor-bearer finished them off.
- **"About twenty men within as it were half a furrow's length in an acre of land" (v14).** The Hebrew is obscure. It describes a small area, roughly the strip a yoke of oxen could plow in part of a day. The point is that twenty men fell in a very short space, very quickly.
- **"The earth quaked, and it became a very great panic" (v15).** The last phrase is literally "a trembling of God," a Hebrew way of saying "an enormous panic," but here it means both: God himself sent the terror, along with an earthquake. The LORD had thundered against the Philistines once before (7:10). As with Gideon's three hundred, God turns a small attack into a rout (Judg 7:21–22).
- **"Count and see who has gone from us" (v17).** Saul's watchmen at Gibeah see the Philistine army melting away. Saul's first response is a roll call. Only then does he discover that his son has started the battle without him.
- **"Bring the ark of God here" (v18).** The Hebrew text reads "ark," but the ark was at Kiriath-jearim at this time (7:1–2; 2 Sam 6:2–3), and it was not used to ask questions of God. The Greek translation reads "Bring the ephod," which fits v3 and the phrase "withdraw your hand" (v19), since the priest would reach into the ephod's pouch to draw the lot. Many scholars think "ephod" is original, and some translations (such as the NIV footnote) mention it. The ESV keeps "ark," as do others.
- **"Withdraw your hand" (v19).** As the noise grows, Saul stops in the middle of asking God. He decides there is no time to wait for an answer. It is the same impatience as at Gilgal (13:8–12): religion when it is convenient, and action without God's word when it is not.
- **"Every Philistine's sword was against his fellow" (v20).** The enemy destroys itself in confusion, like the Midianites under Gideon (Judg 7:22) and later the armies that attacked Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 20:23).
- **The Hebrews with the Philistines, and the men in hiding (vv21–22).** Some Israelites had been serving with the Philistine army, as conscripts or as collaborators. They switch sides. Others who had hidden in the hill country of Ephraim come out once the enemy is running. Victory brings out many who were nowhere to be found when it took courage.
- **"So the LORD saved Israel that day" (v23).** The writer gives the credit to God alone, not to Saul, and not even to Jonathan. The words echo the Red Sea: "Thus the LORD saved Israel that day" (Exod 14:30).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the names Bozez ("shining," from the chalky rock) and Seneh ("the thorn," perhaps from an acacia) and stresses that Jonathan's "it may be" is not doubt but an admission that the outcome "depended upon God." JFB, like the Tyndale notes, prefers "ephod" in v18, saying "there is no evidence that the ark had been brought from Kirjath-jearim." Henry on the panic: "He that made the heart, knows how to make it tremble," and on Saul's halting of the inquiry, "He that believeth, will not make such haste." Guzik distinguishes Jonathan's sign from Gideon's fleece: Jonathan distrusted his own impressions, not God's word. He also groups the latecomers in vv21–22 into those who held back, those who had sold out to the enemy and those who hid, all contrasted with Jonathan's bold faith.

#### Saul's Rash Vow (vv24–46)
- **"Cursed be the man who eats food until it is evening and I am avenged on my enemies" (v24).** Saul puts the army under a curse in the middle of a long day of fighting. Fasting before battle might look devout, but the oath reveals Saul's heart: "*I* am avenged on *my* enemies." It is about Saul. The oath also cost the army its strength on the very day it needed it. "Hard pressed" may describe the army's distress *because of* the oath.
- **"The honey was dropping" (vv25–26).** Wild bees nested in rocks and trees in the hill country, and honey could drip from the combs. The land "flowing with… honey" is right in front of them, and no one dares to touch it.
- **"His eyes became bright" (v27).** Jonathan, who had been out fighting and had not heard the oath, dips his staff in the honeycomb and eats. "Eyes became bright" is a Hebrew way of saying his strength came back. Low blood sugar in exhausted men dims the eyes, and a little honey revived him.
- **"My father has troubled the land" (v29).** The word "troubled" (*'akar*) is loaded. It is the word Joshua spoke to Achan, whose sin brought defeat on Israel: "Why did you bring trouble on us?" (Josh 7:25). Elijah and Ahab later throw the same word at each other: "you troubler of Israel" (1 Kgs 18:17–18). Without meaning to, Jonathan names his father as the real troubler. His point is practical: well-fed soldiers would have won a far bigger victory (v30).
- **"From Michmash to Aijalon" (v31).** Aijalon was about 15–20 miles west, down toward the coastal plain, the valley where the sun once stood still for Joshua (Josh 10:12). It was a great pursuit, by men running on empty.
- **"The people ate them with the blood" (v32).** At evening the oath expired, and the starving soldiers fell on the captured animals, slaughtering them on the ground and eating before the blood drained out. The law strictly forbade eating blood, "for the life of the flesh is in the blood" (Lev 17:10–14; Gen 9:4; Deut 12:23–24). Saul's oath, meant to look devout, pushed the people into real sin.
- **"Roll a great stone to me here" (vv33–34).** Saul acts quickly and well here. A large stone let the animals be killed so that the blood ran off properly, and in his presence. "You have dealt treacherously," he says to the people, though the root of the problem was his own command.
- **"It was the first altar that he built to the LORD" (v35).** The Hebrew can also mean "he began to build it as an altar." Either way, Saul's religious acts in this chapter are real but reactive. They are attempts to manage a crisis rather than signs of a heart turned toward God.
- **"Let us draw near to God here" (v36).** Saul wants to chase the Philistines all night. The army says, "Do whatever seems good to you." Only the priest suggests asking God first. This time Saul does, but "he did not answer him that day" (v37). God's silence is itself a message.
- **"Though it be in Jonathan my son, he shall surely die" (v39).** Saul assumes someone has sinned. He swears a second oath, more reckless than the first. "Not a man among all the people… answered him." The army knew about Jonathan's honey and stayed silent, which says a great deal about how they saw the oath.
- **"Give Urim… give Thummim" (v41).** The Urim and Thummim were sacred lots kept in the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:30; Num 27:21). No one knows exactly what they looked like. The ESV here follows the longer Greek text, which spells out the procedure: Urim if the guilt is in Saul or Jonathan, Thummim if it is in the people. The shorter Hebrew text reads only "Give *tamim*" ("perfect" or "true"), a word that sounds like Thummim. Most scholars think words dropped out of the Hebrew because a copyist's eye skipped from one "Israel" to the next. The ESV footnotes the difference.
- **"Jonathan was taken" (v42).** The lot narrows down to Jonathan, as it narrowed to Achan (Josh 7:16–18). But the parallel is ironic. Achan stole what God had forbidden. Jonathan ate honey he did not know was forbidden, on a day when he had been God's instrument of victory. The lot shows *who* broke the oath, not that God agreed with the oath.
- **"I tasted a little honey… Here I am; I will die" (v43).** Jonathan does not argue or blame. He tells the truth and is ready to take the consequences. "Here I am" is the response of faithful servants like Abraham and Samuel (Gen 22:1; 1 Sam 3:4).
- **"God do so to me and more also; you shall surely die, Jonathan" (v44).** "God do so to me and more also" was a standard oath formula, calling down death on oneself if the promise was not kept. Saul is willing to kill the son God just used to save Israel, to protect his own word and his image as a man of strict religion. Compare Jephthah, who kept a foolish vow at the cost of his daughter (Judg 11:30–39).
- **"Shall Jonathan die, who has worked this great salvation in Israel?" (v45).** The army finally speaks, and they echo Saul's own oath back at him: "As the LORD lives." They see what Saul cannot: Jonathan "has worked with God this day." "Not one hair of his head shall fall" is a proverb of complete protection (2 Sam 14:11; Luke 21:18).
- **"So the people ransomed Jonathan" (v45).** "Ransomed" (*padah*) is the word for redeeming a firstborn son or a slave by paying a price (Exod 13:13–15), and for God redeeming Israel from Egypt (Deut 7:8). The text does not say how. They may have offered a payment or a substitute animal, or the word may simply mean they rescued him. Either way, the king's son is saved by the people's intervention.
- **"The Philistines went to their own place" (v46).** The pursuit ends. The victory that could have been decisive (v30) is left incomplete, and the Philistines remain a threat for the rest of Saul's life.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the oath "very unwise; if it gained time, it lost strength for the pursuit," and on Saul's readiness to kill Jonathan, "Those most indulgent to their own sins are most severe upon others." He adds that "an unhumbled heart suspects every other person." JFB says the oath was made from fear that "so precious an opportunity" would be lost, and that "the more enlightened conscience of the army prevented" Jonathan's death. Guzik lists what was wrong with the vow: its focus (Saul's revenge), its motive, its timing and its effect on the army. He calls it legalism that ended by driving people into real lawbreaking. The Tyndale notes point out the telling "*my* enemies" in v24 and link Jonathan's word "troubled" to Achan and Jephthah, the same links the lexicon shows for *'akar*.

#### Saul Fights Israel's Enemies (vv47–52)
- **"When Saul had taken the kingship over Israel" (v47).** This is a summary of Saul's whole reign, the kind of summary that usually closes a king's story in the books of Samuel and Kings (compare 2 Sam 8:15–18 for David). Placing it here hints that, in God's eyes, Saul's reign is already effectively over. The rest of 1 Samuel is about David's rise.
- **"Against Moab… the Ammonites… Edom… the kings of Zobah, and against the Philistines" (v47).** These are the nations on every side: Moab and Ammon to the east, Edom to the south, Zobah (an Aramean kingdom north of Damascus) to the north, and the Philistines to the west. Saul was a real military leader. David will later fight and subdue the same list (2 Sam 8:1–14).
- **"Wherever he turned he routed them" (v47).** The Hebrew verb here usually means "acted wickedly" or "condemned," and some scholars think the original read "he was victorious," following the Greek. The ESV's "routed" takes the Hebrew in its sense of "put them in the wrong" or "punished them."
- **"Struck the Amalekites" (v48).** This prepares for chapter 15, where Saul's war against Amalek becomes his final failure.
- **Saul's family (vv49–51).** His sons Jonathan, Ishvi and Malchi-shua (Abinadab is added in 31:2). Ishvi is probably the son called Ish-bosheth or Eshbaal elsewhere (2 Sam 2:8; 1 Chr 8:33), who briefly ruled the northern tribes after Saul's death. His daughters are **Merab** and **Michal**, both of whom will be offered to David (18:17–27). His wife is **Ahinoam**. His commander is **Abner son of Ner**, his uncle (or cousin, depending on how v51 is read), who becomes a major figure in 2 Samuel.
- **"When Saul saw any strong man… he attached him to himself" (v52).** This is just what Samuel had warned a king would do: "He will take your sons… and the best of your young men" (8:11, 16). It also sets up the next turn of the story. One day Saul will see a strong, valiant young man named David and take him into his service (16:18–22).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the family list soberly: Saul "had little reason to be proud of his royal dignity," since his reign was full of hard fighting. Guzik calls Saul's kingdom broad but shallow, real victories without a heart after God. The Tyndale notes add that the family list appears here, not at the end of Saul's life, as a signal that none of these sons would follow him on the throne.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two sons of Saul's house stand in this chapter. One, the king, hides, demands, curses and nearly kills to protect his own word. The other, the king's son, goes out against the enemy with no guarantee but the LORD's power, wins salvation for Israel, and then says, "Here I am; I will die." The people refuse to let the deliverer die and ransom him. In the gospel the story is turned around. Jesus, the King's Son, went out alone against the enemy, and he did die, under a curse that was not his own (Gal 3:13), in order to ransom the people (Mark 10:45). Jonathan's faith, "nothing can hinder the LORD from saving by many or by few," is answered in full at the cross, where God saved the many by the One.
