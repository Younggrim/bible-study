*Setting:* Psalm 118 is the last and greatest psalm of the "Egyptian Hallel" (Psalms 113–118), the praise psalms sung at Passover. It closed the Passover meal, so when Jesus and his disciples "sang a hymn" and went out to the Mount of Olives (Matt 26:30), this was very likely the last thing they sang. It was also sung at the Feast of Tabernacles: the Mishnah (written down around AD 200) records worshipers waving palm and willow branches at "Save us, we pray" (v25). The psalm has no title. Its speaker is a leader, most likely a king, who was surrounded by enemy nations, nearly killed, and rescued by God. He now arrives at the temple gates in a victory procession, and the priests and people answer him in turn. Many readers, Matthew Henry among them, have thought of David. Others think of a later king, or of the community returned from exile led by its governor. The psalm does not say. It became one of the most quoted psalms in the New Testament. The crowds on Palm Sunday sang its words (vv25–26), Jesus applied its "rejected stone" to himself (v22), and the apostles preached it. It is also the psalm Martin Luther called his own, saying it had helped him out of troubles no emperor, king or saint could have helped him with. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Oh Give Thanks to the LORD, for He Is Good (vv1–4)
- **"Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good" (v1).** This was one of Israel's most familiar songs. It was sung when the ark came to Jerusalem (1 Chron 16:34), when the temple was dedicated (2 Chron 7:3), when the army marched out under Jehoshaphat (2 Chron 20:21), and when the foundation of the second temple was laid after the exile (Ezra 3:11). It opens Psalms 106, 107 and 136. The reason for thanks is God's own nature: he *is* good.
- **"For his steadfast love endures forever!" (v1).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. "Endures forever" is literally "is to forever." This line is the refrain of the whole psalm, sounded at the start and the end (v29).
- **Israel, the house of Aaron, those who fear the LORD (vv2–4).** The same three groups were called to trust in Psalm 115:9–11: the whole nation, the priests, and the God-fearers, which may mean all the faithful or may include Gentiles who worshiped Israel's God. Each group is told to "say" the refrain. A leader likely called and each group answered, "His steadfast love endures forever." Psalm 136 does the same thing twenty-six times.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes simply that "the trine repetitions are emphatic." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that God's goodness is his nature, so thanks should not depend on what has just been received, and Kidner's observation that refrains like this let a congregation rehearse God's acts together.

#### Out of My Distress I Called on the LORD (vv5–9)
- **"Out of my distress I called on the LORD" (v5).** "Distress" is *metsar*, a narrow place, a tight squeeze. It is the opposite of the next line.
- **"The LORD answered me and set me free" (v5).** Literally, "the LORD answered me in a broad place." The Hebrew word is *merchav*, a wide-open space, and here it is joined to God's short name, "the broad place of Yah." The lexicon takes this as "an extraordinarily spacious place." He was squeezed in, and God gave him room. David uses the same picture: "He brought me out into a broad place" (18:19).
- **"The LORD is on my side; I will not fear. What can man do to me?" (v6).** Literally "the LORD is for me." This is not a claim that nothing bad will happen. It is a claim that nothing people can do will finally matter, because God is for him (56:4, 11). Hebrews quotes this verse to tell believers to be content: "The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me?" (Heb 13:6). Paul's "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31) is the same thought.
- **"The LORD is on my side as my helper" (v7).** "Helper" is the word used in Psalm 115:9–11 ("their help and their shield"). "I shall look in triumph on those who hate me" means he will see them defeated (54:7; 112:8).
- **"It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in man… than to trust in princes" (vv8–9).** Kings in danger looked for powerful allies. Israel's history is full of kings who trusted Egypt or Assyria and were let down (Isa 30:1–3; 31:1; 2 Kings 16:7–9). The psalm says plainly that the best human help is still a worse refuge than God (146:3; Jer 17:5–8).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the "large place" answers to the "straits" of distress, says "men are helpless to hurt him, if God be with him," and adds that "even the most powerful men are less to be trusted than God." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that the psalmist does not say he will not suffer, only that he will not fear. He also lists Spurgeon's reasons why trusting God is better than trusting princes: it is wiser, safer and ends better.

#### In the Name of the LORD I Cut Them Off (vv10–14)
- **"All nations surrounded me" (v10).** The speaker is not a private person. He is a leader whose people are under attack from many nations at once. "Surrounded" comes four times in vv10–12 and "in the name of the LORD I cut them off" three times. The repetition makes the reader feel the enemy closing in, and then feel the turn each time.
- **"In the name of the LORD I cut them off!" (vv10–12).** The verb means to cut down or ward off. Some have connected it to the Hebrew word for circumcision, since the same root (*mul*) is used, but most translators take it in the plain sense of driving off. His weapon is not his own strength but the LORD's name, as David told Goliath: "I come to you in the name of the LORD of hosts" (1 Sam 17:45).
- **"They surrounded me like bees; they went out like a fire among thorns" (v12).** A swarm of bees is many, angry and painful (Deut 1:44). But a fire in dry thorns flares up fast and burns out just as fast (Eccl 7:6). The enemies were fierce but short-lived.
- **"I was pushed hard, so that I was falling, but the LORD helped me" (v13).** The Hebrew reads "you pushed me hard," addressed to the enemy. The ESV smooths it into a passive. He was shoved and was going down. Then God caught him.
- **"The LORD is my strength and my song; he has become my salvation" (v14).** These are the exact words Moses and Israel sang on the far side of the Red Sea (Exod 15:2), and Isaiah repeats them for a future rescue (Isa 12:2). The king's victory is seen as a new exodus. God does not just give strength, a song and salvation. He *becomes* them.
- **Commentators:** JFB compares the enemies to bees, "as numerous and irritating," and says they perished "as the fire of thorns—suddenly," and reads v13 as addressed to the enemy "as if present." Guzik, following Kidner, makes the same point about the thorn fire flaring and dying, and ties v14 back to Miriam's song in Exodus 15. Henry says the account of these troubles is "very applicable to Christ," who was surrounded by enemies at his death.

#### The Right Hand of the LORD Does Valiantly (vv15–18)
- **"Glad songs of salvation are in the tents of the righteous" (v15).** After a victory the camp rings with singing. "Tents" may recall the wilderness, or the booths of the Feast of Tabernacles, when Israel lived in tents for a week.
- **"The right hand of the LORD does valiantly" (vv15–16).** This is the victory chant, three lines, with "the right hand of the LORD" each time. The right hand stands for strength and skill in action (Exod 15:6, 12: "Your right hand, O LORD, glorious in power"). "Does valiantly" means acts with strength, does mighty things. "Exalts" means lifts high, raises up.
- **"I shall not die, but I shall live, and recount the deeds of the LORD" (v17).** He expected to die and did not. Now his life has a purpose: telling what God has done. The story goes that John Wycliffe, lying ill and visited by friars who expected him to recant, raised himself and quoted this verse. Luther is said to have written it on the wall of his study.
- **"The LORD has disciplined me severely, but he has not given me over to death" (v18).** The Hebrew repeats the verb for intensity: "disciplining, he disciplined me." He sees God's hand even in the attack. It was hard training, not abandonment (Prov 3:11–12; Heb 12:5–11). There is a limit set: death will not have him.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls salvation the cause of the rejoicing, explains the right hand as God's "power greatly exerted," and says of v17 that "he would live, because confident his life would be for God's glory." Guzik retells the Wycliffe story and adds that God's discipline is purposeful and limited.

#### Open to Me the Gates of Righteousness (vv19–21)
- **"Open to me the gates of righteousness" (v19).** Now the procession reaches the temple. The king calls for the gates to open so he can come in and give thanks. They are called "gates of righteousness" because only the righteous may enter. Psalms 15 and 24 are entrance liturgies of this kind: "Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?… He who has clean hands and a pure heart" (24:3–4).
- **"This is the gate of the LORD; the righteous shall enter through it" (v20).** This is the reply from inside, probably from the priests or gatekeepers. The gate belongs to God, and he decides who may come in.
- **"I thank you that you have answered me and have become my salvation" (v21).** He goes in, and his first words are thanks, echoing v14.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the gates are opened to the righteous alone and that "the purpose of solemn praise is intimated." Guzik reads the scene as a triumphal entry to Jerusalem and sees beyond it, through Jesus, the entry into heaven itself.

#### The Stone That the Builders Rejected (vv22–24)
- **"The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (v22).** A builder inspects stones and sets aside the ones that seem unfit. Here the one stone thrown out becomes the most important stone in the building. "Cornerstone" is literally "head of the corner." It may mean the great foundation stone at the corner that sets the line of two walls, or a capstone at the top of a corner or arch that holds the structure together. Scholars debate which. Either way it is the stone everything depends on. In the psalm the rejected stone is the king himself, despised by the nations and perhaps by others, but chosen and vindicated by God.
- **Jesus and the apostles read it of him.** After telling the parable of the tenants who killed the owner's son, Jesus asked the temple leaders, "Have you never read in the Scriptures: 'The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone'?" (Matt 21:42; Mark 12:10–11; Luke 20:17). The "builders" were Israel's leaders, who should have recognized him. Peter said the same to the council after the resurrection: "This Jesus is the stone that was rejected by you, the builders" (Acts 4:11). He links it with Isaiah's precious cornerstone in Zion (Isa 28:16) and the stone of stumbling (Isa 8:14) in 1 Peter 2:6–8, and Paul calls Christ the cornerstone of a church made of Jews and Gentiles (Eph 2:20).
- **"This is the LORD's doing; it is marvelous in our eyes" (v23).** No human being turned the rejected stone into the cornerstone. God did. That is why the people marvel. Jesus quoted this line too (Matt 21:42).
- **"This is the day that the LORD has made; let us rejoice and be glad in it" (v24).** In context "the day" is not just any day. It is the day of this victory, the day God reversed things and lifted up the rejected one. The line is often sung as a general praise for each new day, and that is a fair use, since every day is God's gift. But its first meaning is a particular day of rescue. Christians have long heard it of the resurrection, the day the rejected stone was raised.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "these words are applied by Christ (Mt 21:42) to Himself," and takes "this day" as a "period distinguished by God's favor of all others." Henry says the prophecy "may refer to David's preferment; but principally to Christ," who is "the Stone which the builders refused" and "the chief Cornerstone in the foundation," and warns that "rejecters of Christ are rejected of God." Guzik observes that this verse is quoted in the New Testament more often than almost any other Old Testament text, and lists the ways Jesus was rejected: for his hometown, his lack of formal training, his disregard of traditions, and the company he kept.

#### Blessed Is He Who Comes in the Name of the LORD (vv25–29)
- **"Save us, we pray, O LORD!" (v25).** In Hebrew, *hoshi'ah na*, "save, please!" This is where the word "Hosanna" comes from. It began as a cry for help, like the king's cry for rescue, and by Jesus' day had also become a shout of praise and welcome. At the Feast of Tabernacles worshipers shook branches as they sang this verse. So when crowds met Jesus at Jerusalem waving palm branches and shouting "Hosanna!" (John 12:13), they were singing Psalm 118 to him.
- **"O LORD, we pray, give us success!" (v25).** "Success" means to prosper, to have a journey go well. It is a prayer that God's victory would continue.
- **"Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD!" (v26).** The priests welcome the king as he enters. "In the name of the LORD" means coming with God's authority and on his business. The crowds at the triumphal entry shouted these exact words to Jesus, adding "the King of Israel" and "the Son of David" (Matt 21:9; Mark 11:9–10; Luke 19:38; John 12:13). Days before, Jesus had wept over Jerusalem and said, "You will not see me until you say, 'Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord'" (Luke 13:35; Matt 23:39). By the time of Jesus, "the one who comes" was a way of speaking of the Messiah (Matt 11:3).
- **"We bless you from the house of the LORD" (v26).** The "you" in Hebrew is plural, so the priests are blessing the king and all who come with him.
- **"The LORD is God, and he has made his light to shine upon us" (v27).** This recalls the priestly blessing: "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). God has smiled on his people.
- **"Bind the festal sacrifice with cords, up to the horns of the altar!" (v27).** This line is hard to translate, and the ESV's footnote gives alternatives. "Festal sacrifice" is literally "festival" (*chag*). It may mean the festival animal tied with cords and led to the altar, whose four corners had horn-shaped projections. Or it may mean the festival procession bound together with branches, reaching up to the altar, which fits the branch-waving of Tabernacles. Either way, the procession ends in worship at the altar.
- **"You are my God, and I will give thanks to you" (v28).** The king's own voice returns, personal and simple, echoing the Song of the Sea: "This is my God, and I will praise him" (Exod 15:2).
- **"Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good" (v29).** The psalm ends exactly where it began. Everything between, the distress, the enemies, the near-death, the gates, the rejected stone, has only proved the opening line true.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains "save now" as "a form of prayer (Ps 20:9)" that became, in later use, praise, and says the phrase "he that cometh" belongs "typically… to Him of whom the phrase became an epithet." It reads the bound sacrifice as pointing to "the more spiritual offering of praise." Henry turns v27 to believers as "living sacrifices, to be bound to the altar" (Rom 12:1). Guzik points out how exactly the scene was fulfilled: Jesus was welcomed with hosannas and blessings, then rejected and crucified within days. He reads the binding of the sacrifice as the deliverer himself becoming the offering.

#### Pointing to Christ
Few psalms are woven so deeply into the story of Jesus. He rode into Jerusalem to the shouts of vv25–26. He told the temple leaders that he was the rejected stone of v22. And on the night he was betrayed, he sang this psalm as he left for Gethsemane: surrounded, pushed hard until he fell, rejected by the builders, bound, and given over to what looked like death. But "I shall not die, but I shall live" (v17) came true in the fullest way on the third day, and the rejected stone became the cornerstone of a new temple made of living stones (1 Pet 2:4–7). "This is the LORD's doing; it is marvelous in our eyes." The gates of righteousness that only the righteous may enter are now open to all who come through him, for he said, "I am the door" (John 10:9).
