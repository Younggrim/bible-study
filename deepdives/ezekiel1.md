*Setting:* Ezekiel was a priest from Jerusalem, carried off to Babylon in 597 BC with King Jehoiachin and about ten thousand of the city's leading citizens (2 Kgs 24:10–16). He lived with other Judean exiles in a settlement called Tel-abib beside the Chebar canal (3:15), in southern Mesopotamia, several hundred miles from home. Five years into that exile, in the summer of 593 BC, God called him to be a prophet. Back in Judah, Jerusalem still stood, the temple still operated, and Zedekiah reigned as Babylon's vassal. Jeremiah was preaching there at the same time. Many exiles believed the captivity would be short and that God could not be found so far from his temple, and false prophets encouraged that hope (Jer 28–29). Ezekiel's book opens with one of the most overwhelming visions in Scripture: the glory of the LORD, enthroned above living creatures and blazing wheels, appearing not in Jerusalem but in Babylon. Chapters 1–3 tell the story of his call. The chapter is hard to picture, and that is part of the point. Ezekiel keeps saying "the likeness of" and "the appearance of," straining to describe what words cannot hold.

#### Ezekiel in Babylon (vv1–3)
- **"In the thirtieth year" (v1).** Thirtieth year of what? The most common view is Ezekiel's own age. Priests began full service at thirty (Num 4:3, 30), so in the year Ezekiel should have started serving in the temple, God called him to something else, far from the temple. Others count from King Josiah's reform in 622 BC, or from the start of Babylon's ruling dynasty in 626 BC. Verse 2 then gives the date in the usual way.
- **"In the fourth month, on the fifth day of the month… the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin" (vv1–2).** That is late July 593 BC. Ezekiel always dates his messages by Jehoiachin's exile, not Zedekiah's reign. He seems to regard Jehoiachin, still alive in Babylon, as the legitimate king. Ezekiel is the most carefully dated of the prophets, with thirteen dates spread through the book.
- **"As I was among the exiles by the Chebar canal" (v1).** Babylonian business records from Nippur mention a large irrigation canal called *naru kabari*, "the great canal," almost certainly this Chebar. Other clay tablets, from a place the Babylonians called "Al-Yahudu," "the town of Judah," show Judean exiles in this region farming, renting land and keeping their own names for generations. Psalm 137 remembers the rivers of Babylon as the place where the exiles sat and wept.
- **"The heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God" (v1).** The ESV footnote offers "visions from God." Either way, heaven itself opens for a priest with no temple. The same phrase is used at Jesus' baptism and Stephen's death (Matt 3:16; Acts 7:56).
- **"The word of the LORD came to Ezekiel the priest, the son of Buzi" (v3).** Here the narrator changes from "I" to "he," a brief third-person heading before the account returns to Ezekiel's own voice. His name, *Yeḥezqe'l*, means "God strengthens" or "may God strengthen," and God's message to him in chapters 2–3 is exactly that: he will make Ezekiel's forehead hard as flint against a hard people (3:8–9). Ezekiel is a priest, and his book is full of priestly concerns: holiness, the temple, clean and unclean, and God's glory dwelling with his people.
- **"In the land of the Chaldeans… the hand of the LORD was upon him there" (v3).** The key word is "there." God's hand rests on Ezekiel not in Jerusalem but in Babylon. "The hand of the LORD" is Ezekiel's phrase for the overpowering moments when God's Spirit takes hold of him (3:14, 22; 8:1; 37:1; 40:1). It was also said of Elijah and Elisha (1 Kgs 18:46; 2 Kgs 3:15).
- **Commentators:** Guzik and the Tyndale notes both read the thirtieth year as Ezekiel's age, the year he would have begun his priestly service, and see the exile turning a priest into a prophet. JFB gives both options, Ezekiel's age or a Babylonian era beginning with Nabopolassar. JFB also identifies the Chebar with the Habor where the northern tribes had been exiled by Assyria, a link most modern scholars do not accept, since the Habor is a river much farther north. JFB describes the hand of the LORD as God "strengthening him for his high and arduous ministry." Matthew Henry draws the comfort for exiles: "It is a mercy to have the word of God brought to us, and a duty to attend to it diligently, when we are in affliction."

#### The Glory of the Lord (vv4–28)
- **"A stormy wind came out of the north, and a great cloud… and fire flashing forth continually" (v4).** Storm, cloud and fire are the classic signs of God appearing, as on Mount Sinai (Exod 19:16–18) and in the psalms (Ps 18:7–15; 50:3). The north was the direction from which Babylon's armies came down on Judah (Jer 1:14; 4:6). In Canaanite myth it was also where the gods lived (Isa 14:13). This storm is not Babylon's god. It is the LORD. **"As it were gleaming metal"**: the Hebrew is *ḥashmal*, a rare word found only in Ezekiel (vv4, 27; 8:2). Older versions translated it "amber." It probably means a glowing metal, perhaps electrum, an alloy of gold and silver. Modern Hebrew borrowed the word for "electricity."
- **"The likeness of four living creatures… they had a human likeness" (v5).** In chapter 10, Ezekiel sees them again and realizes they are **cherubim** (10:15, 20). Cherubim guarded Eden (Gen 3:24), were carved in gold over the ark (Exod 25:18–22) and stood in Solomon's inner sanctuary (1 Kgs 6:23–28). God was said to be "enthroned on the cherubim" (Ps 80:1; 99:1). They are not the chubby babies of later art but powerful, awe-inspiring guardians of God's holiness.
- **"Each had four faces, and each of them had four wings" (v6).** Four is the number of completeness, as in the four directions or four winds. **"The soles of their feet were like the sole of a calf's foot. And they sparkled like burnished bronze" (v7)**: rounded hooves, not human feet, gleaming like polished metal.
- **"Under their wings on their four sides they had human hands" (v8).** Hands to act and serve. In 10:7 one of them takes burning coals in its hand to scatter over Jerusalem. **"Their wings touched one another. Each one of them went straight forward, without turning" (v9).** The four creatures formed a square, wingtip to wingtip. With a face in every direction, they never had to turn. Whatever way they went, they were facing it.
- **"A human face… the face of a lion… the face of an ox… the face of an eagle" (v10).** The strongest of wild animals, the strongest of domestic animals, the greatest of birds, and humanity, the crown of creation. Together they sum up all living creation at its height, serving God. Visitors to Babylon's palaces saw huge carved guardian figures with a bull's or lion's body, eagle's wings and a human head. Ezekiel's creatures share features with them, but they guard the throne of the true God, not a Babylonian king. In Revelation 4:6–8, the four living creatures around God's throne have one face each: lion, ox, man, eagle. From the second century on, Christian writers linked the four faces with the four Gospels. The most common pairing, from Jerome, gave the man to Matthew, the lion to Mark, the ox to Luke and the eagle to John, which is why those symbols appear in so much Christian art. Others paired them differently. This is a devotional tradition, not something the text itself says.
- **"Wherever the spirit would go, they went, without turning" (v12).** "Spirit" is *ruaḥ*, which also means wind and breath. It is one of Ezekiel's most important words: the "stormy wind" of v4, the Spirit who lifts the prophet (2:2; 3:12), and the breath that brings dry bones to life (37:1–14). Here the creatures move entirely in step with God's Spirit.
- **"Their appearance was like burning coals of fire, like the appearance of torches… and out of the fire went forth lightning" (v13).** Fire is the sign of God's holiness and judgment (Deut 4:24; Heb 12:29). **"The living creatures darted to and fro, like the appearance of a flash of lightning" (v14).** They are alive with energy, faster than the eye can follow.
- **"I saw a wheel on the earth beside the living creatures, one for each of the four of them" (v15).** The whole scene is a vast throne on wheels, a chariot. First Chronicles calls the cherubim over the ark "the chariot" (1 Chr 28:18). Later Jewish tradition called this vision the *Merkabah*, "the chariot." It was considered so holy that the Mishnah ruled it should not be expounded publicly, and Jerome reports a Jewish custom that no one under thirty should read it.
- **"Their appearance was like the gleaming of beryl… a wheel within a wheel" (v16).** "Beryl" is *tarshish*, a precious stone, perhaps yellow topaz or chrysolite. "A wheel within a wheel" probably means two wheels set crosswise through each other, at right angles, so that the throne could move **"in any of their four directions without turning as they went" (v17)**. No earthly chariot could do this. God's throne moves anywhere, instantly, without being steered.
- **"Their rims were tall and awesome, and the rims of all four were full of eyes all around" (v18).** "Rims" literally means "backs." The wheels are enormous and covered with eyes, an image of God seeing everything (2 Chr 16:9; Zech 4:10). In 10:12 the eyes cover the cherubim's whole bodies.
- **"For the spirit of the living creatures was in the wheels" (vv19–21).** Said twice. Creatures and wheels move as one, lifting off the ground together. Nothing in God's service moves on its own. One Spirit drives the whole.
- **"The likeness of an expanse, shining like awe-inspiring crystal" (v22).** The "expanse" is *raqia'*, the same word as the "expanse" or "firmament" God made on the second day of creation (Gen 1:6–8). It comes from a verb for beating metal into a thin sheet. Like the sky separating the waters above from the waters below, it separates the creatures from the throne. Moses and the elders saw something like it on Sinai: "under his feet as it were a pavement of sapphire stone, like the very heaven for clearness" (Exod 24:10).
- **"The sound of their wings like the sound of many waters, like the sound of the Almighty, a sound of tumult like the sound of an army" (v24).** "The Almighty" is *Shaddai*, the ancient name God used with Abraham (Gen 17:1). The sound is overwhelming: rushing water, thunder, the roar of a marching army. In Revelation the voice of the risen Christ is "like the roar of many waters" (Rev 1:15). **"When they stood still, they let down their wings"**, repeated in v25, as if the whole scene falls silent for the voice from above.
- **"The likeness of a throne, in appearance like sapphire" (v26).** The ESV footnote says "lapis lazuli," a deep-blue stone flecked with gold, prized in Babylon. The color of the sky. **"Seated above the likeness of a throne was a likeness with a human appearance."** This is the climax. Above the cherubim, above the expanse, on the throne, is a figure "like a man." Ezekiel is careful: not a man, but "a likeness with a human appearance." Daniel later sees "one like a son of man" coming to the Ancient of Days (Dan 7:13).
- **"Upward from what had the appearance of his waist I saw as it were gleaming metal… downward… the appearance of fire" (v27).** Ezekiel can describe the figure only in light and fire. He cannot see a face. The upper part glows like *ḥashmal*, the lower part blazes like fire. John's vision of the risen Christ uses the same colors: eyes "like a flame of fire," feet "like burnished bronze, refined in a furnace" (Rev 1:14–15).
- **"Like the appearance of the bow that is in the cloud on the day of rain" (v28).** The rainbow is the sign of God's covenant with Noah, his promise never again to destroy the earth by flood (Gen 9:12–17). The storm of v4 carries judgment, but the throne at its center is wrapped in the sign of mercy. John too sees a rainbow around God's throne (Rev 4:3).
- **"Such was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD" (v28).** Notice the layers: not the glory itself, nor its likeness, but "the appearance of the likeness of the glory." Even in the vision, Ezekiel sees God only at a distance, through veil upon veil. "The glory of the LORD" (*kabod*, literally "weight" or "heaviness") is the visible radiance of God's presence. It filled the tabernacle (Exod 40:34–35) and Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 8:10–11). In chapters 8–11 Ezekiel will watch this same glory leave the Jerusalem temple because of its idolatry, and in chapter 43 he will see it return to a new temple. The great surprise of chapter 1 is where the glory appears first: in Babylon, among exiles.
- **"When I saw it, I fell on my face, and I heard the voice of one speaking" (v28).** The natural response to God's holiness (Isa 6:5; Dan 8:17; Rev 1:17). The vision is not an end in itself. It prepares Ezekiel to hear. The voice speaks in chapter 2.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees the living creatures as angels, since "angels derive their being and power from God," and the wheels as providence: "the angels are employed as ministers of God's providence." On the figure on the throne he is direct: "The eternal Son, the second Person in the Trinity, who afterwards took the human nature, is here denoted." The rainbow is "the well-known emblem of the covenant." Guzik identifies the creatures as cherubim and traces them from Eden to Revelation 4. He lays out the old tradition linking the four faces to the four Gospels, notes that early writers did not agree on which face belonged to which Gospel, and prefers the view that the faces represent the highest of all living creation. He reads the eyes as God's knowledge of all things and the wheels as his presence everywhere. On the man on the throne, he reports the widely held view that such appearances were the Son before his incarnation (John 12:41), while stressing that Ezekiel saw only a "likeness." JFB reads the wheels as "the complicated revolutions of God's providence," which "seem tortuous and confused to us" but "are all tending steadily to one aim," and the eyes as showing that God "does nothing by blind impulse." On v26 JFB writes, "The Godhead appears in the likeness of enthroned humanity," a "prelude of the incarnation of Messiah," and calls the rainbow "the symbol of the sure covenant of mercy to God's children remembered amidst judgments on the wicked."

#### Pointing to Christ
On the throne above the cherubim, Ezekiel saw "a likeness with a human appearance." Christians from the earliest centuries have seen here a preview of what the New Testament declares: the eternal Son of God would take a true human nature, and a man now sits on the throne of heaven. John says Isaiah "saw his glory and spoke of him" (John 12:41), and the risen Christ appears to John in Ezekiel's colors of fire and bronze, with a voice like many waters (Rev 1:13–15). The glory that Ezekiel saw only as "the appearance of the likeness" is now seen "in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Cor 4:6). And the comfort of the vision is the same: God is not confined to one place. He came to exiles by a Babylonian canal, and in Christ he comes to his scattered people wherever they are, with judgment in the storm and the rainbow of mercy around his throne.
