*Setting:* Joshua is dead (Josh 24:29), and no single leader takes his place. The book of Judges covers the roughly three hundred years between Joshua and the rise of kings, around 1380–1050 BC on the earlier dating of the exodus, or about 1200–1050 BC on the later dating. Israel is a loose federation of tribes, each settled in its own allotment, with the tabernacle at Shiloh, and with Canaanite towns still scattered through the land (Josh 13:1–7; 23:4–5). The book begins with two introductions. Chapter 1 shows the military failure, tribe by tribe. Chapter 2 shows the spiritual failure behind it. Chapter 1 starts well, with Judah, and gets steadily worse as it moves north.

#### The Continued Conquest of Canaan (vv1–26)
- **"After the death of Joshua" (v1).** The same opening as Joshua 1:1, "After the death of Moses." But this time God names no successor. Joshua had left the work to the tribes and their elders, and the book will show what happens without a godly leader. JFB thinks the Canaanites quickly "seem to have taken advantage of that event."
- **"The people of Israel inquired of the LORD" (v1).** A good start. They probably asked through the high priest with the Urim and Thummim, the sacred lots kept in the breastpiece (Exod 28:30; Num 27:21). The same question is asked at the end of the book, when Israel goes to war against Benjamin: "Who shall go up first for us?" and again the answer is "Judah" (20:18). The two inquiries frame the book, the first against Canaanites and the last against fellow Israelites.
- **"Judah shall go up" (v2).** Judah was the largest tribe and had been given first place (Gen 49:8–10). JFB points out that "the land" here is Judah's own district, not the whole country. Judah's leadership looks ahead to David and to the Messiah, "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" (Rev 5:5).
- **"Judah said to Simeon his brother" (v3).** Judah and Simeon were full brothers, both sons of Leah (Gen 29:33–35), and Simeon's towns lay inside Judah's territory (Josh 19:1, 9). They agree to help each other take their land.
- **Adoni-bezek (vv4–7).** His name means "lord of Bezek." The site of Bezek in Judah is uncertain. A Bezek further north near Beth-shean is known (1 Sam 11:8), but it seems far from Judah's campaign, and JFB places this one south of Jerusalem. Cutting off thumbs and big toes was a known ancient way to humiliate a defeated ruler and make him useless as a warrior: he could no longer grip a sword or keep his footing.
- **"Seventy kings… used to pick up scraps under my table" (v7).** Canaan was a land of city-states, and JFB notes that "every ruler of a city or large town was called a king." Seventy may be a round number for many. The man who humiliated others is paid back the same way, and he sees it himself: "As I have done, so God has repaid me." The law's principle of measure for measure (Exod 21:23–25) is at work. Israel did not take his life. He died later in Jerusalem.
- **"The men of Judah fought against Jerusalem and captured it" (v8).** Jerusalem sat on the border between Judah and Benjamin (Josh 15:8; 18:16). Judah captured and burned the city, but did not hold it. Verse 21 says the Jebusites still lived there, and Joshua 15:63 says Judah "could not drive out the Jebusites." Most interpreters think Judah raided the lower town but did not take the citadel, and the Jebusites returned. Jerusalem stayed Jebusite until David took it (2 Sam 5:6–9).
- **Hebron and Debir (vv10–15).** This repeats, almost word for word, the story told in Joshua 15:13–19. Judges retells it here to place it in order after Joshua's death, or simply to collect Judah's successes in one place. Sheshai, Ahiman and Talmai were the sons of Anak, the giant clan that terrified the spies more than forty-five years earlier (Num 13:22, 28–33). Debir's old name, Kiriath-sepher, means "town of the book" or "scroll town."
- **Othniel and Achsah (vv12–15).** Othniel, Caleb's nephew, will be the first judge (3:9–11), so the reader meets him early as a hero. Achsah shows the same boldness as her father. She asks for "a blessing," and the request is practical: land in the dry south, the Negeb, was useless without water. The meaning of the Hebrew word for "dismounted" is uncertain, but it is clear she came to her father directly, and he gave her both "the upper springs and the lower springs."
- **The Kenites (v16).** The family of Moses' father-in-law (Exod 2:16–21; Num 10:29–32) had followed Israel through the wilderness. They leave "the city of palms," Jericho (Deut 34:3), and settle near Arad in the far south of Judah. One branch of the family will play a key role in chapter 4 (4:11, 17).
- **"Devoted it to destruction. So the name of the city was called Hormah" (v17).** Hormah means "devoted to destruction." The name echoes a vow Israel made in the wilderness (Num 21:1–3). Here Judah keeps the old rule of total destruction (*herem*).
- **Gaza, Ashkelon and Ekron (v18).** Three of the five cities that would later be Philistine strongholds (3:3; 1 Sam 6:17). The Greek Septuagint reads that Judah "did not" take them, which fits what follows: in Samson's day they are Philistine cities. If the Hebrew is right, as the ESV follows it, the conquest did not last. JFB says "the Philistines seem soon to have regained possession of these cities."
- **"The LORD was with Judah… but he could not drive out the inhabitants of the plain because they had chariots of iron" (v19).** Chariots with iron fittings were the tanks of the day, deadly on flat ground and useless in the hills. Judah took the hill country but stopped at the coastal plain. Yet the same God had already given Joshua victory over chariots (Josh 11:4–9; 17:16–18). JFB judges bluntly: "it was distrust, the want of a simple and firm reliance on the promise of God." Even the best tribe shows the first crack.
- **"Hebron was given to Caleb, as Moses had said" (v20).** Caleb at least finished his job. He drove out the Anakim who had frightened the nation a generation before (Josh 14:12).
- **"The people of Benjamin did not drive out the Jebusites" (v21).** The first "did not" in the chapter, and the pattern for everything that follows. The Tyndale notes observe that no mention is made here of the LORD being with Benjamin. "To this day" shows the writer lived before David took Jerusalem, or was quoting an older source.
- **"The house of Joseph also went up against Bethel, and the LORD was with them" (v22).** Bethel, old Luz, was where Jacob dreamed of the ladder (Gen 28:19). Joshua had defeated its king (Josh 12:16), but here the city itself is taken.
- **The man of Luz (vv23–26).** The spies promise him "kindness" (*hesed*), the word used for loyal, covenant faithfulness, if he shows them the way in. The story parallels Rahab (Josh 2), with a key difference. Rahab confessed faith in the LORD and joined Israel (Josh 2:9–11; 6:25). This man simply moved away, to "the land of the Hittites," probably north Syria, and rebuilt his old city under its old name. Canaan was not destroyed. It was just relocated. JFB thinks the promise depended on his "embracing the true religion, or quitting the country."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry approves the first inquiry, noting that Israel was right to "consult the breast-plate of judgment." He says Judah led as "the most numerous and powerful tribe," and that the alliance with Simeon teaches that "those that crave assistance must be ready to give assistance." On Adoni-bezek he writes, "See how changeable this world is, and how slippery its high places are." Guzik makes the same point as JFB about the iron chariots: the issue was trust, not technology. He takes Achsah's request, following Spurgeon, as a picture of prayer that is specific, grateful and bold. He also translates Adoni-bezek as "lord of lightning," but *bezek* is a place name here and the lexicons don't support that reading. "Lord of Bezek" is the plain sense.

#### Failure to Complete the Conquest (vv27–36)
- **A downward list.** The rest of the chapter is a roll call of failure, moving from south to north. Read carefully, it gets worse at each step. First the Canaanites live among Israel (vv27–30). Then Israel lives among the Canaanites (vv32–33), as if Israel were the minority. Finally Dan is pushed out of its land altogether (v34).
- **Manasseh (vv27–28).** Beth-shean, Taanach, Dor, Ibleam and Megiddo were a line of fortified cities across the Jezreel Valley and the coast. The Tyndale notes point out that holding them cut the northern tribes off from the central ones. "The Canaanites persisted in dwelling in that land." Megiddo guarded the main road between Egypt and Mesopotamia, and its name lives on in "Armageddon," the "mountain of Megiddo" (Rev 16:16).
- **"They put the Canaanites to forced labor, but did not drive them out completely" (v28).** The key sentence. Once Israel was strong enough to drive them out, it chose to profit from them instead. JFB says "avarice led the Israelites to spare the idolaters, contrary to the express command of God." Forced labor (*mas*) made the Canaanites useful, but it left their gods in the land (Deut 7:1–5; 20:16–18).
- **Ephraim (v29).** Gezer stayed Canaanite until Pharaoh took it and gave it to Solomon (1 Kings 9:16). Joshua 16:10 had already noted the problem.
- **Zebulun (v30).** The Canaanites "became subject to forced labor." Still under Israel's control, but still there.
- **Asher (vv31–32).** Seven towns, including the great Phoenician ports of Acco and Sidon. And now the wording flips: "the Asherites lived among the Canaanites." Henry says "Asher truckled worse than any of them to the Canaanites."
- **Naphtali (v33).** Again Israel lives "among the Canaanites." Beth-anath and Beth-shemesh mean "house of Anath" and "house of the sun," named after a Canaanite war goddess and the sun god. The names alone show what religion these towns kept.
- **Dan (vv34–35).** "The Amorites pressed the people of Dan back into the hill country." Dan could not even reach its allotted land on the coastal plain. Judges 18 tells how the tribe gave up and moved to the far north. The "house of Joseph" later put these Amorites to forced labor, but Dan never got its land.
- **"The border of the Amorites ran from the ascent of Akrabbim" (v36).** The chapter ends with the enemy's border, not Israel's. Akrabbim, "Scorpions' Ascent," was a pass southwest of the Dead Sea (Num 34:4). Sela means "rock," and its location here is uncertain.
- **Who is missing?** Issachar is not mentioned at all, nor are the eastern tribes. Simeon appears only alongside Judah. The list is not meant to be complete. It is meant to show a pattern.
- **Commentators:** Henry contrasts Judah's courage with the other tribes' cowardice: Judah's "hearts failed them" only "where there were chariots of iron," while "Benjamin failed," "Zebulun spared the Canaanites," and "Dan was straitened by the Amorites." Guzik applies the chapter to believers who make peace with sin instead of removing it, or even profit from it, as Israel did with forced labor. He traces the same slide from Canaanites living among Israel to Israel living among Canaanites, and notes that one compromise makes the next one easier. JFB names greed as the motive and calls it "contrary to the express command of God." The Tyndale notes add that this complacency was itself rebellion, and the next chapter makes that charge in God's name.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judges begins with Israel asking who will lead them, and the answer is Judah. The book will end with the cry "there was no king in Israel" (21:25). The promise to Judah, "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10), stands over the whole book, waiting for a king who will do what the tribes could not. Jesus, the Lion of Judah, does not make peace with the enemy or keep it around for profit. He came "to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8), and he finishes the conquest: "he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet" (1 Cor 15:25).
