*Setting:* The debate with the three friends is over. Bildad's six verses (ch 25) were the friends' last words, and Zophar never took his third turn. Job answered with his hymn to God's power (ch 26), held to his innocence under oath (ch 27), and the poem on wisdom followed (ch 28). Then Job "again took up his discourse" (29:1) for his final speech, chapters 29–31, spoken no longer to the friends but toward God. Chapter 29 looked back with longing to the days "when God watched over me, when his lamp shone upon my head" (29:2–3). Job sat in the city gate, the young men stepped aside, the old men stood, and princes fell silent (29:7–10). He rescued "the poor who cried for help," was "eyes to the blind and feet to the lame," and expected to "die in my nest" after a long life (29:12, 15, 18). Chapter 30 opens with "But now," and almost every line answers something in chapter 29. The honored elder has become a joke to outcasts, God's friendship feels like cruelty, and the man who comforted mourners now plays only mourning songs. The ESV adds no new heading in chapter 30. It continues under "Job's Summary Defense" from 29:1, and the chapter falls into four stanzas (vv1, 9, 16, 24).

#### Job's Summary Defense (vv1–8)
- **"But now they laugh at me" (v1).** "Laugh" is *sachaq*. The lexicon gives its usual sense as laughing at someone in contempt and lists this verse there. But the same verb stood in 29:24, where the lexicon reads it as a friendly laugh: "I smiled on them when they had no confidence." Job used to smile on people to give them courage. Now people laugh at him to shame him. Matthew Henry calls it "a melancholy 'But now'" and says the height of Job's former honor "did but increase the depth of this." "And now" returns at v9 and v16, so the whole chapter is built on then and now.
- **"Men who are younger than I" (v1).** In 29:8 "the young men saw me and withdrew." Now the young mock him. Respect for the old ran deep (Lev 19:32), so this was a double humiliation. JFB: "Orientals feel keenly any indignity shown by the young."
- **"Whose fathers I would have disdained to set with the dogs of my flock" (v1).** Job would not have hired their fathers even for the lowest work on his farms, below the sheepdogs. Dogs in Israel were half-wild scavengers, and the word was an insult (1 Sam 17:43; 2 Sam 16:9). Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's view that this is an expression of total contempt. It is a hard line. The man who was "a father to the needy" (29:16) speaks with real scorn here. He is not describing the poor in general, whom he defends in 31:16–22, but men he regards as worthless and lawless. Still, the bitterness of humiliation shows, and the book does not present every word Job says as a model.
- **"What could I gain from the strength of their hands, men whose vigor is gone?" (v2).** Hunger has drained them, so they are useless to anyone. JFB reads Job as saying that all he needs now is sympathy, which these men cannot give. The Tyndale notes draw out the reversal: the most important people once honored Job (29:11), and now the least important mock him (compare Ps 35:15).
- **"They gnaw the dry ground by night in waste and desolation" (v3).** These are people living on the edge of the desert, outside any town or tribe. JFB thinks of the roughest kind of Bedouin. Henry bluntly blames their idleness: "This beggarly world is full of the devil's poor." The text describes their poverty, not its cause.
- **"They pick saltwort... and the roots of the broom tree for their food" (v4).** "Saltwort" is *malluach*, found only here. The lexicon defines it as "mallow," a plant of the salt marshes. The broom tree (*rotem*) is the desert shrub Elijah sat under when he asked to die (1 Kgs 19:4). Its roots are bitter, though they make good charcoal (Ps 120:4). The ESV footnote offers "warmth" for "food," since the Hebrew can be read either way: the roots may have been dug for fuel.
- **"They are driven out from human company" (vv5–6).** Villages chased them off "as after a thief," and they lived "in the gullies of the torrents," the wadis that flood in the rainy season, and in caves. The Tyndale notes point out that God's people sometimes ended up in such places too: Israel hiding from Midian (Judg 6:2), David in the cave of Adullam with the debtors and the discontented (1 Sam 22:1–2), Elijah by the brook Cherith (1 Kgs 17:3). The outcast's life was not always a sign of wickedness.
- **"Among the bushes they bray" (v7).** "Bray" is the cry of the wild donkey (6:5). Job describes them almost as animals, a detail that comes back on him in v29.
- **"A senseless, a nameless brood, they have been whipped out of the land" (v8).** "Senseless" is the moral fool who has no regard for God (Ps 14:1). "Nameless" means without family or standing. JFB: a "nameless, low-born rabble." In a world where a good name was everything (Prov 22:1), these men had none, and now they look down on Job.
- **Commentators:** All three read vv1–8 as a portrait of society's outcasts, drawn to show how low Job has fallen. Guzik passes on Elmer Smick's point that the long description is a deliberate device: the more worthless the mockers, the deeper Job's shame. None of them pauses over the harshness of Job's own scorn in v1, which modern readers often notice.

#### Job's Summary Defense (vv9–15)
- **"And now I have become their song; I am a byword to them" (v9).** "Song" is *neginah*, usually music on strings. The lexicon lists this verse under a mocking song, beside "the drunkards make songs about me" (Ps 69:12) and Lamentations 3:14. The man whose word once "dropped upon them" like rain (29:22) is now their joke. Job said the same in 17:6: "He has made me a byword of the peoples."
- **"They do not hesitate to spit at the sight of me" (v10).** Spitting in the face was the deepest public insult (Num 12:14; Deut 25:9). The Tyndale notes and the cross-references point to the Servant of the Lord, "I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting" (Isa 50:6), and to Jesus' trial, where "they spit in his face and struck him" (Matt 26:67).
- **"Because God has loosed my cord and humbled me" (v11).** The Hebrew says only "he," and the ESV supplies "God," as JFB does. "Cord" is *yether*. The lexicon lists "cord" among its senses, used of a tent-cord (4:21) and a bowstring (Ps 11:2). As a bowstring, the line answers 29:20, "my bow ever new in my hand." JFB takes it so: an "image from a bow unstrung," leaving Job without strength. As a tent-cord, God has pulled down Job's home and life. Either way, Job sees God behind it all.
- **"They have cast off restraint in my presence" (v11).** The footnote gives the Hebrew as "the bridle." Once God humbled Job, the mob felt free. Henry compares David bearing Shimei's curses (2 Sam 16:11) and observes: "Those that to-day cry Hosannah may to-morrow cry Crucify."
- **"On my right hand the rabble rise" (v12).** "Rabble" is *pirchach*, found only here, which the lexicon defines as "brood," the wretched crowd. The right hand was where an accuser stood: Zechariah saw "Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him" (Zech 3:1; Ps 109:6). JFB: "To rise on the right hand is to accuse."
- **"They cast up against me their ways of destruction" (vv12–14).** Now the language is a siege. The Tyndale notes explain the phrase as a siege ramp, a slope of earth and stones piled against a city wall. Job used the same image of God's attack: "they have cast up their siege ramp against me" (19:12). The mob breaks up the road so no relief can arrive (v13), then pours in "as through a wide breach" (v14). JFB reads "they need no one to help them" as a saying about contemptible people: even the lowest can attack a man who is down.
- **"Terrors are turned upon me" (v15).** "Terrors" is *ballahot*, the word Bildad used for the fate of the wicked: "Terrors frighten him on every side," and he is brought "to the king of terrors" (18:11, 14). Job is living exactly what Bildad said happens to the wicked, which is why the friends' theology wounds him so.
- **"My honor is pursued as by the wind, and my prosperity has passed away like a cloud" (v15).** "Honor" is *nedibah*, which the lexicon defines as "nobility" of rank, used in this sense only here. Job's standing has blown away like a cloud that promised rain and gave none (compare 29:23).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the section as a lesson in the fickleness of crowds and sees Job pointing ahead to Christ, who was likewise despised by the people. JFB also links v9 with Christ's sufferings. JFB is most useful on detail: the unstrung bow (v11), the accuser's position (v12) and the breach in the wall (v14).

#### Job's Summary Defense (vv16–23)
- **"And now my soul is poured out within me" (v16).** The third "now" turns from the mockers to Job's body and soul. A poured-out soul is a life draining away like spilled water (Ps 22:14). Job expected his days to multiply "as the sand" (29:18). Instead "days of affliction have taken hold of me."
- **"The night racks my bones, and the pain that gnaws me takes no rest" (v17).** The pain is worst at night (7:3–4). JFB renders "night pierceth my bones" and wonders about elephantiasis, but the text never names the disease. Guzik passes on Francis Andersen's picture of it as a monster chewing on Job's flesh.
- **"With great force my garment is disfigured" (v18).** The Hebrew is hard, and translations differ. The ESV has the disease twisting Job's clothing out of shape and clinging like a tight collar. JFB notes the inner tunic that grips the neck. The Tyndale notes, and Guzik with the New Living Translation, read God himself seizing Job by the collar and throwing him down, which leads into v19. Either way it reverses 29:14: "I put on righteousness, and it clothed me." Job's clothing was once his dignity. Now it shows his ruin.
- **"God has cast me into the mire, and I have become like dust and ashes" (v19).** Again the Hebrew has only "He." Abraham called himself "dust and ashes" when he dared to speak to God (Gen 18:27). Job sat among the ashes at the start (2:8), and after God speaks he will "repent in dust and ashes" (42:6), a link the Tyndale notes also make. Here the phrase is humiliation forced on him. At the end it will be humility freely chosen.
- **"I cry to you for help and you do not answer me" (v20).** Job now speaks straight to God. "Cry for help" is *shava*, the same verb as 29:12, where Job "delivered the poor who cried for help." When the poor cried to Job, he answered. When Job cries to God, God does not. The cross-references pair the verse with "O my God, I cry by day, but you do not answer" (Ps 22:2) and Psalm 88:14. The Tyndale notes list Job's earlier complaints of God's silence (9:16; 19:7; 23:3–9) and note that God will answer (38:1).
- **"I stand, and you only look at me" (v20).** JFB: standing is "the reverential attitude of a suppliant before a king." Job stands before the throne, and the King only stares. In 29:2 God "watched over" him with care.
- **"You have turned cruel to me" (v21).** "Cruel" is *akzar*. The lexicon lists only four uses: snake venom (Deut 32:33), the ostrich that abandons her young (Lam 4:3), the one fierce enough to rouse Leviathan (41:10), and here, of God. It is one of Job's boldest lines. Henry's concise commentary names the sin that most beset Job at this point: "Harbouring hard thoughts of God." G. Campbell Morgan, through Guzik, says the protest comes close to revolt. JFB has no comment on v21. Yet God's verdict at the end is that Job spoke rightly where the friends did not (42:7). Lament that brings even this to God is not the same as turning from him.
- **"You toss me about in the roar of the storm" (v22).** Job feels like chaff in a gale (21:18; Ps 1:4), as the Tyndale notes observe. There is irony here. God will in fact come to Job "out of the whirlwind" (38:1), but that storm will bring speech, not silence.
- **"You will bring me to death and to the house appointed for all living" (v23).** "Appointed" is from *mo'ed*, which the lexicon defines as an appointed time or place, a meeting. JFB draws a point from it: since Job here expects death, not rescue in this life, his hope in 19:25, "I know that my Redeemer lives," must have looked beyond the grave. Guzik passes on Charles Spurgeon's reminder that Job did not die but was restored twice over, though he had counted himself a dead man.
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline sorts the section by Job's losses: health (vv17–19), communion with God (vv20–22) and hope of long life (v23), and he finds one relief, that death would end his troubles. Guzik passes on Mason's reflection that God blesses those who refuse to let go of him, even in complaint, and Smick's contrast between God's present might against Job and the friendship of 29:4. JFB alone uses v23 to explain 19:25.

#### Job's Summary Defense (vv24–31)
- **"Yet does not one in a heap of ruins stretch out his hand, and in his disaster cry for help?" (v24).** The ESV footnote says the meaning of the Hebrew is uncertain, and no textual variant is catalogued: the difficulty is in the Hebrew itself. The ESV reading is natural: anyone whose world collapses reaches out for help, so surely Job may. The King James reads "he will not stretch out his hand to the grave," and Henry, following it, finds comfort that God's hand will not pursue Job beyond death. JFB discusses both and leans toward a statement of faith about life after death.
- **"Did not I weep for him whose day was hard?" (v25).** This is the heart of Job's case. In his good days he did not just give to the poor. He wept with them (Rom 12:15). Henry names this as Job's second comfort: his conscience witnessed that in prosperity he had felt for those in misery. The friends accused him of the opposite (22:6–9), and chapter 31 lays out the evidence (31:16–22).
- **"When I hoped for good, evil came, and when I waited for light, darkness came" (v26).** Job once "walked through darkness" by God's light (29:3), and people "waited for" him "as for the rain" (29:23). Now he waits, and no light comes. Jeremiah's people said the same: "We looked for peace, but no good came" (Jer 8:15). The friends' rule said a righteous life leads to good. Job lived the life and received the opposite.
- **"My inward parts are in turmoil and never still" (v27).** The Hebrew speaks of the inner organs, where deep emotion was felt. Henry, with the King James, says Job's "bowels boiled with grief, and rested not."
- **"I go about darkened, but not by the sun" (v28).** "Darkened" is *qoder*. The lexicon lists this verse under mourning, the neglected look of a mourner, as in "all the day I go about mourning" (Ps 38:6). Job's skin is dark, not from the sun but from grief and disease, as JFB also reads it.
- **"I stand up in the assembly and cry for help" (v28).** Perhaps the saddest reversal. In 29:7 Job "prepared my seat in the square," and the elders rose for him. Now he stands in the same assembly, not to judge but to beg. "Cry for help" is *shava* again, the cry of the poor in 29:12.
- **"I am a brother of jackals and a companion of ostriches" (v29).** "Jackals" is *tannim*, plural of *tan*, which the lexicon describes as "howling mournfully in waste places." The King James and Henry read "dragons," as if from *tannin*, sea monster. The lexicon takes the ostrich here as a symbol of loneliness. Both creatures haunt ruins and cry eerily at night (Isa 13:21–22), and Micah pairs them in lament: "I will make lamentation like the jackals, and mourning like the ostriches" (Mic 1:8). JFB: both "utter dismal screams." Guzik passes on Clarke's note on the ostrich's mournful cry. Job described the outcasts braying in the bushes (v7). Now his own company is the desert animals. He has been driven further out than the men who mock him.
- **"My skin turns black and falls from me, and my bones burn with heat" (v30).** This is the disease of 2:7: blackened, peeling skin and fever. The Tyndale notes add that dark skin marked hardship, since only the comfortable could stay out of sun and wind. Compare Lamentations 4:8: "Now their face is blacker than soot."
- **"My lyre is turned to mourning, and my pipe to the voice of those who weep" (v31).** Lyre (*kinnor*) and pipe (*uggab*) are the two instruments credited to Jubal (Gen 4:21), the basic music of ancient life. Job complained that the wicked "rejoice to the sound of the pipe" (21:12), while his own plays only funeral songs: "our dancing has been turned to mourning" (Lam 5:15). The chapter ends opposite 29:25, where Job was "like one who comforts mourners." Now he is the mourner, and no one comforts him. JFB: these instruments are "properly appropriated to joy."
- **Commentators:** Henry and JFB follow the King James in v24 and find comfort there, while the ESV hears a plea to be allowed to cry for help. The Hebrew is uncertain, as the footnote admits. Guzik passes on Steven Lawson's point that Job takes God's silence as indifference and so as displeasure, and Morgan's that Job's deepest grief was not disease or mockery but that God gave no answer. On v29 the lexicon supports the ESV's jackals and ostriches over Henry's King James "dragons."

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 30 shows a righteous man brought down to the lowest place: mocked in songs, spat on, surrounded by the rabble, crying to God with no answer. The New Testament never calls Job a prophecy of Christ, but the likeness is hard to miss, and Henry and JFB both saw it. Jesus "was despised and rejected by men" (Isa 53:3). At his trial "they spit in his face" (Matt 26:67), and at the cross the crowd mocked him (Matt 27:39–44). He too cried, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). But there is a difference. Job suffered without knowing why. Jesus went into the dark knowing why, for the sake of others, and the Father answered him: "he was heard because of his reverence" (Heb 5:7), and he was raised. So the believer who stands like Job, crying out and hearing nothing, does not stand alone. A Savior who knows that cry now stands at the Father's right hand, not to accuse but to plead for his people (Rom 8:34). The lyre turned to mourning is not the end of the song. "You have turned for me my mourning into dancing" (Ps 30:11) is the promise Christ's resurrection secures, and Job himself would live to see a small picture of it (42:10–17).
