*Setting:* It is about 603 BC, the second year of Nebuchadnezzar's reign. He has just inherited an empire stretching from the Persian Gulf to the borders of Egypt. Daniel and his three friends are young men in their late teens, newly trained in Babylon's court learning (ch 1). Babylon took dreams seriously. Its scholars kept long manuals of dream omens, and a king's dream could be read as a message from the gods about his reign. Nebuchadnezzar has a dream that terrifies him, and his experts cannot help. The chapter that follows is one of the great passages of the Old Testament: a dream of a towering statue made of four metals, smashed by a stone that becomes a mountain. It lays out the shape of history from Babylon to the coming of God's kingdom, and it sets the theme of the whole book: earthly empires rise and fall, but "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed" (v44).

#### Nebuchadnezzar's Dream (vv1–16)
- **"In the second year of the reign of Nebuchadnezzar" (v1).** This seems to clash with the three years of training in 1:5. But by the Babylonian method of counting (see 1:1), the king's accession year was not counted, so his "second year" could be the third calendar year of the young men's training. JFB and Guzik both offer explanations along these lines. **"His spirit was troubled, and his sleep left him."** Pharaoh was troubled by his dreams the same way (Gen 41:8).
- **"The magicians, the enchanters, the sorcerers, and the Chaldeans" (v2).** These were Babylon's experts in the hidden world: priests who read omens, chanted spells and studied the stars. "Chaldeans" here is a professional title, the learned class of astrologer-priests, rather than the whole nation.
- **"The Chaldeans said to the king in Aramaic" (v4).** Here the book suddenly switches language. From this point until the end of chapter 7, Daniel is written in Aramaic, the international language of the Near East, not in Hebrew. The words "in Aramaic" may have been a note marking the change. Scholars debate why the book has two languages. One likely reason is that chapters 2–7 concern the nations of the world, and are written in the world's common language.
- **"O king, live forever!" (v4).** The standard court greeting (3:9; 5:10; 6:21). Its irony runs through the book: no king lives forever, but God's kingdom does.
- **"The word from me is firm" (v5).** The Aramaic word ('*azda*) is a Persian loanword. The KJV rendered it "the thing is gone from me," meaning the king had forgotten the dream, and older commentators like JFB mention that reading. Most modern scholars take it as "the word is certain" or "publicly declared": the king's decree stands. He has not forgotten. He is testing them.
- **"You shall be torn limb from limb, and your houses shall be laid in ruins" (v5).** Such punishments were real in the ancient world (compare 3:29; Ezra 6:11). Nebuchadnezzar had a reputation for cruelty (Jer 29:22: "whom the king of Babylon roasted in the fire").
- **"Tell me the dream, and I shall know that you can show me its interpretation" (v9).** The king's reasoning is sharp. Anyone can invent a meaning for a dream once told it. If they truly had access to the gods, they could tell him the dream itself. The Tyndale notes explain that the wise men worked from reference books of dream omens. They needed the dream's contents to look up its meaning.
- **"No one can show it to the king except the gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh" (v11).** This is the turning point. The experts admit that their gods are distant and that they have no real line to them. Daniel will answer that there is a God in heaven who does reveal (v28). And the book of John will later announce that God's dwelling *has* come to be with flesh: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14).
- **"The king was angry and very furious, and commanded that all the wise men of Babylon be destroyed" (v12).** Daniel and his friends were not even consulted, but as members of the wise men's class they were included in the death sentence (v13).
- **"Daniel replied with prudence and discretion to Arioch" (v14).** Arioch was "captain of the king's guard," literally "chief of the executioners" or "chief of the butchers," the same title as Potiphar's (Gen 39:1) and Nebuzaradan's (2 Kings 25:8). Daniel, facing a sword, asks calm questions (Prov 15:1).
- **"Daniel went in and requested the king to appoint him a time" (v16).** The king had refused to give the wise men more time (v8), but he gives it to Daniel. Daniel does not yet know the dream. He asks for time in faith that God will answer.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the wise men "confess that none but the gods can do it," which concedes the very thing Daniel will prove. Guzik suggests the king may only vaguely have remembered the dream, but mainly reads the demand as a test of whether his advisers could really reach hidden knowledge. JFB calls the Chaldeans "a certain order of priest-magicians," and notes that v4 begins the Chaldee, that is Aramaic, section of the book, the "vernacular tongue of the king and his court."

#### God Reveals Nebuchadnezzar's Dream (vv17–30)
- **"Daniel went to his house and made the matter known to Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah" (v17).** Daniel's first move is a prayer meeting. The friends are given their Hebrew names here, names that confess the God of Israel.
- **"Seek mercy from the God of heaven concerning this mystery" (v18).** "The God of heaven" is a title used often in the exile and after (Ezra 1:2; Neh 1:4). It speaks of the God over all nations, not a local deity. "Mystery" is *raz*, a Persian loanword meaning a secret, especially a secret about the future known only to God. The Greek translation renders it *mystērion*, the word Paul later uses for God's plan once hidden and now revealed in Christ (Rom 16:25–26; Eph 3:3–6).
- **"Then the mystery was revealed to Daniel in a vision of the night" (v19).** God answers that very night. **"Then Daniel blessed the God of heaven."** Before rushing to the king to save his life, he stops to give thanks.
- **"Blessed be the name of God forever and ever, to whom belong wisdom and might" (v20).** Daniel's psalm of praise (vv20–23) matches exactly what the crisis needed: wisdom to know the dream, and might to rule the kingdoms it describes.
- **"He changes times and seasons; he removes kings and sets up kings" (v21).** This is the theme of the whole book in one line. Babylonian astrologers thought the times were written in the stars. Daniel says God changes them. Nebuchadnezzar will learn this personally (4:17, 25, 32), and so will Belshazzar (5:21).
- **"He gives wisdom to the wise and knowledge to those who have understanding" (v21).** God gives to those already seeking. Compare James 1:5.
- **"He knows what is in the darkness, and the light dwells with him" (v22).** The dream came in the dark of night. Nothing hidden is dark to God (Ps 139:11–12).
- **"To you, O God of my fathers, I give thanks… for you have made known to us the king's matter" (v23).** Daniel praises the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, not a new god discovered in Babylon. And he says "us": the friends' prayers shared in the answer.
- **"Do not destroy the wise men of Babylon" (v24).** Daniel's first words to Arioch are a plea for the lives of the pagan experts. God's answer saves them too.
- **"I have found among the exiles from Judah a man" (v25).** Arioch takes the credit. Daniel had come to him first (v16). Court officials often worked this way.
- **"Are you able…?" (v26).** **"No wise men… can show to the king the mystery… but there is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries" (vv27–28).** Daniel's answer echoes Joseph's: "It is not in me; God will give Pharaoh a favorable answer" (Gen 41:16). He begins by clearing himself out of the way.
- **"What will be in the latter days" (v28).** "Latter days" (literally "the end of the days") is a phrase used of the future, often of the time when God's purposes come to completion (Gen 49:1; Isa 2:2; Hos 3:5). Here it covers the whole stretch of history from Nebuchadnezzar to the coming of God's kingdom.
- **"Thoughts of what would be after this" (v29).** The king had been lying awake wondering what would become of his empire after him. God answered the question he was actually asking.
- **"Not because of any wisdom that I have more than all the living" (v30).** Daniel insists that the gift is God's. The point is "that you may know the thoughts of your mind": God's purpose is for the king to understand.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Daniel "engaged them as partners with him in this matter," and on v30 that Daniel spoke so "that God alone may have all the praise." JFB calls v18 "an illustration of the power of united prayer," citing Matthew 18:19, and explains "times and seasons" as the periods of empires and the right moments for their rise and fall, governed by God rather than chance or fate. JFB takes "the latter days" to include "the Messianic days." Guzik also draws a lesson about praying friends, and stresses Daniel's thanks before he acted.

#### Daniel Interprets the Dream (vv31–45)
- **"Behold, a great image… mighty and of exceeding brightness" (v31).** "Image" (*tselem*) is the word used for idols and statues (3:1), and for the image of God in humanity (Gen 1:26, in Hebrew). Statues of kings and gods were common in Mesopotamia. This one stands for human power at its most impressive.
- **Gold, silver, bronze, iron, iron mixed with clay (vv32–33).** The metals decline in value from top to bottom but increase in hardness, until the feet end in a brittle mixture. The Greek poet Hesiod had described ages of humanity in gold, silver, bronze and iron, and Persian texts used a similar scheme, so the idea of declining metal ages was known in the ancient world. God uses a picture the king could understand.
- **"A stone was cut out by no human hand" (v34).** In contrast to the statue, which human craftsmen made, the stone has no human origin at all. It strikes the image at its weakest point, the feet.
- **"Became like the chaff of the summer threshing floors" (v35).** At harvest, grain was tossed in the air on hilltop threshing floors so the wind would carry away the husks. The whole empire, gold and all, blows away "so that not a trace of them could be found" (compare Ps 1:4; Isa 41:15–16). **"The stone… became a great mountain and filled the whole earth."** Mountains in the Bible are often the place of God's rule, and Isaiah foresaw "the mountain of the house of the LORD" lifted above all (Isa 2:2).
- **"You, O king, the king of kings, to whom the God of heaven has given the kingdom" (v37).** "King of kings" was a real Mesopotamian royal title. Daniel grants it but adds that God gave it. **"You are the head of gold" (v38).** Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar was famous for gold: its temples and gates were covered in it, and Herodotus describes a huge golden statue there. Jeremiah calls Babylon "a golden cup in the LORD's hand" (Jer 51:7).
- **"Another kingdom inferior to you… a third kingdom of bronze" (v39).** Only the first kingdom is named in this chapter. The book itself names the next ones later: Babylon falls to "the Medes and Persians" (5:28; 8:20), and after them comes "the king of Greece" (8:21).
- **"A fourth kingdom, strong as iron… it shall break and crush all these" (v40).** Iron crushes and shatters. This empire will be harsher and stronger than any before it.
- **Two main readings of the four kingdoms.** The traditional view, held by Jewish and Christian interpreters from early times, including Josephus, Jerome, Henry, JFB and Guzik, reads them as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome. It fits the book's own pairing of Media and Persia as one power (5:28; 8:20), and Jesus spoke of Daniel's prophecy as still to be fulfilled in his day (Matt 24:15). Many modern scholars, who date the book to the 160s BC, read them as Babylon, Media, Persia and Greece, with the fourth kingdom ending in the Greek kings who persecuted the Jews in the second century BC. On this view the iron-and-clay marriages are the alliances between Greek dynasties described in 11:6 and 11:17. Both readings agree on the main point: God's kingdom replaces every human empire.
- **"It shall be a divided kingdom… partly strong and partly brittle" (vv41–42).** The clay is "potter's clay," fired earthenware, hard but fragile. The fourth empire has iron's strength but also deep internal weakness.
- **"They will mix with one another in marriage, but they will not hold together" (v43).** Literally "they will mix with the seed of men." Royal marriages were a standard way to seal alliances, and they often failed. Some who hold the Roman view, including Guzik, see the ten toes as a future ten-part form of Rome at the end of the age (compare the ten horns in 7:7, 24). Others read the mixture as the many peoples Rome absorbed without ever truly uniting them, or as the Greek dynastic marriages.
- **"In the days of those kings the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed" (v44).** This is the climax. God's kingdom is different in every way: not made by human hands, not passed to "another people," and lasting "forever." It does not merely survive the empires. It "shall break in pieces all these kingdoms."
- **When does the stone strike?** Christians agree the stone is Christ and his kingdom (compare Ps 118:22; Isa 28:16). Jesus himself alluded to this verse: "Everyone who falls on that stone will be broken to pieces, and when it falls on anyone, it will crush him" (Luke 20:18). Many interpreters, including Henry and many Reformed readers, see the kingdom arriving in Jesus' first coming, during the days of Rome, and growing like the stone into a mountain as the gospel fills the earth (Matt 13:31–33). Premillennial readers, including Guzik and in part JFB, stress that the stone *destroys* the image in one blow, which they see happening at Christ's second coming. Many hold both: the kingdom has come in Jesus and will come in fullness when he returns.
- **"A stone was cut from a mountain by no human hand" (v45).** The stone comes *from* a mountain and *becomes* a mountain (v35): it comes from God's realm and fills the earth. **"The dream is certain, and its interpretation sure."** Daniel ends with confidence.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes plainly, "The head of gold signified the Chaldean monarchy," and "The stone cut out without hands represented the kingdom of Jesus Christ," which he sees growing from small beginnings to fill the world. JFB, following the Roman view, says the iron "sets forth the character of the Roman power, rather than its territorial extent," and reads "without hands" as pointing to the Messiah "not created by human agency, but conceived by the Holy Ghost." JFB sees the stone's blow as a judgment at Christ's return, with the ten toes as Rome's final divided state. Guzik identifies the same four empires, gives rough lengths for each, rejects the reading of the stone as the church, and expects a ten-part revival of Rome at the end of the age.

#### Daniel Is Promoted (vv46–49)
- **"Nebuchadnezzar fell upon his face and paid homage to Daniel, and commanded that an offering and incense be offered up to him" (v46).** The most powerful man on earth falls flat before a teenage captive. The Aramaic verb (*segid*) can mean worship or simply homage. The text does not say whether Daniel accepted or refused it, and it never says the offering was made. Peter and Paul refused such honors outright (Acts 10:25–26; 14:13–15). JFB suggests the king meant to honor Daniel's God through Daniel.
- **"Truly, your God is God of gods and Lord of kings, and a revealer of mysteries" (v47).** This is a real confession, but a pagan one: the king adds Daniel's God at the top of his list, without giving up his other gods. Chapter 3 shows how shallow it was. "Lord of kings" picks up Daniel's point in v21: God sets up and removes kings.
- **"Ruler over the whole province of Babylon and chief prefect over all the wise men" (v48).** Like Joseph, Daniel rises from captive to second-in-command (Gen 41:39–44). He is now over the very experts whose lives he had saved.
- **"Daniel made a request… and he appointed Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego over the affairs of the province" (v49).** Daniel does not forget the friends who prayed with him. **"But Daniel remained at the king's court."** Literally "in the king's gate," where officials sat. This sets up chapter 3, where the three friends face a test without Daniel present.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes simply that the king "fell upon his face and worshipped Daniel," treating it as honor meant for God that Daniel would not have welcomed for himself. JFB thinks the king's worship was directed at God through Daniel, suggests Daniel declined it, and observes that the command for offerings is never said to have been carried out. Guzik points out that a king who honors Daniel's God here will set up a golden image in the very next chapter, a reminder that being impressed by God is not the same as worshiping him alone.

#### Pointing to Christ
Nebuchadnezzar dreamed of the future of his empire and saw every human kingdom, however golden, blown away like chaff. What remains is a stone "cut out by no human hand" that becomes a mountain filling the earth. Jesus came in the days of the fourth kingdom, announcing that "the kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:15), and he called himself the stone the builders rejected (Mark 12:10). His kingdom did not begin with armies but with a manger and a cross, and it has been growing ever since. Revelation hears its last announcement: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever" (Rev 11:15).
