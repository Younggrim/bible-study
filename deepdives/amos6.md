*Setting:* Amos, a sheep-breeder from Judah, is preaching in Israel's northern kingdom around 760 BC. Under Jeroboam II, Israel had recovered its old borders "from Lebo-hamath as far as the Sea of the Arabah" (2 Kings 14:25), and Judah was strong under Uzziah. Both capitals felt untouchable. Chapter 5 ended with the "day of the LORD" turned to darkness, worship God hates, and the threat of exile "beyond Damascus." Chapter 6 is a second "woe" (5:18; 6:1, 4), aimed at the people at the very top: the leaders of Samaria, and even of Jerusalem, lounging at their banquets while the nation rots. Henry calls them "a sinful people studying to put a slight upon God's threatenings." The chapter is about complacency, luxury and pride, and how God answers them.

#### "Woe to Those at Ease in Zion" (vv1–14)
- **"Woe to those who are at ease in Zion" (v1).** "Woe" (*hoy*) was the cry of mourning at a funeral (1 Kings 13:30). Amos is lamenting people who think they are doing fine. "At ease" (*sha'anan*) means carefree, undisturbed, complacent. The word can be good, of a peaceful home (Isa 33:20), but here it means careless security (Isa 32:9, 11). Guzik distinguishes godly rest from this kind of fleshly security, which he says shows itself in laziness, indulgence and indifference to others.
- **Zion as well as Samaria (v1).** This is surprising, since Amos preaches mainly to the north. The Tyndale notes say it shows God plays no favorites: Jerusalem's leaders are just as guilty. Both cities were hilltop fortresses that seemed impossible to capture. "Feel secure" (*botchim*) is the word for trust. They trusted the mountain, not the LORD.
- **"The notable men of the first of the nations, to whom the house of Israel comes!" (v1).** "Notable" means "marked out," the men with names, the elite. Israel thought itself "the first of the nations," the chief people. Ordinary Israelites came to these leaders for judgment and help. The word "first" (*re'shit*) will return twice with a bite: they anoint themselves with the "first" (finest) oils (v6), and they will be the "first" into exile (v7).
- **"Pass over to Calneh… Hamath the great… Gath of the Philistines" (v2).** Three cities to the north and southwest. Calneh (probably Kullani in northern Syria) and Hamath (on the Orontes River in Syria, modern Hama) were city-states that Jeroboam had dominated (2 Kings 14:28). Gath had been broken by Hazael and then Uzziah (2 Kings 12:17; 2 Chr 26:6). **"Are you better than these kingdoms?"** The point is either that these cities had already fallen, so Israel should not think itself safe, or that God had made Israel greater than them, so its ingratitude was worse. Guzik takes the first view. The Tyndale notes add that Calneh fell to Assyria in 738 BC, Hamath paid tribute soon after, and Gath fell in 711. Israel itself fell in 722.
- **"O you who put far away the day of disaster and bring near the seat of violence" (v3).** They push the thought of judgment out of their minds ("it won't happen in our time"), while bringing violence ever closer by their own behavior. "Seat of violence" may mean a reign of violence, the throne of oppression in their courts, or the violence of the invader. The Tyndale notes give both. JFB quotes Calvin: "Ye bring on fever by your intemperance, and yet would put it far off."
- **"Woe to those who lie on beds of ivory" (v4).** The second woe. Beds and couches inlaid with ivory panels, as found in the ruins of Samaria (3:15). "Stretch themselves out" is literally "sprawl," lolling. Ordinary people sat on the floor or on simple mats to eat. The rich reclined, a new and foreign luxury in Israel.
- **"Eat lambs from the flock and calves from the midst of the stall" (v4).** Most Israelites ate meat only at festivals. The Tyndale notes say bread, fruit, vegetables and dairy were everyday food. These men ate the choicest young animals, "calves from the stall," fattened in a pen, as a matter of routine. JFB: the choicest animals taken for their own indulgence.
- **"Who sing idle songs to the sound of the harp and like David invent for themselves instruments of music" (v5).** "Idle songs" is a rare verb, perhaps "improvise" or "babble." David was famous for music and instruments in worship (1 Chr 23:5; 2 Chr 29:26–27). These men saw themselves as gifted like David, but JFB notes the difference: David's music served God, theirs served themselves.
- **"Who drink wine in bowls" (v6).** Not cups, but bowls. The word *mizraq* is the basin used for sprinkling blood at the altar (Exod 27:3; Num 7:13). The Tyndale notes say this adds a sense of sacrilege: they drink from sacred-sized vessels at their parties. **"And anoint themselves with the finest oils."** Perfumed oils for the skin, an expensive luxury. "Finest" is *re'shit*, "first," again.
- **The *marzeach* banquet.** Verse 7 calls this scene "the revelry," Hebrew *marzeach*. Texts from Ugarit and elsewhere describe the *marzeach* as a well-known kind of feast in the ancient Near East, a club for the wealthy with heavy drinking, sometimes linked to honoring the dead or to pagan gods. Jeremiah 16:5 uses the same word for a "house of mourning." Many scholars think Amos is describing exactly this kind of exclusive drinking club.
- **"But are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph!" (v6).** This is the heart of the charge. "Joseph" means the northern tribes (5:6, 15). The nation was being torn apart by injustice, and the leaders felt nothing. "Ruin" is literally "breaking," the fracture of the nation. JFB compares the brothers of the original Joseph, who sat down to eat while he was in the pit (Gen 37:25). The sin is not just luxury but indifference to the suffering of others.
- **"Therefore they shall now be the first of those who go into exile" (v7).** The "first of the nations" (v1) will be first into chains. Assyria regularly deported the ruling class first. JFB: since they were first in rank, they will be first into captivity. **"The revelry of those who stretch themselves out shall pass away."** The *marzeach* is over.
- **"The Lord GOD has sworn by himself" (v8).** Literally "by his life" (*nephesh*). The Tyndale notes call it the most solemn oath God could make (Gen 22:16; Heb 6:13). **"I abhor the pride of Jacob and hate his strongholds."** "Pride" (*ga'on*) can mean majesty or excellency in a good sense; here it means arrogance. JFB takes "excellency of Jacob" as the sanctuary and covenant privileges that Israel trusted. The Tyndale notes observe that "Jacob" recalls the cheat of Genesis 27:36. **"I will deliver up the city and all that is in it."** Samaria will be handed over.
- **"If ten men remain in one house, they shall die" (v9).** Now a short, haunting scene. War is often followed by plague, as JFB notes. Even a house with ten survivors will be wiped out. Amos had warned that a city of a thousand would be left with a hundred, and a hundred with ten (5:3). Now even the ten die.
- **"When one's relative, the one who anoints him for burial, shall take him up" (v10).** The Hebrew word (*mesarpho*) usually means "the one who burns him." Israelites normally buried their dead, so some take it as burning spices in honor of the dead (Jer 34:5; 2 Chr 16:14), and the ESV renders it "anoints him for burial." The Tyndale notes and JFB think it means actual burning of bodies, an emergency measure when too many corpses threatened disease. A relative comes to carry out "the bones."
- **"Is there still anyone with you?" He shall say, "No" (v10).** He calls into the back room of the house. One last survivor answers that there is no one else. **"Silence! We must not mention the name of the LORD."** "Silence" (*has*) is "Hush!" The fear is that calling on God's name, even in a casual oath or a prayer, might draw his attention and bring the judgment on them too. Guzik notes that they had become terrified of God's wrath. JFB reads it as there being no reason left to praise. The people who once said "the LORD is with us" (5:14) now dare not say his name.
- **"For behold, the LORD commands, and the great house shall be struck down into fragments, and the little house into bits" (v11).** Rich and poor will both suffer. "The great house" may also be the royal house, the dynasty. This may point to the earthquake (1:1). When God commands, every kind of house falls.
- **"Do horses run on rocks? Does one plow there with oxen?" (v12).** Two absurd questions. No one gallops horses over rocky crags or plows a cliff with oxen. Guzik calls them two proverbs about futile action. Many scholars divide the Hebrew letters slightly differently, giving "Does one plow *the sea* with oxen?", which the Tyndale notes mention. Both readings make the same point: some things go against nature.
- **"But you have turned justice into poison and the fruit of righteousness into wormwood" (v12).** Israel did something just as unnatural. Justice should heal; they made it poison (*rosh*, a bitter, poisonous plant). Righteousness should bear sweet fruit; they made it bitter, as in 5:7. JFB adds that the people were like a hard rock on which God's favor could not take hold.
- **"You who rejoice in Lo-debar, who say, 'Have we not by our own strength captured Karnaim for ourselves?'" (v13).** Lo-debar and Karnaim were towns east of the Jordan that Jeroboam II won back from Damascus (2 Kings 14:25–28). Amos turns both names into jokes. *Lo-debar* sounds like *lo' dabar*, "no thing" or "nothing." *Karnaim* means "pair of horns," and a horn was the symbol of strength (Deut 33:17; Ps 75:10). The Tyndale notes explain both wordplays. So: "You celebrate taking Nothing, and boast that by your own strength you took Horns." JFB also reads it as "a thing of naught." Lo-debar is the town where Mephibosheth was living when David sent for him (2 Sam 9:4).
- **"By our own strength" (v13).** The heart of the boast. Israel credited its victories to itself, not to God, who had given them as an undeserved mercy (2 Kings 14:26–27). Moses had warned against exactly this: "My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth" (Deut 8:17).
- **"I will raise up against you a nation… and they shall oppress you from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of the Arabah" (v14).** The nation is unnamed, but it was Assyria. Lebo-hamath, "the entrance of Hamath," was Israel's ideal northern border, and the Brook (or Sea) of the Arabah its southern border. These are the exact limits of Jeroboam's conquests in 2 Kings 14:25. The Tyndale notes point out the irony: the very land Israel boasted of winning would be the land the enemy oppressed. JFB notes the same boundaries once marked the crowds at Solomon's great feast (1 Kings 8:65). The borders of Israel's glory became the borders of its ruin.
- **Commentators:** Henry divides the chapter into the people's complacency, their trust in privilege and their own strength, their luxury, and then the severity of the coming judgment. JFB explains the mixing bowls, the oils and the "excellency of Jacob," and compares the leaders' indifference to Joseph's brothers. Guzik focuses on "ease in Zion" as fleshly security and connects the chapter with the principle that God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble (1 Pet 5:5). He does not explain the names Lo-debar and Karnaim, but the Tyndale notes do, and the wordplay is widely accepted. The Tyndale notes also bring out the sacrilege of drinking from altar bowls.

#### Pointing to Christ
The leaders of Samaria lay on ivory beds, drank from altar bowls and "were not grieved over the ruin of Joseph." Jesus did the opposite. Though he was rich, he became poor (2 Cor 8:9). He wept over Jerusalem's coming ruin (Luke 19:41). At his last meal he took a cup and gave it for others, "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (Matt 26:28). The rich man in his parable feasted every day while poor Lazarus lay at his gate, and the end of that story is the end of this chapter (Luke 16:19–25). Jesus calls his people out of careless ease and into grief for others' brokenness and practical love for them, because he first grieved over ours and was broken for it.
