*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. After nine chapters of genealogies, the story begins, and it begins with an ending. The Chronicler does not retell Saul's rise, his early victories, his jealous pursuit of David or his visit to the medium at Endor. His readers knew all that from 1 Samuel. He tells only Saul's last day, the battle of Mount Gilboa around 1010 BC, following 1 Samuel 31 almost word for word, and then adds his own verdict on why it happened. Mount Gilboa is a ridge on the southeast edge of the Jezreel Valley, the great plain that cuts across northern Israel. The Philistines, Israel's enemies on the coast with better iron weapons, had marched far inland to this valley, cutting the northern tribes off from Saul's base in Benjamin. The night before, Saul had sought out a medium because God would not answer him (1 Sam 28). The chapter is short, but it is the doorway to everything that follows. Saul's failure explains why the kingdom passed to David, and the rest of 1 Chronicles is about David.

#### The Death of Saul and His Sons (vv1–14)
- **"The men of Israel fled before the Philistines and fell slain on Mount Gilboa" (v1).** The chapter starts in the middle of the rout, with no introduction. JFB observes that it has no link to the preceding genealogies and retells the battle "almost in the very same words" as 1 Samuel 31. That abrupt start is itself a statement. The Chronicler is not interested in Saul's reign, only in its end.
- **"The Philistines struck down Jonathan and Abinadab and Malchi-shua" (v2).** Jonathan, David's closest friend, the man who had said, "You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you" (1 Sam 23:17), dies beside the father who had tried to kill him. He remained loyal to the end, and Guzik draws attention to that loyalty. David later mourned them both: "Saul and Jonathan, beloved and lovely! In life and in death they were not divided" (2 Sam 1:23).
- **"The archers found him, and he was wounded by the archers" (v3).** The Hebrew can also be read "he was terrified of the archers." JFB takes it that way and suggests Saul was not badly wounded when he decided to die, and that he acted out of "sudden terror and overwhelming depression of spirits." Guzik, following Adam Clarke, takes the other view: Saul was already dying. The ESV's "wounded" is the more common reading.
- **"Lest these uncircumcised come and mistreat me" (v4).** "Uncircumcised" was Israel's term of scorn for the Philistines, who did not share the sign of God's covenant (1 Sam 17:26). Saul feared torture and public humiliation. The Philistines had blinded and mocked Samson (Judg 16:21–25). "Mistreat" can mean to make sport of or abuse.
- **"But his armor-bearer would not, for he feared greatly" (v4).** He may have feared killing the LORD's anointed, as David twice refused to do (1 Sam 24:6; 26:9). JFB suggests respect for the king and dread of what a king's death would mean for the nation.
- **"Therefore Saul took his own sword and fell upon it" (v4).** Saul ended his own life. 2 Samuel 1:6–10 has an Amalekite claim that he finished Saul off at the king's request, but most readers take that as a lie told to win David's favor. Christians have long judged taking one's own life to be a serious sin, since life belongs to God. Guzik adds, with care, that it is not beyond God's forgiveness. The Chronicler does not comment on the act itself. He moves to the larger cause in v13.
- **"Thus Saul died; he and his three sons and all his house died together" (v6).** 1 Samuel 31:6 says "his armor-bearer, and all his men." The Chronicler says "all his house." That is not literally true: Ish-bosheth (Eshbaal, 8:33) survived and reigned briefly over the northern tribes (2 Sam 2:8–10), and Jonathan's son Mephibosheth lived on. JFB notes that both were probably left behind at home because of their youth. The Chronicler means that Saul's dynasty died that day. The Tyndale notes point out that by leaving out the armor-bearer and the troops here, the Chronicler focuses everything on God removing Saul's house and replacing it with David's (v14).
- **"They abandoned their cities and fled, and the Philistines came and lived in them" (v7).** When the king fell, the people fell apart. The Israelite towns of the Jezreel Valley emptied, and the enemy moved in. Guzik links this to Jesus' words, "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (Mark 14:27). A leader's sin harms more than himself.
- **"They stripped him and took his head and his armor" (v9).** The details differ slightly from 1 Samuel 31:9–10. Samuel tells us they fastened Saul's body to the wall of Beth-shean. The Chronicler leaves that out and adds what Samuel does not mention: the head.
- **"To carry the good news to their idols and to the people" (v9).** "Carry the good news" is the Hebrew verb *basar*, the same word Isaiah uses for the messenger who brings good news of God's salvation: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news" (Isa 52:7). Here it is pagan gospel. The Philistines believed their gods had won, and they announced it to the gods themselves.
- **"They put his armor in the temple of their gods and fastened his head in the temple of Dagon" (v10).** Dagon was the chief god of the Philistines, probably a god of grain. The irony runs deep. When the Philistines once captured the ark of the covenant and set it in Dagon's temple, the statue of Dagon fell on its face before the ark, and its head was broken off (1 Sam 5:1–5). Now the head of Israel's king hangs in Dagon's house. Years earlier young David had carried the giant Goliath's head into Jerusalem (1 Sam 17:54). Henry asks, "Shall Dagon have so honourable a share in their triumphs and the true and living God be forgotten in ours?"
- **"When all Jabesh-gilead heard" (v11).** Jabesh-gilead was a town across the Jordan. Saul's first act as king was to rescue it from the Ammonites, who had threatened to gouge out the right eye of every man in the town (1 Sam 11:1–11). The town never forgot.
- **"All the valiant men arose and took away the body of Saul and the bodies of his sons" (v12).** 1 Samuel 31:12 says they "went all night" to Beth-shean, more than ten miles, crossed the Jordan, and took the bodies down from the city wall. It was a dangerous raid into enemy-held territory, done out of loyalty. David later blessed them for it: "May the LORD show steadfast love and faithfulness to you" (2 Sam 2:5–6).
- **"Buried their bones under the oak in Jabesh and fasted seven days" (v12).** 1 Samuel 31:12–13 adds that they burned the bodies first and calls the tree a tamarisk. The Chronicler uses a more general word for a large tree. Seven days was a full time of mourning (Gen 50:10). Later David moved the bones of Saul and Jonathan to the family tomb in Benjamin (2 Sam 21:12–14).
- **"So Saul died for his breach of faith" (v13).** This is the Chronicler's own verdict, found nowhere in Samuel. "Breach of faith" is *maal* twice in one sentence: Saul "broke faith with the LORD." It is the same word that explained the exile of the eastern tribes (5:25) and of Judah itself (9:1). Saul's personal story is Israel's national story in small. Unfaithfulness leads to loss.
- **"He did not keep the command of the LORD" (v13).** The Chronicler likely has two events in mind. Saul offered sacrifice himself instead of waiting for Samuel (1 Sam 13:8–14), and he spared the Amalekite king and the best of the spoil when God had commanded total destruction (1 Sam 15). JFB points to the second. Samuel's words then were, "To obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22).
- **"And also consulted a medium, seeking guidance" (v13).** The night before the battle, Saul visited the medium at Endor and asked her to call up the dead Samuel (1 Sam 28:7–19). The Law forbade it outright (Lev 19:31; Deut 18:10–12), and Saul himself had driven mediums out of the land (1 Sam 28:3). There is a wordplay here. "Consulted" is the verb *shaal*, "to ask," and Saul's name, *Shaul*, means "asked for." Israel had "asked for" a king (1 Sam 8:10; 12:13), and the one they asked for asked a medium.
- **"He did not seek guidance from the LORD" (v14).** But 1 Samuel 28:6 says Saul "inquired of the LORD" and got no answer. The difference is in the verbs. Samuel uses *shaal*, "ask." The Chronicler uses *darash*, "seek," one of the key words of his whole book. He has already used it in v13: Saul went to the medium "seeking guidance," literally seeking (*darash*) there. He sought the medium and did not seek the LORD. To seek the LORD in Chronicles means to turn to him wholeheartedly, with trust and obedience (16:11; 28:9; 2 Chr 7:14; 15:2). Saul asked, once, in desperation. He never really sought. JFB calls his inquiry "a mere mockery," and Guzik, quoting Poole, makes the same point: a careless, last-minute question was not real seeking.
- **"Therefore the LORD put him to death" (v14).** Saul fell on his own sword, yet the Chronicler says the LORD put him to death. Both are true. Scripture often describes human actions and God's purpose in the same event (Gen 50:20; Acts 2:23). Saul's choices were his own, and they were also God's judgment.
- **"And turned the kingdom over to David the son of Jesse" (v14).** "Turned over" means to turn around or hand on. This is the hinge of the whole book. The kingdom was never Saul's to keep. It belonged to God, who had already chosen "a man after his own heart" (1 Sam 13:14). Here David is named for the first time in the story itself. The Chronicler skips the seven years of civil war that followed (2 Sam 2–4) and moves straight to David's anointing over all Israel in the next chapter.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the Chronicler "repeats, not the history of Saul's reign, but only of his death," because that was the way "made for David to the throne." On the abuse of Saul's body: "Advancement makes men a mark for malice." On the cause: "Disobedience is a killing thing." And on the visit to Endor, he calls consulting a medium a sin that "fills the measure of iniquity as soon as any thing." He also explains how Saul could kill himself and yet the LORD be said to kill him: God's purpose governs even the acts of wicked men. Guzik's application is searching: in a crisis, do we really seek God, or do we turn to substitutes that leave us more alone? He also notes that God always raises up "valiant men" like those of Jabesh. JFB and Guzik differ on v3, as noted, over whether Saul was mortally wounded or panicking.

#### Pointing to Christ
Saul was the king the people asked for, tall and impressive, and he died with his sons on a hill in shame, his head displayed in a pagan temple. The verdict is that he did not seek the LORD. The rest of the book shows a different kind of king, and points past David to a greater one. Jesus also died on a hill, publicly shamed, surrounded by enemies who thought they had won. But he died for the breach of faith of others, not his own, and he never stopped seeking his Father, praying even on the cross. The Philistines carried "good news" to their idols that Israel's king was dead. The true good news is that the King who died is alive. Faithful friends took Saul's body down and buried it, and Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus did the same for Jesus (John 19:38–42). Jesus' tomb did not stay closed.
