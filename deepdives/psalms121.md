*Setting:* Psalm 121 is the second of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the songs most scholars think pilgrims sang on the road up to Jerusalem for the great festivals. Psalm 120 ended with a sigh from someone living far away among liars and fighters. Psalm 121 finds the pilgrim on the way. The roads through the Judean hills were steep, rocky and exposed: pilgrims faced heat by day, cold by night, stony paths, wild animals and robbers (Luke 10:30). The psalm has no named author. It may be a conversation. In vv1–2 the pilgrim speaks in the first person ("my help"), and from v3 on another voice answers him with "you," perhaps a companion, a parent sending a child off, or a priest blessing the travelers. Its key word is "keep" (*shamar*), which comes six times in eight verses. It is one of the best-loved psalms of travelers. David Livingstone is said to have read it with his family on the morning he left Scotland for Africa. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### My Help Comes from the LORD (vv1–2)
- **"I lift up my eyes to the hills" (v1).** Lifting the eyes expresses longing and expectation (25:15; 123:1). But what do the hills mean? There are three main readings. The hills may be the mountains around Jerusalem, the pilgrim's goal, where God's temple stood (87:1; 125:2). They may be the hilltops where Canaanite shrines and idols stood (Deut 12:2; Jer 3:23), and the pilgrim asks whether help comes from there and answers no. Or the hills may simply be the hard road ahead, where danger lurks. All three make sense, and they lead to the same answer.
- **"From where does my help come?" (v1).** In Hebrew this is a real question, not a statement. The KJV's "from whence cometh my help" is sometimes read as if help came from the hills. The next line makes plain it does not.
- **"My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (v2).** The answer looks past the hills to the One who made them. "Help" is *'ezer*, strong, real help (115:9–11). "Maker of heaven and earth" is a favorite title in these pilgrim songs (124:8; 134:3). The God who made the mountains is greater than anything on or behind them. If the hills mean idol shrines, the point is sharper: the gods of the high places were made by human hands, but Israel's God made the hills themselves.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the lifting of the eyes "expresses desire… mingled with expectation," and that God's creative power is a pledge of his ability to help. Henry warns against leaning on "men and means, instruments and second causes" instead of on God. Guzik takes the hills as the distant hills around Jerusalem and notes the idol-shrine and robbers readings as less likely. He passes on Boice's point that the eye moves past the mountains to the God who made them, and stresses that God is with his people on the journey, not only at its end.

#### He Who Keeps You Will Not Slumber (vv3–4)
- **"He will not let your foot be moved" (v3).** A new voice answers the pilgrim, now speaking to "you." The Hebrew form can be read as a wish or blessing: "May he not let your foot slip." On the steep stony paths of the hill country, a slip could mean a broken ankle or a fall. God keeps his people's feet steady (66:9; 1 Sam 2:9).
- **"He who keeps you will not slumber" (v3).** "Keeps" is *shamar*, to watch over, guard, protect. It is the word for a shepherd watching his flock, a watchman guarding a city at night (127:1), and the cherubim guarding the way to the tree of life (Gen 3:24). It is also the word in the priestly blessing: "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Num 6:24).
- **"Behold, he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (v4).** "Behold" calls for attention. Travelers had to sleep, and at night they were most exposed. A watchman might doze off. God never does. Elijah mocked the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel: "perhaps he is asleep and must be awakened" (1 Kings 18:27). Israel's God never has to be woken. The verse also widens the scope. The God who keeps one pilgrim is the same God who keeps the whole nation.
- **What about the psalms that cry "Awake"?** Psalms 44:23 and 78:65 picture God as if asleep. Those are honest prayers from people who felt forgotten. Psalm 121 states the underlying truth: whatever it feels like, God is never inattentive.
- **Commentators:** JFB says, "His sleepless vigilance is added," and cites Psalms 38:16 and 66:9 on feet being moved. Henry calls God "a wakeful, watchful Keeper; he is never weary." Guzik notes that "keep" appears six times in the psalm and passes on Maclaren's description of the word as the singer's charm against fear. He links the steady feet to Paul's call to stand firm (Eph 6:13–14).

#### The LORD Is Your Shade (vv5–6)
- **"The LORD is your keeper" (v5).** This is the center of the psalm. "Keeper" is the same word again, now as a title.
- **"The LORD is your shade on your right hand" (v5).** In a hot, dry country, shade was life. A rock's shadow or a tree's could save a traveler from heatstroke (Isa 32:2). The right hand was where a defender stood in battle and an advocate stood in court (16:8; 109:31; 110:5). So God is both shelter and protector, close at hand. The image may also recall the pillar of cloud that went before Israel in the wilderness (Exod 13:21).
- **"The sun shall not strike you by day" (v6).** Sunstroke was a real danger. A boy collapsed and died in a field from it (2 Kings 4:18–20), and Jonah fainted under the sun's heat (Jonah 4:8). Isaiah promises the returning exiles, "neither scorching wind nor sun shall strike them" (Isa 49:10), and Revelation repeats it for the redeemed (Rev 7:16).
- **"Nor the moon by night" (v6).** No one gets moonstroke, but ancient people believed the moon could harm the mind and body. The English words "lunatic" and "lunacy," from the Latin for moon, preserve that belief. The moon may also stand for all the fears and dangers of the night. In the surrounding nations, both the sun and the moon were worshiped as gods. Here they are only lights that the Creator controls (Gen 1:16). Nothing in the sky has power over those God keeps.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the right hand "a protector's place" and says the moon stands poetically for the dangers of night, which it "presides" over (Gen 1:16). Henry says the believer will not be hurt "neither by the open assaults, nor by the secret attempts of thine enemies," and notes that "the right hand is the working hand." Guzik explains how dangerous the Middle Eastern sun could be and ties the shade to Isaiah 25:4 and Psalm 91:1. He passes on Spurgeon's observation that God does not give believers a different sun and moon. They live under the same sky as everyone else, but its power to harm is taken away.

#### The LORD Will Keep Your Going Out and Your Coming In (vv7–8)
- **"The LORD will keep you from all evil; he will keep your life" (v7).** "Evil" (*ra'*) means harm or disaster, not only moral evil. "Life" is *nephesh*, the whole living person. This does not mean that no believer ever suffers. The same psalmists suffered greatly, and Hebrews 11 lists faithful people who were tortured and killed (Heb 11:35–38). It means nothing can touch God's people that he has not permitted, and nothing can finally destroy them. Jesus said, "not a hair of your head will perish," in the same breath as "some of you they will put to death" (Luke 21:16–18).
- **"Your going out and your coming in" (v8).** This is a Hebrew way of saying "everything you do": leaving home and returning, starting work and finishing it, the whole round of life (Deut 28:6; 2 Sam 3:25). For a pilgrim it also means the journey there and the journey home.
- **"From this time forth and forevermore" (v8).** The keeping does not end when the festival does, or even when life does. It is the same phrase heard in the Hallel (113:2; 115:18).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "going out" and "coming in" as "all one's ways," and says "evermore" "includes a future state." Henry says God will "keep thee in life and death," from morning work to evening rest. Guzik passes on Kidner's line that the psalm promises not a cushioned life but a well-armed one, and Spurgeon's comparison of the three "will keep" promises of vv7–8 to a threefold volley of arrows that drives off fear.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 121 promises a Keeper who never sleeps. In Jesus that Keeper came close. He called himself the Good Shepherd who knows his sheep and says, "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28). In his prayer the night before he died, he told the Father, "I have guarded them… and not one of them has been lost," and asked, "keep them from the evil one" (John 17:12, 15). It is the same language as this psalm. He himself slept in a boat during a storm (Mark 4:38), because he was truly human, yet as the Son of God he upheld all things (Heb 1:3). And he walked the pilgrim road up to Jerusalem, likely singing these songs, knowing that this time his going in would mean the cross. His own life was not kept from death. He gave it up, so that those who belong to him could be kept "from this time forth and forevermore."
