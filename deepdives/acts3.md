*Setting:* Jerusalem, soon after Pentecost, about AD 30. The church is a few thousand strong, meeting daily in the temple courts and in homes, and "many wonders and signs were being done through the apostles" (2:43). Luke now slows down to tell one of those signs in detail. It leads to Peter's second sermon and, in chapter 4, to the church's first clash with the temple leaders. Peter and John, partners in the Gospels (Luke 22:8), work as a pair through these chapters.

#### The Lame Beggar Healed (vv1–10)
- **"Going up to the temple at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (v1).** About 3 p.m., the time of the afternoon sacrifice and public prayer (compare Dan 9:21; Luke 1:10). The apostles still keep Israel's hours of prayer. The temple is still their place of worship, and also the place where the crowds are.
- **"A man lame from birth" (v2).** Luke later says he was over forty (4:22). He had never walked, and everyone knew it. Being "carried" and "laid daily" at the gate means friends or family made a living for him there.
- **"The Beautiful Gate" (v2).** No ancient source uses that name, so its location is debated. Most scholars identify it with the Nicanor Gate, the great bronze gate on the east side between the Court of the Gentiles and the inner courts. Josephus says it was made of Corinthian bronze and was worth more than gates plated with silver and gold. A crippled man could not go further in (compare Lev 21:17–20 for priests, and 2 Sam 5:8), so he sat at the threshold of the place he could never enter.
- **"To ask alms" (v2).** Giving to the poor was a central duty in Jewish piety, and worshipers on their way to prayer were a likely source. He had found the best spot in the city to beg.
- **"Look at us" (v4).** Beggars learn not to meet people's eyes. Peter and John look straight at him and ask him to look back. It is a personal encounter, not a coin dropped in passing.
- **"I have no silver and gold, but what I do have I give to you" (v6).** The church had money (2:45), but the apostles personally did not carry it. The man asks for a little help to live with his condition. Peter offers to end it.
- **"In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, rise up and walk!" (v6).** "The name" in Hebrew thought stands for the person himself, his authority and his presence. Peter does not heal in his own name or by a formula. He acts as Jesus' representative, and Jesus acts. "Of Nazareth" names the despised town and the crucified man, deliberately, at the temple gate.
- **"He took him by the right hand and raised him up" (v7).** Peter had seen Jesus take Jairus's daughter by the hand (Luke 8:54), and Jesus had raised Peter's mother-in-law in the same way (Mark 1:31).
- **"His feet and ankles were made strong" (v7).** Luke, a physician (Col 4:14), uses precise words for the anatomy. Men who have never walked must learn to balance. This man is instantly strong enough to leap.
- **"Walking and leaping and praising God" (v8).** Isaiah promised that in the day of God's salvation "the lame man shall leap like a deer" (Isa 35:6). Jesus pointed to such healings as signs that the promised age had come (Luke 7:22). Now the man who sat outside goes in "with them" into the temple.
- **"They were filled with wonder and amazement" (v10).** The crowd knew this man. There was no doubt who he was or what he had been.
- **Commentators:** Guzik points out that Jesus must have passed this man many times on visits to the temple without healing him, which suggests that God's timing matters as much as his power. JFB says Peter and John looked at him "that, through the eye, faith might be aided in its birth," and says of "silver and gold," "What a lofty superiority breathes in these words!" JFB also notes that the detail about feet and ankles reads like "the technical language of a physician," an old argument for Luke's authorship, though later scholars have shown ordinary educated Greek used similar words.

#### Peter Speaks in Solomon's Portico (vv11–26)
- **"He clung to Peter and John" (v11).** A small, human detail. He is not letting them out of sight.
- **"The portico called Solomon's" (v11).** A long covered colonnade on the east side of the temple's outer court, popularly traced to Solomon. Jesus taught there in winter (John 10:23), and the early church met there regularly (5:12).
- **"Why do you stare at us, as though by our own power or piety?" (v12).** Peter refuses any credit, as Paul and Barnabas later refuse worship at Lystra (14:14–15). Not their ability, not their holiness.
- **"The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (v13).** This is how God named himself to Moses at the burning bush (Exod 3:6, 15). Peter's message is not a new religion. It is the God of Israel's fathers keeping his word.
- **"Glorified his servant Jesus" (v13).** "Servant" (*pais*) echoes the Servant of the LORD in Isaiah, whom God says "shall be high and lifted up" after being "despised and rejected" (Isa 52:13; 53:3). Acts uses the title of Jesus four times (3:13, 26; 4:27, 30).
- **"Denied… in the presence of Pilate, when he had decided to release him" (v13).** Pilate, the Gentile governor, wanted to release Jesus three times (Luke 23:4, 14–16, 22), and the crowd refused. Peter uses the word "denied" twice (vv13–14). He knew what it was to deny Jesus himself.
- **"The Holy and Righteous One… a murderer" (v14).** They traded the innocent for Barabbas, a rebel and murderer (Luke 23:18–19, 25). The contrast is meant to sting.
- **"You killed the Author of life" (v15).** *Archēgos* means founder, originator, or leader and prince. The ESV's "Author" stresses the source, the older "Prince" the leader, and both are in the word. The same title appears in 5:31 and of Jesus as "founder" of salvation and faith (Heb 2:10; 12:2). They killed the one through whom life itself came. That is the paradox of the cross.
- **"His name—by faith in his name—has made this man strong" (v16).** The healing came through faith, but faith in *Jesus*. Whose faith? Peter's at least, and probably the man's too once he saw them. Luke stresses that it was the name, received by faith, that did it. **"Perfect health"** (*holoklēria*) means wholeness, every part sound.
- **"I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers" (v17).** Peter softens his tone: "brothers." Jesus prayed on the cross, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). In the law, unintentional sins could be atoned for, while deliberate, defiant sin could not (Num 15:27–31). Ignorance does not make them innocent, but it leaves the door to forgiveness open. Paul says the same of himself (1 Tim 1:13).
- **"That his Christ would suffer, he thus fulfilled" (v18).** "All the prophets" foretold it, Peter says. Jesus taught the same on the road to Emmaus (Luke 24:25–27), and Isaiah 53 is the clearest example.
- **"Repent therefore, and turn back" (v19).** Two verbs. "Repent" is a change of mind. "Turn back" (*epistrephō*) is the Old Testament prophets' great call to return to the LORD. **"That your sins may be blotted out."** *Exaleiphō* means to wipe off or wipe out, the word for wiping away tears (Rev 7:17; 21:4) and for cancelling the record of debt (Col 2:14). Ancient ink could be washed off papyrus, leaving the page blank.
- **"Times of refreshing… from the presence of the Lord" (v20).** *Anapsyxis*, used only here in the New Testament, means cooling, relief, a breathing space after heat or labor. It is the rest and renewal that come from God's own presence. Some read it as the present blessing of forgiveness, others as the final age when Christ returns. The flow of vv20–21 favors the future, with a foretaste now.
- **"Whom heaven must receive until the time for restoring all the things" (v21).** Jesus must stay in heaven for now. "Restoring" (*apokatastasis*) echoes the disciples' question in 1:6 about restoring the kingdom. The answer is bigger than they hoped: not only Israel's kingdom but "all the things" the prophets spoke of, a renewed creation (Rom 8:19–21; Rev 21:5). This verse has sometimes been used to teach that everyone will finally be saved, but Peter's next sentence (v23) rules that out.
- **"A prophet like me" (vv22–23).** Moses promised that God would raise up a prophet like him and that whoever refused to listen would be cut off (Deut 18:15–19). By Jesus' day many Jews expected a special end-time Prophet (John 1:21; 6:14; 7:40). Like Moses, Jesus delivers God's people, speaks God's words face to face, and mediates a covenant. Unlike Moses, he is the Son (Heb 3:5–6). "Shall be destroyed from the people" combines Deuteronomy with Leviticus 23:29, a common way of weaving texts in Jewish preaching.
- **"From Samuel and those who came after him" (v24).** Samuel anointed David, and from David's line the Messiah's promise grew (2 Sam 7). All the prophets point to "these days."
- **"In your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed" (v25).** God's promise to Abraham (Gen 22:18; 12:3). Paul argues that the "offspring" (literally "seed," singular) is finally Christ (Gal 3:16). The blessing was always meant to reach every family on earth.
- **"Sent him to you first" (v26).** "First" implies others after: the Gentiles (13:46; Rom 1:16). **"To bless you by turning every one of you from your wickedness."** The blessing is not first political freedom or wealth. It is being turned from sin.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that "the apostles referred all to their Lord, and refused to receive any honour, except as his undeserving instruments," and on v17 he adds that rejecting Christ through ignorance "can in no case be an excuse." Guzik makes the same point: sin in ignorance is still sin, but it differs from knowing rebellion. JFB calls the "Prince of life" line a "glorious paradox, but how piercing to the conscience of the auditors," and reads the restoration of all things as "the rectification of all the disorders of the fall." Guzik links the times of refreshing and Christ's return to Israel's future repentance but warns against speculating about what would have happened had the nation turned then. JFB sees the likeness to Moses "particularly in intimacy of communication with God."

#### Pointing to Christ
A man who could never enter the temple walks in leaping, and Peter makes sure no one looks at him or at the apostles but at Jesus: the Servant, the Holy and Righteous One, the Author of life, the Prophet like Moses, the seed of Abraham. The same people who denied him are offered forgiveness in his name. The healed ankles are a small sign of the bigger promise: the restoration of all things when the one heaven now holds comes back.
