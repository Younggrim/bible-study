*Setting:* The world of the patriarchs, probably early in the second millennium BC. Abram has been in Canaan for some years. He went down to Egypt in a famine and came back rich (12:10–13:2), and then he and his nephew Lot parted ways because the land could not hold both their flocks. Lot chose the well-watered Jordan plain and "moved his tent as far as Sodom," even though "the men of Sodom were wicked" (13:10–13). Abram settled by the oaks of Mamre at Hebron (13:18). Chapter 14 is unlike anything else in Abram's story: the camera pulls back to international politics, with kings from the far east and a campaign down the length of the land. Abram, the quiet shepherd of the promises, appears as a warrior who beats an army of kings, and then as a worshiper who meets a mysterious king-priest named Melchizedek and refuses the riches of Sodom. It also introduces a figure central to the Bible's teaching about Christ's priesthood.

#### Abram Rescues Lot (vv1–16)
- **"In the days of Amraphel king of Shinar…" (v1).** The chapter opens like an ancient royal chronicle. The four eastern kings come from Shinar (Babylonia, 10:10; 11:2), Ellasar (location uncertain), Elam (in the southwest of modern Iran) and Goiim, which simply means "nations," perhaps a coalition of tribes. None of these kings has been identified for certain in other records. Older scholars equated Amraphel with Hammurabi of Babylon, but that link has been largely abandoned. Still, the names fit the period: Chedorlaomer has the shape of an Elamite royal name, and Tidal resembles the Hittite name Tudhaliya. The Tyndale notes point to such similar names as support for the account.
- **"The Valley of Siddim (that is, the Salt Sea)" (v3).** The five rebel kings rule small city-states at the south end of the Dead Sea. Several "that is" notes in this chapter (vv2, 3, 7, 8, 17) update old place names for later readers. The battlefield now lies under the Dead Sea, a quiet reminder that this region will soon be destroyed (ch 19).
- **"Twelve years they had served Chedorlaomer, but in the thirteenth year they rebelled" (v4).** This is the classic pattern of ancient empire. A powerful king forced smaller states to pay annual tribute. When the vassals stopped paying, the overlord marched to punish them. Chedorlaomer is the one the five kings "served," and he leads the campaign.
- **The route of the campaign (vv5–7).** The eastern kings come down the King's Highway, the main road along the plateau east of the Jordan, and sweep south to the Gulf of Aqaba before circling back toward the Dead Sea. On the way they crush peoples later remembered as giants or ancient inhabitants: the Rephaim, the Zuzim (probably the "Zamzummim" of Deut 2:20), the Emim (Deut 2:10–11) and the Horites of Seir, whom Esau's descendants later displaced (Deut 2:12). This army had flattened everyone in its path.
- **"En-mishpat (that is, Kadesh)… the country of the Amalekites" (v7).** En-mishpat means "spring of judgment." Kadesh is the oasis where Israel will spend much of the wilderness years (Num 20:1). "The country of the Amalekites" names the region by the people who lived there later; Amalek was a descendant of Esau (36:12), not yet born. **Hazazon-tamar** is identified with En-gedi on the western shore of the Dead Sea (2 Chr 20:2).
- **"Four kings against five" (v9).** The numbers favor the rebels, but they are crushed. Numbers do not decide this chapter: soon one household defeats the four.
- **"The Valley of Siddim was full of bitumen pits" (v10).** Bitumen, a natural asphalt (used at Babel, 11:3), still surfaces around the Dead Sea. The Hebrew says "pits, pits of bitumen," doubling the word for emphasis. "Fell into them" can mean the kings died there or hid there. Since a king of Sodom meets Abram in v17, either this king survived or, as Henry assumes, a successor had taken his place.
- **"They also took Lot, the son of Abram's brother, who was dwelling in Sodom" (v12).** Here is why the story is in Genesis at all. Lot's slide is gradual: he looked toward the plain (13:10), pitched his tent near Sodom (13:12), is now "dwelling in Sodom" (14:12), and will later be found sitting in its gate (19:1). He chose the region for its prosperity, and now its prosperity is carried off along with him. **"His possessions"** are named twice (vv12, 16), the very wealth that caused the split from Abram in the first place (13:6).
- **"Abram the Hebrew" (v13).** This is the first time the word "Hebrew" (*Ibri*) appears in the Bible. It is often what foreigners call Israelites (39:14; 1 Sam 4:6), and its origin is debated. One link is to Eber, Abram's ancestor (10:21; 11:14–17). Another is to the verb *abar*, "to cross over," so that a Hebrew is "one from the other side," and Brown-Driver-Briggs suggests "from beyond the Euphrates" (compare Josh 24:2–3). The ancient Greek translation renders it here as *ho peratēs*, roughly "the one who crossed over" or "the migrant." Either way, the label sets Abram apart as an outsider among the peoples of Canaan. Some have connected it with the *Habiru* of ancient texts, roaming bands of displaced people and mercenaries, but the Tyndale notes say the two are not the same.
- **"These were allies of Abram" (v13).** The Hebrew is literally "lords of a covenant with Abram." Mamre, Eshcol and Aner were Amorite chieftains with whom Abram had made a treaty. Abram is a respected figure among his neighbors, and they ride out with him (v24).
- **"When Abram heard that his kinsman had been taken captive" (v14).** "Kinsman" is literally "brother," the same word Abram used when he told Lot, "we are kinsmen" (13:8). Lot had taken the best land and left. Abram does not say, "He made his choice." He goes after him at once.
- **"His trained men, born in his house, 318 of them" (v14).** The word for "trained men," *chanik*, occurs only here in the Bible. The lexicon gives "trained, tried, experienced," men who had proved themselves. It comes from the root *chanak*, "to train" or "dedicate," the verb of Proverbs 22:6 ("Train up a child"). "Born in his house" means these were servants raised within Abram's household, not hired soldiers. That Abram could field 318 fighting men, and still leave others behind with the flocks, shows how large and wealthy his household had become, an early sign of God's promise to make him great (12:2). Jewish interpreters noticed that the letters of Eliezer (15:2) add up to 318, an ingenious reading but not what the text says.
- **"Went in pursuit as far as Dan" (v14).** Dan is in the far north of the land, about 150 miles from Hebron. In Abram's day the town was called Laish; it was renamed Dan only after the tribe of Dan captured it in the time of the judges (Judg 18:29).
- **"He divided his forces against them by night" (v15).** Abram uses surprise. He splits his small force and attacks from more than one side in the dark, so the enemy cannot tell how many are coming. Gideon later does much the same against Midian (Judg 7:16–22). Abram pursues them **to Hobah, north of Damascus**, deep into what is now Syria.
- **"He brought back all the possessions… and the women and the people" (v16).** The rescue is complete. Abram does not just recover Lot. He recovers everyone and everything the kings took, including the people of Sodom. The man who walked through the land as a stranger has just driven its invaders out of the whole length of it. Isaiah 41:2–3 may echo this scene: "Who stirred up one from the east whom victory meets at every step? He gives up nations before him… He pursues them and passes on safely."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "the first war that ever we read of in scripture," and notes that it would not be recorded at all "if Abram and Lot had not been concerned in it." On Lot, Henry and JFB say almost the same thing in nearly the same words: when we go out of the way of duty, "we put ourselves from under God's protection" (Henry). Guzik makes the same point and adds a sad note: after his rescue, Lot went straight back to Sodom. Henry says Abram forgot the quarrel and "takes this opportunity to give a real proof of the sincerity of his reconciliation." He also calls the 318 "catechised servants," taught religion as well as warfare, linking it with 18:19. The lexicon's "trained, tried, experienced" does not carry that religious sense, so Henry's point fits Abram's household but not the word. JFB compares the night raid to the panicked skirmishes of Arab chiefs in his own day. Guzik passes on Barnhouse's view that "Hebrew" comes from a root meaning "to pass over" and that the Greek translation calls Abram "the passenger." The lexicon supports the link to crossing over and confirms the Greek rendering, though it also leaves the connection with Eber open.

#### Abram Blessed by Melchizedek (vv17–24)
- **"The king of Sodom went out to meet him" (v17).** The king of Sodom comes out first, but before he can speak, the story pauses for someone else. The writer sets two kings side by side: the king of Sodom, who will offer goods, and the king of Salem, who offers a blessing. Abram has to choose between them. **The Valley of Shaveh (that is, the King's Valley)** is probably the Kidron Valley beside Jerusalem, where Absalom later set up his monument (2 Sam 18:18).
- **"Melchizedek king of Salem" (v18).** He appears without introduction, family or background, and then disappears from the narrative. His name in Hebrew is *Malki-tsedeq*. Hebrews 7:2 reads it as "king of righteousness," and that is the natural meaning. The lexicon also allows "my king is righteous" or "my king is Zedek," since *tsedeq* may have been a divine title in old Jerusalem. A later king of Jerusalem has a matching name, Adoni-zedek, "my lord is righteous" (Josh 10:1). **Salem** (*Shalem*) is an old short form of Jerusalem, as Psalm 76:2 confirms: "His abode has been established in Salem, his dwelling place in Zion." The name is related to *shalom*, peace, which is why Hebrews calls him "king of peace" (Heb 7:2).
- **"Brought out bread and wine" (v18).** This is royal hospitality: food and drink for a weary army, and a sign of friendship with the victor. Christians have read more into it. Many early church writers, and Roman Catholic and Orthodox tradition, see in it a foreshadowing of the Lord's Supper; Protestants generally see a meal of welcome, not a sacrifice. The text does not explain it.
- **"He was priest of God Most High" (v18).** This is the first time the word "priest" (*kohen*) appears in the Bible. "God Most High" is *El Elyon*. *Elyon* means "highest" or "uppermost," and as a title for God it appears again in poetry such as Psalm 91:1 ("He who dwells in the shelter of the Most High"). Canaanite religion also used *El* and *elyon* for its high god, which is why some scholars think Melchizedek served a Canaanite deity. But Genesis plainly presents him as a priest of the true God, and Abram treats him as one (v22). How knowledge of the true God survived in Jerusalem, the text never says.
- **"Blessed be Abram by God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth" (v19).** "Possessor" translates *qoneh*, from *qanah*, a verb usually meaning "to get, acquire, buy." The lexicon lists this verse under the sense "of God as originating, creating," which is why some translations (NIV, BSB) say "Creator of heaven and earth." God owns heaven and earth because he made them. That matters in a chapter about who owns the spoils of war.
- **"And blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand!" (v20).** The blessing goes both ways: Abram from God, then God for Abram. Abram's 318 men did not win the battle on their own; God "delivered" the enemy. The verb is *miggen*, which the lexicon derives from *magen*, "shield," the very word God uses to Abram in the next scene: "I am your shield" (15:1).
- **"And Abram gave him a tenth of everything" (v20).** This is the Bible's first tithe (*ma'aser*, "a tenth part"), given centuries before the law of Moses commanded tithes (Lev 27:30; Num 18:21). Jacob later vows a tenth to God (28:22). It honors Melchizedek as God's priest and thanks God for the victory. Hebrews builds an argument on this verse: since Abram, the father of Levi, paid tithes to Melchizedek and received his blessing, Melchizedek's priesthood is greater than Levi's (Heb 7:4–10). Christians differ on whether a tithe given before the law binds believers today, or models grateful giving that the New Testament leaves unfixed (2 Cor 9:7).
- **Who was Melchizedek?** Many Jewish interpreters (and some Christians) identified him with Shem, Noah's son, who by the Bible's chronology was still alive. Some Christians have taken him to be an appearance of the Son of God before the incarnation, reading Hebrews 7:3, "without father or mother or genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life," literally. Most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, see him as a real Canaanite king and priest of the true God, and read Hebrews 7:3 as an argument from silence: Genesis records no parents, birth or death for him, so he stands in the text as a picture of an eternal priest. Hebrews says he is "resembling the Son of God" (Heb 7:3), which suggests he is a likeness of Christ, not Christ himself.
- **"Give me the persons, but take the goods for yourself" (v21).** The king of Sodom brings nothing, offers no blessing, and opens with a demand: "Give me." By the customs of war Abram could keep the spoil, but taking it would leave him enriched by Sodom. "Persons" translates *nephesh*, often rendered "soul" or "life," here simply meaning people.
- **"I have lifted my hand to the LORD, God Most High" (v22).** Raising the hand was the gesture of a solemn oath (Deut 32:40; Dan 12:7). Abram picks up Melchizedek's exact title, "God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth," but adds the covenant name, the LORD (*Yahweh*). The Tyndale notes suggest Abram may be clarifying this for Melchizedek's benefit.
- **"Not take a thread or a sandal strap… lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich'" (v23).** From the smallest thread to the cheapest strap, Abram will take nothing of Sodom's. God had promised to bless him (12:2), and he will not let the king of Sodom claim any share of the credit. The man who took Pharaoh's gifts after a lie (12:16) now refuses a king's gifts after a victory. The very next words from God are, "Fear not, Abram, I am your shield; your reward shall be very great" (15:1). He gave up Sodom's reward, and God promised to be his reward.
- **"Nothing but what the young men have eaten, and the share of the men who went with me" (v24).** Abram's vow binds him, not his allies. His servants have already eaten their supplies, and Aner, Eshcol and Mamre are entitled to their portion. He does not force his own costly decision on men who did not make the same commitment.
- **Commentators:** All three see Melchizedek as a type, a God-given picture, of Christ. JFB calls him "a type of the Saviour (Heb 7:1)" and thinks he was "one of the few native princes, if not the only one," who still worshiped the true God. Henry lays out the views fairly: he rejects the rabbinic identification with Shem ("why should his name be changed?"), gives real weight to the view that he was an appearance of the Son of God, and notes that the most common view is that he was a Canaanite prince. Guzik lists the main views, dismisses the fanciful ones, and concludes that whatever his identity, Melchizedek clearly pictures Jesus. Guzik and Henry both notice that Abram borrows Melchizedek's title for God in v22; Henry draws the lesson that "it is good to learn of others how to order our speech concerning God." On the refusal, Henry says Abram would not have it said that God's "promise and covenant" needed Sodom's money to make him rich, and that Abram, though strict with himself, did not "impose those restraints upon the liberties of others." Guzik makes the same point about Abram's allies and adds that Abram wanted the glory for his wealth to go to God alone. JFB says Abram replied with "a generosity unknown in that part of the world." Guzik takes the tithe as a tenth of Abram's assets rather than his income, while Henry, following Hebrews 7:4, limits it to a tenth "of the spoils." JFB has no separate comment on vv2–11, 17, 19 or 22–24.

#### Pointing to Christ
Melchizedek steps into the story for three verses and then vanishes, yet the rest of Scripture keeps returning to him. A thousand years later David hears God swear an oath to his Lord: "You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" (Ps 110:4). Hebrews explains why that matters. Jesus was from the tribe of Judah, not Levi, so he could never be a priest in Aaron's line. But he is a priest in this older order, a king of righteousness and king of peace in Jerusalem who is also a priest of God Most High, who blesses God's people and is greater than Abraham himself (Heb 7:1–17). Abram went out at risk to rescue a relative who had wandered off and been taken captive, and he would not let anyone else claim the credit. Jesus went much further for captives who had wandered much further, and the glory of that rescue belongs to God alone.
