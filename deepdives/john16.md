*Setting:* Late on the night before the crucifixion, about AD 30 or 33, possibly on the walk from the upper room toward the Kidron Valley (14:31; 18:1). This is the last part of Jesus' farewell teaching before his prayer in ch 17. He has just warned the eleven that the world will hate them as it hated him (15:18–25), and promised that the Spirit and they themselves will bear witness to him (15:26–27). Now he finishes the warning, explains why his leaving is actually good for them, describes what the Holy Spirit will do, and promises that their grief will turn to joy. The disciples are still confused, and the chapter shows them honestly: puzzled, sorrowful, then suddenly confident, and about to scatter.

#### The Hatred of the World (vv1–4a)
- **The chapter break falls inside a section.** The ESV's heading "The Hatred of the World" began at 15:18 and runs to the middle of 16:4. These verses finish that warning.
- **"To keep you from falling away" (v1).** The verb is *skandalizō*, to trip someone up or cause them to stumble. Jesus warns them ahead of time so that persecution does not catch them off guard and wreck their faith. Warned believers are better able to stand.
- **"They will put you out of the synagogues" (v2).** The word *aposynagōgos*, "expelled from the synagogue," appears only in John (9:22; 12:42; 16:2). For a Jew, expulsion meant losing worship, community, business and often family. Many scholars think that when John wrote, late in the first century, Jewish Christians were being pushed out of synagogues in this way, which would make these words especially pointed for his first readers.
- **"Whoever kills you will think he is offering service to God" (v2).** "Service" is *latreia*, the word for worship and sacrificial service in the temple. Persecutors will think killing Christians is an act of worship. Saul of Tarsus is the clearest example: he "thought it necessary to do many things in opposing the name of Jesus" (Acts 26:9–11), and Stephen died at the hands of zealous religious men (Acts 7:57–60). Later Jewish writings even state that whoever sheds the blood of the ungodly is like one who offers a sacrifice.
- **"Because they have not known the Father, nor me" (v3).** The root of religious persecution is not too much knowledge of God but too little.
- **"That when their hour comes you may remember" (v4a).** Jesus has his "hour," and his enemies have theirs (Luke 22:53: "this is your hour, and the power of darkness"). Prophecy fulfilled becomes a source of confidence, not panic.
- **Commentators:** JFB names Saul of Tarsus as the example of zeal that persecutes in God's name. Guzik observes that some of history's worst persecutions were carried out in God's name by people who did not know the Father. Henry draws the same lesson with a sharp edge: "Never was such a persecuting church as that which makes ignorance the mother of devotion."

#### The Work of the Holy Spirit (vv4b–15)
- **"I did not say these things to you from the beginning, because I was with you" (v4b).** Jesus had warned of persecution before (Matt 10:16–25), but not with this detail or this urgency. While he was with them, opposition fell on him. Once he leaves, it will fall on them.
- **"None of you asks me, 'Where are you going?'" (v5).** Peter (13:36) and Thomas (14:5) did ask. But their questions were about losing him, not about where he was going and what it would mean. They are so wrapped in sorrow that they do not ask the question that would comfort them.
- **"It is to your advantage that I go away" (v7).** "Advantage" (*sympherei*) means "it is better for you, it is to your profit." The disciples cannot imagine anything better than having Jesus beside them. Jesus says there is: the Spirit within them. In his earthly body Jesus could be in one place at a time. By the Spirit he would be present with every believer everywhere (14:16–18, 23). And the Spirit is given only after Jesus is glorified (7:39).
- **"He will convict the world" (v8).** The verb *elenchō* means to bring to light, expose, prove guilty, convince. It is a courtroom word. In ch 15 the world put Jesus on trial. Now the Spirit, the Advocate, reverses the courtroom and puts the world on trial. "Reprove" (KJV) is too weak, as JFB says: "convict or convince is the thing intended." The Spirit's work is not only to make people feel bad but to show them the truth so that they can turn.
- **"Concerning sin, because they do not believe in me" (v9).** The deepest sin is not one wrong act among others but refusing to trust Jesus, the root from which the rest grow (3:18–19). On Pentecost the Spirit did exactly this: the crowd were "cut to the heart" and asked what to do (Acts 2:37).
- **"Concerning righteousness, because I go to the Father" (v10).** The world condemned Jesus as a criminal and blasphemer. His return to the Father, through resurrection and ascension, proves that he was in the right. God has vindicated him (Acts 2:32–36; 1 Tim 3:16). Many also see here the righteousness God gives to believers through Christ, the only righteousness that will stand. "You will see me no longer" means the Spirit now testifies in place of Jesus' visible presence.
- **"Concerning judgment, because the ruler of this world is judged" (v11).** The verb is perfect tense: Satan "has been judged." At the cross the world thought it was judging Jesus, but Satan was the one condemned (12:31; Col 2:15). If the ruler of the world is judged, those who follow him should take note.
- **"You cannot bear them now" (v12).** Jesus does not dump everything on them at once. They are not ready.
- **"He will guide you into all the truth" (v13).** "Guide" (*hodēgeō*) means to lead along the way, as a guide leads a traveler. The Greek Old Testament uses it in Psalm 25:5: "Lead me in your truth." The promise was first to the apostles and is the basis for the New Testament they wrote: the Spirit would lead them into the full truth about Jesus. Christians rightly trust the same Spirit to help them understand that truth today, but he does not add a new gospel. He leads into what Christ has already given.
- **"He will not speak on his own authority" (v13).** The Spirit, like the Son (5:19; 12:49), does not act independently. "The things that are to come" may mean the events of the cross and resurrection about to happen, the life of the church, and the end of history. Revelation is one of its fruits.
- **"He will glorify me" (v14).** The Spirit's work always points to Jesus. A spirituality that makes much of the Spirit but little of Jesus is not the Spirit's own work.
- **"All that the Father has is mine" (v15).** No created being could say this. The Father, Son and Spirit share one life and one truth. What the Father has belongs to the Son, and the Spirit makes it known.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lists reasons Jesus' departure was an advantage: the Spirit could be with every believer at all times, Jesus' teaching would be preserved, believers would walk by faith, and Christ's finished work would be understood from his throne. He stresses that the Spirit always points to Jesus and never contradicts Scripture. JFB explains the third conviction not as the final judgment but as the prince of this world being overthrown, whose power over people was broken by Christ's death. Henry agrees that "it was expedient for them that he should go away," hard as that was to believe.

#### Your Sorrow Will Turn into Joy (vv16–24)
- **"A little while, and you will see me no longer; and again a little while, and you will see me" (v16).** "A little while" (*mikron*) appears seven times in vv16–19. The first "little while" is the few hours until his death and burial. The second is the three days until the resurrection, when "the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord" (20:20). Many readers also hear a second layer: the present age of his absence, followed by his return.
- **"We do not know what he is talking about" (v18).** John records the disciples' confusion honestly. They whisper to one another rather than ask Jesus, and he answers the question they were afraid to put.
- **"You will weep and lament, but the world will rejoice" (v20).** "Lament" is the word for loud wailing at a funeral. On Friday the disciples would mourn and the world would celebrate. By Sunday that would be reversed.
- **"Your sorrow will turn into joy" (v20).** Not replaced by joy but turned into joy. The cross that caused the sorrow becomes the cause of the joy.
- **"When a woman is giving birth, she has sorrow because her hour has come" (v21).** The prophets used labor pains as a picture of the painful arrival of God's new age (Isa 26:17–19; 66:7–14; Mic 4:9–10). Jesus' "hour" (12:23) is like a birth: agony that brings new life. "A human being has been born into the world" may hint at more: the resurrection is the birth of a new humanity in Christ.
- **"No one will take your joy from you" (v22).** Joy based on circumstances can be taken. Joy based on a risen Lord cannot, because he will never die again (Rom 6:9).
- **"In that day you will ask nothing of me" (v23).** English uses "ask" twice, but the Greek uses two words. The first, *erōtaō*, often means to ask a question: they will no longer need to quiz him as they did in vv17–19. The second, *aiteō*, means to request something: "whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give it to you."
- **"Until now you have asked nothing in my name" (v24).** Praying in Jesus' name is new. It depends on his death and glorification, which open direct access to the Father (Heb 4:14–16; 10:19–22). "Ask, and you will receive" is in the present tense: keep asking.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that God does not simply swap sorrow for joy but transforms the sorrow itself, as the birth image shows, and that resurrection joy cannot be taken. JFB links the "little while" to the death and resurrection and also applies it to the church's grief during Christ's absence and its joy at his return. JFB gives no separate comment on vv17–21. Henry: "Believers have joy or sorrow according as they have or have not a sight of Christ."

#### I Have Overcome the World (vv25–33)
- **"I have said these things to you in figures of speech" (v25).** The word *paroimia* means a figure, a riddle, a saying with a hidden meaning. John used it of the shepherd and the sheepfold (10:6). After the resurrection and the Spirit's coming, Jesus will speak "plainly" (*parrēsia*, openly, boldly) about the Father.
- **"I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf; for the Father himself loves you" (vv26–27).** This does not deny that Jesus intercedes for his people (Rom 8:34; Heb 7:25). It means that his intercession is not needed to change a reluctant Father's mind. The Father is not cold toward them while the Son is warm. "The Father himself loves you." The word for love here is *phileō*, the love of affection and friendship. Their love for Jesus does not earn the Father's love. It shows they are already its objects.
- **"I came from the Father… and now I am leaving the world and going to the Father" (v28).** In one verse Jesus summarizes his whole story: from the Father into the world, then out of the world back to the Father. It is the story of the Gospel of John in miniature (1:1, 14; 13:1, 3).
- **"Ah, now you are speaking plainly" (v29).** The disciples suddenly feel they understand. Their confidence is sincere but premature. They have heard a clear sentence, but they have not yet seen the cross.
- **"Do you now believe?" (v31).** Jesus does not deny their faith, but he questions how strong it is. Within hours it will be tested to breaking point.
- **"You will be scattered, each to his own home, and will leave me alone" (v32).** In Mark 14:27 Jesus quotes Zechariah 13:7: "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered." Exactly this happens in the garden (Mark 14:50).
- **"Yet I am not alone, for the Father is with me" (v32).** Jesus' confidence in the Father's presence holds even as his friends run. Many readers ask how this fits with the cry from the cross, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34). John does not record that cry. The two belong together: Jesus bore real abandonment as he carried sin, and still trusted the Father who was with him, which is why Psalm 22 ends in praise.
- **"That in me you may have peace. In the world you will have tribulation" (v33).** Two locations, two experiences. "Tribulation" (*thlipsis*) means pressure, the crushing of grapes or the squeezing of a press. Believers live in both places at once. Jesus does not promise escape from trouble. He promises peace in himself in the middle of it.
- **"But take heart; I have overcome the world" (v33).** "Take heart" (*tharseite*) means "be courageous, cheer up." Jesus said it to a paralyzed man (Matt 9:2) and to the frightened disciples on the lake (Matt 14:27). "I have overcome" (*nenikēka*, from *nikaō*, to conquer) is in the perfect tense, as if the victory is already won, though the cross is still hours away. John picks up the word in his letters: "this is the victory that has overcome the world—our faith" (1 John 5:4). Martin Luther is reported to have said such a saying was worth carrying from Rome to Jerusalem on one's knees.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Jesus speaks as a conqueror before the cross, and that the church rests on Christ's work, not the courage of its first members, since every one of them was about to run. JFB sees in the disciples' eager reply simple hearts with an immature faith, and reads "Do ye now believe?" as a warning that their faith would soon be tried. JFB also argues that Jesus need not persuade an unwilling Father, since the Father loves those who love his Son. Henry: "Peace in Christ is the only true peace, and in him alone believers have it."

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus faces arrest, betrayal, desertion and death, and spends his last hours of teaching preparing his friends for trouble and promising them joy. He leaves so that the Spirit can come, and the Spirit's whole work is to put the world on trial for rejecting him, prove him righteous, show Satan defeated and lead his people into the truth about him. The grief of the cross is a birth pang, and the joy of the resurrection cannot be taken away. His last word before his prayer is a conqueror's word spoken before the battle: "In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world."
