*Setting:* After Judah in the south, the second great lot goes to "the people of Joseph," the tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh, Joseph's two sons. Jacob had adopted them as his own and blessed the younger, Ephraim, above the elder (Gen 48:5, 13–20). Their land is the heart of the country: the fertile central hills from the Jordan to the Mediterranean, around Bethel, Shechem and Shiloh. Joshua himself was from Ephraim (Num 13:8). Chapters 16 and 17 belong together, as Matthew Henry says ("It is a pity that this and the following chapter should be separated"). Chapter 16 gives Joseph's southern border and then Ephraim's share. It is short, and unlike Judah's chapter it lists no towns. It ends with another note about a city Israel did not take.

#### The Allotment for Ephraim and Manasseh (vv1–10)
- **"The allotment of the people of Joseph went from the Jordan by Jericho" (v1).** "Allotment" is literally "the lot came out," as from a container or a fold of a garment where the marked stones were kept (Prov 16:33). The southern border of Joseph's land starts at the Jordan near Jericho, "east of the waters of Jericho," the spring that Elisha later healed (2 Kings 2:19–22). It climbs through the wilderness into the hills to Bethel. Jericho itself went to Benjamin (18:21).
- **"From Bethel to Luz" (v2).** Luz was the old Canaanite name of Bethel (Gen 28:19), but here they seem to be two places close together, perhaps Bethel as Jacob's shrine and Luz as the town beside it. Bethel ("house of God") was where Jacob dreamed of the ladder and heard God's promise of this very land (Gen 28:10–22). Now Jacob's descendants are settling on the same ground.
- **"The territory of the Archites… the Japhletites" (vv2–3).** Clans living in the area, otherwise unknown except that David's trusted friend Hushai was an Archite (2 Sam 15:32).
- **"Lower Beth-horon, then to Gezer, and it ends at the sea" (v3).** The border runs down the Beth-horon road, the route of Joshua's great pursuit in 10:10–11, to Gezer at the edge of the coastal plain, and on to the Mediterranean. Holding the Beth-horon road gave Ephraim one of the main ways up from the coast into the hills.
- **"The people of Joseph, Manasseh and Ephraim, received their inheritance" (v4).** Manasseh is named first here because he was the older son. But Ephraim's land is described first (vv5–10), and Ephraim became the stronger tribe. At the census in the plains of Moab Manasseh had more men (52,700 to 32,500; Num 26:34, 37). Over time that reversed, as Jacob had foretold: "his younger brother shall be greater than he" (Gen 48:19). In later centuries "Ephraim" became a common name for the whole northern kingdom (Hos 11:8; Isa 7:2).
- **Ephraim's borders (vv5–8).** The description is short and hard to trace on a map. It gives the southern line from Ataroth-addar to Upper Beth-horon, then a northern line through Michmethath and Taanath-shiloh, down past Janoah and Naarah to Jericho, and from Tappuah west along the brook Kanah ("reedy brook") to the sea. Henry admits that scholars "find themselves very much at a loss" tracing it, and that cities have vanished and streams have changed. The purpose was practical: to fix landmarks that later generations must not move (Deut 19:14).
- **No list of towns.** Judah's chapter listed more than a hundred towns. Ephraim's lists none, and Manasseh's (ch 17) lists only a few. Nobody knows why. Henry guessed that Joshua, being from Ephraim, handled his own tribe's towns privately, but that is a guess. Ephraim's territory did include famous places later on: Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood (18:1); Ramah (or Ramathaim) in the hill country of Ephraim, Samuel's hometown; and Timnath-serah, Joshua's own town (19:50).
- **"The towns that were set apart for the people of Ephraim within the inheritance of the Manassites" (v9).** Ephraim received some towns inside Manasseh's area (17:8–9 gives an example, Tappuah). The two tribes from one father lived intertwined. JFB suggests Ephraim's tract proved "too small in proportion to its population and power." The complaint of the Joseph tribes in 17:14–18 grows out of this.
- **"However, they did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer" (v10).** Gezer was a strong city on a hill guarding the main coastal road and the way up to Jerusalem. Joshua had defeated its king and army in the field (10:33; 12:12), but the city itself was never taken. Judges 1:29 repeats the failure. Gezer finally came into Israel's hands centuries later when a Pharaoh of Egypt captured and burned it and gave it as a wedding present to his daughter, Solomon's wife (1 Kings 9:16). The Greek Septuagint adds a sentence at the end of this verse that refers to that later event. Solomon then rebuilt Gezer as one of his fortress cities (1 Kings 9:15), and archaeologists have uncovered a large city gate there that many date to his reign.
- **"But have been made to do forced labor" (v10).** "Forced labor" (*mas*) means a labor gang: people required to work for the state without pay, on building, farming or roads. The law allowed this for distant cities that surrendered (Deut 20:10–11), but the peoples of Canaan were to be driven out, not kept, because they would lead Israel into their worship (Deut 7:1–4; 20:16–18). Ephraim chose the profit of cheap labor over obedience. That compromise looked sensible, but Judges 2:1–3 shows the result: the Canaanites became "thorns in your sides" and their gods "a snare."
- **Commentators:** Henry calls v10 "a brand" on Ephraim, caused "either through carelessness or cowardice," and he cites Calvin's view that taking tribute made it worse, since it showed they spared the Canaanites "out of covetousness" and exposed themselves to their idolatry. He also notes that Ephraim, smaller than Manasseh at the census, soon outgrew it: "Families and kingdoms are diminished and increased… as God pleases." JFB notes that Joseph's two tribes were given one combined lot "that the brethren might be contiguously situated," and explains the brook Kanah as "reedy." Guzik points out that Ephraim had the strength to take Gezer, since Joshua had already beaten its army. He traces Israel's failure to two motives, wanting peace at any price and wanting profit, and warns that the same kind of compromise still keeps believers from all God intends. The Tyndale notes add that Gezer did not belong to Israel until Solomon's day.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joseph's sons received the richest land in the middle of the country, the land Jacob had prayed over and Moses had blessed with "the best gifts of the ancient mountains" (Deut 33:13–17). They did not lose it to a stronger enemy. They let an enemy stay because it was useful. The New Testament knows this temptation well: sin kept around because it seems to serve us. Paul's answer is not a better arrangement with it but a death and a new life: "Put to death therefore what is earthly in you" (Col 3:5), because believers have died and been raised with Christ (Rom 6:6–11). What Ephraim could not finish, Christ has finished. His people are called not to make sin work for them, but to drive it out in his strength.
