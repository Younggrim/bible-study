*Setting:* Babylonia, about 593 BC, soon after Ezekiel's call. The prophet has just been told to shut himself in his house, bound and unable to speak except when God opens his mouth (3:24–27). Now his silence becomes theatre. Chapters 4–5 are a series of sign-acts: messages acted out in front of the exiles instead of spoken. Ezekiel builds a model of Jerusalem under siege, lies on his side for more than a year, and lives on starvation rations cooked over dung. The audience is the first wave of captives, taken in 597 BC, who still believed Jerusalem was safe because the temple stood there and that their exile would be short. Jerusalem would in fact be besieged by Nebuchadnezzar from January 588 BC until its fall in the summer of 586 BC (2 Kings 25:1–4). Ezekiel is acting it out about five years ahead.

#### The Siege of Jerusalem Symbolized (vv1–17)
- **"Take a brick and lay it before you, and engrave on it a city, even Jerusalem" (v1).** The *lebenah* was a mud brick, the basic building material of Mesopotamia. Babylonian scribes wrote on soft clay, and clay tablets with maps and city plans have been found, including a famous plan of the city of Nippur, near where the exiles lived. Ezekiel draws Jerusalem on the clay before it hardens. The exiles walking past would recognize the city at once.
- **"Put siegeworks against it… a siege wall… a mound… camps… battering rams" (v2).** Five terms describe a real ancient siege. The army threw up a wall around the city to cut off supplies, built an earth ramp up to the walls, camped all around, and rolled battering rams up the ramp. Assyrian palace reliefs of the siege of Lachish (701 BC) show exactly this. The exiles would have heard stories of it. Ezekiel, a grown man, sets out what looks like a child's war game, and that is part of its shock.
- **"Take an iron griddle, and place it as an iron wall between you and the city" (v3).** The *maḥabat* was a flat iron plate for baking. The same word is used for the griddle on which priests cooked grain offerings (Lev 2:5; 6:21), so Ezekiel the priest may well have used a temple-style utensil. Set up between him and the clay city, it becomes a wall of iron. Two meanings are possible and both may be in view: the siege wall will be unbreakable, and God has set an iron barrier between himself and Jerusalem. Isaiah 59:2 says the same: "your iniquities have made a separation between you and your God."
- **"Set your face toward it… and press the siege against it" (v3).** In this scene Ezekiel plays the besieger. That means he is standing in God's place. Nebuchadnezzar would be only the instrument. The one really pressing the siege against Jerusalem is the LORD. **"This is a sign for the house of Israel."** A sign (*'ot*) is a visible pledge that something will happen.
- **"Lie on your left side, and place the punishment of the house of Israel upon it" (v4).** The key word is *'awon*, which in Hebrew means both "iniquity" and "the punishment for iniquity." English has to pick one, and the ESV picks "punishment" here. "Bear iniquity" (*nasa' 'awon*) is the language of the law. A guilty person bears his own iniquity (Lev 5:1), and the priests bore the iniquity of the people (Exod 28:38; Lev 10:17). On the Day of Atonement the scapegoat "shall bear all their iniquities on itself to a remote area" (Lev 16:22). Ezekiel does not atone for anything. He acts out what the people's iniquity is costing them.
- **Left and right (vv4–6).** Hebrew directions were given facing east. North was on the left and south on the right. Lying on his left side, facing a model of Jerusalem, Ezekiel would point to the northern kingdom of Israel. On his right side, he would point to Judah in the south.
- **"390 days, equal to the number of the years of their punishment" (v5).** This is one of the hardest numbers in the book, and no explanation has won everyone over. The main views are: (1) roughly 390 years from the building of Solomon's temple or the split of the kingdom, when idolatry took root, to the fall of Jerusalem, since the reigns of Judah's kings from Rehoboam to Zedekiah add up to about that; (2) a period of punishment still to come, running past the exile; (3) a symbolic figure. The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) reads 190 days here and in v9, which some think was an attempt to make the number fit the northern kingdom's history from 722 BC. Most modern translations keep 390 as the Hebrew text has it. What is clear is the point: Israel's guilt was not one generation deep.
- **"Forty days… a day for each year" (v6).** Forty years was the length of the wilderness wandering, when a faithless generation died outside the promised land. God set that sentence in the same terms: "for every day a year" (Num 14:34). Judah's exile is a second wilderness. Add the two figures and the total is 430, the number of years Israel lived in Egypt before the exodus (Exod 12:40). The Tyndale notes suggest this hints that a new exodus would follow the exile, which is exactly where Ezekiel's book later goes (20:33–38).
- **Did he lie there all day?** Probably not. In the same period Ezekiel cooks his food (v12) and acts out more signs (ch 5). Most interpreters think he lay on his side for a set time each day, in public view, for about fourteen months.
- **"With your arm bared" (v7).** A man rolled back his sleeve to fight or to work. Isaiah uses the picture of God himself: "The LORD has bared his holy arm" (Isa 52:10). Ezekiel again acts God's part. The arm is bared against the city.
- **"I will place cords upon you, so that you cannot turn" (v8).** This answers 3:25, where it was not said who would bind him. Here God says plainly that he does it. Ezekiel cannot get out of his assignment until the days are finished, as Jerusalem will not get out of the siege.
- **"Wheat and barley, beans and lentils, millet and emmer… into a single vessel" (v9).** Six kinds of grain and pulse. *Kussemet*, "emmer," is an old hulled wheat (KJV "fitches," others "spelt"). Mixing them into one loaf was not a health recipe. It meant that no single grain was left in enough quantity to bake bread on its own, so people scraped together whatever was in the bins. The law against mixing seeds (Lev 19:19; Deut 22:9) applied to sowing, not baking, but a priest's audience may have sensed the disorder anyway.
- **"Twenty shekels a day" (v10).** A shekel was a weight of about 11.5 grams, so twenty shekels is about eight ounces, roughly 230 grams of bread a day. **"The sixth part of a hin" (v11)** of water is somewhere between a pint and a quart, depending on how the hin is reckoned. This is a diet just short of starvation, eaten "from day to day," meaning at set times, rationed out. Jeremiah describes the reality during the siege: King Zedekiah had Jeremiah given "a loaf of bread daily from the bakers' street, until all the bread of the city was gone" (Jer 37:21; 52:6).
- **"Baking it in their sight on human dung" (v12).** In the treeless plains of Mesopotamia, dried animal dung was, and in many places still is, a common cooking fuel. Human excrement was another matter. The law required it to be buried outside the camp, because "the LORD your God walks in the midst of your camp" and the camp must be holy (Deut 23:12–14). Bread baked over it would be unclean. "In their sight" means the exiles watched the whole thing.
- **"Thus shall the people of Israel eat their bread unclean, among the nations" (v13).** Here God explains the sign. Living in a foreign land, under foreign masters, the exiles could not keep the food laws of the land God had given them (Hos 9:3–4). "The nations" is the word for Gentiles. Being driven among them meant losing the outward marks of being a holy people.
- **"Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I have never defiled myself" (v14).** This is Ezekiel's only recorded protest in the whole book. "Ah" (*'ahah*) is a cry of distress, the same word Jeremiah used at his call (Jer 1:6). Ezekiel names three priestly rules he has kept since childhood: no meat from an animal that **"died of itself"** (*nebelah*, carrion), none **"torn by beasts"** (*terepah*, the source of the later Jewish term *treif*, "not kosher"), and no **"tainted meat"** (*piggul*, sacrificial meat left too long, Lev 7:18; 19:7). Priests were held to these rules with special strictness (Lev 22:8; Ezek 44:31). Peter makes almost the same objection six centuries later: "I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean" (Acts 10:14).
- **"I assign to you cow's dung instead" (v15).** God listens and bends. Ezekiel may use the ordinary fuel of the region. The bread is still siege bread, and the sign still stands, but the prophet's honest plea is heard. God's servants may tell him plainly what they find hard to bear.
- **"I will break the supply of bread in Jerusalem" (v16).** Literally "break the staff of bread," as in the covenant curses of Leviticus 26:26: "When I break your supply of bread, ten women shall bake your bread in a single oven and shall dole out your bread again by weight, and you shall eat and not be satisfied." Ezekiel's sign is that curse coming true. **"By weight and with anxiety… by measure and in dismay"** pairs the scales with the fear. People ration, and they are afraid.
- **"Look at one another in dismay, and rot away because of their punishment" (v17).** "Dismay" is the root *shamem*, the same word used of Ezekiel sitting "overwhelmed" in 3:15. "Rot away" (*maqaq*) means to waste away or fester, the word Leviticus 26:39 uses: "those of you who are left shall rot away in your enemies' lands because of their iniquity." Once more the word is *'awon*, iniquity and its punishment together. Lamentations, written after the fall, describes it in grim detail: mothers who cooked their own children (Lam 4:10), and faces "blacker than soot" (Lam 4:8).
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the whole scene as telling the exiles that God would not rescue Jerusalem this time. He follows Wiersbe in identifying the griddle as a priestly baking plate and the iron wall as God's refusal to look on the city with favor. On the 390, Guzik prefers adding up the reigns of Judah's kings from Rehoboam to Zedekiah, about 394 years, less the few early years when Rehoboam was faithful (2 Chr 11:17). He notes Feinberg's point that the text speaks of the years of *bearing* punishment, not of sinning, and agrees Ezekiel lay down for part of each day. Henry says the 390 years were "supposed to be equal to the years from the establishment of idolatry," and that the whole sign shows "sin is the provoking cause of the ruin of that once flourishing city." The JFB page that could be loaded begins its notes at v9, and on the 390 it suggests the forty days are left out of v9 because the 390 stand for the long stay among the Gentiles. Guzik, following Poole, calls the ration enough to keep a man alive and no more, and stresses that God softened the command without cancelling the message. He draws from Meyer the lesson that God hears honest complaint offered in prayer. Henry observes that Ezekiel does not complain about the hunger, only about defilement, and finds comfort for a clean conscience: suffering is easier to bear "if our hearts can witness that we have always been careful." Henry also writes, "Their plenty having been abused to luxury and excess, they were justly punished by famine." JFB calls the bread "a coarse mixture of six different kinds of grain," reckons the ration at "little more than ten ounces" and the water at "about a pint and a half." JFB argues that because human dung would break the law, this part "must therefore have been done only in vision." Most interpreters think the sign was acted out, with v15 showing that God changed the instruction before Ezekiel carried it out. JFB adds a contrast with Peter's vision in Acts 10: there God set aside a ceremonial law, while moral laws never change.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel lay bound for more than a year bearing the iniquity of Israel and Judah, but his bearing it saved no one. It only showed the people what their sin was costing them. Isaiah, writing earlier, had promised a servant who would truly do what Ezekiel could only act out: "he shall bear their iniquities… he bore the sin of many" (Isa 53:11–12), with the same Hebrew words. Jesus took that iniquity on himself, was bound with cords (John 18:12), and died "outside the camp," the unclean place (Heb 13:11–13). He also called himself "the bread of life" (John 6:35). Jerusalem's bread was broken by judgment and doled out by weight in fear. His body was broken so that his people would never again eat in dismay, but would come to a table where there is enough.
