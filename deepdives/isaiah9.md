*Setting:* About 734–732 BC. Chapter 8 ended in "thick darkness": a nation that had refused God's word, wandering hungry through a ruined land. The first place to feel that darkness was the far north of Israel. In 733–732 BC the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III swept through Galilee, the lands of Zebulun and Naphtali, and deported its people (2 Kgs 15:29). He turned the region into Assyrian provinces. Into that darkness Isaiah announces a great light and a royal child, the high point of the "book of Immanuel" (chs 7–12). Then, from v8, the chapter turns back to the northern kingdom and begins a poem of four stanzas, each ending with the refrain first heard in 5:25: "For all this his anger has not turned away, and his hand is stretched out still." The poem runs on to 10:4. In Hebrew Bibles, v1 is counted as 8:23, so the verse numbers in the rest of the chapter are one lower.

#### For to Us a Child Is Born (vv1–7)
- **"But there will be no gloom for her who was in anguish" (v1).** The "But" answers the darkness of 8:22 directly. The land that suffered most will not stay in the dark.
- **"In the former time he brought into contempt the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali" (v1).** These two tribes lived around the Sea of Galilee, on Israel's northern border. They were the first overrun by every invader from the north, and the first taken into exile by Assyria.
- **"In the latter time he has made glorious the way of the sea, the land beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the nations" (v1).** These three names probably match the three provinces Assyria carved out of Israel's territory: the coastal road, the land east of the Jordan (Gilead), and Galilee. "Galilee of the nations" (or "of the Gentiles") means a district with a mixed population, Jews living among foreigners. The same places that were humiliated first will be honored first.
- **Fulfilled in Galilee.** Matthew quotes these verses when Jesus moved to Capernaum "in the territory of Zebulun and Naphtali" and began preaching there (Matt 4:12–17). For centuries Jerusalem looked down on Galilee: "Search and see that no prophet arises from Galilee" (John 7:52). But the Messiah grew up in Nazareth and did most of his ministry around the Sea of Galilee. The light dawned first in the region that was darkened first.
- **"The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (v2).** Isaiah speaks of the future as if it has already happened, the "prophetic perfect" tense, as if the prophet were already standing in the light. "Deep darkness" is *tsalmaveth*, the word in Psalm 23:4 for "the valley of the shadow of death." Light breaks into the place of death. Zechariah sang this verse over the baby John the Baptist, as he spoke of Jesus coming "to give light to those who sit in darkness and in the shadow of death" (Luke 1:79).
- **"You have multiplied the nation; you have increased its joy" (v3).** A small textual point: the Hebrew consonants read "you have *not* increased the joy," but a marginal note kept by the Jewish scribes (the *qere*, "what is read") says "increased *its* joy." The two words sound the same in Hebrew. The ESV and most translations follow the scribal note, which fits the context. Older translations like the KJV kept the written text.
- **"As with joy at the harvest, as they are glad when they divide the spoil" (v3).** The two happiest scenes in ancient life: bringing in the harvest after months of work, and sharing out the plunder after a victory.
- **"The yoke of his burden… the rod of his oppressor, you have broken as on the day of Midian" (v4).** The yoke is the oppressor's rule, pictured as a heavy wooden collar on an ox. "The day of Midian" recalls Gideon's victory, when God routed a vast army of Midianites with three hundred men carrying trumpets, jars and torches (Judg 7). The point of that story was that the LORD won it, not Gideon's strength (Judg 7:2). The coming deliverance will be the same: God's doing, against impossible odds.
- **"Every boot of the tramping warrior… and every garment rolled in blood will be burned" (v5).** The soldier's boot, a heavy hobnailed army sandal, appears only here in the Hebrew Bible. It was probably an Assyrian word, a fitting choice for the empire's marching troops. All the gear of war is gathered and burned because it will never be needed again (2:4).
- **"For to us a child is born, to us a son is given" (v6).** Three "for" statements (vv4, 5, 6) build to this one. The reason for the light, the joy, the broken yoke and the end of war is a baby. "Born" stresses his real human birth. "Given" suggests the son comes as a gift from God. Many Christian readers see here both his humanity (born) and his eternal sonship (given, from above, John 3:16), though the two lines are also simple Hebrew parallelism. "To us" makes it personal.
- **"The government shall be upon his shoulder" (v6).** The yoke that was on the people's shoulder (v4) is gone. Now the rule rests on *his* shoulder. A king's robe or a symbol of office was sometimes worn on the shoulder (22:22). Ahaz's government had failed. This child will carry it.
- **"His name shall be called" (v6).** In Hebrew, a "name" sums up a person's character. Egyptian pharaohs took several throne names at their coronation, and some scholars think these four names follow that pattern for a Davidic king. Each name is two words.
- **"Wonderful Counselor."** "Wonderful" (*pele'*) is a word used almost only of God and his works, the miraculous (Exod 15:11; Judg 13:18, where the angel of the LORD says his name is "wonderful"). Isaiah later says the LORD "is wonderful in counsel" (28:29), using the same two roots. Judah's counselors had led the nation astray (3:3; 5:21). This king's wisdom will be beyond human.
- **"Mighty God."** In Hebrew *El Gibbor*. Some scholars soften this to "god-like hero" or "mighty warrior," arguing that kings were sometimes spoken of in exalted language (Ps 45:6). But Isaiah uses the exact same phrase, *El Gibbor*, of the LORD himself in the very next chapter: "A remnant will return… to the mighty God" (10:21). In Isaiah's own vocabulary, the child is called by a name of God. This is the plainest statement in the Old Testament that the Messiah would be divine.
- **"Everlasting Father."** In Hebrew *Avi-'ad*, "father of eternity" or "father forever." This does not confuse the Son with God the Father, the first person of the Trinity. Kings in the ancient world were called "father" of their people, meaning their protector and provider (22:21; Job 29:16). Ordinary kings reign for a few years and die. This king will care for his people like a father, forever.
- **"Prince of Peace."** In Hebrew *Sar Shalom*. *Shalom* means far more than the absence of war. It is wholeness, well-being and everything put right. Paul says of Christ, "he himself is our peace" (Eph 2:14), and Jesus said, "Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you" (John 14:27).
- **"Of the increase of his government and of peace there will be no end" (v7).** His kingdom will keep growing and never stop. A curious scribal detail: in the standard Hebrew text, the first word, *lemarbeh* ("of the increase"), is written with a "closed" form of the letter *mem* that normally appears only at the end of a word. It is the only place in the Hebrew Bible where this happens in the middle of a word. Jewish interpreters have puzzled over it for centuries, and some saw it as a hint that the promise was sealed for the future.
- **"On the throne of David and over his kingdom" (v7).** God promised David that his throne would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16). Ahaz, a son of David, sat on that throne but did not trust God. This son of David will rule "with justice and with righteousness," the very things God looked for in his vineyard and did not find (5:7). The angel Gabriel echoed this verse to Mary: "the Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David… and of his kingdom there will be no end" (Luke 1:32–33).
- **"The zeal of the LORD of hosts will do this" (v7).** "Zeal" is God's passionate, jealous love for his people and his glory. The whole promise rests on God's determination, not on human effort.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry captures the wonder of v6: "The same that is the mighty God is a child born; the ancient of days becomes an infant of a span long." He adds that the Son "is given, freely given," and that Christ "shall set his shoulder to it, and will never complain, as Moses did, of his being overcharged." On v7: "the zeal of the Lord of hosts will overcome all opposition." Guzik reads the child born as Jesus' humanity, which he needed to be our substitute, and the son given as his eternal deity. He supports "Mighty God" from 10:21 and insists "Everlasting Father" does not confuse the persons of the Trinity. He sees the government on his shoulder fully realized only when Christ returns to reign. JFB on v3 argues that the very difficulty of the written reading "not" makes it unlikely to be a later addition, and reads the day of Midian as a victory won by a handful of men. Both JFB and Guzik note that "Prince of Peace" is already true: he is "our peace."

#### Judgment on Arrogance and Oppression (vv8–21)
- **"The Lord has sent a word against Jacob, and it will fall on Israel" (v8).** The tone changes abruptly. This is a message against the northern kingdom, "Ephraim and the inhabitants of Samaria" (v9). God's word is pictured as something thrown that lands and does its work (55:11).
- **"The bricks have fallen, but we will build with dressed stones" (v10).** Ordinary houses were made of sun-dried mud brick, which crumbles in rain. Wealthy buildings used cut, squared stone. Sycamore-fig wood was cheap and common in the lowlands. Cedar from Lebanon was the most prized timber in the region. After a first blow, probably an earthquake or an early Assyrian raid, Samaria's people boasted that they would rebuild better than before. It is the sound of a nation that will not hear the warning in its losses.
- **"The LORD raises the adversaries of Rezin against him" (v11).** Rezin of Syria was Israel's ally. His "adversaries" were the Assyrians, who destroyed Damascus in 732 BC. When the ally falls, Israel is exposed.
- **"The Syrians on the east and the Philistines on the west devour Israel" (v12).** Israel is squeezed from both sides. Even the Syrians, once allies, would end up in Assyria's army or turn on Israel after their own defeat. **"For all this his anger has not turned away, and his hand is stretched out still."** The first refrain.
- **"The people did not turn to him who struck them" (v13).** This is the heart of the poem. God's blows were meant to bring them back. "Turn" is *shuv*, repent. They did not turn, and they did not "inquire of the LORD." Amos, preaching to the same kingdom, lists disaster after disaster with the same sad line: "yet you did not return to me" (Amos 4:6–11).
- **"Head and tail, palm branch and reed in one day" (v14).** A proverb for "everyone, top to bottom." The palm branch grows at the top of a tall tree, and the reed grows in the swamp. Isaiah explains it himself: the "head" is the elder and honored man, and the "tail" is the lying prophet (v15). It is biting: the false prophets who wagged along behind the leaders are the tail of the dog.
- **"Those who guide this people have been leading them astray" (v16).** The same charge as 3:12. Bad leaders do not just fail. They lead others to ruin.
- **"The Lord does not rejoice over their young men, and has no compassion on their fatherless and widows" (v17).** This is one of the most shocking lines in the prophets. God is always the defender of orphans and widows (1:17; Ps 68:5). But the corruption had spread so far that even the most vulnerable were caught up in it: "everyone is godless and an evildoer." The judgment would fall on all. **The second refrain.**
- **"Wickedness burns like a fire" (v18).** Sin is pictured as a brushfire. It starts in the thorns and spreads to the forest, until the whole land goes up in a column of smoke. Sin is self-destructive. Its own heat consumes the sinner.
- **"No one spares another… each devours the flesh of his own arm" (vv19–20).** In the chaos people turn on each other, eating whatever they can grab and never being satisfied. "His own arm" probably means his own kin, his nearest support.
- **"Manasseh devours Ephraim, and Ephraim devours Manasseh; together they are against Judah" (v21).** Manasseh and Ephraim were the two sons of Joseph and the two largest northern tribes, brother tribes. The northern kingdom's last years were full of civil war. Four of its last six kings were assassinated (2 Kgs 15:10–30). They fought each other and agreed only in attacking their brother Judah, as in the war of chapter 7. **The third refrain**, and the fourth comes in 10:4.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads vv8–21 as ruin brought on by pride and impenitence, an Israel that would not be taught by milder corrections. JFB explains v10 neatly: sun-dried brick dissolves in rain, sycamore is common, cedar is "free from knots, durable, and precious," so the boast means "we will replace cottages with palaces." JFB notes that conquering nations "often enlist in their armies the subject races," and on v21 says the brother tribes, "disunited in all things else," united only against Judah. Guzik observes that the purpose of God's discipline is repentance, so the sharpest charge is v13: they did not turn to the one who struck them. He also says that in vv18–21 God simply removes his restraint and lets human hatred run its course.

#### Pointing to Christ
In the middle of Isaiah's darkest chapters stands the brightest promise. The light dawned in Galilee, the despised border country, where Jesus walked and taught and healed. The child was born in Bethlehem and the Son was given from heaven. He bears the government on the shoulder that once bore the cross. He is the Wonderful Counselor whose wisdom outshines every king's adviser, the Mighty God in human flesh, the Father-like King who never dies, and the Prince who makes peace between God and sinners. The rest of the chapter shows the alternative: a people who would not turn, whose leaders misled them, whose hatred burned like fire. That is the darkness the light came into. And the refrain, "his hand is stretched out still," finds its answer when the hands of the Son were stretched out on the cross, bearing the anger that had not turned away.
