*Setting:* Babylon has fallen to the Medes and Persians (5:30–31), probably in 539 BC, and a new government is being organised under "Darius the Mede." Daniel was taken captive as a teenager in 605 BC, so he is now in his early eighties. He has served under Nebuchadnezzar, survived the chaos after his death, read the writing on the wall for Belshazzar, and now finds himself trusted by a new dynasty. Chapter 3 showed his three friends refusing to bow to an idol. Chapter 6 is its companion: Daniel refuses to stop praying to his God. Chapter 3 tested whether they would do what God forbade. Chapter 6 tests whether Daniel will stop doing what God commands. This is the last of the stories in Daniel. From chapter 7 on, the book turns to visions.

#### Daniel and the Lions' Den (vv1–28)
- **"120 satraps" (v1).** "Satrap" comes from an Old Persian title meaning "protector of the kingdom." Persian kings ran their empire through these provincial governors. Herodotus counts about twenty great satrapies under Darius I, so the 120 here are probably smaller districts, or officials of different ranks under one title. Esther 1:1 counts 127 provinces in a later reign.
- **"Three high officials, of whom Daniel was one" (v2).** Their job was to make sure "the king might suffer no loss," meaning no theft of taxes and no revolt. An old Jewish exile from the defeated regime is put in charge of the money. That says a great deal about his reputation.
- **"An excellent spirit was in him" (v3).** The same phrase was used of him in Babylon (5:12). The king **"planned to set him over the whole kingdom"**, as Pharaoh did with Joseph (Gen 41:40–41). Daniel's career and Joseph's run side by side all through this book: both are foreign captives, both interpret dreams, both are promoted, both are targets of jealousy.
- **"They could find no ground for complaint" (v4).** The officials go through his record and come up with nothing. **"Faithful"** means trustworthy and reliable: no bribes, no carelessness, no cover-ups. This is one of the finest descriptions of a believer at work anywhere in Scripture.
- **"Unless we find it in connection with the law of his God" (v5).** They realise his only "weakness" is his loyalty to God. Envy (Prov 27:4; Eccl 4:4) drives a plot that has nothing to do with Daniel's job and everything to do with his rank.
- **"Came by agreement" (vv6, 11, 15).** The Aramaic word means to come thronging or in a crowd, and some translate it "came as a mob." It appears three times in the chapter. Each time the plotters move together, they push the king one step further.
- **"All the high officials… are agreed" (v7).** This is a lie. Daniel was one of the three high officials and was never asked. The decree flatters Darius: for thirty days, every prayer in the empire must go only to him. Persian kings were not normally worshipped as gods, so this may have been meant as a short, symbolic act of loyalty to the new king, making him the one channel to heaven for a month. For most people it would have cost nothing. For a Jew who prayed to the LORD daily, it was impossible.
- **"The den of lions" (v7).** Assyrian and Persian kings kept lions for royal hunts, and reliefs show them released from cages. The "den" (*gob*, a pit) seems to have been an underground pit with an opening at the top that could be covered with a stone (v17).
- **"The law of the Medes and the Persians, which cannot be revoked" (vv8, 12, 15).** Esther 1:19 and 8:8 say the same of Persian law. The Greek historian Diodorus tells of a later Persian king, Darius III, who regretted an execution he had ordered but could not undo it. The idea seems to have been that a king's word, once sealed, could not be wrong. The irony is that the king who is being treated as a god for thirty days cannot even save his own best servant.
- **"When Daniel knew that the document had been signed" (v10).** He does not stage a protest, and he does not go into hiding. He goes home and prays exactly **"as he had done previously."** He simply keeps doing what he had always done.
- **"Windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem" (v10).** An upper room was the quiet part of a house, often used for prayer (Acts 1:13; 10:9). Facing Jerusalem goes back to Solomon's prayer at the temple's dedication: if Israel is ever taken captive and prays "toward their land… the city that you have chosen," God will hear (1 Kgs 8:46–49). The temple lay in ruins, but Daniel took God at his word. He had probably prayed like this for over sixty years.
- **"Three times a day" (v10).** The Old Testament does not command set prayer times, but David speaks of praying "evening and morning and at noon" (Ps 55:17). Later Jewish practice settled on three daily prayers.
- **"Prayed and gave thanks" (v10).** With his life on the line he still gives thanks. His prayer life was not an emergency measure. It was a lifelong habit, and the habit carried him through the crisis. Later Jews read this as a model for faithful life in exile.
- **"Daniel, who is one of the exiles from Judah" (v13).** The accusers reach for the same put-down Belshazzar used (5:13). They do not mention his rank or his record. They call him a foreigner and say he "pays no attention to you, O king."
- **"Much distressed and set his mind to deliver Daniel" (v14).** The king sees at once that he has been used. **"He labored till the sun went down"**, probably searching for a legal loophole and finding none. The plotters press him again (v15). A man who rules 120 provinces is helpless against his own signature.
- **"May your God, whom you serve continually, deliver you!" (v16).** It is somewhere between a prayer and a hope. Darius has seen enough of Daniel to think his God might act.
- **"A stone was brought and laid on the mouth of the den" (v17).** It is sealed with the king's signet ring and those of his nobles. Soft clay or wax pressed with a seal would show if anyone tampered with it. The double seal protects both sides: the nobles cannot finish Daniel off, and the king cannot rescue him in secret.
- **"Sleep fled from him" (v18).** The king fasts and refuses entertainment. The word translated "diversions" is uncertain. It may mean music, food, or concubines. The man in the palace has a worse night than the man in the pit.
- **"Servant of the living God" (v20).** At dawn he hurries to the den and calls out "in a tone of anguish." "The living God" is the title the Old Testament uses in contrast to idols (Deut 5:26; Josh 3:10; 1 Sam 17:26). Belshazzar praised gods that "do not see or hear or know" (5:23). Darius asks whether Daniel's God was **"able to deliver,"** the same question the three friends answered in 3:17.
- **"O king, live forever!" (v21).** Daniel's first words are a courteous greeting to the man who put him there. There is no bitterness.
- **"My God sent his angel and shut the lions' mouths" (v22).** Compare 3:28, where God "sent his angel" into the furnace. Hebrews 11:33 remembers those who through faith "stopped the mouths of lions." **"I was found blameless before him; and also before you, O king, I have done no harm."** "Blameless" is the Aramaic word for innocence. Daniel had broken the king's law, but he had done the king no wrong. Obeying God rather than men (Acts 5:29) is not the same as rebellion.
- **"Because he had trusted in his God" (v23).** The text gives the reason for his deliverance plainly. His survival is not luck and not his own goodness. It is God answering faith.
- **"They, their children, and their wives" (v24).** This is hard to read. Persian practice, as Herodotus describes it, often punished whole families for a man's crime. The text reports what the king did, not what God commanded, and the Law of Moses forbade putting children to death for their fathers' sins (Deut 24:16). The detail that the lions seized them **"before they reached the bottom"** answers anyone who might claim the lions simply were not hungry. The trap the plotters set for Daniel closed on themselves (Ps 7:15–16; Prov 26:27; Esth 7:10).
- **Darius's decree (vv25–27).** Like Nebuchadnezzar (3:29; 4:1–3, 34–37), the new ruler of the world writes to "all the peoples, nations, and languages." He goes further than Nebuchadnezzar's first decree, which only forbade speaking against Daniel's God. Darius commands people to "tremble and fear before the God of Daniel." He calls him "the living God," whose **"kingdom shall never be destroyed"**, words that pick up 2:44 and 4:3, 34 and prepare for the visions of chapter 7. Still, he calls him "the God of Daniel," not "my God." The decree honours the LORD without leaving the other gods behind.
- **"During the reign of Darius and the reign of Cyrus the Persian" (v28).** The Aramaic word for "and" can also mean "that is," and some translate "the reign of Darius, *namely* the reign of Cyrus." That would make Darius another name for Cyrus. Others take the two reigns as overlapping, with Darius as a governor or vassal king under Cyrus. Either way, Daniel outlived the empire that took him captive and saw the king under whom the exiles were allowed to go home (Ezra 1:1–4). His last dated vision comes in Cyrus's third year (10:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the decree came "under colour of doing honour to the king, but really intending the ruin of his favourite." He admits that "many a good man" would have thought it wise to skip prayer for thirty days, and he notes the windows faced Jerusalem "though now in ruins." JFB calls the windows "not in vainglory," and says "kings are the slaves of their flatterers." On the king's hard choice he adds, "Not to break a wicked promise, is not firmness, but guilty obstinacy." JFB sees the sealed stone as "typical of Christ's entombment under a seal." Guzik makes the same link: an innocent man condemned, shut behind a sealed stone, and brought out alive at dawn. He also points out that Daniel did not have to start praying in the den, because he had never stopped. He takes "the God of Daniel" as a sign that Darius's faith fell short of saving faith. On v28, Guzik prefers the view that Darius was the governor Gubaru, while the Tyndale notes lean toward Darius being Cyrus himself.

#### Pointing to Christ
The parallels to the Gospels are hard to miss. A faithful servant whom accusers could fault only for his devotion to God is condemned under a ruler who wants to release him and cannot (Matt 27:17–24). He is shut behind a sealed stone (Matt 27:66) and found alive early in the morning (Matt 28:1). The difference matters. Daniel was rescued from death, but Jesus went all the way into death and came out the other side, and he did not go in only for himself. Daniel's enemies fell into the pit they dug. At the cross the innocent one took the place of the guilty, so that those who once stood against God might be brought out alive with him (1 Pet 3:18). Peter calls the devil a "roaring lion" (1 Pet 5:8), and the one whose God shut the lions' mouths is the Lion of the tribe of Judah who has conquered (Rev 5:5).
