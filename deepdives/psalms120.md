*Setting:* Psalm 120 opens a collection of fifteen short psalms (Psalms 120–134), each titled "A Song of Ascents." The Hebrew word, *ma'alot*, comes from the verb *'alah*, "to go up," and in the Bible people always "go up" to Jerusalem. The city sits about 2,500 feet up in the Judean hills, so pilgrims climbed from every direction. The law called every Israelite man to appear before the LORD three times a year, at Passover, the Feast of Weeks and the Feast of Booths (Deut 16:16), and families traveled together in groups. That is how the twelve-year-old Jesus could be lost for a day among relatives on the road home (Luke 2:41–44). Most scholars think these were the songs sung on that journey. Other explanations have been offered. A tradition in the Talmud links the fifteen songs to fifteen steps in the temple courts where the Levites stood to sing, and some connect them with the return from Babylon. But the pilgrim reading fits best, and the songs do move like a journey: from a far-off foreign place (Psalm 120), to the hills on the road (121), to the gates of Jerusalem (122), and finally to the temple at night (134). Psalm 120 has no named author. It is the starting point: a believer far from home, worn down by people who lie and pick fights, longing to be somewhere else. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Deliver Me, O LORD, from Lying Lips (vv1–2)
- **"A Song of Ascents" (title).** Every psalm from 120 to 134 carries this title. Four of them add "Of David" and one "Of Solomon." The rest, including this one, are anonymous.
- **"In my distress I called to the LORD, and he answered me" (v1).** The verbs are past tense. He has prayed before in trouble and been heard, and that memory gives him courage to pray again now. "Distress" (*tsarah*) means a tight, hemmed-in place, like the "distress" of 118:5. A pilgrimage begins, fittingly, with prayer.
- **"Deliver me, O LORD, from lying lips, from a deceitful tongue" (v2).** The danger here is not swords but words: slander, false accusation, deceitful talk. "Deceitful" (*remiyyah*) means treacherous, crafty. Words can wound as badly as weapons. Psalm 52:2 calls a deceitful tongue "like a sharp razor." Lies about a person can ruin a reputation, a livelihood or a life. Naboth was killed on the word of two paid liars (1 Kings 21:10–13), and Jesus was condemned with the help of false witnesses (Mark 14:56–57).
- **Liars cannot be argued with.** The psalmist does not try to answer the slanderers himself. He takes the matter to God. Peter says Jesus did the same: "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return… but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "the psalmist was brought into great distress by a deceitful tongue," and "in this distress, he sought God by fervent prayer," adding that "God can bridle their tongues." JFB says the slander charged on his foes implies his own innocence, and points to Psalm 52:2, 4. Guzik notes that trouble tends to make prayer more urgent and that past answers to prayer give hope for present ones. He widens the prayer: believers need rescue not only from lies told about them but from lies told to them, about God and about themselves.

#### You Deceitful Tongue! (vv3–4)
- **"What shall be given to you, and what more shall be done to you, you deceitful tongue?" (v3).** The psalmist turns from God and speaks straight to the liar's tongue, as if it were a person. The wording echoes an old oath formula, "May God do so to me and more also" (1 Sam 3:17; Ruth 1:17): literally, "What will he give to you, and what will he add to you?" The question is, what will God do to you?
- **"A warrior's sharp arrows" (v4).** The answer fits the crime. Lying words are often pictured as arrows (Ps 64:3; Prov 25:18; Jer 9:8). The liar shot arrows at others. Now God, the true warrior, will shoot his own arrows back (7:12–13; 64:7).
- **"With glowing coals of the broom tree!" (v4).** The broom tree (*rotem*) is a desert shrub, the one Elijah sat under when he fled into the wilderness (1 Kings 19:4). Its roots were prized for charcoal because they burn very hot and hold their heat for a long time. Arrows wrapped in burning material were used in sieges. So the picture is of judgment that pierces and burns, and does not quickly go out. James also links the tongue with fire: "the tongue is a fire… set on fire by hell" (Jas 3:6).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the arrows as "destructive inflictions" and notes that coals of juniper (the KJV's word for the broom) "retain heat long," reading v4 as the answer to the question of v3, the punishment of the wicked. Henry: "The terrors of the Lord are his arrows," which fall on liars. Guzik notes how often Scripture compares a lying tongue to a razor, sword or arrow, and passes on Kidner's point that the liar armed with lies meets God's arrows of truth.

#### Woe to Me, That I Sojourn in Meshech (vv5–7)
- **"Woe to me" (v5).** The tone shifts from confidence to sighing. "Woe" here is a cry of grief, "how miserable I am."
- **"That I sojourn in Meshech, that I dwell among the tents of Kedar!" (v5).** These two places are about as far apart as could be. Meshech was a people in the far north, in the mountains of what is now central or eastern Turkey, descended from Japheth (Gen 10:2) and known to Ezekiel as slave traders and warriors (Ezek 27:13; 38:2). Kedar was a tribe of desert nomads in Arabia to the southeast, descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:13), known for their black goat-hair tents and their archers (Song 1:5; Isa 21:16–17). The psalmist could not have lived in both at once. Most readers take them as figures, the way someone today might say they live "among barbarians." The people around him are as foreign to his faith and values as distant pagan tribes. "Sojourn" means to live as a foreigner, without belonging.
- **"Too long have I had my dwelling among those who hate peace" (v6).** "Peace" is *shalom*, wholeness, right relationships, well-being. These people do not just disagree with him. They hate the very thing he longs for.
- **"I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war!" (v7).** Literally "I am peace." It is not just his opinion. It is who he is. But every time he opens his mouth, even to make peace, they hear a challenge. Some people cannot be reconciled with, however much one tries. Paul says, "If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all" (Rom 12:18), which admits that it is not always possible.
- **An unsettled ending.** The psalm ends with no resolution, no rescue and no praise. That is unusual. But it is the right starting point for a pilgrimage. A person who is content where they are has no reason to set out. The discomfort of living among liars and fighters is what makes him want to go up to Jerusalem, to God's house and God's people. The next psalm finds him on the road (121:1).
- **Being a sojourner.** The New Testament uses the same language for believers. They are "sojourners and exiles" in this world (1 Pet 2:11), whose real citizenship is in heaven (Phil 3:20). Abraham "went to live in the land of promise, as in a foreign land, living in tents… For he was looking forward to the city that has foundations" (Heb 11:9–10).
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the remote lands as a picture of the speaker's "miserable condition," says his foes were maliciously hostile while he wanted peace, and suggests the psalm begins a series that contrasts this misery with the joys of God's worship. Henry urges, "Let us follow after peace and holiness, striving to overcome evil with good." Guzik describes Meshech and Kedar as far-apart peoples who together stand for pagan nations in general, and passes on Morgan's point that being content in a place of deceit would be a poor sort of contentment. He also cites Maclaren's observation that the psalm reverses the usual pattern and ends with a sigh, which drives the soul toward a better home.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 120 is the cry of someone who is "for peace" in a world that wants war. Jesus lived that out fully. He was the Prince of Peace (Isa 9:6), yet when he spoke, people picked up stones (John 8:59; 10:31). False witnesses lied about him, and he did not answer them (Matt 26:59–63). He was a sojourner too: "the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (Matt 8:20), and he was rejected by his own (John 1:11). But he did more than sigh for peace. He "made peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20) and "came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near" (Eph 2:17). So the pilgrim's journey that begins in Psalm 120 does not end in Meshech. It ends in the presence of the God of peace.
