*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC, for Jews rebuilding life around a rebuilt temple in a small Persian province. Having traced Judah (chapters 2–4) and the tribes east of the Jordan (chapter 5), the Chronicler now gives the longest tribal record in the book to Levi, the tribe of priests and temple servants. Its place is deliberate. Judah, the royal tribe, opened the lists. Levi, the worshiping tribe, stands at the center. For the returned exiles, a priest had to prove his descent before he could serve (Ezra 2:61–63), and the whole community's life turned on worship being done rightly. The chapter has three parts: the line of high priests from Aaron to the exile (vv1–15), the Levite clans and the singers David appointed (vv16–48), and the priests' duty with the towns given to the tribe across the land (vv49–81). In the Hebrew Bible, vv1–15 are numbered 5:27–41, so verse numbers in some commentaries run differently.

#### Descendants of Levi (vv1–81)
- **"The sons of Levi: Gershon, Kohath, and Merari" (v1).** Levi's three sons founded the three great Levite clans, each with its own job in carrying the tabernacle in the wilderness (Num 3–4). Kohath carried the holy furniture, Gershon the curtains and coverings, Merari the frames and posts.
- **"The children of Amram: Aaron, Moses, and Miriam" (v3).** Israel's great deliverer, its first high priest and its prophetess were all one family of Kohathites. Miriam is named alongside her brothers, which a genealogy did not have to do.
- **"Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar" (v3).** Aaron's four sons. Nadab and Abihu died when they offered "unauthorized fire" before the LORD (Lev 10:1–2), and the list says no more of them. The high priesthood passed through Eleazar.
- **From Eleazar to the exile (vv4–15).** This is a line of high priests, but not a complete one. Well-known high priests such as Jehoiada, who saved the boy king Joash (2 Kings 11), and the Azariah who stood up to King Uzziah in the temple (2 Chr 26:17–20) do not appear by those names. Eli and his descendants are missing too, because they came from Ithamar, Aaron's other son. Ancient genealogies often skipped generations to show a line of descent rather than every link. The point is that the priesthood ran unbroken, by rightful descent, from Aaron to the fall of Jerusalem.
- **"Ahitub fathered Zadok" (v8).** Zadok served alongside Abiathar under David and became sole high priest under Solomon, when Abiathar, of Eli's family, was removed (1 Kings 2:27, 35). That fulfilled the word to Eli that God would "raise up for myself a faithful priest" (1 Sam 2:35). From here on Zadok's family held the office. Ezekiel's vision of a future temple gives its altar to "the sons of Zadok" alone (Ezek 44:15).
- **"It was he who served as priest in the house that Solomon built" (v10).** This note sits awkwardly. Solomon's high priest was "Azariah the son of Zadok" (1 Kings 4:2, where "son" means grandson), which matches the first Azariah in v9, not the second in v10. Most scholars think the note has slipped one line in copying. The Tyndale notes add that with it in its proper place, there are twelve generations from Aaron to Solomon's temple and twelve more from that temple to the high priest of the rebuilt one, a deliberate pattern.
- **"Hilkiah fathered Azariah" (v13).** Hilkiah is the high priest who found the Book of the Law in the temple under Josiah (2 Kings 22:8), which sparked the last great reform before the exile.
- **"Seraiah fathered Jehozadak" (v14).** Seraiah was the high priest when Jerusalem fell. The Babylonians took him to Riblah and executed him (2 Kings 25:18–21). Ezra the scribe traced his own descent from this Seraiah (Ezra 7:1).
- **"Jehozadak went into exile when the LORD sent Judah and Jerusalem into exile by the hand of Nebuchadnezzar" (v15).** The list ends not with a name but with a deportation. And the Chronicler says plainly who did it: the LORD, using Nebuchadnezzar as his tool. Jehozadak's son Jeshua (Joshua) came home as high priest with Zerubbabel and rebuilt the altar (Ezra 3:2; Hag 1:1). The first readers knew how the story continued. The line had been bent, not broken.
- **"These are the clans of the Levites according to their fathers" (v19).** A second start. The Chronicler now turns from the priests to the wider tribe, tracing each of the three clans for several generations (vv20–30). Some of these names will reappear in the singers' lines below, sometimes in slightly different forms. Uriel (v24) is probably the Zephaniah of v36, and Zimmah (v20) is a grandson, not a son, of Gershom (v42–43), as JFB notes.
- **"Amminadab his son, Korah his son" (v22).** Korah led a rebellion against Moses and Aaron and was swallowed by the earth (Num 16). But "the sons of Korah did not die" (Num 26:11). His descendants became gatekeepers and singers, and eleven psalms carry their name, including Psalm 42 ("As a deer pants for flowing streams") and Psalm 84 ("How lovely is your dwelling place"). The family of a rebel became a family of worshipers.
- **Elkanah and Samuel (vv25–28).** Samuel's father was Elkanah (1 Sam 1:1), so the prophet Samuel was a Levite of the Korah branch of Kohath. 1 Samuel calls Elkanah "an Ephrathite," that is, a man living in Ephraim's hill country. That describes where he lived, not his tribe, and v66 shows Kohathites were given towns in Ephraim. It also explains how the boy Samuel could serve at the tabernacle under Eli (1 Sam 2:11; 3:1).
- **"The sons of Samuel: Joel his firstborn, the second Abijah" (v28).** The traditional Hebrew text reads "the firstborn Vashni, and Abijah." *Vashni* looks like a scribe's mistake for "and the second" (*ve-hasheni*), with Joel's name dropped. The ESV restores Joel from 1 Samuel 8:2 and from v33 below. Samuel's sons took bribes and were the reason Israel asked for a king (1 Sam 8:3–5). Yet Samuel's grandson appears in the next paragraph, leading Israel's worship.
- **"The men whom David put in charge of the service of song… after the ark rested there" (v31).** Once the ark was brought to Jerusalem (chapters 15–16), the Levites no longer needed to carry the tabernacle from place to place. David gave them a new work: music. The Chronicler cares about this more than any other writer in Scripture. Worship with song is not decoration. It is a ministry, with appointed leaders and "their order" (v32).
- **"Heman the singer the son of Joel, son of Samuel" (v33).** The lead singer was Samuel's grandson, of the Kohath clan. His line is traced back about twenty generations to "Levi, son of Israel" (v38). Psalm 88, the darkest psalm in the Psalter, which ends in "darkness," is titled "a Maskil of Heman the Ezrahite." Whether that is this Heman or the wise man of the same name from Judah (1 Kings 4:31; 1 Chr 2:6) is debated, since "Ezrahite" usually points to a Judahite clan. Many readers take the Levite singer to be meant.
- **"His brother Asaph, who stood on his right hand" (v39).** Asaph led from the Gershon clan. He is "brother" in the sense of fellow Levite and fellow musician. Twelve psalms bear his name (Ps 50; 73–83), including Psalm 73, where the writer nearly loses his faith until "I went into the sanctuary of God" (Ps 73:17). His descendants were still singing when the exiles returned (Ezra 2:41).
- **"On the left hand… Ethan the son of Kishi" (v44).** The third leader, from Merari. In later chapters the Merarite leader is called Jeduthun (16:41; 25:1), and most take them as two names for one man. So each of Levi's three clans had a leader in the choir, standing in formation: Kohath in the center, Gershon on the right, Merari on the left.
- **"Their brothers the Levites were appointed for all the service of the tabernacle" (v48).** Not every Levite sang. Others cleaned, guarded, baked, kept accounts and carried water. The Chronicler lists them all as "appointed." No job in God's house was too small to be named.
- **"But Aaron and his sons made offerings on the altar… and to make atonement for Israel" (v49).** Now the line between priest and Levite is drawn clearly. Only Aaron's descendants could offer sacrifice, burn incense, and enter the Most Holy Place. Their purpose is stated in a phrase: "to make atonement for Israel," to cover the people's sin so a holy God could live among them. And all of it was done "according to all that Moses the servant of God had commanded." The Chronicler will show what happens when even a king forgets that (13:9–10; 2 Chr 26:16–21).
- **Aaron to Ahimaaz (vv50–53).** The high-priestly line is repeated, but only down to Ahimaaz, Zadok's son, who served in David's day (2 Sam 15:36). The repetition links the priesthood to David's reign, which is where the Chronicler's main story is about to begin.
- **"These are their dwelling places" (v54).** The last section lists the towns given to the Levites, drawn from Joshua 21 with small differences in names and spelling. Levi received no tribal territory, because "the LORD is his inheritance" (Deut 10:9; Num 18:20). Instead, God told the other tribes to give them 48 towns with surrounding land (Num 35:1–8). The count here is 13 for the priests, 10 for the rest of Kohath, 13 for Gershon and 12 for Merari: 48 in all. Not every name made it through the copying, as JFB notes on v60 and v61, but the totals match Joshua.
- **"With its pasturelands" (vv55–81).** The phrase is repeated over and over. The Hebrew word *migrash* means open common land around a town, where livestock grazed. The lexicon suggests it may come from the idea of land where cattle are driven out to feed. Numbers 35:4 set it at a thousand cubits (about 500 yards) from the wall. It gave Levite families room for their animals without giving them farmland of their own.
- **Hebron and the priests' towns (vv55–60).** The priests' towns lay in Judah and Benjamin, close to where the temple would stand. **Hebron**, where Abraham was buried and David first reigned, went to the priests, but its fields went to Caleb, as Joshua had promised him (v56; Josh 14:13–14). **Beth-shemesh** (v59) is where the ark came back from the Philistines on a cart (1 Sam 6). **Gibeon** (v60) later held the tabernacle and the bronze altar (16:39; 2 Chr 1:3). **Anathoth** (v60) was the hometown of the priest Jeremiah (Jer 1:1).
- **"The cities of refuge" (vv57, 67).** Six towns were set aside where a person who had killed someone by accident could flee and be protected until trial (Num 35:9–28; Josh 20). All six were Levite towns: Hebron, Shechem, Kedesh in Galilee, Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead and Golan (vv57, 67, 71, 76, 78, 80). The Hebrew of vv57 and 67 says "cities of refuge" in the plural, as if every town in the list were one. Joshua 21:13 and 21:21 show that only the first town named was. The plural probably sums up the category loosely. Placing refuge in the care of the men who taught God's law made sense.
- **"Out of the tribes of Issachar, Asher, Naphtali and Manasseh in Bashan" (v62).** The Levite towns were scattered from Galilee to the Negev and across the Jordan to Reuben and Gad (vv78–81). This was the outworking of a curse. Jacob had said of Simeon and Levi, after their massacre at Shechem, "I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel" (Gen 49:7). But when Israel worshiped the golden calf, the Levites stood with Moses (Exod 32:26–29), and the scattering turned into a gift. Spread among all the tribes, the Levites became teachers of God's law throughout the land (Deut 33:10; 2 Chr 17:8–9).
- **A picture of a whole people.** The Chronicler ends Levi's record with a map of all twelve tribes, each contributing towns. The Tyndale notes observe that the list shows Israel's unity and completeness. For returned exiles living in a sliver of their old land, it was a reminder of what Israel was meant to be: one people, gathered around worship, with God's servants in every corner.
- **Commentators:** Henry opens by calling the priesthood "an honour more valuable than either the precedency or the double portion" that Judah and Joseph received (chapter 5), and notes that the priests and Levites "were more concerned than any other Israelites to preserve their pedigree clear." On Heman, Samuel's grandson, he writes, "Thus does the blessing entailed on the seed of the upright sometimes pass over one generation and fasten upon the next." On the Levites who did not sing: "As every one has received the gift, so let him minister." Guzik, following Adam Clarke, suggests that Levi's record is the fullest and most exact because the author was a priest, which fits the old Jewish tradition that Ezra wrote Chronicles. Guzik also draws out the contrast between Simeon and Levi: both were scattered, one as a curse and one as a blessing. He uses Heman and Psalm 88 to point out that some of the deepest songs come out of sorrow. JFB suggests that the high priesthood passed for a time to Ithamar's family (Eli's line) around the days of Uzzi (v5), and explains the "Vashni" problem in v28 the same way the ESV does.

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole chapter rests on descent. A man served at the altar because he was born to Aaron, and the line had to be proven. Yet the line that ran from Aaron to Jehozadak ended in exile, and every priest in it died and needed atonement himself. Hebrews takes this up directly. Jesus was born of Judah, "a tribe… from which no one has ever served at the altar" (Heb 7:13). His priesthood does not rest on a family tree but on "the power of an indestructible life" (Heb 7:16). He makes atonement once for all and does not hand the office on, because he "continues forever" (Heb 7:24). The singers who stood at the ark point forward too. The Son is not ashamed to call his people brothers, saying, "in the midst of the congregation I will sing your praise" (Heb 2:12).
