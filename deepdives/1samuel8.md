*Setting:* Some years after the victory at Mizpah (ch 7), probably around 1050 BC. Samuel has judged Israel faithfully, riding a yearly circuit from his home at Ramah, and under his leadership the Philistines have been held back. But he is getting old. Chapter 8 is a turning point in Israel's history: the end of the era of the judges and the beginning of the monarchy. The elders ask for a king. God grants the request, and he also calls it what it is, a rejection of himself as King. The next four chapters (8–12) tell how Saul became king, and Samuel's farewell speech in chapter 12 looks back on this moment.

#### Israel Demands a King (vv1–9)
- **"When Samuel became old, he made his sons judges over Israel" (v1).** The story echoes Eli's family closely. Eli was old, and his sons were corrupt (2:12–22). Now Samuel is old, and his sons are corrupt too. Judges in Israel had not normally been appointed by men or passed from father to son. God raised them up for a crisis. Samuel's move looks like the start of a family dynasty, which may be part of what went wrong.
- **"Joel… and… Abijah; they were judges in Beersheba" (v2).** Joel means "the LORD is God," and Abijah "my Father is the LORD." Their names were better than their lives. Beersheba lay at the far south of the land, about 50 miles from Ramah, out of their father's daily sight.
- **"They turned aside after gain. They took bribes and perverted justice" (v3).** "Gain" (*betsa'*) means profit, especially dishonest profit. "Perverted justice" is literally "bent *mishpat*." The law was blunt: "You shall not pervert justice… and you shall not accept a bribe, for a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise" (Deut 16:19; Exod 23:8). Samuel himself would later ask the nation, "From whose hand have I taken a bribe?" and no one could name one (12:3). His sons did not follow him.
- **"Then all the elders of Israel… came to Samuel at Ramah" (v4).** The elders were the heads of the tribes and clans. Their concern was real: an aging leader, corrupt successors, and, as 12:12 reveals, a new threat from Nahash king of the Ammonites.
- **"Now appoint for us a king to judge us like all the nations" (v5).** The request had grounds, and God had even anticipated it. Deuteronomy 17:14–20 foresaw a day when Israel would say, "I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me," and gave rules for such a king: one chosen by God, an Israelite, who would not pile up horses, wives or gold, and who would write out and read God's law all his life. Jacob had prophesied that the scepter would belong to Judah (Gen 49:10). So a king as such was not the sin. The sin was in the reason and the spirit of the request. "Like all the nations" was exactly what Israel was not meant to be. God had called them to be "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6), a people set apart.
- **"The thing displeased Samuel… And Samuel prayed to the LORD" (v6).** The Hebrew is "the thing was evil in Samuel's eyes." He was surely hurt. His own sons were the stated reason, and he was being set aside. But he did what he had done all his life. He took it to God.
- **"They have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them" (v7).** This is the theological heart of the chapter. Israel already had a King. Gideon had refused the crown with the words, "I will not rule over you… the LORD will rule over you" (Judg 8:23). The LORD fought their battles, as he had just done at Mizpah with thunder (7:10). By asking for a king like the nations, they were saying they wanted someone they could see, a human guarantee of security, in place of the God they could not see.
- **"Obey the voice of the people" (vv7, 9).** God tells Samuel three times to "obey their voice" (vv7, 9, 22). This is not approval. Sometimes God's judgment is to give people what they insist on (Ps 106:15; Rom 1:24). Yet even here God remains in control. He will choose the king, and through this flawed request he will eventually bring David and David's greater Son.
- **"Forsaking me and serving other gods, so they are also doing to you" (v8).** God sets Samuel's rejection inside a long history: the golden calf, the grumbling in the wilderness, the cycles of idolatry in Judges. Samuel is in good company. He is being treated as God has been treated all along.
- **"Only you shall solemnly warn them and show them the ways of the king" (v9).** "Ways" is *mishpat*, the same word as the "justice" Samuel's sons perverted. It can mean judgment, justice, rights, custom. The people asked for a king "to judge (*shaphat*) us." God tells Samuel to show them what kind of *mishpat* a king will bring. The wordplay is pointed. They want justice from a king, so Samuel will tell them the king's "justice."
- **Commentators:** Henry says Samuel's sons "turned aside after lucre," and that Samuel "prayed unto the Lord for direction what to do." JFB puts Samuel at "about fifty-four years of age," which is a guess, since the text gives no age. It says a visible monarch "would necessarily tend to throw out of view their unseen King and Head." Guzik thinks appointing his sons was probably Samuel's mistake. He also argues that a king was part of God's plan (Deut 17) and that the problem was the motive, "like all the nations." He even suggests that, had Israel waited, David might have been Israel's first king. That is a reasonable speculation but nowhere in the text. Guzik links "they have rejected me" to the cry "We have no king but Caesar" (John 19:15).

#### Samuel's Warning Against Kings (vv10–18)
- **"He will take" (vv11–17).** The word "take" (*laqach*) appears four times, and the whole warning is one long list of what a king will claim. The people imagine a king who gives. Samuel describes a king who takes.
- **"Your sons… to his chariots and to be his horsemen and to run before his chariots" (v11).** A standing army and a royal escort. Chariots were the prestige weapon of ancient kings. Absalom and Adonijah later ran with fifty men before their chariots (2 Sam 15:1; 1 Kgs 1:5). Deuteronomy had warned the king not to "multiply horses for himself" (Deut 17:16).
- **"Some to plow his ground… and to make his implements of war" (v12).** Forced labor on royal lands and in royal workshops. Free Israelite farmers would work the king's fields before their own.
- **"Your daughters to be perfumers and cooks and bakers" (v13).** A royal court needed a large household staff.
- **"The best of your fields and vineyards and olive orchards… give them to his servants" (v14).** Land in Israel was a family inheritance from God, not to be sold off permanently (Lev 25:23). Kings would seize it to reward their officials. Saul did this (22:7), and Ahab's theft of Naboth's vineyard is the classic case (1 Kgs 21).
- **"The tenth of your grain… the tenth of your flocks" (vv15, 17).** The tithe belonged to the LORD (Lev 27:30–32). A king would demand a tenth of his own, a second tax. "And you shall be his slaves" (v17). A nation God had freed from slavery in Egypt would hand itself back into bondage.
- **"In that day you will cry out because of your king… but the LORD will not answer you" (v18).** In Egypt Israel cried out and God heard (Exod 2:23–24; 3:7). In the time of the judges they cried out and God sent deliverers. But a burden they chose for themselves, against his warning, they will have to carry. Every point of this warning came true, most clearly under Solomon, whose forced labor, taxes and huge court (1 Kgs 4:7, 22–28; 5:13–14) led the northern tribes to beg his son, "Lighten the hard service of your father" (1 Kgs 12:4). When Rehoboam refused, the kingdom split.
- **Commentators:** JFB fills these verses with comparisons to "Oriental sovereigns" of his own day, who "claim a right to the services of any of their subjects at pleasure." Those comparisons reflect nineteenth-century travel writing, but his basic point stands: Samuel described how kings in that part of the world actually behaved. Guzik observes that most kings are takers who expect to be served, while Jesus "came not to be served but to serve" (Matt 20:28). He also notes that a clear warning creates accountability: Israel could never say they had not been told. The Tyndale notes sum it up: they asked for a leader and would get a ruler.

#### The LORD Grants Israel's Request (vv19–22)
- **"But the people refused to obey the voice of Samuel. And they said, 'No!'" (v19).** God told Samuel to obey their voice. They will not obey his. The flat "No!" leaves no room for discussion.
- **"That we also may be like all the nations" (v20).** They say it again, and this time openly as the goal, not just the pattern.
- **"Our king may judge us and go out before us and fight our battles" (v20).** This is the saddest line in the chapter. The LORD had just fought their battle at Mizpah while they stood and watched (7:10). Moses had told them, "The LORD your God who goes before you will himself fight for you" (Deut 1:30). Now they want a man to do it. The first king they get will lose his life in battle on Mount Gilboa (ch 31).
- **"He repeated them in the ears of the LORD" (v21).** Samuel takes the people's words to God, as a prophet should. He does not hide them, and he does not argue with God on his own behalf.
- **"Obey their voice and make them a king" (v22).** God gives the final word. "Go every man to his city." The king will not be chosen by vote at this meeting. God will choose him, as chapter 9 shows. Israel will get the king they asked for, and they will learn through him what they would not learn from Samuel's warning.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the people "sneered at Samuel's description as a bugbear to frighten them," and "insisted on being made like all the other nations." Henry notes they would not be talked out of it: "We will have a king over us." Guzik points out that Israel already had a warrior King who had just won their last battle for them, and that giving them a king did not remove God from his throne. Resisting God's rule never removes it. It only loses the blessing of it. He also notes the pattern that will follow: God gives Israel "their king," Saul, and when he fails, "his king," David (16:1).

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel's desire for a king was not wrong in itself. Hannah had sung of God's king (2:10), and God had promised one. What was wrong was wanting a king *instead of* God rather than a king *under* God. The rest of the Bible tells how God answers the longing beneath the request. He gives them Saul, the king the people chose, and then David, "a man after his own heart" (13:14), and promises David a son whose throne would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16). That promise is fulfilled in Jesus, who is both God and the anointed King, so that in him the choice between a human king and God as King disappears. He is the King who goes out before his people and fights their battle (Col 2:15). And where Samuel's king would "take" sons, daughters, fields and freedom, this King says, "The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).
