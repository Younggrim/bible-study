*Setting:* The early years of Saul's reign, around 1050–1045 BC. Saul has been anointed (ch 10), proven in battle against the Ammonites (ch 11) and confirmed as king at Gilgal, where Samuel warned that king and people alike must follow the LORD or be swept away (ch 12). Now the real enemy comes into view. The Philistines were a sea people who had settled on the southern coast of Canaan in five city-states (Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath and Ekron). They had crushed Israel at Aphek and taken the ark (ch 4), and they still kept garrisons deep in Israel's hill country. When Israel asked for a king, part of what they wanted was someone to "fight our battles" (8:20). Chapter 13 shows the new king facing that test, and failing a deeper one. It is the first of three episodes (chs 13, 14 and 15) in which Saul loses his kingdom, matching the three steps by which he gained it (chs 9–11).

#### Saul Fights the Philistines (vv1–7)
- **"Saul lived for one year and then became king, and when he had reigned for two years" (v1).** This verse is one of the hardest in the Old Testament to translate. It follows the usual formula for a king's reign (as in 2 Sam 5:4: "David was thirty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned forty years"), but the Hebrew literally reads "Saul was a son of a year when he became king, and two years he reigned." Saul cannot have been one year old, and he had a grown son, so a number has almost certainly dropped out in copying. Some Greek manuscripts read "thirty years old." Paul says Saul's reign lasted forty years (Acts 13:21), which may include the years of his son Ish-bosheth or be a round number. The ESV translates the Hebrew as it stands and footnotes the problem. Other translations fill in "thirty" and "forty-two." Nothing in the story depends on the exact figures.
- **"Saul chose three thousand men" (v2).** Instead of the huge militia that rescued Jabesh (11:8), Saul forms a small standing force and sends the rest home. Two thousand stay with him at Michmash in the hill country near Bethel, and a thousand go with **Jonathan** at Gibeah, Saul's hometown. This is the first time we meet Jonathan, already old enough to command troops.
- **"Jonathan defeated the garrison of the Philistines that was at Geba" (v3).** The Hebrew word (*netsiv*) can mean a garrison, a military post or an officer's pillar or marker. The Philistines had a post at Geba, just a few miles from the king's home. Samuel had mentioned a Philistine garrison at "Gibeath-elohim" (10:5), which may be the same place. Geba and Michmash faced each other across a deep, steep-sided valley, which becomes the stage for chapter 14.
- **"Let the Hebrews hear" (v3).** "Hebrews" was usually what foreigners called Israelites, often with a sneer (4:6, 9; 14:11; Gen 39:14). Saul may be taking the insult and turning it into a rallying cry. The shofar (ram's horn) was the standard signal to assemble an army (Judg 3:27; 6:34).
- **"All Israel heard it said that Saul had defeated the garrison" (v4).** Jonathan did it, but Saul gets the credit. Commanders were often credited with their troops' victories, so this may not be dishonest. Still, it is a small note that will grow louder. Saul will later be jealous when someone else gets the praise (18:7–8).
- **"Israel had become a stench to the Philistines" (v4).** The verb (*ba'ash*) means "to stink." Jacob used it when his sons' violence made him "stinking" to the Canaanites (Gen 34:30). After decades of quiet occupation, Israel has struck back, and now there will be war. The people are called out to meet Saul at **Gilgal**, down in the Jordan valley, the place where his kingdom had just been confirmed.
- **"Thirty thousand chariots and six thousand horsemen" (v5).** Thirty thousand chariots would be far more than any ancient army fielded, even the Egyptians or the Hittites. Most scholars think a copyist slipped and "three thousand" (*sheloshet*) became "thirty thousand" (*sheloshim*); some Greek and Syriac copies read three thousand, and many translations follow them. Either way, Israel had no chariots at all. The troops "like the sand on the seashore" is the phrase used for the Canaanite coalition Joshua faced (Josh 11:4).
- **"Encamped in Michmash, to the east of Beth-aven" (v5).** The Philistines take Saul's own base. Beth-aven means "house of wickedness" or "house of nothing," and Hosea later uses it as a mocking name for Bethel, "house of God," after it became a center of idol worship (Hos 4:15). Here it is probably a separate small town near Bethel.
- **"The people hid themselves in caves and in holes and in rocks and in tombs and in cisterns" (v6).** The hill country of Benjamin is full of limestone caves, rock clefts and cisterns cut to hold rainwater. The list piles up to show total panic. "Hard pressed" is the same root used of Israel's oppression in Judges (Judg 2:15; 10:9).
- **"Some Hebrews crossed the fords of the Jordan to the land of Gad and Gilead" (v7).** Others flee the country altogether, east across the river. The Hebrew plays on words: the "Hebrews" (*'ivrim*) "crossed over" (*'avru*). The people who got their name from crossing over are now crossing back out of the land. Those who stay with Saul at Gilgal follow him "trembling."
- **Commentators:** JFB reads Saul's 3,000 as a kind of bodyguard and standing force, and offers three ways to handle the chariot number, including the copyist's slip of *shelosh* for *sheloshim*. Guzik makes the same textual suggestion. He notes that Israel had settled for a "peace" of submission to the Philistines and that Jonathan's raid broke it. When God's people stop accepting the enemy's terms, he says, the enemy fights back. He also faults Saul for taking credit for his son's victory. Henry is blunt about what the panic shows: "When men are lifted up in self-sufficiency, they are often led into folly." He adds that the people had wanted a king to save them, and now they have one and are hiding in holes.

#### Saul's Unlawful Sacrifice (vv8–23)
- **"He waited seven days, the time appointed by Samuel" (v8).** At Saul's anointing Samuel had told him, "go down before me to Gilgal… Seven days you shall wait, until I come to you and show you what you shall do" (10:8). Many readers think that standing instruction is what is being tested now, perhaps years later. Others, including the Tyndale notes, think Samuel gave a fresh instruction for this crisis. Either way, Saul knew the prophet's word: wait for Samuel, who would offer the sacrifice and give the LORD's direction.
- **"Samuel did not come… and the people were scattering" (v8).** The pressure is real. Every day Saul's army shrinks, and the Philistines are massing a few miles up the road. Waiting looks like losing. That is exactly what makes it a test of faith.
- **"Bring the burnt offering here to me, and the peace offerings" (v9).** A burnt offering was wholly burned on the altar, a gift of total dedication to God. Peace offerings were shared meals of fellowship (Lev 1; 3). Armies commonly offered sacrifices before battle to seek God's favor (7:9). Whether Saul himself handled the sacrifice or had a priest do it at his command, the sin is the same: he went ahead without the prophet God had told him to wait for. Other kings later offered sacrifices without rebuke (2 Sam 6:17; 1 Kgs 8:62–64). The problem here is not simply that a king touched an altar. It is that he disobeyed a direct word from the LORD through his prophet.
- **"As soon as he had finished… behold, Samuel came" (v10).** The timing is painful. Samuel arrives as the smoke is still rising, apparently still within the seventh day. A little more patience, and none of this would have happened. Saul goes out "to greet him," literally "to bless him." He seems to have no idea he has done anything wrong.
- **"What have you done?" (v11).** This is not a request for information. It is the question God asked Eve in the garden (Gen 3:13).
- **Saul's answer (vv11–12).** He gives three reasons, all about pressure: the people were scattering, "you did not come within the days appointed," and the Philistines were gathering. Then a pious one: "I have not sought the favor of the LORD." "Sought the favor" is literally "softened the face," the idiom for appeasing someone. Saul treats the sacrifice almost like a lucky charm to secure God's help before the battle, and he blames Samuel for being late.
- **"So I forced myself" (v12).** The verb (*'aphaq*, in its reflexive form) usually means "to restrain oneself," as when Joseph held back his tears (Gen 43:31; 45:1). Here, as the lexicon notes, it means the opposite, "to force or compel oneself." Saul presents his disobedience as a reluctant act of duty: "I didn't want to, but I had to." He never says, "I sinned."
- **"You have done foolishly" (v13).** In the Bible "foolish" (*sakal*) is not about intelligence but about moral and spiritual blindness, acting as if God were not there. Saul had all the facts and still chose the wrong thing.
- **"The LORD would have established your kingdom over Israel forever" (v13).** A striking statement. Saul's family could have had a lasting dynasty. God's choice of Judah for kingship (Gen 49:10) was still in the background, and how both would have fit together is not explained. Samuel only says what was lost.
- **"Your kingdom shall not continue" (v14).** Saul himself is not removed yet; he will reign for many more years. What is lost here is his *dynasty*. None of his sons will succeed him. Chapter 15 goes further, and there the LORD rejects Saul himself as king (15:23, 26).
- **"The LORD has sought out a man after his own heart" (v14).** This is the first hint of David. Paul quotes it in his sermon at Pisidian Antioch: "I have found in David the son of Jesse a man after my heart, who will do all my will" (Acts 13:22). The phrase probably carries two ideas together: a man God has chosen according to his own purpose (the same Hebrew expression in 2 Sam 7:21 means "according to your own heart," that is, by God's will), and a man whose heart is set on God's will. David was far from sinless. But when confronted, he said "I have sinned" (2 Sam 12:13), and Saul never truly did. "Prince" (*nagid*) is the same title used at Saul's anointing (9:16; 10:1), the LORD's appointed leader under the LORD's authority.
- **"Samuel arose and went up from Gilgal" (v15).** Samuel leaves without offering any sacrifice or giving any battle instructions. Saul gets what he wanted, a sacrifice before battle, and loses what he needed, the word of the LORD.
- **"About six hundred men" (v15).** Down from three thousand. With those he moves back up into the hills to **Geba**, opposite the Philistine camp at Michmash (v16). The two armies now face each other across the gorge.
- **"Raiders came out of the camp of the Philistines in three companies" (vv17–18).** The Philistines send raiding parties north toward Ophrah, west toward Beth-horon and east toward the wilderness above the Valley of Zeboim (the "valley of hyenas"). Raiders plundered villages and terrorized the population. They did not go south, where Saul's army sat. Israel's land is being stripped while its king can only watch.
- **"There was no blacksmith to be found throughout all the land of Israel" (v19).** The Philistines kept a monopoly on metalworking, especially the newer, harder iron, so that "the Hebrews" could not make swords or spears. Occupying powers have done the same throughout history, disarming a conquered people by controlling their smiths. Archaeology shows that iron tools became common in Philistine areas earlier than in the Israelite hill country, which fits.
- **"The charge was two-thirds of a shekel" (v21).** The Hebrew word here is *pim*, and for centuries no one knew what it meant. The KJV guessed "a file." Then archaeologists found small stone weights inscribed *pym*, weighing about two-thirds of a shekel, and the puzzle was solved. It was a price, and a steep one, perhaps several days' wages just to sharpen a plow. Israelite farmers had to go down to Philistine towns and pay Philistine prices to keep their tools working.
- **"Neither sword nor spear… but Saul and Jonathan his son had them" (v22).** The army of six hundred is armed with farm tools, slings and bows. Only the king and his son have proper weapons. Humanly speaking, the situation is hopeless. That is the setting for what Jonathan does next.
- **"The garrison of the Philistines went out to the pass of Michmash" (v23).** The chapter ends with the Philistines moving to hold the crossing point of the gorge, and the scene is set for chapter 14.
- **Commentators:** All three see the heart of Saul's sin as disobedience to a direct command, not the mere act of sacrifice. JFB says Saul was "invading the priest's office" and was "more ambitious of gaining the glory of a triumph to himself than ascribing it to God." Henry: "Saul broke the order expressly given by Samuel," and "Sins which appear to us very little, have dangerous consequences." Guzik counts Saul's excuses and notes that each one either shifts blame or puts feelings ahead of obedience. He contrasts Saul and David at length: Saul used God to reach his own goals, while David made God himself the goal, and David repented when confronted. The Tyndale notes are more cautious than Guzik about the priestly question, pointing out that David and Solomon offered sacrifices too, and placing the fault in ignoring Samuel's instruction. On the blacksmiths, Henry remarks, "See how politic the Philistines were when they had power," and faults Saul for not addressing the problem early in his reign.

#### Pointing to Christ
Saul faced the enemy with a shrinking army and a prophet who seemed late, and he could not bring himself to wait for God. He took matters into his own hands and called it piety. The "man after his own heart" would be a king who trusted the LORD with his life. That man pointed beyond himself to David's greater Son, who said, "I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me" (John 6:38). In the wilderness, under pressure, Jesus refused to force God's hand (Matt 4:5–7). In Gethsemane, with his followers scattering, he prayed, "not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). Saul's sacrifice was an attempt to secure God's favor without obedience. Jesus offered the one sacrifice that was itself perfect obedience (Heb 10:5–10), and his kingdom, unlike Saul's, will continue forever.
