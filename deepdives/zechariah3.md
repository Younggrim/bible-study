*Setting:* This is the fourth of Zechariah's eight night visions, all given on one night in February 519 BC (1:7). The small community that came home from Babylon was rebuilding the temple, and the first three visions promised that God would judge the nations, return to Jerusalem and fill the city with his presence. But a hard question remained. The exile had happened because of Israel's sin (1:2–6). How could a people with that record serve a holy God again? The temple would need a priesthood, and the priests were as stained as everyone else. Ezekiel had charged the priests before the exile with profaning God's holy things (Ezek 22:26). Zechariah 3 answers in a courtroom scene. Joshua the high priest, representing the whole people, stands accused before God, and God himself cleanses and restores him. Then the vision looks beyond Joshua to a coming figure, "my servant the Branch," and a day when the land's guilt will be removed at once. Unlike the other visions, this one has no interpreting question from Zechariah, and Zechariah himself steps into the scene (v5).

#### A Vision of Joshua the High Priest (vv1–5)
- **"Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD" (v1).** Joshua (Jeshua in Ezra) was the son of Jehozadak, the high priest carried into exile (1 Chr 6:15). He led the people's worship alongside the governor Zerubbabel (Ezra 3:2; Hag 1:1). "Standing before" is the language of priestly service (Deut 10:8; 2 Chr 29:11), but also of standing trial. Here it is both. The scene is the heavenly court, and the angel of the LORD presides as judge.
- **"Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him" (v1).** The Hebrew is *hassatan*, "the satan," literally "the adversary" or "the accuser," with the article, as in Job 1–2. The verb "to accuse" (*sitno*) comes from the same root: "the accuser standing to accuse." The right hand was where an accuser stood in court (Ps 109:6). In the Old Testament the word can describe any adversary (1 Sam 29:4; 1 Kgs 11:14), but here, as in Job, it is a heavenly being whose work is to bring charges against God's people. The New Testament reveals him fully as "the accuser of our brothers… who accuses them day and night before our God" (Rev 12:10). The charge is not stated, but the filthy clothes of v3 make it obvious: this priest, and the nation he represents, is unfit to serve God.
- **"The LORD rebuke you, O Satan!" (v2).** God does not argue the case. He does not deny that Joshua is unclean. He rebukes the accuser. The ground is God's own choice: "The LORD who has chosen Jerusalem rebuke you!" (see 1:17; 2:12). Jude 9 quotes the same words in the mouth of Michael the archangel, who would not bring a slanderous judgment against the devil but said, "The Lord rebuke you." The rebuke belongs to God.
- **"Is not this a brand plucked from the fire?" (v2).** A brand is a burning stick snatched from the fire before it is consumed. Amos used the same image for Israel, rescued half-burned from judgment (Amos 4:11). Joshua and the people had been through the fire of exile and had been pulled out by God. The question carries the argument: God did not rescue this stick from the fire only to throw it back in.
- **"Clothed with filthy garments" (v3).** The Hebrew adjective (*tso'im*) is used only in this passage. It is related to the word for human excrement (Deut 23:13) and for the "filth" of Jerusalem's daughters that God would wash away (Isa 4:4). This is not mere dust but defilement. For a high priest, whose garments were made "for glory and for beauty" (Exod 28:2) and had to be ceremonially pure, it was unthinkable. The point is that the priest, and the people in him, had nothing clean to offer. "All our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment" (Isa 64:6).
- **"Remove the filthy garments from him" (v4).** The angel speaks to "those who were standing before him," other heavenly attendants. Joshua does not take off the clothes himself. He receives the cleansing.
- **"Behold, I have taken your iniquity away from you" (v4).** The angel explains the action. The removal of clothes is the removal of guilt. The verb (*he'evarti*) means "I have caused to pass away." God does not cover over the dirt. He takes it off.
- **"And I will clothe you with pure vestments" (v4).** Joshua is not left naked. The Hebrew word (*machalatsot*) means festive or rich garments, robes for a celebration (Isa 3:22). He is dressed in clothing fit for the presence of God. Isaiah's song sounds the same note: "he has clothed me with the garments of salvation; he has covered me with the robe of righteousness" (Isa 61:10).
- **"Let them put a clean turban on his head" (v5).** Here Zechariah, caught up in the scene, cannot keep silent. He asks for the finishing touch. The high priest's turban carried a gold plate engraved "Holy to the LORD" (Exod 28:36–38), and by wearing it, the high priest "shall bear any guilt from the holy things" of the people. Restoring the turban restored Joshua's office as mediator.
- **"And the angel of the LORD was standing by" (v5).** The angel of the LORD watches over the whole process, approving it. The cleansing is his work.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Joshua "represents the Israel of God, who are all as an unclean thing," and that "The Lord rebuke thee" is the best answer to the accuser, ending the case rather than debating it. On the brand he argues that those God has rescued from great trouble he will not cast back into it. JFB reads the scene as Jerusalem on trial in its high priest, and the festal robes as a picture of righteousness given rather than earned, fulfilled in Jesus. JFB frames the chapter as God's answer to people who felt too unworthy to offer acceptable sacrifice or keep building. Guzik stresses that Joshua was rescued by grace, not merit, and notes that the Hebrew word for "filthy" is unusually strong. He quotes Spurgeon on Christ answering the accuser.

#### The Branch (vv6–10)
- **"The angel of the LORD solemnly assured Joshua" (v6).** The verb means to testify or warn solemnly, as a witness under oath. What follows is a formal charge.
- **"If you will walk in my ways and keep my charge" (v7).** Cleansing is free, but it calls for faithfulness. The two conditions cover personal life ("walk in my ways") and priestly duty ("keep my charge," the regular phrase for the priests' and Levites' service, Lev 8:35; Num 3:7; Ezek 44:16). Grace does not cancel obedience. It makes it possible.
- **"Then you shall rule my house and have charge of my courts" (v7).** "Rule" is literally "judge." After the exile, with no king, the high priest gained more authority over the temple and its courts than before. This verse foreshadows that.
- **"I will give you the right of access among those who are standing here" (v7).** The most remarkable promise. "Those standing here" are the heavenly attendants of vv4–5. A human priest is given access among the angels in God's presence. The phrase may mean the right to come and go freely in God's court, as one who belongs there.
- **"You and your friends who sit before you, for they are men who are a sign" (v8).** Joshua's "friends" are his fellow priests, perhaps sitting before him in council. "Sign" (*mopheth*) means a portent or omen, a person or event that points to something beyond itself. Isaiah said he and his children were "signs and portents in Israel" (Isa 8:18). The cleansed priests were a living preview of something greater.
- **"Behold, I will bring my servant the Branch" (v8).** Here is what they pointed to. "Branch" (*tsemach*, "sprout, shoot") was already a title for the Messiah. Jeremiah had promised, "I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he shall reign as king" (Jer 23:5; 33:15). Isaiah spoke of "the branch of the LORD" (Isa 4:2) and a shoot from Jesse's stump (Isa 11:1, using a different word). The family tree of David had been cut down to a stump by the exile. God promises a new shoot from it. "My servant" joins the Branch to Isaiah's Servant of the LORD, who would bear the sins of many (Isa 52:13–53:12). Zechariah 6:12–13 will add that "the man whose name is the Branch" will build the temple and be a priest on his throne, uniting the two offices that Joshua and Zerubbabel held separately.
- **"On the stone that I have set before Joshua, on a single stone with seven eyes" (v9).** This is one of the most debated images in the book. The stone may be a building stone for the temple (perhaps the top stone of 4:7), a precious stone for the high priest's garments, or a symbol of the Messiah himself. The Old Testament already called the coming one a stone (Ps 118:22; Isa 28:16), and the New Testament takes this up (1 Pet 2:4–7). "Seven eyes" may mean seven facets on a gem, or the all-seeing watchfulness of God. Zechariah 4:10 says the seven are "the eyes of the LORD, which range through the whole earth," and Revelation 5:6 pictures the Lamb with "seven eyes, which are the seven spirits of God sent out into all the earth."
- **"I will engrave its inscription" (v9).** The high priest's turban plate and shoulder stones were engraved like a seal (Exod 28:9–11, 36). What God would engrave is not said. Some early Christian writers saw a hint of the wounds of Christ. Others connect it with the "Holy to the LORD" of the turban. The text leaves it open.
- **"I will remove the iniquity of this land in a single day" (v9).** This is the climax. Once a year, on the Day of Atonement, the high priest offered sacrifices for the sins of the whole people, and the scapegoat carried them away (Lev 16:21–22, 30). That had to be repeated every year. God promises a day when the land's guilt will be removed once, completely. What happened to Joshua personally in vv4–5 will happen to the whole land.
- **"Every one of you will invite his neighbor to come under his vine and under his fig tree" (v10).** This was Israel's classic picture of peace and plenty. It described Solomon's reign (1 Kgs 4:25), and Micah used it for the messianic age: "they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree, and no one shall make them afraid" (Mic 4:4). Removal of sin leads to peace with neighbors. The order matters: first atonement, then shalom.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the priests "are set for signs, for types and figures of Christ's priesthood," and calls Christ "the branch from which all our fruit must be gathered." On v9 he writes that Christ took away sin "in one day, that day in which he suffered and died." JFB describes the Branch as "a tender branch from the almost extinct royal line of David," identifies the stone as Christ the cornerstone and reads "one day" as fulfilled in Christ's single sacrifice "once for all." He gives two views of the seven eyes: God's watchful providence, or (following Calvin) Christ's own perfect gifts. Guzik also reads the stone as the Messiah, full of wisdom. He mentions the early Christian reading of the engraving as wounds without insisting on it, and notes that Joshua's conditional charge echoes God's word to the first Joshua (Josh 1:7).

#### Pointing to Christ
Zechariah 3 is one of the clearest pictures of the gospel in the Old Testament. The accused stands guilty and filthy, the accusation is true, and the only defense is God's choice and God's rescue. God answers the accuser not by pretending the sin is not there but by taking it away and clothing the sinner in clean garments. Paul puts the same courtroom in plain words: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies" (Rom 8:33–34). And the vision is honest that Joshua was only a sign. The Branch it pointed to is Jesus, David's shoot who sprang up from a stump (Isa 11:1; 53:2), the Servant who bore sin, the stone the builders rejected. The "single day" when iniquity would be removed came on a Friday outside Jerusalem, when Jesus offered "a single sacrifice for sins" for all time (Heb 10:12). His name in Hebrew is the same as the high priest's in this vision, Joshua, "the LORD saves." Because of him, the accuser has been "thrown down" (Rev 12:10–11), and the clean turban reading "Holy to the LORD" is given to every believer.
