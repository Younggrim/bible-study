*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Lilies. A Maskil of the Sons of Korah; a love song." After the defeat and lament of Psalm 44, this psalm bursts out in celebration. It is the only wedding song in the Psalter: a court poet sings at the marriage of an Israelite king to a foreign princess. "Lilies" (*shoshannim*) is probably the name of a tune (also over Pss 69 and 80). "A love song" is literally "a song of loves" or "a song of the beloved." Which king? Older writers often thought of Solomon, who married Pharaoh's daughter (1 Kgs 3:1). A few have suggested Ahab and Jezebel, a princess from Sidon near Tyre, because of the "ivory palaces" (1 Kgs 22:39) and the mention of Tyre, but it is hard to imagine that marriage being celebrated in Israel's hymnbook. The Tyndale notes suggest the song may have been used at more than one royal wedding. But something in the psalm is bigger than any wedding in Jerusalem. The king is addressed as "God" and promised an eternal throne (v6), and Hebrews 1:8–9 applies vv6–7 directly to Jesus. JFB notes that "all ancient Jewish and Christian interpreters regarded this Psalm as an allegory." The Aramaic Targum, an ancient Jewish paraphrase, says of v2: "Your beauty, O King Messiah, is greater than the sons of men." Most Christian readers take it as a real royal song that points beyond itself to Christ the King and his bride, the church.

#### I Address My Verses to the King (vv1–5)
- **"My heart overflows with a pleasing theme" (v1).** "Overflows" (*rachash*) is used only here in the Bible. It means to bubble up or stir, like a pot coming to the boil. JFB: "boiling up," as a fountain overflows. The poet can hardly hold in what he wants to say.
- **"My tongue is like the pen of a ready scribe" (v1).** "Ready" (*mahir*) means skilled or quick. Ezra was "a scribe skilled in the Law of Moses" (Ezra 7:6), the same word. The words flow as fast as an expert can write.
- **"You are the most handsome of the sons of men" (v2).** The Hebrew uses an unusual doubled form, as if to say "beautiful, beautiful you are." Kings were expected to look the part (1 Sam 9:2; 16:12). But Isaiah says the Messiah "had no form or majesty that we should look at him" (Isa 53:2). Christian readers have understood this beauty, then, as the beauty of his character and person, not mere looks. Guzik makes the same point.
- **"Grace is poured upon your lips" (v2).** His words are gracious, charming and kind. Luke says that when Jesus preached in Nazareth, "all spoke well of him and marveled at the gracious words that were coming from his mouth" (Luke 4:22). JFB cites that verse. Even officers sent to arrest him came back saying, "No one ever spoke like this man!" (John 7:46).
- **"Therefore God has blessed you forever" (v2).** "Therefore" may mean "because" his words are gracious, or it may mean "this shows that" God has blessed him. Either way, the blessing is "forever," the first hint of a reign beyond one lifetime.
- **"Gird your sword on your thigh, O mighty one" (v3).** The bridegroom is also a warrior. "Mighty one" (*gibbor*) is the word used in Isaiah's title for the coming king, "Mighty God" (*El Gibbor*, Isa 9:6). "Splendor and majesty" are words normally used of God himself (96:6; 104:1).
- **"Ride out victoriously for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness" (v4).** The king fights not for land or plunder but for truth, humility and justice. That is an unusual war aim. "Meekness" is the humility that depends on God (37:11). Revelation 19:11 pictures Christ on a white horse: "in righteousness he judges and makes war."
- **"Let your right hand teach you awesome deeds!" (v4).** His own strong hand will show him what great things to do. "Awesome deeds" are fearsome acts, like God's at the exodus (Deut 10:21).
- **"Your arrows are sharp in the heart of the king's enemies" (v5).** The battle scene is quick and decisive. "The peoples fall under you." The picture fits Psalm 2, where the nations are given to God's Son.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the whole psalm as Christ and his church: "There is more in Christ to engage our love, than there is or can be in any creature." On v5 he turns the arrows into conviction of sin: "The arrows of conviction are very terrible in the hearts of sinners," until they are humbled and reconciled. JFB links the warrior to Isaiah 9:6 and Revelation 19, and reads "ride prosperously" as "conduct a successful war." Guzik notes that the king fights for truth, humility and righteousness, not conquest for its own sake.

#### Your Throne, O God (vv6–9)
- **"Your throne, O God, is forever and ever" (v6).** The most striking line in the psalm. The poet, speaking to the king, calls him *Elohim*, "God." In the Old Testament, *elohim* is sometimes used of human judges or rulers as God's representatives (Exod 21:6, where it may mean "the judges"; Ps 82:6). Some translations soften it: "Your divine throne endures forever" (RSV) or "Your throne is God's throne." But the plain reading, followed by the ancient Greek translation and by Hebrews 1:8, is "Your throne, O God." JFB: "No lawful construction can be devised to change the sense here given."
- **"Forever and ever" (v6).** God promised David a throne that would last forever (2 Sam 7:13, 16). No human king of Judah lasted forever. This line looks past them to the Son of David whose kingdom "will have no end" (Luke 1:33).
- **"The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of uprightness" (v6).** The scepter was the royal rod, the symbol of power. His power is always used rightly. "Uprightness" means level, straight, fair.
- **"You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness" (v7).** Both halves matter. A ruler who loves goodness but tolerates evil is not upright. Jesus loved sinners and hated sin.
- **"Therefore God, your God, has anointed you" (v7).** Now the king, called "God" in v6, has a God over him who anoints him. Hebrews reads this as the Father speaking to the Son: "Of the Son he says, 'Your throne, O God, is forever and ever'" (Heb 1:8). Christians see here a hint of what the New Testament makes clear, that the Son is God and yet distinct from the Father. Guzik takes this as part of the Bible's foundation for the Trinity. "Anointed" is the verb *mashach*, from which comes *Mashiach*, "Messiah," "the Anointed One." In Greek it is *Christos*.
- **"With the oil of gladness beyond your companions" (v7).** Kings were anointed with oil at their coronation (1 Sam 16:13), and oil was also poured on guests at feasts as a sign of joy (23:5; Isa 61:3, "the oil of gladness instead of mourning"). His "companions" are other kings (JFB: "other kings") or his fellow Israelites. He is honored above them all. Hebrews uses the verse to show the Son is greater than the angels (Heb 1:4–9).
- **"Your robes are all fragrant with myrrh and aloes and cassia" (v8).** Expensive perfumes, imported from Arabia, Africa and India. Myrrh and cassia were ingredients of the holy anointing oil of the tabernacle (Exod 30:23–24). Myrrh was among the gifts brought to the infant Jesus (Matt 2:11). Nicodemus brought "a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about seventy-five pounds" to bury him (John 19:39). The spices of the wedding and the spices of the tomb are the same.
- **"From ivory palaces stringed instruments make you glad" (v8).** Palaces with walls or furniture inlaid with carved ivory panels. Solomon had a great ivory throne (1 Kgs 10:18), and Ahab built "the ivory house" (1 Kgs 22:39). Archaeologists have found carved ivory panels at Samaria. Amos condemned the "houses of ivory" of the rich (Amos 3:15).
- **"Daughters of kings are among your ladies of honor" (v9).** Royal marriages sealed alliances, and foreign princesses joined the court (1 Kgs 11:1–3). The Tyndale notes point this out. Christian readers have heard here the nations brought into Christ's kingdom.
- **"At your right hand stands the queen in gold of Ophir" (v9).** The right hand is the place of highest honor (110:1; 1 Kgs 2:19). Ophir was a land famous for fine gold, probably in southern Arabia or East Africa. Solomon's ships brought gold from there (1 Kgs 9:28). "The queen" may be the bride herself, now about to be addressed, or the queen mother.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The throne of this almighty King is established for ever," and "The Mediator is God, else he had been neither able to do the Mediator's work, nor fit to wear the Mediator's crown." He reads the oil of gladness as the Spirit given without measure. JFB keeps the reading "O God" against the alternatives, citing the ancient versions and Hebrews 1:8. Guzik reads the ivory palaces as pointing to the King's heavenly home, which is an application more than the plain sense of the verse.

#### Hear, O Daughter (vv10–15)
- **"Hear, O daughter, and consider, and incline your ear" (v10).** The poet turns to the bride. "Daughter" is a tender address from an older man to a young woman (Ruth 2:8). Three verbs: hear, see ("consider" is literally "see"), and lean in to listen.
- **"Forget your people and your father's house" (v10).** She is a foreigner, and the call is to leave her old loyalties behind, including, it would follow, her old gods. Ruth, a Moabite, did exactly this: "Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16). Genesis 2:24 says a man shall "leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife," and here the bride leaves too. JFB links it to Abraham's call to leave his father's house (Gen 12:1). Jesus spoke of leaving house and family for his sake (Mark 10:29–30).
- **"And the king will desire your beauty" (v11).** The motive is not loss but love. She gives up her old home because the king delights in her.
- **"Since he is your lord, bow to him" (v11).** "Lord" here is *'adon*, master, the word Sarah used of Abraham (Gen 18:12; 1 Pet 3:6). "Bow" is the word for bowing down in homage or worship. For an ancient queen it meant honoring the king. For Christian readers it becomes the church's worship of Christ.
- **"The people of Tyre will seek your favor with gifts" (v12).** Literally "the daughter of Tyre." Tyre was the rich Phoenician trading port on the coast north of Israel, famous for purple dye and sea trade (Ezek 27). Hiram of Tyre supplied cedar for Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 5). The wealthiest neighbors will come courting the new queen. JFB: Tyre represents "the richest nations."
- **"All glorious is the princess in her chamber" (v13).** "Princess" is literally "the king's daughter." It may mean the bride as daughter of a foreign king, or as one now made royal by marriage. The KJV read "all glorious within," which Christian writers often took as inner beauty. The ESV reads "in her chamber," inside the palace, as she prepares to come out.
- **"Robes interwoven with gold… many-colored robes" (vv13–14).** Gold thread woven into the cloth, and embroidered garments. The same word is used for the embroidered work of the tabernacle curtains (Exod 26:36). Revelation says the bride of the Lamb is given "fine linen, bright and pure," which is "the righteous deeds of the saints" (Rev 19:8).
- **"She is led to the king, with her virgin companions following behind her" (v14).** The wedding procession. Bridesmaids accompanied the bride, as in Jesus' parable of the ten virgins (Matt 25:1–13).
- **"With joy and gladness they are led along as they enter the palace of the king" (v15).** The scene ends with the procession entering the palace. The words "joy and gladness" are the wedding words of Jeremiah: "the voice of mirth and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" (Jer 7:34; 33:11).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We must forget our carnal and sinful attachments and pursuits," and "There is nothing glorious in the old man or corrupt nature." He reads the bride's robe as Christ's righteousness given to her. He adds, "None are brought to Christ, but those whom the Father brings." Guzik ties v10 to Genesis 2:24, and sees the bride's glory as coming from her union with the king. JFB suggests the "raiment of needlework" may be tapestries spread for her to walk on, and calls "the king's daughter" a title of dignity.

#### In Place of Your Fathers (vv16–17)
- **"In place of your fathers shall be your sons" (v16).** The poet speaks again to the king (the Hebrew "your" is masculine). His royal line will continue. His sons will be greater than his ancestors.
- **"You will make them princes in all the earth" (v16).** Not just over Israel but over the whole earth. JFB compares a king who rules through viceroys. Christian readers have heard here the promise that Christ's people will "reign on the earth" with him (Rev 5:10; 2 Tim 2:12).
- **"I will cause your name to be remembered in all generations" (v17).** The poet promises the king lasting fame through this song. In a sense it came true: we are still reading it. But only one king's name is truly remembered and praised in every generation.
- **"Therefore nations will praise you forever and ever" (v17).** "Nations" and "forever" again. This was never literally true of Solomon or any other king of Judah. It is true of Jesus, to whom "every knee should bow" (Phil 2:10).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the sons who replace the fathers as the New Testament church, gathered from the Gentiles in place of the old Israel. He urges readers to keep up "the remembrance of Christ" so "that his name may endure for ever." JFB calls the praise "universal and perpetual."

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews does the pointing for us: "Of the Son he says, 'Your throne, O God, is forever and ever'" (Heb 1:8). Jesus is the King whose lips are full of grace, who rides out for truth and meekness and righteousness, who is called God and yet anointed by God, the Messiah, the Christ. And he is a bridegroom. John the Baptist called himself "the friend of the bridegroom" (John 3:29). Paul says Christ "loved the church and gave himself up for her… so that he might present the church to himself in splendor" (Eph 5:25–27). The bride is called to leave her father's house and bow to her Lord, not because she loses everything, but because the King desires her. The whole Bible ends at this wedding: "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Rev 19:9).
