*Setting:* Ezra 3 ended with the foundation of the second temple laid around 536 BC, and with a sound of joy and weeping "heard far away." Ezra 4 tells who heard it and what they did. The returned exiles were a small community in the Persian province of Judah. To the north lay Samaria, once the capital of the northern kingdom of Israel, now home to a mixed population descended from peoples the Assyrian kings had settled there after 722 BC (2 Kgs 17:24–41). Chapter 4 is the book's chapter on opposition, and it is not told in strict time order. Verses 1–5 and 24 cover the temple, from Cyrus (died 530 BC) to Darius I (522–486 BC). In between, verses 6–23 jump ahead to later attacks under Xerxes and Artaxerxes I, decades after the temple was finished, when the issue had become the city walls. The writer gathers the whole story of opposition in one place to show its pattern. One more feature stands out: at v8 the book switches from Hebrew to Aramaic, the international language of the Persian Empire, and stays in Aramaic until 6:18. The official letters are given in the language they were written in.

#### Adversaries Oppose the Rebuilding (vv1–6)
- **"The adversaries of Judah and Benjamin" (v1).** The writer names them by what they turned out to be before telling us what they said. Most of the returnees came from the tribes of Judah and Benjamin (1:5), the old southern kingdom. "The returned exiles" is literally "the sons of the exile" (*bene haggolah*), a name Ezra uses for the true covenant community.
- **"Let us build with you, for we worship your God as you do" (v2).** It sounds generous. But their own words give the problem away: they had worshiped him "ever since the days of Esarhaddon king of Assyria who brought us here." Esarhaddon (681–669 BC) continued the Assyrian policy of moving conquered peoples around the empire. 2 Kings 17:33 describes these settlers exactly: "they feared the LORD but also served their own gods." Their faith was a blend, the LORD added to a shelf of other gods. To share the temple would be to share that mixture.
- **"You have nothing to do with us" (v3).** Zerubbabel, Jeshua and the family heads answer together. This can sound harsh, but the returnees knew why their nation had been exiled: centuries of mixing the worship of the LORD with other gods (2 Kgs 17:7–18). The very first test of the new community was whether it would repeat that sin. They also had a legal point. Cyrus's decree named *them*, "as King Cyrus… has commanded us." The refusal was not a refusal to let outsiders come to God. Later, Gentiles who truly turned from idols were welcomed to keep the Passover with them (6:21).
- **The Samaritans.** These neighbors were the ancestors of the people later called Samaritans. The split between Jews and Samaritans deepened over the next centuries, and the Samaritans eventually built a rival temple on Mount Gerizim. Behind the woman's question at the well, "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, but you say that in Jerusalem is the place" (John 4:20), lies the history that starts here.
- **"Discouraged the people of Judah" (v4).** The Hebrew is literally "weakened the hands" of the people, a common picture for draining someone's courage and energy (Jer 38:4). It was a war of nerves more than open violence: intimidation, threats and making the builders "afraid to build."
- **"Bribed counselors against them" (v5).** They hired advisers or officials at the Persian court to frustrate the project. This went on "all the days of Cyrus… even until the reign of Darius," roughly 536 to 520 BC. Cyrus himself was busy with wars and died in 530, and his son Cambyses (530–522 BC) was occupied with conquering Egypt. A remote building project in a small province was easy to stall.
- **"In the reign of Ahasuerus… they wrote an accusation" (v6).** Ahasuerus is the Hebrew form of the Persian name Xerxes, the same king who appears in the book of Esther. He reigned 486–465 BC, so this verse leaps about fifty years past the temple's completion. "In the beginning of his reign" fits a moment when Egypt had revolted and a new king would be sensitive to any hint of rebellion in a province on the road to Egypt. The word "accusation" is *sitnah*, from the same root as *satan*, "adversary." Nothing more is said about this letter. It apparently failed.
- **Older and newer views on the kings.** Some older commentators tried to keep the chapter in strict time order by identifying "Ahasuerus" and "Artaxerxes" with Cambyses and a usurper who briefly held the throne in 522 BC. Most scholars now agree these are Xerxes and Artaxerxes I, because the names fit, and because vv12–16 are about walls and a city, not the temple.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the offer was a trick to slow the work, and that the refusal "was very just." JFB calls the adversaries "strangers settled in the land of Israel" and sees God's hand in the refusal: a joint project could have led to intermarriage and idolatry and would have blurred the line of descent through which the Messiah would come. JFB holds the old view that Cambyses was hostile to the Jews, but takes the Ahasuerus of v6 to be "the famous Xerxes." Guzik reads the offer as a political move to absorb or sabotage the work, notes that the neighbors' own words revealed a mixed faith, and treats vv6–23 as a survey that interrupts the story. He reads straight from v5 to v24 to follow the temple account.

#### The Letter to King Artaxerxes (vv7–16)
- **"In the days of Artaxerxes" (v7).** This is Artaxerxes I (465–424 BC), the king who later sent Ezra (7:1) and Nehemiah (Neh 2:1). Two letters may be in view: one by Bishlam, Mithredath and Tabeel (v7), and the one quoted in full by Rehum and Shimshai (vv8–16). The exact date is unknown, but it must fall before Nehemiah came in 445 BC. Many think it explains the news that broke Nehemiah's heart: the walls broken down and the gates burned (Neh 1:3).
- **"Written in Aramaic and translated" (v7).** Aramaic had become the common language of trade and government from Egypt to India. At this point the text itself turns to Aramaic, the language of the documents it quotes. Daniel 2:4–7:28 does the same.
- **Rehum the commander and Shimshai the scribe (vv8–9).** Rehum's title is literally "lord of decree," a senior official, perhaps the provincial chancellor. Shimshai was the secretary who drafted the letter. They list everyone who signed on: judges, officials and people descended from deportees from Erech (Uruk, in southern Babylonia), Babylon and Susa in Elam (southwest Iran). A long list of signatures made the complaint look like the voice of the whole province.
- **"The great and noble Osnappar" (v10).** Most scholars identify him as Ashurbanipal (669–about 627 BC), the last great king of Assyria, who crushed a Babylonian and Elamite revolt and would have had reason to resettle rebels far away. The flattering title is meant to remind the Persian king that these settlers were placed here by imperial order, unlike the Jews. "Beyond the River" was the Persian province west of the Euphrates, covering Syria and the land of Israel.
- **"That rebellious and wicked city" (v12).** The letter is clever. Its history is accurate: Jerusalem *had* rebelled against Assyria and Babylon, under Hezekiah (2 Kgs 18:7), Jehoiakim (2 Kgs 24:1) and Zedekiah (2 Kgs 24:20). Its picture of the present is not. The tiny community was "finishing the walls and repairing the foundations," but it was no military threat to an empire.
- **"They will not pay tribute, custom, or toll" (v13).** The three taxes were probably a fixed yearly tribute, a tax on goods, and a road or land toll. The letter goes straight for the king's purse.
- **"We eat the salt of the palace" (v14).** Sharing salt was a sign of a binding bond in the ancient world (Num 18:19 speaks of "a covenant of salt"). To eat a king's salt meant to be on his payroll and so to owe him loyalty. The writers present themselves as loyal servants protecting the king's honor.
- **"Search may be made in the book of the records" (v15).** The Persians kept the archives of the empires they had absorbed, so the Babylonian records of Judah's revolts would be on file. The writers were confident the search would back them up, and they were right.
- **"You will then have no possession in the province Beyond the River" (v16).** This is a wild exaggeration. Judah was a speck in a huge province. But fear makes small things look large, and the letter is built to frighten.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the enemies "basely misrepresented the undertaking, and the undertakers." Guzik points out the method: true facts about the past are used to tell a lie about the present. He sees in this a pattern the accuser still uses against God's people. JFB explains "the salt of the palace" as an Eastern idiom for drawing one's living from the king, and identifies the three taxes as poll tax, property tax and excise on trade. JFB identifies Osnappar with Esarhaddon, though most scholars, and the Tyndale notes, now prefer Ashurbanipal.

#### The King Orders the Work to Cease (vv17–24)
- **"Plainly read before me" (v18).** The Aramaic word probably means "translated" or "read clearly," that is, rendered into Persian for the king.
- **"It has been found that this city from of old has risen against kings" (v19).** Artaxerxes checked the records and found the charge true as history. He also noted that "mighty kings" had once ruled from Jerusalem over the whole region (v20). That was a memory of David and Solomon, whose empire did stretch toward the Euphrates (1 Kgs 4:21). The king read Jerusalem's past glory as a threat.
- **"Until a decree is made by me" (v21).** This clause matters. Artaxerxes did not forbid the rebuilding forever. He left room to change his mind. Persian decrees are sometimes said to have been unchangeable (Dan 6:8; Esth 1:19), but this one was worded to allow a later decree, and Artaxerxes himself issued it about twenty years later when he sent Nehemiah to rebuild the walls (Neh 2:1–8).
- **"By force and power made them cease" (v23).** The officials did not wait. They went "in haste" and stopped the work by force, probably with soldiers. This may be when the newly built walls were torn down and the gates burned (Neh 1:3).
- **"Then the work on the house of God… stopped" (v24).** Here the writer returns from his long aside to the temple story of v5. The point is that the opposition to the temple under Cyrus and Cambyses succeeded in the same way the later opposition to the walls did. The temple work sat idle for about sixteen years, from about 536 to 520 BC, "until the second year of the reign of Darius," when the prophets Haggai and Zechariah stirred the people to build again (5:1; Hag 1:1).
- **What the halt did to the people.** Haggai shows the other side of the delay. By 520 the people were saying, "The time has not yet come to rebuild the house of the LORD," while they built paneled houses for themselves (Hag 1:2–4). Outside pressure stopped the work, but their own priorities kept it stopped.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the enemies "immediately put in execution" the order they obtained, and he draws the lesson that every revival of true religion stirs opposition. Guzik stresses that God's enemies can delay his purposes but never defeat them, and the next chapter proves it. JFB marks this as the beginning of the long hostility between Jews and Samaritans, later sharpened by the rival temple on Mount Gerizim.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezra 4 is a chapter about a work of God that looked defeated. Neighbors offered a partnership that would have mixed false worship with true, and when that failed they used fear, bribery and half-true accusations to stop the building. Jesus faced the same pattern. He was accused of being a threat to Caesar's revenue and rule: "We found this man misleading our nation and forbidding us to give tribute to Caesar, and saying that he himself is Christ, a king" (Luke 23:2). Like the letter to Artaxerxes, the charge used a true claim, that he was a king, to tell a lie about what kind of king he was. For a while it seemed to work, and the true temple, his body, was torn down. But just as the halt of Ezra 4:24 was only "until" Darius, the grave held him only until the third day. Jesus also answered the question this chapter raises about who may worship. To the Samaritan woman he said the hour was coming when the true worshipers would worship the Father "in spirit and truth," neither on Gerizim nor in Jerusalem (John 4:21–24). The mixed worship the returnees refused is not the way in. Jesus himself is.
