*Setting:* Second Chronicles opens at the start of Solomon's reign, about 970 BC. But the book itself was written much later, most scholars think around 400 BC, for Jews who had come back from exile in Babylon and were rebuilding a small, poor community around a modest second temple. First Chronicles ended with David's last great assembly, his gifts for the temple and his charge to Solomon (1 Chr 28–29). The Chronicler retells the history of David's line to show the returned exiles who they are: God's people, gathered around his temple, with a promise to David still standing. So his account of Solomon is focused. He leaves out the bloody settling of scores at the start of the reign (1 Kgs 2), the marriage to Pharaoh's daughter in its original place (1 Kgs 3:1), the famous case of the two mothers (1 Kgs 3:16–28) and, at the end, Solomon's apostasy (1 Kgs 11). Almost everything here leads to the temple. This chapter covers the same events as 1 Kings 3:4–15 and 10:26–29.

#### Solomon Worships at Gibeon (vv1–6)
- **"Solomon the son of David established himself in his kingdom" (v1).** "Established himself" is *hitchazzeq*, "made himself strong." It echoes David's charge to Solomon, "be strong and courageous" (1 Chr 22:13; 28:20), and his prayer that God would be with him (1 Chr 22:11). **"The LORD his God was with him and made him exceedingly great"** answers that prayer (1 Chr 29:25). The Chronicler's opening is all blessing.
- **"Solomon spoke to all Israel" (v2).** Kings tells of Solomon going to Gibeon to sacrifice (1 Kgs 3:4). Chronicles makes it a national occasion, with the commanders, judges and family heads all present. This is typical of Chronicles, which likes to show "all Israel" acting together in worship, as it did when David brought up the ark (1 Chr 13:1–5; 15:3).
- **"The high place that was at Gibeon, for the tent of meeting of God… was there" (v3).** Gibeon was about six miles northwest of Jerusalem. Kings mentions only that Gibeon was "the great high place" (1 Kgs 3:4) and, a verse earlier, that the people were sacrificing at high places because there was no temple yet (1 Kgs 3:2). Chronicles explains why this particular high place was legitimate: Moses' tabernacle was there. David had appointed Zadok and other priests to serve at Gibeon (1 Chr 16:39–40; 21:29).
- **"But David had brought up the ark of God from Kiriath-jearim" (v4).** Since David's day Israel's worship was divided. The ark, the throne of God's presence, was in a tent in Jerusalem (1 Chr 15–16). The tabernacle and its altar, the place of sacrifice, were at Gibeon. The temple would bring them back together (5:5).
- **"The bronze altar that Bezalel… had made" (v5).** Bezalel, the Spirit-filled craftsman of the wilderness, made it nearly five hundred years earlier (Exod 31:1–5; 38:1–2). Chronicles mentions Bezalel only here and in the genealogies (1 Chr 2:20). The point is continuity: Solomon worships at the altar Moses commanded. **"Solomon and the assembly sought it out."** "Sought" is *darash*, a favorite word in Chronicles for wholehearted turning to God (1 Chr 16:11; 2 Chr 7:14; 15:12). The ESV renders it "sought it out," though it could mean "sought him," that is, the LORD. Either way, the king and the people are seeking God together.
- **"Offered a thousand burnt offerings on it" (v6).** A burnt offering was wholly burned. Nothing came back to the worshiper. A thousand of them was a royal-scale act of devotion, and it took many priests, probably over several days. Solomon begins his reign with costly worship.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God's presence will be our strength," and on the altar, "it is the altar that sanctifieth every gift." Henry refuses to set David's love of the ark against Solomon's honoring the altar, and says neither should be condemned. JFB says the scale of the offering "became the rank of the offerer," and names Zadok as officiating priest (1 Chr 16:39). Guzik calls the bronze altar the place of atoning sacrifice, the Old Testament equivalent of coming to the cross, and traces the tabernacle's journeys from Shiloh to Nob to Gibeon.

#### Solomon Prays for Wisdom (vv7–13)
- **"In that night God appeared to Solomon" (v7).** Kings says it was a dream (1 Kgs 3:5, 15). Chronicles simply says God appeared. **"Ask what I shall give you"** is an open invitation from God, one of the most remarkable in Scripture.
- **"You have shown great and steadfast love to David my father" (v8).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. Solomon starts from what God has already done, not from his own needs.
- **"Let your word to David my father be now fulfilled" (v9).** Solomon prays God's own promise back to him (1 Chr 17:11–14). **"A people as numerous as the dust of the earth"** echoes God's promise to Abraham and Jacob (Gen 13:16; 28:14). Solomon sees his kingdom as the fulfilment of the promise to the patriarchs.
- **"Give me now wisdom and knowledge to go out and come in before this people" (v10).** "Go out and come in" is a Hebrew idiom for leading in every part of life, originally used of a military leader going out to battle at the head of his troops and bringing them back (Num 27:17; 1 Sam 18:16). Kings has "an understanding mind to govern your people, that I may discern between good and evil" (1 Kgs 3:9). Chronicles' version is shorter and leaves out Solomon's humble line "I am but a little child" (1 Kgs 3:7). "Govern" here is *shaphat*, the word usually translated "judge." In Israel, ruling well meant judging fairly.
- **"Who can govern this people of yours, which is so great?" (v10).** The key words are "this people of yours." Solomon knows Israel is God's people, not his own possession. That conviction, if he had held to it, would have kept him from the forced labor that later broke the kingdom (1 Kgs 12:4).
- **"Because this was in your heart" (v11).** God looks at the heart behind the request. The list of what Solomon did not ask for is a list of what most kings wanted: **"possessions, wealth, honor, or the life of those who hate you… long life."**
- **"Wisdom and knowledge are granted to you. I will also give you riches, possessions, and honor" (v12).** God gives what Solomon asked, and then what he did not ask. Kings adds a condition about long life: "if you will walk in my ways" (1 Kgs 3:14). Chronicles leaves it out here, though the same condition appears later (7:17–18). Jesus states the principle: "seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (Matt 6:33).
- **"So Solomon came from the high place at Gibeon… And he reigned over Israel" (v13).** Kings says he went to Jerusalem and offered sacrifices before the ark (1 Kgs 3:15). Chronicles moves straight to his reign.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up Solomon's request in a line: "Lord, give me my father's spirit," and says God gave the wisdom "because he asked for it." Henry presents the request, built on God's call and God's promise to David, as a model for prayer. Guzik notes that God met Solomon at Gibeon even without the ark, so God's presence was not tied to one object. He also asks whether wisdom was really the highest thing Solomon could have sought, setting Solomon's shrewdness beside David's deep love for God. JFB adds nothing here beyond a cross-reference to Kings.

#### Solomon Given Wealth (vv14–17)
- **"1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen" (v14).** This summary appears almost word for word in 1 Kings 10:26–29, near the end of Solomon's story. Chronicles moves it to the very beginning, as proof that God kept his promise of riches (v12). It repeats it again in 2 Chronicles 9:25–28. The Tyndale notes point out the different effect: in Kings this wealth leads into Solomon's fall, while in Chronicles it shows God's word coming true.
- **"Silver and gold as common in Jerusalem as stone" (v15).** Kings says "silver" (1 Kgs 10:27). Chronicles adds "and gold." For a community of returned exiles in a small, poor province, this picture of Jerusalem's past glory was a reminder of what God had once done and could do again (Hag 2:7–9).
- **"Cedar as plentiful as the sycamore of the Shephelah" (v15).** The Shephelah is the low hill country west of Judah, where sycamore-figs grew everywhere. Imported cedar became common.
- **"Solomon's import of horses was from Egypt and Kue" (v16).** Kue is Cilicia in southeastern Turkey, famed for horses. Some scholars think "Egypt" (*Mitsrayim*) should be read "Musri," a land in Anatolia near Kue, since Egypt was not famous for horse breeding. If Egypt is right, this breaks Deuteronomy 17:16, which forbade a king to "acquire many horses" or send people back to Egypt for them. The Tyndale notes mention both readings.
- **"Exported to all the kings of the Hittites and the kings of Syria" (v17).** The Hittites here are the small kingdoms of northern Syria that survived the fall of the Hittite empire. Solomon's traders were middlemen in the regional arms trade.
- **Does Chronicles approve?** The Chronicler is writing for people who knew Kings well. He does not repeat the warning, but he does not hide the facts either. Readers who remembered Deuteronomy 17:16–17 would have noticed the horses and the gold. He lets God's promise and the king's later failure both stand, though he chooses to dwell on the promise.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the horse trade as direct disobedience to Deuteronomy 17:16, perhaps justified because Solomon was trading on others' behalf, and lists it as the first step in a downward slide that ends in idolatry (1 Kgs 11). JFB speaks of Solomon's "passion for horses," "greater than that of any Israelitish monarch before or after him," and explains the Hittites as a people who had moved north of Lebanon. The Tyndale notes are gentler and say an Anatolian source would have been allowed.

#### Pointing to Christ
Solomon's worship at Gibeon shows Israel's worship split in two: the ark in Jerusalem, the altar at Gibeon. His temple brought them together for a time. In Christ the throne of God's presence and the altar of sacrifice meet in one person: he is the place where God dwells (John 1:14) and the sacrifice offered once for all (Heb 10:10–12). Solomon asked for wisdom to govern "this people of yours" and received it. Christ is "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24), and to everyone who lacks wisdom God still says, in effect, "Ask" (Jas 1:5).
