*Setting:* The title reads "A Miktam of David." *Miktam* heads six psalms, all David's (16 and 56–60). Its meaning is unknown. Some link it to the Hebrew word for fine gold (*ketem*), hence the old title "golden psalm." The ancient Greek translation rendered it as something like "an inscription on a stone pillar," a psalm worth engraving. JFB suggests simply "a writing." Four of the other five Miktams are set in danger, on the run from Saul or at war (56:title; 57:title; 59:title; 60:title), and Psalm 16 opens with a short plea for protection. But most of it is a song of deep contentment and confidence. Psalm 15 asked who may dwell with God and ended with "shall never be moved." Psalm 16 shows what that life feels like from the inside: God as the psalmist's only good, his portion, his counsellor, and the one who will not abandon him even to death. This psalm holds a special place in the New Testament. On the day of Pentecost, Peter quoted vv8–11 as a prophecy of Jesus' resurrection (Acts 2:25–31), and Paul did the same at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:35–37). Both argued that David died and his body decayed, so the words find their full meaning in David's greater Son.

#### I Have No Good Apart from You (vv1–4)
- **"Preserve me, O God, for in you I take refuge" (v1).** The only request in the psalm. "Preserve" (*shamar*) means keep, guard, watch over, the same verb as the priestly blessing, "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Num 6:24). "Take refuge" is the familiar *chasah* (2:12; 7:1; 11:1). Everything that follows explains why this God is a safe refuge.
- **"I say to the LORD, 'You are my Lord'" (v2).** The same pairing as 8:1: *YHWH*, the covenant name, and *Adonai*, master. To call God "my Lord" is to put oneself under him as servant. It is the most basic Christian confession too: "Jesus is Lord" (Rom 10:9).
- **"I have no good apart from you" (v2).** The Hebrew is compressed and unusual, literally "my good, not beside you" or "not above you." It means God is the source and sum of every good thing in the psalmist's life. Nothing he values stands outside God or above him. The KJV reads "my goodness extendeth not to thee," meaning my good deeds add nothing to you. Most modern translations follow the ESV's sense. Psalm 73:25 says something similar: "Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you."
- **"As for the saints in the land, they are the excellent ones, in whom is all my delight" (v3).** "Saints" (*qedoshim*) means holy ones, people set apart for God. Here it means God's faithful people in Israel. "Excellent ones" (*addirim*) is from the same root as "majestic" in 8:1. Love for God leads to love for his people. John says the same: "everyone who loves the Father loves whoever has been born of him" (1 John 5:1). The Hebrew of this verse is difficult, and some translations read it differently, but the ESV's reading is the most common.
- **"The sorrows of those who run after another god shall multiply" (v4).** The contrast: some turn to other gods, and their pains multiply. "Run after" may have the sense of trading or bargaining for a god. The word for "sorrows" (*atstsevot*) sounds very like a Hebrew word for "idols" (*atsabbim*), a likely wordplay. Kidner also hears an echo of Genesis 3:16, where God tells the woman, "I will surely multiply your pain."
- **"Their drink offerings of blood I will not pour out" (v4).** Israel poured out wine as a drink offering (Num 15:5). Pagan rites in Canaan apparently used blood, which the Law forbade drinking or offering this way (Lev 17:10–14). Some take "of blood" more broadly to mean offerings made with bloodstained hands (Isa 1:15).
- **"Or take their names on my lips" (v4).** He refuses even to speak the names of other gods in worship or oaths. The Law said, "make no mention of the names of other gods, nor let it be heard on your lips" (Exod 23:13; Josh 23:7).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv1–7 as David speaking "as a member of Christ," and calls this section "the language of all good Christians." He finds in it confidence in God, consent to God, love for God's people and loyalty to true worship. JFB calls v2 obscure, defines the saints as "persons consecrated to God," and says v4 "expresses his abhorrence of those who seek other sources of happiness." Guzik passes on Greenham: "I receive all good from thee, but no good can I return to thee," and Boice's test: "Those who love the Lord will love the company of those who also love him."

#### My Chosen Portion and My Cup (vv5–6)
- **"The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup" (v5).** "Portion" (*cheleq*) is the language of dividing land. When Israel entered Canaan, each tribe received a portion, except Levi. God told Aaron, "You shall have no inheritance in their land... I am your portion and your inheritance among the people of Israel" (Num 18:20; Deut 10:9). David, not a priest, claims the same: God himself is his share. "Cup" is what the host pours for each guest, one's lot in life (see 11:6; 23:5). The wicked's cup was fire and sulfur. The psalmist's cup is God himself.
- **"You hold my lot" (v5).** Land in Israel was divided by casting lots (Josh 18:6–10), believed to be guided by God (Prov 16:33). David is saying, "My future is in your hands, and you guard it."
- **"The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places" (v6).** "Lines" are measuring cords used to mark out a field's boundaries. The boundaries of his life, the circumstances God has given, are good. "Pleasant" is the same word as "pleasures" in v11. Notice that David wrote this as a man who often lost land, home and position, and once complained that his enemies had "driven me out this day that I should have no share in the heritage of the LORD" (1 Sam 26:19). His contentment does not rest on circumstances but on God.
- **"Indeed, I have a beautiful inheritance" (v6).** The surprise is in "indeed." It is not only that he has something. What he has is lovely. In the New Testament, believers are given "an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading" (1 Pet 1:4).
- **Commentators:** JFB: "God is the chief good, and supplies all need," and the portion and cup may allude to "the inheritance of Levi." Guzik notes that David, as a youngest son, could expect little family land, yet he found God himself was his inheritance, and passes on Maclaren's point that "Every godly man has the same possession and the same prohibitions as the priests had."

#### I Have Set the LORD Always Before Me (vv7–8)
- **"I bless the LORD who gives me counsel" (v7).** "Bless" here means to praise or thank. God gives counsel, wise guidance for how to live (32:8; 73:24). Psalm 13:2 spoke of taking "counsel in my soul" and finding none. Here the counsel comes from God.
- **"In the night also my heart instructs me" (v7).** Literally "my kidneys instruct me," the deep inner self (as in 7:9; KJV "reins"). In the quiet of the night, what God has taught sinks in and speaks back. Nights are often when fears return (6:6). Here the night is filled with God's teaching.
- **"I have set the LORD always before me" (v8).** A deliberate choice. "Set" means to place, to arrange. He keeps God in front of him, in view, like a traveler keeping his eye on a landmark. "Always" means in every season, not only in crisis.
- **"Because he is at my right hand, I shall not be shaken" (v8).** In the ancient world the right hand was the place of a defender. A warrior's shield was on his left arm, so the right side was the exposed side, where a friend stood to protect him (109:31; 121:5). "Shaken" (*mot*) completes the thread through these psalms: the wicked boasted he would not be moved (10:6), the believer feared being shaken (13:4), the righteous was promised he would never be moved (15:5). Here is why: God stands at his right hand.
- **Peter's quotation begins here (Acts 2:25).** Peter introduces these words: "David says concerning him, 'I saw the Lord always before me, for he is at my right hand that I may not be shaken.'" He hears in them the voice of Jesus, who lived every moment with his Father in view (John 8:29).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the kidneys "the supposed seat of emotion and thought," and says that with God at his right hand the speaker is "sure of safety." Henry reads v8 as "the special presence of God with the Redeemer in his services and sufferings." Guzik says only Jesus perfectly set the LORD always before him, and passes on Horne's comment that Christ kept "a constant and actual sense of the presence of Jehovah."

#### You Will Not Abandon My Soul to Sheol (vv9–10)
- **"Therefore my heart is glad, and my whole being rejoices" (v9).** "Whole being" is literally "my glory" (*kavod*), the same word as in 7:5 and 57:8, meaning one's inner life or honor. The ancient Greek translation reads "my tongue," which Peter quotes: "my heart was glad, and my tongue rejoiced" (Acts 2:26). The two readings come from very similar ideas: one's glory is expressed through one's tongue in praise.
- **"My flesh also dwells secure" (v9).** Heart, soul and body together: the whole person is at rest. Even the body, which will die, rests in hope. Peter's Greek reads "my flesh also will dwell in hope."
- **"For you will not abandon my soul to Sheol" (v10).** *Sheol* is the realm of the dead (see 6:5). "Abandon" means leave behind, desert. Psalm 6:5 asked, "in Sheol who will give you praise?" Here the psalmist sees further. Death will not have the last word. God will not leave him there. "Soul" (*nefesh*) here means the whole living person, not just a spirit.
- **"Or let your holy one see corruption" (v10).** "Holy one" (*chasid*) is the same word as "godly one" in 4:3 and 12:1, someone devoted to God and loved by him. "Corruption" (*shachat*) in Hebrew usually means "the pit," the grave (as in 7:15; 30:9). The ancient Greek translation rendered it "corruption," decay, and that is how Peter and Paul quote it (Acts 2:27; 13:35). Both meanings point the same way: God's holy one will not be left in the grave to rot.
- **How Peter reads it (Acts 2:29–31).** Peter argues plainly: "the patriarch David... both died and was buried, and his tomb is with us to this day." David's body did see decay. So David, "being therefore a prophet, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him that he would set one of his descendants on his throne, he foresaw and spoke about the resurrection of the Christ, that he was not abandoned to Hades, nor did his flesh see corruption." Paul makes the same argument (Acts 13:36–37). Christians have read this in two related ways. Some see David speaking of his own hope of not being left in death, a hope fulfilled finally in Christ's resurrection, which guarantees his people's resurrection too. Others see David speaking directly as a prophet in Christ's voice. Peter's words support both: David spoke of his hope, and foresaw the one in whom it would come true.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the psalm has "something of David in it, but much more of Christ," and that these verses cannot describe David, who saw corruption. He reads them as "the prospect which the Redeemer had of his own resurrection and the glory that should follow." JFB says "soul" often means the whole person, "hell" means "the state of death," not eternal suffering, and the "Holy One" is one who receives divine grace. He says Acts 2:26 follows the Greek "my tongue." Guzik admits Old Testament views of the afterlife are often cloudy (6:5; 88:11), but sees here a clear hope. He quotes Peter: "it was not possible for him to be held by it" (Acts 2:24), and argues that the resurrection proves Jesus' work on the cross was complete.

#### Fullness of Joy (v11)
- **"You make known to me the path of life" (v11).** Not a path *to* life only, but life's path itself, the way that leads through and beyond death. Psalm 1 described two ways. Here God shows the way that does not perish. Peter quotes it: "You have made known to me the paths of life" (Acts 2:28). Jesus would later say, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6).
- **"In your presence there is fullness of joy" (v11).** "Presence" is literally "your face," the face the upright will behold (11:7) and that seemed hidden in 13:1. "Fullness" means joy that is complete, nothing missing. The deepest joy is not in God's gifts but in God himself.
- **"At your right hand are pleasures forevermore" (v11).** In v8 God was at the psalmist's right hand. Now the psalmist is at God's right hand, the place of honor and favor (110:1). "Pleasures" is the word for "pleasant" in v6. The pleasant boundaries of this life lead to pleasures without end. "Forevermore" is the word for "victory" or "everlasting." There is no last day for these pleasures. Peter's sermon goes from this verse straight to Jesus "exalted at the right hand of God" (Acts 2:33).
- **A different kind of pleasure.** The Bible is not against pleasure. It insists that the greatest and longest-lasting pleasures are found in God's presence. The sorrows of those who chase other gods "multiply" (v4). The pleasures of those who have God last forever.
- **Commentators:** JFB says "the path of life" may be plural to express abundant blessedness, and that "at thy right hand" refers to Christ's exaltation (Acts 2:33). Guzik passes on Spurgeon: "God is not going to give his people any left-handed heaven," and Kidner's summary of the psalm: "The refugee of verse 1 finds himself an heir." He also notes C. S. Lewis's line in *The Screwtape Letters*, where the senior devil complains that God is "a hedonist at heart," because everything God makes is meant for joy.

#### Pointing to Christ
Peter preached this psalm on the first Pentecost, and his point was simple: David's tomb was occupied, but Jesus' tomb was empty. Jesus is the "holy one" who set the Father always before him, who did not run after other gods, and who took the LORD as his only portion. He went down into death, and God did not abandon him to the grave or let his body decay. On the third day God made known to him the path of life, and he is now at the Father's right hand, where there are pleasures forevermore. Because he lives, the hope of this psalm belongs to everyone who takes refuge in him. Death cannot keep them, and their inheritance is God himself.
