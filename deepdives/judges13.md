*Setting:* Jephthah and the three minor judges after him are gone. The refrain sounds one last time: "the people of Israel again did what was evil." The new enemy is the Philistines, who will trouble Israel through the days of Samuel, Saul and David. They were a "Sea People" who arrived from the Aegean region (Crete is named as their origin in Amos 9:7) around 1200 BC and settled five cities on the southern coast: Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron and Gath. They had better iron weapons and a strong military organization. The tribe of Dan, given land on the edge of their territory (Josh 19:40–48), was being squeezed out. Samson's story (chs 13–16) gets more space than any other judge's, and it is the only one that begins before the hero is born.

#### The Birth of Samson (vv1–25)
- **"The LORD gave them into the hand of the Philistines for forty years" (v1).** This is the longest oppression in Judges. Notice what is missing: for the first time, Israel does not cry out to the LORD. They seem to have settled into living under the Philistines. In 15:11 the men of Judah will even hand Samson over to keep the peace. God sends a deliverer anyway, unasked. This is grace before anyone asked for it.
- **"A certain man of Zorah… whose name was Manoah" (v2).** Zorah was a Danite town on a ridge above the Sorek Valley, about 15 miles west of Jerusalem, looking down toward Philistine country. The name Manoah is related to the Hebrew word for "rest."
- **"His wife was barren" (v2).** She is never named. Barren women whose sons God gives by promise are a thread through the Bible: Sarah (Gen 18), Rebekah (Gen 25:21), Rachel (Gen 30:22), Hannah (1 Sam 1), and Elizabeth (Luke 1). Each time, the child's birth is God's doing from the start, and each child has a part in God's plan.
- **"The angel of the LORD appeared to the woman" (v3).** This is the same mysterious figure who appeared at Bochim (2:1) and to Gideon (6:11). He speaks as the LORD himself. He comes first to the woman, not to her husband. The Tyndale notes say this fits the instructions: the first part of the vow was hers to keep.
- **"Drink no wine or strong drink, and eat nothing unclean" (v4).** These are the rules of the Nazirite vow (Num 6:1–21). Normally a Nazirite was an adult who chose to set himself apart for a time. He would drink no wine or anything from the grapevine, let no razor touch his head, and touch no dead body. Here, uniquely, the mother keeps the rules while pregnant, and the child is a Nazirite "from the womb" for life (vv5, 7). Only Samuel (1 Sam 1:11) and, in the New Testament, John the Baptist (Luke 1:15) were set apart in a similar way from birth.
- **"He shall begin to save Israel from the hand of the Philistines" (v5).** Note "begin." Samson will not finish the job. Samuel will win a victory over the Philistines (1 Sam 7), and only David will break their power (2 Sam 5:17–25; 8:1). JFB says the word is encouraging but shows the deliverance is still a long way off.
- **"His appearance was like the appearance of the angel of God, very awesome" (v6).** She calls him "a man of God," the usual term for a prophet, but she sensed he was more. "Awesome" means fearful, inspiring dread and wonder. She did not ask his name. In repeating his words to Manoah she adds, "to the day of his death" (v7). It is a dark hint of how the story ends (16:30).
- **"Teach us what we are to do with the child" (v8).** Manoah does not doubt the promise. He asks how to raise the boy. God answers: "God listened to the voice of Manoah" (v9). But the angel comes again to the woman, alone in the field, and she has to run and fetch her husband.
- **"What is to be the child's manner of life, and what is his mission?" (v12).** The angel does not add anything new. He simply repeats what he said to the woman: let her be careful and keep all of it (vv13–14). He adds "anything that comes from the vine," which matches Numbers 6:3–4. The answer to "how do we raise him?" is to obey what God has already said.
- **"Please let us detain you and prepare a young goat for you" (v15).** Manoah offers a meal, the normal hospitality to an honored guest (Gen 18:1–8). The angel refuses to eat but redirects it: "if you prepare a burnt offering, then offer it to the LORD" (v16). The narrator explains, "Manoah did not know that he was the angel of the LORD." Gideon made the same offer, and his meal too was burned up as an offering (6:18–21).
- **"Why do you ask my name, seeing it is wonderful?" (v18).** The word is *pil'i*, "wonderful," meaning beyond understanding, extraordinary. It comes from the same root as Isaiah's title for the coming child: "his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor" (Isa 9:6). Jacob asked the same question of the man who wrestled him and was not told either (Gen 32:29). The name is not hidden because it is unknown but because it is too great to grasp.
- **"To the one who works wonders" (v19).** Manoah offers the goat and grain on a rock as an altar. The ESV's "the one who works wonders" uses the same root as "wonderful" in v18. The name the angel would not explain is shown in what he does next.
- **"The angel of the LORD went up in the flame of the altar" (v20).** As the fire rises, the visitor rises with it and is gone. Manoah and his wife fall on their faces. Now Manoah understands who he has met (v21).
- **"We shall surely die, for we have seen God" (v22).** Manoah identifies the angel of the LORD as God himself. He fears what Gideon feared (6:22–23): no one can see God and live (Exod 33:20). In both stories, God's appearance is real, but veiled.
- **"If the LORD had meant to kill us…" (v23).** His wife answers with simple, solid logic. God accepted our offering, showed us these things and made us a promise. He would not do all that just to kill us. It is the same argument Paul makes in a greater setting: "He who did not spare his own Son… how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?" (Rom 8:32). What God has already done is a pledge of what he will do.
- **"Called his name Samson" (v24).** In Hebrew *Shimshon*, from *shemesh*, "sun," probably meaning "sunny" or "little sun." Across the valley from Zorah lay the town of Beth-shemesh, "house of the sun," once a center of sun worship. Whether the name hints at that or simply at a bright, joyful child, it is a striking name for a Nazirite of the LORD. "The young man grew, and the LORD blessed him," much as was said of Samuel and of Jesus (1 Sam 2:26; Luke 2:52).
- **"The Spirit of the LORD began to stir him" (v25).** The verb is used of something pushing or striking repeatedly. The Spirit was already at work, nudging and impelling him. Mahaneh-dan ("camp of Dan") was a camp of the Danites between Zorah and Eshtaol, perhaps a temporary settlement, and Samson will be buried in this same area (16:31). His story begins and ends between Zorah and Eshtaol.
- **Who is the angel of the LORD?** He speaks as God, accepts an offering made to the LORD, will not give his name, ascends in the altar's flame, and Manoah says, "we have seen God." Many Christian interpreters, from the early church on, have understood this figure as an appearance of the Son of God before his incarnation. Others think he is a created angel speaking with God's full authority, as an ambassador speaks for a king. The text plainly treats meeting him as meeting God.
- **Commentators:** All three see the visitor as divine. JFB calls him "the messenger of the covenant," a divine personage, and says the flame and the "wonderful" name proved it. Guzik identifies him as the Son of God in a preincarnate appearance, linking "wonderful" to Isaiah 9:6. The Tyndale notes speak more generally of a "theophany" (an appearance of God) that points forward to the incarnation. Matthew Henry: "Mercies long waited for, often prove signal mercies." On Manoah's prayer, "duty is ours, events are God's." On the offering, he writes that "without Christ in the heart by faith, our services are offensive smoke." Guzik praises Manoah's wife for her encouragement and notes that God's past work is a promise of future care. Henry ends with a hopeful word: "Those are blessed indeed in whom the Spirit of grace begins to work." JFB reads the Spirit's stirring as kindling patriotic zeal, not the gift of prophecy.

#### Pointing to Christ
An angel announces to a woman who could not have a child that she will bear a son, set apart to God from the womb, who will begin to save Israel. The pattern runs through to Luke 1, where the angel Gabriel announces John's birth and then Jesus' birth to a virgin. Samson would "begin" to save. Jesus would finish: "It is finished" (John 19:30). The one whose name is "Wonderful" and who rose in the flame of the sacrifice points ahead to the child of Isaiah 9:6, "Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God," and to the Son who offered himself as the sacrifice and then ascended into heaven. Manoah's wife reasoned that a God who accepted their offering did not mean to destroy them. At the cross God has accepted the offering once for all, and so those who trust Christ can say with even greater confidence, "we shall not die."
