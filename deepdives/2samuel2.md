*Setting:* About 1010 BC, soon after Saul and three of his sons died on Mount Gilboa (1 Sam 31). David, about thirty years old (5:4), has mourned Saul and Jonathan in a public lament (ch 1). He is still living at Ziklag, the Philistine town Achish gave him, and the Philistines now hold much of northern Israel. David was anointed by Samuel as a boy (1 Sam 16:13), but he has never pushed his way to the throne. This chapter shows the kingdom splitting in two. Judah, David's own tribe in the south, makes him king. Saul's general Abner sets up Saul's surviving son as king over the rest. What follows is the first civil war in Israel's history, and it begins with a deadly "game."

#### David Anointed King of Judah (vv1–7)
- **"After this David inquired of the LORD" (v1).** David does not move until he asks. He probably did this through the priest Abiathar and the ephod with the Urim and Thummim, the sacred lots used to get a yes or no answer from God (1 Sam 23:9–12; 30:7–8). His questions are simple and specific: "Shall I go up?" and "To which?" This is the same habit that turned things around at Ziklag (1 Sam 30:8). Saul, by contrast, asked God too late and got no answer (1 Sam 28:6).
- **"To Hebron" (v1).** Hebron was the chief city of Judah, about nineteen miles south of Jerusalem, high in the hill country. It was full of history. Abraham lived near there and bought the cave of Machpelah, where he, Sarah, Isaac, Rebekah, Jacob and Leah were buried (Gen 23; 49:29–32). Caleb received it as his inheritance (Josh 14:13–14). David had also sent gifts from the Amalekite spoil to Hebron and nearby towns (1 Sam 30:26–31), so he already had friends there.
- **"His two wives also, Ahinoam of Jezreel and Abigail" (v2).** These are the two wives just rescued from the Amalekites (1 Sam 30:5, 18). Michal, Saul's daughter and David's first wife, had been given to another man (1 Sam 25:44). She will come back into the story in chapter 3.
- **"The men of Judah came, and there they anointed David king over the house of Judah" (v4).** This is David's second anointing. The first, by Samuel, was private and showed God's choice (1 Sam 16:13). This one is public and shows the people's choice. A third will come when all Israel anoints him (5:3). David did not seize the crown. The elders of his own tribe came to him.
- **"It was the men of Jabesh-gilead who buried Saul" (v4).** The first thing David does as king is reach out to the town that honored Saul (1 Sam 31:11–13).
- **"You showed this loyalty to Saul your lord" (v5).** "Loyalty" is *hesed*, the great Hebrew word for covenant faithfulness, steadfast love that keeps its commitments. Then David prays, "May the LORD show steadfast love and faithfulness to you" (v6). "Steadfast love" is *hesed* again. David blesses their *hesed* to Saul with a prayer for God's *hesed* to them. *Hesed* will be one of the threads of 2 Samuel, especially when David looks for someone from Saul's house to show it to (9:1).
- **"Saul your lord is dead, and the house of Judah has anointed me king" (v7).** David's kindness is real, and it is also an invitation. He is gently asking Jabesh to transfer its loyalty to him. It seems they did not, at least at first, since Jabesh was in the territory that backed Saul's son (v9).
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls David's habit of asking God first a key to his success. He also suggests David could not receive this anointing while he was still living as a Philistine vassal, and only after his restoration at Ziklag was the way clear. JFB says David inquired "by Urim," and calls the message to Jabesh "a stroke of sound and timely policy." Guzik also sees good policy in it: David needed brave men like these. The Tyndale notes add an interesting wrinkle. David later kept friendly ties with Nahash the Ammonite (10:2), the king Saul had once defeated to rescue Jabesh, which would not have endeared him there.

#### Ish-bosheth Made King of Israel (vv8–11)
- **"Abner the son of Ner, commander of Saul's army" (v8).** Abner was Saul's cousin (1 Sam 14:50–51) and the most powerful man left in Saul's house. He survived Gilboa. He knew David well, and he knew David was God's chosen king, as he will admit himself (3:9–10, 18). He backs Saul's son anyway.
- **"Ish-bosheth the son of Saul" (v8).** His name means "man of shame." Chronicles gives his real name as Eshbaal, "man of Baal" (1 Chr 8:33; 9:39). "Baal" meant "lord" or "master" and could be used of the LORD in early Israel, but it was also the name of the Canaanite storm god. Later scribes seem to have swapped "Baal" for *bosheth*, "shame," in names like this one and Mephibosheth (originally Merib-baal, 1 Chr 8:34). The change was a way of refusing to say the name of a false god.
- **"Brought him over to Mahanaim" (v8).** Mahanaim was east of the Jordan in Gilead, out of the Philistines' reach and far from David. Gilead had strong ties to Saul. The name means "two camps." It is where Jacob saw the angels of God on his way home (Gen 32:2), and David himself will flee there when Absalom rebels (17:24).
- **"Over Gilead and the Ashurites and Jezreel and Ephraim and Benjamin and all Israel" (v9).** The list moves from east to north to the center and the south of the northern tribes. Who "the Ashurites" were is uncertain. Many think it should read "Asherites," the tribe of Asher in the north. Ish-bosheth's "kingdom" was mostly a claim, since much of it was under Philistine control.
- **"Forty years old… and he reigned two years" (v10).** He was the oldest surviving son, but he had not been at the battle. David reigned in Hebron "seven years and six months" (v11). Since Ish-bosheth reigned only two years, there may have been a gap of some years while Abner regained control of the north, or the two years may cover only the end of David's time in Hebron. The text does not explain.
- **Two kingdoms.** This split between "Judah" and "Israel" is a warning sign. It foreshadows the permanent division after Solomon's death (1 Kgs 12), when the northern tribes break away again.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls this "the establishment of a rival kingdom." It notes that David "neither could nor would force matters," preferring to wait. Guzik agrees. He thinks Abner wanted a weak king so he could hold the real power himself, and he sees David's willingness to wait as trust in God. Henry: "The nation in general refused David." The Tyndale notes add that the Philistines likely encouraged the split, because a divided Israel was easier to control.

#### The Battle at Gibeon (vv12–17)
- **"Gibeon" (v12).** Gibeon was a town of Benjamin about six miles northwest of Jerusalem, near the border between the two kingdoms. Abner brings his men out of Mahanaim and across the Jordan, which looks like a move to push into Judah's area.
- **"Joab the son of Zeruiah" (v13).** This is Joab's first real appearance. Zeruiah was David's sister (1 Chr 2:16), so Joab and his brothers Abishai and Asahel were David's nephews. Joab will be David's army commander for nearly his whole reign: brave, capable, loyal to David's throne and utterly ruthless.
- **"The pool of Gibeon" (v13).** Archaeologists have found a great round shaft at Gibeon, about 37 feet across, cut into the rock with a spiral stairway going down to the water table about 80 feet below. Many think it is this pool. Jeremiah 41:12 mentions "the great pool that is in Gibeon."
- **"Let the young men arise and compete before us" (v14).** "Compete" is the Hebrew *sahaq*, "to play" or "to make sport." It is the word used when the Philistines made Samson "entertain" them (Judg 16:25). Abner proposes a contest of champions, like David and Goliath, perhaps to settle things without a full battle. It is a deadly game.
- **"Each caught his opponent by the head and thrust his sword in his opponent's side, so they fell down together" (v16).** All twenty-four die at once. The contest settles nothing. The place gets a name, **Helkath-hazzurim**, which the ESV footnote explains as "field of sword-edges." The word *tsur* usually means rock or flint, and here refers to blades.
- **"The battle was very fierce that day" (v17).** The "game" turns into a full battle, and Abner's men are routed.
- **Commentators:** Henry has a sharp line about the word "play": "Abner meant, Let the young men fight before us, when he said, Let them play before us: fools thus make a mock at sin." Guzik, following Clarke, calls the contest "diabolical play." JFB notes that "some think that the proposal was only for an exhibition" that turned deadly. The Tyndale notes add that the word suggests a high-stakes game and point out that it solved nothing.

#### The Death of Asahel (vv18–32)
- **"Asahel was as swift of foot as a wild gazelle" (v18).** "Gazelle" here is *tsevi*, the same spelling David used for Saul and Jonathan, "your glory, O Israel" (1:19). Asahel was the youngest of the three brothers and famous for his speed. He later appears among David's Thirty, his elite warriors (23:24).
- **"He turned neither to the right hand nor to the left" (v19).** That phrase is usually praise in the Old Testament, for staying on God's path (Deut 5:32; Josh 1:7). Here it describes a young man's single-minded chase that will kill him. He is after the biggest prize on the field, the enemy general. Killing Abner would have ended the war and made his name.
- **"Turn aside… seize one of the young men and take his spoil" (v21).** Abner, a seasoned warrior, does not want to kill this young man. He offers him an easier trophy. **"How then could I lift up my face to your brother Joab?" (v22).** Abner knows what the blood feud will mean. In Israel the nearest male relative was the "avenger of blood" (Num 35:19), and Joab would be duty-bound to avenge his brother.
- **"Abner struck him in the stomach with the butt of his spear" (v23).** The back end of a spear often had a metal spike so it could be planted upright in the ground (1 Sam 26:7). Abner does not even turn to fight. He thrusts backward at the runner close behind him, and Asahel runs onto it with his own speed. The spear goes right through him.
- **"All who came to the place where Asahel had fallen… stood still" (v23).** The soldiers stop at the body in shock. Later, David's men do the same at the body of Amasa (20:12).
- **"But Joab and Abishai pursued Abner" (v24).** The brothers keep going until sundown. The place names, Ammah and Giah, seem to mean "conduit" and "gushing," perhaps from the water system around Gibeon. Their exact locations are unknown.
- **"The people of Benjamin gathered themselves together behind Abner" (v25).** Saul's own tribe rallies on a hilltop for a last stand.
- **"Shall the sword devour forever? Do you not know that the end will be bitter?" (v26).** Abner's appeal is wise, though it is easy to say when one is losing. He reminds Joab that these are "brothers." This is a war between Israelites.
- **"As God lives, if you had not spoken…" (v27).** Joab's reply is hard to read. It may mean "If you had not spoken up, my men would have kept chasing you till morning." Or it may mean "If you had not started this with your challenge this morning, no one would have had to chase anyone." Either way, he blows the trumpet, the ram's horn that started and stopped battles, and the pursuit ends.
- **"Went all that night through the Arabah" (v29).** The Arabah is the Jordan Valley. Abner puts as much distance as he can between his men and Joab's, marching all night and all the next morning back to Mahanaim.
- **"Nineteen men besides Asahel… 360 of Abner's men" (vv30–31).** The numbers show how lopsided the defeat was. The dead on Abner's side were Benjaminites, Saul's own tribe. Saul's house is bleeding away.
- **"Buried him in the tomb of his father, which was at Bethlehem" (v32).** Asahel is buried in David's hometown, in the family tomb. Joab then marches all night back to Hebron. He will not forget Asahel. The next chapter shows what he does with his grief.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains Asahel's ambition: "To gain the general's armor was deemed the grandest trophy." He describes Abner's blow as a "sudden back thrust." Henry draws a lesson from Asahel's death: "Death often comes by ways we least suspect." Guzik treats the killing as self-defense, but notes it will lead Joab to take revenge. On Abner's appeal Henry says, "Those who make light of such unnatural contests, will find that they are bitterness to all concerned." Guzik observes that the ceasefire suited both commanders and settled nothing. The war would drag on, though David grew stronger while Saul's house grew weaker (3:1).

#### Pointing to Christ
David becomes king the right way: he asks God, waits for God and receives the crown from others instead of grabbing it. He also begins his reign by blessing those who were loyal to his rival, offering them *hesed* and inviting them to come under his rule. That is how Jesus, the Son of David, rules. He did not seize his kingdom but received it from the Father (Phil 2:9), and he invites even former enemies to come under his kind rule (Rom 5:10). Abner's question, "Shall the sword devour forever?", has no human answer in this chapter. Joab blows a trumpet and the war only pauses. The real end of hostility comes when Christ "himself is our peace," breaking down the dividing wall and making one people out of two (Eph 2:14–16).
