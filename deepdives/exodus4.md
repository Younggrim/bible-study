*Setting:* Moses is still at the burning bush on Mount Horeb. God has told him he will bring Israel out of Egypt and revealed his name, "I AM WHO I AM," the LORD (3:14–15). He has promised, "I will be with you," and told Moses that the elders "will listen to your voice" (3:12, 18). Moses has already raised two objections ("Who am I?" and "What is his name?"). Chapter 4 brings three more, and then the long road back to Egypt, including one of the strangest scenes in the Bible.

#### Moses Given Powerful Signs (vv1–17)
- **"But behold, they will not believe me" (v1).** Objection three. God has just said they *will* listen (3:18), and Moses contradicts him. After forty years away, he may well remember how the Hebrews mocked him the last time: "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (2:14).
- **"What is that in your hand?" "A staff" (v2).** It was an ordinary shepherd's staff, a symbol of Moses' humble life in Midian. God uses what is already in hand. By v20 it is called "the staff of God."
- **The staff becomes a serpent (vv3–4).** In Egypt the serpent stood for royal and divine power. Pharaoh wore a rearing cobra, the *uraeus*, on the front of his crown. Moses runs from it, and then God tells him to grab it "by the tail," which any snake handler knows is the dangerous way. Obeying means trusting God over instinct. The sign says the LORD has the power Pharaoh only claims.
- **"His hand was leprous like snow" (v6).** The Hebrew *tsara'at*, traditionally "leprosy," covers a range of serious skin diseases, not only Hansen's disease. It made a person unclean and cut off from the community (Lev 13), and only God could heal it (2 Kings 5:7). A hand struck and healed in a moment shows God's power over sickness and cleansing.
- **"They may believe the latter sign" (v8).** God is patient with unbelief. He provides sign after sign so the people will have reason to trust.
- **Water from the Nile becomes blood (v9).** This third sign previews the first plague (7:17–21). The Nile was Egypt's source of life and was honored as divine. Turning it to blood is a direct challenge to Egypt's gods.
- **"I am not eloquent… slow of speech and of tongue" (v10).** Objection four. Literally Moses says he is "heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue." Some think he had a stammer or speech impediment. Others note that Stephen called him "mighty in his words and deeds" in Egypt (Acts 7:22), and think forty years among shepherds had rusted his Egyptian, or that he was simply making excuses. The word "heavy" (*kaved*) will return as one of the words for Pharaoh's hardened heart (7:14).
- **"Who has made man's mouth?" (v11).** God answers again with himself. He made mouths, and he made eyes and ears, including those that do not work. Nothing about Moses' weakness surprises him. **"I will be with your mouth and teach you what you shall speak" (v12).** Jesus makes the same promise to disciples called before rulers (Luke 12:11–12; 21:14–15).
- **"Oh, my Lord, please send someone else" (v13).** The fifth and last objection isn't an objection at all, just a refusal. Moses has run out of reasons and says what he means.
- **"Then the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses" (v14).** God was patient with honest questions. He is angry at refusal. Yet even in anger he gives Moses help: his brother Aaron, three years older (7:7), already on his way. **"He will be glad in his heart."** There is no rivalry between them.
- **"He shall be your mouth, and you shall be as God to him" (v16).** Moses will give the message and Aaron will deliver it, the way a prophet speaks God's words. God repeats the arrangement in 7:1: "Aaron your brother shall be your prophet." As the story goes on, Moses mostly speaks for himself. By Deuteronomy he is giving whole speeches.
- **"Take in your hand this staff" (v17).** The ordinary staff becomes the visible sign of God's power through the plagues, at the Red Sea (14:16) and at the rock (17:5–6).
- **Commentators:** Henry reads Moses' reluctance kindly at first as "a humble diffidence of himself," noting that "the most fit for service are the least forward" and "a great deal of wisdom and true worth is concealed by a slow tongue." But at v13 he hears Moses saying, in effect, "Send by any hand but mine," and says "God justly resents our backwardness to serve him." Guzik is sharper. He argues Moses was "unwilling, not unable," that his speech excuse doesn't fit Acts 7:22, and that Aaron, who later made the golden calf, was more a rebuke than a reward. JFB supposes Moses "labored under a natural defect of utterance," and suggests God's displeasure showed in giving the priesthood to Aaron's line. The Tyndale notes observe that Aaron speaks for Moses less and less as the story goes on.

#### Moses Returns to Egypt (vv18–31)
- **"Please let me go back to my brothers in Egypt" (v18).** Moses asks his father-in-law's permission, as custom required. He doesn't mention the burning bush, perhaps from humility, or perhaps because it would sound unbelievable. Jethro's answer is generous: **"Go in peace."**
- **"All the men who were seeking your life are dead" (v19).** God answers the fear Moses had not voiced. Matthew echoes this verse almost word for word when Joseph is told to bring the child Jesus back from Egypt: "those who sought the child's life are dead" (Matt 2:20). Matthew wants readers to see Jesus as a new Moses.
- **"His wife and his sons… on a donkey… the staff of God in his hand" (v20).** Only Gershom has been named so far. A second son, Eliezer ("my God is help"), is named in 18:3–4. The humble procession is the start of Israel's deliverance.
- **"I will harden his heart" (v21).** This is the first mention of a theme that runs through the plagues. Three Hebrew verbs are used: *chazaq*, "to make strong or stubborn" (here), *kaved*, "to make heavy," and *qashah*, "to make hard" (7:3). The text says both that God hardened Pharaoh's heart and that Pharaoh hardened his own heart (8:15, 32). Most interpreters see God confirming and giving Pharaoh over to the stubbornness he already chose, without overriding his will. Paul uses Pharaoh to show God's freedom in mercy and judgment (Rom 9:17–18). The text holds both truths together without fully explaining how.
- **"Israel is my firstborn son… Let my son go that he may serve me" (vv22–23).** This is the first time the Bible calls Israel God's son. A firstborn son had special standing and inheritance in the family. God claims Israel as his own, in a family relationship, not just as a people he owns. "Serve" is again *avad*: Israel was made to serve God, not Pharaoh. **"I will kill your firstborn son"** looks all the way ahead to the tenth plague (11:5; 12:29). Pharaoh will be warned from the beginning.
- **"The LORD met him and sought to put him to death" (v24).** This is one of the hardest passages in the Old Testament. The Hebrew just says "him," so some think the son was in danger rather than Moses, though most read it as Moses. How God "sought" to kill him isn't said. It may have been a sudden, deadly illness. What is clear is the cause: Zipporah immediately circumcises their son.
- **Why circumcision mattered (v25).** Circumcision was the sign of God's covenant with Abraham, and God said any uncircumcised male "shall be cut off from his people" (Gen 17:10–14). The man about to lead Israel in the name of "the God of Abraham" had failed to give his own son the covenant sign. Perhaps Zipporah had objected, since Midianite custom may have differed. Either way, God would not let his messenger go to Egypt while living in disobedience. **"A flint"** was the traditional circumcision knife, still used in Joshua's day (Josh 5:2).
- **"Touched Moses' feet with it… a bridegroom of blood" (vv25–26).** "Feet" may be a polite way of referring to the private parts, as sometimes in Hebrew (Isa 7:20), or it may be literal. The blood of the circumcision seems to stand in for Moses, a kind of covering, and then **"he let him alone"** (v26). The phrase "bridegroom of blood" (*chatan damim*) is obscure. In Arabic a related root is used for circumcision, so it may be an old ritual phrase. It may also express Zipporah's distress. Some see a foreshadowing of Passover, where blood on the doorposts turns death away (12:13). The narrator adds that she said it "because of the circumcision," as if explaining an old saying. Zipporah and the boys seem to have gone back to Jethro at some point, and they rejoin Moses later (18:2–6).
- **"He went and met him at the mountain of God and kissed him" (v27).** God's word to Moses proves true right away: Aaron does come (4:14). Their meeting at Horeb, where God appeared, is the first confirmation that God is keeping his word.
- **The elders gathered, words spoken, signs done (vv29–30).** This is exactly what God told Moses to do (3:16). Aaron speaks, as arranged.
- **"The people believed… they bowed their heads and worshiped" (v31).** Moses' fear in v1 was unfounded. When they hear that the LORD "had visited" them, they believe. "Visited" is *paqad*, the word of Joseph's dying promise, "God will surely visit you" (Gen 50:24). Their faith here is real but fragile. One hard day in chapter 5 will shake it.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the lodging place "a halting place for the night," and thinks Moses was "overtaken by a sudden and dangerous malady" linked to the neglected circumcision. Henry imagines an angel's sword and draws the lesson: "Omissions are sins, and must come into judgment, and particularly the contempt and neglect of the seals of the covenant." He thinks Zipporah spoke "unreasonably" in calling Moses "a bloody husband." JFB is gentler, saying she spoke "in the painful excitement of her feelings" and acted out of love. Guzik sees the scene as God requiring Moses to give up a compromise at home before he could lead. On v21 Guzik argues that God never forced Pharaoh against his will but gave him over to what he already wanted. The Tyndale notes suggest the people might have taken God's demands less seriously if their deliverer ignored the covenant's one command.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses is a reluctant deliverer, slow to speak, quick to make excuses, and nearly disqualified by disobedience at home. Jesus is the deliverer who never hesitated: "I have come to do your will, O God" (Heb 10:7). God calls Israel "my firstborn son," and the New Testament presents Jesus as the true Son who comes out of Egypt (Matt 2:15) and lives the faithful sonship Israel failed to live. The threat on the road was lifted by blood, and in Christ "we have redemption through his blood" (Eph 1:7), a "circumcision made without hands" (Col 2:11). God gave Moses a brother to speak for him. Christ is our advocate who speaks for us before the Father (1 John 2:1).
