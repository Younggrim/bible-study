*Setting:* Jacob has just died in Egypt at 147, after blessing his twelve sons and charging them to bury him with Abraham and Isaac in the cave of Machpelah in Canaan (49:29–33). Joseph is about fifty-six, still second only to Pharaoh. The family has lived in Goshen for seventeen years, and the famine is long over. This last chapter of Genesis tells of two deaths and one great sentence. It closes the story of Abraham's family, and it also closes the book that began with God creating the world. Genesis opened with "In the beginning, God created." It ends with a coffin in Egypt, and a promise that God will come.

#### Jacob's Death and Burial (vv1–14)
- **"Joseph fell on his father's face and wept over him and kissed him" (v1).** God promised Jacob that "Joseph's hand shall close your eyes" (46:4), and it happened just so. JFB notes that closing the eyes and giving the last kiss was the duty of the family's head. Joseph is the man who weeps in Genesis, and his tears here are the plain grief of a son.
- **"Joseph commanded his servants the physicians to embalm his father" (v2).** Embalming was an Egyptian practice, not a Hebrew one. Joseph uses physicians, not the professional priest-embalmers, perhaps to avoid the religious rituals bound up with Egyptian mummification. The practical reason is clear: the body had to survive a long journey to Canaan.
- **"Forty days were required… And the Egyptians wept for him seventy days" (v3).** The Greek historian Herodotus (5th century BC) says the whole embalming process took seventy days. The Greek historian Diodorus says Egyptians mourned a king for seventy-two days, so seventy days for Jacob was close to royal honor, as the Tyndale notes observe. JFB explains the numbers as thirty days of embalming and forty more of anointing. However the days are counted, all Egypt honored Joseph's father as a great man.
- **"In my tomb that I hewed out for myself" (v5).** Joseph asks Pharaoh's household to pass on his request, perhaps because a man in mourning did not go before the king, as JFB suggests. The quoted words of Jacob are interesting. Abraham bought Machpelah (ch 23); Jacob did not dig it. The verb (*karah*) usually means "dig," but a nearly identical verb means "buy" (Deut 2:6; Hos 3:2), and some translate "that I bought for myself." Or Jacob may have prepared a burial place inside the cave. Joseph adds, **"Then I will return,"** to reassure Pharaoh.
- **"A very great company" (vv7–9).** Pharaoh's officials, the elders of Egypt, chariots and horsemen escort the funeral. It looks like a state funeral, and in a sense it is. Only the children and the flocks stay behind in Goshen. This is the first time Joseph has returned to Canaan since he was sold at seventeen, nearly forty years earlier. JFB estimates a journey of some 300 miles.
- **"The threshing floor of Atad, which is beyond the Jordan" (vv10–11).** A threshing floor was a flat, open space, a natural place for a large gathering. "Atad" means "bramble" or "thornbush." "Beyond the Jordan" is a puzzle, since the direct road from Egypt to Hebron does not cross the Jordan. Either the procession took a long route around the Dead Sea, the way Israel later came at the Exodus, or "beyond" is spoken from the point of view of someone on the east side of the river. The Canaanites named the place **Abel-mizraim**, a pun: *ʾebel* means "mourning," *ʾabel* means "meadow," and *Mizraim* means "Egypt." "The mourning of Egypt" or "the meadow of Egypt." Canaanites watched Egyptians weep seven days for a Hebrew herdsman.
- **"His sons carried him to the land of Canaan and buried him in the cave… at Machpelah" (vv12–13).** Jacob's sons do exactly what he commanded. Jacob lies with Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, and Leah (49:31), in the one corner of the promised land the family owned.
- **"Joseph returned to Egypt" (v14).** He keeps his word to Pharaoh. The whole family goes back. The promised land is honored with a grave, but the time to live in it has not come. God told Abraham four centuries would pass (15:13–16).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the burial as the family rededicating itself to the covenant God: Egypt is where they live, but Canaan is where they belong. JFB gives the most detail on Egyptian embalming and suggests the Egyptian escort stopped at Atad while the family went on alone to the cave. The Tyndale notes observe that this journey into Canaan was made in sorrow to bury a man; the next one would be to live there.

#### God's Good Purposes (vv15–21)
- **"It may be that Joseph will hate us and pay us back" (v15).** With their father gone, the brothers' old fear returns. Esau once planned to kill Jacob as soon as Isaac died (27:41). Maybe Joseph had only been waiting, too. Seventeen years of kindness have not quite convinced them. Guilt has a long memory. Matthew Henry says they grew suspicious of Joseph "not that he had given them any cause to be so."
- **"Your father gave this command before he died" (vv16–17).** They send a message through someone else, quoting Jacob asking Joseph to forgive them. Genesis records no such words from Jacob. Some interpreters, including Guzik, think the brothers made it up out of fear. Others think Jacob may well have said it, since he surely knew by then what they had done. The text does not say. Either way, this is the first time the brothers themselves ask for forgiveness in plain words: **"Please forgive the transgression of the servants of the God of your father."** They appeal to the God they share.
- **"Joseph wept when they spoke to him" (v17).** Henry: "These were tears of sorrow for their suspicion of him, and tears of tenderness upon their submission." It hurt him that after all these years they still feared him.
- **"His brothers also came and fell down before him and said, 'Behold, we are your servants'" (v18).** This is the last time the brothers bow, and the fullest fulfillment of the dreams. When Joseph first dreamed, they mocked him: "Are you indeed to rule over us?" (37:8). Now they offer themselves as his slaves. The Tyndale notes trace the bowing through 42:6, 43:26, 44:14, and here.
- **"Do not fear, for am I in the place of God?" (v19).** Joseph refuses the role they give him. Judgment and vengeance belong to God alone (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19). Henry puts it this way: "Am I in the place of God, to whom alone vengeance belongs?" Jacob once used the same words to Rachel when she blamed him for her barrenness (30:2). Joseph will not sit on God's throne, even though he sits next to Pharaoh's.
- **"You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (v20).** This is the key sentence of the Joseph story and one of the great sentences of the Bible. The same verb appears in both halves. *Ḥashab* means to think, plan, devise. "You planned evil, but God planned it for good." The brothers' sin was real and fully theirs. Joseph does not call it anything else. But inside their plan God was working his own plan, and his plan won. The purpose was **"that many people should be kept alive, as they are today"**: Egypt, the nations who came to buy grain, and above all the family of promise. This is what Joseph meant in 45:5–8, said now without any hesitation. The New Testament says the same about the cross: Jesus was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God," and yet crucified "by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23). Paul's "all things work together for good" (Rom 8:28) is this verse in general form.
- **"So do not fear; I will provide for you and your little ones" (v21).** Joseph does not merely spare them. He promises to keep feeding them and their children. **"He comforted them and spoke kindly to them"** is literally "he spoke to their hearts," the phrase God uses for speaking tenderly to his people (Isa 40:2; Hos 2:14). Forgiveness here is not a single statement but a promise of continued care.
- **Commentators:** Henry ties the two halves of v20 to the dreams: the brothers "thought evil (to disappoint the dreams), but God meant it unto good, in order to the fulfilling of the dreams." Guzik connects v20 with Romans 8:28 and adds that, had the family died in the famine, the line leading to the Messiah would have died with it. Guzik thinks the message from Jacob was likely invented; JFB passes over that question and stresses Joseph's "strongest assurances of his forgiveness." The Tyndale notes say Joseph wept because his brothers still feared reprisal.

#### The Death of Joseph (vv22–26)
- **"Joseph lived 110 years" (v22).** Egyptian texts describe 110 years as the ideal lifespan, a full and blessed life. Joshua also dies at 110 (Josh 24:29). Joseph lived fifty-four years after his father died.
- **"Joseph saw Ephraim's children of the third generation" (v23).** He lived to see great-great-grandchildren through Ephraim, a sign of blessing (Ps 128:6). **Machir** son of Manasseh became the father of a powerful clan that later settled Gilead (Num 32:39–40; Judg 5:14). His children "were counted as Joseph's own," literally "were born on Joseph's knees." Placing a newborn on someone's knees was a way of claiming the child as one's own, as Rachel did with Bilhah's sons (30:3). Joseph does for his great-grandsons what his father did for his sons (48:5, 12).
- **"God will visit you and bring you up out of this land" (v24).** Joseph's last words are not about Egypt, where he spent ninety-three years and rose to power. They are about leaving it. "Visit" is *paqad*, used of God coming to act, to keep a promise, to rescue (21:1, of Sarah). He names the land God "swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob." "Abraham, Isaac and Jacob" becomes a refrain through the rest of the Torah (Exod 3:6; 33:1; Deut 1:8).
- **"God will surely visit you, and you shall carry up my bones from here" (v25).** In Hebrew the verb is doubled for emphasis: "visiting, he will visit." Joseph is so sure of the promise that he arranges his burial around it. Centuries later, God sends Moses from the burning bush to tell Israel that he has "surely visited" them, the same doubled Hebrew verb (Exod 3:16, ESV "observed"), and when Israel finally leaves Egypt, "Moses took the bones of Joseph with him," quoting this oath (Exod 13:19). The bones were buried at Shechem (Josh 24:32), in the field Jacob left to Joseph (48:22), near the place where his brothers had first plotted against him (37:12–14). Hebrews singles this out as Joseph's act of faith: "By faith Joseph, at the end of his life, made mention of the exodus of the Israelites and gave directions concerning his bones" (Heb 11:22).
- **"They embalmed him, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt" (v26).** The last words of Genesis. Joseph is not buried. He is kept, waiting. The word for "coffin" is *ʾaron*, the same word later used for the ark of the covenant. An old Jewish tradition draws the picture: for forty years in the wilderness Israel carried two arks, one holding the bones of Joseph and one holding the tablets of God's law. The book that began with life breathed into dust ends with a body in a box in a foreign land. But the coffin is a promise. Its owner insisted it would not stay there.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read the oath about the bones as faith in God's promises, and both cite Hebrews 11:22. JFB notes that Joseph's body was kept until the Exodus. The Tyndale notes call Joseph's words in vv24–25 a summary of the hope of both Testaments: God will visit his people, bring the curse to an end, and keep his word to Abraham. They also point out that Joseph's body lay waiting in Egypt as a pledge of hope for slaves who would one day go home.

#### Pointing to Christ
"You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive." No sentence in Genesis points more clearly to the cross. Jesus, like Joseph, was rejected by his brothers, handed over to Gentiles, and counted among the dead, and through that great evil God brought about the saving of many. And like Joseph, the risen Jesus does not take revenge on those who wronged him. He says "do not fear," promises to provide, and speaks to their hearts. Genesis ends with a coffin waiting for God's visit. The Gospels open with God visiting his people in person: "he has visited and redeemed his people" (Luke 1:68). The bones in Egypt waited for an Exodus. In Christ, an empty tomb outside Jerusalem guarantees the greater one.
