*Setting:* 1 and 2 Chronicles were one book in the Hebrew Bible, called "the words of the days," meaning the record of events. It was written after the exile, most likely around 450–400 BC, when a small community of Jews had come back from Babylon to a ruined Jerusalem under Persian rule. Jewish tradition credits Ezra. Most scholars simply call the author "the Chronicler," probably a Levite, since the book cares so much about the temple, its music and its priests. Where Samuel and Kings explained to the exiles why they lost the land, Chronicles answers the question of those who came back: are we still God's people, and do his promises to David still stand? It opens with nine chapters of names. To modern readers that is the hardest part of the Bible to get through. To the first readers it was a lifeline. They had lost their king, their independence and much of their land, and these lists told them where they came from and that God had never let the line break. Chapter 1 covers the whole human race from Adam to Jacob's brother Esau, almost entirely condensed from Genesis 5, 10, 11, 25 and 36.

#### From Adam to Abraham (vv1–27)
- **"Adam, Seth, Enosh" (v1).** No introduction, no verbs, just names. The Chronicler starts with the first man, not with Abraham or David. Israel's story is part of the whole human story, and every nation on earth comes from the same father (Acts 17:26). Luke's genealogy of Jesus also runs back to "Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38).
- **No Cain and no Abel (vv1–4).** The line goes straight from Adam to Seth, the son given in place of murdered Abel (Gen 4:25). The Chronicler is not trying to name everyone. He follows the line that leads to the promise. That principle runs through all nine chapters: the side branches are listed briefly and then set aside, and the main line continues.
- **"Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech; Noah" (vv3–4).** Ten generations from Adam to Noah, as in Genesis 5. Enoch "walked with God" (Gen 5:24) and Noah found favor in his eyes (Gen 6:8), but none of that is said here. The first readers knew the stories. The names alone call them to mind.
- **The table of nations (vv5–23).** This is a shortened copy of Genesis 10, which lists about seventy peoples descended from Noah's three sons. The names are often of peoples and places rather than single persons, as JFB points out: "Egypt," the "Jebusites" and the "Amorites" are nations, not men.
- **Japheth (vv5–7).** The peoples to the north and west. **Madai** are the Medes, and **Javan** is the Greeks (the Ionians). **Tarshish** was a distant port in the far west, probably in Spain, the place Jonah tried to flee to (Jonah 1:3). **Kittim** is Cyprus. **Rodanim** is probably Rhodes. Genesis 10:4 reads "Dodanim," and in Hebrew the letters *d* and *r* look almost the same, the cause of many small spelling differences between the two lists.
- **Ham (vv8–16).** The peoples to the south and of Canaan. **Cush** is the region south of Egypt (modern Sudan). **Put** is probably Libya. **Canaan** heads the list of peoples Israel would later displace. **Nimrod** "was the first on earth to be a mighty man" (v10), the founder of the first great kingdoms of Babylon and Assyria (Gen 10:8–12). **The Philistines** came "from" the Casluhim (v12), and elsewhere they are linked with Caphtor, probably Crete (Amos 9:7).
- **Shem (vv17–23).** The peoples of the east. **Elam** was east of Babylon, the region of Susa where Persian kings later ruled (Neh 1:1). **Asshur** is Assyria, and **Aram** is Syria. For the readers of Chronicles, living under Persia after being deported by Babylon and remembering Assyria's cruelty, it was striking to see their captors in the list. Every empire that ever ruled Israel had a place in the family tree God started.
- **"Peleg (for in his days the earth was divided)" (v19).** The name sounds like the Hebrew verb *palag*, "to divide." Most take it as the scattering of the nations at Babel (Gen 11:1–9). It is the only comment the Chronicler makes in this section.
- **"Shem, Arpachshad, Shelah… Abram, that is, Abraham" (vv24–27).** The list now narrows from the nations to one line, ten generations from Shem to Abram (Gen 11:10–26). Luke 3:36 adds a Cainan between Arpachshad and Shelah, following the Greek Old Testament. JFB notes the omission and points to Luke. **"Abram, that is, Abraham"** marks the name change of Genesis 17:5, where God made him "the father of a multitude of nations." The whole first half of the chapter has been about those nations.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the list a reminder that "God has created of one blood all nations of men," and that Israel's origins were real history, unlike the obscure and legendary origin stories of other nations. He also says differences between the lists should not trouble readers, since "the things necessary to salvation are plain enough." Guzik notes that although God's name never appears in these verses, his work is behind nearly every name: Seth given, Enoch taken, Noah preserved, Abraham called. He adds that the lists included the very empires that had held Israel captive. JFB notes that the sons of Noah were "the founders of the new world," and that the Canaanite names are of peoples, not individuals.

#### From Abraham to Jacob (vv28–54)
- **"The sons of Abraham: Isaac and Ishmael" (v28).** Ishmael was older, but Isaac is named first. He is the son of the promise (Gen 17:19–21). Yet the Chronicler lists Ishmael's twelve sons before he goes on (vv29–31), just as God promised Ishmael "twelve princes" (Gen 17:20). Among them, **Kedar** became a well-known Arab tribe (Isa 21:16–17; Ps 120:5), and **Tema** an oasis town in Arabia.
- **"Keturah, Abraham's concubine" (v32).** Genesis 25:1 calls her a wife, and Genesis 25:6 calls her sons the sons of his concubines. She was a secondary wife without the full standing of Sarah. Her son **Midian** fathered the Midianites, the people Moses lived among and later Gideon fought. **Sheba and Dedan** were Arabian trading peoples.
- **"The sons of Isaac: Esau and Israel" (v34).** Chronicles almost always calls Jacob "Israel," the name God gave him (Gen 32:28). It is the name of the whole people, and the Chronicler wants his readers to see themselves in it.
- **Esau's sons (vv35–37).** Esau is the brother who sold his birthright (Gen 25:29–34). Again the side branch is listed first and then closed. **"And of Timna, Amalek" (v36)** is a shorthand. Genesis 36:12 explains that Timna was the concubine of Esau's son Eliphaz, and Amalek was her son. The Amalekites became Israel's long-running enemy, from the wilderness (Exod 17:8–16) to Saul's failure (1 Sam 15) to Haman in the book of Esther, whom Jewish tradition counted as a descendant of Agag the Amalekite (Esth 3:1).
- **The sons of Seir (vv38–42).** Seir was the mountain region south of the Dead Sea, and its first people were the Horites (Gen 36:20). Esau's family moved in and mixed with them. **"Lotan's sister was Timna" (v39)** ties the two lists together.
- **"The kings who reigned in the land of Edom before any king reigned over the people of Israel" (v43).** Esau's descendants had kings long before Jacob's did. The younger brother received the promise, but the older one got a throne first. God's timing is not the world's. Edom's kings did not come from one family line, and each seems to have ruled from his own city.
- **Jobab (v44).** An old tradition, found in an ending added to the Greek translation of Job, identifies Jobab with Job, and JFB passes it on. It is a guess with little support, but Job's land and friends do belong to this region (Job 1:1; 2:11, where Eliphaz the Temanite appears, compare v45 "the land of the Temanites").
- **Small differences from Genesis 36 (vv46–54).** The last king is Hadad here and "Hadar" in Genesis 36:39 (again *d* and *r*), and his city is Pai here and "Pau" there. Genesis 36:39 does not mention Hadad's death, but Chronicles adds "and Hadad died" (v51). Chronicles is copying from Genesis with the small variations that come from copying names across centuries. As Henry says, none of it touches anything that matters for faith.
- **"These are the chiefs of Edom" (v54).** The chiefs were clan leaders, and many of the names are also places. With them the Chronicler closes the book on Esau. The next chapter starts with the twelve sons of Israel.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the genealogy "is from hence confined to the posterity of Abraham," and reflects on the long lists of the dead: "Short is our passage through time into eternity." Guzik calls the list of Edom's kings something like walking through a graveyard, names with no record of their deeds, and draws comfort that God keeps track of every life. He also notes that Esau was not chosen yet still blessed. JFB repeats the tradition that Jobab was Job. Guzik, like most scholars, thinks the author was a Levite, possibly Ezra or a contemporary.

#### Pointing to Christ
Chronicles begins with Adam and narrows, generation by generation, from all nations to Abraham and then to Israel. That narrowing was never meant to shut out the nations. God's promise to Abraham was that in him "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). The New Testament opens the same way, with "the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham" (Matt 1:1). Luke runs his list all the way back to Adam (Luke 3:38). The line kept through all these names led to the one in whom the scattered nations of verses 5–23 would be gathered back together (Rev 7:9).
