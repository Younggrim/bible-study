*Setting:* Egypt, in the spring, on the night of the tenth plague. Moses has announced that "about midnight" every firstborn in Egypt will die (11:4–5), and he has walked out of Pharaoh's presence for the last time. Chapter 12 slows the story down. Before the judgment falls, God gives Israel the Passover, and the narrative stops to set out how it is to be kept "throughout your generations." Then midnight comes, Egypt wails, and Israel finally walks out. This chapter is the heart of Exodus and one of the great hinges of the whole Bible. Jesus will eat his last supper at this feast, and the New Testament will call him "our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7).

#### The Passover (vv1–28)
- **"This month shall be for you the beginning of months" (v1–2).** God resets Israel's calendar around their rescue. The month is Abib, meaning "fresh ears of grain" (13:4), later called by its Babylonian name Nisan (Neh 2:1), in March–April. Israel also kept a civil year that began in the autumn, but the religious year starts here. Their life as a people begins with redemption.
- **"Every man shall take a lamb according to their fathers' houses" (v3).** The lamb is chosen on the tenth day and kept until the fourteenth (v6). For four days it lives with the family, which made the sacrifice personal and costly. The Hebrew word *seh* covers a young sheep or goat, so v5 allows either. Small households share with neighbors **"according to what each can eat"** (v4), so no one is left out and nothing is wasted.
- **"Without blemish, a male a year old" (v5).** A sacrifice had to be whole and healthy, the best and not the leftovers (Mal 1:8). Peter draws on this: we were redeemed "with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Pet 1:19).
- **"Kill their lambs at twilight" (v6).** Literally "between the two evenings." Jewish tradition took it as mid-afternoon to sunset, and in Jesus' day the lambs were slaughtered at the temple from about 3 p.m. The Samaritans and some others read it as between sunset and dark. **"The whole assembly"** kills its lambs at once, a single act of a whole people.
- **"Put it on the two doorposts and the lintel" (v7).** The lintel is the beam across the top of the door. The doorway marked the boundary of the home, the place of coming in and going out (compare Deut 6:9). The blood is not for decoration. It marks a house where death has already happened, the lamb's death in place of the firstborn.
- **"Roasted on the fire; with unleavened bread and bitter herbs" (v8).** Roasting whole was quick and kept the animal intact (v9: "its head with its legs and its inner parts"). Unleavened bread is flat bread made without yeast, quick to bake. The bitter herbs, probably wild greens like endive or chicory, recalled the bitterness of slavery (1:14 says the Egyptians "made their lives bitter"). Later Jewish practice added more symbols, but these three are the core.
- **"Let none of it remain until the morning" (v10).** This was a sacred meal, not ordinary food to be saved for leftovers.
- **"With your belt fastened, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand" (v11).** People normally ate reclining or sitting with sandals off. Israel eats standing, dressed for the road, ready to leave. **"Eat it in haste."** The word *chippazon* means hurried flight, used again of the exodus in Deuteronomy 16:3. **"It is the LORD's Passover."** The word *pesach* names the feast from what God does in vv13 and 23.
- **"On all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgments: I am the LORD" (v12).** The plagues were more than a disaster. They were a trial of Egypt's gods, and this is the verdict (Num 33:4). Pharaoh was thought to be a god, and his firstborn was his divine heir.
- **"When I see the blood, I will pass over you" (v13).** The verb is *pasach*, "to pass or spring over," and so to spare. Isaiah uses it of God protecting Jerusalem "like birds hovering": "he will spare and rescue it" (Isa 31:5). Some scholars therefore read it as "stand guard over" rather than "skip." Either way, the point is the same. Safety did not depend on how good the family was, or how strongly they felt about it, but on the blood. **"The blood shall be a sign for you."** The sign was for Israel's assurance as well as God's notice.
- **"A memorial day… a statute forever" (v14).** "Memorial" is *zikkaron*, a remembrance. Israel is to relive this night every year. Jewish families still keep it, and the Passover *seder* (order of service) retells this chapter.
- **"Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread" (v15–20).** The Feast of Unleavened Bread runs from the 14th to the 21st (v18) and becomes joined to Passover. Every scrap of leaven, a lump of old fermented dough kept to start the next batch, had to be cleared from the house. The haste of the first night (v34, 39) becomes a yearly reminder. Later Scripture uses leaven as a picture of sin that spreads (Matt 16:6; 1 Cor 5:6–8; Gal 5:9), though not always (Matt 13:33). **"Cut off from Israel" (v15)** means removed from the community, by death or exclusion. **"Whether he is a sojourner or a native" (v19).** Foreigners living among Israel were bound by the same law.
- **"Take a bunch of hyssop and dip it in the blood that is in the basin" (v22).** Hyssop is a small, bushy plant (probably a kind of marjoram) whose stems make a good brush. It becomes the plant of cleansing in Israel's law (Lev 14:4; Num 19:18), David prays "purge me with hyssop" (Ps 51:7), and a sponge on a hyssop branch is lifted to Jesus on the cross (John 19:29). The word for "basin" can also mean "threshold," so some think the blood was caught at the doorstep itself. **"None of you shall go out of the door of his house until the morning."** Safety is inside the marked house.
- **"Will not allow the destroyer to enter" (v23).** "The destroyer" (*hammashchit*) is God's agent of judgment, like the angel who struck Jerusalem in David's day (2 Sam 24:16) and Sennacherib's army (2 Kgs 19:35). Hebrews 11:28 says Moses kept the Passover "so that the Destroyer of the firstborn might not touch them." It is still the LORD who passes through to strike.
- **"When your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?'" (v26).** The feast is designed to make children ask questions. The answer is a story, not a theory: **"he passed over the houses of the people of Israel… but spared our houses" (v27).** Note "our houses," spoken by people who were not there. Every generation counts itself among the rescued.
- **"The people bowed their heads and worshiped… so they did" (vv27–28).** Unlike Pharaoh, Israel hears and obeys. Hebrews 11:28 calls it faith.
- **Commentators:** Henry opens with "It is good to begin the day, and begin the year, and especially to begin our lives, with God." On the lamb he writes, "Christ is the Lamb of God," and adds, "It was not enough that the blood of the lamb was shed, but it must be sprinkled." The meal was eaten "in a departing posture," and "all the old leaven of sin must be put far from us." JFB notes that "the smallest deformity or defect made a lamb unfit for sacrifice," explains "twilight" as "the interval between the sun's beginning to decline, and sunset," and the bitter herbs as meant "to remind the Israelites of their affliction in Egypt." On v12 JFB suggests "gods" may mean "princes and grandees," though he records the Jewish tradition that Egypt's idols were broken that night. The Hebrew *elohim* can mean rulers (Ps 82:6), but Numbers 33:4 points to real gods. Guzik stresses that believing in the blood was not enough; each family had to put it on the door, and that the four days with the lamb made its death costly. The Tyndale notes add that God could not simply exempt Israel this time, because death comes through sin, and sin must be either punished or atoned for.

#### The Tenth Plague: Death of the Firstborn (vv29–32)
- **"At midnight the LORD struck down all the firstborn" (v29).** Exactly as foretold in 11:4–5, the list runs from **"the firstborn of Pharaoh"** to **"the firstborn of the captive who was in the dungeon."** No one is too high or too low.
- **"There was not a house where someone was not dead" (v30).** "A great cry" is the same word as Israel's cry under slavery (3:7, 9). Not every home had a firstborn son, but many had a firstborn father or grandfather, and the text speaks of the whole nation in grief.
- **"Up, go out from among my people" (v31).** The man who said "on the day you see my face you shall die" (10:28) now sends for Moses in the night. Every one of his conditions collapses: **"both you and the people… as you have said… your flocks and your herds… as you have said" (vv31–32).** Moses gets everything he asked for.
- **"And bless me also!" (v32).** The proud king, whom Egyptians called a son of the gods, asks a Hebrew slave's God to bless him. It is not a confession of faith, but it is a complete reversal.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It was at midnight, which added to the terror of it," and "the Egyptians could have no help, no comfort, from their neighbours, all being involved in the same calamity." He paraphrases Pharaoh's last words: "let me have your prayers, that I may not be plagued for what is past, when you are gone." JFB ties the judgment to Egypt's earlier crime of drowning Israel's sons. Guzik connects the plague with Egyptian belief in Pharaoh's divinity and sees it as Pharaoh losing his own firstborn because he would not release God's firstborn (4:22–23). The Tyndale notes observe that Pharaoh recognized the LORD's power without ever admitting the LORD was God.

#### The Exodus (vv33–42)
- **"We shall all be dead" (v33).** The Egyptians fear that the plague will spread to everyone. They hurry Israel out.
- **"Their dough before it was leavened" (v34).** There is no time to let bread rise. The kneading bowls, wrapped in cloaks and slung over shoulders, show how fast they left. The feast in vv15–20 will remember exactly this.
- **"Thus they plundered the Egyptians" (v36).** God promised this to Abraham (Gen 15:14) and to Moses (3:21–22). "Plundered" is a word for stripping a defeated enemy. Israel has won a war without lifting a sword. The asking (*sha'al*) was a request, not a loan, and the Egyptians gave freely because **"the LORD had given the people favor."**
- **"From Rameses to Succoth" (v37).** Rameses was one of the store cities Israel had built (1:11), in the eastern Nile Delta. Succoth means "booths" or shelters, and many scholars place it at Tell el-Maskhuta in the Wadi Tumilat, a route east toward the wilderness.
- **"About six hundred thousand men on foot, besides women and children."** With families, that suggests a total of two million or more, which raises real questions about how such a crowd crossed the desert. Some take the number at face value as God's great blessing (1:7). Others suggest the Hebrew *'eleph*, "thousand," can also mean a clan or military unit, which would make the total much smaller. Faithful interpreters hold both views. What is not in doubt is that Israel had grown from seventy people (1:5) into a nation.
- **"A mixed multitude also went up with them" (v38).** Non-Israelites came too: perhaps Egyptians who had come to fear the LORD (9:20), other slaves, or people who saw a chance to escape. Some later stirred up complaining (Num 11:4), but the door was open to outsiders from the start, as vv43–49 make clear.
- **"The time that the people of Israel lived in Egypt was 430 years" (v40).** God told Abraham his offspring would be afflicted "four hundred years" (Gen 15:13), a round number. The Greek Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch read "in Egypt and in the land of Canaan," which would count from Abraham and leave about 215 years in Egypt. Paul's 430 years from the promise to the law (Gal 3:17) may follow that reading. The standard Hebrew text, which the ESV follows, gives the full 430 years to Egypt. Scholars still debate which is original.
- **"On that very day, all the hosts of the LORD went out" (v41).** "Hosts" means armies or ranks. A slave people walks out like an army, with the LORD as their commander, on the day God set.
- **"A night of watching by the LORD" (v42).** God kept watch over Israel that night, so Israel keeps watch to the LORD every year after. The word for "watching" (*shimmurim*) is related to the verb "to keep" in Psalm 121:4: "he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep."
- **Commentators:** JFB says "borrow conveys a wrong meaning," since the Hebrew means to ask or demand, and Israel was owed unpaid wages. He calls Succoth "probably nothing more than a place of temporary encampment," takes the mixed multitude as "a great rabble" of slaves and poor people, and counts the 430 years from Abraham, so that the stay in Egypt itself "did not exceed two hundred fifteen years." Henry says God "inclined the hearts of the Egyptians to furnish them with what they desired." Guzik pictures the long years as a pregnancy and the plagues as labor pains, with Israel born as a nation in a day. The Tyndale notes set out the problems with the large numbers fairly and leave the question open.

#### Institution of the Passover (vv43–51)
- **"No foreigner shall eat of it" (v43).** These extra rules answer a question the mixed multitude raised: who may share the meal? A "foreigner" here is an outsider passing through, with no commitment to Israel's God.
- **"Every slave that is bought for money may eat of it after you have circumcised him" (v44).** Circumcision was the sign of God's covenant with Abraham (Gen 17:10–14). A slave who joined the household joined the covenant too. A **"hired worker"** (v45), there temporarily, did not.
- **"You shall not break any of its bones" (v46).** The lamb was to be eaten whole, in one house, nothing taken outside. John sees this fulfilled when the soldiers did not break Jesus' legs on the cross: "Not one of his bones will be broken" (John 19:36; compare Ps 34:20).
- **"If a stranger shall sojourn with you and would keep the Passover" (v48).** A foreigner who settled in Israel could keep the Passover once he and his males were circumcised. **"He shall be as a native of the land."** **"There shall be one law for the native and for the stranger" (v49).** From its first night, Israel's feast was open to any outsider willing to come under God's covenant. Outsiders like Rahab and Ruth will later be welcomed into Israel in the same spirit.
- **"All the people of Israel did just as the LORD commanded" (v50).** The chapter closes on obedience, and with v51 restating the great fact: **"on that very day the LORD brought the people of Israel out."**
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that only those inside the covenant could eat, but anyone could enter it, and he connects this with Paul's call to "celebrate the festival" with the bread of sincerity and truth (1 Cor 5:7–8). He and JFB both point to John 19:36 for the unbroken bones. The Tyndale notes say the real issue for outsiders was whether they would fully join Israel and obey the LORD.

#### Pointing to Christ
This is one of the clearest pictures of the gospel in the Old Testament, and the New Testament says so directly. John the Baptist calls Jesus "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). Jesus chose Passover to die, and at the Passover meal he took the bread and cup and said, "This is my body… this is my blood of the covenant" (Mark 14:22–24). Like the lamb, he was without blemish (1 Pet 1:19), and not one of his bones was broken (John 19:36). Paul writes, "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7). The firstborn of Israel were not spared because they were better than Egypt's, but because a lamb died in their place and its blood was on the door. In the same way, salvation rests not on the strength of our feelings or the goodness of our record, but on Christ's blood. And the door was open to any outsider who would come in.
