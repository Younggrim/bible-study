*Setting:* Paul writes from Ephesus in about AD 53–55. Chapters 12–14 are one long answer to another question in the Corinthians' letter, introduced as before by "Now concerning" (7:1; 8:1). The church was rich in spiritual gifts (1:7), but it had come to treat them as a ladder of status, and one gift above all, speaking in tongues, as proof of being truly "spiritual." Chapter 12 lays the foundation: every gift comes from the one Spirit, every believer has a part, and the church is a single body in which no member is optional. Chapter 13 shows the way all gifts must be used, which is love. Chapter 14 then deals with tongues and prophecy in the gathered church.

#### Spiritual Gifts (vv1–11)
- **"Now concerning spiritual gifts" (v1).** The Greek has just one word, *pneumatikōn*, "the spiritual things" or "the spiritual people." "Gifts" is the translators' addition. The word may be the Corinthians' own label, since they liked to call themselves "spiritual" (3:1; 14:37). From v4 Paul uses a different word, *charismata*, "gifts of grace." That shift already makes his point. These are not marks of spiritual rank but free gifts of grace.
- **"When you were pagans you were led astray to mute idols" (v2).** Literally "when you were Gentiles," though Paul now treats the Corinthians as part of God's people. Pagan worship in Corinth could be wild and ecstatic, with people swept along in frenzy. "However you were led" suggests being carried away by forces outside one's control. The idols themselves are "mute," unable to say anything, which contrasts with the living God whose Spirit gives speech. The point is that being carried away is not, in itself, a sign of the Holy Spirit.
- **"No one speaking in the Spirit of God ever says 'Jesus is accursed!'" (v3).** *Anathema* means something handed over to God's curse (Gal 1:8–9). Why would anyone in church say such a thing? Scholars have suggested several answers: an ecstatic speaker in the meeting actually cried it out; Jewish opponents pressed believers to curse Jesus in the synagogue (Paul had once done exactly that, Acts 26:11); or Paul simply states the most extreme opposite case to make his rule clear. Some scholars also point to curse tablets found at Corinth, in which a god is asked to curse someone, and read the cry as "Jesus *grant* a curse." Whatever the background, the test is plain.
- **"No one can say 'Jesus is Lord' except in the Holy Spirit" (v3).** "Jesus is Lord" (*Kyrios Iēsous*) is probably the oldest Christian confession (Rom 10:9; Phil 2:11). In the Greek Old Testament *Kyrios* is the word used for God's own name, the LORD, and in the Roman world "Caesar is lord" was a loyalty oath. Anyone can say the words, of course. Paul means saying them truly, with a life placed under Jesus. The first sign of the Spirit is not a dramatic gift but a heart that bows to Jesus.
- **"Varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit… same Lord… same God" (vv4–6).** Three parallel lines: gifts (*charismata*) from the Spirit, services (*diakoniai*) for the Lord, and activities (*energēmata*, workings) from God. Paul is not making a doctrine class out of it, but he quietly names Spirit, Lord (Jesus) and God (the Father) together as the source of everything the church does. It is one of the clearest early outlines of the Trinity (compare 2 Cor 13:14). Variety in the church reflects the unity-in-diversity of God himself.
- **"It is the same God who empowers them all in everyone" (v6).** "Them all in everyone" covers every gift and every believer. God is working in all of them, not only in the people with striking gifts.
- **"To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good" (v7).** Two key phrases. "To each": every believer has a gift (also v11; 1 Pet 4:10). "For the common good": a gift is not a personal trophy but a tool to build others up. That is the test Paul will apply in chapter 14.
- **"The utterance of wisdom… the utterance of knowledge" (v8).** Literally "a word of wisdom" and "a word of knowledge." These were the Corinthians' favorite words (1:5, 17–25; 8:1). The Bible does not define them precisely. Many think a "word of wisdom" is God-given insight into how to apply his truth to a situation, and a "word of knowledge" God-given understanding of the truth itself, or a specific insight given by revelation. It is wiser to admit some uncertainty than to build exact definitions on these few words.
- **"To another faith" (v9).** Not saving faith, which every believer has, but an unusual confidence that God will act in a particular case, "faith so as to remove mountains" (13:2; Matt 17:20).
- **"Gifts of healing" (v9).** Both words are plural in Greek: "gifts of healings." This may suggest different gifts for different kinds of sickness, or that each healing is itself a fresh gift.
- **"The working of miracles" (v10).** Literally "workings of powers" (*energēmata dynameōn*). "Workings" is the same word as "activities" in v6, and *dynamis* is the word for power often used of Jesus' miracles.
- **"Prophecy" (v10).** In the New Testament, prophecy is speaking a message given by God for the church's "upbuilding and encouragement and consolation" (14:3). It can include prediction (Acts 11:28; 21:10–11), but it is not mainly about the future. Prophecy in the church was to be weighed and tested (14:29; 1 Thess 5:20–21).
- **"The ability to distinguish between spirits" (v10).** The gift to tell whether a message or a power is from God, from a demonic spirit or merely from a human being. It pairs naturally with prophecy (1 John 4:1).
- **"Various kinds of tongues… interpretation of tongues" (v10).** Speaking in a language the speaker has not learned, by the Spirit's prompting. At Pentecost the tongues were real human languages that visitors recognized (Acts 2:6–11). Paul speaks of "tongues of men and of angels" (13:1) and of speech no one understands without interpretation (14:2), so many think the Corinthian gift included non-human speech. Christians differ here. Notice that tongues and interpretation come last in this list and in v28. That is almost certainly deliberate, since the Corinthians had put tongues first.
- **"Who apportions to each one individually as he wills" (v11).** The Spirit is a person who chooses, not a force people can tap. No one chooses his own gift, so no one can boast of it, and no one can despise the gift given to someone else.
- **Readings differ on today.** Most Pentecostal, charismatic and "continuationist" believers hold that all these gifts continue in the church today and should be sought and used under Scripture's guidance. "Cessationist" believers hold that the more miraculous gifts (such as prophecy, tongues and healing) served to confirm the apostles' message while the New Testament was being given, and ceased with that foundational age (Eph 2:20; Heb 2:3–4). Many others hold a middle view: open to God acting this way, but cautious. All agree that the Spirit still gives every believer gifts for the common good, and that love matters more than any gift.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that "gifts" in v1 is supplied by translators and that "activities" in v6 and "working" in v10 share the same word, *energēmata*, which the lexicon confirms. He sees vv4–6 as a quiet picture of the Trinity, and JFB agrees, calling the three terms "an ascending climax." Matthew Henry stresses that gifts are not the same as grace, since a person can have great gifts and still not have a heart right with God (compare Matt 7:22–23). On v3 he writes, "No man can call Christ Lord, with a believing subjection to him," apart from the Spirit. JFB defines a word of wisdom as revealed insight into redemption and a word of knowledge as the gift of explaining truths "ALREADY REVEALED." The two commentators part ways on whether the gifts continue. Guzik argues at length that they have not ceased and that the New Testament never separates "sign gifts" from others. JFB treats the gifts as "the signs of the Spirit's continued efficacious presence" in the church, though it describes several of them as belonging to the apostolic age.

#### One Body with Many Members (vv12–31)
- **"So it is with Christ" (v12).** Paul does not say "so it is with the church," but "so it is with Christ." The church is so united to Christ that it can be called by his name. Jesus said the same to Paul on the Damascus road: "Why are you persecuting *me*?" (Acts 9:4).
- **The body picture.** Comparing a community to a body was common in the ancient world. The Roman historian Livy tells of Menenius Agrippa, who calmed a strike by the common people with a fable about the limbs refusing to feed the belly. That version told the lower classes to stay in their place. Paul turns it the other way: the "higher" members must honor the weaker. Corinth also had a famous healing shrine of Asclepius, where excavators found clay models of hands, feet, eyes, ears and other body parts, left as thank-offerings for healing. A body cut into separate parts was a familiar sight in Corinth.
- **"In one Spirit we were all baptized into one body" (v13).** Every believer was placed into the body of Christ by the Spirit. Readers differ on what exactly this baptism is. Many hold it is the Spirit's work at conversion, shown outwardly in water baptism. Some Pentecostals read "baptism in the Spirit" elsewhere (Acts 1:5) as a later, separate experience, but even many of them take this verse to describe conversion, since Paul says "we *all*." Either way the stress falls on "all" and "one." There are no first- and second-class Christians.
- **"Jews or Greeks, slaves or free" (v13).** The deepest dividing lines in that world, ethnic and social, are crossed (Gal 3:28; Col 3:11). At Corinth, where rich and poor were divided even at the Lord's Supper (11:21–22), this was pointed.
- **"All were made to drink of one Spirit" (v13).** A picture of being soaked through, like dry ground watered (Isa 32:15; 44:3; John 7:37–39). Some, including JFB, also hear an echo of the cup of the Lord's Supper.
- **"If the foot should say, 'Because I am not a hand…'" (vv15–16).** The first problem: members who think they are not needed. The foot compares itself with the hand, the ear with the eye, and decides it does not belong. Paul says feelings of inferiority do not change the facts. A believer belongs to the body whether it feels that way or not.
- **"If the whole body were an eye…" (v17).** A comic picture: a giant eyeball, rolling around unable to hear or smell. A church where everyone had the same gift, such as tongues, would be a monster, not a body.
- **"God arranged the members in the body, each one of them, as he chose" (v18).** The same note as v11. Every member is where God put it, on purpose.
- **"The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you'" (v21).** The second problem: members who think others are not needed. This is aimed at the "strong," the gifted and the well-off in Corinth, who looked down on others. "The head to the feet": the highest part of the body cannot do without the lowest.
- **"The parts of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable" (v22).** Note "seem." Some of the most vital organs are hidden and fragile. In the church, the quiet, unseen and frail are not extras but essential.
- **"Our unpresentable parts are treated with greater modesty" (vv23–24).** The body's private parts are covered with special care. We give them honor by clothing them, while the face needs no covering. Paul applies it: the less visible members of the church deserve more care, not less.
- **"God has so composed the body, giving greater honor to the part that lacked it" (v24).** "Composed" means mixed or blended together, as colors are blended. God built the body so that its weaker parts would be honored. That is the way the gospel works: God chose "what is weak in the world" (1:27).
- **"That there may be no division in the body" (v25).** "Division" is *schisma*, the word from 1:10 and 11:18. The whole body picture is aimed at the factions in Corinth.
- **"If one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together" (v26).** A stubbed toe makes the whole body wince. In a real body, members cannot be indifferent to each other. The second half is harder than the first. It is often easier to grieve with others than to be glad when someone else is honored (Rom 12:15).
- **"You are the body of Christ and individually members of it" (v27).** Paul makes it direct. The Corinthian church, in its local gatherings, is a living expression of Christ's body, and each believer has a place in it.
- **"First apostles, second prophets, third teachers" (v28).** The first three are numbered, and they are the ones who speak God's word to the church. Apostles were eyewitnesses commissioned by the risen Christ, the church's foundation (Eph 2:20), and in that full sense they had no successors. Prophets spoke revealed messages. Teachers explained and passed on the faith. Then the list becomes simply "then… then": miracles, healings, helping, administrating and tongues.
- **"Helping, administrating" (v28).** These are the only places these two words appear in the New Testament. "Helping" (*antilēmpseis*) is taking hold of a load with someone, giving practical aid. "Administrating" (*kybernēseis*) comes from the word for a ship's pilot, the one who steers. The lexicon gives "steering, pilotage," and then "government." Paul slips two humble, ordinary-looking gifts into a list of dramatic ones, and tongues again comes last.
- **"Are all apostles?… Do all speak with tongues?" (vv29–30).** Each question in Greek begins with a word (*mē*) that expects the answer "No." Not everyone speaks in tongues, and no gift is a requirement for being fully Christian. Readers who hold that every believer should speak in tongues usually distinguish a private prayer language from a public gift. Others think these questions rule that out.
- **"But earnestly desire the higher gifts" (v31).** "Earnestly desire" (*zēloute*) can be read two ways in Greek. Most translations read a command: seek the gifts that build up the church most, as 14:1 and 14:12 say. Some scholars read it as a statement, perhaps with irony: "You are eagerly seeking what you think are the greater gifts," followed by Paul's correction. Either way, "higher" is measured by how much a gift helps others, as chapter 14 will show, and prophecy, not tongues, is Paul's example.
- **"And I will show you a still more excellent way" (v31).** Literally "a way beyond comparison." Love is not one more gift on the list. It is the road all the gifts must travel on, and it leads straight into chapter 13.
- **Commentators:** All three find in the body a call to humility and care. Matthew Henry: "the meanest member of his body is as much a member as the noblest." On v26 he writes, "Christian sympathy is a great branch of Christian duty," and on honor, "we should rejoice with them and reckon ourselves honoured in them." JFB links the passage to Menenius Agrippa's fable and quotes Chrysostom's picture of the whole body bending to pull a thorn from the heel. It reads "helps" as deacons' work and "governments" as the guiding work of elders, from the helmsman image, which the lexicon supports. JFB also notes that the oldest manuscripts read "the greatest gifts" in v31. Guzik reads v13 as the Spirit's baptism rather than water baptism. He holds that the apostles' foundational authority is not repeated, that a person claiming to be a prophet must meet the standard of Deuteronomy 18, and that tongues are not for everyone. He calls the gifts containers and love the contents. Henry ends where Paul does: "True charity is greatly to be preferred to the most glorious gifts."

#### Pointing to Christ
The body has one head, and one name: "so it is with Christ." Every gift is his gift, poured out by the Spirit he sent after he ascended (Eph 4:7–8). He is the one who gave "greater honor to the part that lacked it," stooping to touch lepers and seat the poor at his table. And he is the member who suffered for all, so that his body might share his life. The first work of the Spirit is to make a person able to say, and mean, "Jesus is Lord."
