*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. This chapter closes the long law section of Moses' second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapter 25 ended with laws about honest weights and a command to remember Amalek. Now Moses looks ahead to the day Israel will actually live in the land and gives two short liturgies, words to be spoken aloud at the sanctuary: one when a farmer brings the first of his harvest, and one when he has finished giving the third-year tithe to the poor. The chapter ends with a solemn exchange of vows between the LORD and Israel. Chapters 27–28 then turn to the blessings and curses that seal the covenant. The whole section began with worship at "the place that the LORD your God will choose" (12:5), and it ends there too.

#### Offerings of Firstfruits and Tithes (vv1–11)
- **"When you come into the land… and have taken possession of it and live in it" (v1).** Moses speaks as if it is certain. Israel has not crossed the Jordan yet, and the Canaanite cities are still standing, but the ritual is planned for a harvest that has not been planted. The words assume that God will keep his promise.
- **"Some of the first of all the fruit of the ground" (v2).** The first ripe produce of the year, a token portion carried **"in a basket."** The firstfruits belonged to God (Exod 23:19; Num 18:12–13). Many think this was a special offering for the first harvest in the land, though Jewish tradition later made it a yearly custom between Pentecost and the autumn feasts, with farmers walking to Jerusalem in procession. Giving the first, before knowing how big the rest of the harvest would be, was an act of trust.
- **"The place that the LORD your God will choose, to make his name to dwell there" (v2).** This phrase runs through Deuteronomy (12:5, 11; 14:23; 16:2). It was first Shiloh (Josh 18:1) and later Jerusalem. God's "name" dwelling there means his presence and ownership are made known in that place.
- **"I declare today… that I have come into the land that the LORD swore to our fathers to give us" (v3).** Every farmer, generation after generation, says, "I have come into the land." Even an Israelite born there centuries later speaks as one who has personally received the promise. The gift is never old news.
- **"The priest shall take the basket… and set it down before the altar" (v4).** The basket goes before the altar, the place of sacrifice and meeting with God. The produce is set at God's feet, as the owner of the land, before it is eaten.
- **"A wandering Aramean was my father" (v5).** This is Jacob. His mother Rebekah came from Aram (Gen 25:20), he took his wives from Aram, and he served twenty hard years there under Laban (Gen 31:38–42). Aram is roughly Syria and upper Mesopotamia, which is why the KJV has "A Syrian." The word translated "wandering" is *'oved*, from *'abad*, whose basic meaning in the lexicon is "perish." The older translation "ready to perish" (KJV) reflects that. The ESV's "wandering" reflects a sense found in a few places, of an animal that strays and is lost (compare 22:3). Either way the point is the same: Israel began with one homeless, vulnerable man, with nothing to boast of.
- **"Few in number… there he became a nation, great, mighty, and populous" (v5).** Jacob's household was seventy people when they went down to Egypt (Gen 46:27). The promise to Abraham of descendants like the stars (Gen 15:5) was fulfilled in Egypt, in the very place of slavery.
- **"The Egyptians treated us harshly… then we cried to the LORD… and the LORD heard… and saw" (vv6–7).** The confession follows Exodus 2–3 closely: oppression, crying out, God hearing, seeing and knowing (Exod 2:23–25; 3:7). Notice the shift from "my father" to "us." The worshiper does not say "they" were enslaved but "we." The story of the nation becomes each person's own story.
- **"With a mighty hand and an outstretched arm, with great deeds of terror, with signs and wonders" (v8).** These are Deuteronomy's standard words for the Exodus (4:34; 7:19). The plagues and the Red Sea are recalled at the harvest. God the Redeemer and God the giver of rain are the same God.
- **"A land flowing with milk and honey" (v9).** Milk from flocks and herds on good pasture, and honey, probably both bees' honey and the syrup of dates and figs. It describes a rich land for herders and farmers.
- **"Now I bring the first of the fruit of the ground, which you, O LORD, have given me" (v10).** The confession ends by speaking directly to God. Scholars sometimes call vv5–10 a small "creed," because it tells the history of salvation in a few sentences. The farmer's offering is a response to grace, not a payment for it. David says the same in 1 Chronicles 29:14: "All things come from you, and of your own have we given you."
- **"You shall rejoice in all the good… you, and the Levite, and the sojourner" (v11).** Worship ends in a meal and joy, and the joy is shared. The Levite had no land of his own, and the "sojourner" was a foreigner living in Israel without family land (see 10:18–19). Israel had been sojourners in Egypt (v5), so they must welcome sojourners now.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the offering quietly denied Baal, the Canaanite storm god, any credit for the harvest. He follows Clarke in seeing the remembrance of humble beginnings as a guard against pride, and he observes that the four hundred years in Egypt are spoken of as a mere "sojourn," a reminder that a hard season need not define a person's whole life. JFB calls Israel God's "tenants-at-will," who owed him tribute, and reads v5 as "a wandering Syrian," noting that "the ancestors of the Hebrews were nomad shepherds." Henry draws out the joy: "Whatever good thing God gives us, it is his will that we make the most comfortable use we can of it."

#### Offerings of Firstfruits and Tithes (vv12–15)
- **"The third year, which is the year of tithing" (v12).** Every third year the tithe was not taken to the sanctuary but stored in each town for **"the Levite, the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow"** (14:28–29). These four groups appear again and again in Deuteronomy. They had no land and no male provider, so they lived on the edge. God built care for them into Israel's farming calendar. **"That they may eat… and be filled"**: not scraps, but enough.
- **"I have removed the sacred portion out of my house" (v13).** The Hebrew verb is "burn" or "purge," the same word used for purging evil from Israel (13:5). The Tyndale notes explain it as meaning the giver has fully handed over what belonged to God. Nothing holy was kept back.
- **"I have not transgressed… nor have I forgotten" (v13).** This is not a claim to be sinless. It is a statement that this particular command has been kept honestly. Similar statements appear in the Psalms (Ps 18:20–24; 26:1–6). The worshiper is saying, "I did what you asked with this tithe."
- **"I have not eaten of the tithe while I was mourning, or removed any of it while I was unclean, or offered any of it to the dead" (v14).** Three things that would make the food unfit for its holy use. A mourner and a person ceremonially unclean could not handle holy things (Num 19:11–22; Hos 9:4). "Offered any of it to the dead" refers to the widespread practice of leaving food at tombs to feed the dead, which archaeology confirms in Canaan and Egypt and which went with ancestor worship and seeking the dead (18:11). God's gifts were for the living poor, not for the spirits of the dead.
- **"Look down from your holy habitation, from heaven, and bless your people Israel and the ground" (v15).** The only request in the whole liturgy. God's true home is heaven, not the sanctuary (compare Solomon's prayer, 1 Kgs 8:27–30). The giver who has blessed the poor asks God to bless the land, so that next year there will be more to share. Generosity and prayer for provision go together (Prov 11:24–25; 2 Cor 9:6–11).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains Israel's two tithes and says the declaration in v13 means "nothing which should be devoted to the divine service had been secretly reserved for personal use." It takes v14 to forbid holding back the sacred portion while mourning "under a pretense of poverty." Henry calls this the "protestation and prayer," and adds: "what comfort can we take in it, unless therewith our God gives us his blessing?" Guzik draws several principles from the prayer: giving follows God's word, sits within a life of obedience, is truly handed over, stays clear of superstition, and looks to God for blessing. With Trapp, he says the words are a testimony of obedience, not boasting.

#### Offerings of Firstfruits and Tithes (vv16–19)
- **"This day the LORD your God commands you" (v16).** "This day" and "today" appear five times in vv16–18. The covenant is renewed now, on the plains of Moab, with this generation. **"With all your heart and with all your soul"** echoes the Shema (6:5). The Tyndale notes point out that it is all or nothing: there is no halfhearted way to keep this covenant.
- **"You have declared today that the LORD is your God" (v17).** The verb is unusual, a causative form of "say" found only here and in v18. It may mean "you have caused the LORD to declare" or "you have agreed to the LORD's statement." Either way, both parties make a public declaration, like vows at a wedding. Israel says the LORD is their God and promises to walk in his ways.
- **"And the LORD has declared today that you are a people for his treasured possession" (v18).** "Treasured possession" is *segullah*, a word for a king's private treasure, personal property kept apart (used of David's gold and silver in 1 Chr 29:3). It first appears at Sinai: "you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples" (Exod 19:5). The Greek Old Testament translated it with words Paul and Peter apply to the church: "a people for his own possession" (Titus 2:14; 1 Pet 2:9).
- **"In praise and in fame and in honor high above all nations… a people holy to the LORD" (v19).** Israel's honor was meant to come from being holy, set apart to God, not from armies or wealth. The nations would see the LORD's goodness in his people (4:6–8). This is the vocation of a "kingdom of priests" (Exod 19:6).
- **Commentators:** Henry says these laws "were not the dictates of his own wisdom" but God's commands, so Israel must "do them, and dispute them not; do them, and draw not back." He calls the covenant "mutual" and says "holiness is true honour, and the only way to everlasting honour." Guzik notes that Moses is wrapping up the second of his three sermons, and that people usually need to be reminded and urged far more than they need new information. He distinguishes Israel's national calling from personal salvation, which has always come by faith (Gen 15:6; Rom 4). JFB has no notes on vv16–19.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus is called "the firstfruits" of those who have died (1 Cor 15:20–23). His resurrection is the first sheaf of a harvest that guarantees the rest: all who belong to him will rise as he did. The confession "a wandering Aramean was my father" is the story of grace, of a homeless and helpless people made a nation by God's power. The church tells the same kind of story: we were "no people," and now we are "God's people," "a people for his own possession" (1 Pet 2:9–10). That is the same *segullah* language, bought not by the plagues of Egypt but by the blood of Christ, "who gave himself for us to redeem us… and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works" (Titus 2:14). And the joy of the harvest table that included the Levite, the sojourner, the orphan and the widow looks ahead to the feast of the kingdom, where the poor, the crippled and the outsider are brought in (Luke 14:21–23).
