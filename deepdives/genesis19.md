*Setting:* This is the same day as chapter 18, probably sometime around 2000 BC, though dates for the patriarchs are approximate. That afternoon the LORD and two angels ate at Abraham's tent by the oaks of Mamre, near Hebron. The LORD told Abraham that the "outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great" (18:20), and Abraham pleaded for the city, down to ten righteous people (18:32). Now the two angels go on down to Sodom, in the Jordan valley near the Dead Sea, and arrive by evening. Lot, Abraham's nephew, chose this well-watered valley years before when the two men separated (13:10–12). He moved his tent "as far as Sodom" and was later living in the city when Abraham rescued him from captivity (14:12–16). Chapter 19 answers Abraham's question, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (18:25), with judgment on a city, mercy to one compromised family, and a sad aftermath.

#### God Rescues Lot (vv1–22)
- **"Lot was sitting in the gate of Sodom" (v1).** The city gate was where elders met, trials were held and business was sealed in public (compare 23:10; Ruth 4:1). Lot is no longer a herdsman camped outside the city. He has become a man of standing inside it. The scene mirrors chapter 18: Abraham at his tent door at midday, Lot at the city gate at evening, both bowing, offering to wash feet and serving a meal, though Lot's is hasty unleavened bread (v3).
- **"No; we will spend the night in the town square" (v2).** The angels decline, perhaps as polite custom or a test. Lot knows what Sodom's square is like at night, so "he pressed them strongly" (v3). He wants to protect these strangers.
- **"Both young and old, all the people to the last man" (v4).** The narrator piles up the phrases: the whole male population is there. This is the evidence the LORD said he would "go down to see" (18:21), and it shows that there were not ten righteous men in Sodom.
- **"That we may know them" (v5).** The verb is *yada*, "know," the ordinary Hebrew word that is also used for sexual relations (4:1). Lot's reply ("do not act so wickedly") and his offer of daughters "who have not known any man" (v8) make the meaning plain: the crowd intends to rape the visitors. The suggestion that they only wanted to "get acquainted" does not fit the context.
- **What was Sodom's sin?** Scripture gives more than one angle. Ezekiel names "pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease" and neglect of "the poor and needy," and says they "did an abomination before me" (Ezek 16:49–50). Jude says Sodom and the nearby cities "indulged in sexual immorality and pursued unnatural desire" (Jude 7). Peter speaks of the "sensual conduct" and "lawless deeds" that distressed Lot (2 Pet 2:7–8). Some today argue that the sin here was only violence and a gross breach of hospitality. The historic Christian reading has seen both the violence and the same-sex intent as part of Sodom's guilt, alongside the pride, greed and cruelty Ezekiel names. On any reading, this is a society where the weak have no protection, the cause of the "outcry" (18:20).
- **"Behold, I have two daughters" (v8).** This is the hardest moment in Lot's story. A host was bound to protect anyone "under the shelter of my roof," and Lot tries to keep that duty by handing over his own daughters. The text reports it without approving it, and nothing in Scripture commends it. Judges 19 tells a near-identical story at Gibeah in Israel ("Bring out the man… that we may know him," Judg 19:22), where a woman is actually handed over and dies. That story shows Israel fallen so low that one of its own towns looked like Sodom.
- **"This fellow came to sojourn, and he has become the judge!" (v9).** After all his years there, the city still sees Lot as a resident foreigner. "Become the judge" uses the same Hebrew root as "Judge of all the earth" in 18:25. The irony is sharp. They mock a weak man who tries to judge them, while the true Judge has already sent his messengers to the door.
- **"They struck with blindness" (v11).** The word *sanverim* is rare. The lexicon defines it as "sudden blindness" and notes it appears only here and in 2 Kings 6:18, where God blinds the Aramean army at Elisha's prayer. Even blind, the men "wore themselves out groping for the door."
- **"He seemed to his sons-in-law to be jesting" (v14).** "Jesting" is *tsachaq*, the lexicon's word for "laugh," used here in the sense "jest." It is the same verb used when Abraham and Sarah laughed at the promise of Isaac (17:17; 18:12) and the root of Isaac's name. The promise of life sounded too good to be true. Now the warning of judgment sounds like a joke. The angels had offered rescue to the whole household (v12), but these men stay. The Hebrew can mean men already married to Lot's daughters or engaged to them, and since v8 says the daughters at home had not known a man, many take them as engaged.
- **"But he lingered" (v16).** The verb (*mahah*) means "linger, tarry, delay." Even after the angels' warning, Lot cannot quite leave. So the angels take him, his wife and his daughters by the hand and pull them out, "the LORD being merciful to him." Lot is not saved because he was quick to obey, but because God was merciful.
- **"Do not look back or stop anywhere in the valley. Escape to the hills" (v17).** Three commands: don't look back, don't stop, go up. The valley Lot once chose because it looked "like the garden of the LORD" (13:10) is now the danger zone.
- **"Let me escape there, is it not a little one?" (vv18–20).** Lot fears the hills more than the doomed plain. His request for a small town nearby is the opposite of Abraham's prayer in chapter 18. Abraham pleaded for a whole city for others' sake. Lot pleads for a little town for his own.
- **"I will not overthrow the city" (v21).** God grants even this weak request. "Overthrow" is *haphak*, "overturn," and it runs through the chapter (vv21, 25, 29). The "overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah" becomes a stock phrase in the prophets for total judgment (Deut 29:23; Isa 13:19; Amos 4:11).
- **"I can do nothing till you arrive there" (v22).** Judgment waits until the righteous are safe. This is the answer to Abraham's question in 18:25. The Judge does not sweep away the righteous with the wicked. "Therefore the name of the city was called Zoar." The name sounds like the Hebrew for "little," echoing Lot's "is it not a little one?" It was earlier called Bela, one of the five cities of the valley (14:2).
- **Commentators:** All three see Lot as a real believer who had drifted. Guzik traces the slide from looking toward Sodom (13:10) to living in it (14:12) to sitting in its gate. JFB says much the same, that Lot "was gradually drawn in, dwelt in the city." On the offer of the daughters, Guzik calls it horrible and unjustifiable, only partly explained by how binding the duty to a guest was felt to be. Henry says Lot "unadvisedly and unjustifiably" made the offer and gives the classic principle: "of two evils we must choose the less; but of two sins we must choose neither." The Tyndale notes go further and call Lot hypocritical. On the sons-in-law, Henry writes, "They had not forty days to repent in, as the Ninevites had." Guzik says compromise had emptied Lot's words of weight: a person can be saved and still waste a life (1 Cor 3:15). On the lingering, JFB charitably suggests sorrow at "losing all his property" or shock, while Guzik sees a heart still attached to Sodom. Henry draws the gospel point: "The salvation of the most righteous men must be attributed to God's mercy, not to their own merit." On Zoar, Henry says, "It was Lot's weakness to think a city of his own choosing safer than the mountain of God's appointing," and JFB calls Lot's plea "a strange want of faith and fortitude." JFB has no notes on vv5–11, 14 and 20.

#### God Destroys Sodom (vv23–29)
- **"The sun had risen on the earth" (v23).** Lot reaches Zoar at sunrise. The morning looks ordinary. Jesus uses that ordinariness as a warning: "they were eating and drinking, buying and selling, planting and building, but on the day when Lot went out from Sodom, fire and sulfur rained from heaven" (Luke 17:28–29).
- **"Sulfur and fire from the LORD out of heaven" (v24).** "Sulfur" (*gophrith*, older English "brimstone") later becomes a picture of God's judgment (Ps 11:6; Ezek 38:22; Rev 14:10). The Dead Sea region is rich in bitumen, sulfur and salt and lies on a major fault line, so some propose an earthquake igniting gas and bitumen. Others see a direct act of God without natural means. Either way, the text is clear that the LORD did it, at the time he said, after the righteous were out.
- **"The LORD… from the LORD" (v24).** The divine name appears twice. Some early Christian writers, such as Justin Martyr, saw a hint here of two divine persons, the Son on earth acting from the Father in heaven. Most interpreters today read it as Hebrew emphasis: the fire came straight from God himself.
- **Where was Sodom?** Tradition and many scholars place "the cities of the valley" (v29) near the southern end of the Dead Sea, where ruins such as Bab edh-Dhra have been suggested. Others argue for Tall el-Hammam, northeast of the Dead Sea. The question is unsettled, and the land that once looked "like the garden of the LORD" (13:10) became a byword for judgment (Deut 29:23).
- **"Lot's wife, behind him, looked back" (v26).** She was trailing behind and turned, against the plain command of v17. The Tyndale notes say the verb (*nabat*) implies a long, intense gaze, not a glance. The lexicon defines it simply as "look," sometimes with the sense "regard, pay attention to." It is the same verb used when God told Abraham to "look toward heaven" and count the stars (15:5). So the verb alone does not prove a lingering stare. What the story shows is that her heart was still turned toward Sodom. Jesus' three words are the Bible's own commentary: "Remember Lot's wife" (Luke 17:32). He uses her to warn against turning back for what has been left behind.
- **"A pillar of salt" (v26).** The Dead Sea shore is lined with salt formations, and at Mount Sodom (Jebel Usdum) some pillars have long been nicknamed "Lot's wife." Ancient Jewish writers, including Josephus, claimed the pillar could still be seen in their day. That is tradition. The text says only that she was caught in the judgment she had been warned to flee.
- **"Abraham went early in the morning to the place where he had stood before the LORD" (v27).** This ties back to 18:22. Abraham returns to where he prayed, to see what became of his prayer.
- **"Like the smoke of a furnace" (v28).** The word is *kibshan*, a kiln for lime or pottery. The lexicon notes it is also used of Sinai when the LORD came down in fire (Exod 19:18). Abraham sees only smoke and does not yet know whether Lot is alive.
- **"God remembered Abraham and sent Lot out" (v29).** In Scripture, "remember" (*zakar*) means more than recalling. It means acting on a commitment. The phrase echoes "God remembered Noah" (8:1), when God brought one family through his judgment on the world. It does not say God remembered Lot's righteousness, but *Abraham*. The city was not spared, since not even ten were found, but the heart of Abraham's prayer was answered: the righteous were not swept away with the wicked (18:23).
- **Lot, "righteous"?** Peter calls him "righteous Lot," a man "greatly distressed" and "tormenting his righteous soul" over what he saw (2 Pet 2:7–8). Genesis shows a weak, compromised man. Both are true. Lot grieved over Sodom's evil, yet he stayed, and it cost him nearly everything. Peter's point is that "the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trials" (2 Pet 2:9), even godly people as feeble as Lot.
- **Commentators:** On v24 Henry offers both readings: God acting "from himself," or "God the Son from God the Father." JFB argues the means were probably volcanic, but adds, "Whether it was miraculously produced, or the natural operation employed by God, it is not of much consequence to determine." His idea that Lot's wife was caught in "liquid lava" goes beyond the text. Henry gives five possible faults in her look, chief among them that she disobeyed a direct command and "hankered after her house and goods." He adds, "This pillar of salt should season us." Guzik, like the Tyndale notes, says the verb means a long, intent look, which the lexicon does not clearly support. On v29 Henry observes, "when he prayed for Sodom, he heard him for Lot." JFB writes that if God is a "consuming fire" to the wicked, "He is the friend of the righteous," and points to "a greater than Abraham" who intercedes for us. Guzik notes that God could not act until Lot was safe (v22), which answers 18:25. JFB has no notes on vv23, 25 and 28.

#### Lot and His Daughters (vv30–38)
- **"He was afraid to live in Zoar" (v30).** The town Lot begged for (v20) no longer feels safe, so he goes up to the hills after all, the place the angels first told him to go (v17). The man who once chose the best of the land (13:10–11) now lives in a cave. His wife, his sons-in-law and his wealth are gone.
- **"There is not a man on earth to come in to us" (v31).** She may have thought the destruction was wider than it was, or meant that no man would marry into a ruined family living in a cave. She wants to "preserve offspring," to keep the family line alive, which mattered enormously in that world. But the plan she proposes is incest, which God's law later forbids outright (Lev 18:6–7).
- **"Let us make our father drink wine" (vv32–35).** The text tells this plainly and briefly. Over two nights the daughters get their father drunk, and each in turn sleeps with him. Twice the narrator says Lot "did not know when she lay down or when she arose." The story recalls Noah, who also got drunk after God brought him through judgment, with shameful consequences in his family (9:20–25).
- **Who is responsible?** The daughters planned it. Lot let himself be made drunk two nights running, which is his own failure, even if he was not aware of what followed. Genesis does not stop to pass verdict, as Hebrew narrative often does not, but the details speak. There is a hard echo of v8. Lot had offered his daughters to the men of Sodom. Now his daughters, raised in Sodom, use him. Some readers note the daughters' desperation and see a likeness to Tamar in Genesis 38, who also took a shocking step to keep a family line alive. Seeing their desperation does not excuse what they did.
- **"Moab… Ben-ammi" (vv37–38).** The name Moab sounds like the Hebrew for "from father," and the old Greek translation adds that explanation to the verse, though the lexicon notes the true origin of the name is uncertain. Ben-ammi means "son of my people," or "son of my kin." Each name quietly points back to the story. "To this day" shows these were well-known neighbors east of the Jordan.
- **Lot's descendants in the larger story.** Moab and Ammon became frequent enemies of Israel. Balak of Moab hired Balaam to curse Israel (Num 22), and both peoples were barred from Israel's assembly (Deut 23:3–4). Yet God also protected their land "because I have given Ar to the people of Lot" (Deut 2:9, compare 2:19). He did not forget that they were Abraham's kin. And out of Moab came Ruth, the great-grandmother of David, named in Jesus' genealogy (Matt 1:5). Naamah the Ammonite was the mother of King Rehoboam (1 Kings 14:21), also in that line (Matt 1:7). Lot's story ends in shame, but God's grace toward his descendants does not end there.
- **Commentators:** JFB has no notes at all on vv30–38. Henry begins, "It is a sad story," and says of the daughters' plot that "theirs was, doubtless, the greater guilt." He mentions an older suggestion that they hoped to carry on a holy line, and answers it: "Good intentions are often abused to patronise bad actions." He warns that "Solitude has its temptations as well as company," and points to Lot's fall in the hills after resisting Sodom's evil in the city: "Lord, what is man!" Henry notes Ruth in the Messiah's genealogy, and closes on the fact that Lot is never mentioned again in Genesis: drunkenness, "as it makes men forgetful, so it makes them forgotten." Guzik sees the same irony as v8 and argues that Scripture records the sin in order to condemn it, not to excuse it. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both trace the daughters' choice to Sodom's influence, and Guzik sums up Lot's life as a warning about loving the world.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus points to this chapter himself. He warns that his coming will be as sudden as the fire on Sodom (Luke 17:28–30), and he says unbelieving towns that saw his works face a heavier judgment than Sodom (Matt 11:23–24). So the chapter is first a real warning: God's judgment is just, and it comes. But the chapter also shows how God saves. Lot was not saved by his own strength or quick obedience. He lingered and bargained, and the angels took him by the hand, "the LORD being merciful to him." He was spared because "God remembered Abraham" (v29), saved on the strength of another man's intercession. Christians are rescued from a greater judgment the same way, through a greater intercessor who "always lives to make intercession" for his people (Heb 7:25). Even the dark ending points forward. From Moab came Ruth, and through her line came the Son of David, who welcomes outsiders and sinners, the descendants of Lot's shame included.
