*Setting:* Mark is the shortest Gospel and probably the first written, most likely in the mid to late 60s AD. Early church tradition, going back to Papias around AD 120, says John Mark wrote down the preaching of Peter, and that he wrote in Rome for Christians facing persecution under Nero. This is the same Mark who once deserted Paul and was later restored (Acts 13:13; Col 4:10; 2 Tim 4:11), and Peter calls him "Mark, my son" (1 Pet 5:13). Mark explains Jewish customs and translates Aramaic words, so he was writing mainly for Gentiles. He skips the birth of Jesus and his early years altogether. In a few quick verses he moves from John the Baptist to Jesus' baptism and temptation, and then into a crowded first day of ministry in Capernaum on the Sea of Galilee, around AD 27–28. His favorite word, *euthus* ("immediately"), comes about ten times in this chapter alone.

#### John the Baptist Prepares the Way (vv1–8)
- **"The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (v1).** This first line is a title for the whole book. "Gospel" (*euangelion*) means "good news." In the Roman world the word was used for public announcements of an emperor's birthday, victory or accession. A famous inscription from Priene (9 BC) calls the birthday of Augustus "the beginning of the good news" for the world. Mark announces a different king. "Christ" is the Greek for the Hebrew "Messiah," the anointed one. Some manuscripts lack "the Son of God" (the ESV notes this), but the phrase is in very old and strong witnesses and most translations keep it. Either way the title frames the book: the Father calls Jesus "my beloved Son" (v11), and near the end a Roman centurion says, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39).
- **"As it is written in Isaiah the prophet" (v2).** The quotation joins Malachi 3:1 ("I send my messenger") and Exodus 23:20 to Isaiah 40:3 ("the voice of one crying in the wilderness"). Mark names Isaiah, the larger and better-known prophet, as Jewish writers often did when combining texts. Later scribes, troubled by this, changed it to "in the prophets," the reading behind the KJV. In Isaiah, the way is prepared for "the LORD," Israel's God himself. Mark applies it to Jesus. From the first verses, the one who comes after John is God coming to his people.
- **"John appeared, baptizing in the wilderness" (v4).** "Wilderness" was a charged word. Israel was born as a nation in the wilderness after the exodus, and prophets promised a new exodus that would start there (Isa 40:3; Hos 2:14). Jewish groups already practiced washings, and the Qumran community near the Dead Sea had repeated ritual baths. John's baptism was a one-time act tied to repentance, a public sign of turning back to God. "Repentance" (*metanoia*) means a change of mind that turns the whole life around.
- **"Camel's hair and… a leather belt… locusts and wild honey" (v6).** John dressed like Elijah, described as "wearing a garment of hair, with a belt of leather" (2 Kgs 1:8). Malachi had promised that Elijah would come before the day of the LORD (Mal 4:5), and Jesus later says John was that Elijah (9:11–13). Locusts were allowed as food under the law (Lev 11:22) and are still eaten in the region. His food and clothes said he did not belong to the comfortable world of the cities.
- **"The strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and untie" (v7).** Untying a master's sandals was one of the lowest tasks a slave could do. A later rabbinic saying held that a disciple should do every service for his teacher that a slave does, except untie his sandals. John, a famous prophet, says he is not fit even for that.
- **"He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit" (v8).** The prophets promised that God would pour out his Spirit in the last days (Joel 2:28; Ezek 36:26–27). John can wash the outside. The one coming will change people from within. The promise is fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 1:5; 2:4).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Mark's word "immediately" (*euthus*) appears over forty times in the book, giving it its brisk pace, and follows Lane on the political ring of "good news" in the Roman world. Matthew Henry observes that Mark starts with John, not the manger: "The gospel did not begin so soon as the birth of Christ," and that John "was clothed with camels' hair, not with soft raiment." JFB defends "in Isaiah the prophet" as the reading "adopted by all the latest critical editors," and calls Malachi's words a later development of Isaiah's prophecy. It refers the rest of the section to its notes on Matthew 3.

#### The Baptism of Jesus (vv9–11)
- **"Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee" (v9).** Nazareth was a small, unimportant village (John 1:46). Jesus walks perhaps seventy miles south to the Jordan. Why would the sinless one be baptized for repentance? Matthew gives Jesus' answer: "to fulfill all righteousness" (Matt 3:15). He stands with sinners in the water, the first step of a road that ends with him bearing their sins on the cross.
- **"He saw the heavens being torn open" (v10).** Matthew and Luke say "opened." Mark uses a violent word, *schizō*, "to tear, split." Isaiah had prayed, "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down" (Isa 64:1). Mark uses the same verb only once more, when "the curtain of the temple was torn in two" at Jesus' death (15:38). The Gospel is framed by two tearings: heaven torn open at the start, and the way into God's presence torn open at the end.
- **"The Spirit descending on him like a dove" (v10).** Isaiah said the Spirit would rest on the Messiah (Isa 11:2; 42:1; 61:1). The dove may recall the Spirit hovering over the waters at creation (Gen 1:2) or Noah's dove over the waters after the flood. Here is a new beginning.
- **"You are my beloved Son; with you I am well pleased" (v11).** The Father's words echo two passages. Psalm 2:7, "You are my Son," is spoken to the anointed king. Isaiah 42:1, "my chosen, in whom my soul delights," is spoken of the Servant who suffers. Some also hear Genesis 22:2, where Abraham is told to offer "your son, your only son… whom you love." In one sentence the voice names Jesus as King, Son and suffering Servant. Father, Son and Spirit all appear together in the scene.
- **Commentators:** Guzik follows Bruce that the word for the heavens parting means a sudden tearing open, and lists the witnesses to Jesus that Mark lines up in these verses: Mark himself, the prophets, John and the Father. Henry remarks, "Many have the heavens opened to receive them, but they do not see it," while Jesus saw them opened. JFB refers this section to its notes on Matthew 3.

#### The Temptation of Jesus (vv12–13)
- **"The Spirit immediately drove him out into the wilderness" (v12).** The verb is *ekballō*, "to throw out, drive out," the same word Mark uses for casting out demons (1:34, 39). It is a strong word. Straight from the Father's approval, the Spirit sends Jesus into testing. Being God's beloved does not mean being spared hard places.
- **"Forty days, being tempted by Satan" (v13).** Matthew and Luke describe three specific temptations (Matt 4:1–11; Luke 4:1–13). Mark gives the bare fact. The forty days recall Israel's forty years in the wilderness, where God's son Israel (Exod 4:22) was tested and often failed, and Moses' and Elijah's forty-day fasts (Exod 34:28; 1 Kgs 19:8). Jesus succeeds where Israel failed.
- **"He was with the wild animals" (v13).** Only Mark mentions them. The Judean wilderness had jackals, hyenas, wild boars and leopards. Some read the detail as adding to the danger and loneliness. Others hear Isaiah's picture of the messianic age, when wolf and lamb live together (Isa 11:6–9), and Adam among the animals in Eden before the fall. The phrase "with the wild animals" may suggest that peace. Both readings are offered by careful scholars. Mark's first readers in Rome, some later thrown to beasts in the arena, may have found comfort here. Either way, "the angels were ministering to him," as an angel fed Elijah (1 Kgs 19:5–7).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes, following Morgan, that "drove" is the word used later for casting out demons, and leans toward the peaceful reading of the wild animals: Jesus among them like Adam before the fall. The Tyndale notes take the darker reading, that wild animals and Satan together make the wilderness a place of evil. JFB refers the scene to its notes on Matthew 4.

#### Jesus Begins His Ministry (vv14–15)
- **"After John was arrested" (v14).** Literally "handed over," the same word used of Jesus' betrayal (9:31; 14:41). John's fate is a preview of Jesus' own. Herod Antipas, ruler of Galilee, imprisoned him (6:17–29). John's Gospel shows that Jesus had already ministered for some time in Judea before this (John 2–4). Mark starts with the Galilean ministry.
- **"The time is fulfilled" (v15).** The word for "time" is *kairos*, a decisive, appointed moment, not just the passing of time. What the prophets waited for has arrived (Gal 4:4).
- **"The kingdom of God is at hand" (v15).** God's kingdom means his reign, his rule breaking in, not first a territory. The Jews longed for God to rule again as king, and many expected a political liberator. Jesus announces that in his own person God's reign has drawn near. The rest of Mark shows what that looks like: demons driven out, diseases healed, sins forgiven, and in the end a King enthroned on a cross.
- **"Repent and believe in the gospel" (v15).** Two commands, one response. Repent: turn around. Believe: trust and rest on the good news. Neither works without the other.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God keeps time; when the time is fulfilled, the kingdom of God is at hand." Guzik notes the use of *kairos* rather than *chronos* and stresses that repentance is a change of direction, not just feeling sorry. JFB has no note on these verses.

#### Jesus Calls the First Disciples (vv16–20)
- **"Simon and Andrew… casting a net into the sea" (v16).** The Sea of Galilee is a freshwater lake about thirteen miles long, and fishing was a major industry there. The casting net was a circular net thrown by hand from the shore or shallows. John's Gospel shows that Andrew and Simon had met Jesus earlier through John the Baptist (John 1:35–42). The call here is not out of nowhere, but it still costs everything.
- **"Follow me, and I will make you become fishers of men" (v17).** Disciples usually chose their rabbi. Here the rabbi chooses them. "Follow me" makes Jesus himself the center of discipleship, not a school of teaching. "I will make you become" promises a process: Jesus will shape them. Jeremiah used fishing as a picture of God gathering people for judgment (Jer 16:16). Jesus turns the picture to rescue.
- **"James the son of Zebedee and John his brother… mending the nets" (v19).** The family owned a boat and employed **"hired servants" (v20)**, so this was a business of some size. Leaving "their father Zebedee in the boat" was a costly break in a culture where sons carried on the family trade and cared for their parents. The nets and the boat were their living, and they left them "immediately." Peter, James and John become Jesus' inner circle (5:37; 9:2; 14:33).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ will have followers. If he set up a school, he will have scholars." Guzik follows Bruce that "I will make you become" means gradual training, and points out that Jesus called ordinary working men, not scholars. The Tyndale notes, like Guzik, link the call to the earlier meeting in John 1. JFB refers the passage to its notes on Matthew 4.

#### Jesus Heals a Man with an Unclean Spirit (vv21–28)
- **"Capernaum… on the Sabbath he entered the synagogue" (v21).** Capernaum was a fishing town on the northwest shore of the lake, on a trade road, and it became Jesus' base (2:1). The white limestone synagogue ruins seen there today are later, but they sit on dark basalt foundations that many think belong to the synagogue of Jesus' day.
- **"He taught them as one who had authority, and not as the scribes" (v22).** The scribes were the trained experts in the law. Their teaching leaned on quoting earlier authorities: "Rabbi so-and-so says." Jesus spoke on his own authority: "But I say to you" (Matt 5:22).
- **"A man with an unclean spirit" (v23).** "Unclean" is Mark's usual word for demons. Unclean things in the law kept people from God's presence, and these spirits stand opposed to the holy God.
- **"I know who you are, the Holy One of God" (v24).** The demon knows exactly who Jesus is, while the people are still wondering. "What have you to do with us?" is an Old Testament idiom for "Leave us alone" (1 Kgs 17:18). "Us" speaks for the whole demonic realm. In the ancient world, knowing and saying someone's true name was thought to give power over them. The demon may be trying to defend itself. It fails.
- **"Be silent, and come out of him!" (v25).** Literally "Be muzzled." Jesus uses no spells, no rituals, no long incantations, which other exorcists of the time used, as Josephus describes. Two commands, and it is done. Why silence? Jesus will not accept testimony from demons, and he did not want his identity announced before his time and in their terms. Scholars call this pattern in Mark the "messianic secret" (1:34, 44; 3:12; 8:30). The crowds expected a political Messiah, and only the cross would show what kind of Messiah he was.
- **"A new teaching with authority! He commands even the unclean spirits" (v27).** For the crowd, word and deed go together. His teaching is backed by power. The same authority that taught them now drives out the demon.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The devil is here called an unclean spirit, because he has lost all the purity of his nature." He says Jesus taught "as one that knew the mind of God, and was commissioned to declare it." JFB notes that the man was so ruled by the spirit that "his personality was sunk for the time in that of the spirit," and passes on Bengel's view that Jesus silenced the testimony itself, not the demon's cries, since he would accept no witness from that side. Guzik contrasts Jesus' simple command with the magical formulas of the period's exorcists, citing Josephus.

#### Jesus Heals Many (vv29–34)
- **"The house of Simon and Andrew" (vv29–30).** Peter was married; Paul says he took his wife on his travels (1 Cor 9:5). Excavations in Capernaum uncovered a house that by the late first century seems to have been used as a meeting place for Christians, later replaced by an octagonal church. Many think it was Peter's house. The detail "with James and John" and the vivid memories here are part of why many trace Mark's account to Peter.
- **"He came and took her by the hand and lifted her up" (v31).** "Lifted up" (*egeirō*) is the same verb used for raising the dead and for Jesus' own resurrection (16:6). Touch, raise, restore.
- **"And she began to serve them" (v31).** She was healed completely, with no days of weakness after the fever. Her response is service. The word is *diakoneō*, used of the angels in v13 and of Jesus himself: "The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve" (10:45).
- **"That evening at sundown they brought to him all who were sick" (v32).** The Sabbath ended at sunset. Carrying a burden on the Sabbath broke the scribes' rules, so the townspeople waited until it was over.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "when the fever left her, it did not, as usual, leave her weak." JFB calls the naming of the four disciples one of "the first of those traces of Peter's hand" in this Gospel, and notes that only Mark says Jesus lifted her up. Guzik, following Barclay, contrasts Jesus' touch with the magic remedies for fever found in the Talmud.

#### Jesus Preaches in Galilee (vv35–39)
- **"Rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark… there he prayed" (v35).** After the long evening, Jesus rises in the dark to pray alone. Mark records Jesus praying three times, each at a moment of crisis or pressure: here after his first success, after feeding the five thousand (6:46), and in Gethsemane (14:35). "Desolate place" is the same word as "wilderness." Jesus returns to the place of testing to be with his Father.
- **"Everyone is looking for you" (v37).** "Searched for" can carry the sense of hunting someone down. Simon thinks Jesus is missing a great opportunity. The crowds want more healings.
- **"Let us go on to the next towns, that I may preach there also, for that is why I came out" (v38).** Jesus' priority is preaching the message of the kingdom, not becoming a local healer in demand. "Came out" may mean came out of Capernaum, or, as some read it, came out from the Father (John 16:28). Prayer, it seems, has clarified his mission.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that pressure drove Jesus to prayer, not away from it, and that preaching stood at the center of his mission. JFB reads "came I forth" as coming from the Father, citing John 16:28, and pictures Peter leading the search. The Tyndale notes tie the prayer in v35 directly to the preaching that follows.

#### Jesus Cleanses a Leper (vv40–45)
- **"A leper came to him" (v40).** Biblical "leprosy" covered a range of skin diseases, not only what is now called Hansen's disease. The law required the person to live outside the camp, wear torn clothes, cover his lip and cry "Unclean, unclean" (Lev 13:45–46). Rabbis said healing leprosy was as hard as raising the dead (compare 2 Kgs 5:7). The man was cut off from family, synagogue and temple. He should not have come near at all.
- **"If you will, you can make me clean" (v40).** He has no doubt about Jesus' power. His question is about Jesus' willingness. He asks to be made "clean," not just healed. He wants to be restored to God and his people.
- **"Moved with pity, he stretched out his hand and touched him" (v41).** The ESV notes that some manuscripts read "moved with anger." It is found in a few manuscripts, mainly Codex Bezae and some Old Latin copies. Most scholars follow "pity," though some argue "anger" is original, since a scribe would be more likely to soften it than to invent it. If "anger" is right, it is anger at the disease and the misery it caused, not at the man. Either way, Jesus touched him. Under the law, touching a leper made a person unclean. Instead, Jesus' cleanness flows the other way. The man had probably not felt a human touch in years.
- **"Sternly charged him and sent him away at once" (v43).** "Sternly charged" is *embrimaomai*, a strong word that originally meant to snort, as a horse does. The lexicon gives "to admonish sternly" here. It shows deep feeling and urgency. "Sent away" is again *ekballō*, as though Jesus thrust him out.
- **"Go, show yourself to the priest and offer… what Moses commanded, for a proof to them" (v44).** Leviticus 14 lays out the cleansing ritual: an inspection by a priest, two birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn, hyssop, and then offerings at the temple. Jesus honors the law. The healed man's appearance before the priest would be a "proof," a testimony that the kingdom had come in power.
- **"But he went out and began to talk freely about it" (v45).** His joy is understandable, but his disobedience has a cost. Jesus "could no longer openly enter a town." The irony is plain: the leper had lived outside the towns, and now he is back in while Jesus has to stay out "in desolate places." In a small way Jesus takes the outcast's place.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The poor leper put an if upon the will of Christ," but none upon his power, and Christ answered the doubt at once. Guzik dwells on the cruelty lepers suffered and on the touch, forbidden by the law yet leaving Jesus clean and the man healed, and he draws the lesson that obedience to Jesus is better than our own idea of how to help him. JFB refers the section to its notes on Matthew 8.

#### Pointing to Christ
In one chapter Mark shows who Jesus is. The prophets point to him, John bows before him, the Father names him beloved Son, the Spirit rests on him, Satan tests him and fails, fishermen drop their nets for him, a demon confesses him, a fever obeys him, and a leper is touched by him. He has authority over everything that ruins human life, and yet he rises in the dark to pray and is willing to take the outcast's place outside the town. Heaven is torn open at the start of Mark's story, and the temple curtain will be torn at its end. In between, the Son of God goes about the work of bringing sinners near.
