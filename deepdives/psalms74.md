*Setting:* "A Maskil of Asaph." A *maskil* is probably a psalm of instruction or a skillfully made song; Henry calls it "a psalm to give instruction." Psalm 74 is a community lament over the destruction of God's sanctuary in Jerusalem. The temple has been hacked to pieces and burned, the meeting places of God across the land are gone, and no prophet can say how long it will last. That matches the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, when Nebuchadnezzar's commander "burned the house of the LORD" (2 Kgs 25:8–9), and most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, Henry and JFB, read it that way. It reads like a companion to Lamentations. If so, "Asaph" means the family of singers descended from David's musician, who were still serving after the exile (Ezra 2:41; 3:10). Some have dated it to the desecration of the temple by the Greek king Antiochus IV in 167 BC, but that temple was defiled, not burned down. Others suggest the old Asaph wrote it prophetically, or about the loss of the tabernacle at Shiloh (1 Sam 4; Ps 78:60). Guzik lists these options. Psalm 74 follows Psalm 73 well: the earlier psalm asked why the wicked prosper over the righteous person; this one asks why God seems to have abandoned his whole people.

#### Remember Your Congregation (vv1–3)
- **"O God, why do you cast us off forever?" (v1).** The opening question is bold. "Cast off" (*zanach*) means to reject, to spurn (43:2; 44:9). The people know God's anger is behind the disaster, not just Babylon's armies. JFB notes there is "no disavowal of guilt implied." They do not claim innocence, as in Psalm 44. They know the exile was judgment for sin (2 Kgs 24:3–4; Lam 1:5). But "forever"? That is what they cannot bear. Morgan, cited by Guzik, calls this "the wail of a believer": the questions come from faith, not unbelief.
- **"Why does your anger smoke against the sheep of your pasture?" (v1).** The temple is still smoking, and so, it seems, is God's anger. JFB notes the word "smoke" suggests anger that keeps burning (Deut 29:20). Yet they still call themselves God's sheep (79:13; 95:7; 100:3). A shepherd does not burn his own flock.
- **"Remember your congregation, which you have purchased of old" (v2).** "Remember," in the Bible, means to act on what one knows, not only to recall (Gen 8:1). The psalmist reminds God of what he did at the exodus. "Purchased" (*qanah*) and "redeemed" (*ga'al*) are both words from the Song of the Sea: "the people whom you have redeemed… the people… whom you have purchased" (Exod 15:13, 16). **"The tribe of your heritage"**: Israel as God's own inheritance. **"Mount Zion, where you have dwelt."** JFB notes the terms grow in force: congregation, purchased, redeemed, Zion.
- **"Direct your steps to the perpetual ruins" (v3).** Literally "lift up your feet," come and look. The ruins seem permanent. "The enemy has destroyed everything in the sanctuary!"
- **Commentators:** Henry defends the bold questions: "we hope not too great a liberty, for Christ himself, upon the cross, cried out, My God my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" But he adds that "the people of God must not think that because they are cast down they are therefore cast off" (Rom 11:1). He notes that they mourn the temple more than their own homes: "The desolation of God's house should grieve us more than the desolation of our own houses." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, sees redemption as a plea: a purchased people belong to the one who paid for them.

#### They Set Your Sanctuary on Fire (vv4–8)
- **"Your foes have roared in the midst of your meeting place" (v4).** "Meeting place" (*mo'ed*) is the place where God met his people, as in "tent of meeting." Where Israel worshiped in reverent silence and song, the enemy roars like lions. Lamentations 2:7 says the same: "they raised a clamor in the house of the LORD as on the day of festival."
- **"They set up their own signs for signs" (v4).** Military standards and religious emblems now stand where God's signs had been. The word *'ot* ("sign") is used of the tribal banners in Numbers 2:2, as Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes. "Signs" returns in v9: their signs are up, ours are gone.
- **"They were like those who swing axes in a forest of trees. And all its carved wood they broke down with hatchets and hammers" (vv5–6).** Solomon's temple was paneled in cedar carved with cherubim, palm trees and open flowers, overlaid with gold (1 Kgs 6:18, 29). The enemy treats this artwork like underbrush to be cleared. JFB says some phrases here are "very obscure," but the sense is plain: they destroyed the temple's beauty "with the violence of woodmen." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, applies the scene to every attack on the church by mockery and clever argument.
- **"They set your sanctuary on fire; they profaned the dwelling place of your name, bringing it down to the ground" (v7).** God had put his "name" there, his revealed presence (Deut 12:11; 1 Kgs 8:29). "Profaned" means made common, treated as unholy. Second Kings 25:9 records that the Babylonians "burned the house of the LORD."
- **"They said to themselves, 'We will utterly subdue them'; they burned all the meeting places of God in the land" (v8).** The plural *mo'adei-El*, "meeting places of God," is puzzling. The KJV calls them "synagogues," but most scholars think synagogues as an institution began during or after the exile. Guzik, following Clarke, takes the word to mean any places where people met to worship. Others think of local shrines or "the high places" that Josiah had earlier closed (2 Kgs 23:8), or of places where prophets taught (2 Kgs 4:23). JFB admits "there is much doubt."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the "meeting places" as "the synagogues, or schools of the prophets, which, before the captivity, were in use, though much more afterwards," where God's word was read without sacrifices. He notes the enemies' motive: "So great was their rage against religion that the religious houses, because religious, were all levelled with the ground." JFB: "roar—with bestial fury," and the "signs" were "idolatrous objects, or tokens of authority."

#### How Long, O God? (vv9–11)
- **"We do not see our signs; there is no longer any prophet, and there is none among us who knows how long" (v9).** The worst part is not knowing. The signs of God's presence, the ark, the altar, the festivals, are gone. And no prophet speaks. This was strange, since Jeremiah had prophesied seventy years of exile (Jer 25:11; 29:10), and Ezekiel and Daniel were active. JFB suggests Jeremiah's word "may not have been generally known or understood," and the scattered prophets were far from most of the people. Poole, cited by Guzik, takes it as poetic overstatement for a great scarcity. Lamentations 2:9 says the same: "her prophets find no vision from the LORD."
- **"How long, O God, is the foe to scoff? Is the enemy to revile your name forever?" (v10).** The cry "how long?" runs through the laments (6:3; 13:1–2; 79:5; 80:4) and still sounds in heaven (Rev 6:10). Notice the concern: not only "how long will we suffer?" but "how long will your name be mocked?"
- **"Why do you hold back your hand, your right hand? Take it from the fold of your garment and destroy them!" (v11).** God's right hand is his power to act, the hand that shattered Egypt (Exod 15:6, 12). The picture is of God standing with his hand tucked inside his robe, folded across his chest and doing nothing. The "fold of the garment" (*cheq*) is the bosom, the pocket of a robe. Moses put his hand into this fold at the burning bush (Exod 4:6–7). Guzik notes the psalmist never doubts God's power, only his apparent inaction.
- **Commentators:** Henry observes what they missed most: "They do not complain, 'We see not our armies…' but, 'no prophets, none to tell us how long.'" JFB lists the "signs" as "altar, ark, &c." (2 Chr 36:18–19). Boice, cited by Guzik, applies v9 to a church that neglects Scripture and so loses its sense of God's voice.

#### Yet God My King Is from of Old (vv12–17)
- **"Yet God my King is from of old, working salvation in the midst of the earth" (v12).** The turn. Everything has been lost except this: God is still king, and has been from the start. The singular "my King" shows faith holding on personally. "In the midst of the earth" means in full view, on the world stage. Morgan, cited by Guzik, says the eye of faith sees God as King in the middle of ruin.
- **"You divided the sea by your might; you broke the heads of the sea monsters on the waters" (v13).** First the Red Sea (Exod 14:21). "Sea monsters" (*tanninim*) are great serpents or dragons. Ezekiel calls Pharaoh "the great dragon that lies in the midst of his streams" (Ezek 29:3), and Isaiah links the dragon with the exodus: "Was it not you who cut Rahab in pieces, who pierced the dragon? Was it not you who dried up the sea?" (Isa 51:9–10).
- **"You crushed the heads of Leviathan; you gave him as food for the creatures of the wilderness" (v14).** Leviathan is a great sea creature, named in Job 3:8; 41:1, Psalm 104:26 and Isaiah 27:1, where he is "Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent." The name probably means "twisting" or "coiling." Texts from Ugarit, a Canaanite city, describe Baal defeating a seven-headed sea serpent called Lotan, the same name, and the psalm's "heads" (plural) reflect that picture. The Tyndale notes say the multiple heads suggest a mythical monster. The psalmist borrows the imagery of his neighbors' myths to say: it was not Baal but the LORD who conquered the chaos monster, and he did it in history, at the sea, against Pharaoh. Kidner and VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, make this point. Henry: "Pharaoh was the leviathan; the Egyptians were the dragons." The monster's carcass becomes food for desert creatures, a picture of total defeat (Ezek 29:5). JFB notes some take it of the Egyptian dead washed up on shore (Exod 14:30).
- **"You split open springs and brooks; you dried up ever-flowing streams" (v15).** God made water flow from the rock in the wilderness (Exod 17:6; Num 20:11) and dried up the Jordan so Israel could cross, though it was in flood (Josh 3:15–17). He can make water where there is none and remove it where there is too much.
- **"Yours is the day, yours also the night… you have made summer and winter" (vv16–17).** From history the psalm widens to creation. God set the sun and moon in place, fixed the boundaries of land and sea (Gen 1:9–10; Jer 5:22), and made the seasons. After the flood God promised that "summer and winter, day and night, shall not cease" (Gen 8:22). The God who keeps his promise to the seasons will keep his promise to his people.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The lamenting church fastens upon something here which she calls to mind, and therefore hath she hope," as in Lamentations 3:21. He sees the leviathan's crushed heads as "typical of Christ's victory over Satan," fulfilling Genesis 3:15. He reads vv16–17 as an argument: if God keeps the order of nature, "he will keep his covenant with his people." Maclaren, cited by Guzik, notes how often "you" repeats in these verses: God's own character and past acts are the ground of hope. Guzik notes some see Leviathan as a picture of Satan, while cautioning that Scripture does not simply adopt the myths.

#### Have Regard for the Covenant (vv18–23)
- **"Remember this, O LORD, how the enemy scoffs, and a foolish people reviles your name" (v18).** "Foolish" (*naval*) is the word in 14:1: "The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God.'" It describes moral and spiritual folly, not low intelligence. Deuteronomy 32:6 uses the same word of Israel itself.
- **"Do not deliver the soul of your dove to the wild beasts" (v19).** Israel is God's turtledove (*tor*), small, gentle and defenseless among predators (Song 2:14). The turtledove was also the poor person's sacrifice (Lev 12:8). **"Do not forget the life of your poor forever."** The people are now "your poor," those who have nothing left but God.
- **"Have regard for the covenant" (v20).** The great appeal. God had bound himself by promise to Abraham, to Israel at Sinai, and to David. The psalmist does not appeal to Israel's faithfulness, but to God's. Meyer, cited by Guzik, notes that for Christians the covenant is sealed by Christ's blood (Luke 22:20; Heb 13:20). **"For the dark places of the land are full of the habitations of violence."** "Dark places" may mean hiding places where violent men lurk, or the whole land fallen into the darkness of lawlessness.
- **"Let not the downtrodden turn back in shame; let the poor and needy praise your name" (v21).** "Downtrodden" means crushed. The prayer asks God to act so that those who come to him are not sent away disappointed. Instead of the enemy reviling his name (v18), let the poor praise it.
- **"Arise, O God, defend your cause" (v22).** Literally "plead your own case." The temple's ruin is God's own cause, his honor at stake. The prayer echoes Moses' cry when the ark set out, "Arise, O LORD" (Num 10:35; Ps 68:1). **"Remember how the foolish scoff at you all the day!"**
- **"Do not forget the clamor of your foes, the uproar of those who rise against you, which goes up continually!" (v23).** The psalm ends without an answer. The last word is the enemy's noise, still rising. Like Psalm 88, it leaves the lament unresolved, and Scripture lets it stand that way. The praise of the poor (v21) is a hope, not yet a fact.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The cause of religion is God's own cause and he will certainly plead it." He notes that "nothing encourages them more to hope that God will appear against their enemies than when they have arrived at such a pitch of wickedness as to reproach God himself." On "dark places," he says Babylon, though famous for learning, was dark without the knowledge of God, and "where there was no true divinity there was scarcely to be found common humanity." JFB reads "multitude" in v19 as "literally, 'beast,'" a pack of men, and the "turtledove" as "the meek and lonely Church." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes that faith can turn even the blasphemies of fools into arguments in prayer. Trapp calls the covenant "a hive of heavenly honey."

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 74 mourns a temple in ashes and a people who cannot see God's signs. Jesus wept over Jerusalem and foretold another destruction of the temple (Luke 19:41–44; 21:6), and then spoke of his own body as the temple: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19–21). His body was broken and his enemies roared around him, but God did not withdraw his hand forever. He raised him up. In Christ, God crushed the head of the ancient serpent (Gen 3:15; Rev 12:9; 20:2), and he made a new covenant in his blood (Luke 22:20), so that the poor and needy who seek him need never be turned away ashamed. The psalm's unanswered cry, "Have regard for the covenant," is answered at the cross.
