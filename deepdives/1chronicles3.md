*Setting:* Chronicles was written for Jews back in Judah after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Chapter 2 traced Judah's line down to David and his brothers. This chapter takes David's own family from his sons, through every king of Judah to the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, and on past the exile into the Chronicler's own day. It is the most important list in the first nine chapters. God had promised David, "your throne shall be established forever" (2 Sam 7:16). By the Chronicler's time there had been no king on that throne for well over a century, and Judah was a small province of the Persian Empire. This list answers a silent question: is David's line still alive? It is. The family is named, generation after generation, right down to the readers' own time.

#### Descendants of David (vv1–24)
- **Six sons born in Hebron (vv1–4).** This list matches 2 Samuel 3:2–5. Each has a different mother, a sign of David's many marriages, several of them political. **Amnon** raped his half-sister Tamar and was killed by Absalom (2 Sam 13). **Daniel**, Abigail's son, is called Chileab in 2 Samuel 3:3, and nothing more is known of him. **Absalom**'s mother was the daughter of the king of Geshur, a small Aramean kingdom, which is where Absalom fled after killing Amnon (2 Sam 13:37). **Adonijah** tried to seize the throne as David was dying (1 Kings 1). The Chronicler lists these sons without comment. His readers knew the stories.
- **"He reigned for seven years and six months… And he reigned thirty-three years in Jerusalem" (v4).** Hebron was David's first capital, over Judah alone (2 Sam 2:1–4; 5:5). Then he took Jerusalem and made it the capital of all Israel.
- **"Four by Bath-shua, the daughter of Ammiel" (v5).** This is Bathsheba. Her father is called Eliam in 2 Samuel 11:3. "Ammiel" and "Eliam" use the same two parts in reverse order (*ammi*, "my people" or "kinsman," and *el*, "God"). Chronicles does not tell the Bathsheba story at all, but this line quietly assumes it.
- **"Shimea, Shobab, Nathan and Solomon" (v5).** Solomon is listed last of the four, though 2 Samuel 12:24 suggests he was the first son born after the child who died. He may simply be placed last for emphasis, since his line continues in v10. JFB notes the oddity. **Nathan** matters too. Luke traces Jesus' ancestry through David's son Nathan (Luke 3:31), while Matthew traces it through Solomon (Matt 1:6). Many think Luke gives Mary's line or the line of natural descent, and Matthew the royal, legal line.
- **Nine more sons (vv6–8).** 2 Samuel 5:14–16 gives a shorter list. Two names, **Elishama** and **Eliphelet**, appear twice here. A likely reason is that sons of those names died young and the names were given again to later sons, which families sometimes did. **"Besides the sons of the concubines, and Tamar was their sister" (v9).** Only one of David's daughters is named, the one whose story was a tragedy (2 Sam 13). Her name is kept.
- **The kings of Judah (vv10–14).** From Solomon to Josiah, sixteen kings in an unbroken line from father to son, about 970 to 609 BC. This is remarkable. The northern kingdom of Israel went through nine different royal families in two hundred years. Judah had one, David's, for nearly four centuries. The only break was Athaliah, the queen mother who seized power for six years (2 Kings 11), and she is left out because she was not of David's line. A few names differ from Kings: **Joram** is also Jehoram, and **Azariah** is also called Uzziah (2 Kings 15:1; Isa 6:1). Good kings and bad kings stand side by side without comment: faithful Hezekiah, then his son Manasseh, the worst king of all (2 Kings 21). Chronicles will later tell of Manasseh's repentance (2 Chr 33:12–13).
- **"The sons of Josiah" (v15).** **Johanan** the firstborn is not mentioned anywhere else and may have died young. **Jehoiakim**, **Zedekiah** and **Shallum** all reigned. Shallum is the king also called Jehoahaz, who reigned three months before Egypt deposed him (2 Kings 23:30–34; Jer 22:11). The order here is not strictly by age. Kings shows Shallum was older than Zedekiah (2 Kings 23:31; 24:18).
- **"Jeconiah his son, Zedekiah his son" (v16).** Jeconiah (also Jehoiachin and Coniah) reigned three months before Nebuchadnezzar took him to Babylon in 597 BC (2 Kings 24:8–15). The Zedekiah named after him is puzzling. King Zedekiah was Jeconiah's uncle, Josiah's son (2 Kings 24:17). "His son" here may mean his successor on the throne, or this may be a different Zedekiah, a son of Jeconiah. Interpreters are divided.
- **"The sons of Jeconiah, the captive" (v17).** The Hebrew word *assir* means "prisoner." The KJV treated it as another son's name, "Assir," but most modern translations, and JFB, read it as a description. Jeconiah spent 37 years in a Babylonian prison before being released (2 Kings 25:27–30). Clay tablets found in Babylon, listing rations of oil given to prisoners, actually name "Jehoiachin, king of Judah" and his sons. They are among the most striking archaeological confirmations of the Old Testament.
- **What about Jeremiah's curse? (v17).** Jeremiah said of Jeconiah: "Write this man down as childless… for none of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David" (Jer 22:30). Yet here he has seven sons. The curse explains itself in its second half. He had children, but none of them would reign as king. And none did. "Childless" meant childless as far as the throne was concerned.
- **"The sons of Pedaiah: Zerubbabel and Shimei" (v19).** Zerubbabel led the first group of exiles home from Babylon in 538 BC and, as governor of Judah, rebuilt the temple (Ezra 3–6; Hag 1:1). Everywhere else he is called "the son of Shealtiel" (Ezra 3:2; Matt 1:12). Here he is the son of Pedaiah, Shealtiel's brother. The most common explanation is levirate marriage: if Shealtiel died without a son, his brother Pedaiah would marry the widow, and the firstborn would be counted as Shealtiel's legal heir (Deut 25:5–6). Others suggest Shealtiel adopted his nephew. Either way, the line holds. Some scholars also identify **Shenazzar** (v18) with Sheshbazzar, the "prince of Judah" who brought the temple vessels back (Ezra 1:8). That is possible but not certain.
- **Haggai's signet ring.** God told Jeconiah through Jeremiah: "Though Coniah… were the signet ring on my right hand, yet I would tear you off" (Jer 22:24). Seventy years later, God told Jeconiah's grandson through Haggai: "I will take you, O Zerubbabel… and make you like a signet ring, for I have chosen you" (Hag 2:23). The ring torn off is put back on. The curse on the throne was not the end of God's promise to the family.
- **"Shelomith was their sister" (v19).** A daughter named in a royal line is unusual. A seal from this period, reading "Shelomith, maidservant (or official) of Elnathan the governor," has been found, and some scholars think it may be the same woman. That is an interesting possibility, not a proven link.
- **After Zerubbabel (vv20–24).** The names are almost all unknown to us. **Hattush** (v22) appears in Ezra 8:2 among those who came back with Ezra in 458 BC. The Hebrew of v21 is unclear about whether its names are brothers or a chain of fathers and sons. The Greek translation reads them as six generations. Depending on how it is read, the list runs between about six and eleven generations after Zerubbabel, bringing it to around 400 BC or a little later. This is one of the main clues for dating Chronicles: the author was writing for people who could see their own neighbors' grandfathers in this list.
- **"Anani, seven" (v24).** The last name in the royal line is Anani, which means "cloudy" or "my cloud." Later Jewish interpreters, in the Aramaic Targum on Chronicles, read it as a pointer to the Messiah, linking it to the Son of Man who comes "with the clouds of heaven" (Dan 7:13). That is tradition, not the plain meaning of the text, but it shows that Jewish readers saw this list as a line still waiting for its king.
- **Commentators:** Henry says, "Of all the families of Israel, none were so illustrious as the family of David," and states the point of the chapter plainly: "From this family, as concerning the flesh, Christ came." Guzik notes that Daniel and Chileab are probably one man, that Bath-shua is Bathsheba, and that the entire list of kings runs unbroken except for Athaliah, who was not of David's line. Following Clarke, Guzik reads Jeremiah's "childless" as loss of the throne, not of children. JFB points out that Solomon, though eldest, is "named the last of Bath-sheba's children," and reads v17 as "Jeconiah the prisoner." The Tyndale notes date the end of the list to about 400 BC.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter carries the promise to David through the darkest stretch of Israel's history, from Solomon's glory to Jeconiah in chains, and then into the dim years when the family had no throne at all. The list does not end with a king. It ends with seven brothers in a small province, waiting. Matthew picks up exactly this line (Jeconiah, Shealtiel, Zerubbabel) and carries it to "Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called Christ" (Matt 1:12–16). The angel's word to Mary is the answer to this chapter's waiting: "the Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David… and of his kingdom there will be no end" (Luke 1:32–33).
