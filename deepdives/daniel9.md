*Setting:* "The first year of Darius" (v1), the year Babylon fell to the Medes and Persians, 539 BC or just after. The night of Belshazzar's feast has passed, and the empire Daniel served for over sixty years is gone. Daniel is about eighty. Reading the prophet Jeremiah, he realises that the seventy years of exile God had set are almost over, and he responds not with celebration but with one of the great prayers of the Bible. While he is still praying, Gabriel arrives with an answer that reaches much further than Daniel asked: the prophecy of the "seventy weeks," one of the most studied and most debated passages in Scripture. Chapters 7 and 8 showed the beasts and the persecutor. Chapter 9 turns to sin, forgiveness, and God's plan to deal with sin once and for all.

#### Daniel's Prayer for His People (vv1–19)
- **"Darius the son of Ahasuerus, by descent a Mede" (v1).** On who this Darius was, see the note on 5:31. "Ahasuerus" is the Hebrew form of the Persian royal name the Greeks wrote as "Xerxes." This cannot be the Xerxes of Esther, who reigned later (486–465 BC), so it is either an earlier man of that name or a royal title. **"Was made king"** suggests that someone else, presumably Cyrus, put him on the throne.
- **"I, Daniel, perceived in the books" (v2).** "The books" means the sacred writings, and Daniel names Jeremiah, his older contemporary. This is a rare window into how one prophet read another. Jeremiah had promised: "These nations shall serve the king of Babylon seventy years" (Jer 25:11–12), and "When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you… and bring you back to this place" (Jer 29:10). Daniel's exile began in 605 BC. Sixty-six or sixty-seven years had passed. The time was nearly up.
- **How the seventy years were counted.** From the first deportation (605 BC) to the first return (538 BC) is about sixty-seven years. From the destruction of the temple (586 BC) to its rebuilding (515 BC) is about seventy. Both counts work, and seventy may also be a round number for a full lifetime (Ps 90:10). Chronicles adds that the land rested for seventy years to make up its neglected Sabbaths (2 Chr 36:21; Lev 26:34–35).
- **"Then I turned my face to the Lord God" (v3).** God's promise does not make Daniel passive. It drives him to pray. Jeremiah's own letter said as much: "Then you will call upon me and come and pray to me, and I will hear you" (Jer 29:12). **"Fasting and sackcloth and ashes"** were the outward signs of grief and repentance (Neh 9:1; Jonah 3:5–6).
- **"O Lord, the great and awesome God, who keeps covenant and steadfast love" (v4).** Daniel opens by praising God's faithfulness before he mentions Israel's failure. "Steadfast love" is *hesed*, God's loyal covenant love. The words come from Deuteronomy 7:9 and are echoed in Nehemiah's prayer (Neh 1:5; 9:32). This is the only chapter in Daniel that uses God's covenant name, *Yahweh*, "the LORD" (vv2, 4, 8, 10, 13, 14, 20). It is a prayer of covenant people to their covenant God.
- **"We have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled" (v5).** Daniel piles up words for sin: missing the mark, twisting what is straight, acting guilty, rebelling. Notice the pronoun. Daniel is one of the few people in the Old Testament about whom nothing bad is recorded, yet he never says "they." He says "we." He stands with his people under God's judgment, as Moses, Ezra and Nehemiah did (Exod 32:31–32; Ezra 9:6–7; Neh 1:6).
- **"We have not listened to your servants the prophets" (vv6, 10).** Jeremiah spent forty years warning Jerusalem, and almost no one listened (Jer 25:3–7). The exile was not a failure of warning. It was a failure to hear.
- **"To you, O Lord, belongs righteousness, but to us open shame" (vv7–8).** "Open shame" is literally "shame of face," the red-faced embarrassment of someone caught. The contrast runs all through the prayer: God is righteous (vv7, 14, 16), we are guilty. Daniel does not argue that the exile was unfair. He agrees with God's verdict.
- **"The curse and oath… in the Law of Moses" (vv11–13).** Moses had laid out blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience, ending in exile (Lev 26:14–39; Deut 28:15–68). Israel had sworn to keep the covenant (Deut 29:12–14). **"He has confirmed his words"** (v12): God did exactly what he said he would. That is terrible, but it is also hopeful. The same God promised to bring his people back if they turned to him from exile (Lev 26:40–45; Deut 30:1–5).
- **"Yet we have not entreated the favor of the LORD" (v13).** Even seventy years of exile had not produced real repentance. Daniel's prayer is an attempt to do what the nation had failed to do.
- **"Who brought your people out of the land of Egypt" (v15).** Daniel turns from confession to appeal, and he grounds it in the exodus. God had "made a name for yourself" by rescuing Israel from Egypt. A second rescue from Babylon would be a second exodus, as the prophets had promised (Isa 43:16–19; Jer 16:14–15).
- **"Your city Jerusalem, your holy hill" (v16).** Daniel keeps saying "your": your city, your people, your sanctuary, your name (vv16–19). Jerusalem has become **"a byword,"** a joke among the nations. Because God's name is attached to the city, its ruin brings shame on him (Ezek 36:20–23).
- **"Make your face to shine upon your sanctuary" (v17).** These are the words of the priestly blessing (Num 6:25), now prayed over a ruined temple.
- **"Not because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy" (v18).** This is the heart of the prayer and of biblical prayer generally. Daniel brings no merit at all. His only argument is God's character and God's name.
- **"O Lord, hear; O Lord, forgive. O Lord, pay attention and act. Delay not" (v19).** The prayer ends in short, urgent cries, each one beginning "O Lord." Within a year or so, Cyrus issued the decree that let the Jews go home (Ezra 1:1–4).
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the lesson that "in every prayer we must make confession," and reads "for the Lord's sake" (v17) as "for the Lord Christ's sake," a reading later Christian readers have shared, though Daniel himself is pleading God's own honour. JFB says Daniel "takes his countrymen's place of confession of sin, identifying himself with them," and identifies "the books" with Jeremiah's letter to the exiles (Jer 29:10). Guzik makes the point that God's promises invite prayer rather than replace it. He notes that Daniel combined careful study of Scripture with deep emotion, and that the heart of his request was for God's face to shine again.

#### Gabriel Brings an Answer (vv20–23)
- **"While I was speaking and praying" (vv20–21).** The repetition stresses the timing. The answer arrives before Daniel finishes (compare Isa 65:24: "while they are yet speaking I will hear"). **"Gabriel, whom I had seen in the vision at the first"** is the messenger of 8:16.
- **"In swift flight" (v21).** The Hebrew is unclear. It may mean "in swift flight" or "being wearied with weariness." If the second, it refers to Daniel's exhaustion, not Gabriel's. Translations differ, and the point does not affect the meaning of the passage.
- **"At the time of the evening sacrifice" (v21).** The evening lamb was offered about 3 p.m. (Exod 29:38–41). There had been no sacrifice in Jerusalem for about fifty years, but Daniel still kept its hour for prayer. Compare Ezra praying "at the evening sacrifice" (Ezra 9:5) and Peter and John going to the temple "at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1).
- **"At the beginning of your pleas for mercy a word went out" (v23).** God answered at once, though the messenger arrived a little later. In 10:12–13 the same pattern appears with a longer delay.
- **"For you are greatly loved" (v23).** The word means "precious," something treasured (used of costly things in 10:3; 11:38). Gabriel says it again in 10:11, 19. This is how heaven regards a man who has just confessed his people's sin as his own.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "greatly beloved" as "the object of God's delight" and puts the evening oblation at "the ninth hour, three o'clock." Guzik compares Daniel to John, "the disciple whom Jesus loved," and suggests that understanding often comes through prayer as well as study.

#### The Seventy Weeks (vv24–27)
- **"Seventy weeks are decreed" (v24).** The Hebrew word is *shavu'im*, "sevens," units of seven. The text does not say "of years," but almost all interpreters take it as seventy sevens of years, 490 years. Daniel had been thinking about seventy years. Gabriel answers with seventy times seven, which recalls the law's seven-times-seven Jubilee cycle (Lev 25:8). The exile will end, but the full restoration God intends will take much longer. Some interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, think the numbers are symbolic periods rather than an exact count of years.
- **"About your people and your holy city" (v24).** The prophecy concerns Israel and Jerusalem specifically.
- **Six goals (v24).** Three deal with sin: **"to finish the transgression, to put an end to sin, and to atone for iniquity."** "Atone" (*kipper*) is the word used for the sacrifices and the Day of Atonement (Lev 16). Three deal with what replaces it: **"to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal both vision and prophet, and to anoint a most holy place."** "Seal" here probably means to confirm by fulfilling. "A most holy place" is literally "a holy of holies." It may mean the temple's inner room, a new temple, or a person: some early Christians read it as the anointing of Christ himself.
- **"From the going out of the word to restore and build Jerusalem" (v25).** The count starts with a decree to rebuild the city. Four decrees are candidates: Cyrus in 538 BC (Ezra 1:1–4; compare Isa 44:28), Darius I in about 520 BC (Ezra 6:1–12), Artaxerxes I in 458 BC to Ezra (Ezra 7:11–26), and Artaxerxes I in 445 BC to Nehemiah, which explicitly covered the city walls (Neh 2:1–8).
- **"To the coming of an anointed one, a prince" (v25).** The Hebrew is *mashiach nagid*. *Mashiach* means "anointed," the word behind "Messiah." Kings and priests were anointed, and so was Cyrus (Isa 45:1). The ESV has "an anointed one," leaving the identity open. The KJV's "Messiah the Prince" reads it as a title of Christ.
- **"Seven weeks. And for sixty-two weeks" (v25).** There is a real question of punctuation here. The Hebrew accent marks, added by Jewish scholars in the early Middle Ages, put a break after "seven weeks," and the ESV follows them: the anointed one comes after 49 years, and then the city is rebuilt over 434 years. The KJV and many older translations join the numbers: 7 + 62 = 69 weeks, or 483 years, until "Messiah the Prince." Both readings have careful defenders.
- **"Built again with squares and moat, but in a troubled time" (v25).** This fits the rebuilding under Ezra and Nehemiah, which went on under constant opposition (Neh 4:1–23; 6:1–16).
- **"An anointed one shall be cut off and shall have nothing" (v26).** "Cut off" is used for being killed or put to death (Gen 9:11; Lev 7:20). "Shall have nothing" is literally "and there is not to him." The KJV's "but not for himself" is a less likely rendering. It may mean he is left without followers, without his kingdom, or without anything at all.
- **"The people of the prince who is to come shall destroy the city and the sanctuary" (v26).** Note that it is the prince's *people* who destroy. **"Its end shall come with a flood"** pictures an overwhelming army (11:10, 22, 40; Isa 8:7–8). **"Desolations are decreed"**: war and ruin to the end, but under God's decree.
- **"He shall make a strong covenant with many for one week" (v27).** Who is "he"? The nearest person in the sentence is "the prince who is to come," but some read it as the anointed one. **"For half of the week he shall put an end to sacrifice and offering."** **"On the wing of abominations shall come one who makes desolate"**: "wing" may mean a high point of the temple, or the edge of the altar, or an army spreading out. **"Until the decreed end is poured out on the desolator"**: the destroyer is himself destroyed at the time God has set.
- **Four main ways of reading the seventy weeks.** (1) *Fulfilled in Christ.* Counting from 458/457 BC, 483 years brings us to about AD 26–27, when Jesus began his ministry. He is "cut off" in the middle of the final week, his death ends the need for sacrifice, and he confirms the new covenant "with many" (Matt 26:28). The Romans under Titus, "the people of the prince," then destroy the city in AD 70. Many Reformed and older Protestant interpreters take this view. (2) *Fulfilled in Christ, with a future final week.* Counting from 445 BC in 360-day prophetic years, 483 years lead to Jesus' entry into Jerusalem. The 70th week is separated from the rest by the church age and is still future: a coming ruler will make and break a covenant with Israel, and the last three and a half years match the "time, times, and half a time" of 7:25. This is the dispensational view. (3) *Fulfilled in the Maccabean crisis.* Counting from 587 or 538 BC, the "anointed one cut off" is the high priest Onias III, murdered in 171 BC (11:22), and the desolator is Antiochus IV, who stopped the sacrifices in 167 BC (8:11–13). Most critical scholars take this view. (4) *Symbolic.* The seventy sevens stand for the whole period from the return to the end of history, with its three stages, rather than an exact count of years. Jesus' own words point beyond Antiochus: he speaks of "the abomination of desolation spoken of by the prophet Daniel" as still to come (Matt 24:15).
- **The abomination of desolation.** The Tyndale notes point out that the Hebrew phrase *shiqqutsim meshomem* may play on *Ba'al Shamayim*, "lord of heaven," the Semitic title of the Zeus whose altar Antiochus set up in the temple (1 Macc 1:54). By turning "heaven" (*shamayim*) into "desolation" (*shomem*), the phrase mocks the pagan god.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the seventy weeks a form of "concealed definiteness." He starts the count with Ezra's return in 457 BC, places Jesus' death "in the middle of the last week, A.D. 30," and reads "he shall confirm the covenant" as Christ. He identifies "the people of the prince" as "the Romans, led by Titus." Henry is less precise. He leans toward Cyrus's decree as the starting point, but says that if the 490 years end at Christ's death, "I am willing enough to be of that opinion." He reads the covenant as Christ's covenant of grace and the destroyers as the Roman armies. Guzik follows the second view above: he starts from Artaxerxes' decree of 445 BC, uses Sir Robert Anderson's 360-day years to arrive at Jesus' triumphal entry, and puts the seventieth week in the future. He admits the dates are debated. So the three commentators represent two of the four main views, and all three see Christ's death in "cut off."

#### Pointing to Christ
Daniel prayed for forgiveness for Israel's sins and the end of the exile. God answered with much more: a plan "to finish the transgression, to put an end to sin, and to atone for iniquity, to bring in everlasting righteousness." Whatever the precise counting, the New Testament says this was accomplished at the cross. Jesus, the Anointed One, was "cut off" and seemed to have nothing, abandoned by his followers and stripped even of his clothes. But his death did what the evening sacrifice Daniel remembered could never do: it made "a single sacrifice for sins" for all time (Heb 10:12) and brought in a righteousness that lasts forever (Rom 3:21–26). Daniel prayed, "not because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy." In Christ, that mercy has a name.
