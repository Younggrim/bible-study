*Setting:* The collection copied by "the men of Hezekiah" (25:1–29:27) continues. Chapter 26 is the most tightly organized chapter in the book, three portrait galleries hung one after another. Verses 1–12 are about the fool (every verse but one names him), vv13–16 about the sluggard, and vv17–28 about people who wreck relationships with their words: the meddler, the "only joking" deceiver, the gossip, the quarrelsome, and the smooth-talking hater. Nearly every saying is a vivid comparison drawn from weather, animals, farming, warfare or the potter's workshop. The ESV has no headings inside the collection, so the headings below follow these three portraits. The famous pair "Answer not a fool… Answer a fool…" (vv4–5) sits near the start and shows how proverbs are meant to be used: wisely, with an eye on the situation.

#### A Portrait of the Fool (vv1–12)
- **"Like snow in summer or rain in harvest, so honor is not fitting for a fool" (v1).** In Israel the dry season runs from about May to October, and the wheat harvest fell in early summer. Snow then was unheard of, and rain at harvest could ruin a crop. When Samuel called down thunder and rain at the wheat harvest it was a sign of God's displeasure (1 Sam 12:17–18). Honoring a fool is just as out of place and just as damaging. JFB: the fool's unworthiness is implied. Henry: "Honour is out of season to those unworthy and unfit for it." Compare 19:10.
- **"Like a sparrow in its flitting, like a swallow in its flying, a curse that is causeless does not alight" (v2).** Birds dart around without landing on you. A curse without cause is the same: it can't land, because curses have no magic power of their own. Only God makes a curse stick, and he won't back an undeserved one. Balaam: "How can I curse whom God has not cursed?" (Num 23:8). Goliath cursed David by his gods (1 Sam 17:43), and it came to nothing. The Hebrew scribes preserved an alternative reading here, "a causeless curse comes to him," meaning it returns on the one who utters it, but the ESV's reading is standard. JFB: penal evil "falls on none without a reason." Henry: the curse does "no more harm than the bird that flies over his head."
- **"A whip for the horse, a bridle for the donkey, and a rod for the back of fools" (v3).** Animals that won't listen to reason need force. So does a fool. Psalm 32:9: "Be not like a horse or a mule, without understanding, which must be curbed with bit and bridle." Guzik passes on Kidner's point that this warns would-be fools too, meaning us. Henry adds that God can still change hardened hearts.
- **"Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest you be like him yourself. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes" (vv4–5).** Two sayings that seem to contradict each other, placed side by side on purpose. Both are true, and wisdom knows which one the moment needs. Sometimes answering a fool on his own terms drags you down to his level: shouting back, trading insults. Jesus stayed silent before Herod (Luke 23:9), and Hezekiah's officials said nothing to the Assyrian commander's taunts (2 Kgs 18:36). Other times silence lets the fool think he has won, and a sharp reply exposes his folly: Jesus answered the Sadducees' trick question so well that "they no longer dared to ask him any question" (Luke 20:27–40). JFB: don't answer "by like folly," but do answer "by reproof." The Tyndale notes: proverbs depend on the situation. Henry: "We are to fit our remarks to the man, and address them to his conscience." The pair shows that proverbs are not machine rules but tools for a wise person to use.
- **"Whoever sends a message by the hand of a fool cuts off his own feet and drinks violence" (v6).** A messenger was the sender's feet, going where he couldn't. A foolish messenger is like chopping them off. "Drinks violence" means swallowing a big dose of trouble. Contrast the faithful messenger of 25:13. JFB: he gets damage "abundantly."
- **"Like a lame man's legs, which hang useless, is a proverb in the mouth of fools" (v7).** A fool can memorize a proverb and still not know how to use it, like legs that are there but can't walk. Wisdom isn't knowing sayings but knowing how and when to apply them, which vv4–5 just showed. The Tyndale notes: a fool may know a proverb but not use it correctly.
- **"Like one who binds the stone in the sling is one who gives honor to a fool" (v8).** A sling stone has to fly free. Tie it in and it's useless, or it swings back and hits the slinger. Honoring a fool is that pointless and that risky. JFB: honor given to a fool is "thrown away." David's sling worked because the stone was released (1 Sam 17:49).
- **"Like a thorn that goes up into the hand of a drunkard is a proverb in the mouth of fools" (v9).** A drunk grabs a thorn bush and doesn't feel it, or waves a thorny branch around and hurts others. A fool with a proverb misuses it, hurting himself and others. JFB: "as vexatious and unmanageable" as a thorn in a drunkard's hand. Satan himself quoted Scripture to Jesus out of context (Matt 4:6).
- **"Like an archer who wounds everyone is one who hires a passing fool or drunkard" (v10).** One of the hardest verses in Proverbs to translate. Nearly every word can be read more than one way, and the old versions differ widely. The King James has "The great God that formed all things both rewardeth the fool, and rewardeth transgressors," and Henry and Guzik follow that line of reading toward God's providence. JFB, closer to the ESV, rejects supplying "God" and reads it as society being harmed by employing worthless people. The ESV picture is someone shooting arrows at random: hiring whoever happens to wander past, sober or not, is just as dangerous. The Tyndale notes: employers should be cautious.
- **"Like a dog that returns to his vomit is a fool who repeats his folly" (v11).** A deliberately disgusting picture. Dogs in the ancient Near East were mostly half-wild scavengers, not pets. Peter quotes this proverb about people who knew the truth and went back to sin (2 Pet 2:22). JFB: "Though disgusting to others, the fool delights in his folly." The Tyndale notes: even when folly causes serious trouble, a fool keeps at it.
- **"Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (v12).** The surprise ending of the fool's gallery. After eleven verses mocking the fool, there's someone worse: the person who thinks he's wise. A fool might at least learn. The self-satisfied can't, because he doesn't think he needs to. Jesus told the Pharisees, "If you were blind, you would have no guilt; but now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains" (John 9:41). JFB: "The self-conceited are taught with more difficulty than the stupid." Guzik passes on Morgan's line: "The greatest fool is the fool who does not know he is a fool." 29:20 repeats the second line.
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Ross's point that vv4–5 aren't a contradiction, because human problems are complicated, and Bridges' that wisdom knows when to speak and when to be silent. JFB and the ESV agree on v10 against the King James reading that Henry and Guzik follow.

#### A Portrait of the Sluggard (vv13–16)
- **"The sluggard says, 'There is a lion in the road! There is a lion in the streets!'" (v13).** A near repeat of 22:13. Lions did live in the Jordan thickets, but not in town streets. The sluggard is endlessly creative when it comes to avoiding work. Henry: he "hates every thing that requires care and labour."
- **"As a door turns on its hinges, so does a sluggard on his bed" (v14).** Ancient doors turned on a pivot set in a socket in the threshold. A door moves back and forth all day and never goes anywhere. So does the sluggard rolling over in bed. Henry: the lazy turn on their hinges without moving any closer to heaven.
- **"The sluggard buries his hand in the dish; it wears him out to bring it back to his mouth" (v15).** Repeats 19:24, with a new ending: it "wears him out." Even eating is too much effort. The Tyndale notes point out the repeat.
- **"The sluggard is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who can answer sensibly" (v16).** "Seven" stands for a full number, a whole council of advisers. The phrase "wise in his own eyes" ties back to v12. Laziness and conceit go together. The sluggard always has a reason why everyone else is wrong. JFB: the thoughtless are "ignorant of their ignorance." Guzik passes on Kidner's note that the sluggard never recognizes himself in this portrait.
- **Commentators:** Henry applies vv13–16 to spiritual laziness: "Bodily ease is the sad occasion of many spiritual diseases." JFB gives only cross-references for vv13–15, since all three repeat earlier sayings.

#### A Portrait of the Troublemaker (vv17–28)
- **"Whoever meddles in a quarrel not his own is like one who takes a passing dog by the ears" (v17).** "Meddles" is literally "gets worked up" over someone else's fight. Grab a stray dog by the ears and you can't hold on and can't let go without being bitten. JFB: dangerous "whether one holds on or lets go." The Tyndale notes: wise people stay out of others' arguments. Jesus declined to settle an inheritance dispute between two brothers (Luke 12:13–14). This doesn't forbid peacemaking (Matt 5:9), which is invited and fair, but meddling, which is neither.
- **"Like a madman who throws firebrands, arrows, and death is the man who deceives his neighbor and says, 'I am only joking!'" (vv18–19).** Flaming arrows were used to set besieged cities on fire. A lunatic shooting them around doesn't make the fire less deadly. Neither does saying "just kidding" after a lie. The damage is done. JFB: "Such are reckless of results." Henry: "He that sins in jest, must repent in earnest, or his sin will be his ruin." Paul lists "crude joking" among things that are "out of place" (Eph 5:4).
- **"For lack of wood the fire goes out, and where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases" (v20).** Stop feeding the fire and it dies. Stop passing on gossip and the fight burns out. Every person who refuses to repeat a story removes a log. James: "the tongue is a fire" (Jas 3:6). Guzik passes on Meyer's point that gossip stops when it reaches someone who won't pass it on.
- **"As charcoal to hot embers and wood to fire, so is a quarrelsome man for kindling strife" (v21).** The opposite of v20: the quarrelsome person adds fuel. JFB: the contentious "excite strife." Henry: contention "puts families and societies into a flame."
- **"The words of a whisperer are like delicious morsels; they go down into the inner parts of the body" (v22).** Repeated word for word from 18:8. The "delicious morsels" are tidbits swallowed greedily. Gossip tastes good and goes deep.
- **"Like the glaze covering an earthen vessel are fervent lips with an evil heart" (v23).** The standard Hebrew text reads "silver dross" (*kesep sigim*). Many scholars, comparing a word found in texts from ancient Ugarit, now read it as one word meaning "glaze," and the ESV follows. Either way, the picture is a cheap clay pot coated with a shiny silver-colored finish, impressive outside, worthless underneath. Guzik passes on Waltke's note that silvery slag was actually used as a glaze on pottery. "Fervent lips" are warm, eager words. The old Greek translation has "smooth lips," which fits the next verses. Jesus called the Pharisees "whitewashed tombs" (Matt 23:27). JFB: warm professions can't give value to insincerity any more than silver coating gives value to rough earthenware. Henry: "A wicked heart disguising itself, is like a potsherd covered with the dross of silver."
- **"Whoever hates disguises himself with his lips and harbors deceit in his heart; when he speaks graciously, believe him not, for there are seven abominations in his heart" (vv24–25).** The glazed pot explained. "Seven abominations" means a heart full of evil (6:16–19 lists seven things God hates). Joab greeted Amasa, "Is it well with you, my brother?" and stabbed him while holding his beard as if to kiss him (2 Sam 20:9–10). Henry: "Always distrust when a man speaks fair unless you know him well." Psalm 55:21: "His speech was smooth as butter, yet war was in his heart."
- **"Though his hatred be covered with deception, his wickedness will be exposed in the assembly" (v26).** The "assembly" was the public gathering where cases were judged. Hidden hatred comes out eventually, in this life or at the last judgment. Jesus: "Nothing is covered up that will not be revealed" (Luke 12:2). JFB: "Deceit will at last be exposed."
- **"Whoever digs a pit will fall into it, and a stone will come back on him who starts it rolling" (v27).** Hunters dug pits to trap animals. The schemer falls into his own. Someone rolling a boulder uphill to drop on an enemy has it roll back on him. Haman was hanged on his own gallows (Esth 7:10), and Daniel's accusers were thrown into the lions' den they had prepared for him (Dan 6:24). Psalm 7:15–16 says the same. Henry: "it is digging a pit, it is rolling a stone." The Tyndale notes connect 1:17–19.
- **"A lying tongue hates its victims, and a flattering mouth works ruin" (v28).** The chapter ends with the root: lying is a form of hatred, whatever words it uses. JFB: "Men hate those they injure." Henry: "A wise man will be more afraid of a flatterer than of a slanderer."
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read v23 as fine-sounding words covering a bad heart, and Guzik passes on Waltke's note on silver slag used for glaze. Henry and the Tyndale notes both read vv24–26 as hidden hatred that is eventually exposed. Guzik passes on Kidner's phrase that deceit is "practical hatred."

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 26 shows the fool who can't use wisdom, the sluggard who won't, and the deceiver whose fine words hide hatred. Jesus is the opposite of all three. He is wisdom in person, who knew perfectly when to answer a fool and when to stay silent (vv4–5; Matt 22:46; 27:14). He worked "while it is day" (John 9:4) and finished the work the Father gave him (John 17:4). His lips were never a glaze over an evil heart; "no deceit was found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22), and his gracious words came from a heart full of love. The schemers who dug a pit for him seemed to win, but the stone rolled back (v27): the stone sealing his tomb was rolled away, and the plot of his enemies became the means of their defeat (Col 2:15). And "a curse that is causeless does not alight" (v2), yet Christ, who deserved no curse, willingly became "a curse for us" (Gal 3:13), so that every curse we did deserve would never land on us.
