*Setting:* After Egypt (ch 46) and the Philistines (ch 47), Jeremiah turns east to Moab, the kingdom across the Dead Sea on the high plateau of what is now Jordan. Moab descended from Lot, Abraham's nephew (Gen 19:36–37), so Moab and Israel were relatives, and bitter ones. Moab's king hired Balaam to curse Israel (Num 22–24). Moabite women drew Israel into idolatry at Peor (Num 25). A Moabite king oppressed Israel in the judges' days (Judg 3:12–30). Yet Ruth, the great-grandmother of David, was a Moabite (Ruth 4:17), and David sheltered his parents in Moab (1 Sam 22:3–4) before later conquering it (2 Sam 8:2). For much of its history the plateau north of the Arnon River was disputed land, claimed by Israel's tribe of Reuben and by Moab. This is the longest oracle against a nation in Jeremiah apart from Babylon. It borrows heavily from Isaiah's earlier oracle against Moab (Isa 15–16) and from the old songs in Numbers 21 and 24. The judgment came by Babylon, probably in 582 BC. Josephus says that in Nebuchadnezzar's 23rd year he campaigned against Ammon and Moab, and Moab disappears from history as a nation soon after.

#### Judgment on Moab (vv1–47)
- **The towns of Moab (vv1–5).** The chapter names more than twenty places, and many are known from an extraordinary find. In 1868 a black basalt stone was discovered at Dibon (v18), set up around 840 BC by Mesha king of Moab (2 Kgs 3:4). The Moabite Stone, now in the Louvre, celebrates Mesha's revolt against Israel. It names Chemosh as Moab's god, says Omri king of Israel had oppressed Moab "because Chemosh was angry with his land," and boasts of taking Nebo from Israel and devoting its people to destruction. It also names Kiriathaim, Aroer, Beth-diblathaim, Beth-baal-meon (Beth-meon), Jahaz, Horonaim and Kerioth, all of them in this chapter. Jeremiah is naming real towns, some of them conquered from Israel centuries before.
- **"Woe to Nebo, for it is laid waste!" (v1).** Nebo here is the town near Mount Nebo, where Moses viewed the promised land and died (Deut 34:1–5). **"Kiriathaim"** means "double city." **"The fortress is put to shame"** may be a place name, Misgab, "the high stronghold."
- **"In Heshbon they planned disaster against her" (v2).** The Hebrew plays on words: in *Heshbon* they *hashbu*, "planned." Heshbon, once the capital of the Amorite king Sihon (Num 21:26), lay on Moab's northern edge, and the enemy uses it as a base. **"You also, O Madmen, shall be brought to silence."** Another pun: *Madmen* will be *tiddommi*, "silenced." The town is unknown, and some think it is a wordplay on Dimon, Isaiah's name for Dibon (Isa 15:9).
- **"A cry from Horonaim… at the ascent of Luhith they go up weeping" (vv3–5).** Horonaim, "two caves," and Luhith lay in the south. Verse 5 is borrowed almost word for word from Isaiah 15:5. Refugees climb the road weeping, and the ESV footnote says the Hebrew is literally "weeping goes up with weeping."
- **"Flee! Save yourselves! You will be like a juniper in the desert!" (v6).** The word (*'aro'er*) may mean a desert shrub, standing alone in dry land. It sounds like Aroer, a Moabite town (v19), so it may be another pun. The Greek translation reads "wild donkey." The point is the same: fugitives scattered in the wilderness.
- **"Because you trusted in your works and your treasures, you also shall be taken" (v7).** "Works" may mean fortifications or the wealth gained from trade, since the King's Highway, the main caravan road from Arabia to Damascus, ran through Moab. **"And Chemosh shall go into exile with his priests and his officials."** Conquerors in the ancient world carried off the statues of a defeated people's gods to show that their own gods had won. Chemosh was Moab's national god, "the abomination of Moab" (1 Kgs 11:7). Solomon built him a high place outside Jerusalem, and Josiah tore it down (2 Kgs 23:13). In a desperate moment a king of Moab sacrificed his own son to him on the city wall (2 Kgs 3:27). Now Chemosh will be the one led away captive. Moab is called "the people of Chemosh" in v46 and in Numbers 21:29.
- **"The valley shall perish, and the plain shall be destroyed" (v8).** "The valley" is probably the Jordan valley and the lowland by the Dead Sea, and "the plain" (*mishor*) is the flat tableland of the plateau (v21). Nothing high or low escapes.
- **"Give wings to Moab, for she would fly away" (v9).** The Hebrew word is uncertain. It may mean "wings," or "salt," as if to sow the land with salt. Most take it as wings: Moab would need wings to get away.
- **"Cursed is he who does the work of the LORD with slackness, and cursed is he who keeps back his sword from bloodshed" (v10).** This verse shocks modern readers. It is spoken to the army God has sent, warning it not to hold back. It echoes Saul's failure to finish the judgment on Amalek (1 Sam 15) and Ahab's sparing of Ben-hadad (1 Kgs 20:42). "Slackness" (*remiyyah*) can also mean "deceit," halfhearted or false service. The verse is about a specific judgment God had decreed, not a general license for violence. Many readers have applied the first half more broadly: God's work deserves wholehearted effort.
- **"Moab has been at ease from his youth and has settled on his dregs" (v11).** This is the chapter's best-known image. Winemakers let new wine sit on its dregs (the sediment of skins and yeast) for a time, then poured it carefully into another jar, leaving the dregs behind. Wine never poured off becomes thick and harsh. **"He has not been emptied from vessel to vessel, nor has he gone into exile; so his taste remains in him, and his scent is not changed."** Moab sat high on its plateau, away from the main invasion routes along the coast, and it had rarely been deported or uprooted. Its comfort never changed it. Zephaniah uses the same image for the complacent in Jerusalem, who "are thickening on their dregs" (Zeph 1:12). Ease without testing let Moab's pride settle in.
- **"I shall send to him pourers who will pour him, and empty his vessels and break his jars in pieces" (v12).** The image continues. The "pourers" are the Babylonians. They will not decant Moab gently. They will tip it out and smash the jars.
- **"Then Moab shall be ashamed of Chemosh, as the house of Israel was ashamed of Bethel, their confidence" (v13).** Bethel was where Jeroboam set up a golden calf for the northern kingdom (1 Kgs 12:28–29). The calf did not save Israel from Assyria in 722 BC. Chemosh will do no better.
- **"How do you say, 'We are heroes and mighty men of war'?" (v14).** Moab's soldiers boast. **"Declares the King, whose name is the LORD of hosts" (v15).** As with Pharaoh (46:18), the true King answers the boast.
- **"Say, 'How the mighty scepter is broken, the glorious staff'" (v17).** The neighbors are called to sing a funeral song. The scepter and staff are the symbols of Moab's rule over others. Balaam had prophesied that "a scepter shall rise out of Israel; it shall crush the forehead of Moab" (Num 24:17). That line returns at the end of the chapter (v45).
- **"Come down from your glory, and sit on the parched ground, O inhabitant of Dibon!" (v18).** Dibon was a major city, Mesha's hometown, where the Moabite Stone was found. Its people are told to come down from their honor and sit in the dust like mourners. Compare the same command to Babylon (Isa 47:1).
- **"Stand by the way and watch, O inhabitant of Aroer! Ask him who flees" (v19).** Aroer stood on the edge of the deep Arnon gorge, the natural border of Moab. Its people watch the refugees streaming south and ask what happened. **"Tell it beside the Arnon, that Moab is laid waste" (v20).**
- **"Judgment has come upon the tableland" (vv21–24).** A long list of towns follows, from north to south, many of them on the plateau that Israel once held: Holon, Jahzah, Mephaath, Dibon, Nebo, Beth-diblathaim, Kiriathaim, Beth-gamul, Beth-meon, Kerioth, Bozrah. **Kerioth** had a sanctuary of Chemosh, according to the Moabite Stone. This **Bozrah** is a Moabite town, probably the Bezer of Deuteronomy 4:43, not the Edomite Bozrah of 49:13. The point of the list is its completeness: "all the cities of the land of Moab, far and near."
- **"The horn of Moab is cut off, and his arm is broken" (v25).** The horn is the symbol of an animal's strength, and the arm is the symbol of power to act. Both are gone.
- **"Make him drunk, because he magnified himself against the LORD" (v26).** Moab must drink the cup of God's wrath (25:15–28), and it will "wallow in his vomit." **"Was not Israel a derision to you? Was he found among thieves, that whenever you spoke of him you wagged your head?" (v27).** Moab had mocked Israel in its disasters, shaking its head as people did at a criminal caught in the act. Zephaniah had heard "the taunts of Moab" against God's people (Zeph 2:8–10), and Ezekiel says Moab sneered that Judah was "like all the other nations" (Ezek 25:8). Those who laugh at the fall of God's people will be laughed at.
- **"Leave the cities, and dwell in the rock… like the dove that nests in the sides of the mouth of a gorge" (v28).** Moab's land was cut by deep canyons, and wild doves nested in the cliffs. People who once lived in walled towns will hide in the rocks.
- **"We have heard of the pride of Moab—he is very proud—of his loftiness, his pride, and his arrogance, and the haughtiness of his heart" (v29).** In Hebrew, four of these words come from the same root (*g'h*, "be high"), piled one on another, with "haughtiness of heart" added at the end. Isaiah 16:6 says almost the same. The repetition makes the point: pride is Moab's basic sin. **"His boasts are false, his deeds are false" (v30).**
- **"Therefore I wail for Moab; I cry out for all Moab" (v31).** Who is speaking? It is the voice of the oracle, either the LORD himself or the prophet speaking for him. In Isaiah the same lament appears (Isa 16:7–11). Judgment does not mean God is cold. He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezek 18:23). **Kir-hareseth** was a great fortress in the south, probably modern Kerak, where Mesha held out against Israel (2 Kgs 3:25).
- **"O vine of Sibmah! Your branches passed over the sea, reached to the Sea of Jazer" (v32).** Moab's vineyards were famous. Their branches reached out as far as the Dead Sea. "The Sea of Jazer" is unclear and may be a copying slip from Isaiah 16:8, which has "they reached to Jazer." **"On your summer fruits and your grapes the destroyer has fallen."**
- **"I have made the wine cease from the winepresses; no one treads them with shouts of joy" (v33).** At harvest, workers trod grapes while singing a rhythmic shout, *hedad*. Now there is still a *hedad*, a shout, but **"the shouting is not the shout of joy."** It is the battle cry of the invaders. The same word, two meanings.
- **"From the outcry at Heshbon even to Elealeh… from Zoar to Horonaim" (v34).** The cry crosses Moab from north to south. **"The waters of Nimrim also have become desolate."** Nimrim was a well-watered spot near the southeast of the Dead Sea. Even the springs fail.
- **"Him who offers sacrifice in the high place and makes offerings to his god" (v35).** The worship of Chemosh will stop, because there will be no one left to offer it.
- **"My heart moans for Moab like a flute" (v36).** Flutes were played at funerals (compare Matt 9:23). God's heart is pictured as a mourning flute, playing the lament. **"The riches they gained have perished."**
- **"Every head is shaved and every beard cut off. On all the hands are gashes, and around the waist is sackcloth" (v37).** These are the mourning customs of the region (compare Isa 15:2–3). **"I have broken Moab like a vessel for which no one cares" (v38).** Jeremiah had smashed a clay jar to picture Jerusalem's fate (19:10–11). Moab now is a pot thrown out because no one wants it.
- **"One shall fly swiftly like an eagle and spread his wings against Moab" (v40).** Nebuchadnezzar swoops like a great bird of prey (compare Deut 28:49; Ezek 17:3). The same words are used of Edom (49:22). **"The heart of the warriors of Moab shall be… like the heart of a woman in her birth pains" (v41).** The mighty men of v14 now writhe in helpless fear. **"Moab shall be destroyed and be no longer a people, because he magnified himself against the LORD" (v42).**
- **"Terror, pit, and snare" (vv43–44).** In Hebrew these sound alike: *pachad*, *pachat*, *pach*. They come straight from Isaiah 24:17–18. **"He who flees from the terror shall fall into the pit, and he who climbs out of the pit shall be caught in the snare."** Escape one danger and fall into the next. Amos used a similar picture: a man flees a lion and meets a bear (Amos 5:19).
- **"Fire came out from Heshbon, flame from the house of Sihon" (v45).** Verses 45–46 rework an ancient victory song from Numbers 21:28–29, sung when Israel defeated the Amorite king Sihon of Heshbon, who had earlier conquered Moab: "Woe to you, O Moab! You are undone, O people of Chemosh!" **"It has destroyed the forehead of Moab, the crown of the sons of tumult."** This echoes Balaam's prophecy, "it shall crush the forehead of Moab and break down all the sons of Sheth" (Num 24:17). Jeremiah replaces the obscure "Sheth" with a similar-sounding word, *sha'on*, "tumult." So the chapter ends where Israel's story with Moab began, in the days of Moses. The Greek translation does not have vv45–47.
- **"Yet I will restore the fortunes of Moab in the latter days, declares the LORD" (v47).** After forty-six verses of doom, an unexpected promise. The same is said of Ammon (49:6) and Elam (49:39), and in a different form of Egypt (46:26). Moab never returned as a nation. Many Christian readers see the promise fulfilled when the gospel reached the peoples of that region, so that Gentiles from every nation come into God's family (compare Isa 25:6–10, which pictures a feast for all peoples). There is already a sign of it in Scripture. The law barred Moabites from the assembly of the LORD (Deut 23:3), yet Ruth the Moabite found refuge under Israel's God and became the ancestor of David and of Jesus (Matt 1:5).
- **"Thus far is the judgment on Moab."** An editor's closing line marking the end of the oracle (compare 51:64).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry, on v7: "The reed will break that is leaned upon." On v10 he says the LORD's work "must not be done by the halves." On v11 he writes, "While bad people are as happy as they used to be in the world it is no marvel if they are bad as they used to be." On vv26–27: "Those that are glad at calamities, especially the calamities of God's church, shall not long go unpunished." JFB notes that Moab's pride is named six times "to show the exceeding hatefulness of his sin," compares v10 with Saul's half-obedience against Amalek, and reads the eagle of v40 as the opposite of the God who bore Israel "on eagles' wings." On v47 JFB says "Gospel blessings, temporal and spiritual, to the Gentiles in the last days, are intended." Guzik dwells on the wine image and draws a pastoral contrast. What is judgment for Moab is, for God's people, the painful pouring from vessel to vessel that refines them. He also stresses that God grieves over a judgment even when it is deserved. The Tyndale notes point out the wordplay of v43 and its link to Isaiah.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moab sat undisturbed on its dregs, proud and comfortable, and mocked Israel when it fell. God answered with the pourers, the eagle and the pit. But the chapter's last word is mercy: "I will restore the fortunes of Moab." The thread of that mercy runs through a Moabite widow named Ruth, who said, "Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16), and whose great-grandson was David. From her line came the true King. He did not merely restore Moab's fortunes. He opened the door that the law had shut, so that people from every nation, Moab included, can be welcomed into God's people through him (Eph 2:11–19). The God whose heart moaned like a flute for Moab sent his Son to make the outsider a son.
