*Setting:* Babylonia, among the Judean exiles by the Chebar canal, in the winter of 586–585 BC. For seven years Ezekiel has warned that Jerusalem would fall, and for much of that time he has been unable to speak except when God gave him a message (3:26–27; 24:25–27). Chapters 25–32 turned to the nations around Israel. Now the book turns back to Israel, and chapter 33 is the hinge. God renews Ezekiel's call as watchman, a runner arrives with the news that the city has fallen, Ezekiel's mouth is opened, and from here on his message moves from judgment toward hope (chs 34–48). The chapter also shows the two groups who most needed that hope and were least ready for it: the survivors back in the ruined land, and the exiles who now flocked to hear the prophet but would not change.

#### Ezekiel Is Israel's Watchman (vv1–9)
- **"If I bring the sword upon a land" (v2).** God begins with a parable from ordinary life. A walled town in wartime chose a man to stand on the wall or a hilltop and watch the roads. The people of the land "take a man from among them": he is one of them, chosen for the job.
- **"Blows the trumpet and warns the people" (v3).** The trumpet is the *shofar*, a ram's horn. It was not a musical instrument so much as an alarm, the sound that meant "enemy coming" (Amos 3:6; Jer 4:5; Joel 2:1). Everyone in the town knew what it meant.
- **"His blood shall be upon his own head" (vv4–5).** If the alarm sounds and someone ignores it, his death is his own fault. **"He heard the sound of the trumpet and did not take warning"** is repeated so no one misses it. Verse 5 adds the hope in the parable: "if he had taken warning, he would have saved his life."
- **"His blood I will require at the watchman's hand" (v6).** A silent watchman is guilty. The person who dies is still "taken away in his iniquity," so his own sin is real, but the watchman shares the blame for the death. "Require blood" is the language of Genesis 9:5, God calling a killer to account.
- **"So you, son of man, I have made a watchman for the house of Israel" (v7).** Now the parable is applied. In the story the people chose the watchman. Ezekiel was chosen by God, and his warnings come "from my mouth," not from reading the news. This repeats almost word for word the charge God gave him at his call (3:16–21). The difference is the audience. In chapter 3 it was a private commission. Here it is spoken publicly, so the people hear what the prophet's job is and what their own responsibility is.
- **"O wicked one, you shall surely die" (v8).** This was the heart of Ezekiel's message for seven years. The warning was not cruelty. It was the only thing that could save anyone.
- **"You will have delivered your soul" (v9).** The watchman is responsible for sounding the alarm, not for how people respond. That freed Ezekiel from carrying a burden that was not his. It also bound him: silence was never an option, however unpopular the message.
- **Why repeat the call now?** The Tyndale notes point out that the people who would not believe the message of judgment before the fall would find it just as hard to believe the message of hope after it. Ezekiel faced rejection both times, and both times he might have been tempted to go quiet. God tells him again that he cannot.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the office plainly: the prophet's "business is to warn sinners of their misery and danger," and "if souls perish through his neglect of duty, he brings guilt upon himself." JFB notes that an ordinary watchman was appointed by the people, but Ezekiel's appointment "is far more solemn" because it comes from God himself. Guzik, following Block, describes the shofar as the alarm of a coming attack. He applies the passage to Christian leaders today: a watchman's work is to proclaim God's truth, not to go looking for faults, and one who cries "danger" over everything will not be believed when real danger comes.

#### Why Will You Die, Israel? (vv10–20)
- **"Our transgressions and our sins are upon us, and we rot away because of them" (v10).** For the first time the people admit their sin. But this is despair, not repentance. "Rot away" is *maqaq*, to decay or pine away, the word of the covenant curse in Leviticus 26:39: "those of you who are left shall rot away in your enemies' lands because of their iniquity." Ezekiel used the same word in 24:23. They have finally believed the curse, and now they think it is all that is left. **"How then can we live?"** is a hopeless question, not a real one.
- **"As I live, declares the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (v11).** God swears an oath by his own life. The same truth was stated in 18:23, 32. God is not reluctant to forgive. What he wants is "that the wicked turn from his way and live." Peter says the same of the Lord's patience: he is "not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Pet 3:9).
- **"Turn back, turn back from your evil ways, for why will you die?" (v11).** The doubled command is God pleading. The Hebrew verb is *shuv*, the Old Testament's main word for repentance, literally to turn around and go back. Judgment had come, but the door had not closed. That is the difference between God's judgment and fate.
- **Two case studies (vv12–16).** God works out the principle with two people. A righteous man who "trusts in his righteousness and does injustice" will find that "none of his righteous deeds shall be remembered" (v13). A wicked man who turns from sin and makes things right will find that "none of the sins that he has committed shall be remembered against him" (v16). The past, good or bad, does not lock anyone in. The direction someone takes now is what counts.
- **"If the wicked restores the pledge, gives back what he has taken by robbery" (v15).** Repentance is concrete. The "pledge" was the item a poor borrower left as security for a loan, sometimes his cloak, which the law required to be returned by nightfall (Exod 22:26–27; Deut 24:12–13). Turning means giving back what was taken. **"The statutes of life"** are God's laws, which point the way to life (Lev 18:5).
- **What is this "life" and "death"?** In context Ezekiel is speaking first of physical life and death in the coming judgments, and of a place in the restored people of God. The principle reaches further, since God's desire for repentance is the same for eternal life. Christians have used verses 12–13 on both sides of the debate about whether a true believer can lose salvation. It is wiser not to load that debate onto this text. Ezekiel's point is that no one can rest on a past record, and no one is beyond turning back.
- **"The way of the Lord is not just" (vv17, 20).** The verb behind "just" pictures something weighed and measured correctly. The people accuse God of using crooked scales. God's answer is that it is "their own way that is not just" (v17). This exchange repeats 18:25–29 almost exactly. **"I will judge each of you according to his ways" (v20).** The nation as a whole had been judged, but each person would still answer for his own path.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "When there is a settled separation between a man and sin, there shall no longer be a separation between him and God." On v13 he warns that a man who "trusts to his own righteousness" is easily led into sin. JFB calls v11 "a yearning tenderness" that still upholds God's justice, and ties it to 2 Peter 3:9. On "statutes of life" JFB notes the law's failure to give life was "not the fault of the law, but of man's sinful inability to keep it." Guzik reads v10 as remorse that stops short of repentance, citing Wiersbe. He notes that the passage is about individual responsibility, not a treatment of eternal security, and ties the call to show faith by deeds to James 2:14–17.

#### Jerusalem Struck Down (vv21–33)
- **"In the twelfth year of our exile, in the tenth month, on the fifth day" (v21).** That is about January 585 BC, counting from King Jehoiachin's exile in 597. Jerusalem's wall was breached in the summer of 586 and the city burned a month later (2 Kgs 25:3–9). The news took about five months to travel the 900 miles or so by the usual route along the Euphrates, which fits a refugee on foot. A few Hebrew manuscripts and the Syriac read "eleventh year," which would shorten the gap, but the ESV follows the standard text.
- **"A fugitive from Jerusalem came to me and said, 'The city has been struck down'" (v21).** God had told Ezekiel this man would come (24:26–27). In Hebrew the message is just two words, and they confirm seven years of warning.
- **"He had opened my mouth… and I was no longer mute" (v22).** "The hand of the LORD" in Ezekiel marks an overwhelming experience of God (1:3; 3:14; 37:1). The night before the runner arrived, God restored Ezekiel's speech. His years of enforced silence, broken only by God's messages, were themselves a sign. Now that the city had fallen and the prophet was proven true, he could speak freely, and what he would speak was mostly hope.
- **"Abraham was only one man, yet he got possession of the land; but we are many" (v24).** The first message after the fall is about the people still in Judah, living in "these waste places," the ruins. They reasoned: if God gave the land to one man, surely it belongs to the many of us who are left. It sounds like faith in God's promise. In fact it was a land grab. The leaders and landowners had been deported, and the poor who were left were taking over the empty estates (2 Kgs 25:12; Jer 39:10). Abraham himself never owned the land. He lived in it as a stranger and owned only a grave (Gen 23; Acts 7:5; Heb 11:9).
- **"You eat flesh with the blood… lift up your eyes to your idols and shed blood" (v25).** God answers the claim with their own conduct. Eating blood broke a command older than Moses (Gen 9:4; Lev 17:10–14). Idols and murder are the sins Ezekiel has named all along. **"You rely on the sword… each of you defiles his neighbor's wife" (v26).** "Rely on" is literally "stand upon": they live by violence. The point is the one Jesus and Paul make: the true children of Abraham are those who share his faith and obedience, not simply his bloodline (John 8:39; Rom 4:12).
- **"Shall you then possess the land?" (vv25–26).** The question is asked twice, and the answer is obvious.
- **Sword, beasts and pestilence (v27).** These are the covenant curses of Leviticus 26:22–25, the same trio Ezekiel used in 5:17 and 14:21. Those in the ruins, in the open country and hiding in caves will all be reached. Jeremiah 40–44 tells what happened next: the governor Gedaliah was murdered, the survivors fled to Egypt against God's word, and disaster followed them there.
- **"The mountains of Israel shall be so desolate that none will pass through" (v28).** The "mountains of Israel" will be addressed again in chapter 36, where God promises to make the same mountains fruitful. The desolation is not the last word.
- **"Your people who talk together about you by the walls and at the doors" (v30).** Now the exiles. Ezekiel had become the talk of the settlement. Since his warnings had come true, people were eager to hear him: **"Come, and hear what the word is that comes from the LORD."**
- **"They hear what you say but they will not do it" (vv31–32).** This is said twice. **"Their heart is set on their gain"** shows what really ruled them. Twice the Hebrew uses *agabim*, a word for sensual love, which the ESV renders "lustful talk" and "lustful songs." The lexicon gives "love-song" for v32 and notes that many scholars, following some ancient versions, read a different word in v31 ("lies"). Either way the picture is clear: they treated the prophet like a popular singer with "a beautiful voice" who "plays well on an instrument." They enjoyed him. They did not obey him.
- **"When this comes—and come it will!—then they will know that a prophet has been among them" (v33).** The fall of Jerusalem had already proven Ezekiel right once. The coming judgment, and the coming restoration, would prove it again. James puts the same warning to Christian hearers: "be doers of the word, and not hearers only" (Jas 1:22).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the survivors "call this groundless presumption strong faith," and that "those are unteachable indeed, who do not learn their dependence upon God, when all creature-comforts fail." On v31 he observes that "many come to find somewhat to oppose: far more come of curiosity or mere habit." JFB reads "stand upon your sword" as trust "not on right and equity, but on force and arms," and calls v32 "a lover's song": they "praise thy eloquence, but care not for the subject of it." JFB dates the fall to the eleventh year and so counts a year and a half for the news to arrive. Most modern scholars count Ezekiel's years differently, from the spring, and reach about five months, as the Tyndale notes do. Guzik, citing Block and Taylor, says the exiles treated Ezekiel like a celebrity performer. He draws on G. Campbell Morgan's contrast between hearing that only enjoys and hearing that obeys, and warns against treating preaching as entertainment.

#### Pointing to Christ
The watchman who must warn, the God who swears he takes no pleasure in death, and the people who hear but will not do, all come together in Jesus. He wept over Jerusalem because it did not know the time of its visitation (Luke 19:41–44). He ended the Sermon on the Mount with a man who hears and does and a man who only hears (Matt 7:24–27). And the "why will you die?" of verse 11 became, at the cross, God's own answer: he did not merely take no pleasure in the death of the wicked. He gave his Son so that the wicked could turn and live.
