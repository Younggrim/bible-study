*Setting:* Psalm 137 is the one psalm that can be dated with some confidence. It was written by someone who had lived through the fall of Jerusalem and been carried into exile in Babylon. In 586 BC the Babylonian army under King Nebuchadnezzar broke through Jerusalem's walls after a long siege, burned the temple, tore down the walls and marched most of the survivors some 900 miles to Babylonia, in modern Iraq (2 Kings 25:1–21; Jer 52). The psalm looks back on the early years there ("we sat down and wept"), and may have been written during the exile or soon after the first exiles returned in 538 BC. Matthew Henry places it toward the end of the captivity, when the exiles "saw the destruction of Babylon hastening on apace." It has no title and no author's name. It moves from grief, to a vow of loyalty, to a fierce cry for justice. Its last verse is among the hardest lines in the Bible to read, and it is better faced honestly than skipped. In the Psalter it comes right after Psalm 136, with its refrain that God "remembered us in our low estate" (136:23). Psalm 137 shows what that low estate felt like. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

#### By the Waters of Babylon (vv1–3)
- **"By the waters of Babylon" (v1).** Literally "by the rivers of Babylon." JFB notes that "Babylon" here stands for the whole country. Babylonia lay between the Tigris and Euphrates Rivers, and it was crossed by a network of irrigation canals. Ezekiel lived among the exiles "by the Chebar canal" (Ezek 1:1, 3). Exiles may have gathered by the water because it was a quiet place outside the towns, or because Jewish prayer gatherings later met near rivers (compare Acts 16:13). Judah was a land of hills and seasonal streams. Babylonia was flat, wide and full of water. The landscape itself said: this is not home.
- **"There we sat down and wept, when we remembered Zion" (v1).** Sitting was the posture of mourning (Job 2:13; Neh 1:4; Lam 2:10). "Zion" is the hill of the temple in Jerusalem and often stands for the whole city. What they remembered was not only buildings. It was the place where God had put his name, where the festivals were kept and where the Songs of Ascents had been sung on the way up. Now it lay in ruins (Lam 1:1–4).
- **"On the willows there we hung up our lyres" (v2).** The "willows" (*'aravim*) were probably the Euphrates poplar, a tree that grows thick along Babylonian riverbanks. The "lyre" (*kinnor*) was a small stringed instrument, the kind David played (1 Sam 16:23). The temple musicians played lyres in worship (1 Chron 25:1). Hanging them up meant laying music aside. They did not break them. Many think that suggests they hoped to play them again one day. The study notes suggest that the trees may also hint at the curse of exile, because Deuteronomy speaks of one "hanged on a tree" as under God's curse (Deut 21:23). That is a possible echo, not a certain one.
- **"For there our captors required of us songs, and our tormentors, mirth" (v3).** "Tormentors" (*tolalenu*) is a rare word, probably meaning "those who plundered us" or "those who mocked us." The Babylonians wanted entertainment. Assyrian carvings from this period show captive musicians playing lyres for their conquerors, so the request fits what is known of the time. Kidner, as Guzik reports, points to one such relief.
- **"Sing us one of the songs of Zion!" (v3).** The songs of Zion praised the God who lived in Jerusalem and defended it: "God is in the midst of her; she shall not be moved" (Ps 46:5); "Great is the LORD... in the city of our God" (Ps 48:1). To ask the exiles to sing those songs among the ruins of their faith was cruel mockery. It meant: where is your God now? (Ps 42:3, 10).
- **The sound of grief.** In Hebrew, vv1–3 repeat the ending *-nu* ("we," "us," "our") nine times: we sat, we wept, we remembered, we hung, our lyres, they asked us, our captors, our tormentors, for us. Boice, as Guzik reports, hears it as a mournful sound running through the lines. The count holds in the Hebrew.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the captors' request came from curiosity or mockery, and that the exiles' answer shows compliance "was incongruous with their mournful feelings." He adds that the willows "may have grown there then, if not now." Henry outlines the stanza as the exiles' mournful posture and their enemies' abuse. Guzik notes, with Boice, that they hung up their lyres instead of destroying them, as if saving them for better days. He also lists the many losses behind their tears: family, home, the temple, their past and their own sin, which had brought the exile on them.

#### How Shall We Sing the LORD's Song? (vv4–6)
- **"How shall we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land?" (v4).** The question is not only "how can we bear to?" but "how is it right to?" The songs of Zion were worship, not performance. To sing them as party music for those who had destroyed the temple would turn praise into a show for scoffers. Babylon was also a "foreign land" in a deeper sense. The land of Israel was the LORD's land (Lev 25:23), and exile meant being sent away from his presence (Ps 42:4; Hos 9:3–4). Yet the exiles did learn to worship there. Daniel prayed three times a day toward Jerusalem (Dan 6:10), Ezekiel saw God's glory by a Babylonian canal (Ezek 1), and Jeremiah told the exiles to seek the welfare of the city where they lived and pray to the LORD on its behalf (Jer 29:7). Some of the psalms themselves were probably gathered and edited in exile.
- **"If I forget you, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget its skill!" (v5).** The voice changes from "we" to "I." Each singer takes a personal oath. The right hand plucked the strings of the lyre. The Hebrew says only "let my right hand forget," without saying what it forgets, and Guzik notes Poole's point that this leaves the curse open-ended. Some scholars suggest a slight change in the Hebrew to read "let my right hand wither." The ESV supplies "its skill," which fits the musical context.
- **"Let my tongue stick to the roof of my mouth" (v6).** The tongue sang the songs. A musician who forgot Jerusalem should lose the use of both hand and voice. JFB ties the two curses to playing and singing. Ezekiel uses the same picture of a tongue cleaving to the mouth for being struck dumb (Ezek 3:26).
- **"If I do not set Jerusalem above my highest joy!" (v6).** Literally, "if I do not raise Jerusalem above the head of my joy." Jerusalem would be the first and greatest delight, above everything else. The study notes explain why: Jerusalem was the center of God's reign and the place of fellowship with him. To love Jerusalem was to love God's presence and his promises. Jewish custom has kept this vow alive. At weddings a glass is broken to recall Jerusalem's destruction even at the height of joy, and Guzik passes on a report, through Trapp, of an old custom of leaving part of a house unfinished as a reminder.
- **Commentators:** JFB says that joyful singing in Babylon "would imply forgetfulness of their desolated homes and fallen Church." Henry calls this the exiles' "patient restraint" under provocation and their "constant love for Jerusalem." Guzik passes on Morgan's point that singing for their captors would have betrayed both their faith and their lost city, and VanGemeren's point that the vow was a pledge never to forget God's covenant, temple and promises.

#### Remember, O LORD, Against the Edomites (vv7–9)
- **"Remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem" (v7).** The Edomites were descendants of Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Gen 36:1). They lived south and east of the Dead Sea. Israel and Edom were "brothers" (Deut 23:7; Amos 1:11), and that made their betrayal worse. When Babylon took Jerusalem, Edom cheered, joined in the looting and caught fugitives to hand them over. Obadiah condemns it: "On the day that you stood aloof... you were like one of them... do not stand at the crossroads to cut off his fugitives" (Obad 11, 14). Lamentations, Ezekiel and Joel say the same (Lam 4:21–22; Ezek 25:12–14; 35:1–15; Joel 3:19). "The day of Jerusalem" is the day it fell. "Remember against" is a prayer for God to act in justice. JFB says it means "to punish."
- **"Lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations!" (v7).** The Hebrew verb *'aru* means "strip," "make naked." The Edomites urged the Babylonians to tear the city down to bare rock. A people who should have been brothers cheered for total destruction. Psalm 133 sang of brothers dwelling in unity. Here a brother nation gloats over its brother's ruin.
- **"O daughter of Babylon, doomed to be destroyed" (v8).** "Daughter of Babylon" is a poetic name for the city and its people (Isa 47:1; Jer 50:42). JFB identifies it as the people. "Doomed to be destroyed" (*hashedudah*) is literally "the devastated one," as if her ruin were already certain. Isaiah and Jeremiah had foretold it (Isa 13; 47; Jer 50–51). The psalmist speaks to Babylon directly, as though its fate were settled. Guzik suggests this is because Babylon's fall was so certain it needed only to be announced. Babylon fell to the Persians under Cyrus in 539 BC, and later decline left it a ruin.
- **"Blessed shall he be who repays you with what you have done to us!" (v8).** "Blessed" (*'ashre*) is the same word that opens Psalm 1, "Blessed is the man." It means "happy" or "fortunate." The prayer is for exact justice, the punishment fitting the crime. Jeremiah had prophesied the same thing: "Repay her according to her deeds; do to her according to all that she has done" (Jer 50:29; 51:24, 56). Revelation picks up the words against the final "Babylon": "Pay her back as she herself has paid back others" (Rev 18:6).
- **"Blessed shall he be who takes your little ones and dashes them against the rock!" (v9).** This is the line that shocks every reader. The study notes simply call the prayer for vengeance shocking. It helps to see what it is and what it is not. Killing children was a known horror of ancient war, and the Babylonians had done it in Jerusalem (Lam 2:20–21; 2 Kings 25:7 for Zedekiah's sons). Isaiah had already foretold that Babylon's "infants will be dashed in pieces before their eyes" when the Medes came against it (Isa 13:16). The psalmist is not describing what he will do. He hands his rage to God and asks God to repay in kind, in line with what the prophets had said. The psalm shows honest grief and anger brought into prayer instead of acted out.
- **How Christians read v9.** Faithful readers have taken different paths. Some, like Augustine, read the "little ones" as the first stirrings of sin, to be dashed against the rock, Christ, before they grow. That is a spiritual application, not the plain sense. Many today read it as an honest prayer that God permits his people to pray, without endorsing every feeling in it. They note that it leaves vengeance to God (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19). Others stress that it is a cry for God's justice against an empire that had done these very things, a justice the prophets had promised. All agree that Jesus calls his followers to love their enemies and pray for those who persecute them (Matt 5:44), and that Christians pray the psalm in the light of the cross.
- **Commentators:** Henry insists that the psalmists' joy in an enemy's ruin is "not from a spirit of revenge, but from a holy zeal for the glory of God," adding, "Far be it from us to avenge ourselves." He says "the destroyers shall be destroyed," and "let not those expect to find mercy who, when they had power, did not show mercy." JFB reads the verses as a prophecy resting on "God's righteous judgment, and not the passions of the chafed Israelites." Guzik passes on Kidner's point that the slaughter of children was a grim, common sequel to ancient conquest, and he notes Isaiah 13:16. He agrees with Boice that believers can sympathize with the psalmist while being called, under the New Testament, to pray for their enemies' good. Guzik also observes that both Edom and Babylon did come to ruin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 137 gives voice to exiles with nowhere to sing, and to wounds too deep for polite words. It does not hide the pain or the anger. It brings both to God and leaves the justice with him. Jesus entered that exile. He wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41). He was mocked by his captors, who demanded a show: "Prophesy! Who is it that struck you?" (Luke 22:64), and "Let him come down now from the cross" (Matt 27:42). He set Jerusalem above his highest joy, setting his face to go there knowing it would kill him (Luke 9:51). And where the psalm prays for judgment on the oppressor, Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). He did not cancel God's justice. He bore it. Evil will be repaid. The fall of the last "Babylon" is still to come (Rev 18), and God's people are told to rejoice because "God has given judgment for you against her" (Rev 18:20). But for any who turn to him, the judgment has already fallen on Christ. The exiles who could not sing will one day sing a new song in the new Jerusalem, where God will "wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Rev 21:2–4).
