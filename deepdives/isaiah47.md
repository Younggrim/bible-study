*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapter 46 showed Babylon's gods carried off like baggage. Chapter 47 turns from the gods to the city itself, pictured as a pampered queen dragged off her throne and set to work as a slave. It is a taunt song, a form Isaiah used against Babylon before (14:4–23). Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar (605–562 BC) was the greatest city of its world, with huge double walls, the Ishtar Gate, palaces and temples. Its priests and scholars were famous for astronomy, astrology and magic, which they used to read the future. That is where this chapter strikes. Babylon said, "I am, and there is no one besides me," and that claim belongs only to God. The city fell to Cyrus in 539 BC, and Revelation borrows this chapter's words for the last "Babylon" (Rev 18).

#### The Humiliation of Babylon (vv1–15)
- **"Come down and sit in the dust, O virgin daughter of Babylon" (v1).** "Daughter of Babylon" is a poetic way of naming the city as a woman. "Virgin" means never conquered. Babylon had not been taken by a foreign army in living memory. Sitting in the dust was the posture of mourning and disgrace (3:26; Job 2:8, 13). Babylon is called "tender and delicate," the language of a spoiled noblewoman who has never had to work (Deut 28:56).
- **"Sit on the ground without a throne, O daughter of the Chaldeans!" (v1).** The Chaldeans were the people from southern Mesopotamia whose dynasty ruled the Neo-Babylonian empire. The queen loses her throne and sits on the ground.
- **"Take the millstones and grind flour" (v2).** Grinding grain with a hand mill was the lowest daily drudgery, done by slave women and prisoners (Exod 11:5; Judg 16:21; Matt 24:41). Two flat stones were rubbed together for hours each day.
- **"Put off your veil, strip off your robe, uncover your legs, pass through the rivers" (v2).** A noblewoman's veil marked her rank. A slave woman went bare-headed and hitched up her skirt to wade rivers on the road into exile. This is exactly what Babylon did to the people it deported, including Judah. Now it happens to Babylon.
- **"Your nakedness shall be uncovered, and your disgrace shall be seen. I will take vengeance" (v3).** Being stripped was a common image for a conquered city's shame (Nah 3:5). "Vengeance" here is not personal spite. It is the just Judge settling accounts for what was done to his people (34:8; Rom 12:19). "I will spare no one" is literally "I will not meet a man." It may mean no one will be allowed to plead for her, or that God will make no truce.
- **"Our Redeemer—the LORD of hosts is his name—is the Holy One of Israel" (v4).** In the middle of the taunt a voice breaks in, the voice of God's people. The one who humbles Babylon is Israel's *go'el*, the next of kin who rescues and avenges family (41:14). Babylon's fall is Israel's freedom.
- **"Sit in silence, and go into darkness… you shall no more be called the mistress of kingdoms" (v5).** "Mistress" means lady or queen. Babylon ruled an empire of subject kingdoms. Now she is silent and in the dark, like a prisoner in a cell (42:7).
- **"I was angry with my people; I profaned my heritage; I gave them into your hand" (v6).** God is honest about why Babylon had power over Judah. It was his discipline for his people's sin (42:24–25). Babylon was a tool.
- **"You showed them no mercy; on the aged you made your yoke exceedingly heavy" (v6).** This is the charge. Babylon was allowed to punish, but it went beyond what God sent it to do, with cruelty even to the old. Lamentations describes the elders sitting on the ground in silence and the old men gone from the gate (Lam 2:10; 5:12). Zechariah gives the principle: "I was angry but a little, and they furthered the disaster" (Zech 1:15). Being God's instrument does not excuse the instrument's own cruelty (10:5–15).
- **"You said, 'I shall be mistress forever,' so that you did not lay these things to heart or remember their end" (v7).** Babylon never thought about where her cruelty would lead. Pride makes people forget that every empire ends (Dan 4:30–32).
- **"You lover of pleasures, who sit securely" (v8).** Babylon was famous for wealth and luxury. "Sit securely" means feeling safe, with no thought of danger. Babylon's walls were thought impregnable.
- **"Who say in your heart, 'I am, and there is no one besides me'" (v8).** This is God's own claim, made again and again in these chapters (45:5, 6, 18, 21; 46:9), now on the lips of a city. It is the root of Babylon's sin. It is the same pride as the king of Babylon in 14:13–14: "I will make myself like the Most High." Babylon puts itself in God's place.
- **"I shall not sit as a widow or know the loss of children" (v8).** In the ancient world a widow who had lost her children had lost her protection, her income and her future. A city that loses its king and its people is pictured as that kind of widow (Lam 1:1). Babylon thought it could never happen.
- **"These two things shall come to you in a moment, in one day" (v9).** Babylon fell in a single night. Daniel 5 tells of Belshazzar's feast, the hand writing on the wall, and "that very night Belshazzar the Chaldean king was killed" (Dan 5:30). Revelation 18:7–8 quotes this verse almost word for word about the final "Babylon": "I sit as a queen, I am no widow… her plagues will come in a single day."
- **"In spite of your many sorceries and the great power of your enchantments" (v9).** Babylon's religion was full of spells, incantations and rituals to ward off demons and disaster. Clay tablets of these spells survive in large numbers. None of them would hold off this disaster.
- **"You felt secure in your wickedness; you said, 'No one sees me'" (v10).** This is the thought of every sinner who thinks no one is watching (Ps 10:11; 94:7). God sees.
- **"Your wisdom and your knowledge led you astray" (v10).** Babylon's scholars were among the most learned people in the world. They kept astronomical records for centuries and studied mathematics and medicine. But knowledge cut off from God became pride and led them astray (1 Cor 1:20). "I am, and there is no one besides me" is repeated, like a refrain of self-worship.
- **"Evil shall come upon you, which you will not know how to charm away" (v11).** "Charm away" probably translates a word linked with magic. Some read it as "you will not know its dawn," meaning it will come without warning. Either way, Babylon's experts in warding off evil will not see it coming and cannot stop it.
- **"Disaster shall fall upon you, for which you will not be able to atone" (v11).** "Atone" is *kapper*, the word for covering sin by sacrifice or payment. Babylon's priests offered rituals to buy off angry gods. This disaster cannot be bought off.
- **"Stand fast in your enchantments… perhaps you may be able to succeed" (v12).** This is sarcasm. "Go on, try your spells. Maybe they'll work this time." Elijah mocked Baal's prophets the same way (1 Kgs 18:27). "With which you have labored from your youth" means Babylon had worked at magic all its history.
- **"You are wearied with your many counsels" (v13).** All the advice and forecasts have only worn Babylon out, like the weary animals of 46:1.
- **"Those who divide the heavens, who gaze at the stars, who at the new moons make known what shall come upon you" (v13).** "Divide the heavens" refers to astrologers mapping the sky into sections and signs. Babylon kept long series of omen texts that tied events in the sky, like eclipses and the positions of planets, to events on earth. "New moons" were the monthly forecasts. Daniel shows these same experts helpless before Nebuchadnezzar's dream and Belshazzar's writing on the wall (Dan 2:10–11; 5:7–8). The Bible forbids such practices to God's people (Deut 18:10–12).
- **"Behold, they are like stubble; the fire consumes them" (v14).** The experts cannot save Babylon. They cannot even save themselves.
- **"No coal for warming oneself is this, no fire to sit before!" (v14).** This picks up 44:16, where the idol-maker warmed himself by the fire and said, "Aha, I am warm." The fire of God's judgment is not a cozy fire to warm one's hands at. It is a fire that consumes (Heb 12:29).
- **"Those with whom you have labored, who have done business with you from your youth" (v15).** These may be the astrologers, or Babylon's merchant partners. In Revelation 18:11–19 the merchants who grew rich from Babylon stand far off and weep. Either way they scatter "each in his own direction," and the chapter's last words fall heavily: **"there is no one to save you."** This is the answer to God's "besides me there is no savior" (43:11).
- **Commentators:** Henry compares the chapter to Jonah's warning to Nineveh, a "fair warning" that might still have led to repentance. He notes that God "can tell men what they say there, though they never speak it out," since Babylon spoke its pride "in its heart." JFB explains the hand-mill as "the office of a female slave in the East; most degrading." JFB and Guzik both read v6 as Babylon going beyond its commission by showing no mercy, and JFB links Zechariah 1:15. On v9, JFB says the city was taken "in a single night" by Cyrus. He also says it was "literally fulfilled" again when Babylon later revolted against Darius and was retaken. JFB explains the astrologers' sky charts in detail. Guzik draws the lesson of v14: God's judgment is not a fire to be comfortable beside, and he cites James 4:6, "God opposes the proud." Guzik also mentions a commentator who applies the chapter to a worldly church. That is an application, not what the text says.

#### Pointing to Christ
Babylon said in her heart, "I am, and there is no one besides me," and lost everything. Jesus, who had every right to say "I am" (John 8:58), "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" (Phil 2:6–7). Babylon was made a slave against her will. Christ became a servant by choice and was stripped and shamed so that others might be clothed. The spells of Babylon could not atone, and its fire gave no warmth. The one who could atone did, once for all (Heb 9:26). Where Babylon's last word was "no one to save you," the gospel's is that God "is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him" (Heb 7:25).
