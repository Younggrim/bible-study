*Setting:* The dedication of Solomon's temple in Jerusalem, about 959 BC, during the great autumn festival of the seventh month. In chapter 6 Solomon knelt on a bronze platform before the whole nation and prayed that God would hear every prayer made toward this house, and he closed by asking God to arise to his resting place and not turn away the face of his anointed. Chapter 7 gives God's answer twice: first in fire and glory before everyone, then in words spoken to Solomon alone at night. The chapter follows 1 Kings 8:62–9:9, but Chronicles adds the fire from heaven, the Levites' music and, above all, the promise of 7:13–15, which has no parallel in Kings. The first readers had come home from exile to a rebuilt temple. For them God's answer to Solomon was also the explanation of their own history and the way back to God.

#### Fire from Heaven (vv1–3)
- **"As soon as Solomon finished his prayer, fire came down from heaven" (v1).** Only Chronicles records this. Kings moves straight from the prayer to Solomon's blessing and the sacrifices (1 Kgs 8:54–62). The fire is God's visible "yes." Fire from the LORD had done the same at the first offerings in the tabernacle (Lev 9:24) and when David offered on this very threshing floor (1 Chr 21:26). Later it would fall on Elijah's altar on Carmel (1 Kgs 18:38). Each time it marks a place and a sacrifice God accepts.
- **"And the glory of the LORD filled the temple" (v1).** This is the second time. The cloud of glory filled the house when the ark was brought in (5:13–14), and now it fills it again at the end of the prayer. The Chronicler frames Solomon's whole prayer between two appearances of God's glory, so his readers would see that God really did come to live among his people, as he had filled the tabernacle in the wilderness (Exod 40:34–35).
- **"The priests could not enter the house of the LORD" (v2).** Earlier the priests could not stand to minister inside (5:14). Now they cannot even enter. The house belongs to God before it belongs to anyone who serves in it.
- **"They bowed down with their faces to the ground on the pavement" (v3).** The people were outside in the courts and could see the fire and the glory "on the temple." Their response is full prostration, the deepest posture of worship in the ancient Near East.
- **"For he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever" (v3).** This is the same refrain the singers sang when the cloud first came (5:13), and it echoes through the Psalms (Pss 106:1; 118:1; 136, where it ends all twenty-six verses). "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. Faced with consuming fire, the people do not run. They give thanks, because the fire has fallen on the sacrifice and not on them.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the fire "a token of the divine acceptance of Solomon's prayer," and says the priests "were unable for some time to perform their usual functions." Guzik notes that the people answer God's power not with terror but with praise for his goodness and mercy. He also passes on an old Jewish tradition, reported by the Puritan John Trapp, that this fire was kept burning until the Babylonian exile. That is a tradition, not something the text says, though Leviticus 6:13 did command that the altar fire never go out.

#### The Dedication of the Temple (vv4–10)
- **"22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep" (v5).** The numbers match 1 Kings 8:63. They are enormous, and some readers have wondered whether they are possible. Most of these were peace offerings, in which the fat was burned and the meat eaten by the worshipers. So this was also a national feast that fed a huge crowd for two weeks. The scale shows a king and people giving lavishly for the house of God.
- **"So the king and all the people dedicated the house of God" (v5).** "Dedicated" is *chanak*, to inaugurate or put into use. The related noun *chanukkah* ("dedication") is used for this altar in v9, and much later gave its name to the Jewish feast of Hanukkah, which remembers the temple's rededication in 164 BC after it was defiled. Jesus was in the temple at that feast (John 10:22).
- **The priests and the Levites (v6).** Chronicles adds what Kings does not mention: priests at their posts, Levites playing the instruments David had made, trumpets sounding, and the refrain "for his steadfast love endures forever" repeated again. The Chronicler loved the Levites' ministry of music, which David had organized (1 Chr 15–16; 25). His readers still had Levitical singers in the second temple (Ezra 3:10–11), and he shows them that their worship stood in an unbroken line from David.
- **"Solomon consecrated the middle of the court" (v7).** The great bronze altar, about 30 feet square (4:1), was still too small. So Solomon set apart part of the courtyard pavement as extra altar space for the day. Need governed practice here, but the space was first "consecrated," made holy for God's use.
- **"From Lebo-hamath to the Brook of Egypt" (v8).** Lebo-hamath ("the entrance to Hamath") lay far to the north in what is now Lebanon or Syria. The Brook of Egypt is usually identified with the Wadi el-Arish in the northern Sinai. These were the ideal borders of the promised land (Num 34:5, 8). For once, the whole land God promised to the patriarchs was gathered in one place to worship.
- **Seven days, seven days and a solemn assembly (vv8–10).** The dates fit together. The dedication of the altar ran seven days (the 8th to the 14th of the seventh month). Then came the Feast of Booths, the 15th to the 21st (Lev 23:34). Booths ended with a closing "solemn assembly" on the eighth day (*ʿatseret*, the 22nd; Lev 23:36), and the people went home on the 23rd. 1 Kings 8:65–66 is briefer and sounds as if the people left on the eighth day. Chronicles gives the fuller calendar, so there is no real contradiction. The Day of Atonement fell on the 10th of that month (Lev 23:27) and so came during the dedication week. Neither account mentions it.
- **"Joyful and glad of heart for the prosperity that the LORD had granted to David and to Solomon and to Israel" (v10).** Kings mentions only David here (1 Kgs 8:66). Chronicles adds Solomon. The Chronicler presents David and Solomon together as one founding reign: David prepared the temple and Solomon built it. "Prosperity" is literally "the good" (*tovah*), the same root as "he is good" (v3).
- **Commentators:** Guzik finds the number of sacrifices staggering and makes the point that all the music and praise could not replace the shedding of blood. Sacrifice stood at the center of the day. JFB explains the courtyard as "a temporary place for additional altars," and says the dedication came "immediately previous to the feast of tabernacles," with the 23rd as the day the feast's gathering ended. Henry says the people went home with hearts full of God's goodness, and that those who begin with God's service are likely to prosper in their own affairs.

#### If My People Pray (vv11–22)
- **"Thus Solomon finished the house of the LORD and the king's house" (v11).** This verse skips ahead in time. Kings explains that the temple took seven years and the palace thirteen, twenty years in all (1 Kgs 6:38–7:1; 9:10). So the next appearance of God came years after the dedication, when all Solomon's building was done. "Successfully accomplished" is the verb *tsalach*, to prosper or succeed, a favorite word of the Chronicler for what follows seeking God (1 Chr 22:11, 13; 2 Chr 26:5; 31:21).
- **"Then the LORD appeared to Solomon in the night" (v12).** This is the second time God appeared to Solomon. The first was at Gibeon at the start of his reign, when he asked for wisdom (1:7–12; 1 Kgs 3:5; 9:2). Both came at night. This one comes at the height of Solomon's success, when everything he planned had been done, and it is mostly warning.
- **"I have heard your prayer and have chosen this place for myself as a house of sacrifice" (v12).** "Chosen" answers Solomon's speech about God choosing Jerusalem and David (6:5–6). "A house of sacrifice" appears only here. Solomon had stressed the temple as a house of prayer, and God adds that it is first the place where sacrifice is made. Prayer is heard because of the sacrifice.
- **"When I shut up the heavens so that there is no rain, or command the locust… or send pestilence" (v13).** Verses 13–15 are found only in Chronicles. God picks up the very disasters Solomon listed (6:26–28) and says plainly that he is the one who sends them. Drought, locusts and plague were among the covenant curses for disobedience (Deut 28:21–24, 38). In this passage they are discipline, sent to bring the people back.
- **"If my people who are called by my name" (v14).** The promise is spoken to Israel, the covenant people who bore God's name (Deut 28:10). It is not addressed to any nation that happens to need help. Christians rightly apply it to God's people today, the church called by Christ's name, but its first concern is the faithfulness of God's own people, not the politics of a country.
- **Four steps, one turning (v14).** "Humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways." The Hebrew *kanaʿ*, "humble oneself," is a key word in Chronicles. It shows up again and again in the stories that follow: Rehoboam and the princes humble themselves and are spared (12:6–7, 12), Hezekiah humbles himself (32:26), wicked Manasseh humbles himself in exile and is restored (33:12, 19), Josiah humbles himself (34:27), and the last king, Zedekiah, "did not humble himself" (36:12). "Seek" (*baqash*, and its partner *darash*) is the Chronicler's other great word for true faith. "Turn" is *shuv*, repent. These are not four separate tasks but one movement of the heart back to God.
- **"Then I will hear from heaven and will forgive their sin and heal their land" (v14).** These are three promises to match the four conditions. "Heal" is *rapha*, the root of "the LORD your healer" (Exod 15:26). Here it means restoring a land struck by drought, locusts and plague. But forgiveness comes before healing. As Henry puts it, "Pardoning mercy makes ways for healing mercy."
- **"Now my eyes will be open and my ears attentive" (v15).** God answers Solomon's closing words almost word for word (6:40). Solomon asked, and God says yes.
- **"My eyes and my heart will be there for all time" (v16).** "My name may be there forever" means God's presence and ownership. "My heart" adds affection. God does not just tolerate this house. He loves the people who meet him there.
- **"If you will walk before me as David your father walked" (v17).** Now God speaks to Solomon personally ("you" is singular in vv17–18). David was not sinless, but he was wholehearted toward God and repented when confronted (1 Kgs 15:5). That was the standard Solomon was called to.
- **"You shall not lack a man to rule Israel" (v18).** Kings has "a man on the throne of Israel" (1 Kgs 9:5). Chronicles uses "to rule," *moshel*, the word in Micah's promise of a coming "ruler in Israel" from Bethlehem (Mic 5:2). For readers with no king on David's throne, the change kept their eyes on a future ruler from David's line. Most scholars read it as part of the Chronicler's hope for the Messiah.
- **"But if you turn aside… I will pluck you up from my land" (vv19–20).** The "you" turns plural. The warning is for the whole people. "Pluck up" is the language of uprooting a plant (Deut 29:28; Jer 12:14–17). Henry pictures God pulling them out like weeds from a garden.
- **"I will make it a proverb and a byword among all peoples" (v20).** A "proverb" (*mashal*) and a "byword" (*shenina*, a sharp, mocking saying) are what nations say about someone whose fall is notorious. The same two words appear in the covenant curses (Deut 28:37). The house God has just filled with his glory could become a joke.
- **"Why has the LORD done thus to this land and to this house?" (v21).** Moses had foretold this very question (Deut 29:24–28). And it happened. Babylon burned the temple in 586 BC (36:19), and Jeremiah and Lamentations record the mockery (Jer 22:8–9; Lam 2:15–16). The Chronicler's readers knew how the story ended. The answer is not that God failed but that "they abandoned the LORD" (v22).
- **"The God of their fathers who brought them out of the land of Egypt" (v22).** Chronicles often leaves out the Exodus (it dropped it in 6:11), so the mention here stands out. To abandon God was to forget the one who freed them.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that humbling, praying and seeking God's face are essential, "and yet all this will not do unless they turn from their wicked ways." Guzik, following the scholar Martin Selman, treats the four verbs as one attitude and traces them through later kings who humbled themselves, prayed and sought God. He holds that the promise was first for Israel in its land, but that the same God still answers his humble, repentant people. He also warns that so glorious a building could become an object of trust in itself. On the warning, Henry: the lofty house "shall be an astonishment, it shall come down wonderfully." JFB gives no separate notes on vv13–22, only a short introduction calling the dedication "an occasion of intense national interest." The Tyndale notes connect "a man to rule Israel" with the promised ruler of Micah 5:2.

#### Pointing to Christ
Fire fell on the sacrifice, and the people who saw it sang that God is good. In Jesus that picture becomes real. God's judgment fell on the one sacrifice, not on the people, and God's glory came to dwell in a living temple (John 1:14; 2:19–21). The house that would become a byword was torn down, but the promise of "a man to rule Israel" did not fail. The Son of David now rules, and through him the way of 7:14 stays open: God's people who humble themselves, pray, seek his face and turn find that he hears from heaven, forgives and heals (1 John 1:9).
