*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, right after the Day of Atonement law (Lev 16). Chapter 17 opens a new part of Leviticus. Many scholars call chapters 17–26 the "Holiness Code," because the call "be holy, for I am holy" runs through them. Chapters 1–16 dealt mostly with the sanctuary, priests and purity. From here the focus widens to how the whole people live day to day. Chapter 17 is a bridge: it is still about sacrifice and blood, but it is addressed to "Aaron and his sons and to all the people of Israel" (v2), and it reaches into every household that killed an animal.

#### The Place of Sacrifice (vv1–9)
- **"Kills an ox or a lamb or a goat in the camp, or… outside the camp" (v3).** While Israel lived in the wilderness, any ox, sheep or goat killed for food had to be brought to the tabernacle and offered as a peace offering. Meat from domestic animals was normally eaten only on special occasions, so this was workable in a camp gathered around the tent. Ordinary people shared in the meat of peace offerings (7:11–21), so the rule did not take meat off the table. It turned every meal of meat into worship.
- **"Bloodguilt shall be imputed to that man. He has shed blood" (v4).** Strong words. Killing an animal outside God's appointed place is treated almost like murder. Life belongs to God, and taking it without returning its blood to him at the altar is a kind of theft of life. **"Cut off from among his people"** means removed from the covenant community, whether by God's hand or by the community. Scholars debate exactly how it was carried out.
- **"Their sacrifices that they sacrifice in the open field" (v5).** Some Israelites were already killing animals in the countryside and offering them on their own. The law brings these sacrifices back **"to the priest at the entrance of the tent of meeting,"** where the blood goes on the altar and the fat is burned **"for a pleasing aroma to the LORD"** (v6).
- **"So they shall no more sacrifice their sacrifices to goat demons, after whom they whore" (v7).** Here is the real reason. "Goat demons" is *se'irim*, the plural of the ordinary word for a male goat (as in 16:5), literally "hairy ones." BDB also lists a meaning "satyr, demon," pictured in goat form. Isaiah pictures them haunting ruined places (Isa 13:21; 34:14), and King Jeroboam later set up priests "for the goat idols" (2 Chr 11:15). **"After whom they whore"** uses the language of sexual unfaithfulness, the prophets' regular picture of idolatry (Hos 1:2). Israel was God's bride, and sacrifices to other powers were adultery. Note "no more": this was already happening. Israel brought habits with them out of Egypt (Josh 24:14; Ezek 20:7).
- **One altar, one God.** Centralizing sacrifice protected worship from drifting into the local cults. Paul says much the same about pagan sacrifice in his day: "what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God" (1 Cor 10:20).
- **"Any one of the house of Israel, or of the strangers who sojourn among them" (vv8–9).** The rule covered foreigners living in Israel too. Burnt offerings and other sacrifices had to come to the one altar. This phrase, Israel "or the stranger," runs through the chapter (vv8, 10, 12, 13, 15). Life in God's camp meant living by God's rules, whoever you were.
- **What happened in the land?** Once Israel spread across Canaan, bringing every animal to one sanctuary was impossible. Deuteronomy 12:15, 20–24 adjusts the rule: in the land, animals could be killed for food at home, as long as the blood was poured out on the ground. Sacrifices, though, still had to come to "the place that the LORD your God will choose" (Deut 12:5–14).
- **Commentators:** JFB connects the rule to Israel's "strong addictedness" to idolatry after leaving Egypt and reads the "goats" as goat-shaped deities like the Greek god Pan, whose worship was licentious. The Tyndale notes add that the male goat symbolized fertility and the underworld in the ancient world. Guzik notes that pagans sacrificed wherever they liked, on hills and in groves, and draws a lesson against modern do-it-yourself religion that sets its own terms for approaching God. He ties v7 to 1 Corinthians 10:20–21.

#### Laws Against Eating Blood (vv10–16)
- **"I will set my face against that person who eats blood" (v10).** This is one of the strongest warnings in Leviticus. God himself becomes the opponent of the one who eats blood. The ban is old. God gave it to Noah long before Moses: "You shall not eat flesh with its life, that is, its blood" (Gen 9:4). Leviticus has already repeated it (3:17; 7:26–27), and here it gets its fullest explanation.
- **"For the life of the flesh is in the blood" (v11).** "Life" is *nephesh*, often translated "soul." BDB's basic sense is "that which breathes," the living being. As blood drains, life ebbs. Blood stands for life itself, and life belongs to God.
- **"I have given it for you on the altar to make atonement for your souls" (v11).** This is the key verse on sacrifice in the Old Testament. The word *nephesh* is used three times in the verse: the *life* of the flesh is in the blood, atonement for your *souls* (*nephesh*), and the blood makes atonement "by the life." A life is given for a life. God himself provides the means (**"I have given it"**). Sacrifice is not people bribing God. It is God's gift, a way he made for sinners to be spared. Because blood was set apart for this holy purpose, it could not be treated as food.
- **"It is the blood that makes atonement by the life" (v11).** Hebrews sums up the principle: "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins" (Heb 9:22).
- **"Pour out its blood and cover it with earth" (v13).** Wild game could not be offered on the altar, but its blood still had to be treated with respect: poured out and buried. JFB notes that pagan hunters sometimes poured out blood as an offering to the god of the hunt. Israel was to cover it instead. Exposed blood elsewhere in Scripture cries out to God (Gen 4:10; Job 16:18; Isa 26:21), so covering it was a kind of burial, giving the life back to its Maker.
- **"For the life of every creature is its blood: its blood is its life" (v14).** The reason is repeated, in case anyone missed it.
- **"What dies of itself or what is torn by beasts" (vv15–16).** An animal that died naturally or was killed by a predator still had its blood in it. Eating it made a person unclean, and he had to wash and bathe. If he refused, **"he shall bear his iniquity."** A minor uncleanness became real guilt when the simple remedy was ignored. Elsewhere such meat is to be thrown to the dogs (Exod 22:31) or given or sold to a foreigner (Deut 14:21).
- **Does this rule still apply?** When the early church welcomed Gentiles, the Jerusalem council asked them to "abstain… from what has been strangled, and from blood" (Acts 15:20, 29). Most Christians understand this as a way to keep fellowship with Jewish believers at shared meals, since Paul later teaches that food itself does not commend us to God (1 Cor 8:8; Col 2:16). A minority of Christians still abstain from blood as food, pointing to the ban's roots in Noah, before Moses. Both groups agree on the deeper point: life belongs to God, and atonement comes through blood.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "it is the blood that makes atonement for the soul" because the sinner deserved to die and another life had to be given in his place. He adds that "the blood of beasts is no longer the ransom, but Christ's blood only," since "the coming of the substance supersedes the shadow." Still, he thinks the law teaches lasting lessons: a horror of cruelty and a reverence for Christ's blood. JFB says God "reserved the blood to Himself and allowed men only one use of it," for sacrifice. Guzik notes that if Israel had to treat even an animal's blood with such care, treating the blood of Christ as common is far worse (Heb 10:29).

#### Pointing to Christ
Leviticus 17:11 explains why the cross had to be bloody. "The life of the flesh is in the blood," and God gave blood on the altar to make atonement. Jesus "entered once for all into the holy places… by means of his own blood" (Heb 9:12). The same blood Israel was forbidden to drink, Jesus offers to his people: "Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life" (John 6:54). The law kept blood from Israel's table because it belonged to God for atonement. In Christ that atoning life is given to us, so that "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7).
