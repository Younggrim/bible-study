*Setting:* Paul writes from Corinth in about AD 57, near the end of his third journey, to a church in Rome he has never visited, a mix of Jewish and Gentile believers. In 3:21–26 he announced the heart of the gospel: God's righteousness is given "through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe," and so all boasting is excluded (3:27). He then claimed that this does not overthrow the law but upholds it (3:31). Romans 4 is the proof. Paul goes back to Abraham, the founder of Israel and the most honored man in Jewish memory, and shows that even he was put right with God by faith and not by works. If that is true of Abraham, it is true of everyone.

#### Abraham Justified by Faith (vv1–12)
- **"What then shall we say was gained by Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh?" (v1).** Paul writes as a Jew to Jews ("*our* forefather"). In his day many Jewish writings praised Abraham as a model of law-keeping. Sirach says he "kept the law of the Most High," and some writings even describe him as sinless. If anyone could have earned God's approval, it was Abraham. "According to the flesh" also sets up the turn later in the chapter: he is father by birth to Jews, but father by faith to "us all" (v16). One important early manuscript (Codex Vaticanus) lacks the word "gained," so the question would simply be "What shall we say about Abraham?" The sense is the same either way.
- **"He has something to boast about, but not before God" (v2).** Paul takes up "boasting" again from 3:27. Abraham may have looked impressive to people, but before God no one has a claim. Paul uses the word "justified" (*dikaioō*) in its courtroom sense: declared to be in the right.
- **"Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness" (v3).** This is Genesis 15:6, the key text of the chapter. God had just taken Abram outside and told him to count the stars: "So shall your offspring be" (Gen 15:5). He was old and childless, and he took God at his word. Paul's whole argument turns on the question "what does the Scripture say?" rather than on tradition.
- **"Counted" (vv3–11, 22–24).** The Greek *logizomai* is a bookkeeping word: to count, reckon, credit to someone's account. It appears eleven times in this chapter (ESV "counted"). It translates the Hebrew *chashab* in Genesis 15:6, which also means to reckon or regard. Righteousness was not something Abraham produced and God then noticed. God credited it to him. The same word for God not counting sin (v8) is used in 2 Corinthians 5:19: "not counting their trespasses against them."
- **Wages and gifts (vv4–5).** A worker's pay is not a favor. It is owed. Paul's unstated point is that God never owes his creatures anything. So whatever he gives is a gift. The person who "does not work but believes" is not lazy. He has stopped trying to make God his debtor and simply trusts him.
- **"Him who justifies the ungodly" (v5).** This is the most startling phrase in the chapter. The law told Israel's judges never to "acquit the wicked" (Exod 23:7; Prov 17:15). Yet God does exactly that, and Paul has already explained how he can do it and still be just: through the sacrifice of Christ (3:25–26). The word "ungodly" (*asebēs*) fits Abraham too. Joshua 24:2 says his family "served other gods" beyond the Euphrates. God called him out of idolatry, not out of moral achievement.
- **David's witness (vv6–8).** Paul adds a second witness, as the law required two (Deut 19:15). Abraham is from the law (Genesis), David from the writings (Psalm 32:1–2). Jewish teachers often linked passages by a shared key word, and the link here is "count": Genesis says righteousness was counted, and the psalm says sin is *not* counted. David knew what he was talking about. Psalm 32 is traditionally read in light of his adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah, sins for which the law offered no sacrifice. His only hope was forgiveness freely given.
- **"Forgiven… covered… will not count" (vv7–8).** Three pictures of one mercy. Sins are lifted off and carried away, covered from God's sight, and never entered on the record. Notice what David calls "blessed." It is not the man who never sinned, but the man whose sin God has dealt with.
- **Before or after circumcision? (vv9–10).** Some Jewish readers might reply that this blessing still belongs only to the circumcised. Paul answers from the order of events in Genesis. Abraham is counted righteous in Genesis 15. He is circumcised in Genesis 17, when he was ninety-nine (Gen 17:24). Ishmael was born in between, when Abraham was eighty-six (Gen 16:16), so at least thirteen or fourteen years separate the two. God accepted Abraham while he was, in Jewish terms, a Gentile.
- **"A sign… a seal" (v11).** Genesis calls circumcision "a sign of the covenant" (Gen 17:11). Paul adds "seal" (*sphragis*), the stamp pressed into wax to certify a document as genuine. A seal does not create what it certifies. It confirms what is already true. Circumcision confirmed a righteousness Abraham already had by faith.
- **Father of two families (vv11–12).** The purpose was to make Abraham father of uncircumcised believers (Gentiles who trust God) and also of circumcised believers who "walk in the footsteps" of his faith. The phrase pictures walking in a line behind someone, stepping where he stepped. Physical descent alone does not make someone Abraham's child (compare John 8:39; Gal 3:7).
- **Paul and James.** James also quotes Genesis 15:6 and says Abraham was "justified by works" when he offered Isaac (Jas 2:21–23). Most Protestant readers see no contradiction: Paul is asking how a sinner is accepted by God, and James is asking what real faith looks like, so James points to Genesis 22 as faith shown to be living. Roman Catholic teaching reads justification as including inner renewal as well as a declared verdict, and so holds faith and works together differently. Some recent scholars (the "New Perspective") stress that Paul's "works of the law" refers especially to markers like circumcision that separated Jew from Gentile, though most still see a wider point about earning. All agree Paul's main claim here: Abraham was accepted by trusting God's promise, before anything he did.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws on the banking image for *logizomai*: righteousness is deposited to Abraham's account, as money is credited to a depositor. The lexicon supports the image: the word's basic sense is to count or reckon. He presses that justifying the ungodly is not a legal fiction but a real verdict made possible by the cross. Henry hears David's psalm and sums it up: "Pardoned people are the only blessed people." He calls sacraments "signs to represent and instruct, seals to ratify and confirm," and says Abraham's true children are "not those that sit in their chairs, and bear their names, but those that tread in their steps." JFB describes the person who "worketh not" as one who, "despairing of acceptance with God by 'working' for it," casts himself on God's mercy. JFB treats vv9–12 as one block, with no separate comment on verses 10–12.

#### The Promise Realized Through Faith (vv13–25)
- **"Heir of the world" (v13).** Genesis promised Abraham a land (Gen 12:7; 15:18), countless offspring, and blessing for "all the families of the earth" (Gen 12:3). By Paul's day Jewish writers already spoke of Abraham inheriting the whole world. Paul agrees, and sees the promise widened in Christ: the meek "shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5), and God's people will share the renewed creation (Rom 8:17–21). The point here is the source of the promise: it came "through the righteousness of faith," not through the law, which was given some 430 years later (Gal 3:17).
- **"Faith is null and the promise is void" (v14).** If the inheritance depended on keeping the law, faith would be emptied of meaning and the promise would be cancelled, because no one keeps the law well enough to claim it.
- **"The law brings wrath" (v15).** The law is good (7:12), but it cannot give life to people who break it. It can only define the offence and announce the penalty. "Transgression" (*parabasis*) means stepping over a clearly marked line. Sin existed before Moses (5:13), but the law turned it into deliberate, conscious law-breaking.
- **"That is why it depends on faith… rest on grace and be guaranteed" (v16).** Faith and grace belong together. If the promise rested on human performance, it would be as shaky as the people trying to keep it. Because it rests on grace, it is "guaranteed" (*bebaios*, firm, secure) to all Abraham's offspring, Jew and Gentile alike. "The father of us all" is said to a mostly Gentile church in Rome.
- **"I have made you the father of many nations" (v17).** From Genesis 17:5, where God changed Abram ("exalted father") to Abraham ("father of a multitude"). Note the tense: "I *have* made you," said when he had one son who was not the heir and none by Sarah. God speaks of it as already done.
- **"Who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (v17).** Abraham's faith rested on what God is like. This is the God of creation, who speaks things into being (Gen 1; Ps 33:9), and the God of resurrection. Jewish prayers of the time praised God as the one who "gives life to the dead." Both descriptions fit Abraham's case exactly: Sarah's womb was dead, and the nations of believers did not yet exist.
- **"In hope he believed against hope" (v18).** Every natural ground for hope was gone, so he hoped in God instead. He held on to one sentence: "So shall your offspring be" (Gen 15:5).
- **"His own body, which was as good as dead" (v19).** The word is *nenekrōmenon*, "having been made dead," and Sarah's barrenness is literally her womb's "deadness" (*nekrōsis*). Paul is preparing for v24, where God raises Jesus from the dead. Abraham was "about a hundred years old" (Gen 17:17).
- **"Considered" or "did not consider"? (v19).** The earliest manuscripts read that Abraham "considered" his body, and modern translations follow them. Later manuscripts add "not," which is where the KJV gets "he considered not his own body." Both make sense. The older reading is the stronger picture: Abraham looked the facts full in the face and still trusted God. His faith was not denial of reality.
- **Abraham's faith, honestly told.** Paul says "no unbelief made him waver" (v20), yet Genesis records Abraham laughing at the promise (Gen 17:17), trying to secure an heir through Hagar (Gen 16), and twice passing Sarah off as his sister (Gen 12; 20). Paul is not claiming Abraham never struggled. He is describing the settled direction of his trust in God's promise. Faith that saves is not flawless faith. It is faith in a faithful God.
- **"No unbelief made him waver" (v20).** "Waver" (*diakrinō*) here means to be divided in one's mind, to hesitate between two options. Jesus uses the same word for doubt in prayer (Mark 11:23), and James for the double-minded (Jas 1:6). Abraham did not keep reopening the question.
- **"He grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God" (v20).** Trusting God's word honors him, and giving him that honor strengthened Abraham's trust. This is the opposite of 1:21, where humanity "did not honor him as God or give thanks to him." Abraham's story reverses the fall.
- **"Fully convinced that God was able" (v21).** *Plērophoreō* means fully assured, carried along to full persuasion. Paul uses it again of a person "fully convinced in his own mind" (14:5). The question was never whether Abraham was able, but whether God was.
- **"Not written for his sake alone, but for ours also" (vv23–24).** Genesis 15:6 was recorded as a pattern. The God Abraham trusted to bring life from a dead womb is the God who "raised from the dead Jesus our Lord." The object of faith is the same God, now known by his greatest act. Paul expects all Scripture to work this way (15:4; 1 Cor 10:11).
- **"Delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification" (v25).** This verse may be an early Christian confession. "Delivered up" (*paradidōmi*) is the word for handing someone over to judgment, used of Judas, of Pilate, and of the Father "who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (8:32). It echoes Isaiah 53, where the servant is "pierced for our transgressions" and, in the Greek Old Testament, "delivered up for our sins" (Isa 53:5; 53:12). The two halves are balanced. The Greek preposition *dia* in both can mean "because of" or "for the sake of." Most read it as: he died to deal with our sins, and was raised to bring about our justification. The resurrection is God's public declaration that the payment was accepted.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees the God who "gives life to the dead" as also describing believers, who were dead in sin (Eph 2:1). He notes that Abraham's faith was not passive: he and Sarah acted in reliance on the promise. He favors the "did not consider" reading at v19, which the earliest manuscripts do not support, though his point about faith resting on God's power stands either way. He calls v25 a summary of the whole gospel. Henry, on why we doubt: "Our waverings rise mainly from our distrust of the divine power." On v25 he uses a debtor's image: "By the merit of his death he paid our debt, in his resurrection he took out our acquittance," meaning the receipt that he was set free. JFB, working from the KJV, says Abraham "paid no attention to those physical obstacles, both in himself and in Sarah." On v15 it adds, "It is just the law that makes transgression," and on v25 it takes both uses of "for" as "on account of," the second meaning "in order to." JFB gives verses 16 and 23 no separate comment.

#### Pointing to Christ
Abraham believed that God could bring life out of a body as good as dead, and he was counted righteous. Christians believe the same God did something greater: he brought his Son back from the dead after handing him over for our sins. Abraham looked forward to a promised offspring. Paul later names that offspring plainly: "who is Christ" (Gal 3:16). The faith is the same faith, and the righteousness counted to Abraham is counted to everyone who trusts the God "who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord."
