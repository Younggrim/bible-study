*Setting:* Chapter 39 ended with Isaiah telling Hezekiah that everything in his palace, and some of his descendants, would be carried off to Babylon. Chapter 40 opens on the other side of that disaster. The tone changes completely, from warning to comfort, and the setting moves forward about 150 years to the Jewish exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC, whose city and temple lie in ruins. Babylon has replaced Assyria as the great power. Readers have long debated how these chapters came to be written. The traditional view, held by Jesus' contemporaries and by many conservative scholars, is that Isaiah himself wrote them by prophetic foresight, speaking ahead of time to people not yet born. Many modern scholars think a later prophet in the exile wrote chapters 40–55 in Isaiah's tradition. The New Testament quotes these chapters under Isaiah's name (Matt 3:3; John 12:38–41). Either way, chapter 40 is the overture to the rest of the book. Its themes return again and again: comfort, the way through the desert, the enduring word, good news, the shepherd, and God beyond compare.

#### Comfort for God's People (vv1–5)
- **"Comfort, comfort my people, says your God" (v1).** The doubled word is for emphasis, like a parent saying "There, there." The command is plural, so God is telling a group of messengers, perhaps prophets or the heavenly council, to comfort his people. Notice "my people" and "your God." This is covenant language (Exod 6:7). The exile looked like the end of that relationship, but the first word is that God still claims them. "Comfort" (*nacham*) means to console, but also to give relief and encouragement.
- **"Speak tenderly to Jerusalem" (v2).** Literally "speak to the heart of Jerusalem." The same phrase is used of a man wooing a woman (Gen 34:3; Hos 2:14) and of reassuring someone who is afraid (Gen 50:21). God speaks to his people like a husband coming to bring his wife home.
- **"Her warfare is ended" (v2).** "Warfare" (*tsava'*) means military service, a hard term of duty. The time of punishment has been served.
- **"Her iniquity is pardoned" (v2).** The deeper problem was never Babylon. It was sin, and the comfort starts there.
- **"She has received from the LORD's hand double for all her sins" (v2).** This phrase is much discussed. BDB gives *kiflayim* simply as "the double." Some take it as meaning she has been punished twice as much as she deserved, but that would make God unjust. Most read it as a figure for full, complete punishment, "ample" as JFB says. Motyer, followed by Guzik, connects the word with folding a sheet in half, so that the punishment exactly matches the sin, like the two halves of a folded page. The lexicon gives "double" and does not decide that question. The main point is clear: the account is settled.
- **"A voice cries: 'In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD'" (v3).** When a king traveled in the ancient world, workers went ahead to fill potholes, cut through hills and straighten the road. Babylonian kings built grand processional ways for their gods. Here the road is for the LORD himself, and it runs through the desert between Babylon and Jerusalem. It is a new Exodus: as God led Israel through the wilderness from Egypt, he will come through the wilderness again. In chapter 35 the people traveled the highway to God. Here God travels it to them, as the Tyndale notes point out.
- **John the Baptist (v3).** All four Gospels apply this verse to John (Matt 3:3; Mark 1:3; Luke 3:4–6; John 1:23), and John applied it to himself: "I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness." The Gospels follow the Greek translation, which reads "a voice of one crying in the wilderness," while the Hebrew puts the wilderness with the road: "In the wilderness prepare the way." Both are true of John, who preached in the wilderness to prepare people for the Lord. And the "LORD" whose way he prepared turned out to be Jesus. Malachi 3:1 builds on this verse: "I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me."
- **"Every valley shall be lifted up, and every mountain and hill be made low" (v4).** Road building on a grand scale. Luke quotes vv4–5 at length (Luke 3:5–6), and the picture became one of preparing hearts: the proud brought low, the humble lifted up (Luke 1:52).
- **"The glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together" (v5).** Ezekiel saw God's glory leave Jerusalem before the exile (Ezek 10:18–19; 11:23). Here it returns, and not just for Israel but for "all flesh." John says of Jesus, "We have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father" (John 1:14). Luke ends his quotation with "all flesh shall see the salvation of God," following the Greek.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is the will of God that his people should be a comforted people, even in the worst of times." On v3 he writes, "Make room for Christ: Make straight a highway for him." JFB sees the double "Comfort" as "twice repeated to give double assurance," and reads "double" in v2 as "ample punishment in her twofold captivity," not twice what she deserved. Guzik follows Motyer on "double" as a folded sheet, exact payment for sin, and stresses that pardon is not God simply letting people off.

#### The Word of God Stands Forever (vv6–8)
- **"A voice says, 'Cry!' And I said, 'What shall I cry?'" (v6).** A second voice, and this time the prophet answers, as Isaiah did in his call (6:8, 11). The traditional Hebrew text reads "And he said," but the great Isaiah scroll from the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek read "And I said," which the ESV follows. His question sounds tired: what can I say, when people are like grass?
- **"All flesh is grass, and all its beauty is like the flower of the field" (v6).** In Israel, grass and wildflowers spring up after the winter rains and are burned brown within weeks by the hot east wind off the desert. "Beauty" is *chesed*, the word normally used for God's steadfast love and loyalty, here meaning human goodness or faithfulness. Even the best of human loyalty does not last.
- **"When the breath of the LORD blows on it" (v7).** "Breath" is *ruach*, which also means wind and spirit. The desert wind that withers the grass is pictured as God's own breath. JFB prefers "wind of Jehovah." **"Surely the people are grass."** This includes Israel and the mighty Babylonians alike.
- **"The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever" (v8).** This is the anchor of the chapter. People fail, empires fall and promises made by kings are forgotten, but what God has said will happen. Isaiah returns to this in 55:10–11: "my word… shall not return to me empty." Peter quotes vv6–8 and then adds, "And this word is the good news that was preached to you" (1 Pet 1:24–25). Jesus said, "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35). James also borrows the picture of the withering flower for the rich (Jas 1:10–11).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the word of the Lord "sure and what may be safely relied on," set against the beauty of human power that fades. Guzik ties this passage to Peter's use of it, where the enduring word is the ground of a believer's new birth and love for others (1 Pet 1:22–25). JFB contrasts human transience with "God's promises alone steadfast."

#### The Greatness of God (vv9–31)
- **"Go on up to a high mountain, O Zion, herald of good news" (v9).** "Herald of good news" is *mebasseret*, from *basar*, "to bring good news." The Greek Old Testament translates it with *euangelizō*, the root of the New Testament word "gospel." The form is feminine. Either Zion herself is the messenger, telling the towns of Judah (ESV), or the messenger brings good news *to* Zion (KJV, JFB). Messengers climbed hills so their voices would carry (52:7).
- **"Behold your God!" (v9).** The entire good news fits into three words. Not "Behold your rescue" or "Behold your home," but your God himself.
- **"The Lord GOD comes with might, and his arm rules for him" (v10).** The arm is God's power in action, as in the Exodus "with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm" (Deut 26:8). **"His reward is with him."** He comes bringing the exiles home as the reward of his victory. Jesus echoes this: "Behold, I am coming soon, bringing my recompense with me" (Rev 22:12).
- **"He will tend his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms" (v11).** The mighty arm of v10 is the same arm that carries the lambs. Power and tenderness are in the same God. Shepherds carried weak or newborn lambs in the fold of their cloak against their chest, and they moved slowly with ewes heavy with young or nursing. The picture is of a God who adjusts his pace to the weakest of the flock. Psalm 23, Ezekiel 34 and John 10 all build on this. Henry: "He takes particular care of those that most need his care, the lambs that are weak."
- **"Who has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand and marked off the heavens with a span?" (v12).** A span is the width of a spread hand from thumb to little finger, about nine inches. God holds the oceans in his cupped palm and measures the sky with his hand. These are rhetorical questions, and the answer is "only God."
- **"Who has measured the Spirit of the LORD, or what man shows him his counsel?" (v13).** No one advises God. Paul quotes this verse at the end of his great discussion of God's plan in Romans 11:34: "Who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?" and in 1 Corinthians 2:16.
- **"The nations are like a drop from a bucket… the dust on the scales" (v15).** A drop of water falling from a bucket, or a speck of dust on a merchant's scale too small to affect the weight. That is how Babylon looks to God. "Coastlands" refers to the islands and shores of the Mediterranean, the far ends of the known world.
- **"Lebanon would not suffice for fuel, nor are its beasts enough for a burnt offering" (v16).** If every cedar in Lebanon were cut down for an altar fire and every animal in its forests offered, it would still not be a sacrifice worthy of him.
- **"To whom then will you liken God?… An idol!" (vv18–19).** The answer to the question is a sarcastic exclamation. In Babylon the exiles would have seen magnificent gold-plated statues of Marduk and other gods carried in procession. Isaiah describes how they are made: a craftsman casts the metal, a goldsmith covers it with gold and makes silver chains for it.
- **"He who is too impoverished for an offering chooses wood that will not rot… to set up an idol that will not move" (v20).** Even a poor man wants a god, so he picks a good piece of wood and hires a craftsman to make sure it does not topple over. The irony is sharp: a god you have to keep from falling over (41:7; 46:7; 1 Sam 5:3–4). The Hebrew of the first line is difficult, and some versions read it differently.
- **"Do you not know? Do you not hear? Has it not been told you from the beginning?" (v21).** Four questions, like a teacher with a student who should know better. Creation itself has been saying this since the beginning (Ps 19:1; Rom 1:20).
- **"It is he who sits above the circle of the earth, and its inhabitants are like grasshoppers" (v22).** "Circle" is *chug*. BDB defines it as "vault," used only three times, of the dome of the sky (Job 22:14) and the horizon drawn on the face of the deep (Prov 8:27). Some readers have taken it as a hint that the earth is a sphere, as JFB does. That may read more into the word than it holds. It most likely means the circle of the horizon or the vault of the sky arching over the earth. Its point is God's throne high above everything. From that height, people look like grasshoppers, which is how the Israelite spies felt next to the giants of Canaan (Num 13:33).
- **"Who stretches out the heavens like a curtain, and spreads them like a tent to dwell in" (v22).** To God, the sky is as easy to put up as a tent is for a nomad (Ps 104:2). The Tyndale notes observe that this contradicts Babylon's belief that the heavenly bodies were gods.
- **"Who brings princes to nothing" (v23).** The rulers of Babylon, too. **"Scarcely are they planted, scarcely sown… when he blows on them, and they wither" (v24).** The grass picture of vv6–8 returns. The empire Nebuchadnezzar built fell to Cyrus of Persia only about twenty-three years after his death.
- **"To whom then will you compare me, that I should be like him? says the Holy One" (v25).** Now God himself asks the question of v18. "The Holy One" is Isaiah's great title, God utterly set apart.
- **"Lift up your eyes on high and see: who created these?" (v26).** The Babylonians were the great astronomers of the ancient world, and they worshiped the stars as gods and read the future from them (47:13). Isaiah tells the exiles to look at the same night sky and see not gods but an army under God's command. **"He who brings out their host by number, calling them all by name."** Like a general calling the roll each night, God brings the stars out, and "not one is missing." Psalm 147:4 says the same, right after "He heals the brokenhearted."
- **"Why do you say, O Jacob… 'My way is hidden from the LORD, and my right is disregarded by my God'?" (v27).** Here is what the whole chapter has been building toward. The exiles' real problem is not unbelief in God's existence. It is the feeling that God has stopped noticing them, that their "way" (their life and circumstances) is hidden from him, and their "right" (their cause, their case for justice) has been dropped. The argument of vv12–26 answers it: if God calls every star by name and none goes missing, he has not lost track of Israel. The Tyndale notes link this with the laments of the exile (49:14; Lam 5:20).
- **"The LORD is the everlasting God, the Creator of the ends of the earth. He does not faint or grow weary" (v28).** The God of Israel is not a local deity defeated when his temple fell. He is eternal and made the whole earth, Babylon included. **"His understanding is unsearchable."** His ways may not be explained, but he never runs out of strength or understanding.
- **"He gives power to the faint, and to him who has no might he increases strength" (v29).** The God who never tires gives his strength to those who have none.
- **"Even youths shall faint and be weary, and young men shall fall exhausted" (v30).** Even the strongest people, young men in their prime, run out. Natural strength is not enough. Motyer, followed by Guzik, notes that "faint" refers to the failure of a person's own strength and "weary" to exhaustion from hard labor.
- **"But they who wait for the LORD shall renew their strength" (v31).** "Wait" is *qavah*. BDB suggests its root idea may be twisting or stretching, as of a cord drawn tight, then the tension of enduring and waiting. A related noun means a cord or line. Whatever the root, it means waiting with eager expectation, looking to God, not passive sitting. **"Renew"** is *chalaf*, to change or exchange, as one puts on fresh clothes: they exchange their weakness for his strength.
- **"They shall mount up with wings like eagles" (v31).** The Hebrew word *nesher* can mean eagle or vulture, a great bird that rides the thermals without flapping. God carried Israel out of Egypt "on eagles' wings" (Exod 19:4; Deut 32:11). Psalm 103:5 says God renews youth "like the eagle's." JFB notes the old belief that eagles moulted and renewed their feathers. **"They shall run and not be weary; they shall walk and not faint."** The order seems to go down, from soaring to running to walking. Many readers see this as true to life: there are moments of soaring, seasons of running, and long stretches of simply walking on without giving up. That last, plain kind of strength may be the greatest gift of all.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees vv12–26 as the greatness of a God "what no creature can compare with, much less contend with," and he describes idols as "despicable things and worthy of the greatest contempt." On v31 he writes, "Pious and devout affections are the eagles' wings on which gracious souls mount up." Guzik warns against practical atheism, believing God exists but living as if he does not care, which is the complaint of v27, and stresses that waiting means actively relying on God. JFB reads the "circle of the earth" as the globe and the eagle's renewal as moulting. The lexicon gives "vault" for *chug*, which favors the sky or horizon rather than a sphere.

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 40 begins with a voice in the wilderness preparing the way for the LORD, and the Gospels open with John the Baptist doing exactly that for Jesus. The glory of the LORD was revealed when "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). The good news, "Behold your God!" came true in a person. Jesus called himself the good shepherd who knows his sheep and lays down his life for them (John 10:11), the one with the mighty arm who still carries the lambs. And the strength promised in v31 is given through him to those who wait: "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Cor 12:9).
