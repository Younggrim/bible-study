*Setting:* The long debate is over. Job's three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, have spoken in three rounds (chs 4–25), and each time their answer was the same: God punishes the wicked, Job is suffering, so Job must have sinned. Job has answered every speech, and in chapters 29–31 he summed up his case, ending with a sweeping oath of innocence and a challenge for God himself to answer: "Here is my signature! Let the Almighty answer me!" (31:35). The narrator closed it: "The words of Job are ended" (31:40). Everyone expects God to speak next. Instead a new voice breaks in. Elihu, a younger man who has been listening the whole time, speaks for six chapters (chs 32–37), the longest single speech in the book. Chapter 32 is his introduction. He explains who he is, why he is angry and why he cannot keep quiet any longer, and almost all of it is about himself before he has said a word about Job's case. Then God answers out of the whirlwind (chs 38–41). How to read Elihu, as God's forerunner, as one more wrong-headed counselor, or as something in between, is one of the great questions of the book.

#### Elihu Rebukes Job's Three Friends (vv1–5)
- **"So these three men ceased to answer Job" (v1).** The friends do not concede. They stop, because "he was righteous in his own eyes." The phrase is the narrator reporting their view, not God's verdict. God had already called Job "blameless and upright" (1:8), and at the end he will say the friends "have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (42:7). The silence is the silence of men who have run out of arguments without changing their minds.
- **A prose interlude (vv1–5).** Like the opening chapters (chs 1–2) and the ending (42:7–17), these verses are prose, a narrator's voice between the poems. Elihu's speech proper starts in verse 6, in poetry.
- **"Elihu the son of Barachel the Buzite, of the family of Ram" (v2).** No one else in the book gets a pedigree like this. The three friends are named only by their towns (2:11). Elihu gets his father, his clan and his family. His name means "He is my God" (the lexicon gives "He is (my) God"), and his father's, Barachel, means "God blesses." Buz was the name of a son of Nahor, Abraham's brother, and Buz's brother Uz shares a name with Job's homeland (Gen 22:21; Job 1:1). Jeremiah lists Buz with the desert peoples of Arabia (Jer 25:23). So Elihu is a kinsman from the same eastern world as Job, and perhaps a distant relative of Abraham's family. "Ram" is uncertain. It may be a clan name, or a form of Aram, who is also in Nahor's family line (Gen 22:21).
- **"Burned with anger" (vv2–5).** The phrase comes four times in four verses, a drumbeat. The Hebrew literally speaks of anger that "burned" or "was kindled." Elihu is angry at Job "because he justified himself rather than God" (v2), that is, Job defended his own innocence at the expense of God's justice. Job had said things like "he has taken away my right" (27:2), and to Elihu that was the heart of the problem. The Tyndale notes suggest Elihu saw his anger as zeal for God's honor. Readers have long asked whether it was entirely that.
- **Angry at the friends too (v3).** They "had found no answer, although they had declared Job to be in the wrong." They condemned him without being able to prove it. That is fair criticism, and God will agree (42:7).
- **A scribal note on v3.** An ancient Jewish scribal tradition lists this verse among a small number of places where scribes said they had softened the wording out of reverence. By that tradition the line originally said the friends "declared *God* to be in the wrong," meaning that by failing to answer Job they had in effect let God's justice lose the argument. The ESV keeps the standard Hebrew text, and either way the point is that the friends' failure left the case unanswered.
- **"Elihu had waited to speak... because they were older than he" (v4).** In the ancient Near East age spoke first. The friends themselves had leaned on it: "Both the gray-haired and the aged are among us, older than your father" (15:10). Elihu has been sitting at the edge of the circle for all thirty chapters, which the reader learns only now.
- **Commentators:** Henry traces the name the same way: "a Buzite, from Buz, Nahor's second son," and he reads "Ram" as Aram, "whence the Syrians or Aramites descended." He explains the detail simply: "Elihu was not so well known as the rest, and therefore is more particularly described." JFB gives the name as "God is Jehovah" and sees Elihu as foreshadowing Christ, pointing ahead to the "mediator" of 33:23–26. The lexicon gives "He is my God," and the name does not contain the divine name in that way, so the gloss should not be pressed. Guzik passes on the old guesses about who Elihu "really" was (Balaam, Ezra, even Christ) and dismisses them with Adam Clarke as finding mysteries where there are none. He also notes that some critics think the Elihu speeches were added later, but takes the detailed family line as a sign that he was a real man. On the anger, Guzik observes that Elihu is caught in the same either/or as the friends: if Job is right, God must be wrong. The book's ending shows that God can be just and Job can be sincere at the same time.

#### Elihu Rebukes Job's Three Friends (vv6–14)
- **"I am young in years, and you are aged" (v6).** Elihu opens with an apology. "Timid" translates a word whose root JFB connects with crawling, as a small creature keeps low (compare Deut 32:24, where the same root is used of things that crawl in the dust). He was afraid to speak up.
- **"Let days speak, and many years teach wisdom" (v7).** This was the received rule, and Scripture honors it (Lev 19:32; Prov 16:31). Elihu had held to it.
- **"But it is the spirit in man, the breath of the Almighty, that makes him understand" (v8).** This is Elihu's key claim. "Breath" is *neshamah*, the word used when God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" and man became a living creature (Gen 2:7). Elihu will say it again: "the breath of the Almighty gives me life" (33:4). Understanding is a gift of God, not a product of age.
- **What kind of spirit?** Two readings. Some, like JFB, hear Elihu claiming something like prophetic inspiration, a special word from God for Job. Others, including the Tyndale notes, think he means the God-given spirit that every human being has by creation (Acts 17:25), so that a young man has as much right to think as an old one. The second is more modest, but by verse 18 Elihu sounds like a man who thinks God has filled him.
- **"It is not the old who are wise" (v9).** The Hebrew word translated "old" is literally "great" or "many," but in this context it means many in years, and the ESV rightly reads it so. Elihu is not saying old people are never wise, only that years alone do not guarantee it. Scripture agrees: David says, "I understand more than the aged, for I keep your precepts" (Ps 119:100), and Paul tells Timothy, "Let no one despise you for your youth" (1 Tim 4:12). But the other side is true as well. The young who know they are right are not always right.
- **"I waited for your words" (vv11–12).** He listened carefully, and "there was none among you who refuted Job." Elihu agrees with Job on one thing at least: the friends lost the argument.
- **"Beware lest you say, 'We have found wisdom; God may vanquish him, not a man'" (v13).** Elihu blocks the friends' last retreat. They might say, "Job is too stubborn for us. Let God deal with him." Elihu will not let them hide their failure behind God. A man ought to be able to answer Job, and Elihu intends to be that man.
- **"I will not answer him with your speeches" (v14).** Job never addressed Elihu, so Elihu comes to the argument fresh, and promises a new approach. Whether he delivers it is debated. Some of his points repeat the friends. But he does bring something new: suffering as God's discipline and warning, sent to rescue a man rather than to punish him (33:14–30; 36:8–15).
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline gives Elihu's five reasons for speaking: he lacked an old man's experience but "had the understanding of a man"; he "had patiently heard all they had to say"; he had something new to offer; he was full, so speaking would be "a refreshment to him"; and he would speak impartially. Henry is the most sympathetic of the three and notes that "God gave him no rebuke" at the end, when the friends were rebuked. JFB reads verse 8 as a claim to inspiration "as a divinely commissioned messenger to Job." Guzik grants that Elihu's principle in verse 9 is sound (he passes on the old proverb about beardless sages and gray-haired children), but he hears in vv10–14 a young man already sure he will do far better than his elders.

#### Elihu Rebukes Job's Three Friends (vv15–22)
- **"They are dismayed; they answer no more" (v15).** Elihu switches from speaking *to* the friends to speaking *about* them, as if turning to Job or to the onlookers. JFB notes this change of address is a familiar move in a rebuke. The Tyndale notes point out that translations differ on whether vv15–16 are addressed to the friends or to Job, but the sense is the same: the old men have gone quiet.
- **"Shall I wait, because they do not speak?" (v16).** He waited for them out of respect. Now that they have stopped, respect no longer requires silence.
- **"I also will answer with my share" (v17).** Literally "my part." He wants his turn in the debate.
- **"I am full of words; the spirit within me constrains me" (v18).** The word for spirit is *ruach*, which also means "breath" or "wind." Elihu means the spirit pressing him to speak. But the book has already used *ruach* for empty talk: Eliphaz mocked Job's "windy knowledge" (15:2) and Job asked, "Shall windy words have an end?" (16:3). The Tyndale notes observe that the reader may wonder which kind of *ruach* fills Elihu. The text leaves it to the reader, and his speech must be judged by what it says.
- **"Like wine that has no vent; like new wineskins ready to burst" (v19).** New wine kept fermenting after it was poured into goatskins. The gas needed a vent. Without one, even a new, stretchy skin would split. Jesus used the same everyday picture when he said new wine must go into new wineskins (Matt 9:17). Elihu is the young man bursting with new wine while the old men have run dry. The image is vivid and a little comic. He takes twenty-two verses to announce that he is about to speak.
- **"I must speak, that I may find relief" (v20).** Literally, "that there may be room (or air) for me." Zophar had said something similar: "my thoughts answer me, because of my haste within me" (20:2). There is a holy compulsion to speak: Jeremiah said God's word was "a burning fire shut up in my bones" (Jer 20:9). But speaking to relieve one's own pressure is not the same as speaking to help a sufferer.
- **"I will not show partiality to any man or use flattery" (vv21–22).** "Show partiality" is literally "lift up the face," the Hebrew idiom for favoring someone because of who he is. Job had accused the friends of exactly this, of showing partiality for God by lying on his behalf (13:7–10). "Flattery" translates a verb that means to give someone a title or a name of honor. Elihu will not soften his words with polite titles for the great man Job or the elder friends. "Else my Maker would soon take me away" (v22): he believes God himself would judge a flatterer.
- **The length matters.** Elihu's speeches run longer than any other single speaker's in the book, longer even than God's. Yet no one answers him, Job does not reply, and God, when he speaks, neither praises nor rebukes him. He is simply passed over. Readers fall into three main camps. Some, following older interpreters like Henry, see Elihu as a true messenger who prepares the way for God, and note that much of what he says, especially about God's greatness in the storm (chs 36–37), leads straight into the whirlwind. Others see a brash young man who repeats the friends' errors with more words and more confidence. Many land between: Elihu says true and valuable things, especially that suffering can be God's discipline rather than his punishment, but he says them with too much heat and too little compassion, and he still assumes Job must be guilty of something.
- **Commentators:** Henry hears in vv18–20 the heart of a faithful minister who is "full of matter" and suffers when silenced: "I shall burst like bottles of new wine when it is working." He reads v20 as "It is a great refreshment to a good man to have liberty to speak for the glory of God and the edification of others." He also notes the friends "seemed not fully satisfied themselves with what they had said, else they would have adjourned the court." JFB explains "refreshed" literally as "that there may be air to me" and reads the new wineskins as the young Elihu set against the old friends. It links vv21–22 to Job's charge that the friends were partial to God (13:8). Guzik is much less impressed. He notes that Elihu claims he cannot flatter anyone while flattering himself throughout, and he passes on one scholar's joke that it takes Elihu twenty-four verses to say "Look out, I'm going to speak!" (Elihu's introduction runs on into 33:7). Guzik calls him a young man painfully unaware of how he sounded.

#### Pointing to Christ
Elihu enters at the moment everyone is waiting for God to speak, and he claims the breath of the Almighty gives him understanding. Whatever one makes of him, he points beyond himself. The book needs more than another voice in the debate. It needs God to answer, and God will. In the fullness of time God did more than speak out of a storm. He sent his Son, on whom the Spirit rested without measure (John 3:34), who was young by the elders' reckoning and still astonished the teachers with his understanding (Luke 2:46–47). Jesus spoke without partiality and without flattery (Matt 22:16), yet "a bruised reed he will not break" (Isa 42:3). Elihu was full of words and burning with anger. Christ is the Word himself (John 1:1), full of grace and truth (John 1:14). He does not speak to relieve his own pressure but to bring relief to the weary: "Come to me... and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).
