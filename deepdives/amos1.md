*Setting:* Amos preached around 760 BC, in the long reigns of Uzziah of Judah (792–740 BC) and Jeroboam II of Israel (793–753 BC). Assyria had crushed Damascus about 800 BC and then fell into a weak spell of its own, so for forty years no great power pressed on Israel. Jeroboam won back old territory (2 Kings 14:25–28), trade boomed, and the rich built winter houses, summer houses and houses inlaid with ivory (3:15). Religion was busy too, at the royal shrines of Bethel and Gilgal. But the wealth rested on courts that could be bought, debt slavery and the crushing of the poor. Into this confident, prosperous kingdom God sent a man from the south. Amos was not a professional prophet. He came from Judah, crossed the border into Israel and preached at Bethel, the king's own sanctuary (7:10–13). He was a contemporary of Hosea in Israel, and slightly earlier than Isaiah and Micah in Judah. His book opens with a sermon that seems at first to be about everyone except Israel.

#### Judgment on Israel's Neighbors (vv1–15)
- **"The words of Amos" (v1).** His name probably means "carried" or "burden-bearer," from a root meaning "to load." Guzik notes he should not be confused with Amoz, Isaiah's father, a different name in Hebrew. "Words… which he saw" is a striking phrase. Prophets did not just hear God's message, they saw it, by vision or revelation (Isa 1:1; 2:1). Amos will report five visions in chapters 7–9.
- **"Who was among the shepherds of Tekoa" (v1).** Tekoa was a small town on the edge of the Judean wilderness, about ten miles south of Jerusalem and six south of Bethlehem, on high, dry ground suited to sheep and goats. The word for shepherd here is *noqed*, used elsewhere only of Mesha king of Moab, who paid Israel a tribute of 100,000 lambs (2 Kings 3:4). The Tyndale notes point out that it may mean a sheep-breeder or owner of flocks, not just a hired hand. Later Amos calls himself a "herdsman" (*boqer*) and "a dresser of sycamore figs" (7:14). So he was a working man, perhaps with a fair-sized business, but certainly not trained in the schools of the prophets and not on anyone's payroll. Henry pictures him as "a poor keeper of cattle." Guzik compares him with the fishermen Jesus called.
- **"Concerning Israel" (v1).** "Israel" here means the northern kingdom, the ten tribes, with its capital at Samaria. Amos does speak to Judah and to the nations, but his main audience is the north.
- **"Two years before the earthquake" (v1).** This earthquake was so severe that it was remembered for centuries. Over two hundred years later Zechariah could say "as you fled from the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah" (Zech 14:5), and everyone knew which one he meant. Archaeologists have found signs of a major earthquake around 760 BC at Hazor, Gezer and other sites, with walls collapsed and tilted, and geologists estimate it was very large. Jewish tradition, recorded by Josephus, linked it to the day Uzziah burned incense in the temple and was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21), but that is tradition, not the text. The note tells us Amos's ministry was short, perhaps less than a year, and that the earthquake came after it, which his hearers may have taken as God confirming his warnings (8:8; 9:1).
- **"The LORD roars from Zion" (v2).** The verb is *sha'ag*, the roar of a lion about to spring (3:4, 8). Henry: "The lion roars before he tears; God gives warning before he strikes." Amos the shepherd knew what a lion's roar meant for a flock (3:12). The LORD roars "from Zion… from Jerusalem," the place of the temple, not from Bethel or Dan, where the northern kingdom worshiped. Guzik notes that this was a pointed reminder of where true worship belonged.
- **Joel's last word is Amos's first.** In the Hebrew order of the twelve Minor Prophets, Joel comes right before Amos. Joel 3:16 reads, "The LORD roars from Zion, and utters his voice from Jerusalem." In Joel the roar is judgment on the nations and protection for God's people. Amos takes the same words and turns them: the lion is about to roar against Israel.
- **"The pastures of the shepherds mourn, and the top of Carmel withers" (v2).** The roar of God dries up the land from bottom to top, from the shepherds' pastures in the south to Carmel in the north. Mount Carmel, on the coast near modern Haifa, gets heavy rain and stays green even in summer. Its name means "garden land" or "orchard." If Carmel withers, nothing is safe. The Tyndale notes call it a sign of a severe and widespread drought. Carmel also recalls Elijah's contest with the prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18), which Guzik suggests may have been in mind.
- **"Thus says the LORD" (v3).** This is the messenger formula, the words a royal messenger used to begin the king's message. Each of the eight oracles in chapters 1–2 begins with it.
- **"For three transgressions of Damascus, and for four" (v3).** This is a Hebrew numerical saying, like "three things are too wonderful for me; four I do not understand" (Prov 30:18; also Prov 30:15–31; Job 5:19). It does not mean exactly four sins. Henry: "that is, with many transgressions." Guzik puts it as sin piled upon sin. The cup is full, and the last sin named is the one that overflows it. JFB suggests that three plus four, seven, may also hint at the full measure of guilt. Amos names only one sin for each nation, the fourth.
- **"Transgressions" (v3).** The word is *pesha'*, "rebellion." It is the word used when a subject nation revolts against its overlord (2 Kings 1:1; 8:20). These nations had no covenant with God like Israel's. Their sin is rebellion against the King of all the earth, who has written certain basic laws on every human conscience (Rom 1:20; 2:14–15). None of these oracles charge the nations with breaking the law of Moses. They are charged with crimes against human beings: atrocities, slave trading, betrayal.
- **"I will not revoke the punishment" (v3).** Literally "I will not turn it back." The "it" is not defined. It probably means the judgment, or perhaps the roar itself, God's word once spoken. JFB: God will not "reverse" the sentence. The phrase is repeated in all eight oracles.
- **Damascus: "They have threshed Gilead with threshing sledges of iron" (vv3–5).** Damascus was the capital of Aram (Syria), Israel's enemy to the northeast. Gilead was Israel's territory east of the Jordan, the frontier with Aram. A threshing sledge was a heavy wooden board studded underneath with sharp stones or iron teeth, dragged over grain to break the heads from the stalks (Isa 41:15). To "thresh" a land with iron sledges is a picture of grinding it to pieces, whether literally, by dragging captives under them, as some ancient writers thought, or figuratively. Henry allows both. The background is 2 Kings 10:32–33 and 13:3–7, when Hazael of Damascus left Israel's army "like the dust at threshing." Elisha had wept when he foresaw what Hazael would do (2 Kings 8:12).
- **"The house of Hazael… the strongholds of Ben-hadad" (v4).** Hazael seized the throne of Damascus about 843 BC by murdering the king (2 Kings 8:15). His son Ben-hadad succeeded him. The Tyndale notes date them about 843–802 and 802–792 BC. "Fire" in these oracles is the fire of war, as invading armies burned the cities they took. Each oracle except Israel's repeats it, like a drumbeat.
- **"The gate-bar of Damascus… the Valley of Aven… Beth-eden" (v5).** The gate-bar was the beam that locked a city's gates. Breaking it means the city falls open. "Aven" means "wickedness," probably a nickname for a valley of Aram, perhaps the Bekaa between the Lebanon ranges, and a pun on a city's real name. Beth-eden ("house of delight") was an Aramean state on the Euphrates. Both point to the whole Aramean world.
- **"The people of Syria shall go into exile to Kir" (v5).** In 9:7 God says he brought the Arameans up from Kir, perhaps in Mesopotamia. So judgment is a reversal of their history, a return to where they started. It happened about 732 BC, when Tiglath-pileser III of Assyria took Damascus, killed its king Rezin and deported its people "to Kir" (2 Kings 16:9). Guzik and JFB both point to that fulfillment.
- **Gaza: "They carried into exile a whole people to deliver them up to Edom" (vv6–8).** Gaza was the southernmost of the five Philistine cities, on the coast road between Egypt and Asia, a natural trading hub. Its crime was mass slave trading: raiding whole villages and selling everyone, men, women and children, to Edom, which probably passed them on to markets in Arabia. Guzik, drawing on Boice, notes that taking captives in war was common in the ancient world, but enslaving entire populations for profit was a deeper evil. Joel 3:4–8 makes a similar charge against Philistia and Tyre.
- **"Ashdod… Ashkelon… Ekron" (v8).** Four of the five Philistine cities are named. Gath is missing. The Tyndale notes and JFB explain that Gath had already been conquered by Hazael (2 Kings 12:17) and then by Uzziah (2 Chr 26:6), and was no longer a major power. **"The remnant of the Philistines shall perish."** Uzziah broke down the walls of Ashdod, and the Assyrians later subdued all these cities. By the Persian period the Philistines had vanished as a distinct people. **"Says the Lord GOD"** is *Adonai Yahweh*, the sovereign Lord, a favorite title of Amos.
- **Tyre: "They delivered up a whole people to Edom, and did not remember the covenant of brotherhood" (vv9–10).** Tyre was the great Phoenician seaport, on an island just off the coast of modern Lebanon. Its crime was the same slave trade, made worse by treachery. "Covenant of brotherhood" likely means a treaty. Hiram king of Tyre had been a friend of David and Solomon (2 Sam 5:11; 1 Kings 5:1, 12), and Hiram called Solomon "my brother" (1 Kings 9:13). Ahab of Israel later married Jezebel of Sidon. Henry: "Herein they forgot the brotherly covenant." The Tyndale notes add that Tyre had a reputation as a city where anything could be bought (Isa 23). The island city was very hard to take. Nebuchadnezzar besieged it for thirteen years, and Alexander the Great finally captured it in 332 BC by building a causeway out to it.
- **Edom: "He pursued his brother with the sword and cast off all pity" (vv11–12).** Edom, south and east of the Dead Sea, descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Gen 25:23–30; 36:1). So Edom's "brother" is Israel by blood. The grudge went back to Esau's anger at Jacob (Gen 27:41), and Edom kept it alive for a thousand years (Num 20:14–21; 2 Kings 8:20–22; Obadiah). "Cast off all pity" is literally "destroyed his compassions." The word is *rachamim*, from the word for womb, the tender feeling between children of one mother. Edom crushed exactly the feeling brothers should have. **"His anger tore perpetually, and he kept his wrath forever."** "Tore" (*taraph*) is a wild animal tearing prey. Henry: "Those that are least courageous are commonly most cruel." Guzik applies it: hatred nursed for years becomes a beast that devours.
- **"Teman… Bozrah" (v12).** Teman, meaning "south," was a region of Edom famous for its wise men (Jer 49:7; Job 2:11). Bozrah (modern Buseirah in Jordan) was its capital. Babylon broke Edom's power in the sixth century BC.
- **Ammon: "They have ripped open pregnant women in Gilead, that they might enlarge their border" (vv13–15).** The Ammonites, east of the Jordan, descended from Lot, Abraham's nephew (Gen 19:38). Like Damascus, they wanted Gilead. Their crime was an atrocity that ancient armies sometimes committed (2 Kings 8:12; 15:16; Hos 13:16) to terrorize a population and wipe out the next generation, so that no one would be left to claim the land. JFB makes that point, and Guzik says they sinned against the future by destroying unborn children. The motive makes it worse: all this for a little more land.
- **"I will kindle a fire in the wall of Rabbah" (v14).** Rabbah, "the great one," was Ammon's capital, today's Amman in Jordan. **"With shouting on the day of battle, with a tempest in the day of the whirlwind."** War cries and storm: the attack would come with sudden, overwhelming force, as JFB says.
- **"Their king shall go into exile, he and his princes together" (v15).** "Their king" in Hebrew is *malkam*. That is also the name of Ammon's god, Milcom (1 Kings 11:5, 33), so many hear a double meaning. Jeremiah 49:3 repeats the line and clearly means the god: "Milcom shall go into exile, with his priests and his officials." The Tyndale notes and JFB mention this reading. In the ancient world, a conquered nation's gods were carried off with them, and here the god goes into exile alongside the king.
- **The shape of the sermon.** The oracles circle Israel: Damascus to the northeast, Gaza to the southwest, Tyre to the northwest, Edom to the southeast, then Ammon and Moab to the east (2:1–3). Then they close in on Judah (2:4–5), Israel's own brother. Picture a crowd at Bethel cheering as each enemy is condemned. The Tyndale notes say the order starts with the most obvious enemy and ends with the least obvious. Seven oracles feel complete. Then comes the eighth, and the lion's roar lands on Israel itself (2:6).
- **Are all these oracles original?** Some scholars think the oracles on Tyre, Edom and Judah were added later, because they are shorter and lack some phrases the others share. Others answer that a skilled speaker varies the pattern and that the sermon needs all eight to work. Nothing in the text itself requires the theory, and the book presents the whole speech as from Amos.
- **Commentators:** Henry summarizes the reason for these judgments as the nations' cruelty to God's people and ties it to God's pleading with the nations in Joel 3. JFB notes that an Assyrian obelisk names Hazael, and he calls the Tyrian treaty the league of Hiram with David and Solomon. All three take "three… and four" as many sins, not an exact number. Guzik stresses that God judged these nations by standards of humanity they knew without the written law, and he finds the fulfillment of the Damascus oracle in 2 Kings 16:9.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's roar in this chapter is a judgment on cruelty: grinding down the weak, selling people for profit, hatred kept alive forever, violence against the unborn. Every nation is accountable to him, whether or not it has his law. That is bad news for them, and it is meant to be bad news for Israel, and for every reader who cheers when someone else is condemned. The lion who roars from Zion is the same "Lion of the tribe of Judah" who "has conquered" (Rev 5:5), and when John turns to look at him, he sees "a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (Rev 5:6). The God who will not turn back his judgment took it upon himself in Christ, so that people from every nation named here, Syrian, Philistine, Phoenician, Edomite and Ammonite, could be ransomed (Rev 5:9).
