*Setting:* Israel's camp is still at Gilgal by the Jordan, where it has been since the crossing. The main battles are over, and the work of dividing the land west of the Jordan begins. Chapter 13 recorded the land Moses had already given east of the river. Chapter 14 introduces the division of Canaan itself (chapters 14–19), and before a single lot is cast, the story stops for one old man. Caleb was one of the twelve spies sent into Canaan forty-five years before. Only he and Joshua told Israel to trust God and go in (Num 13–14). Now he comes to claim what God promised him. The long section on the land begins with Caleb receiving his inheritance and ends with Joshua receiving his (19:49–51), the two faithful spies framing the whole.

#### The Inheritance West of the Jordan (vv1–5)
- **"Eleazar the priest and Joshua the son of Nun and the heads of the fathers' houses" (v1).** God had named this committee years earlier: Eleazar, Joshua, and one leader from each tribe (Num 34:16–29). Caleb himself was Judah's representative (Num 34:19). Eleazar, Aaron's son and the high priest, is named first. JFB explains why: the division was to be made by lot, "a solemn appeal… to God," so the priest leads. Having every tribe represented meant no tribe could claim it was cheated.
- **"Their inheritance was by lot" (v2).** Moses had commanded this (Num 26:52–56; 33:54). The lot was probably a set of marked stones or pieces drawn or thrown, perhaps connected with the Urim and Thummim the high priest carried (Num 27:21). Proverbs 16:33 explains the idea: "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD." The lot set the general location of each tribe, and the size of each tribe's share was then adjusted to its population (Num 26:54). Each family could know that its land came from God, not from a leader's favor.
- **"For Moses had given an inheritance to the two and one-half tribes beyond the Jordan" (v3).** A quick recap of chapter 13, to explain why only nine and a half tribes are left.
- **"The people of Joseph were two tribes, Manasseh and Ephraim" (v4).** Jacob had adopted Joseph's two sons as his own (Gen 48:5), giving Joseph the double share that belonged to the firstborn (1 Chr 5:1–2). With Levi receiving no territory and Joseph counting as two, the land is still divided into twelve portions. The number twelve is kept, even though the names shift.
- **"Only cities to dwell in, with their pasturelands" (v4).** The Levites were not landless and homeless. They received towns with surrounding grazing land for their animals, spread through all the tribes (Num 35:1–8). Chapter 21 lists them.
- **"The people of Israel did as the LORD commanded Moses" (v5).** The obedience formula again (11:15). JFB notes that this probably means they began the preparations, since surveying the land would take time (18:4–9).
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that the land "would have been subdued in vain if it had not been inhabited." He notes that the ten princes chosen years before seem to have all been alive and present, so that every tribe "might be satisfied that there was fair dealing." JFB stresses that Eleazar is named before Joshua because a lot was a direct appeal to God. Guzik explains how Israel can be twelve tribes, or thirteen, depending on whether Levi is counted and Joseph's sons are counted separately.

#### Caleb's Request and Inheritance (vv6–15)
- **"The people of Judah came to Joshua at Gilgal" (v6).** Caleb does not come alone. He brings the leaders of his own tribe. JFB and Henry both suggest why: Caleb was one of the officials in charge of dividing the land, and he did not want anyone to think he had used his position to grab a prize for himself. He makes his request openly, with his tribe as witnesses.
- **"Caleb the son of Jephunneh the Kenizzite" (v6).** The Kenizzites were originally a non-Israelite people, listed among the nations of Canaan in God's promise to Abraham (Gen 15:19). The name Kenaz also appears in the family of Esau (Gen 36:11). Caleb's family seems to have joined Israel at some point, perhaps at the exodus with the "mixed multitude" (Exod 12:38), and became part of Judah. By the time of the spies, Caleb represented the whole tribe (Num 13:6). One of the two most faithful men of his generation had roots outside Israel, like Rahab and later Ruth.
- **"You know what the LORD said to Moses… concerning you and me" (v6).** Joshua and Caleb were the only two left of the adults who came out of Egypt. Everyone else from that generation, all the men twenty and older, had died in the wilderness (Num 14:29–30; 26:64–65). They shared a memory nobody else in the camp had.
- **"I brought him word again as it was in my heart" (v7).** Caleb's report was honest. He said what he believed, not what would please the crowd. When the other spies frightened the people, Caleb hushed them and said, "Let us go up at once and occupy it, for we are well able to overcome it" (Num 13:30).
- **"My brothers… made the heart of the people melt" (v8).** The same phrase used of the Canaanites' terror when they heard of Israel (2:11; 5:1). Forty-five years earlier, the ten spies did to Israel what the LORD later did to Canaan.
- **"Yet I wholly followed the LORD my God" (v8).** This phrase appears three times in this short passage (vv8, 9, 14). In Hebrew it reads literally "I filled after the LORD," meaning followed him completely, with nothing held back. It is God's own description of Caleb from Numbers 14:24: "my servant Caleb, because he has a different spirit and has followed me fully." Caleb is quoting what God said about him. Henry calls it no vainglory, "any more than it is for those who have God's Spirit witnessing with their spirits that they are the children of God" to speak of what God has done.
- **"The land on which your foot has trodden" (v9).** Moses' promise is recorded in Deuteronomy 1:36: "to him and to his children I will give the land on which he has trodden." The spies' journey took them through Hebron, where they saw the sons of Anak (Num 13:22). Caleb's foot walked the very hills that terrified everyone else, and God promised him those hills.
- **"The LORD has kept me alive… these forty-five years… I am this day eighty-five years old" (v10).** The numbers give the timeline of the conquest. Caleb was forty at Kadesh in the second year after the exodus. Thirty-eight years of wandering followed (Deut 2:14), so the conquest has taken about seven years. Caleb survived the wilderness that buried his whole generation. As Henry puts it, while we live "it is God that keeps us alive."
- **"I am still as strong today as I was in the day that Moses sent me" (v11).** Moses wrote that a long life reaches seventy or eighty years, and even then "their span is but toil and trouble" (Ps 90:10). Caleb is an exception, and he credits the LORD. "For war and for going and coming" means for leading men in the field, as the same phrase is used of a military leader in Numbers 27:17.
- **"So now give me this hill country" (v12).** At eighty-five Caleb could have asked for a quiet farm in a settled valley. He asks for the hardest ground in the land, the hills around Hebron, held by the Anakim with "great fortified cities." It is exactly the place that made the ten spies say Israel could never win (Num 13:28, 33). Caleb wants to prove, in his old age, that what he said at forty was true.
- **"It may be that the LORD will be with me, and I shall drive them out" (v12).** "It may be" is not doubt about God's power. It is humility about himself. Caleb does not presume that God owes him victory. He rests on God's word ("just as the LORD said") and on God's presence ("the LORD will be with me"), not on his own strength.
- **"Then Joshua blessed him, and he gave Hebron to Caleb" (v13).** The two old friends from Kadesh stand together once more. Joshua's blessing is a prayer for God's help, and the grant is made outside the lot, because God had already decided it (Henry). Hebron was one of the most important places in Israel's history: Abraham lived there and bought the cave of Machpelah to bury Sarah (Gen 13:18; 23), and Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and their wives were buried there. It would later become David's first capital (2 Sam 2:1–4). Later still it became a city of refuge and a Levitical city, while Caleb's family kept its fields and villages (20:7; 21:11–12).
- **How was Hebron taken?** Chapter 10:36–37 described Joshua striking Hebron, and 11:21 says Joshua cut off the Anakim from Hebron. Chapter 15:13–14 says Caleb drove out the three sons of Anak there, and Judges 1:10 credits the men of Judah. Most likely Joshua's campaign broke the city's power, the Anakim later returned or held out in the hills, and Caleb, with Judah's help, finished the job. Ancient writers often credit a leader with what his people did under him.
- **"Because he wholly followed the LORD, the God of Israel" (v14).** The narrator adds his own verdict, agreeing with Caleb and with Moses. The inheritance is a reward for faithfulness, but Caleb's faithfulness was itself trust in God's promise.
- **"The name of Hebron formerly was Kiriath-arba" (v15).** "City of Arba," or possibly "city of four" (perhaps four clans or four villages). The text explains it as named after Arba, "the greatest man among the Anakim." The city of the greatest giant goes to the man who trusted God when everyone else saw only giants.
- **"And the land had rest from war" (v15).** The same words that closed chapter 11. Caleb's request comes in a time of general peace, but he chooses to go on fighting.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Caleb "this phoenix of his age," and notes that "Those that follow God fully when they are young shall have both the credit and comfort of it when they are old." He adds that God not only kept his promise to Caleb but gave more than promised: strength along with life. Henry also mentions the old view that "Kenizzite" might be a title from a victory over the Kenizzites, the way Roman generals took the names of lands they conquered, but the simpler reading, which the Tyndale notes take, is that his family came from that people. JFB says Joshua "not only admitted the claim, but in a public and earnest manner prayed for the divine blessing" on Caleb's efforts. Guzik points out that the ten spies measured the giants by their own strength while Caleb measured them by God's. He stresses that Caleb asked for a fight rather than an easy retirement, and that growing older does not have to mean growing weaker in faith.

#### Pointing to Christ
Caleb's life turned on one choice made forty-five years earlier: to believe God's promise when everyone around him believed their fear. He waited a long time, through a wilderness he did not deserve, for a reward God had sworn to give, and God kept him alive to receive it. Hebrews 11 lists people who "died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar" (Heb 11:13). Caleb was one of the few who lived to see his promise kept on earth. For every believer the final inheritance comes through another faithful one. Where Israel failed, Jesus "wholly followed" his Father without fail, "obedient to the point of death" (Phil 2:8), and God gave him the inheritance of the nations (Ps 2:8). Those united to him are "fellow heirs" (Rom 8:17), and the giants of sin and death cannot keep them from it.
